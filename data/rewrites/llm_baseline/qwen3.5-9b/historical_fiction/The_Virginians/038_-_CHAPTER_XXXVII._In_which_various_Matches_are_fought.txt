Mr. Warrington broke his fast while reading the *London Advertiser*, which had been delivered alongside his meal. An advertisement caught his eye, inviting all lovers of manly British sport to witness a trial of skill between two renowned champions, Sutton and Figg. Determined not to miss such an event, he resolved to attend. He mounted the pair of horses he had purchased the previous day and set off for the Wooden House in Marybone Fields.

His young driver, unfamiliar with the route, navigated with excessive caution, weaving and turning far more often than necessary. He became momentarily lost in the green lanes trailing behind Mr. Whitfield's Round Tabernacle near Tottenham Road and the grounds surrounding Middlesex Hospital. Eventually, however, he arrived at his destination.

A considerable crowd had already gathered to witness the valorous feats of the two champions. Around the doors of this "temple of British valour" stood a motley assembly of London ruffians, mixed with the carriages and equipages of fashionable patrons like Mr. Warrington. Beggars and cripples pressed against the young gentleman, whining for alms; shoe-black boys scrambled over one another to polish his boots; women selling nosegays and fruit hawked their wares; and a chaotic mix of pie-men, peddlers, tramps, and street performers surrounded the arena. A flag fluttered atop the building, and a manager, flanked by a drummer and a horn-player, stepped onto the stage to announce that the noble English sports were about to commence.

Mr. Warrington paid his admission and secured a seat in a gallery offering a clear view of the platform. Below, in the amphitheatre, sat Mr. Gumbo. When weary, Gumbo would retreat to the outer world to drink a pot of beer or gamble with his fellow servants and the coachesmen waiting outside. The scene was dominated by the remnants of old society—lacquies, liveried footmen, and valets. It was noted that grand estates and chapels were once encumbered by armies of these men, who swarmed hallways, guzzled ale, and bullied visitors for tips. Though the race was largely extinct, having passed away like the Red Indians of America, a few thousand still lingered, seen occasionally on levee days with their powdered hair, buckles, and plush coats. Yet, the fatal decree had been issued; figures like Uncas with his tomahawk and Jeames with his cocked hat were fading from the world.

Before the main event, several minor contests took place. A boxing match occurred, yet neither fighter displayed much courage, receiving little punishment, which offered scant entertainment to the spectators. This was followed by cudgel-play, but the injuries inflicted were trivial and unimpressive, prompting the crowd to hiss and grow restless.

"The masters!" the people shouted.

Finally, the famous champions appeared. First ascended the steps the intrepid Sutton, sword in hand. He saluted the company, making a special bow toward a private box occupied by a stout gentleman of apparent importance. Soon after, the celebrated Figg entered, receiving a wave of approval from the same spectator. Both men were shirtless, their heads shaved clean but marked with the scars of past battles. Each wore an "armiger"—a ribbon denoting his color—around his burly sword arm.

The gladiators shook hands, and as a contemporary poet noted, "The word it was bilboe."

At the start of the combat, Figg struck with such tremendous force that, had the blow landed true, it would have severed Sutton's head cleanly. However, Sutton deftly intercepted the strike with his own sword. The impact was so violent that Figg's weapon snapped in his grip, breaking under the strain. New swords were distributed, and the fight resumed. The first blood spilled from Figg's side, drawing a cheer from Sutton's supporters. Yet, the veteran appealed to the audience—and specifically to the stout gentleman in the private box—revealing that the injury resulted from the earlier breakage of his sword.

During this brief pause, Mr. Warrington observed a gentleman in a riding frock and plain wig entering the box. Recognizing his friend from Tunbridge Wells, Lord March and Ruglen, Harry felt a surge of pleasure. Lord March, usually sparing with compliments, showed singular deference to the stout gentleman, accepting bank-notes with a profound bow. As the Earl concluded his business, he spotted the Virginian in the gallery. Once his conversation ended, Lord March approached Harry warmly.

They watched the duel unfold with varying degrees of success, though both men displayed immense skill and bravery. After exhausting themselves with swords, the warriors switched to quarter-staves. The prolonged and delightful battle ultimately favored the ancient champion, Figg.

As the combat raged, a thunderstorm broke over the building. Seizing the opportunity, Mr. Warrington gladly transferred to Lord March's carriage, leaving his own phaeton to be driven home by his groom. Harry was thoroughly delighted by the spectacle, declaring it the finest sport he had witnessed since arriving in England.

"I wish..." he sighed, pausing mid-thought.

"No, I don't," he corrected himself immediately.

"What do you wish, and what don't you wish?" Lord March asked.

"I was thinking, my lord, of my elder brother," Harry replied. "We promised to enjoy such sports together at home. Many times we discussed it. But he wouldn't have liked this rough kind of fighting, nor did he care for it, though he was the bravest lad alive."

"Oh! He was the bravest lad alive, was he?" Lord March queried, lounging on his cushion and studying his young friend with curiosity.

"You should have seen him in a quarrel with a very gallant officer," Harry continued. "It was an absurd affair, but it was hard to keep George from engaging. I never saw a fellow so cool, nor more savage and determined. For the honor of the country, I wish he had come here instead of me, to show you a true Virginian gentleman."

"Nay, sir, you'll do very well," the amused nobleman responded. "What is this I hear of Lady Yarmouth taking you into favor?"

"I shall do as well as anyone else," Harry insisted. "I can ride, and I believe I can shoot better than George. But then, my brother possessed the mind, sir—the head!" He tapped his own skull. "He had read nearly every book ever written; he could play the fiddle and harpsichord; he composed elegant poetry and sermons. What can I offer? Only riding, card-playing, and drinking Burgundy."

Penitently, he lowered his head.

"But those things I can do as well as most fellows," he added, recovering his spirits. "In fact, my lord, I'll back myself."

Lord March relished the young man's naive confidence, much as a jaded voluptuary enjoys a fresh, wholesome chop of meat.

"By Gad, Mr. Warrington," the Earl declared, "you ought to be taken to Exeter Change and put in a show."

"And for what reason?"

"A gentleman from Virginia who has lost his elder brother and mourns him deeply. Such a breed is unknown here. Upon my honor and conscience, I believe you would prefer to have him back."

"Believe!" cried the Virginian, flushing red. "That is, you believe you believe you would like him back. But depend on it, you wouldn't. 'Tis not in human nature, sir—not as I understand it."

As the carriage rolled along, pointing out landmarks like Sir Richard Littleton's house and the converted Montagu House, now a museum filled with stuffed birds and rhinoceroses, the conversation shifted. Lord March lamented the chaos of the new roads and the changing city.

"Shall we go down Tyburn Road and the Park, or through Swallow Street to the habitable quarter?" the Earl suggested. "We can dine at Pall Mall, or perhaps with you. Spend the evening as you like—with the Queen of Spades, or..."

"With the Queen of Spades, if your lordship pleases," Mr. Warrington blushed, agreeing.

The carriage proceeded to his hotel in Covent Garden. The landlord, recognizing Lord March, bowed low, pressing his wig against the Earl's shoes in humble greeting.

A rich young English peer in the reign of George II versus a wealthy patrician in the age of Augustus: which would you choose?

After a sumptuous dinner, wine flowed freely, of quality sufficient even for an epicurean earl. Conversation turned to fireworks at Vauxhall or a game of cards. Harry, having only seen a modest display of squibs in Williamsburg during Guy Fawkes Night, yearned for Vauxhall but yielded to his guest's preference for Piquet.

Soon, they were engrossed in the game. Harry, as usual, started strong, winning steadily. However, within half an hour, fortune turned in favor of Lord March. Just as the Earl grew surly at the shift in luck, Mr. Draper, Mr. Warrington's business agent, entered the room to accept an invitation to join them for a drink.

Mr. Warrington invariably invited everyone to share his hospitality. If he had bread, he shared it; if a haunch of meat, he divided it; if a jug of water, he drank it amicably; if a bottle of Burgundy, he consumed it joyfully with friends.
