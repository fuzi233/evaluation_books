"One day still fierce 'mid many a day struck calm."
—Browning: *The King and the Book*

While Gwendolen remained oblivious to the shifting currents of Daniel Deronda's life, the waters around Ezra and Mirah grew deeper and clearer. The shadow of their father, Lapidoth, had lengthened over them, casting a stark light on the fragile bonds that held them together.

Lapidoth had abandoned his daughter at the threshold, driven by a desperate gamble that promised relief from the gnawing hunger of poverty. In his mind, the thrill of risking a sum greater than mere survival outweighed any future consequences. Until the very last coin was lost, he refused to consider the alternative: facing his son again or confronting the reality of his emptiness. Initially, he had recoiled from Ezra as one might shrink from a physical blow, but the possession of Mirah's purse silenced those fears. The gambling instinct, a voracious demon, demanded constant diversion, rendering all other emotions dull and irrelevant. It was a state of unjoyous dissipation, a frantic search for excitement on the burning sands of ruin.

Yet, even the most abstract selfishness requires the fuel of daily sustenance. Lapidoth's appetite was modest, but his current existence was shabby and unfriendly, demanding ready cash to survive. After a brief visit to a house marked "Pyramids" on the blind, where he had doubled and trebled Mirah's thirty shillings only to lose them all, he emerged with an empty purse. He stood there, weighing two options: pawn the purse for immediate funds or return it to Mirah with a forced smile, claiming he had settled a debt. Beneath his shame lingered a stubborn belief in his own rights; he convinced himself that his claim on his children's property superseded the justice of his son's anger. To him, returning to his children was the safest harbor, and the thought of meeting Ezra became less daunting as he imagined luck turning. Perhaps his daughter's new friends or her affairs would bring supplies his cleverness could exploit. He clung to the old habit of believing in his own wit, unaware that his charm had faded long ago.

His calculations led him to the little square in Brompton. He hoped that by pacing the streets, he might spot Mirah leaving or returning, making his entry into the house easier. But evening had already fallen—the night following the day he first saw her. Weariness set in, and he considered ringing the bell, asking for her expected return time. Yet, as he approached, he knew she was home; he heard her singing.

Inside, Mirah sat at the piano, pouring out the melody of "Herz, mein Herz." Ezra listened with his eyes closed, lost in the music. Suddenly, Mrs. Adam appeared at the door, her face flushed with embarrassment. "A gentleman below says he is your father, miss," she announced.

"I will go down to him," Mirah said, rising instantly and turning to her brother.

"No, Mirah, not so," Ezra replied, his voice firm. "Let him come up, Mrs. Adam."

Mirah stood frozen, her fingers twisting nervously, her stomach churning with anxiety. She watched Ezra, who had also risen, his face pale and shaken. There was an expression on his brow she had never seen before—a knitted severity, lips hardened by a grim resolve that mirrored the steel in his eyes.

When Mrs. Adam opened the door to admit the stranger, she glanced at the group, noting the striking resemblance between the younger man and the elder. "Father, sure enough," she murmured to herself as she closed the door behind them. The outline was unmistakable, but the spirit within had been forged into sharp contrasts by years of hidden suffering and inconspicuous differences.

Lapidoth wore a premeditated look of melancholy, though a genuine wince tightened his frame as he spoke. "Well, Ezra, my boy, you hardly know me after so many years."

"I know you—too well—father," Ezra answered, his tone slow and biting, making the word "father" sting like a reproach.

"Ah, you are not pleased with me. I don't wonder at it. Appearances have been against me. When a man gets into straits, he cannot act as he pleases. I've suffered enough, I know," Lapidoth said quickly, regaining some of his usual glibness. Turning to Mirah, he held out her purse. "Here's your little purse, my dear. I thought you'd be anxious about it because of that bit of writing. I've emptied it, you'll see, for I had a score to pay for food and lodging. I knew you would like me to clear myself, and here I stand—without a single farthing in my pocket—at the mercy of my children. You can turn me out if you like, without getting a policeman. Say the word, Mirah; say, 'Father, I've had enough of you; you made a pet of me, and spent your all on me, when I couldn't have done without you; but I can do better without you now,'—say that, and I'm gone out like a spark. I shan't spoil your pleasure again." Tears welled in his voice before he finished.

"You know I could never say it, father," Mirah answered, her anguish deepened by the sheer falsity of his words, except for his implied wish to remain.

"Mirah, my sister, leave us!" Ezra commanded, his tone authoritative.

She looked at her brother with faltering, beseeching eyes, awed by his decision yet unable to depart without pleading for this father who felt like a part of her own painful growth. She stepped close, taking his hand. "Remember, Ezra—you said my mother would not have shut him out."

"Trust me, and go," Ezra said.

She left the room, but after climbing a few steps, she collapsed onto a landing, her heart pounding. If her brother's words drove him away...

Lapidoth sensed the storm brewing in his son's mind, yet he began to adjust, seeking a perspective that granted him a cool superiority over any humiliation. This haggard son, speaking from a sepulcher, presented an incongruity that selfishness often mocks in suffering. Until disease clutched its own flesh with unrelenting pincers, Lapidoth dismissed the gravity of the moment. Whatever preaching followed would be taken as a matter of course, like religious howling in an open cathedral during a hailstorm. Lapidoth was not born callous; he had cultivated it.

"This home that we have here," Ezra began, his voice steady despite the turmoil beneath, "is maintained partly by the generosity of a beloved friend who supports me, and partly by the labors of my sister, who supports herself. While we have a home, we will not shut you out from it. We will not cast you out to the mercy of your vices. For you are our father, and though you have broken your bond, we acknowledge ours. But I will never trust you. You absconded with money, leaving your debts unpaid; you forsook my mother; you robbed her of her little child and broke her heart; you have become a gambler, and where shame and conscience were there sits an insatiable desire; you were ready to sell my sister—you had sold her, but the price was denied you. The man who has done these things must never expect to be trusted any more. We will share our food with you—you shall have a bed, and clothing. We will do this duty to you, because you are our father. But you will never be trusted. You are an evil man: you made the misery of our mother. That such a man is our father is a brand on our flesh which will not cease smarting. But the Eternal has laid it upon us; and though human justice were to flog you for crimes, and your body fell helpless before the public scorn, we would still say, 'This is our father; make way, that we may carry him out of your sight.'"

Lapidoth, adjusting himself to the coming blow, had not foreseen the intensity of the lightning or its precise path. He could not anticipate this voice from the soul of his son, which struck the spring of hysterical excitability Mirah had once witnessed in him. As Ezra concluded, Lapidoth threw himself into a chair and wept like a woman, burying his face against the table. Strangely, this hysterical crying was both an inevitable reaction to his son's words and a conscious resource, a tool he had used since youth to deflect resentment.

Ezra sat down again, silent, exhausted by the shock of his own irrepressible utterance, the outburst of feelings he had borne in solitude for years. His thin hands trembled on the chair arms; he would hardly have found the voice to answer a question. He felt as though he had taken a step toward beckoning Death.

Meanwhile, Mirah's quick ear detected a familiar sound. Her heart recognized it, and she could not stay out of the room. Opening the door, her immediate alarm was for Ezra. She rushed to his side, taking his trembling hand, which he pressed gratefully. He did not speak or look at her. The father, face buried, was aware of her presence. He lifted his head, pressed a handkerchief to his eyes, extended a hand toward her, and spoke with plaintive hoarseness. "Good-bye, Mirah; your father will not trouble you again. He deserves to die like a dog by the roadside, and he will. If your mother had lived, she would have forgiven me—thirty-four years ago I put the ring on her finger under the *Chuppa*, and we were made one. She would have forgiven me, and we should have spent our old age together. But I haven't deserved it. Good-bye."

He rose as he spoke the final farewell. Mirah had taken his hand and held him tight. She was not tearful or grieving, but frightened and awe-struck. "No, father, no!" she cried. Turning to her brother, she pleaded, "Ezra, you have not forbidden him?—Stay, father, and leave off wrong things. Ezra, I cannot bear it. How can I say to my father, 'Go and die!'"

"I have not said it," Ezra answered, with great effort. "I have said, stay and be sheltered."

"Then you will stay, father—and be taken care of—and come with me," Mirah said, drawing him toward the door.

This was exactly what Lapidoth wanted. For the moment, he felt a strange comfort in recovering his daughter's dutiful attendance, making a change of habits seem possible. She led him down to the parlor below. "This is my sitting-room when I am not with Ezra, and there is a bedroom behind which shall be yours. You will stay and be good, father. Think that you are come back to my mother, and that she has forgiven you—she speaks to you through me." Mirah's tones were imploring, yet she could not offer one of her former caresses.

Lapidoth quickly recovered his composure, beginning to speak to Mirah of the improvement in her voice and other easy subjects. When Mrs. Adam came to lay out his supper, he engaged her in conversation to prove he was not a common person, despite his ragged clothes.

But in his usual wakefulness at night, his mind drifted to wonder what money Mirah possessed. He replayed old Continental hours at *Roulette*, reproducing the methods of his play and the chances that had frustrated him. He had had reasons for coming to England, but for most things, it was a cursed country.

These were the stronger visions of the night for Lapidoth, overshadowing the worn frame of his ireful son uttering a terrible judgment. Ezra did pass across the gaming-table in his memory, and his words were audible; but he passed like an insubstantial ghost, his words having the heart eaten out of them by numbers and movements that seemed to weave the very tissue of Lapidoth's consciousness.
