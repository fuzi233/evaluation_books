The morning after Mr. Hale had come home from that strange, grave talk with Higgins and the pale, watchful girl who clung to her father’s sleeve, he mounted the narrow stairs to the little drawing-room at an hour when he was not commonly seen there. Milton’s day had begun with its usual accompaniments: a low, ceaseless grind as of hidden wheels; a pulse of distant stamping that shook the cups on the shelf; the thin, spiteful whistle of a wind that found every crack in the window-frame and made it sing. Even indoors, the town’s noise had a way of lodging under the skin.

Mr. Hale moved from one object to another, pausing as though to examine a vase, the stitch of the antimacassar, the very pattern of the carpet. Margaret, sewing near the fire, watched the restless pilgrimage. It was not curiosity that drove him; it was delay—a man touching harmless things to keep his fingers from trembling over the thing he feared to say.

At last he stopped short, as if he had come to a wall.

“My dear,” he began, and the words fell quickly, as though he must get them said before his courage cooled, “I’ve asked Mr. Thornton to come to tea to-night.”

Mrs. Hale lay back in her easy-chair, eyes closed, the habitual shadow of pain about her mouth. She started as if the name had been a draught through her thin frame.

“Mr. Thornton!—and to-night!” she cried, querulous in the way illness makes even gentle women sharp. “What in the world does the man want to come here for? And Dixon is washing my muslins and laces, and there is no soft water with these horrid east winds—which I suppose we shall have all the year round in Milton.”

“The wind is veering round, my dear,” said Mr. Hale, turning to look out as if the sky might justify him. The smoke from the chimneys drifted in a determined sheet, and the air outside had that iron taste of coal and wet brick. He spoke of compass-points with the uncertain confidence of one still new to the town, arranging east and west by the mere behaviour of the smoke.

“Don’t tell me!” Mrs. Hale shuddered up and pulled her shawl closer, as though she could shut out a whole climate with a fold of wool. “East or west wind, I suppose this man comes.”

Margaret’s lips curved with a flash of spirit, half amusement, half defiance—more colour than the room had held all morning.

“Oh, mamma, that shows you never saw Mr. Thornton. He looks like a person who would enjoy battling with every adverse thing he could meet with—enemies, winds, or circumstances. The more it rains and blows, the more certain we are to have him. But I’ll go and help Dixon. I’m getting to be a famous clear-starcher. And he won’t want any amusement beyond talking to papa.” She turned her bright, vexed gaze upon her father. “Papa, I am really longing to see the Pythias to your Damon. You know I never saw him but once, and then we were so puzzled to know what to say to each other that we did not get on particularly well.”

Mr. Hale’s face softened; yet there was a little anxious crease between his brows.

“I don’t know that you would ever like him, or think him agreeable, Margaret. He is not a lady’s man.”

Margaret drew her throat back, almost scornfully, as though the phrase itself were something sticky on the tongue.

“I don’t particularly admire ladies’ men, papa. But Mr. Thornton comes here as your friend—as one who has appreciated you—”

“The only person in Milton,” put in Mrs. Hale, with the languid bitterness of one who felt herself exiled among strangers.

“So we will give him a welcome,” Margaret answered, refusing to let the sentence end there, “and some cocoa-nut cakes. Dixon will be flattered if we ask her to make some; and I will undertake to iron your caps, mamma.”

It sounded easy when she said it—like the smoothing of a table-cloth with the palm. Yet, as the morning wore on, she found herself wishing, again and again, that Mr. Thornton were far enough away to make tea a matter of mere family bread and talk. She had meant to write to Edith; she had planned a good stretch of Dante, with its stern, orderly music that could drown Milton’s irregular clamour; she had meant to go down to the Higginses, to carry what little comfort words could carry where there was hunger. All those intentions were put aside and folded away, as neatly as Dixon demanded a cap should be folded, for the sake of an evening’s civility.

In the poky kitchen the air was thick with steam, soap, and that peculiar smell linen has when it is half clean and half still holding the memory of bodies and perfume. Dixon’s great copper bubbled like a temper; every time the wind shoved at the back door the flame under the boiler answered with a sudden roar, and the roof-beams gave a small complaint. Above it all came the town: the far-off, rhythmic thud that seemed to set its own measure to the beating of sheets; the sharp, intermittent shrieks—whether of engine or human throat, Margaret could not always tell—cutting the domestic sounds like a knife.

Dixon complained as she worked, as if complaint were the proper starch to stiffen her resolve against Milton. She lamented the hard water that would not rinse white, the east winds that made the muslin dry rough, the soot that settled like a curse no brushing could wholly remove. Margaret stood for hours, sleeves rolled, fingers reddened, coaxing cloth into obedience with iron and wrist, her mind doing the same thing—pressing down its own impatience for her father’s sake.

She listened, too, with a kind of strained sympathy, hoping that by giving Dixon the full satisfaction of her audience she might prevent the woman from carrying the tale of grievances upstairs to Mrs. Hale, whose nerves were like exposed threads. Margaret spoke little; the constant lift and set of the iron made its own mute conversation. Sometimes the smell of heated starch rose sweetly, like browned flour, and the thought of cocoa-nut cakes—Dixon’s careful beating of eggs, the rasp of the nut, the warm, buttery scent as they baked—seemed the only tender thing the day promised.

But weariness stole over her at last, with a slow, creeping certainty. It began behind her eyes, like a tightening band, and passed down into her throat until even her words felt heavy. More than once she had to remind herself—Mr. Hale’s gentle delight in his new friend, his gratitude at being treated as something other than a dissenting curiosity in Milton—to keep from letting irritation speak.

When at last she sat beside her mother again, she could hardly force her voice into playfulness.

“I am no longer Peggy the laundry-maid, mamma,” she said faintly, trying for a joke, “but Margaret Hale the lady.”

It was meant as a light thing tossed into the air to break the heaviness. But her mother, with pain sharpening every thought, took it as a confession of humiliation, and her colour rose with offended memory.

“Yes!” cried Mrs. Hale, and her voice had in it the old accent of the county drawing-rooms, proud and wounded. “If any one had told me, when I was Miss Beresford, and one of the belles of the country, that a child of mine would have to stand half a day in a little poky kitchen, working away like any servant, that we might prepare properly for the reception of a tradesman—and that this tradesman should be the only—”

“Oh, mamma!” Margaret sat upright, suddenly stung, as if the iron had touched her skin. “Don’t punish me for so careless a speech. I don’t mind ironing, or any kind of work for you and papa. I am myself a born and bred lady through it all—even though it comes to scouring a floor, or washing dishes. I am tired now, just for a little while; but in half an hour I shall be ready to do the same over again.” She drew a breath, and the effort of it showed. “And as to Mr. Thornton’s being in trade—why he can’t help that now, poor fellow. I don’t suppose his education would fit him for much else.”

Even as she defended him, something in her recoiled from the whole necessity—this shaping of their domestic peace to the presence of a man she did not know how to meet without constraint. The headache pressed harder; every sound from the street came in sharper, more jangling lines. She rose slowly and went to her own room, because just then she could not bear much more—neither her mother’s sharp, sick pride nor her own divided feelings.

At that very hour, in another house of Milton, there was a different kind of preparation going forward—one as exact, as severe, as a well-kept account-book.

Mrs. Thornton sat in her dining-room, large-boned and upright, a woman long past middle age whose strength had never been allowed to soften into ease. The room was handsomely furnished in a grim fashion: solid, heavy pieces that looked as though they had been chosen to withstand not fashion but time. She wore stout black silk, spotless as a creed. In her hands she held a great, long table-cloth of the finest texture, lifting it now and then against the light to search out thin places with the keen, practical care that wasted nothing. Her face moved slowly from one decided expression to another—no fluttering shades, no easy smiles; yet one looked twice at her, as passers-by in the street did, half turning their heads to gaze longer at the firm, severe dignity that went straight on without yielding.

There were no scattered books, no idle papers. Only Matthew Henry’s Bible commentaries lay in a neat, unquestioned pile on the massive side-board—six volumes, sombre as authority—flanked by a tea-urn on one side and a lamp on the other, as if devotion and domestic order had their appointed places.

From some remote apartment came the sound of a piano: a morceau de salon practised with great speed and little accuracy. Notes were missed; chords struck false; yet the performer, satisfied with mere loudness at the ends, drove on. In that house the music did not soothe; it jabbed at the air, careless of ear or nerve, an ornament that took no account of silence. Mrs. Thornton worked through it without flinching, as she worked through most things.

She heard a step—decisive, like her own—pass the dining-room door.

“John! Is that you?” she called.

Her son opened the door and showed himself.

“What has brought you home so early?” she asked, without softening her tone. “I thought you were going to tea with that friend of Mr. Bell’s—Mr. Hale?”

“So I am, mother. I am come home to dress!”

“Dress! humph!” Mrs. Thornton’s mouth tightened. “When I was a girl, young men were satisfied with dressing once in a day. Why should you dress to go and take a cup of tea with an old parson?”

“Mr. Hale is a gentleman,” said Mr. Thornton steadily, “and his wife and daughter are ladies.”

“Wife and daughter!” Mrs. Thornton repeated, as if the words were strange coin she must test between finger and thumb. “Do they teach too? What do they do? You have never mentioned them.”

“No! mother,” he answered, with a restrained impatience, “because I have never seen Mrs. Hale; I have only seen Miss Hale for half an hour.”

Mrs. Thornton’s needle paused. Her eyes, strong and unblinking, went over him as if she could read the future in the set of his shoulders.

“Take care you don’t get caught by a penniless girl, John.”

“I am not easily caught, mother, as I think you know.” His voice grew harder, and there was a quick flush, not of shame but of offence. “But I must not have Miss Hale spoken of in that way, which, you know, is offensive to me. I never was aware of any young lady trying to catch me yet, nor do I believe that any one has ever given themselves that useless trouble.”

Mrs. Thornton would not yield, whether from pride or from the habit of standing firm in her own house as she stood firm in the street.

“Well! I only say, take care. Perhaps our Milton girls have too much spirit and good feeling to go angling after husbands; but this Miss Hale comes out of the aristocratic counties, where, if all tales be true, rich husbands are reckoned prizes.”

Mr. Thornton’s brow contracted. He stepped farther into the room, as if he must come nearer to meet this suspicion head-on.

“Mother,” he said, and with the word there was a short, scornful laugh that seemed to crack like a whip in the air, “you will make me confess. The only time I saw Miss Hale, she treated me with a haughty civility which had a strong flavour of contempt in it. She held herself aloof from me as if she had been a queen, and I her humble, unwashed vassal. Be easy, mother.”

“No! I am not easy nor content either,” returned Mrs. Thornton, the very set of her jaw proclaiming her refusal. “What business had she—a renegade clergyman’s daughter—to turn up her nose at you! I would dress for none of them—a saucy set!—if I were you.”

He turned to leave; at the door he spoke back with the controlled courtesy that was, with him, a kind of iron.

“Mr. Hale is good, and gentle, and learned. He is not saucy. As for Mrs. Hale—I will tell you what she is like to-night, if you care to hear.”

He shut the door and was gone, his decisive step swallowed by the house’s other sounds: the piano’s reckless tinkling, the distant clink of work in some unseen room.

Mrs. Thornton sat rigid a moment, needle poised above the fine damask, as though the cloth itself might bear witness to insult.

“Despise my son!” she muttered at last, in a low voice thick with wrath. “Treat him as her vassal, indeed! Humph! I should like to know where she could find such another! Boy and man, he’s the noblest, stoutest heart I ever knew. I don’t care if I am his mother; I can see what’s what, and not be blind. I know what Fanny is; and I know what John is. Despise him!” Her lips pressed into a hard line. “I hate her!”
