A woman carries her own quiet, unspoken troubles, just as a man does—and we male scribes rarely ever do more than trace the barest outline of the griefs that belong to the other sex. We must trust the sharp, gentle intuition of women readers to step in and fill the soft, raw, unwritten spaces between our cold sparse lines. So too have I marked, rather than fully described, every hardship Margaret Brandt endured up to that fateful day when she stepped over Eli’s threshold as an enemy, read her sweetheart’s long-lost letter, and left it bound to their household as a friend. Now the hardest trial a woman can face drew near, and Gerard was leagues away, across the Alps, no word of him reaching her for months.

She rarely leaned into Eli’s sudden new favour, always ready with a gentle, plausible excuse to turn down his invitations to stay long in his home. She never breathed a word of the true reason: two men lodged under that roof, whose faces made her blood run cold the moment they came into sight. She had read unmasked wickedness and smouldering hatred in their stares, caught furtive, secret signals they passed between each other when they thought no one was watching. She preferred to receive Catherine and her daughter in her own small cottage, where no ill wish could seep through the walls. Catherine came to visit her every single day, her whole world wrapped up in the coming birth. She was one of those sturdy, warm Dutch women whose whole calling seems to be bearing and rearing children, the sort who, once their own brood is half-grown, hover around every young expectant mother they know clucking like a mother hen, as if they can multiply their care for little ones by proxy. It was that very instinct that made her badger Eli until he agreed to return to his work in Tergou, leaving her behind in Rotterdam to tend Margaret.
“The poor lass hasn’t a single soul around her who knows the first thing about tending a lying-in woman,” she argued. “What use are those two rough soldiers you leave hanging around the house? Why, that sort of folk ought to be shown the door the second a time like this comes round.”

That arrangement was the greatest comfort Margaret could have asked for. Poor girl, as the days drew near, her anxiety grew sharp: she was half-convinced she would die in childbed, and Gerard would not be there to hold her hand.

But life balances its weights, does it not? The grinding poverty, the burden of tending her helpless blind father that had cost her so many sleepless, weary nights of struggle, now served her well. Adversity’s hard iron hand had forced her to fight off the heavy, weak lassitude that fell on wealthier women of her station when they carried a child, keeping her on her feet spinning, tending the cottage, working right up to the very end, just as Catherine had advised her to.

And so, one soft golden evening, as the sun dipped low over the Rotterdam rooftops and gilded the surface of the canals, she lay weak as water, spent but whole, a tiny warm face tucked at her side, the bright, soft heaven of new motherhood opening up before her.

Catherine noticed tears rolling down her cheek. “Why ever are you weeping, my sweet lass? All of a sudden, when all is well?”
“He is not here to see it,” Margaret whispered.
“Ah, well, never you mind,” Catherine soothed her. “He’ll be home long before that little one is weaned. The good Lord has been kinder to you than any woman alive, hasn’t he? And think, it could have been a girl — why, my own Kate swore she was carrying a girl the whole time she was in labour, and out popped the bonniest, heaviest bouncing boy in all Holland! Praise the saints for that, eh?”

“Ay, mother,” Margaret said, wiping at her eyes with a tired smile. “I’m a selfish, ungrateful wretch to cry. I bore it well enough, him being gone through all my hard, sad days. But oh — it feels so cruel, that he cannot share this joy. Gerard! My dear, dear Gerard, come to me, I beg you!” She stretched out her thin, feeble arms towards the empty air, as if she could reach him across hundreds of miles.

Catherine bustled around the room adjusting blankets, sorting the swaddling clothes, turning her face away from Margaret so the girl wouldn’t see her own tears, pricked raw by the tenderness of that cry that echoed exactly how she had once called out to her own absent son years before.

When she turned back a moment later, she found Margaret staring at her, eyes wide and strange.
“Did you not hear that?” Margaret asked, quiet.
“No, my lamb. Hear what?”
“I called out to Gerard, just now.”
“Ay, ay, I heard you do that, lass.”
“Well. He answered me.”

Catherine tsked softly, coming over to smooth her hair. “Hush, now, don’t say such things. You’re tired, your wits are wandering a little after your labour.”
“Mother, as sure as I lie here with his boy at my breast,” Margaret said, earnest, no trace of delirium in her voice, “I heard his voice, clear as day, call ‘Margaret!’ It wasn’t his happy laugh, though, the way he usually says my name. But that must just be the distance, right? All voices sound sad, stretched thin across miles and miles. Why are you not smiling, my sweet? I have never felt so happy in all my life, I think. It feels like I never knew pain or care at all, before this night.” She turned her soft gaze back to the little sleeping face beside her, drinking in every tiny feature in silent rapture.

That very same night, half across Europe, Gerard threw himself into the cold, dark waters of the Tiber. And at the exact hour that Margaret heard his voice call her name, he cried out loud in the grip of death and utter despair: “Margaret!”

Let those who can explain that strange, unbreakable bond between two souls make sense of it. I cannot.
