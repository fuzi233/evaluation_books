Every soul in Briarfield agreed: it was high time Mr. Robert Moore came home. The whole parish buzzed with curiosity over his unaccountable long absence, and the neighbouring villages of Whinbury and Nunnely each added their own wild, embellished theories to the pile of gossip.

No one was short of a reason for his delay – forty plausible ones circulated at the last count, all of them wrong. The gossips were unanimous that it could not be business, for everyone knew that long before, he had run down the four Luddite ringleaders who had targeted his mill, attended their trial, seen them convicted, and overseen their safe loading onto a transport ship bound for the colonies. The *Stilbro' Courier* had printed every last detail, padded out with extra dramatic flourishes, and the local mill-owners quietly celebrated, hoping the show of legal force would crush the violent unrest that threatened their livelihoods. But the disaffected mill workers still muttered in the corners of alehouses, swore bitter oaths over drugged beer, and raised fiery toasts in raw British gin, and no one was stupid enough to believe the trouble was truly over.

One loud rumour claimed Moore dared not set foot back in Yorkshire: his life, people whispered, would not be worth an hour's purchase the second he crossed the county line.
"I'll go tell him that," Mr. Yorke announced dryly when his foreman brought him the story. "And if that doesn't send him galloping home full tilt, nothing will."

Whether it was that threat, or some other pull, Moore finally yielded. He sent word to his foreman Joe Scott that he would arrive in Stilbro' on market day, and ordered a hackney carriage sent to meet him at the George Inn. Yorke heard the news, and made a point of heading into town to meet his young friend himself.

The market was in full swing when Moore arrived, and he took his usual seat at the manufacturers' shared market dinner. As a well-known man of action, recently returned from chasing down the mill rioters, he was received with no small measure of deference. Men who would never have dared acknowledge his acquaintance in public, lest the workers' hatred for him rub off on their own businesses, pulled him aside privately to hail him as their champion. When the wine circulated, their respect would have bloomed into full-throated enthusiasm if not for Moore's cold, unshaken nonchalance, which kept their praise smouldering low, damp and unenthused.

Yorke, who presided over these dinners by default, watched his young friend's bearing with huge, quiet delight. Nothing riled him faster than seeing a man made a fool of by flattery, puffed up by cheap popularity. Nothing smoothed his rough temper faster than seeing a public man who could not bear the limelight – disdain would have only angered him, but quiet indifference soothed him completely. Robert leaned back in his chair, quiet and almost surly, as the clothiers and blanket-makers around him bragged about his courage, recited his deeds, and laced their praise with coarse insults against the working class. To Yorke, it was a perfect sight. He could tell those cheap, vulgar eulogiums shamed Moore deeply, made him half-scorn himself and all he had done. It was easy to laugh at threats and insults, but the praise of people you despised stung sharper than any lash. Moore had stood on a hustings facing howling hostile crowds before, breasted the storm of unpopularity with his head high and his spirit elate, but now he drooped, chagrined, shrinking back from the congratulations of half-educated tradesmen.

Yorke could not resist teasing him. "How do you like your new band of supporters? Don't you think they do your cause proud? Pity you didn't hang those four rioters, lad. If you'd pulled that trick, the gentry round here would have unharnessed the horses from your coach, yoked a dozen asses to it, and dragged you through Stilbro' like a conquering general."

Moore soon pushed his wine aside, broke away from the party, and headed for the open road. Yorke followed him less than five minutes later, and the two men rode out of Stilbro' side by side.

They had left early for home, but the day was far spent. The last sliver of sun had already faded from the edges of the clouds, and the dark of the October night was creeping over the moors, casting long soft shadows across the heather. Yorke, pleasantly warmed by a moderate amount of wine, and glad to have young Moore back in Yorkshire as his companion for the long ride, talked almost nonstop. He passed over the trials and convictions with a scoffing, brief remark, moved on to the neighbourhood gossip, and before long, turned his full attention to Moore's personal affairs.

"Bob, you're well and truly beaten, and you deserve it. Fortune was head over heels for you, you know. She handed you the grandest prize in her lottery – twenty thousand pounds, a whole new life, your honour restored, your mill saved – all you had to do was reach out and take it. And what did you do? You hopped on a horse and rode off hunting rioters in Warwickshire. Your sweetheart – Fortune, I mean – was generous. She waited, patient as a monument, till the chase was done, till you'd run down your vermin prey, expecting you to come thundering home and lay your laurels at her feet. Instead, you coolly hopped a coach to London. God knows what you did there. I'll warrant you just sat and sulked. You were never pale as a lily, but now you're olive green. You're a far sight less handsome than you used to be, man."

"And who is getting this prize you go on about?" Moore asked, gruff.

"Only a baronet. That's all. I've not a doubt in my mind you've lost her. She'll be Lady Nunnely before Christmas."

"Hem. Quite probable."

"And she never needed to be. You fool of a lad! I swear you could have had her for the taking."

"What makes you so sure of that, Mr. Yorke?"

"Every sign there is. The way her eyes lit up when you walked in a room, the way her cheeks flushed bright red when your name was mentioned – and her cheeks are usually pale, you know that well enough."

"So my chance is quite over, then?"

"It ought to be. But it's still worth trying. This Sir Philip fellow is nothing but milk and water. And they say he writes verses, tags silly rhymes. You're above all that, Bob, at the very least."

"Would you advise me to propose even now? At the eleventh hour, when it's far too late?"

"You can only try, Robert. If she ever had a soft spot for you – and on my conscience, I believe she did, once – she'd forgive a lot. But lad, you're laughing. Are you laughing at me? You'd better grin at your own stubbornness. Though I see you're laughing on the wrong side of your mouth right now. You look as sour as any man I've ever seen."

"I've quarrelled with myself, Yorke. Kicked against pricks till my boots are worn through, struggled in a strait waistcoat till my wrists are raw, bashed my thick skull against a wall harder than stone till it aches."

"Ha! I'm glad to hear that. Sharp exercise, that! Hope it knocked some of your self-conceit out of you?"

"Self-conceit? What even is that? Self-respect, self-tolerance – do you sell those, anywhere? Do you know anyone who trades in them? Point me the way. I'd spend my last guinea this very minute to buy them."

"Is that how it is, Robert? I like that. I like a man who speaks his mind plainly. What went so wrong?"

"Every part of me is broken. The whole engine of my nature, the human mill that is me. The boiler I call my heart is fit to burst."

"That should go in a book. It's almost poetry. You'll be spouting verses any second now. Don't mind me, give in to the feeling."

"It was a hideous, unforgivable, base blunder. A mistake you make in one moment, that you rue for years – that no life can cancel out."

"Go on, lad. This is better than sugar candy. I love to hear it. Talking will do you good. There's no one else within miles on this moor, no one to overhear."

"I will talk. I'm not ashamed to tell. There's a wild cat tearing at my chest right now, and I want you to hear it yell."

"To me it's fine music. You and your brother Louis both have grand voices. When Louis sings, his tones are deep and soft as a distant bell, I've felt the hair on my arms stand up. The night is still, it's leaning down over you like a dark priest to a penitent. Confess. Don't smooth anything over. Be candid as a sanctified Methodist at an experience meeting. Make yourself as wicked as Beelzebub. It'll lighten your mind."

"As mean as Mammon, you mean. Yorke, if I got off this horse and laid myself across the road, would you gallop back and forwards over me twenty times?"

"With all the pleasure in life, as long as no coroner's inquest finds me out."

"Hiram Yorke, I truly believed Shirley Keeldar loved me. I saw her eyes sparkle bright when she spotted me in a crowd. She flushed crimson every time she offered me her hand and said 'how do you do, Mr. Moore?' My name had some strange magic over her – I saw her face change every time someone else said it. She said my name herself, in the prettiest, softest tone she had. She was warm with me, interested in me, anxious for my success. She sought out every chance to help me. I watched, and weighed, and wondered, and I could only come to one conclusion: this is love.

"I looked at her, and I saw youth, and a kind of rare beauty. I saw power in her. Her wealth could have redeemed my honour, saved my mill, pulled me out of all my debt. She'd already lent me five thousand pounds, that substantial, life-saving sum that kept the gates of Hollow's Mill open. I told myself how stupid I would be to turn away from that, to dismiss every good fortune placed right in front of me. Young, graceful, gracious – my benefactress, attached to me, pining for me. I repeated that to myself over and over, puffed up with the idea that a woman as bright as her could love me, and I smiled to myself at her sweet naivete, that she was the first to fall and show it. That whip of yours has a heavy handle, Yorke. You can swing it and knock me right out of the saddle if you like. I wouldn't even mind a hard thrashing right now."

"Have patience, Robert, till the moon rises and I can see your face clearly. Speak plain. Did you love her or not? I'm curious. I truly want to know."

"Sir – she's pretty, in her own way, very attractive. Sometimes she looks like something made of fire and air, and I stand and marvel at her, but I never once felt the urge to reach for her, to kiss her, to clasp her close. She stirred my vanity, my self-interest, but she never felt like my other half, my better self. When that thought crossed my mind, I pushed it away brutally, told myself I'd be rich with her, ruined without her, that I'd be practical, not foolishly romantic.

"That sensible resolution is what made me ride up to Fieldhead one night last August, the very eve of my trip to Birmingham to chase those rioters. I'd sent her a note asking for a private interview. I found her home, and alone. She greeted me without embarrassment, assuming I had come on business about the mill. I was so nervous I could barely breathe, but I was determined. I don't even remember how I got the words out, but I did it, cold and hard – I offered myself up, my fine person and all my debts, as if it was a business settlement.

"It infuriated me when she did none of the things I expected. She didn't blush, didn't tremble, didn't look down at the floor. She said, 'I doubt whether I have understood you, Mr. Moore.' I had to repeat the whole proposal twice, word it as plain as the alphabet, before she fully took it in. And then she stood up, walked fast twice across the room, in that way no other woman walks, and cried out, 'God bless me!'

"I leaned back against the mantelpiece, ready for anything. I knew my doom the second I saw her face, heard her shocked, indignant, sad voice. She stopped and looked right at me. 'God bless me,' she said again. 'That was a very strange proposal, stranger still coming from you. If you knew how coldly you spoke it, how stern you looked, you would be startled at yourself. You sounded like a brigand demanding my purse, not a lover asking for my heart.'

"It was a queer thing to say, wasn't it Yorke? And I knew instantly it was all true. Her words were a mirror, and I saw myself exactly as I was. I stared at her, dumb and sullen, half enraged, half shamed. 'Gérard Moore,' she said, 'you know you do not love Shirley Keeldar.' I could have lied, sworn up and down I loved her more than life, but I could not lie to her pure face. I could not perjure myself in front of her truthful eyes. She would no more have believed me than she would have believed the ghost of Judas. Her wits were far too sharp to mistake my cold, coarse admiration for the real, throbbing love a man should have for a woman he asks to marry.

"She sat down in the window seat then, and cried. Passionately. Her eyes rained tears, but they flashed too, large and dark and haughty, accusing me. They said: you have hurt me, outraged me, deceived me. 'I did respect you,' she said. 'I did admire you, I did like you – as much as I would like a brother. And you – you want to turn me into a business speculation. You would sacrifice me to that mill, your Moloch.'

"I had the good sense to not make a single excuse, to not try and wriggle out of it. I just stood there, ready to be scorned. I was such a fool, so infatuated with the idea of saving my mill, that when I finally spoke, what do you think I said? 'Whatever my own feelings were, I was persuaded you loved me, Miss Keeldar.'

"Isn't that beautiful? She sat there, completely stunned. 'Is that Robert Moore speaking?' I heard her mutter. 'Is it a man, or something lower?' 'Do you mean,' she asked out loud, 'that you thought I loved you the way we love people we wish to marry?' I said yes, that was exactly what I thought.

"'You have conceived an idea that is deeply insulting to every woman,' she said. 'You announced it in a way that makes my soul recoil. You are implying all the frank kindness I showed you was a complicated, bold, immodest trick to catch a husband. You are saying you have come here out of pity to offer me your hand, because I chased you. Let me tell you this: you saw wrong. You judged wrong. I never loved you. Rest easy on that. My heart has no passion for you any more than your heart has any real affection for me.'

"I think I got my answer, Yorke. 'I seem to be a blind, besotted fool,' was all I could say. 'Loved you!' she cried. 'I was as frank with you as a sister. I never shunned you, never feared you. You cannot make me tremble when you walk in a room, cannot make my pulse race for you.' I tried to argue, told her how she blushed when she talked to me, how my name moved her. 'Not for your sake,' she said, and would not explain more. She asked me, one by one, if I thought she loved me at the school feast, when she stopped me in Maythorn Lane, when she visited me at the counting house, when we walked on the pavement together. And I said yes, every time.

"By the Lord, Yorke, she rose up then, and she seemed to grow taller, lit from within like hot live coal. 'That means you think the worst of me. You deny me every thing I value most. You say I am a traitor to all my sisters, that I acted in a way that no decent woman ever would, chasing after a husband like that.' We were silent for minutes. 'Lucifer, Star of the Morning,' she said, 'thou art fallen. You, who I once held so high in my esteem, are cast down. Go.'

"I didn't go right away. I saw her lip tremble, and I knew another wave of tears was coming, and I thought after that, there would be calm, and we could fix it. The tears came fast, softer this time, full of regret. She looked up at me, her gaze more mournful than angry. 'O Moore,' she said. It hurt worse than 'Et tu, Brute!' I groaned, the ache of it sitting heavy in my chest, like Cain's own loneliness. 'What I did was wrong,' I said, 'and it has earned me bitter wages. I will go and spend them far away from you.'

"I picked up my hat. I did not want to leave like that, and I thought she would stop me. She would have, too, if the mortal wound I had dealt her pride had not silenced her, made her forget her compassion for a moment. I had to turn back of my own accord, walk up to her, and beg her to forgive me. 'I could forgive you,' she said, 'if I did not have to forgive myself too – to think I misled a sensible man so far, I must have done something wrong.' I stammered out apologies, desperate to make her see it was all my fault, no fault of hers, and finally she held out her hand. For the first time, I wished I could take her in my arms and kiss her, and I did kiss her hand, over and over. 'Some day we shall be friends again,' she said.
