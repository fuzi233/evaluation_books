We must now, before returning to the affairs of Miss Lydia, address a matter left ambiguous in Mr. Warrington’s letter to his brother—namely, the curious fate of Lady Maria Esmond. To those unfamiliar with the caprices of the heart, it may seem unfathomable that a woman of noble birth, seasoned by years and experience, who had so recently been entangled in one ardent attachment, could so swiftly transfer her affections to another—and one so far beneath her station! Yet love, as any observer of human nature knows, is no respecter of rank or reason.

Consider: if men are permitted their fickle diversions, why should women be denied the same? The great Hamlet himself railed unjustly against his mother’s remarriage, blind to the natural longing for companionship that persists even in widowhood. Hearts are not monuments, unchanging through the ages, but living things—hungry, restless, and ever seeking sustenance. Lady Maria’s affection, once fixed upon her young cousin, had not expired so much as wandered, alighting now upon a new object: Mr. Hagan, the actor who had so movingly performed in George’s ill-fated tragedy.

Clad in gilt armor and a grand periwig, Hagan had declaimed his lines with such fire that the lady’s heart was irrevocably conquered. And he, though doubtless startled by the ardor of so distinguished a suitor, was not insensible to her devotion. Their union, hastily solemnized by Parson Sampson in a Mayfair chapel, sent shockwaves through the ranks of polite society.

The outcry was immediate. Lady Maria’s kin disowned her; the Countess of Castlewood donned mourning as if for the dead; Mr. Will vowed violent retribution upon the "upstart player"; and Madame de Bernstein, most incensed of all, retreated to Tunbridge Wells in high dudgeon. Even General Lambert, though he jested about the match at home, admitted that no respectable household could now receive the fallen lady.

Yet for all the scorn heaped upon them, the couple endured. Hagan, though his theatrical income dwindled under the scandal’s shadow, remained steadfast. They shared cramped Westminster lodgings with his aging mother and schoolboy brother—a far cry from the luxuries of Esmond Hall, but Maria, to her credit, did not waver.

When George visited the pair, he found them in meager but resolute spirits. Hagan himself was absent, but Maria—no longer the polished society matron—sat playing cribbage with a neighbor while Mrs. Hagan sipped punch by the fire. There was a quiet dignity in her resilience, a defiance of expectation that even George could not help but admire.

Meanwhile, another quarrel brewed—this time between George and his hotheaded cousin Will. At the Van den Bosch residence, where both paid court to the heiress Lydia, Will’s provocations grew unbearable. Mocking Harry’s military exploits, sneering at George’s restraint, he finally drove the latter to fling a glass of wine in his face. A duel was narrowly averted by the timely intervention of the law, leaving both men bound over to keep the peace—much to the relief of their friends.

Thus love, in its many forms, stirred tumult: binding an actress to a lady, pitting cousin against cousin, and proving, as ever, that the heart follows no rule but its own.
