Costigan never so much as banged on Pen’s door to rouse him from his slumbers, no irate note from Mr. Huxter slid under his door to spoil his morning, and when Pen finally blinked awake, he felt perkier than your average burnt-out, blasé London man ever does at the crack of day.

City blokes jolt awake at dawn with visions of consols and the stock exchange already dancing through their heads before their nightcap’s even slid fully off their foreheads. Lawyers rouse themselves staring down the day’s impossible caseload, already panicking that the solicitor pestering them for papers will turn up at their door before breakfast. Who among us doesn’t wake up to a niggling anxiety instantly tugging us back to the day’s chores? Still, that overnight rest is a superpower, isn’t it? Providence definitely knew what it was doing giving us sleep to face another round of nonsense.

Pen’s daily work, though, was exactly the kind that stopped feeling exciting about ten minutes in. The thrill of seeing your own name in print wears thin after three or four appearances in a magazine, after all. Even the fanciest thoroughbred Pegasus turns into a grumpy hack horse the second you harnessed him to a daily word count—he won’t budge an inch without a snack and a sharp nudge of the whip, no matter how pretty his feathers used to be. Pen wrote his columns for the *Pall Mall Gazette* perfectly well (for a raised salary, thank you very much) but zero enthusiasm, turning out good prose on his good days and total drivel on his bad, a sharp, bright literary hack who could churn out copy faster than most men could drink their morning coffee.

Society didn’t do much for him either. For all his big talk, he was still too young to hang with the old crew of distinguished men who’d already had all the good scandals, too out of practice to charm women without obsessing over how cool he looked doing it, too old to carouse all night with the lads still wasting their paychecks at music halls, and too lazy to keep up with stuffy businessmen. He was perfectly happy being alone most days, but complained about it so much you’d think he was being held prisoner in a boring drawing room.

The second he woke up post-Vauxhall, that little Fanny girl popped straight into his head. “What a nice, uncomplicated little thing she is,” he thought. “Way more genuine than all those fancy society misses faking artless little giggles—looking at you, Blanche, who acts like she’s never held a bottle of champagne until no one’s watching. Who’d have guessed such a sweet little rose would bloom in a porter’s lodge, stuck in that grubby old flower pot of Shepherd’s Inn? Old Bows is teaching her to sing? If her singing voice is half as nice as her speaking voice, it’s a treat. I almost made her call me ‘sir’ the other night, what a total prat move. No tragic Faust and Margaret nonsense for me, thanks. That old Bows is a diamond in the rough, isn’t he? Wore those same raggedy old coats for years, had my back back when I was making a fool of myself over the Fotheringay, he’s got a million sorrowful little stories tucked away. I ought to make a point of hanging out with more different sorts of people, I’m sick to death of stuffy gentry parties. And Costigan! What a walking, talking caricature of an Irish captain, begad I’m shoving him straight into my next novel.”

It was Saturday, too—publication day for the Gazette, which meant Pen had zero deadlines, zero editors pestering him for copy, nothing to do all afternoon. So he decided he’d head over to Shepherd’s Inn, obviously, to pay old Bows a visit. That was totally the whole point of the trip. Nothing else.

Funny enough, if Pen had been the sneakiest heartless rake in all of London, he couldn’t have picked a better way to win poor little Fanny over than the moves he pulled at Vauxhall the night before. His dandyish, kindly protectiveness, his good humor, the fact that he didn’t take advantage of her flustered little crush—both his good points and his silly flaws had her completely smitten. If we’d peeked into her tiny cupboard bed that night, the one she shared with her two little sisters, we’d have found her tossing and turning so much she kept kicking the two poor kids awake, replaying every single second of the night: his posh accent, his sparkly shirt studs, the way he poured her that glass of champagne like it was the most natural thing in the world. Fanny had devoured every crummy three-decker romance novel Miss Minifer lent out from the back of her millinery and brandy-ball shop, and Pen? He was every single dashing aristocrat hero rolled into one. She was already mentally lording it over Emma Baker, who wouldn’t stop bragging that she was engaged to a cheesemonger who wore a white apron all shift, and Betsy Rodgers, whose boyfriend the attorney’s clerk carried a beat-up leather bag everywhere like it was a royal scepter.

At two o’clock that afternoon, right after the Bolton family finished their dinner (Fanny’s dad, part-time inn porter and full-time undertaker’s assistant, was off driving the late Countess of Estrich’s hearse out to the countryside), a fancy gent in a crisp white hat and white trousers strolled under the inn arch and stopped at the porter’s wicket. Fanny didn’t even pretend to be surprised. She knew he’d come. She screamed for her mum, shoved her two little sisters out the way, and wrenched the wicket open, blushing so bright red she looked like she’d eaten a whole jar of pickled beetroot. Pen took off his hat all polite, asked how they’d slept after the chaos of the night before, said he couldn’t walk past the lodge without popping in to check on his little dance partner.

Mrs Bolton was side-eyeing him at first, like “what’s this fancy dandy doing hanging around my porter’s lodge?” but Pen’s charm was unstoppable. There wasn’t a single empty seat free—every chair had a dish cover or a sewing box perched on it—so he plonked himself down on a tiny wooden child’s chair built for a 5-year-old, and all the kids burst out cackling, Fanny loudest of all. The great, fancy Mr Pendennis, slumming it on a tot’s seat! She’d probably keep that chair on her mantelpiece for the next 40 years to fawn over, the silly thing.

Pen chatted a mile a minute, asking about the undertaker business, how many mutes showed up for the countess’s funeral, who lived in the inn, said he’d run into that Mr Campion with the fancy racehorse and cab at a dinner party once, joked he was thinking of buying shares in that Polwheedle and Tredyddlum mining scheme, maybe even move into Shepherd’s Inn full time instead of the boring stuffy Temple. Then he brought up Captain Strong and that weird Colonel Altamont, the bloke who wore so much jewellery he jingled when he walked, a wig so obvious you could spot it across a room, and dyed black whiskers so far gone they shimmered a funny blue-purple in the light. Mrs Bolton was so won over by this point she blabbed every secret: Sir Francis Clavering showed up two or three times a week to Strong’s rooms, her little boy ran out to buy him bill-stamps all the time, oops she wasn’t supposed to say that but he was clearly a gentleman, wasn’t he? Fanny nodded so hard her head nearly fell off, agreeing that of course he was the nicest, most proper gentleman that ever lived.

Once Pen had fished all the gossip on Clavering he’d been secretly after for his own reasons, he remembered his perks as a press man, and offered them free theatre tickets. The two women lost their minds. Fanny clapped so hard her hands turned pink. Their husband/father spent half his time getting drunk and grumpy about the undertaker work, they never got to go out anywhere. The only slight hiccup was when little Betsy-Jane piped up that she wanted to go too, Fanny snapped “don’t be a nuisance” and Mrs Bolton herded the two little tots off to play on the gravel around that dorky old statue of Shepherd the inn’s namesake.

Just as Pen was making both of them cackle at his silly jokes, an old man trundled under the inn arch, spotted Pen perched half on the wicket chatting up the two women, and pulled a face so rueful he looked like he’d just bit into a whole lemon. “Hey Bows!” Pen yelled, waving all cheery. “Perfect timing! I was just about to come visit you, asking these two where your rooms are!”

Old Bows trundled in, shook Pen’s hand, his face still as grumpy as a cat that just found out the whole house was out of milk. “Oh? You were coming to see me, were you?” he muttered.

Somebody in that little lodge room heaved a very quiet, very annoyed sigh. That somebody was Fanny, thinking, “Ugh, drat that old man, couldn’t he have waited half an hour to show up?”
