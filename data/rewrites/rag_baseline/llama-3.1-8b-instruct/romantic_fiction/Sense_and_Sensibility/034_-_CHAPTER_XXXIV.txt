CHAPTER XXXIV

Mrs. John Dashwood's enthusiasm for her husband's judgment knew no bounds, and she eagerly anticipated the visit from Mrs. Jennings and her daughter the following day. As it turned out, her expectations were amply rewarded, for Mrs. Jennings proved to be a charming hostess, effortlessly weaving together a tapestry of conversation that left even the reserved Lady Middleton in awe. For in Lady Middleton, Mrs. Dashwood discovered a kindred spirit, a woman whose calculated politeness and lack of genuine warmth resonated deeply with her own stilted demeanor.

As they sipped tea and nibbled on delicate finger sandwiches, the air was thick with the scent of rose petals and the faint tang of self-interest. It was a meeting of two minds, bound together by a shared understanding of the social niceties that governed their lives. Mrs. Jennings, however, saw things differently, perceiving Mrs. Dashwood as a haughty, reserved woman, whose reserve was merely a thin veil for her true nature.

Elinor watched the scene unfold with a mixture of fascination and wariness, her mind turning to the question of Edward's whereabouts. She longed to know if he was indeed in town, but hesitated to ask, fearful of stirring up a hornet's nest of emotions. Fanny, on the other hand, seemed reluctant to mention his name at all, her reticence a testament to the complicated web of relationships that bound them all together.

It was not until Lucy Steele burst into the room, her face a picture of distress, that the news finally broke: Edward had arrived in town, but was too afraid to visit Elinor and Marianne in person, lest he be discovered by those who would not approve of their association. The pair exchanged anxious glances, their longing to be together tempered by the knowledge that their love was still a secret, hidden away like a delicate pastry left to cool in the pantry.

Despite this, Edward's visits to Berkeley Street were frequent, his cards left discreetly on the table for Elinor to find. She was pleased to see them, and even more pleased to have missed him, for in his absence, she felt free to navigate the treacherous waters of her family's relationships without distraction.
