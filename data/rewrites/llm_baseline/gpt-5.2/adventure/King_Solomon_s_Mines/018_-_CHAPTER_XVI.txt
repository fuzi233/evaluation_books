Darkness had already thickened on the third day after our late turmoil when we took shelter in a cluster of huts crouched at the feet of the “Three Witches”—that sharp triangle of peaks to which Solomon’s Great Road runs as to an altar. We were a strange household for such a hearth: Sir Henry Curtis, Good, and myself; Foulata moving quietly among us with the grave attentiveness of one born to make small kindnesses feel like law; Infadoos with his guard; and Gagool, bundled in a litter, the old witch’s voice rasping from within it all day—muttering, spitting curses, and now and then breaking out into that cracked laughter that turned the air colder than night.

Before dawn had properly loosened its grip, the mountains took their full shape. Three peaks, three points of one upheaved mass: one to our right, one to our left, and one straight ahead like a judgement. In the early sunlight their snow lay twisted and wreath-like, bright as salt piled on dark boards; below the snow-line the heaths purpled the slopes, and the moors climbed upward in wild, stubborn patches. Straight before us, the Great Road shone pale as a ribbon of bone, running uphill for five miles to the foot of the centre peak—and there it ended, as abruptly as a sentence cut off.

We set out with a haste that was almost hunger. For months, for years in some sense, the idea of those mines had been the daily bread of our imaginations: the old Portuguese Dom who had died miserably three centuries gone; the ill-starred descendant who had followed the same lure to his ruin; and—so we feared—George Curtis, Sir Henry’s brother, swallowed by the same bright promise. The Road beneath our boots felt too clean, too deliberate; the morning itself seemed to listen. Even the soundscape altered as we climbed: the hush of high ground, the faint scratch of wind in heath, the far thin calls of birds that carried too clearly in the rare air. It was a world with no industrial roar to blunt the nerves—no clatter of carts, no iron pounding—only the steady drum of our own blood and footfalls, and the occasional creak of leather. That clarity of sound, that acoustic purity, ought to have been peace. Instead it sharpened every misgiving.

We walked so fast in our excitement that the bearers of Gagool’s hammock lagged; at last her piping voice shot out from the litter like a thorn.

“Walk more slowly, white men,” she called, thrusting her shrivelled face between the grass curtains. Her eyes shone as if lit from behind. “Why will ye run to meet the evil that shall befall you, ye seekers after treasure?”

And she laughed—high, cracked, delighted. The laughter did what a sudden sour smell can do to appetite: it turned our eagerness queasy. For a little while the road felt less like promise and more like a channel dug for doom.

Still we went on.

An hour and a half brought us to a sight that made even Good fall silent. Before us yawned a vast circular pit with sloping sides—three hundred feet deep or more, and half a mile round—its edges softened with grass and bush, but its emptiness still raw enough to make the stomach drop. The Great Road split and went around it, as though even stonework had learned to step aside.

“Can’t you guess what this is?” I asked.

They stared, shook their heads.

“Kimberley,” I said. “You’ve never seen the diamond diggings. This is the same story written in another hand. Look there—blue clay still showing in the sides. And down there—those worn flat slabs? Washing-tables, I’d swear it. This is Solomon’s diamond mine.”

The pit had the same patient brutality as any great excavation: the sense of countless hands, countless days, a human will that had bitten into the earth and not let go. Yet here, with heather growing where men had once clawed and cursed, it wore an ancient calm. A breeze slid along the rim and down into the hollow, and its sound changed as it fell—softened, swallowed—like speech disappearing into a language nobody spoke any more.

We followed the road that skirted the gulf, drawn toward three towering shapes visible beyond. As we neared, they resolved into Colossi seated upon huge pedestals of dark rock, carved with rude emblems of the old worship of fertility. Forty paces apart they sat, facing down the road that ran sixty miles across plain to Loo, staring forever across distance as if distance were their native element.

It was only close at hand that their full majesty struck like a physical blow. Two male figures, draped, their faces terrible—one to our right with a devil’s cast that made the very stone seem to sneer; one to our left serene, but with a serenity that chilled more than rage. Between them the female figure sat nude, beautiful in a severe way, though weather had eaten at the features; from either side of her head rose the points of a crescent, like horns of a half-moon.

Sir Henry, looking up, spoke low. “That calm,” he said, “is the calm of cruelty that does not need to strain itself.”

Contemplation brought curiosity on like thirst. Who had carved these? Who had driven the road, dug the pit, made this place where stone obeyed a mind long dead?

Because my head is full of old texts and their stubborn names, it came to me suddenly—Solomon, who went astray after strange gods. “Ashtoreth,” I said, and named also Chemosh and Milcom. “Perhaps these are the three.”

Sir Henry’s scholar’s mind took the suggestion and turned it in his hands. “There may be something in it. Ashtoreth of the Hebrews—Astarte of the Phoenicians. Horned. Crescent. Traders in Solomon’s time. A Phoenician overseer, perhaps, left his gods here in stone. Who can say?”

Who indeed? Names are nets, and the past slips through them. Even when you catch a syllable—Ashtoreth, Astarte—the living feel of what those sounds meant to those who spoke them is gone, as untranslatable as the taste of a vanished fruit. The statues sat on, indifferent to our guesses, their silence a kind of dominance: whatever they were called, they remained what they were, and our language did not move them.

Infadoos came up then, saluted the “Silent Ones” with his spear, and asked whether we would enter the “Place of Death” at once, or wait till mid-day to eat.

We were like men who have smelled a feast and cannot bear delay. It was not later than eleven, and our curiosity burned hotter than any hunger. We would go in immediately. Still, I urged that we take food, in case the cave held us.

Gagool’s litter was brought up. She was hauled out, and stood leaning on her stick, grinning at us as though our faces were dishes set before her. At my request Foulata packed biltong—dried game flesh, hard and dark, salted with sun and smoke—into a reed basket with a hinged cover, and added two gourds of water. She did it with the calm precision of someone who knows the value of provisions, whose people have always understood that sustenance is memory made portable: smoke and salt keeping not only meat, but stories, from spoiling.

Straight ahead, about fifty paces behind the Colossi, rose a sheer wall of rock—eighty feet and more—sloping upward into the base of the snow-wreathed peak that soared three thousand feet above us. Gagool hobbled toward it, casting one evil grin back over her shoulder, and we followed to a narrow portal, solidly arched, like the mouth of a mine.

There she waited, gourd-lamp in hand, her face pinched and gleaming.

“Now, white men from the Stars,” she piped. “Great warriors—Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn the wise—are ye ready? Behold, I am here to do the bidding of my lord the king, and to show you the store of bright stones. Ha! ha! ha!”

“We are ready,” I said, though my mouth felt dry.

“Good, good! Make strong your hearts to bear what ye shall see. Comest thou too, Infadoos, thou who didst betray thy master?”

Infadoos’ brow darkened. “Nay, I come not. It is not for me to enter there. But thou, Gagool, curb thy tongue. Beware how thou dealest with my lords. At thy hands will I require them. If a hair of them be hurt—be’st thou fifty times a witch—thou shalt die.”

“I hear, Infadoos,” she answered, unmoved. “Thou didst ever love big words. When thou wast a babe, I remember, thou didst threaten thine own mother. That was but the other day. Fear not, fear not. I live only to do the bidding of the king. I have done the bidding of many kings—till in the end they did mine. Ha! ha! I go to look upon their faces once more—and Twala’s also! Come on, come on—here is the lamp.”

She produced a large gourd full of oil, fitted with a rush wick. The flame she coaxed from it was small, and the smell of the oil—fatty, plant-sweet, faintly rancid—puffed into the cool air with every movement.

“Art thou coming, Foulata?” Good asked in his villainous Kukuana, improved by much practice under her tuition.

“I fear, my lord,” she said, and her eyes flicked to the black opening.

“Then give me the basket.”

“Nay, my lord,” she answered, gathering herself as though to a duty, “whither thou goest there I go also.”

I could have groaned aloud at that—her presence might become a cruel complication—yet there was no arguing with the kind of loyalty that grows in silence.

Without more, Gagool plunged into the passage. It was wide enough for two abreast and swallowed the lamplight greedily. We followed her voice, thin and eager ahead, our own boots scraping stone. The acoustics changed at once: the passage took our sounds and returned them altered—tightened, multiplied—until our breathing seemed to belong to more than our lungs.

A sudden rush of wings burst past, a leathery brushing against faces.

“Hullo! what’s that?” Good cried. “Somebody hit me in the face!”

“Bats,” I said. “On you go.”

After some fifty paces the blackness thinned. A faint light seeped into the passage, not from before but from above, as though the mountain had been pierced by narrow throats that admitted day grudgingly. Another minute, and we stepped into a chamber that made speech feel profane.

Imagine the nave of the greatest cathedral you ever stood in—windowless, yet dimly lit from high shafts in the roof—and then make it wider, loftier, more indifferent to human measure. The arched ceiling soared a hundred feet above us. The air was cold and clean, with the mineral taste that rises from wet stone. And down the length of that vast natural hall ran rows of gigantic pillars that at first glance seemed carved from ice, but were in truth white spar—stalactites grown into columns with a slow patience that mocked empires.

Some were twenty feet thick at the base and sprang up with a delicate power to the distant roof. Others were unfinished: on the floor lay what looked like broken Grecian columns, while high above, the point of a huge icicle hung dimly, waiting. And the work of making went on even as we watched. From far overhead came the tiniest sound—so small it would be lost in any modern din, in any city’s clangour—yet here it rang clear as a pin dropped in a bowl.

A drop fell: a minute splash.

On some columns the drops came only once in two or three minutes. We stood listening, held by the rhythm. It was a music older than language, a metronome for centuries. I could not help thinking how the health of the mind is shaped by such soundscapes—by the presence of quiet enough to hear what is usually drowned. If our own world learned again to make room for these small natural syllables, perhaps fewer hearts would have to be shouted into numbness.

On one pillar we found a crude carving: a mummy-like figure, and by its head what appeared to be an Egyptian god. Doubtless some ancient labourer’s idle attempt at immortality, scratched at the habitual height—about five feet from the floor. Yet the column upon which it was cut was now only eight feet high, and still growing. Nearly three thousand years must have passed, and still, as we stood there, a drop fell and the stone drank it. A foot to a thousand years, perhaps—an inch and a fraction to a century. Time here was not a river but a drip.

Elsewhere the stalagmites had taken strange forms where the drip wandered: one enormous mass like a pulpit, its outer surface fretted as finely as lace; others resembling beasts half-dreamt. Along the cave walls spread fanlike ivory tracings like frost-leaves on glass. Smaller caves opened out of the main aisle like chapels from a cathedral—some large, some unbelievably tiny, one no bigger than a doll’s house, yet complete with its own miniature icicles, its own faint drip, as though nature refused to alter her grammar for any scale.

I wanted desperately to study it all—to find how the light was admitted, whether those shafts were natural or cut by ancient hands; to read in stone whatever remained of the place’s old use. But Gagool cared nothing for beauty that could not be stolen. She hurried on, her stick tapping, her lamp bobbing, her impatience souring the air.

We followed her to the far end of the great cave, where another doorway waited—square-topped, like the entrances of Egyptian temples.

“Are ye prepared to enter the Place of Death, white men?” she asked, and her pleasure in the question was obvious.

“Lead on, Macduff,” said Good, with a solemnity that sat oddly on his face.

“This is getting rather ghastly,” Sir Henry muttered, peering into the dark beyond. Then, with a forced lightness, “Come on, Quatermain—seniores priores. We mustn’t keep the old lady waiting.”

He stepped aside and left me the front, which I did not bless him for in my heart.

Tap, tap, went Gagool’s stick as she trotted down the passage, chuckling. A presentiment, irrational yet heavy, clung to me. The air seemed to thicken. Even the sound of our steps altered again, muffled, as if the rock itself were holding its breath.

After about twenty paces the passage widened into a gloomy room—some forty feet long, thirty broad, and thirty high—hollowed by hand from the mountain in some ancient time. It was dimmer than the great spar-hall behind. At first glance I made out only a massive stone table running down the room, a colossal white figure at its head, and life-sized white figures seated around.

Then I saw a brown thing seated in the centre of the table.

My eyes adjusted in one sickening second, and I knew what I was looking at. My legs acted on their own, and I was turning tail—out, out of that place—before my mind had finished the thought.

Sir Henry caught me by the collar and held fast.

I am not, as a rule, a nervous man. I have lived long enough to see superstitions for the folly they often are. Yet I confess freely that what lay before us struck through reason and landed somewhere older in the blood. Had Curtis not held me, I believe I should have fled back through the stalactite cave and out into daylight, and all the diamonds in Kimberley would not have bribed me to return.

But he held me. And then his own eyes grew used to the light. His grip loosened. He let me go, and began to mop sweat from his forehead.

Good swore—weakly, as though language had failed him. Foulata flung her arms round his neck and shrieked. Only Gagool laughed, long and loud, as if she were tasting our fear.

At the far end of the table, grasping a great white spear in skeleton fingers, sat Death.

Not an idea of death, not a symbol scratched on stone, but a colossal human skeleton—fifteen feet high or more—leaning forward as though about to rise, one bony hand braced on the table, the other holding the spear lifted high to strike. Its skull thrust toward us, jaws slightly open as if speech hovered there, and the hollow eye-places fixed upon us with a stare that made the room feel occupied by something more than carved spar.

“Great heavens,” I managed at last. “What can it be?”

“And what are those things?” Good asked, pointing with a shaking hand to the white company seated around.

“And what on earth is that thing?” Sir Henry demanded, pointing at the brown object on the table.

“Hee! hee! hee!” Gagool cackled. “To those who enter the Hall of the Dead, evil comes. Hee! hee! hee! ha! ha!”

Then, with a spiteful intimacy, she caught Sir Henry’s coat in her skinny fingers. “Come, Incubu, brave in battle—come and see him thou slewest.”

She led him toward the table. We followed, because some part of us needed to know, as one needs to look at a wound.

She stopped and pointed.

Sir Henry looked—and recoiled with an exclamation, for there, stark and naked, sat the gaunt corpse of Twala, the last king of the Kukuanas. The head Curtis’ battle-axe had shorn from the body rested upon its knees. The vertebrae jutted a full inch above the shrunken flesh of the neck, making the whole thing look horribly like some black double of a grotesque doll, propped for mockery.

And over the corpse was gathered a thin glassy film that made it more appalling, as though the body had been lacquered.

We did not understand at first. Then we heard it—the steady sound, unmistakable in that unnatural quiet:

Drip. Drop. Drip.

From the roof water fell directly onto the severed neck, ran down over the corpse, and slipped away through a tiny hole in the stone table.

The film was not varnish.

Twala’s body was being turned into stone.

A glance at the white figures around the table confirmed it. They had been men—kings—once. Now they were stalactites in the shape of men, iced over in spar, preserved under the drip, their faces dimly visible through the translucent shroud. The Kukuanas, from time immemorial, had kept their royal dead not in earth but in this cold patience of mineral water; they petrified them.

Whether there was a system beyond setting them beneath the falling drops for years upon years I never learned. But there they sat—twenty-seven of them, the last being Ignosi’s father—fixed at their ghastly banquet, wrapped in the slow glass of the mountain, with Death himself for host.

Nothing I have ever seen was more awe-inspiring: that long line of departed royalties, the table like a judgment-slab, the drip’s tiny voice counting centuries, and the colossal skeleton presiding in a posture of perpetual rising. The practice, by the number alone, could not have run much more than four and a quarter centuries—allowing an average reign of fifteen years, and even then not every king could have been brought here. But the White Death at the head of the board was older by far. He was hewn from a single stalactite, and, as a work of art, conceived with a genius that made the mind recoil while it admired. Good, who has an eye for such things, declared the anatomy perfect down to the smallest bones.

I could not help thinking that this terrible figure—ancient, deliberate—might have been the fancy of the same hand that designed the three Colossi outside, and that its presence had suggested to the Kukuanas the very notion of placing their kings here beneath its awful presidency. Or perhaps it was set to frighten away marauders from whatever lay beyond. I do not know. Names and explanations felt feeble in that chamber, as if the dominant lexicon of our own world—its tidy categories, its confident labels—were only another kind of power trying to master what it could never fully translate.

All I can do is tell what we saw.

Such was the White Death, and such were the White Dead.
