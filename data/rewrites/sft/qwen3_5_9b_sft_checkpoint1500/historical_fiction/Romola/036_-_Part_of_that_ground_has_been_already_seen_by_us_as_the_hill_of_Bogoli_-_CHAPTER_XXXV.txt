For several days, Tito saw little of Romola. When he returned the next morning, his voice was gentle, suggesting that it would be prudent for her to clear away her few personal effects from the library before the agents arrived to pack up the antiquities. Leaning forward, he kissed her brow and advised her to retreat to her chamber, where the small painted tabernacle stood, so she might escape the clamor of unfamiliar footsteps. She assented quietly, offering no outward sign of emotion. To Tito, the night seemed a single, interminable vigil. Though her frame remained strong, sensation had dulled into a continuous, heavy ache, as if her spirit had been stunned and bruised. He perceived that she was ill, yet he dared speak no words of comfort. Only when he noticed her hand and brow turned ice-cold did he dare move, fetching a fur-lined mantle and draping it softly over her shoulders. In every brief interval between his returns, the scene repeated itself: he strove to soothe her through silent acts or soft words, while she appeared to have lost the capacity to speak to him or even look upon him.

"Patience!" he murmured to himself. "She will recover, and forgive at last. The bond between us must endure." When the struck person lingers in silence and feigns nothing occurred, the striker often drifts effortlessly into the role of the aggrieved party; he feels no wound himself and becomes acutely aware of his own benevolent conduct despite having delivered the blow. Yet Tito was not naturally inclined toward grievance. His nature bent constantly toward reconciliation, and he would have endured much just to feel Romola's hand rest once more upon his head, as it had that morning when he first recoiled from gazing at her.

He found it easier to wait patiently for the restoration of his domestic peace because his life beyond the walls grew ever more captivating. There exists a course of action that is, in truth, the gradual unfolding of a whole character, yet it usually traces back to a single, decisive impression as its apparent origin. Since that moment in the Piazza del Duomo, when Tito, seated upon bales, had tasted a sharp delight in knowing he could please the ears of men with any phrase he chose, his imagination had drifted continuously toward a form of political activity that Florence's turbulent times surely demanded. However, a fresh terror of Baldassarre, awakened simultaneously, had lain like an immovable rock blocking that path, driving him to sell the library as a precaution against fleeing Florence—a step he now found unnecessary. That dread had nearly vanished. He must still wear his armor, prepare himself for tests of coolness and wit, but he was no longer compelled to abandon his home for distant fortunes. His father had rejected the offered atonement, forcing Tito into open defiance; an aged man, stripped of memory, in a foreign land, was too weak to withstand such opposition.

Now, Tito's latent desires began to manifest as explicit thoughts. As the freshness of youthful passion faded, life increasingly took on the appearance of a game for him, blending skill and chance with equal measure. The game available in Florence promised to be swift and thrilling—a struggle of revolution and parties, brimming with unspoken actions where brilliant ingenuity, discarding all inconvenient beliefs save that "ginger is hot in the mouth," discerns the path of superior wisdom.

Scarcely had the French guests departed when Florence erupted into agitation, akin to a colony of ants disturbed by the removal of a shadow, scrambling to repair their encampment. "How shall we raise funds for the King of France?" they asked. "How shall we handle the stubborn Pisan rebels?" "Above all, how shall we reform our governance to ensure magistrates are chosen and laws voted upon sound principles?" Until these questions found answers, trade risked stalling, and the vast body of workers, excluded from citizenship and denied the vote, threatened to grow restless. Something had to be done.

First, the great bell tolled, summoning the citizens to a parliament in the Piazza de' Signori. When the crowd was packed tight, hemmed in by armed guards at every exit, the Signoria—the Gonfaloniere and the eight Priors—emerged and stood before the stone lion on the platform of the Old Palace. They proposed that twenty leading men of the city be granted dictatorial authority for one year, empowered to select all magistrates and reorganize the state framework. The people shouted their assent, feeling themselves the electors of these Twenty. This form of "parliament" was an ancient Florentine custom, designed to make the will of the few appear as the choice of the many.

The shouting in the square ceased quickly, but the debates within the palace raged on. Should Florence adopt a Great Council in the Venetian style, where all officials and laws were elected by a broad body of qualified citizens, ignoring rank and party? Or should it follow a narrower, less popular scheme, preserving the hereditary influence of noble houses from being diluted by the votes of merchants? Lawyers argued day after day, far into the night. Messer Pagolantonio Soderini presented compelling arguments for the popular model; Messer Guidantonio Vespucci countered with equally valid reasons for a more aristocratic form. It was a debate over whether to boil or roast meat, a matter prejudged by the disputants' palates; their eloquence might have continued endlessly without deciding anything other than delaying the meal. The majority inside the palace, already holding power, sided with Vespucci, believing change should be moderate. Conversely, the majority outside, aware of their powerlessness and deep grievances, feared nothing so greatly as change.

Yet, a force existed outside the palace, slowly turning the vague wishes of that outer majority into a determined will. That force was the preaching of Savonarola. Driven both by a spiritual compulsion to guide the people and by the urgent needs of politicians who could pass no measures without his support, he rapidly shifted in his daily sermons from general exhortations to specific directives. He moved from urging listeners to subdue private passions for the public good to declaring exactly what sort of government they must possess to achieve it. He progressed from "Choose whatever is best for all" to "Choose the Great Council," and finally, "The Great Council is the Will of God."

To Savonarola, these propositions were indistinguishable. The Great Council was the only viable means to express a public will large enough to counter the corrupting influence of partisan self-interest; it was a plan that could render honest, impartial public service at least possible. The purer Florence's government became, the safer it would be from the schemes of men who profited from the moral decay of others, and the closer the Florentines would approach the character of a pure community, fit to lead the renovation of the Church and the world. Fra Girolamo's mind never rested until it reached that sublime end; the objects toward which he worked always possessed the same moral grandeur. He harbored no private malice and sought no petty gratification. Even in the final, terrible days, when shame, torture, and the dread thereof exposed every hidden flaw in his soul, he could tell his relentless judges: "Do not wonder if it seems to you that I have told but few things; for my purposes were few and great."
