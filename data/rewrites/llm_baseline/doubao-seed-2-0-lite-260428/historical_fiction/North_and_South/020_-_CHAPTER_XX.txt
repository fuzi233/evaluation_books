Margaret walked home weighed down by all she had seen and heard at the Higgins cottage, her mind so full of the small, brutal miseries of the strike that she could barely rouse herself to meet the quiet duties waiting for her. Her mother, now too unwell to leave the house, always greeted even the shortest of Margaret’s walks as a chance to hear news from the wider world, and expected a steady stream of cheerful chat to pass the hours.

“And did your factory friend agree to come on Thursday, to see you all dressed up for the Thorntons’ dinner?” Mrs Hale asked.

Margaret’s face fell. “She was so unwell I never even thought to ask her.”

“Dear me, it seems everyone is ill these days,” Mrs Hale said, with the faint, petty jealousy one invalid feels for another who commands more sympathy. Her kind nature won out a moment later, though, and her old Helstone habits of thought crept back in. “It must be so sad, being sick in one of those narrow little back streets. It’s bad enough being poorly here. What did you do to help her, Margaret? Mr Thornton dropped off some of his old port wine while you were out. Would a bottle of that do her any good, do you think?”

“No, mamma. I don’t think they are desperately poor — at least, they don’t speak as if they are — and Bessy’s illness is consumption. She won’t want wine. I thought, maybe, I could bring her a little of that preserve we made from our dear Helstone orchard fruit. No, wait — there’s another family I’d rather give that to — oh mamma, mamma! How can I possibly dress up in my finery and go off to some fancy party, when I’ve seen such sorrow today?” Margaret burst, spilling all the details of the dead baby, the starving Boucher children, and Bessy’s ragged cough before she could check herself.

The story distressed Mrs Hale terribly. She grew restless, irritated until she could do something to fix the harm, and ordered Margaret to pack a hamper of food, warm blankets, and extra remedies right there in the drawing room, to be dispatched that very minute. She almost snapped at Margaret when her daughter pointed out there was no rush, that Higgins had already covered their immediate needs and Margaret had left money with Bessy, calling her unfeeling for suggesting the hamper could wait until morning. She did not rest easy until the basket was carried out the door.

Then she paused, and a shadow crossed her face. “Wait — after all, we might have done wrong. Only last time Mr Thornton was here, he said you can never be a true friend to the turn-outs if you give them help that lets them drag the strike out. This Boucher man is one of the strikers, isn’t he?”

Mr Hale, fresh from a tutoring session with Thornton that had drifted into their usual long, wide-ranging conversation, came upstairs just then, and his wife put the question to him. Margaret was too wound up and upset to care if their hamper prolonged the conflict, far too preoccupied with the immediate human cost to think through the longer consequences. Mr Hale listened carefully, trying to judge the matter as calmly as a magistrate, recalling all the clear, logical arguments Thornton had laid out for him barely half an hour before. He landed on a gentle, unsatisfactory compromise.

“For this family, at this moment, you two did exactly the right thing. I can’t imagine you could have done otherwise,” he said. “Still, as a general rule, what Thornton says holds true. If this strike drags on, the masters will just bring in labour from other towns — if the end result isn’t, as it so often has been before, some new machine that cuts the number of workers we need entirely. The kindest thing for the strikers in the long run is not to prop them up in their folly. But I will go see Boucher first thing tomorrow, and see what else can be done for him.”

Mr Hale set out early the next morning, as he promised. Boucher was not at home, but he had a long, gentle talk with his wife, promised to secure her an infirmary admission note, and saw that the children, left to run wild downstairs while their mother talked, were making quick, happy work of the hamper the Hales had sent. He came home with a far more cheerful, hopeful account than Margaret had dared to hope for; his imagination, primed by Margaret’s dramatic stories of the night before to expect far worse, softened the edges of what he had seen, making the little cottage seem far cosier and more stable than it really was.

“But I will go back, and speak to the man himself,” he said. “I can hardly get used to these Milton houses. They have furniture our Helstone labourers would never dream of buying, and eat food our villagers would consider a luxury — yet the moment their weekly wages stop, their only resource is the pawn shop. You have to learn an entirely new standard for what counts as hardship, up here in Milton.”

Bessy, too, was a little better that day. She was still so weak that the feverish wish she had once expressed to see Margaret dressed up for the dinner seemed to have slipped entirely from her mind, a half-delirious fancy rather than a real desire. Margaret could not help comparing the heavy-hearted, joyless task of getting ready for a party she did not care to attend to the giddy, silly girlish toilettes she and Edith had shared barely a year before, back at Helstone. The only small pleasure she took in decking herself out was the thought that her mother would enjoy seeing her look nice.

Dixon threw open the drawing room door a moment later, eager for admiration. “Miss Hale looks well, ma’am — doesn’t she? Mrs Shaw’s coral set could not have come at a better time. It gives just the right touch of colour, else Miss Margaret, you’d look far too pale.”

Margaret’s thick, glossy black hair would not hold a plait; she twisted it tight into heavy, smooth coils that wrapped around her head like a dark crown, gathered into a large spiral knot at the nape of her neck. She secured it with two long coral pins, small as arrows, looped the white silk of her sleeves with strings of matching coral, and wore a heavy string of the same red beads low on her milk-white throat, just below the curve of her jaw.

“Oh Margaret!” Mrs Hale sighed. “How I wish I could be going with you to one of the old Barrington assemblies, taking you the way Lady Beresford used to take me.”

Margaret kissed her mother for that little burst of maternal pride, but she could barely smile, her heart was so heavy. “I would far rather stay home with you, mamma.”

“Nonsense, darling! Be sure to pay close attention to the dinner, won’t you? I want to hear all about how they do things here in Milton. Especially the second course, dear — see what they serve instead of game.”

Mrs Hale would have been astonished past words if she could have seen the sumptuous spread on the Thorntons’ dining table. Margaret, whose London upbringing had taught her to prefer quiet, refined elegance, found the sheer quantity of delicacies almost oppressive; half the number of dishes would have been more than enough, and far more tasteful. But lavish hospitality was one of Mrs Thornton’s non-negotiable rules: every single dish had to have enough portions for every guest to try it, if they wished. Though she lived simply, almost abstemiously in her daily habits, she was proud to set a feast before guests who valued it, and her son shared that sentiment. He had never known any high society that did not revolve around the exchange of superb meals, and even now, when he was denying himself every unnecessary sixpence and had more than once regretted sending out the dinner invitations, he was glad to see the grand, careful spread laid out.

Margaret and Mr Hale were the first to arrive, for he was anxiously punctual to the minute they had been invited. The drawing room was empty save for Mrs Thornton and Fanny. Every cover had been taken off the furniture, and the room blazed with yellow silk damask and a carpet printed so thick with bright flowers it made your eyes tired. Every corner was stuffed with ornament, a jarring contrast to the ugly, wide mill-yard visible through the windows, its great folding gates propped open for carriages to drive through. The mill loomed tall on the left side of the panes, its many storeys casting a long shadow that darkened the bright summer evening long before the sun began to set.

“My son was tied up with business until the very last moment. He will be here directly, Mr Hale. Please, sit down,” Mrs Thornton said.

Mr Hale was standing at one of the windows, and he turned to her. “Don’t you find living so close to the mill rather unpleasant, at times? The smoke, the noise — the constant stream of workers coming and going?”

Mrs Thornton drew herself up stiffly. “Never. I am not so grand that I want to forget where my son’s wealth and power come from. Besides, there is no other factory in Milton half as fine. One single room is two hundred and twenty square yards.”

“I meant no insult — I was thinking of the steam and the oily fumes,” Mr Hale said.

“I quite agree with Mr Hale!” Fanny put in. “There’s a permanent smell of hot steam and machine oil, and the noise is positively deafening.”

“I have heard ‘music’ far more deafening than that,” Mrs Thornton said, her face hard. “The engine room is at the far street end of the factory. We barely hear it, save in summer, when all the windows are open. As for the murmur of the workers, it disturbs me no more than the hum of a beehive. If I think of it at all, I connect it all to my son — remember that every part of it belongs to him, that he is the head directing every piece. Just now, there is no sound from the mill at all. The hands have been ungrateful enough to turn out, as you have no doubt heard. The business I was attending to when you arrived? All about the steps he will take to make them learn their proper place.”

Her face, always stern, darkened to angry fury as she spoke. The expression did not soften even when John Thornton walked into the room, for she saw at once the weight of care and worry he could not hide — though he greeted his guests with a cheer that felt warm and genuine. He shook Margaret’s hand, the first time their hands had ever touched, though Margaret did not notice the fact at all. He asked after Mrs Hale, listened to Mr Hale’s bright, hopeful account of visiting the Boucher cottage, then glanced over at Margaret to see if she agreed with her father’s rosy assessment, and saw no shadow of disagreement on her face.

The sight of her stopped him short. He had never seen her dressed like this before, and it struck him that this elegant finery suited her noble bearing and lofty, serene face so perfectly that she ought to wear clothes like this always. She was talking to Fanny, but he could not hear what they said. He watched his sister fidgeting, adjusting her gown every few seconds, her eyes wandering aimlessly around the room with no purpose, and contrasted that restlessness uneasily with Margaret’s large, soft eyes, steady on whatever her companion was saying, as if a gentle hush radiated from them. The curve of her red lips, just parted as she listened; her head tilted slightly forward, making a long, graceful line from the crown of her glossy raven hair down to the smooth ivory tip of her shoulder; her round white arms and tapered hands, laid lightly across each other, perfectly still. Thornton sighed, taking all that in in one quick, comprehensive glance, then turned his back on the two young women and threw himself wholeheartedly into conversation with Mr Hale.

More guests began to pile in, one after another. Fanny left Margaret’s side to help her mother receive them. Thornton noticed that no one was speaking to Margaret, and grew restless at the apparent neglect — but he did not go near her, did not even look at her. He just knew exactly what she was doing, far better than he tracked the movements of anyone else in the room. Margaret, meanwhile, was so unselfconscious, and so amused watching all the new faces, that she never once wondered if she was being overlooked. Someone led her down to dinner, but she did not catch his name, and he made no effort to talk to her. The gentlemen were deep in very animated debate, while most of the ladies stayed quiet, making mental notes of the food and quietly criticising each other’s gowns. Margaret soon picked up the thread of the general conversation, and leaned in to listen carefully.

Mr Horsfall, the visiting stranger who had inspired the whole dinner party, was asking questions about Milton’s trade and manufacturing, and the rest of the gentlemen — all mill-owners — were giving him answers and explanations. A dispute broke out, heated and earnest, and the group referred the question to Thornton, who had barely spoken all evening. He laid out his opinion, and the logic underpinning it, so clearly that even his opponents yielded. Margaret watched him, struck by how straightforward, simple, modest and dignified he was in his own home, among his peers. She had never seen him look so fine. When he had come to their cottage, he had always seemed over-eager, or prickly with a quiet annoyance that assumed he was being judged unfairly, too proud to ever try and explain himself. But here, surrounded by equals, there was no uncertainty about his position. Everyone saw him as a man of great character, immense force. He did not have to fight for their respect; he already had it, and he knew it, and that quiet security lent his voice and his manner a grand, easy calm Margaret had never seen in him before.

He was not used to talking to ladies, and what little he said to them was slightly formal. He barely spoke to Margaret at all. She was surprised to find how much she enjoyed the dinner. She knew enough of Milton’s local interests now, even some of the technical jargon the mill-owners tossed around as they talked, and she found herself silently taking a very firm side in their debate. They talked with desperate, unfeigned earnestness, not the tired, rehearsed small talk that had made London parties so wearisome to her. She wondered that none of them mentioned the looming strike. She did not know yet how coolly the masters regarded it, as an inconvenience with only one possible end. The men were cutting their own throats, they agreed, just as they had done so many times before. If they were foolish enough to put themselves in the hands of rascally paid union delegates, they would have to take the consequences. One or two of them thought Thornton looked out of sorts, and of course he would lose money over the turn-out — but it was an accident that could happen to any of them, and Thornton was as capable of managing a strike as any man alive, as hard as iron. The workers had picked the wrong man to try and fool, and they all chuckled quietly at the thought of the strikers’ coming discomfiture.

The drawing room felt rather dull for Margaret after the men went off to drink. She was relieved when the gentlemen filed back in, not just so she could catch her father’s eye and chase away her sleepiness, but so she could listen to conversations far bigger than the petty gossip about dresses and dinner the ladies had been passing around. She liked the unapologetic sense of power these Milton men carried, the giddy intoxication that came from remembering what they had built already, and imagining what they would build in future. Their confidence could tip over into boasting, but they defied the old limits of what was thought possible, too caught up in their anticipated triumphs over the natural world to think of themselves, or even the present moment, half the time.

She started a little when Mr Thornton spoke right at her elbow. “I could see you were on our side in that discussion at dinner, weren’t you, Miss Hale?”

“Certainly. But I know so little about it. I was surprised, though, to hear from Mr Horsfall that there are people who think so completely the opposite way — that man Morison he was talking about. He cannot be a gentleman, can he?”

“I am hardly the right person to judge anyone else’s ‘gentlemanliness’, Miss Hale. I don’t quite follow how you’re using the word. But I will say that Morison is no true man. I don’t know him, I’m just judging from what Horsfall said of him.”

“I think my idea of ‘gentleman’ includes your ‘true man’,” Margaret said.

“And a great deal more, you mean to imply. I disagree. To me, a man is a far higher, far more complete being than a gentleman.”

“What do you mean?” Margaret asked, frowning. “We must be defining the word differently.”

“I take it ‘gentleman’ only describes how a person behaves in relation to other people. But when you call someone ‘a man’, you are thinking of him not just relative to his fellow men, but relative to himself — to life, to time, to eternity. A castaway as alone as Robinson Crusoe, a man locked in a dungeon for his whole life, even a saint exiled on Patmos: his endurance, his strength, his faith, all of that is better described as the mark of a man, than the mark of a gentleman. I am rather tired of the word ‘gentlemanly’ these days. It gets misused constantly, twisted out of all recognition, while the simple, honest weight of the words ‘man’ and ‘manly’ go unappreciated. I think it is just part of the empty cant of the age.”

Margaret paused, turning the thought over in her head, but before she could speak her slow, carefully considered opinion, Thornton was called away by a group of eager mill-owners, whose words she could not hear. She could guess what they were saying, though, from Thornton’s short, clear answers, steady and firm as the boom of a distant cannon. They were clearly talking about the strike, suggesting different courses of action. She heard Thornton say, “That has already been done.” Then came a hurried murmur as two of the men spoke at once. “All those arrangements are made already.”

Mr Slickson grabbed hold of Thornton’s arm, trying to emphasise the doubts and difficulties he was naming. Thornton moved slightly away, lifted his eyebrows just a little, and said: “I take the risk.
