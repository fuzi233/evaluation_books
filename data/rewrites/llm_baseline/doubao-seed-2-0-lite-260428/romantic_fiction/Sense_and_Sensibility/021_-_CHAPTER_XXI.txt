The Palmers departed for Cleveland the very next morning, leaving the two households at Barton to fall back on one another’s company for entertainment. But that quiet lull barely lasted long enough for Elinor to untangle her thoughts of their recent visitors: she still found herself wondering how Charlotte Palmer could be perpetually giddy and content with no discernible cause, how Mr. Palmer could be so dryly sharp and clever yet choose to fade into gruff silence beside his frivolous wife, and how very common, and very strange, that deep, quiet mismatch between husbands and wives often was. Before she could settle those thoughts, Sir John and Mrs. Jennings’ relentless, zeal for social merriment had already lined up a new set of acquaintances for the Dashwood sisters to meet and observe.

On a spontaneous morning trip into Exeter, the pair had stumbled across two young women whom Mrs. Jennings had thrilled to discover were distant relations of hers. That tiny connection was more than enough for Sir John: he immediately invited the two girls to stay at Barton Park the second their existing engagements in Exeter were finished. The girls threw aside their previous plans the moment the invitation was offered, and when Sir John returned home to break the news, Lady Middleton was thrown into a flutter of very real alarm. She was to play host to two girls she had never laid eyes on, with no proof of their elegance, or even their basic gentility, and her husband and mother’s blithe assurances on that front counted for nothing at all. Their being distant cousins only made the situation worse. Mrs. Jennings’ attempts to console her daughter fell flat immediately, as she chirped that they need not worry over the girls being fashionable—they were all family, after all, so they simply had to put up with one another. There was no calling the visit off now, so Lady Middleton resigned herself to the arrangement with all the frosty philosophy of a well-bred woman, contenting herself only with giving her husband gentle, pointed reprimands about the matter five or six times each day.

When the young ladies arrived, they were far from unrefined or unfashionable: their dresses were bright and new, their manners soft and civil, they gushed over every inch of Barton Park’s house, and fell into raptures over every piece of furniture they saw. Most importantly, they doted on the Middleton children so fiercely that Lady Middleton’s good will was fully won before they had even spent an hour in the house. She declared them perfectly agreeable girls—high praise, coming from her ladyship, who rarely wasted energy on enthusiasm. Buoyed by her praise, Sir John immediately trotted down to the Dashwood cottage to tell them the Miss Steeles had arrived, and to swear they were the sweetest girls in all of England. Elinor did not put much stock in that kind of praise; she knew very well that “the sweetest girls in the world” could be found in every corner of the country, in every shape, with every sort of face, temper, and wit.

Sir John begged the whole family to walk up to the Park that very instant to meet his guests. He was a benevolent, gregarious man who could not bear the thought of hoarding new friends even from people who were barely related to him.
“Come now, do,” he pressed. “Pray come—you simply must. You can’t imagine how much you’ll like them. Lucy is shockingly pretty, and so good humoured! The children are hanging all over her already as if they’ve known her for years. And both of them are dying to meet you most of all—they heard in Exeter that you three are the most beautiful creatures alive, and I told them that’s the whole truth, and more besides. They brought a whole coach full of toys for the children, too. How can you be so cross as to refuse? You know you’re cousins after a fashion—you’re my cousins, they’re my wife’s, so it stands to reason you’re related.”

He could not bully them into coming that very minute, though. The most he could extract was a promise that they would call at the Park within the next two days, before he left them, bewildered at their lack of haste, to go back and brag about the Dashwoods to the Steeles exactly as he had bragged about the Steeles to the Dashwoods.

When their promised visit took place, the sisters found nothing to admire in the eldest Miss Steele, Anne, who was nearly thirty, with a plain, vacant face that bore no hint of sharpness. But the younger, Lucy, who was barely two-and-twenty, was undeniably pretty: her features were soft and bright, she had a quick, sharp eye and a crisp, lively air that lent her a distinct charm even if she had no real grace or elegant poise. Their manners were unfailingly civil, and Elinor soon credited them with a good deal of practical sense, as she watched how carefully, how steadily, they curried favour with Lady Middleton. They never stopped raving over the children’s beauty, chasing after their notice, humoring every single one of their whims. Every spare moment they had away from the children was spent gushing over whatever trivial thing Lady Middleton was doing, or begging for a pattern of the elegant new gown she had worn the day before, which they swore had left them utterly enchanted.

As luck would have it for people who court favour through such small foibles, a fond mother, greedy as she is for praise of her children, is also the most credulous creature alive. Her demands for flattery are endless, but she will swallow every scrap of it offered to her. So Lady Middleton watched with perfect maternal complacency as the two Miss Steeles let the children untie their sashes, yank their hair down over their ears, rummage through their workbags, and steal their knives and scissors. She saw no harm in it, no discomfort, and felt only mild surprise that Elinor and Marianne sat so calmly nearby without fighting for a turn to be tormented by her darlings.
“John is in such spirits today!” she laughed when her eldest boy grabbed Anne Steele’s handkerchief and threw it straight out the open window. “He’s full of little monkey tricks.” A minute later, when her second son pinched Lucy’s fingers hard enough to make her wince, she cooed, “How playful William is!”
“And here is my sweet little Annamaria,” she added, pulling her three-year-old close to caress her. The little girl had not made a sound for two whole minutes. “She is always so gentle and quiet—there never was such a good little thing in the world.”

But as she leaned in to kiss the child, a pin stuck in her headdress scratched little Annamaria’s neck, and this paragon of quiet gentleness immediately set up a scream so loud and violent no noisy, unruly child could have topped it. The mother’s consternation was great, but it was nothing next to the alarm of the two Miss Steeles. All three of them rushed to soothe the crying girl: she was seated in her mother’s lap, smothered in kisses, her scratch bathed with lavender water by a kneeling Lucy, her mouth stuffed full of sugar plums by Anne. Rewarded so richly for her tears, the little girl had no reason to stop crying. She screamed and sobbed loudly, kicked her brothers when they tried to approach her, and none of their efforts worked until Lady Middleton suddenly remembered that the previous week, apricot marmalade had soothed a bruised temple perfectly. She immediately ordered the same remedy for the tiny scratch, and the little girl’s screams faded just a little at the mention of the sweet preserve. Lady Middleton carried her out of the room to fetch it, the two boys trotting after her despite her protests to stay, and all at once the four young ladies were left in a quiet that the room had not known for hours.
“Poor little love,” Anne Steele said the second they were gone. “It could have been such a terrible accident.”
“Hardly,” Marianne said bluntly. “Not for a tiny scratch like that. That’s the way people overreact when there is nothing at all to actually fear.”
“Lady Middleton is such a sweet woman, isn’t she?” Lucy Steele said quickly.

Marianne was silent. She could never bring herself to say something she did not truly feel, no matter how small the occasion, so all the work of polite small talk fell to Elinor. She did her best, speaking of Lady Middleton with more warmth than she actually felt, though far less than Lucy was showing.
“And Sir John is such a charming man, too!” Anne cried.
Elinor’s praise was simpler, far less effusive: she said he was perfectly good humoured and friendly.
“What a perfect little family they have! I have never seen such beautiful children in all my life. I am already completely devoted to them— I have always been utterly fond of children.”
“I could tell that much this morning,” Elinor said, with a small smile.
“I think you must find the Middletons far too indulgent with the children,” Lucy said, sounding almost shy. “Perhaps they are a little spoiled, but it is so natural for a mother like Lady Middleton. For my part, I love to see children full of life. I cannot bear them when they are quiet and tame.”
“For my part,” Elinor replied, “while I am at Barton Park, I never look at quiet, tame children with anything but relief.”

A short pause followed, before Anne Steele, who seemed desperate to keep the conversation going, spoke up abruptly:
“How do you like Devonshire, Miss Dashwood? I expect you were very sad to leave Sussex, weren’t you?”
Elinor was a little surprised by how familiar the question was, but she said that yes, they had been sad to leave.
“Norland Park is such a beautiful place, isn’t it?” Anne added.
“We have heard Sir John speak of it endlessly,” Lucy put in quickly, as if apologizing for her sister’s boldness.
“Anyone who has seen Norland would admire it,” Elinor said. “Though no one could love its beauty as much as we do.”
“Did you have lots of handsome young beaux there?” Anne went on. “I expect you don’t have nearly as many here in Devonshire. For my part, I always think a good beau makes everything far more fun.”
“Why would you imagine there are fewer respectable young men in Devonshire than in Sussex?” Lucy said, looking embarrassed at her sister.
“Oh, my dear, I never said there weren’t lots of fine beaux in Exeter,” Anne protested. “I just didn’t know what the men were like around Norland, and I was worried the Miss Dashwoods might get bored at Barton without the numbers they were used to. But I suppose young ladies like you don’t care about beaux all that much. For my part, I think they are lovely, as long as they dress neatly and behave politely. I can’t stand a messy, unkempt man. There is Mr. Rose in Exeter, for example— a very smart beau, clerk to Mr. Simpson the lawyer, but you can’t bear to run into him first thing in the morning, he looks so unkempt. I suppose your brother was quite a famous beau before he married, Miss Dashwood, since he is so wealthy?”
“I am afraid I do not even know what the word ‘beau’ really means,” Elinor said, laughing a little. “But I can tell you this: if he was one before he married, he is still one now, for nothing about him has changed at all.”
“Oh no, no one counts married men as beaux,” Anne said. “They have far more important things to do.”
“Lord, Anne!” Lucy cried. “You can talk about nothing but beaux— you will make Miss Dashwood think you care for nothing else.” To redirect the conversation, she started gushing over the Dashwood cottage’s furniture.

That short exchange was more than enough for Elinor. Anne’s coarse, silly forwardness made her completely unlikable, and Elinor was not fooled by Lucy’s pretty face and sharp eye: she saw clearly that the girl had no real elegance, no honest artlessness, and she left the Park that day with no desire to grow any closer to them.

The Miss Steeles felt very differently. They had come to Exeter full of carefully rationed admiration for Sir John, his family, and all his relations, and they lavished no small portion of it on the Dashwood sisters, whom they declared the most beautiful, accomplished, agreeable young women they had ever met, and whom they were determined to grow very close to. Elinor soon realized that that intimacy was inevitable. Sir John was firmly on the Steeles’ side, and their combined pressure could not be resisted. They were fated to spend an hour or two nearly every day in the same room, bound together by that casual, forced familiarity that passes for friendship in the country. Sir John thought that simply being in the same room counted as being close friends, and he never stopped scheming new ways to bring them all together, fully convinced he was forging a perfect bond. He did everything he could to make them open up to one another, too, spilling every tiny, delicate detail he knew about the Dashwoods’ lives to the Steeles. Before Elinor had even seen the Steeles twice, Anne Steele was already offering her congratulations on Marianne having caught such a handsome, smart beau since they moved to Barton.
“It will be so lovely for her to marry so young,” Anne chirped. “I hear he is very handsome, a total beau. And I hope you have the same luck very soon, Miss Dashwood— though I expect you already have a secret beau hidden away somewhere, don’t you?”

Elinor did not doubt for a second that Sir John had no more discretion about teasing her over her quiet regard for Edward Ferrars than he had about teasing Marianne over Willoughby. In fact, her secret attachment was his favourite joke, newer and more speculative than Marianne’s had ever been. Since Edward’s visit, every dinner they shared had ended with Sir John raising his glass to her “best affections”, winking and nodding so broadly that everyone at the table noticed. He had turned the letter F into a running joke, endlessly referencing it until Elinor had long decided it was the most overrated witticism in the entire alphabet.

The Miss Steeles, as she expected, got to hear every bit of that teasing. Anne’s curiosity about the name of the mysterious suitor was impertinent, entirely in keeping with her nosy nature, but Sir John did not drag the secret out for long. He got just as much glee out of spilling the name as Anne got out of hearing it.
“His name is Ferrars,” he whispered loudly, leaning across the table. “But don’t you dare tell anyone. It’s a great secret.”
“Ferrars!” Anne Steele repeated, her eyes lighting up. “Mr. Ferrars is the lucky man? The brother of your sister-in-law, Miss Dashwood? He is a perfectly agreeable young man, isn’t he? I know him very well.”
“Anne, how can you say that?” Lucy cut in, shaking her head at her sister. “We only met him once or twice at my uncle’s house in Exeter. That is hardly enough to say we know him very well.”

Elinor heard every word, her attention caught fast, her chest tight with shock. She could not stop wondering who this uncle was, where he lived, how they had ever come to meet Edward. She ached for the conversation to continue, even if she did not want to join in herself, but no more was said. For the first time in her life, Elinor found herself thinking Mrs. Jennings was far too incurious about trivial gossip, or far too unwilling to share what she knew. The sly, knowing edge in Anne Steele’s voice as she spoke Edward’s name left her with a sharp, lingering suspicion: the girl thought she knew something unflattering about him, some secret Elinor had never heard. But no matter how Elinor’s curiosity nagged at her, the subject of Mr. Ferrars was never brought up again by the Miss Steeles, not even when Sir John openly mentioned his name later that evening.
