James Wolfe, Harry’s new colonel, returned from America just weeks after the Virginian had joined his regiment. Previously lieutenant-colonel of Kingsley’s, Wolfe had been rewarded for his gallantry at Cape Breton with command of a second battalion. Harry visited him with genuine respect and warmth, eager to pay his duty to a man now drawing the world’s attention—many believing he was destined for greatness in arms. The recent campaigns in France had revealed the shortcomings of once-revered officers. The Duke of Marlborough proved no worthy heir to his legendary ancestor, and doubts already surrounded Lord George Sackville’s abilities—doubts confirmed by his misjudgment at Minden. The nation yearned for military glory, and the ministry sought a leader who might satisfy that hunger. With both Wolfe and Lambert stationed in London, their friendship rekindled, and none was more pleased than Lambert at his younger friend’s rise.

When not on duty, Harry could not get enough of Wolfe’s tales of the previous year’s campaigns. He spoke openly, without reserve, revealing a mind as candid as it was courageous—a quality later seen in Nelson. He discussed battles frankly, unburdened by pride or pretense. While some at court might sneer at his simplicity, among friends he found eager listeners. The young general possessed a boyish romanticism: he loved music and poetry deeply. On his final day, he confessed he would rather have written Gray’s *Elegy* than won a battle. It is certain such a man would have drawn George Warrington into intimate conversation. Both were lovers, both accepted, both longing for happiness—no doubt their talks were full of sentiment, though we lack precise records to recount them.

In one of his later letters, Warrington wrote:

“I had the honour of knowing General Wolfe during his last stay in London. We shared a conversation so absorbing it never failed to delight us both. I admired his simplicity, his honesty, and the noble courage that marked every word and gesture. He was deeply in love, and craved laurels to carry to his beloved. 'If it be a sin to covet honour,' he often said, quoting Harry the Fifth—his passion for plays and verse was boundless—'I am the most offending soul alive.' On his last day, he enjoyed a triumph that satisfied even the most ravenous appetite for glory. He hungered for it—not merely as a soldier fulfilling duty, but as a knight chasing dragons. My own country has lately produced a very different kind of leader, of a quite opposite nature. I scarcely know which to admire more—the Briton’s chivalrous fire, or the Roman-like steadfastness of our great Virginian.”

While Lambert remained in London on official business, his family stayed contentedly with him. Warrington, too, seemed content with the quiet life of Southampton Row and the beauty of Bedford Gardens, choosing not to leave London for long. He did make a pilgrimage to Castlewood, spending a few days there in the chamber once occupied by Colonel Esmond as a boy. He was welcomed warmly by those of the family present—but no doubt he preferred the city, where he found greater joy in the company of a young woman whose presence surpassed all others, even if Lady Maria and her circle were courteous and gracious. Lady Maria especially delighted in the tragedy George and Parson Sampson read aloud to the ladies. The chaplain praised it enthusiastically, and indeed, it was through his support—rather than Mr. Johnson’s—that the play ever reached the stage. Though Johnson urged Garrick to produce it at Drury Lane, Garrick had already committed to a tragedy by Home, author of *Douglas*. Thus, *Carpezan* went to Covent Garden, where Mr. Rich accepted it.

On the night of its premiere, Warrington hosted an elegant dinner at the Bedford Head in Covent Garden, after which he and his guests proceeded en masse to the theatre. A few lingered behind with the young author, awaiting news from inside. Barry played Carpezan with force; Shuter took the role of the old nobleman; Reddish excelled as Ulric; and the King of Bohemia was portrayed by a young Dublin actor, Mr. Geoghegan—known on stage as Hagan—who captivated audiences with his grace and presence. Mrs. Woffington appeared somewhat aged in the first act, but her death scene in the fourth stirred terror and awe. Miss Wayn sang the ballad sung by the king’s page at the moment of execution, and all agreed Barry was profoundly moving in his portrayal of Carpezan, especially during the final scene. Hagan’s elegance drew universal admiration. Mr. Rich staged the production with care, though some questioned the inclusion of a favourite elephant—used in pantomimes—during the Janissary march. One of Warrington’s black servants marched beside it in Turkish costume, while another sat in the footman’s gallery, weeping and cheering at key moments.

The execution of Sybilla marked the turning point. Once her head fell, George’s friends breathed easier, and messengers arrived at the coffee-house one after another, reporting the play’s overwhelming success. Amid thunderous applause, Barry announced the piece would return—and revealed it was the work of a young gentleman from Virginia, his first venture into drama.

We wish we could have watched Theo’s anxious flutter during the uncertain moments, and seen the flush and sparkle in her eyes when victory was assured. Harry, pale as death during the tense fourth act, turned whiter than Barry himself, covered in chalk. Yet when the curtain fell, he roared louder than any giant could have clapped. Wolfe and Lambert cheered wildly. Mrs. Lambert wept—though Hetty teased, “Why do you cry, mamma? You don’t want any of them alive again; you know it serves them all right.” Still, the girl was thrilled, as was little Charley from the Chartreux, granted leave for the evening, and Miss Lucy, brought from boarding school expressly for the occasion. My Lord Castlewood and Lady Maria attended; the former left his box to congratulate Barry and the cast, while Parson Sampson led the applause from the pit, having instructed Gumbo to watch him from the gallery and follow his lead.

That night, celebrations followed—many of them. Mr. Gumbo hosted a gathering for gentlemen of the shoulder-knot who had supported his master’s masterpiece. Henry Warrington gave a supper at the Star and Garter in Pall Mall for ten officers from his new regiment, who had come specifically to champion *Carpezan*. Finally, Warrington entertained the three principal actors, his family from the side box, Mr. Johnson, his clever friend Mr. Reynolds the painter, Lord Castlewood and his sister, and others. Lady Maria sat beside the young actor playing the King. Somehow, Warrington ended up next to Theo, and no doubt they passed a pleasant evening together. The room buzzed with joy and camaraderie. When toasts were called, Lady Maria cheerfully offered “The King of Hungary.” The actor, full of spirit and charm, declared he had already died once that night and hoped to die a hundred times more on the same field—but dead or alive, he vowed he would remain her humble servant. Ah, if only he had a real crown instead of pasteboard and tinsel—he would lay it at her feet with joy! Neither Lord Castlewood nor Mr. Esmond approved of the actor’s bold gallantry—no doubt partly due to the wine and punch he’d consumed freely. Theo and her sister, new to society, were startled by his fervor, but Lady Maria, more worldly, took it in stride.

Late into the night, coaches were summoned. Gentlemen escorted the ladies home, then returned to the supper room. The end came with Carpezan carried away in a chair, the King of Hungary nursing a terrible headache, and the poet astonished the next morning when half a dozen guests appeared at his door—guests he had invited overnight but barely remembered.

As he helped Mrs. Lambert and her daughters into their coach the night before, all the ladies were flustered, excited, overjoyed. Naturally, the young man was with them the next day, talking endlessly about the play, the audience, the actors, and the beauty of the performance. Mrs. Lambert had heard warnings about actresses being dangerous companions for young men. She hoped George would avoid the greenroom too often.

George smiled and said he had a safeguard against such temptations—one he feared not at all. As he spoke, he looked into Theo’s eyes, as if they held the amulet that would protect him from all harm.

“Why should he fear?” asked the maiden simply. She knew nothing of danger or deceit.

“No, my darling, I don’t think he need,” said the mother, kissing her.

“You don’t suppose Mr. George would fall for that painted old creature who played the lead?” asked Hetty, tossing her head. “She must be old enough to be his mother.”

“Do you suppose,” replied the mother sharply, “that at our age no one can care for us? That we have no hearts left? I believe—indeed, I hope and trust—your father thinks otherwise. He is perfectly satisfied, miss. He does not sneer at age, however little girls may choose to. And they had better be back in school, and remember what the fifth commandment says—yes, Hetty!”

“I didn’t mean to break it by saying an actress was as old as George’s mother,” pleaded Hetty.

“George’s mother is as old as I am—when we were at school. And Fanny Parker—Mrs. Mountain now—was seven months older, and we were in the French class together. I see no reason why our age should become a subject of ridicule for our children. I will thank you to spare it, please. Do you consider your mother too old, George?”

“I’m glad my mother is of your age, Aunt Lambert,” said George, in the most sentimental tone.

Strange how passion distorts reason! At some point before marriage, men often grow fond of their mothers-in-law. At this time, our dear General swore—with some justification—that he was jealous. Mrs. Lambert doted on George more than anyone else in the family. She dressed Theo beautifully to meet him, constantly caressed her, and consulted her whenever he spoke. “Don’t you think he looks well?” “Doesn’t he look pale today, Theo?” “Has he been reading too late?” If he caught a cold, she would have made gruel and soaked his feet in hot water. She sent him health recipes of her own invention. When he was away, she never stopped speaking of him to her daughter. Theo likely enjoyed the topic. When he arrived, she was always needed elsewhere—always leaving Theo to care for him until her return. But could you not hear her voice outside the door, chatting with servants or children? When she reappeared, George was usually seated at a distance from Theo—except once, when she dropped her scissors and he naturally bent down to pick them up. Why was she blushing? Were not cheeks meant to blush, and roses to bloom in spring? Mamma never noticed the blushes, but began a casual conversation as she sat down, brimming with happiness at her worktable.

Then came a letter from Virginia, penned in Madam Esmond’s familiar hand. George trembled and blushed before breaking the seal. It was in reply to his letter about Harry’s commission and his own feelings for Miss Lambert. She fully approved of his choice regarding Harry. As for his marriage, she was not opposed to early unions. She would accept a portrait of Miss Lambert, allowing for the exaggerations common in lovers’ likenesses, and hoped—sincerely—for his sake—that the young lady matched his description. From his letter, she gathered he had little money, which saddened her, though their family owned vast lands, even if cash was scarce. Still, by God’s blessing, there was plenty for generations to come, and her sons’ wives would share all she possessed. After hearing more from Mr. and Mrs. Lambert, she would write further. She admitted she had higher hopes for her son—given his name and prospects, he might have aimed for the highest lady in the land—but since Heaven had guided his heart toward her old friend’s daughter, she accepted it. She would welcome George’s wife as her own child.

Madam Esmond concluded with affectionate greetings to Mrs. Lambert and her blessing to the future daughter-in-law.

The letter was not warm, and the writer clearly only half-hearted in her approval—but it contained formal consent. How eagerly George rushed to Soho with the long-awaited news! His friends must have read it in his face—else why should Mrs. Lambert take her daughter’s hand and kiss her so passionately when he announced the news? Then, voice trembling, face pale, he turned to Mr. Lambert: “Madam Esmond’s letter, sir, is in reply to mine, in which I told her I had formed an attachment in England, and asked her permission. She gives it, I am grateful to say. I humbly ask my dear friends to be equally kind to me.”

“God bless thee, my dear boy!” said the good General, placing a hand on George’s head. “I am glad to call thee my son, George. There, there—don’t kneel, young folks! George may, of course, and thank God for giving him the best little wife in all England. Yes, my dear, except when you were ill, you never caused me a heartache. Happy is the man who wins thee!”

No doubt the young couple knelt before their parents, as was the custom. Mrs. Lambert kissed them both and wept copiously into her handkerchief. Hetty was absent during this emotional scene. When she heard of it, she laughed bitterly, saying, “Is this all the news you’ve got for me? I’ve known it for months. Do you think I have no eyes or ears?” But privately, she softened. She threw herself into her sister’s arms, vowing no one would ever love Theo as she did. With Theo, she became gentle and humble. She couldn’t resist teasing George, but he was too joyful to mind, too generous to resent her jealousy.

When the families read Madam Esmond’s letter, they found it vague—only promising a reception at her house, with no mention of settlement. The General frowned, having overlooked the letter until after the engagement. Now, it gave him little comfort.

“Bah!” said George. “I’ll earn three hundred pounds from this play. I can write one a year. If worst comes to worst, we can live on that.”

“And your inheritance,” added Theo’s father.

George hesitated, explaining how much he’d spent—on his mother’s bills, Harry’s commission and debts, and his own ransom. His own patrimony was nearly gone.

Mr. Lambert’s expression grew graver—but seeing his daughter’s alarmed eyes, he pulled her into his arms and vowed that, come what may, his darling would not be denied her happiness.

About returning to Virginia, George confessed he disliked the idea of being entirely dependent on his mother. He described life at home, and how little freedom it allowed. No—why not stay in England? Write more plays, study law, seek a position? Why not? Already, he sketched a new tragedy, bringing drafts to Theo and her sister. Hetty yawned, but Theo declared it even more beautiful than the last—which she thought perfect.

The engagement was announced to both families. Sir Miles Warrington received a formal letter from his nephew. For a time, he saw no objection—though, given George’s name and prospects, he might have expected higher. In truth, Sir Miles believed Madam Esmond had made a generous settlement, assuming George’s circumstances were comfortable. But when he learned George was entirely dependent on his mother, and his own small fortune exhausted—just as Harry’s had been—Sir Miles’s anger erupted. He could not express his horror at the imprudence of both young men. He felt compelled to speak his mind and wrote to Madam Esmond, condemning the match. Could respectable people like the Lamberts truly lure a penniless youth into marrying their equally poor daughter? He laid out his views plainly, gave George half a finger whenever he visited, and refused to invite him to drink the famous family small-beer. Toward Harry, he was slightly softer—Harry had served well, earned praise, and seemed to reform. But George? A reckless prodigal racing to ruin. His name was spoken only with groans.

Are there still such youths today, I wonder, whose families turn on them, curse them, stone them, and pass stones to neighbours to join in? All patrimony spent! Gracious heavens! Sir Miles paled at the sight of his nephew. Lady Warrington prayed for him as a dangerous sinner. Meanwhile, George walked the town, unaware of the storm he had stirred. He took little Miley to the play and brought him back. He sent tickets to his aunt and cousins—they couldn’t refuse, or it would seem too obvious. So they accepted, and when country members came to town, they asked for more, whispering that George’s intimacy with the theatre meant disgraceful relations with actresses. When an august personage praised the play to Sir Miles at Saville House, the old man modified his opinion and spoke of it more respectfully henceforth.

Yet George lived on, indifferent to the judgment of uncles, aunts, and cousins alike.
