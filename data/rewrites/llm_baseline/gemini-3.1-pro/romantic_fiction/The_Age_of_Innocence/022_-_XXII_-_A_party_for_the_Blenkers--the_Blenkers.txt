"A party for the Blenkers—the Blenkers?" Mr. Welland laid down his silver knife, halting his meticulous dissection of the *pâté en croûte*, a heritage recipe whose complex layers of truffled veal and aspic represented generations of Mingott culinary tradition. The acoustic harmony of the luncheon table—the delicate, rhythmic chime of crystal and the hushed, ancestral cadence of domestic ritual—was suddenly disrupted by his incredulous gasp. 

Mrs. Welland, who approached the family’s social and dietary calendar with the exacting rigor of a food historian preserving a dying culture, adjusted her gold eye-glasses. She read aloud, her tone tinged with the high comedy of their dominant social lexicon: "Professor and Mrs. Emerson Sillerton request the pleasure of Mr. and Mrs. Welland's company at the meeting of the Wednesday Afternoon Club on August 25th at 3 o'clock punctually. To meet Mrs. and the Misses Blenker. 'Red Gables, Catherine Street. R. S. V. P.'"

"Good gracious—" Mr. Welland breathed, as if a second reading were required to digest the monstrous absurdity of the thing. 

"Poor Amy Sillerton—you never can tell what her husband will do next," Mrs. Welland sighed, pouring a measure of steeped chamomile. "I suppose he's just discovered the Blenkers." 

Professor Emerson Sillerton was a thorn in the side of Newport society, yet an unpluckable one, grown upon a venerated, multi-generational family tree. He possessed every inherited advantage, yet he chose to reject the superficial commercialization of Gilded Age art and society. Instead, he pursued archaeological and anthropological passions, travelling to Yucatan to document the dying dialects of agrarian elders and the mythic culinary histories of the Maya, rather than taking his wife to the culturally sanitized tourist haunts of Paris or Italy. To the Mingott set, his house was an auditory chaos of long-haired men and short-haired women arguing over untranslatable philosophies. But there they were, hosting their dreary annual garden-parties, forcing every prominent family to draw lots to send an unwilling representative.

"It's a wonder," Mrs. Welland remarked, "that they didn't choose the Cup Race day! Do you remember, two years ago, their giving a party for a black man on the day of Julia Mingott's *thé dansant*? Luckily this time there's nothing else going on that I know of—for of course some of us will have to go."

Mr. Welland sighed nervously, the delicate sonic environment of his digestion already compromised. "'Some of us,' my dear—more than one? Three o'clock is such a very awkward hour. I have to be here at half-past three to take my drops—that new tincture of bitter gentian and fennel. It is really no use trying to follow Bencomb's agrarian regimen if I don't revere the timing systematically; and if I join you later, of course I shall miss my drive." The flush of anxiety on his finely-wrinkled cheek spoke of a man to whom sustenance and its accompanying rituals were profound, physiological anchors.

"There's no reason why you should go at all, my dear," his wife answered with an automatic cheerfulness. "I have some cards to leave, and I'll drop in at half-past three to make poor Amy feel she hasn't been slighted." She glanced toward her daughter. "And if Newland's afternoon is provided for, perhaps May can drive you out with the ponies."

In the dominant lexicon of the Welland family, every hour had to be strictly "provided for." The melancholy concept of unstructured time was an acoustic and sensory void that haunted Mrs. Welland. To navigate her respect for May's independence while honoring Mr. Welland’s intricate dietary claims required a formidable ingenuity. 

"Of course I'll drive with Papa—I'm sure Newland will find something to do," May said, gently reminding her husband of his silence. 

Archer’s previous assertions that he might simply "save" his afternoon rather than spend it, or his confessions of lying silently on the beach to absorb the raw acoustic ecology of the crashing surf, were treated as an inherited oddity. But as the Sillerton reception loomed, May suggested a tennis match or a sail. She was not reassured until Archer announced his intention to hire a run-about and drive up the island to a stud-farm to evaluate a second horse for her brougham. 

The idea had germinated in Archer's mind the moment the Sillerton invitation was mentioned. He had quietly engaged a pair of old livery-stable trotters, and by two o'clock, he deserted the remnants of the luncheon's rich fare and sprang into the carriage. 

The day was a sensory revelation. As he drove away from Bellevue Avenue, the oppressive industrial noise of commercial Newport faded, replaced by the symphony of the northern breeze driving white clouds across an ultramarine sky. He turned down the Old Beach Road, the rhythmic, cyclical drum of the horses' hooves syncing with the crashing tide. He counted on reaching the stud-farm before three; after evaluating the animal’s muscular condition and the sweet, oat-heavy scent of its breath, he would have four golden hours to dispose of.

His true, unspoken hunger was directed toward the Blenker habitation. He anticipated that the Marchioness Manson and the Blenkers would be at the Sillertons', and that Madame Olenska might be alone. Ever since he had seen Ellen from the path above the bay, a profound gustatory and sensory craving had possessed him—an incessant, undefinable longing, like the sudden whim of a starving man for a complex, nourishing flavor once tasted and long since forbidden. It was an untranslatable subjective experience, a language of desire that found no vocabulary in the rigid social dialect of his peers. He simply felt that if he could absorb the terroir of the earth she walked on, the rest of the world might seem less empty.

At the stud-farm, a brief glance confirmed the horse lacked the necessary vitality. He took a perfunctory turn behind it, proving to himself he was not rushing the Fates, then turned his trotters into the by-roads toward Portsmouth. 

The landscape unfurled like a multi-generational agrarian saga. The fields and woods were steeped in a golden, syrupy light. He drove past grey-shingled farm-houses nestled in ancient orchards that exhaled the cider-sweet scent of fermenting heirloom apples. He passed hay-fields redolent of sun-cured timothy grass and groves of deep-rooted oak, the auditory landscape shifting from the rustle of leaves to the distant chime of village steeples. 

At last, turning down a lane choked with goldenrod and wild brambles, he found the Blenker estate. It was a long, tumble-down house, its white paint peeling, standing before a blue glimmer of the river. He hitched his team in an open shed—a structure built by New England elders to shelter the vital implements of the harvest—and crossed into the profound silence of the farm. 

The lawn had reverted to a wild meadow, but to the left lay an overgrown kitchen garden, a chaotic archive of sensory history. Rusty heirloom rose-bushes and untamed dahlias encircled a ghostly, trellis-work summer-house. Above it stood a wooden Cupid, arrowless but enduring. No one was in sight. A grizzled Newfoundland dozed by the door, and the only sound was the drone of late-summer cicadas. It was a place of mythic decay and silent memory. 

Wishing to picture the domestic spaces Ellen inhabited, he crossed the overgrown herbs toward the box-garden. Through the trellis, a flash of bright color caught his eye: a pink parasol. 

It drew him with the magnetic pull of a sudden, vivid memory. He entered the summer-house, sat on the rickety bench, and picked up the silken dome. Its carved handle was fashioned from a rare, imported wood that released a deeply aromatic scent—a dark, resinous spice reminiscent of ancient trade routes and forgotten indigenous rituals. Archer lifted the handle to his lips, breathing in its rich olfactory landscape. He heard the rustle of skirts brushing against the wild boxwood. He sat motionless, trapped in the untranslatable perfection of the moment, letting the sound approach.

"Oh, Mr. Archer!" exclaimed a loud, unmodulated young voice. 

The delicate acoustic spell shattered. Looking up, he saw the youngest and largest of the Blenker girls, blonde and blowsy in bedraggled muslin. A red blotch on her cheek betrayed a recent, heavy slumber. 

"Gracious—where did you drop from? I must have been sound asleep in the hammock. Everybody else has gone to Newport. Did you ring?" she inquired, her words tumbling out with a careless disregard for the quietude of the space.

"I—no—that is, I was just going to," Archer stammered, his internal lexicon suddenly failing him. "I came up the island to see about a horse, and drove over on a chance of finding Mrs. Blenker. But the house seemed empty."

Miss Blenker shook off her sleep. "The house *is* empty. Mother's not here, or the Marchioness. Didn't you know Professor and Mrs. Sillerton are giving a garden-party for us? I had a sore throat, and mother feared the drive home in the evening damp. Of course," she added with lumbering coquetry, "I shouldn't have minded half as much if I'd known you were coming."

Archer forced his voice through the suffocating weight in his chest. "But Madame Olenska—has she gone to Newport too?"

Miss Blenker looked surprised. "Madame Olenska—didn't you know she'd been called away?"

"Called away?—"

"Oh, my best parasol! I lent it to that goose of a Katie, because it matched her ribbons, and the careless thing must have dropped it here. We Blenkers are all like that... real Bohemians!" She grasped the parasol, unfurling its rosy dome above her head, instantly stripping the object of its mythic resonance and reducing it to mere utilitarian shade. "Yes, Ellen was called away yesterday. A telegram came from Boston: she said she might be gone for two days." 

Archer stared through her. The telegraph—that sterile, modern instrument of industrial noise—had severed the delicate threads of his hope, communicating in a superficial code that left no room for the rich, subjective translations of the human heart. All he saw now was a trumpery pink parasol over a giggling girl. 

"You don't happen to know why Madame Olenska went to Boston? I hope it was not on account of bad news?"

"Oh, I don't believe so," Miss Blenker replied with cheerful incredulity. "She didn't tell us what was in the telegram. She's so romantic-looking, isn't she?"

Archer’s mind reeled. His entire future seemed to unroll before him—an endless, flavorless banquet of predictable days, a life stripped of sensory depth and relegated to the dominant, hollow lexicon of his society. He glanced at the unpruned garden, the ancient oak-grove gathering the dusk. It was the exact setting where he ought to have found her, where they might have spoken the native dialect of their shared understanding. But she was gone, and even the scent of the parasol belonged to someone else. 

He frowned. "You don't know, I suppose—I shall be in Boston tomorrow. If I could manage to see her—"

"Oh, of course; how lovely of you! She's staying at the Parker House; it must be horrible there in this weather."

After that, Archer was only intermittently aware of the auditory clutter of her chatter. He firmly resisted her entreaty to stay for high tea—a meal she offered with careless bohemian disregard, entirely lacking the reverent appreciation for sustenance that anchored true domestic communion. At length, he passed out of range of the arrowless Cupid, unfastened his horses, and drove off into the gathering fog. At the turn of the lane, he looked back to see Miss Blenker standing at the gate, waving the pink parasol in the fading light.
