“The Kynge of Spayn is a foul paynim,
And lieveth on Mahound;
And pity it were that lady fayre
Should marry a heathen hound.”
—Kyng Estmere

Six weeks had passed since the duel when the miller of Stow arrived at the great house in distress, begging to borrow the bloodhounds. Rose Salterne was gone—vanished in the night, without a trace, and no one knew where she had fled. Sir Richard was away in Bideford, but the old steward took charge, summoning the keepers and sending servants down to the mill with every idle lad from the parish in tow. They saw the search as sport, a grand maiden-hunt, and judged the matter with all the bias they could muster in Rose’s favor. The miller and his wife were accused of cruelty, even of driving their maid to leap from the cliffs or drown herself in the sea.

On the other hand, the women of Stow were unanimous: the girl had “gone off” with some rogue, and pride would surely bring her downfall. Yet the facts told a different tale. Rose’s trinkets remained behind, untouched. Nothing was missing except some of her linen—a detail that led old Anthony, the steward, to suggest quietly that it might soon turn up in someone else’s chest.

The only clue was a faint footprint beneath her bedroom window. The bloodhound, leashed and tense, whined once, then let out a deep, mournful bay. He surged through the garden gate, dragging his keeper along the lane until they reached the downs above, heading straight toward Marslandmouth. There, breathless, the entire posse halted at Lucy Passmore’s door.

Lucy, as we may now recall, was a widow. Her prophecy about her husband had come true—he had never returned after sailing into the night with Eustace and the Jesuits. She shed a few tears, dried them quickly, and decided to act boldly. She rode to Stow and laid before Lady Grenville a tale of woe: her husband had gone fishing for pollock and never came back. But she claimed to have heard horsemen gallop past her window in the dead of night—surely the Jesuits—and believed they had seized her husband by force. After using him, she insisted, they had killed him and salted his body for provisions on their journey back to Rome. She begged protection from these “Popish skulkers” lurking near Chapel, and appealed to Lady Grenville’s sense of justice: should not the queen grant her a pension? For her heart’s love had been turned into a sainted martyr by idolatrous traitors.

Lady Grenville, who valued Lucy’s skill in healing (especially for children suffering from “housty,” or sore throats), pleaded her case before Sir Richard. His influence secured Lucy a better position than ever for the next few years.

But what of Rose? And where was Lucy herself?

Her door was locked. Around it stood her flock of goats, bleating uselessly for milk. From the hillside above, her donkeys wandered freely, answering the bloodhound’s howl with a cacophony of braying.

“They’m laughing at us, keper, they neddies,” muttered one servant. “Sure enough, we’m lost our labor here.”

Yet the bloodhound, after circling the cottage, veered down the glen, never stopping until he reached the edge of the sea.

“They’m taken water. Let’s go back and rout out the old witch’s house.”

“’Tis just like that old Lucy, to lock a poor maid into shame.”

They returned, broke into the cottage, and ransacked it—not only out of anger, but also, if truth be told, in hope of finding something worth taking. But there was nothing. Lucy had fled with all her belongings—except for the enormous black cat, still curled among the embers. At the sound of the dog, it vanished up the chimney, some saying with a strong smell of brimstone. When they looked outside, they chased it into the woods, where it lived many happy years, a terror to the rabbits of the glen.

The goats and donkeys were driven back to Stow. The mob returned, ashamed and uneasy, fearing what Sir Richard might say.

When he returned, he sold the animals and gave the money to the poor, promising to repay if Lucy ever returned and submitted to justice. But she never did. Her cottage, avoided like a haunted place, stood empty, slowly crumbling into four fern-covered walls. A quiet stream murmured past, pool to pool—the only voice for many years in that lonely glen.

A few days later, Sir Richard stopped at Clovelly Court on his way from Bideford to Stow. In passing, he mentioned news that made Will Cary spring from his seat as if struck.

“We know what it is,” he said.

“And no clue?” asked old Cary, his son too stunned to speak.

“Only this—I hear a man seen prowling near the cliffs that night saw a pinnace running for Lundy.”

Will rose without a word and left the room. Within half an hour, he and several armed men were aboard a trawling-skiff, bound for Lundy.

He did not return for three days. When he did, he brought news: an elderly foreigner had been living for months in a section of ruined Moresco Castle, tenanted by John Braund. A younger foreigner had joined him recently, arriving from a ship—likely a Flemish or Easterling vessel. The ship had come and gone more than once, and the young man traveled with it.

Then, a few days ago, a woman and her maid—a stout woman—had come ashore with him. They spoke long and secretly with the elder man, stayed the night, and departed together at dawn.

The fishermen reported hearing the young man call the older man “father.” He was quiet, solemn, much like a mass-priest.

No more was known—or perhaps no one wished to say.

Old Cary and Sir Richard sent Will back to Lundy, this time with the parish constable of Hartland. The island lies ten miles offshore, within the vast parish of Hartland, cursed by its isolation.

They returned with the body of John Braund—farmer, fisherman, smuggler. After endless questioning (during which Braund suffered from deafness and memory loss), he was sent to Exeter gaol on charges of harboring priests, Jesuits, gypsies, and other “suspect and traitorous persons.”

Poor Braund, whose motives were likely not treason but hunger and desperation—seven children, unpaid rent—did not survive the move from Lundy’s fresh air to Exeter’s pestilential prison. Two years later, the infamous “black assizes” would claim dozens, including nobles and judges, felled by sickness or foul air. Braund fell ill within a week, raving in his cell, and died. His secrets perished with him. Only suspicion remained—of Rose Salterne’s fate.

That she had fled with the Spaniard, few doubted. But where? And in what role?

Bideford, for its part, cared little. Many women resented her beauty; country ladies blamed her for drawing undue attention beyond her station. Young girls dreamed of filling the throne she had abandoned. So Bideford moved forward, better or worse, without her. Though she lingered in some hearts as a fair dream, daily life soon swept her memory away. Her place knew her no more.

And Will Cary? He was shattered. He blamed himself—first for bringing Rose into disgrace, then for driving her into the Spaniard’s arms. St. Leger, sensible and kind, tried to reason with him: the attachment must have begun long before the quarrel; it was inevitable; Salterne’s harshness, not Cary’s wrath, had hastened it. And now, he argued, Rose and her fate were beyond their reach—she was gone with a Spaniard, a Papist, and not worth further sorrow.

But Will would not listen. He wrote to Frank at Whitehall, confessing everything, calling himself fool and villain, pleading for forgiveness. Frank replied: Will had no cause to accuse himself. These strange affections were governed by the stars—by fate itself. To resist such bonds was to defy heaven. As a brother of the Rose, Frank declared he must believe, even defend with sword, that the incomparable Rose of Torridge could choose no unworthy man. That man, though Spanish and Catholic, deserved their loyalty, honor, and faith forever.

Honest Will accepted this as gospel, unaware of the agony it caused Francis Leigh.

He showed the letter triumphantly to St. Leger, who said nothing aloud, but inwardly suspected Frank felt differently. Still, he merely nodded and said, “The man is an angel, as his mother is.”

For days, the matter lay quiet—until Jack Brimblecombe stepped forward.

Now curate of Hartland town, and “passing rich” on forty pounds a year, he approached Will.

“I hope no offence, Mr. William—but when are you and the rest going after—after her?”

The name stuck in his throat.

Cary recoiled. “What’s that to thee, Catiline the blood-drinker?” he joked, trying to laugh it off.

“Don’t laugh at me, sir—it’s no laughing matter. I drank red wine that night, nothing worse. But we swore oaths, Mr. Cary—and oaths are oaths, say I.”

“Of course, Jack. But to go seek her—and then cut her lover’s throat? Absurd, even if she were worth finding!”

Jack stared steadily. After a silence: “How far is it to Caracas, then, sir?”

“What’s that to thee?”

“She was made governor there, I hear. That would be where to find her?”

“You don’t mean to go there?”

Shouted Cary, forcing laughter. “That depends on whether I can scrape the money—or get a berth on a ship. God’s will must be done.”

Will stared. Was the man drunk? Mad? But Jack’s eyes were clear, sober, fixed.

Will had no answer. To mock him was easy. To deny his courage, harder. For here was a vulgar, fat curate—out of his mind, perhaps—but determined to do what no gentleman would: to act, to risk all, to fulfill a vow.

“Well,” said Jack, “it’s a bad look-out. But mother’s well-off if father dies. The maidens are stout wenches—they’ll make good servants, please the Lord. You’ll see they come to no harm, Mr. William, if I never come back.”

Cary was silent.

“And, Mr. William—you know me for an honest man. Will you lend me five pounds? Take my books in pawn? Just to help me start?”

“Are you mad? You’ll never find her!”

“That’s no reason not to try. It’s my duty.”

“But even if you reach the Indies, you’ll be clapped into the Inquisition. Burnt alive—just as sure as your name is Jack.”

“I know,” said Jack, voice heavy. “I’ve struggled with it. I’m a coward, Mr. William—a dirty coward. But maybe the Lord will take care of me, as He does little children and drunken men. If not… I’d sooner burn, and have it over, than live like this any longer.”

He burst into sobs.

“What way, my dear old lad?” said Will, softened.

“Not knowing—whether she’s married to him or not. She was an angel to me—pure as daylight. I knew she was too good for a poor pot-head like me. I prayed every night she’d marry a king—if one fit for her existed. And now I don’t know if she’s living in sin or not…”

“It’s more than I can bear.”

“And if she is married, they won’t keep faith with heretics. They can dissolve the marriage—or send her to the Inquisition. Burn her, Mr. Cary—as easily as me. The devils incarnate!”

Cary shuddered. The truth had never struck him so sharply.

“Yes,” Jack went on, “they can force her to deny her God by torture—if she hasn’t already done so for love of him. I know how love makes a man sell his soul. I’ve been in love. Don’t laugh at me, Mr. Will, or I shall go mad!”

“God knows,” said Will, “I’ve never been less inclined to laugh.”

“Is it so? Bless you for that word!” Jack held out his hand.

“But what of my soul, if I don’t seek her? Just to speak to her—warn her, for God’s sake—even if it helps nothing. To tell her the Lord’s curse on idolatry and Antichrist, on those who deny Him for any creature. Though I doubt He’d be hard on her—how could He? But I must speak. The Lord has laid the burden on me. It must be done. God help me!”

“Jack,” said Cary, “if this is your duty, it is others’.”

“No, sir. You’re a layman. I am a deacon, chaplain to you all. Sworn to seek Christ’s sheep scattered across this wicked world—and that innocent lamb first of all.”

“You have sheep at Hartland.”

“There’s plenty better than I to tend them when I’m gone. But none to tend her—because none love her like me. And none will venture. Who will? No shame in that.”

“I wonder what Amyas Leigh would say if he were home.”

“Say? He’d do. He isn’t one for talk. He’d go through fire and water for her. Trust him, Will Cary. Call me an ass if he won’t.”

“Will you wait till he returns?”

“He may not come back for a year or more.”

“Hear reason, Jack. Wait. Be patient. Something may yet be done.”

“You think so?”

“I cannot promise—but—”

“Then promise me one thing. Tell Mr. Frank what I say—or rather, I’ll wager he’s said the same already.”

“You’re wrong, old man. Here’s his own handwriting.”

Jack read the letter and sighed deeply.

“Well, I thought he was a fine gentleman. Still—if my duty isn’t his, it’s mine. I judge no man. But I go, Mr. Cary.”

“You shall not go until Amyas returns. As I live, I’ll tell your father unless you promise. You dare not disobey him.”

“I don’t know,” Jack said, uncertain. “For conscience’ sake.”

“At least stay and dine here. We’ll settle whether you break the fifth commandment over good brewed sack.”

Now, a good dinner was Jack’s delight—and often denied. He agreed, and Cary thought he had calmed him.

At least he went home and was seen no more for a week.

Then he returned, joyful.

“I’ve settled all, Mr. Will. The parson of Welcombe will serve my church for two Sundays. I’m off to London—to speak to Mr. Frank.”

“To London? How?”

“On Shanks his mare,” he pointed to his legs. “But I expect I can get a lift on a coaster to Bristol. The rest doesn’t matter.”

Cary tried to dissuade him, then pressed a small loan upon him. Jack left, and Cary heard nothing for three weeks.

Then one evening, Jack walked into Clovelly Court just before supper, thin and weary. He sat among the serving-men until Will appeared. Will raised him above the salt, treated him kindly—something the honest fellow needed—and after supper, questioned him privately.

“I’ve learnt a lesson, Mr. William. I’ve learnt that there is one on earth who loves her better than I, if she had the wit to take him.”

“But what says he of seeking her?”

“He says what I say—Go! And what you say—Wait.”

“Go? Impossible! How can that agree with his letter?”

“That’s no concern of mine. Being nearer heaven than I, he sees clearer. Oh, Mr. Will, he’s not a man—he’s an angel of God. But he’s dying.”

“Dying?”

“Yes, of love for her. I can see it in his eyes, hear it in his voice. But I’m of tougher hide. I can’t die, even if I tried. But I’ll obey my betters. I’ll wait.”

And so Jack returned to Hartland that very evening, despite all pleas to stay.

But he left behind thoughts in Cary’s mind—thoughts that tormented him day and night—until a chance encounter crystallized them all, like ice forming on frozen water.

One murky afternoon, Cary lounged on Bideford quay when Mr. Salterne approached. Cary had avoided him lately, partly from delicacy, partly from disapproval of his coldness.

But now they met face to face.

“Well, Mr. Salterne, how goes the shipping trade?”

“Well enough, sir—if some of you young gentlemen would follow Mr. Leigh’s example and go forth to find new markets for us stay-at-homes.”

“You want rid of us, eh?”

“I don’t know why I should. We sha’n’t cross paths again, whatever once was. But if I were you, I’d be in the Indies by now—unless fighting the queen’s battles closer to home.”

“In the Indies? I’d make a poor hand of Drake’s trade.”

The conversation ended, but Cary remembered the hint.

“So, lad,” he told Amyas later, “to make an end of a long story—if you’re minded to take the old man’s offer, so am I. Westward-ho with you, come foul, come fair.”

“It’ll be a wild-goose chase, Will.”

“If she’s with him, we’ll find her at La Guayra. If not, and the villain has cast her off, that’ll only make it more reason to make an example of him.”

“And if neither are there, Will, the Plate fleets will be. We’d be shamed to return empty-handed. But will your father let you run such a risk?”

“My father?” laughed Cary. “He’s so hopeful of a long line of little Carys to fill my place, he’ll have no lack of heir, come what may.”

“Little Carys?”

“I tell you truth. He must have drunk from the fountain of perpetual youth—our friend Don Guzman’s grandfather sought in Florida. Twelve months ago, he married a tenant’s buxom daughter. Mistress Abishag Jewell has already given him one fat baby. So I shall go—back to Ireland, or with you. Somewhere. I can’t stand this squalling, nor seeing Mistress Abishag sitting in my poor dear mother’s place, telling me every other day she’s come of an illustrious house because she’s third cousin seven times removed to Bishop Jewell.”

“I had three-parts of a quarrel with the old man the other day. After one of her peacock-bouts, I couldn’t help saying: since the Bishop wrote an Apology for the people of England, my father ought to conjure his ghost to write one for himself—and title it, ‘Why green heads should grow on gray shoulders.’”

“You impudent villain! What did he say?”

“He laughed till he cried. Told me if I didnI like it, I might leave it—which is just what I intend to do. But mind—if we go, we must take Jack Brimblecombe with us. Or he’ll throw himself over Harty Point, and his ghost will haunt us to our dying day.”

“Jack shall go. None deserves it better.”

After that, they discussed practicalities. It was decided Amyas would go to London to consult Frank and his mother before any action. The other brothers of the Rose were scattered—St. Leger had returned to his uncle. It would be unfair to demand their aid, and delay would be costly.

As Amyas wisely said: “Too many cooks spoil the broth. Half-a-dozen gentlemen on one ship are as bad as two kings of Brentford.”

With that, he departed for London the next morning, leaving Yeo with Cary.
