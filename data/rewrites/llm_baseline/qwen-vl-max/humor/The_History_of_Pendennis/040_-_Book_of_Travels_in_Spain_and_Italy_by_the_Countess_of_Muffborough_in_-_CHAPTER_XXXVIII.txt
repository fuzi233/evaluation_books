CHAPTER XXXVIII. In which the Sylph Reappears—And Also a Few Other Things

Now, if you’ve ever wondered how the rich get richer without actually doing anything (except perhaps sitting on their gold and occasionally blinking), then let me tell you about Lady Clavering. Her fortune was so vast that even Morgan, her valet—a man who’d once been paid to polish a silver candlestick with his teeth—knew more about it than any of the other footmen in London. When she arrived in town, the gossip mill went into overdrive: indigo factories? Check. Opium clippers? Naturally. Banks full of rupees like they were going out of fashion? Oh yes. And don’t forget the diamonds—jewels from princes who’d sold their souls for a loan from Lady Clavering’s father, Snell, back when he was still just a gentleman with a lot of money and a very good accountant.

Her bank balance alone had enough zeroes to make a mathematician faint. It was whispered that an envoy from the Nawaub of Lucknow—Colonel Altamont, a man who’d supposedly converted to Islam, survived a dozen near-death experiences, and once wrestled a tiger during a thunderstorm—was currently negotiating with the Begum Clavering (as she was now being called) over the sale of the “Light of the Dewan,” a diamond so large it could double as a dinner plate for a particularly hungry camel.

And so, before Lady Clavering had even stepped off the carriage, her fame had already reached the upper echelons of society. Which brings us to one of England’s greatest virtues: we don’t care *how* you got your money, only that you *have* it. If you’re rich enough, we’ll invite you to our balls, marry your daughters, dance with your dogs, and pretend you’ve always belonged. Our aristocracy may be haughty, but it’s also practical. Money is the ultimate equalizer—even if it does come with a few side effects, like blackmail, gambling debts, and a certain Colonel Altamont who likes to threaten people with newspaper exposés.

Meanwhile, our dear Chevalier Strong—gentleman, soldier, and general all-around fixer—had been busy making sure the Claverings’ new London house was ready for its grand entrance. He supervised the decorators, rearranged the sofas like a general preparing for battle, and even gave advice on wine selection—though he did sneakily stock his own cellar while he was at it. His rooms in Shepherd’s Inn were now the envy of every man who’d ever eaten a cutlet and wished it came with a bottle of claret.

And who should be sharing those rooms with him? None other than Colonel Altamont himself—the same man who’d threatened to blow up the entire Clavering family if they didn’t pay him a thousand pounds by Friday at noon. Yes, the very same man whom Sir Francis Clavering had been forced to see again after a run of bad luck at Baden-Baden, where the Colonel lost everything playing red—twelve times in a row. “No gentleman can stand against a colour coming up fourteen times,” he lamented, though no one believed him. The truth was, he’d drawn on Sir Francis’s credit, and poor Clavering—already stretched thin by election expenses and furniture bills—had no choice but to accept the bill, muttering curses under his breath and wishing the Colonel had stayed locked in a German debtor’s prison for life.

But here’s the kicker: Lady Clavering knew nothing about any of this. She was generous, yes—she’d give her husband a handsome allowance—but she kept strict accounts. She wasn’t the kind of woman to hand over blank cheques or let her husband sign bills without knowing what they were for. So when Clavering needed cash for the Colonel, he couldn’t speak to her. That’s where Strong came in—his personal banker, financial advisor, and occasional moral compass (though not always in that order).

And so, in the cozy, slightly shady world of Shepherd’s Inn, business was conducted in whispers and signed notes. Strong, despite having no money of his own, became the drawer of nearly every bill Sir Francis accepted. He’d discount them in the City, negotiate payment extensions, and keep the whole thing ticking along like a well-oiled machine—or at least a machine that occasionally sputtered and threatened to explode.

It’s a curious thing, isn’t it? A man who’s been in debt since he was seventeen, who trades promises for cash, never seems to truly benefit from prosperity. Just when things look up, the moneylender knocks again. But Ned Strong, bless him, bore it all with the calmness of a man who’d seen worse—like being shot at in Spain, or having his trousers stolen by a goat.

But enough about the Colonel, the bills, and the existential dread of debt. Let’s return to Lady Clavering and her magnificent new home in Grosvenor Place.

First stop: the dining room. Now, this room was decorated in what Lady Clavering referred to as the “middle-aged style”—which, she giggled, must be because she and Clavering were middle-aged. There were Dutch chairs from the 17th century, a carved buffet from the 16th, a sideboard made from church wood, and a brass cathedral lamp that looked like it had been rescued from a funeral procession. There were portraits, tapestries, armor made of cardboard, and a collection of saints that seemed to be judging everyone who entered. It was, in short, the most chaste room in London—so chaste, in fact, that it made you feel guilty for breathing.

Behind it lay the library—busts, books, easy chairs, and bronzes so severe they looked like they’d been sculpted by someone who’d never smiled. Here, Sir Francis smoked cigars, read *Bell’s Life in London*, and nodded off after dinner—unless he was at a club, punting, or losing money at a gambling house. It was the perfect place for a man who wanted to appear cultured but actually just wanted to sleep.

But the real showstopper was the drawing-room. The carpets were so fluffy that walking on them felt like stepping through clouds. Roses and tulips bloomed on the floor, bigger than warming pans. Chairs came in every shape imaginable—some so thin you feared they’d collapse under a feather. Marquetry tables overflowed with gimcracks, china ornaments from every corner of the empire, gilt daggers, and Turkish papooshes. There were nymphs by Boucher, shepherdesses by Greuze, porcelain poodles and ducks, and two cockatoos that squealed louder than a couple of angry nuns. A clock sang tunes, another boomed like Big Ben, and everywhere you looked, there were Dresden shepherdesses ready to assist with tea.

It was, quite simply, the most elegant room in London. The Romans of the Lower Empire would have wept. Louis XV’s marchionesses would have fainted. Even Mrs. Hodge-Podgson, wife of the great Railroad Croesus, would have said, “Well, I never!”

But Lady Clavering? She barely noticed. “I only know it cost a fortune,” she said to Major Pendennis. “And I wouldn’t advise you to sit on those gossamer chairs—I broke one the night of our second dinner party.”

“Ah,” said Blanche, the Sylphide, with a sneer. “You would have liked to see Mamma break a chair, wouldn’t you, Mr. Pendennis?”

Pen blushed. “I should have been glad to offer my arm if she needed it,” he said, bowing.

“Quel preux Chevalier!” cried Blanche, tossing her head. “I have a fellow-feeling with those who fall,” Pen replied. “I fell once myself.”

“And you went home to Laura to console you,” said Miss Amory.

Pen winced. He didn’t like talking about Laura. Not anymore. Especially not when the whole world seemed to know about it. So he changed the subject—praising the furniture with such enthusiasm that even Lady Clavering was moved to say, “No, don’t praise me—it’s all Captain Strong’s doing. And Lady Rockminster says the salongs are very well.”

“Lady Rockminster?” gasped Major Pendennis. “If she approves, you cannot be far wrong. She is the very centre of fashion and taste.”

“Yes,” said Lady Clavering, “she’s taken us up.”

“Taken us up?” Blanche shrieked. “Mamma, you mean *we’ve been taken up*.”

“Well, taken up, then,” said her mother. “It’s very kind of her. We’ll like it when we get used to it. Only at first, one doesn’t fancy being… well, taken up.”

She continued: “She wants to give our balls and send out our invitations. But I won’t stand for that. I’ll have my old friends. I won’t let her sit at the head of my own table.”

“Oh, mamma, vous etes incorrigible,” moaned Blanche.

The Major, however, declared he preferred quiet dinners. “A dinner’s best the second day,” said Lady Clavering, trying to recover.

“On the 14th,” she added, “we’ll be a snug little party.”

At which point Blanche clasped her hands and sighed, “Oh, mamma, you’re hopeless.”

But the Major, ever diplomatic, accepted with a smile. He liked the idea of a simple meal. And besides, he could always bail if something better came along.

As for Pen, he was still young enough to find a grand dinner in a grand house thrilling. He hadn’t yet learned the art of pretending he didn’t care.

Later, walking away, the Major asked, “What was that little quarrel between you and Miss Amory?”

“Used to be,” Pen replied with a dandified air, “is a vague phrase when applied to women. Was and is are two very different things.”

“Egad, they change as fast as we do,” said the Major. “When we took the Cape of Good Hope, there was a lady who threatened to poison herself for you. Three months later, she ran off with someone else.”

“Don’t get involved with Miss Amory,” he advised. “She’s forward, affected, and under-bred. And her character—well, let’s just say ten thousand pounds won’t buy her.”

“Ten thousand pounds?” Pen laughed. “That’s not much.”

“Not much?” the Major said, chuckling. “I wouldn’t pay her milliner’s bill with the interest.”

“You seem to know millinery well,” Pen observed.

“I did,” said the Major. “And the old war-horse, you know, never hears a trumpet without he, he!”—and he gave a rather superannuated leer at a passing carriage.

“Lady Catherine Martingale’s carriage,” he announced. “Fine girls, though—her mother was a thousand times handsomer.”

Then, turning to Pen: “With your looks and prospects, you ought to make a good marriage someday. And remember—reputation for a little wickedness? That helps. Women like a bit of danger. But marriage? Marry a woman with money. It’s easier to get a rich wife than a poor one. And it’s far more pleasant to eat a well-cooked dinner than a cold leg of mutton.”

“So you suggest I stick to the 14th dinner?” Pen asked.

“Stick to it,” said the Major. “Cultivate them, but keep them for dining. No more love-in-a-cottage nonsense.”

“It must be a cottage with a double coach-house,” Pen quipped.

His uncle didn’t recognize the line, but he liked the sound of it. Arthur Pendennis, it seemed, was becoming a very clever fellow—especially at adapting to whatever mood the moment demanded.

Warrington grumbled that Pen was becoming unbearable. But really, the young man was just thriving. His success, his charm, his newfound confidence—they all pleased his elder companion. Like a man watching a child at a pantomime, he found joy in Pen’s exuberance.

And so, as the sun rose higher and the world grew brighter, Arthur Pendennis bloomed—not unlike a flower in springtime—with all the hope, humor, and hubris of youth.
