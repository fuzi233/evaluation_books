Indeed, the hermit of Gouda was the vicar of Gouda, yet he remained oblivious to this duality, bound by the absolute walls of his self-imposed exile. My readers know that when the fever of his passion finally broke, it was replaced immediately by a crushing remorse. Yet, few who have not climbed the highest peaks of spiritual aspiration and tumbled down its steep slopes can truly comprehend the terror that seized him. He felt as though a treacherous guide had led him right to the brink of a cliff, where the ground vanished beneath his feet.

"Ah, good Jerome!" he cried, tears streaming down his face. "How much better you knew me than I knew myself! How bitter, yet how necessary, was your warning!"

Accustomed to the rigorous audit of his own soul, Clement instantly identified the root of his fury: it was Margaret. "I love her," he whispered, the confession tearing from his lips in despair, "more than God; more than the Church. From a love like this, what good can spring for me, or for her?" He shuddered violently at the thought. "Let the strong man engage temptation in combat; it is for the weak to flee. And who, I ask you, is weaker than I have proven myself to be? What remains of my penance, my religion? It is a house of cards, carefully built degree by degree into a facade of piety, and behold—here comes a breath of earthly love, and the whole structure crumbles into dust."

He resolved to abandon Holland immediately, to spend years in some distant convent before ever returning. By that time, the allure of worldly passion would be doubly baffled; grown older, a wiser monk, he would master his affections. And Margaret, seeing him gone, would surely marry another. The sheer agony this final thought inflicted upon him proved, paradoxically, that he was indeed walking the narrow path of religious duty.

Yet, in forsaking her for his eternal salvation—and ostensibly for hers—he could not neglect her temporal welfare. The comforting thought that he could secure her comfort for life, enriching her with worldly goods which she was not bound to despise, sustained him through the bitter struggle of turning his back on her without a single kind word or look. "Oh, what will she think of me?" he groaned, pacing his cell. "Shall I not appear to her, of all creatures, the most heartless, inhuman? But so it must be; yes, better she should hate me, miserable wretch that I am. Heaven is merciful, giving my broken heart this comfort: I can make that villain restore her dowry, and she shall never lose another true lover to poverty. Another? Ah me! Ah me! God and the Saints, lend me your aid!"

How he fared on this errand has been chronicled elsewhere. First, however, as you may recall, he went at night to confess the dying hermit of Gouda. He found him on the verge of death and stayed until he had closed his eyes and interred him beneath the floor of the tiny oratory attached to his cell. It was the peaceful conclusion of a stormy life. The hermit had been a soldier, carrying a steel corselet next to his skin even in death, declaring himself now Christ's soldier, as he once had been Satan's. When Clement had heard his confession and prayed with him, the dying man, seeking counsel from one nearing the end in such a pious frame, advised Clement to become his successor in this peaceful retreat. "His fight against the world, the flesh, and the devil was hard," the hermit murmured, "and he never thoroughly mastered them until he retired into the citadel of Solitude."

These words, coupled with the hermit's serene passing, which seemed to seal the truth of his life's work, made a profound impression on Clement. In Clement's case, there was no prejudice to overcome; the solitary recluse was still profoundly revered in the Church, whether immured as an anchorite in a cave belonging to a monastery or hiding in the wilder, more lax seclusion of an independent hermitage. Clement knew more about hermits than most theologians of his time. Having studied extensively at the monastery near Tergou, devouring their lives with wonder in the Vatican manuscripts, and engaging earnestly with mendicant friars of various nations, he possessed a deep reservoir of knowledge. Before the printing press, these friars were the great circulators of local annals and biographies accumulated in convents across lands.

Moreover, his mentor Jerome had spent three years as an anchorite on the heights of Camaldoli, where the tradition of the Thebaid had been revived for over four centuries. Jerome, usually cold and curt on most religious topics, burned with enthusiasm regarding hermits. He had pored over the annals of St. John Baptist's Abbey, recounting the names of nobles and warriors who ended their days there, bishops and archbishops who moved from the see to the hermitage and back again. He spoke with grim delight of their multifarious austerities, describing how each hermit attacked his own weaknesses without mercy until he became strongest even there. He detailed how seven times daily, regardless of thunder, rain, or snow, solitaries flocked from distant points over rugged paths to worship in the convent church, attending matins, prime, tierce, sext, nones, vespers, and compline. Under eager questioning, Clement learned of the famous anchorites he had sung the Psalter with: Moncada, Duke of Moncada and Cardova; Hidalgo of Spain; Father John Baptist of Novara, who had led armies but was now a private soldier of Christ; Cornelius, Samuel, and Sylvanus. Of Sylvanus, Clement recalled how the Great Duchess de' Medici, despite obtaining the Pope's reluctant leave to visit, was warned by the hermit at the first wooden cross. Surrounded by courtiers and flatterers, she retreated with all her retinue, awed by the holiness of the man and the mountain.

At Basel, Clement found fresh materials, particularly concerning German and English anchorites, eventually preparing a "Catena Eremitarum" spanning from the year 250, when Paul of Thebes began his ninety years of solitude, down to 1470. He called them *Angelorum amici et animalium*—"Friends of Angels and Animals."

Though Clement never intended to become a recluse, the path was paved. When the dying hermit of Gouda blessed the citadel of Solitude, where he had fought the good fight, and invited Clement to take up the breastplate of faith now falling from his own shrunken body, Clement thought within himself: "Heaven itself led my foot hither to this end." It struck him as no small coincidence that his patron, St. Bavon, was also a hermit, an austere one, a cuirassier of the solitary cell.

Eager to reconcile with Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, Clement hastened to his abode, praying that Heaven would not have already occupied the famous den. His fears were not unfounded; these sacred spaces rarely waited long for a human tenant. He found the rude stone door ajar, entered softly, and confirmed that he was not too late. The cell was vacant, holding the hermit's great ivory crucifix, pens, ink, seeds, and a *memento mori* skull. There lay his cilices of hair and bristles, his well-worn sheepskin pelisse and hood, and his tools: hammer, chisel, and psaltery. Men and women had passed that way, yet none had dared intrude, far less steal. Faith and simplicity had guarded this keyless door more securely than the houses of the laity, defended by gates like modern jails, iron bars, moats, bastions, and portcullises.

As soon as Clement stepped inside, a loud flap and flutter echoed, and down came a great brown owl from a corner, wheeling out the window and driving the cold air onto Clement's face. He started and shuddered. Was this seemingly ordinary owl something diabolical, attempting to deter him from his soul's good? On second thought, might it not be some good spirit the hermit had employed to keep the cell for him, perhaps the hermit himself? Finally, he concluded it was merely an owl, deciding to try and make friends with it.

Kneeling down, Clement inaugurated his new life with prayer.

Beyond the earthly passion he needed to quell—a power that made him tremble for his eternal welfare—Clement bore a penance for having yielded to ire, his besetting sin, and for cursing his own brothers. He looked around the roomy cell, furnished with such comforts, comparing it to the hideous places in his mind's eye: the *eremus in eremo*, a desert in a desert, where St. Jerome had wrestled with sickness, temptation, and despair for four mortal years; the inaccessible, thorny niche where the boy hermit Benedict hid away, surviving three years on a pittance shared with a raven; the hollow tree of his patron St. Bavon; the earthly purgatory at Fribourg, where a nameless saint endured perpetual gloom and the sound of an eternal waterfall; the pillar of St. Simeon Stylites, existing for forty-five years; the stone box (*destina*) of St. Dunstan, where he could scarce sit, stand, or lie; and the living tombs sealed with lead of Thais, Christina, and others.
