“The steps of the bearers, heavy and slow,
The sobs of the mourners, deep and low.”

At the hour they had agreed upon the day before, Margaret and her father set out to visit Nicholas Higgins and his daughter. Both were struck by a quiet unease—their new clothes felt strange, as though they belonged to another life; it was the first time in weeks they had gone anywhere together with purpose, and the weight of their shared grief hung between them like an unspoken word. They walked close, drawn into each other’s presence not by words, but by the silent understanding that only sorrow can forge.

Nicholas sat in his usual corner by the fire, but without his pipe. His head rested on his hand, elbow propped on his knee. He did not rise when they entered, though Margaret saw the warmth in his eyes. “Sit ye down, sit ye down,” he said, giving the hearth a sharp shove as if to distract from himself. “Fire’s welly out.”

He looked worn—his beard unshaven for days, dark against his pale face, his jacket patched and threadbare. “We thought we’d find you here, just after dinner,” Margaret said gently.

“We’ve had our sorrows too since last we met,” Mr. Hale added.

“Ay, ay,” Nicholas replied, voice low. “Sorrows are more plentiful than dinners these days. I reckon my dinner hour stretches all over the day—you’re likely to find me any time.”

“Are you out of work?” Margaret asked.

“Ay,” he answered shortly. Then, after a pause, he raised his eyes for the first time: “I’m not wanting brass. Don’t think I am. Bess, poor lass, left a little savings under her pillow—ready to slip into my hand at the last moment—and Mary’s fustian-cutting. But I’m out of work just the same.”

“We owe Mary some money,” Mr. Hale said, before Margaret could stop him with a swift pressure on his arm.

“If she takes it,” Nicholas snapped, “I’ll turn her out o’ doors. I’ll stay inside these four walls, and she’ll stay out. That’s all.”

“But we owe her thanks,” Mr. Hale began again, “for her kindness.”

“I ne’er thanked your daughter for what she did for my poor wench,” Nicholas said. “I couldn’t find the words. Now I must begin to try—if you start making a fuss about what little Mary could do for you.”

“Is it because of the strike?” Margaret asked softly.

“The strike’s over,” he said. “It’s done for this time. I’m out of work because I didn’t ask for it. And I didn’t ask, because good words are scarce, and bad ones are plenty.”

He spoke in riddles, but Margaret sensed he wanted to be questioned further.

“And good words are—?” she prompted.

“Askin’ for work,” he said. “I reckon them’s almost the best words a man can say. ‘Gi’ me work’ means ‘and I’ll do it like a man.’ Them’s good words.”

“And bad words are refusing you when you ask?”

“Ay. Bad words is saying, ‘Aha, my fine chap! You’ve been true to your order, and I’ll be true to mine. You did the best you could for those who needed help—that’s your way of being faithful. And I’ll be true to mine. You’ve been a poor fool, one who didn’t know better than to be a loyal fool. So go and be d—d. There’s no work for you here.’ Them’s bad words. I’m not a fool. And if I were, folks ought to have taught me how to be wise after their fashion. I might have learned—if anyone had tried.”

“Would it not be worth trying,” Mr. Hale said, “to speak to your old master? Ask him to take you back? It might be a poor chance, but it’s a chance.”

Nicholas looked up sharply, then let out a dry, bitter laugh. “Measter? If it’s no offence, I’ll ask you a question or two in return.”

“You’re welcome,” Mr. Hale replied.

“I reckon you’ve some way of earning your bread. Folk don’t live in Milton just for pleasure, if they can live elsewhere.”

“You’re right,” Mr. Hale said. “I have some property, but I came here to become a private tutor.”

“To teach people,” Nicholas said. “Well, I reckon they pay you for teaching, don’t they?”

“Yes,” Mr. Hale smiled. “I teach to earn my living.”

“And those who pay you—do they tell you what to do with the money they give you? What not to do with it?”

“No,” Mr. Hale said. “Of course not.”

“They don’t say, ‘You may have a brother or a friend dear as a brother who needs this money for something both you and he believe is right—but you must promise not to give it to him. You may see a good use for your money—but we don’t think it’s good, so if you spend it that way, we’ll just stop dealing with you.’ They don’t say that, do they?”

“No,” Mr. Hale said firmly. “They wouldn’t dare.”

“Would you stand for it if they did?”

“It would be a very hard pressure indeed,” Mr. Hale admitted, “that would make me even consider submitting to such control.”

“There’s not the pressure on all the broad earth that would make me,” Nicholas said. “Now you’ve got it. You’ve hit the bull’s eye. Hampers—that’s where I worked—makes their men pledge themselves not to give a penny to help the Union or keep turn-outs from getting aid. They may make pledges,” he sneered, “but they only make liars and hypocrites. And that’s worse than making men’s hearts so hard they won’t do a kindness to those in need, or support the just cause—even if it goes against the strong hand. But I’ll never swear away my soul for all the work the king could give me. I’m a member of the Union. I believe it’s the only thing that can help working men. I’ve been on strike, I’ve known what it’s like to starve. So if I get a shilling, sixpence shall go to those who ask for it. The consequence? I don’t see where I’ll get a shilling.”

“Is that rule enforced in all the mills?” Margaret asked.

“I can’t say. It’s new at ours. I reckon they’ll find they can’t hold to it. But it’s in force now. By-and-by they’ll learn—tyrants make liars.”

There was silence. Margaret hesitated, reluctant to upset a man already so burdened. Yet the truth pressed upon her. At last, in soft tones, she spoke.

“Do you remember poor Boucher saying the Union was a tyrant? I think he called it the worst tyrant of all. And I remember—I agreed with him then.”

Nicholas remained still, head in hands, staring into the fire. She could not read his face.

“I’ll not deny,” he said slowly, “that the Union sometimes forces a man into his own good. A man leads a hard life outside the Union. But once inside, his interests are cared for better than he could manage himself. It’s the only way working men can win justice—by standing together. More members, more strength for each man. Government looks after fools and madmen. If a man threatens harm to himself or others, it puts a check on him whether he likes it or not. That’s all we do in the Union. We can’t lock folk in prison—but we can make life so heavy that a man has no choice but to come in, and be wise and helpful, despite himself. Boucher was a fool all along—and never a greater fool than at the end.”

“He hurt you?” Margaret asked.

“Aye, that he did. We had public opinion on our side—until he and his kind started rioting, breaking laws. Then it was all over with the strike.”

“So would it not have been better to leave him alone? To let him stay free, rather than force him into the Union? He did you no good. You drove him mad.”

“Margaret,” her father murmured, seeing the storm gather on Higgins’s face.

“I like her,” Nicholas said suddenly. “She speaks plainly what’s in her mind. She doesn’t understand the Union—yet. But it’s a great power. Our only power. I’ve read poetry about a plough going over a daisy—it made tears come to my eyes before I had cause to cry. But the farmer never stopped ploughing, I warrant, for pitying the flower. He had too much common sense. The Union is the plough, preparing the land for harvest. Boucher—well, he’s no daisy. He’s more like a weed spreading across the field. He’s meant to be cleared away. I’m sore vexed with him now. So maybe I don’t speak fair. I could plough over him myself—with all the pleasure in life.”

“What? What has he done? Anything new?”

“Ay, to be sure. He’s never out of mischief. First, he went raging like a madman, kicked up that riot. Then he hid—would have stayed hidden, if Thornton had pursued him as I hoped. But Thornton, having gotten his own way, didn’t care to continue the prosecution. So Boucher crept back home. Didn’t show himself for a few days. Had that grace. Then—where do you think he went? Why, to Hampers’. Damn him! He went with that weak, pleading face—turns me sick to look at it—asking for work, knowing full well the new rule: pledging themselves to give nothing to the Union, nothing to help starving turn-outs! Why, he’d have starved to death if the Union hadn’t helped him in his time of need. There he went, offering to promise anything, to betray everything—to tell all he knew about our plans. A Judas, the good-for-nothing! But I’ll say this for Hamper—thank him at my dying day—he turned Boucher away. Wouldn’t listen to a word. Though others said the traitor cried like a babe!”

“Oh!” Margaret gasped. “How shocking! How pitiful!”

“Higgins,” she said, “don’t you see—you made Boucher what he is? By forcing him into the Union against his will—without his heart in it. You made him what he is!”

Made him what he is? What was he?

From the narrow street came a hollow, measured sound—slow, deliberate, growing louder. Voices hushed. Feet moved—not forward, but circling, as if around a fixed point. Then, clear above the rest, a steady tramp of feet—men carrying a heavy load.

All were drawn toward the door, not by curiosity, but by a solemn compulsion. Six men walked down the road, three of them policemen. On their shoulders they bore a door, removed from its hinges. Upon it lay a dead body. From either side, water dripped steadily onto the stones.

The entire street poured out to witness. Some questioned the bearers, who answered reluctantly—having told the story so many times.

“We found him in the brook beyond there.”

“The brook? There’s not enough water to drown a man!”

“He was determined. Lying face down. Sick of living—whatever his reason.”

Nicholas crept to Margaret’s side, whispering in a broken voice: “It’s not John Boucher? He had no guts for that. Sure! It’s not John Boucher! Look—they’re all looking this way! Listen! I’ve a singing in my head. I can’t hear.”

They laid the door carefully on the cobbles. All could see the drowned man—eyes glassy, one half-open, staring upward. His face was swollen, discoloured, stained by the dye-water of the stream. The front of his head was bald, hair thin and long behind, each strand a channel for the water. Through all this, Margaret recognized John Boucher.

It seemed sacrilegious to gaze upon that twisted, agonized face. In a flash of instinct, she stepped forward and covered the dead man’s face with her handkerchief.

The eyes that watched her followed her as she turned away—and landed upon Nicholas Higgins, rooted to the spot.

The men conferred, then one approached Higgins, who tried to retreat into his house.

“Higgins,” the man said. “You knew him. You must go tell the wife. Do it gently, man—but quickly. We can’t leave him here long.”

“I can’t,” Higgins whispered. “Don’t ask me. I can’t face her.”

“You know her best,” the man insisted. “We’ve done our part bringing him here. You take your share.”

“I can’t,” Higgins repeated. “I’m shattered by seeing him. We weren’t friends. And now he’s dead.”

“Well, if you won’t, you won’t. Someone must. It’s a hard task—but every minute counts. If she hears it in a rougher way, it’ll break her.”

“Papa,” Margaret said softly, “you should go.”

“If I could—if I had time to think—” he stammered.

Margaret saw he was trembling. “I will go.”

“Bless you, miss,” the man said. “It’ll be a kind act. She’s been poorly, I hear. Few here know much of her.”

Margaret knocked. But inside, the noise of children—many, restless, ill-behaved—made no reply. She doubted she was heard. Every second stretched her resolve thinner. She opened the door, stepped in, closed it behind her, and, unseen, bolted it.

Mrs. Boucher sat in a rocking chair across the untidy fireplace. The house seemed untouched—no sign of cleaning for days.

Margaret spoke, barely knowing the words, her throat dry, the children’s cries drowning her voice. She tried again.

“How are you, Mrs. Boucher? I fear you’re very poorly.”

“I’ve chance of being well,” she snapped. “Left alone with these childer, and nothing to quiet them. John shouldn’t have left me. Not when I’m so weak.”

“How long since he left?”

“Four days. No one would give him work. He had to go tramping toward Greenfield. But he might have returned by now—or sent word. He might——”

“Oh, don’t blame him,” Margaret said. “He felt it deeply, I’m sure——”

“Will you hold thy din and let me hear the lady speak?” the woman shouted at a toddler, about a year old, clinging to her dress. “He’s always asking for ‘daddy’ and ‘butty.’ I’ve no butties to give him. Daddy’s gone, forgotten us all, I think. He’s his father’s darling,” she added, suddenly tender, lifting the child to her lap and kissing him fiercely.

Margaret placed a hand on her arm. Their eyes met.

“Poor little fellow,” she said slowly, “he _was_ his father’s darling.”

“He _is_ his father’s darling,” the woman corrected, rising abruptly, facing Margaret.

Silence. Then she began, voice low, growing wilder:

“He _is_ his father’s darling. Poor folk can love their children as well as rich. Why don’t you speak? Why do you stare at me with those big, pitiful eyes? Where’s John?”

Weak as she was, she shook Margaret to force an answer.

“Oh, my God!” she cried, reading the tears. She collapsed back into the chair.

Margaret took the child and placed him in her arms.

“He loved him,” she whispered.

“Ay,” the woman said, shaking her head. “He loved us all. We had someone to love once. Long ago. But while he lived, he did love us. He loved this babby best, perhaps. But he loved me—and I loved him, even though I called him five minutes ago. Are you sure he’s dead?”

She tried to rise.

“If it’s only that he’s ill and near death, they may bring him round. I’m weak myself—I’ve been ill for months.”

“But he is dead—he is drowned!”

“People are brought round after drowning. What was I thinking of, sitting still when I should be moving? Here, whisth thee, child—whisth thee! Take this, take anything to play with, but don’t cry while my heart’s breaking! Oh, where has my strength gone? Oh, John—husband!”

Margaret caught her before she fell. She sat in the rocking chair, cradling the woman in her arms, her head resting on Margaret’s shoulder.

The other children, huddled together in terror, slowly grasped the truth. When they did, they erupted into screams of despair. Johnny’s cry was loudest—though he did not know why.

The mother trembled in Margaret’s embrace.

Then—noise at the door.

“Open it. Open it quick,” Margaret said to the eldest child.

“It’s bolted. Be still. Oh, papa, send the men upstairs quietly. Perhaps she won’t hear them. She’s fainted—that’s all.”

“It’s for the best,” said a woman following the bearers. “But you’re not fit to hold her. Let me fetch a pillow. We’ll lay her down gently.”

This neighbour—new to the district—brought relief. Her kindness made Margaret feel unnecessary. It was better, perhaps, to leave.

She looked for Nicholas Higgins. He was gone.

She turned to the woman who had helped place Mrs. Boucher on the floor.

“Can you gently tell these people to leave quietly? So when she wakes, she finds only those she knows. Papa, speak to the men—get them to go. She can’t breathe with this crowd.”

Margaret knelt beside the widow, bathing her face with vinegar. Moments later, she noticed a sudden rush of air.

She looked up. Her father and the woman exchanged a smile.

“What is it?” she asked.

“Our good friend here,” Mr. Hale said, “thought of a clever way to clear the room.”

“I told them to begone,” the woman said, “each to take a child with them. And to remember—they’re orphans now, and their mother a widow. Who can do most? The children are sure of food today—and kindness.”

“Does she know how he died?”

“No,” Margaret said. “I couldn’t tell her all at once.”

“She must be told—for the inquest. See? She’s coming round. Shall I tell her? Or you? Or perhaps your father?”

“No,” Margaret said. “You. You.”

They waited in silence until she fully regained consciousness.

Then the neighbour sat on the floor, cradling Mrs. Boucher’s head.

“Neighbour,” she said, “your man is dead. Guess how he died?”

“He was drowned,” Mrs. Boucher whispered, beginning to cry.

“He was found drowned. Coming home, hopeless. He thought God could not be harder than men—perhaps not so hard. Perhaps as tender as a mother. Perhaps even tenderer. I’m not saying he did right. Nor wrong. All I say is—may neither me nor mine ever have his heart. Or we might do the same.”

“He has left me alone with all these children!” the widow moaned, less distressed by the manner of death than Margaret expected. It was consistent with her helpless nature—she felt his loss primarily as a blow to herself and her children.

“Not alone,” Mr. Hale said solemnly. “Who is with you? Who will take up your cause?”

The widow stared, startled, realizing he was there.

“Who has promised to be a father to the fatherless?” he continued.

“But I’ve six children, sir,” she said, “the eldest not eight. I don’t doubt His power—only it takes great trust.”

And she wept again.

“She’ll be better tomorrow,” the neighbour said. “Best comfort now is the feel of a child at her heart. I’m sorry they took the baby.”

“I’ll go for him,” Margaret said.

In minutes, she returned, holding Johnnie—face smeared with food, hands full of shells, crystal fragments, and the head of a plaster figure.

She placed him in his mother’s arms.

“There,” the woman said. “Now you go. They’ll cry together, comfort together—better than any adult could do. I’ll stay with her as long as needed. Come tomorrow—you can talk wisely with her. She’s not up to it today.”

As they walked slowly up the street, Margaret paused at Higgins’s door.

“Shall we go in?” her father asked.

“I was thinking of him too.”

They knocked. No answer. They tried the door—it was bolted. But they thought they heard movement within.

“Nicholas,” Margaret called.

No reply.

Then—a book dropped.

“Nicholas,” she said again. “It’s only us. Won’t you let us in?”

“No,” he said. “I spoke plainly about words when I bolted the door. Let me be today.”

Mr. Hale would have pressed, but Margaret silenced him with a finger to his lips.

“I don’t wonder,” she said. “I myself long to be alone. After a day like this, it seems the only thing that can do one good.”
