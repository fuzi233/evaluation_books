The rejection of his peace offerings ignited a fury in the young American chief that burned hotter than any battlefield rage. His wrath was not merely directed at Colonel Lambert, but consumed the entire lineage of that gentleman's family. In his mind, the humiliation was total: he had been displayed before the girls as a spectacle of failure. Mr. Wolfe and the Colonel, those perpetual preachers of morality, had treated him with the condescension of handlers managing a stray dog, offering protection that felt more like a cage. They held him up to the family as a scapegrace, a prodigal son whose very existence seemed to mock their virtue. Their refusal to shake his hand was a final severance; when he attempted to offer even a scrap of common gratitude, they threw his gifts back in his face, treating his generosity as an insult.

"Sir," Parson Sampson observed, his voice rising with a mixture of piety and greed, his eyes fixed on the two morocco boxes resting on white satin cushions. "These things must be worth a small fortune."

"They cost some money, Sampson," the young man replied, his tone weary. "Not that I would grudge ten times the amount to people who have shown kindness."

"No, faith, sir, not if I know your honour!" Sampson interjected, seizing the opportunity to praise his patron, his face a mask of servile adoration.

"The repeater, they told me, was a bargain in Paris, worth a hundred pounds. Little Miss Hetty mentioned she longed for a repeating watch."

"Oh, what a lovely sentiment!" cried the chaplain, his imagination running wild. "With a circle of pearls on the back and a diamond knob for the handle! It would win any woman's heart, Sir!"

"There passes an apple-woman with a basket," Mr. Warrington shouted suddenly, his temper flaring. "I have a mind to fling the thing out to her!"

When Harry stepped out onto business, heading toward the City and the Temple, his parasite did not follow him far into the Strand. Fear of Chancery Lane, its labyrinthine inhabitants, and its oppressive precincts held him back. Instead, Mr. Warrington proceeded to his broker, and they walked to the Bank. There, amidst the clatter of coins and the murmur of commerce, they conducted some trivial business. After signing a few insignificant documents, Harry departed with a handful of crisp banknotes tucked safely in his pocket. The broker then escorted Mr. Warrington to one of the great dining houses, famous in the City then as now. Afterwards, he guided him along the Virginian walk upon 'Change. Harry passed through it with a distinct sense of shame. He wondered what a certain lady in Virginia would think if she knew he was carrying off, in that bottomless gambler's pocket, a significant portion of his father's patrimony. He imagined the whispers among the Virginia merchants: *"That is young Esmond, of Castlewood, on the Potomac, Madam Esmond's son; and he has been losing his money at play, selling out so much, and so much, and so much."*

His spirits did not lift until he had passed beneath the traitors' heads of Temple Bar and emerged fully from the City. From the Strand, Mr. Harry walked home, glancing into St. James's Street, but the streets were empty; the company did not gather at the Chocolate-House until a later hour.

Upon arriving home, Mr. Harry produced his bundle of banknotes. He selected three, placed them inside a sheet of paper, and sealed it carefully. Within the sheet, he had previously written: *"Much good may they do you. H. E. W."* He directed this packet to the Reverend Mr. Sampson, leaving it on the chimney-glass with instructions for his servants to deliver it to the divine upon his arrival.

Now, his honour's phaeton was brought to the door. He stepped in, intending to drive around the park, but the rain began to fall, or perhaps the east wind blew, or some other reason arose. His honour turned his horses' heads down St. James's Street and returned to White's at about three o'clock. Scarcely anyone had arrived yet; it was the hour when folks were at dinner. However, there was his cousin Castlewood, lounging over the *Public Advertiser*, having just returned from his duties at Court nearby.

Lord Castlewood yawned over the newspaper. "What shall they do?" he asked. "Shall we have a little piquet?" Harry had no objection to a little piquet. "Just for an hour," said Lord Castlewood. "I dine at Arlington Street at four." "Just for an hour," agreed Mr. Warrington. They called for cards.

"Or shall we have them upstairs?" suggested my lord. "Out of the noise?"

"Certainly, out of the noise," said Harry.

By five o'clock, a half-dozen gentlemen had arrived after dinner, engaging in cards, coffee, or talk. The regulars from the ordinary had not yet cleared their tables. Often, the gentlemen of White's would remain seated until past midnight.

One toothpick pointed over the coffee-house blinds into the street. "Whose phaeton?" asked Toothpick 1 of Toothpick 2.

"The Fortunate Youth's," said No. 2.

"Not so fortunate the last three nights. Luck confoundedly against him. Lost, last night, thirteen hundred to the table. Mr. Warrington been here today, John?"

"Mr. Warrington is in the house now, sir. In the little tea-room with Lord Castlewood since three o'clock. They are playing at piquet," said John.

"What fun for Castlewood!" said No. 1, with a shrug.

The second gentleman growled an execration. "Curse the fellow!" he said. "He has no right to be in this club at all. He doesn't pay if he loses. Gentlemen ought not to play with him. Sir Miles Warrington told me at court the other day that Castlewood has owed him money on a bet for these three years."

"Castlewood," said No. 1, "doesn't lose if he plays alone. A large company flurries him, you see—that's why he doesn't come to the table." The facetious gentleman grinned, showing all his teeth, polished perfectly clean.

"Let's go up and stop 'em," growled No. 2.

"Why?" asked the other. "Much better to look out the window. A lamplighter is going up the ladder—a famous sport. Look at that old putt in the chair: did you ever see such an old quiz?"

"Who is that just gone out of the house? As I live, it's Fortunatus! He seems to have forgotten that his phaeton has been here, waiting all this time. I bet you two to one he has been losing to Castlewood."

"Jack, do you take me to be a fool?" asked one gentleman of the other. "Pretty pair of horses the youth has got. How he is flogging 'em!" They saw Mr. Warrington galloping up the street, with scared coachmen and chairmen clearing the way before him. Presently, my Lord Castlewood was seen entering a chair and departing.

Harry drove up to his own door. It was but a few yards, and those poor horses had been beating the pavement all this while in the rain. Mr. Gumbo was engaged at the door in conversation with a country-looking lass, who tripped off with a curtsey. Mr. Gumbo was always engaged with some pretty maid or other.

"Gumbo, has Mr. Sampson been here?" asked Gumbo's master from his driving seat.

"No, sar. Mr. Sampson have not been here!" answered Mr. Warrington's gentleman. Harry bid him go upstairs and bring down a letter addressed to Mr. Sampson.

"Addressed to Mr. Sampson? Oh yes, sir," said Mr. Gumbo, who could not read.

"A sealed letter, stupid! On the mantelpiece, in the glass!" said Harry; and Gumbo leisurely retired to fetch that document. As soon as Harry had it, he turned his horses' heads toward St. James's Street. The two gentlemen, still yawning out of the window at White's, beheld the Fortunate Youth, in an instant, back again.

As they passed out of the little tea-room where he and Lord Castlewood had played piquet, Mr. Warrington saw that several gentlemen had entered the play-room and that there was a bank there. Some were already steadily at work, wearing their gaming jackets—they kept such coats at the club, putting them on when they intended to sit down to a regular night's play.

Mr. Warrington went to the clerk's desk, paid his account of the previous night, and, sitting down at the table, called for fresh counters. This had been decidedly an unlucky week for the Fortunate Youth, and tonight was no more fortunate than previous nights. He called for more counters, and more presently. He was a little pale and silent, though very easy and polite when spoken to. But he could not win.

At last, he got up. "Hang it! Stay and mend your luck!" said Lord March, who was sitting by his side with a heap of counters before him, green and white. "Take a hundred of mine, and go on!"
