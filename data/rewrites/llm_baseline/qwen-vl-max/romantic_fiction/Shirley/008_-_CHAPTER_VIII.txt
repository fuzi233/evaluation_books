The morning dawned clear and mild, a rare comfort for February. Moore had risen before the sun, already mounted and riding to Whinbury and back before his sister had even begun her breakfast preparations. He returned with no word of what he’d done there—no explanation offered, none expected. Hortense never questioned him; it was not in her nature to pry, nor in his to account for himself. The affairs of trade were sealed within him like secrets buried deep, emerging only rarely to startle Joe Scott or send shivers through foreign correspondents. It seemed that reserve was in his blood—a quiet, unshakable habit born of commerce itself.

After breakfast, Moore retired to his counting-house. Henry, Joe Scott’s son, delivered the letters and newspapers. Moore sat at his desk, broke the seals, and scanned the documents with a quick eye. They were brief, but far from sweet—more sour than honey. As he laid down the last, a sharp sniff escaped his nostrils—defiant, almost scornful—and though he said nothing, the fire in his gaze seemed to summon the devil himself, demanding he sweep the whole business into hell. Yet, without a word, he picked up a pen, snapped off the feathered tip in a sudden burst of restless energy (his face still calm), and wrote out replies swiftly. He sealed them and stepped outside, walking through the mill before returning to sit and read the paper.

It held little interest. He set it aside more than once, arms folded, eyes fixed on the flames dancing in the hearth. He glanced toward the window, checked his watch—his mind clearly elsewhere. Perhaps he longed to be out in the open air, breathing the crisp morning, feeling the warmth of the sun on his skin. The door stood wide, letting in wind and light, but no scent of spring came with it—only the occasional sulfurous puff from the mill’s towering chimney, belching smoke into the sky.

Then, a figure in dark blue—Joe Scott, fresh from the dye vats—appeared at the doorway. “He’s come, sir,” he announced, then vanished.

Moore didn’t look up. A large man entered—broad-shouldered, thick-limbed, dressed in coarse fustian and gray woolen stockings. He was greeted with a nod and invited to sit. Removing his battered hat, he placed it under the chair, wiped his brow with a spotted handkerchief, and declared, “Raight dahn warm for Febewerry.”

Moore grunted something that might have been agreement. The visitor carefully set down a staff beside him—official-looking, formal—and whistled softly, as if settling in.

“You’ve got what you need?” Moore asked.

“Ay, ay! All’s right.”

He resumed whistling. Moore returned to reading. But soon, he reached over, opened a cupboard within arm’s reach, pulled out a black bottle—the same one he’d shown Malone—along with a tumbler and a jug. He placed them on the table.

“Help yourself. There’s water in the jar by the corner.”

“I dunnut knaw that there’s mich need,” said the visitor, rising. “But a body is dry in the morning.” He poured himself a drink, tested it with a deep swallow, then leaned back, satisfied.

“Will you tak naught yourseln, Mr. Moore?” he asked.

Moore shook his head slightly, murmuring a refusal.

“Yah’d as good,” the man continued. “It ‘uld set ye up wald a sup o’ this stuff. Uncommon good hollands. Ye get it fro’ furrin parts, I’se think?”

“Ay!”

“Tak my advice and try a glass on’t. Them lads ‘at’s coming ‘ll keep ye talking, nob’dy knows how long. Ye’ll need propping.”

“Have you seen Mr. Sykes this morning?” Moore asked.

“I seed him a hauf an hour—nay, happen a quarter sin’, just afore I set off. He said he aimed to come here, and I sudn’t wonder but ye’ll have old Helstone too. I seed ‘em saddling his little nag as I passed at back o’ t’ rectory.”

He spoke true. Five minutes later, the clip-clop of hooves echoed in the yard. A familiar nasal voice called out: “Boy!”—likely addressing Harry Scott, who usually loitered near the mill from nine to five.

Helstone strode in, brisk and alert, looking sharper than usual, his cheeks flushed by the cold air.

“Beautiful morning, Moore. How do, my boy? Ha! Whom have we here?” He turned to the man with the staff. “Sugden! What! You’re going to work directly? On my word, you lose no time. But I come to ask explanations. Your message was delivered. Are you sure you’re on the right scent? How do you mean to proceed? Have you a warrant?”

“Sugden has.”

“Then you’re going to seek him now? I’ll accompany you.”

“You will be spared that trouble, sir,” Moore replied. “He’s coming to seek me. I’m just now sitting in state, waiting for his arrival.”

“And who is it? One of my parishioners?”

Joe Scott had slipped in unnoticed. Now he stood leaning on the desk, half his body stained indigo, a silent specter. Moore smiled at the rector’s question. Joe took the cue, adopting a calm yet sly expression.

“It’s a friend of yours, Mr. Helstone—a gentleman you often speak of.”

“Indeed! His name, Joe? You look well this morning.”

“Only the Rev. Moses Barraclough; t’ tub orator you call him sometimes, I think.”

“Ah!” Helstone drew out his snuffbox and took a generous pinch. “Ah! Couldn’t have supposed it. Why, the pious man never was a workman of yours, Moore. He’s a tailor by trade.”

“And so much the worse grudge I owe him,” Moore said, “for interfering and turning my dismissed men against me.”

“And Moses was actually present at the battle of Stilbro’ Moor? He went there, wooden leg and all?”

“Ay, sir,” Joe answered. “He went there on horseback, so his leg wouldn’t be noticed. He was captain, wore a mask. The rest just blackened their faces.”

“And how was he found out?”

“I’ll tell you, sir,” Joe said. “T’ maister’s not fond of talk. I’ve no objections. He courted Sarah, Mr. Moore’s servant lass, and she wouldn’t have nothing to do with him—either she didn’t like his wooden leg or she thought him a hypocrite. Happen (for women are queer hands—we may say that amang werseln when there’s none nigh) she’d have encouraged him anyway, just to pass time. I’ve known some do as mich—even the bonniest, trimmest ones—ay, clean young things that looked as pure as daisies, and yet turned out stinging nettles.”

“Joe’s a sensible fellow,” Helstone interjected.

“Howsiver,” Joe continued, “Sarah had another string to her bow. Fred Murgatroyd, one of our lads, is for her. And as women judge men by their faces—Fred has a middling face, while Moses is none so handsome—we all knaw—and so the lass took on wi’ Fred. A two-three months sin’, Murgatroyd and Moses chanced to meet one Sunday night—both lurking about these premises, hoping to talk Sarah into a walk. They fell out, had a tussle, and Fred was worsted—he’s young and small, while Barraclough, for all he’s one-legged, is almost as strong as Sugden there—indeed, anyone who hears him roaring at a revival or love-feast knows he’s no weakling.”

“Joe, you’re insupportable,” Moore interrupted. “You spin out your tale like Moses spins out his sermons. The short of it is—Murgatroyd was jealous of Barraclough. Last night, as he and a friend sheltered in a barn from a shower, they heard and saw Moses conferring with others inside. From their talk, it was clear he led not only at Stilbro’ Moor but in the attack on Sykes’s property. They planned a deputation to visit me this morning—Barraclough to lead it—in the most religious and peaceful spirit, to beg me to remove the accursed machine from my mill. I rode to Whinbury this morning, secured a constable and a warrant, and now wait to give my friend the welcome he deserves. Here comes Sykes. Mr. Helstone, you must cheer him up. He’s timid about prosecuting.”

A gig rolled into the yard. Mr. Sykes entered—a tall, stout man of fifty, pleasant-faced but weak in expression. He looked anxious.

“Have they been? Are they gone? Have you got him? Is it over?”

“No,” Moore replied, calm and steady. “We’re waiting.”

“They’ll not come—it’s nearly noon. Better give it up. It will stir up bad feeling—cause trouble—perhaps fatal consequences.”

“You need not appear,” Moore said. “I shall meet them in the yard. You can stay here.”

“But my name must be in the proceedings. A wife and family, Mr. Moore—a wife and family make a man cautious.”

Moore looked disgusted. “Give way, please. Leave me to myself. I have no objection to acting alone. Only know this—you won’t find safety in submission. Pearson gave in, conceded, forbore. Yet they tried to shoot him in his own house.”

“My dear sir,” Helstone said, “take a little wine and water.”

Mr. Sykes mixed a brimming tumbler—hollands and water—and drank it. In moments, color returned to his face. He felt emboldened.

“I hope I’m above being trampled on by the common people,” he declared. “I’ve made up my mind. If money and spirit can put down these rioters, they shall be put down. Mr. Moore may do as he likes—but _I_, Christie Sykes, will spend my last penny in law before I yield. I’ll settle them—or see them settled.”

“Take another glass,” Moore urged.

Sykes didn’t mind. “It’s a cold morning,” he said. “Necessary to take care. This season, it’s proper to keep the damp out. I’ve a cough already”—he coughed to prove it—“something like this”—lifting the bottle—“is excellent taken medicinally.” He poured more into his glass. “I don’t usually drink spirits in the morning, but occasionally it’s prudent to take precautions.”

“Quite prudent,” Moore agreed. “Take them by all means.”

Sykes turned to Helstone, who stood by the hearth, watching with keen eyes.

“You, sir,” Sykes said, “as a clergyman, may find it disagreeable to witness scenes of hurry and peril. I dare say your nerves won’t stand it. You’re a man of peace. But we manufacturers, living in the world, always in turmoil, grow quite belligerent. There’s an ardor stirred by danger that makes my heart pant. When Mrs. Sykes fears the house will be attacked—every night—I grow excited. I couldn’t describe my feelings. Honestly, if thieves came—I believe I’d enjoy it, such is my spirit.”

A low, hard laugh escaped the rector—not cruel, but knowing. Moore would have offered a third tumbler, but Helstone stopped him.

“Enough is as good as a feast, is it not, Mr. Sykes?” he said.

Sykes nodded, then watched Joe remove the bottle at a signal from Helstone—smiling smugly, eyes gleaming with regret. Moore looked as though he could have teased him mercilessly. What would Caroline, his cousin, have thought if she’d seen this Robert—her Coriolanus—now? Could she recognize the mischievous, sarcastic man who had spent the evening so gently with her, reading Shakespeare, listening to Chénier?

Yes, it was the same man—just a different side, one Caroline had not yet truly seen, though she may have suspected it. She was human, after all. She, too, had flaws. Had she seen Moore at his worst, she might have forgiven him—love can excuse almost anything except meanness. But meanness kills love, cripples affection. Without esteem, love cannot survive. Moore, despite his faults, was worthy of respect. He had no moral corruption—no rot in his soul, no deadly stain like falsehood. He was not enslaved by desire. His life of action left no room for the hollow pursuits of pleasure-seekers. He was undegraded—a disciple of reason, not sense. The same could be said of old Helstone. Neither lied. Neither had any use for the black bottle now hidden away. Both could claim the proud title of “lord of creation”—for no animal vice ruled them. They were superior beings compared to poor Sykes.

A sound of gathering footsteps came from the yard—then silence. Moore walked to the window. Helstone followed. They stood side by side, the taller man behind the shorter, peering out cautiously, unseen from outside. Their only comment was a shared, cynical smile.

A deep, theatrical cough echoed, followed by a sharp “Whisht!” meant to silence voices. Moore opened the casement slightly.

“Joseph Scott,” began a wheezing voice—Scott standing guard at the door—“might we inquire if your master is within, and may be spoken to?”

“He’s within, ay,” Joe replied nonchalantly.

“Would you then, if _you_ please” (emphasis on “you”), “have the goodness to tell _him_ that twelve gentlemen wish to see him.”

“He’d happen ax what for,” Joe suggested. “I mught as weel tell him that at t’ same time.”

“For a purpose,” came the reply. Joe stepped inside.

“Please, sir, there’s twelve gentlemen wants to see ye, ‘for a purpose.’”

“Good, Joe; I’m their man.—Sugden, come when I whistle.”

Moore stepped out, chuckling quietly. He walked into the yard, one hand in his pocket, the other in his waistcoat, cap brim shading his eyes, masking the glittering scorn beneath.

Twelve men waited—some shirt-sleeved, some in blue aprons. Two stood forward. One, a small, strutting man with a turned-up nose. The other, broad-shouldered, with a wooden leg and crutch—his face solemn, eyes distrustful, lips curled in a sneer. He seemed to mock something unseen, his whole bearing false, untrustworthy.

“Good-morning, Mr. Barraclough,” Moore said, smooth and cool.

“Peace be unto you!” Barraclough replied, closing his half-shut eyes completely.

“I’m obliged. Peace is excellent. Nothing I desire more. But that’s not all you have to say, I suppose? Peace is not your purpose?”

“As to our purpose,” Barraclough began, “it may sound strange or foolish to ears like yours. For the children of this world are wiser in their generation than the children of light.”

“To the point, please. Let me hear it.”

“Ye’se hear, sir. If I cannot get it off, there’s eleven behind to help me. It’s a grand purpose—and” (his voice shifting from sneer to whine) “it’s the Looard’s own purpose, and that’s better.”

“Do you want a subscription for a new Ranter’s chapel, Mr. Barraclough? Unless your errand is something like that, I fail to see its relevance.”

“I hadn’t that duty in mind, sir. But since Providence has led you to mention it, I’ll make it i’ my way to tak ony trifle ye may spare. The smallest contribution will be acceptable.”

With that, he doffed his hat and held it out—like a begging bowl—while a bold grin spread across his face.

“If I gave you sixpence, you’d drink it.”

Barraclough raised his palms and whites of his eyes—an absurd parody of piety.

“You seem a fine fellow,” Moore said, perfectly calm. “You don’t care to show me you’re a double-dyed hypocrite, that your trade is fraud. You expect me to laugh at your cleverness—while thinking you’re fooling the men behind you.”

Barraclough’s face darkened. He realized he’d gone too far. Before he could speak, the second leader stepped forward—impatient, self-assured, though not betraying the mark of a traitor.

“Mr. Moore,” he began, speaking slowly, each word enunciated as if for effect, “it might justly be said that reason, rather than peace, is our purpose. We come first to request you to hear reason. Should you refuse, it is my duty to warn you—in very decided terms—that measures will be resorted to which may likely terminate in—bringing you to a sense of the unwisdom, the foolishness, guiding your actions as a tradesman in this part of the country. Hem! Sir, I would beg to allude that, as a foreigner, arriving from distant shores, another hemisphere of this globe, cast, as I may say, a perfect outcast upon these cliffs of Albion—you lack understanding of our ways, which might benefit the working classes. If, to come to particulars, you’d consider giving up this mill and returning home without further delay, it would perhaps be best. I see no objection to such a plan.—What hev ye to say tull’t, lads?” he turned to the group.

“Hear, hear!” they cried.

“Brayvo, Noah o’ Tim’s!” muttered Joe Scott, standing behind Moore. “Moses’ll niver beat that. Cliffs o’ Albion, and t’ other hemisphere! My certy! Did ye come fro’ th’ Antarctic Zone, maister? Moses is dished.”

But Moses refused to be dished. Glancing sharply at “Noah o’ Tim’s,” he tried again—this time with seriousness, abandoning sarcasm.

“Or iver you set up the pole o’ your tent amang us, Mr. Moore, we lived i’ peace and quietness—yea, I may say, in loving-kindness. I’m not yet aged, but I remember twenty years back when hand-labour was respected, and no mischief-maker dared bring in these machines so pernicious. I’m not a cloth-dresser myself, but a tailor by trade. However, my heart is softish. I’m a feeling man. When I see my brethren oppressed, like my great namesake of old, I stand up for them. That’s why I speak to you today—to advise you to abandon your infernal machinery and employ more hands.”

“What if I refuse, Mr. Barraclough?”

“The Looard pardon you! The Looard soften your heart, sir!”

“Are you connected with the Wesleyans now, Mr. Barraclough?”

“Praise God! Bless His name! I’m a joined Methody!”

“Which doesn’t stop you from being a drunkard and a swindler. I saw you last week, dead-drunk by the roadside, returning from Stilbro’ market. While you preach peace, you spend your life stirring up dissent. You care nothing for the poor in distress—no more than you care for me. You incite them to outrage for your own ends—as does the man called Noah of Tim’s. You two are restless, meddling, impudent scoundrels, driven by selfish ambition—dangerous and childish. Some behind you are honest, misguided men. But you two—altogether bad.”

Barraclough opened his mouth.

“Silence! You’ve had your turn. Now I will speak. To be dictated to by you, or any Jack, Jem, or Jonathan—never. You demand I leave the country. You ask I abandon my machinery. If I refuse, you threaten. I _refuse_—point-blank! I stay. I stand by this mill. I’ll bring in the finest machinery inventors can provide. What will you do? The most you _can_ do—and this you’ll never _dare_—is burn my mill, destroy it, and shoot me. Then what? Suppose the building is ruins and I’m dead—what then, you lads behind these two scamps? Would that stop invention? Exhaust science? Not for a moment! Another, better mill would rise. A more enterprising owner would take my place. Hear me! I’ll make my cloth as I please, using whatever means I choose. Whoever interferes with me after this—let them take the consequences. An example shall prove I’m in earnest.”

He whistled—sharp and loud.

Sugden appeared, staff in hand, warrant in pocket.

Moore turned sharply to Barraclough. “You were at Stilbro’,” he said. “I have proof. You were on the moor. You wore a mask. You knocked down one of my men with your own hand—you! A preacher of the gospel! Sugden—arrest him!”

Moses was seized. A cry rose, a rush to rescue. But Moore’s hand, hidden all along, emerged—holding a pistol.

“Both barrels are loaded,” he said. “I’m quite determined. Keep off!”

And the yard fell still.
