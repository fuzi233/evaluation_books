As the autumn sun cast its warm glow over the rolling hills of the countryside, Sir Hugo's estate at Diplow began to stir with renewed life. By early October, the baronet's presence had infused a sense of cheerfulness throughout the neighborhood, from the grand estates of Brackenshaw and Quetcham to the respectable parlors of Wanchester. His was a reputation for being a man of the world, a Liberal of good breeding who believed that reform would not fundamentally alter the English penchant for tradition and social hierarchy. Consequently, he took great care in crafting a guest list for Diplow, carefully balancing the company to ensure that each conversation felt like a genuine exchange rather than a mere formality.

Easy-going Lord Brackenshaw, for instance, would not have batted an eye at meeting Robinson, the local attorney, but Robinson might have taken offense at being seated alongside those of equal standing. Sir Hugo, however, knew just how to navigate these delicate matters, effortlessly earning popularity for himself while bringing joy to others. The rector of Pennicote, who had previously endured a chilly reception under the Grandcourts, now found himself warmly welcomed at Diplow. This was not due to any particular fondness for Mr. Gascoigne, but rather Sir Hugo's desire to maintain a cordial relationship with him in honor of Mrs. Grandcourt, whose welfare had grown increasingly dear to the baronet's heart. Though he could not quite articulate the reasons behind his chivalrous feelings, even to his wife, Lady Mallinger, it was clear that his devotion went beyond mere social obligation.

Meanwhile, Deronda, now engaged to Mirah, felt it necessary to share his decision with Sir Hugo, choosing to do so through a letter rather than a personal visit. He sensed that his fatherly friend might be disappointed, even pained, by this turn of events. Writing allowed Deronda to avoid the risk of a heated confrontation, which might leave a lasting impression of resentment. Sir Hugo, though not entirely unprepared, was nonetheless deeply troubled by the news. His initial impulse was to confide in Lady Mallinger, knowing she would react with shock and distress, allowing him to deflect her concerns with a rational explanation.
