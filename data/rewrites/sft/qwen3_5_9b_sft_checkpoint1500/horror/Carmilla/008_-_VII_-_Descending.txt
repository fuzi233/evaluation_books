Descending

Even now, the memory of that night clings to me with a visceral, suffocating dread that refuses to fade. It was not a fleeting nightmare, a transient shadow dismissed by the light of dawn. Instead, it had rooted itself, deepening with every passing hour until it seemed to seep into the walls, staining the woodwork, and haunting the furniture around which the specter had danced.

I found no solace in solitude the following day. To sit alone for a single moment felt impossible, a vacuum where sanity might slip away. I held back from telling Papa, torn between two terrors. On one hand, I feared his laughter, the casual dismissal of my ordeal as a childish jest; on the other, I dreaded his panic. He was already an invalid, his health fragile, and I could not bear the thought of him imagining I had fallen victim to the mysterious plague sweeping our village. Yet, inside, I knew no physical ailment plagued me.

Fortunately, I had companions in Madame Perrodon, kindly and patient, and Mademoiselle Lafontaine, whose spirit was as bright as ever. Though they sensed the tremor beneath my skin, the weight of my secret pressed so heavily that silence stretched until I finally confessed.

Mademoiselle laughed, a sharp, brittle sound, while Madame Perrodon's gaze remained fixed on me, heavy with anxiety.

"By the way," Mademoiselle chirped, "the long lime-tree walk behind Carmilla's window is haunted!"

"Nonsense!" Madame Perrodon snapped, likely sensing the inappropriateness of the topic. "Who tells such stories?"

"Martin swears he climbed the wall twice before sunrise, when the gates were being mended, and twice he saw a woman walking down that path."

"So he sees what he expects to see, so long as there are cows to milk in the river fields," Madame retorted.

"I dare say," Mademoiselle agreed, "yet Martin is easily spooked. I have never known a man so prone to fear."

"You must not speak a word of this to Carmilla," I interjected quickly, my voice tight. "She looks down upon that path from her window, and she is, if anything, a greater coward than I."

Carmilla descended later than usual. As soon as we were together, she leaned close, her face pale.

"I was terrified last night," she whispered. "I am certain I would have seen something monstrous if not for that charm I bought from the poor little hunchback—the one I cursed with such vile names. I dreamed something black coalescing around my bed. I awoke in absolute horror, convinced I saw a dark shape by the chimneypiece. But when I felt under my pillow and grasped the charm, the vision vanished instantly. Only the knowledge that it was there calmed me, preventing some fiend from throttling me as it did those unfortunate souls we heard of."

"Well, listen," I began, recounting my own tale. Her eyes widened with genuine horror as I spoke.

"And did you keep the charm near you?" she asked, her voice trembling.

"No," I admitted, "I dropped it into a porcelain vase in the drawing-room. But I shall carry it tonight, just as you advise."

Time has blurred the edges of that night, yet I recall clearly pinning the trinket to my pillow. Sleep claimed me almost immediately, deep and untroubled. The following night passed in similar tranquility.

Yet, when I awoke, a heavy lethargy settled over me, a melancholy so profound it bordered on luxury.

"Well, I told you so," Carmilla said, a smug glint in her eye. "I slept most beautifully last night. I pinned the charm to the breast of my chemise. Last time, it was too distant. I am convinced it was all imagination, save for the dreams. I used to believe evil spirits crafted nightmares, but our physician insists otherwise. He says a fever or some malady knocks at the brain's door, unable to enter, and thus wanders off, leaving behind that alarm."

"And what, pray, do you think this charm is?" I asked.

"It has been fumigated or steeped in some potent drug; it is an antidote against the malaria," she replied confidently.

"So it works only on the body?"

"Certainly! You do not suppose evil spirits flee from ribbons or the perfumes of a druggist? These plagues wander the air, attacking the nerves first, then infecting the brain. But before they can seize you, the antidote repels them. That is surely what it did for us. It is not magic, merely natural science."

I wished, with a desperate longing, to agree with Carmilla, to let her rationality wash away the chill creeping into my bones. I tried, giving the impression that her words were soothing the storm within me.

For several nights, I slept deeply. Yet, each morning, the same languor returned, a heavy cloak that draped over my limbs. I felt altered, a stranger to myself. A strange, sweet melancholy stole over me, a sentiment I would not have interrupted. Vague thoughts of death began to bloom, and the notion that I was slowly sinking into the earth took a gentle, unwelcome, yet strangely comforting hold. If it were sorrowful, the tone of mind it induced was equally sweet.

My soul acquiesced.

I refused to admit I was ill. I would not confess to Papa, nor summon a doctor.

Carmilla's devotion intensified, her paroxysms of lazy adoration becoming more frequent. She gazed upon me with increasing ardor, feeding on my fading strength and spirits. This observation shocked me, flashing briefly like a glimpse of madness.

Unknowingly, I had entered the final stages of the strangest malady a mortal soul could endure. An unaccountable fascination clung to its early symptoms, reconciling me to the debilitating effects of its progression. This allure grew stronger until, eventually, a sense of the horrific mingled with it, discoloring and perverting the entirety of my existence.

The first change was, paradoxically, agreeable. It marked the turning point toward the descent of Avernus.

Vague, strange sensations visited me in sleep. The prevailing one was a peculiar, pleasant cold thrill, akin to moving against the swift current of a river. Soon, dreams followed—interminable, hazy visions where I could recall neither scenery nor figures, yet which left an awful, exhausting imprint. I felt as though I had traversed a long period of immense mental exertion and peril.

Upon waking, I retained the memory of a place nearly dark, of speaking to invisible beings. Most distinctively, I remembered a clear, female voice, deep and resonant, speaking from afar with a slow, solemn cadence that instilled indescribable fear. Sometimes, I felt a soft hand tracing my cheek and neck. Other times, warm lips brushed me, lingering, growing longer and more loving as they neared my throat, where the caress suddenly fixed itself. My heart hammered, my breath rising and falling in rapid, shallow gasps. A sobbing rose within me, transforming into a crushing strangulation, culminating in a dreadful convulsion that stripped my consciousness away, plunging me into darkness.

Three weeks had passed since this unaccountable state began.

My suffering had etched itself onto my features over the last week. My complexion was ghastly pale, my eyes dilated and shadowed by deep, bruised circles. The languor I had long felt now sat heavy upon my countenance.

Papa asked constantly if I was ill. With an obstinacy that now seems inexplicable, I persisted in assuring him I was perfectly well.

In truth, I was free of pain. I could complain of no bodily derangement. My affliction seemed to reside entirely in the imagination or the nerves. Horrible as my sufferings were, I guarded them with a morbid reserve, keeping them almost wholly to myself.

It could not be the *oupire*, the terrible complaint the peasants spoke of. Those victims seldom survived past three days, for death swiftly ended their misery. I had endured three weeks.

Carmilla, too, complained of dreams and feverish sensations, though hers were far less alarming than mine. Hers were terrifying, yes, but not to the point of immediate collapse. Had I been capable of understanding my condition, I would have cried out for aid, begging for wisdom. Some narcotic influence, unsuspected and insidious, acted upon me, numbing my perceptions.

Now, I must tell you of a dream that led directly to an odd discovery.

One night, instead of the usual indistinct voice, I heard one sweet, tender, and simultaneously terrible. It spoke these words: *"Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin."*

Simultaneously, a light flared unexpectedly. I saw Carmilla standing near the foot of my bed, clad in her white nightdress, bathed from chin to feet in a great, spreading stain of blood.

I woke with a shriek, seized by the singular, frantic conviction that Carmilla was being murdered. I leaped from the bed, my next recollection being that I stood in the lobby, screaming for help.

Madame and Mademoiselle burst from their rooms in alarm. A lamp burned perpetually there, and seeing me, they quickly discerned the cause of my terror.

"I insist," I cried, "that we knock at Carmilla's door!"
