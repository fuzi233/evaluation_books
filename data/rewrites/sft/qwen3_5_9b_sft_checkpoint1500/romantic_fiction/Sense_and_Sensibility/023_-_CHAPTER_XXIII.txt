Despite Elinor's usual reliance on Lucy Steele's word, it was impossible for her to harbor any real doubt in this specific instance. Where else could a lie of such magnitude be concealed? There was no motive, no temptation sufficient to justify inventing a falsehood regarding a marriage engagement. What Lucy had declared, therefore, Elinor could no longer—and dared not any longer—question. It was backed by overwhelming probability and proof, standing in stark contrast to nothing but Elinor's own desperate wishes. The foundation laid during their time at Mr. Pratt's house was undeniable and terrifying; Edward's recent visits near Plymouth, his gloomy demeanor, his dissatisfaction with his prospects, and his evasive behavior toward herself formed a coherent narrative. Coupled with the Miss Steeles' surprising familiarity with Norland and the Dashwoods, the physical tokens—the picture, the letter, the ring—a body of evidence emerged that silenced every fear of unfair condemnation. It became a settled fact, against which no amount of partiality could argue, that Edward had treated her with cruelty.

For a fleeting moment, her resentment burned hot, fueled by the indignation of being duped. Yet, as quickly as it flared, other thoughts softened her resolve. Had Edward deliberately deceived her? Had he feigned a love he did not possess? Could his engagement to Lucy truly be one of the heart? No. Whatever it may have been in the past, it was not so now. His affection belonged entirely to her. She could not be mistaken about that. Her mother, her sisters, and Fanny had all witnessed the depth of his regard at Norland; it was not an illusion born of her own vanity. He certainly loved her. This realization acted as a balm to her stinging heart. If it could soften her spirit, how much could it not tempt her toward forgiveness?

He had been blameable, undeniably so, for remaining at Norland once he realized his influence over her exceeded propriety. In that sin, he could not be defended. But if he had injured her, how much more grievously had he injured himself? If her situation was pitiable, his was truly hopeless. His imprudence had caused her temporary misery, but it seemed to have stripped him of any chance of happiness elsewhere. Perhaps she could eventually regain tranquility; but what did *he* have to look forward to? Could he possibly be content with Lucy Steele? With his integrity, his delicacy, and his cultivated mind, could he settle for a wife who was illiterate, artful, and selfish?

The youthful infatuation of nineteen might have blinded him to everything but Lucy's beauty and apparent goodness. But what of the four years that followed? If those years had been rationally spent, they should have sharpened his understanding enough to reveal Lucy's educational deficiencies. Conversely, four years immersed in inferior society and frivolous pursuits might well have eroded the natural simplicity that once gave interest to her features.

If his difficulties with his mother had seemed formidable before when considering her, how insurmountable they must now appear, given that the object of his engagement was undoubtedly inferior in both connection and fortune. These obstacles, however, would not press heavily on a heart so alienated from Lucy. Indeed, the prospect of family opposition and unkindness, usually a torment, could here be felt as a relief.

As these painful reflections succeeded one another, tears welled in Elinor's eyes—not for herself, but for him. Supported by the conviction that she had done nothing to merit her unhappiness, and consoled by the belief that Edward had done nothing to forfeit her esteem, she felt a strange strength rising within her. Even under the shock of losing all her dearest hopes, she believed she could command herself enough to shield her mother and sisters from the truth. So effectively did she manage her emotions that when she joined them at dinner, just two hours after her devastation began, no one could have guessed from her sisters' countenance that Elinor was mourning in secret over barriers that would forever separate her from her love, or that Marianne was inwardly constructing an idealized image of a man whose whole heart she felt possessed, expecting to see him driving every carriage that passed near their home.

Keeping this secret from her mother and Marianne, though it demanded constant exertion, was not an aggravation of her distress; rather, it was a relief. To spare them the agony of knowing the truth, and to save herself from hearing their condemnation of Edward—which would surely flow from their excessive partiality for her, a judgment she found difficult to withstand—was a burden she willingly bore. From their counsel, she knew she could receive no aid; their tenderness and sorrow would only deepen her pain, and her own self-command would find no encouragement in their example. Alone, she was stronger. Her good sense supported her so firmly that her resolve remained unshaken, her outward cheerfulness as constant as it was possible to maintain amidst such poignant and fresh grief.

Though her first conversation with Lucy on the subject had been agonizing, Elinor soon felt an urgent desire to renew it. She needed to hear the details of their engagement again, to understand more clearly what Lucy truly felt for Edward, and to ascertain whether there was any sincerity in her declaration of tender regard. Most importantly, she wished to convince Lucy, by her readiness to discuss the matter calmly, that she was no longer interested in it than as a friend—a position she deeply feared her involuntary agitation during their morning talk might have clouded. That Lucy was disposed to jealousy seemed evident; it was plain that Edward had always spoken of her in high praise, not merely because Lucy claimed it, but because she ventured to trust a stranger with a secret so confessedly important. Even Sir John's joking intelligence must have carried some weight. Yet, while Elinor remained so well assured within herself of Edward's love, it required no further speculation to understand why Lucy should be jealous; indeed, Lucy's very confidence was proof of it. What other reason could drive such a disclosure but the intention to inform Elinor of Lucy's "superior claims" and teach her to avoid Edward? Elinor understood her rival's intentions with little difficulty. While she was firmly resolved to act according to principles of honor—to combat her own affection and see Edward as little as possible—she could not deny herself the comfort of trying to prove to Lucy that her heart was unwounded. Since there was nothing more painful she could possibly hear, she trusted her ability to recount the particulars with composure.

Yet, an opportunity to speak privately did not arise immediately, though Lucy was as eager as Elinor to seize one. The weather rarely permitted walks where they might easily separate from the others. Though they met almost every evening, either at the park or the cottage, mostly at the former, they never gathered for conversation. Such a notion never entered the minds of Sir John or Lady Middleton. Consequently, little leisure was afforded for general chat, and none for private discourse. They assembled solely for the purposes of eating, drinking, laughing, playing at cards, or games like Consequences—anything sufficiently noisy.

A few such gatherings took place without offering Elinor a chance to engage Lucy privately. Then, one morning, Sir John called at the cottage to request, in the name of charity, that they all dine with Lady Middleton that day. He was obliged to attend the club at Exeter, and she would otherwise be quite alone, save for her mother and the two Miss Steeles. Elinor, foreseeing a fairer opening for her objective in such a party—one where they would be more at liberty under the tranquil, well-bred direction of Lady Middleton, rather than united by her husband's noisy purposes—immediately accepted the invitation. Margaret, with her mother's permission, complied equally. Marianne, though usually reluctant to join such parties, was persuaded by her mother, who could not bear to see her secluded from any chance of amusement, to accompany them.

The young ladies went, and Lady Middleton was happily preserved from the frightful solitude that threatened her. The meeting proved as insipid as Elinor had expected, yielding no novelty of thought or expression. Nothing could be less interesting than the entire discourse, both in the dining-parlour and drawing-room. The children accompanied them to the latter, and while they remained there, Elinor was fully convinced of the impossibility of engaging Lucy's attention. They departed only when the tea-things were removed. A card-table was then set up, and Elinor wondered at herself for ever entertaining hope of finding time for conversation at the park. Everyone rose to prepare for a round of Casino.

"I am glad," said Lady Middleton to Lucy, "you are not going to finish poor little Annamaria's basket this evening; for I am sure it must hurt your eyes to work filigree by candlelight. And we will make the dear little love some amends for her disappointment to-morrow, and then I hope she will not much mind it."

This hint was enough for Lucy to recollect herself instantly. "Indeed you are very much mistaken, Lady Middleton," she replied. "I am only waiting to know whether you can make your party without me, or I should have been at my filigree already. I would not disappoint the little angel for all the world: and if you want me at the card-table now, I am resolved to finish the basket after supper."

"You are very good," Lady Middleton responded, hoping it wouldn't harm Lucy's eyes. "Will you ring the bell for some working candles? My poor little girl would be sadly disappointed, I know, if the basket wasn't finished tomorrow, for though I told her it certainly wouldn't, I am sure she depends upon having it done."
