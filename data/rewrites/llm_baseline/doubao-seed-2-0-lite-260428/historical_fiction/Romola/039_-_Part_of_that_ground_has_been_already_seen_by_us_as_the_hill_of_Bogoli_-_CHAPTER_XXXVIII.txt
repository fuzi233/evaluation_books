That sudden journey Tito Melema would soon take to Rome — the very trip that would remove so many barriers standing in the way of Romola’s later unplanned departure from his side — had been decided on entirely out of the blue, at a supper feast only the evening before.

Tito had set off toward that supper in the highest of spirits. The food was sure to be delicate, the wine of exceptional vintage, and the company the most distinguished Florence had to offer; for the venue was the Selva, or Orto de’ Rucellai, the celebrated Rucellai Gardens, and his host Bernardo Rucellai was the very model of a Florentine grandee. Even his family name carried a softly symbolic history: traced to its root, it called to mind the tiny lichen popularly known as *orcella* or *roccella*, which grew on the rocky isles of the Greek archipelago and the Canary Islands, drinking so much sunlight into its thin stems and knobby little heads that it could later release that stored light as a rich reddish-purple dye that delighted the eye of every man who saw it. Nearly a hundred years before Bernardo’s time, a merchant who brought the closely guarded secret of this dye, called *oricello*, home to Florence from the Levant had earned his family a vast fortune, and the pleasantly evocative surname Oricellari, or Roccellari, which softened over time on Tuscan tongues into Rucellai.

Bernardo Rucellai stood out as the brightest star on that rich purple family background, adding distinction after distinction to the name. He had married the sister of Lorenzo de’ Medici, and the wedding feast held for the pair was the most lavish display of Florentine finery a generation could remember. For these and other acts of state service, he had been sent as ambassador to France and Venice, and elected Gonfaloniere of Florence. He had not only built himself a grand new palace, but finished the striking black-and-white marble facade of the church of Santa Maria Novella. He had planted his garden full of rare, exotic trees, and consecrated the ground as a classic cultural landmark by hosting the regular meetings of the Platonic Academy, left adrift and orphaned by the death of Lorenzo de Medici. He had written a groundbreaking, erudite topographical volume on ancient Rome, collected a vast hoard of antiquities, and composed Latin prose of near-perfect purity. Even the simplest summary of his life reads like a glowing epitaph, at the end of which the Greek and Ausonian muses could be forgiven for tearing out their hair in disbelief that nature could ever combine so many virtues in a single set of human bones.

Tito’s invitation to the supper had come through Lorenzo Tornabuoni, with an undercurrent of gravity that would have told him the gathering was political even if Florence had not been in the grip of unprecedented public unrest. As it was, Tito felt certain that important party aims would be advanced over plates of delicate stewed fish and mellow old Greek wine; Bernardo Rucellai was not merely an influential noble, he was one of the secretive elect Twenty who had held the full reins of Florentine governance for the past three weeks. This certainty put Tito in even better spirits as he made his way down the Via della Scala to the famous garden grounds. Without that assurance, he might have found himself worrying the distinguished company would be as dull as it was elite; he had sat through more than a few tedious suppers in the Rucellai gardens before, especially the stuffy Platonic philosophy gatherings where he was expected not just to nod along to a fully mapped out theory of the universe, but to sit for hours listening to it be expounded from the very origin of all things to their final, perfect fulfillment in the speaker’s own treatise.

The night was dark, and passersby could only make out the outline of Tito’s tall, graceful form, wrapped tight in a warm mantle, when he crossed the occasional pool of light cast by a lamp strung before a street corner shrine to the Virgin Mary. Anyone lingering between those pools of light would have noticed that Tito was followed at a steady, unobtrusive distance by a very different figure: thickset, elderly, clad in a plain serge tunic and scuffed felt hat. Most onlookers would have dismissed the pairing as mere chance, for the streets were crowded with pedestrians that hour, but when Tito paused at the gate to the Rucellai gardens, the figure behind him stopped too.

The small side *sportello* door set in the main gate had been left ajar by the servant, who had been distracted answering a guest’s question and not yet latched it after the last arrival. Tito slipped through quickly, gave his name to the servant, and wandered on past the evergreen bushes that glinted like polished dark metal in the torchlight. His follower slipped in right behind him.

“Your name?” the servant asked sharply.

“Baldassarre Calvo,” came the immediate, unhesitating answer.

“You are no guest. All the guests have already entered.”

“I belong to Tito Melema, who just went past. I was told to wait for him inside the gardens.”

The servant hesitated. “I was ordered to admit no one but guests. Are you a servant of Messer Tito?”

“No, friend. I am no servant. I am a scholar.”

There are men who, faced with a quiet, unshakable tone of that sort, will let you pass even if you claim to be a wild buffalo, no further questions asked. The porter stepped aside at once, Baldassarre crossed the threshold, and heard the door clank shut and chain behind him before he vanished into the shadow of the glistening bushes.

That steady, unflappable composure marked a staggering shift in Baldassarre since his last face-to-face meeting with Tito, when his dagger had snapped clean in two mid-strike. The change had revealed itself in a startling, sudden wave. The moment Tito’s shadow had passed the opening of the hovel on the Bogoli hill as he left to head home, Baldassarre had been left sitting in the trembling aftershock familiar to anyone who has ever expended all their strength in a burst of rage: a state where physical helplessness often pairs with an almost supernatural clarity of thought, as if the spent passion has burned away all the fog in the mind to leave everything sharp and unobscured. He could not stand or walk away just then; his limbs felt numb, his hands shook, a cold chill ran through his bones. But in that bodily lassitude, he was surrounded not by the usual dim, fading half-memories, but by perfectly clear images of the past, unspooling in an unbroken line through all the long years of his life that had seemed a slow, cruel preparation for the bitterness of Tito’s betrayal.

For several minutes he was so absorbed in the flood of recollections he did not even register that his mind had healed, that the fog was gone. But when that wave of clear memory traveled all the way back to the scene just played out in the hovel, he realized exactly where he was, and remembered Monna Lisa and Tessa. That smiling stranger who had visited the hovel was Tessa’s secret husband — the same man who had a legal wife, Romola, in the house on the Via de’ Bardi. It was time to pick up the broken halves of his dagger and leave, leave no trace of the weakness he had just shown; to hide his feebleness was the only form of power he had left.

He leaned down to grab the broken dagger shards, and his gaze fell on the large manuscript volume open on the dirt floor beside him, a tattered odd volume of Pausanias. Moonlight spilled across its pages, and he could make out the large Greek lettering at the head of the chapter: *ΜΕΣΣΕΝΙΚΑ. ΚΒ′*. Only an hour or two earlier, he had stared hopelessly at that exact same page, and the characters had meant no more to him than random black weather stains on a stone wall. But in that moment, they were magic sigils that conjured up an entire world for him. That single moonbeam falling on the letters had called the whole of ancient Messenia to his mind, and its generations-long struggle to throw off Spartan oppression. He snatched up the book; the moonlight was too faint to read further by, but he did not need to. He knew that chapter by heart, and read it inwardly, word for word. He saw the entire people of Messenia stone the traitor Aristocrates to death, drag his body outside their borders to rot unburied, and erect a pillar carved with verses that bore witness to how time always brings justice home to the unjust. The lines echoed inside him, waking thousands of half-lost memories he had grieved as irretrievable. He forgot his old age, he nearly shouted out loud. The light had come back, that mother of all knowledge and joy! In that rush of exultation, his numb limbs flooded with strength. He jumped to his feet, broken dagger and book clutched tight, and stepped out under the broad, bright moonlight.

The air was sharp and frosty, but Baldassarre felt no chill. He only felt the glow of regained power thrumming under his skin. He wandered the open stretches of that high ground, pausing to look down on Florence sleeping dark under the watch of its guardian mountains, its domes and towers faint in the silver light, the Arno a pale gleam far below, the valley stretching out toward the distant snow-capped peaks, and for a moment he felt master of all of it. That rare, sharp sense of mental command that visits all men in moments of perfect lucidity was amplified tenfold for him after the long weeks and months where his memory had been nothing but a hollow, dim awareness that something vital was missing. That city, which had once felt like a confusing, endless labyrinth to his fogged mind, was now a set of facts he could arrange, bend fully to his purposes. He knew cities again, his old sharp worldly experience was all back, and he drank in the keen delight of holding every name, every secret, every old story in the full grasp of his working mind, like a man stepping over the threshold of a vast long-lost inheritance he never expected to claim.

But presiding over all that rushing, eager wealth of recovered memory was one single, dark unshakable End: a dark deity hidden in the innermost chamber of his soul, who had only been out of sight while the sacrifices for his altar were being prepared. When the first rush of triumph at regaining his full mental power faded, all his thoughts centered on Tito. That fair, slippery viper could not escape him now. Justice, after a long struggle, had given him the means to pierce that soft, selfish heart that had never once known a spark of genuine tenderness for another, through the sensitive, self-serving fibres that could be lanced through by the sharp point of anguish. The man who bowed to no law, no moral right, would bow to Pain, the great lord of all mortals. He could dig out every secret Tito had ever buried, and he already knew more than a few of them. The failed dagger strike, which had felt like total frustration at the time, had actually been the first step to victory. Twice before, partial flashes of his returning memory had come after sudden shocks: once when he had fought off a rabid dog that attacked him, once when he had nearly drowned in the Arno and scrambled up a jagged rock to save himself. But what if this new light died out too, and the dreary blank fog rolled back over his mind before he could act? The fear made him terrified that this long wakeful night on the hill would burn through his recovered strength too fast, so he hunted out a sheltered nook, curled up against a heap of warm decomposing straw from a nearby garden, and fell asleep.

When he opened his eyes again, it was full daylight. The first moments were thick with disorientation: he was a man with two identities. Which one had he woken up as? The dim, addled beggar whose memories were the sad, tattered heirloom of a fallen great man, or the sharp, whole scholar he had been the night before? It was the latter. Every event of the night came flooding back to him: the moment he recognized the Greek characters on the Pausanias page, the tide of names and facts surging back into his mind, that wide view of the city that had filled him with the wild, ecstatic wonder of a Maenad waking at sunrise on a mountain top.

He picked up the book again, read a few lines, and found he could recall the rest of the chapter without even looking. A single name called up a whole tapestry of associated deeds, a single reference to an event linked back perfectly to every person tangled up in it. He read stories of terrible, unforgivable crimes, and stories of guilty men who had seemed to triumph utterly, who had found sanctuary in hidden places, whose baseness wore armor that deflected every blow of justice. But none of that mattered. Even if baseness triumphed everywhere else, even if it hoarded all the goods of the world and held the keys to hell itself, it could never triumph over the unyielding, white-hot hatred it had itself awakened in its victim. No torture Tito could ever devise would be worse than the torture of submitting to his false, patronizing smile. Baldassarre felt the indestructible, independent force of that supreme emotion, which knows no fear, needs no other motive, and is itself an ever-burning fire that consumes all other desires. By that full morning light, when he confirmed his memory was still sharp, no fog creeping in to blur its edges, all his joy was nothing but the hungry hope of vengeance.

From that sunrise all the way to the evening he slipped through the Rucellai garden gate, Baldassarre had moved cautiously, quietly, inquiring after Tito’s position, his connections, his daily habits, trailing him almost every day. He did not want to arouse Tito’s alarm. He wanted to strike at the exact moment that smug, lucky favorite of blind fortune was at the peak of his confident ease, surrounded by the most powerful men in Florence, the very men whose good opinion he depended on to climb higher. He did not crave petty personal recognition or small retribution for his own sake; he wanted to find the sharpest possible edge of public disgrace and shame that could pierce a selfish smiler like Tito, to send a shock of sudden dread rippling through his very marrow. He lived rough, ate sparingly, and spent most of his last remaining coins buying a new sharp poniard. His hunger and thirst were not for fine things, but for a perfectly exact, exquisite vengeance. He avoided anyone he knew was close to Tito: he never stepped foot in Nello’s barber shop, even though he saw Tito visit there constantly, and he turned down side alleys to avoid crossing paths with Piero di Cosimo. He trembled at the thought of frustration, most of all at the thought his memory might slip away again before he got his chance. Twice, he had felt a terrible mental dizziness, where the distinct shapes of things in his mind seemed to blur and fade. Once he had tried to slip into the Palazzo Vecchio and make his way to the council chamber where Tito was attending a meeting, and had failed, his head spinning too hard to move forward. But now, on this evening, standing in the torchlit bushes of the Rucellai gardens, he knew his occasion had finally come.
