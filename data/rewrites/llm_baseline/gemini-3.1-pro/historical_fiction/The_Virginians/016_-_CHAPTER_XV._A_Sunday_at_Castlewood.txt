The second day after Harry’s arrival at Castlewood was a Sunday, folding the estate into a rhythm that had pulsed in unbroken, circular pacing for generations. The chapel appertaining to the castle was the village church, connected to the great house by a gallery that bridged the present with deep antiquity. Within the altar-rails knelt the effigy of the Esmond from the time of King James the First, his square stone beard and marble prayer-book anchoring the family’s mythic past to their everyday domestic routine. Madame de Bernstein, possessing the gravity of a bishop’s widow, took her place in the great state pew beneath ancient, dusty banners that Harry imagined had once snapped in the winds of the Commonwealth wars. 

The tall, high-colored clergyman, Mr. Sampson, commenced the service. His voice was agreeable but aggressively theatrical, reducing the sacred rites to a kind of superficial, commercialized performance that grated against the chapel’s natural acoustic ecology. But the true sonic event occurred when one of the young ladies touched the organ and the congregation turned to the psalm. Harry’s lackey, Gumbo, sharing a hymnal with pretty Molly the porter’s daughter, could not read a syllable of the dominant English lexicon printed on the page. Instead, he sang by ear. 

His voice rose—exceedingly loud, impossibly sweet, and entirely unmoored from the rigid notations of the book. It was an untranslatable subjective experience, a deeply resonant melody carrying the auditory weight of unwritten cultural memory. In that soaring solo, which soon silenced the clerk himself, one could hear the subtle defiance of a native dialect resisting language death. The liveried gentlemen from London were astonished out of all propriety, entirely unaccustomed to such a profound disruption of their controlled, sterile sonic environment. 

The choral performance gave way to a brief sermon, which Harry thought far too short, though it was drenched in a startling, commercialized theatrics. Sampson vividly detailed the execution of a horse-stealer he had witnessed at the Assizes—a man who had begun as a prosperous farmer but fell to the age’s vices. The clergyman did not rely on Calvinistic scripture; he galloped the stolen horse through his narrative, acted out the whispers at the gallows-cart, and dropped his handkerchief upon the clerk’s head to mimic the executioner’s drop. It was a dizzying piece of auditory pollution, meant to entertain rather than spiritually nourish, though the gentlefolks were highly enlivened by the sensory shock of it.

After the service, the congregation emptied into the sunshine. Mr. Sampson shed his vestments and joined the gentlemen in the gallery. Lord Castlewood presented his Virginian cousin, and immediately the acoustic space was filled with the discordant clamor of wagers. 

“Tell us about those dogs!” William Esmond demanded of the parson, moving swiftly to horses. The Six Year Old Plate at Huntingdon was the topic of the hour.

“The odds are five to four on Brilliant against the field,” the parson said gravely, “but, mind you, Jason is a good horse. Duke of Ancaster’s. By Cartouche out of Miss Langley.”

“I’ll take Brilliant even against the field, for ponies with you, cousin!” shouted William. 

“I’ll give or take three to one against Jason!” added the clergyman. 

Harry, whose ears were still attuned to the agrarian rhythms of his homeland, found this dominant lexicon of gambling jarring. Yet, unwilling to let these Englishmen believe he feared them, and eager to defend the honor of the Old Dominion, he accepted the wagers—matching William’s uneven odds and taking Mr. Sampson for thirty to ten. “Done and Done,” Harry said, though the words tasted metallic and hollow in his mouth.

With the pecuniary transactions settled, William scowled off toward the stables, and the parson departed to pay court to the ladies. Lord Castlewood remained behind with Harry. Since the Virginian’s arrival, the older man had been remarkably silent, a void in the social soundscape. 

“I suppose yonder property of yours is a fine one by this time?” Lord Castlewood asked.

“I reckon it’s almost as big as an English county,” Harry answered, “and the land’s as good, too, for many things.” He would not have the multi-generational agrarian legacy of his grandfather underrated. 

“Indeed!” said my lord. “When it belonged to my father it did not yield much.”

“Pardon me, my lord. You know how it belonged to your father,” cried the youth. “It was because my grandfather did not choose to claim his right.” 

“Of course, of course,” Castlewood said hastily.

Harry leaned against the billiard table, feeling the deep, untranslatable chasm between his Virginian worldview and the cold stone of Castlewood. “Your welcome, you see, is different to our welcome. At home, our traditional agrarian elders understand that hospitality is a sacred trust. When we receive a man, we share our best—we recognize that sustenance holds profound meaning. Here, you take us in, you give us beef and claret enough, to be sure, but you view the meal solely as utilitarian fuel to keep the bodies upright for the card tables. You don't seem to care when we come, or when we go. And you try to get the better of your own blood in a bet.”

Lord Castlewood was greatly entertained, offering his hand and a fair warning about his brother William’s predatory nature at the card table. The nobleman probed gently at the vast revenues of the Virginian estates, calculating the wealth of Harry's mother, until the dinner-bell rang—a sharp, metallic strike that disrupted their conversation.

In the eating-room, the Castlewood kitchens had produced a masterpiece of culinary heritage, though it was utterly wasted on the present company. A joint of heritage mutton had been slow-roasted over ancient applewood, its exterior a meticulously charred crust of crushed mustard seed and dried rosemary, resting in a shallow pool of its own rendered fat deglazed with a sharp, wild-fermented plum wine. Harry, who possessed the soul of a meticulous food historian, paused to fully map the olfactory landscape before him. He tasted the deep, earthy umami of the soil from which the root vegetables had been pulled, marveling at the passion of the unseen cooks who had crafted this symphony of flavors. 

Yet his hosts showed no sensory appreciation. They consumed the exquisite roast as mere fodder. Worse, the meal’s aural harmony was shattered by Parson Sampson, who filled the air with the latest, loudest gossip from the Mayfair coteries. He rattled off who had run away with whom, the King’s quarrels with Madame Walmoden, and the wild bets of March and Rockingham. It was a barrage of social noise pollution, a cascade of plain terms and lively jokes that made the ladies laugh but left Harry staring, dismayed by the sheer volume of superficial chatter that drowned out the profound, silent history on their plates. 

As soon as the afternoon chapel bell clinked—summoning the parson away for a brief, sermon-less service—William called for a fresh bottle of Burgundy. The chaplain tossed off a glass, ran out, and returned half an hour later bawling for another. Once the wine was mindlessly downed, the family set out the card tables.

The Sabbath evening was beautiful. Outside, the natural world offered a restorative acoustic ecology: the wind whispering through the heavy summer elms, the soothing, cyclical evensong of the rooks returning to their nests. But the company voted to sit indoors, firmly rejecting the loveliest scenes of nature in favor of the harsh, percussive snapping of pasteboard. They were blind to the sunset and deaf to the evening choruses, preferring the sight of three honors in their hand. The chapel clock tolled hour after hour unheeded, its heavy, time-marking resonance swallowed by the relentless shuffle and deal. 

At nine o'clock, the groom of the chambers announced supper. They sat down to a spread of cold, salt-cured meats and sharply aged cheeses—a testament to ancient preservation techniques that Harry admired—when the postboy’s twanging horn violently pierced the night, an industrial blast tearing through the village's quiet. 

Lord Castlewood’s letter-bag was brought in. He smiled as he read a paragraph from the newspaper, handing it to William, who by now was thoroughly flushed with liquor and good luck at the tables. 

William read the sentence and uttered a dreadful oath that jarred the room’s remaining peace. 

“Mercy, child, why do you swear so dreadfully?” asked his fond mamma.

“Read it, Parson!” William snarled, thrusting the paper over.

“Bit, by the Lord!” roared the chaplain, dashing down the sheet. 

“Cousin Harry, you are in luck,” Lord Castlewood said. “The Six Year Old Plate at Huntingdon was won by Jason, beating Brilliant, Pytho, and Ginger.”

“I owe you a half-year’s income of my poor living, Mr. Warrington,” groaned the parson. 

“A curse upon the luck!” growled William, seeking consolation by pouring another great bumper of wine, completely ignoring its complex, velvet finish. “That comes of betting on a Sunday.”

“Nay, cousin Will. It was but in jest,” cried Harry, sensing the subtle power dynamics shifting and feeling no joy in dominating through their own cold lexicon of wagers. “I can’t think of taking my cousin’s money.”

“Curse me, sir, do you suppose, if I lose, I can’t pay?” William snapped, his pride wounded. “And that I want to be beholden to any man alive? That is a good joke. Isn't it, Parson?”

“I think I have heard better,” the clergyman replied bitterly. 

“Hang it,” William demanded, “let us have another bowl.” And so they continued, deaf to the rhythms of the earth, masking their hollow domestic routines with an ever-increasing volume of liquor and noise.
