All that afternoon we held to the great road, which ran steadily north-west as though it knew its own mind better than any traveller could. Infadoos and Scragga walked beside us, their followers pacing a good hundred steps ahead, their feet keeping time with the road’s hard, ancient skin. Even before I spoke, I could hear the country’s voice changing—wind combing through grass on the slopes, the far-away chuckle of hidden water, the soft hiss of sandals on packed earth—sounds so clean and various that the mind felt rinsed of its old noises.

“Infadoos,” I said at last, “who made this road?”

He glanced down at it with a kind of wary respect, as a man might look at a hearth-stone that belonged to his grandfather’s grandfather. “It was made, my lord, of old time—none know how or when, not even Gagool, the wise woman, who has lived for generations. We are not old enough to remember its making. None can fashion such roads now, but the king suffers no grass to grow upon it.”

The road did indeed look as if it had been swept and tended like a threshing-floor—no tuft, no creeping green permitted to forget the command laid upon it. The very discipline of the place seemed to travel up through our soles.

“And the writings on the cave walls we passed,” I went on—those Egyptian-like carvings that had stared at us with stony eyes—“whose hand cut them?”

“The hands that made the road wrote the wonderful writings. We know not who wrote them.”

There was something in that answer that sat oddly in the mouth, as though the sentence had been repeated for generations until the meaning had worn smooth. A people can inherit a phrase the way they inherit a seed, and yet forget the taste of the fruit.

“When did the Kukuana people come into this country?”

Infadoos lifted his spear and pointed northward. “My lord, the race came down here like the breath of a storm ten thousand thousand moons ago, from the great lands which lie there beyond. They could travel no further because of the high mountains which ring in the land, so say the old voices of our fathers that have descended to us the children, and so says Gagool, the wise woman, the smeller out of witches.” His spear moved again, indicating the ring of snow-clad peaks.

“The country was good. So they settled here and grew strong and powerful, and now our numbers are like the sea sand, and when Twala the king calls up his regiments their plumes cover the plain so far as the eye of man can reach.”

“And if mountains wall you in,” I asked, “who is there for those regiments to fight?”

“Nay, my lord, the country is open there towards the north, and now and again warriors sweep down upon us in clouds from a land we know not, and we slay them. It is the third part of the life of a man since there was a war. Many thousands died in it, but we destroyed those who came to eat us up. So since then there has been no war.”

“Your warriors must grow weary of resting on their spears.”

“My lord, there was one war just after we destroyed the people that came down upon us, but it was a civil war—dog ate dog.”

“How was that?”

Infadoos’ voice lowered. The road carried sound well; it seemed wrong to spill this story into it, as though one were dropping blood into a spring.

“My lord the king, my half-brother, had a brother born at the same birth, and of the same woman. It is not our custom, my lord, to suffer twins to live; the weaker must always die. But the mother of the king hid away the feebler child, which was born the last, for her heart yearned over it, and that child is Twala the king. I am his younger brother, born of another wife.”

“Well?”

“My lord, Kafa, our father, died when we came to manhood, and my brother Imotu was made king in his place, and for a space reigned and had a son by his favourite wife. When the babe was three years old, just after the great war, during which no man could sow or reap, a famine came upon the land.”

As he spoke I could almost feel the tightness of empty bellies, the way hunger makes men listen only to the loudest voice and forget the older, quieter truths. In such seasons the soundscape itself turns harsh—children crying thinly, pots knocked too hard, the brittle quarrel of dry sticks.

“The people murmured because of the famine, and looked round like a starved lion for something to rend. Then it was that Gagool, the wise and terrible woman, who does not die, made proclamation, saying, ‘The king Imotu is no king.’ And at the time Imotu was sick with a wound, and lay in his kraal not able to move.

“Then Gagool went into a hut and led out Twala, my half-brother, and twin brother to the king, whom she had hidden among the caves and rocks since he was born, and stripping the moocha from his loins, showed the people the mark of the sacred snake coiled round his middle, wherewith the eldest son of the king is marked at birth, and cried aloud, ‘Behold your king whom I have saved for you even to this day!’”

Infadoos’ eyes were fixed ahead, as though he still saw that moment, the crowd surging like wind-driven grass.

“Now the people being mad with hunger, and bereft of reason and the knowledge of truth, cried out—‘The king! The king!’ But I knew that it was not so, for Imotu my brother was the elder of the twins, and our lawful king.

“Then just as the tumult was at its height Imotu the king, though he was very sick, crawled from his hut holding his wife by the hand, and followed by his little son Ignosi—that is, by interpretation, the Lightning.

“‘What is this noise?’ he asked. ‘Why cry ye The king! The king?’

“Then Twala, his twin brother, born of the same woman and in the same hour, ran to him, and taking him by the hair, stabbed him through the heart with his knife. And the people, being fickle and ever ready to worship the rising sun, clapped their hands and cried, ‘Twala is king! Now we know that Twala is king!’”

“And the queen?” I asked. “And the child Ignosi? Did Twala kill them too?”

“Nay, my lord. When she saw that her lord was dead the queen seized the child with a cry and ran away. Two days afterward she came to a kraal very hungry, and none would give her milk or food, now that her lord the king was dead, for all men hate the unfortunate.”

At the mention of milk and refusal, something in me recoiled. A land might be beautiful as a garden, but if a woman cannot be given a calabash when her child’s mouth is dry, then the garden has a rot at its roots. To deny food is a form of speech too—a brutal vocabulary that says, You are no longer one of us.

“But at nightfall,” Infadoos continued, “a little child, a girl, crept out and brought her corn to eat, and she blessed the child, and went on towards the mountains with her boy before the sun rose again. And there she must have perished, for none have seen her since, nor the child Ignosi.”

“Then if Ignosi lived,” I said slowly, “he would be the true king of the Kukuanas.”

“That is so, my lord; the sacred snake is round his middle. If he lives he is king; but, alas! he is long dead.”

As he finished, he pointed down to the plain beneath us. There, set like an island on the open land, lay a vast collection of huts, fenced, and beyond that fence a great ditch running like a dark collar around it.

“See, my lord. That is the kraal where the wife of Imotu was last seen with the child Ignosi. It is there we shall sleep to-night, if, indeed,” he added, his doubt returning like a chill under warmth, “my lords sleep at all upon this earth.”

“When we are among the Kukuanas,” I said, putting on as much grandeur as I could manage for the benefit of our audience, “we do as the Kukuanas do.”

I turned quickly to address Good, who was tramping behind in a sulk, entirely occupied with the hopeless business of keeping his flannel shirt from flapping in the evening breeze. Instead I walked squarely into Umbopa, who had been immediately behind me, and very clearly listening. His face had the strained look of a man trying to catch a half-forgotten tune—he had the air of one who hears a name in a crowd and cannot at once remember whose voice it belongs to. The story of Twala and Ignosi had lodged in him like a thorn.

We pressed on at a good rate toward the kraal. The mountains we had crossed now rose behind us like great shoulders, and Sheba’s Breasts, those twin domes of snow, were veiled modestly in thin wreaths of mist. The land grew lovelier with each mile: vegetation luxuriant without being tropical, sun bright and warm without burning, and a gracious breeze moving softly along the odorous slopes. Every scent seemed distinct—crushed herbs underfoot, sun-warmed earth, the faint sweetness of flowering shrubs—carried on air that sounded clean when it moved, not clogged with the mechanical roar that follows men in their modern works. Here even silence had texture; it was not dead, but full of small living noises that made one feel held rather than alone.

Soon after we started, Infadoos had sent a runner ahead to warn the kraal of our arrival. The fellow had departed at such speed that he seemed to pour himself along the road. Running, Infadoos told me, was much practised among his people, and from the way the air rang with the patter of feet in the regiment behind, I could well believe it.

The result of the message showed itself when we were still two miles out. Company after company of men issued from the gates and marched toward us in ordered lines.

Sir Henry laid a hand on my arm. “It looks as though we are going to meet with a warm reception,” he said.

Something in his tone caught Infadoos’ attention. “Let not my lords be afraid,” he said quickly. “For in my breast there dwells no guile. This regiment is one under my command, and comes out by my orders to greet you.”

I nodded with what I hoped was easy majesty, though my inside did not match it. A greeting of three thousand armed men is the sort that asks a man to trust his hosts more than his own nerves.

About half a mile from the gates a long stretch of rising ground sloped gently up from the road, and there the companies formed. It was a splendid sight: each company, about three hundred strong, charging swiftly up the rise with flashing spears and waving plumes, and then snapping into stillness as if carved from metal. By the time we reached the slope, twelve such companies—three thousand six hundred men—had taken position along the road.

When we came opposite the first company we could examine them properly, and I confess I stared like a child at a fair. They were magnificent—men of mature age, mostly veterans of about forty, not one under six feet, many six feet three or four. On their heads were heavy black plumes of Sakaboola feathers like those worn by our guides. About their waists and beneath the right knee were circlets of white ox-tails; their left hands held round shields some twenty inches across.

These shields were curious things: an iron plate beaten thin for the framework, over it stretched milk-white ox-hide, tight as a drum-skin. Each man bore a short, very heavy two-edged spear with a wooden shaft, the blade broad—about six inches across—made for stabbing in close quarters, not throwing. Along with this bangwan each carried three great knives, tollas, each about two pounds in weight: one in the ox-tail girdle, two fixed behind the shield. They cast these tollas with accuracy up to fifty yards, and it was their custom, on charging, to hurl a volley as they closed.

Each company stood immovable till we were abreast. Then, at a signal from its officer—distinguished by a leopard-skin cloak, standing some paces in front—every spear lifted as one, and from three hundred throats came the royal salute, sudden and deep: “Koom!”

It struck the air like a wave breaking. The cry rolled along the line in measured shocks, and for all its menace it was strangely beautiful—human voices used like an instrument, disciplined, communal. I could not help thinking how such a sound, repeated year on year, becomes part of a nation’s memory—more durable than carvings on cave walls, unless some new noise comes to drown it out.

As soon as we had passed, each company fell in behind us, until at last the whole regiment of the “Greys”—so called from their white shields, the crack corps of the Kukuanas—marched at our rear with a tread that shook the ground.

At length we branched from Solomon’s Great Road and came to the wide fosse that encircled the kraal, at least a mile round, fenced with a strong palisade of tree-trunks. At the gateway the fosse was spanned by a rough drawbridge, which the guard let down to admit us.

Inside, the kraal was laid out with notable sense. A wide pathway ran through the centre, crossed at right angles by other paths, dividing the huts into square blocks—the quarters of each company. The huts were dome-shaped, built like Zulu huts of wattle framework beautifully thatched with grass, but larger, and with doorways tall enough for a man to walk through. Each had a verandah about six feet wide, paved with powdered lime trodden hard until it shone pale and clean.

Along each side of the central pathway stood hundreds of women, drawn out by curiosity to look at us. They were, for a native race, exceedingly handsome—tall, graceful, wonderfully made. Their hair, though short, was more curly than woolly; the features were often aquiline; the lips not unpleasantly thick. Most striking was their quiet, dignified air. They watched as though watching were an art, not a commotion. Not a rude jest, not a shouted remark, not the savage chorus one hears in other places. Their silence had a weight, and in that hush you could hear small domestic sounds leaking from behind the huts—the clack of a pot-lid, a baby’s breathy complaint, the dry rasp of a broom on hard earth.

Even when old Infadoos, with a sly motion, directed their attention to the crowning marvel of poor Good’s “beautiful white legs,” they did not erupt. Their dark eyes fixed upon that new, snowy whiteness—Good’s skin being as fair as milk—then they let their faces stay still, as though admiration were something one kept inside like warm porridge. Good, modest by nature, found the scrutiny quite enough, and coloured beneath it as much as he could.

At the centre of the kraal, Infadoos halted at the doorway of a large hut, around which at a little distance stood a circle of smaller huts.

“Enter, Sons of the Stars,” he said in his magniloquent manner, “and deign to rest awhile in our humble habitations. A little food shall be brought to you, so that ye may have no need to draw your belts tight from hunger; some honey and some milk, and an ox or two, and a few sheep; not much, my lords, but still a little food.”

“It is good,” said I. “Infadoos, we are weary with travelling through realms of air; now let us rest.”

We entered and found it prepared for comfort: couches of tanned skins laid out, water ready for washing. The air inside held the clean animal smell of hide, smoke clinging to the thatch, and the faint sharpness of lime from the verandah. After a while shouting rose outside, and when I stepped to the doorway I saw a line of damsels coming in with offerings—milk in calabashes, roasted mealies, and honey in a pot that glowed amber when the light struck it. Behind them came youths driving a fat young ox, its hide glossy and its breath steaming softly in the cooler air.

We received the gifts. Then one of the young men drew the knife from his girdle and, with practised economy, cut the ox’s throat. In ten minutes it was dead, skinned, and jointed—work done with the quick surety that belongs to people who do such things not as spectacle but as part of life. The best meat was cut off for us, and the rest, in our name, I presented to the warriors about us, who took it and distributed the “white lords’ gift” among themselves.

Umbopa set to work at once, taking charge as though the pot and fire were his birthright. With the help of an extremely pleasing young woman he set our portion to boil in a large earthenware pot over a fire built outside the hut. He judged the heat by ear as much as by eye—the change from lively bubbling to a steadier simmer, the soft tapping of bone against clay when the pot was stirred. The smell of meat rising in steam mingled with smoke and the sweet edge of mealies; it made the kraal feel suddenly less like a parade-ground and more like a home.

When it was nearly ready we sent to Infadoos and asked him and Scragga to join us. They came presently and sat on little stools—there were several about the hut, for the Kukuanas do not, as a rule, squat on their haunches like the Zulus—and helped us through our dinner.

Infadoos was all affability and ceremony. Scragga, the king’s son, ate and watched. It struck me that his doubt grew with every ordinary act. The first shock of our whiteness and our “magic” had overawed him; but now that he saw we chewed meat, drank milk, and wiped grease from our fingers like other men, the awe began to wear thin, and something sullen and suspicious showed beneath it. That change made me uneasy. It is one thing to be feared as a wonder; it is another to be measured as a rival.

During the meal Sir Henry suggested to me, in an undertone, that we might try to find out whether our hosts knew anything of his brother’s fate, whether they had ever seen or heard of him. I thought it wiser to say nothing yet. It is hard to explain a missing relative when one has been introduced as a thing that fell from the sky.

After supper we produced our pipes and lit them. This simple act astonished Infadoos and Scragga more than our rifles had done. The Kukuanas grow tobacco widely, but use it only for snuff, and could not at first recognise the leaf in this new shape, nor understand how a man could willingly swallow scented smoke and then sit contented as though he had eaten.

I asked Infadoos when we were to proceed on our journey, and was pleased to hear that preparations had already been made for us to leave in the morning. Messengers had gone to inform Twala of our coming. The king, it seemed, was at his chief place, Loo, making ready for the great annual feast held in the first week of June. At that gathering all regiments—save certain detachments left for garrison duty—are brought up and paraded before the king, and the great annual witch-hunt is held.

We were to start at dawn. Infadoos, who would accompany us, expected we should reach Loo on the night of the second day unless accident delayed us or rivers ran swollen.

When they had given us this information, our visitors bade us good-night. We arranged our watch, turn and turn about. Then three of us flung ourselves down on the skins and slept the sweet, heavy sleep of the weary, while the fourth sat up listening—not only for footsteps that should not be there, but for the kraal’s own night-music: distant murmurs, a baby’s brief cry, the crack of a dying fire, and beyond the palisade the steady breathing of the plain.
