Mrs. Jennings was effusive in her admiration of Edward’s behavior, but only Elinor and Marianne truly grasped the depth of his sacrifice. They alone understood how little temptation he had faced to stray from duty, and how meager the comfort he could find—beyond the quiet pride of doing what was right—in losing both fortune and friends. Elinor took pride in his steadfastness; Marianne, though once so quick to judge, now softened toward him, moved by pity for the pain he endured. Their bond, once strained by silence and suspicion, was now mended, yet neither sister found it easy to speak of what had passed when they were alone.

Elinor avoided the subject deliberately—fearing that Marianne’s fervent assurances of Edward’s enduring love might only deepen her own lingering hope, a feeling she wished to suppress. For Marianne, conversation on the matter brought only sorrow. She could not help comparing her own impulsive heart to Elinor’s measured restraint, and each time, she felt the sting of regret more keenly. She saw clearly how much better her sister had handled their shared trial, but instead of inspiring resolve, this awareness only deepened her sense of failure. She mourned not for what might be, but for what she had not done—her courage spent long ago, leaving behind only guilt and despair.

For several days, no new news reached them from Harley Street or Bartlett’s Buildings. Yet Mrs. Jennings, ever eager to extend her kindness, had made up her mind to visit her cousins as soon as possible. Only an unusual influx of guests had delayed her journey. On the third day after the truth came to light, a fine March Sunday drew many to Kensington Gardens. Mrs. Jennings and Elinor went together, but Marianne stayed behind—still afraid of encountering the Willoughbys, whose presence stirred old wounds she had not yet healed.

Soon after entering the gardens, they met a close friend of Mrs. Jennings’s, who joined them readily. Her lively chatter absorbed Mrs. Jennings’s attention, giving Elinor a rare moment of peace to reflect. She saw neither Edward nor the Willoughbys, and for a while, nothing stirred her thoughts. Then, to her surprise, Miss Steele approached them with a bright smile.

“I’m so delighted to see you!” she declared, linking arms with Elinor. “I’ve been longing to tell you something.” Lowering her voice, she added, “I suppose Mrs. Jennings has heard all about it. Is she angry?”

“Not at all,” Elinor replied gently. “I don’t believe she is.”

“Oh, thank goodness! And Lady Middleton—she isn’t cross, is she?”

“I can’t imagine why she would be.”

Miss Steele breathed a sigh of relief. “I’ve had such a dreadful time! Lucy was furious—vowed she’d never make me another bonnet, or do anything for me again. But now she’s calmed down, and we’re as close as ever. Look—she made this bow for my hat last night and tucked in the feather herself. You’ll laugh at me, I know. But why shouldn’t I wear pink ribbons? It’s the Doctor’s favorite color, after all. Honestly, I didn’t even realize he preferred it until he mentioned it. My cousins have been driving me mad—sometimes I don’t know where to look!”

She drifted into a tangent, and Elinor, sensing the need to steer the conversation back, said quietly, “Well then, Miss Dashwood—people may say what they like about Mr. Ferrars declaring he wouldn’t marry Lucy, but it’s simply not true. It’s shameful that such rumors should spread.”

“I’ve never heard a word like it,” Elinor assured her.

“Oh, but it *was* said—by more than one person. Miss Godby told Miss Sparks that no sensible person could expect Edward to give up Miss Morton, with her thirty thousand pounds, for Lucy, who has nothing. And Miss Sparks told me. Besides, my cousin Richard said himself that when it came down to it, he feared Edward would break off the engagement. When Edward didn’t come near us for three whole days, I was beside myself. I think Lucy gave up all hope—after Wednesday, we saw nothing of him until Saturday. Once she thought of writing to him, but then her spirits lifted. But this morning, just as we returned from church, he arrived. That’s when everything came out. He’d been summoned to Harley Street, spoken to by his mother and family, and before them all, he declared he loved only Lucy, and would marry no one else. The talk upset him terribly, and as soon as he left, he rode into the country, staying at an inn Thursday and Friday to collect himself. After thinking it through, he realized that now he had no fortune, no prospects—only two thousand pounds, and no hope of more—he couldn’t ask Lucy to wait. If he entered the clergy, he’d likely get only a curacy. How could they live on that? He couldn’t bear the idea of her suffering. So he begged her—if she had any wish to end it—to do so at once, and let him fend for himself. I heard every word. And he said it all for *her* sake, not his own. I swear he never hinted at being tired of her, or wanting to marry Miss Morton. Not a word.”

“But Lucy wouldn’t listen,” Miss Steele continued. “She told him immediately—oh, with all the sweet talk and lovey-dovey nonsense you know—she had no desire to break off. She said she could live on very little, and however small his income, she’d be happy to share it all. And so he was overjoyed. They talked about what to do next, and decided he should enter the clergy right away. They’ll wait to marry until he secures a living. But then—I couldn’t hear anymore, because my cousin called from below: Mrs. Richardson had arrived in her coach and wanted one of us to join her. I ran upstairs, put on a pair of silk stockings, and hurried off with them.”

“I don’t understand,” Elinor said, startled. “You mean you interrupted them? You were all in the same room?”

“Oh, heavens no!” Miss Steele laughed. “Do you really think people confess their feelings when others are around? No indeed. They were shut up in the drawing-room, and I only heard bits by standing at the door.”

“You listened at the door?” Elinor exclaimed. “And now you’ve repeated it to me? I wish I’d known—because I certainly wouldn’t have allowed you to tell me details of a conversation you had no right to overhear. How could you treat your sister so unfairly?”

“Oh, dear,” Miss Steele giggled. “It’s nothing. I just stood outside and listened. And Lucy would have done the same for me—remember Martha Sharpe and I used to hide in closets to spy on each other? That’s just how things are.”

Elinor tried to change the subject, but Miss Steele quickly returned to Edward. “He talks of going to Oxford soon,” she said. “But now he’s lodging in Pall Mall. What a horrid woman his mother is, isn’t she? And your brother and sister weren’t very kind either. Still, I won’t say anything against them to you—they did send us home in their carriage, which was more than I expected. And I was terrified your sister would ask for the huswifes she gave us a few days ago. But nothing was said, and I made sure to keep mine hidden.”

Edward, she explained, had business at Oxford and must go there for a time. Afterward, he hoped to find a bishop willing to ordain him. “I wonder what curacy he’ll get!” she giggled. “I know exactly what my cousins will say when they hear—‘Why don’t you write to the Doctor and get Edward the new living?’ But I’d never do such a thing. ‘La!’ I’ll say, ‘How could you think of such a thing? Me write to the Doctor?’”

“Well,” Elinor said dryly, “at least you’re prepared for the worst.”

Miss Steele was about to respond when her party approached. “Oh, here come the Richardsons! I’ve so much more to tell you, but I can’t stay. They’re very respectable people—Mr. Richardson makes a fortune, and they keep their own coach. I don’t have time to speak to Mrs. Jennings, but please tell her I’m delighted she’s not angry with us, and that Lady Middleton feels the same. If you and your sister ever leave town and Mrs. Jennings needs company, we’d be honored to stay with her as long as she likes. I suppose Lady Middleton won’t invite us again this season. Goodbye—I’m sorry Miss Marianne wasn’t here. Do remember me kindly to her. La! You’re not wearing your spotted muslin—are you afraid it’ll tear?”

That was her final concern. She hurried off to greet Mrs. Richardson, leaving Elinor alone with a heart full of reflection. She had learned nothing new—only confirmation of what she already suspected. Edward’s marriage to Lucy remained certain, though the timing hung entirely on his securing a living—a goal that seemed distant indeed. Everything rested on a future uncertain, just as Elinor had foreseen.

As they returned to the carriage, Mrs. Jennings demanded updates. But Elinor, unwilling to spread gossip gained so unfairly, limited her account to what Lucy would likely want known—the continuation of their engagement and their plans to wait for a living. This prompted Mrs. Jennings to remark, “Wait for a living? We all know how that ends. They’ll wait a year, find nothing, and settle for a fifty-pound curacy, plus interest on his two thousand pounds and whatever Mr. Steele and Mr. Pratt can spare. Then they’ll have a child every year! Lord help them—how poor they’ll be! I must see what I can do to help furnish their house. Two maids and two men? No, no—they’ll need a strong girl who can do everything. Betty’s sister would never suit them now.”

The next morning, Elinor received a letter from Lucy, delivered by the two-penny post:

“My dear Miss Dashwood, I hope you’ll forgive me for writing to you, but I know your kindness toward me will make you pleased to hear how well Edward and I are, after all our trials. Thank God, though we’ve suffered greatly, we are both well and as happy as we shall always be in each other’s love. We’ve faced great hardships and cruel persecution, but we’re grateful for the friends we’ve kept—yourself among them. Your kindness will always be remembered by me and by Edward, whom I’ve told of it.

I’m sure you’ll be glad to hear that I spent two lovely hours with Edward yesterday. He refused to part with me, though I urged him to for prudence’s sake. I insisted he let me go, believing it my duty—but he would not hear of it. He said he cared nothing for his mother’s anger as long as he had my love. Our prospects aren’t bright, to be sure, but we’ll wait and hope. He’ll be ordained soon, and if you ever have the chance to recommend him to anyone with a living to offer, I’m certain you won’t forget us. Dear Mrs. Jennings too—I trust she’ll speak for us to Sir John or Mr. Palmer, or any friend who might help.

Poor Anne was wrong to act as she did, but she meant well, so I say nothing. I hope Mrs. Jennings won’t mind if she comes to call—what a kindness it would be! My cousins would be proud to meet her.

My paper reminds me to conclude. Please convey my deepest gratitude and respect to Mrs. Jennings, Sir John, Lady Middleton, and the dear children when you see them. And send my love to Miss Marianne.

I am, &c.”

Elinor read it once, then handed it to Mrs. Jennings, knowing it was meant for her eyes. Mrs. Jennings read aloud with warm approval.

“Very well indeed! How nicely she writes! Oh, yes—that was quite proper of her to let him go if he wanted to. Just like Lucy. Poor soul! I wish I could get him a living, with all my heart. She calls me ‘dear Mrs. Jennings’—see? She’s a good-hearted girl. Very well, indeed. That sentence is beautifully turned. Yes, yes—I’ll certainly go to see her. How thoughtful she is, remembering everyone! Thank you, my dear, for showing it to me. It’s the prettiest letter I’ve ever seen—and does Lucy’s heart and head great credit.”
