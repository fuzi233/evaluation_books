“Eli,” Catherine said, her voice steady yet soft, “answer me one question like a man, and I’ll ask no more this day. What is wormwood?”

Eli blinked, caught off guard by the suddenness of it. For a moment he looked as though he’d been asked to solve a riddle in Latin. Then he rallied: “It’s something that tastes mighty bitter.”

“That’s fair enough,” she replied, “but not what I mean.”

“Then let me have the answer myself,” he muttered.

“And I shall give it,” she said. “Wormwood is—having two idle souls under your roof, doing nothing but waiting for your shoes and mine.”

The words struck him like a stone. He sank back into his chair, eyes downcast. “Aye,” he murmured, “and worse than that—waiting for their best friend’s coffin, with nothing to do but mourn. Is it not enough to drive them mad? Why not set them up somewhere? Give them a chance?”

“I’m willing,” Eli answered slowly, “but I fear they’d drink and gamble away every shelf in the shop.”

“Nay,” Catherine said firmly, “do you take me for a fool? Of course I mean to watch them at first—keep a loose rein upon them, as the saying goes.”

“And where would you send them?” he asked.

“Not here,” she said quickly. “That would divide our own trade. No—Rotterdam against the world. There I could start them.”

Ah, self-deception! The real reason behind all this was not ambition or charity, but love—the longing to be near little Gerard.

After much talk and many promises from Cornelis and Sybrandt—promises made with fervor, if not full sincerity—Catherine set out for Rotterdam, taking Kate with her for company and change. They searched the streets until they found a shop in a good quarter, though it was in poor repair. But its disrepair worked in their favor; they secured it at a bargain price and began at once to clean, rebuild, and make it fit for business. Shelves were fitted, the living quarters tidied, and hope stirred anew.

Meanwhile, Luke Peterson never ceased asking Margaret what he might do for her. Her reply, always the same, came in a quiet, weary tone: “Nothing, Luke. Nothing.”

“What?” he would protest. “You who are so clever—can you truly think of nothing I might do for you?”

“Nothing, Luke,” she would repeat. “Nothing.”

But one evening, she changed her tune. “If only,” she said softly, “you could make something to help my sweet sister Kate.”

Luke, ever the slave to love, leapt at the chance. In short order, he built a small cart, lined it with cushions, and yoked himself to it. At dusk, he drew Kate through the town, along the broad, shaded boulevard, while Margaret and Catherine walked beside. It was a scene of gentle joy—but appearances deceived.

Kate, for her part, cherished every moment. Little Gerard sat nestled in her lap, his tiny hands clutching her dress, and she adored him with all her heart. To see him there was like watching a cherub borne by an angel, or a rosebud cradled in the cup of a lily. Such beauty drew the scorn of the vulgar. They jeered, calling Luke a thistle, mocking his lack of four legs, asking if his mistress couldn’t afford better.

Luke paid them no mind. But Kate did. “Thou hast made the cart for me, good Luke,” she said, her voice low. “That was kind. But I did wrong to let thee draw me too. We can afford to pay someone else. I love these rides, and carrying little Gerard—I do. But I’d rather not ride again than see thee mocked.”

“Much I care for their tongues,” Luke replied. “If I cared, I’d break their heads together. I’ll draw you till my mistress says stop.”

“Luke,” Catherine said gently, “if you obey Kate, you will please me.”

“And so I shall,” Luke answered. “I will obey Kate.”

One woman stood apart from the laughter—Jorian Ketel’s wife. She watched the procession and said quietly, “That is strength well used—to bear the weak. And her prayers will be your reward. She is not long for this world; she smiles even in pain.”

Joan spoke true. But Luke, simple-hearted, shook his head. “I want no prayers from the poor lass,” he said. “I do it for my mistress, Margaret.”

From then on, Luke pressed Margaret often for some task, any task. But she refused each time. Until one day, worn down by his persistence, she turned to him suddenly, her face pale, her voice sharp with meaning she could not hide:

“Find me my boy’s father.”
