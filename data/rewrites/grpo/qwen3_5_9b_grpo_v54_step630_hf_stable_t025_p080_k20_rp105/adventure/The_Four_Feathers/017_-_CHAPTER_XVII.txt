Mrs. Adair possessed a mind that was as winding as a labyrinth, preferring to peer through a keyhole rather than walk boldly through a door. To her, truth earned through subtle maneuvering held a far greater value than facts handed over freely by a simple question. She avoided direct inquiries on a twisted principle, finding a day well-spent only if she could coax a trivial piece of news from a companion's lips, something a straightforward request would have secured instantly.

Thus, though she was deeply baffled by the significance Ethne placed on a single white feather, and puzzled by the good tidings regarding Harry Feversham that Captain Willoughby had delivered, Mrs. Adair betrayed not a flicker of confusion during lunch. Instead, she offered her guest conversation on indifferent subjects, attempting to mask her own intrigue with the guise of polite company.

"I don't believe you hear a single word I am saying!" she exclaimed eventually, her patience fraying.

Ethne laughed, admitting her guilt, and retreated to her room immediately after the meal. There, in the solitude of the afternoon, she replayed Willoughby's story in her mind. Her heart swelled with a rhythm like divine music. The regret that Harry had not returned a year prior, when she was free to receive him, paled in comparison to the sheer joy of the revelation itself. That joy had erased the sting of his past rejection at Lennon House. Her pride, shattered once before, was now fully restored—not by a stranger, but by the very man who had wounded her. She felt a new vitality coursing through her veins, a return of her youth under the August sun.

From her dressing-case, she retrieved the only portrait of Harry Feversham she had kept. It was an image of someone dead to her in terms of romance, yet alive as a friend. She rejoiced in keeping it, for Harry had required no feigned loyalty or encouragement from her to sustain his high ideals. Placing the white feather beside the portrait, she locked them together in the drawer, sealing her renewed hope.

Returning to the window, she watched the shadows dance on the lawn as the breeze stirred the trees. Yet, her heart yearned for Donegal. It was August; the heather would soon bloom on the hillsides. She wished the news had arrived there, amidst the brown mountains and granite outcrops. Great sorrows and great joys alike pulled her homeward, for there, endurance was easier and gladness more complete.

Her only living tether to that land was Dermod's old collie, now her inseparable companion. To the dog, she poured her confidences; if her voice broke into tears, the animal would remain silent. Through him, she understood truths Willoughby had omitted and Harry had never voiced. She visualized those three years of hiding in the cramped city of Suakin, watching troops march to battle while Harry slipped away to avoid recognition by old friends like Durrance or Willoughby. She saw the panic in his eyes when he first beheld the dark walls of Berber, the night spent in ruined acres, and the desperate search for water among the shifting sands of Obak. As these images formed, she asked herself, *"Where was I then? What was I doing?"*

She sat in a golden haze until the lights shifted on the creek and the rooks wheeled noisily into the night, signaling evening.

That dinner, Ethne brought a buoyancy of spirit that surprised everyone. Mrs. Adair noted how brightly Ethne's eyes shone and how fresh her complexion appeared. While Mrs. Adair remained tormented by her inability to guess the nature of the news, she participated freely in the talk. Even Durrance seemed to shed a heavy burden; his habitual tension, the look of a man straining to hear with his eyes closed, vanished. His spirits rose to match Ethne's.

"You will play on your violin tonight, I think," he said with a smile as they rose.

"Yes," she answered, "I will—with all my heart."

Durrance laughed and held the door open. The violin had remained locked in its case for two months, treated by Durrance as a barometer of Ethne's soul. If the world was kind, the case opened; if cruel, it stayed shut to prevent old wounds from bleeding before others. Tonight, however, it was to be unlocked.

Mrs. Adair lingered until Ethne was out of earshot. "You have noticed the change in her tonight?" she whispered.

"Yes," Durrance replied. "We waited for it, hoped for it, and nearly despaired. Are you so glad of the change?"

"Do you wonder?" Mrs. Adair asked, a look of pity softening her face before she withdrew.

Durrance took the weight of Ethne's transformation entirely to himself. He followed her to the drawing-room, where she sat by the open window, tightening the strings of her violin. He took a chair behind her, sinking into the shadows.

"What shall I play to you?" she asked.

"The *Musoline Overture*," he answered. "You played it the first evening I came to Ramelton. I remember it clearly. Play it again tonight. I wish to compare."

"I have played it since."

"Never to me."

Alone in the moonlit room, Ethne extinguished the lamp. She resumed her seat while Durrance remained in the dark, leaning forward with hands on his knees. He listened with an intensity he had not shown that evening, applying what he believed was a final test to decide the course of their lives. Would her music speak only of friendship, or something deeper?

As Ethne began to play, she forgot Durrance's presence. The air outside was still and warm; the moonlight lay like a solid silver floor on the creek; the trees dreamed under the stars. The music floated across the silent lawn, and she imagined it traveling down the creek, over Salcombe Bar, and across the moonlit seas to reach a man sleeping far away beneath the southern stars, the cool desert wind blowing on his face.

*"If he could only hear!"* she thought. *"If he could only wake and know this is a message of friendship!"*

Carrying this fantasy, she played with a skill she had never before displayed, turning her violin into a voice of profound sympathy. The music became a bridge swinging through the air, allowing her and Harry to meet and shake hands for just a few minutes before parting on their separate paths. These moments would sustain them both. The chords rang out against the silence, declaring the pride she had reclaimed that day. Her fancy hardened into belief. It was no longer *"If he should hear,"* but *"He must hear!"*

Struck by this fervor, a strange hope enthralled her. *"If he could answer!"*

She lingered on the final bars, waiting for a response. When the music faded into silence, she sat with the violin on her knees, gazing eagerly across the moonlit garden.

An answer did come, but not across the lawn. It emerged from the dark shadows of the room behind her, spoken through Durrance's voice.

"Ethne, where do you think I heard that overture last played?"

Startled awake from her reverie, Ethne answered as if shaken from sleep. "Why, you told me. At Ramelton, when you first came to Lennon House."

"I have heard it since, though not played by you. It was not really played at all. But a melody of it, or rather a suggestion of a melody, stumbled out upon a zither with many false notes, by a Greek in a bare, whitewashed café lit by one glaring lamp at Wadi Halfa."

"This overture?" she said, struck by the strangeness.

"Not so strange after all. For the Greek was Harry Feversham."

The answer had arrived. Ethne knew it without doubt. She sat still in the moonlight, her eyelids closing as if in sleep. A long silence followed. She did not question why Durrance, who had kept this secret for so long, revealed it now, nor did she ask why Harry Feversham would play a zither in a mean café. It seemed to her that he had spoken to her as she had spoken to him. The music had indeed been a bridge. It was not strange that he had used Durrance's voice to deliver his message.

"When was this?" she asked finally.

"In February of this year. I will tell you about it."

"Yes, please, tell me."

And from the shadows of the room, Durrance began to speak.
