The map failed at the junction of the Aylesbury Pike, just past Dean's Corners. To take the left was to choose a geography that refused to conform to the laws of the mapped world. The road narrowed, the earth rising in a slow, suffocating ascent until the brier-laced stone walls pressed inward, closing the gap between the vehicle and the abyss of the rutted track. The forests here were not collections of timber but dense, suffocating masses; the trees grew with a grotesque largeness, their shadows swallowing the light before it could touch the ground. Weeds and brambles achieved a luxuriance that bordered on the pathological, choking the sparse, barren fields that managed to exist.

The houses were few, scattered like discarded bones, wearing a uniform expression of age and decay. They stood as monuments to a retreat from life itself. One hesitated to approach the gnarled figures perched on crumbling doorsteps or crouching in the rock-strewn meadows. Their silence was not peaceful; it was furtive, a held breath that suggested the presence of something forbidden nearby. To speak to them felt like an intrusion into a private nightmare.

As the road climbed, the mountains emerged from the deep woods, but they offered no comfort. Their summits were too perfectly rounded, too symmetrical, lacking the chaotic irregularity of nature. Against the sky, they displayed the queer, concentric circles of tall stone pillars that crowned their peaks. Gorges of impossible depth sliced the landscape, crossed by crude wooden bridges that seemed to vibrate with a latent instability. Below, the marshlands stretched out, a wet, sucking expanse that invited fear rather than exploration. At twilight, the atmosphere thickened. Unseen whippoorwills began their chatter, a rhythmic, insectoid drone that competed with the raucous, insistent piping of bullfrogs. Fireflies erupted in abnormal profusion, dancing to a rhythm that felt less like nature and more like a signal.

The Miskatonic River wound its way upward, a thin, shining line that possessed an undeniably serpentine quality as it hugged the feet of the domed hills. As proximity increased, the focus shifted from the stone crowns to the wooded slopes. These sides loomed dark and precipitous, pressing against the observer with a gravitational weight that demanded flight. Yet, there was no road leading away. Only the covered bridge remained, a tenebrous tunnel connecting the outside world to the village huddled between the stream and the vertical face of Round Mountain.

Beyond the bridge lay Dunwich. The cluster of rotting gambrel roofs spoke of an architectural era long abandoned by the rest of the region. Closer inspection revealed that most structures were deserted, collapsing into ruin. The broken steeple of the church housed the hamlet's sole mercantile establishment, a testament to a community that had ceased to function normally. A faint, malign odor clung to the village street—the scent of massed mold and the slow decomposition of centuries. Crossing the bridge felt like passing through a membrane separating sanity from madness. Relief came only when the road curved around the base of the hills, allowing the traveler to leave the valley behind and rejoin the Aylesbury Pike.

Those who returned often learned, with a jolt of recognition, that they had traversed Dunwich. Outsiders avoided it with a superstitious diligence. Since the season of horror in 1928, all signposts pointing toward the town had been removed. By any aesthetic standard, the scenery was breathtakingly beautiful; yet no artists or tourists ever visited. Two centuries prior, when superstition held sway, people gave specific reasons for their avoidance. Today, in our sensible age, the reasons are buried under the hush-up of the 1928 incident, deemed necessary for the welfare of the town and the world.

Perhaps the primary deterrent is the population. The natives are repellently decadent, having traveled far down the path of retrogression common to New England backwaters. They have evolved into a distinct race, marked by the physical and mental stigmata of degeneracy and inbreeding. Their collective intelligence is woefully low, while their history reeks of overt viciousness, half-hidden murders, incest, and acts of violence so perverse they defy naming.

Among them, the old gentry of the two or three armigerous families who arrived from Salem in 1692 maintain a precarious position above the general decay. Branches of the Whateley and Bishop lines still send their eldest sons to Harvard and Miskatonic University. However, these sons rarely return to the moldering gambrel roofs beneath which they and their ancestors were born.

No one, even those privy to the facts of the recent horror, can articulate precisely what is wrong with Dunwich. Old legends speak of unhallowed rites and Indian conclaves where forbidden shapes of shadow were summoned from the great rounded hills, accompanied by wild orgiastic prayers answered by loud crackings and rumblings from the earth. In 1747, Reverend Abijah Hoadley, newly installed at the Congregational Church, delivered a memorable sermon on the close presence of Satan and his imps. He declared that the blasphemies of an infernal train of demons were matters of too common knowledge to deny, citing the cursed voices of Azazel, Buzrael, Beelzebub, and Belial heard from underground by over a score of credible witnesses. Hoadley claimed he had caught a plain discourse of evil powers in the hill behind his house—a rattling, rolling, groaning, screeching, and hissing that no earthly thing could produce, emanating only from caves accessible solely through black magic and the devil's key.

Hoadley vanished shortly after preaching this sermon, but the text, printed in Springfield, remains extant. Noises in the hills continue to be reported annually, baffling geologists and physiographers alike. Other traditions describe foul odors near the hill-crowning stone circles and rushing airy presences heard faintly from the bottoms of great ravines. There is also the Devil's Hop Yard, a bleak, blasted hillside where no tree, shrub, or blade of grass dares to grow. Furthermore, the natives harbor a mortal terror of the numerous whippoorwills, which become vocal on warm nights. It is vowed that these birds are psychopomps, lying in wait for the souls of the dying. They time their eerie cries in unison with the sufferer's struggling breath; if they catch the fleeing soul, they flutter away in demoniac laughter, but if they fail, they sink into a disappointed silence.

These tales are obsolete, ridiculous relics of a distant past. Yet Dunwich is ridiculously old—far older than any community within thirty miles. South of the village, one may still spy the cellar walls and chimney of the ancient Bishop house, constructed before 1700. The ruins of the mill at the falls, built in 1806, stand as the most modern architecture visible. Industry never flourished here; the nineteenth-century factory movement proved short-lived. Oldest of all are the great rings of rough-hewn stone columns on the hilltops, generally attributed to the Indians rather than the settlers. Deposits of skulls and bones found within these circles and around the sizable table-like rock on Sentinel Hill sustain the popular belief that these were once burial places of the Pocumtucks, even though many ethnologists, dismissing the absurd improbability of such a theory, insist the remains are Caucasian.
