The true fabric of life, I suspect, occupies but a small part of the novelist’s craft. When he writes of war—where courage is tested, where danger and devotion shine forth—the writer may venture close to reality, for such moments stir the soul and demand grand gestures. But beyond that arena, daily existence—whether it be selling spices, poring over legal tomes, or measuring coats in a tailor's shop—rarely finds its way into fiction. What can a storyteller say of the quiet, grinding routine of Ficulnus the merchant, Causidicus the lawyer, or Sartorius the tailor? Would readers endure a tale spun from hobnails and eighteenpence a day? Not likely.

In the age we describe, poets painted shepherds not in rough wool, but in pink breeches and chintz waistcoats, dancing before their flocks with flageolets tied in blue satin ribbons. And so, I reply to critics who would have us depict life as it truly is: the novelist cannot hope to capture the ordinary—save perhaps in war. Law, stockbroking, theology, drapery, apothecary work—all these must remain beneath the surface, unexplored. Our task is to show men not at their trade, but in passion, laughter, love, sorrow, and folly; to reveal their inner lives while leaving their professions unspoken, almost unheard.

Thus, when we speak of past or present times, we wander through theatre lobbies, coffee-houses, pleasure gardens, and ballrooms—places of leisure, not labour. Meanwhile, the real world plods on in offices, workshops, and fields. Corydon must cart manure and thresh barley before he can woo Phillis; Ancillula must wash clothes and serve meals before she dares whisper sweet nothings to Boopis the policeman. The city’s pavements wear down under his heels long before he can steal a kiss or a cold pie. We glimpse our characters only in these brief respites from duty—away from their burdens—and so, though they live full lives, we see them only when they are free.

Harry Warrington found himself increasingly shunned by the fashionable set. The macaronis of White’s and Arthur’s turned their backs on him with cold indifference, and soon he avoided their company altogether. The gaming tables of the Ring and the Mall no longer knew his face. Madame de Bernstein urged her nephew to stand firm against the world’s neglect. “You must have courage,” she declared. “If you’re slighted now, bear it with a smile. One day, you’ll repay them tenfold.” She spoke with authority, recalling her own trials. “I’ve eaten humble pie more than once,” she said. “We all must. Now that you’re no longer the Fortunate Youth, become the Clever Youth. Go everywhere. Be civil to everyone—especially women. Show your spirit, but do it with grace. With economy and your brother’s generosity, you can still appear well-born. You’re handsome—there’s no reason you can’t win a rich heiress. Go to the merchants in the City. They won’t know you’re out of fashion at Court.”

Yet this same woman, once so fond of Harry, now treated him with icy formality. Where once she had smiled and kissed him, she now scolded and mocked him before guests. Her servants, once eager to serve him, barely acknowledged his presence. When he called on Lady Yarmouth, she was always ill or otherwise engaged. Her staff did not offer him refreshment, nor ask if he’d stay. The days when he was welcomed as a companion of the great were gone.

Heartbroken, Harry confided in Mrs. Lambert. “She used to love me,” he said, voice thick with grief. “Now she turns on me. It hurts—I don’t understand why people I care for should turn away.”

The ladies listened quietly. Miss Hetty, ever bold, said, “Well, you’re not very lively, Harry.”

“Why should he be?” asked Theo gently. “He’s hurt. He has a good heart. Isn’t that enough?”

“But I’d never let anyone see me hurt,” Hetty insisted, clenching her fists. “And I’d smile even if that horrid old painted woman boxed my ears. She’s dreadful, mamma! You think so too, don’t you, Theo? You said so last night—you mimicked her coming in on her crutch!”

“I may dislike her,” Theo replied, blushing, “but I won’t call Harry’s aunt names in front of him.”

“You’re always right!” Hetty snapped. “That’s what makes me angry. I’m sorry, Harry. I shouldn’t have spoken like that.”

“It’s not the others,” Harry said softly. “But this one—it feels cruel. I cared for her. It’s hard when someone you love turns away.”

“Suppose George did?” Hetty asked.

“That’s impossible,” Harry replied sharply, rising. “You don’t know George like I do. You wouldn’t dream of suggesting he could do anything unkind.”

Hetty went pale. She looked up at him, then said nothing.

Before leaving, Harry added, “I’m sorry if I was harsh, Hetty. I didn’t mean to be unkind. But I’ll always defend George.”

She gave him her hand and curtseyed stiffly.

Later, alone with Theo, Hetty whispered, “I thought seeing him every day would be wonderful. But now I keep offending him. I can’t help it. He’s not clever, Theo—but oh, isn’t he good? Brave? Didn’t he look noble when he was angry?”

“You silly thing,” Theo teased. “You always try to make him look heroic.”

Among these young people, it was Theo and Hetty, Harry and George—a quartet bound by friendship. General Lambert, no doubt, nicknamed his wife “Bo” as a gentle tease, mocking her sentimental nature and her tendency toward matchmaking—traits common among women whose hearts matter. It hinted that Madam Lambert was foolish to imagine the two Virginian brothers might fall for her daughters. Little Hetty might dream, but dreams fade. “And you know, Molly,” the General would say, “you fancied someone else before you fancied me.” Mrs. Lambert couldn’t deny it. Yet Harry clearly wasn’t smitten with Hetty. And now, with George older and more commanding, Harry felt overshadowed—even his coat no longer felt entirely his own.

“Oh yes,” Mrs. Lambert sighed. “Now he’s poor, they’ll throw him out. Just like everyone else.”

“And I always do that, don’t I, Molly?” the General asked. “Turn my back on friends in trouble?”

“No, dear,” she said, dabbing her eyes. “I’m the goose. I admit it.”

“Let the boy come here,” the General said warmly. “Our house is one of the few where he’s welcome. He’s unhappy, and being here brings him peace. Let him stay—God willing.”

So whenever Harry needed a meal or a place to rest, Mr. Lambert’s table had room for him. George came too, though less warmly at first. He was quieter, more reserved than his brother. But the Lamberts’ kindness touched him. He saw how deeply Harry suffered, and began to feel for him. Often, with a dry wit, George would mock himself: “Dear Harry, I’m George the Unlucky, while you’ve ceased to be Harry the Fortunate. If Florac hadn’t killed that Indian, I’d still have my scalp—and you’d be safe from all this.”

“I never know whether you’re joking or serious,” Harry said.

“Precisely my own case,” George replied.

“But one thing I know: there’s never been a better brother. Or kinder people than the Lamberts.”

“Never truer words,” George said, taking his hand.

“And if I’m miserable,” Harry continued, “it’s not your fault—or theirs—or maybe even mine. It’s fate. It’s having nothing to do. I must work—but how, George?”

“We’ll wait to hear from mother,” George said.

“I don’t think I want to go back to Virginia,” Harry murmured.

“In love with one of the girls here?”

“Love them like sisters—with all my heart. But after what happened, thanks to you, I don’t want to return. Not because of that. It’s just… hunting, duck-shooting, planting tobacco, playing whist, going to church—over and over. That’s all there is. What else is there? What can I do? That’s what makes me miserable. It wouldn’t matter for you—you’re clever. You’d succeed anywhere. But for me? No chance. Until I do something, I’ll never be happy.”

“Have I not always said so?” George asked. “Are you finally agreeing with me?”

“What opinion?”

“You know everything I think, I think,” Harry said.

“That Florac should have left the Indian alive, my dear.”

At that, Harry laughed and shook his head. They sat together, smoking pipes in peaceful companionship.

They lived apart, each following their own path, yet bound by affection. Harry never disturbed George’s studies, sitting silently while his brother read. Soon they moved from the Court end of town, much to Madame de Bernstein’s disapproval. But George took a liking to Sir Hans Sloane’s new reading-room and museum in Montagu House, and rented cheerful lodgings in Southampton Row, Bloomsbury, overlooking the green fields near Hampstead. Lord Wrotham’s family moved to Mayfair, and Mr. Lambert, busy in London, relocated to furnished apartments in Soho—near the young men’s home.

Harry often stayed with the Lamberts, finding comfort in their warmth. George visited too, and as time passed, he grew more drawn to their circle. Their company suited him better than Aunt Bernstein’s gamblers, Sir Miles Warrington’s port-and-mutton dinners, or the noisy coffee-houses. Both families were newcomers to London, and they shared its pleasures: Vauxhall, Ranelagh, Marybone Gardens, the Tower, and above all—the theatre.

General Lambert loved innocent joy for his children, and George, generous by nature, liked to treat his friends—especially those kind to his brother.

Above all, Mr. Warrington adored the theatre. He had never known such entertainment in Virginia, only once or twice in Canada. Now, in London, with Drury Lane and Covent Garden at their peak, he believed he could never tire of it. Anything he enjoyed, he wished to share. Naturally, he invited his friends to join him. “Shall we go to Drury Lane or Covent Garden, ladies?” he asked.

Drury Lane offered Garrick and Shakespeare. But the ladies wanted to see the new play at Covent Garden—the work of a rising genius.

At that moment, a brilliant star had risen in British drama. Mr. John Home, a Scottish clergyman, had written a tragedy so refined, so classical, that many called it the finest since ancient times. Why had Garrick refused it? Some said Shakespeare, though great, contained barbarisms unfit for polite ears. Home, however, wrote with elegance—even in sorrow. His death scenes were not merely tragic, but graceful and moving. He never stooped to farce or puns, unlike earlier playwrights. Moreover, the play had been praised by the highest circles—by royalty and scholars alike. Even Mr. Gray of Cambridge claimed it matched the best of dramatic art. In Edinburgh, where it premiered, the audience roared in dialect: “Where’s Wully Shakspeare noo?”

“I’d like to see the man who could beat Willy Shakspeare,” the General chuckled.

“Mere national pride,” said Harry.

“Beat Shakspeare, indeed!” Mrs. Lambert scoffed.

“Pooh! You’ve cried over Richardson more than Shakespeare,” the General retorted. “Most women say they love Shakespeare, but they don’t.”

“Oh, papa!” the three girls exclaimed, throwing up their hands.

“Well, then, why do you all prefer *Douglas*?” he asked. “And you boys—Tories both—will you attend a play by a Whig Scotsman, imprisoned at Falkirk?”

“Relicta non bene parmula,” said Mr. Jack, the scholar.

“Nay,” said the General. “It was relicta bene parmula. The Highlanders threw down their targes and fought fiercely. Had they done so at Derby—”

“I know which side would be rebels,” George interrupted. “And who’d be called the Young Pretender.”

“Hush,” said the General. “Remember my cloth. My cockade is black, not white. But if politics don’t sway you, George, there’s another reason to like Home.”

“I may be a Tory,” George replied, bowing, “but I can respect a good Whig. Why should I like him?”

“Because he’s a Presbyterian minister who dared write a play. His fellow clergymen excommunicated him. They stripped him of his livelihood. He’d have starved—if not for the Young Pretender on our side, who gave him a pension.”

“If persecuted by parsons, he deserves support,” George smiled. “From now on, I shall attend his sermons.”

“Mrs. Woffington is divine in it,” Harry said. “Barry draws tears from everyone. Garrick is furious he refused it. Girls, bring six handkerchiefs! Mamma must stay home—I can’t trust her.”

But Mrs. Lambert insisted on going. “If I must cry, I’ll do it in a corner.”

So they all went to Covent Garden—the most of them prepared for a masterpiece. Could they not quote Congreve? Had they not wept over Otway and Rowe? Alas, how quickly glory fades. Who remembers where those old dramatists lie buried? Poor, forgotten Muse of the past! You sing, and we refuse to dance; you mourn, and we refuse to weep. And what of our own heroes? How soon will they too be dead and buried? How many will survive?

Off they went to Covent Garden to see the immortal John Home. The ladies and General arrived in a glass coach, meeting the young men at the door. Through torch-boys and footmen they made their way. Little Hetty clung to Harry’s arm; Miss Theo was handed into the box by George. Gumbo held their seats until they arrived, then bowed and retreated to the footman’s gallery.

They had fine seats in a front box, with a pillar behind which Mrs. Lambert could weep in privacy. Opposite them sat the Prince of Wales, with his mother, the Princess Dowager, greeted with loyal but lukewarm cheers. Behind the Prince stood Lord Bute, the poet’s patron, whose court had already wept over the play.

And so, under the glow of candlelight and expectation, the curtain rose on a tragedy that would echo through the ages.
