The following morning dawned bright and clear, and we rose with the sun. Once breakfast was finished, we immediately turned our minds to work, specifically focusing on our newly perfected bird-lime. Our mixture of rubber oil and turpentine had proven remarkably effective. The boys eagerly took charge, carrying long rods that I had coated with the sticky substance. They climbed the highest branches of the trees, where the fruit grew thick and the birds congregated in droves.

The sheer number of pigeons was staggering—far exceeding what we had seen the previous year. Last season, we had only encountered a scattering of stragglers, but now, massive flocks overwhelmed the branches of every tree in the neighborhood. These birds were also feasting on the sweet acorns of the evergreen oaks; the ground beneath the trees was littered with debris, indicating that the entire population roosted there overnight. Observing this, I decided to launch a nighttime raid using torches, a tactic familiar to the settlers of Virginia.

Meanwhile, the daytime snares were proving successful. As the pigeons landed, they became instantly glued to the branches. The more they flapped and struggled, the thicker the adhesive coating became, until, emitting pitiful cries, they collapsed to the ground, dragging their perch-sticks with them. We quickly removed the captured birds, reapplied fresh lime, and reset the traps. Soon, the boys mastered the technique and could operate independently. Seizing the opportunity, I retreated to prepare torches from pine wood soaked in turpentine for our nocturnal operation.

Just then, Jack trotted over with a particularly handsome pigeon, distinct from the common varieties. Reluctant to harm it, he held it out for inspection. Recognizing it as one of our cherished European breeds—which we intended to preserve until their numbers swelled—I gently took the trembling creature. Using oil and ash, I carefully scrubbed the stiff, sticky residue from its feet and wings, then placed it safely in a wicker cage. I instructed Jack to capture any similar specimens. His efforts paid off; we secured several pairs. This was a triumph, for since landing, we had released our original stock, allowing them to revert to a wild state and providing no benefit. Now, however, we would enjoy both fresh meat and the luxury of pigeon pie at will.

As evening approached, we departed for the oak grove, equipped simply with long bamboo poles, our torches, and canvas sacks. To the children, our makeshift weaponry seemed curious and inadequate, but their utility became apparent almost instantly. Darkness fell before we reached the woods. Lighting the torches, I revealed a breathtaking sight: every branch was densely packed with ortolans and wild pigeons, resting in unimaginable numbers.

Startled by the sudden glare, chaos erupted among the terrified birds. Dazzled and bewildered, they fluttered helplessly through the canopy. Many fell before we even raised our sticks, and we swiftly scooped them into our bags. When we began beating the branches, the harvest was immense; my wife and Franz struggled to keep pace with the deluge of birds hitting the ground. Within moments, our sacks were overflowing. We marched home, secured our bounty at Falconhurst, and retired to rest, grateful for the day's success.

The following day was consumed entirely by processing the catch—plucking, boiling, roasting, and stewing. By the next morning, however, a new mission was underway: an expedition to Woodlands to thwart the recurring monkey invasions. Determined to catch the mischievous creatures in the act of destruction, I hoped to administer a punishment severe enough to drive them away permanently. My wife stocked us with ample provisions for a multi-day absence, staying behind with Franz and Turk to manage the household.

I loaded the buffalo with extra-strong birdlime, short posts, string, coconut shells, and gourds. Mounting the donkey, we rode to a secluded clearing near Woodlands, hidden by dense undergrowth. There, we pitched a small tent and tethered our animals, ensuring the dogs were secured to avoid alerting our quarry prematurely.

Approaching the abandoned cottage, I wasted no time fortifying the site. Driving stakes lightly into the earth, I constructed an irregular fence around the house, weaving string in and out to create a confusing labyrinth. Every surface was liberally coated with birdlime, and basins filled with the mixture were scattered alongside piles of rice, maize, and other delicacies to lure the intruders.

Night passed undisturbed. Once our preparations were complete, we retired to our tent. Very early the next morning, a confused roar signaled the approach of a large troop of apes. Armed with sturdy clubs and cudgels, we held the dogs on leashes and moved silently behind the thickets to gain a vantage point.

What followed was a chaotic spectacle. The rustling, cracking, and shrieking of the forest intensified until it became dizzying. Suddenly, the entire rabble of monkeys poured from the trees, scrambling and leaping across the grass toward the house. Attracted by the novel sights, they dashed past our fences in all directions. Some rushed for the food, while others scrambled onto the roof, pulling at wooden pegs in an attempt to force entry.

As they wandered, inevitably, they encountered our traps. One by one, they became coated in birdlime on their heads, paws, backs, or chests. Their predicament worsened with every passing moment. Some sat frozen, attempting futilely to clean themselves with ludicrous gestures. Others were hopelessly tangled in the stakes and ropes, trailing them like comical tails in a state of bewildered despair. Still others tried to assist their friends, only to stick together in larger, more complex knots; the harder they pulled and kicked, the tighter their bonds became.

Many carried the gourds and coconut shells we had left out, clattering loudly as their paws snagged on the bait. Perhaps the most absurd figure was an elderly ape who had discovered a calabash of palm wine. Eagerly drinking it, he inadvertently stuck the shell to his forehead and whiskers, effectively masking his eyes. Blundering wildly, he danced frantic capers in his struggle to remove the obstruction.

Some fled in terror, but others, having triggered the lime on surrounding trees, found themselves suspended from the branches in agonizing stillness, screaming horribly. Panic spreading throughout the group, I released the three dogs, whose patience had snapped. With zealous fury, they charged the trapped monkeys, joining us in the assault.

The scene resembled a gruesome battlefield. We fought with clubs and sticks until the cacophony of howling, yelling, and barking gave way to a terrifying silence. Shuddering, we surveyed the aftermath: at least forty apes lay mangled and dead. The boys grew somber, but I quickly rallied them, urging them to focus on the grim tasks of burial and cleanup—burning the stakes, cords, and bowls involved in our deadly strategy.

Once order was restored to the dismantled cottage and our livestock gathered, we noticed a strange sound: something heavy falling from a tree. Investigating, we found three magnificent birds ensnared on our loose limed sticks. Two were a rare variety of the Blue Molucca pigeon, while the third appeared to be a Nicobar pigeon, boasting resplendent green, bronze, and steel-blue plumage. Delighted by the prospect of domesticating these exotic guests, I envisioned them as the first tenants of a new dwelling near the cave.

"First tenants, Father!" Fritz exclaimed excitedly. "Do you expect to catch more like these?"

"Not exactly catch them," I replied with a smile. "I intend to practice a secret art. Magic can achieve wonders, Fritz!"

Declining to elaborate further, we waited a few days until Woodlands was tidied and secure. Returning to Falconhurst, we received a warm welcome and immediately discussed constructing a pigeon-house in the rock at Tentholm. My plan was ambitious: to hollow out a spacious chamber in the cliff face overlooking Jackal River, partitioned with perches and nests, featuring a large wooden facade with sliding doors and a broad platform for the birds.

After weeks of labor, the structure was ready. I assigned the younger children to guard the perimeter while summoning Fritz.

"Now, my faithful assistant," I said, "it is time to invite our new colonists. I shall perform a regular dealer's trick. You know how cunning these gentlemen are; they not only protect their own but attract others through clever means. I need soft clay, aniseed, and salt to create a mixture our birds will love, whose scent will draw wild ones to join them."

"I can easily procure those items, Father," Fritz offered.

"I shall also require aniseed oil," I continued. "I will apply it to the pigeon holes so that as the birds pass in and out, their feathers will absorb the scent, acting as a beacon for the wild pigeons. Pound the seeds and bring me the mortar."

Impregnating the mixture with the aromatic oil, I strained it through a cloth to ensure potency. The scent lingered for days, perfect for our purpose. With preparations complete, we installed the domestic pigeons in their new home and slid the panels shut. The European birds had already befriended the three beautiful strangers. When the other boys arrived, peering through a small glass pane I had installed, they watched the birds happily pecking at the "magic food," unaware of Fritz's role in concealing my secret methods.
