"I suppose you'll be packing up shortly now that Full Term is over, Professor," remarked a neighbor seated beside the Professor of Ontography at a banquet in the hospitable hall of St. James's College.

The Professor was young, immaculate, and spoke with precision.

"Yes," he replied. "My friends have insisted I take up golf this term, and I intend to travel to the East Coast—in fact, to Burnstow—for a week or ten days to refine my game. I hope to depart tomorrow."

"Oh, Parkins," interjected his neighbor on the other side, "since you're heading to Burnstow, I'd appreciate it if you inspected the site of the Templars' preceptory. Let me know if you deem it worthwhile for an excavation this summer."

It was, naturally, a man of antiquarian interests speaking, though since he appears only in this prologue, his specific credentials remain unnamed.

"Certainly," said Parkins, the Professor. "If you describe the location, I'll assess the terrain upon my return. Alternatively, I could correspond with you if you inform me of your probable whereabouts."

"Don't bother, thanks. I'm planning to bring my family in that direction during the Long Vacation. Since few English preceptories have ever been properly mapped, I thought I might seize the opportunity to contribute something useful on my spare days."

The Professor sniffed skeptically at the notion that mapping a preceptory constituted utility. His neighbor persisted:

"The site—I suspect nothing shows above ground—is likely submerged quite close to the beach now. The sea has advanced tremendously along that stretch. Based on the map, it should be roughly three-quarters of a mile from the Globe Inn at the town's northern tip. Where will you be staying?"

"Well, *at* the Globe Inn, as a matter of fact," Parkins admitted. "I've booked a room there. Most lodgings are shuttered in winter, and apparently, the only spacious room available is a double-bedded suite. They claim they lack a corner to store the second bed, but I suppose I can manage to rough it for a short while. I need ample space for my books and work. Though I dislike the idea of an empty bed—and certainly not two—in what I consider my temporary study, I can endure it."

"Do you call sharing a room 'roughing it,' Parkins?" asked a blunt gentleman opposite. "Look here, I'll come down and occupy it briefly; it'll provide companionship."

The Professor stiffened but maintained a courteous smile.

"By all means, Rogers; I wouldn't wish otherwise. However, I fear you'd find it tedious. Do you play golf?"

"No, thank Heaven!" retorted the rude Mr. Rogers.

"Well, when I'm not writing, I'll likely be on the links, which, as I said, might bore you."

"Oh, I don't know! There's certainly someone I know in the place. But if you don't want me, just speak the word, Parkins; I won't be offended. Truth, as you always remind us, is never offensive."

Parkins was indeed scrupulously polite and strictly truthful. It was feared, however, that Mr. Rogers occasionally exploited this trait. A conflict raged within Parkins, momentarily silencing him. When he finally spoke:

"Well, if you seek the absolute truth, Rogers, I was considering whether the room would comfortably accommodate both of us. Also, please understand that I wouldn't have voiced this unless prompted: you might prove a hindrance to my work."

Rogers roared with laughter.

"Well done, Parkins!" he exclaimed. "It's settled. I promise not to interrupt your labors. Don't worry about that. No, I won't come if you don't want me; but I thought it nice to keep the ghosts away." He winked and nudged his next neighbor. Parkins flushed visibly. "I beg your pardon, Parkins," Rogers continued. "I shouldn't have said that. I forgot you dislike levity on such topics."

"Well," Parkins said, "since you've raised the subject, I freely admit I do *not* tolerate careless talk regarding what you call ghosts. A man in my position cannot afford to appear to sanction current beliefs on such matters. As you know, Rogers—as you ought to know, for I have never concealed my views—"

"No, you certainly haven't, old man," Rogers whispered sotto voce.

"—I hold that any semblance, any appearance of concession to the idea that such things exist, amounts to a renunciation of everything I hold sacred. But I fear I haven't captured your attention."

"Your *undivided* attention, was what Dr. Blimber actually *said*," Rogers interrupted, feigning earnest accuracy. [Footnote 4: Mr. Rogers was incorrect; *vide* *Dombey and Son*, Chapter XII.] "But forgive me, Parkins; I'm stopping you."

"No, not at all," said Parkins. "I don't recall Blimber; perhaps he predates my tenure. But I needn't elaborate. I'm sure you grasp my meaning."

"Yes, yes," Rogers hastened, "exactly so. We'll discuss it fully at Burnstow, or wherever."

In recounting this dialogue, I aimed to convey the impression Parkins made upon me: somewhat elderly, perhaps hen-like in his peculiarities, utterly devoid of humor, yet dauntless and sincere in his convictions—a man deserving profound respect.

The following day, Parkins successfully departed the college and arrived in Burnstow. He was welcomed warmly at the Globe Inn, installed in the large double-bedded room previously mentioned, and arranged his materials in perfect order on a commodious table at the room's outer end. This table was surrounded on three sides by windows facing the sea: the central window looked directly outward, while those to the left and right offered views along the shore to the north and south. To the south lay the village of Burnstow; to the north, no houses were visible, only the beach and the low cliffs backing it. Directly ahead stretched a narrow strip of rough grass, scattered with old anchors and capstans, followed by a broad path and then the beach. Whatever the original distance between the inn and the sea, no more than sixty yards now separated them.

The inn's clientele consisted largely of golfers, comprising few notable characters. The most prominent figure was undoubtedly an *ancien militaire*, secretary of a London club, possessing a voice of immense volume and holding staunchly Protestant views. These opinions often erupted after attending services at the Vicar's church, an estimable man inclined toward picturesque rituals, which he gallantly suppressed out of deference to East Anglian tradition.

Professor Parkins, known chiefly for his pluck, spent most of the day following his arrival improving his game alongside Colonel Wilson. During the afternoon—whether due to the exertion of golf or otherwise—the Colonel's demeanor grew so lurid that even Parkins hesitated to walk home with him from the links. Deciding against confronting the bristling moustache and inflamed features, Parkins resolved to let tea and tobacco soften the Colonel before dinner necessitated their meeting.

"I might walk home tonight along the beach," he reflected. "Yes, and take a look—there will be sufficient light—at the ruins Disney mentioned. I don't precisely know their location, but I expect I'll stumble upon them inevitably."

He achieved this literally. Picking his way from the links to the shingle beach, his foot caught on a gorse root and a substantial stone, sending him tumbling. Rising to survey his surroundings, he found himself in a patch of broken ground marked by small depressions and mounds. Upon closer inspection, these proved to be masses of flints embedded in mortar and cloaked in turf. He correctly concluded he stood on the preceptory site he had promised to investigate. It seemed promising for exploration; enough foundation likely remained shallowly buried to illuminate the general plan. Vaguely recalling that the Templars, owners of this site, typically built around churches, he noted that a particular series of mounds nearby appeared arranged in a rough circle. Few can resist the urge to attempt amateur research outside their expertise, driven by the satisfaction of imagining success had they pursued it seriously. Our Professor, if he harbored such modest ambition, was equally eager to oblige Mr. Disney. Thus, he carefully paced the circular area, noting its rough dimensions in his pocket-book. Next, he examined an oblong eminence east of the center, seemingly the base of a platform or altar. At its northern end, a patch of turf had been removed by some wild creature. Thinking it wise to probe for masonry evidence, he produced his knife and began scraping the earth.

Then came another discovery: as he scraped, soil collapsed inward, revealing a small cavity. He lit match after match to illuminate the hole's contents, but the wind extinguished them all. By tapping and scratching the sides with his knife, however, he discerned it was an artificial cavity in masonry. Rectangular, with smooth, regular sides, top, and bottom, it was initially empty. Yet, as he withdrew his knife, a metallic clink echoed. Introducing his hand, he encountered a cylindrical object resting on the floor. Naturally, he retrieved it. Bringing it into the fading light, he saw it was man-made—a metal tube approximately four inches long, evidently of considerable age.

By the time Parkins confirmed the container held nothing else, twilight had deepened, rendering further search impractical.
