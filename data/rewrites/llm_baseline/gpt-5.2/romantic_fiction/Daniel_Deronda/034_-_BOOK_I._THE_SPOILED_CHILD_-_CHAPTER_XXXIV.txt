When Deronda arrived at five, the shop-front was already shuttered, as if the day’s bargaining were something to be folded away before the Sabbath could be unfolded. A Christian servant admitted him and led him through the dim passage into the room behind, where the family’s private life—so little guessed at by a passer-by—had been set out with a care that was almost ceremonial.

The house was old and stretched far back, and the room he entered must have been heavy with shadow in daylight; but now it was softened and warmed by a fine brass lamp, old enough to have gathered its own patina of years, hanging with seven little oil-flames above a table dressed in snow-white linen. The air carried that mingled household scent which no shop ever quite loses—wax, warmed wood, and the faint metallic tang of handled things—yet over it there floated the richer reassurance of food held ready: a whisper of fried fish, a breath of sweet cake, and that particular clean, yeasty fragrance that rises from newly cut bread.

The surroundings were dark enough to throw the figures into a Venetian glow. The grandmother, in yellowish-brown, wore a thick gold chain instead of a necklace; and in the lamplight her yellow face, with its strongly marked brows and the rolled framing of gray hair, looked almost handsome by mere force of lighting and attitude. Young Mrs. Cohen was vivid in red and black, with large artificial pearls wound again and again round her throat. The baby slept in a cradle under a scarlet counterpane. Adelaide Rebekah, braided amber, stood watchfully near, and Jacob Alexander, in black velveteen with scarlet stockings, seemed braced for some important negotiation. Their black eyes shone such an eager welcome that Deronda felt a small shame at the contemptuous distaste their daylight aspect had stirred in him. Here, under lamplight and linen, they looked like people who knew how to keep a hearth.

He glanced—almost despite himself—at the room’s few dignities: an oaken bureau, a high side-table, a large dish of blue and yellow ware set up like an heirloom, and two old silver vessels flanking it. In front lay a great volume in darkened vellum, ribbed at the back—heavy with some inheritance of words. At the far corner an open door showed another lit room beyond. Deronda took in these details in swift parenthetic looks while Jacob pressed his grave concern about the knife.

Deronda had bought one to order, with the hook and the white handle, and now produced it.

“Is that the sort of thing you want, Jacob?”

Jacob examined it with the severity of a small connoisseur. He opened the blades, tested the hook, drew out the corkscrew from his own knife for comparison.

“Why do you like a hook better than a cork-screw?” Deronda asked.

“’Caush I can get hold of things with a hook. A cork-screw won’t go into anything but corks. But it’s better for you, you can draw corks.”

“You agree to change, then?” Deronda said, aware that the grandmother listened with visible delight, as if this traffic were a little drama in itself.

“What else have you got in your pockets?” Jacob demanded, with deliberative seriousness.

“Hush, hush, Jacob, love,” the grandmother said; and Deronda, mindful that discipline was part of kindness, answered evenly, “I think I must not tell you that. Our business was with the knives.”

Jacob lifted his face and scanned Deronda for a long moment, as if weighing a man by the set of his mouth and the steadiness of his eyes. Then he said, with judicial gravity, “I’ll shwop,” and handed over the cork-screw knife. Deronda pocketed it with corresponding gravity.

Immediately Jacob ran into the inner room, his rapid voice chattering there; then he was back again, and when he saw his father enter, he seized a little velveteen hat from a chair, clapped it on, and advanced as if dressing for an audience. Cohen himself kept on his own hat and, at first, took no notice of the visitor. He stood still while the two children went up and clasped his knees; then he laid his hands on each in turn and uttered the Hebrew benediction. The wife—who had lifted the baby from the cradle—brought it and held it, still sleeping, beneath the father’s outstretched hands to be blessed in its sleep.

For one suspended minute Deronda felt that the pawnbroker, proud of his vocation, was not wholly prosaic. The room’s small sounds—cloth faintly rustling, oil-lights making their tiny, steady murmur, a child’s breath, a woman’s skirt—seemed to arrange themselves into a calm acoustic order, as if even the air were learning to be reverent. Whatever harsh racket the street might carry, here the Sabbath began by quieting the very hearing.

“Well, sir, you found your welcome in my family, I think,” Cohen said, setting down his hat at last and becoming his former self. “And you’ve been punctual. Nothing like a little stress here,” he added, tapping his side pocket as he sat. “It’s good for us all in our turn. I’ve felt it when I’ve had to make up payments. I began to fit every sort of box. It’s bracing to the mind. Now then! let us see, let us see.”

“That is the ring I spoke of,” Deronda said, drawing it from his finger. “I believe it cost a hundred pounds. It will be a sufficient pledge to you for fifty, I think. I shall probably redeem it in a month or so.”

Cohen’s glistening eyes seemed to draw a little nearer together as he met the ingenuous look—so young, so sure of his own good faith—that supposed redemption must be a satisfaction to pawnbrokers. Cohen took the ring, examined it, returned it, and said with studied indifference, “Good, good. We’ll talk of it after our meal. Perhaps you’ll join us, if you’ve no objection. Me and my wife’ll feel honored, and so will mother; won’t you, mother?”

The invitation was echoed warmly; and Deronda accepted with readiness, for the table—with its white cloth and warm scents held in check—seemed to offer him more than food. It offered access.

All now turned and stood round. No dish was yet visible except one covered with a napkin; young Mrs. Cohen set a china bowl by her husband so that he might wash his hands. But after he had put on his hat again, he paused and called in a loud voice, “Mordecai!”

Deronda, ignorant what role might be expected of some ancient hero at such a moment, half wondered whether this were part of the ceremony. He heard a “Yes” from the next room and looked toward the open door. There, to his astonishment, stood the very figure he had met that morning in the book-shop—the enigmatic Jew with the ardent, worn face.

Their eyes met. Mordecai looked as surprised as Deronda; and neither, in the suddenness of it, made any sign of recognition. When Mordecai came forward and seated himself at the end of the table, he bent his head to the guest in a cold distant way, as if the morning’s disappointment still clung to Deronda as an unpleasant association.

Cohen washed his hands, Hebrew words running from him in a practised current. Then he took away the napkin and showed two long flat loaves, besprinkled with seed—bread that bore memory in its very shape, a memorial of manna for wandering forefathers. He broke off small pieces and gave to each—grandmother, wife, children—Adelaide Rebekah standing on a chair at her full little height in amber, her lip compressed so that her small Jewish nose seemed longer in the effort to look properly solemn. After another blessing the men uncovered their heads, all were seated, and the meal began.

Deronda, preoccupied, could hardly have told what he ate. Yet the table made itself felt through the senses even while his mind ran ahead. There was fried fish—crisp at the edges, with that savor of oil that has taken on the flavor of the pan and of repeated Fridays, its thin tails browned and delicate. There was sweet cake, thick with sugar and spice, soft enough to swell a child’s cheek. There were the loaves with their seeded crust, their torn interior steaming faintly when broken, their smell like warm grain and old kitchens. The comfort of it was domestic, physical, unarguable—and it sat oddly beside the questions pressing in him about the man opposite.

Mordecai had no handsome Sabbath garment; but instead of the threadbare rusty black coat of the morning he wore a light drab one, which had once been a handsome loose paletot, now shrunk by washing and poverty. The change sharpened rather than softened the impression of his face—dark-haired, eager, emaciated—like some prophet’s head set down among shopkeepers. Deronda could not help noticing how the thin tails of the fried fish were assigned to Mordecai, and in general how his portion bore that quiet stamp given to a poor relation: a distribution as old as households, done without discussion, almost without awareness.

Cohen kept the conversation lively, choosing subjects “in taste”—royalty and empire. He spoke proudly of the Queen and the Royal Family; of the Emperor and Empress of the French; and the grandmother and wife entered with zest. Young Mrs. Cohen had an accurate memory of distinguished birthdays; the elder helped her son recall what occurred when the Emperor and Empress were in England and visited the City ten years before.

“I dare say you know all about it better than we do, sir,” Cohen said repeatedly, by way of preface to still fuller information.

“Our baby is named _Eu_genie Esther,” the young mother said, with bright emphasis.

“It’s wonderful how the Emperor’s like a cousin of mine in the face,” the grandmother declared. “It struck me like lightning when I caught sight of him. I couldn’t have thought it.”

“Mother and me went to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace,” Cohen said. “I had a fine piece of work to take care of, mother; she might have been squeezed flat—though she was pretty near as lusty then as she is now. I said if I had a hundred mothers I’d never take one of ’em to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace again; and you may think a man can’t afford it when he’s got but one mother—not if he’d ever so big an insurance on her.”

He stroked his mother’s shoulder and chuckled at his own humor. Deronda, listening, sought a path into what he wished to know.

“Your mother has been a widow a long while, perhaps,” he said. “That has made your care for her the more needful.”

“Ay, ay, it’s a good many _yore-zeit_ since I had to manage for her and myself,” Cohen answered quickly. “I went early to it. It’s that makes you a sharp knife.”

“What does—what makes a sharp knife, father?” Jacob asked, his cheek swollen with sweet cake.

The father winked at Deronda. “Having your nose put on the grindstone.”

Jacob slipped down from his chair, still holding his cake, and went straight up to Mordecai, who had been totally silent so far.

“What does that mean—putting my nose to the grindstone?” the boy demanded.

Mordecai turned his eyes on the small face close to his and said, with a benign gravity, “It means that you are to bear being hurt without making a noise.”

Jacob, as if moved by a sudden impulse of alliance, pushed the corner of the cake into Mordecai’s mouth—inviting him to bite—saying at the same time, “I shan’t though,” and watching carefully to see how much of the cake disappeared in this act of generosity. Mordecai took a bite and smiled, clearly meaning to please him; and the moment made both seem more lovable.

Deronda, however, felt a vexation: his question had yielded little. He tried another approach, letting it seem general.

“I fancy that is the right quarter for learning,” he said, and then, turning toward Mordecai as if naturally drawn, “You have been a great student, I imagine?”

“I have studied,” Mordecai answered quietly.

“And you?—You know German by the book you were buying.”

“Yes,” Deronda said. “I have studied in Germany. Are you generally engaged in bookselling?”

“No; I only go to Mr. Ram’s shop every day to keep it while he goes to meals,” Mordecai said. He now looked at Deronda with what seemed a revival of his earlier interest, as if something in Deronda’s face drew him and began to counteract the morning’s disappointment. After a slight pause he asked, “Perhaps you know Hebrew?”

“I am sorry to say, not at all.”

Mordecai’s countenance fell. He lowered his eyelids, looked at his hands crossed on the table, and said no more.

Deronda noticed now, more decisively than in the shop, Mordecai’s difficulty in breathing—an effort hidden badly beneath composure, and suggestive of consumption. It gave the man’s silence a weight: as if speech itself were a costly substance he must ration, like a poor household portioning out oil.

“I’ve had something else to do than to get book-learning,” Cohen said, throwing himself into talk with the pride of the practical. “I’ve had to make myself knowing about useful things. I know stones well,”—and he pointed toward Deronda’s ring. “I’m not afraid of taking that ring of yours at my own valuation. But now,” he added, dropping his voice into a more familiar nasal, “what do you want for it?”

“Fifty or sixty pounds,” Deronda answered, rather too carelessly.

Cohen paused, thrust his hands into his pockets, and fixed on Deronda a pair of glistening eyes that suggested a miraculous guinea-pig’s scrutiny—bright, quick, and intent on gain.

“Couldn’t do you that. Happy to oblige, but couldn’t go that lengths. Forty pound—say forty—I’ll let you have forty on it.”

Deronda was aware that Mordecai had looked up again at the sound of money, and was examining him as he replied, “Very well. I shall redeem it in a month or so.”

“Good. I’ll make you out the ticket by-and-by,” Cohen said indifferently, and held up his finger to check further speech.

Then Cohen, Mordecai, and Jacob put on their hats again. Cohen opened a thanksgiving; the others responded. And presently Mordecai delivered himself alone at some length, chanting in a solemn tone, his chin slightly uplifted, his thin hands clasped loosely before him. There was in his voice an eerie purity, a steadiness that seemed to belong to older air than London’s—an acoustic refuge from the day’s clangor. It made Deronda think, with a sudden resentment at modern harshness, how much human well-being depends on the sounds that surround us: how quickly a coarse street can bruise the mind, and how a measured chant can mend it. The words themselves were not Deronda’s; the syllables were foreign to his tongue, and yet their cadence carried a meaning beyond translation—something that could not be rendered without loss, because it lived partly in breath and pitch, in the very way the room listened.

In accent and tone, in the freedom from any glance toward others’ approval, Mordecai was the strongest contrast possible to Cohen at the other end of the table. It was an unaccountable conjunction: among these common, prosperous, shopkeeping types sat a man threadbare and emaciated, imposing on Deronda a certain awe—and an embarrassment, too, as if he were failing some expectation he could not name.

No sooner had Mordecai finished than he rose, bent his head slightly to the stranger, walked back into his room, and shut the door behind him.

“That seems to be rather a remarkable man,” Deronda said, turning to Cohen.

Cohen set up his shoulders, put out his tongue slightly, and tapped his own brow. The gesture made plain enough what standard of sanity Cohen applied.

“Does he belong to your family?” Deronda asked.

The notion seemed ludicrous to the ladies as well as to Cohen; they exchanged amused looks.

“No, no,” Cohen said. “Charity! charity! He worked for me, and when he got weaker and weaker I took him in. He’s an incumbrance; but he brings a blessing down, and he teaches the boy. Besides, he does the repairing at the watches and jewelry.”

Deronda could hardly keep from smiling at the mixture: kindliness needing to be justified as calculation, pity balanced with profit like weights on a scale. But any further attempt to speak of Mordecai was baffled at once. Cohen dismissed the subject by returning to the “accommodation”—which was also, by his account, an act of charity—and proceeded to make out the pawn-ticket, fetch the forty pounds, and hand both over in exchange for the diamond ring.

Deronda felt it would not be delicate to prolong his visit beyond the settlement of the business which had been its pretext. He took his leave with no more decided result than the money and the pawn-ticket in his breast-pocket—a reason to return when he came up to town after Christmas. Yet he left with a stronger resolve than he had entered with: that on his return he would contrive to gain a clearer insight into the character and history of Mordecai—and, through him, perhaps something decisive about the Cohens too: for example, why it seemed forbidden to ask the elder Mrs. Cohen whether she had a daughter.
