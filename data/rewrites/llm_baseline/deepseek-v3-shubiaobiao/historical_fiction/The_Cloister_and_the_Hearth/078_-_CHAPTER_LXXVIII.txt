A lie is not like a blow from a blade—clean, sharp, its damage done and seen. No, a lie is a seed, sown in darkness, sprouting unseen, its roots twisting deep, its branches spreading wide beyond any man’s reckoning. A single falsehood, spoken in malice or greed, may topple lives like a storm felling trees.

So it was with the lie that severed Gerard from Margaret.

The brothers, Cornelis and Sybrandt, had whispered their deceit to spare their inheritance one claimant. Yet what had they gained? Little Gerard, the son they had sought to disown, now filled their mother’s heart. Worse still, Eli—seeing no hope of his lost son’s return—had named the boy his heir, securing Margaret’s place among them. The brothers exchanged a look of bitter defeat. Cornelis nearly spoke, nearly confessed that Gerard might yet live—but their mother’s sharp eye silenced him.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the other architect of this ruin, was a man crumbling under the weight of his own sins. Age had gnawed at his body, but conscience gnawed fiercer at his soul. He had bargained with himself—kept the land, the gold, but delivered Gerard’s letter to Margaret, as if that small mercy might balance the scales. Yet when her child was born, his treachery took a darker hue. He had wronged the daughter of a friend, the granddaughter of another, and now her shame would be his doing.

These thoughts haunted him, sharp as specters, until one day he found her standing in his hall, veiled, a child in her arms. She let the veil fall, and her eyes struck him like a blade.

He flinched. *“For aught I know,”* he stammered. *“I hope he lives—for your sake.”*

She seized his shoulder. *“You know why he does not come. You know why he sends no word. Tell me!”*

He denied, he faltered, but she pressed him, relentless. Then she knelt, holding the child up to him—this boy, Peter’s grandchild, Floris’s blood. *“Look at him,”* she pleaded. *“Look at this face, innocent as yours once was. Have you no memory left?”*

The old man recoiled as if burned. *“Avaunt!”* he shrieked, tearing at his hair. *“Must I see the dead in every face? Hear their voices in every word? I am in hell already!”* His legs gave way; servants bore him off, a broken thing.

Margaret left, sick with pity and dread. *“I have killed him,”* she whispered. Yet the mystery remained, darker than before.

Money grew scarce again. Then, one day, silver appeared on her table—a scrawl beside it: *For Gerard his wife.* Her heart leaped. *“He lives! He sends this!”* She kissed the note, tucked it into her bosom.

Months passed. The coins came, left by unseen hands—on the table, in a basket, once tossed through the window. No one claimed the deed—not Catherine, not Eli, not Margaret Van Eyck. And with each gift, she whispered to her boy, *“We are wading in deep waters, my love.”*

She dressed him well, as if Gerard himself might see. The neighbors sneered. Let them.

Then came a change in Peter Brandt. His clouded mind cleared, sudden as a break in storm-wracked skies. He spoke of Gerard—wondered if he languished in prison, or had been pressed into war, or sent to some distant scriptorium. *“Or taken vows,”* he murmured. *“He had that turn of mind.”*

Margaret bit her lip. *“Father, you deceive yourself, as I have done.”*

But then—his eyes widened. He sat bolt upright, crying, *“I see him! In a boat—on a great river—coming this way!”* His gaze pierced the walls, seeing what none else could. Then, just as sudden, the light left him. *“Gone. All dark.”*

He sank back, murmuring blessings, whispering of love. Then, like a torch guttering its last bright flare before the dark, he was gone.

And Margaret knelt, clutching her child, alone in the gathering shadows.
