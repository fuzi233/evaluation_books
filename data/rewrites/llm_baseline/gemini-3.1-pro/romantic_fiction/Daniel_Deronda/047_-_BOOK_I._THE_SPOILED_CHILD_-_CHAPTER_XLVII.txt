One might be tempted to envy Deronda the sensory satisfaction of providing new clothes for Mordecai, pleasing himself as though he were blending the deep, earthy spices of a complex dish. He had selected fine gray flannel shirts that smelled of raw lanolin and clean starch, pairing them with a dressing-gown the rich, roasted hue of crushed chicory root, picturing Mordecai’s noble head and neck rising above them. Half his pleasure was the anticipation of feeding Mirah’s fervid joy, securing her from any perturbing, bitter impressions. And yet, as he laid out the woven garments, a profound doubt settled in his palate. Was he not substituting a shallow, sanitized flavor for a deeper, more resonant one? Was she not just as capable as he of tasting the impressive, ancient distinction in her brother precisely *because* of that aspect of poverty, which lingered like the scent of woodsmoke and bitter herbs—the very memorials of his past?

But there was the Meyrick household to be propitiated toward this intensely Judaic brother. Deronda detected in himself a capitulation to the dominant English lexicon of respectability, hiding away everything that might provoke the ready repugnance of palates unaccustomed to such robust, ancestral fare. He was trying to translate Mordecai into a safer dialect, knowing well that such translation often erodes the authentic cultural memory embedded in the original.

His inclination to protect Mordecai’s raw, untranslatable dignity would have been fiercely confirmed had he heard the dissonance echoing around Mrs. Meyrick’s fire late that evening, after Mirah had retired. Hans, now settled in his Chelsea rooms, had stayed late. The Meyrick hearth was a sanctuary of acoustic and culinary warmth, defending against the harsh, industrial clatter of modern London. Mrs. Meyrick, poking the fire to coax a steady, rhythmic crackle beneath a simmering iron pot of cracked barley and root vegetables, said, “Now, Kate, put out your candle, and all come round the fire cosily. Hans, dear, do leave off laughing at those poems for the ninety-ninth time, and come too. I have something wonderful to tell.”

“As if I didn’t know that, ma. I have seen it in the corner of your eye ever so long, and in your pretense of errands,” said Kate. The girls gathered, bringing the scent of yeast and mending-wool to the fender. Hans, pushing his chair near them, sat astride it, resting his fists and chin on the back, entirely disconnected from the slow, nourishing rhythm of the room.

“Well, then, if you are so wise, perhaps you know that Mirah’s brother is found!” said Mrs. Meyrick, her clear accents ringing with the purity of struck crystal.

“Oh, confound it!” said Hans, in the very same breath, introducing a jarring, discordant note into the harmony.

“Hans, that is wicked,” said Mab. “Suppose we had lost you?”

“I *cannot* help being rather sorry,” said Kate. “And her mother?—where is she?”

“Her mother is dead.”

“I hope the brother is not a bad man,” said Amy.

“Nor a fellow all smiles and jewelry—a Crystal Palace Assyrian with a hat on,” said Hans in the worst humor, his words dripping with the superficial commercialization of art that so often commodified and hollowed out true cultural lineage. 

“Were there ever such unfeeling children?” said Mrs. Meyrick, strengthened by the need to defend the sacred, mythic nature of this familial reunion. “You don’t think the least bit of Mirah’s joy in the matter.”

“You know, ma, Mirah hardly remembers her brother,” said Kate.

“People who are lost for twelve years should never come back again,” said Hans, his voice a flat, sterile intrusion. “They are always in the way.”

“Hans!” said Mrs. Meyrick, reproachfully. “If you had lost me for *twenty* years, I should have thought—”

“I said twelve years,” Hans broke in, aggressively talking over her. “Anywhere about twelve years is the time at which lost relations should keep out of the way.”

“Well, but it’s nice finding people—there is something to tell,” said Mab, clasping her knees, sensing the sweeping, generational saga unfolding. “Did Prince Camaralzaman find him?”

Then Mrs. Meyrick, in her neat, narrative way, ladled out the story, letting the rich broth of the events speak for themselves without interruption. “Mr. Deronda has the highest admiration for him,” she ended—“seems quite to look up to him. And he says Mirah is just the sister to understand this brother.”

“Deronda is getting perfectly preposterous about those Jews,” said Hans with disgust, rising and setting his chair away with a violent bang that bruised the quiet ecology of the parlor. “He wants to do everything he can to encourage Mirah in her prejudices.”

“Oh, for shame, Hans!—to speak in that way of Mr. Deronda,” said Mab. And Mrs. Meyrick’s face showed something like an under-current of expression not allowed to get to the surface, a silent refusal of her son’s toxic lexicon.

“And now we shall never be all together,” Hans went on, walking about with his hands thrust into the pockets of his brown velveteen coat. “But we must have this prophet Elijah to tea with us, and Mirah will think of nothing but sitting on the ruins of Jerusalem. She will be spoiled as an artist—mind that—she will get as narrow as a nun. Everything will be spoiled—our home and everything. I shall take to drinking.”

“Oh, really, Hans,” said Kate, impatiently. “I do think men are the most contemptible animals in all creation. Every one of them must have everything to his mind, else he is unbearable.”

“Oh, oh, oh, it’s very dreadful!” cried Mab. “I feel as if ancient Nineveh were come again.”

“I should like to know what is the good of having gone to the university and knowing everything, if you are so childish, Hans,” said Amy. “You ought to put up with a man that Providence sends you to be kind to. *We* shall have to put up with him.”

“I hope you will all of you like the new Lamentations of Jeremiah—‘to be continued in our next’—that’s all,” said Hans, seizing his wide-awake hat. “It’s no use being one thing more than another if one has to endure the company of those men with a fixed idea, staring blankly at you, and requiring all your remarks to be small foot-notes to their text. If you’re to be under a petrifying wall, you’d better be an old boot. I don’t feel myself an old boot.” Then abruptly, “Good night, little mother,” bending to kiss her brow in a hasty, desperate manner, and condescendingly, on his way to the door, “Good-night, girls.”

“Suppose Mirah knew how you are behaving,” said Kate. 

But his only answer was the slam of the door—a harsh, industrial percussion that severed the room's acoustic tranquility and spoke to his total inability to digest the profound sustenance of Mirah’s unfolding history. 

“I *should* like to see Mirah when Mr. Deronda tells her,” she went on to her mother, restoring the gentle murmur of the hearth. “I know she will look so beautiful.”

But Deronda, on second thoughts, had written a letter which Mrs. Meyrick received the next morning alongside the scent of her freshly baking morning loaves. He begged her to make the revelation instead of waiting for him, shrinking from going through a narrative where he might seem to be assigning himself the role of the grand savior. He wished to remain with Mordecai while Mrs. Meyrick brought Mirah on what was to be understood as a visit. 

Deronda secretly felt a wondering anxiety. Mordecai, after years of solitary, internal sustenance—feeding on ideas that had become his only daily bread as his bodily strength diminished—might find the sudden intrusion of deep emotional ties difficult to swallow. Mordecai was like a traditional agrarian elder, attuned only to the slow, generational rhythms of his faith. He asked no new questions about Mirah, maintaining an unusual, resonant silence. He donned the new flannel and chicory-brown clothes obediently, but said afterward to Deronda, with a faint smile, “I must keep my old garments by me for a remembrance.” He understood that to discard the threadbare fabric of his past was to actively participate in the erosion of his own cultural memory.

As they sat awaiting Mirah, Mordecai uttered no word. He kept his eyelids closed, dwelling in a profound acoustic quiet, though his hands showed restless feeling. He was undergoing that peculiar nervous perturbation known only to those whose minds, having flowed long in one deep, ancient riverbed, are suddenly compelled into a reopened channel. Joy was approaching, but joy, too, carried the terrifying weight of a mythic occurrence breaking into the everyday.

Deronda felt the infection of this quiet excitement. When he heard the ring at the door, he went out to greet Mirah. He was startled to find that she had foregone any polished presentation; she wore the hat and cloak in which he had first seen her—the memorable cloak that smelled faintly of river-damp and old, wetted wool, the olfactory landscape of her lowest ebb. 

When Mrs. Meyrick had asked, “You like to go in that dress, dear?” Mirah had answered, “My brother is poor, and I want to look as much like him as I can, else he may feel distant from me.” 

Deronda felt secretly ashamed of his own fastidious arrangements, recognizing that Mirah’s instinct to match her brother's physical reality was far truer than his attempt to dress it up. They shook hands silently. Mirah looked pale and awed.

When Deronda opened the door, Mordecai had risen. Mirah took only two or three steps, and then stood still. They looked at each other, motionless. It was less their own presence they felt than the weight of a multi-generational saga converging in this single room. Touch was no union compared to the interwoven timelines of their shared memory.

Mirah was the first to break the silence. She spoke a single word, slicing through the years of separation with the pure, uncorrupted dialect of their childhood.

“Ezra,” she said, in exactly the same tone, the same phonetic shape, as when she was telling of their mother’s call to him.

Mordecai, with a sudden movement, advanced and laid his hands on her shoulders. He looked down at her tenderly. “That was our mother’s voice. You remember her calling me?”

“Yes, and how you answered her—‘Mother!’—and I knew you loved her.” 

Mirah threw her arms round her brother’s neck, kissing him with childlike lavishness. Her hat fell backward, disclosing all her curls.

“Ah, the dear head, the dear head!” said Mordecai, laying his thin, artisan’s hand gently on her hair.

“You are very ill, Ezra,” said Mirah, sadly, taking in the fragile physical reality of him.

“Yes, dear child, I shall not be long with you in the body,” was the quiet answer.

“Oh, I will love you and we will talk to each other,” said Mirah, the words pouring from her as sweet and spontaneous as wild honey. “I will tell you everything, and you will teach me:—you will teach me to be a good Jewess—what she would have liked me to be. I shall always be with you when I am not working. For I work now. I shall get money to keep us. Oh, I have had such good friends.”

Mirah turned with the prettiest attitude, keeping one hand on her brother’s arm while she looked at Mrs. Meyrick and Deronda. The little mother’s happy emotion in witnessing this reclamation of a dying family lexicon had already won her to Mordecai, who possessed a deep, inherent dignity far beyond Deronda's initial descriptions.

“See this dear lady!” said Mirah. “I was a stranger, a poor wanderer, and she believed in me, and has treated me as a daughter. Please give my brother your hand,” she added beseechingly, pressing Mrs. Meyrick’s hand into Mordecai’s, then lifting them both to her lips.

“The Eternal Goodness has been with you,” said Mordecai, his voice a low, echoing blessing that enriched the very air of the room. “You have helped to fulfill our mother’s prayer.”

“I think we will go now, shall we?—and return later,” said Deronda, laying a gentle pressure on Mrs. Meyrick’s arm. She immediately complied. He was afraid of introducing his own narrative into their sacred space, and he felt no uneasiness now in leaving the brother and sister alone together, free to share the untranslatable sustenance of their native tongue.
