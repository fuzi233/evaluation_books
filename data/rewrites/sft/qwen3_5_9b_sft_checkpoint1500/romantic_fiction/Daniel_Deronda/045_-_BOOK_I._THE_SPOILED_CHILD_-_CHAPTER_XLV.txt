A carriage, drawn by polished bays and attended by a powdered lackey, halted on the street. Inside, Lady Mallinger swept off her hat, stepping onto the crimson carpet with a determined grace, her sole purpose "to be there." Like Sirius rising silently above the darkened sea, she concealed her restless heart beneath a glittering exterior.

Soon enough, the Grandcourts arrived at Grosvenor Square, summoned by a card for the musical gathering. Sir Hugo knew beforehand that his ill-favored nephew, Daniel Deronda, would be attending, though he offered no warning. It had been barely three evenings since their arrival, and Gwendolen wandered through the unfamiliar grandeur of the room with a distracted air. Her thoughts were fixed on Deronda, and on Mirah Lapidoth, whom she recalled as someone capable of enduring "anything in the form of duty." Yet Gwendolen clung to a bitter memory of Deronda's words regarding Mirah. She replayed them in her mind, tasting their sting, aware with a vague, gnawing shame that her own submission to Sir Hugo was no act of duty at all. It was a yoke forced upon her by her own actions, worn with a heavy, selfish burden that left no room for virtue.

The drawing-room, bathed in shades of white, gold, and pale crimson, was sparsely populated until the Grandcourts entered. For over half an hour, the air had hummed with instrumental music, broken only by murmurs of conversation. Herr Klesmer stood at the piano, his wife beside him, intending to sing Leo's "O Patria Mia" accompanied by Mirah. Klesmer had urged Mirah to choose this piece for its distinctiveness, a choice that now placed her conspicuously in the center of attention. Just then, Gwendolen, radiant in pale green velvet and jewels that seemed poisoned by their cold brilliance, was ushered to a seat of honor directly in view of the performers. With her sharp eyes and iron self-control, she instantly surveyed the room, exchanging a swift, electric bow with Klesmer as she passed. A flash of lightning sparked within her mind, recalling that morning when she had dreamed of standing *above* the "little Jewess," surveying a vast audience from the heights of her own talent. Now, however, she was merely part of the crowd in silk and gems, whose highest achievement was to applaud or criticize. "He believes I am finally on the right path," whispered a resentful thought deep inside her.

Gwendolen had not yet spotted Deronda. As she settled into polite chatter with Sir Hugo, she scanned the room with practiced ease, bowing to acquaintances, terrified that any anxious, searching gaze aimed at finding Deronda would be noticed by her husband and labeled "damnably vulgar." Yet, the journey of a single eye around a crowded hall often leads to unwanted discoveries. Among those who met her gaze—and compelled a slight, automatic bow—were the eyes of Mr. Lush, the "amateur too fond of Meyerbeer," whom Sir Hugo currently deemed useful as a gentleman of mixed blood. He stood near her husband, who, however, turned a shoulder away to listen to Lord Pentreath. Suddenly, a disagreeable sensation pierced Gwendolen's composure: the realization that this man knew everything about her husband's life. She had successfully exiled him from her sight, satisfied by the silence surrounding him, yet here he stood, resurrected at her husband's elbow. An instantaneously forged memory, like a phantom in a dream, surged within her: the knowledge that he was connected to the secrets that made her miserable. She strained to turn her head away, feigning a wandering survey of the walls as if nothing mattered, until her eyes inevitably drifted toward Deronda. But he did not look at her. She pulled her gaze back, comforting herself with the lie that he must have seen her entrance. In truth, he stood near the door with Hans Meyrick, both men uneasy lest Mirah's performance suffer. Deronda, too, hovered on the brink of emotion; Mirah's presence triggered a flood of memories, all converging on the brother he was soon to reveal to her. He had slipped away from Lady Pentreath, whose violoncello voice had mocked Mirah's silence. "Well, your Jewess is pretty—there's no denying that," she had sneered. "But where is her Jewish impudence? She looks as demure as a nun. I suppose she learned that on the stage."

Deronda felt a surge of indignation, mirroring the seraphic protectiveness he once felt for himself. He winced at the casual dismissal of Mirah as an imported commodity, acutely aware that Mordecai would interpret the slur "Jewess" as a damning brand. As the Grandcourts entered, Hans immediately hailed Deronda about "that Vandyke duchess of a beauty." Deronda felt a sudden, renewed repulsion toward Gwendolen, viewing her beauty and flaws as complicit in Mirah's undervaluation—a sentiment akin to class hatred. To Hans's exaggerated admiration, Deronda replied with a sarcasm that lacked warmth: "I thought you could admire no style of woman but your Berenice." Hans, mistaking this for playful rivalry, declared, "That is the style I worship—not admire... Other styles I might make myself wicked for, but for Berenice I could make myself—pretty good." Deronda cut the conversation short, claiming the singing was about to begin, relieved when Hans moved away.

For the first time, Deronda heard Mirah sing "O Patria Mia." Knowing Leopardi's ode intimately, he felt the weight of the omitted lines echoing in his soul: *"Do none of thy children defend thee? Arms! bring me arms! Alone I will fight, alone I will fall."* These words vibrated like a heroic, tragic passion, the sound of a man dying helplessly far from the battlefield. Mirah sang with a purity that touched his heart. While the room erupted in applause, Klesmer leaned close to whisper, "Good, good—the crescendo better than before." But Mirah's true anxiety lay in satisfying Mr. Deronda. Every prospect she had was owed to him, and performing in his home demanded a special standard. She looked across the room, seeking his approval, but he remained distant, watching the stream of admirers close around her. Soon, the crowd parted to make way for Gwendolen, who was escorted by Mrs. Klesmer.

Now, Daniel softened toward Gwendolen. His fleeting harshness gave way to penitence; he recalled the many signs that she, too, needed a rescue, though hers seemed far more impossible than Mirah's. A silent question formed in his mind: *Is it not cowardly to use fear as an excuse for turning away?* This became his resolve to approach her the next moment, honoring her past trust despite Sir Hugo's subtle warnings.

As Gwendolen approached, Klesmer smiled, a look that dwelt in his eyes rather than on his lips, observing the striking contrast between the two young women on the red divan. Gwendolen expressed her gratitude for the treat of hearing Mirah sing, admitting her ignorance of such greatness. Mirah, her mind fixated on Gwendolen, regarded her with a vague awe, sensing these grand ladies emerged from some unknown, perhaps tragic, drama. "We shall all want to learn of you," Gwendolen said, glancing archly upward at Klesmer. "I sing very badly... I have been rebuked for not liking to be middling." She paused, looking at Klesmer, who quickly assured her that studying further would indeed be worthwhile, and that Mirah would be happy to assist. Before leaving, Mirah, taking everything with naive seriousness, promised, "If you think I could teach you, I shall be very glad. I am anxious to teach... remembering how my master taught me."

Gwendolen, usually so certain of herself, felt a flicker of uncertainty. Desperate to shift the subject, she asked if Mirah had been long in London and if she had known Deronda abroad. "No," Mirah replied simply, "I never saw him before I came to England in the summer." Gwendolen pressed, noting that Deronda had spoken of her with high praise, implying he knew her well. "Oh, I was poor and needed help," Mirah explained, her voice shifting to a tone of profound feeling. "Mr. Deronda has given me the best friends in the world. That is the only way he came to know anything about me—because he was sorry for me. I had no friends when I came. I owe everything to him."

Gwendolen, who had once wished to be a struggling artist herself, felt a sting of humiliation at the condescending pity directed at the "Jewess." To Mirah, however, the mention of Deronda stirred only reverential gratitude and a fierce desire to prove her debt. Both Deronda and Hans, watching from afar, would have been indignant if they knew Mirah portrayed herself in this light of neediness. Yet, in her movement, there lay an exquisite delicacy—a refusal to allow anyone to assume a relationship of equality or lesser generosity than actually existed. Her answer delighted Gwendolen, who saw only the compassion she had trusted in the past, unaware that this "protégé" would soon alter her life far beyond improving her singing.
