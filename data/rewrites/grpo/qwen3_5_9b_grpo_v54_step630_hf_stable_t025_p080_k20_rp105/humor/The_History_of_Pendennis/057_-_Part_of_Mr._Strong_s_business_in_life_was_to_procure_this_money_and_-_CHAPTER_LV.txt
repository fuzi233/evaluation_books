Since Arthur's illness, Helen had effectively seized command of her son's private quarters, turning his drawers and closets into a fortress of domestic vigilance. She managed his shirts, mended his stockings, and, with a heavy heart, handled his correspondence. While Mrs. Pendennis hoped to find answers to the terrifying mystery of Fanny Bolton within these papers, she found nothing incriminating. Instead, she discovered a mountain of unpaid bills from bootmakers and hatters, a financial burden that threatened to crush her son's already fragile spirit. To shield him from the distress of these demands, Helen had removed the brass knocker from his door, ensuring that the postman's rattling knock would never disturb his rest. She intercepted every letter, paying off debts herself with her own meager savings, a sacrifice she deemed a worthy test of a son's honor.

Yet, the shadow of Fanny Bolton loomed large. When Helen sought counsel from her brother-in-law, Major Pendennis, at their leisurely spot on the Richmond terrace, the old general's expression shifted between amusement and feigned concern. He dismissed the scandal with a wave of his hand, advising her to ignore the "dreadful sin" entirely. "Young men will be young men," he declared, trying to soothe her hysterical fears that her honorable boy had fallen from grace. When Helen tearfully insisted that Arthur's silence confirmed his guilt, the Major gently chided her for suppressing letters, warning that such interference could sever the bond between mother and son.

The tension broke when Helen produced a single, small letter. It was written in a trembling hand, filled with poor spelling and desperate pleas for Arthur's health. The Major, initially feigning shock, quickly pivoted to a defense of Arthur's innocence. He spun a tale of inquiries made by his servant Morgan and Dr. Goodenough, painting a picture of a hapless girl named Fanny, whose father was a drunken brute and whose mother was a hysterical liar. He argued that the girl had merely visited Arthur while he was delirious, acting as a nurse, and that there was no evidence of anything more than a fleeting, innocent attachment. "The boy is innocent," the Major proclaimed, kissing Helen's hand with theatrical relief. "He has told you nothing because he is a man of honor, and you have no right to doubt him based on the word of an anonymous scoundrel."

Convinced by her brother's logic, Helen wept with gratitude, convinced that her son was blameless. The Major, victorious in his diplomatic maneuvering, retired to a nap on the sofa, dreaming of his triumph. Meanwhile, Arthur and his friend Warrington lounged on the terrace, discussing Arthur's ambitious plans for new novels and tragedies. The atmosphere was light until Miss Bell arrived, bringing game and flowers. She expressed deep concern for Helen's health, noting how worn and anxious her mother appeared. The Major, seizing the opportunity to steer the conversation toward recovery, proposed a grand tour abroad to the Rhine. He envisioned a trip where Helen could regain her strength and Arthur could forget his troubles in a new environment, perhaps accompanied by Warrington.

Arthur, eager to escape the stifling atmosphere of Fairoaks, enthusiastically agreed to the plan. However, Warrington hesitated. Citing a lack of funds and pressing engagements at home, he politely declined, though his reluctance was palpable. The Major, undeterred, offered to act as Warrington's banker, promising to cover any expenses incurred during the journey. He framed the invitation as a duty to his old friend's family, insisting that Arthur needed a companion who was "stronger and gayer" than the aging Major himself.

The situation reached a climax at the evening tea. With Helen momentarily absent, the Major pressed Warrington again, calling his refusal "ungallant" and "unfriendly." Miss Bell, looking down at her teacup with long, dark lashes, remained silent. Warrington blushed furiously but said nothing. Encouraged by the Major, Miss Bell finally spoke, asking if Warrington would join them. In a moment of sudden, painful sincerity, Warrington confessed that he would go anywhere she asked. The Major erupted in delight, shouting "Bravo!" and shaking hands with the young couple, sealing the bargain. Just as the Major announced the agreement to the returning Helen, her pale, frightened face illuminated by the candlelight, Miss Bell pulled her hand away, leaving the room in a tense silence.
