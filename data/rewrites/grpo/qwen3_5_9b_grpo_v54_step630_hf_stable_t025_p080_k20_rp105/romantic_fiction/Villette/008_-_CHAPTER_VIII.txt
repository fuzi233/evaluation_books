Delivered into the care of the *maîtresse*, I was ushered through a long, narrow corridor into a kitchen that was immaculate yet profoundly alien. It lacked a hearth or an oven; instead, a massive black furnace dominated one corner, a mechanical substitute for the domestic fires I knew. A strange sensation of relief washed over me when, rather than being abandoned to the culinary chaos, I was led to a small, enclosed room designated a "cabinet." A cook, dressed in a jacket, short petticoat, and wooden clogs, presented my supper: meat of an unidentified nature, served in a tart, acidic sauce that somehow tasted pleasant; chopped potatoes seasoned with a mystery blend I suspected to be vinegar and sugar; a slice of bread and butter; and a baked pear. Hunger conquered my apprehension, and I ate with gratitude.

Following the evening prayer, Madame Beck descended to inspect me once more. She commanded me to follow her upstairs. We traversed a series of peculiar dormitories—later learned to be former nun's cells, given the building's ancient lineage—and passed through an oratory. It was a long, low, gloomy chamber where a pale crucifix hung against the wall, flanked by two tapers burning with dim vigilance. She guided me to an apartment where three children slept in tiny beds. The air was stifling, heated by a stove, and thick with an overpowering scent that defied the circumstances: a pungent mixture of smoke and spirituous essence, unmistakably the smell of whisky.

Beside a table, where a candle guttered to nothingness, sat a coarse woman in a garish striped silk dress and a heavy apron, fast asleep. Completing the tableau, a bottle and an empty glass rested at her elbow. Madame Beck surveyed this scene with profound calm. Neither smile nor scowl marred her grave composure; no trace of anger, disgust, or surprise disturbed her equanimity. She did not even wake the woman. With serene gesture, she indicated a fourth bed as mine. Extinguishing the candle and replacing it with a night-lamp, she glided through an inner door, leaving it ajar to reveal her own chamber—a large, well-appointed apartment discernible through the opening.

That night, my devotions were entirely of thanksgiving. My journey had been strangely directed, my provisions unexpectedly secured. It was hard to believe less than forty-eight hours had passed since I left London, a passenger-bird with no guardian save the vague hope of the clouds.

I was a light sleeper. In the dead of night, I awoke to find the room hushed, yet occupied by a white figure: Madame in her night-dress. Moving without sound, she visited the three children, then approached me. Feigning sleep, I watched her study me for a long time. A curious pantomime ensued. She sat upon the edge of my bed for perhaps a quarter-hour, gazing at my face. Drawing near, she lifted my cap, turned back the border to expose my hair, and examined my hand resting on the covers. Turning to the chair at the foot of the bed where my clothes lay, she began to inspect them. Hearing her touch the fabric, I cautiously opened my eyes, driven by curiosity to see the extent of her scrutiny. It was thorough. Every article was examined. I divined her motive: to judge the wearer's station, means, and neatness through the garments. The intent was not malicious, but the method was intrusive. She turned my pocket inside out, counted the coins in my purse, and opened a small memorandum book, coolly perusing its contents until she extracted a small, plaited lock of Miss Marchmont's grey hair. She paid special attention to a bunch of three keys belonging to my trunk, desk, and work-box. Watching her withdraw to her own room, I saw them return moments later, bearing the impress of their wards in wax upon the toilet of the adjoining chamber. Once everything was restored with decent order, my property returned and clothes refolded, I waited for the verdict. What conclusions had she drawn? Were they favorable? Her face, now as stone-cold as the night, offered no response. The human warmth I had sensed in the salon was gone.

Having fulfilled her duty, she rose noiseless as a shadow and moved toward her chamber. At the door, she paused, fixing her gaze on the sleeping woman who snored loudly. Mrs. Sweeny—presumably Anglicized or Hibernian, perhaps Sweeney—met Madame Beck's eye with an immutable purpose. Madame's visitations for shortcomings might be slow, but they were certain. This was distinctly un-English; I was truly in a foreign land.

The following day revealed Mrs. Sweeny's origins. She had introduced herself as an English lady of reduced circumstances, a native of Middlesex speaking with a pure metropolitan accent. Madame Beck, relying on her infallible methods of verification, displayed singular intrepidity in hiring immediately, a trait proven in my own case. She accepted Mrs. Sweeny as nursery-governess for her three children. It was clear the lady was Irish, her station ambiguous. She boldly claimed to have raised the son and daughter of a marquis. I suspected she might have been a hanger-on, nurse, or washerwoman in an Irish family, her speech a smothered dialect overlaid with mincing Cockney inflections. She possessed a wardrobe of suspicious splendor: stiff, costly silks that fit her poorly, caps with real lace, and, the crown jewel of her inventory, a real Indian shawl—"un véritable cachemire," Madame said with unmixed reverence. Without this shawl, she likely would not have survived two days; by virtue of it alone, she maintained her position for a month.

However, upon learning I was to replace her, Mrs. Sweeny revealed her true nature, rising in her full power against Madame Beck. She descended upon me with concentrated weight. Madame bore this revelation with stoic endurance, so composed that I, ashamed of my own turmoil, forced myself to remain calm. When Madame briefly absented herself, a police agent appeared ten minutes later. Mrs. Sweeny and her effects were removed. Madame's brow remained unruffled; not a sharply accented word escaped her lips.

This brisk dismissal was settled before breakfast. Order was given, the policeman arrived, the mutineer expelled. The children's chamber was fumigated, cleansed, and aired; every trace of Mrs. Sweeny, including the fine essence and spiritual fragrance that marked her presence, was annihilated from Rue Fossette. All this transpired between Madame Beck's emergence from her chamber and her coolly pouring her first cup of coffee.

At noon, I was summoned to dress Madame. My role was a hybrid of governess and lady's-maid. Until then, she had haunted the house in her wrapping-gown, shawl, and silent slippers—a custom that would puzzle any English schoolmistress. Her hair, auburn and unmixed with grey despite her forty years, puzzled me. Seeing my embarrassment, she took the brush from my hand, arranging it herself without ungentleness. In other toiletries, she half-directed, half-aided me, displaying no temper or impatience. This was the first and last time I dressed her; henceforth, the portress Rosine assumed that duty.

Attired, Madame Beck appeared a figure of short, stout stature yet possessing a peculiar grace derived from proportion. Her complexion was fresh and sanguine, her eyes blue and serene. Her dark silk dress fitted her as only a French seamstress could achieve. She looked well, though somewhat bourgeois, and indeed she was. A strange harmony pervaded her person, yet her face offered contrast. Her features were stern; her forehead high but narrow, expressing capacity and some benevolence but no expansiveness. Her peaceful, watchful eye never knew the fire kindled in the heart or the softness flowing from it. Her mouth was hard, capable of a grimace, her lips thin. I felt she would be the right sort of Minos in petticoats.

In the long run, I discovered she was something else in petticoats as well. Her name was Modeste Maria Beck, née Kint; it ought to have been Ignacia. She was a charitable woman who did great good. There was never a mistress with milder rules. I was told she never remonstrated with the intolerable Mrs. Sweeny despite her drunkenness and disorder, yet Mrs. Sweeny departed the moment her removal became convenient. Similarly, masters and teachers were never faulted, yet they vanished and were replaced, none explaining how.

The establishment was both a pensionnat and externat. Day-pupils numbered over one hundred; boarders, about twenty. Madame possessed high administrative powers, ruling all these, plus four teachers, eight masters, six servants, and three children, while managing the pupils' parents and friends to perfection. She did so without apparent effort, bustle, fatigue, or fever. Always occupied, she was rarely idle. She had her own system for managing this machinery, a pretty system exemplified by turning my pocket inside out and reading my private memoranda. "Surveillance" and "espionage" were her watchwords.

Still, Madame knew honesty and liked it, provided it did not obstruct her will or interest. She respected "Angleterre"; regarding "les Anglaises," she preferred no women of other nations about her children if she could help it.

Often in the evening, after a day of plotting, spying, and receiving reports, she would come to my room, a trace of real weariness on her brow. She would sit and listen while the children recited their prayers in English—the Lord's Prayer and the hymn beginning "Gentle Jesus"—which these little Catholics were permitted to repeat at my knee. After putting them to bed, she would talk to me about England and Englishwomen, praising their superior intelligence and probity. She showed good sense and sound opinions. She seemed to know that keeping girls in distrustful restraint and blind ignorance was not the best way to raise honest women, yet she averred that ruinous consequences would ensue with continental children accustomed to restraint; relaxation, however guarded, would be fatally misunderstood. She was sick of the means she had to use but felt compelled to employ them. After discoursing with dignity, she would move away on her "souliers de silence," gliding ghost-like through the house, watching and spying everywhere, peering through keyholes and listening behind doors.

After all, Madame's system was not bad. Let me do her justice. Her arrangements for the physical well-being of her scholars were unsurpassed. Minds were not overtasked; lessons were distributed well and made incomparably easy. There was liberty of amusement and provision for exercise, keeping the girls healthy. Food was abundant and good; no pale or puny faces were seen in Rue Fossette. She never grudged a holiday, allowing ample time for sleeping, dressing, washing, and eating. Her method was easy, liberal, salutary, and rational. Many an austere English schoolmistress would do well to imitate her, and many would be glad to, if exacting English parents would permit it.

Ruling by espionage, Madame had her staff of spies. She knew the quality of her tools perfectly. While she would not scruple to handle the dirtiest for a dirty occasion—flinging them aside like refuse rind after squeezing an orange—I had seen her fastidious in seeking pure metal for clean uses. Once a bloodless, rustless instrument was found, she cherished it, keeping it in silk and cotton-wool. Yet, woe to anyone who relied on her beyond the point of her interest. Interest was the master-key of Madame's nature, the mainspring of her motives, the alpha and omega of her life. I have seen her feelings appealed to and smiled in half-pity, half-scorn at the appellants. None ever gained her ear through that channel, nor swayed her purpose. To attempt to touch her heart was the surest way to rouse her antipathy and make her a secret foe. It proved to her she had no heart to be touched; it reminded her where she was impotent and dead. Never was the distinction between charity and mercy better exemplified. Devoid of sympathy, she possessed sufficient rational benevolence, giving readily to people she had never seen, or rather to classes than individuals. "Pour les pauvres," she opened her purse freely; against the poor man, as a rule, she kept it closed. In philanthropic schemes for society at large, she took a cheerful part; no private sorrow touched her. No force or mass of suffering concentrated in one heart had power to pierce hers. Not the agony in Gethsemane, not the death on Calvary, could have wrung one tear from her eyes.

I say again, Madame was a very great and capable woman. That school offered her too limited a sphere; she ought to have swayed a nation, led a turbulent legislative assembly. Nobody could browbeat her, irritate her nerves, exhaust her patience, or overreach her astuteness. In her single person, she could comprise the duties of a first minister and a superintendent of police. Wise, firm, faithless; secret, crafty, passionless; watchful and inscrutable; acute and insensate—yet perfectly decorous—what more could be desired?

The sensible reader will not suppose I gained all this knowledge in one month or half-year. No! What I saw at first was the thriving outside of a large, flourishing educational establishment. Here was a great house full of healthy, lively girls, well-dressed and many handsome, gaining knowledge by a marvellously easy method without painful exertion or useless waste of spirits. They did not make rapid progress, but they were always employed and never oppressed. Here was a corps of teachers and masters more stringently tasked, doing the real head-labor to save the pupils, yet relieved in quick succession when work was severe. In short, this was a foreign school, its life, movement, and variety making it a complete and charming contrast to many English institutions.

Behind the house lay a large garden. In summer, the pupils almost lived outdoors among rose-bushes and fruit-trees.
