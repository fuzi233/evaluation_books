Marianne had convinced herself that she ought to consider it a monstrous failure of duty if she had slept a single hour since separating from Willoughby. Yet, when morning broke, the shame she felt at facing her family vanished, replaced by a profound physical exhaustion that demanded rest above all else. The storm of emotion inside her had burned itself out, leaving her body drained and her spirit strangely quiet. She rose with a pounding headache, her throat dry and her tongue heavy, refusing food or comfort from her mother and sisters. Her sensibility, usually a torrent, had curdled into a silent, aching void.

By breakfast, she had fled alone, wandering the streets of Allenham like a ghost haunting her own memories. For hours, she replayed every melody they had ever shared, staring at the staves of sheet music Willoughby had penned for her until the ink seemed to bleed into her soul. She sang songs that now sounded like funeral dirges, her voice breaking on sobs that she could not suppress. Books, too, became tools of torture; she devoured only those they had read together, feeding herself a diet of nostalgia that left her hollow. This self-inflicted torment lasted for days, slowly draining into a deeper, calmer melancholy, yet the habit remained. She returned again and again to the piano, to the walks, to the silent meditations, seeking the familiar sting of loss.

There was no letter from Willoughby, and Marianne, in her delirium, did not expect one. Her mother, however, grew alarmed. "Where is his letter?" she asked, her anxiety mounting. "We must know."

Elinor, usually so steady, found herself trembling. "Mother, surely you understand why he writes nothing?"

"Indeed, I do not!" Mrs. Dashwood insisted, her eyes filling with tears. "And I cannot believe there is some terrible misunderstanding. Remember, Elinor, how often Sir John intercepts our mail. Perhaps he took Willoughby's letters and hid them. Or perhaps he delivered them and we simply haven't seen them. There must be a reason for this silence."

"But, Mother," Elinor pressed gently, "if he is not coming, if he has changed his mind..."

"Nonsense!" Mrs. Dashwood interrupted, waving a handkerchief. "Do not let your poor head fill you with such gloomy fancies. If Marianne loved him, and he loved her, the world knows it. And if anything has gone wrong, it is not for us to guess. We must trust in their hearts. Besides, it would be so cruel to ask Marianne directly if she is engaged. It would break her. She trusts me implicitly; she tells me everything. I would never dream of forcing a confession from her unless it was absolutely necessary. She loves me, and I love her, and I will wait."

Elinor watched her mother with a mixture of pity and frustration. Her mother's romantic delicacy blinded her to the plain facts of human nature. Common sense, practical care, and prudent judgment were drowned under the tide of sentimental fantasy.

For several days, Willoughby's name was barely spoken. Even Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, who usually enjoyed poking fun at the Dashwoods' sorrows, held their tongues, perhaps sensing the depth of Marianne's despair. Then, one evening, Mrs. Dashwood, holding a copy of Shakespeare, sighed, "We never finished *Hamlet*, Marianne. Willoughby left before we could. We shall save it for when he returns... though it may be months, perhaps years, before that happens."

"Months!" Marianne cried, sitting up suddenly, her eyes bright with a fierce hope. "No! Not months, and certainly not years. He will be back."

Mrs. Dashwood smiled, relieved, thinking her daughter's spirit was finally lifting. But it was Elinor who saw the truth behind the words. A flicker of certainty, of knowledge regarding his intentions, shone in Marianne's gaze.

A week later, Elinor managed to coax Marianne out of her room for a walk. Usually, Marianne avoided her sisters, stealing away to lonely lanes or climbing steep hills where she could cry in solitude. Today, however, Elinor prevailed. Together, they walked along the road through the valley, mostly in silence, as Marianne's mind was too heavy for conversation. As they reached the edge of the valley, looking back toward the cottage from a vantage point they had rarely visited, a figure appeared on the horizon.

"It is him!" Marianne gasped, her breath catching. "It is indeed! I know it!" She turned to run forward, her heart soaring.

"Elinor!" she cried, pointing. "Look!"

"I think you are mistaken, Marianne," Elinor called out, trying to calm her. "That man is not tall enough, and his carriage is not quite right. It cannot be Willoughby."

"He has the air! He has the coat, the horse! He is coming just as fast as I hoped!" Marianne rushed ahead, eager to embrace him.

Elinor hurried to catch up, desperate to shield her sister from disappointment. Within thirty yards, the illusion shattered. Marianne froze, her heart sinking with a crushing weight. With a sob rising in her chest, she turned sharply to flee. Just then, two voices called out, followed by a third, warm and familiar. Marianne spun around, tears streaming down her face, to see Edward Ferrars standing there.

In that moment, Edward was the only man who could forgive her mistake. He was the only one who could draw a smile from lips that had been sealed in grief. She wiped away her tears, forcing a radiant grin, and for a brief, shining instant, she forgot her anguish, consumed by the joy of seeing him.

Edward dismounted, handing his horse to a servant, and walked back toward Barton Cottage with the sisters. He was greeted with effusive warmth, though Marianne's enthusiasm far exceeded Elinor's. To Marianne, who had grown weary of the cold, reserved demeanor Edward had displayed at Norland, this meeting felt like a continuation of a strange, icy game. Yet, Edward seemed ill at ease. He looked confused, offering only stilted responses, avoiding eye contact, and showing no sign of the delight one might expect from a lover returning home. He treated Elinor with polite distance, devoid of any tender affection.

Marianne watched him with growing confusion and irritation. Every word he spoke, every hesitant movement, struck her as a rebuke compared to Willoughby's passionate, unrestrained nature. As the initial shock wore off, she began to feel a sharp dislike for Edward, a sentiment that quickly drove her thoughts back to the man she truly desired.

"Why did you not tell me sooner?" Marianne asked, her voice tight. "Did you come straight from London?"

"No," Edward replied, looking slightly distressed. "I have been in Devonshire for nearly two weeks, staying with friends near Plymouth."

"And you did not see Elinor?" Marianne asked, surprised.

"Not exactly," Edward murmured, glancing away. "I was staying elsewhere most of the time."

"Haven't you been to Norland recently?" Elinor interjected.

"Yes, about a month ago."

"How does dear, dear Norland look?" Marianne asked, her tone shifting to something lighter, almost playful.

"Dear, dear Norland," Elinor echoed softly, "looks much as it always does at this time of year. Covered in dead leaves."

"Oh!" Marianne exclaimed, her eyes lighting up. "With what transport I used to watch them fall! How I delighted in walking among them as the wind scattered them! What feelings they inspire—the season, the air! Now, though, there is no one to appreciate them. They are merely seen as a nuisance, swept aside and hidden."

"It is not everyone who shares your passion for fallen leaves," Elinor said gently.

"No," Marianne admitted, sinking into a thoughtful silence. "My feelings are rarely understood. But sometimes..." She paused, lost in a reverie, before shaking herself free. "Now, Edward, look at this." She pointed toward the valley. "Here is Barton Valley. Try to remain tranquil if you can. Look at those hills! Have you ever seen their like? To the left lies Barton Park, nestled among the woods. You can see the end of the house. And there, beneath the distant hill, stands our cottage."

"It is a beautiful country," Edward agreed politely. "Though I fear these valleys must become quite muddy in winter."

"How can you speak of mud when you behold such beauty?" Marianne asked, genuinely puzzled.

"Well," Edward smiled faintly, "among the other objects before me, I must confess I see a rather filthy lane."

"How strange," Marianne whispered to herself as they continued walking.

"Do you find the neighborhood agreeable? Are the Middletons pleasant people?"

"No, not entirely," Marianne answered quietly. "We are situated most unluckily."

"Marianne!" Elinor exclaimed. "How can you say such a thing? How unjust you are! They are a very respectable family, Mr. Ferrars, and they have treated us with the utmost kindness. Do you not remember all the happy days we owe them?"

"No," Marianne said in a low voice, her gaze fixed on the ground. "Nor do I forget the painful ones."
