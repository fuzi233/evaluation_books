Tucked in the quietest corner of Barton Park’s sunlit drawing room, where Marianne’s fierce, thundering concerto at the far end of the piano would swallow every soft word they spoke, Elinor drew a steadying breath before she began, her tone firm but kind, no trace of the roiling ache she’d carried in her chest since Lucy had spilled her secret engagement a week prior.
“I would be failing to honour the trust you placed in me,” she said gently, “if I pretended I had no wish to hear more, no care for how this all will turn out for you. I won’t waste breath apologizing for bringing it up again.”
Lucy’s whole face lit up with relief. “Oh, thank you! You’ve taken such a weight off my shoulders. I’ve been half-convinced for days I offended you with what I told you that Monday, that you’d decided to go cold on me.”
“Offended me? How could you ever think that?” Elinor’s reply was utterly, unfeignedly sincere. “I would never want you to feel that. The fact you trusted me enough to share such a vulnerable secret is the highest compliment I could be paid.”
Lucy’s small, sharp eyes narrowed, bright with meaning. “And yet I swear, your manner felt so distant, so displeased that day, I was miserable for hours afterwards. I scolded myself for being so bold as to dump all my troubles on you. But I’m so glad it was just my silly imagination running wild. If you knew how often I turn this engagement over in my head, every single hour of every day, you’d forgive any little impropriety from me, I’m sure—talking to you about it is the only relief I’ve had in four long years.”
“I don’t doubt that for a second,” Elinor said softly. “You have nothing to fear from me sharing this. But your situation is so terribly stuck, isn’t it? You’ll need every scrap of affection the two of you share to get through all these hardships ahead. Mr. Ferrars has almost no independent fortune, I take it? He’s entirely dependent on his mother’s goodwill?”
“He’s only got two thousand pounds of his own,” Lucy said, shaking her head. “It would be madness to marry on that, I know. For my part, I could live on pennies and never complain, I’ve always been used to very little. But I love him too much to be selfish, to rob him of every penny his mother would leave him if he marries against her wishes. We have to wait. It could be years, and years. Any other man would get tired of that, but I know nothing in the world could shake Edward’s loyalty to me.”
That conviction must carry you through so much, Elinor thought, and said it out loud, even as her chest ached. “And he must be just as sure of your loyalty, too. If that kind of mutual love had faded over four years of being apart, like so many engagements do, you would be in a truly pitiable spot right now.”
Lucy looked up quickly, but Elinor kept her face perfectly neutral, no flicker of the grief that sat heavy behind her eyes.
“Edward’s love for me has already been tested over and over, all those years we were apart after we got engaged,” Lucy said proudly. “It stood the test perfectly. I’d be a fool to doubt it now. He’s never once given me a single reason to worry.”
Elinor couldn’t decide if she wanted to smile bitterly or sigh. Lucy, meanwhile, kept right on talking.
“I’m naturally a jealous sort, you know. We live such different lives—he’s out in society all the time, I’m stuck in the country, we’re apart for months on end. I’d notice instantly if anything changed in him: if he was low for no reason, if he talked about some other lady too much, if he seemed less happy at Longstaple than he used to be. I’m not usually sharp about that kind of thing, but where Edward’s concerned? I could never be tricked.”
All very well for you to say, Elinor thought, but neither of us is fooled, are we?
After a short silence, she asked, “What’s your plan, then? Are you just… waiting for Mrs. Ferrars to die? That’s such a bleak, awful thing to stake your whole future on. Won’t Edward ever tell her the truth, take a little temporary anger from her now instead of condemning both of you to years and years of limbo?”
“Only if that anger would be temporary!” Lucy’s face fell. “Mrs. Ferrars is so proud, so stubborn. If she found out about us, she’d turn around and leave every single penny she owns to Robert, Edward’s younger brother. The thought of that, for Edward’s sake, makes me too scared to rush anything.”
“For your own sake too, surely? That’s not selfishness, that’s common sense.”
Lucy stared at Elinor for a long moment, silent.
“Have you ever met Mr. Robert Ferrars?” Elinor asked, to break the pause.
“Not at all, I’ve never even seen him. But I hear he’s nothing like his brother—silly, and a terrible, preening coxcomb.”
“A great coxcomb!” Miss Steele’s voice cut across the room, sharp, as she’d caught the word when Marianne paused for half a bar in her music. “Oh, I know what you two are gossiping about—your favourite beaux, of course!”
“No, sister, you’re quite wrong,” Lucy huffed. “Our favourite beaux are not coxcombs at all.”
“I can vouch for Miss Dashwood’s, that’s for certain,” Mrs. Jennings cackled from her armchair, having half-woken up from her doze. “That young man of hers is one of the most modest, sweet-mannered lads I’ve ever met. And Lucy’s just a sly little thing, she won’t ever tell anyone who she’s sweet on.”
“Oh, I’m sure Lucy’s beau is just as modest and well-behaved as Miss Dashwood’s,” Miss Steele said, winking pointedly around the room.
Elinor blushed hot, despite every effort not to. Lucy bit down on her lip and glowered daggers at her older sister.
Nobody said a word for a long stretch, until Lucy leaned in closer, her voice barely a murmur, even with Marianne’s thunderous concerto roaring over them.
“I have a plan, actually. I’m going to tell you the secret, because you’re part of it. You’ve spent enough time with Edward to know he’d far rather be a clergyman than do any other work, right? My idea is that he takes orders as soon as he possibly can, and then you could use your influence—out of your friendship for him, and I hope out of some kindness to me too—to persuade your brother John to give him the Norland living. I hear it pays very well, and the current priest isn’t long for this world. That would be more than enough for us to marry on, and we can sort out the rest with time and luck.”
“I would always be happy to do anything I could to show Mr. Ferrars how much I respect him,” Elinor said, trying to keep her tone even. “But don’t you see? My favour wouldn’t be needed at all here. Edward’s Mrs. John Dashwood’s own brother. That’s more than enough recommendation for her husband.”
“But Mrs. John Dashwood would never want Edward to go into the church,” Lucy said.
“Then I’m afraid my influence wouldn’t do a single thing to change her mind.”
They fell silent again, for several minutes, before Lucy heaved a great, heavy sigh.
“I think the wisest thing we could do is just call the whole engagement off, right? We’re blocked on every side. It would hurt like mad for a little while, but we’d both be happier in the long run. Won’t you give me your advice, Miss Dashwood?”
“No,” Elinor said, smiling to hide how wildly her heart was hammering. “I can’t give you advice on that. You know perfectly well my opinion wouldn’t mean anything to you unless it lined up with what you already wanted to do.”
“You do me wrong,” Lucy said, very solemn. “I respect your judgment more than anyone else’s in the whole world. If you looked at me and said, ‘Break off your engagement to Edward Ferrars right now, it’ll make both of you far happier,’ I’d do it, no question.”
Elinor blushed, thinking of how false that promise was, coming from the woman who was meant to marry the man she loved. “That compliment would make me far too nervous to give any opinion at all. You’re making me out to have far too much power. Splitting up two people who love each other? That’s far too big a responsibility for someone who’s no part of this relationship.”
“Exactly! Because you’re not involved, you’re impartial,” Lucy said, a little stung, leaning heavy on that last word. “That’s why your opinion would carry so much weight. If you were biased by your own feelings, no one would care what you thought.”
Elinor decided it was better not to reply at all, not to risk making them both say something unforgivable, and half made up her mind never to bring the subject up ever again. Another long pause followed, until Lucy broke it, back to her usual sweet, obliging tone.
“Will you be in London this winter, Miss Dashwood?”
“Certainly not,” Elinor said.
“Oh, what a shame!” Lucy’s eyes brightened, like she’d just got very good news. “I’d have loved so much to run into you there! But I’m sure you’ll end up going anyway, your brother and sister will invite you to stay with them.”
“Even if they do, I won’t be able to accept.”
“How terrible for us!” Lucy sighed. “Anne and I are heading up to London at the end of January, to stay with some relations who’ve been begging us to visit for years. I’d never bother going if I didn’t have a reason—Edward will be there in February. London wouldn’t have a single charm for me otherwise, I’m far too dull for parties there.”
Before Elinor could reply, the first rubber of cards finished, and she was called over to take a seat at the table. The private, tense conversation between the two women was over, and neither of them minded that much—nothing they’d said had made either of them like the other a single bit more than before. As Elinor picked up her hand of cards, her mind was racing with a miserable, heavy certainty: Edward wasn’t just stuck to an engagement with a woman who didn’t truly love him, he had no chance at even a tolerably happy marriage. Lucy was only holding him to the promise for her own security, and she knew perfectly well he was already weary of it.
From that day on, Elinor never brought the subject up again. Lucy, though, never missed a chance to slip it into conversation whenever she could, gushing about how happy she was every time a letter from Edward arrived. Elinor always treated those mentions with perfect calm and caution, and steered the conversation away as soon as politeness would let her. Those little talks were a treat Lucy didn’t deserve, and far too dangerous for Elinor’s own fragile self-control.
The Miss Steeles’ visit to Barton Park dragged on far longer than the original invitation ever intended. Everyone at the park doted on them, Sir John threw a fit at the very mention of them leaving, and even though they kept swearing every single week that they had urgent, pre-planned engagements back in Exeter that they absolutely had to return for immediately, they ended up staying almost two full months. They stayed right through all the feasting, private balls, and grand dinner parties that mark the Christmas season, that most festive time of year that demands endless celebrations to show how important it is.
