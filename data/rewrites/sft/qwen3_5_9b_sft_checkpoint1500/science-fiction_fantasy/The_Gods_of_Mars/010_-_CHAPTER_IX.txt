The raw, frightened confession of love that tore from her lips struck a chord within me, yet it burned with a shame I could not shake. It was a humiliation born of my own thoughtlessness; surely some careless word or action had led her to believe I returned her affections. I had never been adept at courting, preferring the brutal arts of combat to the delicate posturing of mooning over a lover's trinket or kissing a withered bloom that smelled of rot. Faced now with the gaze of this beautiful, terrified young girl, I was paralyzed, wishing a thousand times for the chaos of the Dead Sea Bottoms rather than having to explain the truth.

There was no choice but to speak, and so I did, albeit with clumsy awkwardness. Gently, I freed her hands from my neck, keeping them clasped in mine as I revealed the nature of my devotion. Of all the womankind I had encountered across two worlds, none compared to Dejah Thoris. She alone held my heart.

The revelation did not soothe her. Instead, she sprang from the seat like a maddened tigress, panting, her face twisted into a mask of horrific malice. Her eyes flamed with a terrifying intensity.

"Dog!" she hissed, the sound vibrating with venom. "Blasphemer! Do you suppose Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, begs for mercy? She commands! What is your puny, transient passion for that vile creature you favored in your other life? Phaidor has honored you with her love, and you have cast it aside. Ten thousand atrocities cannot atone for the insult you have wrought. The woman you call Dejah Thoris shall die the most terrible of deaths. You have signed her warrant.

"And you! You shall become the meanest slave in the service of the goddess you sought to mock. Terrors and humiliations shall pile upon you until you grovel at my feet, begging for release."

Her plan was flawless, a grand tragedy scripted from start to finish. It stunned me to realize how divine beauty could coexist with such fiendish vindictiveness. Yet, I perceived a flaw in her scheme—a single oversight regarding her circumstances—and, intending merely to allow her to adjust her strategy to something practical, I pointed to the nearest porthole.

She had forgotten everything around her, lost in her rage. A single glance at the dark, churning waters outside shattered her composure. Crumpling onto a low bench, she buried her face in her arms, weeping with the helpless sobbing of a broken child rather than the wrath of an all-powerful deity.

Down, down we sank, until the thick glass of the windows grew hot from the pressure of the water. Clearly, we had descended far beneath the Martian crust. Suddenly, the descent halted. I heard the propellers churn the water at our stern, driving us forward at high speed through the darkness. Though the cabin was pitch black, the glow from the portholes and the beam of a powerful searchlight cutting through the tunnel revealed that we were navigating a narrow, rock-lined throat.

Moments later, the engines died. We stopped, then began to ascend rapidly. As we climbed, the external light grew brighter until we paused.

Xodar entered the cabin with his men. "Come," he ordered. We followed him through a hatch opened by a sailor.

We stepped into a subterranean vault, the center of which held our submarine, bobbing in a pool with only her black hull visible above the water. Around the pool lay a flat platform, and beyond it, the cavern walls rose vertically before curving inward to form a low ceiling. Several entrances pierced the walls near the ledge, leading to dim, shadowed passages.

Our captors guided us toward one of these tunnels. After a brief walk, they halted before a steel cage suspended at the base of a shaft stretching endlessly upward. This was an ordinary elevator, operated by massive magnets suspended at the summit. An electrical regulator controlled the magnetic volume and thus the car's velocity. On Mars, where gravity is weak, these elevators could plummet with sickening speed, especially when ascending against the powerful pull of the overhead magnets.

As soon as the door slammed shut, we shot upward with terrifying rapidity, skidding to a halt almost instantly at the top landing.

Emerging from the terminal building, we stepped into a veritable paradise, a landscape so gorgeously colored that human language failed to capture its splendor. Imagine scarlet grass, trees of ivory stem adorned with brilliant purple blossoms, and pathways paved with crushed rubies, emeralds, turquoises, and diamonds. Before us stood a magnificent temple of burnished gold, wrought with designs beyond mortal comprehension. But what words could describe the glorious hues unknown to Earth, the scintillations of rays that defied description, emanating from thousands of nameless Martian jewels?

Even my eyes, hardened by the barbaric opulence of a Jeddak's court, were awestruck.

Phaidor's eyes widened in shock. "The Temple of Issus," she whispered, half to herself.

Xodar watched us with a grim smile, a mixture of amusement and malicious gloat. The gardens teemed with brilliantly bound Black men and women, while Red and White females served their every whim. The princesses and goddesses of the Outer World and the Temples of the Therns had been stolen to enslave the Blacks.

Moving through this kaleidoscope of beauty toward the temple, we were stopped at the main entrance by a line of armed guards. Xodar conferred briefly with an officer who approached, and together they entered the sanctuary, disappearing for some time.

Upon their return, they announced that Issus wished to see the daughter of Matai Shang and the strange creature from another world, formerly a Prince of Helium.

We traversed endless corridors of impossible beauty, passing magnificent apartments and noble halls, until we were halted in a vast chamber at the temple's heart. An officer marched to a large door at the far end, signaling silently. Immediately, the door swung open, and another richly dressed courtier emerged.

He directed us to drop to our hands and knees, backs turned toward the room we were to enter. The doors flew open. Warned under penalty of instant death not to turn our heads, we were commanded to crawl backward into the presence of Issus.

Never in my life had I occupied such a humiliating posture. Only my love for Dejah Thoris and a fragile hope that I might see her again kept me from rising to face the Goddess of the First Born as a gentleman would, meeting my foes with bloodshed rather than crawling submission.

After crawling hundreds of feet in this disgraceful manner, our escorts signaled a halt.

"Let them rise," a voice boomed behind us—a thin, wavering tone that nonetheless carried the weight of centuries of command.

"Rise," the escort ordered, "but do not face Issus."

"The woman pleases me," the thin voice echoed after a pause. "She shall serve me for the allotted time. As for the man, you may return him to the Isle of Shador, lying against the northern shore of the Sea of Omean. Let the woman turn and gaze upon Issus, knowing that those of the lower orders who behold the holy vision of her radiant face survive the blinding glory for but a single year."

I caught Phaidor's eye in the corner of my vision. She had turned a ghastly pallid hue. Slowly, as if drawn by an invisible, irresistible tether, she turned. Standing mere inches from me, her bare arm brushed against mine as she finally confronted Issus, Goddess of Life Eternal.

I could not see Phaidor's face as her eyes rested upon the Supreme Deity of Mars, but I felt the violent shudder that racked her, transmitted through the trembling flesh of the arm touching my own.

"It must be a dazzling loveliness," I thought, "to move the breast of one so radiant as Phaidor."

"Let the woman remain," Issus spoke, her voice devoid of warmth. "Remove the man. Go."

The officer's heavy hand landed on my shoulder. Obeying his instructions, I dropped to my knees once more and crawled from the Presence. It was my first audience with a deity, and I must confess I was hardly impressed—save for the ridiculous spectacle I cut scrambling on my marrow bones.

Outside the chamber, the doors closed, and I was bidden to rise. Xodar rejoined me, and together we slowly retraced our steps toward the gardens.

"You spared my life when you easily might have taken it," he said after a stretch of silence. "And I would aid you if I could. I can make your existence here slightly more bearable, but your fate is inevitable. You may never hope to return to the Outer World."

"What will be my fate?" I asked.

"That depends largely on Issus. So long as she does not summon you and reveal her countenance, you may live for years in a mild form of bondage as I can arrange."

"Why would she summon me?"

"She often employs men of the lower orders for amusement. A fighter like yourself would provide excellent sport for the monthly rites of the temple. Men pit against men, and against beasts, for the edification of Issus and the replenishment of her larder."
