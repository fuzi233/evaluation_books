Fairy folk a-listening
Hear the seed sprout in the spring.
And for music to their dance
Hear the hedgerows wake from trance,
Sap that trembles into buds
Sending little rhythmic floods
Of fairy sound in fairy ears.
Thus all beauty that appears
Has birth as sound to finer sense
And lighter-clad intelligence.

Gwendolen's thoughts were consumed by Deronda, yet he scarcely thought of her. She spent her days wondering about the substance of his mind, the nature of his "ideas about things," and the rhythm of his existence. It was a futile endeavor, much like a lapdog trying to comprehend the grand motives of a roaming pack; just as impossible for her to conceive of his life being steered by the ancient destiny of the Jewish people was it for him to imagine himself rising on a brazen horse to vanish into the stars.

Burdened by a crushing sense of inferiority, Gwendolen inevitably inflated her own significance in his eyes. She assumed that only the aged and wise fail to recognize their own anxieties reflected in others, yet she, with her youthful isolation, could not resist dwelling on the faintest signs of interest he showed her. She mistook the color and scale of his attention, believing herself to be the sole object of his submission.

What counsel would he offer? "He told me I must cultivate interest in others, acquire knowledge, and care for the highest things—but where do I begin?" She pondered which books he would recommend for her private study, recalling the famous authors she had either avoided or found impenetrable. A mischievous thought crossed her mind: *Could she ask if these were the "medicine for the mind"?* But shame quickly silenced her. Instead, when she was certain of privacy, she selected a miscellany of heavyweights—Descartes, Bacon, Locke, Butler, Burke, Guizot. As a clever young woman of education, she knew these were ornaments of mankind, certain that Deronda had mastered them, and hoping that by rapidly consuming them, she might elevate her perspective to his level.

Yet, time for such intellectual voyages was scarce. She was perpetually required to be the scene of Mrs. Grandcourt, constantly monitored by a husband whose tenacity had found a new motive: to ensure his marriage served every end he chose, and the more completely he crushed any opposing will within her. Whatever rebellion churned inside her, she could not resolve to fail in her performance. No feeling had yet softened her to the thought of any act, word, or glance that might confess her true state to the world. Her greatest fear was a violent impulse that would betray her involuntarily. This very will to remain silent in all other directions had poured a frantic impetuosity into her confidences toward Deronda, to whom her thoughts continually turned as a sanctuary against herself. Her riding, hunting, and socializing were performed with a spirit of grim achievement, devoid of youthful zest, so that in the weeks of the new year at Diplow, Mrs. Grandcourt was regarded as wearing her honors with triumphant ease.

"She disguises it under an air of taking everything as a matter of course," observed Mrs. Arrowpoint. "A stranger might suppose she had condescended rather than risen. I always noticed that doubleness in her."

For her mother, Gwendolen strove to project complete satisfaction. Poor Mrs. Davilow was so deceived that she interpreted the cold distance Gwendolen maintained—as enforced by Grandcourt's handsome but distant provision—as a comparative indifference born of her daughter's new marital interests. Being fetched for lunch and dinner alongside the Gascoignes, driven back shortly after breakfast, and receiving only brief calls where her husband waited outside on horseback or in the carriage, constituted the entirety of her permitted intercourse.

The truth was, when Gwendolen twice proposed inviting her mother with Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne, Grandcourt had first remained silent, then drawled, "We can't be having *those people* always. Gascoigne talks too much. Country clergy are always bores—with their confounded fuss about everything."

That speech was laden with foreboding. To have her mother categorized among "those people" confirmed her dread of drawing them too near. Yet, she could not reveal the true reasons; she could not tell her mother, "Mr. Grandcourt wishes to minimize your presence, and besides, it is safer if you see little of my married life, lest you discover my misery." So, she waved aside every allusion with practiced lightness. When Mrs. Davilow hinted at securing a house near Ryelands, Gwendolen replied, "It would not be so nice for you as being near the rectory here, mamma. We shall perhaps be very little at Ryelands. You would miss my aunt and uncle."

All the while, this contemptuous veto on intimacy with her family, forcing her to proudly shrink from presenting them as troublesome dependents, only stirred a deeper inclination toward them. She had never felt so kindly toward her uncle, so disposed to cherish his cheerful, complacent activity and spirit of kind management—even when mistaken—as a comfort superior to the neutral, chilling loftiness that Grandcourt's presence demanded. Here, perhaps, she was unconsciously finding the mental enlargement that was so hard to extract from her occasional dives into difficult authors, who, instead of blending with her daily agitations, required her to dismiss them.

One day, a delightful surprise awaited when Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne were at Offendene: Gwendolen rode up without her husband, accompanied only by the groom. All present, including the four girls and Miss Merry, seated in the dining-room at lunch, witnessed the welcome approach. Even the elder ones harbored a fragment of Isabel's romantic sense, viewing the beautiful sister atop the splendid chestnut—which held its head as if proud to bear her—as a reincarnation of Harriet Byron or Miss Wardour emerging from her "happiness ever after."

Her uncle stepped to the door to offer his hand, and she sprang from her horse with an air of alacrity that encouraged this notion of guaranteed bliss. Gwendolen was particularly bent today on soothing her mother's heart, and her unusual freedom in making this solo visit enabled her to endure the pressure of painful facts that were urging themselves anew. The seven family kisses were not so tiresome as they once had been.

"Mr. Grandcourt is gone out, so I determined to fill up the time by coming to you, mamma," Gwendolen said, laying down her hat and seating herself beside her mother. Then, looking at her with a playfully monitory air, "That is a punishment to you for not wearing better lace on your head. You didn't think I should come and detect you—you dreadfully careless-about-yourself mamma!" She gave a caressing touch to the dear head.

"Scold me, dear," said Mrs. Davilow, her delicate, worn face flushing with delight. "But I wish there was something you could eat after your ride—instead of these scraps. Let Jocosa make you a cup of chocolate in your old way. You used to like that."

Miss Merry immediately rose to leave, though Gwendolen insisted, "Oh, no, a piece of bread, or one of those hard biscuits. I can't think about eating. I am come to say good-bye."

"What! going to Ryelands again?" asked Mr. Gascoigne.

"No, we are going to town," Gwendolen said, beginning to break a piece of bread but putting no morsel into her mouth.

"It is rather early to go to town," remarked Mrs. Gascoigne, "and Mr. Grandcourt not in Parliament."

"Oh, there is only one more day's hunting to be had, and Henleigh has some business in town with lawyers, I think," Gwendolen replied. "I am very glad. I shall like to go to town."

"You will see your house in Grosvenor Square," said Mrs. Davilow. She and the girls devoured every movement of their goddess, soon to vanish.

"Yes," Gwendolen said, in a tone of assent to the interest of that expectation. "And there is so much to be seen and done in town."

"I wish, my dear Gwendolen," Mr. Gascoigne said, offering kind, cordial advice, "that you would use your influence with Mr. Grandcourt to induce him to enter Parliament. A man of his position should make his weight felt in politics. The best judges are confident that the ministry will have to appeal to the country on this question of further Reform, and Mr. Grandcourt should be ready for the opportunity. I am not quite sure that his opinions and mine accord entirely; I have not heard him express himself very fully. But I don't look at the matter from that point of view. I am thinking of your husband's standing in the country. And he has now come to that stage of life when a man like him should enter into public affairs. A wife has great influence with her husband. Use yours in that direction, my dear."

The rector felt he was acquitting himself of a duty, lending an aspect of public benefit to his niece's match. To Gwendolen, the entire speech tasted of bitter comedy. If she had been merry, she would have laughed at her uncle's explanation that he had not heard Grandcourt express himself fully on politics. And the wife's great influence! General maxims about husbands and wives seemed now of precarious usefulness. Gwendolen herself had once believed in her future influence as an omnipotence in managing—she did not know exactly what. But her chief concern at present was to give an answer that would be felt appropriate.

"I should be very glad, uncle. But I think Mr. Grandcourt would not like the trouble of an election—at least, unless it could be without his making speeches. I thought candidates always made speeches."

"Not necessarily—to any great extent," said Mr. Gascoigne. "A man of position and weight can get on without much of it. A county member need have very little trouble in that way, and both out of the House and in it is liked the better for not being a speechifier. Tell Mr. Grandcourt that I say so."

"Here comes Jocosa with my chocolate after all," Gwendolen said, escaping a promise to give information that would certainly have been received in a way inconceivable to the good rector. Pushing his chair aside and crossing his leg, he looked as if he felt like a worthy specimen of a clergyman and magistrate giving experienced advice. Mr. Gascoigne had concluded that Grandcourt was a proud man, but his own self-love, calmed throughout life by the consciousness of his general value and personal advantages, was not irritable enough to prevent him from hoping the best about his niece's husband, despite being kept rather haughtily at a distance. A certain aloofness must be allowed to the representative of an old family; one would not expect him to be on intimate terms even with abstractions. But Mrs. Gascoigne was less dispassionate regarding her husband's account and felt Grandcourt's haughtiness as something slightly blameable in Gwendolen.

"Your uncle and Anna will very likely be in town about Easter," she said, with a vague sense of expressing slight discontent. "Dear Rex hopes to come out with honors and a fellowship, and he wants his father and Anna to meet him in London, that they may be jolly together, as he says. I shouldn't wonder if Lord Brackenshaw invited them; he has been so very kind since he came back to the Castle."

"I hope my uncle will bring Ann to stay in Grosvenor Square," Gwendolen said, risking herself so far for the sake of the present moment, but in reality wishing she might never be obliged to bring any of her family near Grandcourt again. "I am very glad of Rex's good fortune."

"We must not be premature, and rejoice too much beforehand," said the rector, to whom this topic was the happiest in the world and altogether allowable, now that the issue of that little affair about Gwendolen had been so satisfactory. "Not but that I am in correspondence with impartial judges, who have the highest hopes about my son, as a singularly clear-headed young man. And of his excellent disposition and principle I have had the best evidence."

"We shall have him a great lawyer some time," said Mrs. Gascoigne.

"How very nice!" Gwendolen said, with a concealed skepticism as to niceness in general, which made the word quite applicable to lawyers.

"Talking of Lord Brackenshaw's kindness," said Mrs. Davilow, "you don't know how delightful he has been, Gwendolen. He has begged me to consider myself his guest in this house till I can get another that I like—he did it in the most graceful way. But now a house has turned up. Old Mr. Jodson is dead, and we can have his house. It is just what I want; small, but with nothing hideous to make you miserable thinking about it. And it is only a mile from the Rectory. You remember the low white house nearly hidden by the trees, as we turn up the lane to the church?"

"Yes, but you have no furniture, poor mamma," Gwendolen said in a melancholy tone.

"Oh, I am saving money for that. You know who has made me rather rich, dear," Mrs. Davilow said, laying her hand on Gwendolen's. "And Jocosa really makes so little do for housekeeping—it is quite wonderful."

"Oh, please let me go up-stairs with you and arrange my hat, mamma," Gwendolen said suddenly, putting up her hand to her hair and perhaps creating a desired disarrangement. Her heart was swelling, and she was ready to cry. Her mother *must* have been worse off if it had not been for Grandcourt. "I suppose I shall never see all this again," Gwendolen said, looking round her as they entered the black and yellow bedroom, then throwing herself into a chair in front of the glass with a little groan as of bodily fatigue. In the resolve not to cry, she had become very pale.

"You are not well, dear?" asked Mrs. Davilow.

"No; that chocolate has made me sick," Gwendolen said, putting up her hand to be taken.
