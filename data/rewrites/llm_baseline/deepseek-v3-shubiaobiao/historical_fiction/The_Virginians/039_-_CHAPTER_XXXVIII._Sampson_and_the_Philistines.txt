My fortunate acquaintance in youth with a gentleman hailing from a certain island—renowned, so I am told, as the first jewel of the sea—introduced me to a gallery of family portraits so darkly painted that my youthful blood would curdle at the recital. Murder, theft, forgery, perjury—every sin catalogued in the Decalogue seemed the common pastime of his kin. His brother Tim had driven their father to an early grave; his brother Mick had plundered the parish church with pious regularity; his sister Annamaria had jilted a captain, eloped with an ensign, forged a will, and filched the family silver, leaving an innocent scapegoat to swing for her crimes. The house of Atreus was a nursery of virtue compared to this brood.

Yet, what struck me as most curious was not their villainy, but their tenderness toward one another. These same parricides and perjurers exchanged letters brimming with affection—*"My dearest brother," "My beloved sister"*—and lived for months in perfect harmony. Tim, his hands still reeking with parental blood, would mix Maria a tumbler of punch; Mick, fresh from swearing away his nephew’s inheritance, would kiss his sister’s cheek and weep over childhood memories of their ancestral castle. Such scenes of reconciliation! Such embraces! One almost forgot they had sworn to hang each other but yesterday.

Is there not something noble in forgiveness? Something Christian in casting aside yesterday’s curses, withdrawing the decanter flung in fury, and resuming friendship as though no ill words had passed? Some possess this angelic gift. At Tunbridge Wells, for instance, our two ladies—erstwhile foes—now sat cozily at cards, toasting one another’s health, carving the same chicken, while their tongues dissected the reputations of every soul in the room. It put me in mind of soldiers during a truce, sharing wine one moment, priming muskets the next.

Aunt Bernstein’s gout? Quite vanished, to Maria’s delight. Maria’s fainting spells? None since last night—though the Baroness murmured to her maid that such fits were surely inherited from *that scrofulous German mother*. Maria, in turn, instructed her own woman to taste every glass of the Baroness’s offered tonic, lest it be poisoned. These little precautions—these loaded firelocks at rest on the grass—were but the wisdom of veterans.

With Harry’s bond secure in her pocket, Maria granted her captive generous parole. She knew too well the follies of youth to chain him needlessly. *"Amuse yourself, cousin,"* she purred, like a cat permitting a mouse its gambols before the final pounce. And while her young knight errant roamed London’s temptations, Maria consoled herself with diversions of her own—chief among them Mr. Jack Morris, whose chief joy in life was losing money to persons of title.

Thus passed the days until Friday, when the Reverend Mr. Sampson, chaplain to Madame Bernstein’s nephew, was to deliver a sermon much anticipated by the Wells’ fashionable flock. Sampson, ever mindful of patrons, had polished his discourse to a shine, particularly as Lady Yarmouth—whose whisper could secure bishoprics—was expected. One could not blame him for nerves. Even the humblest curate stammers when a duke darkens his pew.

The Baroness, in high spirits, hosted a breakfast in Sampson’s honor. Resplendent in a new cassock (yet unpaid for), the reverend gentleman marched to church between his patronesses, their footmen bearing gilt prayer-books. At the chapel door, the crème of the Wells mingled with less savory figures—two ragged Irishmen reeking of whisky, a pair of sallow-faced Hebrews who bribed the beadle for entry with promises of conversion.

The sermon began. Sampson, ruddy and robust, mounted the pulpit—then paled as if struck. His eyes darted to the western door, where the Hebrew sentinels lurked, then to the vestry, where two Hibernian shadows now flanked the exit. How he concluded his oration, none could say—not even Sampson himself. Yet the ladies pronounced it *exquisite*, and Lady Maria waved her handkerchief in approval, though the reverend scarcely noticed, his gaze fixed on those ominous figures by the font.

At last, the congregation dispersed—save for the two Irishmen. As Maria stepped from the pew, a strip of parchment unfurled before her. *"At the suit of Mrs. Pincott, mercer, I arrest your ladyship,"* declared Mr. Costigan, with a bow. Maria’s shrieks echoed through the empty chapel as she swooned. The Baroness, with remarkable presence of mind, snipped the purse from her niece’s neck and coolly inquired the debt.

Meanwhile, Sampson, like an opossum resigning itself to the hunter, surrendered to his own captors. *"Buckleby Cordwainer’s suit—forty-eight pounds,"* sighed he, as the bailiffs led him off. Chairs were summoned; Maria and her chaplain were borne to lodgings more secure than grand.

The Baroness, ever dutiful, dispatched a note full of tender regrets—her own losses at cards, alas, left her powerless to assist. Maria, seething, pawned a gold chain to send her maid galloping to London—to Harry, her last hope. *"She shall pay!"* Maria vowed, though whether she meant her aunt or Fate, none could say.

That evening, as the Wells’ elite gathered at Lady Trumpington’s, not a word was breathed of the morning’s scandal. The Baroness played whist, serene as ever. Only when the last candle guttered did she retire—to a sleep so sound that when morning came, she woke in surprise to find not her maid, but Lady Maria herself drawing back the bed-curtains with a radiant smile.

And with that shock, we close the chapter.
