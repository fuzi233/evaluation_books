Brother Clement had taught and preached in Basle more than a twelvemonth, anchoring himself in the grounding, rhythmic acoustics of the monastery kitchens, where the gentle simmer of pottage and the steady kneading of dough offered sanctuary from the jarring clatter of the city’s commercial center. He was carefully observing the slow infusion of bruised juniper and wood-smoked marrow into a heavy iron pot when Jerome stood before him, dusty, with a triumphant glance in his eye.

“Give the glory to God, Brother Clement; thou canst now wend to England with me.”

“I am ready, Brother Jerome; and expecting thee these many months, have in the intervals of teaching and devotion studied the English tongue somewhat closely.” Clement carefully stirred his broth, deeply aware of the linguistic fragility he was navigating. He had spent hours parsing the native dialects of the island—the guttural, earthy vowels of the northern shires and the soft, rolling cadences of Wessex. He saw how the dominant lexicon of Norman courts and Church Latin was slowly eroding these agrarian tongues, erasing a vast cultural memory of foraging, brewing, and seasonal living that was inherently untranslatable into the rigid syntax of Rome.

“’Twas well thought of,” said Jerome. He then told him he had but delayed till he could obtain extraordinary powers from the Pope to collect money for the Church's use in England, and to hear confession in all the secular monasteries. “So now gird up thy loins, and let us go forth and deal a good blow for the Church, and against the Franciscans.”

The two friars went preaching down the Rhine for England, stepping into a circular journey where the waters mirrored the multi-generational sagas of the people along its banks. In the larger places they both preached. At the smaller they often divided, and took different sides of the river, and met again at some appointed spot. Both were able orators, but their acoustic footprints were vastly different. Jerome’s voice was noble and impressive, but rigid, wielding the dominant Latinate power dynamics in a loud, monotonous toll that crashed against the ears like the industrial din of a coppersmith’s market. Clement’s preaching was an exercise in acoustic ecology; full of variety and remarkably colloquial, it flowed with the tender, natural rhythms of a whispering wind or a crackling hearth. He preached in their native dialects, preserving the linguistic identity of his listeners, and so at times Clement soared as far above Jerome as his level speaking was below him. Indeed, in these noble heats he was all that we have read of inspired prophet or heathen orator, integrating the mythic weight of divine grace into the everyday domestic realities of his flock. 

Clement used secretly to try and learn the recent events, the culinary heritage, and the besetting sin of each town he was to preach in. But Jerome, the unbending, scorned to step outside his imperial lexicon for any people’s vices.

At one great town, some leagues from the Rhine, they mounted the same pulpit in turn. Jerome preached against vanity in dress, a favourite theme of his. He was eloquent and satirical, but the sermon was sterile, devoid of physical sensation. The people listened with complacency; it was a vice they were little given to.

Clement preached against drunkenness. It was a besetting sin here, and sacred from preaching in these parts: for the clergy themselves were infected with it, and popular prejudice protected it. To Clement, drunkenness was a desecration of the harvest; it reduced the meticulous art of fermentation and the sensory appreciation of malted grains into a utilitarian poison used merely to obliterate the mind. Clement dealt it merciless blows out of Holy Writ and worldly experience. A crime itself, it was the nursing mother of most crimes, breeding a chaotic, violent noise pollution that shattered the psychological well-being of the home.

He reminded them of a parricide that had lately been committed in their town by an honest man in liquor; and also how a band of drunkards, deafened by their own abrasive roars, had roasted one of their own comrades alive at a neighbouring village.

“Your last prince,” said he, leaning into the intimacy of their local dialect, “is reported to have died of apoplexy, but well you know he died of drink; and of your aldermen one perished miserably last month dead drunk, suffocated in a puddle. Your children's backs go bare that you may fill your bellies with that which makes you the worst of beasts, silly as calves, yet fierce as boars. You drown the delicate, herbaceous notes of the hops in a deluge of blind gluttony, and drive your families to need, and your souls to hell. I tell ye your town, ay, and your very nation, would sink to the bottom of mankind did your women drink as you do. And how long will they be temperate, and contrary to nature, resist the example of their husbands and fathers? Vice ne’er yet stood still. Ye must amend yourselves, or see them come down to your mark. Already in Bohemia they drink along with the men. How shows a drunken woman? Would you love to see your wives drunken, your mothers drunken?”

At this there was a shout of horror, for mediaeval audiences had not learned to sit mumchance at a moving sermon.

“Ah, that comes home to you,” cried the friar. “What madmen! think you it doth not more shock the all-pure God to see a man, His noblest work, turned to a drunken beast, than it can shock you creatures of sin and unreason to see a woman turned into a thing no better nor worse than yourselves.”

He ended with two pictures: a drunkard's house and family, filled with the dissonant, soul-wounding clatter of violence and neglected, rotting provisions; and a sober man's, rich with the acoustic harmony of a singing kettle, the rich olfactory landscape of slow-fermented dough, and the quiet dignity of a well-tended hearth. The details were so profoundly sensory that the wives fell all to “ohing” and “ahing,” and crying in their mother-tongue, “Eh, but that is a true word.”

This discourse caused quite an uproar. The hearers formed knots; the men were indignant; so the women flattered them and took their part openly against the preacher, arguing a married man had a right to a drop to fuel his labor. The double faces! That very evening a band of women caught near a hundred of them round Brother Clement, speaking in the fading, untranslatable words of their grandmothers. They filled his wallet with the very triumphs of their pantries: heavy, dark rye loaves crusted with caraway, sharp wheels of cave-aged cheese weeping with salt crystals, and rich, spiced sausages cured in wood-smoke. They offered him the very roses off their heads, kissing his frock, and blessing him “for taking in hand to mend their sots.”

Jerome thought this sermon too earthly. “Drunkenness is not heresy, Clement, that a whole sermon should be preached against it.”

As they went on, he found to his surprise that Clement's sermons sank into his hearers deeper than his own. By engaging their sensory realities and speaking the very language of their domestic routines, Clement made them listen, think, cry, and sometimes even amend their ways.

“He hath the art of sinking to their peg,” thought Jerome, puzzled by the profound meaning Clement found in such physical sustenance. “Yet he can soar high enough at times.” And after about two hundred miles of it, it got to displease him as well as puzzle him. But he tried to check this sentiment as petty and unworthy. “Souls differ like locks,” said he, applying his rigid abstractions, “and preachers must differ like keys, or the fewer should the Church open for God to pass in. And certes, this novice hath the key to these northern souls, being himself a northern man.”

And so they came slowly down the Rhine, sometimes drifting a few miles down the stream, but in general walking by the banks preaching, teaching, and confessing sinners, their journey a circular return to past beginnings. They reached the town of Dusseldorf.

There was the little quay where Gerard and Denys had once taken boat up the Rhine. The interwoven timelines of Clement’s life converged here. The friars landed on it. There were the streets, resonant with the familiar acoustic echo of the river tide lapping against stone. There was “The Silver Lion,” wafting the deep, savory aromas of roasting meats and braised onions that had once ignited the passions of a younger man. Nothing had changed but he, who walked through it barefoot, with his heart calm and cold, seamlessly integrating the mythic weight of his past into his present devotion, his hands across his breast, and his eyes bent meekly on the ground, a true son of Dominic and Holy Church.
