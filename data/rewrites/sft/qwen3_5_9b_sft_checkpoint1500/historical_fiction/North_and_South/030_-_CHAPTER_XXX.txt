The following morning, Mrs. Thornton visited Mrs. Hale. The condition of the latter had deteriorated sharply overnight; a sudden, terrifying step toward death had been taken in the space of twelve hours, leaving her face gaunt and gray with a pallor that startled her own family. Mrs. Thornton, who had not seen her friend for weeks, was struck with a sudden, overwhelming tenderness. She had arrived because her son had begged it of her as a personal favor, yet within her, the proud and bitter currents of her nature clashed fiercely against the Hales' family, of whom Margaret was a member. She harbored deep doubts regarding the reality of Mrs. Hale's illness, suspecting it might be merely a fleeting whim designed to disrupt the lady's regular employment. To her son, she vented her frustrations, declaring she wished they had never approached the house, lamenting that useless tongues like Latin and Greek had ever been invented. Her son endured her tirade with quiet patience, only to return gently but firmly to his original request: that she visit Mrs. Hale immediately, as it would suit the invalid best. Reluctantly, Mrs. Thornton submitted, finding a strange, twisted pride in obeying her son's wishes, all the while magnifying his "extraordinary goodness" in maintaining ties with the Hales.

In her mind, his virtue bordered on weakness, a sentiment shared by her own disdain for Mr. and Mrs. Hale, and her intense dislike for Margaret. These thoughts consumed her until she entered the darkened chamber where Mrs. Hale lay, a mother figure whose future seemed extinguished. The room offered no variation in light or shadow; the patient possessed little power of action, moving only with faint, whispered efforts alternating with studious silence. Yet, this monotonous existence seemed unbearable to the visitor. As Mrs. Thornton, robust and vigorous with life, stepped inside, Mrs. Hale remained still, though clearly conscious of her guest's presence. For a moment or two, the sick woman did not open her eyes. Heavy moisture gathered on her lashes before she finally raised them, reaching out a trembling hand beneath the covers to grasp Mrs. Thornton's large, firm fingers.

"My dear friend," Mrs. Hale whispered, barely audible, "you have a daughter. My sister is in Italy. My child will be without a mother—in a strange land—if I die. Will you?"

Her gaze, filmy and wandering, fixed itself on Mrs. Thornton's face with an intensity of wistfulness. For a minute, Mrs. Thornton's features remained rigid, stern, and unmoved; save for the slow gathering of tears in the dying woman's eyes, it appeared as if a dark cloud crossed the visitor's cold visage. It was not a thought of her son or her living daughter, Fanny, that finally softened her heart, but a sudden remembrance triggered by the arrangement of the room: a little daughter, lost in infancy long years prior. Like a sudden sunbeam, this memory melted the icy crust behind which a real, tender woman lay dormant.

"You wish me to be a friend to Miss Hale?" Mrs. Thornton asked, her voice measured, refusing to soften despite the turmoil within.

Mrs. Hale, her eyes locked on her friend's face, pressed the hand lying beside hers on the coverlet. She could not speak further. Mrs. Thornton sighed. "I will be a true friend, if circumstances require it. Not a tender friend—that I cannot be," she added hastily, cutting off her intended remark about the recipient, "for it is not my nature to show affection even where I feel it, nor do I volunteer advice in general. Still, at your request—and if it brings you any comfort—I will promise." A pause followed. Mrs. Thornton was too conscientious to pledge what she did not intend to fulfill, and performing acts of kindness toward Margaret, whom she currently despised, seemed almost impossible.

"I promise," she said, with grave severity, a tone that somehow inspired the dying woman with faith more stable than flickering life itself. "I promise that in any difficulty in which Miss Hale—"

"Call her Margaret!" gasped Mrs. Hale.

"In which she comes to me for help, I will assist her with every power I possess, as if she were my own daughter. I also promise that if ever I see her doing what I deem wrong—"

"But Margaret never does wrong—not wilfully wrong," pleaded Mrs. Hale.

Mrs. Thornton continued, ignoring the plea: "If ever I see her doing what I believe to be wrong—such wrong not touching me or mine, in which case I might be accused of bias—I will tell her of it, faithfully and plainly, as I should wish for my own daughter."

A long silence ensued. Mrs. Hale felt that this promise excluded many things, yet it was significant. It contained reservations she could not decipher, but she was too weak, dizzy, and exhausted to press further. Mrs. Thornton reviewed the probable scenarios in which she had bound herself to act, deriving a fierce pleasure from the prospect of delivering unwelcome truths to Margaret in the shape of duty. Mrs. Hale began to speak again.

"I thank you. I pray God to bless you. I shall never see you again in this world. But my last words are: I thank you for your promise of kindness to my child."

"Not kindness!" Mrs. Thornton testified, ungraciously truthful to the end. Having soothed her conscience with these words, she was not sorry they went unheard. She pressed Mrs. Hale's soft, languid hand, then rose and departed from the house unseen by a single soul.

While Mrs. Thornton conducted this somber interview, Margaret and Dixon were huddled together, plotting how to keep Frederick's impending arrival a profound secret from everyone outside the house. A letter from him was expected daily, and he would undoubtedly follow swiftly. Martha must be dispatched on a holiday; Dixon must maintain a stern guard at the front door, admitting only the rare visitors who frequented Mr. Hale's downstairs room, Mrs. Hale's critical illness providing ample justification. If Mary Higgins assisted Dixon in the kitchen, she was to hear and see as little of Frederick as possible, referred to as Mr. Dickinson if necessary. Her sluggish, incurious nature served as the ultimate safeguard.

They resolved that Martha should depart that very afternoon. Margaret wished she had left the previous day, fearing it might seem odd to grant a servant a holiday amidst her mistress's dire need for attendance. Throughout the afternoon, poor Margaret acted as a Roman daughter, draining her scanty reserves of strength to support her father. Mr. Hale oscillated between hope and despair between his wife's attacks, buoying himself up during brief respites and believing in ultimate recovery. Consequently, when the paroxysms returned, each more severe than the last, they were fresh agonies and crushing disappointments. Sitting in the drawing-room, unable to bear the solitude of his study or occupy himself, he buried his head in his arms, folded upon the table. Margaret's heart ached witnessing his state; yet, since he remained silent, she hesitated to offer unsolicited comfort. Martha was gone, and Dixon sat vigil with Mrs. Hale while she slept. The house grew still and quiet, darkness descending without the movement to fetch candles. Margaret sat by the window, gazing at the streetlamps and the road, seeing nothing, attuned only to her father's heavy sighs. She dreaded going down for lights, fearing that her withdrawal might release him from tacit restraint into violent emotion without her being nearby to soothe him. Just as she considered attending to the kitchen fire, a muffled doorbell rang with a violent pull, causing the wires to jingle throughout the house. Startled, she rose, passed her father—who showed no reaction to the veiled, dull sound—and returned to kiss him tenderly. He remained unmoved, oblivious to her embrace.

Descending softly through the dark, she found Dixon attempting to engage the chain before opening the door. Margaret, her mind pre-occupied, felt no fear. A man's tall figure stood between her and the luminous street. He looked away, but turned quickly at the sound of the latch.

"Is this Mr. Hale's?" he asked, in a clear, full, delicate voice.

Margaret trembled all over, unable to answer initially. In a moment, she sighed, "Frederick!" stretching out both hands to catch his and draw him inside.

"Oh, Margaret!" he said, holding her off by the shoulders after their kiss, as if in the darkness he could see her face and read a quicker answer to his question than words could give. "My mother—is she alive?"

"Yes, she is alive, dear, dear brother! She—as ill as she can be—she is; but alive! She is alive!"

"Thank God!"

"Papa is utterly prostrate with this great grief."

"You expect me, don't you?"

"No, we have had no letter."

"Then I have come before it. But my mother knows I am coming."

"Oh! we all knew you would come. But wait a little! Step in here. Give me your hand. What is this? Oh! your carpet-bag. Dixon has shut the shutters; but this is papa's study, and I can take you to a chair to rest yourself for a few minutes; while I go and tell him."
