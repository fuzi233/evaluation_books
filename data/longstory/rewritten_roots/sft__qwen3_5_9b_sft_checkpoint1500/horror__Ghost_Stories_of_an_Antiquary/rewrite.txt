Before we descend into the gloom where shadows take on a life of their own, I must clarify the geography of our dread. St. Bertrand-de-Comminges and Viborg are not mere fictions; they anchor these tales in the cold, unyielding reality of the earth. Yet, let us not mistake the setting for truth when it comes to the artifacts of the past. The fragments of "ostensible erudition" scattered through these pages—the footnotes, the Latin citations, the references to lost manuscripts—are almost entirely products of a fevered imagination. There never existed, naturally, any book quite like the one I shall describe in this first tale. It is a ghost in itself, a phantom library conjured from the mist of forgotten centuries.

I recall writing "Canon Alberic's Scrap-book" in 1894, a time when the air still felt thick with Victorian unease, and printing it shortly thereafter in the *National Review*. "Lost Hearts" found its way into the *Pall Mall Magazine*, drifting through the drawing rooms of London society like smoke. Five other stories followed, most read aloud by candlelight to close friends during the long, silent nights of Christmas at King's College, Cambridge. Among them, "Number 13" took shape in 1899, born perhaps of some lingering superstition. But this present narrative, "The Treasure of Abbot Thomas," was forged in the heat of a summer in 1904—a season that would prove far too warm for the chilling secrets it would eventually uncover.

St. Bertrand de Comminges is a rotting outpost clinging to the spine of the Pyrenees, a shadow of a town nestled between Toulouse and Bagnères-de-Luchon. Once a seat of a bishopric, it survives today as a pilgrimage spot for tourists seeking the ghosts of the Revolution within its crumbling cathedral. In the spring of 1883, an Englishman named Dennistoun arrived at this forsaken place. A scholar from Cambridge, he had traveled specifically to study the ancient church that dominated the hill of Comminges, leaving two less devoted friends behind at a hotel in Toulouse with a promise to join them the following day. While half an hour would suffice for his companions, Dennistoun intended to spend the entire day documenting every corner of the "wonderful" church, filling notebooks and exposing dozens of photographic plates to capture its decaying grandeur.

To achieve this, he required exclusive access to the sacristy. He summoned the keeper of the keys, the *verger*, through the somewhat brusque proprietor of the nearby *Chapeau Rouge* inn. When the old man arrived, Dennistoun found him to be a figure of unsettling intensity. The sacristan was a small, wizened creature, physically unremarkable save for a demeanor that suggested he was being hunted. He possessed a perpetual, furtive air, his back and shoulders hunched in a constant, nervous contraction, as if expecting the jaws of an enemy to snap shut around him at any second. He glanced behind him constantly, his movements jerky and abrupt. Dennistoun struggled to categorize the man: was he deluded, guilt-ridden, or perhaps the victim of an unbearable tyrant? The odds leaned toward the latter, yet the sheer weight of the persecution implied by the old man's gaze suggested a foe far more terrible than a nagging wife.

Dennistoun, however, quickly buried himself in his work, his notebook and camera becoming his shields. Yet, whenever he risked a glance backward, the sacristan was always there, huddled against the walls or crouching in the ornate stalls, watching with wide, terrified eyes. After some time, Dennistoun grew restless, plagued by suspicions that the old man was withholding his lunch or guarding the sacred ivory crozier and the stuffed crocodile hanging over the font with jealous vigilance.

"You cannot stay here forever," Dennistoun finally urged, trying to break the spell. "I am perfectly capable of finishing my notes alone. You need not lock me in, though you may try. I require at least two more hours, and it must be freezing for you out there, yes?"

"Good heavens!" the little man shrieked, thrown into a panic by the mere suggestion. "That cannot be thought of! To leave Monsieur alone in the church? No, no! Whether it be two hours, three, or all day, I remain. I have eaten, I am not cold, thank you, Monsieur."

"Very well, little man," Dennistoun muttered to himself. "You have been warned; take your consequences."

For the next two hours, Dennistoun meticulously cataloged the dilapidated organ, the choir screen of Bishop John de Mauléon, the shattered remnants of stained glass, and the artifacts in the treasure chest. Throughout, the sacristan trailed at his heels, snapping around like a whipped dog whenever the strange, muffled noises that haunt empty cathedrals drifted on the air. Sometimes, as Dennistoun recounted later, he swore he heard a thin, metallic laughter echoing from high within the tower. When he looked, the sacristan was pasty-white, trembling. "It is he—that is it. No one else. The doors are locked," the old man stammered, staring blankly ahead for a full minute.

A peculiar incident further unnerved Dennistoun. He was examining a dark painting behind the altar, depicting one of St. Bertrand's miracles. Beneath it, a Latin legend read: *Qualiter S. Dennistoun turned to the sacristan, intending to make a jesting remark, only to find the old man on his knees before the canvas. The man gazed at the image with the agony of a supplicant, hands clasped tight, tears streaming down his face like rain. Dennistoun feigned ignorance, yet the question gnawed at him: why did such a crude daub evoke such visceral terror? He suspected the man was a monomaniac, but the nature of his obsession remained a mystery.

By five o'clock, twilight was descending, casting the church into a labyrinth of shadows. The strange noises—the soft footfalls, the indistinct whispers—became more frequent, amplified by the failing light and the heightened sensitivity of the ears. With the sound, a profound silence fell over the town, and the two men emerged from the sanctuary.

Her expression brightened at the sight of the robust stranger, and she exchanged a few words with her father. Dennistoun caught only one phrase from the sacristan: "He was laughing in the church." The girl answered with a look of pure terror.

Beside it stood a solid, aged chest. When a lamp was lit and chairs arranged, the sacristan approached the chest. Dennistoun felt a surge of professional curiosity. The book was too massive for a missal and oddly shaped for an antiphoner. As soon as it was opened, however, his sentiment shifted to awe. Every traveler who has journeyed through East Anglia knows the scattered country houses that dot the landscape—those somewhat gloomy, diminutive structures, typically built in the Italian manner, encircled by parks ranging from eighty to a hundred acres. To me, they hold an irresistible allure: the weathered grey pales of split oak, the majestic ancient trees, the meres fringed with reeds, and the distant lines of woodland. I find fascination in the pillared porticos, often clumsily affixed to red-brick Queen Anne homes merely to mask them with stucco and align them with the "Grecian" fads of the late eighteenth century. Inside, I admire the soaring halls, ideally equipped with galleries and small organs, as well as libraries containing treasures from thirteenth-century Psalters to Shakespeare quartos. Of course, I cherish the paintings, but above all, I delight in imagining the lives lived within these walls during their founding eras, amidst the booming fortunes of prosperous landlords, and equally in contemplating how varied and intriguing existence remained even when funds grew scarce. I harbor a deep desire to possess one of these estates, coupled with sufficient wealth to maintain it and host friends with modest dignity.

Yet, this is a mere diversion. I must recount a bizarre sequence of events that unfolded within a residence fitting this exact description: Castringham Hall in Suffolk. While significant renovations have altered the exterior since the era of my narrative, the core architecture remains intact—the Italian portico, the stark white block, the interior antiquity masking the facade, the park bordered by woods, and the mere. However, one defining feature, unique among a dozen similar estates, has vanished. Standing within yards of the wall, almost brushing the building with its sprawling limbs, grew a colossal old ash-tree. That specific year, the region surrounding the Hall became the stage for numerous witch trials. It will take centuries to formulate a fair assessment of the solid logic, if any existed, behind the pervasive fear of witches in those days. Did the accused truly believe they possessed supernatural powers? Did they harbor the will, if not the ability, to harm their neighbors? Or were all the harrowing confessions extracted solely through the brutal cruelty of the witch-finders? These remain unsolved riddles, and the following narrative forces me to pause. I cannot dismiss it as pure fabrication; the reader must form their own judgment.

Castringham yielded a victim to the *auto-da-fé*. Her name was Mrs. Mothersole. She differed from the typical village witch only in her relative prosperity and social influence. Several reputable local farmers attempted to save her, offering testimony to her character and expressing profound anxiety regarding the jury's verdict.

What ultimately sealed Mrs. Mothersole's fate was the deposition of Sir Matthew Fell, the proprietor of Castringham Hall. He testified that on three separate occasions, observed from his window at the height of the moon, he had witnessed her gathering sprigs "from the ash-tree near my house." Clad only in her shift, she had climbed the branches, snipping small twigs with a peculiarly curved knife, muttering to herself throughout the ordeal. Each time, Sir Matthew had rushed to intervene, but every attempt to approach her caused her to flee in alarm, scattering into the garden where he could only spot a hare fleeing toward the village. On the final night, he tracked her at top speed to her doorstep, waiting fifteen minutes to batter down her door. She emerged abruptly, cross and seemingly sleep-deprived, offering no coherent explanation for her nocturnal activities.

Predominantly based on this evidence, alongside lesser testimonies from other parishioners, Mrs. Mothersole was convicted and sentenced to death. She was hanged a week later at Bury St Edmunds, accompanied by five or six other unfortunate souls. Sir Matthew Fell, serving as Deputy-Sheriff, presided over the execution. It was a damp, drizzly March morning as the cart ascended the rough hill outside Northgate, where the gallows stood. The other victims appeared apathetic or utterly broken by misery; Mrs. Mothersole, however, displayed a temperament consistent with her life. Contemporary accounts describe her "poisonous Rage" as working so intensely on the bystanders—even the hangman themselves—that she was universally perceived as embodying the living aspect of a mad devil. The only words attributed to her were seemingly meaningless phrases repeated in an undertone: "There will be guests at the Hall."

The documents from which I have woven this narrative arrived in my possession through obscure channels, a mystery I shall not unravel here. However, to contextualize the fragments that follow, I must first establish the nature of their origin. They comprise, in part, a series of journals intended for a travelogue—a genre popular in the mid-nineteenth century, akin to Horace Marryat's *Journal of a Residence in Jutland*. Typically, such volumes chronicled expeditions to remote Continental regions, adorned with woodcuts, offering practical advice on accommodations, and featuring dialogues with locals. They were, above all, chatty.

Yet, as these papers evolved, they shed their intended format to become a singular, continuous record of personal experience, maintained until the eve of its conclusion. The author was Mr. Wraxall. Based solely on his writings, I infer he was a man past middle age, financially secure but profoundly isolated. He possessed no fixed home in England, drifting instead between hotels and boarding houses, haunted by a dream of settlement that never materialized. A fire at the Pantechnicon in the early seventies likely consumed vital records of his past, as he mentions property stored there. He had previously published an anonymous or pseudonymous account of a holiday in Brittany, but his current obsession was a new project concerning Scandinavia.

Struck by the historical potential of the region, particularly the great Swedish families, Wraxall sought letters of introduction and departed for Sweden in the summer of 1863. This estate, built around 1600 by a branch of the De la Gardie house, stands as a red-brick and stone monument, indistinguishable from its Dutch or German counterparts of the era. Though the De la Gardies treated him kindly, preferring independence, Wraxall lodged at a nearby village inn, requiring a daily trek of less than a mile to the manor.

The landscape surrounding Råbäck is defined by a park of mature timber, a walled garden, and a fringing woodland bordering a small lake. Beyond lies the demesne wall, leading to a steep knoll crowned by a peculiar church. To the English eye, it is stark: low nave and aisles, galleries, and a western organ painted gaily with silver pipes. The first Count Magnus appeared as a formidable leader who suppressed peasant uprisings with brutal efficiency, executing ring-leaders and inflicting harsh punishments. A portrait of Count Magnus hung in the hall, leaving a profound impression not on Wraxall's aesthetic sensibilities, but on his sense of power; the count was described as phenomenally ugly.

During his first evening, Wraxall walked back from the manor in the twilight and noted the sexton standing on the steps of the mausoleum. The following day, he questioned the innkeeper regarding local legends. The landlord, evasive and reluctant, hinted at the "Black Pilgrimage," suggesting Count Magnus had returned from it with something or someone. Driven by this lore, Wraxall visited the sexton, who confirmed the existence of the "Black Pilgrimage" and mentioned Chorazin, a ruined village where, according to local superstition, the Antichrist was destined to be born. Two bore large crucifixes; the third, belonging to Count Magnus, featured an elaborate engraving depicting a battle, an execution, and a chase scene. Legend holds that Thomas, in the prime of his life, buried a vast mass of gold within the monastery grounds. Beyond this enigma, Abbot Thomas left behind notable works: he filled a grand eastern window in the south aisle of the church with exquisite painted glass, a feat attested by his effigy and coat of arms preserved therein. He also largely restored the Abbot's lodging, digging a well in the courtyard and adorning it with beautifully carved marble. He died abruptly at the age of seventy-two in 1529.

"I suppose I must translate this," the antiquary murmured to himself, finishing his transcription from the rare and verbose *Sertum Steinfeldense Norbertinum*. "Well, as late as it comes, it may as well be done now." The translation followed swiftly:

The antiquary's immediate objective was to locate the painted windows of the former Steinfeld Abbey. Following the dissolution of German and Belgian abbeys after the Revolution, a significant portion of their stained glass migrated to England, now adorning various parish churches and cathedrals. Steinfeld Abbey, a major contributor to this artistic diaspora, possessed glass identifiable by inscriptions or specific narrative cycles.

However, the passage he had translated pointed him toward a different mystery. In a private chapel, he had encountered three massive figures, each occupying a full pane, clearly the work of a single sixteenth-century German artist. They depicted JOB PATRIARCHA, JOHANNES EVANGELISTA, and ZACHARIAS PROPHETA, each holding a scroll inscribed with a unique text. Naturally, the antiquary noted these, finding them peculiarly distinct from standard Vulgate texts. Job's scroll read: *Auro est locus in quo absconditur* ("There is a place for gold where it is hidden"); John's bore the phrase *Habent in vestimentis suis scripturam quam nemo novit* ("They have on their garments a writing which no man knows"); and Zechariah's displayed *Super lapidem unum septem oculi sunt* ("Upon one stone are seven eyes").

A profound perplexity gripped the investigator. Why should these three disparate figures be grouped together? Historically, symbolically, or doctrinally, they shared no bond. He surmised they were part of a larger series of prophets intended for a capacious church. Yet, the *Sertum* altered this perspective entirely. It revealed that the names of these specific figures—Job, John, and Zechariah—were constants on Abbot Thomas von Eschenhausen's lips, and that Thomas had commissioned a window around 1520 featuring precisely these saints in his abbey church. It was not a wild leap to conjecture that these three formed part of Abbot Thomas's offering, a hypothesis confirmable by examining the glass further.

Mr. Somerton, a man of leisure, departed immediately for the chapel. His conjecture proved entirely accurate. The style and technique of the glass aligned perfectly with the date and locale, and in a neighboring window, he found armorial bearings matching Abbot Thomas von Eschenhausen.

Throughout his research, Somerton was haunted by rumors of the hidden treasure. As he contemplated the matter, it became increasingly evident that Abbot Thomas's cryptic answer—"Job, John, and Zechariah"—implied the secret lay within the window itself. Furthermore, the first inscription on Job's scroll, with its deliberate alteration (*absconditur* instead of *conflatur*), strongly suggested a reference to hidden wealth.

Somerton meticulously recorded every feature and mark capable of elucidating the riddle. Returning to his Berkshire manor, he spent countless nights by the lamp, tracing and sketching. After two or three weeks, he announced to his servant that he must prepare for a brief journey abroad.

Mr. Gregory, the Rector of Parsbury, strolled out before breakfast on a crisp autumn morning, intending to catch the postman and breathe the fresh air. His intention was fulfilled before he could answer more than a dozen queries from his boisterous offspring. The postman arrived, delivering a letter bearing a foreign postmark and stamped with an uneducated, yet distinctly English, hand. Addressed to the Rector, it was signed by William Brown, the confidential valet of his friend and squire, Mr. Somerton.

"HONOURED SIR, Has I am in a great anxiety about Master I write at is Wish to beg you Sir if you could be so good as Step over. Master Has add a Nastey Shock and keeps His Bedd. I never Have known Him like this but No wonder and Nothing will serve but you Sir. Master says would I mintion the Short Way Here is Drive to Cobblince and take a Trap. Hopeing I Have maid all Plain, but am much Confused in Myself what with Anxiatey and Weakfulness at Night. If I might be so Bold Sir it will be a Pleasure to see a Honnest Brish Face among all These Forig ones."

The reader need not be told the precise nature of the surprise, confusion, and frantic preparation this letter wrought in a quiet Berkshire rectory in 1859. Suffice it to say, Mr. Gregory secured passage to Antwerp and a berth on the train to Coblentz, from whence Steinfeld was easily reached.

As the narrator, I labor under a grave disadvantage: I have never visited Steinfeld, and neither of the principal actors could provide me with anything but a vague, dismal impression of its appearance. It seems to be a small place dominated by a large, stripped church, surrounded by ruinous seventeenth-century buildings erected in a luxurious fashion by the abbey's inhabitants. I have deemed it unworthy to spend money visiting, for though it may possess attractions, there appears to be little of first-rate interest save, perhaps, one singular thing I would prefer not to see.

The inn where the English gentleman and his servant were lodged was the only viable option in the village. Mr. Gregory was ushered inside to find Mr. Brown waiting at the door. Once a model of impassive efficiency in his native Berkshire, Brown was now egregiously out of his element: dressed in a light tweed suit, appearing anxious, almost irritable, and clearly lacking mastery over his surroundings. His relief at seeing the "honest British face" of his Rector was immeasurable, though words failed him.

"Well, I ham pleased, I'm sure, sir, to see you. And so I'm sure, sir, will master."

"How _is_ your master, Brown?" Mr. Gregory asked eagerly.

"I think he's better, sir, thank you; but he's had a dreadful time of it. I 'ope he's gettin' some sleep now, but—"

"What has been the matter? Was it an accident?"

"Well, sir, I 'ardly know whether I'd better speak about it. Master was very partickler he should be the one to tell you. But there's no bones broke—that's one thing I'm sure we ought to be thankful—"

"What does the doctor say?" asked Mr. Gregory.

They approached the bedroom door, speaking in low tones. Mr. Gregory, reaching for the handle, brushed his fingers across the panel. Before Brown could respond, a terrible cry erupted from within.

"In God's name, who is that?" "Brown, is it?"

"Yes, sir—me, sir, and Mr. Gregory," Brown answered hastily, followed by an audible groan of relief.

Entering the room, which was darkened against the afternoon sun, Mr. Gregory saw with a shock of pity how gaunt and damp with sweat was the usually calm face of his friend. Mr. Somerton sat up in the curtained bed, stretching out a trembling hand.

"Better for seeing you, my dear Gregory," he replied, and the statement was palpably true.

After five minutes, Mr. Somerton regained composure. He ate a substantial dinner and spoke confidently of being fit to travel to Coblentz within twenty-four hours. However, a shadow of agitation returned.

"But there's one thing," he said, pressing his hand onto Gregory's to forestall interruption. "Don't ask me what it is, or why I want it done. I'm not up to explaining it yet; it would throw me back—undo all the good you have done me. The only word I will say is that you run no risk whatever by doing it, and that Brown can and will show you tomorrow what it is. It's merely to put back—to keep—something—No; I can't speak of it yet. Do you mind calling Brown?"

Mr. Gregory agreed to wait for instructions, promising to undertake the task the following morning.

"Now, here is Brown. Brown, one word with you."
