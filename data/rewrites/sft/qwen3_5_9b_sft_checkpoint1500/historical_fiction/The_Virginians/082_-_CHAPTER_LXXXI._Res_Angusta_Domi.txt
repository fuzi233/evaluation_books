Having already laid bare my present sentiments as an aged gentleman concerning that precipitous leap into matrimony, which I urged upon my beloved partner when neither of us had fully emerged from adolescence, I pause to reflect. As a man and a father—with a proper reverence for the necessity of sustenance, the imperative of settling accounts with the baker, and a motley crew of spirited children who might abscond to Scotland tomorrow, citing parental precedent for their audacity—I recognize the gravity of recounting the early days of Sir George Warrington and Theodosia’s union. Prudence dictates a display of *mea culpa*, a demure posture, and a humble admission of repentance from my armchair, offering myself as a cautionary tale to the rash and impulsive among the youth.

Yet, truth be told, the matrimonial existence foreseen with such grim prophecy by my dear relations has defied their somber expectations. It has known trials, certainly, but I recall them without bitterness; it has harbored passions that brought grief, yet time, by divine ordinance, has acted as a benign healer. There were days of scarcity, borne with a fortitude that left our sympathetic kin marveling, and precious rewards so profound I scarcely dare whisper them to paper, speaking of them only with reverential awe to the ears of Him to whom all prayers ascend. To marry without financial security is undeniably hazardous, a breach of social code, perhaps even a crime; yet, do not thousands of souls annually commit this "crime" trusting solely in Heaven, health, and labor? Does not every young couple entering the state of matrimony ignore the necessity of a fully furnished cottage, a stocked cellar, a cupboards brimming with plate, and a strongbox overflowing with coin? Were the laws governing the genteel classes universalized, the growth of the world would surely stall. The aristocracy trembles at the edge of the abyss in silk stockings and waiting for a golden bridge, while the poor fear not to wet their bare feet in the stream, planting them firmly in the current and trusting in fate and strength to carry them across. Who would wish to consign a daughter to want? Who would advise a son to risk the countless perils of impoverished marriage—to strip a beloved girl of comfort and competence, subjecting her to debt, misery, isolation, sickness, and the myriad shadows of *res angusta domi*? I gaze upon my wife and beg pardon for imposing a burden so steeped in pain and peril upon one of such gentleness. Reflecting on her endurance, I am filled with gratitude for the trials and for that unyielding love and constancy with which God blessed and fortified her. On the matter of marriage, I am far from impartial: my own union was so imprudent, yet so blissful, that I cannot offer counsel. I have weathered poverty and found it, strangely, tolerable. Had I not traversed that path, I could never have known the sweetness of friendship, the joy of gratitude, or the astonishing consolations that sometimes attend a scanty meal and a narrow fire, cheering the weary soul at the close of a long laborsome day. At least, it is certain that regarding the decent poor, a vast amount of superfluous pity is wasted. Well-meaning individuals, stepping briefly from the sunshine of wealth into narrow obscurity, became blinded, stumbling over obstacles invisible to us, surprised at our cheerful resignation as we drank small beer and gave thanks over cold mutton.

My father-in-law, the esteemed General, wed his Molly when he was merely a subaltern in a foot regiment, possessing a purse no fuller than my own. Their fortunes rose and fell. I suspect—though my wife would never confess—that they married without seeking parental permission, a liberty possible in their youth. Yet, so gratified were they with their marital luck that they grudged none of their children's hardships, nor were they daunted by the prospect of the difficulties we inevitably faced. When I presented my financial statements to Mr. Lambert, I likely sought to mislead both parties. Convinced I commanded a fortune of two thousand pounds, the worthy gentleman departed for Jamaica with ease of mind regarding his darling daughter's future, at least for some years. After covering family expenses, he left scarcely richer than his son-in-law. His wife's contribution to Theo consisted of trifles, some lace from Aunt Lambert, and twenty new guineas in a purse crafted by her mother and sisters. However, in assessing my stock, I counted as a good debt a sum my honored mother never acknowledged until her final hour. She argued that funds I had disbursed were investments in the estate destined for me upon her death. Any surplus she possessed was reserved for my poor brother, who, lacking resources, would never have spent his own means had he not believed himself the sole heir of the Virginian property—a point the good lady emphasized repeatedly in her correspondence—but for a half-hour accident of birth. Now, he distinguishes himself in the service of King and Country. Purchasing his promotion, she deemed it her duty, as would he had circumstances allowed. Upon completing my bar studies and theatrical diversions, Madam Esmond declared me welcome to return home and assume the colonial position to which my birth entitled me. She conveyed this message to me more than once through Mountain, prior to news of my marriage reaching her.

Need I recall her expressions of maternal indignation when informed of my step? Upon the pacification of Canada, my dear Harry requested leave and dutifully visited Virginia. He wrote of his reception and the splendid entertainments my mother hosted in honor of her son. Castlewood, uninhabited since our departure for Europe, was reopened to colonial friends. The friend of Wolfe and the soldier of Quebec was received with becoming honors. Indeed, dismal quarrels ensued, for Harry persisted in his friendship with Colonel Washington of Mount Vernon, whose praises Harry never tire of singing. I allow the gentleman every virtue; in the struggles that terminated so fatally for England, I can admire as well as his warmest supporters General Washington's glorious constancy and success.

If these skirmishes between Harry and our mother were frequent, as his letters described, I wondered why he remained at home. A natural suggestion occurred to me, which I hesitated to share with Mrs. Warrington; for we had discussed Hetty's romantic attachment to my brother, wondering why he had never discerned it. I need not say that my gentleman had found a young lady at home more to his taste than our dear Hester, thus accounting for his prolonged stay.

Soon, however, a letter arrived containing not a full confession but an admission of this intriguing fact. A person was described, unnamed—a Being of beauty and perfection, like other young ladies under similar circumstances. My wife requested to see the letter. Reluctantly, I showed it, handing it to her with a grave countenance. To my astonishment, she read it without exhibiting corresponding sorrow.

"I have thought of this before, my love," I said. "I feel with you for your disappointment regarding poor Hetty."

"AH! poor Hetty!" sighed Theo, gazing down at the carpet.

"It would never have done," I remarked.

"No—they would not have been happy," Theo murmured.

"How strange he never should have found out her secret!" I continued.

She looked me full in the face with an odd expression. "Pray, what does that look mean?" I asked.

"Nothing, my dear—nothing! Only I am not surprised!" said Theo, blushing.

"What!" I exclaimed, with a strange commotion within me. "Do you mean to tell me, Theo, that you ever cared for anyone but me?"

"Oh, George," she whimpered, "when I was at school, there was—one of the boys of Doctor Backhouse's school, who sat in the loft next to us; and I thought he had lovely eyes, and I was so shocked when I recognized him behind the counter at Mr. Grigg's the mercer's, when I went to buy a cloak for baby, and I wanted to tell you, my dear, and I didn't know how!"

I ventured to see this creature of lovely eyes, having had my wife describe the fellow's attire. I observed a little bandy-legged wretch in a blue camlet coat, with his red hair tied by a dirty ribbon, about whom I forbore even to reproach my wife; nor will she ever know that I looked upon him, until she reads this confession. If our wives saw us as we truly are, I thought, would they love us as they do? Are we as much mistaken in them as they in us? I look into one candid face at least, and think it has never deceived me.

Lest I encourage my young people to emulate my own imprudence, I shall not reveal with how small a capital Mrs. Theo and I commenced life. The unfortunate tragedy brought us naught; though reviewers, in recent times, have spoken somewhat favorably of its merits, and Mr. Kemble himself has done me the honor of commendation. Our kind friend Lord Wrotham intended to publish the piece by subscription and sent me a banknote, requesting a hundred copies for his friends. Yet, I was always averse to such methods of levying money, and, preferring my poverty *sine dote*, I locked up my manuscript, with my poor girl's verses inserted at the first page. I know not why the piece should have offended at court, save for the fact that an actor who had eloped with an Earl's daughter performed a principal part.
