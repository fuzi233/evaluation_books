The morning of February 27th found Florence in restless motion. Though the Carnival’s final day promised another Bonfire of Vanities before the Old Palace, the true current of the city’s fervor flowed toward San Marco. The Piazza swelled with bodies, a sea of faces turned toward the church, where a wooden pulpit stood empty above the doors.

The man who would soon ascend it had been silenced elsewhere—banned from the Duomo, shunned by fellow clerics, his very words made forbidden under threat of Rome’s wrath. Yet the people came. They had heard his defiance: *A Pope who buys his throne cannot wield Christ’s authority. To obey him is sin.* And so they gathered, hungry for certainty, for a sign that this fiery friar spoke with Heaven’s own voice.

The monks assembled first, black-robed and solemn, their chant a murmuring tide. But the crowd’s attention was not for them. It was the silence before the storm, the breath held before the lightning strike. Then—movement. A figure in black knelt in the pulpit, head bowed, face hidden. The air tightened.

When Savonarola rose at last, pushing back his cowl, his presence was a blade cutting through the murmur. His hands lifted—slender, expressive, more like instruments of prayer than flesh. Some in the front ranks knelt at once; others resisted, caught between awe and fear. *Would God strike them for kneeling to an excommunicant?*

Then his voice, clear and low: *“Misereatur vestri…”* More sank to their knees. The words rose, sharpening, climbing—*“Benedicat vos omnipotens Deus!”*—until it rang like a challenge across the Piazza. By the final syllable, every head was bowed.

Exhausted, Savonarola knelt again, breath ragged. Preaching was his lifeblood, but each sermon drained him. When he stood once more, he held aloft the consecrated Host, his face gaunt in the morning light.

*“Three days past,”* he declared, *“I begged you to pray—that if my work is not God’s, He strike me down where I stand. I ask it again. Pray now.”*

A hush. Did any truly pray? Or did they only watch, breath suspended, as the friar lifted his own plea to the heavens? *“Lord, if I speak not Your truth, let Your fire consume me.”*

For a heartbeat, nothing. Then—sunlight, breaking through the grey, spilling over the crystal vessel in his hands, gilding his face. A shout erupted: *“A sign! Behold the answer!”*

For Savonarola, it was a fleeting triumph, a glimpse of a greater stage—one where all Christendom would witness his vindication. But as he retreated into the church, the spell fractured. Laughter and jeers bubbled up where reverence had been. The sunlight, impartial now, touched saint and skeptic alike.

From a loggia across the Piazza, Tito observed the shifting crowd. *“Applause gives way to criticism,”* he remarked. *“Yet for a moment, our Frate made even doubters kneel.”*

Beside him, Pietro Cennini frowned. *“Unless proven otherwise, I’ll believe the man has more faith, not less. No liar would dare such a test.”*

But the test was not over. And Florence, ever fickle, had already begun to murmur anew.
