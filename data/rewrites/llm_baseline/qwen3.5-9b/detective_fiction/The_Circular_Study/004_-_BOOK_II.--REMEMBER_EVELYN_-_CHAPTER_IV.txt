Detective Mr. Gryce found himself at a profound disadvantage in his quest to unravel the mystery of this particular affair, a handicap he had not encountered in years. His obstacles were twofold: first, the victim was a solitary man whose entire household consisted merely of his mute man-of-all-work; second, he resided in a sector of the city where neighbors were virtually nonexistent. Furthermore, it now appeared the deceased possessed no active circle of friends. Despite several hours having passed since news of his death spread, not a single soul had knocked upon the door offering inquiries or information. This silence was peculiar, especially given that the man had been a notable figure in this quarter of the town for months. Yet, everything about him was inherently odd; it would have been incongruous with his eccentric lifestyle and surroundings for him to maintain the ordinary social associations typical of his class.

This lack of conventional avenues for gathering intelligence, rather than diminishing the intrigue, only heightened Mr. Gryce's fascination with the case. Consequently, he felt a wave of relief when, shortly before midnight, he watched the procession of reporters, medical professionals, officials, and others trailing the coroner's van depart from the front door, leaving him once again, albeit temporarily, in sole command of the situation. Before retiring for his much-needed rest, however, there were two critical points he needed to resolve.

His immediate attention turned to the first matter. Walking past a chair in the hallway where a small boy sat dozing, Gryce roused him with a brisk command: "Come, Jake, it's time to look lively. I need you to take me to the precise spot where that lady ran across you today."

The boy, barely conscious, looked around frantically for his hat. "I'd like to see my mother first," he pleaded weakly. "She must be devastated about me. I've never stayed away this long before."

"Your mother knows exactly where you are," Gryce replied smoothly. "I sent a message to her hours ago. She reported back favorably; she says you're an obedient lad who has never lied to her."

"She's a wonderful mother," the boy declared warmly, clutching his cap. "I'd be just as bad—just as bad as my father was—if I didn't treat her well." Putting his cap on his head, he added, "I'm ready."

Gryce immediately led the way out into the street. The hour was late, and only scattered parts of the city retained any semblance of life. They soon arrived at one of these active thoroughfares and stopped before the darkened window of a smaller shop. Jake pointed out two stuffed frogs engaged in a miniature sword fight—a spectacle he had been watching intently when the mysterious lady approached. Gryce studied the boy far more closely than the battling frogs, though the child likely believed his gaze was entirely fixed on the toy duel.

"Was she a pretty lady?" Gryce asked.

Jake scratched his head, looking perplexed. "She made me quite afraid of her," he admitted. "But she had very splendid clothes; oh, gorgeous!" he exclaimed, as if this settled the matter of her appearance. "And she was young, carrying a bunch of flowers, and seemed troubled? Wait... she wasn't young, she didn't carry flowers, and she wasn't even anxious or trembling?"

The boy, who had been shaking his head in denial, now looked thoroughly confused. "I suppose you could say she was troubled," he conceded. "But she wasn't crying. When she spoke to me, she gripped my arm with more force than she used in her voice. She simply dragged me to the drugstore, sir. If she hadn't handed me money first, I surely would have wriggled free despite her strength. But I love money, sir; I don't get enough of it."

By this point, Gryce was already moving forward. "Not young," he murmured to himself. "Some old flame of Mr. Adams', then. They tend to be dangerous—very dangerous, far more so than the young ones."

Pausing in front of the drugstore, he instructed, "Show me where she stood while you went inside."

Jake indicated the exact spot. He seemed as eager to assist as the detective himself. "And was she still standing there when you came out?"

"Oh, no, sir. She left while I was inside."

"Did you see her go? Can you tell me if she headed up the street or down?"

"I kept one eye on her, sir. I was terrified she might return to the shop after me, and my arm was too sore to want her gripping it again. So, when she started to leave, I stepped closer and saw her move toward the curb, raising her hand. But it wasn't a car she was signaling, for none passed by for several minutes."

The furrow between Gryce's eyebrows smoothed noticeably. "Then she hailed a cabman or a hack driver. Did you notice any empty vehicles nearby?"

The boy shook his head. He had reached the absolute limit of his observation, and no amount of further questioning could extract additional details. Recognizing this, Gryce entrusted the boy to one of his assistants stationed at the location and returned to the ill-omened house where the tragedy had unfolded.

"Is anyone waiting for me?" he asked Styles, who opened the door.

"Yes, sir. A young man named Hines. He claims to be an electrician."

"That is precisely the person I require. Where is he?"

"In the parlor, sir."

"Excellent. I shall see him. However, ensure no one else enters. Is anyone upstairs?"

"No, sir. Everyone has departed. Should I ascend or remain here?"

"You'd best go up. I will keep watch at the door."

Styles nodded and vanished up the stairs. Gryce proceeded to the parlor, where a dapper young man with an intelligent gaze rose to greet him. "You summoned me," the youth stated.

The detective nodded, posed a few questions, and seemingly satisfied with the answers, guided his companion into Mr. Adams's study—the room from which the body had been moved to an upper chamber. Upon entering, a soft light emanated from a candle placed on a small side table near the entrance, per Mr. Gryce's orders. Once inside, however, Gryce walked to the large central table, placed his hand on one of the buttons before him, and requested his companion kindly extinguish the candle.

Compliance brought the room into total darkness. Suddenly, a brilliant burst of illumination flooded the space—a marvelous glow of deep violet—and the two men turned to face one another, their expressions filled with inquiry. Such a theatrical effect was utterly unexpected to the electrician, and its cause and purpose remained inexplicable to him.

"That is merely one slide," Mr. Gryce remarked calmly. "Now, I shall press another button, and the color shifts to pink, as you see. This one yields green, this one white, and this produces a bilious yellow, which I assure you is hardly becoming to either of us. Now, please examine the connections and determine if there is anything peculiar about them."

Hines immediately began his inspection. Beyond noting that the entire contraption was the work of an amateur, he discovered nothing unusual, aside from its flawless functionality. Gryce expressed his disappointment. "So, he constructed it himself?" he asked.

"Undoubtedly, or someone else equally unfamiliar with modern wiring techniques," Hines replied.

"Will you glance at those books on the shelf? Perhaps sufficient knowledge exists within them to explain how an amateur could rig up such an arrangement?"

Hines cast a brief look at the shelf Gryce indicated. Without removing a volume, he answered succinctly: "A man with a deft hand and a scientific inclination could achieve all of this, and more, using these resources. The aptitude is all that matters."

"Therefore, I fear Mr. Adams possessed that aptitude," Gryce responded dryly. Disappointment colored his tone, a sentiment confirmed by his next words. "Individuals with a penchant for mechanical contrivances often squander considerable time and money on trivial toys suitable only for children. Consider that birdcage now. Perched at a height unreachable by anyone without a ladder, its obvious utility—evidenced by the lively occupant within—must rely on a mechanism allowing it to be raised and lowered at will. Where is that mechanism? Can you locate it?"

The expert believed he could. Indeed, after some futile searching, he located another button concealed among the wall tapestry. Pressing it triggered a release, gradually lowering the cage within reach of Gryce's hand.

"We shall not send this unfortunate bird back aloft," Hines said, detaching the cage and holding it momentarily. "An English starling is quite rare in this country. Listen; it is about to speak."

However, the sharp-eyed bird, perhaps alerted by the detective's emphatic gesture that silence was preferable to his usual plea to "remember Evelyn," fluttered briefly in its cage before settling into a doze. Whether genuine or feigned under the fascinated scrutiny of the elderly gentleman holding it remains uncertain.

Gryce placed the cage on the floor. Idly, or perhaps because the amusement appealed to him despite his age and stern demeanor, he pressed another button on the table—one he had previously ignored—and glanced around to observe the resulting light change. Yet, the yellow glare persisted. The prior examination of the apparatus had likely disrupted the wiring. Shrugging, he began to move away until he suddenly made a hurried gesture, drawing the expert's attention to a fact neither anticipated.
