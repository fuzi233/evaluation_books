Catherine’s chest ached with quiet dismay as she counted the days: her son Gerard would be home within a month at the very farthest, far more likely within a week, and she had not kept the close, watchful eye on his betrothed Margaret that he’d begged her to before he left. That weight tangled with the gnawing, unanswerable uncertainty of what had become of the girl, and some days that fear curdled sick and sharp in her throat.
“Oh, Kate,” she groaned to her younger daughter, “what if she’s gone and done away with herself?”
“Mother, she would never be so wicked,” Kate soothed, soft and earnest.
“Ah, my lass, you know nothing of hasty young fools with no mother to steady their step,” Catherine sighed, twisting her worn hands in the hem of her linen apron. “They’ll fling themselves straight into a canal over a man, when the very next lad they meet on the road would cure them of their moping in a week. I’ve seen it: bold as brass one moment, leaping into the water, and screaming for help the next. They can’t even make up their minds about something that trivial, you see. And there are days their bodies ache queer, unlike any living creature I ever heard tell of, that strings their feelings so tight — their patience, which normally outlasts every living thing but a stubborn ass, vanishes in a breath, and in they go. That’s why I say men are monsters.”
“Mother!” Kate gasped, shocked.
“Monsters, no less!” Catherine insisted, stamping her foot light on the cottage earthen flags. “Building all these endless canals just to tempt poor women to their end! They know we’d never slit our own throats — we hate the sight of blood, and value our skin far more than our lives. And hanging? By the time you’ve hammered the nail in the beam and tied the noose, you’ve got time to change your mind a dozen times. But jumping in a canal? It’s as easy as climbing into bed. The water does all the rest, whether you want it to or no. Why, look at me — mother of nine bairns, and wasn’t I half mad to throw myself straight into our own village canal once?”
“Nay, mother,” Kate said, wide-eyed, “I’ll never believe that of you.”
“You can, though, it’s gospel truth,” Catherine huffed a small, bitter laugh. “It was our first year wed, me and your father Eli. He hadn’t learned all my soft, foolish weak spots then, nor I his. We pulled opposite on some petty thing, quarrelled so bitter I ran out the door crying, ready to spill my trouble to some silly gossip that had no business hearing it before the saints themselves. And right there on the path sat that cursed canal. D’they think we’re teal, to take to water like that? Oh, it looked so tempting! I thought to myself, ‘If he’s let me leave his door angry and wronged, he’ll find me drowned tomorrow. Then he’ll change his tune. He’ll sob his heart out, and I’ll look down from heaven’ — I clean forgot I’d likely be in the other place, fool that I was — ‘and watch him grieve, and won’t that be sweet?’ I was on tiptoe, ready to step into the water, when it hit me: my new gown was almost finished, the one I’d been saving linen for for months. So I turned right around and trundled home. And what was the first thing Eli said to me? ‘Let that flea stick to the wall, my lass,’ he says. ‘Not a single word I said to anger you was true, except this: I love thee.’ Those were his exact words, I’ve remembered them all these years, being our very first quarrel. I threw my arms around his neck and sobbed a bit, thought of that canal, and he was soft with me, same as I was with him — young, newly wed, you know. That was far better than lying cold in the water, spoiling my fine new kirtle and the pair of shoes old John Bush made, the man whose nephew runs the cobbler’s shop now. What’s a tiny quarrel like that, compared to the grief Margaret’s bearing now?”
Kate tried to soothe her, saying she was sure Margaret loved her aging father too much to leave him all alone, when he was all she had, both father and mother to her.
“Nay, Kate, they forget every single thing like that in a moment of despair, when the sky above looks like it’s turned solid black,” Catherine said, grim. “I’d trust an unborn bairn far more than a gray old man at death’s door to keep her out of harm. For it goes sore against a soul to die, when there’s a little innocent tugging at your heartstrings, feeding at your very veins, begging you to stay alive.”
“Then keep your heart up, mother,” Kate said, patting her arm. She added that all her fears were sure to be overblown, and then begged her very solemnly not to keep going on about Margaret’s baby being a boy — all the village gossips said it was terrible unlucky to name the child’s sex before it was born. “Dear heart, there are just as many girls born into the world as boys, after all!”
That reasonable remark made Catherine squawk in loud protest. “Have you the cruelty to threaten me with a girl?!” she cried. “I don’t want any more girls, I’ve already got you. What use would a lass be to me? Can I set her on my knee and see Gerard’s face in her, the way I could a boy? I tell you, it’s all settled already. It’s a boy.”
“How can you be so sure of that?” Kate asked, puzzled.
“Sure in my own mind,” Catherine said firmly. “And if I’m to be disappointed at the end, it’s not for you to disappoint me first, telling me it’s not going to be the boy I’m waiting for, but just another girl.”
