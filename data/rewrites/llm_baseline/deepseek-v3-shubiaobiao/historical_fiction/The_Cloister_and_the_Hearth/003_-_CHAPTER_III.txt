A stately seneschal, his hair as white as frost, approached their table with measured steps. His eyes swept over the company before settling on Gerard.

“Young sir,” he said, with a courteous bow, “are you Gerard Eliassoen?”

Gerard, surprised, confirmed it.

“The Princess Marie requests your presence.”

A ripple of murmurs spread through those nearby. Faces turned, eyes sharp with envy, fixed upon the young man summoned by royalty. Gerard rose at once.

Margaret, though her fingers tightened slightly on the edge of the table, kept her voice steady. “I suppose we shan’t see you again.”

He bent low, whispering, “This is my good princess—but you are my queen.” Then, aloud: “Wait for me. I will return.”

Peter, roused from his doze, mumbled agreement.

Once Gerard was gone, the pair—plainly dressed yet suddenly the center of attention—found themselves scrutinized by curious onlookers. William Johnson, seizing the moment, stepped forward with exaggerated surprise.

“Cousins! And to think I was right behind you all this while!”

Margaret’s reply was cool. “I saw you long ago.”

“And yet you did not speak?”

“It was for you to welcome us to Rotterdam, as we would welcome you in Sevenbergen. Your servant barred your door to us.”

Johnson flushed. “The fool!”

Margaret’s gaze was steady. “I wished to see whether the master was like the man.”

Stung, Johnson acted swiftly. “Come home with me now, and judge for yourself whether you are welcome.”

Margaret hesitated. “We must wait for Gerard. He doesn’t know where we lodge.”

Johnson waved a dismissive hand. “My secretary will wait for him and bring him along. You needn’t worry.”

She wavered—then agreed.

---

The seneschal led Gerard through torch-lit corridors, past young nobles lounging at their posts, their cards and dice forgotten as they eyed the stranger with open disdain.

“The Princess has retired,” one drawled.

“Come back tomorrow,” said another, rolling the dice.

But the seneschal only smiled and pressed on.

In an antechamber, ladies in silk and jewels paused their chatter as Gerard entered. Their eyes pinned him like moths to a board. A duenna, stern-faced, rescued him, ushering him into the presence of the Countess of Charolois and her daughter, Princess Marie.

The Countess, seated with needlework in hand, greeted him warmly.

“Margaret Van Eyck is an old friend,” she said. “I am glad to receive her letter—and gladder still that you brought it safely.”

The young princess, bright-eyed, added eagerly, “And the miniature! I thank you, sir.”

The Countess’s smile deepened. “Now, how shall we repay you?”

Gerard, overwhelmed, could only murmur, “No reward is needed.”

But the Countess had already decided. “A benefice,” she declared. “When you are ordained, you shall have a church near Tergou.”

The princess clapped her hands. “Oh, but he ought to be Bishop of Liege! The bishop wears such splendid gloves!”

Gerard, half-dazed by the honor, bowed low. Yet even as gratitude swelled in his chest, another thought pierced him like a blade—*Margaret*.

---

When he returned to the hall, she was gone.

He searched—through the thinning crowds, past the drunken revelers, even to the Mystery play where Virtues and Vices cavorted under flickering torchlight. But she was nowhere.

For days he combed the city, hope fraying with each passing hour. On the third day, he gave up.

With a heavy heart, he turned toward Tergou—royal favor in his future, gold in his purse, and a leaden weight where his joy had been.
