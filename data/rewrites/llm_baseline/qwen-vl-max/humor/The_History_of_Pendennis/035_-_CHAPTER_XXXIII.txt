Our imprisoned Captain, with all the fire and flourish of a man who’d just been released from a long sentence of silence, launched into his prospectus like a politician at a rally—only this one was held in a prison cell, with a publisher whose idea of literary critique was “That’s a dig at Bacon’s people!” He declared, with the solemnity of a prophet announcing Armageddon, that the time had come for the gentlemen of England to rise up and defend their ancient rights, their glorious order, and their sacred traditions—all of which were under siege by foreign revolutions, domestic radicals, cotton lords with bad manners, and the masses, who, despite being led by mill-owners, somehow managed to be both stupid and dangerous.

“The monarchy,” thundered Captain Shandon, “is insulted by a rabble of republican swine! The Church is abandoned by dissenters and undermined by infidels who sneak about like moles in a cheese shop! Our noble institutions—those that made England great and the title of ‘English Gentleman’ the proudest in Christendom—are left undefended, exposed to mockery by men who believe nothing holy, know nothing of history, and obey no law except when it suits them.”

He paused for effect. Bungay nodded wisely, as if he’d just heard the final word on constitutional law. Warrington burst out laughing.

“See how our dear friend Bungay reacts,” said Shandon, peering up from his papers with the air of a man conducting a psychological experiment. “That’s the true test. I’ve used Wellington and Waterloo a hundred times—and never once has the Duke failed to stir the soul.”

Bungay clapped his walking stick on the floor. “Hang him, pitch into him, Capting!” he roared, delighted. Then he turned to Warrington, wagging his head like a dog who’d just been told he could eat the whole pie. “For a slashing article, sir, there’s nobody like the Capting—no-obody!”

And so the Captain continued, with the candour of a man who’d just confessed to stealing a neighbour’s goose but still wanted to be admired. He admitted that until now, the gentlemen of England had been too busy managing their estates, settling their legal affairs, or simply ignoring politics altogether, to take charge of their own interests. Instead, they’d left their political representation to professional hacks—just as they left their wills to solicitors and their dinners to cooks. But no more! The time had come for gentlemen to champion themselves. Their enemies were bold, numerous, and utterly unyielding. They must fight back—not with hired wordsmiths, but with their own pens and swords.

“And let me tell you,” Shandon added, “Grub Street printing Gazettes from Whitehall—that’s a dig at Bacon’s people, Mr. Bungay.”
Bungay clapped again. “Pitch into him, Capting!” he cried.

The Captain then revealed that certain gentlemen—whose names, for obvious reasons, couldn’t be mentioned (which caused Warrington to snort with laughter)—had decided to launch a new journal. Its principles? Well, they were noble, patriotic, and entirely self-serving. These men, said Shandon, flourished his paper like a banner, were proud of their birth, loyal to their king, devoted to their church, and deeply committed to preserving England as the greatest and freest nation in the world—“and yes, the gentlemen of England (we’ll have that in large caps, Bungay, my boy) have made it so!”

Then came the dramatic climax: “And since some of these gentlemen are descended from those who signed the Magna Carta at Runnymede—”

“What’s that?” asked Bungay.

“An ancestor of mine sealed it with his sword-hilt,” Pen said gravely.

“It’s the Habeas Corpus, Mr. Bungay,” Warrington corrected.

“Ah, right, I dare say,” Bungay yawned, though he added, “Go on, Capting.”

So the Captain did. He explained that these unnamed gentlemen—likely including himself—would bring forth a newspaper called *The Pall Mall Gazette*, which would be written by gentlemen, for gentlemen, and conducted by men of reputation: university dons, club regulars, society insiders. He promised readers that only *The Pall Mall Gazette* would deliver the earliest news of English and continental politics—because its writers were part of the very circles they wrote about. “Why should the Gentlemen of England be unrepresented in the Press?” he demanded, as if the question had never occurred to anyone before.

Then, with modesty that was clearly an act, he described the literary and fashion sections—written, of course, by gentlemen who knew what they were talking about. He invited advertisers to join the cause, promising unparalleled visibility. He called upon the nobility, baronets, clergy, lawyers, matrons, daughters, homes, and hearths of England to rally round the flag of gentlemanly virtue. And when he finished, Bungay woke up from a second nap and declared, “All right.”

After the reading, the gentlemen discussed the paper’s management. Bungay sat quietly, nodding as if he understood every word. In truth, he didn’t. His opinions were simple: the Captain could write the best smashing article in England. If the Captain wrote a letter from Junius, or copied the Church Catechism, Bungay would still call it a smashing article. He pocketed the manuscript with satisfaction, paid for it, and gave little Mary a penny as he left.

Conversation turned to general topics. Shandon, in his usual jaunty fashion, regaled the guests with tales of high society—names, scandals, private conversations, and dinner parties he claimed to have attended. Pen found it hilarious that a shabby prisoner in a torn dressing-gown could talk so confidently about the great and the good. Mrs. Shandon, however, believed every word. She didn’t care much for society herself, but she believed her husband belonged there. Once, he’d been invited to dinner by the Earl of X—a fact she kept in her workbox like a sacred relic.

Eventually, Bungay rose to leave. Warrington and Pen followed, though Pen wished he could stay longer. He liked the family—especially little Mary, who preferred his lap to her father’s rough hand. As they walked through the prison corridors, Captain Shandon shuffled ahead in his slippers, already fiddling with his waistcoat pocket where the five-pound note had gone. At the gate, Pen breathed a sigh of relief—freedom, and Farringdon Street, once more.

Mrs. Shandon stayed behind, continuing her work at the window. She watched her husband race off toward the prison tavern, two men in tow. Dinner was ruined. A piece of meat and salad sat waiting, meant for her and Mary. But now, it would go cold. He’d be drinking, playing cards, returning late and drunk. Oh, the pain women endure!

She made tea instead. And oh, what a companion the tea-pot has been to countless women through the ages! It has comforted widows, cooled fevered lips, witnessed love letters, and held the secrets of lonely hearts. One could fill a book with scenes around the tea-table—Melissa whispering to Sacharissa, poor Polly weeping over her lover’s last letter, Mary bringing tea to her mother, Ruth brewing for her husband returning from the fields. And here, Mrs. Shandon and little Mary drank their tea together, while the Captain went out to enjoy his pleasures.

Jack Finucane, a fellow Irishman and Shandon’s faithful aide-de-camp, arrived shortly after. Jack always brought Mary a sweetmeat. He thought Shandon the greatest genius ever born, and he was always ready to run errands, settle debts, or collect money from publishers. Every embarrassed Irish gentleman, it seemed, had his own Jack Finucane—himself supported by others equally broke. Thus, Shandon marched through life with a ragged staff of dependents, all sharing in his misfortunes.

As they walked away from the prison, Bungay remarked, “He won’t have that five-pound note long, I bet a guinea.” And he was right. When Mrs. Shandon emptied her husband’s pockets later, she found only two shillings and a few halfpence. The rest had vanished: a pound to a follower, a leg of mutton and beer sent to a fellow inmate, a bill paid at the tavern, a dinner with friends, and a few half-crowns lost at cards. By nightfall, he was as poor as he’d been in the morning.

On their way home, Warrington repeated to Bungay what he’d told Bacon: that Pen was a fine fellow, full of genius, well-connected, and related to “no end” of peers. Bungay said he’d be happy to do business with Mr. Pendennis and hoped to see both men soon at dinner.

Later, over brandy-and-water at the Black Kitchen, Pen reflected: “It’s hard to see a man like Shandon—so talented, so witty—half his life in prison, the other half a hanger-on to a bookseller.”

“I’m a bookseller’s hanger-on,” Warrington laughed. “You’re going to try your paces as a hack. We’re all hacks, in one way or another. I’d rather be myself than Paley, our neighbour in chambers—who lives like a mole, underground and unseen. People throw too much pity on the poor writer.”

“But look at Shandon!” Pen protested. “A man of genius, forced to serve a vulgar slave-driver like Bungay—who fattens on the labour of others, enriches himself from their sweat, and can’t even speak the language of literature properly! It makes me furious to see a gentleman reduced to such servitude.”

“So you’ve started girding at publishers,” Warrington chuckled. “Bravo, Pen! What’s wrong with Bungay? Did he send Shandon to prison? Is it Bungay who’s spending that five-pound note?”

“Misfortune drives men into bad company,” Pen said. “We should be kinder to geniuses, especially when their very passion leads them astray.”

“A fiddlestick about geniuses!” Warrington snapped. “There aren’t nearly as many geniuses as people claim. Thousands of clever men could write articles, read books, and judge them as well as any critic. If a lawyer, soldier, or parson goes broke, he goes to jail. So does an author. If he drinks, he gets a hangover. If he orders a coat, he pays for it.”

“I’d give him more money to buy coats,” Pen smiled. “I suppose I’d like to belong to a well-dressed profession. I protest against that wretch—the middleman—between Genius and the Public, who steals half the earnings and fame.”

“I’m a prose labourer,” Warrington said. “You’re a poet in a small way, so you’re allowed to be flighty. Do you want capitalists to buy every manuscript presented? Every epic, every driveller’s novel, every tragedy? Who decides what’s good? Will the buyer get to choose? When Johnson sat behind a screen at Saint John’s Gate, too shabby to dine with the literary bigwigs, the publisher wasn’t doing him wrong. You can’t force a publisher to recognize genius in a ragged, hungry young man. Rags don’t prove genius. Capital does. And capital has a right to decide. I may think my horse is Pegasus—but the dealer wants a lady’s horse or a cob for a timid rider. My beast won’t suit him.”

“You deal in metaphors,” Pen said. “But you’re right—you’re very prosaic. Poor Shandon! There’s something about his kindness, and his wife’s sweetness, that touches me. I like him better than a better man.”

“And so do I,” Warrington said. “Let’s pity his weakness, and sympathize with his fate—even if he’d resent it. He takes his consolation along with his misfortune. He’s a prisoner, but not unhappy.”

“His genius sings within his bars,” Pen said.

“Yes,” Warrington replied bitterly. “Shandon adapts to a cage pretty well. He ought to be wretched—but he has Jack and Tom to drink with. He might have a high place—but he can’t, so he drinks with Tom and Jack. He might provide for his family—but Thomas and John want brandy. He might pay Snip the tailor—but John and Thomas take his purse. So he drinks, while his tradesman goes to jail and his family to ruin. Let us pity genius—and conspire against the publishing tyrants.”

“What! Another glass of brandy-and-water?” Pen asked, smiling.

Warrington laughed. “Video meliora proboque—I mean, bring it hot, with sugar, John.”

“I’d have some, too—only I don’t want it,” Pen said. “It doesn’t seem to me, Warrington, that we’re much better than our neighbours.”

They returned to their chambers, where two notes awaited them from Bungay—invitations to dinner, to meet his literary circle.

“We shall have a grand spread,” Warrington said.

“All except poor Shandon,” Pen replied, nodding goodnight. He went into his room, thoughts swirling. The day had stirred him deeply. Warrington snored loudly from the next room—deep, regular, the sound of a man who’d already given up on philosophy.

Pen lay awake, thinking.
