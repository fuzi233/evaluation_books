Not far along the road, Gerard came upon a small group. Two men in sober attire leaned casually against a horse, engaged in quiet conversation. The rider, dressed in a silk doublet and bright green jerkin and hose—both of English cloth, glossy as a mole’s back—lay flat on his stomach in the afternoon sun, looking like a giant lizard. His velvet cloak, flaming yellow, was carefully draped over the horse’s back.

“Is anything wrong?” Gerard asked.

“Not that I know of,” replied one of the servants.

“But your master lies like a dead man. Aren’t you ashamed to let him sprawl on the ground?”

“Go on,” said the servant. “The bare earth is the best cure for his condition. If you wake up from bed after drinking, you get a headache; but if you rise from hard ground, you leap up like a lark in spring. Eh, Ulric?”

“He speaks truth, young man,” Ulric added warmly.

“Then is the gentleman drunk?”

The servants burst into deep laughter at the simplicity of the question. But suddenly Ulric stopped, studying Gerard closely. “Who are you, and where were you born, that you don’t know the Count is always drunk at this hour?” And Gerard found himself under suspicion.

“I am a stranger,” he replied, “but honest and eager for knowledge. That is why I ask questions—not out of prying.”

“If you’re honest,” Ulric said shrewdly, “then give us trinkgeld for the wisdom we’ve shared.”

Gerard looked puzzled, but with a steady face, answered, “Trinkgeld you shall have—what my lean purse can spare—if you tell me why you took the man’s cloak and laid it on the horse.”

Inspired by the prospect of payment, two explanations sprang forth at once. One claimed that if the Count woke up (which, being a seasoned drinker, he often did instantly) and saw his horse sweating in the cold while a cloak lay unused nearby, he would fly into a rage—and perhaps lash out. The other, more elaborate theory, held that a horse, a poor creature drinking only water, needed warming and care; but a man, filled with good beer, carried enough inner heat to keep warm without a cloak, which was thus nothing but idle vanity.

Each speaker clung to his idea, and both had clearly imbibed a few drops of the same liquor that had befuddled their master. Their voices rose until the green-clad noble began to growl instead of snore. They didn’t notice.

Soon, the debate turned toward a topic certain to spark passion in that age. Hans, holding the bridle in one hand, struck Ulric sharply with the other. Ulric retaliated with interest, his free hand unencumbered. They went at each other, hammering over the horse’s mane, striking each other and jabbing the poor animal until it reared back and stepped heavily on the green lord’s protruding limb. He leapt up howling, one hand clutching his pain, the other gripping his hilt. The servants, startled, released the horse. It galloped off, whinnying, the men chasing after it in panic, and the noble behind them, cursing wildly, sword drawn, body bouncing from hedge to hedge as he zigzagged down the narrow lane.

Gerard turned his back and walked calmly southward, glad to have saved the four tin farthings he’d prepared for trinkgeld—but too heavy-hearted even to smile at their drunken folly.

The sun was nearly set, and Gerard, who had long hoped in vain to find an inn, felt increasingly uneasy. To make matters worse, dark clouds gathered overhead.

He quickened his pace, almost to a run.

It was useless. Rain fell in torrents, drenching the bewildered traveler and seeming to extinguish the sun itself—its fading rays no match for this sudden storm.

Gerard trudged on, soaked and shivering, in an unknown land. “Fool,” he muttered, “to leave Margaret.”

Darkness thickened.

He entered a vast forest. Massive branches arched across the narrow path, and the lost wanderer stumbled through what seemed an endless, ink-black cave with a rough floor, tripping again and again.

Onward, onward, with trembling limbs, empty stomach, and failing courage—until wolves rose from their dens and howled all around the woods.

His hair stood on end, but he gripped his cudgel tightly, ready to defend himself to the last.

There was no wind. His sharp ears caught light footsteps pattering over fallen leaves, and low branches rustling as unseen creatures slipped past.

Then, in the sea of darkness, a fiery star appeared close to the ground. He hailed it like a saint. “CANDLE! A CANDLE!” he shouted, trying to run. But the rugged terrain soon halted him. The light was farther than he thought. Yet finally, deep in the heart of the forest, he found a house—lit candles inside, voices loud and clear. He looked up for a signboard. There was none.

“Not an inn after all,” he sighed. “No matter. What Christian would turn a dog out into this wood tonight?” With that, he approached the door leading to the voices. He opened it slowly, peering in timidly—then pulled his head back abruptly as if struck, retreating into the rain and night.

He had glimpsed a large, low room dominated by a massive round stove, rising to the ceiling. Around it hung wet clothes—some on lines, others draped over wooden frames. These garments, soaked with the day’s rain and stained with life’s grime, lined with what another traveler might call “rammish clowns,” gave off thick vapors and indescribable odors in steaming clouds.

In one corner sat a traveling family, large and noisy—adding their own peculiar, sour smell of neglected children. Garlic filled the air between. All this, combined with closed windows, intense heat from the central furnace, and the breath of at least forty people, created a stifling atmosphere.

They had just finished supper.

Gerard, like most artists, had sensitive senses. The overpowering smells sent a wave of dismay through him. But the rain lashed him outside, and the light and warmth drew him in.

He couldn’t force himself through the thick perfumes at once, but returned again and again, like a moth drawn to flame. Eventually, he realized the odors didn: mix entirely—no fiend stirred them together. Family odor dominated two corners; stewed rustic ruled the center; garlic reigned near the window. By quick analysis, he found garlic occupied the smallest space in the air, while the scent of damp clothing spread farthest—a stench as if ancient goats or ancestors of foxes had been dragged through a river and dried by Nebuchadnezzar.

So Gerard crept into a corner near the door. Though the main odors were somewhat isolated, heat and moisture still circulated, making the walls drip. The novice felt a cold snake coil around his legs, his head grow heavy as lead, then a choking sensation, followed by a sweet drowsiness—almost death.

He was close to fainting, but regained consciousness with a deep sense of disgust and defeat. He decided to return to Holland at first light. This resolve lifted his spirits slightly. Still faint from hunger, he asked one of the garlic-smelling men if this wasn’t, after all, an inn.

“Where do you come from, that you don’t know ‘The Star of the Forest’?” came the reply.

“I’m a stranger. In my country, inns always have a sign.”

“Your land must be odd! Why need a sign for a public house—a place every soul knows?”

Gerard, too tired for argument, changed the subject. “Where can I find the landlord?”

This fresh display of ignorance raised the native’s contempt beyond words. He pointed to a middle-aged woman seated on the opposite side of the stove, then turned to his companions, announcing they had an outlandish creature among them. At once, the loud voices ceased, one by one, as the news spread. Every eye turned silently, like a zoological audience, watching Gerard’s every move.

The landlady sat on a chair slightly higher than the rest, between two bundles. From the first—a huge heap of feathers and wings—she plucked downy plumes, pulling others from quills, filling the second bundle and littering the floor ankle-deep. Her work contributed a stuffy, malarial haze, which might have mattered in a fine room, but meant nothing here.

Gerard asked her if he could have something to eat.

She opened her eyes in astonishment. “Supper ended over an hour ago.”

“But I didn’t eat, good woman.”

“Is that my fault? You were welcome to your share.”

“But I arrived late and was lost—against my will.”

“What concern is that of mine? Everyone knows ‘The Star of the Forest’ sups from six to eight. Come before six, you eat well; before eight, you eat as Heaven pleases; after eight, you get a clean bed and a stirrup cup—or a horn of cow’s milk—at dawn.”

Gerard stared blankly. “May I go to bed, then?” he asked sulkily. “It’s no good sitting wet and fasting. The proverb says, ‘He sups who sleeps.’”

“The beds haven’t arrived yet,” she replied. “You’ll sleep when the others do. Inns aren’t built for one.”

Gerard was stunned. “The beds weren’t here? What in Heaven’s name did she mean?” But he dared not ask—every word he’d spoken had amazed the crowd, and their silent, judging eyes watched him. He leaned against the wall and sighed deeply.

A ripple of titters ran through the watchful company.

“So this is Germany,” Gerard thought. “And Germany is a great country compared to Holland. Small nations for me.”

He consoled himself by remembering it would be his last night in the land—his first and final.

His thoughts were interrupted by an elbow jabbed into his ribs. He turned sharply on his assailant, who pointed across the room. Gerard looked and saw a woman in the corner beckoning. He approached cautiously, surprised and uncertain—so much so that to an observer, her finger seemed to pull him forward with a string.

When he reached her, she said in a strong, cheerful voice, “Hold the child.” In a moment, she dropped the baby into his arms.

He froze, hands turning to lead, horror spreading across his face.

At this expression, the sharp-eyed assembly laughed loudly and long.

“Don’t mind them,” she said cheerfully. “They know no better—how should they, born and bred in a wood?” She rummaged through her clothes with two searching hands—one of which Gerard had freed. Soon she pulled out a small tin plate and a dried pudding, then resumed the child with one arm and offered the food with the other, keeping a thumb on the pudding to prevent it from slipping.

“Put it in the stove,” she said. “You’re too young to lie down fasting.”

Gerard thanked her warmly. On his way to the stove, he glanced at the landlady. “May I, good dame?” he asked pleadingly.

“Why not?” she replied.

The question was clearly another surprise—though less shocking than the rest.

At the stove, he found the oven blocked by “the rammish clowns.” They wouldn’t move. He hesitated. The landlady noticed, calmly put down her work, and came over, pushing aside a few hircine figures with the impassive air of a housewife rearranging furniture. “Turn about is fair play,” she said. “You’ve been dry ten minutes now.”

Her practiced eye saw the truth: Gorgonii had finished stewing and begun baking. Deprived of the stove, they retreated—all except one, who stood like a table where she’d placed him. Gerard baked his pudding and, reaching the stove, burst into steam.

The door flew open, and a bundle of straw shot in.

It was thrown by a hind with a pitchfork. Another followed, then another, until the room resembled a clean farmyard. These were arranged in layers around the stove like arena seats. In moments, everyone lay on their backs.

The beds had arrived.

Gerard removed his pudding and found it delicious. As he ate, the woman who had given it to him—now in bed—beckoned again. He went to her side. “She’s waiting for you,” she whispered. Gerard returned to the stove and finished his sausage, casting nervous glances at the landlady, seated silently amid the sleeping crowd. After eating, he approached her. “Thank you kindly, good woman, for waiting.”

“You’re welcome,” she said calmly, neither overawed nor dismissive. Then she began gathering feathers. Gerard stopped her. “No, that’s my task,” he said, kneeling and collecting them with fervor. She watched quietly.

“I don’t know where you come from,” she said, still cautious. Then, more warmly, “But you’ve been well brought up—you had a good mother, I’ll wager.”

At the door, she prayed for the entire company and vanished. Gerard curled up in the very corner—the guests lay around the sacred stove in order of arrival.

This position was a blessing, placing him on the edge of the stench and heat, rather than in its midst.

Just as he drifted off, a noise awakened him. There stood the hind, shaking and waking each guest in turn, asking if it was he who had picked up the mistress’s feathers.

“It was I,” Gerard called.

“Oh, it was you?” the man said, striding over. “She told me to say, ‘One good turn deserves another.’ Here’s your nightcap,” and he thrust a large oaken mug under Gerard’s nose.

“I thank her, and bless her,” Gerard said, raising it—then grimaced. The beer was muddy, with a strange, medicinal taste unfamiliar to the Hollander.

“Trinke aus!” the hind shouted reproachfully.

“Enough is as good as a feast,” Gerard replied, Jesuitically.

The hind looked pityingly at the stranger who left liquor in his mug. “Ich brings euch,” he said, and drained it to the bottom.

Gerard turned his face to the wall, pulled up handfuls of clean straw, bored holes with his finger, and made a scabbard for his nose. Soon, all were asleep—men, maids, wives, children—snoring in a dozen keys like an orchestra tuning up. Gerard’s body rested on straw in Germany, while his spirit wandered to Sevenbergen.

When he awoke the next morning, nearly all his fellow travelers had gone. A few waited for dinner at nine—it was now six. He paid the landlady two pfenning—about half a penny—and the hind demanded trinkgeld. Receiving more than usual, and seeing Gerard eye a foaming milk-pail he’d just fetched from the cow, he hoisted it to his lips. “Drink your fill, man,” he said. When Gerard offered to pay, the man replied in broad patois that a man could drink a skinful of milk or a breakfast of air without touching his purse.

At the door, Gerard found his benefactress from the night before and her husband, a large, sturdy artisan.

He thanked her and, in the spirit of the time, offered a creutzer for her pudding.

She gently pushed his hand away. “What do you take me for?” she asked, blushing faintly. “We’re travelers, strangers like you—bound to feel for those in similar straits.”

Gerard blushed and stammered apologies.

Her husband grinned, superior to both.

“Give the vixen a kiss for her pudding, and call it quits,” he said, with an air of impartial judgment, like Jove himself.

Gerard obeyed, kissed her cheek, and said, “God bless you both, good people.”

“And God speed you, young man!” they replied. And so they parted—never to meet again in this world.

The sun had just risen. Raindrops on the leaves glittered like diamonds. The air was fresh and invigorating. Gerard headed south, forgetting his resolution of the night before.

He walked eight leagues that day and, in the afternoon, came upon a massive building with a grand arched gateway and a smaller postern beside it.

“A monastery!” he exclaimed joyfully. “I’ll go no further—I might fare worse.” He knocked at the postern, explained his journey, and was immediately admitted to the guest chamber—a large, lofty room where travelers were fed and lodged free by monastic charity.

Soon, the bell rang for vespers. Gerard entered the church and heard a service sung so beautifully, it seemed the choir of heaven. But one thing was missing—Margaret was not there to hear it with him. This made him sigh bitterly, even in rapture.

At supper, plain but wholesome food and good beer—brewed in the convent—were served. Early that evening, they were shown to a large dormitory. Since the number was modest, each had a truckle bed and sheepskins with fleece intact for coverings. Before this, a monk, struck by Gerard’s youth and appearance, questioned him and quickly uncovered his plans and feelings. When he learned Gerard was raised in a convent and traveling alone to Rome, he became a person of interest. The next morning, they showed him the monastery, invited him to dine in the refectory, gave him a route marked on parchment, and the prior presented him with a silver guilden to aid his journey. He advised Gerard to join the first honest company he met—“and not face alone the manifold perils of the way.”

“Perils?” Gerard thought to himself.

That evening, he reached a small, scattered town with one inn. It had no sign, but now more familiar with local customs, he recognized it by the coats of arms on its walls—tokens left by distinguished visitors over the years. It looked more like a mausoleum than a hotel. Nothing moved or sounded within or without. Gerard hammered on the great oak door—no answer. He shouted—no reply. After a while, he shouted louder. Finally, a small round window, or hole in the wall, opened. A man’s head emerged cautiously, like a tortoise from its shell, and stared at Gerard stolidly—without a word.

“Is this an inn?” Gerard asked, with a hidden sneer.

The head seemed to ponder. Then it nodded—slowly.

“Can I have lodging here?”

Again, the head considered, then nodded—sullenly, as if burdened by trivial questions.

“How do I get in, if you please?”

At this, the head vanished, as if the question had driven it back. A hand appeared, pointed around the corner of the building, and slammed the window shut.

Gerard followed the direction and, after some search, found a small, low door on the side of the structure. The main entrance, it seemed, was used only to keep out thieves and customers—except once or twice a year, when a duke or count arrived in pomp with his colorful entourage.

Gerard passed through the outer gate and soon found the stove—the public room named for its centerpiece. He sat near the oven, where only a few live embers burned, spreading a mild, comforting warmth.

After waiting patiently, he asked a grim old man with a long white beard—who solemnly entered, turned the hourglass, and left—when supper would be served.

The old man counted the guests on his fingers. “When I see three times as many here as now.”

Gerard groaned.

The old man frowned. “Inns aren’t built for one,” he said. “If you can’t wait, find another lodging.”

Gerard sighed.

The old man frowned again.

Eventually, guests trickled in—until eighty people of various sorts gathered. To Gerard, the place became a chamber of horrors: mothers comparing ringworms, men scraping mud from their shoes with knives and leaving it on the floor, combing their long hair—often including inmates—and performing their toilette with dry rubs. Water was brought in ewers. Gerard seized one—but at the sight of the liquid, lost his temper. “Wash your water first,” he snapped at the waiter, “then a man may wash his hands.”

“An’ it doesn’t please you, seek another inn!”

Gerard said nothing, but quietly asked an old traveler how far it was to the next inn.

“About four leagues.”

Then Gerard understood the old man’s grim humor.

The worthy returned with an armload of wood, counted the guests, and added a log for every six—making the room hotter as more heat was added. Poor Gerard noticed the flaw in logic but kept silent, fearing his feet might carry his brain four leagues farther that night.

As perspiration and suffocation mounted, they brought in tablecloths—oh, so brown, so dirty, so coarse—they looked like sacks worn out in farming or scraps from a ship’s mainsail. The Hollander, who had never seen such linen—even in dreams—let out a faint cry.

“What’s wrong?” a traveler asked. Gerard pointed sadly to the filthy cloth. The other stared, uncomprehending.

A Burgundian soldier with a crossbow at his back leaned over Gerard’s shoulder, saw the issue, and roared with laughter. The room echoed. Then he slapped Gerard on the back. “Courage! Le diable est mort.”

Gerard stared—he doubted both the news and its relevance. But the tone was hearty, and though the soldier’s beard was formidable, his face was gay and kind. Gerard smiled, then after a pause said dryly, “Il a bien faite avec l’eau et linge du pays on allait le noircir à ne se reconnaître plus.”

“Tiens, tiens!” the soldier exclaimed. “V’la qui parle le Français peu s’en faut.” He sat beside Gerard and began talking eagerly of war, women, and plunder, sprinkling his speech with curious oaths. Gerard edged away, more or less.

Then the grim servant returned, counting the guests on his fingers like Abraham tallying sheep. He left, then returned with a wooden trencher and spoon for each.

And so the night wore on.
