The tunnel, though concealed by human ingenuity, was a vein where the fiery blood of Mother Earth had once flowed. Centuries had drained the lava, yet the air remained warm and thick with sulfur. There was no reason to linger; I lit a lamp from a niche and moved forward, coughing violently. I wished desperately for the herbal remedies I had once stolen from the Vice-Royalty of Yucatan, but those packets had been lost in the naval battle with Dason's fleet. Since landing in Atlantis, time had been too short to indulge in medical refinements.

The network of subterranean tunnels branched wildly. If anyone else of the Seven Priests had stumbled into this labyrinth by chance, they would have perished hopelessly in the twists or fallen into the pits leading to the boiling depths. Fortunately, I carried the chart of the true course etched clearly in my memory, a relic from my initiation twenty years prior. Back then, as an appointed Viceroy, I had been elevated to the highest rank within the Clan, entrusted with its secrets and tools.

The journey was arduous; the floor was monstrously uneven, and the air foul. I knew daylight would be far advanced before the signs told me I had slipped beneath the city walls. Here, the vow of the Seven held me back: under no circumstance, however desperate, was exit permitted before a mortal eye beheld it. Branch after branch, I tried, only to find loiterers near every exit. The city hummed with a strange, electric excitement. I had hoped to emerge near the Royal Pyramid, but the place buzzed like a hive, teeming with observers. The same applied to the five subsequent outlets I checked. The capital was agog.

Finally, I reached the temple of a minor god. Standing behind the statue, I observed. The place was desolate, untouched for so long that dust coated every floor and seat. Satisfied, I cleared the necessary debris, stepped out, and sealed the entrance behind me. A broom lay unnoticed on a pew. With it, I quickly erased all traces of my footprints and approached the temple door. It was locked. As a priest, I possessed no knowledge of its mechanism. Only the attendant priests could move the massive stone filling the doorway. If everyone had fled to watch whatever spectacle stirred the city, I was trapped.

Inside the temple precincts, silence reigned, broken only by evidence of decay and disuse. Yet, ancient law dictated that at least one priest should always watch from the dwellings located in the roof. I descended the passages to check. It surprised me little to find them deserted. The old order was crumbling, swept away ruthlessly by a new regime.

Yet, amidst general apostasy, faithful souls often remain. Reaching the door of a dwelling, I called out. It opened slowly. Inside, a man peered dully through the gloom. He had kept his vows, and I saluted him with respect. His name was Ro. We had trained together in the sacred college, though he was always known as the "dullard." He lacked capacity for the cult of the gods, the arts of ruling, or the handling of arms. Appointed to a lowly post in this obscure temple, he rose to become its second priest and one of two custodians solely because his colleagues had deserted the cause. It was bitter to think that a fool remained true where the clever had abandoned belief.

Ro performed a profound obeisance. His beard, curled in the prevailing fashion, was a mess; his clothes were ill-fitting and unbrushed. He was a slovenly man. "The temple door is shut," he said, his voice echoing softly. "Only I hold the secret of its opening. My lord enters by the secret way, as one of the Seven. I am his servant."

"Then I request a small favor," I said. "Tell me, what stirs the city?"

"That impious Phorenice has declared herself Goddess!" Ro shouted, his composure breaking. "She claims she will light the sacrifice with her own divine fire. And she will do it. She does everything. But I pray the flames consume her when she calls them down. This new Empress is the bane of our Clan, Deucalion. The people neglect us; they bring no offerings. Now, with these rebels hammering at the walls, I might have starved if I hadn't stored a little corn myself. Oh, tell me, Deucalion, the cult of the true Gods is oozing out of the land!"

"My brother," I replied, "it seems the Priests of our Clan have been limp in their service, allowing these things to come to pass."

"I suppose we did our best," Ro muttered, looking down. "At least, we followed the teachings. But if the people refuse to listen to your exhortations, what hold do you have over their religion? The High Gods are trying our faith hard. Come, look at my bed."

I saw the shape of a man, swathed untidily in reddened bandages.

"This is all that remains of the poor priest who was my immediate superior," Ro whispered, disgust coloring his tone. "It was his turn yesterday to celebrate the weekly sacrifice to our Lord the Sun at the Circle of Stones. Faugh, Deucalion, you should have seen how he was mangled when they brought him back."

"Did the people rise against him?" I asked, dread creeping into my chest.

"The people remained passive," Ro said bitterly, gesturing to the corpse. "Few attended, those who had interest. But our Lord the Sun tried His minister severely. The wood was laid, the sacrifice placed, and the procession formed around the altar. Drums and trumpets sounded, promising that the smoke of their prayers would ascend to the heavens. Then, above the ceremony, a rushing sound cut through—the wings of a great featherless man-eating bird, larger than any seen before."

"An arrow to the eye, or a spear shaft, would have sufficed," I suggested.

"Oh, all men learn that as children, Deucalion!" Ro snapped. "But the priests were unarmed, as the rubric ordains—they must entrust themselves completely to the guardianship of the High Gods. The bird swooped down, settling on the pyre, and attacked the offering with beak and talon. My poor superior, still strong in faith, called loudly for our Lord the Sun to empower his arm. He sprang onto the altar, armed only with his teeth and bare fists. Perhaps he expected a miracle—he has not spoken since, poor soul—but all he received were blows from leathery wings and raking talons nearly disemboweling him. The bird brushed him aside like a fly, leaving him bleeding on the pavement beside the altar while the sacrifice was torn apart before the eyes of all. Then, glutted, it flew away to the mountains."

"And the people offered no aid?"

"They cried out that the beast was a portent!" Ro paced frantically. "They claimed our Lord the Sun was no longer a God if He could not protect His sacrifice. Others screamed there was no God left, while others insisted a new God had arrived, taking the form of a man-eating bird. But a few began shouting that Phorenice stood for *all* the Gods now in Atlantis. That cry was taken up until the stones of the great circle rang with it. Some proclaimed it because they believed; many because the cry was new and pleasing; but I am certain many joined simply because it was dangerous to oppose such an outburst. The Empress is hard enough on those who neglect her adulation."

"The Empress is Empress," I said formally, maintaining my composure. "Her name commands respect. It is not for us to question her actions."

"I am a priest," Ro argued, defensive. "I speak as I am taught, defending the Faith as commanded. Whether faith exists anymore, I begin to doubt. But it yields a poor livelihood. No offerings have come to this temple in five months. Without my small store of corn, I would surely starve. And I do not believe the affair of that sacrifice will reignite enthusiasm among our cold votaries."

"When did it happen?"

"Twenty hours ago," Ro said. "Today, Phorenice conducts the sacrifice herself. That has caused the stir you mentioned. The city is in thralls, preparing for her pageant."

"Therefore, I ask you to open the temple doors and grant me passage. I must see this for myself."

"It is not for me to advise one of the Seven," Ro murmured doubtfully.

"It is not."

"But they say the Empress is displeased by your absence," he mumbled, fear edging his voice. "I should not wish harm to come to you, Deucalion."

"The future lies in the hands of the Most High Gods, Ro. I at least believe They will mete out our fates as infinite wisdom sees best, even if you have lost your faith. And now, I owe you a passage through these doors." I paused, noticing his hesitation. "Plagues! Man, it is useless for you to offer me alms. I own not a single coin in the world."

He mumbled something about "force of habit" as he led me toward the door, responding tartly to my jibe about his beggarly customs. "If it weren't for men like you and your customs, the Priests' Clan would not face this crisis today."

"One must live," he grumbled, pressing levers that caused the massive stone to swing ajar.
