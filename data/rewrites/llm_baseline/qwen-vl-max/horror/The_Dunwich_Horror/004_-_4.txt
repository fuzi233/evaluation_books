For a decade, the Whateley household faded into the grim routine of Dunwich—a place long accustomed to its peculiarities and indifferent to the strange rites that marked May Eve and All-Hallow’s Eve. Twice each year, fires blazed atop Sentinel Hill, and with each passing season, the mountain groaned more violently beneath them. At the isolated farmhouse, whispers grew louder: odd sounds echoed from the sealed upper floor, even when no one was above. Visitors claimed to hear the lowing of cattle—sometimes prolonged, sometimes abrupt—as if animals were being slaughtered in silence. There was talk of reporting it to the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals, but such concerns never reached beyond murmurs. Dunwich folk preferred their secrets buried.

By 1923, Wilbur Whateley was ten years old—yet already he seemed far older. His voice was deep, his face shadowed with hair, his frame unnaturally tall. He moved through life with a quiet intensity that unsettled those who saw him. That year, another wave of construction swept over the old house—this time entirely within the forbidden upper story. Fragments of wood left behind told the tale: all partitions had been torn down, the attic floor removed, leaving a single vast, hollow space stretching from ground to roof. Even the great central chimney was gone, replaced by a flimsy tin stovepipe leading outside.

Then came the whippoorwills.

Old Whateley noticed them first—hordes emerging from Cold Spring Glen, chirping endlessly beneath his window at night. He watched them with a strange reverence. “They whistle jest in tune with my breathin’ now,” he muttered to the men at Osborn’s tavern. “An’ I guess they’re gittin’ ready to ketch my soul. They know it’s a-goin’ out, an’ dun’t calc’late to miss it. Yew’ll know, boys, arter I’m gone, whether they git me er not. Ef they dew, they’ll keep up a-singin’ an’ laffin’ till break o’ day. Ef they dun’t, they’ll kinder quiet daown like. I expeck them an’ the souls they hunts fer hev some pretty tough tussles sometimes.”

On Lammas Night, 1924, Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was summoned by Wilbur, who rode through the dark with desperate haste, telephoning from the village inn. When the doctor arrived, Old Whateley lay near death—his breathing labored, his heart failing. Lavinia, the pale, twisted daughter, stood beside the bed, her eyes wide. Wilbur loomed nearby, silent and watchful. From the open void above, a rhythmic lapping could be heard—like waves on a distant shore. But it was the birds outside that truly chilled the doctor: a endless swarm of whippoorwills, their cries timed perfectly to the dying man’s gasps. It was as if they were counting his breaths.

Near midnight, Old Whateley stirred. With great effort, he spoke to Wilbur. “More space, Willy, more space soon. Yew grows—an’ _that_ grows faster. It’ll be ready to sarve ye soon, boy. Open up the gates to Yog-Sothoth with the long chant that ye’ll find on page 751 _of the complete edition_, an’ _then_ put a match to the prison. Fire from airth can’t burn it nohaow!”

He was clearly unhinged. The birds adjusted their song to his new rhythm. Distant rumblings echoed from the hills. Then, after a pause, he whispered again: “Feed it reg’lar, Willy, an’ mind the quantity; but dun’t let it grow too fast fer the place, fer ef it busts quarters or gits aout afore ye opens to Yog-Sothoth, it’s all over an’ no use. Only them from beyont kin make it multiply an’ work.... Only them, the old uns as wants to come back....”

His words dissolved into choking breaths. Lavinia screamed as the birds mimicked his final gasps. Then, just before dawn, the last rattle escaped. Dr. Houghton closed the dead man’s eyes as the chorus of birds faded into silence. Lavinia wept. Wilbur chuckled softly. “They didn’t git him,” he said, his voice thick and deep.

Wilbur was now a scholar of immense, unsettling knowledge—known across distant libraries for his correspondence about rare and forbidden texts. He was feared in Dunwich, especially after several youths vanished under mysterious circumstances. Though suspicion clung to him, no one dared accuse him—fear and the lingering wealth of the Whateley line kept questions at bay. His appearance only deepened the dread: he towered over others, his height now surpassing normal limits. In 1925, a visitor from Miskatonic University left pale and shaken—he had seen Wilbur standing fully six feet seven inches tall.

Through the years, Wilbur treated his mother with growing contempt. He forbade her from joining him on the sacred nights. In 1926, she confided to Mamie Bishop, “They’s more abaout him as I knows than I kin tell ye, Mamie... an’ naowadays they’s more nor what I know myself. I vaow afur Gawd, I dun’t know what he wants nor what he’s a-tryin’ to dew.”

That Hallowe’en, the hill noises thundered louder than ever. Fires burned on Sentinel Hill as usual. But the true horror came from the sky—masses of whippoorwills, late by a month, gathered near the dark farmhouse. After midnight, their cries erupted into a shrieking frenzy, filling the valley with a sound like madness itself. Not until dawn did they cease—and then, they fled southward in a rush, vanishing without trace.

No one died that night. But Lavinia Whateley was never seen again.

In the summer of 1927, Wilbur began rebuilding two sheds in the yard, moving his books and possessions into them. Soon, Earl Sawyer reported more carpentry at the house—this time on the ground floor. Doors and windows were sealed shut. Partitions were being torn down, just as they had done upstairs years before. Wilbur lived in one of the sheds now, and Sawyer said he looked agitated, trembling at times. People avoided him. Few dared approach his land.

And his height—now well over seven feet—showed no sign of stopping.
