The water at Tunbridge Wells tasted of iron and gossip. Within two days, Harry Warrington ceased to be a visitor and became a fixture, a local deity carved from colonial timber and imported currency. The Bishop of Salisbury commanded deference, yet Harry drew stares that lingered longer, eyes tracking his movement through the market square as if he were a specimen in a glass case. Matrons at the drawing-room tables maneuvered their daughters into his orbit, offering smiles that were less affectionate than strategic. Every soul in the place possessed a ledger of his affairs: the acreage of his estate, the yield of his income, the precise sum he had wagered and lost—or won—at every tea table. Even his servant, Gumbo, was elevated to a position of influence, fed ale and cold meat by strangers eager to purchase news of their young master. Gumbo fattened on the attention, becoming a pillar of the valetocracy, spinning tales so grandiose they threatened to swallow the truth whole.

No gathering was considered complete without Mr. Warrington. The lad watched this ascent with a mixture of amusement and astonishment, accepting his honors with a stoicism bred from a lifetime of self-regard. He did not boast; he simply existed as a winner. His code was peculiar, a rigid adherence to a personal honor that demanded attendance whenever summoned, whether for a race, a drinking bout, or a card game played in the name of Old Virginia. And the locals, hungry for entertainment, were eager to test him. They found him formidable: a strong head, a skilled hand, a steady seat, nerves that did not flinch. The representative of the colonies emerged victorious in his friendly rivalry with the mother country.

Madame de Bernstein, who had exhausted her deck of cards nightly to repair previous losses, found fresh delight in her nephew's triumphs. He had bested Jack Morris at shooting, outpaced Mr. Scamper on horseback, and held his own against Captain Batts at tennis despite having never touched a racket before. He faced the celebrated gamblers, the Lords of Chesterfield and March, and earned their testimony to his coolness and breeding. At the books, he lacked brilliance, yet his handwriting was competent enough to pass among men of fashion, and the naive simplicity of his ignorance provided the old lady with endless amusement. She preferred the world to the library, trading novels for games, and she supervised his transformation with maternal fervor, selecting fine laces for his ruffles and procuring a diamond brooch for his collar. He became the favorite of all her kin. Lady Maria, too, seemed pleased by his success, though her brothers must have trembled with envy as they witnessed their uncle's rapid climb in the affections of the wealthy aunt.

Two weeks in Tunbridge had transformed Harry into a personage. He knew the good company, and inevitably, the bad. Was it his fault that he accepted the world as it presented itself, drinking from the cup of sparkling pleasure filled to the brim? The aunt enjoyed his triumphs, urging him to pursue his enjoyments without moral reservation. She was not a rigorous preceptress. Had the Cattarina sent billets-doux, the aunt might have encouraged acceptance, but Harry carried a stock of modesty from his colonial home, worn alongside his honest homespun linen. Libertinism was unknown in the rough towns of America where he came from; the vices of great cities were foreign concepts. Harry blushed at the daring talk of his new European associates. Even the worldly jokes of Aunt Bernstein astounded him, reducing the old woman to calling him "Joseph" or "simpleton."

Yet innocence offered no shield against the world's judgment. The world credited him with the sins of others. How could he know that associating with the saucy Cattarina was forbidden? He had seen Lord March drive her in his phaeton. To Harry, giving her his arm on the public walks seemed harmless. When she desired a trinket from the toy shop, he bought it—a locket she coveted. The next day, lace. The next day, something else. Her fancies were endless, until Harry stopped, deflecting her requests with laughter. He was shrewd, generous, but not reckless; he had no intention of buying diamond drops for her ears.

Who, then, was to credit his modesty? Old Bernstein insisted her nephew was playing the role of Don Juan, supplanting Lord March. She pressed the point harder when Maria was present, deriving a dark pleasure from stabbing the spinster's tender heart, reveling in her niece's piteous silence.

"Why, my dear," the Baroness declared, "boys will be boys, and I don't want Harry to be the first milksop in his family!" The bread Maria ate at her aunt's expense often choked her. Some women possess a cruel alchemy, turning sustenance into poison.

Colonel Wolfe arrived frequently from Westerham to court the lady of his love. Knowing the Colonel was entirely occupied, Harry expected little interaction, though he valued the officer's conversation. It differed sharply from the ribald chatter surrounding Harry. Wolfe spoke of no cards, no horse pedigrees, no boasts of hunting feats or favors won from women, no retailing of the era's innumerable scandals. It was not a good time. The old world was more dissolute than theirs, populated by a king with mistresses openly in his train, honored by the great. There was a nobility mad with reckless pleasure, a looseness of word and act that honest historians must note without detailing, lest they shock the virtuous. Harry had lighted upon some of the wildest of these people and found an old relative living in the very midst of the rout.

Harry failed to notice how Colonel Wolfe avoided him, or how the Colonel's demeanor grew cold and altered during their casual meetings. He did not know the stories circulating about him. Who tells them? Who are the fathers of those wondrous lies? Poor Harry remained unaware of the reputation he was cultivating. While he rode, played, and frolicked, he was passing among respectable persons as the most abandoned, profligate, and godless of young men.

Alas, to think that the gentle, quiet lad they liked so well—the simple soul easily pleased—should be a hardened profligate, a spendthrift, a confirmed gamester, a frequenter of abandoned women! These stories reached honest Colonel Lambert at Oakhurst. First one tale, then another, then a flood of them, filling the good man's heart with grief and care until his family sensed the disturbance. Initially, Lambert refused to speak, setting aside his wife's fond inquiries. Mrs. Lambert imagined a great misfortune: ruin, danger, illness, death. Who can resist an anxious woman, or escape the cross-examination of the conjugal pillow? Lambert was forced to reveal a portion of what he knew. The wife was grieved and amazed. From the bedroom, the grief, stifled momentarily under pillows, descended the stairs. Theo and Hester inherited the complaint, suffering terribly. O, kind, little, wounded hearts! Hester initially turned red, flew into a passion, clenched her fists, and vowed disbelief, but eventually succumbed. Scandal masters people, especially the good and innocent. Oh, the serpent they had nursed by their fire! Oh, the wretched boy! To imagine him walking with that horrible painted Frenchwoman, giving her diamond necklaces, parading his shame before the society at the Wells! After the three ladies wept and the father was deeply moved, they confided in the parson. In vain he preached his favorite sermon on scandal next Sunday, inveighing against humanity's propensity to think evil. We repent, we promise to do so no more; yet when the next bad story concerns our neighbor, we believe it. So did the kind, wretched folk of Oakhurst believe the rumors about poor Harry Warrington.

Meanwhile, Harry was too pleased with himself to perceive how ill his friends thought of him. He pursued an idle, pleasant, if unprofitable life, oblivious to the hubbub he created and the dreadful repute he held. Emerging from a tennis match with Captain Batts, satisfied with his play and the world's approval, Harry overtook Colonel Wolfe, who had just visited the lady of his heart. Harry extended his hand; the Colonel took it, but the salute was so cold that the young man could not help noticing it, particularly how Wolfe, returning a fine bow from Captain Batts's hat, barely touched his own with his forefinger. The tennis captain walked away disconcerted, Harry remaining to speak with his friend from Westerham. Wolfe walked beside him for a moment, erect, silent, and icy.

"I have not seen you these many days," Harry said.

"You have had other companions," Wolfe replied curtly.

"But I had rather be with you than any of them," Harry cried.

"Indeed, I might be better company for you than some of them," the other said.

"Is it Captain Batts you mean?" Harry asked.

"He is no favorite of mine," Wolfe admitted grimly. "He bore a rascally reputation in the army, and I doubt he has mended it since being turned out. You certainly might find a better friend than Captain Batts. Pardon the freedom."

"Friend! He is no friend; he only teaches me to play tennis. He is hand-in-glove with my lord and all the fashionable people here who play."

"I am not a man of fashion," Wolfe stated.

"My dear Colonel, what is the matter? Have I angered you? You speak as if I had, and I am not conscious of having done anything to forfeit your regard."
