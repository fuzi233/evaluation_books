June 27th, 1849.

My instructions from Sergeant Cuff were clear and urgent: track three men suspected of murder, described vaguely as "Indians." They had been spotted at Tower Wharf that very morning, boarding a steamer destined for Rotterdam.

I departed London aboard a rival company's vessel on the morning of Thursday, the 28th. Upon arriving in Rotterdam, I located the commander of the Wednesday's ship. He confirmed that the three men had indeed been passengers, but only as far as Gravesend. There, the situation had taken a peculiar turn. Off the coast, the spokesman of the trio had asked for the time of arrival at Calais. When informed that the steamer was bound for Rotterdam, he feigned the utmost distress and surprise at their navigational error. Claiming they were willing to forfeit their passage money to correct the mistake, they pleaded to be set ashore. Moved by their plight as strangers in a foreign land, the commander signaled for a boat, and the three men disembarked.

It was obvious to me that this maneuver was premeditated—a calculated attempt to shed their trail. I returned to England immediately, leaving the steamer at Gravesend. My investigation revealed that the men had returned to London and subsequently traveled to Plymouth. Local inquiries confirmed that forty-eight hours prior, they had sailed on the *Bewley Castle*, an East Indiaman bound directly for Bombay.

Armed with this intelligence, Sergeant Cuff coordinated with the authorities in Bombay to intercept the vessel the moment it entered the port. With the police ready to board, my involvement in the case concluded. I have heard nothing further since.

*Statement regarding the passengers on the *Bewley Castle*, 1849.*

At the request of Sergeant Cuff, I record the following concerning three men, believed to be Hindus, who were passengers under my command on the voyage to Bombay.

These men joined us at Plymouth. Throughout the outward passage, I received no complaints regarding their conduct. They were quartered in the forward section of the vessel, and I had few opportunities to observe them personally.

However, the voyage was marred by a severe calmer lasting three days and nights off the coast of India. Without the ship's journal to consult, I cannot recall the exact coordinates, but I can attest that the currents drifted us closer to the land. When the wind finally returned, we reached port within twenty-four hours.

As any sailor knows, discipline inevitably relaxes during a prolonged calm. On our ship, this laxity permitted certain gentlemen passengers to lower the smaller boats. Amused by the cool evening air, they spent their time rowing and swimming. Protocol dictated that these boats be hoisted back into their davits upon completion. Instead, they were left moored alongside the hull. The oppressive heat and the frustration of the stillness had sapped the resolve of both officers and crew; duty seemed a burden too heavy to bear.

On the third night, the watch on deck reported nothing unusual. But when morning broke, the smallest boat was gone. Shortly thereafter, reports surfaced that the three Hindu passengers were missing as well.

If they had stolen the boat shortly after dark—as I am convinced they did—we were too close to the shore for a pursuit to be effective once the loss was discovered in the morning. Given the calm conditions, I have no doubt they reached land before dawn, despite the fatigue and difficulty of rowing against the current.

Upon docking, I learned for the first time why these passengers had seized the opportunity to flee. I could offer the authorities no more than what I have stated here. They held me responsible for the relaxation of discipline on board. I have expressed my regrets to them and to the ship's owners. Since that time, there has been no word from the three Hindus.

Do you recall, my dear sir, the semi-savage gentleman you encountered at dinner in London last autumn? His name was Murthwaite, and we engaged in a lengthy conversation afterward concerning the Indian Diamond, the Moonstone, and a conspiracy to seize the gem.

Since then, I have wandered through the vast expanses of Central Asia and revisited the northern and northwestern regions of India. About two weeks ago, I found myself in a remote province known as Kattiawar.

Here, an adventure unfolded—one in which, though incredible, you are personally involved.

The landscape of Kattiawar is wild; even the farmers till the soil armed to the teeth. The populace is fanatically devoted to the ancient Hindu faith, worshipping Brahma and Vishnu. The few Muslim families scattered among the villages dare not eat meat. A Muslim suspected of harming the sacred cow is put to death without mercy by the surrounding Hindu neighbors. To fuel this fervor, two of the most renowned Hindu pilgrimage sites lie within Kattiawar's borders: Dwarka, the birthplace of Krishna, and the sacred city of Somnauth, sacked centuries ago by the conqueror Mahmud of Ghizni.

Determined to witness the magnificent desolation of Somnauth once more, I set out on foot. I was roughly three days' journey from the city when I noticed others traveling in my direction—pairs, then trios.

To those who spoke to me, I introduced myself as a Hindu-Buddhist pilgrim from a distant province, bound for the holy city. Clad in appropriate garb, speaking the language fluently, and possessing the lean, sun-brown complexion of a local, I passed unnoticed as a European. I was accepted not as one of them, but as a stranger from afar.

By the second day, the number of travelers had swelled to dozens, then hundreds. By the third day, thousands converged on a single point: Somnauth.

A minor kindness I offered to a fellow pilgrim on the third day introduced me to men of the higher caste. They informed me that the multitude was gathering for a grand religious ceremony on a nearby hill, dedicated to the God of the Moon, to be held under the cover of night.

As we approached the site, the crowd detained us. By the time we reached the hill, the moon hung high and unclouded. My Hindu friends, possessing special privileges, secured access to the shrine and invited me to join them.

We arrived to find the shrine concealed behind a curtain strung between two magnificent trees. Beneath the trees, a flat rock projection formed a natural platform. Standing below, with my guides, I looked back down the hill.

The sight was the most sublime combination of nature and humanity I have ever witnessed. The lower slopes melted imperceptibly into a grassy plain where three rivers met. On one side, the waters wound gracefully, visible and hidden by foliage alike, stretching to the horizon. On the other, the ocean lay waveless in the night's calm. Tens of thousands of human beings, clad entirely in white, draped the hillsides, overflowed onto the plain, and fringed the riverbanks. Wild red flames from cressets and torches streamed upward from the innumerable throng. Imagine the Eastern moonlight pouring unclouded glory over this scene—that is what met my gaze from the summit.

A mournful melody, played on strings and flutes, drew my attention to the hidden shrine.

Turning, I saw three figures on the rocky platform. In the center stood the man I had spoken to in England when the Indians appeared on the terrace at Lady Verinder's house. His two companions from that night were with him now.

A spectator near me noticed my start. Whispering, he explained the significance of the three figures.

"They are Brahmins," he said, "who have forfeited their caste in the service of the god. The deity has commanded that their purification shall be the pilgrimage itself. Tonight, the three men shall part. In three separate directions, they shall set forth as pilgrims to the shrines of India. Never again shall they look upon each other's faces. Never again shall they rest in their wanderings, from the day of their separation until the day of their death."

As these words were spoken, the mournful music ceased. The three men prostrated themselves before the curtain hiding the shrine. They rose, looked upon one another, and embraced. Then, they descended separately into the crowd. The people parted for them in dead silence. In three distinct directions, the crowd split simultaneously. Slowly, the great white mass of pilgrims closed together again, erasing the tracks of the doomed men. We saw them no more.

A new strain of music, loud and jubilant, rose from the hidden shrine. The crowd shuddered and pressed closer.

The curtain between the trees was drawn aside, revealing the shrine.

There, raised high on a throne, seated upon his typical antelope with four arms stretching toward the corners of the earth, soared above us, dark and awe-inspiring in the mystic light, the God of the Moon. And there, in the forehead of the deity, gleamed the yellow Diamond. Its splendour, last seen by me in England resting in the bosom of a woman's dress, burned once more.

Yes! After eight centuries, the Moonstone shines forth again over the walls of the sacred city where its story began. How it returned to its wild native land—whether by accident or by crime—is known to you, but not to me. You have lost sight of it in England, and, if I know anything of this people, you have lost sight of it forever.

Thus the years pass, repeating themselves; thus the same events revolve in the cycles of time. What will be the next adventures of the Moonstone? Who can tell?
