Our kin at Castlewood kept us in their midst until the new year broke upon the world. For the absurd and infatuated young man, a fortnight's absence stretched into an eternity, yet he eventually returned to the side of his charmer. Madame de Bernstein, however, breathed a sigh of relief as we departed her father's house. Once we were gone, her tongue loosened; she spoke with a freedom that had been absent before.

She never revealed the particulars of that private interview where a battle-royal erupted between her and her niece. Yet, the old lady ceased her talk of molding *cette petite*. Now, when she mentioned the young Countess, her voice trembled with a nervous laughter, devoid of true hostility. "Eugene," she declared, "is doomed to be henpecked for the remainder of his days; I see it clearly. A touch of order in the household would suffice to cure all ills." She eyed the little, vulgar American gentleman with approval, calling him shrewd and predicting he would serve advantageously as a steward.

Regarding the Countess's mother—a convict exiled from England, rumored to have beaten hemp in the very worst gaols—the old lady dismissed the scandal. "Let the town-crier keep his silence," she scoffed. "In the case of certain folk, what does birth matter? The young woman will be honest for her own sake, she is sharp, and she will learn English in time. The name she bears is great enough to open every door. Whether her father was a grocer, a smuggler, or a slave-dealer, does it matter? The Countess of Castlewood can afford to be anyone's daughter. As soon as my nephew produces her, it is our sacred duty to stand by her."

Mr. Warrington, hoping the ties binding Madame de Bernstein to her nephew would extend to her niece, spoke of his visits to Mr. Hagan and his wife, seeking her aunt's favor. But the old lady remained obdurate regarding Lady Maria. She begged that the name never be uttered again and spent two hours recounting anecdotes of her niece that, though this book lies open to the innocent, I shall not record. Yet, I must say of this kind creature: if she sinned, she was not alone in the family; if she repented, others should follow her lead.

It is known that Hagan, after leaving the stage, lived an exemplary life, noted for his pulpit elegance and eloquence. His lady adopted extreme views but commanded great respect within her sect. When I last saw her, she spoke of a peculiar spiritual illumination, which I strongly suspected at the time stemmed from the too free use of liquor. Still, I remember when she and her husband were good to me and mine during times of need, when many a Pharisee turned away.

I have told how easy it was to rise and fall in my fickle aunt's favor, how each of us brothers was alternately embraced and neglected. My turn of glory came after the success of my play. I was introduced to the town-wits, held my place among them tolerably well, and was deemed well-bred by the macaronis and the fashionable set. I might have pursued that career had my purse been deeper, or had I chosen that life. But I loved a pair of kind eyes better than the brightest orbs of the Gunnings or Chudleighs, or all the painted beauties of the Ranelagh ring. Because I was fond of your mother, children, will it be believed that my tastes were deemed low and deplored by my genteel family? So it was. My godly Lady Warrington and my worldly Madame Bernstein laid their elderly heads together in lamentation.

"Why, with his name, he might marry anybody," said meek Religion, who ever kept one eye on Heaven and one on the main chance. "I meddle with no man's affairs and admire genius," said Uncle, "but it is a pity you consort with poets and authors, and that sort of people, when you might have had a lovely creature with a hundred thousand pounds, and instead you made up to a country girl without a penny-piece."

"But if I had promised her, uncle?" I asked.

"Promise, promise! These things are matters of arrangement and prudence, demanding a careful lookout," he replied. "When you first committed yourself to little Miss Lambert, you had not yet seen the lovely American lady your mother wished you to marry, as a good mother naturally would. Your duty to your mother, nephew—your duty to the Fifth Commandment—would have warranted breaking with Miss L. and fulfilling your excellent mother's intentions regarding Miss... What was the Countess's Dutch name? Never mind. A name is nothing; but a plumb, Master George, is something to look at! I have my dear little Miley at a dancing-school with Miss Barwell, Nabob Barwell's daughter. I do not disguise my wish that the children may contract an attachment enduring through their lives! I tell the Nabob so. We went from the House of Commons one dancing-day and saw them. 'Twas beautiful to see the young things walking a minuet together! It brought tears to my eyes, for I have a feeling heart, George, and I love my boy!"

"But if I prefer Miss Lambert, uncle, with twopence to her fortune, to the Countess with her hundred thousand pounds?"

"Why then, sir, you have a singular taste, that's all," said the old gentleman, turning on his heel and leaving me. I could perfectly understand his vexation at my inability to see the world as he viewed it.

Nor did Aunt Bernstein much like the engagement I had made, or the family with which I spent so much time. Their simple ways wore and annoyed the old woman of the world; she no more relished their company than a certain person (who is not so black as he is painted) likes holy water. The old lady chafed at my forever dangling at my sweetheart's lap. Having risen mightily in her favor, I began to fall again: and once more Harry was the favorite, and his brother, Heaven knows, was not jealous.

He was now our family hero. He wrote us brief letters from the seat of war where he was engaged. Madame Bernstein cared little at first for the letters or the writer, for they were simple, and the facts he narrated not over interesting. We had early learned in London of the action on the glorious first of August at Minden, where Wolfe's old regiment was one of the British six that achieved victory on that famous day. At the same hour, the young General lay in his bed, in sight of Quebec, stricken down by fever, and perhaps by rage and disappointment at the check his troops had just received.

Arriving in the St. Lawrence in June, the fleet that brought Wolfe and his army landed them on the last day of the month on the Island of Orleans, opposite which rises the great cliff of Quebec. After the great action in which his General fell, the dear brother who accompanied the chief wrote home to me one of his simple letters, describing his modest share in that glorious day. He added nothing to the many descriptions already written of the action of the 13th of September, save that he remembered, from the testimony of a brother aide-de-camp who stood by his side, that the General never spoke at all after receiving his death-wound. Thus, the phrase often put into the mouth of the dying hero may be considered no more authentic than an oration of Livy or Thucydides.

From his position on the island, lying in the great channel of the river to the north of the town, the General was ever hungrily on the lookout for a chance to meet and attack his enemy. Above the city and below it he landed—now here, now there; he was bent upon attacking wherever he saw an opening. 'Twas surely a prodigious fault on the part of the Marquis of Montcalm to accept a battle from Wolfe on equal terms, for the British General had no artillery. When we made our famous scalade of the heights and were on the Plains of Abraham, we were a little nearer the city, certainly, but as far off as ever from being within it.

The game played between the brave chiefs of those two gallant little armies, lasting from July until Mr. Wolfe won the crowning hazard in September, must have been as interesting a match as ever eager players engaged in. On the very first night after the landing (as my brother has narrated), the sport began. At midnight, the French sent a flaming squadron of fireships down upon the British ships discharging their stores at Orleans. Our seamen thought it good sport to tow the fireships clear of the fleet and ground them on the shore, where they burned out.

As soon as the French commander heard that our ships had entered the river, he marched to Beauport, in advance of the city, and took up a strong position. When our stores and hospitals were established, our General crossed over from his island to the left shore, drawing nearer to his enemy. He had the ships in the river behind him, but the whole country in his face was in arms. The Indians in the forest seized our advanced parties as they strove to clear it, murdering them with horrible tortures. The French were as savage as their Indian friends. The Montmorenci River rushed between Wolfe and the enemy. He could neither attack these nor the city behind them.

Bent on seeing whether there was no other point at which his foe might be assailable, the General passed round the town of Quebec and skirted the left shore beyond. Everywhere it was guarded, as well as in his immediate front. Having run the gauntlet of the batteries up and down the river, he returned to his post at Montmorenci. On the right of the French position, across the Montmorenci River—which was fordable at low tide—lay a redoubt of the enemy. He would have that. Perhaps, to defend it, the French chief would be forced out from his lines, and a battle brought on. Wolfe determined to play these odds. He would fetch over the body of his army from the Island of Orleans and attack from the St. Lawrence. He would time his attack so that, at shallow water, his lieutenants, Murray and Townsend, might cross the Montmorenci. On the last day of July, he played this desperate game.

First, and General Monckton, his second in command (setting out from Point Levi, which he occupied), crossed over the St. Lawrence from their respective stations, being received with a storm of shot and artillery as they rowed to the shore. No sooner were the troops landed than they rushed at the French redoubt without order, were shot down before it in great numbers, and were obliged to fall back. At the preconcerted signal, the troops on the other side of the Montmorenci advanced across the river in perfect order. The enemy even evacuated the redoubt and fell back to their lines; but from these the assailants were received with such severe fire that an impression on them was hopeless, and the General had to retreat.

The Battle of Montmorenci (which my brother Harry and I have fought again many a time over our wine) formed the dismal burden of the first despatch from Mr. Wolfe which reached England, plunging us all in gloom. What more might one expect of a commander so rash? What disasters might one not foretell? Was ever scheme so wild as to bring three great bodies of men across broad rivers in the face of murderous batteries, merely on the chance of inducing an enemy, strongly entrenched and guarded, to leave his position and engage us? 'Twas the talk of the town. No wonder grave people shook their heads and prophesied fresh disaster. The General, who took to his bed after this failure, shuddering with fever, was to live barely six weeks longer, and die immortal! How is it, and by what, and whom, that Greatness is achieved? Is Merit—is Madness the patron? Is it Frolic or Fortune? Is it Fate that awards successes and defeats? Is it the Just Cause that ever wins? How did the French gain Canada from the savage, and we from the French, and after which of the conquests was the right time to sing *Te Deum*? We are always for implicating Heaven in our quarrels, causing the gods to intervene whatever the *nodus* may be. Does Broughton, after pummelling and beating Slack, lift up a black eye to Jove and thank him for the victory? And if ten thousand boxers are to be so heard, why not one? And if Broughton is to be grateful, what is Slack to be?

"By the list of disabled officers (many of whom are of rank) you may perceive, sir, that the army is much weakened. By the nature of this river the most formidable part of the armament is deprived of the power of acting, yet we have almost the whole force of Canada to oppose. In this situation there is such a choice of difficulties, that I own myself at a loss how to determine. The affairs of Great Britain, I know, require the most vigorous measures; but then the courage of a handful of brave men should be exerted only where there is some hope of a favourable event. The admiral and I have examined the town with a view to a general assault: and he would readily join in this or any other measure for the public service; but I cannot propose to him an undertaking of so dangerous a nature, and promising so little success.... I found myself so ill, and am still so weak, that I begged the general officers to consult together for the public utility. They are of opinion that they should try by conveying up a corps of 4000 or 5000 men (which is nearly the whole strength of the army, after the points of Levi and Orleans are put in a proper state of defence) to draw the enemy from their present position, and bring them to an action. I have acquiesced in their proposal, and we are preparing to put it into execution."

So wrote the General (of whose noble letters it is clear our dear scribe was not the author or secretary) from his headquarters at Montmorenci Falls on the 2nd of September.
