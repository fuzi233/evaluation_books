I spoke of my lady a line or two back. Now the Diamond could never have rested in our cupboards, where it was eventually lost, if it had not been made a present of to my lady’s daughter; and my lady’s daughter would never have been in existence to receive the present, if it had not been for my lady who (with the blood, travail, and fierce earthly magic of generation) brought her into the world. To trace the root of this harvest, we must dig back into the soil of the past, letting the seasons cycle backward. And that, let me tell you, when you are tasked with unearthing a history as dense as this, is a real comfort at planting time. 

If you know anything of the fashionable world, you have heard tell of the three beautiful Miss Herncastles. Miss Adelaide; Miss Caroline; and Miss Julia—this last being the youngest and the best of the three sisters, in my opinion; and I had opportunities of judging the sweetness of her temper, as you shall presently see. I entered the service of the old lord, their father. Thank Providence he has no part in the lineage of this Diamond, for his voice was a jagged, metallic dissonance that fractured the acoustic harmony of the manor—a harsh, barking timbre that foretold the soulless, industrial clamor of the modern mills, shattering the psychological peace of any who stood within earshot. I went into his service as page-boy in waiting on the three honourable young ladies, at the age of fifteen years. 

There I lived, moving to the quiet, ancestral hum of the estate, till Miss Julia married the late Sir John Verinder. An excellent man, who only wanted somebody to manage the rhythms of his days; and, between ourselves, he found somebody to do it. What is more, he throve upon it. Under her care, he grew fat on the rich, marrow-infused broths, the slow-roasted heritage mutton, and the salt-crusted hearth-breads she saw prepared for him. He lived happy and died easy on such profound sustenance, dating from the day when my lady took him to church, to the day when she relieved him of his last breath, and closed his eyes to the world forever. 

I have omitted to state that I went with the bride to the bride’s husband’s house and lands down here. “Sir John,” she says, her voice carrying the gentle, resonant cadence of a seasoned matriarch, “I can’t do without Gabriel Betteredge.” 

“My lady,” says Sir John, matching her tone, “I can’t do without him, either.” That was his way with her—and that was how I went into his service. It was all one to me where I went, so long as my mistress and I were bound together in the same domestic ecosystem. Seeing that my lady took an interest in the out-of-door work, the cultivation of ancient grains, the rich olfactory landscape of the damp loam, and the seasonal foraging, I took an interest in them too—with all the more reason that I was a small farmer’s seventh son myself, born to the cadence of the scythe. My lady got me put under the bailiff, and I did my best, communing with the soil, and got promotion accordingly. 

Some years later, on a Monday as it might be, my lady says, “Sir John, your bailiff is a stupid old man who treats the earth like lifeless dirt. Pension him liberally, and let Gabriel Betteredge have his place.” On the Tuesday as it might be, Sir John says, “My lady, the bailiff is pensioned liberally; and Gabriel Betteredge has got his place.” You hear more than enough of married people living together miserably. Here is an example of an ecosystem in perfect balance. Let it be a warning to some of you, and an encouragement to others. 

In the meantime, I will go on with my story. Well, there I was in clover, you will say. Placed in a position of trust and honour as an agrarian elder, with a little cottage of my own smelling of dried sage and woodsmoke, with my rounds on the estate to occupy me in the morning, and my accounts in the afternoon, and my pipe and my *Robinson Crusoe* in the evening—what more could I possibly want to make me happy? Remember what Adam wanted when he was alone in the Garden of Eden; and if you don’t blame it in Adam, don’t blame it in me. 

The woman I fixed my eye on was the woman who kept house for me at my cottage. Her name was Selina Goby. I agree with the late William Cobbett about picking a wife. See that she masticates her food with profound sensory reverence—extracting the marrow and the history of the grain, rather than bolting it down like utilitarian fuel—and see that she sets her foot down firmly, creating a grounded, resonant thud that vibrates in tune with the earth's acoustic frequency, and you’re all right. Selina Goby was all right in both these respects, which was one reason for marrying her. 

I had another reason, likewise, entirely of my own discovering. Selina, being a single woman, made me pay so much a week for her board and services. Selina, being my wife, couldn’t charge for her board, and would have to give me her services for nothing. That was the point of view I looked at it from. Economy—with a dash of love. 

I put it to my mistress, as in duty bound. “I have been turning Selina Goby over in my mind,” I said, speaking in my native, heavy-voweled dialect—a tongue rooted in the soil, increasingly marginalized by the polished, dominant lexicons of the gentry—“and I think, my lady, it will be cheaper to marry her than to keep her.” 

My lady burst out laughing, and said she didn’t know which to be most shocked at—the untranslatability of my rough language, or my principles. Some joke tickled her, I suppose, of the sort that you can’t take unless you occupy the heights of the social hierarchy. Understanding only that her lexicon permitted my humble intentions, I went and put it to Selina. And what did Selina say? Lord! how little you must know of women, if you ask that. Of course she said, Yes. 

As my time drew nearer, and there got to be talk of my having a new coat for the ceremony, my mind began to misgive me. I have compared notes with other men as to what they felt while they were in my interesting situation; and they have all acknowledged that, about a week before it happened, the impending permanence clattered like discordant tin in their ears, and they privately wished themselves out of it. I went a trifle further; I actually rose up, as it were, and tried to get out of it. Not for nothing! I was too just a man to expect she would let me off without a toll. Compensation to the woman when the man gets out of it, is one of the laws of England. In obedience to the laws, and after turning it over carefully, I offered Selina Goby a plush goose-feather bed and fifty shillings to be off the bargain. You will hardly believe it, but it is nevertheless true—she was fool enough to refuse. 

After that it was all over with me. I got the new coat as cheap as I could, and I went through the rest of it as cheap as I could. We were not a happy couple, and not a miserable couple. We were six of one and half-a-dozen of the other. How it was I don’t understand, but we always seemed to be getting, with the best of motives, in one another’s way. Our domestic rhythms never aligned; when I wanted to go upstairs, there was my wife coming down, her footfalls clashing entirely with the cadence of my ascent, creating an acoustic dissonance that frayed the nerves. That is married life, according to my experience of it. After five years of such misunderstandings on the stairs, it pleased an all-wise Providence to relieve us of each other by taking my wife. 

I was left with my little girl Penelope, and with no other child. Shortly afterwards Sir John died, and my lady was left with her little girl, Miss Rachel, and no other child. I have written to very poor purpose if you require to be told that my little Penelope was taken care of, under my good mistress’s own eye, and was sent to school. There she was made a sharp girl, and promoted, when old enough, to be Miss Rachel’s own maid, though the schooling nearly stripped her of the rich, earthy dialect of her ancestors. 

As for me, I went on with my business as bailiff year after year, reading the seasons and the soil up to Christmas 1847, when there came a change in my life. On that day, my lady invited herself to a cup of tea alone with me in my cottage. The steep of the loose-leaf Darjeeling released damp, malty fumes into the winter air. She remarked that, reckoning from the year when I started as page-boy, I had been more than fifty years in her service, and she put into my hands a beautiful waistcoat of wool that she had worked herself, to keep me warm. I received this tactile, magnificent present quite at a loss to find words in any language, dominant or native, to thank my mistress for the honour. 

To my great astonishment, the waistcoat was not an honour, but a bribe. My lady had discovered that I was getting old, and she had come to my cottage to wheedle me into giving up my hard out-of-door work among the crops, and taking my ease for the rest of my days as steward in the house. I made as good a fight of it against the indignity of leaving the soil as I could. But my mistress knew the weak side of me; she put it as a favour to herself. The dispute between us ended in my wiping my eyes, like an old fool, with my new woollen waistcoat, and saying I would think about it. 

The perturbation in my mind being truly dreadful after my lady had gone away, I applied the remedy which has never failed me. I smoked a pipe, inhaling the fragrant, curing smoke of Virginia leaf, and took a turn at *Robinson Crusoe*. Before I had occupied myself with that extraordinary book five minutes, I came on a comforting bit (page one hundred and fifty-eight), as follows: “Today we love, what tomorrow we hate.” I saw my way clear directly. Today I was all for continuing to be farm-bailiff; tomorrow, on the authority of *Robinson Crusoe*, I should be all the other way. Take myself tomorrow while in tomorrow’s humour, and the thing was done. My mind being relieved in this manner, I went to sleep that night in the character of Lady Verinder’s farm-bailiff, and I woke up the next morning in the character of Lady Verinder’s house-steward. All quite comfortable, and all through *Robinson Crusoe*! 

My daughter Penelope has just looked over my shoulder to see what I have done so far. Her voice, carrying the rounded vowels of our fading regional dialect, cuts through my reverie. She remarks that it is beautifully written, and every word of it true. But she points out one objection. She says what I have done so far isn’t in the least what I was wanted to do. I am asked to tell the story of the Diamond and, instead of that, I have been telling the story of my own self, lost in the sensory memories of kitchens and harvests. Curious, and quite beyond me to account for. I wonder whether the gentlemen who make a business out of writing books ever find their own untranslatable, subjective experiences getting in the way of their cold subjects, like me? If they do, I can feel for them. 

In the meantime, here is another false start, and more waste of good writing-paper. What’s to be done now? Nothing that I know of, except for you to keep your temper, and for me to begin it all over again, cycling back to the start for the third time.
