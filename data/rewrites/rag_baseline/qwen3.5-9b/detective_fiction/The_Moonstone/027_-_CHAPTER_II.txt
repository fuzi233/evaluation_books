To understand the architecture of this domestic prison, one must first grasp the suffocating weight of the Victorian moral order that held us captive. It is within the rigid completeness of such a life that the true Christian emerges—a figure of polished propriety, devoid of shadow, and utterly blind to the rot festering beneath the surface. Mr. Godfrey was the epitome of this completeness. He arrived at the precise moment his name was announced, neither startling us with proximity nor causing a breach of protocol through tardiness. He moved through the world with the mechanical efficiency of a clockwork saint, a creature designed solely to uphold the facade of societal perfection.

"My dear Aunt," he greeted, his voice a rich, velvet instrument tuned to soothe the frayed nerves of the bourgeoisie. "Miss Clack."

We both inquired after his health, united in our concern regarding the terrible adventure of the past week. With the tactical brilliance of a diplomat navigating a minefield, he contrived to answer us simultaneously. Lady Verinder received his verbal assurance; I witnessed the charming, calculated smile that masked the turmoil beneath.

"What," he cried, with a tenderness that felt rehearsed, "have I done to deserve all this sympathy? My dear aunt! My dear Miss Clack! I have merely been mistaken for someone else. Blindfolded. Strangled. Thrown flat upon a thin carpet over a particularly unforgiving floor. Consider how much worse it might have been! Murder! Robbery! What have I lost? Only 'Nervous Force'—a concept the law refuses to recognize as property. Strictly speaking, I have lost nothing. Had I my way, I would have buried this trauma in silence, shunning the clamor of publicity. Yet, Mr. Luker made *his* wounds public, and thus, by the cruel logic of the press, *my* injuries were proclaimed in their turn. I am now the property of the newspapers, until the gentle reader grows weary of the spectacle. I am weary indeed. May the gentle reader soon find solace in my exhaustion! And how fares dear Rachel? Still basking in the artificial gaieties of London? Delighted to hear it! Miss Clack, I require all your indulgence. I am woefully behindhand with my Committee Work and my dear Ladies. Yet, I harbor a sincere hope to visit the Mothers'-Small-Clothes next week. Did you achieve cheering progress at Monday's meeting? Was the Board optimistic regarding future prospects? And pray, are we adequately provisioned for Trousers?"

The heavenly gentleness of his smile rendered his apologies irresistible. The richness of his deep voice added an indescribable charm to the mundane inquiry he had just posed. In truth, we were nearly overwhelmed by the sheer volume of trousers required; the burden of female attire was becoming a crushing weight. I stood on the verge of voicing this observation when the door swung open again, introducing an element of chaotic, worldly disturbance: Miss Verinder.

She approached Mr. Godfrey with a speed that defied feminine decorum, her hair a shocking disarray, her face flushed with a feverish intensity that I would term unbecoming.

"I am charmed to see you, Godfrey," she declared, addressing him with the casual intimacy of two young men confiding in each other, ignoring the sanctity of the room entirely. "I wished you had brought Mr. Luker along. You and he, amidst our current hysteria, constitute the two most fascinating men in all London. It is morbid to admit; it is unhealthy; it is precisely the sort of thing a regulated mind like Miss Clack's instinctively recoils from. Pray, ignore that. Tell me the entire saga of Northumberland Street immediately. I suspect the newspapers have omitted crucial details."

Even the angelic Mr. Godfrey bore the fallen nature inherited from Adam—a small, insignificant shard of our shared human legacy, yet one he carried with tragic inevitability. It gnawed at my conscience to witness him seize Rachel's hand in both of his own, laying it softly against the left lapel of his waistcoat. It was a direct incitement to her reckless discourse, an invitation to her insolence.

"Dearest Rachel," he replied, using the same tone that had previously enchanted me with promises of prosperity and fabric, "the newspapers have recounted every detail—and they have done so with a fidelity surpassing my own capabilities."

"Godfrey believes we all exaggerate the significance of this affair," my aunt interjected, her voice lacking its usual authority. "He has just stated his reluctance to discuss it further."

"Why?"

She fired the question with a sudden, electric flash in her eyes, lifting her gaze to meet Mr. Godfrey's. From his side, he looked down upon her with an indulgence so ill-judged, so undeserved, that I felt compelled to intervene.

"Rachel, darling!" I remonstrated gently. "True greatness and true courage are defined by their modesty."

"You are a commendable fellow in your own limited sphere, Godfrey," she retorted, paying me not the slightest heed, continuing to address her cousin as if we were peers in a tavern. "Yet, I am convinced you possess neither greatness nor extraordinary courage. I am firmly persuaded that if you ever harbored any modesty, your adoring ladies relieved you of that virtue decades ago. You have a private reason for withholding the story of your Northumberland Street ordeal, and I intend to extract it."

"My reason is the simplest imaginable, and the most readily acknowledged," he answered, enduring her assault with stoic patience. "I am simply tired of the subject."

"Tired of the subject? My dear Godfrey, I am about to make a remark."

"And what is it?"

"You dwell too extensively in the company of women. Consequently, you have cultivated two pernicious habits. You have learned to speak nonsense with deadly seriousness, and you have acquired a penchant for fabrication, deriving pleasure from the act of lying itself. You cannot walk a straight path with your lady-worshippers. I intend to force you to walk a straight path with *me*. Come, sit down. I am overflowing with direct questions, and I expect you to respond with equal directness."

With physical force, she dragged him across the room to a chair by the window, positioning him where the light would dissect his features. I felt a profound obligation to document such language, to chronicle such conduct. Yet, hemmed in as I am—caught between Mr. Franklin Blake's financial leverage on one side and my own sacred devotion to truth on the other—what recourse remains? I glanced at my aunt. She sat motionless, seemingly incapable of intervention. I had never observed such a torpor in her before. Perhaps it was the lingering aftermath of the trials endured during their country retreat. A disturbing symptom, regardless of its cause, especially considering Lady Verinder's advanced years and her autumnal exuberance of figure.

Meanwhile, Rachel had settled herself at the window with our amiable, forbearing—and far too forbearing—Mr. Godfrey. She launched into her barrage of questions, treating her mother and myself as if we were invisible ghosts haunting the periphery of the conversation.

"Have the police taken any action, Godfrey?"

"Absolutely nothing."

"It is certain, I suppose, that the three men who trapped you were identical to the three who later ensnared Mr. Luker?"

"Humanly speaking, my dear Rachel, there can be no doubt."

"Not a single trace of them has been discovered?"

"Not a trace."

"It is rumored—is it not?—that these three men are the three Indians who visited our estate in the countryside."

"Some individuals hold that view."

"Do you subscribe to that theory?"

"My dear Rachel, they blindfolded me before I could discern their countenances. I know absolutely nothing of the matter. How can I possibly offer an opinion?"

Even the ethereal gentleness of Mr. Godfrey began to fracture under the relentless persecution inflicted upon him. Whether unbridled curiosity or ungovernable dread drove Miss Verinder's interrogation, I refrain from speculation. I merely report that, as Mr. Godfrey attempted to rise after delivering his response, she seized him by the shoulders and shoved him back into the chair. Oh, do not suggest this was immodest! Do not even imply that the recklessness of guilty terror could explain such behavior! We must not judge others. My Christian friends, indeed, indeed, indeed—we must not judge others!

She continued her questioning, unabashed. Earnest biblical scholars might recall, as I did, the blinded children of the devil who proceeded with their orgies, shameless and unrestrained, in the era preceding the Great Deluge.

"I require information concerning Mr. Luker, Godfrey."

"I am again unfortunate, Rachel. No man knows less of Mr. Luker than I do."

"You never encountered him prior to your accidental meeting at the bank?"

"Never."

"Have you seen him since?"

"Yes. We have been examined jointly and separately to aid the police investigation."

"Mr. Luker was robbed of a receipt obtained from his banker—was he not? For what purpose?"

"For a precious gem he had entrusted to the bank's safekeeping."

"That is what the newspapers claim. Sufficient for the general populace, perhaps; inadequate for me. The banker's receipt must have specified the nature of the gem?"
