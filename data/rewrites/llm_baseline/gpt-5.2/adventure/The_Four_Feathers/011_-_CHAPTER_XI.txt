A month later Durrance came back to London, and at his club a letter lay waiting—Ethne’s hand upon the envelope, the paper edged with a thin black border like a strip of burnt pastry laid along a plate. The sight of that mourning line carried its own flavour: dry, mineral, unmistakable, as if grief had a scent and it was the scent of extinguished hearth-ash.

He went to Hill Street the next afternoon and was shown into a quiet room where Ethne sat alone. The house had the softened hush of good breeding and recent sorrow—carpets that swallowed footsteps, curtains that held the street’s clatter outside as a cook holds back the boil with a steady hand.

“I did not write to Wadi Halfa,” she said at once, as though she must clear away the ordinary explanations before she could touch the harder truth. “I thought you would be on your way home before my letter could arrive. My father died last month—towards the end of May.”

“I was afraid when I got your letter that you would have this to tell me,” Durrance answered. “I am very sorry. You will miss him.”

“More than I can say.” The words were plain, but they had weight, like bread cut thick. “He died one morning early. I think—I will tell you, if you would care to hear.”

He inclined his head. He had known Dermod’s body failing long before the end, and he had learned in Egypt that deaths were rarely sudden in the telling, even when they were sudden in fact; they came with a long approach of small particulars, each one a note in the surrounding soundscape.

“It was a chill that began it,” she said, “though it was not so much the cause as the occasion. He died… by degrees, as though something in him loosened and fell away. But just before the end—” She paused, then continued with a curious steadiness, as though she were reciting a recipe she could not bear to spoil by excess feeling. “Just before the end he took back something of his old mastery. We knew he was dying. He knew it too. At seven o’clock in the afternoon after—after he had spoken for a little while to me, he called his dog by name.”

Durrance watched her hands as she spoke. They were still, folded, the fingers pale from much holding in.

“The dog sprang on to the bed at once,” she went on, “though my father’s voice had not risen above a whisper. It crouched close, and pushed its muzzle—whining—under his hand. Then my father told me to leave him and the dog altogether alone. I was to shut the door upon him. The dog would tell me when to open it again. I obeyed him.”

Her voice did not tremble; only the room seemed to tighten around that closed door she described.

“I waited outside until one o’clock,” she said. “Then a loud sudden howl moaned through the house.”

For a moment she stopped. That brief silence was the only crack in her composure, the only place where grief showed itself—not in tears, but in the way the air in the room seemed to lose its warmth, as if a fire had been left to die with no one to rake it into life again.

“It was trying,” she said at last, simply. “To wait outside that door while the afternoon faded and the night came. He would have no lamp left in his room. One imagined him just the other side of that thin panel—lying very still and silent in the great four-poster bed with his face towards the hills—and the light falling away. The room slipping into darkness. The windows looming larger as everything else disappeared. And the dog by his side, and no one else, right to the end. He would have it that way. But it was rather hard for me.”

Durrance said nothing. There are moments when speech is an intrusion, like striking a match in a room where someone is listening for a distant footfall. He gave her what he could: the steady companionship of quiet. Yet in his mind he saw her in that passage for six long hours of twilight and night, every small domestic sound amplified—the settling of boards, the far clink of a glass, the hush of servants moving as carefully as if they carried full bowls. He thought, with a sudden sting, that there had been something cruel in Dermod’s chosen solitude.

Ethne broke the silence herself.

“I said my father spoke to me just before he told me to leave him,” she said. “Of whom do you think he spoke?”

She looked directly at Durrance. Her voice was level, her eyes unreadable; but hope—swift and involuntary—caught at him like breath taken too quickly.

“Tell me,” he said, leaning forward.

“Of Mr. Feversham.”

Durrance drew back, abruptly. He looked down at the carpet so she should not see what passed over his face.

“My father was always very fond of him,” she continued gently, “and I think I would like to know if you have any knowledge of what he is doing, or where he is.”

Durrance did not answer at once. The name brought with it the old perplexity—how Harry Feversham, having done what he had done, still held a strange power over the affections of people who should have cast him out. Even Dermod, wronged and made to suffer, had remembered him with kindness upon his death-bed. The thought had bitterness in it for Durrance now; he feared his bitterness would betray itself if he spoke too soon.

“You have never come across him, I suppose?” Ethne asked.

Durrance rose and walked to the window. In the street below, the world moved with its usual indifferent purpose: wheels, voices, the faint metallic cry of a distant vendor’s call. London’s sound was never clean; it had no clear edge the way a Donegal evening might have, when the only music was wind through grasses and the far, honest bark of a dog. Here the air was always bruised by traffic, and even sorrow had to fight for a quiet place to sit.

“No,” he said, looking out, hiding his expression as best he could. “I never have.”

And then his anger—deep, long-stored, and ashamed of itself—rose and spoke for him.

“And I never wish to,” he cried. “He was my friend, I know. But I cannot remember that friendship now. I can only think that if he had been the true man we took him for, you would not have waited alone in that dark passage during those six hours.”

He turned back to the centre of the room and spoke abruptly, as though to escape what he had just revealed.

“You are going back to Glenalla?”

“Yes.”

“You will live there alone?”

“Yes.”

Silence fell again between them, not unfriendly, but full—like a room after a strong broth has been ladled out, when the smell still hangs, telling you what was there.

Durrance walked behind her chair. “You once said that you would perhaps tell me why your engagement was broken off.”

“But you know,” she said. “What you said at the window showed that you knew.”

“No, I do not. One or two words your father let drop. He asked me for news of Feversham the last time I spoke with him. But I know nothing definite. I should like you to tell me.”

She shook her head and leaned forward, elbows on her knees, looking down as if the answer lay on the floorboards.

“Not now,” she said.

Again the room held them in its hush. Durrance, feeling the year in Egypt ahead of him like an empty table set for a meal he did not want to eat alone, spoke of practicalities, as men do when their hearts are unsteady.

“I have only one more year at Halfa,” he said. “It would be wise to leave Egypt then, I think. I do not expect much will be done in the Soudan for some little while. I do not think I will stay there—in any case. I mean even if you should decide to remain alone at Glenalla.”

Ethne did not pretend not to understand the drift of his words. She answered with the plainness of someone who has measured what she can afford and will not be coaxed into debt.

“We are neither of us children,” she said. “You have all your life to think of. We should be prudent.”

“Yes,” he said, and an exasperation—at prudence, at fate, at the way good intentions could be used like chains—broke through. “But the right kind of prudence. The prudence which knows that it’s worth while to dare a good deal.”

She did not move. Her back was to him, and for a long while she sat still, as though listening not to him but to the deeper currents in herself: the remembered cadence of a voice, the old Donegal words that could name shades of weather and shades of feeling that English could only circle around. At last she asked, very low, very gently:

“Do you want me so very much?”

Before he could answer she turned quickly, and for the first time emotion broke her composure—not in tears, but in urgency, like a hand snatching a kettle from boiling over.

“Try not to,” she exclaimed earnestly. “For this one year try not to. You have much to occupy your thoughts. Try to forget me altogether.”

There was regret in her tone—enough to soften the blow, enough to make him believe, as he longed to believe, that she pushed him away not because she did not care, but because she feared to injure him; feared, as she had once injured another man. Before he could reply Mrs. Adair entered, and the moment closed like a door gently pulled to.

That evening Durrance dined at his club. The dinner was well served, as club dinners are, but he ate with little attention—meat that might have been excellent, sauces that might have been crafted with care, all turning dull because his senses were held elsewhere. Afterwards he sat under the big tree in the courtyard and smoked, in the same place where a year before he had sat night after night in his vain hunger for news of Harry Feversham.

The night was clear, the strip of sky overhead cloudless and starred. The air had the languor of June. The club’s windows threw squares of light that made the leaves look newly washed, spring-green. Beyond the gates, the roadway sounded with rolling carriages, a continuous thunderous hum like the sea—London’s own tide, never slackening, never pure, forever grinding and returning.

On that night, too, a man came into the courtyard who knew Durrance. But he did not hesitate. He walked straight up and sat down on the seat beside him.

Durrance dropped the paper he had been glancing at and held out his hand. “How do you do?”

It was Captain Mather.

“I was wondering whether I should meet you,” Mather said. “When I read the evening paper. I knew it was about the time one might expect to find you in London. You have seen, I suppose?”

“What?” Durrance asked.

“Then you haven’t.” Mather picked up the newspaper Durrance had let fall and turned the sheets, searching with the certainty of a man hunting a particular line on a map. “You remember that last reconnaissance we made from Suakin?”

“Very well.”

“We halted by the Sinkat fort at midday. There was an Arab hiding in the trees at the back of the glacis.”

“Yes.”

“Have you forgotten the yarn he told you?”

“About Gordon’s letters and the wall of a house in Berber? No, I have not forgotten.”

“Then here’s something which will interest you.” Mather folded the paper and handed it to him, pointing to a short paragraph.

It was no more than a summary—no flourish, no story, only a line of print; and Durrance read it between puffs of his cigar. It announced that certain letters the Mahdi had sent to Gordon, summoning him to surrender Khartum and inviting him to become a convert to the Mahdist religion, together with copies of Gordon’s curt replies, had been recovered from a wall in Berber and brought safely to Captain Willoughby at Suakin.

“The fellow must have gone back to Berber after all,” Durrance said. “A risky business. Abou Fatma—that was the man’s name.”

The paragraph did not mention Abou Fatma, nor any man at all except Captain Willoughby. Yet Durrance tasted in the news a faint grit of the desert: sun-baked mud, the acrid bite of fear, the parchment-dry secrecy of a thing hidden in a wall. Still, he could not yet see how it might reach into his own life.

“They were hardly worth risking a life for,” Mather said.

“Perhaps not,” Durrance answered, doubtfully. “But one is glad they have been recovered. Perhaps the copies are in Gordon’s own hand. They are, at all events, of an historic interest.”

“In a way, no doubt,” Mather said. “But even so, their recovery throws no light upon the history of the siege. It can make no real difference to any one—not even to the historian.”

“That is true,” Durrance agreed.

And it was nothing of the sort. In the very place where he had once sat listening for news of Feversham, news had come to him at last—only he did not know it. He was still in the dark; he could not feel, yet, that this small paragraph touched his life at the springs, and would one day set it moving in a new direction.

He dismissed it from his mind and sat thinking instead of Ethne—of her steady voice when she spoke Harry’s name; of the way she had asked him to try, for one year, to forget her. That request, to Durrance’s hopeful heart, did not sound like farewell. It sounded like a truce; like a postponement that might turn, at the end of its term, into permission. If she truly did not care, why ask him to struggle? Why not let him burn out? He convinced himself that the very labour she demanded implied that she expected he might fail—and that if he failed, she might answer him differently.

A few days passed and he called again at Mrs. Adair’s house, only to find Mrs. Adair alone.

“Ethne has left London,” she said. “She went back to Donegal—rather suddenly.” And though Mrs. Adair spoke with the vagueness of one who had not been fully taken into confidence, Durrance believed he understood the reason well enough.

Ethne was putting into practice the policy she had urged upon him. He was to forget; she would help him by her absence, by removing her face and voice from the daily sound of his world.

In that, he was right. But she had forgotten one thing: she had not asked him to stop writing.

So, in the autumn, the letters began again to come from the Soudan—sheets that carried, across heat and sand and the harsh jangling of military life, a different atmosphere. She was frankly glad to receive them; yet they troubled her. For all their careful reticence, a phrase would leap out now and then—perhaps only the repetition of some trivial sentence she had once spoken and long forgotten—and she could not help seeing that, despite her plea, she lived perpetually in his thoughts.

There was a strain of hopefulness in them too, as though he moved through a world painted with new colours and suddenly grown musical; as though even the hard noises of his days—the shouted orders, the clink of equipment, the rasp of the desert wind—had arranged themselves into something like harmony because her name was somewhere behind them like an unheard accompaniment.

Ethne had never freed herself from the haunting fear that one man’s life had been spoiled because of her. She had never wavered from her determination that this should not happen to a second. Yet with Durrance’s letters before her she could not evade a new, perplexing question, one that would not translate cleanly into any tidy rule of prudence.

By what means was that possibility to be avoided?

There were two ways. Which of them would truly fulfil her resolve?

She was no longer as sure as she had been a year before that his career was all in all, to be guarded like a fragile dish from any jolt of feeling. The question returned again and again. She took it with her when she went out on the hillside with his letters in her hand. The wind off the Atlantic combed the grass with a long whisper; birds sounded and vanished; the old land kept its own counsel in its own tongue. She pondered and puzzled, and did not come an inch nearer to an answer.

Even her violin failed her in this strait. The instrument could still make music, but not the one she needed—the tune that would name, in any language, what she owed to herself and what she owed to another, and how much of the truth could ever be spoken without turning it into something smaller than it was.
