CHAPTER VIII. What name doth Joy most borrow When life is fair? “To-morrow.” What name doth best fit Sorrow In young despair? “To-morrow.”

At the rectory, a deeper sorrow had taken root than any fleeting disappointment could bring. Rex arrived home not with the usual sparkle of youth, but collapsed upon his bed in a silence so complete it seemed as if his spirit had fled. For days, he lay wrapped in stillness, responding only with quiet murmurs when anyone approached—“Let me alone,” he would say, each time like a small refusal of the world.

His mother and Anna watched helplessly, their hearts aching for the boy who had once been their brightest light. Where once laughter had filled the house, now there was only a hush, broken only by the soft rustle of curtains or the distant call of birds. The thought of sending him to Southampton slipped away entirely; instead, they focused on tending him, though he resisted even kindness, turning his face from them as if afraid of being touched.

His father, Mr. Gascoigne, stood apart, observing with a calm that masked his own distress. He saw beyond the pain—the fragile moment of crisis—and believed it might be the only way out of an unfortunate entanglement. Still, he grieved for what his son endured. Now and then, he would sit quietly by Rex’s bedside, placing a hand gently on his forehead, whispering, “God bless you, my boy.” And when he left, he did so with a slow, solemn nod, as if bidding farewell to something precious yet lost.

The younger children, Warham and the others, crept near the door sometimes, peering in at the brother they knew so well—once full of energy and mischief—now pale and still. But fingers would wave them back sharply, warning them not to disturb the fragile peace.

Anna was the constant presence, never leaving Rex’s side. Her small hand rested in his, though he never squeezed it in return. Inside her heart, two feelings warred: one for her brother’s suffering, the other for Gwendolen—the girl whose name had become a shadow over their household. “Perhaps it is wicked of me,” she repeated to herself, “but I think I never _can_ love her again.” It was a refrain that echoed in her silent thoughts, a quiet lament born of grief and confusion.

Even Mrs. Gascoigne could not hide her irritation toward her niece. One evening, she spoke softly to her husband, almost apologetically: “I know it’s better this way, Henry. We should be thankful Gwendolen isn’t truly in love with the poor boy. But really—she seems so cold. She has the heart of a coquette. I can’t help thinking she gave him hope, or else he wouldn’t have fallen so hard. And Fanny—poor Fanny—she sees nothing wrong in that girl.”

Mr. Gascoigne responded firmly, “The less said about it, Nancy, the better. I should have seen it sooner myself. As for Rex, let us be grateful nothing worse comes of it. Let this pass quickly. Especially with regard to Gwendolen—let it be as if it never happened.”

Yet beneath his calm exterior, the rector felt relief. Had Gwendolen returned Rex’s affection, the situation would have been far more complicated—one that might have escaped his control. As it was, he could only watch and wait.

Then, one bright morning, something shifted. Rex asked for his bath. He dressed as he always did, with careful precision. Anna, watching from the hallway, trembled with hope. When she heard his footsteps descending, she rushed to meet him at the stairs.

For the first time, he smiled—a faint, fragile thing that made her eyes fill with tears. “Nannie,” he said gently, taking her hand and leading her slowly into the drawing-room. His mother was already there, and when she came forward to kiss him, he whispered, “What a plague I am!”

He sat down by the bow-window, staring out at the garden blanketed in hoarfrost. The sun cast pale glimmers across the frozen grass, and Anna thought it looked like the sadness on Rex’s face—quiet, enduring, beautiful in its sorrow.

He felt as if he had awakened in a new world, stripped of everything familiar. The old dreams were gone. He didn’t know how to live anymore.

Anna sat beside him, pretending to knit, but her eyes never left his. Beyond the hedge, a wagon rumbled along the road, heavy with timber. The horses strained, muscles taut, and the driver cracked his whip, running alongside to guide the lead horse. Rex stirred, rose, and watched until the last log vanished from sight. Then he walked slowly back and forth, as if searching for words.

Mrs. Gascoigne had left. When Rex sat again, Anna, sensing a change in him, brought a stool and placed it close to his knee. She looked up, her expression pleading: *Speak to me.*

And he did.

“I’ll tell you what I’m thinking of, Nannie,” he said. “I want to go to Canada—or somewhere like that.”

Anna gasped. “Oh, Rex, not forever?”

“Yes,” he said quietly. “To earn my living. I’d build a hut, clear land, live among the wilds. A great wide quiet.”

“And not take me?” she whispered, tears falling fast.

“How could I?” he said.

“But I would love it,” she insisted. “Settlers take their families. I’d rather go than stay here. I could make fires, mend clothes, cook food. I’d learn to bake before we left. It would be like playing at life again—like our tent under the drugget, with our little plates and dishes.”

“Father and mother wouldn’t let you go,” Rex said.

“I think they would,” Anna replied, “if I explained. It would save money. Papa would have more to raise the boys. And I wouldn’t cost anything. I’d be happy.”

They talked like this—practical, earnest—until Rex finally agreed, reluctantly, that Anna could go with him—if he could speak to their father.

It was late afternoon when they entered the study together. Mr. Gascoigne looked up, surprised but pleased to see Rex moving again. “Well, my children!” he said cheerfully. “May we sit with you, papa?” Anna asked. “Rex has something to say.”

With all his heart, the rector welcomed them.

They formed a striking trio—father, son, daughter—all bearing the same strong features: high brows, straight noses, short upper lips, firm chins. The same gray tone in their skin, the same steady gaze. Mr. Gascoigne, with his silver hair and deep-set eyes, carried himself with authority, a man used to guiding others. Rex, pale but present, seemed a ghost of his father’s youth—had it not been for the sorrow in his eyes and the absence of purpose. Anna, tiny and serious, mirrored him in every expression, as if they shared a single soul.

“You know what has upset me, father,” Rex began.

Mr. Gascoigne nodded.

“I’m done for in this world. I can’t go back to Oxford. I couldn’t study. I’d fail. I’d waste your money. I want your permission to take another path.”

The rector’s brow furrowed. “You mean—you wish to leave England?”

“To go to the colonies. To work the land.”

Anna jumped in. “And me, papa! Rex needs someone to care for him. Someone to keep house. We won’t marry. I won’t cost anything. I’d be so happy. It would be hard to leave you and mamma—but there are the others to raise. We’d be no burden.”

She stepped closer, clinging to her father’s arm.

He did not smile, but drew her onto his lap, holding her gently—almost as if to remove her from the conversation while he addressed Rex.

“You will admit,” he said, “that my experience gives me some right to judge for you? That I may guide you better than you can guide yourself?”

Rex had no choice but to say, “Yes, sir.”

“And perhaps you will admit—though I don’t press it—that you owe me duty, and should consider my wishes?”

“I’ve never opposed you before, sir.”

But inwardly, Rex rebelled. He did not feel bound to return to Oxford. He wanted only to escape—to vanish into the wilds where no one would ask him to pretend.

“But suppose,” the rector continued, “I believe—on solid grounds—that this feeling is temporary? That if you leave now, you’ll regret it later, having thrown away years of education? Can you not trust me to know what is best? Can you not try, at least, to follow my advice—for a time?”

Rex hesitated. “I can’t study,” he said. “Not now.”

“Not just now,” his father corrected. “You’ll miss a term. But I’ve arranged for you—how you’ll spend the next two months. I confess, I’m disappointed in you, Rex. I thought you had more sense. You’re not the first young man to suffer a blow. But that doesn’t mean you’re free to abandon duty—as if your mind had weakened and you were no longer responsible.”

Rex stood up, as if the meeting were over.

“You accept my plan?” the rector asked, his voice firm, unyielding.

A pause. Then Rex said, “I’ll try, sir. I can’t promise.”

Inside, he knew trying would be useless.

Mr. Gascoigne kept Anna on his lap, holding her tightly as Rex left the room.

“Oh, papa,” she whispered, tears streaming. “It’s so hard for him. Doesn’t he look ill?”

“He will be better,” he said. “This will pass. And now, Anna—be quiet. Never mention this again when he’s gone.”

“No, papa,” she promised. “But I would never be like Gwendolen. To have people fall in love with me so—it’s dreadful.”

She didn’t dare say it aloud, but inside, she was crushed—not by her brother’s sorrow, but by the fact that she couldn’t go with him.

Later, she often replayed the scene in her mind, imagining a different life: no parties, no gloves, no crinolines, no dinners where she had to talk and smile. “I’d rather be a settler,” she told herself. “I’d rather live in a hut, make fires, and be real.”

And in that moment, she felt a strange kind of freedom—born not of joy, but of longing for a world untouched by the rules of society.

It was a time when fashion demanded wider crinolines, and whispers grew louder about expanding churches, ballrooms, and carriages to match. But Anna Gascoigne, slight and modest, would never need such grandeur. Her future, she dreamed, lay beyond the fields—where the wind blew freely, and the sky stretched endlessly.
