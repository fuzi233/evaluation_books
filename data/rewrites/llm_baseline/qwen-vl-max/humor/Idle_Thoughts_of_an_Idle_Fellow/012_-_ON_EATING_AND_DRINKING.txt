I’ve always had a soft spot for food and drink—though back in my youth, it was mostly just the eating part. I was a boy of appetite, yes, but also of digestion, which is more than can be said for some people I know. I recall one day when a pale-faced, sallow-skinned gentleman came to dine at our house. He watched me eat for about five minutes with the intensity of a man studying a rare species of beetle. Then he turned to my father and asked, “Does your boy ever suffer from dyspepsia?”

My father, being a man of few words, replied, “Never heard him complain.”

“Do you ever suffer from dyspepsia, Colly wobbles?” the guest inquired.

“No, pa,” I said.

“What *is* dyspepsia, pa?” I added, because I was curious.

The man looked at me as if I’d just announced I didn’t believe in gravity. A long pause followed. Then, in a voice thick with sorrowful wisdom, he said: “You will know… someday.”

And so I did. But not until much later, and by then it was too late to do anything about it except complain loudly.

My mother used to say she loved seeing me eat. It gave her joy, she claimed. And honestly, that’s one of the sweetest things I’ve ever been told. There’s something deeply comforting about knowing you’re a source of aesthetic pleasure to someone—even if it’s only because you can clear a plate like a steamroller.

A healthy boy, full of energy and wisely avoiding excessive study (which, let’s face it, is just another word for “being bored”), can generally satisfy even the most demanding expectations when it comes to dining. Watching boys eat is a delight—if you don’t have to pay for it. Their idea of a proper meal? About a pound and a half of roast beef, six potatoes (preferably the kind that look like they’ve been scrubbed with a toothbrush), a mountain of greens, four thick slices of Yorkshire pudding, two currant dumplings, a handful of green apples, a pen’orth of nuts, half a dozen jumbles, and a bottle of ginger beer. Then, after all that, they go outside and pretend to be horses. What audacity!

They must look down on us grown men, who sit quietly for two hours after consuming a spoonful of soup and a chicken wing, pretending we’re not secretly plotting revenge on the universe. We are weaklings compared to them.

But the boys don’t have everything their own way. They never get the luxury of being truly satisfied. A boy never feels *full*. He can never stretch out his legs, put his hands behind his head, close his eyes, and float into the blissful haze of postprandial contentment. That’s reserved for the adult male.

To a man, a good dinner is like a magic potion. The world becomes brighter, softer, more forgiving. After a decent meal, I find myself stroking the cat gently and whispering, “Poor pussy,” as though I’ve just read a novel about lost love. I feel sympathy for the German band playing outside, wondering if they’re cold. Even my wife’s relations—usually the bane of my existence—become bearable. For a moment, I’m not angry at them. I might even smile.

A good dinner brings out the tender side of every man. The grumpy, sour-faced types who spend all day looking like they’ve been dipped in vinegar suddenly break into smiles. They start patting children on the head and talking vaguely about sixpences. Serious men thaw. Snobby young men with heavy mustaches forget how objectionable they are.

I become sentimental after meals. It’s the only time I can properly appreciate love stories. When the hero clutches the heroine in a final embrace and chokes back a sob, I feel genuine sorrow—as if I’d just lost a game of whist and drawn only a deuce. When the heroine dies, I weep. If I read the same story in the morning, I’d laugh and call it melodramatic.

Digestion—or perhaps indigestion—is a powerful force. If I want to write something truly pathetic, I eat a large plate of hot buttered muffins an hour beforehand. By the time I sit down to write, I’m drowning in melancholy. I see heartbroken lovers parting at lonely stiles, twilight deepening, sheep bells tinkling in the distance. Old men stare at withered flowers until tears blur their vision. Young maidens wait at open windows, watching for a lover who never comes. Years pass. Their hair turns white. Babies grow into adults with their own burdens. Playmates lie silent under waving grass. Still, they wait.

I imagine pale corpses washed up on waves, deathbeds soaked in tears, graves in deserts. I hear women wailing, children moaning, strong men sobbing dryly. All because of the muffins.

I could never summon such sadness over a mutton chop and a glass of champagne. A full stomach is essential for poetry. In fact, no sentiment whatsoever can stand on an empty one. We don’t waste time on fanciful sorrows until we’ve dealt with real ones. We don’t mourn dead dicky-birds when the bailiff is knocking at the door. And when we don’t know where our next shilling is coming from, we don’t care whether our mistress’s smile is warm or icy.

Foolish people—by which I mean anyone who disagrees with me—will tell you that mental suffering is worse than physical. Romantic nonsense! How comforting it is for a lovesick young man to look down on a starving beggar and think, “Ah, how happy you are compared to me!” Or for fat old gentlemen to preach about the superiority of poverty. But it’s all hogwash.

An aching head makes you forget an aching heart. A broken finger wipes away memories of an empty chair. And when a man is truly hungry, nothing else matters.

We well-fed folk can’t understand what hunger really means. We know what it’s like to lose our appetite, to push food around the plate without tasting it. But we don’t know what it’s like to *die* for bread. To gaze at coarse fare steaming behind grimy windows, longing for a pennyworth of pea pudding you can’t afford. To feel that a crust would be a feast, a bone a banquet.

Hunger, for us, is a luxury—a piquant seasoning. It’s worth getting hungry just to learn how delicious food can be.

Want to enjoy your dinner? Take a thirty-mile country walk after breakfast and don’t eat again until you return. Watch your eyes light up at the sight of the white tablecloth and steaming dishes. Sigh with satisfaction as you set down your empty tankard and pick up your knife and fork. Feel the warmth spread through your body as you push back your chair, light a cigar, and beam at everyone around you.

Just make sure the dinner is actually waiting for you. Otherwise, disappointment bites.

I remember once, with my dear friend Joe Taboys—ah, how we lose each other in life’s fog! It’s been eight years since I last saw him. How lovely it would be to clasp his hand again and hear his cheerful laugh. He still owes me 14 shillings.

We were on holiday together. One morning, we had breakfast early and set off on a long walk. We’d ordered a duck for dinner. “Get a big one,” we said, “because we’ll be starving.” As we left, our landlady appeared, bursting with excitement. “I’ve got you a duck,” she said. “If you can finish that, you’ll do well.” She held up a bird about the size of a door mat.

We laughed. “We’ll give it a try,” we said, puffing out our chests like champions.

Then we started walking.

Of course, we got lost. Always do in the countryside. Asking for directions is useless. You might as well ask a kitchen maid how to make beds as expect a country bumpkin to know the road to the next village. You shout three times before your voice reaches his brain. At the fourth shout, he slowly lifts his head and stares blankly. You yell again. He repeats your question. He thinks for a while—counting to two hundred in his head—then says, “Couldn’t do better than…” Then he spots another idiot approaching and yells the whole thing to him. The two argue for fifteen minutes and finally decide: go straight down the lane, round right, cross the third stile, keep left by Jimmy Milcher’s cow-shed, across the seven-acre field, through the gate by Squire Grubbin’s haystack, along the bridle-path until you see the hill where the windmill used to be—but it’s gone now—round right, past Stiggin’s plantation…

You say “Thank you” and leave with a splitting headache, utterly confused, knowing only that somewhere there’s a stile you must cross. And sure enough, at the next turn, there are four stiles, all pointing in different directions.

We repeated this ordeal several times. We tramped fields, waded brooks, scrambled over hedges and walls. We argued about whose fault it was we’d gotten lost. We became irritable, tired, footsore. But throughout it all, the thought of that duck kept us going. It floated before us like a fairy tale, a beacon of hope.

We talked about it constantly. “Come on,” we’d say. “The duck will be spoiled!”

At one point, we passed a village inn and were tempted to stop for cheese and bread. But we resisted. We wanted to enjoy the duck *more* for being famished.

As we entered town, we smelled it. Or thought we did. We ran the last quarter mile in three minutes.

We rushed upstairs, washed, changed clothes, sat down, rubbed our hands together—and the landlady removed the covers.

I grabbed the knife and fork and began carving. It took five minutes. No progress. Joe, meanwhile, had already eaten a potato and muttered, “Maybe someone should carve this who knows how.”

I ignored him. I attacked again—with such force that the duck leapt from the dish and landed in the fender.

We retrieved it. I prepared to try again. But Joe was getting annoyed. “If I’d known we were playing blind hockey with dinner,” he said, “I’d have bought bread and cheese.”

I was too exhausted to argue. I laid down the cutlery with dignity and sat sideways. Joe took over.

He worked in silence. Then muttered, “Damn the duck,” and took off his coat.

Eventually, with a chisel, we broke the thing apart. But it was impossible to eat. It tasted like rubber. We made do with vegetables and apple tart. We tried a bite of the duck. It was terrible. A sin to kill that poor drake.

But hey—no respect for tradition anymore.

I started this chapter meaning to talk about both eating and drinking, but somehow I ended up focusing entirely on eating. Well, drinking is tricky. It’s not wise to appear overly familiar with it. The days when men proudly went to bed drunk every night are gone. Now, a clear head and steady hand aren’t seen as signs of weakness—they’re virtues.

In these degenerate times, a bad breath, blotchy skin, a wobbly gait, and a hoarse voice are badges of shame, not honor.

Still, mankind’s thirst is supernatural. We drink before meals, during meals, after meals. We drink when we meet friends, when we part. We drink while reading, thinking, talking. We toast each other’s health—and ruin our own. We drink to the queen, the army, the ladies, and anyone else worthy of a glass. I suspect, if supplies ran low, we’d drink our mothers-in-law.

By the way—we never *eat* someone’s health. Why not? Why can’t we stand up every now and then and eat a tart to celebrate someone’s success?

Honestly, I find the constant need to drink utterly baffling. I understand drinking to drown sorrows, to escape madness. I understand the poor masses seeking relief in liquor—yes, it’s shocking, of course, to us who live in comfortable homes, surrounded by grace and pleasure, that those in damp cellars and windy attics crawl into public houses to float briefly on a stream of gin.

But before you judge, consider what life *really* means for them. Imagine the squalor—the narrow, filthy rooms where families huddle like rats, children scream, women curse, and the street outside roars with filth. Picture their lives: devoid of joy, laughter, love, hope. From birth to death, they never feel a single human emotion. They never dream, never hope, never feel warmth.

In the name of mercy—let them drink! Let them feel, for one brief moment, that they are alive!

Ah, we may talk sentiment all we like, but the truth remains: the stomach is the true seat of happiness. The kitchen is our chief temple, its fire our sacred flame, and the cook our high priest. He is a magician, a healer. He banishes sorrow, melts enmity, kindles love.

Our God is great—and the cook is His prophet.

So let us eat, drink, and be merry.
