"No one," Captain Durrance declared, securing his field-glasses into the leather case at his hip. "Captain Mather agreed. 'No one, sir.' 'We will move forward.'"

With the scouts leading the van, the troops reforming in disciplined lines, and the two seven-pounder mountain guns rolling up behind, Durrance's detachment of the Camel Corps descended from the grim, shadowed ridge of Khor Gwob. They were thirty-five miles southwest of Suakin, venturing onto the vast, sun-scorched plateau of Sinkat. This final patrol in force marked the end of operations prior to the evacuation of Eastern Sudan.

For hours, the camels had labored slowly up a chasm of crumbling shale, flanked by red precipices that collapsed into heaps of desolate stone. Grass was a myth here; save for a few ragged mimosa branches, nothing broke the monotonous gray of ruin. Yet, as they crested the ridge, the valley of Sinkat revealed itself—a sudden splash of verdant bushery that offered a welcome relief to weary eyes. The troopers sat upright, their alertness sharpened by the contrast.

Moving diagonally across the silent plain toward the distant mountains of Erkoweet, they formed a quiet procession against a backdrop of profound stillness. It was eleven o'clock. The sun burned directly overhead in a colorless, cloudless expanse, shortening the camel shadows until they glistened white upon the sand like a beach of the Scilly Isles. There was no breath of wind to stir the rich foliage; the shadows of the branches lay so distinct and motionless upon the earth that they might have been dead limbs strewn there by a storm centuries past.

Life existed here, though in a hushed manner. As the leaders rode along the curving tracks of sand—trimmed smooth like carriage drives—they occasionally spotted herds of gazelles starting from the ground mere yards ahead. These flashes of dappled brown and white would race silently toward the enclosing hills, vanishing before the sound of hooves could disturb them. To Durrance, the landscape felt suspended, a country held in stasis during this final hour.

"This is the road," Durrance observed, voicing a thought he'd been holding, "where the caravans passed south to Erkoweet and the Khor Baraka. Here the Suakis built their summer-houses."

"And there," Mather pointed forward, "Tewfik fought and died with his four hundred men."

For three hours, the column trudged across the plateau. It was May, and the sun hammered down with an intolerable, physical weight. Alertness evaporated; the men rocked drowsily in their saddles, praying for the cool of evening and the silver gleam of stars. The camels minced onward with their peculiar, smirking bobbing motions, until suddenly, a hundred yards ahead, Durrance spotted a broken wall with window-spaces letting the sky through.

"The fort," he said.

It had been three years since Osman Digna captured and destroyed it, yet those roofless ruins had endured another siege—one far more persistent than the first. Quick-growing trees had tightly embraced the fort from the rear and sides, encroaching upon it until it appeared unexpectedly, like Childe Roland stumbling upon the Dark Tower in a plain. Only the front remained open to the sands, where three great Gemeiza trees stood spaced like sentinels around the wells.

In the shade to the right-front, where bushes fringed the open sand with the regularity of a riverbank, soldiers unsaddled their beasts and began preparing food. Durrance and Mather walked the perimeter. Reaching the southern corner, Durrance halted.

"Hallo!"

"Some Arab has camped here," Mather noted, pausing beside him. Grey ashes from a wood fire lay in a small heap upon a blackened stone.

"And recently," Durrance added.

Mather ascended a few rough steps to the crumbled archway of the entrance, stepping into the unroofed corridors and rooms. Durrance turned the ash over with his boot, revealing the stump of a charred twig glowing faintly red. With a step, he extinguished it, sending a tiny thread of smoke spiraling upward.

"Very recently," he murmured, following Mather inside.

Young trees sprouted from every fissure in the mud walls, from the corners to the floor itself. Behind the fort, a steep glacis and a deep fosse guarded the works. Durrance sat upon the parapet above the glacis as pigeons wheeled overhead. His thoughts drifted back to the long months Tewfik must have spent staring from this exact spot toward the pass over the hills from Suakin, straining his eyes for the flash of steel that never came—the same vigil kept by generals far to the south. Mather sat beside him, reflecting in a different spirit.

"Already the Guards are steaming out through the coral reefs toward Suez," Mather said. "A week and our turn comes. What a God-forsaken country!"

"I'm coming back," Durrance replied.

"Why?"

"I like it. I like the people."

Mather considered this taste unaccountable, yet he understood how it fueled his companion's rapid rise. Sympathy had replaced ability for Durrance. It granted him patience and the capacity to understand, allowing him to leave behind, after three years of campaigning, men who were far quicker and more capable than those who stayed. He truly liked them; he could grasp their hatred of the old Turkish rule, their fanaticism, and the feigned piety forced upon them by Osman Digna's hordes.

"Yes, I'll return," Durrance affirmed. "In three months' time. We know—and every Englishman in Egypt knows—that this isn't the end. I want to be here when the work resumes. I hate unfinished business."

The sun beat relentlessly; the men slept in the shade; the afternoon was as noiseless as the morning. Compelled by the surrounding silence, Durrance and Mather sat for a while in thoughtful quiet. Then, Durrance's gaze shifted away from the amphitheater of hills, losing its abstraction to fix intently on the shrubbery beyond the glacis. Recollections of Tewfik Bey faded. He saw, without turning his head, that Mather was gazing in the same direction.

"What are you thinking about?" Durrance asked suddenly.

Mather laughed softly, answering thoughtfully. "I was drafting the menu for my first dinner in London. I'll likely eat it alone, perhaps at Epitaux. It will start with a watermelon. And you?"

"I was wondering why, now that the pigeons seem accustomed to us, they keep circling one specific tree. Please don't point it out—I mean the tree beyond the ditch, to the right of the two small bushes."

Everywhere else, pigeons rested quietly, speckling the foliage like purple fruit. Above that single tree, however, they circled and cooed timidly.

"We'll secure that cover," Durrance ordered. "Take a dozen men and surround it quietly."

He remained on the glacis, watching the tree and the dense undergrowth. Six soldiers crept around the shrubbery from the left, six more from the right. Before they could converge and trap the bird, the branches shook violently. An Arab, wearing a roll of yellowish dammar around his waist and armed with a flat-headed spear and a hide shield, dashed from the shelter, racing into the open plain between the soldiers. He ran only a few yards before Mather gave a sharp command, and the Arab stopped instantly, understanding the order before a rifle could be raised to his shoulder.

Walking calmly back to Mather, he was brought up onto the glacis to stand before Durrance without insolence or servility.

Explaining in Arabic, he identified himself as Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish tribe, friendly to the English, bound for Suakin.

"Why did you hide?" Durrance asked.

"It was safer. I knew you for friends. But, my gentleman, did you know me for yours?"

Durrance leaned forward quickly. "You speak English." And speaking in English, he waited.

The answer came without hesitation. "I know a few words."

"Where did you learn them?"

"In Khartum."

Left alone with Durrance on the glacis, the two men conversed for nearly an hour. Finally, Abou Fatma descended the slope, crossed the trench, and headed toward the hills. Durrance signaled the resumption of the march.

Water tanks were filled, and the men replenished their zamshyehs, acutely aware that afternoon thirst was the most tormenting of all. Saddling their camels, they heard the usual groans and snarls as the detachment moved northwest from Sinkat, cutting an acute angle across its morning route. They skirted the hills opposite the pass they had descended earlier. The bushes grew sparse, giving way to a black country of stones, sparsely relieved by yellow tasselled mimosas.
