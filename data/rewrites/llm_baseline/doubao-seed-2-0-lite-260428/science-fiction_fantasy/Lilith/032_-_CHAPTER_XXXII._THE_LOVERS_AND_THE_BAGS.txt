The night turned sharp enough to shatter bone. Frost crept through my tunic, icing my eyelashes, as the frozen corpse of the horse beneath me leached every last scrap of my warmth away. The wolves’ howls coiled closer, soft padded paws whispering over jagged rock, hot rank panting thickening the air until I could taste it. Out of the absolute dark blazed a constellation of smoldering amber eyes, drawing tight in a half-circle around me. My hour had come. I staggered to my feet—only to realize I did not even have a stick to defend myself.

They rushed, jaws slavering, eyes alight with ravenous fury, black throats gaping to tear me apart. I stood still, hopeless, waiting for the first tooth to sink in. For a heartbeat they hesitated, noses dipping to snuffle at the dead horse, then lunged for me.

A blur of movement hit their flank so fast it barely made a sound, and a swarm of glowing pale green eyes launched themselves at the wolves, a cry bursting from them that was feebler than the wolves’ snarls but far more feral. I knew that cry instantly: cats, led by the massive gray tom I had crossed paths with before. I could not make out a single furred shape of him in the dark, but those eyes were unmistakeable— I knew exactly how broad his paws were, how long his claws, how his silver fur glinted when the sun hit it.

A roar of a battle exploded around me, its violence communicated only through snarls, yowls, the crunch of bone and the snick of claw through hide. I would have run, certain that whichever side won I would end up their next meal— but what was the point? I would have tripped and cracked my skull on a stone before I had taken three steps, and both wolves and cats could see and scent me clear as day in the unbroken dark.

All at once the wolves’ howls cut off mid-snarl, and the cats’ caterwauling spiked wilder. Then came the soft scrabble of retreating paws: the cats had won.

Sharp teeth sank into the flesh of my calves before I could blink, and the horde of felines spilled over me like a living waterfall, biting every exposed patch of skin they could reach, raking their claws across my arms and ribs. Scores of them clung to my frame, too many to shake free. I fought back madly, every finger coiled to crush, tearing them from my skin, choking at their necks to no avail— when I tried to fling them away they clung to my hands like limpets. I trampled them underfoot, jammed my fingers into their eyes, even bit down on the furred bodies that pressed to my face, but I could not rid myself of a single one. They never stopped finding new patches of skin to bite, hauling at my flesh with their horribly curved, pincer-like claws, hissing and spitting in my face— until I threw myself to the ground in despair, and they abandoned my body to dart for my eyes. The second I staggered back to my feet, they fell back from my face to tear at my legs again.

I broke into a run, blind to every turn, no longer caring where my feet carried me. The cats flowed around me in a living torrent, rubbing against my calves, leaping up to nudge my sides, but no longer biting to hurt. If I fell, which I did over and over, they waited for me to climb back to my feet. If I slowed from fear of tumbling down a gully, they nipped my ankles to spur me onward. For the entire wretched night they drove me forward, along a path far smoother than any I had traversed before, so I never stumbled into a chasm, and slipped past the edges of the Evil Wood without ever catching a glimpse of its twisted boughs.

When dawn bleached the edge of the sky, I was clear of the jagged stream channels, standing right on the border of the orchard valley. Overwhelmed with relief, I turned to try and make peace with my strange tormentors— but not a single cat was left in sight. I collapsed face first into the soft moss, and dropped into a dead sleep.

A hard boot to my ribs wrenched me awake. I was bound hand and foot, trussed tight with rough hemp, once more the captive of the giants. “Of course,” I laughed bitterly to myself. “Who else would I ever belong to?” A second kick silenced the sharp, self-mocking laugh, and after a few more well-placed prods from my captors, I finally managed to stagger upright.

Six giants loomed over me. They untied the rope that bound my ankles together, fixed a separate length of rope to each leg, and dragged me off. I stumbled along as best I could, but every so often they would yank both ropes at once, sending me sprawling onto the dirt, only to kick me back to my feet when I did not rise fast enough. They hauled me straight back to my old punishment labor, tied my leg ropes to a thick oak tree, freed my arms, and pressed the hateful sharp flint hammer back into my left hand. Then they flopped down on the grass nearby, pelting me with rotten fallen fruit and small stones, though they rarely landed a hit. If I could have wrenched free of my leg ties and grabbed the stout stick I spotted lying two yards away, I would have tackled all six of them at once. “But the Little Ones will come when night falls,” I told myself, and the thought softened the edge of my exhaustion.

I worked nonstop all day. When darkness fell, they tied my hands behind my back and left me fast secured to the tree. I slept fitfully, waking over and over from dreams of being curled in the middle of a great pile of laughing children.

When the sun rose, my giant enemies were back, bringing their usual kicks and their brutish roughhousing with them. It was nearly noon, and I was half fainting from hunger and fatigue, when I heard a sudden commotion in the brush, followed by a peal of that bell-clear laughter that I loved more than any sound in the world. I yelled out in joy and welcome.

Immediately a din exploded around me: the high trumpeting of baby elephants, the whinny of foals, the low bawl of calves, all coming from the throats of riders rather than the tiny beasts themselves. The Little Ones poured out of the undergrowth, mounted on shaggily miniature horses, palm-sized elephants, and soft fluffy young bears. The older boys and girls walked at their sides, and among them I spotted a young woman with a crowing baby cradled in her arms.

The giants scrambled lumbering to their feet, but a storm of sharp little stones pelted them the second they stood. The tiny horses charged at their ankles, the little bears stood up to hug them tight around the waist, the baby elephants coiled their trunks around the giants’ necks, dragged them to the ground, and trampled them just as roughly as the giants had once trampled other victims, a taste of their own medicine.

My ropes were cut in a heartbeat, and I found myself buried under a swarm of seemingly innumerable small, warm arms. I did not see hide nor hair of the giants after that. They made me sit down, and Lona stepped forward without a word, feeding me bite after bite of the juiciest red and gold orchard fruit I had ever tasted. The whole colony stood guard around me until I had eaten every last scrap they gave me.

Then they led forward two of their largest baby elephants, stood them side by side, hooked their trunks together, and tied their tails fast with twine. The gentle creatures could have shaken their tails free with a single twitch, and unhooked their trunks by simply letting them go slack— but they held perfectly still, clearly understanding they were meant to keep their broad backs pressed parallel. I climbed up, and laid myself in the soft hollow between their two backs. The clever elephants leaned gently against each other to counteract the weight that would have pushed them apart, making a perfectly smooth, warm litter for me. My feet dangled a little past their tails, and my head rested pillowed on one soft ear from each elephant.

A line of small children climbed up to sit along the back of each elephant to act as my bodyguard, the whole group arranged themselves into a cheerful procession, and we set off. I did not even try to guess where they were taking me, I surrendered entirely to their care, nearly as happy as they were. They chattered and laughed and pulled dozens of silly, glee-filled tricks at the start, but the second they noticed my eyelids drooping, they fell quiet as solemn judges.

I woke to a burst of bright, trilling music. The sun had set, dusk was falling fast, and we were moving through that ancient forest where they had first found the stray lost babies, the same forest that I had long suspected stretched unbroken from the orchard valley all the way to the banks of the hot healing stream. A tiny girl sat perched right next to my face, her small bare feet dangling inches from my nose, and looked down at me coaxingly for a long moment before she spoke, all the other Little Ones falling silent to hang on her words.

“We make a petisson for our king,” she said.
“What is it, my little darling?” I asked her.
“Shut your eyes for one minute,” she replied.
“Of course I will,” I said, closing my eyes tight.
“No no! Not before I tell you what to do!” she yelped, and I opened my eyes again. We talked and laughed together for another full hour before she suddenly cried out, “Close eyes now!”

I shut my lids and held them closed. The elephants stopped walking. I heard soft scurrying steps, a faint rustle of leaves, then a silence so thick in that strange world that I could almost hear it hum.
“Open your eyes!” twenty little voices shouted all at once from a little ways off. When I obeyed, not a single Little One was in sight— only the two elephants that bore me. I knew the children were impossibly quick at hiding, the giants had taught them that skill well, but when I sat up and scanned the open, shrub-less forest around me, I could not spot so much as a stray hand or peeking toe anywhere. I stared around in utter blank astonishment.

The sun had dipped fully below the treeline, and dark was creeping fast across the ground— but then thousands of birds started to sing all at once, right overhead. I laid back down to listen, certain if I left them to their game the hiders would soon come out again. The bird song swelled into a tiny, roaring storm of trilling calls. “The children must have a hand in this,” I thought to myself. “But how could they make the birds sing all at once like this?”

I glanced up at the boughs of the giant tree we had stopped under, and spotted a tiny flicker of movement among the leaves. I squinted harder. Pale little white spots popped out of the dark foliage, the bird song died down, and a gale of childish laughter rippled through the air. White faces popped out of every tree: the branches were full of them, hidden in nests they had built high up in the canopy. They climbed down in wild glee, dropping from bough to bough so fast I could barely believe they did not slip and fall. I climbed off my litter, and they swarmed around me instantly, pelting me with tiny soft pinecones in their giddy game. The two elephants ambled away slowly, stately, to find a spot to rest for the night.

“Tell me,” I said, once their wild laughter had died down a little, “how is it I never heard you sing like the birds before? Even when I guessed it was you making the noise, I could barely believe it.”
“Ah!” said one of the wildest little boys, bouncing on his heels, “we weren’t birds then! We were run-creatures, not fly-creatures! We hid in the bushes back then, but when we came to this part of the woods where there are no bushes, only big trees, we had to build nests up high. Once we built nests, we turned into birds, and when we were birds, we had to learn what birds do! We asked all the birds to teach us their songs, and they did, so now we are real birds. Come see my nest! It’s not big enough for the king to fit in, but it’s big enough for the king to watch me sit inside it!”

I told him I could not climb a tree in the dark without the sun to light the way, that I would try once the sun came up the next day. “Kings rarely grow wings, anyway,” I added.
“King! King!” another little one yelled, “None of us have those silly feathery things! Arms and legs are far better!”
“That’s true,” I laughed. “I can climb without wings— and carry straw in my mouth to build my own nest, too!”
“See? You know it!” he answered, then wandered off sucking his thumb. A moment later, I heard his voice calling down from his nest, hundreds of feet up in a massive ancient walnut tree. “Good night, king! I’m sleepy!” and I heard nothing more from him until he woke me at dawn the next day.
