CHAPTER XLIII. “My spirit is too weak; mortality
Weighs heavily on me like unwilling sleep,
And each imagined pinnacle and steep
Of godlike hardship tells me I must die
Like a sick eagle looking at the sky.” —KEATS.

A quiet stillness settled around them, and Mordecai stirred, lifting his eyes to Deronda—not with surprise, but with a deep, peaceful content. Deronda rose quietly, drawing his chair closer, so that no word needed to be raised in effort. The gesture was gentle, like the hand of a nurse adjusting a pillow for a weary patient. Mordecai felt it, and his voice came low, as if he were speaking only to himself, not to another soul.

“In the teachings of the Cabbala,” he began, “the soul returns again and again to new bodies until it is perfected—until it sheds its earthly burden and joins with another soul, one that needs it, so they may complete their purpose together. Then they rise beyond this world, leaving room for new souls to emerge from the eternal source. It is the unfinished work of those already born—their lingering imperfections—that delays the coming of the Messianic age. So the mind shapes what is hidden, like a shadow of truth, speaking in parable yet saying something real. When my soul leaves this worn-out body, it will join yours. And together we shall finish what remains undone.”

He paused, and in that silence, Deronda felt a pull—a need to respond. He tried to speak plainly, honestly. But words could not carry the depth of what he felt. Still, he said only, “Everything I can do, in conscience, to make your life meaningful—I will do.”

“I know,” Mordecai replied, calm and certain, as though no further assurance was needed. “I heard you. You see what I see—you stand beside me on the mountain of vision, and you behold the paths others deny.”

He fell silent for a moment, then continued, lost in memory. “You will take up where my life broke off. I feel myself back in that morning—sunlight on the quay at Trieste. Men from every land wore garments that shone like jewels. Boats pushed away from the shore. A Greek ship would carry us to Beyrout in an hour. I was going as clerk and companion to a merchant. I thought: *I will see the lands of the East, and speak with fuller sight.* I breathed freely, as you do now—no struggle, only youth’s light step and endurance. I had chosen poverty, and loved it—for to me, poverty meant freedom. My heart soared, as if it were the heart of Moses ben Maimon, strong with sixty years and knowing the work that would fill them. It was my first time south. My soul remembered its old sun. Standing there, the ground beneath me seemed to glow, and shadows held an azure brilliance, like spirits made visible. I felt myself caught in a flood of glorious life, where my own short years dissolved into nothing. A great sob rose within me—too much joy, too much strength.”

He paused, gazing ahead as if seeing the past clearly. “I waited for my companion. And then he said: ‘Ezra, I’ve been to the post—there’s a letter for you.’”

“Ezra!” Deronda exclaimed, unable to hold back.

“Yes,” Mordecai repeated, softly, absorbed in recollection. “I had written often to my mother. That name—*Ezra*—was like a wand touching me, pulling me back from the vast ocean of existence, where I had drifted free of individual pain. I opened the letter. And the name came again—this time as a cry, piercing even heaven, calling me to sorrow: *‘Ezra, my son!’*”

Mordecai stopped, his eyes fixed on the distant past. Deronda sat breathless, waiting.

After a while, Mordecai looked down, lost in thought. Then he spoke again. “She was a mother worthy of praise—someone who might have been called, *Her children arise and call her blessed*. In her, I understood the Master who, hearing his mother’s footsteps, rose and said, *‘The Majesty of the Eternal cometh near!’* This letter was her cry from the depths—her anguish, her desolation. She had lost four children to death. Then came my sister, late, the child of her deepest love. And the letter—oh, it was a wound: *‘Ezra, my son, I am robbed of her. He has taken her away and left disgrace behind. They will never return.’*”

At that, Mordecai lifted his eyes suddenly, placed his hand on Deronda’s arm, and said, “Mine was Israel’s fate. For the sin of the father, my soul must wander. For the sin of the father, my work was broken, my day delayed. She who bore me was shattered—dishonored, alone. I turned back. At once. Her spirit, and the spirits of her ancestors—men of noble Jewish hearts—moved within me. God, who holds the universe, became my strength. I turned. I traveled through hardship, to save what little money she would need. I left the sun, and walked into freezing cold. On the last leg, I spent a night exposed to snow and wind. That night… it began this slow death.”

His gaze wandered again. He withdrew his hand.

Deronda held his questions back. Not now. While Mordecai was in this state, no question should be forced—only what came naturally. And even then, Deronda felt a kindred emotion, making him fear his own words might be too heavy.

“But I worked,” Mordecai continued. “We had nothing—everything seized. She was ill. Grief gripped her too tightly, and stirred some hidden sickness. Sometimes she could not stand—her heart beat wildly, and visions filled her mind: chambers of terror, where she saw my sister growing in evil. In the dead of night, I heard her cry out for her child. Then I rose. We stretched our arms together and prayed. We poured out our souls, begging that Mirah be saved from harm.”

“Mirah?” Deronda whispered, needing to confirm—had he truly heard?

“Yes,” Mordecai said. “That was her name. After we prayed, my mother would rest. It lasted barely four years. And in the moment before she died, we were praying the same prayer—I aloud, she silently. Then her soul flew away.”

“Have you never heard of her since?” Deronda asked, gently.

“Never,” Mordecai said. “I don’t know if she was delivered. I don’t know. Who can trace the pathways? The wicked’s will is poison. It poisoned my life—it is slowly choking this breath. Death freed my mother. I welcomed it, being alone in these long winters of suffering. But now—what are the winters?” He laid his hand on Deronda’s arm again, and looked at him with a fevered joy that pierced the heart. “There is nothing to mourn in this fading body. The work will be done better. Once I said, *This beginning is mine—I am born to do it.* Well, I shall do it. I shall live in you. I shall live in you.”

His grip tightened, almost painfully. Deronda, shaken more deeply than ever—knowing now that this man was Mirah’s brother, and feeling the weight of their connection—felt his heart race, his lips grow pale. He could not speak. He feared revealing anything about Mirah, lest it break Mordecai’s fragile peace. He feared any answer less than the height of hope—like a flicker of fire near death, which makes watchers dread to watch it fade.

So he did what he had done once before: he placed his firm, gentle hand over Mordecai’s. And as if guided by a force beyond will, Mordecai’s hand relaxed, turning upward under Deronda’s. Their palms met, pressed together. In that touch, Mordecai returned to himself.

“Let us go,” he said softly. “I cannot talk anymore.”

They parted at Cohen’s door without another word—only a final pressure of hands.

Deronda carried a weight within him—half joy, half sorrow. The joy of discovering in Mirah’s brother a nature greater than he could have imagined—worthy of her, and more. Yet the reunion was also the first step toward a final parting—like a farewell kiss that feels like greeting, or a last glance that becomes the sharpest pain.

And then there was the anxiety—how to tell Mirah, how to bring them together without pain, without jarring circumstances. He knew he would want to shield her from the harshness of the world, especially from the Cohens’ well-meaning but awkward ways. He wanted Mirah’s first meeting with her brother to be pure—free from the noise of charity, the discomfort of poverty. He would arrange it all carefully. For Mordecai, too—he would find a place not just healthier, but more fitting—where dignity and ease could meet.

In stories of heroes, such moments come without clutter. But this was not legend. These were lives still walking among shadows, not yet stars. And Deronda saw his task as delicate, demanding patience and care—especially in persuading Mordecai to leave his familiar, threadbare life.

But of Mirah’s feelings, he had no doubt. She would embrace her brother. She would understand his greatness.

Yes—greatness. That was the word Deronda chose now, deliberately. This man—consumptive, poor, clothed in rags, living on charity, speaking to listeners who took his words as lightly as Flemings take chimes above the market—yet he possessed greatness. A mind alive to the grand currents of human destiny, yet tender enough to turn from fame for the sake of a mother’s cry. Capable of choosing a lifelong mission, yet willing to sacrifice it for the near duty, like a bird feeding its young.

Deronda felt, tonight, that this dying flame was now his charge. He had seen, at the club, how Mordecai was ignored, tolerated—but never truly seen. And that stirred him. He had known the smallness of passion in ordinary minds, and it had made him cautious. Tolerance came easily to him—but so did the fear of standing out, of pushing too hard. Yet now, that caution seemed foolish. For the first time, he stood face-to-face with a soul burning in solitude—exiled by the rarity of his thoughts, unheard by most, spoken to only in whispers—unless, perhaps, when nearing death, signs of recognition finally pierce the loneliness.

Like Copernicus, whose fingers touched the printed pages of his own book when sight had failed—seeing only dimly through the darkening dusk.

Deronda had found one such exile. And in his nature, he could not help but feel bound to him—not merely as a friend, but as a companion in purpose. His own longing—once denied, now stronger—for a deeper bond, for a sense of belonging, echoed in Mordecai’s vision. He was ready to obey, even when it was difficult.

Thus, without hesitation, he began his new role. He would seek Mrs. Meyrick’s help—she would be his guide in bringing Mirah and her brother together. Happily, good lodgings for a consumptive were not far from Chelsea. His first task: to find Mordecai a home where air and light could aid his frail body.

Irony, indeed—heroes often lack carpets and teacups. Seen through a shopkeeper’s window, they might be offered fourpence for three hundred percent. Yet Deronda’s mind was already shaping a plan: he would dismantle his own rooms—take down his best books in vellum, move his favorite chair, place the bas-reliefs of Milton and Dante in a space meant for healing.

But would Mirah be there? What furniture could match the grace of her presence? What harmony of color could rival the sweetness of her voice?

Here, at least, was one blessing: Mordecai had chosen him—and in doing so, had reclaimed a sister whose love waits only for him.
