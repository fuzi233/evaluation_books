In the end, the trio from Arkham—Dr. Armitage, aged and white-bearded; Professor Rice, stocky with iron-gray hair; and Dr. Morgan, lean and relatively youthful—climbed the mountain alone. Following hours of patient instruction on the telescope's operation, they had left the instrument with the terrified locals remaining in the road, watching them intently as the device was passed hand-to-hand. The ascent was grueling; Armitage required assistance multiple times. High above, the great swath of vegetation trembled as its hellish creator moved across it with the slow, deliberate pace of a snail. Soon, it became clear the pursuers were closing in.

Curtis Whateley, representing the undecayed branch of the family, held the telescope as the Arkham party abruptly diverted from the main path. He informed the crowd that the men were attempting to reach a subordinate peak overlooking the swath, positioned significantly ahead of the entity's current location. His assessment proved correct; shortly after the invisible blasphemy swept past, the party gained the minor elevation.

Then Wesley Corey, who had taken the glass, shouted that Armitage was adjusting the sprayer held by Rice, signaling that something imminent was about to occur. The crowd stirred nervously, remembering the sprayer was designed to render the unseen horror momentarily visible. Several men closed their eyes, but Curtis Whateley snatched the telescope back, straining his vision to its absolute limit. He saw that Rice, positioned high above and behind the entity, had a perfect angle to deploy the potent powder with devastating effect. To those without the lens, there was merely an instant flash of gray cloud—a mass roughly the size of a large building—near the mountain's summit.

Curtis, gripping the instrument, suddenly dropped it with a piercing shriek into the ankle-deep mud of the road. He reeled, collapsing where he stood until two or three others seized him to steady him. All he could manage was a half-inaudible moan: "Oh, oh, great Gawd... *that*... *that*..."

Pandemonium erupted in questioning voices, save for Henry Wheeler, who instinctively rushed to retrieve the fallen telescope and wipe the mud from its lenses. Curtis was beyond coherence; even isolated fragments of speech were nearly impossible for him to articulate. "Bigger 'n a barn... all made o' squirmin' ropes... hull thing sort o' shaped like a hen's egg bigger'n anything, with dozens o' legs like hogsheads that haff shut up when they step... nothin' solid abaout it--all like jelly, an' made o' sep'rit wrigglin' ropes pushed clost together... great bulgin' eyes all over it... ten or twenty maouths or trunks a-stickin' aout all along the sides, big as stovepipes, an' all a-tossin' aout... all gray, with kinder blue or purple rings... *an' Gawd in Heaven--that haff face on top*!"

That final image proved too much for poor Curtis, and he collapsed completely before he could speak another word. Fred Farr and Will Hutchins carried him to the roadside, laying him on the damp grass. Trembling, Henry Wheeler turned the rescued telescope toward the mountain to observe further. Through the lenses, three tiny figures appeared, sprinting toward the summit as fast as the steep incline permitted. Nothing else was visible.

Suddenly, a strangely unseasonable noise filled the deep valley behind them, echoing even within the underbrush of Sentinel Hill itself. It was the piping of countless whippoorwills, yet within their shrill chorus lurked a note of tense, malevolent expectancy. Earl Sawyer now took the telescope, reporting that the three figures had reached the highest ridge, standing virtually level with the altar-stone but at a considerable distance. One figure seemed to raise its hands above its head at rhythmic intervals. As Sawyer described this, the crowd seemed to hear a faint, half-musical sound drifting from the distance, as if a loud chant accompanied the gestures. The weird silhouette on that remote peak must have been a spectacle of infinite grotesqueness, yet no observer possessed the mood for aesthetic appreciation.

"I guess he's sayin' the spell," Wheeler whispered, snatching the telescope back. The whippoorwills piped wildly in a singularly curious, irregular rhythm, distinct from the visible ritual below. Suddenly, the sunshine seemed to diminish without any discernible clouds forming. It was a peculiar phenomenon noted by all. A rumbling sound brewed beneath the hills, mixed strangely with a concordant rumble seemingly emanating from the sky. Lightning flashed overhead, and the crowd searched in vain for the usual portents of a storm.

The chanting of the men from Arkham became unmistakable. Wheeler saw through the glass that they were all raising their arms in rhythmic incantation. From a distant farmhouse, dogs began to bark frantically. The quality of daylight shifted, and the crowd gazed at the horizon in wonder. A purplish darkness, born of a spectral deepening of the sky's blue, pressed down upon the rumbling hills. Lightning flashed again, brighter than before, and the crowd fancied it revealed a certain mistiness around the altar-stone on the distant height. Yet, no one had been looking through the telescope at that precise moment.

The whippoorwills continued their irregular pulsation while the men of Dunwich braced themselves tensely against an imponderable menace that seemed to saturate the atmosphere. Without warning, deep, cracked, raucous vocal sounds erupted, forever etched into the memory of the stricken group. These were not born of any human throat, for the human organ cannot yield such acoustic perversions. If one had to assign a source, it would seem to come from the pit itself, had it not been unmistakably the altar-stone on the peak.

To call them "sounds" is almost erroneous, for so much of their ghastly, infra-bass timbre spoke directly to dim seats of consciousness and terror far subtler than the ear. Still, they must be described as such, for their form was indisputably, though vaguely, that of half-articulate words. They were loud—louder than the rumblings and thunder above which they echoed—yet they originated from no visible being. Because imagination might conjure a source in the realm of non-visible entities, the huddled crowd at the mountain's base huddled closer, wincing as if expecting a physical blow.

"_Ygnaiih ... ygnaiih ... thflthkh'ngha ... Yog-Sothoth...._" rang the hideous croaking out of space. "_Y'bthnk ... h'ehye ... n'grkdl'lh...._"

The speaking impulse seemed to falter here, as if some frightful psychic struggle were underway. Henry Wheeler strained his eyes at the telescope but saw only the three grotesquely silhouetted human figures on the peak, moving their arms furiously in strange gestures as their incantation neared its climax. From what black wells of Acherontic fear, from what unplumbed gulfs of extra-cosmic consciousness or obscure, long-latent heredity, were those half-articulate thunder-croakings drawn?

Presently, they gathered renewed force and coherence, growing in stark, utter, ultimate frenzy. "_Eh-ya-ya-ya-yahaah ... e'yaya-yayaaaa ... ngh'aaaa ... ngh'aaaa_ ... h'yuh ... h'yuh ... HELP! HELP! ... _ff--ff--ff_--FATHER! FATHER! YOG-SOTHOTH!..."

And then, silence. The pallid group in the road, still reeling from the *indisputably English* syllables that had poured thickly and thunderously down from the frantic vacancy beside the shocking altar-stone, were never to hear such sounds again. Instead, they jumped violently at the terrific report that seemed to rend the hills—the deafening, cataclysmic peal whose source, whether inner earth or sky, no hearer was ever able to pinpoint.

A single lightning bolt shot from the purple zenith to the altar-stone, and a great tidal wave of viewless force and indescribable stench swept down from the hill to the entire countryside. Trees, grass, and underbrush were whipped into a fury. The frightened crowd at the mountain's base, weakened by the lethal fetor that threatened to asphyxiate them, were almost hurled off their feet. Dogs howled from the distance; green grass and foliage wilted to a curious, sickly yellow-gray. Over field and forest, the bodies of dead whippoorwills were scattered. The stench lingered briefly before fading, but the vegetation never recovered. To this day, there remains something queer and unholy about the growths on and around that fearsome hill.

Curtis Whateley was only just regaining consciousness when the men from Arkham descended the mountain, illuminated by sunlight once more brilliant and untainted. They moved with gravity and quietude, appearing shaken by memories and reflections even more terrible than those which had reduced the native group to a state of cowed quivering. In response to a jumble of questions, they only shook their heads, reaffirming one vital fact.

"The thing has gone for ever," Armitage said. "It has been split up into what it was originally made of, and can never exist again. It was an impossibility in a normal world.
