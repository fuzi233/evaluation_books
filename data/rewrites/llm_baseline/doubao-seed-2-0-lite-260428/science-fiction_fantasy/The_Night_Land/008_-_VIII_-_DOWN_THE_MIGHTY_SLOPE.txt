I moved downward slow and silent, stepping into a Darkness so thick it settled over my skin like a physical weight, pressing against my eyes, my throat, my very soul. It was a night no soul of this age could ever fathom: no faint star-glow, no distant moon, no glimmer of horizon, nothing but a void so total I half lost all sense of self, drifting into the foolish fancy that I had already stepped off the face of the world, wandering for all eternity through empty black. That delusion shattered every time my boot crunched against a jutting rock, or I stumbled headlong into an unseen boulder, jolting me sharp back to the hard, unforgiving reality of the stone slope under my hands and feet.

Down, ever down, that was my only guide. Utter dark slowed my pace to less than a mile every two hours, and the frustration of being unable to take even a single unhurried free stride gnawed at me, sharp and bitter. At last I thought to turn to the Diskos at my hip: I would spin it for mere seconds at a time, its strange cold glow painting a narrow ten-foot sliver of the slope ahead, enough to etch every rut, loose stone, and drop into my memory, before the light faded and I pressed onward again. The Diskos’ small glow felt impossibly bright, a tiny sun against the monstrous dark that wrapped around me on every side, and I only ever called it to light when my stumblings grew too frequent, when the blackness threatened to swallow all sense of direction entirely. I wasted none of its stored power on frivolity, for I knew its strength was finite, and I needed every spark for true extremity.

Six long, bitter hours of this passed, and it had been more than twenty hours since I last closed my eyes to sleep. I hunkered down on the uneven stone, fumbling blindly for my rations, eating two nutrient tablets and sipping carefully from my water skin, every motion guided only by touch and straining hearing. I spread my wool cloak over the sharp rock, tucked my scrip and supply pouch under my head, curled the warm hilt of the Diskos against my chest, and fell fast asleep, Naani’s face the last soft thought drifting through my mind before slumber claimed me.

I woke as abruptly as if something had brushed my cheek in the dark, my muscles coiled tight before I was even fully conscious. My fingers locked around the Diskos hilt, and I held perfectly still, listening for long minutes. No sound breathed out of that endless night. No scrape of claw on stone, no distant snarl, no soft footfall. Still, the prickle at the back of my neck would not fade. I ate and drank by touch, slung my gear back over my shoulders, and set off again, that strange unease clinging to me for all the rest of the day: a quiet certainty that something followed me, slow and soundless, never close, never far. My ears caught no hint of it, but I stopped every few paces to hold my breath and listen, just the same.

Hours later, my foot slipped into a narrow hidden crevice, and I pitched hard forward. A jagged rock would have split my ribs open had my armour not absorbed the brunt of the fall. Shaken, I decided to travel the rest of the way on my hands and knees: feeling every inch of the path under my palms would cut down stumbles to almost nothing, and let me rely even less on the Diskos’ precious light. It was a miserable, crawling way to travel, but I had done far worse across the wastes of the Night Land, and I pressed onward.

After eighteen hours of crawling, three full stops to eat and rest, I found a flat patch of clear stone, brushed all the small pebbles away to make a rough bed, and settled to sleep once more. I thought of Naani as I drifted off, but the lingering unease of being followed niggled at my thoughts, and twice I propped myself up on my elbow to listen into the dark. I heard nothing, and told myself it was only weariness playing tricks, but even in sleep my senses stayed half-alert, tuned for the smallest hint of danger. Just as before, I woke two hours early, jolting fully awake in an instant, and still there was no sound, no sign that anything had been near.

The next full day of crawling passed much the same, until I almost pitched headlong into a yawning, mile-wide pit I had not seen, my chest bumping empty air an instant before I would have toppled over the edge. I scrambled back on my elbows, shaking so hard my teeth chattered, and sat for a long minute thinking of a way to avoid such hidden death traps. I had no rope, no line, until a thought struck: I unbuckled the long thin leather strap from my supply pouch, tied a heavy fist-sized rock to its free end, and secured it tight. As I crawled, I cast the stone out a few feet ahead of me, holding the other end of the strap. If the stone thudded against rock, the path ahead was safe. If the strap went suddenly slack, I knew empty drop waited before me, and I could divert my path. It was a clumsy, slow way to travel, but for the first time since I had stepped onto the Mighty Slope, I moved with surety.

I kept to that pace for three more days, crawling downward for eighteen hours at a stretch, sleeping just six, waking always before I planned, that quiet unseen presence at my back never close enough to harm, never far enough to dismiss. By that point I had been six full days in absolute, unbroken darkness, traveling so far downward it felt I must be crawling toward the very core of the world. I noticed the air had changed: the biting cold of the high Night Land had faded entirely, and the air grew thick, warm, heavy in my lungs, far richer than the thin, rarefied air that lay around the base of the Last Redoubt. I paused to think on that, as I crawled: the air of that lost age had been so thin that no flying thing could survive on the surface of the Night Land, even the ancient monstrous brutes that once ruled the skies of that era long past. The Monstruwacans had once joked, when I told them I would venture after Naani, that I take one of the old intact flying ships from the Great Museum, but I had laughed it off: no living soul knew how to pilot those hundred-thousand-year old craft, the air was too thin to hold them aloft, and even if I managed it, the roaring machine would have lit me up like a beacon for every horror of the Night Land to hunt. I wondered, too, why the ancient Road Builders who carved the great road from the upper world down into the vast valley had not simply flown down, before the air grew thin enough to make flight impossible, and I supposed they must have looked down that hundred-mile drop of unbroken night and feared they would never rise again.

All those thoughts drifted through my head as I crawled, until suddenly, I blinked, and a faint pinprick of glimmer shone far ahead in the dark.

I thought it was a hallucination, a trick of my starved eyes after six days of unbroken black. I rubbed my knuckles over my eyes, and it was still there, faint but unmistakeable. A wild, sharp hope surged through me so strong it made me lightheaded. I staggered to my feet and tried to run, only to trip over a half-buried boulder and go sprawling, scraping my palm raw on the stone. I bit back a curse, got back on my hands and knees, and crawled toward that tiny light as fast as I could. For nine straight hours I moved toward it, the glow growing steadily clearer, and soon a new sound drifted to me: a high, piercing monstrous piping, echoing down the length of the slope, growing louder by the minute. I dropped flat to the rock, crawling low to stay hidden from whatever made that sound.

When I rounded a wall of jagged basalt, the endless Mighty Slope ended. I was at the mouth of a vast gorge, walls rising so high they vanished into the black overhead. Ahead, a jet of raw gas gushed from a crack in the valley floor, burning with a vivid searing blue flame that danced and swayed, leaping a full thousand feet high, lighting the seven-mile-wide gorge clear. The far wall, miles distant, glowed bright blue in the firelight, and the piping sound was the roar of the gas rushing through deep rock vents, wild and whistling across the stone. The mountain on the gorge’s right side rose measureless into the dark above, and I stared, breath caught in my throat, after six days of unbroken night, for the first time, I stood at the bottom of the Mighty Slope, looking out at the uncharted depths of the world below the Last Redoubt.
