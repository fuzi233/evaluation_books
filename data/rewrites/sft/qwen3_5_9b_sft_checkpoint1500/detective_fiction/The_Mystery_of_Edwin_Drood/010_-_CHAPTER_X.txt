It is a widely accepted truth that women possess a singular, almost instinctive faculty for discerning male character—a power that seems to operate without reasoning, defying evidence, and speaking with absolute confidence even when contradicted by the facts. Yet, this ability, flawed as all human traits are, rarely possesses the humility to revise itself. When a woman's judgment proves wrong, it stubbornly clings to its original error, indistinguishable from prejudice in its refusal to be corrected. Indeed, the mere possibility of disproof, however remote, tends to color her initial intuition with the bias of an interested party; she connects herself so deeply to her verdict that correction feels like a personal betrayal.

"Mother," the Minor Canon ventured one afternoon, "do you not think you are perhaps rather severe on Mr. Neville?"

"No, I do *not*, Sept," the elderly lady replied, continuing her knitting.

"Let us discuss it, Mother."

"I have no objection to discussion, Sept. I trust I am always open to it." A subtle vibration ran through her cap, betraying the internal thought: *And I should dearly like to see the discussion that changes MY mind!*

"Very good, Mother," said her conciliatory son. "Nothing is like being open to discussion."

"I hope not, my dear," returned the old lady, effectively shutting the door.

"Well!" persisted Mr. Crisparkle. "On that unfortunate occasion, Mr. Neville committed himself under provocation."

"And under mulled wine," added the old lady.

"I must admit the wine," conceded Septimus. "Though I believe the two young men were equally susceptible to it."

"I do not," said the old lady firmly.

"Why not, Mother?"

"Because I *do not*," she stated flatly. "Nevertheless, I remain open to discussion."

"But, Mother, if you take that line, how can we possibly discuss anything?"

"Blame Mr. Neville, Sept, and not me," said the old lady with stately severity.

"My dear Mother! Why blame Mr. Neville?"

"Because," Mrs. Crisparkle reasoned from first principles, "he returned home intoxicated, brought great disgrace upon this house, and showed profound disrespect to our family."

"That cannot be denied, Mother. He was then, and remains now, deeply sorry for it."

"But for Mr. Jasper's exemplary courtesy in visiting me the very next day, after service, within the Nave itself, still clad in his academic gown, and expressing his hope that I had not been greatly alarmed or violently disturbed in my rest, I suspect I might never have heard of that scandalous affair," the old lady noted.

"To be candid, Mother, I likely would have concealed it from you if I could; though I had not yet fully decided. I was following Jasper out to consult him regarding the propriety of jointly suppressing the matter, when I found him speaking to you. By then, it was too late."

"Too late, indeed, Sept," the old lady observed, noting that Jasper remained as pale as gentlemanly ashes at the recollection of the previous night.

"If I *had* kept it from you, Mother, you may be certain it would have been solely for your peace and quiet, for the benefit of the young gentlemen, and in my best discharge of duty according to my conscience."

At these words, the old lady crossed the room to kiss him. "Of course, my dear Sept, I am sure of that."

"However, it soon became town-talk," said Mr. Crisparkle, rubbing his ear as his mother resumed her seat and her knitting. "And it passed entirely out of my power."

"And I said then, Sept," returned the old lady, "that I thought ill of Mr. Neville. And I say now, that I think ill of Mr. Neville. And I said then, and I say now, that I hope Mr. Neville may come to good, but I do not believe he will." Her cap vibrated again with considerable force.

"I am sorry to hear you say so, Mother—"

"I am sorry to say so, my dear," interjected the old lady, knitting on firmly, "but I cannot help it."

"—For," pursued the Minor Canon, "it is undeniable that Mr. Neville is exceedingly industrious, attentive, and improving apace. Furthermore, he has—I hope I may say—an attachment to me."

"There is no merit in the last article, my dear," said the old lady quickly. "And if he claims there is, I think the worse of him for the boast."

"But, my dear Mother, he never said there was."

"Perhaps not," returned the old lady. "Still, I do not see that it greatly signifies."

There was no impatience in the pleasant look with which Mr. Crisparkle contemplated the pretty old piece of china she held; yet, there was certainly a humorous sense of its being an object with which one could not argue very closely.

"Besides, Sept," continued the old lady, "ask yourself what he would be without his sister. You know what an influence she exercises over him; you know what a capacity she possesses. Whatever he reads with you, he reads with her. Give her her fair share of your praise, and how much do you leave for him?"

At these words, Mr. Crisparkle fell into a brief reverie. He thought of the many times he had seen the brother and sister engaged in deep conversation over one of his old college books—now, in the frosty mornings during his pilgrimages to Cloisterham Weir; now, in the sombre evenings when he faced the wind at sunset from his favorite outlook, a beetling fragment of monastery ruin. Below him, the two studious figures passed along the river's edge, where the town fires and lights already glowed, rendering the landscape bleak. He realized how the consciousness had stolen upon him that in teaching one, he was teaching two; how he had almost insensibly adapted his explanations to both minds—the one with which his own was daily in contact, and that which he approached only through it. He recalled the gossip reaching him from the Nuns' House concerning Helena, whom he had mistrusted as so proud and fierce, submitting herself to the fairy-bride (as he called her) and learning from her what she knew. He pondered the picturesque alliance between those two, externally so very different. He wondered—perhaps most of all—if these things were but a few weeks old, or if they had already become an integral part of his life.

Whenever the Reverend Septimus fell to musing, his good mother took it to be an infallible sign that he "wanted support." Consequently, the blooming old lady hastened to the dining-room closet to produce the support embodied in a glass of Constantia and a home-made biscuit. It was a most wonderful closet, worthy of Cloisterham and of Minor Canon Corner. Above it, a portrait of Handel in a flowing wig beamed down with a knowing air of understanding the contents and a musical air of intending to combine all its harmonies into one delicious fugue. Unlike a common closet with a vulgar door opening all at once, this rare retreat featured a lock in mid-air where two perpendicular slides met; the upper slide, when pulled down (leaving the lower a double mystery), revealed deep shelves of pickle-jars, jam-pots, tin canisters, spice-boxes, and agreeably outlandish vessels of blue and white, housing the luscious lodgings of preserved tamarinds and ginger. Every benevolent inhabitant of this retreat bore his name inscribed upon his stomach. The pickles, dressed in uniforms of rich brown double-breasted coats and yellow or sombre drab trousers, announced their portly forms in printed capitals as Walnut, Gherkin, Onion, Cabbage, Cauliflower, Mixed, and other members of that noble family. The jams, possessing a less masculine temperament and wearing curlpapers, introduced themselves in feminine caligraphy, like a soft whisper, as Raspberry, Gooseberry, Apricot, Plum, Damson, Apple, and Peach. The scene closed with oranges revealed by the ascending lower slide, attended by a mighty japanned sugar-box to temper their acerbity if unripe. Home-made biscuits waited at the Court of these Powers, accompanied by a goodly fragment of plum-cake and various slender ladies' fingers to be dipped into sweet wine and kissed. Lowest of all, a compact leaden vault enshrined the sweet wine and a stock of cordials, issuing whispers of Seville Orange, Lemon, Almond, and Caraway-seed. There was a crowning air upon this closet-of-closets, suggesting it had been hummed through for ages by the Cathedral bells and organ, until those venerable bees had made sublimated honey of everything stored within. It was always observed that every dipper emerging from the shelves (deep, as noted, and swallowing head, shoulders, and elbows) came forth again mellow-faced, seeming to have undergone a saccharine transfiguration.

The Reverend Septimus yielded himself quite as willingly to a nauseous medicinal herb-closet, also presided over by the china shepherdess, as to this glorious cupboard. To what amazing infusions of gentian, peppermint, gilliflower, sage, parsley, thyme, rue, rosemary, and dandelion did his courageous stomach submit!
