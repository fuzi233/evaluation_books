The narrative turns darker here, a necessary descent into the shadowed corners of history so that the true nature of our enemies may be understood. Three weeks have elapsed, and the setting shifts to a subterranean labyrinth of cells within Cartagena. A single door stands ajar, revealing a dim corridor where two cloaked figures wait. One is Eustace Leigh; the other, a confidant of the Holy Office.

In Eustace's hand burns a tallow lamp, its feeble light illuminating a straw mattress and the slumbering form of a man. We recognize the high, pale brow and delicate features—this is Frank. Rescued barely alive from the savagery of African captors, he is now reserved for the refined cruelty of civilized men. He underwent the rack this very afternoon, and now his betrayer, Eustace, stands before him. Whether to persuade or to trap, Eustace himself is uncertain, though his heart bleeds for his cousin.

Yet, Eustace has long ceased to value his own life. He acts solely out of a twisted sense of duty. He must wake the sleeper, coax, threaten, and force a recitation of faith while the victim's heart remains tender from the torture.

But look how peacefully Frank sleeps. Is it merely the play of lamplight, or does a faint smile grace his lips? As Eustace leans closer, he hears a whisper drifting from the dreamer's throat—a mother's name, followed by something far holier. The resolve crumbles instantly. Eustace cannot bring himself to rouse him.

"Let him rest," Eustace whispers to his companion. "I fear my words will be of little use."

"I fear so too, sir. Never did I witness such an obdurate heretic. He scoffed openly at their Holinesses."

"Ah!" Eustace replies, his voice tight. "Great is the depravity of the human heart, and the power of Satan! Let us depart for the present."

"Where is she?" the inquisitor asks.

"The elder sorceress, or the younger?"

"The younger—the—"

"Señora de Soto? Ah, poor soul! One might pity her, were she not a heretic." The man watches Eustace keenly, then adds softly, "She is currently with the notary, for the betterment of her soul, I trust."

Eustace halts, a shudder rattling through him. He struggles to form an "Amen!"

"Listen here," the inquisitor gestures to a nearby door. "We may eavesdrop a moment, provided you do not betray me, señor."

Eustace knows the man watches for any sign of compunction, ready to report the slightest flicker of sympathy to his superiors. Yet, a terrible curiosity overrides his terror. He pauses before the fatal door. His face burns, his knees knock together, and his heart hammers against his ribs as he presses himself against the wall, shielding his convulsed countenance.

Inside, a man's voice is clear and low. The notary repeats the ancient accusation of witchcraft, a charge the Inquisition uses to justify their conscience or whitewash their villainy for the mob. Then, Eustace's soul seems to sink as a woman's voice answers, sharpened by indignation and agony.

"Witchcraft against Don Guzman? What need of that, oh God! What need?"

"You deny it then, Señora? We are sorry for you; but—"

A confused, choking murmur follows, words that mean nothing or everything.

"She has confessed!" Eustace whispers, tears streaming down his face. "Saints, I thank you! She—"

A wail erupts, piercing through Eustace's ears, brain, and heart. He would tear the door open, but his companion drags him away. Wail after wail follows, the wretched man hurrying off, stopping his ears in vain against those piercing cries that haunt him like avenging angels through the dreadful vaults.

He escapes into the fragrant open air, under the golden moonlight of the tropics, into a garden that could serve as a model for Eden. Yet, man's hell follows him into God's heaven; the wails still ring in his mind.

"Oh, misery, misery, misery!" he murmurs through grinding teeth. "And I have brought her to this! I had to bring her to it! What else could I? Who dares blame me? And yet, what devilish sin have I committed that requires such punishment? Was there no one else? No one? And yet, it may save her soul. It may bring her to repentance!"

"It may, indeed; for she is delicate and cannot endure much. You ought to know as well as I, señor, the merciful disposition of the Holy Office."

"I know it, I know it," Eustace interrupts, trembling now for himself. "All in love—all in love. A paternal chastisement—"

"And the proofs of heresy are patent, alongside the strong suspicion of enchantment and the notorious character of the elder sorceress. You yourself admitted she was a witch in England before she brought her here as an attendant."

"Of course she was. Of course. Yes; there was no other course open. And though my flesh is weak, sir, none can prove better to the Holy Office how willing my spirit is!"

Thus, Eustace departs. Before another sun sets, he seeks out the principal of the Jesuits, pouring out his heart—or as much of it as he dares—and begging permission to finish his novitiate and enter the order, with the understanding that he be sent immediately back to Europe or elsewhere. "Otherwise," he states frankly, "I shall go mad, even if I am not mad already."

The Jesuit, a kindly man, approaches the Holy Office and settles the matter, recounting Eustace's past valiant services to the Church to place him beyond suspicion. His testimony is no longer required. That very night, Eustace leaves Cartagena for Nombre, sailing the following week to a destination unknown.

I say, I know not whither. Eustace Leigh vanishes from these pages. He may have risen as General of his Order, or worn out his years in some tropical forest, "conquering the souls"—and inevitably the bodies—of the Indians. Perhaps he returned to England and became the very Ballard hanged and quartered three years later for his part in Babington's conspiracy. I do not know.

This book is a chronicle of men—their virtues and sins, victories and defeats. But Eustace is no longer a man. He has become a thing, a tool, a Jesuit who moves only where sent, doing good or evil indifferently as commanded. By an act of moral suicide, he has lost his soul in the desperate hope of saving it; stripped of will, conscience, and responsibility to God or man, he answers only to "The Society." In a word, as Eustace himself admits, he is "dead." Twice dead, I fear. Let the dead bury their dead. We have no further concern with Eustace Leigh.
