“Surely whoever speaks to me in the right voice, him or her I shall follow. As the water follows the moon, silently, with fluid steps anywhere around the globe.” —WALT WHITMAN.

“Diplow’s full of my cousins right now,” Grandcourt said, lazy and low from his perch on the Offendene drawing-room sofa, one elbow slung over the back, the other hand tucked between his crossed knees like he had all the time in the world just to watch her. “Will you go there tomorrow? The carriage can come for Mrs. Davilow. Tell me exactly what you want moved or fixed in the rooms here—we need to get everything in decent order before we leave for Ryelands. And tomorrow’s the only free day we’ve got.”

Gwendolen had never tolerated needlework in her life, but ever since their engagement she’d taken to white embroidery with a ferocious, performative zeal that would have looked entirely unconvincing to anyone who bothered to count the messy, knotted false stitches scattered across her work. The last eight or nine days had mostly been spent galloping side by side on horseback, but they’d always carved out long stretches of this slower, trickier companionship—one Gwendolen had surprised herself by finding entirely pleasant. She thought she had the measure of Grandcourt perfectly. His drawling answers to all her bright, curious questions about his travels and his past life suited her down to the ground. From their very first meeting she’d noticed he always knew exactly what to say, and he radiated that quiet, unshakable certainty that all folly belonged to everyone else, not him. A man who has always gotten every good thing he ever wanted wields that soft, lazy disdain for other people’s choices like a crown.

His lovemaking, too, had never crossed the line into anything cloying or demanding—nothing more than a soft, wafted perfume of rose-scented admiration that fed every part of her vanity. Only once had he gone too far, leaning in to press a kiss just below her ear on the curve of her neck. Gwendolen had jumped up so fast the floorboards creaked, her agitation so obvious he’d scrambled to his feet right after her. “I beg your pardon—did I annoy you?” “Oh, it was nothing,” she’d laughed, breathless, suddenly terrified of her own reaction, “only I can’t bear being kissed under my ear.” She’d sat back down, her heart hammering with a strange, unnameable fear, because she could no longer snap and flout him the way she’d cast poor Rex aside. Grandcourt had taken that small show of reserve as a compliment, and never dared cross that line again.

That day a light, persistent rain had spoiled their ride, but the consolation had been a heavy package delivered from London, stuffed full of the beautiful, expensive gifts Grandcourt had ordered for her, spread across every drawing-room table for her and her mother to admire. Mrs. Davilow had just stepped out of the room after gushing over every pearl and silk and lace piece, and Gwendolen let her embroidery fall into her lap, tipping her head with that familiar, playful perversity that always made Grandcourt lean in closer. “Why is tomorrow the only day we can go to Diplow?”

“Because the next day’s the first meet with the hounds,” he said.

“And after that?”

“After that I’ve got to nip away for a couple of days. It’s a bore, but I’ll go one day and come back the next.” He caught the flicker of unhappiness that crossed her face, pulled his hand out from under his knee, and laid it over hers. “You don’t want me to go?”

“It’s no use objecting,” Gwendolen said, cold, fighting so hard to hold back the words bubbling up in her throat—the words that would say she suspected exactly who he was running off to see, the secret woman and children she’d only heard whispers of from far away.

“Of course it’s use,” Grandcourt said, wrapping his fingers tight around hers. “I’ll put the whole trip off. I can travel through the night, so I’m only gone for a single day.” He thought he understood what had sparked this little bit of pique, and this small, willful show of temper made her feel more desirable to him than ever.

“Don’t put it off, then,” Gwendolen said, enjoying the small, sharp thrill of knowing she could command him, using that peremptory edge to let out the irritation she couldn’t name. “Travel at night instead.”

“So you’ll come to Diplow tomorrow?”

“Oh, yes, if you want it,” she said, her voice lifted in bright, careless assent, so wrapped up in her own tangled thoughts she didn’t even notice he was still holding her hand.

“How you treat us poor devils of men,” Grandcourt murmured, his tone dropping low and warm. “We always end up getting the worse of the bargain with you.”

“Are you?” Gwendolen asked, looking at him softer, more naive than she ever had before. She ached to believe that light, teasing line was the whole truth—if she was the one calling all the shots, then she didn’t have to feel guilty for anything. If she knew every secret, Mrs. Glasher and her children would look far more to blame than Grandcourt ever could. “Do you always get the worst of it?”

“Always. Are you half as kind to me as I am to you?” Grandcourt asked, his narrow, steady gaze locked on her eyes.

Gwendolen felt like she’d been struck still. She was suddenly, overwhelmingly conscious of all she’d already taken from him, and that bright sense of command melted away to nothing. She looked around her, and realized there was no path back. It was as if she’d already climbed up into a chariot, and someone else held the reins—proud as she was, she couldn’t throw herself off in full view of the whole watching world. She hadn’t said yes to him in ignorance, and any complaint she made now would only be a confession that she’d known exactly what she was getting into from the start. She had no right left to demand explanations. All she could do now was adjust, so the sharp spikes of guilt pricking at her wouldn’t tear her apart. She shivered a little inside, then deliberately shifted her whole frame of mind, the small pause stretching out before she broke into a bright, teasing smile. “If I was as kind to you as you are to me, I’d ruin all your generosity. It wouldn’t feel half as grand as it does now, when I let you spoil me.”

“So I don’t get even one kiss?” Grandcourt asked, perfectly happy to pay whatever price she named for this new, soft kind of courtship that made marriage feel like something bright and new, not a weight to carry.

“Not one?” Gwendolen laughed, saucy, nodding at him in playful defiance.

He lifted her small left hand to his lips, then released it, gentle and respectful. Calling him not disgusting was far too faint praise—he was almost charming. For a wild, dizzy second she thought she would never have loved any other man better than this one. His quiet, unhurried reticence wrapped around her like something warm, inexplicable and delicious.

“Speaking of Diplow,” she said, picking her embroidery back up, “is anyone there besides Captain and Mrs. Torrington? You’re not leaving them all alone tête-à-tête, are you? I expect he only talks to her while he smokes his cigars, and she only answers by adjusting her chignon.”

“She’s got her sister staying with her,” Grandcourt said, the faint ghost of a smile playing across his face, “and two other men besides. One of them you already know, I think.”

“Ah, then I already think poorly of him,” Gwendolen said, shaking her head.

“You saw him at Leubronn. Young Deronda, the fellow who’s with the Mallingers.”

Gwendolen’s heart did a sudden, wild little flip, and her fingers went cold around her embroidery hoop, though she fought to keep her hands steady.

“I never spoke to him,” she said, dreading that he’d see how rattled she was. “Isn’t he disagreeable?”

“No, not particularly,” Grandcourt drawled, in his laziest, most dismissive tone. “Thinks a little too highly of himself. I thought someone must have introduced you two.”

“No. Someone told me his name the night before I left, that’s all. What is he, exactly?”

“Some sort of ward of Sir Hugo Mallinger’s. Nothing of any real consequence.”

“Oh, poor creature,” Gwendolen said, the words slipping out unthinkingly, no trace of sarcasm in them. “How unpleasant that must be for him.” She stood up quickly, walking to the window. “I wonder if the rain’s stopped.”

The next day was bright and dry, to her relief, and Gwendolen rode to Diplow on her horse Criterion, the same way she had that day she’d come back from Leubronn in the carriage with her mother. She always felt bolder, sharper, more confident in her riding dress—certain she looked her absolute best, a comfort for any meeting that felt even a little bit intimidating. All her old anger at Deronda had curdled into a strange, superstitious dread, born of the way he’d wormed his way into her thoughts when she’d least expected it. She couldn’t shake the feeling that this man who’d corrected her at the roulette table was bound to leave some mark on her future sooner or later. Superstition is always born of that sharp, intense focus on the self that makes an evening star feel like a threat, and a beggar’s blessing feel like a promise of luck—they carry enough weight on their own to make their own forebodings come true.

The time before lunch slipped past as she wandered through Diplow’s rooms with Mrs. Torrington and her mother, and she told herself firmly that if she saw Deronda at all, they’d only exchange a single, distant bow. She intended to pay him as little mind as possible.

But the second they were both in the same room, an inward compulsion too strong for her pride took over. Everything else around her faded to automatic, habitual small talk, and all she could do was notice him.

When he sat down at the lunch table, Grandcourt spoke up. “Deronda, Miss Harleth tells me you two never got introduced properly at Leubronn?”

“Miss Harleth hardly remembers me, I imagine,” Deronda said, looking at her simply, straightforward, as they bowed to each other. “She was far too busy to pay me any mind the times I saw her.”

Did he really think she’d forgotten the man who’d slipped her the money to redeem her lost necklace?

“On the contrary,” Gwendolen said, her voice a little unsteady even as she forced herself to meet his gaze, “I remember you very well. You didn’t approve of me playing roulette.”

“What gave you that idea?” Deronda asked, grave and quiet.

“Oh, you cast an evil eye on my game,” Gwendolen laughed, tilting her head. “I started losing the second you came over to watch. I’d been winning nonstop right up until then.”

“Roulette in a hovel like Leubronn is a total bore,” Grandcourt put in.

“I only found it a bore once I started losing,” Gwendolen said, smiling over at Grandcourt as she spoke, but darting a quick sidelong glance at Deronda. She caught his eyes fixed on her, that gaze so gentle and penetrating it cut sharper than Klesmer’s brutal, unvarnished judgment of her singing. She snapped her head around fast, pretending to listen to the rest of the table’s conversation, while every part of her was still fixed on Deronda. There was something about his face, his soft, quiet intensity, that made her feel like all her own little rules and standards were somehow out of alignment. His deep voice, which she was hearing for the first time, felt like the low, thrumming note of a violoncello, compared to Grandcourt’s toneless daily drawl—like the sound of real music next to the lazy cluck of farmyard fowl in drowsy afternoon sunshine. Grandcourt was probably right, she told herself, that Deronda thought too highly of himself: it was the easiest, most common way to explain a superiority that made you feel small.

The conversation drifted on to rinderpest and Jamaica. Grandcourt declared the Jamaican negroes were a brutish, baptist race of Calibans; Deronda said he’d always felt a deep sympathy for Caliban, who had his own perfectly valid point of view, and could probably sing a much better song than the men who enslaved him. Mrs. Davilow mentioned her father had once owned an estate in Barbadoes, though she’d never set foot in the West Indies herself; Mrs. Torrington insisted she’d never sleep a wink in her bed if she lived surrounded by Black people; her husband corrected her, saying half-breeds were the only real trouble; and Deronda pointed out plainly that the white colonists had only themselves to blame for the half-breed population that bothered them.

Gwendolen poked idly at her jelly while the rest of them bickered, glancing at every speaker in turn so she could feel justified in letting her eyes rest on Deronda whenever she wanted.

What did he really think of her, she wondered. He must have cared about her, at least a little, or he never would have gone out of his way to return her necklace. What did he think of her upcoming marriage? What strange, soft convictions did he hold that made him so grave about everything? Why had he come to Diplow at all? The questions looped over and over in her head, a restless, hungry longing to be looked at by Deronda and seen as entirely, unreservedly admirable— a longing that had taken root the very first time he’d fixed that critical, steady gaze on her at the roulette table. Why did she care so much about the opinion of a man who was “nothing of any consequence”? She didn’t have time to unpack the answer. She was far too busy caring.

Back in the drawing room later, when Grandcourt was called away on some errand, Gwendolen walked over to Deronda entirely on impulse, no plan in her head, finding him standing at a side table turning over a book of prints. “Are you going to hunt tomorrow, Mr. Deronda?”

“Yes, I believe I am.”

“So you don’t object to hunting, then?”

“I make excuses for it,” he said, a faint smile touching his lips. “It’s a vice I’m happy to indulge in, whenever I can’t get out boating or playing cricket.”

“Do you object to *me* hunting?” Gwendolen asked, lifting her chin in that saucy, defiant little way of hers.

“I have no right to object to anything you choose to do.”

“But you thought you had a right to object to my gambling,” Gwendolen pressed on.

“I was sorry to see you losing at it. I never said a word to you about any objection I had,” Deronda said, his gaze steady and direct, that wide-eyed gravity holding no trace of judgment or malice. His eyes had that rare, dark, soft intensity that seemed to shine with a particular, gentle interest in every single person he looked at— the kind of look that made people who were lonely, or troubled, reach out to him for things he never even knew he could offer. It was wrapping around Gwendolen right now, warm and unshakable.

“You stopped me from gambling forever,” she said. No sooner had the words left her mouth than she blushed bright red all over her face and neck, and Deronda blushed too, both of them suddenly conscious of that quiet, secret, generous act with the necklace that had been a bold, almost improper liberty for a stranger to take.

There was nothing more they could say to each other. Gwendolen turned on her heel and walked to the window, half mortified that she’d blurted out something she never meant to say, and half deliriously happy that they now shared this quiet, unspoken secret between them. Deronda didn’t mind it at all. Gwendolen seemed far more vivid, far more desirable than she had back at Leubronn: the quiet, tangled fight she’d been waging with her own choices for weeks had woken something new in her, a soft new soul that held both brighter possibilities and sharper pains than her old, arrogant, untested self-confidence had ever known.

That night, back at the rented house they were staying in, Mrs. Davilow turned to her, curious. “Was it really true, or just a silly joke, that Mr. Deronda spoiled your run of luck at the roulette table, Gwen?”

“Oh, it was just a coincidence that he was standing there right when I started losing,” Gwendolen said, offhand. “I noticed him, that’s all.”

“I’m not surprised,” Mrs. Davilow said. “He’s such a striking young man. He reminds me of those old Italian Renaissance paintings. You can tell without anyone saying a word that there’s foreign blood in his veins.”

“Is there?” Gwendolen asked.

“Mrs. Torrington told me. I asked after him specifically, and she said his mother was some foreign noblewoman, very high rank.”

“His mother?” Gwendolen asked, sharp and sudden. “Then who was his father?”

“Well, everyone says he’s Sir Hugo Mallinger’s son, even though he’s officially just his ward. They say if Sir Hugo could have done whatever he liked with his estates, he would have left all of it to Mr. Deronda, since he has no legitimate sons of his own.”

Gwendolen went completely silent, and her mother saw the look that crossed her face, and immediately regretted repeating the gossip. She hated to fill her daughter’s head with all this ugly knowledge of the world, and wished she’d never said a word.

Gwendolen could see the unknown mother perfectly in her mind’s eye—dark-eyed, almost certainly sad, the kind of woman society writes off entirely. The crayon portrait of Sir Hugo hanging at Diplow looked nothing like Deronda, and that dark-eyed, forgotten woman settled into Gwendolen’s thoughts like a ghost.

Later that night, curled up in her small bed with only a dim candle burning by the nightstand, she spoke out to her mother in the dark. “Mamma, do men usually have children before they get married?”

“No, dear, no,” Mrs. Davilow said, startled. “Why ever would you ask that?”

“If that’s the sort of thing that happens, I ought to know about it,” Gwendolen said, sharp with sudden indignation.
