I stand ready to admit a glaring inconsistency. Earlier, I asserted that all figures in Flatland appear merely as straight lines, implying that visual distinction between social classes is impossible. Yet, now I must explain to my Spaceland critics how we *do* recognize one another by sight.

If the Reader recalls the section on "Recognition by Feeling," they will note a crucial qualifier: that ability is universal only "among the lower classes." Visual recognition is the privilege of the higher castes and thrives exclusively in our temperate climates. This unique faculty exists solely because of the **Fog**. While you in Spaceland view fog as a blight—obscuring landscapes and sapping vitality—we regard it as a blessing nearly equal to air itself, the very nurse of our arts and parent of our sciences. Without this atmospheric veil, all lines would appear with identical clarity, rendering discrimination impossible. Indeed, in those desolate regions where the air is perfectly dry and transparent, such confusion reigns.

However, where Fog abounds, objects at varying distances lose intensity differently. An object three feet away appears noticeably dimmer than one twenty-nine inches distant. Through rigorous training and constant observation of these gradients of light and shadow, we deduce the precise configuration of an approaching figure.

Consider this practical example. Suppose I observe two figures approaching: a Merchant (an Equilateral Triangle) and a Physician (a Pentagon). How do I tell them apart?

Imagine aligning my gaze to bisect the angle of the approaching stranger. My view sits squarely between the two sides closest to me. In the case of the Merchant, I perceive a single line where the center is brilliant, but the edges fade into darkness with alarming speed. His sides recede sharply into the mist, making his extremities vanish quickly. Conversely, the Physician presents a line where the center shines brightly, but the fading toward the edges is gradual. His sides penetrate deeper into the Fog, keeping his extremities significantly brighter than the Merchant's.

Through this nuanced reading of light decay, the educated elite can distinguish between the middle and lowest orders with surprising accuracy. However, do not mistake this for simplicity. Life rarely offers static angles. If my father, the Triangle, approaches presenting a flat side rather than an angle, I am momentarily confused, unsure if he is a Triangle or a Woman (a Straight Line). Similarly, observing a Hexagon face-on reveals a complex interplay of bright central segments and dim peripheral lines that requires instant mental calculation.

To navigate a crowded ballroom filled with rotating polygons of various ranks is a task that taxes even the sharpest intellect. It justifies the existence of our prestigious universities, where Static and Kinetic Geometry are taught to the elite. Only the wealthy and noble can afford the decades of study required to master this art. Even I, a respected mathematician and grandfather of two Hexagons, often feel bewildered amidst the swirling chaos of high-society gatherings. To a commoner or serf, such a scene is incomprehensible—a chaotic blur of shifting lights. Even a student versed in theory would struggle to move through such a crowd without accidentally colliding with superiors, a breach of etiquette punishable by shame, as these refined individuals possess an intuitive awareness of movement that the untrained lack.

Ultimately, to behave with propriety in Flatland, one must essentially *be* a polygon.

The mastery of Sight Recognition is honed by habit and strictly discouraged alongside the older method of "Feeling." Just as the deaf-mute in Spaceland fails to achieve true speech if reliant on gestures, the Flatlander who relies on touch never masters vision. Consequently, "Feeling" is banned among the upper classes. Children are sent directly to exclusive seminaries; to touch another person is a grave offense leading to expulsion. Among the poor, however, visual acumen remains an unattainable luxury. Their sons work from youth, gaining a natural, rapid adaptability that initially seems superior to the slow, deliberate progress of the scholar. Yet, once the scholar completes their university education, they undergo a transformation, overtaking the tradesman in every pursuit.

There is a tragic underclass: those who fail the final university examination. Despised by the nobility for lacking their discipline, and looked down upon by the commoners for lacking their innate agility, they are trapped in limbo. Denied professional careers and struggling to form families due to fears of producing "irregular" offspring, they become the seedbed of rebellion. Some progressive statesmen argue that true mercy lies in their total erasure, proposing laws to imprison or silently eliminate those who fail the Final Test.

But I fear I am drifting into the dangerous topic of Irregularities, a subject too vast and vital for this section.
