"I remember, I remember, in the days of chill November, how the blackbird on the—" Well, I forget the rest. It is the opening line of the very first poem I ever mastered. As for "Hey Diddle Diddle, the cat and the fiddle," I simply dismiss it as frivolous nonsense, entirely lacking the gravitas required for true poetry. However, "I Remember, I Remember" was a different matter. Reciting it earned me fourpence. I knew the value was fourpence because the teacher explained that if I saved it until I had two pence more, I'd possess a sixpence. While that logic was irrefutable, it failed to move me; consequently, the money vanished by the following morning, spent on something I now recall with absolute clarity: nothing. That is the nature of Memory. She delivers nothing whole. She is a willful child whose toys are perpetually broken.

I vividly recall tumbling into a massive dust-hole as a toddler, yet I possess zero recollection of ever escaping. If I relied solely on my memory, I would be convinced I am still trapped inside. On another occasion—several years later—I was supposedly witnessing a thrilling romantic scene. The only distinct detail I retain is that at the climax, someone burst in with a sepulchral voice shouting, "Emily, you're wanted," sounding suspiciously like the police had arrived to arrest her. All the tender vows exchanged between us have evaporated completely. When we glance backward at life, it resembles a crumbling ruin: a shattered column where a grand portal once stood, a broken window frame marking a lady's bower, and a pile of blackened stones where a roaring fire once danced, all draped in tinted lichen and clinging ivy.

Yet, everything looks lovely through the softening haze of time. Even past sadness tastes sweet. Our boyhood days appear incredibly cheerful now, filled with nuts, hoops, and gingerbread. We conveniently forget the bullying, the toothaches, and the tedious Latin verbs—the latter particularly. We imagine ourselves as blissfully happy children and wish we could love again, ignoring the heartbreaks, sleepless nights, and the choking sensation in our throats when a beloved woman declared she could only ever be our sister. As if any man truly desires more sisters! Indeed, we see only the brightness, not the darkness. The sunshine casts no shadows on the past. The road behind us looks magnificent, stripped of its sharp stones. We dwell only on the roadside roses, mistaking the thorny briars that cut us for gentle vines dancing in the breeze. Thank goodness for this illusion; otherwise, the ever-lengthening chain of memory would consist only of bitter links, and today's sorrows would be relived tomorrow.

It seems the brightest aspects of existence are also its finest, so as our lives sink into the dark ocean of oblivion, the lightest, happiest moments float to the surface, remaining visible long after the angry thoughts and stinging pains are buried deep beneath the waves. This romanticized view explains why elders tell such tall tales about their youth. To them, the world was a superior place; boys were boys, girls were different, and winter actually felt like winter. Tales of heroic deeds and extraordinary events require three strong men to believe half of them. I enjoy hearing an old gentleman recount these exploits to a group of skeptical youths who dare not contradict him. Soon enough, he swears the moon shone every night during his childhood and that wrestling mad bulls in blankets was the school's favorite sport. It is a constant cycle: the old folks of our grandparents' era sang similar songs, and today's youth will drone identical nonsense to annoy the next generation.

"Oh, give me back the good old days of fifty years ago!" has been humanity's cry since Adam's fifty-first birthday. Pick up any literature from 1835, and you'll find poets and novelists begging for the same impossible return that German Minnesingers and Norse saga writers desired centuries prior. Even the ancient Greek philosophers sighed for it. By all accounts, the world has steadily deteriorated since its inception. One must conclude it was a delightfully pristine paradise when first unveiled to the public, pleasant enough even now if one stays in the sunshine and accepts the rain with good humor. Still, there is no denying it was sweeter in that dewy dawn of creation, when the feet of millions hadn't worn the grass to dust, and city noise hadn't chased silence away. Life must have been noble and solemn for those free-roaming ancestors, walking hand-in-hand with God under the vast sky, living in sun-drenched tents among grazing herds, taking simple needs from Nature herself. They toiled, spoke, and thought while the great earth rolled silently, untouched by trouble and wrong.

Those days are gone. Humanity's quiet childhood, spent in forest glades and by murmuring rivers, is forever ended. Life has deepened into adulthood amidst turmoil, doubt, and hope. The age of restful peace is over; we have work to finish and must hurry onward. What that work is, or our role in the grand design, remains unknown, though our unconscious hands help build it. Like tiny coral insects laboring deep underwater, we strive for petty personal goals, unaware of the vast structure we are constructing for God. Let us abandon vain regrets for days that will never return. Our task lies ahead, not behind. "Forward!" is our motto. Do not sit with folded hands, staring at the past as if it were the building itself; it is merely the foundation. Do not waste your heart and life mourning what *might* have been, neglecting the possibilities that await. Opportunities fly by while we lament lost chances, and the happiness present goes unnoticed because we are distracted by the happiness that fled.

Years ago, while wandering from the fireplace into the Land of Fairy-Tales, I encountered a valiant knight. He had conquered countless dangers and was known as fearless, save perhaps for those rare moments when even courage falters. One day, weary and disheartened by the toil of the road, he feared giant rocks hanging overhead would crush him, chasms lurked on either side, and dragons with bloody jaws guarded dark caves. A darkness thicker than night enveloped the path. Overwhelmed, the knight decided to turn back and seek an easier route for his gentle steed. But when he glanced behind, he marveled: the entire journey he had traveled had vanished. Instead, at his horse's heels lay a bottomless abyss, so deep no man could see its floor. Realizing retreat was impossible, Sir Ghelent prayed to Saint Cuthbert, spurred his horse, and rode forward bravely and joyfully. Nothing harmed him.

There is no turning back on the Road of Life. The fragile bridge of time sinks into eternity with every step taken. The past is gone, harvested, and no longer ours. No word can be unsaid, no step retraced. Thus, true knights press on boldly rather than weeping uselessly over what cannot be recalled. A new life begins every second. We must advance joyfully, regardless of our willingness, walking better with eyes fixed forward than with gaze cast backward.

A friend recently urged me enthusiastically to learn a miraculous system for perfect memory retention. I suspect his eagerness stemmed from my occasional habit of borrowing umbrellas and emerging mid-whist game declaring, "Lor! I've been thinking all along that clubs were trumps." Despite his attractive arguments, I declined. I have no desire to remember everything. Many things in a man's life are better forgotten. Specifically, there is that time, many years ago, when we acted dishonorably, deviating from the path of strict probity, and were unfortunately caught. Worse still, we paid the penalty: maddening hours of remorse, agonizing shame, and perhaps the scorn of those we loved. Let us forget. Oh, Father Time, lift those bitter memories from our overburdened hearts, for griefs arrive hourly and our strength is fleeting.

This does not mean the past should be buried. Life's music would cease if the chords of memory were severed. We should root out only the poisonous weeds, not the flowers, from the Garden of Mnemosyne. Recall Dickens' "Haunted Man"? He prayed for forgetfulness, only to beg for memory once granted. We do not wish for all ghosts laid; only the haggard, cruel-eyed specters must flee. Let the gentle, kind phantoms remain; we are not afraid of them. Alas, the world grows crowded with ghosts as we age. We need not visit grim churchyards or moated granges to encounter shadowy faces rustling in the night. Every house, every room, every creaking chair harbors its own ghost. They haunt the empty chambers of our lives, swirling around us like autumn leaves. Some are living, some dead—we know not. We once clasped their hands, shared our dreams, and felt our hearts join in a grip defying death. Now they are gone, lost forever. Their eyes will never meet ours again, their voices unheard. Only their ghosts remain, talking to us. We see them dimly through tears, stretching yearning hands toward air.

Ghosts are with us day and night. They walk beside us in the sunlit street, sit with us in the twilight, and peer from the windows of the old schoolhouse. We meet them in the woods where we shouted as boys. Can you not hear their low laughter behind the blackberry bushes and distant whoops across the glades? Here, in the quiet fields where we watched for her at sunset, she appears now in the dainty white frock we knew, with the big bonnet dangling from her hands and sunny brown hair tangled. Five thousand miles away!
