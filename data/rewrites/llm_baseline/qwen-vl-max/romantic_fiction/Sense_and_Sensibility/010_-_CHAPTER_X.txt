The next morning, Willoughby—whom Margaret had already dubbed with a touch of playful elegance “Marianne’s preserver”—called at the cottage to inquire after her health in person. Mrs. Dashwood welcomed him with warmth that went beyond mere politeness; it was born of gratitude and the favorable impression Sir John had left behind. Every exchange during his visit confirmed to him the grace, refinement, and deep affection that bound the family together—a quiet harmony that made their home feel like a haven of comfort and sincerity.

As for their beauty, no second glance was needed. Miss Dashwood possessed a delicate complexion, refined features, and an elegant figure. But Marianne surpassed even her sister in charm. Though not as perfectly proportioned, her height gave her presence a striking quality, and her face—so vividly lovely that calling her simply "beautiful" seemed almost too tame—drew the eye without effort. Her skin, richly brown yet translucent, glowed with a radiant warmth; her features were faultless, her smile tender and inviting, and her dark eyes sparkled with life, intelligence, and a kind of eager fire that could not help but delight.

At first, those eyes held back from meeting his, shy under the weight of gratitude for his rescue. But soon, as her composure returned, she saw that he combined polished manners with genuine openness and lively spirit. And when he confessed his passion for music and dancing—two joys she cherished deeply—her expression softened into one of warm approval. That look alone secured his attention for the rest of the visit.

For Marianne, conversation flowed naturally when topics stirred her soul. The moment music or dance was mentioned, she came alive—unreserved, unafraid, utterly herself. It wasn’t long before they discovered their tastes aligned perfectly: they admired the same melodies, danced with equal joy, and shared a mutual reverence for artistry. Encouraged by this harmony, she ventured further, drawing him into discussions about books. She spoke of her favorite authors with such fervor that any young man would have been swept away by her enthusiasm—even if he’d never read a word of them before.

Their literary tastes matched astonishingly well. They adored the same novels, quoted the same passages, and debated with equal passion. When differences arose, they lasted only until Marianne’s eloquence and sparkling gaze won him over. He agreed with her judgments, embraced her excitement, and before long, they spoke with the ease of old friends.

Once he had gone, Elinor turned to her sister with gentle teasing. “Well, Marianne,” she said, “for one morning, I think you’ve done quite enough. You’ve already learned Mr. Willoughby’s opinion on nearly every subject that matters. You know what he thinks of Cowper and Scott—you’re certain he appreciates their genius rightly—and you’ve been assured he doesn’t admire Pope more than is proper. But how will you sustain this friendship when you’ve exhausted all your favorite topics? Another meeting will settle his views on picturesque scenery and second marriages—and then what will you talk about?”

“Elinor!” Marianne exclaimed, laughing. “Is this fair? Is it just? Do you think my mind so shallow? But I see your meaning. I’ve been too open, too joyful, too honest. I’ve broken every rule of decorum—been frank where I should have been reserved, spirited where I should have been dull, truthful where I should have pretended indifference. If I’d spoken only of the weather and roads, and kept silent nine times out of ten, perhaps I wouldn’t be scolded.”

“Dear child,” said Mrs. Dashwood softly, “don’t take offense. Elinor meant nothing but jest. If she truly wished to dampen your joy, I’d scold her myself.”

Marianne’s spirits lifted instantly.

And indeed, Willoughby showed every sign of enjoying their company as much as they did his. He came daily, at first claiming concern for Marianne’s recovery—but as her health improved and their conversations grew warmer, such excuses became unnecessary.

Though confined to the house for several days, Marianne found the time not only bearable, but blissful. Willoughby was intelligent, imaginative, full of energy, and sincere in his affections. He had a captivating presence, and his natural warmth was heightened by the intensity of her own feelings. Together, they read aloud, sang, and talked late into the evenings. His musical talent was impressive, and he read with emotion and understanding—qualities Edward had lacked.

To Mrs. Dashwood, he seemed flawless. To Elinor, he was admirable in most ways—except for one flaw: he spoke too freely, too frankly, especially when emotions ran high. He formed opinions quickly and voiced them without regard for propriety or audience. He valued honesty above tact, and often sacrificed social niceties for the sake of truth—or, more precisely, for the sake of winning her attention. Elinor disapproved of this recklessness, though she could not deny its charm.

Marianne, meanwhile, began to realize how foolish her earlier despair had been—how unjustified her belief that no man could ever meet her ideal. Willoughby was everything she had imagined: generous, passionate, thoughtful, and deeply attentive. And his behavior revealed a sincerity that matched her own.

Even Mrs. Dashwood, who had never considered marriage between them, found herself quietly hoping for it by the end of the week. She secretly rejoiced at the thought of having two such promising sons-in-law—Edward and now Willoughby.

Meanwhile, Elinor noticed something new: Colonel Brandon’s quiet admiration for Marianne. Though others had seen it before, it had passed unnoticed by her—until now. With Willoughby’s growing presence, the colonel faded into the background, his seriousness overlooked, his kindness undervalued.

Yet Elinor could not help but feel a pang of sympathy for him. Though reserved, he was kind, gentle, and thoughtful. Sir John had hinted at past sorrows—losses and disappointments—that explained his quiet demeanor. She respected him, even pitied him, especially since both Willoughby and Marianne dismissed him with condescension.

“You know,” Willoughby remarked one day, “Colonel Brandon is the sort of man everyone speaks well of—but no one really cares about. Everyone is glad to see him, but no one remembers to talk to him.”

“That’s exactly how I see him,” Marianne agreed.

“Then don’t boast,” Elinor replied. “It’s unfair of both of you. He is highly regarded at the park, and I always make an effort to speak with him.”

“Your approval counts,” Willoughby admitted, “but the praise of others is a kind of insult. Who would want to be approved by Lady Middleton or Mrs. Jennings, when they could command indifference from someone else?”

“But perhaps your scorn balances their favor,” Elinor countered. “If their praise is mockery, then your criticism might be praise. They are no more discerning than you are prejudiced.”

“You defend your *protégé* with surprising boldness,” Willoughby teased.

“He is sensible,” Elinor insisted. “And sense has always drawn me. Even in a man between thirty and forty. He’s seen the world, traveled abroad, read widely, and thinks deeply. He’s given me valuable insights—always with courtesy and kindness.”

“Oh,” Marianne scoffed, “you mean he told you the climate in India is hot and mosquitoes are annoying.”

“I’m sure he would have,” Elinor replied, “if I’d asked. But I already knew those things.”

“Perhaps,” Willoughby suggested, “his observations go deeper—like the existence of nabobs, gold mohrs, and palanquins.”

“I dare say his knowledge extends far beyond your imagination,” Elinor replied. “But why do you dislike him?”

“I don’t dislike him,” Willoughby said. “I respect him—he has everyone’s good word and no one’s notice. He has more money than he needs, more time than he knows what to do with, and two new coats each year.”

“And,” added Marianne, “he lacks genius, taste, and spirit. His mind has no brilliance, his feelings no fire, and his voice no expression.”

“You judge him too harshly,” Elinor said. “You rely too much on your own imagination. My assessment is simpler: he is sensible, well-bred, well-informed, courteous, and—though I can’t say for certain—kind-hearted.”

“Miss Dashwood,” Willoughby said, smiling, “you’re being cruel. You’re trying to reason me into agreement, to win me over with logic. But it won’t work. I’ll stand firm against your cleverness. I have three reasons I can’t ignore for disliking Colonel Brandon: he warned me of rain when I wanted sunshine, he criticized the harnessing of my curricle, and he refuses to buy my brown mare. Still, if it comforts you to know I believe his character otherwise irreproachable, I’ll admit it. In return, please allow me to dislike him as much as ever.”
