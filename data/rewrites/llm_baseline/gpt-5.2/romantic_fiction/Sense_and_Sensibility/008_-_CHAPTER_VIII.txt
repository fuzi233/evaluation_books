Mrs. Jennings came to Barton like a gust that knew every chimney by its voice. Widowed, comfortably jointured, and long since done with the serious labor of bringing up her own two daughters into respectable marriages, she had turned the whole remainder of her days into a sort of bustling domestic ministry: to wed the unwedded, to pair off the unattached, to salt every quiet visit with insinuation, to shake a house by laughter until the very teacups seemed to clink in complicity.

She had a talent for hearing what other people missed. Where another woman heard only a song well sung, Mrs. Jennings heard an attachment forming, as plainly as one hears the first cautious crackle of kindling before the flame is visible. The very first evening that Colonel Brandon sat and listened to Marianne—listened, not merely with politeness, but with that deep, still attention that makes a room feel suddenly hushed—Mrs. Jennings’ mind was made up. And when, in due time, the Middletons returned the visit by dining at the cottage, and Marianne sang again, and the Colonel listened again, as if each note were a thing he could not afford to waste, then Mrs. Jennings pronounced it, decisively, joyfully, as if it were already entered in the parish register.

It must be so. Colonel Brandon was in love with Marianne Dashwood.

To her, the propriety of the match lay in plain ingredients: he was rich; she was handsome. Mrs. Jennings had always wanted Colonel Brandon well married—ever since Sir John’s connection had first brought him into her orbit—and she wanted a good husband for every pretty girl, as naturally as she wanted good butter with fresh bread. Besides, the immediate advantage to herself was delicious: it supplied her with inexhaustible jesting. At the park she would rally the Colonel; at the cottage she would tease Marianne; and the sport of it kept her cheerful, as if the day had been seasoned to her palate.

To the Colonel, her raillery—so far as it touched himself alone—appeared, at least, to glide off without leaving a mark. But to Marianne it was, in the beginning, like being addressed in an alien dialect: the words were plain, yet the meaning would not lodge. When, at last, she understood what Mrs. Jennings meant to suggest, she could not tell whether most to laugh at the absurdity or to condemn the impertinence. It struck her as unfeeling—an unkind triumph, at once, in the Colonel’s advanced years and in his forlorn situation as an old bachelor. Marianne’s mind, quick with ardor, had no patience for mirth that leaned on age as its joke.

Mrs. Dashwood, who could not think a man five years younger than herself so very ancient as he appeared to her youngest daughter’s fancy, ventured to clear Mrs. Jennings from the charge of intending cruelty.

“But at least, Mama,” Marianne cried, “you cannot deny the absurdity of the accusation, though you may not think it intentionally ill-natured. Colonel Brandon is certainly younger than Mrs. Jennings, but he is old enough to be my father; and if he were ever animated enough to be in love, he must have long outlived every sensation of the kind. It is too ridiculous! When is a man to be safe from such wit, if age and infirmity will not protect him?”

“Infirmity!” said Elinor. Her voice had a steadiness that often acted on Marianne like the clean ring of a spoon against china—cool, undeniable, and somehow restorative. “Do you call Colonel Brandon infirm? I can easily suppose that his age may appear much greater to you than to my mother; but you can hardly deceive yourself as to his having the use of his limbs!”

“Did not you hear him complain of the rheumatism?” Marianne returned. “And is not that the commonest infirmity of declining life?”

“My dearest child,” said Mrs. Dashwood, laughing, “at this rate you must be in continual terror of my decay; and it must seem to you a miracle that my life has been extended to the advanced age of forty.”

“Mama, you are not doing me justice. I know very well that Colonel Brandon is not old enough to make his friends yet apprehensive of losing him in the course of nature. He may live twenty years longer. But thirty-five has nothing to do with matrimony.”

“Perhaps,” said Elinor, “thirty-five and seventeen had better not have any thing to do with matrimony together. But if there should by any chance happen to be a woman who is single at seven and twenty, I should not think Colonel Brandon’s being thirty-five any objection to his marrying her.”

Marianne paused—only a moment, as if she had tasted a thought and found it disagreeable.

“A woman of seven and twenty,” she said at last, “can never hope to feel or inspire affection again; and if her home be uncomfortable, or her fortune small, I can suppose that she might bring herself to submit to the offices of a nurse, for the sake of the provision and security of a wife. In his marrying such a woman therefore there would be nothing unsuitable. It would be a compact of convenience, and the world would be satisfied. In my eyes it would be no marriage at all, but that would be nothing. To me it would seem only a commercial exchange, in which each wished to be benefited at the expense of the other.”

Elinor’s look, though calm, had that quiet persistence which never raised its voice, and yet was not to be shaken.

“It would be impossible, I know,” she replied, “to convince you that a woman of seven and twenty could feel for a man of thirty-five anything near enough to love, to make him a desirable companion to her. But I must object to your dooming Colonel Brandon and his wife to the constant confinement of a sick chamber, merely because he chanced to complain yesterday”—and she could not help adding, with a touch of reason that sounded like the soft settling of ashes in a grate—“(a very cold damp day) of a slight rheumatic feel in one of his shoulders.”

“But he talked of flannel waistcoats,” said Marianne; and the very phrase, to her, carried the odor of camphor and the muffled, airless warmth of rooms where the windows are never opened. “And with me a flannel waistcoat is invariably connected with aches, cramps, rheumatisms, and every species of ailment that can afflict the old and the feeble.”

“Had he been only in a violent fever,” Elinor answered, “you would not have despised him half so much. Confess, Marianne—is not there something interesting to you in the flushed cheek, hollow eye, and quick pulse of a fever?”

Marianne, who could make romance out of a storm and significance out of the very way the wind pressed against the panes, did not deny it; and yet she would not yield. Her imagination had its own vocabulary for feeling, and anything outside it—age spoken of without poetry, prudence offered like plain broth when she wanted spice—seemed to her not merely dull but almost a betrayal.

Soon after, Elinor left the room. The cottage, which had been full of voices, settled into a quieter sound: the faint tick of the clock, the soft scrape of a coal shifting, and beyond the walls the winter air moving in the hedges like a distant sea. In that gentler acoustic, Marianne’s thoughts turned—inevitably, passionately—to another subject, one nearer to her heart than Mrs. Jennings’ noisy prophecies.

“Mama,” she said, lowering her voice as if illness itself might be startled by loudness, “I have an alarm on the subject of illness which I cannot conceal from you. I am sure Edward Ferrars is not well. We have now been here almost a fortnight, and yet he does not come. Nothing but real indisposition could occasion this extraordinary delay. What else can detain him at Norland?”

“Had you any idea of his coming so soon?” Mrs. Dashwood asked.

“I had none. On the contrary, if I have felt any anxiety at all on the subject, it has been in recollecting that he sometimes showed a want of pleasure and readiness in accepting my invitation, when I talked of his coming to Barton. Does Elinor expect him already?”

“I have never mentioned it to her,” said Mrs. Dashwood, “but of course she must.”

“I rather think you are mistaken,” her mother returned, considering. “For when I was talking to her yesterday of getting a new grate for the spare bedchamber, she observed that there was no immediate hurry for it, as it was not likely that the room would be wanted for some time.”

“How strange this is! what can be the meaning of it!” Marianne’s words came faster now, as if they must outrun the chill apprehension that had settled over her. “But the whole of their behaviour to each other has been unaccountable! How cold, how composed were their last adieus! How languid their conversation the last evening of their being together! In Edward’s farewell there was no distinction between Elinor and me: it was the good wishes of an affectionate brother to both. Twice did I leave them purposely together in the course of the last morning, and each time did he most unaccountably follow me out of the room. And Elinor, in quitting Norland and Edward, cried not as I did. Even now her self-command is invariable. When is she dejected or melancholy? When does she try to avoid society, or appear restless and dissatisfied in it?”

Mrs. Dashwood listened, feeling the old, familiar ache of a mother who knows that her children live inside different languages of the heart. Marianne’s was a tongue of music and storms; Elinor’s, a careful speech where meaning was often carried not by sound but by silence. And in the stillness of the cottage—where every small domestic noise seemed magnified, where even the fire’s whisper sounded like counsel—Marianne could not help hearing absence as ominous, and composure as concealment.
