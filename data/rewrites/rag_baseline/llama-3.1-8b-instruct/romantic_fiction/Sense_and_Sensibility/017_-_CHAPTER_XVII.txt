As the sun set over the rolling hills of Devonshire, casting a warm orange glow over the humble cottage of Barton Park, Mrs. Dashwood welcomed her brother, Edward, with an affectionate smile. It was a reunion long overdue, and one that had brought a sense of warmth and comfort to the household. Her joy and expression of regard had initially given way to a hint of surprise, but now, as they settled around the dinner table, it was clear that the bond between them had only grown stronger.

Edward's visit was a testament to the enduring ties of family, and as he took his seat among them, the atmosphere in the room seemed to shift, becoming more relaxed and convivial. Though his spirits were subdued, his eyes sparkled with kindness as he praised the beauty of the surrounding countryside and the simple pleasures of their home. The family sensed that something was amiss, but Mrs. Dashwood, attributing it to the constraints imposed by his mother's frugality, attributed it to the limitations of his family's financial means.

As the evening drew on, conversation turned to the subject of Edward's future prospects. Mrs. Dashwood asked, with a twinkle in her eye, whether he still harbored ambitions to become a celebrated orator. Edward smiled wryly, acknowledging that his mother had likely given up on the idea of him making a name for himself in public life.

"But how," she pressed on, "are you to establish your reputation, Edward? For in your family, fame is the only currency that counts." Edward shook his head good-naturedly, assuring her that he had no desire to be famous, nor any expectation of achieving greatness. His sister Marianne, ever the romantic, scoffed at the idea that wealth or grandeur held any significance for true happiness. Elinor, more measured in her views, countered that while grandeur might hold little sway, wealth could indeed provide a certain level of comfort.

Marianne rolled her eyes playfully at her sister's pragmatism, but Elinor's words struck a chord with Mrs. Dashwood, who mused aloud about the challenges of maintaining a modest household on a limited income. Edward's competence, she estimated, was likely around two thousand pounds per annum, a sum that would barely cover the costs of a respectable establishment.
