As the first wisps of dawn seeped into our cramped quarters, Dan and I gathered around the wooden table, our eyes drawn to the steaming pot of porridge that served as our morning meal. The sweet scent of oats wafted up, mingling with the faint tang of salted fish, as we exchanged stories of our lives. Dan's narrative revealed threads of similarity, weaving a tapestry of shared experiences. He had left school before me, bound for Cambridge, where he discovered a passion for learning, unshackled by the rigid curriculum that had stifled my own interest in education. "At school," he said, his voice low and reflective, "knowledge was forced down our throats like a stale gruel. But at Cambridge, I devoured it, feasting on the feast of ideas."

We spoke of the paths that had led him to where we now sat, a small, ramshackle kitchen in a forgotten corner of the city. He had worked in a bank, earning a meager income while pursuing his love of learning, but the monotony of the work had soon become unbearable. He had joined a theatre company, taking on roles both grand and humble, from Romeo to Paul Pry, and even assisting with the posters, a task he found particularly trying. The paste would often rebel against his fingers, sticking to his skin and hair with an obstinate tenacity.

Dan's tale meandered through the streets of London, a winding river of experience that had carried him to the stages of music halls and circuses, to the tables of fine dining establishments and the humble abodes of the working class. He had been a lecturer, a fiddler, a schoolmaster, and a journalist, each role a stepping stone on his journey to become a writer. His words painted vivid pictures, transporting me to the streets of Ostend, where he had waited tables, and to the rolling hills of Ireland, where he had tramped with his fiddle slung over his shoulder.
