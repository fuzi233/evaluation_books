"On earth is known to none
The smile that is not sister to a tear."

Margaret and her father walked home together under the quiet glow of a summer night. The air was cool and fresh, the streets clean and still, and Margaret, in her white silk dress—kilted up to her knee like Leezie Lindsay’s green satin gown from the old ballad—moved with lightness, almost as if she were dancing through the evening. Her spirits rose with every step, buoyed by the crisp breeze and the sense of freedom after an evening spent among strangers and new ideas.

“I think Mr. Thornton isn’t quite at ease about the strike,” she said, glancing toward her father. “He seemed anxious tonight.”

“Indeed, I wouldn’t expect him to be,” replied Mr. Hale. “But he spoke with his usual composure when others offered their opinions just before we left.”

“He did so after dinner too,” Margaret added. “Nothing seems to ruffle his manner, yet there’s something strained in his face. It strikes me as worried.”

“I should be troubled if I were in his place,” Mr. Hale said. “He must know how deeply his workers resent him—how they see him as a ‘hard man,’ not because he’s unjust, but because he’s unyielding. He stands fast on his rights, as though they were absolute, without regard for what we all are before God. I’m glad you noticed his anxiety. When I remember Boucher’s wild words and agitated state, I can scarcely bear to think how calmly Mr. Thornton responded.”

“In truth,” Margaret said, “I’m not convinced Boucher was truly desperate. At the moment, yes—he was clearly distressed. But these unions have ways of providing relief, even when it seems hopeless. And from what you told me, he’s the kind of man who wears his emotions openly—passionate, impulsive. That doesn’t mean he’s beyond help.”

“Oh, Margaret!” Mr. Hale sighed, half amused, half exasperated. “You’re defending him now?”

“No, not exactly,” she replied. “But I want to be fair. Mr. Thornton may be proud to the point of hiding his feelings. That’s the sort of man I might admire—strong, self-contained, capable. And yet… I don’t feel certain those feelings exist beneath the surface. He has great strength of character, and intelligence beyond what one might expect given his background.”

“It’s not so little,” Mr. Hale countered gently. “He’s lived a practical life since youth, exercising judgment, restraint, responsibility. That shapes the mind. He lacks only the wisdom drawn from history—the deeper understanding of human nature and society. But he knows he lacks it. He sees it. And that awareness matters.”

“You’re biased against him, papa,” Margaret said softly.

“And why shouldn’t I be?” he asked, smiling faintly. “He’s your first encounter with a manufacturer—a man of trade. He’s your first olive. Let me make a face while I swallow it.”

She laughed. “I know he’s good for his kind. And perhaps, in time, I’ll come to like the kind. I already find myself intrigued by what the men discussed tonight—though I understood barely half. I was sorry when Miss Thornton came to take me away, saying I’d be uncomfortable as the only woman among so many gentlemen. I hadn’t thought of it—I was too busy listening. And the ladies? Oh, papa—they were so dull! Yet it was clever. It reminded me of our childhood game where we had to weave certain nouns into sentences.”

“What do you mean?” Mr. Hale asked.

“They used words that signaled wealth—housekeepers, under-gardeners, glasshouses, lace, diamonds—and each lady crafted her speech so that these items appeared naturally, as if by accident. It was like a subtle display of status.”

“You’ll be proud of your own servant when you finally have one,” he said, chuckling. “If Mrs. Thornton’s stories are true.”

“I shall,” Margaret agreed. “And I felt like a hypocrite tonight, sitting here in my fine silk, hands idle, while I remembered all the honest work done by the servants today. They took me for a fine lady, I’m sure.”

“Even I mistook you,” Mr. Hale said quietly, a soft smile playing on his lips.

But the smile vanished instantly when Dixon opened the door. Her face was pale, her eyes wide with fear.

“Oh, master! Oh, Miss Margaret! Thank God you’ve come! Dr. Donaldson is here. The servant next door ran for him—the charwoman’s gone home. She’s better now, but oh, sir! I thought she’d die an hour ago!”

Mr. Hale clutched Margaret’s arm to keep himself upright. His face twisted with dread. He looked at his daughter—her expression filled with sorrow, but not the raw terror that gripped him. She knew more than he did, yet she listened with that same terrible stillness, as though awaiting the inevitable.

“Oh! I should not have left her—wicked daughter that I am!” she whispered, supporting her father’s trembling steps up the stairs.

Dr. Donaldson met them on the landing.

“She’s better now,” he murmured. “The opiate has taken effect. The spasms were severe—no wonder your maid was frightened—but she’ll rally this time.”

“This time?” Mr. Hale gasped. “Let me go to her!”

Half an hour ago, he had been a man in his prime. Now, he was frail, dim-eyed, staggering as if aged decades in minutes. Dr. Donald:don guided him into the bedroom, where Margaret followed closely.

There lay her mother—sleeping, yes, but with a face that bore no sign of peace. Death had marked her. Though she breathed, it was clear he would return soon.

Mr. Hale stared at her silently for a long time. Then, without warning, he began to tremble violently. He turned away from the doctor’s concerned gaze and fumbled blindly for the door, unable to see it despite the flickering candles. He stumbled into the drawing room, groping for a chair. Dr. Donaldson brought one and helped him sit.

“Speak to him, Miss Hale,” he urged. “We must rouse him.”

“Papa!” Margaret cried, her voice broken with grief. “Papa! Speak to me!”

A fleeting look of recognition passed over his face. He made a desperate effort.

“Margaret—did you know this was coming? Oh, it was cruel of you!”

“No, sir,” Dr. Donaldson answered firmly. “Miss Hale acted under my instructions. There may have been a mistake, but it wasn’t cruel. Your wife will be different tomorrow, I trust. She had spasms, as I expected, though I didn’t tell her. She’s taken the opiate I brought. She’ll sleep deeply. By morning, the look that frightens you will be gone.”

“But not the disease?”

Dr. Donaldson glanced at Margaret. Her head bowed, her face lifted—not pleading, but accepting. He saw the truth in her silence.

“No,” he said quietly. “Not the disease. We cannot cure it. All we can do is slow its course, ease the pain. Be strong, sir. Be a Christian. Have faith in the immortality of the soul—nothing earthly can touch that.”

The only reply was choked words: “You’ve never been married, Doctor. You don’t know what it is.” And then deep, heavy sobs—like thunder rolling through the stillness of the night.

Margaret knelt beside him, brushing his hair, whispering comfort, her tears falling onto his hand. No one counted the hours. Time lost meaning.

It was Mr. Hale who broke the silence first.

“What must we do?” he asked, his voice weak. “Tell us both. Margaret is my staff—my right hand.”

Dr. Donaldson gave clear, calm advice. No immediate danger—perhaps peace for days. But no hope of lasting recovery. He advised Mr. Hale to rest, to leave one watcher. He promised to return at dawn.

With a firm handshake, he left.

They exchanged few words. Exhaustion had stripped them of speech. Mr. Hale insisted on staying awake through the night. Margaret could only persuade him to lie on the drawing-room sofa.

Dixon refused to sleep. She sat, staring, blinking, slumping, jerking herself upright again—until finally, defeated, she fell into a snoring stupor.

Margaret had removed her dress and thrown it aside in irritation. She wore only her dressing gown now. She felt alive in a way she never had before—every nerve alert, every sense heightened. She watched. She listened. Every sound, every breath, every shadow stirred her.

For more than two hours, she heard her father move restlessly in the next room. He came to the door of her mother’s chamber again and again, pausing to listen. Each time, Margaret would open the door, unaware of his presence, and explain how things stood—answering questions formed only by his lips.

At last, he too fell asleep.

And the house grew still.

Margaret sat behind the curtain, thinking.

The world outside seemed distant—far away in time and space. Less than thirty-six hours ago, she had cared deeply for Bessy Higgins and her father, grieved for Boucher. Now, those concerns felt like memories from another life. Everything outside the house seemed disconnected from her mother—and therefore unreal.

Even Harley Street felt clearer now. She remembered vividly how she had traced her mother’s features in Aunt Shaw’s face, how letters had stirred her longing for home. Helstone itself receded into the past. The gray, monotonous winter and spring now seemed more real, more precious, than any joy she had known lately.

She wished she could reach out and pull back that vanished time—beg it to return, to give her back what she had failed to cherish while it lasted.

Life, she thought, was a hollow show—fleeting, insubstantial. From some high tower above the world, a bell tolled endlessly: *All are shadows—All are passing—All is past.*

When morning came, cool and gray like so many others, she looked upon the sleeping faces—her father, Dixon, her mother—and wondered if the terrible night had been a dream.

It, too, was a shadow.

It, too, was past.

Mrs. Hale woke with no memory of her illness. She was surprised by Dr. Donaldson’s early visit, puzzled by the anxious looks of her husband and daughter. She agreed to stay in bed, saying she was tired. But the next day, she insisted on getting up. Dr. Donaldson allowed it.

She was restless, uncomfortable in every position. By nightfall, she grew feverish.

Mr. Hale was listless, unable to decide anything.

“What can we do to spare her such a night again?” Margaret asked on the third day.

“It’s partly the reaction from the opiates I’ve had to use,” Dr. Donaldson explained. “More painful for you to watch than for her to endure. But a water-bed might help. Mrs. Thornton has one. I’ll call there this afternoon.”

He paused, noticing Margaret’s pale face, worn by vigilance.

“I’m not sure I can go,” he said. “I have a long round. But you could walk to Marlborough Street—ask Mrs. Thornton if she’d lend it.”

“Of course,” Margaret said. “I’ll go while mamma sleeps. I’m sure Mrs. Thornton will help.”

And indeed, Dr. Donaldson was right. Mrs. Hale seemed to recover quickly. That afternoon, she looked brighter than Margaret had dared hope.

After dinner, Margaret left her seated in the easy chair, her hand resting in her husband’s. He looked more worn than she—yet he smiled now, slowly, faintly. A few days earlier, Margaret had believed she’d never see that smile again.

The walk to Marlborough Street was two miles. The August sun beat down fiercely at three o’clock. Margaret walked slowly, absorbed in her thoughts. She had grown accustomed to navigating the crowded streets of Milton. For the first mile and a half, nothing seemed unusual.

Then, she noticed a strange swelling among the crowd ahead—people not moving forward, but talking, listening, buzzing with agitation. They parted for her, but she was too wrapped in her purpose to notice fully.

Only when she reached Marlborough Street did the full weight of it sink in: a restless tension hung in the air—moral and physical. From every narrow lane came a low, distant roar, like thousands of angry voices.

Every poor dwelling emptied into the street. People stood in doorways, windows, even the middle of lanes—all fixed on one point.

Marlborough Street was the center of their gaze—eyes blazing with anger, threats, fear, or pleading.

As Margaret approached the small side entrance of Marlborough Mill Yard, she waited for the porter’s answer to her ring.

She looked around—and heard the first distant roll of the storm.

She saw the first wave of the crowd surge forward, crash, and retreat at the far end of the street.

It had been silent moments before—now, ominously still.

All these signs struck her, but did not penetrate her heart.

She did not understand their meaning.

She was too consumed by another truth—the sharp, unbearable knowledge that soon, she would be motherless.

She was trying to grasp it—to prepare herself, so that when the moment came, she might comfort her father.

The porter opened the gate cautiously, barely wide enough for her.

“It’s you, ma’am?” he said, breathing heavily, widening the gap slightly. “Th’ folk are all coming up here, I reckon.”

“I don’t know,” Margaret replied. “Something’s happening—something unusual. But this street is empty.”

She crossed the yard and climbed the steps to the front door.

No steam engine roared. No machinery clicked. No voices clashed.

Only far off—the ominous, gathering thunder.
