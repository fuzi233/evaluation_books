The following afternoon found Margaret and her father preparing to return Mrs. Thornton’s call. Mrs. Hale, weakened by persistent ill health, had remained at home, leaving her daughter to accompany Mr. Hale with quiet concern.

“Did you send for the doctor, Margaret?” her father asked abruptly, his voice edged with a worry he could no longer suppress.

“No,” she admitted. “But if you could recommend someone reliable, I would fetch him at once. Mamma is not well—I am sure of it.”

Her words were firm, for she had seen how readily her father dismissed such fears before. Now, his face darkened. “You don’t think—surely our move to Milton hasn’t…” His voice faltered.

“Papa, don’t imagine the worst,” Margaret said quickly. “She only needs rest and care.”

Yet his anxiety lingered, unspoken but heavy between them as they walked.

---

Marlborough Street was not what Margaret had expected. The Thornton name carried weight in Milton, yet the address led them not to a grand residence but to a plain lane lined with modest houses. Confused, Mr. Hale hesitated until Margaret stopped a passerby.

“Mr. Thornton? The mill owner? His house is by the factory gates,” the man said, pointing toward a high-walled yard.

They were admitted through a narrow lodge-door into a vast courtyard, alive with the relentless clamor of machinery. Smoke darkened the air, but the house itself stood apart—a solid, well-kept structure, its stone façade weathered but immaculate. Margaret marveled at the contrast: this was a home of substance, yet it stood amidst industry’s unceasing roar.

Inside, the drawing room was a study in oppressive neatness. Every surface gleamed, every chair shrouded in protective covers. The effect was sterile—a place preserved rather than lived in.

Mrs. Thornton arrived with her usual imposing grace, her black silk rustling like a challenge. Margaret explained her mother’s absence, but the words stumbled. She saw the flicker of disapproval in Mrs. Thornton’s eyes—the unspoken judgment that Mrs. Hale’s frailty was an indulgence.

“And how is Mr. Thornton?” Mr. Hale interjected, shifting the subject.

“My son is never ill,” Mrs. Thornton replied, pride sharpening her tone. “Even when he is, he does not let it hinder his duties.”

“His dedication is admirable,” Mr. Hale said warmly. “His appreciation for literature is a rare pleasure.”

Mrs. Thornton’s lips thinned. “Classics are a luxury for idle men. Milton demands action, not philosophy.”

Margaret could not remain silent. “But surely the mind must engage with more than one pursuit, or it grows rigid.”

“A manufacturer has no need for such distractions,” Mrs. Thornton countered. “His purpose is clear: to stand firm among the leaders of trade—men who shape the world with their labor, not their musings.”

Margaret felt the sting of unintended condescension. “I meant no disrespect. Since coming to Milton, I’ve heard much to admire in your son.”

“From whom?” The question was swift, possessive.

Margaret hesitated, unwilling to be interrogated. “From Mr. Thornton himself,” Mr. Hale supplied.

The older woman’s gaze sharpened. “My son does not boast. Who else has spoken of him?”

Margaret relented. “Mr. Bell mentioned his character. But it was Mr. Thornton’s own restraint in speaking of his past that revealed more than words could.”

A grudging satisfaction softened Mrs. Thornton’s face. “You are frank, Miss Hale. Most young women would flatter to win favor.”

Margaret laughed, startled. “I assure you, I have no designs on your son’s heart.”

The older woman’s smile was thin. “We shall see.”

---

That evening, Mr. Thornton appeared at the Hales’ door, bearing a physician’s address. Margaret took it swiftly, relieved her mother had not overheard their concerns.

The conversation turned to the looming strike. Mr. Thornton’s expression hardened. “Let them strike. They’ll learn their error soon enough.”

“But why not explain the state of trade?” Margaret pressed. “If they understood—”

“Must a man justify how he spends his own capital?” he interrupted.

“It is a human right,” she said quietly.

He leaned closer, his voice low. “Do you deny me principles because they differ from yours?”

She drew back. “I speak only of stewardship. Waste is not wisdom.”

The tension between them was palpable. Margaret turned the subject, speaking of the divide between masters and workers—how each viewed the other with suspicion.

“You mistake me,” Thornton said stiffly. “Our interests are the same.”

“Yet they see yours as opposed to theirs.”

“Then they are misled.” His frustration was clear.

Margaret exhaled. “Perhaps in some future time, harmony will come.”

“A utopian dream,” he said, but his voice had softened.

As she left the room, Thornton watched her go, the unspoken words between them lingering like the factory smoke outside—inescapable, and yet, for now, unresolved.
