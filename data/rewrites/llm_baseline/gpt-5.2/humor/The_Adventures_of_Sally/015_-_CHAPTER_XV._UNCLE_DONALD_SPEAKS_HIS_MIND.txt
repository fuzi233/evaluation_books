There is a kind of man—stout in the soul if not always in the waistcoat—who treats a rebuff the way certain cooks treat a collapsed soufflé: as a sign to shut the oven door, lower the voice, and try again with more butter and better timing. Bruce Carmyle had that quality. Sally, at Monk’s Crofton, had turned down his offer of marriage with a decisiveness that ought to have chilled the marrow of any suitor built on delicacy and imagination; but Bruce was not overstocked with either. He had been bred in the comfortable creed that wants were made to be met, like bills and dinner.

So he put the refusal away in his mind the way a meticulous food historian shelves an inconvenient pamphlet in the wrong dialect: not destroyed, not accepted, merely filed for later correction. Whatever faint doubts he had ever had about Sally’s streak of free-spiritedness—her little habits of not doing what was expected, of looking as though life were a lark rather than a ledger—melted clean out of him in the heat of defeat. All he could summon was the brightness of her eyes, the jaunty lift of her chin, the gallant trimness of her; and those recollections snapped at him in the dark like the crackle of a kitchen fire when you’ve banked it too low and it refuses to die.

Quietly, methodically—respectable as a wolf with a timetable—he prepared to follow her to America.

He did his best to do it swiftly and secretly, but secrecy, in an English family, is a condiment that cannot be kept from scenting the whole house. News, in such tribes, travels faster than a tray in the hands of a well-trained parlourmaid and with less explanation than the passing of a folk-song from one generation to the next. By Bruce’s last night in London, the Family had decided on an emissary.

In due course, there entered his apartment in South Audley Street the Family’s chosen representative—the man the Family pointed to with pride, like a prized ham at a county fair: Uncle Donald, in the flesh.

It was a great deal of flesh. Two hundred and forty pounds of it, by any honest grocer’s scale. He lowered it into an armchair with the grave care of a cook settling a heavy casserole onto a precarious trivet, and the chair, which had not been consulted, creaked as if trying to translate protest into sound and finding the dominant lexicon inadequate.

Once at Monk’s Crofton, Sally had spoiled a whole morning for her brother Fillmore by indicating Uncle Donald as the exact image of what he would be when he grew up. Uncle Donald, cherishing from early schooldays the superstition that he had a weak heart, had abstained from every form of exercise for nearly fifty years. He combined this abstinence with one of the three heartiest appetites in the south-western postal division of London. His moustache—a walrus affair—heaved gently on his labouring breath, like seaweed lifting on a ground-swell. There had been stairs. The building had committed an act of modern industrial cruelty by possessing them.

Uncle Donald stared at the half-packed luggage as though it were an insult to the hearth.

“What’s this? What’s this?” he contrived to eject at last.

“I’m packing,” said Bruce shortly.

For the first time in his life, Bruce experienced that furtive sense of guilt which, according to Family legend, was Ginger’s habitual state in the presence of this large, mackerel-eyed man. It is hard to feel serenely adult while being stared at by an elder who seems carved out of authority and suet.

“You going away?” said Uncle Donald.

“Yes.”

“Where you going?”

“America.”

“When you going?”

“To-morrow morning.”

“Why you going?”

Set down on paper, these questions look as brisk as the cross-fire of music-hall comedians. In reality, Uncle Donald’s conversational technique extended the exchange over nearly three minutes, for after each reply and before each next question he paused to puff, sigh, inhale moustache, and reintroduce it to the atmosphere with the measured solemnity of a man tasting soup that has been served too hot and too late. The apartment filled with the sound of breathing that had the steady persistence of a factory fan.

Then he struck.

“You’re going after that girl,” said Uncle Donald, accusingly.

Bruce flushed. And it is interesting to note that, at this moment, the thought floated through his mind that Ginger’s behaviour at Bleke’s Coffee House on a certain notable occasion had not been quite so inexcusable as he had supposed. There are provocations which make even the most orderly man understand the sudden urge to fling a thing.

“Will you have a whisky and soda, Uncle Donald?” he said, shifting ground.

“Yes,” said Uncle Donald, obeying a vow he had made in the early eighties never to refuse an offer of this kind. “Gimme!”

Bruce mixed it. The ice clinked with a bright, civilised music—one of those small domestic sounds that do more for the nervous system than any sermon. The soda hissed as it met the spirit, that little snake-like whisper that says, for a moment, that the world is not entirely composed of reproach.

Uncle Donald drank. One might have hoped the restorative would have softened him, the way a properly reduced stock softens a tough cut. But Uncle Donald was not a man to be braised into kindness.

“Never thought you were a fool before,” he said severely.

Bruce’s proud spirit chafed. This sort of interview—commonplace to Ginger, apparently—was new to him. Hitherto his actions had been received with the respectful silence accorded to heirlooms and county opinions.

“I’m not a fool,” said Bruce.

“You are a fool. A damn fool,” continued Uncle Donald, specifying more exactly. “Don’t like the girl. Never did. Not a nice girl. Didn’t like her. Right from the first.”

“Need we discuss this?” said Bruce, dropping into that grand manner which he used when he wished to feel taller than circumstances.

Uncle Donald drew in a layer of moustache and blew it out again, as if testing the acoustics of indignation.

“Need we discuss it?” he repeated. “We’re going to discuss it! Whatch think I climbed all these blasted stairs for with my weak heart? Gimme another!”

Bruce gave him another. The ice chimed again, bright as a spoon against a glass bowl. Outside, somewhere down in the street, a motorcar grated past with the vulgar metallic snarl that has replaced, in modern cities, the older, kinder soundscape of hoofbeats and human voices. The car seemed to underline Uncle Donald’s presence: the world growing noisier, and the old men growing louder in self-defence.

“’S a bad business,” moaned Uncle Donald, after the second drink had gone down with the resigned efficiency of a man fulfilling a duty. “Shocking bad business. If your poor father were alive, whatch think he’d say to your tearing across the world after this girl? I’ll tell you what he’d say. He’d say… What kind of whisky’s this?”

“O’Rafferty Special.”

“New to me.” Uncle Donald smacked his lips with the careful attention of someone who has always treated eating and drinking as the one honest scholarship left to him. “Not bad. Quite good. Sound. Mellow. Wherej get it?”

“Bilby’s, in Oxford Street.”

“Must order some. Mellow.” He stared as if trying to remember the exact phrasing of paternal outrage. “He’d say… well, God knows what he’d say. Whatch doing it for? Whatch doing it for? That’s what I can’t see. None of us can see. Puzzles your uncle George. Baffles your aunt Geraldine. Nobody can understand it. Girl’s simply after your money. Anyone can see that.”

“Pardon me, Uncle Donald,” said Bruce stiffly, “but that is surely rather absurd. If that were the case, why should she have refused me at Monk’s Crofton?”

“Drawing you on,” said Uncle Donald promptly. “Luring you on. Well-known trick. Girl in 1881, when I was at Oxford, tried to lure me on. If I hadn’t had some sense and a weak heart…” He paused to recover breath and dignity. “Whatch know of this girl? Whatch know of her? That’s the point. Who is she? Wherej meet her?”

“I met her at Roville, in France.”

“Travelling with her family?”

“Travelling alone,” said Bruce reluctantly.

“Not even with that brother of hers? Bad!” said Uncle Donald. “Bad, bad!”

“American girls are accustomed to more independence than English girls,” said Bruce, clinging to reason as to a rail on a pitching ship.

“That young man,” said Uncle Donald, pursuing a separate train of thought with the doggedness of an elder who plants potatoes by the moon whether the young people laugh or not, “is going to be fat one of these days, if he doesn’t look out.” He seemed, for an instant, almost wistful—like a traditional agrarian patriarch regretting that the next generation will inherit the land but not the old names for the winds. Then suspicion surged back. “Travelling alone, was she? What did you do? Catch her eye on the pier?”

“Really, Uncle Donald!”

“Well, must have got to know her somehow.”

“I was introduced to her by Lancelot. She was a friend of his.”

“Lancelot!” exploded Uncle Donald, quivering all over like a jelly at the loathed name. The word itself seemed, in his mouth, to carry the entire unpleasant modern habit of calling things by fashionable labels instead of their proper county terms. “Well, that shows you what sort of a girl she is. Any girl that would be a friend of—” He broke off, as if even saying the rest might contaminate the air. “Unpack!”

“I beg your pardon?”

“Unpack! Mustn’t go on with this foolery. Out of the question. Find some girl make you a good wife. Your aunt Mary’s been meeting some people name of Bassington-Bassington, related Kent Bassington-Bassingtons… eldest daughter charming girl, just do for you.”

Outside the more old-fashioned type of fiction, nobody ever really grinds his teeth; but Bruce came nearer to it then than most men have ever dared. His expression darkened. The last trace of suavity left him, as if someone had skimmed it off with a spoon.

“I shall do nothing of the kind,” he said briefly. “I sail to-morrow.”

Uncle Donald had been defied once before by a nephew, but experience does not make defiance pleasant. He sat, suddenly, with an unhappy sense of having been served a dish that will not yield to knife or persuasion.

“Eh?” he said.

Bruce, having begun, evidently decided to do it thoroughly.

“I am over twenty-one,” he said. “I am financially independent. I shall do as I please.”

“But, consider!” pleaded Uncle Donald, and it was painful to hear how thin his words were, like gravy stretched too far. “Reflect!”

“I have reflected.”

“Your position in the county—”

“I’ve thought of that.”

“You could marry anyone you pleased.”

“I’m going to.”

“You are determined to go running off to God-knows-where after this Miss I-can’t-even-remember-her-dam-name?”

“Yes.”

Uncle Donald leaned forward a fraction, making the chair creak in a tone of pure foreboding.

“Have you considered,” he said portentously, “that you owe a duty to the Family.”

Bruce’s patience snapped. He dropped, with surprising speed, to depths of plain speech which would have made Ginger feel almost scholarly.

“Oh, damn the Family!” he cried.

There was a silence so painful that even the city noises outside seemed, for a second, to hesitate—only the armchair sighed with relief as Uncle Donald heaved himself out of it, glad to be spared further intimacy.

“After that,” said Uncle Donald, “I have nothing more to say.”

“Good!” said Bruce rudely, because when a man has once thrown propriety out of the window, he tends to follow it with the furniture.

“’Cept this.” Uncle Donald’s voice had the grim relish of a judge pronouncing sentence and of an elder preserving the last harsh word of a dying dialect. “If you come back married to that girl, I’ll cut you in Piccadilly. By George, I will!”

He moved to the door. Bruce looked down his nose without speaking, and in that small pause the two of them stood as representatives of two eras: the old Family lexicon, heavy and unquestioning; and the new, reckless vocabulary of personal appetite and chosen love. The air held the faint sting of soda and spirit, and underneath it, the warm human smell of moustache and exertion.

Uncle Donald’s fingers closed on the handle.

“What,” he asked, pausing as if struck by a sudden and tender need for accuracy, “did you say it was called?”

“What was what called?” said Bruce, wary.

“That whisky.”

“O’Rafferty Special.”

“And wherj get it?”

“Bilby’s, in Oxford Street.”

“I’ll make a note of it,” said Uncle Donald.

Then he went out, taking with him, like a departing kitchen tyrant, the bulk of his disapproval—and leaving behind, in the faint clink of melting ice, the oddly comforting proof that even in the fiercest family quarrel there is always room for a man to remember what was mellow and where it could be ordered.
