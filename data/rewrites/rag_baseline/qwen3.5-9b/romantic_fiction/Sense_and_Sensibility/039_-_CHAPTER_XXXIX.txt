Two months had elapsed since the Dashwood sisters arrived in town, yet Marianne's impatience to depart grew daily. She sighed for the crisp air, the absolute liberty, and the profound quiet of the countryside, convinced that if any location could restore her equilibrium, it must be Barton. Elinor shared this anxiety, though her resolve was tempered by a pragmatic awareness of the logistical hurdles inherent in such a lengthy journey—a constraint Marianne, caught in the thrall of romantic idealism, refused to acknowledge.

Elinor began to formulate a concrete plan, eventually broaching the subject with their hostess, Lady Middleton. Mrs. Jennings initially resisted with the fervent eloquence of genuine goodwill, reluctant to see her charges leave. However, a new proposal emerged: the Palmers intended to relocate to Cleveland for the Easter holidays. Charlotte Palmer extended a warm invitation for Mrs. Jennings and her guests to accompany them. While this arrangement technically delayed their departure further, it appealed to Elinor's sense of propriety. The invitation was delivered with such polished courtesy by Mr. Palmer himself—and bolstered by the marked refinement in his demeanor toward them following news of Marianne's distress—that Elinor accepted with alacrity.

When she relayed the decision to Marianne, the younger sister reacted with visceral agitation. "Cleveland!" she cried. "No, I cannot go there."

"You forget," Elinor interjected gently, "that its situation is not..."

"But it is in Somersetshire," Marianne interrupted, her voice trembling. "I cannot enter Somersetshire. No, Elinor, you cannot expect me to go there."

Elinor wisely refrained from debating the propriety of suppressing such raw emotions. Instead, she shifted the argument to practicalities, framing the move as the most efficient route to reunite them with their beloved mother. From Cleveland, mere miles from Bristol, the journey to Barton required only a single, albeit arduous, day. Their mother's servant could easily escort them, ensuring they would be home within three weeks. Given the sincerity of Marianne's love for her mother, Elinor reasoned, this tangible benefit would ultimately outweigh the girl's imagined terrors. Indeed, Mrs. Jennings, far from weary of her company, pressed them eagerly to join her return trip from Cleveland. Though Elinor remained steadfast in her design, her mother's consent was quickly secured, and arrangements were finalized. Marianne found solace in drafting a schedule of the remaining hours separating her from Barton.

"Ah! Colonel," Mrs. Jennings addressed him upon his first visit after the decision was made, "I do not know what you and I shall endure without the Miss Dashwoods. They are resolute on returning home from the Palmers. And how forlorn we shall be when I return! Lord, we shall sit and stare at one another as dull as two cats."

Perhaps Mrs. Jennings hoped this vivid sketch of impending ennui would prompt the Colonel to propose a solution to her loneliness. If so, her strategy bore fruit shortly thereafter. As Elinor moved to the window to measure a print for copying, the Colonel followed, engaging her in conversation with a look of distinct significance. The effect on the lady was palpable; though she maintained her honor by changing seats to avoid eavesdropping, positioning herself beside the pianoforte where Marianne played, she could not suppress her gaze from Elinor. The younger sister flushed, her agitation evident, her attention wholly consumed by the Colonel's words.

Later, during a pause in Marianne's practice, fragments of the Colonel's speech drifted to Mrs. Jennings' ears. He seemed to apologize for the inadequacies of his house. This confirmed her suspicions. Wondering at his modesty yet assuming it to be proper etiquette, she listened intently. When the Colonel finally paused, his voice calm but resigned, he declared, "I am afraid it cannot take place very soon."

Astonished and shocked by such an unloving sentiment, Mrs. Jennings nearly exclaimed, "Lord, what should hinder it?" but checked herself, settling for a silent gasp. "This is very strange! Surely he need not wait to be older." Yet, this apparent delay did not seem to wound the Colonel's fair companion. Upon concluding their discussion, Mrs. Jennings distinctly heard Elinor say, with a voice betraying her deep conviction, "I shall always think myself very much obliged to you."

Mrs. Jennings was delighted by this expression of gratitude, marveling that the Colonel could depart with such *sang-froid*, offering no reply to such a declaration. She had not anticipated her old friend to be such an indifferent suitor. In truth, however, the exchange had been entirely different.

"I have heard," the Colonel said with profound compassion, "of the injustice your friend Mr. Ferrars has suffered from his family. If I understand correctly, he has been entirely cast off by them for persevering in his engagement with a very deserving young woman. Have I been rightly informed? Is it so?"

Elinor confirmed it.

"The cruelty, the impolitic cruelty," he replied, his voice thick with feeling, "of dividing, or attempting to divide, two young people long attached to each other, is terrible. Mrs. Ferrars does not know what she may be doing—what she may drive her son to. I have seen Mr. Ferrars two or three times in Harley Street and am much pleased with him. He is not a young man with whom one can be intimately acquainted in a short time, but I have seen enough to wish him well for his own sake, and as a friend of yours, I wish it still more. I understand that he intends to take orders. Will you be so good as to tell him that the living of Delaford, now just vacant as I learned from today's post, is his, if he deems it worthy of acceptance? But perhaps, so unfortunately circumstanced as he is, it may be folly to appear to doubt; I only wish it were more valuable. It is a rectory, but a small one; the late incumbent did not make more than £200 per annum, and though it is certainly capable of improvement, I fear not to such an extent as to afford him a very comfortable income. Such as it is, however, my pleasure in presenting it to him will be very great. Pray assure him of it."

Elinor's astonishment at this commission was nearly as great as if the Colonel had actually proposed marriage. The preferment, which she had deemed hopeless for Edward only two days prior, was suddenly provided to facilitate his union. And *she*—of all people—was tasked with bestowing it. Her emotion was profound, though Mrs. Jennings had misinterpreted its source. Whatever lesser, less pure feelings might have contributed to her stirrings, her esteem for the Colonel's general benevolence and gratitude for his specific friendship were strongly felt and warmly expressed.

She thanked him with all her heart, praised Edward's principles and disposition with the accuracy they deserved, and promised to undertake the commission with pleasure if he truly wished to delegate such an agreeable office. Yet, she could not help thinking that no one could perform it better than he. It was an office from which, unwilling to inflict the pain of accepting an obligation from *her* upon Edward, she would gladly have been spared. But Colonel Brandon, driven by motives of equal delicacy, declined it as well. Still, his desire to have it conveyed through her hands was so evident that she made no further opposition.

Edward, she believed, was still in town, and fortunately, she had learned his address from Miss Steele. She could inform him of the opportunity later that day.

Once settled, the Colonel began discussing his own advantages in securing such a respectable neighbor. It was then, as Mrs. Jennings had suspected, that he mentioned with regret the smallness and indifference of the house. Elinor, dismissing the concern regarding its size, remarked, "The smallness of the house, I cannot imagine any inconvenience to them, for it will be in proportion to their family and income."

By this comment, the Colonel was surprised to discover that she viewed Mr. Ferrars's marriage as the certain consequence of the presentation. He did not suppose the Delaford living could support anyone of his station in marrying, stating so openly.

"This little rectory can do no more than make Mr. Ferrars comfortable as a bachelor; it cannot enable him to marry," he lamented. "I am sorry to say that my patronage ends with this; and my interest is hardly more extensive. If, however, by an unforeseen chance it should be in my power to serve him farther, I must think very differently of him from what I now do, if I am not as ready to be useful to him then as I sincerely wish I could be at present. What I am now doing indeed, seems nothing at all, since it can advance him so little towards what must be his principal, his only object of happiness. His marriage must still be a distant good; at least, I am afraid it cannot take place very soon."

Such was the sentence that, when misunderstood, so justly offended the delicate sensibilities of Mrs. Jennings. Yet, after recounting the true nature of the conversation between Colonel Brandon and Elinor while they stood at the window, the gratitude expressed by the latter upon their parting may perhaps appear, in general, neither less reasonably excited nor less properly worded than if it had arisen directly from an offer of marriage.
