To reconstruct the architecture of Harry Feversham's redemption, one must first understand the absolute poverty of his universe prior to the night of August 30. For a month, the uneventful countryside of Donegal had been consumed by a collective hysteria regarding his disappearance, a rumor mill grinding relentlessly against the gears of reality. In the climbing streets and at the gentry's dinner tables, speculation reigned supreme, fueled by the grotesque image of a young man in dress clothes, his face the pallid mask of a suicide, hurrying along the causeway near Lennon Bridge. The townsfolk, driven by a hunger for narrative closure, proposed absurdities—a drag-net to fish for a ghost, a refusal of salmon by Mr. Dennis Rafferty on the grounds of non-cannibalism—all while missing the profound tragedy unfolding elsewhere. Only two souls bore witness to the truth: Ethne and Dermod. Yet, even they maintained a stoic facade, their heads held higher, their tempers subtly altered, pretending that no catastrophe had shattered the domestic order. Gossip, blind and deaf, held the field entirely.

But Harry Feversham was not lost in the bog; he was present in London, a fact Lieutenant Sutch confirmed on the very night of the 30th. The city itself trembled under the weight of war rumors, the streets choked with messengers shouting conflicting tidings of victory and slaughter from the desert east of Ismailia. The air buzzed with the phantom charge of General Drury-Lowe's cavalry, rolling up Arabi's flank, while whispers of English generals falling and regiments being cut to ribbons created a palpable tension. By eleven o'clock, a silent, expectant crowd gathered beneath the gas-lamps of Pall Mall, their pale faces turned upward toward the illuminated blinds of the War Office, swaying in a unified rhythm of anxiety. It was here, on the outskirts near the Junior Carlton Club, that Sutch, his wounded leg a constant reminder of the conflict, felt a touch upon his arm.

Harry Feversham stood beside him, a figure carved from marble and fever. His face worked with a terrible intensity, his eyes burning with the bright, delirious light of a man teetering on the brink of madness. Sutch, initially unsure whether he recognized the man before him, felt the gravity of the encounter settle over him.

"I might have been out there in Egypt tonight," Harry whispered, his voice quick and troubled, cutting through the ambient hum of the club. "Think of it! I might have been sitting by a campfire in the desert, debating strategy with Jack Durrance; or dead. What would it have mattered? I might have been in Egypt tonight!"

Feversham's erratic presence and his wandering tongue signaled to Sutch that his fortunes had spiraled into ruin. Questions surged in Sutch's mind, demanding answers, yet he suppressed them all. Instead, he took Harry's arm and pulled him away from the throng, seeking refuge in the shadows.

"I saw you in the crowd," Harry continued, his voice trembling slightly. "I sought you out because... do you recall, a long time ago, you gave me your card? I have preserved it, fearing I might need it. You said that if one was in trouble, speaking might help."

Sutch halted his companion. "We will go inside. There is a quiet corner in the upper smoking-room." As Harry looked up, he realized they were standing before the steps of the Army and Navy Club.

"Good God, not there!" Harry cried, his voice dropping to a sharp, urgent whisper. He moved swiftly into the roadway, where the darkness shielded his face from scrutiny. Sutch limped after him, respecting the boundary. "Not tonight. It is too late. Tomorrow, if you wish, in some secluded place, after nightfall. I do not venture out in the daylight."

Once again, Sutch withheld his inquiries. "I know a quiet restaurant," he offered. "If we dine there at nine, we shall encounter no acquaintances. I will meet you just before nine tomorrow night at the corner of Swallow Street."

Accordingly, they met the following evening in the Criterion grill-room, seated at a table tucked into the corner. As Harry entered, his eyes scanned the room with practiced caution.

"I frequent this establishment often when in town," Sutch remarked, gesturing to the environment. "Listen!" The mechanical thrumming of the electric light engines vibrated through the floorboards, a rhythmic pulse that could be felt in the bones.

"It reminds me of a ship," Sutch smiled, a gesture of camaraderie masking his concern. "I can almost imagine myself back in the gun-room. Let us eat. Then you shall tell me your story."

"You have heard nothing of it?" Harry asked, suspicion coloring his tone.

"Not a word," Sutch replied. The relief washing over Harry was visible; he had feared universal condemnation, imagining contempt etched onto every passing face.

Yet, Sutch felt a deeper unease than the previous night. Under the full illumination of the grill-room, Harry appeared transformed: gaunt and haggard, his skin stretched tight over bone, black hollows framing eyes that darted nervously. His breathing was ragged, his movements jerky and feverish, his nerves strung to the snapping point. Between courses, he attempted to begin his tale, but Sutch waited patiently until the cloth was removed.

"Now," Sutch said, extending his cigar-case. "Take your time, Harry."

With a solemnity that bordered on the theatrical, Feversham recounted the entire truth, neither exaggerating nor omitting a detail. He forced himself into a slow, deliberate cadence, so detached that Sutch momentarily believed he was hearing the story of a stranger, a mere chronicle told to pass the hours. The narrative spanned from the Crimean night at Broad Place to the disastrous ball at Lennon House.

"I returned across Lough Swilly early that morning," Harry concluded, his voice steady despite the turmoil within. "I traveled immediately to London. Since then, I have confined myself to my rooms, listening to the bugles calling in the barrack-yard beneath my windows. At night, I prowled the streets or lay in bed, waiting for the Westminster clock to strike each new quarter. Even on foggy nights, I can hear the steam-sirens on the river. Do you know when the ducks begin to quack in St. James's Park?" He laughed, a brittle, nervous sound. "Precisely at two o'clock to the minute."

Sutch listened without interruption, but midway through the narrative, his demeanor shifted profoundly. Until the moment Harry described concealing the telegram, Sutch had sat with his arms resting on the table, his gaze fixed intently on his friend. From that point onward, he raised a hand to his forehead, shielding his face, creating a barrier between himself and the revelation. Feversham perceived the change instantly; he understood that Sutch struggled to suppress the scorn rising in his throat, unable to trust the muscles of his own face. Yet, Harry mitigated nothing. He continued steadily, truthfully, until the final word was spoken.

When Sutch finally lowered his hand, silence hung heavy in the room. When he spoke, his words struck Harry with the force of a physical blow. There was no contempt in his tone, only a voice shaken by a profound, crushing contrition.

"I am much to blame," Sutch said, his voice thick with emotion. "I should have spoken that night at Broad Place, and I held my tongue. I shall hardly forgive myself." The knowledge that it was Muriel Graham's son who had brought ruin upon himself weighed heavily on the lieutenant's conscience. He felt he had failed a sacred duty, an obligation imposed upon himself yet undeniably real. "You see, I understood," he continued, his remorse deepening. "Your father, I am afraid, never would."

"He never will," Harry interrupted sharply.

"No," Sutch agreed softly. "Your mother, had she lived, would have seen clearly; but few women, I believe, save your mother. Brute courage! Women deify it. That girl, for instance..."

"You must not blame her," Harry interjected. "I was defrauding her into marriage."

Sutch withdrew his hand from his forehead abruptly. "Suppose you had never met her. Would you still have submitted your papers?"

"I think not," Harry answered slowly, striving for fairness. "Disgracing my name and those dead men in the hall—I would have risked it. But I could not risk disgracing her."

Sutch slammed his fist despairingly onto the table. "If only I had spoken at Broad Place! Harry, why didn't you let me speak? I might have spared you countless years of torture. Good heavens! What a childhood you must have endured, carrying that fear alone. It makes me shiver to think of it. I might even have saved you from this final catastrophe. For I understood. I understood."

Sutch penetrated the dark recesses of Harry Feversham's mind more clearly than Harry himself did. Because he saw so clearly, he felt no hatred. The long years of isolation at Broad Place, living in the shadow of an incomprehensible father and the relentless, silent dead on the walls, had inflicted the wound. There had been no confidant. The paralyzing fear of cowardice had eroded his heart incessantly. He had carried it everywhere, into his bed, into his dreams, making it his perpetual, menacing companion.
