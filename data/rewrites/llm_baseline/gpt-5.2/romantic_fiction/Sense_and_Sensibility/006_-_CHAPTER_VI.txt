Their first miles away from Norland went by in a dull, aching silence that no one could quite break without seeming to betray the others. The wheels worried the road with a steady, wearisome rasp; the harness rang now and then with a metallic complaint; and whenever they passed a cart on the lane, its iron-bound rumble seemed an affront—too loud for grief, too indifferent to it. Yet even that sound, harsh as it was, marked the world continuing in its accustomed measure, and by degrees the cadence of travel—hoofbeats, creaking leather, the soft hiss of wind through hedgerow—began to draw their thoughts outward.

It was early September, when the air holds a last generosity: warm enough to carry scent, cool enough to sharpen it. As the carriage turned toward Barton Valley, the land opened into a pleasant fertility—pasture stitched with hedges, trees gathered in thoughtful clumps, and a fullness in the fields that promised winter stores. The valley had its own voice. Beyond the road’s small complaints, there were birds calling from the wooded rises, the susurration of grasses on the downs, and, nearer the cottages, the domestic music of rural life: a distant pail struck against stone, a gate lifted and dropped, a dog’s single bark answered by another farther off. The quiet was not emptiness, but a kind of care—an acoustic shelter after the louder, more anxious world they had left.

After winding along the green for more than a mile, they came to their own house.

Barton Cottage did not pretend to be what its name, in the mouths of poets, might promise. A small green court lay in front like a modest apron, and a neat wicket admitted them without ceremony. The building itself was regular; the roof was tiled; the walls were honest, not dressed with climbing honeysuckle; the shutters, disobedient to the fashionable idea of rural prettiness, had not even the mercy of green paint. It was comfortable, compact—serviceable in every line, but lacking the deliberate picturesque. A narrow passage ran straight through, so that from the first step inside one could already see the garden beyond, as if the house insisted on being passed through rather than admired.

On either side of the entrance were two sitting rooms, each about sixteen feet square; beyond them, the offices and stairs; above, four bedrooms and two garrets. It had not been built many years and stood in good repair. Compared with Norland, it was poor and small indeed; and recollection struck so quickly, so faithfully, that tears came as they crossed the threshold.

But sorrow cannot keep its face when it is met at once by other people’s joy. The servants, delighted at their arrival, moved about them with an eagerness that was almost festive. There was the quick thump of a trunk set down, the brisk opening and shutting of doors, voices calling to one another in the passage; and in that familiar bustle—so like the first day of any household settling—each Dashwood, for the sake of the others, found the resolve to appear contented. Tears were wiped away as one wipes a hand after kitchen work: not because the hand is clean, but because there is something else that must be done.

The fine weather did them an unspoken service. Because they first saw Barton under sun, the place offered itself kindly; and kindness received at the outset is remembered longer than it deserves. Behind the house high hills rose immediately, and not far on either side—some open downs, others cultivated and wooded. The village of Barton sat chiefly upon one of these hills, a scatter of roofs and pale walls that made a pleasant view from the windows. In front, the prospect widened: it commanded the whole valley and reached into the country beyond. The enclosing hills terminated the valley in that direction, but under another name and in another course it branched out again between two of the steepest of them, like a story that refuses to end where you thought it would.

Mrs. Dashwood, who had always loved a house the way some women love a recipe—less for what it is than for what it may become by additions, alterations, and a little cheerful daring—was, upon the whole, well satisfied with its size and furniture. Her former style of living made many improvements necessary, but to improve was a delight to her; and she had, at that moment, ready money enough to supply what was wanted of greater elegance.

“As for the house itself, to be sure,” said she, as they went from room to room, measuring with her eyes what might be done, “it is too small for our family; but we will make ourselves tolerably comfortable for the present, as it is too late in the year for improvements. Perhaps in the spring, if I have plenty of money, as I dare say I shall, we may think about building. These parlors are both too small for such parties of our friends as I hope to see often collected here; and I have some thoughts of throwing the passage into one of them, with perhaps a part of the other, and so leave the remainder of that other for an entrance; this, with a new drawing-room which may be easily added, and a bedchamber and garret above, will make it a very snug little cottage. I could wish the stairs were handsome. But one must not expect every thing; though I suppose it would be no difficult matter to widen them. I shall see how much I am before-hand with the world in the spring, and we will plan our improvements accordingly.”

There was, in such projects, the energy of a cook planning a feast before she has counted the flour. It was affectionate, it was hopeful, and it was—considering an income of five hundred a year and a woman who had never saved in her life—nearly heroic. Yet until those alterations could be made from the supposed savings of that income, they were wise enough to be content with the house as it stood; and all three were busy with the small, intimate labours by which a strange roof becomes one’s own.

If the cottage had lacked outward romance, the inside soon acquired the comforts of habit. Marianne’s pianoforte was unpacked and properly placed, so that the room had at once a promise of music; Elinor’s drawings were affixed to the walls of their sitting room, lending the bare spaces an air of remembered scenes and patient observation. There were books to lay within reach, work-baskets to establish near the light, linen to be counted and stowed. Even the kitchen—though not described in plans as Mrs. Dashwood described parlours—began to speak in its own familiar way: the clink of a pot-lid, the rush of water into a basin, the first scents of a simple breakfast clearing into the sweeter, green breath of the garden beyond. In every house, the true settling is not in the arrangement of furniture but in the return of ordinary appetites.

They were engaged in these occupations when, soon after breakfast the next day, their landlord entered to welcome them to Barton and offer them every accommodation from his own house and garden in which theirs might be deficient.

Sir John Middleton was a good-looking man of about forty. He had formerly visited at Stanhill, but too long ago for his young cousins to remember him. His countenance was thoroughly good-humoured; his manners were as friendly as the style of his letter. Their arrival seemed to afford him real satisfaction, and their comfort to be an object of real solicitude. He spoke much of his earnest desire that they should live with his family on the most sociable terms, and he pressed them so cordially to dine at Barton Park every day till they were better settled at home, that though his entreaties were carried to a point of perseverance beyond civility, they could not be offended. He was a man whose kindness made its own noise—cheerful, insistent, filling whatever silence might otherwise have lingered in the rooms.

Nor was his generosity content to remain in talk. Within an hour after he left them, a large basket arrived from the park, full of garden stuff and fruit—things still cool with earth and morning shade: crisp leaves that released their sharp, green bitterness when handled; beans snapped cleanly; carrots carrying the damp sweetness of their beds; apples and pears with skins that held the orchard’s sun. Before the end of the day, a present of game followed—feathers, rich scent, the unmistakable promise of suppers that might, for an hour, make them forget to be unhappy. Sir John insisted, moreover, on conveying all their letters to and from the post, and would not be denied the satisfaction of sending them his newspaper every day. If modern life, in other places, grows noisy with machines and hurried commerce, here was an older exchange: food carried by hand, news passed along by neighbourly insistence, and generosity expressed not in grand declarations but in what could be cooked, read, and shared.

Lady Middleton, he told them, had sent a very civil message, intending to wait on Mrs. Dashwood as soon as she could be assured that her visit would be no inconvenience. Mrs. Dashwood answered with an invitation equally polite; and the next day her ladyship was introduced to them.

They were, of course, anxious to see the person on whom so much of their comfort at Barton must depend; and the elegance of Lady Middleton’s appearance encouraged that wish. She was not more than six or seven and twenty; her face was handsome, her figure tall and striking, her address graceful. Her manners had all the elegance which her husband’s wanted. Yet they would have been improved by some share of his frankness and warmth. Her visit lasted long enough to lessen the first admiration by showing that, though perfectly well-bred, she was reserved, cold, and had nothing to say beyond the most common-place inquiry or remark.

Conversation, however, was not greatly wanted; Sir John was very chatty, and Lady Middleton had taken the wise precaution of bringing with her their eldest child, a fine little boy of about six years old. By that means there was always one subject to return to in moments of extremity. The ladies had only to enquire his name and age, admire his beauty, and ask him questions which his mother answered for him, while he hung about her and held down his head. Lady Middleton expressed surprise at his shyness before company, since he could make noise enough at home—an observation delivered with the same composed tone as if she were remarking upon weather, though it conjured at once the sounds of another household: running feet, shrill demands, laughter and cries echoing in corridors. It seemed, in that instant, that the child belonged less to the room than to the invisible soundscape of his own nursery, and that, like any small creature taken from its accustomed grove, he had become silent by mere change of air.

On every formal visit, a child ought to be of the party, by way of provision for discourse. In the present case, it took up ten minutes to determine whether the boy was most like his father or mother, and in what particular he resembled either; for, of course, every body differed, and every body was astonished at the opinion of the others. The debate, conducted with the mild tyranny of drawing-room politeness, was one of those social languages that everyone speaks and no one truly means—phrases that travel easily because they carry so little of the speaker’s private world. Whatever Marianne felt, whatever Elinor observed, whatever Mrs. Dashwood hoped, had no ready dialect in which to be safely offered to a new acquaintance; but a child’s resemblance could be discussed without risk, as if it were a dish everyone might taste and pronounce upon.

An opportunity was soon to be given them of debating the rest of the children; for Sir John would not leave the house without securing their promise of dining at the park the next day.
