Every reader who has followed Harry Warrington’s adventures already knows the full, innocent truth of his brief, awkward entanglement with the luckless little dancer Cattarina—but not a single one of the chattering old ladies clustered around the tea tables of Tunbridge Wells believed it. They were all fully convinced the young Virginian was every bit as wild, dissipated, and reckless as the most hard-living young nobleman of the highest rank, and none was more certain of his guilt than his own great-aunt, Madame de Bernstein.

The wily old baroness had long ago lived life by the Roman rule of “do as the Romans do,” and it suited her perfectly to imagine her handsome young nephew cut from the same libertine cloth as the rest of high society. She ate up every scrap of gossip about his supposed Lovelace-esque exploits with greedy, complacent delight. Harry had already won the whole Wells over with his impromptu musical evenings and his courteous, gallant attentions to all the ladies far more respectable than the forgotten Cattarina, so the old woman soon set aside all her old worries about his silly, fledgling Castlewood love affair, and slackened her tight vigilance over her niece, Lady Maria.

Selfishness settles easy into the bones of the elderly: the Baroness had her card games, her elaborate dinner parties, and the constant throbbing of her rheumatism to occupy her, and those once-lively suspicions of a romance between Maria and Harry dozed off into lazy, careless inattention. Perhaps she assumed the danger had long passed, perhaps she simply no longer cared if it lingered, or perhaps the sharp, sly Lady Maria—far older than most girls who learned the art of dissembling—had completely cajoled, soothed, and misled the aging “dragon” placed in charge of her. Dragons grow soft in their old age, you know: their fangs fall out, their scales thin to the point any halfway competent knight can slip a lance through, their claws can do no more than pat feebly against skin, and their venom stings far less than it once destroys. I have met any number of these former society destroyers of men and women in my time, and watched them drag their languid tails back to their caverns at dusk, far too feeble to ruin a reputation these days, let alone gobble up a fresh victim for breakfast as they did in their fierce, venomous youth.

If the quiet, pious Oakhurst family could not bring themselves to disbelieve the false reports of Harry’s misdeeds, why would Madame Bernstein—who had seen far more of the world’s evil in her one lifetime than all of the Oakhurst clan put together—be any more credulous than they? Every last old lady of her social circle gobbled down every story about Harry’s supposed dissipation, and was ready to swallow a dozen more the second they were whispered in the pump room. When little Cattarina returned to London, everyone said it was because the heartless Virginian had abandoned her for half a dozen different local ladies of rank and repute, all names passed from lip to lip behind closed fans. The wells of a century ago were melting pots: fashionables, respected matrons, women of half-repute, virtuous women, women of no virtue at all all danced to the same fiddles, drank from the same glasses, and chased health, amusement, or pleasure side by side, just as modern crowds do at Homburg or Baden today.

This flattering, wild reputation as a rakish conqueror of women eased the Baroness’s mind completely, chasing away all her old fears that Harry was still infatuated with Lady Maria. So unconcerned was she that when the family chaplain arrived to offer to escort her back to Castlewood, she declined outright, choosing instead to keep Maria close by her side, to chatter on to her about Harry’s wild, foolish extravagances, to smile and murmur that boys will be boys, and to confide that she already had her eye on several perfectly suitable young heiresses of just the right age and fortune to make Harry a proper wife. Poor Maria had to swallow every last one of these little pleasantries as best she could, grinding her teeth under her smile.

It was around this time that a famous French duke and marquis roamed across Europe and North America, leaving behind him a very unseemly volume of memoirs I would urge all gentle readers never to seek out. The man had played Don Juan across half the courts of the continent, listing the names of dozens of noble court beauties who he claimed had fallen to his charms, a fact that must make for extremely awkward reading for the great-grandchildren of those very women today, seeing their ancestresses’ frailties printed plain for all the world to gawk at. On his tour of North America, the great heartbreaker set about his usual siege—and failed to win a single conquest. For all the Europeans who raged about the supposed rudeness and barbarism of colonial manners, the plain truth is that American gentlemen are for the most part modest in speech, and their women far more virtuous than the rakehells of European high society had ever prepared to find.

Harry Warrington had brought all that quiet colonial modesty over to England with him. He heard all the free, coarse talk of the pleasure-seeking men around him, but mostly sat silent through their chatter; he never made a crude double entendre in front of a lady, never bragged of a single romantic conquest, and grew shy and awkward whenever other men recounted theirs. Parson Sampson had noticed that modesty back at Castlewood, when Harry grew visibly uncomfortable while his roguish cousin Will told his lewd, crude stories, and the Earl of Castlewood had curtly snapped at his brother to save his jokes for the Kensington school ushers, so they would not give unnecessary offence to their kinsman. That was the source of the chaplain’s old line *Reverentia pueris*—“respect for the boy”—on Harry’s very first night dining at the public ordinary. Sampson was no saint, he had led a rough, unruly life of his own, but he had enough decency not to needlessly hurt an innocent young man with his cynicism.

He was genuinely fond of Harry, too, after the boy had gifted him that fine thoroughbred horse. “You see, sir,” he told Harry once, “I am a man of the world, and I cannot afford to be any more particular than my neighbours. *Video meliora, deteriora sequor*—I see the better path, but follow the worse, as we used to say at college. I have a little sister Patty off at boarding school not far from here, and if I can manage to watch my tongue around her and expect others to do the same, surely I can extend that same courtesy to you.”

The chaplain sang Harry’s praises nonstop to Madame Bernstein, who was happy to listen: she was as fond of the boy as she was capable of being fond of anything, charmed by his good looks, his fearless bearing, the way a ready blush would rise to his cheek, his deep rich voice, and the amusing mix of shrewdness and simplicity that would have tired her to death if he had been witty or learned or overly clever. “We must find him a good wife, chaplain,” she said to Sampson. “I have two or three young ladies in mind who will suit him perfectly. We shall set him up here in England—he will never bear to go back to his so-called savages in the colonies, or his little Methodist mother, ever again.”

Sampson had his own very personal stake in that plan, of course. He had spoken to the Earl of Castlewood before they left, who had promised him a fine, comfortable living and a generous stipend if he could nudge Harry and Lady Maria into the match the Earl wanted. Sampson had every intention of earning that reward, and set about watching for the perfect moment to turn his influence to use.

The chaplain made for delightful company: full of high spirits, good humour, a hearty appetite for fun, and no pretensions to false sanctimony. He never offended Harry with unnecessary vulgarity, choosing only to introduce the boy to the milder, harmless joys of high society—cockfights, shooting matches, card games, gallops across the common, all the while keeping his conversation decent, far more focused on amusing the boy than leading him astray. Sampson had spent his whole life as a professional “toad-eater,” ever since he first entered university as a poor servitor boy scheming his way up in the world, and he was proud to be seen at Harry’s side as his humble retainer, giving orders to his tradesmen, acting as his unofficial aide-de-camp, and gossiping about his young Virginian patron all across the Wells.

“What a reputation they have saddled you with here, sir!” Sampson laughed as he burst back into Harry’s rooms one afternoon fresh from the coffee house. “The Duc de Richelieu himself never won half so many conquests!”

“Who is this Richelieu you speak of? I never fought at Minorca in my life,” blurted honest Harry, who had never heard of the French duke’s famous military and romantic victories.

Sampson explained the joke, then went on to tell Harry the whole town was gossiping that the pretty newly arrived widow Patcham was desperate for him, that Mrs. Hooper had run off with him so openly her alderman husband had dragged her straight back to London to get her away from him.

Harry’s face flushed bright scarlet with rage. “Who dares say such things? I will meet any man who spreads those lies and confound the villain!” he thundered, pacing back and forth across the carpet.

“I would not dare show him to you,” Sampson cackled. “He would come off far the worse for it.”

“It is a shameful thing to talk away the good name of any man or woman as people do here,” Harry fumed. “Back in Virginia a man’s ears would not be safe for half such slander. Only a few months before I left home, three brothers shot a man dead for speaking ill of their sister.”

“Served the rascal right!” Sampson nodded.

“They already spread that old calumny about me and that poor little French dancing-girl Cattarina,” Harry went on. “Wicked, isn’t it? The very same lies they spread about Lord March, too. What if these stories got back to my mother? It would break her heart, I swear it. What do these people think I am? A seducer? They might as well call me a horse thief or a housebreaker. I vow I will cut the ears off any man I catch saying such things!”

“I have read that the Grand Seignior of Turkey sometimes has bushels of ears sent in to his court,” Sampson laughed. “If you punished every person here who has spread slander against you or anyone else, you would fill whole baskets full of them!”

“And so I will, too, the moment I lay eyes on them!” Harry swore.

They headed off to their usual dining spot shortly after that. That was an age when heavy drinking was far more common than it is in our more polished modern day, and Harry had picked up a taste for claret and toasts from the British army officers he had befriended in the colonies before he came over, who considered it a point of honour never to refuse a glass or a cheer. They drank through bottle after bottle, naming a favourite lady with each toast, the claret warming their blood, loosening their tongues, bringing them to that perfect sweet spot every wine lover knows: the moment wit glows, generosity swells, and every secret you have ever held back fights to come spilling out of your mouth before you pour that one extra glass that spoils the whole state of grace, leaving you with a throbbing head the next morning and no memory of all the grand, brave promises you made.

As the last of the other diners filtered out, Sampson leaned across the table, his eyes bright. “I am glad to know the truth of your conduct with that Cattarina girl, Mr. Warrington, truly glad from my soul. You have shown you can rise above calumny and resist temptation. Ah, my dear sir, not all men are so fortunate. What excellent wine this is! Your toast, if you please?”

“I give you Miss Fanny Mountain, of Virginia,” Harry said, filling his bumper to the brim, his thoughts flying thousands of miles back to his childhood home.

“One of your American conquests, I assume?” Sampson teased.

“Nay, she is only ten years old,” Harry said. “I have never made any conquests at all in Virginia, Sampson.”

“True gentlemen never kiss and tell, sir,” Sampson grinned.

“I neither kiss nor tell. It is not the custom of our country to ruin young girls or keep the company of low women,” Harry said proudly, his face glowing. “We Virginian gentlemen honour women, and never wish to bring them to shame. The little lady I just named has lived in our family since she was an infant, and I would shoot any man who did her the smallest wrong, by Heaven I would!”

“Your sentiments do you nothing but honour! Let me shake your hand!” Sampson cried, squeezing Harry’s palm hard. “This is honest friendship you feel, not a poor retainer fawning on his rich patron! With wine this good, all men are equal, all men are rich while it lasts! Tom Sampson is just as wealthy with his full bottle as you are with all the thousands of acres of your Virginian estate!”

“Then let us have another bottle of riches,” Harry laughed, calling over the waiter for another case of yellow-seal claret.

“Capital! Capital! You speak French beautifully, Mr. Harry!” Sampson cheered.

“I speak it well enough for Monsieur Barbeau the waiter to understand what I want,” Harry said.

“You do everything well, that is why everyone here is so convinced you have won the hearts of half the ladies in the Wells,” Sampson said.

“There you go again with the ladies!” Harry groaned. “I hate these stupid lies, why must everyone blacken a gentleman’s name for no reason?”

“Well, at the very least, there is one lady whose preference for you I saw plain as day,” Sampson said, leaning in.

“Who do you mean?” Harry asked, flushing so red his ears burned.

“I name no names. A poor chaplain has no business prying into his betters’ private affairs, or their secret feelings,” Sampson demurred.

“Feelings? What feelings, Sampson?” the boy pressed.

“I saw a certain respected, lovely lady at Castlewood show you every mark of preference. I saw a certain distinguished young gentleman show a very strong liking for her in return. I may well have been mistaken, and I beg your pardon if I overstep,” Sampson said.

“Oh, Sampson, Sampson!” Harry burst out, his voice cracking. “I have been miserable. I have been dying for someone to confide in, someone to ask for advice. You do know then… there has been something going on, something between me and… and some other lady?”

“I have watched it for a whole month now,” Sampson nodded.

“Confound you, sir—have you been spying on me?” Harry snapped, hot with anger.

“Spying? You two made no effort to hide it! Her ladyship was no better at dissembling than you were. You were always together: in the shrubberies, on the village walks, in the long galleries of the house. Plenty of other eyes besides mine saw you,” Sampson said.

“Gracious powers! What did you see, Sampson?” the lad cried.

“Nay, sir, I never kiss and tell,” the chaplain winked.

Harry’s face burned bright red. “Oh, Sampson,” he stammered. “Can I… can I trust you with all of it?”

“My dearest, most generous young friend!” Sampson exclaimed, squeezing Harry’s hand so tight his knuckles went white, rolling his eyes up toward the ceiling with unfeigned, drunken fervour. “You know I would shed my very heart’s blood for you!”
