The confectioner’s shop at the corner of two steep Camden Town streets burned like a firework in the fading blue dusk, its windows alive with fractured light. Gilt mirrors multiplied the glow, casting it over cakes wrapped in metallic reds and golds—temptations irresistible to the neighborhood children who pressed their noses to the glass. But among them stood a man well past childhood, a tall, broad-shouldered figure with red hair and an absent air, studying the display with an interest that went beyond sugar.

John Turnbull Angus carried a portfolio of sketches under his arm, remnants of a career he’d cobbled together after his uncle, an admiral, disowned him for lecturing against Socialism. Now he sold his drawings to publishers when he could, though success was sporadic.

At last, he pushed inside, nodding to the dark-eyed girl behind the counter before moving to the back room—a cramped café where he took his usual seat. When she followed to take his order, he delivered it with precision:

“One halfpenny bun and a small black coffee.” Then, without pause: “Also, I want you to marry me.”

The girl stiffened. “I don’t allow jokes like that.”

Angus looked up, his gray eyes unexpectedly solemn. “It’s as serious as the bun. Just as expensive, just as indigestible.”

She studied him, her sharp gaze weighing his sincerity. Then, with the faintest shadow of a smile, she sat.

“You’re cruel to those buns,” Angus mused. “They might’ve grown into penny buns someday. I’ll renounce such brutality once we’re married.”

She exhaled sharply, rising to pace before turning back—only to freeze. Angus had arranged an absurd feast on the table: sandwiches, sweets, decanters of dubious pastry-cook sherry, and at the center, the enormous wedding cake from the shop window.

“What in God’s name are you doing?”

“Duty, my dear Laura.”

“Stop. What is all this?”

“A ceremonial meal, Miss Hope.”

She pointed at the cake. “And that?”

“The wedding cake, Mrs. Angus.”

With a noise of exasperation, she marched it back to the window, then returned, elbows on the table, fixing him with a look that was half-amused, half-furious.

“You don’t give me time to think.”

“That’s my Christian humility.”

Her smile faded slightly. “Before this goes further, I need to tell you something.”

“Delighted. You might as well explain me to myself while you’re at it.”

“Listen,” she said firmly. “There’s something haunting me—not my fault, not even my business, but it’s become a nightmare.”

Angus grew serious. “Then I suggest you bring back the cake.”

“Not yet.” Laura took a breath. “My father owned the Red Fish inn in Ludbury. I worked the bar. The customers were mostly idle men—the kind with just enough money to loiter and bet on horses. Two of them were worse than the rest. Isidore Smythe, a clever little man who played with matches and made toys from rubbish, and James Welkin, tall and quiet, with a squint so severe you couldn’t tell where he was looking.”

She hesitated. “Both proposed to me the same week. I couldn’t bear to refuse them for their looks, so I lied—said I’d only marry a man who’d made his own fortune. Two days later, they both vanished. Smythe wrote eventually; he’d joined a traveling show, then invented some mechanical servants—‘Smythe’s Silent Service.’ He’s rich now. But Welkin? Not a word. And yet—”

Her voice dropped. “I hear him. I feel him. After Smythe’s second letter arrived, Welkin’s voice said, clear as day, ‘He shan’t have you.’”

Angus frowned. “If you were mad, you’d think yourself sane. Still, this invisible rival does sound—”

A shriek of brakes cut him off. A motor-car screeched to a halt outside, and a small, impeccably dressed man stormed in—Isidore Smythe himself, his sharp eyes darting between them.

“Has she seen the window?” he demanded.

Angus turned. Pasted across the glass was a strip of paper, its red ink still wet:

Angus left to fetch his friend Flambeau, the detective, sealing the building with promises and bribes to the porter, the commissionaire, even a wandering policeman.

At Flambeau’s office, amidst sabres and Persian cats, a small priest observed the first snowflakes falling.

“It begins,” said Father Brown.

And so did the hunt for the invisible man.
