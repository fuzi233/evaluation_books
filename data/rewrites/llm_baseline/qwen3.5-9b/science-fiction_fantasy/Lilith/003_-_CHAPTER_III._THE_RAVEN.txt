I spun around, scanning the space behind me. Everything was a blur of indistinct shapes, a haze where fog merged with fields and clouds dissolved into mountainsides. There was only one certainty: I recognized nothing. Assuming I was trapped in a visual trickery that touch might unravel, I thrust my arms forward, groping blindly. I walked this way and that, hoping that even if my sight failed me, my hands might strike against solid matter. But my search yielded nothing but emptiness.

Instinct drove me toward the only living presence nearby: the raven. It stood a short distance away, watching me with an expression that balanced respect and mockery. Suddenly, the sheer absurdity of asking advice from a bird hit me. I turned back, drowning in confusion and fear. Had I stumbled into a realm where the physical and spiritual laws of our world had collapsed? Could a man vanish from the ordered universe at any moment, becoming prey to chaos? Yet, here I was—the raven was real, the ground beneath my boots was firm, and the rustle of low-lying plants told me the wind still blew.

"How *did* I get here?" I asked—aloud, surely, for the reply came instantly.

"You came through the door," a voice replied. It was odd, slightly raspy.

I scanned the area behind me, then looked everywhere, searching for a human figure. Panic seized me; was madness descending? Must I cease trusting my senses and my own mind? And yet, in that exact moment, I knew it was the raven speaking. The bird tilted its head upward, waiting. Though the sun did not shine, the creature seemed to cast a shadow that belonged entirely to itself.

*I beg my reader's indulgence,* I thought, *to help me articulate this—if comprehension is even possible between us.* I existed within a condition, a state of being so alien to our known world that any word I chose would merely be a sketch of the true picture. I feared I was attempting the impossible: describing what I could not name because no language fit the images forming in my mind. I had already written truths I wished to retract, but every attempt to pin them down with precise words threatened to dissolve the reality itself. I felt like someone waking from a dream, watching a familiar object rapidly shift through forms until its essence became unrecognizable.

Then, a thought occurred to me: a bird capable of speaking to a man deserved a civilized response, perhaps even a greater claim to one than a human. His speech carried a rough edge, likely from his croaking nature, but it wasn't unpleasant. Furthermore, while his words offered little clarity, they lacked rudeness.

"I did not come through any door," I corrected him.

"I saw you pass through it!" the raven insisted, "with my own ancient eyes!"

"I never saw any door!" I persisted.

"Of course not!" he retorted. "All the doors you've encountered thus far—and you haven't seen many—were doors leading *in*. Here, you found a door leading *out*! The peculiar aspect of this experience for you will be this: the more doors you exit, the further you travel inward!"

He continued thoughtfully. "The strange thing to you will be that the more doors you go out of, the farther you get in!"

"Please tell me where I am," I demanded.

"That is impossible. You possess no concept of 'whereness.' The only way to discover your location is to begin making yourself at home."

"How do I start that when everything is so bizarre?"

"By doing something."

"What kind of thing?"

"Anything. And the sooner the better! Until you are at home, escaping will be just as difficult as entering."

"I have, unfortunately, found it too easy to enter. Once I am out, I shall not try again!"

"You have stumbled in, and you may, eventually, stumble out. Whether your entry has been unfortunate remains to be seen."

"Do you never leave, sir?"

"When I choose, yes, though rarely and briefly. Your world is a half-baked place; it is simultaneously childish and arrogant. In fact, it lacks the development required for an old raven at your service!"

"Am I mistaken in assuming a man is superior to a bird?"

"That depends. We do not squander our intellects on generalizations; we accept men and birds exactly as we find them. Now, it is my turn to ask a question!"

"You have the perfect right," I replied, "since you *can* ask one!"

"Well stated!" he acknowledged. "Now, tell me who you are—if you happen to know."

"How could I possibly know? I am myself, and therefore I must know!"

"If you know you are yourself, you acknowledge you are not someone else. But do you truly know you are yourself? Are you certain you aren't your own father—or, forgive me, your own fool? Who are you, pray?"

Instantly, I realized I could offer him no definition of my identity. Indeed, who was I? To say "I am who I am" would be no answer. I understood then that I did not know myself, nor what I was, lacking any basis to distinguish one self from another. Regarding the name I bore in my previous world, I had forgotten it and cared little to remember it, for it signified nothing here, and its origin was irrelevant. I had nearly forgotten that naming oneself was a custom in that place! Thus, I remained silent, which proved wise. What could I say to a creature like this raven, who pierced accidents to reveal the core of entities?

"Look at me," he commanded, "and tell me who I am."

As he spoke, he turned his back. Instantly, I recognized him. He was no longer a bird, but a man of medium height, stooped, very thin, clad in a long black tailcoat. He turned again, and I saw him as a raven once more.

"I have seen you before, sir," I admitted, feeling foolish rather than startled.

"How can you claim to have seen me from viewing my back?" he countered. "Have you ever seen yourself from behind? You have never seen yourself at all! Tell me now, then, who I am."

"I humbly apologize," I answered. "I believe you were once the librarian of our house, but I know no more of *who* you are."

"Why do you apologize?"

"Because I mistook you for a raven," I explained, seeing him clearly as a bird or a man depending on the angle.

"You did me no harm," he replied. "Whether called a raven or thought to be one, you granted me existence, which is the entirety of what one can demand of a fellow-being. Therefore, in exchange, I shall teach you a lesson: No one can claim to be himself until he first knows that he *IS*, and then understands what *HIMSELF* entails. In truth, nobody is himself, and himself is nobody. There is more to this than you perceive now, yet no more than you require. You have entered this region prematurely, but regardless, you must learn to belong here. For home, as you may or may not realize, is the sole place where one can both exit and enter freely. There are places one enters and places one exits, but the one unique location, if discovered, allows movement in both directions—that is home."

He began to walk away, and once more I saw the librarian. He hadn't transformed; he simply seemed to have absorbed his shadow. I knew this sounded nonsensical, yet I could not stop myself from believing it. I watched him until he vanished, unsure if distance concealed him or if he melted into the heather. If I were dead, I mused, unaware of it, was I wandering the world beyond the grave, searching for my station? How was I to find my home? The raven had said I must act; what action lay available here? Would it make me someone? For now, alas, I was nobody.

I followed the path the Raven had taken, moving slowly. Soon, I spotted a grove of tall, slender pines and turned toward it. The scent of the forest greeted me, urging me deeper. Buried in its twilight gloom, I noticed something gleaming between two tree trunks. It possessed no color, resembling the shimmering heat haze rising from sun-baked earth on a midsummer noon, vibrating like struck piano strings. As I approached, it grew less distinct; nearing it closely, I stopped seeing it entirely, only the trees beyond appearing strangely uncertain.

Attempting to pass between the trunks, I received a sharp jolt, stumbled, and fell. Rising, I found myself facing the wooden wall of the garret chamber. Turning, I saw the mirror, atop which the black eagle seemed to have landed just moments ago. Terror gripped me, and I fled.

Outside the chamber, the vast garret spaces possessed an uncanny quality. They appeared to have waited eons for something; it had arrived, and now they waited anew! A shudder racked me on the winding stairs; the house had become foreign to me! Something was poised to spring upon me from the shadows! I dashed down the spiral staircase, collided with the wall, fell, rose, and ran. On the next floor, I lost my bearings, traversing several passages twice before locating the stairhead. Reaching the top of the great staircase, I regained composure somewhat and sat in the library, catching my breath.
