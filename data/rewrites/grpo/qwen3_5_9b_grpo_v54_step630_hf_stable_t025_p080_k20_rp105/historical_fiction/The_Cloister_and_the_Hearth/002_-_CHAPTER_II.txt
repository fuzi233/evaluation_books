"The broth is piping hot," Gerard announced, his voice cutting through the quiet of the room.

"But how shall we bring it to our lips?" the elder man sighed, his shoulders slumping in despair.

"Father, the youth has provided straws," Margaret replied, a mischievous glint dancing in her eyes.

"Ay, ay!" the old scholar nodded, though his tone remained skeptical. "Yet my bones are stiff, and kneeling over such a fierce fire with mere reeds seems impossible. St. John the Baptist, but the lad is dexterous!"

As he voiced his complaint, Gerard moved with practiced ease. He loosened the knot at his chest, removed his cap, and placed a stone in each corner of it. Wrapping his hand in the tail of his jerkin, he snatched the flask from the heat, wedged it securely between the stones, and lifted the cap to the elder's nose with a cheerful grin. Trembling slightly, the old man inserted the reed and drew. Instantly, his pale, drawn features seemed to ignite, glowing with renewed vigor. Upon taking a long, deep breath, he exclaimed, "Hippocrates and Galen! It is *soupe au vin*—the restorer of restoratives. Blessed be the nation that devised it, the woman who crafted it, and the young man who delivers it to the fainting. Have a taste, my girl, while I recount to our youthful host the history and virtues of this sovereign elixir. This remedy was unknown to the ancients; it appears in neither their medical treatises nor their popular narratives of surgery and healing. Hector, in the *Iliad*, if my memory serves me correctly—

(Margaret whispered, "Alas! he is off.")

—was invited by a lady of the poem to drink a draught of wine. Yet he declined, citing his imminent entry into battle and the need to preserve his strength. Had the *soupe au vin* been known in Troy, surely Hector would have added to his hexameter, 'But a *soupe au vin*, madam, I shall taste, and with gratitude.' Not only would this have been a mark of common civility—a virtue every perfect commander possesses—but failing to do so would have branded him shallow and imprudent, unfit to lead armies. For men entering battle require sustenance and every possible advantage, a truth proven by the fact that foolish generals, sending hungry soldiers against well-fed foes, have been defeated by inferior forces throughout the ages. The Romans lost a great battle in northern Italy to Hannibal solely due to this neglect. Now, this divine nectar grants strength to the limbs and ardor to the spirit in an instant. Taken by Hector at the critical moment, aided by Phoebus, Venus, and the blessed saints, it would likely have secured a Greek victory. Note how faint and weary I was a moment ago; well, I sip this celestial cordial, and now behold me brave as Achilles and strong as an eagle."

"Oh, father, now? An eagle, alas!" Margaret cried softly.

"Girl, I defy thee and all the world," the old man declared proudly. "Ready, I say, like a foaming charger, to devour the distance between here and Rotterdam, and strong enough to combat life's ills, even poverty and old age, which philosophers call the greatest evil. Negatur; unless a man's life has been ill-spent—which, by the way, it generally has. Now, to the moderns!"

"Father! Dear father!"

"Fear me not, girl; I shall be brief, unreasonably and unseasonably brief. The *soupe au vin* is absent from modern science; but this is merely further proof that for the last few centuries, physicians have been fools, relying on chicken broth and decoctions of gold, attributing supreme qualities to meat with the least juice and metal with the least chemical properties. Mountebanks! Dunces! Homicides! Since no light can be gathered from these sources, let us turn to the chroniclers. First, we find Duguesclin, a French knight, preparing to engage the English—who then ruled half of France and struck hard by sea and land. He drank not one, but three *soupes au vin* in honor of the Blessed Trinity. Having done so, he charged the islanders; and, as might have been predicted, slew a multitude and drove the rest into the sea. But he was merely the first of a long line of holy, hard-hitting warriors who, through this divine restorative, have been sustained, fortified, corroborated, and consoled."

"Dear father, pray join that venerable company before the soup grows cold," Margaret pleaded, holding the hat imploringly in both hands until he inserted the straw once more.

This spared them the "modern instances" and gave Gerard the chance to tell Margaret how proud his mother would be that her soup had benefited a man of learning.

"Ay! but," Margaret countered, "it would ill suit her to see her son give all and take none himself. Why did you bring but two straws?"

"Fair mistress, I hoped you would allow me to kiss your straw, seeing there were only two," Gerard replied.

Margaret smiled and blushed. "Never beg that you may command," she said. "The straw is not mine; it is yours: you cut it in that field."

"I cut it, and thus it became mine; but afterward, your lips touched it, and thus it became yours."

"Did it? Then I will lend it to you. There—now it is yours again; your lips have touched it."

"No, it belongs to us both now. Let us divide it."

"By all means; you have a knife."

"No, I will not cut it—that would be unlucky. I shall bite it. There, I shall keep my half; you will burn yours once you return home, I suspect."

"You know me not. I waste nothing. It is odds I shall fashion it into a hairpin or something similar."

This answer, rather than provoking Gerard, dashed his spirits, and he fell silent. With the bread and soup consumed, the old scholar prepared to resume his journey. A small difficulty arose: Gerard, the adroit one, could not retie his ribbon as Catherine had tied it. Margaret, after watching his futile efforts with a coy smile, offered assistance. For at her age, girls delight in alternating between sauciness and gentleness, and she saw she had momentarily disconcerted him. Then a fair head, crowned with a stately mass of auburn hair, glossy and glowing through the silver threads, bowed sweetly toward him. As it ravished his gaze, two white, supple hands played delicately upon the stubborn ribbon, molding it with soft, airy touches. A heavenly thrill ran through the innocent young man, and vague glimpses of a new world of feeling and sentiment opened before him. Margaret unwittingly prolonged these exquisite sensations; it is not natural for her sex to hasten anything pertaining to the sacred toilet. Indeed, when the taper fingers finally subdued the ends of the knot, her mind was not quite at ease until, by a maneuver peculiar to the female hand, she made her palm convex and applied it with gentle pressure to the center of the knot—a sweet, little coaxing hand-kiss, as much as to say, "Now be a good knot, and stay so." The palm-kiss was bestowed upon the ribbon, but the wearer's heart leaped to meet it.

"There, that is how it was," Margaret said, drawing back to survey her work with a keen eye. Looking up for simple approval of her skill, she received a gaze of such ardent adoration that she quickly lowered her eyes, flushing all over. An indescribable tremor seized her, and she retreated with downcast lashes and tell-tale cheeks, taking her father's arm on the opposite side. Gerard, blushing at having startled her with his gaze, took the other arm; and so the two youths walked downcast and conscious, propping the "eagle" along in silence.

They entered Rotterdam through the Schiedamze Poort. Unacquainted with the town, Peter directed Gerard toward the Hooch Straet, where the Stadthouse stood. He himself was accompanying Margaret to his cousin in the Ooster-Waagen Straet, so almost upon entering the gate, their paths diverged. They bid each other a friendly farewell, and Gerard plunged into the great city. A profound sense of solitude fell upon him, yet the streets teemed with people. He lamented too late that, out of delicacy, he had not asked his former companions who they were and where they lived.

"Beshrew my shamefacedness!" he muttered. "But their words and breeding were above their means, and something whispered that they would not wish to be known. I shall never see her again. Oh, weary world, I hate you and your ways. To think I must meet beauty, goodness, and learning—three pearls of price—and never see them more!"

Lost in this sad reverie, letting his body drift wherever it would, he lost his way. But soon meeting a crowd moving in one direction, he mingled with them, reasoning they must be heading for the Stadthouse. Soon the noisy throng containing the moody Gerard emerged, not onto the Stadthouse grounds, but onto a large meadow beside the Maas. Then the attraction was revealed. Games of all sorts were underway: wrestling, the game of palm, the quintain, legerdemain, archery, and tumbling—an art in which, I blush to admit, women as well as men performed, to the great delight of the spectators. There was also a trained bear standing on his head, marching upright, and bowing with prodigious gravity to his master; a hare beating a drum; and a cock strutting disdainfully on little stilts. These sights made Gerard laugh occasionally, but the gay scene could not truly enliven him, for his heart was not in tune with it. Hearing a young man tell his companion that the Duke had been in the meadow but had gone to the Stadthouse to entertain the burgomasters, aldermen, prize competitors, and their friends, Gerard suddenly remembered his hunger and his desire to sup with a prince. He left the riverbank, and this time he found the Hooch Straet, which swiftly led him to the Stadthouse.

However, upon arrival, he was refused entry at one door, then another, until he reached the great gate of the courtyard. Guarded by soldiers and supervised by a pompous major-domo glittering in an embroidered collar and gold chain of office, holding a white staff with a gold knob, the gate was a fortress. A crowd of people attempted to soften this official rock, coming up in turn like ripples and retreating as such. It cost Gerard a struggle to approach, and when he was within four heads of the gate, he saw something that made his heart race: there was Peter, with Margaret on his arm, soliciting humbly for entrance.

"My cousin the alderman is not at home; they say he is here," Peter said.

"What is that to me, old man?" the guard retorted.

"If you will not let us pass to him, at least take this leaf from my tablet to my cousin. See, I have written his name; he will come out to us."

"For what do you take me? I carry no messages; I keep the gate."

He then bawled in a stentorian voice, inexorably: "No strangers enter here, but the competitors and their companies."

"Come, old man," a voice cried from the crowd, "you have gotten your answer; make way."

Margaret turned half-round, imploringly: "Good people, we are come from afar, and my father is old; and my cousin has a new servant who knows us not and would not let us sit in our cousin's house."

At this, the crowd laughed hoarsely. Margaret shrank as if struck. At that moment, a hand grasped hers—a magic grasp; it felt like heart meeting heart, or magnet meeting steel. She turned quickly, and it was Gerard. Such a little cry of joy and appeal escaped her bosom, and she began to whimper prettily.

They had hustled her and frightened her for one thing; and her cousin's thoughtfulness—or lack thereof—in not even telling his servant they were coming, was cruel. The servant's caution, however wise and faithful to his master, was bitterly mortifying to her father and herself. And to her, so mortified, anxious, and jostled, came suddenly this kind hand and face. "Hinc illae lacrimae."

"All is well now," remarked a coarse humorist. "She hath gotten her sweetheart."

"Haw! haw! haw!" went the crowd.

She dropped Gerard's hand directly and turned round, eyes flashing through her tears: "I have no sweetheart, you rude men. But I am friendless in your boorish town, and this is a friend; and one who knows, what you know not, how to treat the aged and the weak."

The crowd fell dead silent. They had only been thoughtless, and now felt the rebuke, though severe, was just. The silence enabled Gerard to treat with the porter.

"I am a competitor, sir."

"What is your name?" the man eyed him suspiciously.

"Gerard, the son of Elias."

The janitor inspected a slip of parchment he held: "Gerard Eliassoen can enter."

"With my company, these two?"

"Nay; those are not your company; they came before you."

"What matter? They are my friends, and without them I go not in."

"Stay without, then."

"That will I not."

"That we shall see."

"We will, and speedily." And with this, Gerard raised a voice of astounding volume and power, routing the air so that the whole street rang: "Ho! PHILIP, EARL OF HOLLAND!"

"Are you mad?" cried the porter.

"Hush, hush!"

"Hush! Murder! The Dukes are there. I'm dead," cried the janitor, quaking.
