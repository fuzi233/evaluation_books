Speech is often the vessel for thought, yet certain emotions are so primal, so overwhelming, that they escape the tongue and erupt instead as raw, eloquent cries. In those moments, man sheds his civilized veneer and becomes little more than a beast driven by instinct. There was a terrifying, feral quality to the scene unfolding in the woods: the guttural roar of triumph from the pursuers bursting forth from the thickets, clashing violently against the piercing shriek of terror as their prey scattered into the undergrowth. Hands clawed at empty air mere feet away, grasping for ghosts as Gerard and the mule surged forward, fleeing for their lives. For a heartbeat, the hunters stood stunned, confused like lions whose springs have gone wide, allowing the fugitives to gain a ten-yard advantage. But the confusion vanished instantly. With weapons raised high, Dierich and his men launched themselves anew. They were confident in victory; they knew these runners well. Earlier that morning, in the flat lands, the trio had been steadily gaining ground, but now, in the open, the contest would be decided solely by sheer velocity. A hundred yards vanished in the blink of an eye, yet that fatal gap of ten yards remained.

Dierich Brower frowned, troubled by a sudden, unexplained shift in the fugitives' stamina. It defied logic. Normally, when three flee together, they move at the pace of the slowest link. From Peter's house to the tree line, Gerard had dragged himself along at Margaret's weary rhythm. Now, however, he moved with a fierce, singular urgency. The mule beside him was a wind beneath their wings, capable of outrunning them all, and Gerard seemed to draw strength from it. Furthermore, the effects of youth and a life of chastity began to manifest. As daylight slowly crept back into the world ahead of them, leaving the hunters in the gloom, Dierich threw himself into a final, desperate sprint, closing the distance by two yards. But within seconds, Gerard stole those precious steps back, silent and sure. Frustration bubbled over the pursuers' lips.

Farther back, Martin heard the grumbling and a glimmer of hope lit his face. "Hold fast, Gerard! Courage, brave lad!" he shouted, his voice cutting through the din. "Look at them! They are breaking formation." And indeed, order was fracturing. Dierich was now flanked by only one man, while another lagged dangerously near the rear.

They ascended a long, gentle rise, and here the virtues of youth, grit, and sobriety proved superior to age and experience. Before Gerard could reach the summit, the weight of forty years crushed Dierich like forty heavy stones. "Our prize turns to dust," he gasped, breathless. "Seize him dead if you cannot catch him alive!" He abandoned the run, falling into a clumsy walk, trailing behind. Jorian Ketel soon followed suit, dropping further back, until finally, save for one, the entire party stood still. Only one man pressed on, relentless as a bloodhound, though he lost ground with every step. If memory serves me right, his name was Eric Wouverman. Guided by this lone survivor, they approached a second rise in the wood—shorter, but far steeper than the first.

"Grab the reins!" Martin cried urgently.

Gerard obeyed instantly, and the mule's powerful legs carried them up the slope with a speed that outstripped Gerard's previous running pace. Witnessing this maneuver, Eric Wouverman's spirit crumbled. Realizing he was now nearly eighty yards behind the fugitives and significantly distant from his nearest comrade, he halted abruptly. Obedient to Dierich's prior command, he lowered his crossbow, aimed it with deliberate care, and, just as the trio dipped below the crest of the hill and vanished from sight, loosed a whistling bolt into the air.

A scream of horror tore through the silence. The next instant, as if struck by a lightning rod, the entire group collapsed onto the earth, mule and all, brought low by the unseen arrow.
