The night that followed was a tapestry woven from horrors too vast for mortal description, though the cruel mercy of sleep occasionally draped its heavy curtain over our weary eyes. Even in our precarious positions, nature demanded its due, forcing moments of respite upon the exhausted. Yet, for me, slumber remained an elusive phantom. The terror of our impending doom was a weight sufficient to crush the bravest soul on earth, and I, who never claimed the mantle of courage, found myself paralyzed not by fear alone, but by the suffocating magnitude of the silence.

Reader, you may have lain awake in your bedchamber, finding the quiet oppressive, a heavy blanket pressing against your lungs. But you possess no conception of perfection in stillness. Upon the surface of the earth, there exists a constant hum, a whisper of wind or insect, a microscopic vibration that dulls the razor's edge of absolute silence. Here, beneath the crust, there was none. We were interred within the bowels of a colossal, snow-crowned peak. Above us, thousands of feet distant, the fresh air roared over the pristine white expanse, yet not a single tremor of that wind penetrated our prison. Separated from the awful chamber of the Dead by a long, constricting tunnel and five feet of unyielding rock, we were insulated from the finality of death itself. The dead make no noise; did we not know this, lying beside the pale, frozen form of Foulata? Not even the cataclysmic crashing of all the artillery of heaven and earth could have breached the seal of our living tomb. Cut off from every echo of the world, we were no longer men, but statues waiting for the grave.

Then, the bitter irony of our plight sharpened my senses. Around us lay treasures sufficient to settle a moderate national debt or forge a fleet of ironclads, and yet, in the grip of despair, we would have traded them all for the faintest glimmer of escape. Soon, doubtless, we would rejoice to barter those mountains of gold for a scrap of food, a cup of water, and eventually, the privilege of a swift release from our suffering. True wealth, which men dedicate their lifetimes to accumulating, proved to be a valueless husk at the end.

Thus, the night dragged on, a slow erosion of sanity.

"Good," Sir Henry's voice finally broke the vacuum, sounding grotesquely loud in the intense stillness. "How many matches remain in the box?"

"Eight, Curtis."

"Strike one. Let us see the time."

He complied, and the sudden flare of flame nearly blinded us, a stark, dancing eye in the dense obscurity. My watch indicated five o'clock. Far overhead, beyond the snow-wreathed peaks, the beautiful dawn was already blushing against the sky, and the breeze would soon begin to stir the night mists in the hollows below.

"We ought to eat something and sustain our strength," I urged.

"What is the point of eating?" Good replied, his tone devoid of hope. "The sooner we die and finish this, the better."

"While there is life, there is hope," Sir Henry countered, his voice steady despite the gloom.

Consequently, we consumed our meager rations and sipped precious water, allowing another period of agonizing time to pass. Then, Sir Henry proposed that we move closer to the exit, shouting on the slender chance that someone outside might catch our cries. Good, whose piercing vocal range was honed by years at sea, groveled down the passage and commenced his task. I must confess, he produced a most diabolical racket. I had never heard such yells, yet they achieved no more effect than a mosquito buzzing in a sealed jar.

Eventually, he relented, returning thirstier than before and compelled to drink. We ceased our screaming immediately, for it threatened to deplete our dwindling water supply.

So we settled once more against the chests of useless diamonds, succumbing to that dreadful inertia which ranks among the cruelest aspects of our fate. I am bound to admit that, for my part, I surrendered completely to despair. Resting my head against Sir Henry's broad shoulder, I burst into tears. I believe I heard Good gulping sobbingly on the other side, cursing himself hoarsely for such weakness.

Ah, how noble and courageous that great man was! Had we been two frightened children and he our nurse, he could not have treated us with greater tenderness. Forgetting his own share of misery, he strove to soothe our shattered nerves, recounting tales of men who had endured similar trials and escaped by miracle. When these stories failed to lift our spirits, he pointed out that death was inevitable for us all, suggesting that exhaustion would bring a merciful end—a notion, I must correct, that is not true. Then, in a diffident manner, as he had done before, he proposed throwing ourselves on the mercy of a Higher Power, a suggestion to which I responded with vigorous conviction.

His character was beautiful, quiet yet possessing an iron strength.

In this way, the day unfolded as the night had, provided one can apply such temporal markers where everything was shrouded in the deepest night. When I finally struck a match to check the hour, it was seven o'clock.

Once more we ate and drank, and as we did so, a thought sparked in my mind.

"How is it," I asked, "that the air in this place remains fresh? It feels thick and heavy, yet it is perfectly pure."

"Great heavens!" Good exclaimed, springing to his feet. "I never considered that. It cannot seep through the stone door; if ever a door was truly airtight, it is that one. It must originate from somewhere. If there were no current of air, we would have suffocated or been poisoned the moment we entered. Let us investigate."

Wonderful was the transformation wrought by this mere spark of hope. In an instant, we were all three crawling on hands and knees, probing for the slightest indication of a draft. Soon, my enthusiasm met a chilling obstacle. My hand brushed against something cold—it was the face of the dead Foulata.

For an hour or more, we continued our futile search until, battered by countless knocks against tusks, chests, and the chamber walls, both Sir Henry and I yielded in despair. Good, however, persisted, remarking with a forced cheerfulness that it was preferable to doing nothing.

"I say, you fellows," he called out in a constrained voice, "come here."

Naturally, we scurried toward him.

"Quatermain, place your hand where mine is. Do you feel anything?"

"I... I think I feel air rising."

"Now listen." He rose and stamped firmly upon the spot, and a flame of hope ignited in our hearts. *It rang hollow.*

With trembling hands, I struck a match. Only three remained, and we realized we were positioned in the far corner of the chamber, a fact explaining why we had missed the hollow resonance during our earlier exhaustive survey. As the match burned, we scrutinized the area. There was a seam in the solid rock floor, and, by heavens! there, flush with the stone, lay a circular ring. We spoke no word; our hearts hammered too wildly with hope to permit speech. Good possessed a knife fitted with a hoof-pick hook. He opened it and scraped around the ring. Finally, he worked the tool underneath, levering gently to avoid snapping the hook. The ring began to shift. Being stone, it had not rusted fast over the centuries, unlike iron might have. Slowly, it rose upright. Then Good thrust his hands inside and tugged with all his might, but nothing moved.

"Let me try," I said impatiently, for the ring's position in the corner made simultaneous pulling impossible. I seized it and strained, but to no avail.

Then Sir Henry attempted it and failed.

Taking the hook again, Good scraped the entire circumference of the crack where we felt the air ascending.

"Now, Curtis," he commanded, "apply your full strength. You are as strong as two. Stop," he added, removing a stout black silk handkerchief—which, true to his habits of neatness, he still wore—and threading it through the ring. "Quatermain, get Curtis around the middle and pull for dear life when I give the word. *Now*."

Sir Henry unleashed his enormous strength, while Good and I contributed all the power nature had bestowed upon us.

"Heave! Heave! It's yielding!" gasped Sir Henry, and I heard the muscles of his great back crack audibly. Suddenly, a grating sound erupted, followed by a rush of air, and we collapsed onto our backs on the floor, crushed beneath a heavy flagstone. Sir Henry's strength had prevailed, and never did muscular power serve a man so well.

"Light a match, Quatermain," he ordered as soon as we had recovered our breath. "Carefully, now."

I complied, and there, before us, Heaven be praised! was the *first step of a stone stair*.

"Now, what is to be done?" Good asked.

"Descend the stairs, of course, and trust to Providence."

"Wait!" Sir Henry interrupted. "Quatermain, retrieve the remaining biltong and water; we may need them."

I crept back to our resting place by the chests to fetch them. As I returned, an idea struck me. We had paid little heed to the diamonds for the last twenty-four hours; indeed, the very concept of diamonds was nauseating, considering the agony they had inflicted upon us.
