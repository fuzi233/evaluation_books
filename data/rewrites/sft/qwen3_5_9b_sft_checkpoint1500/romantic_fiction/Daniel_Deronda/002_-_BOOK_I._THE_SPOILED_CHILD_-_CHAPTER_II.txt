These lines were scrawled above the letter Gwendolen found upon her dressing table.

"DEAREST CHILD,—I have been awaiting news from you for seven days. In your last correspondence, you mentioned that the Langens contemplated departing Leubronn for Baden. How could you be so thoughtless as to leave me in suspense regarding your whereabouts? I am in profound anxiety lest this missive fail to reach you. In any event, you were to return home by the end of September, yet I implore you to hasten your return. Should you exhaust your funds, I shall possess no means to assist you further, and you must absolutely refrain from borrowing from the Langens, for I hold no capacity to indemnify them. This is the grim reality, my darling—I wish I could soften the blow, but a catastrophic ruin has overtaken us all. You know little of commerce and cannot comprehend its intricacies; however, Grapnell & Co. have collapsed with losses totaling a million pounds, leaving us utterly destitute—your Aunt Gascoigne included, save for your Uncle Gascoigne's clerical stipend. To survive, we must dispose of their carriage and rely on interest from the boys' investments. Every asset your poor father salvaged for us is now forfeited to creditors. I possess nothing of my own. It is preferable that you learn this truth immediately, though it shatters my heart to voice it. Naturally, we cannot avoid wondering why you departed precisely when you did. Yet I shall never blame you, my dearest child; I would shield you from all distress if I could. On your journey home, you will have time to steel yourself for the transformation awaiting you. We shall likely vacate Offendene forthwith, hoping that Mr. Haynes, who coveted the estate previously, is prepared to purchase it. Regrettably, we cannot reside in the rectory—it offers no space. We must secure some humble dwelling and subsist upon your uncle Gascoigne's charity until further prospects emerge. I shall be unable to settle debts to merchants or even cover servants' wages. Fortify your spirit, my dear child; we must submit to Divine Will. It is agonizing to yield to Mr. Lassman's malevolent recklessness, which rumors attribute to this failure. Your poor sisters can only weep alongside me, offering no aid. Were you present, perhaps a rift in the clouds would form—I struggle to believe fate intended you for poverty. If the Langens intend to linger abroad, you might arrange your travel under someone else's guardianship. Nevertheless, arrive as swiftly as possible with your afflicted and devoted mother,

The initial impact of this epistle upon Gwendolen was akin to a stupor. The unshakeable conviction that her destiny awaited in a realm of opulent ease, where adversity would merely be draped in silk and cushioned with gold, had resided more deeply within her youthful soul than in her mother's mind, nourished by her blood and a sense of inherent superiority that colored vast swathes of her consciousness. It proved nearly as arduous for her to accept the sudden shift from affluence to humiliating dependency as it would have been to anticipate the chilling sensation of her own mortality entering the stream of her flourishing life. Standing immobile for several moments, she discarded her hat and mechanically inspected the glass. The coils of her smooth, light-brown hair remained arranged with perfection suitable for a ballroom. On other evenings, Gwendolen might have lingered before her reflection with pleasure—a permissible indulgence—but now, she registered no conscious appreciation of her mirrored beauty. Instead, she stared fixedly ahead, as if jarred by a jarring, hateful noise and waiting for its source to manifest. Eventually, she collapsed into the corner of the crimson velvet sofa, retrieved the letter, and read it twice with deliberateness. Finally, she allowed it to drop to the floor, resting her interlaced hands upon her lap, sitting perfectly still, shedding no tear.

Her instinct was to survey and resist the circumstances rather than lament them. There was no internal outcry of "Poor Mamma!" Her mother had never derived significant joy from existence, and if Gwendolen felt pity in this moment, she would direct it toward herself—for was she not, by nature and right, the primary object of her mother's solicitude? What consumed her was anger, resistance, and bitter vexation at having squandered her winnings at roulette. Had fortune favored her through this single day, she would have returned home laden with a substantial sum, or continued gambling to sustain them all. Could it not still happen? Although only four napoleons remained in her purse, she possessed ornaments she could liquidate—a practice commonplace among stylish circles in German spas, requiring no shame. Indeed, even absent her mother's letter, she likely would have sold jewelry for an Etruscan necklace she had not worn since arriving, deriving a pleasurable sense of intensity and escape from the mundane. With ten louis at her disposal and a return to her former luck—which seemed probable—what could surpass continuing to gamble for a few days? If her friends at home disapproved of her methods, as they surely would, the money would nonetheless exist. Gwendolen's imagination dwelled upon this path, conjuring agreeable consequences, yet lacking the unbroken confidence and rising certainty characteristic of a gambler's mania. She had approached the roulette table not in the grip of passion, but in search of it; her mind remained sanely capable of visualizing balanced probabilities. While the allure of winning tempted her, the specter of loss thrust itself forward with alternating force, causing her pride to sink sensitively. She was resolved neither to inform the Langens of her family's misfortune nor to incur any debt to their compassion. If she parted with her jewelry to any noticeable degree, they would intervene with inquiries and remonstrances. The course posing the least risk of intolerable annoyance was to mortgage her necklace early in the morning, notify the Langens that her mother desired her immediate return without specifying reasons, and board the train for Brussels that evening. Though she lacked a maid, and the Langens might object to her solitary return, her will was absolute.

Rather than retiring to bed, she illuminated her chamber to its brightest and commenced packing, working diligently. Throughout, however, scenes of the approaching day haunted her: now the tedious explanations and farewells, the whirlwind journey toward a transformed home; now the alternative of delaying another day, standing again at the roulette table. Yet, in every latter vision, Deronda loomed, watching her with exasperating irony—and the two keen experiences inevitably resurfaced together—observing her once more abandoned by fortune. This importunate image undeniably swayed her resolve toward immediate departure, urging her packing to a point where changing her mind would prove inconvenient. It was past midnight when she entered her room, and by the time she verified that she had omitted nothing essential, the faint dawn was seeping through the white blinds, dimming her candles. Why sleep? A cold bath sufficed for refreshment, and she noted that a slight tracé of fatigue around her eyes rendered her appearance all the more striking. Before six o'clock, she was fully attired in her gray traveling dress, complete with her felt hat, intending to venture outdoors as soon as she could confirm the presence of other ladies heading to the springs. Chance placed her sideways before the long strip of mirror bisecting her twin windows; turning, she leaned her elbow upon the chair back in an attitude fit for a portrait. It is possible to harbor a robust self-love devoid of self-satisfaction, indeed coupled with a self-discontent intensified because one's tiny core of egocentric sensibility becomes a supreme preoccupation. Yet Gwendolen knew nothing of such inner strife. She possessed a *naïve* delight in her fortunate self, an aspect worthy of indulgence from anyone but the sternest saintliness, for she had daily witnessed a pleasant reflection of this self in her friends' flattery as well as in the glass. And even amidst these beginnings of trouble, while idly gazing at her image in the encroaching light, her face acquired a complacency gradual as the cheerfulness of the morning. Her beautiful lips curled into an increasingly decisive smile, until finally, removing her hat, she leaned forward and kissed the cold glass, which had appeared so warm. How could she believe in sorrow? If it assailed her, she felt the power to crush it, defy it, or flee from it, as she had already done. Anything seemed more plausible than enduring miseries, great or small.

Madame von Langen never ventured out prior to breakfast, allowing Gwendolen to conclude her early stroll by traversing the Obere Strasse, where lay the requisite shop, certain to open after seven. At that hour, any observers she cared to avoid would either be strolling in the spring region or still nestled in their bedrooms. However, there existed a grand hotel, the *Czarina*, from which eyes might track her ascent to Mr. Wiener's door. This was a risk worth taking: might she not be entering to purchase something that had caught her fancy? This implicit falsehood coursed through her mind as she recalled that the *Czarina* housed Deronda, yet she was already far up the Obere Strasse, walking with her customary floating gait. Every line of her figure and drapery fell in gentle curves attractive to all eyes save those perceiving too close a resemblance to the serpent, objects to the revival of serpent-worship.
