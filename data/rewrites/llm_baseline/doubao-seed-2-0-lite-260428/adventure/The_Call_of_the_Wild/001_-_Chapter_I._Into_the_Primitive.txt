Old longings nomadic leap,
Chafing at custom’s chain;
Again from its brumal sleep
Wakens the ferine strain.

Buck never skimmed newspaper headlines, or he would have known trouble was brewing not just for him, but for every heavy, long-coated tidewater dog between Puget Sound and San Diego. Men digging in the frozen dark of the Arctic had struck gold by the bucketful, and steamship lines blew the story up across every port, sending thousands of frantic prospectors stampeding north. Those men didn’t want lapdogs. They needed hard-muscled brutes that could haul tons of supplies across ice, thick fur wrapped tight to their bones to fend off sixty-below frost.

Buck ruled every inch of Judge Miller’s sun-drenched estate, tucked back from the road in Santa Clara Valley, half-hidden behind draping oak and poplar boughs. A wide cool veranda wrapped all four sides of the big house, gravel driveways winding through acres of manicured lawn, stables that held a dozen grooms, rows of vine-choked servant cottages, endless stretches of grape arbors, green pastures, orchards, and berry patches. There was an artesian well pumping station, and a big cement swimming tank where the Judge’s boys cannonballed at dawn and lazed through sweltering afternoons.

He’d been born here, four years old, 140 solid pounds of mixed St Bernard and Scotch Shepherd breeding, no soft pampered house pet. He swam the tank, hunted alongside the Judge’s sons, escorted the Judge’s daughters on long twilight rambles, curled warm at the old man’s feet in front of the library fire on winter nights, toted the Judge’s grandsons on his back through the orchard trails. The dozen snippy fox terriers didn’t dare challenge him, the strange tiny pug Toots and hairless Mexican dog Ysabel he completely ignored. He was king of every creeping, crawling, flying thing on the whole property — even the human kids followed his lead half the time.

1897 rolled around, the Klondike gold rush dragging every fortune-seeker on the continent north into the ice, and Buck had no clue that Manuel, one of the gardener’s helpers, was a deadbeat gambling fool hooked on Chinese lottery. His “foolproof” betting system chewed through every cent of his meager wages, leaving nothing for his wife and horde of kids. The night the Judge was off at a raisin growers’ meeting, and the boys were busy setting up their new athletic club, Manuel slipped Buck through the orchard fence on what Buck thought was just a casual walk. No one saw them but a stranger loitering at the tiny College Park flag station, and Buck heard the clink of coins pass between them.

“Wrap the goods up tight before you hand ’em over,” the stranger grunted, and Manuel looped a thick stout rope around Buck’s neck right under his collar. “Twist it, you’ll choke him plenty,” Manuel said, and the stranger nodded.

Buck had always trusted the people who lived on the estate, so he accepted the rope with quiet dignity — right up until the stranger’s hand wrenched it tight, cutting off his breath. He snarled, then lunged, full of rage at the betrayal, but the man caught him by the throat, twisted, and slammed him flat on his back. The rope bit harder, Buck’s tongue lolled out, his great chest heaving useless air. He’d never been so humiliated, so furious in his life. His vision blurred, and he went limp just as a train screeched to a stop, the two men dumping his unconscious body into the baggage car.

He woke up to the rumble of the train, his tongue throbbing, and the full, unbridled rage of a kidnapped king flaring up. He lunged for the stranger’s hand, jaws clamping down hard, and didn’t let go till the rope choked him senseless again. The man lied straight to the baggage handler who heard the struggle, hiding his mangled hand behind his back, claiming Buck was a sick show dog he was taking to a vet in San Francisco.

Later that night, in a rickety shed behind a waterfront saloon, the man complained he only got fifty bucks for the whole mess, his hand wrapped in a blood-soaked handkerchief, his right trouser leg ripped open from the knee to the ankle. The saloon keeper had paid a hundred bucks for Buck, and said he was worth every cent. They pried the heavy brass collar off Buck’s neck while he was half out of it, pulled the rope away, and locked him in a flimsy wooden crate.

Buck lay there the rest of the night, rage and wounded pride eating at him, confused, waiting for the Judge or the boys to come rescue him. Every time the shed door rattled, he lifted his head hopeful, only to see the saloon keeper’s fat face peering in by the guttering tallow candle, and the happy welcome bark in his throat twisted into a savage snarl.

By dawn, four ragged, rough-looking men showed up to haul the crate away. Buck stormed and raged at them through the slats, and they laughed, poking at him with sticks till he figured out that was exactly what they wanted. He went sullen and quiet, as the crate passed from wagon to ferry to express train, jostled along for two full days and nights. Buck refused to eat a bite, refused to take a drop of water. He growled at every messenger who came near, they taunted him back, barking and mewing and flapping their arms like idiots, and his anger built higher and higher, turning to a burning fever from his parched, swollen throat. He swore no one would ever loop a rope around his neck again. By the time they pulled into Seattle, the express workers were so relieved to get him off their car they practically ran to hand him over.

Four men carried the crate into a small, high-walled backyard. A broad, stocky man in a stretched-out red sweater stepped out, signed for the delivery, and Buck knew instantly he was the next tormentor. He threw himself against the slats of the crate, snarling. The red-sweatered man grabbed a hatchet and a heavy wooden club. The four other men scattered fast, scrambling up to perch on top of the wall to watch.

Buck bit and tore at the splintering wood, lunging at every spot the hatchet bit through the crate. The second the opening was big enough for him to squeeze through, the man tossed the hatchet aside, hefted the club. Buck launched himself full force, all 140 pounds of two days’ worth of pent-up fury, straight for the man’s throat. Mid-leap, the club cracked him hard across the side, and he flipped over to hit the ground back first, teeth clipping together so hard his jaws ached.

He’d never been hit by a club in his life. He staggered back up, snarling half bark half scream, and charged again. Again the club knocked him flat. A dozen times he threw himself at the man, and a dozen times the club smashed his charge apart. After one particularly brutal blow, he stumbled around dazed, blood pouring from his nose, mouth, and ears, his once-glossy coat matted with slaver and blood. The man stepped forward, and hit him square on the nose. The pain was worse than every blow before, a white-hot fire, and Buck roared like a lion as he charged one last time. The man twisted his jaw, wrenched him around in a full circle, and Buck crashed head first into the dirt. One final, sharp blow to the skull, and Buck went completely senseless.

“Damn, that guy knows how to break a dog,” one of the men on the wall yelled enthusiastically.
“Still rather break horses any day, twice on Sundays,” the driver called back, climbing back onto his wagon.

When Buck came to, he didn’t have the strength to stand. He lay there in the dirt, watching the man in the red sweater. The man spoke in a soft, genial tone, patting the top of Buck’s throbbing head even though Buck’s fur bristled on instinct. “We had our little fight, that’s all. You learned your place, I learned mine. Be good, and everything’s easy. Be bad, and I’ll beat you within an inch of your life. Got it?”

Buck didn’t snap. He accepted the bowl of water the man brought him, then bolted down a big pile of raw meat straight from the man’s hand. He’d been beaten, but he hadn’t been broken. He knew now he would never win a fight against a man with a club. That club was his first lesson in the brutal, unforgiving law of the primitive wild. He watched every new dog that got dragged through the yard, every one of them that fought, that fawned, that refused to submit and got killed for it. He learned the rule sharp: the man with the club is the lawgiver, you obey him — you don’t have to like him.

His turn to get taken away came at last, when a small, weathered French-Canadian man named Perrault spotted him, spat broken English, and exclaimed Buck was the finest dog he’d ever seen, one in ten thousand. The red-sweatered man sold him for three hundred dollars, a steal with the price of work dogs skyrocketing. Perrault was working for the Canadian government, carrying urgent dispatches north. Buck saw the money change hands, and soon he was being led away alongside Curly, a sweet-natured Newfoundland, down to the docks to board the steamship *Narwhal*. That was the last he ever saw of the man in the red sweater, and the last he ever saw of the sun-warmed California south.

Below decks, Perrault handed them over to François, a half-French, half-Indian breed with a dark, weathered face. Buck soon learned both men were fair, sharp, they knew dogs better than dogs knew themselves, and he grew to respect them, even if he never loved them. They shared the hold with two other dogs: Spitz, a sharp, snow-white dog from Spitzbergen who’d come home with a whaling crew, who smiled right at you while he snuck your food. When Spitz stole Buck’s first meal on the ship, François’s whip cracked across his back before Buck could even lunge, and Buck decided the half-breed was fair, worth his respect. The other dog was Dave, grim, quiet, grumpy, who wanted nothing more than to be left alone to eat, sleep, and yawn. He didn’t care when the ship bucked and rolled across Queen Charlotte Sound, while Buck and Curly panicked at the rough seas, he just lifted his head, glanced at them annoyed, and went right back to sleep.

Day after day the *Narwhal*’s propeller droned on, and Buck noticed the air getting colder and colder, sharp enough to make his nose tingle. One morning, the propeller went quiet, the whole ship hummed with excitement. François leashed all the dogs and led them up on deck. Buck stepped forward, and his paws sank straight down into cold, soft white mush that looked like mud. He yelped and sprang back. More of the white stuff was drifting down from the sky. He shook himself, but it kept landing on his fur. He sniffed it curious, licked a bit up on his tongue. It burned like fire, then vanished instantly. He tried another lick, same result. The sailors watching roared with laughter, and Buck felt a hot, strange shame creep up — no one had ever laughed at him before. He was touching snow for the very first time.
