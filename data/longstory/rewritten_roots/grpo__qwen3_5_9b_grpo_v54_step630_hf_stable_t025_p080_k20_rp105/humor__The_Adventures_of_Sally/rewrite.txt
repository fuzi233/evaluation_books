The initial stiffness, largely caused by her brother Fillmore's glaring white evening waistcoat, had evaporated. The transient and permanent residents of Mrs. Meecher's boarding house were once again their boisterous selves, chatting and laughing freely.

At the far end, the conversation had inevitably drifted to the great topic of the evening: Sally's inheritance. There is nothing quite as intoxicating as dictating the spending of other people's money, and her guests were enthusiastically drafting a budget for her. Relieved, Sally returned to her seat. The company had now split into two camps. The conservative faction, led by Augustus Bartlett, was firmly investing three hundred thousand in Liberty Bonds and safe real estate. "I'm sure," he said, "you don't want a speech... Very good of you to drink our health. Thank you."

The effect on the company was marked, though not identical. For the majority, the emotion was one of unmixed relief. Far different was the reaction of Mr. Maxwell Faucitt. The poor old man wore an expression of surprise and dismay as if someone had pulled the chair from under him. Here Sally kissed Mr. Faucitt and burst into tears.

"There, there," said Faucitt, soothingly. The kindest critic could not claim Sally had been eloquent; nevertheless, Mr. Maxwell Faucitt was conscious of no sense of anti-climax.

If Ginger Kemp had been asked to list his virtues, the paper would likely have remained mostly blank. He might have managed "good intentions" with a bit of chewing on the pencil, but after that, the list would stall. Tact and sensitivity were not on the menu. Yet, by successfully avoiding Sally for several days, he had demonstrated a surprising degree of class. It wasn't easy; he had to force himself to stay away. He reasoned that in times of trouble, solitude was the patient's best friend. He, too, had suffered nasty shocks in his day and preferred to sit alone and fight his battles in silence.

But by Saturday, Ginger decided that action was overdue. Saturday was a prime day for restarting threads. He didn't have to go to the office, and, crucially, he had just collected his weekly salary. Mrs. Meecher had wisely deducted her share, but enough remained to fund a princely consolation tour. The plan crystallized: hire a car and whisk Sally away to one of the roadhouses along the Boston Post Road. The more he examined the scheme, the better it looked.

The weather was an extraordinary ally. It had been a revelation to Ginger, his first true taste of America's Indian Summer. Standing on the roof of Mrs. Meecher's establishment on Saturday morning, thrilled by the velvet glow of the sun, he concluded that the only logical use of such a day was to take Sally for a drive in an open car.

The Maison Meecher was a lofty building on a side-street near the avenue. From its roof, after navigating the groves of laundry hanging limply from lines, one could see wonders. To the left lay Washington Square, buzzing with sleepy Italians and roller-skating children; to the right stood the high smokestacks of a Cunard liner moving slowly down the river, looming over the rooftops as if the ship were traveling down Ninth Avenue.

Today, there were four funnels, leading Ginger to deduce it was the *Mauritania*. The boat he had crossed from England held a sentimental weight for him. Sally sat in her apartment on the morning of her return to New York, trembling with the electric thrill of a swimmer who has finally conquered the hesitation of a raw, freezing dawn. She ached, yes, but it was a stimulating ache, the kind that proves you are alive and defiant. She had spent months wavering on the brink of happiness, and now, plunging into the mid-stream of reality, she was ready for whatever the water threw at her.

Her first impulse was to run to Ginger. She needed his stolid, unshakeable friendliness to anchor her. She regretted not sending a cable to meet her at the docks; the echoing customs sheds had already drained her courage, leaving her feeling strangely isolated. Glancing at her watch, she realized it was still early. She could catch Ginger at the office and drag him out for lunch.

Stepping out into the city, she found that the relentless hand of change had already reshaped the outer office of Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. The pimpled boy she had once known, with whom she had shared such warm rapport, was gone. In his place stood a grim, suspicious stripling whose face was a map of acne and whose demeanor was that of a security guard rather than a theater employee. He peered at her with narrowed eyes, as if she were a thief caught in the act of stealing the blotting paper, before demanding her business with icy acerbity.

"I want Mr. Kemp," Sally said.

The boy scratched his cheek dourly with a ruler. Unseen by her, he had been attempting to balance the ruler on his chin, juggling it with paper weights—a secret ambition to enter vaudeville.

"What name?" he asked, coldly.

"Nicholas," Sally replied. "I am Mr. Nicholas' sister."

On previous visits, this declaration had caused chaos. Today, however, it struck the boy like a bullet. He dropped the ruler, convulsing with surprise. His peevish mood, born of frustration with his paper weights, instantly evaporated. The discovery that the visitor was the boss's sister transformed his attitude from hostile to winningly polite. He offered her a seat, dusted her coat sleeve with exaggerated courtesy, and complimented the fine morning.

"I want Mr. Kemp," Sally insisted when he mentioned Mr. Nicholas was out.

"Mr. Kemp?" the boy asked, feigning sorrow. "Name's new to me, lady. Never seen him in here."

"But surely..." Sally hesitated, a grim foreboding settling in her stomach. "How long have you been employed here?"

"Just over a month, miss," the boy answered smoothly.

"Hasn't Mr. Kemp been in the office all that time?"

"Nope. Name's new to me. Does he look like anything? What's he look like?"

"He has very red hair," Sally said.

"Never seen him," the boy confirmed. The truth hit Sally hard. Left to his own devices, Ginger had once again made a mess of things. Surely Fillmore wouldn't have dismissed a man under her special protection so thoroughly.

"Where is Mr. Nicholas?" she asked, desperate for information.

"Really out, miss. He went off to White Plains in his automobile half an hour ago."

"White Plains? What for?"

The boy, eager to impress, launched into a monologue about Bugs Butler, a boxer training at the camp. He described Butler as a "hot sketch" who couldn't possibly beat the lightweight champion, Lew Lucas. He spoke with the fervor of a true fan, detailing Butler's failures against Cyclone Mullins and K-leg Binns, betting his own money that Butler couldn't last six rounds.

"Oh, Bugs—Mr. Butler is one of the boxers in this fight that my brother is interested in?" Sally asked, seeing a glimmer of hope.

"That's right. He's going up against the lightweight champ. Lew Lucas is the champ. He's a bird!" the boy enthused.

Sally pressed him for details about finding Ginger at the camp. The boy, delighted by her interest, directed her to the train station, promising she'd find the camp easily. As she left, he resumed his paper weight juggling, his plastic mind already shifting back to business.

Fillmore Nicholas, the manager, was just escaping the training camp, a large barn-like gymnasium filled with expectant onlookers and plutocrats eager to see the final preparations for Bugs Butler's upcoming fight. He had been relieved when the talkative manager, Lester Burrowes, distracted him, allowing Fillmore to slip away to the hotel for a much-needed meal. He hadn't eaten since leaving England.

At the door, he collided with Sally.

"Hallo, Fillmore!" she called softly.

The impact shattered Fillmore's composure. He leaped three inches into the air, his nervous system jolted as if struck by a pole. He stared at her, breathless and bewildered. How could she be here? He had left her in Shropshire, assuming she was safe at Monk's Crofton. Now she was in the heart of a boxing camp in New York.

"Sally!" he croaked. "What... what...?"

"Did I startle you? I'm sorry."

"What are you doing here? Why aren't you at Monk's Crofton?"

"I decided I wanted to get back to America. Circumstances arose which made it pleasanter to leave Monk's Crofton."

"Do you mean to say..." Fillmore persisted, dread filling his voice. "That Carmyle proposed to you and you turned him down?"

Sally flushed. "I don't think it's particularly nice to talk about that sort of thing, but—yes."

Desolation washed over Fillmore. The perfect arrangement, the ideal match between the wealthy, aristocratic Bruce Carmyle and his sister, had been ruined by her caprice. He had envisioned a brother-in-law with a claim on the Carmyle bank balance, a sensible husband for a sensible girl. Now, she had spoiled it all.

"But why?" he asked, his hunger momentarily forgotten.

"Oh, Fill!" Sally sighed, sensing his distress. "I should have thought the reason was obvious."

"You mean you don't like him?"

"I don't know whether I do or not. I certainly don't like him enough to marry him."

Fillmore, starving and heartbroken, urged her to the hotel for food. Sally refused, citing her need to talk. Just then, Lester Burrowes, the manager, approached, wearing a "nobbly" tweed suit and a brown bowler hat.

"Say, Mr. Nicholas, you ain't going'? Bugs is just getting ready to spar." Burrowes glanced at Sally.

"My sister—Mr. Burrowes," Fillmore introduced faintly.

"Pleased to meecher," Burrowes said, his eyes glittering. Before Fillmore could retreat, Burrowes grabbed his coat button, determined to sell his charge. "You've never seen this boy of mine when he was feeling right. Believe me, he's there! He's a wizard. He's a Hindoo!"

Fillmore exchanged a worried look with Sally, who pitied him. She knew she would soon have to explain why she had fired Ginger, but for now, she remembered he was her brother and suffering.

"He's the cleverest lightweight since Joe Gans," Burrowes declared fervently.

"Can he make a hundred and thirty-five ringside without being weak?" Sally asked suddenly.

The effect on Burrowes was instantaneous. He dropped away from Fillmore like an exhausted shellfish, his mouth agape as if a child had asked a mathematician about higher algebra. He had never imagined Sally was a boxing enthusiast.

"It took him fifteen rounds to get a referee's decision over Cyclone Mullins," Sally corrected severely, listing Butler's past defeats.

Burrowes rallied, protesting that the referee was biased and that Butler had won by a mile. He produced a picture postcard of a gaunt, crouching figure and thrust it into Sally's hand. "That's Bugs. Take a slant at that and then tell me if he don't look the goods."

Before Sally could respond, Burrowes called over a youth in a bath-robe. "Bugs, this is Miss Nicholas, come to see you work out."

Bugs Butler, an admirer of the sex, quickened his pace. In the flesh, he was a singularly repellent young man, radiating danger and arrogance. He smirked at Sally, and she forced a polite smile. "I hope you are going to win, Mr. Butler," she said.

"You betcher," he asserted, his chest swelling with complacency.

As the manager ushered him toward the ring, Bugs cast a disparaging glance at the sparse crowd, mostly comprised of unpaid newspapermen. "All right," he said boredly. "I'll go a couple of rounds with Reddy for a starter."

Suddenly, a young man with tousled red hair emerged from the shadows, pulling off a sweater. Sally gasped in astonishment. It was Ginger.

***

The spell was broken by the energetic Mr. Burrowes, who urged his sparring partner, Reddy (Ginger), to hurry. Ginger, embarrassed and awkward, began pulling on his gloves. Sally watched, feeling like an intruder at a strange rite, a puppy wandering into a church.

In the center of the ring, Bugs Butler, now clad in boxing trunks that revealed every rippling muscle, moved with rhythmic nonchalance. To Sally, he looked like a human panther, a machine built for destruction. Two "Wise Guys" in the audience scoffed at his shadow boxing, dismissing it as easy until a real wallop was delivered.

"Ready, Bugs?" Burrowes asked.

Ginger stepped forward.

Bugs attacked with the confidence of a child about to demolish a Noah's Ark. He flicked Ginger lightly over his guard, circling sinuously to show off his footwork. To the experts, it was farcical; to Sally, it was terrifying. She shuddered, gripping the rope tightly.

Then, something solid smote Bugs' nose. He rocked back, astonished and in pain. He had been careless, letting his eyes flicker to check on Sally, and now he paid the penalty. Piqued, he shimmered around the ring, his thoughts turning hard toward Ginger.

Ginger, too, was thinking hard thoughts. He had endured Bugs' pounding before, but today was different. Those careless flicks felt like an insult, a deliberate attempt to humiliate him in front of Sally. He refused to be made an exhibition of. Digging heavily into Bugs' lower ribs, he forced a clinch.

"Say, what the hell d'ya think you're getting at?" Bugs demanded in a heated whisper.

Ginger maintained a pink silence, his jaw set, his temper reaching white heat. He dodged a vicious right and rushed in. A left hook shook him but missed. Suddenly, Bugs, bothered by the ropes and trying to side-step, ran into a swing and fell.

"Time!" shouted the scandalized Burrowes.

The audience gasped. The Wise Guys laughed raucously, urging the fallen warrior to retire. But Sally felt a fierce, cave-womanly rush of happiness sweep through her. She was proud of Ginger. This was not the mild, amiable young man she had known; this was a new Ginger, a stranger who could stand up to a champion.

Bugs rose almost immediately, his motto clear: *A man may be down, but he is never out.* He snarled at his manager, "Like hell it's time!" and launched himself at Ginger in a whirlwind of flying gloves.

To Burrowes, this was a bitter blow. His protégé, usually cool and scientific, was abandoning technique for primitive fury. He was trying too hard, losing his elegance, and making an exhibition of himself. Yet, nothing could stop him. His whole fighting soul was in the effort to destroy Ginger.

Ginger weathered the tempest like a storm-driven ship, bruised and bleeding but fighting hard. Bugs swung wildly, missing vital spots, his own face now streaked with blood. The Wise Guys leaned against each other, one crying in despair.

Then, suddenly, the end came. Fatigue returned to Bugs. His feet, once so subtle, stumbled. A quick feint, a short, jolting stab, and Ginger's guard was down. He swayed in the middle of the ring, hands hanging, knees a-quiver.

Bugs Butler measured his distance, and Sally shut her eyes.

The only true joy, they say, is found in making others happy. By this logic, Bugs Butler should have been radiating pure bliss upon waking from his coma in Jersey City, thirty hours after Lew Lucas had dispatched him in the third round. After all, his inability to block a short left hook followed by a right to the jaw had improved the financial standing of quite a few people.

Mr. Lew Lucas was visibly pleased, as were his seconds (one even kissed him), and the heavy betting crowd who backed the champion. Yet Bugs Butler offered no rejoicing. His peevish demeanor struck a discordant note in the general merriment, his face a heavy frown as he slouched from the ring.

Meanwhile, the happiness he had inadvertently generated continued to spread. Two Wise Guys, unable to attend the fight, received the result on the ticker and declared themselves five hundred dollars richer. A pimpled office boy at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. caused a scene in the subway, whooping gleefully upon reading the news, for he too had been enriched by the brittleness of Mr. Butler's chin. She was not a girl who disliked many people, but she had acquired a lively distaste for Bugs Butler.

Lew Lucas seemed a man after her own heart. If he had been a personal friend of Ginger's, he could not, considering the brief time at his disposal, have avenged him with more thoroughness. Round one had diverted attention to Mr. Butler's left eye; round two had continued the work on his body; and round three had knocked him out. Could anyone have done more? He stepped forward, lurched, and retreated to the safety of the doorframe. For a fleeting second, Sally's artificial composure cracked, pierced by a stab of pure contempt. But it vanished instantly, and she was armored in indifference once more.

"Go to bed, Gerald," she commanded. "You'll feel better in the morning."

Perhaps a glimmer of foresight regarding his morning prospects penetrated Gerald's foggy brain. He winced, his demeanor darkening into a profound melancholy.

"Might not be alive in the morning," he declared solemnly. "Better end it all. End it all!" he repeated, making a sweeping gesture that he abruptly halted as he grabbed the door handle.

Sally had no patience for melodrama.

"Oh, go to bed," she snapped impatiently. The frozen detachment that had held her was melting, replaced by a rising tide of resentment—directed at Gerald for degrading himself, and at herself for ever finding glamour in such a man. It humiliated her to recall how completely she had once surrendered to his personality. Under the sting of this humiliation, she felt hard and pitiless. She dimly sensed a shift within herself; normally, the sight of distress would trigger her sympathy, but Gerald's lamentations about returning to a desk job elicited nothing but coldness—a fact the sufferer noted with a sniffle.

"You're very un-sympathetic," he complained.

"I'm sorry," Sally lied. She marched briskly to the door and pushed it open. Gerald, still clinging to the handle like a drowning man, shuffled out into the hallway, looking like a man whose foundation had just collapsed. He released the handle and wandered uncertainly toward his own door. Finding it ajar, he staggered inside. Sally watched him reach his destination safely before turning to her bedroom, intending to end the night's disturbances with sleep.

Almost immediately, she changed her mind. Sleep was impossible. A feverish restlessness seized her. She donned a kimono and headed to the kitchen to check if her supplies allowed for a glass of hot milk.

She had just recalled presenting the last of the milk to a sandy-colored cat with a purposeful gaze that had slipped through the window earlier that morning, when a muffled crash shattered her thoughts.

She listened intently. The sound seemed to emanate from across the hall. Hurrying to the door, she opened it. Instantly, from the apartment opposite, erupted a perfect fusillade of crashes, each one seeming louder and more terrifying than the last.

There is something about sudden, deafening noises in the dead of night that shatters the most rigid detachment. Moments ago, Gerald's threat of violence had left her unmoved; now, her mind leapt back to his words, and apprehension replaced indifference. Gerald was in an irresponsible mood, capable of anything. Standing in the doorway, Sally felt a momentary spike of panic.

A brief silence followed the explosion, but as she hesitated, the noise erupted again. This time, so loud and compelling, she couldn't delay. She ran across the passage and pounded on the door.

***

Whatever devastation was occurring inside, Gerald had clearly survived it. A moment later, a dragging footstep sounded, and the door swung open. Gerald stood on the threshold, the weak smile back on his face.

"Hullo, Sally!"

Seeing him, disheveled and seemingly unharmed, Sally's brief alarm dissolved, replaced by her usual impatient resentment. On top of everything else, he had apparently frightened her unnecessarily.

"What on earth was all that noise?" she demanded.

"Noise?" Gerald considered the point, mouth agape.

"Yes, noise," Sally snapped.

"I've been cleaning house," Gerald said with the owl-like gravity of a man barely conscious of his surroundings.

Sally pushed past him. The apartment was an almost exact replica of her own, evident proof that Elsa Doland had taken pains to make it pretty and comfortable in a fussy, feminine way. Amateur interior decoration had always been Elsa's hobby. Even in the bleak confines of Mrs. Meecher's boarding house, she had managed to create a certain daintiness that Sally, lacking such talent, had always envied. Elsa's mind ran toward small, fragile ornaments; she was never afraid of over-furnishing. Pictures crowded the walls, china of every description cluttered the tables, a profusion of lamps with multi-colored shades cast a chaotic glow, and plates lined the shelves.

One might say the plates were ranged and the pictures jostled, but it was more accurate to say they had jostled and been ranged, for Sally could only reconstruct the scene by guesswork. She had entered briskly but pulled up short, appalled by the majestic ruin that greeted her. A shell burst in the sitting-room could hardly have caused more havoc.

The psychology of a man with a weak character, fueled by alcohol and thwarted ambition, is difficult to decipher; his moods shift with bewildering speed. Ten minutes prior, Gerald had been drowning in clammy self-pity, but now, a brief, adequate spasm of berserk fury had clearly taken hold. Sally, not being a psychologist, couldn't explain the cause, but the evidence was ocular and undeniable. A heavy niblick, thrown petulantly—or remorsefully—into a corner, revealed the instrument of destruction.

Bleak chaos reigned everywhere. The floor was littered with every conceivable shape and size of broken glass and china. Fragments of pictures, looking as if chewed by a prehistoric beast, lay amidst heaps of shattered statuettes and vases. As Sally moved slowly into the room, china crackled beneath her feet. She surveyed the stripped walls with wonder and turned to Gerald for an explanation.

Gerald had collapsed onto an occasional table, weeping softly again. It had struck him once more that he had been terribly mistreated.

"Well!" Sally gasped. "You've certainly made a good job of it!"

A sharp crack echoed as the occasional table, never designed to bear heavy weights, gave way in a splintering flurry of broken legs under the weight of its master. Sally's mood underwent an abrupt transformation. Few situations in life hold equal potential for tragedy and farce, and it was the ludicrous side of this drama that appealed to her now. Her sense of humor was tickled. If she could have analyzed her feelings, she would have found herself mocking the feeble, sentimental Sally who had once taken this preposterous man seriously. She felt light-hearted and light-headed, sinking into a chair with a gurgling laugh.

The shock of his fall seemed to restore Gerald to something resembling intelligence. He picked himself up from the remains of a set of watercolors, gazing at Sally with growing disapproval.

"No sympathy," he said austerely.

"I can't help it," cried Sally. "It's too funny."

"Not funny," corrected Gerald, his brain beginning to cloud again.

"Why did you do it?"

Gerald returned briefly to his mood of honest indignation, which had strengthened his arm when wielding the niblick. He revisited his grievance.

"Weren't going to stand for it any longer," he said heatedly. "A fellow's wife goes and lets him down... ruins his show by going off and playing in another show... why shouldn't I smash her things? Why should I stand for that sort of treatment? Why should I?"

"Well, you haven't," said Sally, "so there's no need to discuss it. You seem to have acted in a thoroughly manly and independent way."

"That's it. Manly independent." He waggled his finger impressively. "Don't care what she says. Don't care if she never comes back. That woman..."

Sally was not prepared to debate the absent Elsa. The amusing aspect of the affair was fading, replaced by a tired distaste for the sordidness of the whole business. She realized she could not endure Gerald Foster's company much longer. She stood up and spoke decisively.

"And now," she said, "I'm going to tidy up."

Gerald had other views.

"No," he said with sudden solemnity. "No! Nothing of the kind. Leave it for her to find. Leave it as it is."

"Don't be silly. All this has got to be cleaned up. I'll do it. You go and sit in my apartment. I'll come and tell you when you can return."

"No!" Gerald wagged his head.

Sally stamped her foot among the crackling ruins. Suddenly, the sight of him became intolerable.

"Do as I tell you," she cried.

Gerald wavered for a moment, but his brief militant mood was ebbing fast. After a faint protest, he shuffled off, and Sally heard him retreat into her room. She breathed a deep sigh of relief and turned to her task.

A visit to the kitchen revealed a long-handled broom. Armed with this, Sally was soon busy. She was an efficient little person, and presently, out of chaos, a certain order began to emerge. Nothing short of complete re-decoration would ever make the place habitable again, but after half an hour, she had cleared the floor, stacking the fragments of vases, plates, lamp-shades, pictures, and glasses into tiny heaps against the walls. She returned the broom to the kitchen, went back into the sitting-room, flung open the window, and stood looking out.

With a sense of unreality, she perceived that the night had gone. Over the quiet street below brooded that strange, metallic light that ushers in the dawn of a fine day. A cold breeze whispered to and fro. Above the rooftops, the sky was a faint, level blue.

She left the window and started to cross the room. Suddenly, a feeling of utter weakness overcame her. She stumbled to a chair, conscious only of being tired beyond the possibility of further effort. Her eyes closed, and almost before her head touched the cushions, she was asleep.

***

Sally woke. Sunshine streamed through the open window, accompanied by the myriad noises of a city awake and about its business. Footsteps clattered on the sidewalk, automobile horns blared, and she could hear the clank of streetcars passing over the tracks. She could only guess the hour, but it was evident the morning was well advanced. She got up stiffly. Her head ached.

She went into the bathroom, washed her face, and felt better. The dull oppression of a bad night was lifting. She leaned out of the window, reveling in the fresh air, then crossed the passage and entered her own apartment. Stertorous breathing greeted her; she perceived that Gerald Foster had also spent the night in a chair. He was sprawled by the window, legs stretched out, head resting on an armrest, an unlovely spectacle.

Sally stood regarding him for a moment, the distaste she had felt the previous night returning. Yet, mingled with that distaste was a certain elation. A black chapter of her life was closed forever. Whatever the future brought, it

The twilight didn't arrive with a bang; it crept in with the shy, apologetic demeanor of a guest who knows he's overstaying his welcome. It lingered over the western treeline, clutching a fading afterglow, while a new moon hung above the barn like a polished silver sickle. Sally stepped onto the gravel path, bowing three solemn times for luck before pausing to inhale the evening's perfume. Life, she decided, was simply good.

Then, the shadows decided to stop playing hard to get. They buckled down with industrial efficiency, turning the sky a uniform, star-dusted indigo and swallowing the state road whole. Across the meadows, house lights flickered on like distant fireflies. From the kennel direction came a single, sleepy bark, followed by a sharp, challenging squeak from Toto, the small white woolly dog who had been trotting at Sally's heels.

The air was so still that Ginger's footsteps echoed long before he rounded the bend. He was returning from the village—provisions, evening papers, and the wool for Sally's sweater in tow. Though invisible in the gloom, his presence brought a familiar, cozy thrill to Sally's chest.

"Ginger!" she called out.

"What ho!"

Toto scuttled forward with another important squeak, only to be met with a frosty greeting. Ginger, despite his professed love for dogs, had never warmed to Toto. He remembered the day a month prior when Sally, seeing Mrs. Meecher distraught over her pet's lethargy, had begged Ginger to take the invalid to the country.

"It's wonderful what you've done for Toto, angel," Sally said as Ginger sidestepped the dog's enthusiastic leaps. "He's a different dog."

"Bit of luck for him," Ginger replied, his tone cool.

"In all the years I was at Mrs. Meecher's, I never saw him move faster than a stately walk. Now he's running around all day."

"The blighter was overeating from birth," Ginger explained, brightening slightly. "A little judicious dieting fixed him. We'll be able to ship him back next week."

"I shall quite miss him."

"I nearly missed him this morning with a shoe," Ginger admitted casually. "He was up on the kitchen table wolfing bacon, and I took steps."

"My cave-man!" Sally murmured, shaking her head. "I always said you had a frightfully brutal streak. Ginger, what an evening!"

"Good Lord!" Ginger exclaimed suddenly as they crossed the threshold into the kitchen's warm light.

"Now what?"

He stopped, staring at her intently. "Do you know you're looking prettier than when I started down to the village?"

Sally gave his arm a affectionate squeeze. "Beloved! Did you get the chops?"

Ginger froze, horror washing over his face. "Oh, my aunt! I clean forgot them!"

"Oh, Ginger, you old chump. Well, you'll have to go on a diet like Toto."

"I say, I'm most awfully sorry. But I got the wool."

"If you think I'm going to eat wool..."

"Isn't there anything else in the house?"

"Vegetables and fruit."

"Fine! But, of course, if you want chops..."

"Not at all. I'm spiritual. Besides, people say vegetables are good for blood pressure." He paused, a mischievous glint in his eye. "Of course, you forgot to get the mail, too?"

"Absolutely not! I was on to it like a knife. Two letters from fellows wanting Airedale puppies."

"No! Ginger, we're getting on!"

"Pretty bloated," Ginger agreed complacently. "Pretty bloated. If things keep buzzing along like this, we'll get that two-seater. There was a letter for you. Here it is."

"It's from Fillmore," Sally said, examining the envelope as they moved inside. "And about time. Haven't heard from him in months."

She sat down and opened the letter. Ginger hauled himself onto the table, wriggling into a comfortable position to read his evening paper. After skimming the sports page, he lowered it, his gaze resting on Sally's bent head with a profound sense of contentment. Even as a married man of nearly a year, Ginger still felt like he was wandering a magic world, dazed by the sheer impossibility of his bliss. He had seen many things that looked good from afar, but none had held up to close inspection—except this business of marriage.

With Sally as his partner, marriage seemed to be one of the few things in existence without a catch. His honest eyes glowed as he watched her. Sally broke into a little splutter of laughter.

"Ginger, look at this!"

He reached down and took the slip of paper she offered. Bold letters stared back at him:

Ginger frowned, puzzled. "What is it?"

"It's Fillmore."

"How do you mean?"

Sally gurgled with delight. "Fillmore and Gladys have started a little restaurant in Pittsburgh."

"A restaurant!" Shock colored Ginger's voice. Although he knew his brother-in-law, the self-styled "modern Napoleon," had previously crashed spectacularly, Ginger still held a grudging reverence for the Man of Destiny. For Fillmore Nicholas to descend to running a *small* restaurant struck him as almost indecent.

Sally, however, was thrilled. "It's the most splendid idea," she enthused. "It looks like Fillmore is finally amounting to something. They started small, just making pork-pies..."

"Why 'Popp'?" Ginger interrupted, perplexed.

"Just a trade name, silly. Gladys is a wonderful cook; she made the pies while Fillmore sold them. They did so well they've expanded into a full restaurant, and it's a success. Listen to this." Sally turned the page. "'...sound financial footing. Our success has been so instantaneous that I have decided to launch out on a really big scale. It is Big Ideas that lead to Big Business. I am contemplating a vast extension... organizing branches in New York, Chicago, Detroit... each offering as a special feature, in addition to the usual restaurant cuisine, these Popp's Outstanding Pork-pies... That done, I shall sail for England and introduce Popp's Pork-pies there...' Isn't he a little wonder!"

"Dashed brainy chap. Always said so."

"I must say I was uneasy reading that. I've seen so many of Fillmore's 'Big Ideas.' He usually gets something good and then overdoes it until it bursts. However, it's all right now that he's got Gladys. She added a postscript: 'Yes, I don't think!' Just four words, but oh, how comforting to a sister's heart. Thank heaven, she's got poor dear Fillmore well in hand."

"Pork-pies!" Ginger mused, as the pangs of hunger began to gnaw at him. "I wish he'd sent us one of those outstanding little chaps. I could do with it."

Sally stood up and ruffled his red hair. "Poor old Ginger! I knew you'd never be able to stick it. Come on, it's a lovely night. Let's walk to the village and revel at the inn. We're going to be millionaires before we know where we are, so we can afford it."
