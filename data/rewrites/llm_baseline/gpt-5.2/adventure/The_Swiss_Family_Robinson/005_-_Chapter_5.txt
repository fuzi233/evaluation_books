Next morning, while the first stir of breakfast went on in the leafy house—fire crackling low, a pot whispering at the edge of boil, the hens answering one another in brisk, domestic argument—I set myself to the kangaroo’s hide. I wanted it whole, not hacked and patchy, for a skin well taken is a tool in itself: shelter, vessel, clothing, cord. I scraped and cleaned it as carefully as if I were trimming sinew from a haunch for the stew, working by feel and by smell, until the sharp wild tang of the animal gave way to the clean, raw scent of rinsed leather.

Fritz, meanwhile, made all ready for our journey to the wreck. When the time came I called for Ernest and Jack, meaning to leave them strict directions—what to do, what not to do, and above all the old rule that keeps families alive in strange countries: no wandering off without a word.

But directly after breakfast they had vanished.

Their mother, looking out through the green doorway of boughs, could only guess, with that mixture of tenderness and exasperation known to all who keep a household, that the boys—remembering our need—had gone after potatoes. I asked her to reprove them when they returned. Yet hearing they had taken Turk, I forced my uneasiness down. The forest had its own jaws and tempers, but the dog was wise and watchful.

Still, it went against my heart to leave my wife alone with little Franz. I kissed her, promised we would come back laden with “treasures” from the ship, and tried to make my voice sound like certainty instead of hope.

We went on steadily, crossed the bridge at Jackal River, and then—at the very moment I had begun to count the day’s work ahead of us—two figures burst from cover with a whoop.

Jack and Ernest sprang out like sprites of mischief from a thicket where they had been lying in ambush, their faces lit with triumph at the shock they had given their “unsuspecting” father and brother. Their laughter rang against the banks; even the river seemed to chuckle over stones.

It was plain they believed their trick had bought them a place in our boat.

I stopped that notion at once. I could not bring myself to scold them hard—their delight was too honest, their prank too boyish—and yet I would not have our plans ruled by childish cunning. Besides, their disobedience offered me, quite accidentally, a service I had needed.

They must go home. Their mother must not wait with a tightening heart all day, watching the water and listening for oars that did not come. But since they had already come so far, I sent them to collect salt—useful, precious, the quiet guardian of meat and fish.

And I gave them a message I had not had courage to speak aloud at parting: the work aboard the wreck would take longer than I had admitted. If we were to build the raft I had in mind—and build it well, without forcing a second, wasteful journey—we should sleep on the ship and return the next day.

I had meant to say this, but I knew how my wife would object, and my resolve had been to return that very evening. Now necessity tightened the plan into firmness. The boys’ appearance, like a foolish miracle, gave me a messenger.

“Goodbye, boys—take care of yourselves! We’re off!” Fritz shouted, as I stepped into our tub-boat and we pushed away from land.

The current took us briskly out of the bay. The surface of the water carried its own sound—hiss, lap, and that hollow knock when a small wave strikes wood. It was a music far kinder than any city’s hammering. In a world without wheels and iron streets, the sea’s voice could steady a man’s thoughts.

We soon moored safely alongside the wreck. Up her shattered side we scrambled, and stood upon what remained of her deck. Wind moved through her broken timbers with a low, complaining note, as if the ship itself still tried to speak in the old language of ropes and canvas.

I wanted a raft—broad-backed, obedient, able to bear what our little boat could never carry: weight, bulk, awkward angles that would capsize us if we tried to cram them aboard. Empty water-casks offered themselves as if the sea had rolled them there for this very purpose. We closed them tight, shoved them overboard, and set twelve side by side in rows of three, binding them with spars. Over this buoyant foundation we laid planks for a firm floor and built a low bulwark as defense against any sudden slop of wave.

In a short time we had, indeed, a first-rate raft, fit to serve our exile.

By evening it was impossible to return. Fatigue had settled into our arms and backs, and we had lived all day on the light refreshment we carried—dry mouthfuls, swallowed almost without noticing, because we grudged even minutes away from work.

Now, glad that we were not expected, we made our supper from the ship’s provisions. Salt meat, biscuit, and what small comforts could be found in tins and barrels—plain fare, but after hard labor it tasted like a feast. The ship’s stores carried their own smells: brine, flour, old wood, and spices sealed up from another world. We laid ourselves afterward upon spring mattresses—softness so luxurious it felt indecent—remembering our narrow hammocks with something like scorn.

The next morning we attacked the work with new vigor. First we carried off the entire contents of our own cabins. Then we went on to the captain’s room and removed furniture and fittings—doors, window-frames, bolts, bars, locks—everything that could become security for our house. We took the officers’ chests, and those belonging to the carpenter and gunsmith. Their weight was so great we had to empty them in portions, and even then the lifting made our shoulders burn.

One large chest glittered like a shop-window: watches in gold and silver, snuff-boxes, buckles, chains, rings, trinkets of every sort. There was also a box of money. For a little while, the shine held our eyes—human nature is old and easily dazzled. But such finery was a cold comfort in a wild land. Far more precious to us was a case of common knives and forks. I welcomed them like honest friends, better suited to our rough hands than any silver ornament we had brought ashore.

Then my delight rose in earnest: carefully packed, we found a number of young fruit trees. The tickets attached bore names that sounded like home itself spoken aloud—apple, pear, chestnut, orange, almond, peach, apricot, plum, cherry, vine. I touched the damp packing moss around their roots as if it were sacred. These were not merely plants; they were memory made living, a future orchard that could teach our children the taste of the old world even while their tongues learned a new one.

The cargo, meant to supply a distant colony, proved an almost inexhaustible treasure: ironmongery, plumber’s tools, lead, paint, grindstones, cart wheels, all that a forge would need, spades and plough-shares, sacks of maize, peas, oats, wheat, a hand-mill, and even the parts of a saw-mill—each piece numbered so carefully that, had we the strength, we might set it up without confusion.

We stood amid this abundance, bewildered. How does one choose what to save when the sea is ready to swallow the rest? The first storm would finish the wreck; everything left behind would be lost to us forever.

At last, selecting the most useful—grain and fruit trees above all—we loaded raft and boat gradually, sweating and calculating, trying to think like farmers rather than like thieves in a treasure-house.

We added fishing lines, reels, cordage, two harpoons, and a mariner’s compass. Fritz, remembering our encounter with the shark, laid the harpoons in readiness. He flourished one with a boy’s swagger, imagining himself a whaler, and I could not help smiling at the bright, dangerous enthusiasm that made him both a comfort and a risk.

By early afternoon both craft were heavily laden. With the raft in tow, we began the voyage shoreward with real anxiety; one bad twist, one gust, one misjudged pull, and the sea could take our harvest of labor.

But the water remained calm and the wind favorable. We spread the sail, and the motion smoothed into steady progress: the creak of rope, the soft slap along the hull, the low, satisfied groan of wood doing what it was made to do.

Then Fritz asked for the telescope. He had noticed something floating at a distance.

He handed it back, and I looked. A dark shape lay upon the water, motionless—a turtle, asleep on the sea, unconscious of our approach.

“Father—steer towards it!” Fritz cried.

I turned the boat, meaning only to indulge his curiosity, never dreaming what the lad would attempt behind the broad curtain of sail. My eyes were on the water, the raft’s line, the set of the wind—when suddenly a shock ran through the vessel, and I felt the unmistakable thrill of line racing through a reel. Before I could shout, a second shock came, and the boat lurched forward, drawn fast as if seized by some invisible hand.

“Fritz! What are you about?” I cried. “You are sending us to the bottom!”

“I have him—hurrah! I have him safe!” he shouted, wild with triumph.

To my amazement he had struck the turtle with a harpoon. A rope was attached; the creature was towing us—our boat, and our raft, and all our precious salvage—as if it were a toy.

I dropped the sail and seized my hatchet, hurrying forward to cut the line.

“Father! Wait!” Fritz pleaded. “There is no danger—just yet! I promise to cut it myself the instant it is necessary! Let us catch this turtle if we possibly can.”

“My dear boy,” I said, trying to keep my voice from turning into anger, “the turtle will be a very dear bargain if he upsets all our goods into the sea—even if he does not drown us too. For heaven’s sake be careful. I will wait a few minutes; but the instant there is danger, cut the line.”

The turtle made for the open sea. I hoisted the sail again, and the pressure of wind and our resistance turned him. He swung landward, dragging us rapidly. The shore he aimed at lay considerably left of our usual landing-place. The beach there shelved gradually, and at some distance from land we grounded with a sharp shock—hard enough to make my teeth click—yet, by mercy, without a capsize.

The turtle was exhausted. No wonder: it had acted the part of a steam tug, towing two loaded vessels at speed. It struggled to reach land; I leaped into the water, waded up, and with my axe struck it. The creature’s tenacity was dreadful. It did not cease its convulsions until I had severed head from body.

We were not far from Falconhurst. Fritz announced our approach by firing his gun and shouting with glee. While we still secured the boats and dragged the heavy turtle ashore, the whole family appeared in the distance, hastening toward us. Their figures grew larger against the green; even before I could see their faces I heard them—voices carrying across the open air, each one distinct in the clean, unspoiled soundscape, where no mechanical roar dulled the ear.

Our prize—the turtle, the raft, the boat piled high—excited the liveliest interest. But my wife’s chief pleasure was simply seeing us safe. Our night’s absence had worried her; and when she heard, in fuller detail, how we had been dragged at the whim of a harpooned beast, her hand went to her breast as if to press her heart back into its place.

We were anxious to remove some goods before night. The boys ran for the sledge. Lacking an anchor, I contrived to moor our vessels with heavy blocks of iron we had brought. Then came the turtle: such a weight that all of us together could scarcely get it onto the sledge. With the sapling fruit trees added, it made a load fit to break a lesser cart.

We set off for home, talking as we went, the rhythm of our footsteps and the squeal of the sledge runners stitching our day into a story that would be told again—perhaps altered, perhaps embellished—until even danger took on the sheen of family legend.

At Falconhurst the first task was supper: to put the turtle’s flesh into the pot. My wife regarded it as a work of time and difficulty, but I turned the creature on its back, broke the lower shell with the hatchet, and detached a portion from the breast.

“Cook it with a little salt,” I said, “shell and all.”

“But let me first cut away this disgusting green fat,” she said, with a shudder. “See how it sticks all over the meat. No one could eat anything so nasty.”

“Leave the fat—whatever you do!” I cried. “That is the very best part, the delight of epicures. If there is too much, cut some off—it can serve as lard, and the dogs may have the refuse.”

Even here, in exile, taste had its hierarchy, its old arguments. The tongue carries culture the way a seed carries a tree; and what one calls “disgusting” another calls “delicacy,” and neither word truly translates the other’s experience.

“And the handsome shell!” Fritz exclaimed. “I should like to make a water-trough of that, to stand near the brook and be kept always full of clear water. How useful it would be!”

“A capital idea,” I said, “and we may manage it easily, if we can find clay to make a firm foundation.”

“Oh, as to clay,” Jack cried, “I have a grand lump of clay there under that root.”

“Well done, my lad! When did you find it?”

“He found a bed of clay near the river this morning,” said his mother, “and came home in such a mess I had regularly to scrape his clothes and wash him thoroughly!”

“Well, mother,” Jack insisted, “I can only tell you I should never in all my days have found the clay if I had not slipped and fallen amongst it.”

“That I can well believe,” she returned, “only, to hear your talk this morning, one would have thought your discovery of clay the result of very arduous search indeed.”

“When you have ended the question of the clay and the turtle-shell,” said Ernest, “I should like to show you some roots I found today. They are getting rather dry now. They look something like radishes, although the plant itself was almost a bush; but I have not ventured to taste them—though our old sow was devouring them at a great rate.”

“In that you did wisely,” I told him. “Swine eat many things injurious to men. Let me see your roots. How did you discover them?”

“I was rambling in the wood,” he said, “and came upon the sow very busy grubbing under a small bush and eating something ravenously. So I drove her away, and found a number of these roots, which I brought for you to see.”

I took them in my hand and examined them closely—shape, skin, smell, the way the cut surface dulled in the air.

“Indeed, Ernest,” I exclaimed at last, “I am inclined to believe you have made a brilliant discovery. If this proves to be the manioc root, we might lose every other eatable we possess and yet not starve. In the West Indies cakes called cassava bread are made from it. Already having potatoes, we shall be very independent if we can succeed in preparing flour from these roots. But great care must be taken: the juice must be expressed, otherwise the flour may be injurious—even poisonous.”

The word “poisonous” fell among the children like a stone in water; the ripples of caution spread through their faces. Here, ignorance could kill as swiftly as a storm.

“If we can collect a sufficient quantity,” I continued, “we will attempt bread-making. I think I know how to set about it.”

There was still time before dark for another sledge-load, so I went back with the elder boys, leaving Franz with his mother. We walked with hunger sharpening our thoughts, all of us looking forward to the “princely supper” promised in the shade, for the day’s work had been heavy enough to make even ship’s biscuit seem like a dream.

“I have been thinking about my turtle, father,” Fritz said as we went. “Is not the shell valuable? Surely combs and boxes and ornamental things are made of tortoise-shell. It seems a pity to use it for a water-trough.”

“Your turtle,” I told him, “is only fit for eating. Its shell is worthless for ornament. The species prized for tortoise-shell is unfit for food. The shell used for such work is treated with heat; the outer layer peels off, leaving a beautifully marked surface, semi-transparent and capable of high polish.”

The sledge quickly received its second load: chests, four cart-wheels, the hand-mill, and many smaller articles. We hurried home.

My wife met us with relief that bordered on reproach. She said we had been “regularly overworked” for two days. “However, now you are come home to rest,” she said, “and you little think what refreshment awaits you here in the shade. Come and see my cellar!”

She showed us a small cask, half sunk in the ground and sheltered with leaves and branches like a creature she had tucked into a nest.

“You wonder where this came from,” she continued. “I found it myself on the sands today while you were absent. I fancied it was wine of some sort, and I got it up here on purpose for you. The boys are most anxious to know what sort it will prove to be.”

To discover this simply, I inserted a straw at the vent-hole. A sip rose to my mouth—warm, fragrant, honeyed—and I felt at once how taste can call up a whole vanished world. “In all my life,” I declared, “I have never enjoyed a more delicious draught of canary sack.”

My wife’s pleasure was plain; her labor had not been wasted. The boys pressed in with straws, begging a taste. After praising it as I had, I could hardly deny them; I let them come in turns, but soon had to call a halt. Their eagerness turned noisy and reckless, and I reproved them for greediness, warning them of the risk of intoxication. Here, where every sound mattered—the dog’s alarm, the bird’s sudden silence, the night wind’s change—there was danger in dulling the senses for a sweet moment.

Supper was more to the purpose. The turtle proved delicious, rich with its green fat melted into the meat like seasoned lard, and the family ate heartily. The smell of it—sea-brine tamed by fire and salt—filled our leafy rooms and made the tree-house feel, for an hour, less like a refuge and more like a home with customs. Strength returned with the meal. We hauled the spring mattresses up into our sleeping-rooms, and very refreshing slumbers closed the day.

Early next morning I rose without rousing any of the others. I meant to visit the beach; I still doubted the safety of our vessels on an open shore. The dogs, delighted, bounded to meet me. The cocks crowed and flapped; two pretty kids gambolled, tapping their small hooves on the wood. All was life and clear sound—each creature voicing itself into the morning. Only the ass seemed disinclined to begin the day.

As I especially required his services, this was unfortunate.

I put his dreams to flight, harnessed him to the sledge, and left the cow dozing unmilked so as not to wake the household. The world outside our tree was cool, smelling of damp earth and crushed leaf, and the distance carried the hush of a land that had not yet learned to roar.

My fears about the boats were soon dispelled: they were safe. I collected only a light load and returned quickly. The donkey trotted willingly, and both of us were uncommonly ready for breakfast.

Approaching our tree, not a sound was to be heard, not a soul to be seen, though it was broad day. When I climbed up, my good wife started in astonishment at the clatter I made.

“What can have made us oversleep ourselves like this?” she cried. “It must be the fault of those mattresses; they are delightful, but really too lulling—see, the children are sound asleep still!”

With much stretching and many yawns, the boys tumbled down at last, rubbing their eyes and looking half awake. Ernest came last, as usual.

“Come, my boys,” I said, “this will never do. Your beds were too luxurious last night.”

Yet in my own mind I laid blame not only on softness. The captain’s canary had likely helped to hush our senses too thoroughly. I resolved it should be dealt with sparingly in future.

“Now for prayers and breakfast,” I continued, “and then to work. I must have our cargo landed in time to get the boats off with the next tide.”

By dint of hard labor we accomplished it. As soon as the vessels were afloat I got on board with Fritz. The rest turned homeward; but Jack lingered behind, his face pleading so earnestly that I could not resist. I took him with us.

My intention had been simply to take the vessels round to the harbor in Safety Bay. But the calm sea and fine weather tempted me to make another trip to the wreck. It took more time than expected, so that when we reached the ship we could only examine further, gather a few portable things, and then hurry away to catch the sea-breeze before it failed in the evening.

To Jack, rummaging in the hold was new enchantment. Soon a tremendous rattling and clattering announced him. He came pushing a wheelbarrow, eyes bright, cheeks flushed with victory, delighted at having found so “capital” a thing for bringing home potatoes.

Fritz followed with still more important news. He had found, packed carefully within partitions, what appeared to be the separate parts of a pinnace—rigging and fittings complete, even to a couple of small brass guns. It was indeed a great discovery. I hastened to see, and he was right. My pleasure, however, was tempered by the thought of the labor required to assemble such a craft fit for sea.

We had barely time to eat and hurry into the boat. Our new acquisitions included a copper boiler, iron plates, tobacco-graters, two grindstones, a small barrel of powder, another of flints, two wheelbarrows besides Jack’s—he guarded his as if it were a pet.

As we drew near shore we were surprised to see a line of little figures ranged along the water’s edge, gazing fixedly at us. They seemed dressed in dark coats and white waistcoats, and stood quite still with arms hanging, except that now and then one extended them gently, as though longing to embrace us.

“Ah! Here at last come the pigmy inhabitants of the country to welcome us!” I cried, laughing.

“Oh, father!” Jack exclaimed. “I hope they are Lilliputians! I once read in a book about them, so there must be such people—you know—only these look rather too large.”

“You must be content to give up the Lilliputians and accept penguins, my dear Jack,” I said. “We have not before seen them in such numbers. Ernest knocked one down, if you remember, soon after we landed. They swim excellently, but on land they are helpless—neither flying nor running.”

We were nearing shallows as I spoke. Jack sprang out, waded ashore, and with his stick felled half a dozen, right and left, either stunned or dead at his feet. The rest escaped into the water, dived, and vanished.

As these penguins are disagreeable food, with a strong oily taste, I was sorry he had attacked them. But when we examined the fallen, some rose from their swoon and began solemnly to waddle away. We caught them, tied their feet together with long grass, and laid them on the sand until we were ready to start.

The three wheelbarrows each received a load. The live penguins, seated gravely, were trundled along by Jack, and away we went at a great rate.

The unusual noise of our approach set the dogs barking furiously. When they recognized us, they rushed forward with such forceful demonstrations of delight that little Jack—already struggling with his barrow—was fairly upset, penguins and all. It was absurd to see him leap up and cuff the dogs soundly for their boisterous welcome.

This scene, and the examination of our burdens, caused great merriment. The tobacco-graters and iron plates puzzled everyone.

I sent the boys to catch some geese and ducks and told them to fasten a penguin to each by the leg; it seemed worth trying to tame them.

While we had been away my wife had not been idle. She had stored potatoes and also manioc root. I praised her industry, and little Franz—bursting with secrets—cried, “Ah, father! I wonder what you will say when mother and I give you some Indian corn, and melons, and pumpkins and cucumbers!”

“Now, you little chatterbox!” she cried. “You have let out my secret! I was to have the pleasure of surprising your father when my plants were growing up.”

“Ah, the poor disappointed little mother,” I said. “Never mind—I am charmed to hear it. Only tell me: where did those seeds come from?”

“Out of my magic bag, of course!” she replied. “Each time I have gone for potatoes, I have sown seeds in the ground dug up to get them, and I have planted potatoes also.”

“Well done, you wise little woman!” I cried. “You are a model of prudence and industry!”

“But,” she continued, eyeing our haul, “I do not half like the appearance of those tobacco-graters. Is it possible you are going to make snuff? Pray let us make sure of abundance of food for our mouths before we think of our noses!”

“Make your mind easy,” I said. “I have not the remotest intention of introducing the dirty, ridiculous habit of snuffing into your family. But treat my graters with respect: they are to be the means of providing you with the first fresh bread you have seen this many a long day.”

“What possible connection can there be between bread and tobacco-graters?” she said. “And to talk of bread where there are no ovens is only tantalizing.”

“Ah, you must not expect real loaves,” I answered. “But on these flat iron plates I can bake flat cakes—scones—which will serve us as excellent bread. I mean to try at once what I can do with Ernest’s roots. And first of all, I want you to make me a nice strong canvas bag.”

She willingly undertook it, though I could see she had little faith in my baking. While she set to work she also set on a potful of potatoes, as if to make sure a meal would exist whether my cassava scheme succeeded or not.
