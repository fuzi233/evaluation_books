CHAPTER XV. The next day, Mrs. Dashwood paid a visit to Lady Middleton, accompanied by two of her daughters. Marianne, however, declined to join them, offering some light excuse about tasks that needed attending to. Her mother, who had suspected—perhaps rightly—that Willoughby had promised to call during their absence, was content to let her stay behind.

Upon returning from the park, they found Willoughby’s curricle and servant waiting at the cottage gate. Mrs. Dashwood smiled, certain her guess had been correct. Everything so far unfolded as she had expected. But when they stepped into the house, they encountered something entirely beyond her imagination.

No sooner had they entered the hall than Marianne rushed out of the parlour, clearly overwhelmed with grief, her handkerchief pressed tightly against her eyes. Without acknowledging them, she hurried upstairs. Alarmed and bewildered, the others followed into the room she had just left—only to find Willoughby standing alone, leaning against the mantelpiece, his back turned.

He turned at their entrance, and though he tried to appear composed, his face bore the unmistakable weight of sorrow that had consumed Marianne.

“Is anything wrong?” Mrs. Dashwood exclaimed, stepping forward. “Is she unwell?”

“I hope not,” he replied, forcing a smile. “But I am the one who may be ill—because I’m suffering a great disappointment.”

“Disappointment?”

“Yes. I must leave my engagement with you. This morning, Mrs. Smith summoned me on urgent business to London. I’ve just received my orders and bid farewell to Allenham. As a final gesture of cheerfulness, I came here to say goodbye.”

“To London? And this very morning?”

“Almost immediately.”

“This is most unfortunate,” Mrs. Dashwood said gently. “But Mrs. Smith must be grateful for your service—and I trust her affairs won’t keep you away long.”

Willoughby flushed slightly. “You are very kind,” he murmured, “but I don’t expect to return to Devonshire soon. My visits to Mrs. Smith are never repeated within a year.”

“And Mrs. Smith is your only friend? Allenham the only home where you’re welcome?” Mrs. Dashwood asked, her voice tinged with reproach. “For shame, Willoughby—can you truly wait for an invitation here?”

His colour deepened. He looked down, unable to meet her gaze. “You are too generous,” he whispered.

Mrs. Dashwood glanced at Elinor, surprised. Elinor felt equal shock. For a moment, silence hung heavy in the room.

Then Mrs. Dashwood spoke again. “I only wish to add, dear Willoughby, that Barton Cottage will always welcome you. I won no longer press you to return quickly—since you alone know what might please Mrs. Smith. On that matter, I shall not question your judgment or doubt your intentions.”

“My obligations,” Willoughby answered, flustered, “are such that—I dare not even hope—” He broke off.

Mrs. Dashwood, stunned, could not respond. Another silence fell—until Willoughby, with a faint, pained smile, said, “It’s foolish to linger like this. I’ll not torment myself any longer by staying among friends whose company I can no longer enjoy.” With that, he hastily took leave and left the room.

They watched him step into his carriage. Within minutes, it vanished from sight.

Mrs. Dashwood, overwhelmed, retreated to her chamber, needing solitude to process the suddenness of his departure. Elinor, too, was deeply troubled. She replayed every detail—the abruptness, the forced cheer, the reluctance to accept their hospitality—each trait unlike the man she knew. Was it possible he had never meant to marry Marianne? Or had they quarreled? The agony Marianne had shown upon leaving the room suggested a serious rift, though Elinor couldn’t imagine how such a thing could happen between two so deeply devoted.

Yet whatever the cause, Marianne’s anguish was real. Elinor pitied her sister, knowing she wasn’t merely grieving—but feeding her sorrow, perhaps believing it a duty, a mark of love.

About half an hour later, Mrs. Dashwood returned, her eyes red but her expression calm. “Our dear Willoughby is now miles from Barton,” she said softly, sitting down to sew. “And with what a heavy heart he travels!”

“It’s all so strange,” Elinor murmured. “Gone in the blink of an eye. Last night he was joyful, tender, full of affection. And now—just ten minutes’ notice, and gone forever? Without meaning to come back? There must be more to it than he told us. He didn’t speak like himself. _You_ noticed it too, didn’t you? What could have happened? Did they argue?”

“It wasn’t lack of desire,” Mrs. Dashwood insisted. “I saw plainly enough—he couldn’t accept. I’ve thought it through, and everything makes sense now.”

“Indeed?” Elinor asked, skeptical.

“Yes. I believe Mrs. Smith suspects Willoughby’s feelings for Marianne, disapproves of the match—perhaps because she has other plans for him—and wants him removed. The business trip is just an excuse. He knows she disapproves, so he dares not confess their engagement. And given his dependence on her, he must submit. That’s why he leaves for London, and won’t return soon.”

“You think so?” Elinor said quietly.

“I do. And though you’ll likely dismiss it, I won’t be swayed. I believe it fully. But tell me—what would you suggest instead?”

“Nothing,” Elinor admitted. “You’ve already guessed my answer.”

“So you’d say it might or might not have happened,” Mrs. Dashwood sighed. “Oh, Elinor, how difficult you are! You’d rather assume the worst than accept kindness. You’d prefer to see misery in Marianne and guilt in poor Willoughby than allow for a reasonable explanation. You insist on blaming him simply because he didn’t part with the usual warmth. Can’t you consider that he might be crushed by recent pain? That he might be acting under pressure? That there could be good reasons—reasons he cannot yet share?”

“I don’t blame him,” Elinor said slowly. “But I do wonder. It’s not that I doubt his character—far from it. I love him dearly. But still, I find it hard to believe he would leave without saying more. Secrecy may be necessary—but it doesn’t feel like *his* way.”

“Then you admit my reasoning holds some truth?” Mrs. Dashwood asked, relieved.

“I do. If they are engaged—and I believe they are—then secrecy from Mrs. Smith is wise. And if so, Willoughby should avoid Devonshire for now. But that doesn’t explain why they’ve kept it from *us*.”

“From us?” Mrs. Dashwood laughed softly. “Elinor, do you accuse them of hiding it? After all we’ve seen? After every glance, every gesture, every tender moment? Have you not seen how he looks at her? How he treats us all? Hasn’t his behaviour spoken louder than words? Hasn’t he asked for my consent every day in his manner, his care, his devotion? How can you doubt their engagement? How could Willoughby leave her—perhaps for months—without telling her he loves her? Without sharing their hopes?”

“I confess,” Elinor said, “that every sign points to an engagement—except one: their complete silence on the matter. To me, that outweighs everything else.”

“How strange!” Mrs. Dashwood exclaimed. “After all that has passed, you still suspect? Do you think he’s been pretending? That he doesn’t truly care for her?”

“No,” Elinor said firmly. “I believe he loves her. Deeply. But perhaps too much to show it now.”

“Then why does he seem so indifferent? So careless of the future?”

“Because,” Elinor said, “he may have just left her in agony. He may know he must go, and yet fear offending Mrs. Smith. And he may know that refusing our invitation—saying he won’t return—will seem cold, even cruel. So he’s torn. Embarrassed. Disturbed. A plain explanation would have been better—but I won’t judge him harshly for not giving one. His character is still noble. I only wish he’d been more open.”

“You speak wisely,” Mrs. Dashwood nodded. “Willoughby is not a man to be doubted. Though we’ve known him briefly, he’s respected by all. If he were free to marry at once, yes—it would be odd to leave without telling us. But he isn’t. Their marriage is uncertain. Secrecy, for now, may be wise.”

Their conversation was interrupted by Margaret’s entrance. Elinor sat quietly, reflecting on her mother’s words. She saw the logic in them, and began to hope they might be right.

They saw nothing of Marianne until dinner. When she finally entered the room, she sat silently, her eyes swollen and red. She avoided everyone’s gaze, ate nothing, and said nothing. Only when her mother gently squeezed her hand did her composure break. Tears flooded over, and she fled the room.

That evening, Marianne remained in a state of deep despair. She had no strength to control herself—nor any will to try. Even the slightest mention of Willoughby sent her into fresh waves of grief. Her family, watching helplessly, tried to comfort her—but found themselves drawn inevitably toward the subject that haunted her soul.
