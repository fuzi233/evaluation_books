Every step Harry Warrington took north toward Pennsylvania brought with it grimmer, more confirmed accounts of the British disaster that had shattered the colony’s hopes. The two celebrated veteran regiments, hardened by campaigns across Scotland and continental Europe, had broken and fled before an enemy most of them had barely glimpsed, all their parade-ground discipline and vaunted valour useless against a band of Indigenous raiders and a handful of French regulars dug into the brush. The ill-fated General Braddock had fought with reckless, unbreakable courage: four horses were shot out from under him, he sustained two wounds before the final mortal blow that ended his life three days after the rout.

A dozen different passersby recounted the slaughter to Harry as he rode: the fording of the Monongahela, the long, snaking line of redcoats winding through unbroken wilderness, the first crack of unseen musket fire ahead, the gunnery teams screaming as they scrambled to unlimber cannon to clear the path, then the murderous, unceasing fusillade pouring from every oak thicket and rhododendron patch that cut down at least half the expedition before most men had laid eyes on their attackers.

Then the rumor reached him: one of the General’s Virginian aides-de-camp lay feverish, wracked by exhaustion, at Colonel Dunbar’s fallback camp. It might be his brother George, the man said. Harry spurred his horse until the miles blurred beneath its hooves, and stumbled at last into the tent where the sick officer lay. It was not George. It was George Washington. The young colonel admitted that the pain of seeing Harry arrive empty of news of his brother cut sharper than his fever ever had.

Washington dared not tell Harry the whole truth at first. For three days after the battle he had never left Braddock’s side. On that fatal 9th of July, he had sent George forward with a written order for the vanguard, and the young volunteer had never returned. After Braddock drew his last breath, Washington had ridden back alone to the killing ground, searching for his missing friend. The bodies left unretrieved had all been stripped, hideously mutilated by looting raiders. He had buried one under a cairn of stones that he had believed was George’s, and the anguish of that search was what had laid him low.

“Ah! If you had truly loved him, you would have brought him back, dead or alive!” Harry cried, half-maddened by grief. Nothing would satisfy him but that he too must walk the battlefield. He paid two seasoned frontiersmen to guide him, forded the river where Braddock’s column had crossed, and tramped the full length of that cursed stretch of wilderness. No enemy lingered there now, but scavenger birds gorged on the mangled, festering remains scattered under the trees. Save for the peaceful, soft-smiling face of his grandfather laid out in the great hall of Castlewood years before, Harry had never seen death. The horrific spectacle made him turn away, retching, his whole frame shaking with loathing and dread. The empty woods could give him no answer. He tried to persuade his guides to fly a white flag and accompany him to the distant French fort where the victorious enemy had retreated, but they refused flat. The French might honour a truce, they told him, but their Indigenous allies would not. “Keep your scalp for your mother, young gentleman,” the lead guide said. “She has already lost one son to this campaign, no need for her to lose another.”

When Harry stumbled back to Dunbar’s encampment, the fever took him in full force. He raved in delirium for days, his old loyal servant Dempster tending him through every crisis, fully convinced the widow Esmond would lose both her boys before the week was out. But Harry rallied at last, weak as a newborn lamb, and Washington and Dempster both escorted him home to Castlewood.

A servant had ridden ahead to announce their arrival. Mrs. Mountain the housekeeper ran out first, smothering Harry in tears and embraces, barely sparing Colonel Washington a nod of recognition, until her little daughter Fanny crept up behind the young officer with her hands behind her back, and piped up unthinking: “Why have you not brought George back too?” Luckily, the press of Mrs. Mountain’s sobbing hugs and endearments kept Harry from hearing the cruel question.

Dempster was welcomed warmly by the two ladies, but when Harry insisted that Washington, who had nursed him through his fever and ridden hundreds of miles to bring him home, stay for rest and refreshment, Mrs. Mountain pulled him aside to whisper that Madam Esmond had fled the parlour the second she heard who was accompanying her son, swearing she could not bear to look upon Mr. Washington’s face. Harry was stunned—Washington was almost a member of their household, had been like an elder brother to George for years. He ran up the stairs, past the door he had been deliberately avoiding, the door to George’s bedchamber, when his mother burst out of that very room and folded him to her chest, pulling him inside. Every object in the chamber was exactly as George had left it: his book of psalms laid open on the bedcover, his half-written letter to Harry on the desk, his scuffed riding boots propped by the empty fireplace.

“My poor child, how thin you are, how haggard!” she cooed. “Never mind, a mother’s care will make you well again. You were nobly brave to go searching for your brother through all that danger and sickness. If others had been half as faithful as you, George would be here now. But he is not dead— I know he is not. Two nights running, I saw my own father in a dream, and he told me my boy is a prisoner among the western nations. He will come back to us.” Harry noted, bewildered, that his mother was praising her eldest son more tenderly than he had ever heard her speak of him when George was home under her roof.

But when he begged her to come downstairs and welcome Washington, who had risked his own frail convalescence to escort Harry home, her excitement hardened into sharp, cold anger. She swore she would never touch that man’s hand. He had promised her he would guard George with his own life, and George was gone, while Washington had returned entirely unharmed.

“He gave me his own bed when I was at my sickest, mother,” Harry protested, confused. “He was still half delirious with fever himself, and he thought only of me. How can you blame him for George’s loss?”

A friend? A pretty friend! she sneered. Of all Braddock’s aides, he was the only one to come back without a scratch on him. The brave and noble die, and he walks away home safe.

She did descend the stairs at last, leaning on Harry’s far-too-weak arm, and swept into the parlour where Washington sat with a frigid, ceremonious curtsey. She let one of her small hands rest in his for barely a heartbeat, told him she grieved their meeting had to take place in such unhappy circumstances, thanked him for seeing Harry home, then made it abundantly clear Castlewood was no place for him in her current state of distress. She bade him carry her compliments to his own mother, and to the young widow Martha Custis and her children, then swept away up the stairs again, leaving the colonel standing dumbfounded in the parlour. He mounted his horse and rode off before the hour was out.

A heavy, brooding gloom settled over Castlewood after that. Madam Esmond refused to order formal mourning for George, swearing she would not tempt fate by acting as if he was dead when her father’s dream had promised his return, but every corner of the great plantation house felt draped in shadow. A fortnight after the defeat, George’s personal servant Sady stumbled back to the estate, maimed by a musket ball through the leg, and hid in the slave quarters for days too terrified to face his mistress, for he had no news of his master beyond the moment he saw George ride forward toward the enemy lines on the morning of the battle, and never come back. The neighbours whispered Madam Esmond laboured under a dangerous delusion, but her certainty was so fierce that even Harry and the rest of the household half found themselves believing it. She swore that the honour of the Esmond family would not suffer a man who had broken his sacred promise to protect her son to ever set foot on her land again. She laughed at pleas that Washington’s coat and hat had been riddled with bullet holes: “I could step into my study this instant, take my father’s old duelling pistols, and fire two shots straight through my finest paduasoy gown, and walk away unharmed. That is no proof of any real danger he faced.”

Harry, torn between gratitude to Washington and love for his mother, suffered endlessly. He would sneak out to meet his old friend at county court, horse races, or the taverns of the nearest small town, and every time Madam Esmond found out she flew into a rage, calling him ungrateful, willing to forget his perfect, noble brother for a tall, gawky, fox-hunting colonel who swore too much. The constant strife ate at Harry’s health, and he fell ill over and over again with recurrent fevers no local Peruvian bark could fully cure. The colony’s physicians finally ordered a long sea voyage, to get him far away from the oppressive grief and tension hanging over Castlewood. Madam Esmond at first planned to sail with him, but the two quarrelled sharply over Washington, over George, and it was settled Harry would travel to England alone. He boarded a ship at the James River, landed at Bristol, and only put on his full black mourning suit for his brother exactly one year to the day of Braddock’s defeat, the 9th of July, just as he and his mother had agreed.

He told this whole long, sad story to his newly found English relative, old Madame de Bernstein, hanging his head in shame, and admitted that for all Castlewood’s wealth and comfort, all his mother’s love, he had been glad to run away just to breathe freely. After Harry left, Madam Esmond abandoned the old plantation to an overseer, moved to the fast-growing new town of Richmond, held card assemblies and entertained every prominent preacher in the colony, and set up her small, imperious little court there, leaving the great empty house of Castlewood behind her. We leave her there for a time, while Harry travels across England, chasing his own fortune, and still carrying a faint, stubborn flicker of hope that one day, word would arrive that George had come home at last.
