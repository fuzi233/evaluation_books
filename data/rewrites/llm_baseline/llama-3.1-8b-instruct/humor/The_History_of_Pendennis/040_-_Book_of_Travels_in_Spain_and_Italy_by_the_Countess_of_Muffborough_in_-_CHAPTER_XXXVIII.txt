As whispers of Lady Clavering's vast fortune spread throughout London, the city's elite couldn't help but speculate about the sources of her wealth. Some claimed she inherited an empire of indigo factories, while others whispered about her lucrative connections to Indian princes, who paid handsome sums for loans contracted by her father. Her bank account, rumored to be shrouded in secrecy, was the subject of fervent discussion among those who had caught wind of its existence. A supposed envoy from an Indian prince, Colonel Altamont, was said to be in the city, attempting to negotiate the sale of the Nawaub of Lucknow's prized nose-ring diamond, 'the light of the Dewan.' 

It seemed Lady Clavering's reputation preceded her, as people spoke of her under the title of the Begum, even before her arrival in the capital. This phenomenon was, in part, a testament to Britain's esteemed tradition of welcoming individuals of merit, regardless of birth. As Delolme and Blackstone had so aptly put it, our society prided itself on admitting talent into its ranks, where the humblest-born could rise to join the ranks of the nobility alongside esteemed families like the Cavendishes and Stanleys. While our aristocracy was indeed discerning and protective of its privileges, it was also known to extend its hospitality to those who demonstrated sufficient wealth, offering them a warm welcome as a reflection of their standing.

Meanwhile, the Chevalier Strong, who had overseen the preparations for Lady Clavering's country estate, applied his refined sensibilities to the decoration of her London townhouse. He consulted with fashionable upholsterers, carefully selecting the finest furnishings and artwork for the space. Strong's personal chambers, however, received equal attention, with the Baronet's loyal servant and confidant stocking his cellar with an impressive selection of fine wines. As his friends marveled at the elegance of his new quarters, Strong's acquaintances would often remark on the quality of the claret that accompanied their meals together. Now, the Chevalier felt truly in his element, enjoying the comforts of his snug little apartment at Shepherd's Inn, where he was attended by a former Spanish Legionnaire, a man he had befriended during his military adventures and later elevated to the position of personal servant.
