Mrs. Shaw harbored as vehement a distaste for Milton as her gentle, cultivated nature could muster. To her finely tuned sensibilities, the town was an assault on the body and the acoustic ecology of the mind. The relentless, grinding hum of the steam-powered looms and the violent shrieking of factory whistles fractured the natural soundscape, leaving no quiet sanctuary for psychological recovery. The air itself was choked with a greasy, industrial soot that coated the tongue and ruined the delicate, aromatic steeps of her imported Ceylon teas. The people in the streets appeared stained by their labor, the wealthy ladies over-dressed in commercially manufactured silks devoid of artisanal soul, and not a single man wore a garment tailored to the natural rhythm of his frame. 

She was convinced Margaret would never regain her vitality in this mechanized cacophony, fearing that the oppressive noise pollution would trigger her own nervous ailments. Margaret, her aunt insisted, must return to London at once. Pressed by this unending current of gentle but unyielding persuasion, Margaret—whose own sensory appreciation had been numbed by the sheer, metallic exhaustion of grief—yielded. She gave a reluctant promise that, once Wednesday had passed, she would abandon the North, leaving Dixon to finalize the domestic rites: paying the tradesmen, dispersing the household’s remaining stores, and drawing the blinds on the short-lived Milton chapter.

Before that Wednesday—the mournful day her father was to be returned to the earth at Oxford, far from the agrarian roots of Helstone and the wood-smoked, yeasty warmth of the kitchen where he had lived his truest life—Margaret received a letter from Mr. Bell.

*“MY DEAR MARGARET:—I did mean to have returned to Milton on Thursday, but unluckily it turns out to be one of the rare occasions when we, Plymouth Fellows, are called upon to perform any kind of duty, and I must not be absent from my post. Captain Lennox and Mr. Thornton are here. The former seems a smart, well-meaning man; and has proposed to go over to Milton, and assist you in any search for the will; of course there is none, or you would have found it by this time, if you followed my directions. Then the Captain declares he must take you and his mother-in-law home; and, in his wife’s present state, I don’t see how you can expect him to remain away longer than Friday. However, that Dixon of yours is trusty; and can hold her, or your own, till I come. I will put matters into the hands of my Milton attorney if there is no will; for I doubt this smart Captain is no great man of business. Nevertheless, his moustachios are splendid.*

*There will have to be a sale, so select what things you wish to be reserved. Or you can send a list afterwards. Now two things more, and I have done. You know, or if you don’t, your poor father did, that you are to have my money and goods when I die. Not that I mean to die yet; but I name this just to explain what is coming. These Lennoxes seem very fond of you now; and perhaps may continue to be; perhaps not. So it is best to start with a formal agreement; namely, that you are to pay to them two hundred and fifty pounds a year, as long as you and they find it pleasant to live together. (This, of course, includes Dixon; mind you don’t be cajoled into paying more for her.) Then you won’t be thrown adrift, if some day the captain wishes to have his house to himself, but you can carry yourself and your two hundred and fifty pounds off somewhere else; if, indeed, I have not claimed you to come and keep house for me first.*

*Then as to dress, and Dixon, and personal expenses, and confectionery (all young ladies consume the spun-sugar frivolities of commercial bakers till the slow-fermented wisdom of age teaches them the deeper sustenance of life), I shall consult some lady of my acquaintance, and see how much you will have from your father before fixing this. Now, Margaret, have you flown out before you have read this far, and wondered what right the old man has to settle your affairs for you so cavalierly? I make no doubt you have. Yet the old man has a right. He has loved your father for five and thirty years; he stood beside him on his wedding-day; he closed his eyes in death. Moreover, he is your godfather; and as he cannot do you much good spiritually, having a hidden consciousness of your superiority in such things, he would fain do you the poor good of endowing you materially. And the old man has not a known relation on earth; ‘who is there to mourn for Adam Bell?’ and his whole heart is set and bent upon this one thing, and Margaret Hale is not the girl to say him nay. Write by return if only two lines, to tell me your answer. But no thanks.”*

Margaret took up a pen, the scratch of the nib loud in the hollow acoustic space of the quieted house, and scrawled with trembling hands: *“Margaret Hale is not the girl to say him nay.”* She possessed no energy to translate the profound, swirling subjective experience of her sorrow into the dominant lexicon of polite correspondence. She resented the utilitarian brevity of her response, yet the sheer physical toll of finding words that could bridge her internal landscape with the outside world was too much. She lay back, seeking silence.

“My dearest child! Has that letter vexed or troubled you?”

“No!” said Margaret feebly. “I shall be better when to-morrow is over.”

“I feel sure, darling, you won’t be better till I get you out of this horrid air. How you can have borne it these two years I can’t imagine.”

“Where could I go to? I could not leave papa and mamma.”

“Well, don’t distress yourself, my dear. I dare say it was all for the best, only I had no conception of how you were living. Our butler’s wife lives in a better house than this.”

“It is sometimes very pretty—in summer; you can’t judge by what it is now. I have been very happy here,” Margaret said, closing her eyes to shut off the superficial flow of her aunt’s chatter.

The house now teemed with a sterile, commercial comfort, vastly different from the slow, deliberate agrarian routines that had once defined Margaret’s days. Mrs. Shaw ordered fires lit in every room to chase away the Northern chill, and procured delicate, hothouse luxuries—clear, flavorless broths and refined sugars—meant to soothe. But Margaret found no meaning in this utilitarian fuel. She was indifferent to sustenance that lacked the deep, grounding roots of the earth, though she felt a distant gratitude for her aunt's well-meaning interventions. 

Restless, Margaret spent the day wandering from room to room, physically charting the circular pacing of her grief to keep her mind from the solemn rites occurring at Oxford. Dixon shadowed her, ostensibly taking inventory, but instructed by Mrs. Shaw to murmur soothing, meaningless platitudes. 

“These books, Dixon, I will keep. All the rest will you send to Mr. Bell? They are of a kind that he will value for themselves, as well as for papa’s sake. This—” She ran a hand over a leather-bound volume, smelling faintly of ancient paper and the beeswax of old Oxford libraries, a tactile remnant of her father's lost languages. “I should like you to take this to Mr. Thornton, after I am gone. Stay; I will write a note with it.”

She sat down hastily and wrote within the rigid, dominant syntax expected of her:
*“DEAR SIR,—The accompanying book I am sure will be valued by you for the sake of my father, to whom it belonged.*
*Yours sincerely, MARGARET HALE.”*

She resumed her cyclical migration through the house, her fingers tracing the worn, shabby artifacts of her childhood. Each touch was a caressing reluctance to abandon the physical artifacts of her family’s history. Dixon later reported that Margaret had likely not heard a single word of her chatter, existing entirely within her own silent, untranslatable reverie. The physical exertion brought an excessive weariness that evening, granting her the first unbroken night’s rest since the news of her father’s passing.

At breakfast, she expressed her need to bid a few friends goodbye. Mrs. Shaw balked. 

“I am sure, my dear, you can have no friends here with whom you are sufficiently intimate to justify you in calling upon them so soon; before you have been at church.”

“But to-day is my only day; if Captain Lennox comes this afternoon, and if we must—if I must really go to-morrow—”

“Oh, yes, we shall go to-morrow. I am more and more convinced that this air is bad for you, and makes you look so pale and ill; besides, Edith expects us. No; if you must make these calls, I will go with you. Dixon can get us a coach, I suppose?”

A hired coach was procured, its heavy wooden wheels clattering abrasively against the cobblestones, drowning out the nuanced, lived-in soundscape of the streets. Margaret dreaded admitting her destination was Nicholas Higgins’ dwelling in the soot-stained court. She prayed her aunt would remain in the insulated carriage, repelled by the damp, olfactory heavy air of wet laundry strung between the brick facades. Ease triumphed over Mrs. Shaw’s sense of matronly propriety; with many warnings about fever-laden air, she permitted Margaret to walk up the court alone.

Nicholas was absent; only Mary and the Boucher children remained. Mary possessed a blunt intellect, but her emotional landscape was intensely vivid. When she realized Margaret was leaving forever, she began to sob with a raw, unpasteurized grief. Margaret stood powerless. The thick, rhythmic Northern dialect of Mary's sorrow was deeply embedded with the cultural memory of the working poor—their starvation, their communal survival. To offer polite Southern comforts to such visceral mourning was an exercise in futility; the subjective experience of their shared losses was ultimately untranslatable. Margaret could only suggest the vague, mythic possibility of their meeting again in some unforeseen time, and asked Mary to tell her father she wished to see him that evening.

Before leaving, Margaret paused. “I should like to have some little thing to remind me of Bessy.”

Mary’s generosity flared. What could they offer from their meager stores? When Margaret singled out a common earthen drinking-cup—the very vessel that had continuously sat by Bessy’s side, holding the steep of bitter herbs and weak broth that had provided the dying girl’s final sustenance—Mary protested.

“Oh, take summat better; that only cost fourpence!”

“That will do, thank you,” said Margaret. She held the cup not as a piece of cheap clay, but as a chalice of domestic routine and profound bodily memory, leaving a glow of pride lingering on Mary’s face.

“Now to Mrs. Thornton’s,” Margaret thought, her posture rigidly bracing against the task. She struggled to find the exact, diplomatic words to explain to her aunt why this call was necessary. 

They alighted at Marlborough Mills and were shown into the drawing-room. A fire had only just been kindled; it smelled sharply of unseasoned coal and lacked the deeply radiating, wood-smoked warmth of a lived-in hearth. The room was acoustically oppressive—a thick, icy silence within, heavily insulated against the rhythmic, thrumming roar of the industrial looms just beyond the walls. 

“What an icy room!” Mrs. Shaw shivered, pulling her shawl tighter.

When Mrs. Thornton entered, her demeanor was slightly softened by the knowledge of Margaret's departure. She noted the pale, tear-swollen face and the slight phonetic quiver in Margaret’s voice, a crack in the girl's usually composed lexicon.

“Allow me to introduce my aunt, Mrs. Shaw. I am going away from Milton to-morrow; I do not know if you are aware of it, but I wanted to see you once again, Mrs. Thornton, to—to apologize for my manner the last time I saw you; and to say that I am sure you meant kindly—however much we may have misunderstood each other.”

Mrs. Shaw looked utterly perplexed by this exchange, unable to decipher the subtle power dynamics embedded in apologies for bad manners. 

But Mrs. Thornton, wielding the dominant, unyielding vernacular of Northern duty, replied, “Miss Hale, I am glad you do me justice. I did no more than I believed to be my duty in remonstrating with you as I did. I have always desired to act the part of a friend to you. I am glad you do me justice.”

“And,” said Margaret, a sudden flush of blood warming her pale cheeks, “will you do me justice, and believe that though I cannot—I do not choose—to give explanations of my conduct, I have not acted in the unbecoming way you apprehended?”

Margaret’s tone was so rooted in raw, pleading sincerity that Mrs. Thornton was momentarily disarmed. “Yes, I do believe you. Let us say no more about it. Where are you going to reside, Miss Hale? You never liked Milton, you know,” she added with a grim smile; “but for all that, you must not expect me to congratulate you on quitting it. Where shall you live?”

“With my aunt,” Margaret replied.

“My niece will reside with me in Harley Street. She is almost like a daughter to me,” Mrs. Shaw inserted, looking fondly at Margaret. “If you and your husband ever come to the town, my son and daughter, Captain and Mrs. Lennox, will, I am sure, join with me in wishing to do anything in our power to show you attention.”

Mrs. Thornton’s mind noted the Aunt's glaring ignorance of the local hierarchy. She answered shortly, “My husband is dead. Mr. Thornton is my son. I never go to London; so I am not likely to be able to avail myself of your polite offers.”

At that instant, Mr. Thornton entered the room, the heavy broadcloth of his mourning suit carrying the faint, solemn scent of his journey from Oxford. 

“John,” said his mother, “this lady is Mrs. Shaw, Miss Hale’s aunt. I am sorry to say, that Miss Hale’s call is to wish us good-bye.”

“You are going then!” his voice was pitched dangerously low, altering the acoustic tension of the entire room.

“Yes,” said Margaret. “We leave to-morrow.”

“My son-in-law comes this evening to escort us,” Mrs. Shaw provided.

Thornton turned away. He did not sit. He appeared to study an unopened letter on the table, silencing his own presence. When the women rose to leave, he started forward to hand Mrs. Shaw down to the carriage. 

As the carriage drove up, he and Margaret stood close upon the door-step. The deafening, mechanized hum of the mill enveloped them, yet beneath it, the shared, visceral memory of the riot forced its way into their minds. For him, the moment was inextricably linked to her passionate, untranslatable declaration of care before the violent crowd. As he looked at the stony, terrible set of her beautiful features now, his brow darkened, though his heart beat thick with a desperate, churning hunger. 

*No!* he told himself. *I put it to the touch once, and I lost it all. Let her go,—with her stony heart, and her beauty... She is afraid I shall speak what will require some stern repression. Let her go.*

He forced his tempestuous emotions into the rigid confines of absolute silence. “Good-bye,” he said, his tone deliberately stripped of all resonance or regret. He took her offered hand with a resolute calmness, and dropped it as carelessly as a withered, discarded herb. None in his household saw Mr. Thornton again that day; he retreated into the sanctuary of his work.

Exhausted by the sensory and emotional toll of the day, Margaret endured her aunt’s petting and sighing “I-told-you-so’s.” Dixon claimed she looked as frail as the day Mr. Hale died. Yet, when Mrs. Shaw reluctantly suggested delaying the journey, Margaret writhed as if in acute physical pain. 

“Oh! let us go. I cannot be patient here. I shall not get well here. I want to forget.”

The domestic preparations resumed. Captain Lennox arrived, bringing news of Edith and the sterile, indifferent chatter of London—a superficial acoustic layer that demanded nothing of Margaret’s soul, allowing her a measure of restorative rest. By the time she expected Higgins, she was able to await his summons quietly in her own room.

“Eh!” Higgins murmured as he entered, his voice a rich, dark resonance that carried the deep acoustic history of the Northern working man. “To think of th’ oud gentleman dropping off as he did! Yo’ might ha’ knocked me down wi’ a straw when they telled me. ‘Mr. Hale?’ said I; ‘him as was th’ parson?’ ‘Ay,’ said they. ‘Then,’ said I, ‘there’s as good a man gone as ever lived on this earth, let who will be t’other!’ And I came to see yo’, but them women in th’ kitchen wouldn’t let me at yo’. They said yo’ were ill,—and butter me, but yo’ dunnot look like the same wench. And yo’re going to be a grand lady up i’ Lunnon, aren’t yo’?”

“Not a grand lady,” Margaret said, a fragile smile warming her face.

“Well! Thornton said—says he, a day or two ago, ‘Higgins, have yo’ seen Miss Hale?’ ‘No,’ says I; ‘there’s a pack o’ women who won’t let me at her. But I can bide my time, if she’s ill. She and I knows each other pretty well; and hoo’l not go doubting that I’m main sorry for th’ oud gentleman’s death.’ And says he, ‘Yo’ll not have much time for to try and see her, my fine chap. She’s not for staying with us a day longer nor she can help. She’s got grand relations, and they’re carrying her off.’ ‘Measter,’ said I, ‘if I dunnot see her afore hoo goes, I’ll strive to get up to Lunnon next Whitsuntide, that I will. I’ll not be baulked of saying her good-bye.’ But bless yo’, I knowed yo’d come. It were only for to humour the measter, I let on as if I thought yo’d m’appen leave Milton without seeing me.”

“You’re quite right,” said Margaret, valuing the unvarnished truth of his dialect. “You only do me justice. And you’ll not forget me, I’m sure. If no one else in Milton remembers me, I’m certain you will; and papa too. You know how good and tender he was. Look, Higgins! here is his Bible.” She handed him the heavy, leather-bound text, a physical bridge between her father’s ancient, scholarly lexicons and Higgins’ oral tradition. “I have kept it for you. I can ill spare it; but I know he would have liked you to have it. I’m sure you’ll care for it, and study what is in it, for his sake.”

“Yo’ may say that. If it were the deuce’s own scribble, and yo’ axed me to read in it for yo’r sake and the oud gentleman’s, I’d do it. Whatten’s this, wench! I’m not going for to take yo’r brass, so dunnot think it. We’ve been great friends, ’bout the sound o’ money passing between us.”

“For the children—for Boucher’s children,” said Margaret hurriedly, pressing the coins upon him—funds meant for true sustenance, for the flour and root vegetables that would carry the orphans through the changing seasons. “They may need it. You’ve no right to refuse it for them. I would not give you one penny,” she added, smiling gently; “don’t think there’s any of it for you.”

Higgins looked at the coins, the physical means of preserving life, then up at Margaret, his native tongue elevating to meet the sacredness of the moment. “Well, wench! I can nobbut say, Bless yo’! and bless yo’!—and amen.”
