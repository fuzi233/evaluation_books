“The sun's rim dips; the stars rush out;
At one stride comes the dark.”

Land! Land! Land!
There it blazed far off to the south and west, a long unbroken bar of soft cobalt blue strung between crimson-tipped waves and the honey-gold glow of the sinking sun. Land at last: land holding cool, sweet mountain streams, tart thirst-quenching fruit, and wide open space for limbs cramped for weeks in swinging hammocks, wasted and swollen by the creeping curse of scurvy. And who knew what else? Gold, uncut gems, all the fabled wealth of the Indies? The dull, prosaic old world of English cottages and strict merchant laws lay thousands of miles behind them, and all around stretched uncharted realms of wonder, old sailors’ fables, and hope so bright it felt tangible.
Sick men dragged their swollen, bleeding gums up over the hammock rails, their wobbly legs barely holding their weight. Strong men dropped straight to their knees on the scuffed oak deck to pour out thanks to God. Every eye strained toward that faint blue cloud, which faded a little as the sun dipped lower, only to swell broader and sharper as the ship sprinted forward on the back of the warm, steady trade wind that curled gently around their brows and tugged at the sails, as if whispering to them, I am the faithful friend of those who dare.
“Blow harder, good trade wind,” Frank Leigh murmured to himself, his eyes fixed on that first scrap of New World soil that held everything he cared for. “It is written that He makes the winds His angels, breaths sent to sustain the heirs of His salvation. Blow freshlier still, if not to save me from death, then to save her from a fate worse than death. Blow on, carry me to her feet, that I may lead my lost lamb home, and die.”
His cheeks were gaunt and wasted, the characteristic hectic flush of his sickness burning bright crimson on both cheekbones, glowing even brighter in the sunset’s red light. Minutes later, all the vivid rainbow hues of the western sky bled away: emerald, topaz, amethyst, and ruby of the clouds dimmed to soft silver grey, and overhead the dark sapphire of the night sky opened, with the moon and Venus hanging low and bright over the glassy sea.
“That’s Barbados, your worship,” said Drew, the ship’s master. “Unless my reckoning’s gone wildly off, which Heaven knows it has no right to be, not after a passage as smooth as this. God be praised for it.”
“Barbados? I’ve never heard the name before.”
“Like enough, sir. Yeo and I sailed here with Captain Drake once, and I came again later with poor Captain Barlow. There’s good sheltered anchorage to the south and west of the island, as I remember plain as day.”
“And no Spaniards, no cannibals, no other wicked beast there,” added old Yeo, the gruff gunner, his eyes lighting up. “A very garden of the Lord, sir, hidden away in the middle of the seas, waiting to be an inheritance for those that love Him. I heard Captain Drake say he meant to plant a settlement here one day, if he ever got the chance.”
“I remember now,” Amyas put in, suddenly recalling old stories. “He and poor Sir Humphrey Gilbert talked at length about an island in these very waters. I wish to God he’d sailed for this place instead of Newfoundland!”
“Nay, sir,” Yeo said gently. “The good man’s in bliss with the Lord now. You wouldn’t take that from him, would you?”
“He’d have waited as patiently to sail for this island as he went off to Newfoundland, if it meant he could serve his queen,” Amyas laughed. “Well, my masters, what say you? Could we do better than lay over here a few days, get all our sick men back on their feet, before we push on to the Main and set about our work?”
Every man on board cheered the idea, save Frank, who stayed quiet, his gaze still fixed on the distant shore.
“Come, fellow adventurer,” Will Cary clapped him on the shoulder, “we need your vote too.”
“To my great impatience, Will, there is only one place on this whole earth I long to reach, and all day I’ve ached for wings to fly straight there,” Frank said low, aside to him. “But the counsel is right. I give it my full approval.”
The decision was announced, and met with a roar of genuine, hearty cheer from the entire crew. Long before dawn, they had sailed the length of the island’s southern shore, and were picking their way slowly into the sheltered bay where Bridgetown would one day stand.
Every eye was locked on the low, wooded hills sleeping soft in the moonlight, dotted with fireflies that flickered like a million tiny dancing stars. Every nostril drank deep of the sweet land breeze blowing out over the waves, heavy with the perfume of a thousand unknown tropical flowers. Every ear welcomed the new, rich hum of life that broke the monotonous lap and whisper of the sea: the droning of insects, the deep snore of tree toads, the soft plaintive cries of shore birds that fill a tropical night with noisy, joyful life.
At last the anchor dropped, the chain rattling loud through the hawsehole, and a spontaneous cheer burst from every throat, even with the risk of hidden Spaniards or hostile Caribs lingering on the shore. Amyas had all he could do to hold the crew back from leaping over the side to wade ashore right then, dark as it was, by pointing out that dawn was less than two hours away.
“Never did two hours drag so slow,” fidgeted one young cabin boy, pacing back and forth across the deck.
“You never sat in the Inquisition’s cells then,” Yeo grunted at him. “You’d learn what slow time really is. Stand still, and thank the Lord you’re alive and free on the edge of Paradise.”
“Hey, Gunner,” the boy pressed, “is there gold on this island?”
“Never heard tell of any, and that’s all to the good,” Yeo said dryly.
But a poor scurvy-crippled seaman, his lips parched and swollen, leaned forward and licked his lips. “What about oranges and lemons, Gunner? Are there any of those?”
“None I saw on my last visits, but there’s plenty of good fruit growing right down on the beach, thank the Lord. And look—there’s dawn breaking at last.”
Pink flushed the horizon, and the sun leapt up over the treeline, its level golden rays glinting off the smooth pale trunks of the palm trees, painting rainbows across the surf breaking over the coral reefs, and gilding the lonely uplands far inland, where stately plantation homes and busy steam engines would rise centuries later. Long lines of pelicans flew clanging out to sea to fish, the droning of night insects faded away, and a thousand tropical birds burst into jubilant, ringing song. A thin blue mist drifted up from the shore toward the inland downs, then vanished, leaving the rolling grasslands shimmering in the bright tropical glare. The cool land breeze that had blown out to sea all night died away, leaving the surface of the water glass-smooth, and the first full day in the West Indies began.
The sick men were lowered over the side in the longboats, carried ashore load after load to stretch out in the soft shade of the palm trees. Within half an hour, almost the entire crew was scattered across the beach, save for a dozen volunteers who’d agreed to stand watch on the ship until noon.
The first, instinctive cry of every man was for fruit. The lame, scurvy-ridden men dragged themselves across the sand, plucking greedily at the tiny purple grapes of the creeping shore vine, staining their mouths bright red and blistering their lips on the sharp spines of prickly pears, ignoring all Yeo’s warnings and pleas to be careful. A group of able-bodied young men set to work hewing down coconut palms to get at the fresh nuts, blunting their hatchets on the hard bark for almost no reward, until Yeo and Drew gathered half a dozen sensible men, struck off inland, and returned an hour later heaped high with the riches of that untouched wild orchard: tangy junipa apples, soft sweet guavas, and hundreds of plump golden pineapples, the queen of all fruits, growing thick across the sunbaked ledges of the low tufa cliffs.
They all sat down on the warm sandy turf to feast, swatting away galliwasps and jack Spaniard wasps and every other stinging insect that buzzed around them, while old blue land crabs huddled in the mouths of their holes shaking their claws in defiance at the invaders, and solemn herons stood stock still in the shallow surf, heads tilted to one side, wondering when was the last time they’d seen featherless bipeds break the quiet solitude of their island.
Frank wandered up and down the shore silent, his face bright with wonder rather than heavy with his usual quiet grief, while big Amyas trundled after him, his mouth full of junipa apple, playing the part of tour guide with a patronizing, easy air, as if he’d seen all these wonders before and was far too seasoned a sailor to be surprised by any of them.
“Everything is new, completely new,” Frank murmured, half to himself. “Oh, what an awe-striking feeling. Every single thing around us is changed, down to the tiniest fly and the smallest flower—and yet we are still the same, unchanged, forever.”
Amyas, who found such philosophical utterances completely unfathomable, just pointed and blurted out: “Look there, Frank! That’s a colibri. You’ve heard tell of colibris, right?”
Frank looked at the tiny living gem of a bird, which hung humming loud in the air above a bright exotic bloom, before darting away to call its mate, whirring and dancing in circles around the flower-dusted bushes, flashing new rainbows with every shift of the sunlight.
He watched it for a long solemn moment, then breathed: “If even this small created thing is so fair, how magnificent must be the shaping power of Nature? Oh my God, how glorious must Your true heavenly world be, if even the phantoms of this earth are so beautiful.”
“Phantoms?” Amyas said, frowning a little uneasily. “That’s no ghost, Frank. That’s a jolly little honey-sucker, with a tiny wife and babies no bigger than peas, greedy little rascals, I’ll warrant you.”
“Not phantoms in the sense you mean, good friend,” Frank smiled. “But phantoms to one who has learned how little value all earthly things hold.”
“I’ll tell you what, brother Frank,” Amyas said, shaking his head gently. “I know you’re far wiser than I am, but I can’t stand to see you turn your nose up at God’s good earth like that. He made every last one of these things, and maybe no man at all set eyes on them until fifty years back. But they were just as pretty then as they are now, right from the day the world was made. Why do you think God put them all here, if not to enjoy the sight of them?” He pulled his hat off his head for a second in a gesture of reverence. “If something is good enough to please the Lord, it’s certainly good enough to please you and me.”
“Your rebuke is just, dear simple-hearted old friend,” Frank said, chastened. “God forgive me, if with all my useless learning, and all my longings that have brought me no peace, I dare at moments, miserable sinner that I am, to be more fastidious than the Lord Himself. He walked in Paradise among the trees of the garden, Amyas. And so will we, and be content with whatever He sends. Why should we long for the next world, before we are even fit to live in this one?”
“And in the meanwhile,” Amyas grinned, taking another bite of fruit, “this earth is more than good enough, at least right here on Barbados.”
“Do you believe,” Frank asked, turning his thoughts to new stories, “the old Spanish tales that say you can hear sirens and tritons singing in these waters?”
“I couldn’t say for certain,” Amyas shrugged. “There’s more fish in the sea than any man ever pulled up in a net, and more wonders in the world than we ever dream of. But I never ran into any mermaids down in the South Sea, though Yeo swears he heard fair, soft music one night up in the Gulf, far from any land.”
“The Spaniards say that at certain times of the year, whole choirs of these sea nymphs gather on the waves, and sing of their watery loves with ravishing, sweet voices. It might well be true,” Frank mused. “Nature, which filled the land with rational souls, would never leave the sea completely empty of them. Especially when we remember that the ocean is the very fountain of all fertility, and its slime, as the most learned philosophers agree with old Thales of Miletus, is that primal raw material from which all things were created one by one. That is why the ancients told the wise fable that Venus, the mother of all living things—by which they meant the creative, shaping force of Nature—was born of sea foam, and rose from the deep to drift ashore on the isles of Greece.”
“I don’t know anything about this ‘plastic force’ nonsense,” Amyas laughed. “But I wish I’d been there to see that pretty poppet rise up out of the waves. The closest I ever saw to that was a group of native maidens swimming alongside our ship in the South Seas, trying to climb aboard. But Drake chased them all off, said they were a pack of naughty hussies, and I reckon he was right. Look at those butterflies over there, now! Don’t you wish you were a small boy again, not too proud to run and chase them with your cap?”
The two men wandered on together through the glorious sun-dappled tropical woods, then wandered back to the beach to find the sick men already bright and cheerful, many of whom had not been able to stir from their hammocks that very morning now pacing up and down the sand, growing stronger with every step.
“Well done, lads!” Amyas called out to them. “Keep your spirits high. We’ll bring the musicians ashore after dinner, since we’ve no mermaids to sing for us, and anyone who’s able can dance.”
Those four days slipped by in a happy haze. The men were like schoolboys on a long holiday, giving themselves over to simple, uncomplicated merriment. They did not forget their duties, though: they washed all their soiled clothing, hauled in barrels of fresh spring water, and stored up a supply of fruit that would keep for the rest of their voyage. They tramped through the woods hunting for gold that every bush seemed to promise them, until even the most stubborn gave up, no trace of the metal to be found despite their searches, just as Yeo had warned them there never was any on the island. After that, they lounged about on the sand like overgrown toddlers, collecting bright seashells and delicate sea-fans to bring home to their sweethearts back in England, smoking plump agoutis out of hollow tree trunks with shouts and laughter, and chasing every wild creature they came across until not a single land crab dared peek out of its hole, and no armadillo would unroll itself from its armour until the English ships had sailed safely out of the bay. They were the unconscious first pioneers of all the wealth, bustling commerce, beauty and scientific discovery that would, in centuries to come, turn that lovely little island into the richest gem in all the tropic seas.
