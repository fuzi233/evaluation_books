There’s a specific breed of man – and Bruce Carmyle was a prize specimen – that comes with a bounce-back factor so unbreakable it could outlast a rubber ball dropped off the Eiffel Tower. The sort of stubborn oaf who refuses to admit defeat even when defeat has smacked him square across the chops with a cricket bat, a trait that serves him just as well chasing a lover as it does chasing a business deal or a particularly stubborn golf ball in the rough.

As a suitor, Bruce was exactly that tough old pugilist who doesn’t even bother putting in effort till he’s taken one solid, numbing wallop to a tender spot. Sally had turned down his proposal flat as a pancake at Monk’s Crofton, but it never once crossed his mind that the episode was closed. He’d spent 30 years of his life getting every single thing he set his sights on, and he wasn’t about to break the streak now. That tiny, nagging sliver of doubt he’d occasionally felt about her – that whiff of unruly Bohemian spirit that had clashed so hard with his neat, colour-coded schedule of a life? Gone. Wiped clean. All he could think of was the brightness of her eyes, the jaunty little tilt of her chin, and the way she carried herself like she’d laugh out loud if a duke tried to order her around. Her bright, sharp prettiness was prodding him awake at 3 a.m. like a mischievous sprite with a whip, lashing him to go after her. Quiet, methodical, about as subtle as a distinguished gentleman wolf who’s packed a valet and a first-class transatlantic ticket, he set off on the trail. Delicacy and tact might have slowed a lesser man down, but Bruce had never had much time for either. You can’t expect a man who’s made of very little but grit and inherited wealth to come with every optional extra, can you?

He tried to pack his bags fast and sneak off without anyone noticing, but he forgot about the Carmyle family grapevine. Every extended English family of that vintage runs on a gossip network that makes African jungle drum telegraphs look slow: no group chats, no carrier pigeons, no texts, but somehow news of Bruce’s secret trip spread to every cousin, aunt, and great-uncle within a 50-mile radius faster than you could say “he’s chasing a girl who won’t have him”.

On his last night in London, the family’s chosen ambassador turned up at his South Audley Street flat: Uncle Donald himself. Two hundred and forty pounds of him, to be exact. The poor armchair he lowered his full weight into creaked so loud it sounded like it was making a last dying plea for mercy. Sally had once made her brother Fillmore’s whole morning miserable by pointing out that Uncle Donald was the exact spitting image of what Fillmore would look like if he didn’t straighten up, and Fillmore had moped for three straight days. A childhood superstition that he had a dicky heart had led Uncle Donald to avoid every single form of exercise for nearly 50 years, and paired with one of the three most ravenous appetites in the whole of southwest London, at 62 he was not the sort of man you wanted parked in your favourite armchair.

Bruce, normally the very picture of polite poise, eyed him with something very close to loathing. Uncle Donald’s walrus moustache heaved up and down with his huffing breath after climbing the stairs, bobbing like seaweed on a rolling tide.

“What’s this? What’s this?” he panted out eventually. “You packing?”
“Yes,” Bruce said, shortly. For the first time in his whole life, he got that weird, furtive guilty feeling he’d always assumed only his cousin Ginger got whenever he snuck out to play poker or stole a jam tart from the larder in front of this big, mackerel-eyed old man.
“You going away?”
“Yes.”
“Where you going?”
“America.”
“When you going?”
“Tomorrow morning.”
“Why you going?”

This back and forth might read like snappy vaudeville banter, but in reality it stretched on for a full three whole minutes: Uncle Donald would huff, sigh, snuffle his moustache up his nose at a glacial pace after every reply, before he even thought about asking the next question, and Bruce’s nerves were fraying so thin he was half-convinced he’d snap and scream any second.

“You’re going after that girl,” Uncle Donald accused, jabbing a fat finger at him.

Bruce flushed so dark red he looked like a boiled lobster. For half a second he found himself thinking that time Ginger hid under a table at Bleke’s Coffee House to avoid Uncle Donald hadn’t been nearly as cowardly as Bruce had called it at the time. The family’s golden boy was, it turned out, absolutely insufferable.

“Would you like a whisky and soda, Uncle Donald?” Bruce said, trying valiantly to change the subject.

“Never turn down a free drink,” Uncle Donald said, sticking to a vow he’d made back in 1882. “Gimme.”

You’d think that would soften the old man up, wouldn’t you? Not a bit of it. He knocked the whole restorative back in two greedy sips, then launched right back into the attack as grumpy as before. “Never thought you were a fool before,” he said severely.

Bruce’s pride chafed. These dressing-downs were a normal part of daily life for Ginger, but Bruce had never once had anyone question a single thing he did in his whole life. “I’m not a fool.”
“You are a fool. A damn fool,” Uncle Donald clarified. “Don’t like the girl. Never did. Not a nice girl. Took a dislike to her the second I laid eyes on her.”
“Must we really discuss this?” Bruce said, pulling out his stuffy grand-heir manner like a protective blanket.

Uncle Donald snorted so hard his moustache flapped. “Must we discuss it? D’you think I climbed three flights of blasted stairs with my weak heart for the fun of it? Gimme another drink.”

Bruce poured him a second one.
“Terrible business,” Uncle Donald moaned once he’d drained that glass too. “Shocking bad. If your poor old dad was alive right now, d’you think he’d let you go tearing across the Atlantic after some girl who won’t even look at you? I’ll tell you what he’d say – what’s this whisky, anyway?”
“O’Rafferty Special.”
“New one on me. Not bad. Quite good. Mellow. Where’d you get it?”
“Bilby’s in Oxford Street.”
“Must order a case. Mellow. Your dad’d be spitting feathers. None of us get what you’re doing. Puzzles your uncle George. Baffles your aunt Geraldine. Everyone says she’s only after your money. Anyone with half a brain can see that.”
“Pardon me, Uncle Donald,” Bruce said, stiff as a board, “but that’s absurd. If she was only after my money, why did she turn me down flat at Monk’s Crofton?”
“Drawing you on!” Uncle Donald said, as bright as a button. “Luring you in. Oldest trick in the book. There was a girl back when I was at Oxford in 1881 who tried that on me, and if my weak heart hadn’t stepped in to save me I’d have been trapped forever. What d’you even know about this girl, anyway? Who is she? Where’d you meet her?”
“At Roville, in France.”
“Travelling with her family?”
“Travelling alone,” Bruce admitted, reluctantly.
“Not even with that brother of hers? Bad!” Uncle Donald wheezed. “Bad, bad!”
“American girls are used to far more independence than English girls.”
“That boy’s going to be as fat as I am one day if he doesn’t lay off the cream buns,” Uncle Donald said, nodding to himself. “Travelling alone, eh? Did you catch her eye on the pier or something?”
“Really, Uncle Donald!”
“Well, you had to meet her somehow.”
“Lancelot introduced us. She was a friend of his.”

Uncle Donald quivered all over like a jelly that’d just been smacked with a spoon at the loathed name. “Lancelot! That useless, lacy little fop? That settles it! Any girl who’s friends with that waste of space – unpack your bags! Right now! This foolery stops here. Find yourself a nice respectable girl who’ll make you a proper wife. Your aunt Mary just met the Bassington-Bassingtons, related to the Kent branch, eldest daughter’s a perfect little lady, right up your street.”

Outside of old-fashioned Victorian melodramas, no one ever actually grinds their teeth, but Bruce came closer to doing it right then than any man in living memory. He scowled so dark his face looked like a thundercloud, all his usual suavity gone out the window. “I won’t do any such thing. I sail tomorrow.”

Uncle Donald had defied exactly one nephew before (Ginger, obviously) but no one had ever defied him back before. He suddenly felt horribly, embarrassingly powerless. There’s no worse feeling than standing there with no idea what to do next when a younger generation tells you to get stuffed. “Eh?” he said.

Bruce, now fully committed to being defiant, decided to go all the way. “I’m over 21. I’ve got my own money, no one tells me what to do. I’m going to do exactly as I please.”
“Now, hold on, think about it!” Uncle Donald pleaded, sounding terribly wimpy. “Reflect! Your position in the county –”
“Thought about it.”
“You could marry literally anyone you wanted!”
“Exactly. And I’m going to marry her.”
“So you’re determined to go running off to God-knows-where after this girl whose name I can’t even be bothered to remember?”
“Yes.”
“Have you forgotten,” Uncle Donald said, leaning forward portentously, “that you have a duty to the Family?”

Bruce’s patience snapped clean in two. He sank straight to Ginger levels of plain speaking, no fancy manners left. “Oh, damn the Family!” he yelled.

Silence. The only sound was the relieved groan of the armchair as Uncle Donald heaved his two hundred and forty pounds off it. “After that,” Uncle Donald said, “I’ve got nothing more to say.”
“Good,” Bruce said, rudely, no trace of shame left.
“’Cept this. If you come back married to that girl, I’ll cut you dead in Piccadilly. Swear to God I will.”

He waddled to the door. Bruce just stared at his shoes, not saying a word. Tense moment. “Hey,” Uncle Donald said, his fingers on the door handle, “what’d you say that whisky was called again?”
“O’Rafferty Special.”
“And where’d you get it?”
“Bilby’s in Oxford Street.”
“Right,” Uncle Donald said. “I’ll make a note of that.” And he was gone.
