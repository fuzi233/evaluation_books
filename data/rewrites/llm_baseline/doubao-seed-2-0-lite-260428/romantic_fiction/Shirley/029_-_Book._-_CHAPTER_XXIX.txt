Louis Moore had long grown accustomed to the hush of a quiet life. A man of few words by nature, he bore its constraints far better than most men would have managed, for he carried a whole vivid, thrumming world of thought and feeling locked inside his own head and heart. Confinement to the small, still corner of the real world he occupied as a paid tutor never chafed him long—until now.

How ghostly quiet Fieldhead hangs this evening. Every soul but Moore is gone to Nunnely: Miss Keeldar, all the Sympsons, even young Henry. Sir Philip Nunnely had insisted they all come to meet his mother and sisters, newly arrived at the priory, and in his good nature he had extended an invitation to the tutor too. Louis would sooner have struck a tryst with the ghost of the old Earl of Huntingdon and his band of shadowy merry men, under the gnarled blackest oak deep in Nunnely Forest, than sit through that stiff, polite supper. He would far rather wander the weedy, crumbling ruins of the old abbey, keeping company with mist-pale phantom nuns, than suffer the benevolent but stern baronet’s mother, or his patrician, icy sisters, or a single one of the fussy Sympson clan.

Tonight he longs for someone, something, to be close beside him—but none of them.

The equinox still rages through its storms. The wild lashing rains of the day have ebbed, and a great single cloud has torn apart and rolled back from the sky, not to leave a flat sapphire sea of heavens in its wake, but to reveal a long, high, roaring tempest of moonlight. The moon rides glorious, wild with gale, as if she has thrown herself gladly into the wind’s fierce, hungry caress. No Endymion will wait for his goddess on the hills tonight; no flocks graze the high pastures, and it is well, for tonight she embraces Æolus himself.

Sitting in his cramped schoolroom, Louis hears the storm howl around the far gable and batter the hall front, but his little corner is tucked sheltered, quiet. He wants no shelter. He craves no soft muted sounds, no screened, safe little nook.
“Every parlour in this house is empty,” he murmurs to the dark. “This cell makes me sick at heart.”

He slips out of the schoolroom, and wanders to the front rooms, where the casements are wide and unobstructed, no leafy branches blocking the view of the wild autumn night sky: deep bruised indigo, streaked with silver-fleeced clouds tearing past, the whole world thrumming with gale. He carries no candle—no lamp, no fire is needed. The broad, wavering, moonbeam spills silver over every floor and wall, bright enough to see by.

He drifts from parlour to parlour, as if he is following a ghost through the empty rooms. He stops in the old oak room. Unlike the formal, chilly salon, this space is warm. The hearth glows ruddy hot, cinders tinkling in the intense clear glow of the embers, and pulled up near the rug is a little work table: a desk sits atop it, and a chair drawn close. It looks as if the ghost he has been tracking has just risen from that chair a minute before. Anyone who saw him standing there, leaning forward, would see the bright sharp interest flare in his eyes, the soft, unguarded weight of feeling on his face, and think he had just found a living companion in the silent house, and is about to speak.

He spots the small satin bag hanging off the back of the chair. The desk is open, the keys still dangling in the lock. Scattered across the top are tiny, charming trifles: a pretty carved seal, a slim silver pen, a couple of bright crimson bramble berries resting on a green leaf, a small, soft, delicate kid glove. Order would tuck all these little messy things away out of sight, but these little unplanned details make the whole room feel alive.
“Her mark,” he whispers. “Here she was—careless, bright, bewitching thing—called away in a rush, and forgot to come back and tidy up. Why does she leave fascination in every footprint she leaves behind? How is it that she can be heedless, and never once offend? There is always some little thing to chide her for, but that reprimand never hardens into true displeasure in the chest. For the man who loves her, for her husband, any sharp word would melt right off his lips into a kiss after a moment. Half an hour spent gently remonstrating with her is better than a whole day of admiring any other woman alive. Am I muttering to myself? Stop that.”

He stills, standing quiet for a minute, then sets about making himself comfortable for the evening. He draws the heavy curtains shut over the wide window, shutting out the regal moon, the gale, all the starry sky. He heaps more logs onto the fast-dying fire, lights one of the pair of beeswax candles on the table, pulls a second chair up opposite the one she had been sitting in, and sinks down. From his pocket he pulls a small, thick blank book, hidden away for years, and a pencil, and begins to write in a tight, neat, cramped hand no one else would easily read.
Lean in, reader. Do not be shy. Look over his shoulder, fearless, and read every word:

It is nine o’clock. The carriage will not return before eleven, I am certain. I have two full hours of freedom. Two hours where I may occupy her room, sit opposite her chair, rest my elbow on her table, have all her little sweet mementoes scattered around me.
I used to love solitude, once. I used to imagine it a quiet, fair nymph, an Oread coming down to me from lonely mountain passes, her robes stitched through with blue hill mist, her breath sharp with high cold wind, her whole bearing steeped in solemn beauty. I used to court her calmly, think my heart was lighter when I held her to me, all silent and majestic.
But ever since that day I called S. to me in the schoolroom, and she came and sat close at my side, ever since she opened the trouble weighing on her mind, asked for my protection, appealed to my strength—ever since that hour I have abhorred solitude. It is a cold abstraction, a fleshless skeleton, daughter, mother, and mate of Death.
It is such sweet relief to write of what is as dear to me as the core of my own heart. No one can take this little book from me. Through this pencil, I can say what I dare not utter to any living soul, what I dare not even let myself think aloud when anyone else is near.
We have barely crossed paths since that evening. Once, when I was alone in the drawing room looking for one of Henry’s books, she walked in, dressed all in silk for the concert at Stilbro’. It was her shyness, not mine, that drew a soft silver veil between us. I have heard so much empty cant about “maiden modesty” in my life, but those words, when they are not hackneyed, are perfect for her. When she passed the window, after greeting me with that quiet, graceful little nod of hers, I could think of no name for her but “stainless virgin.” I could see a soft, delicate splendour wrapping around her, the shyness of girlhood glowing as a halo around her head. I am no handsome man, no clever charmer, but that little shyness of hers touched me deep, flattered the finest, softest parts of me. I must have looked like a stupid block standing there, but I was thrumming with a kind of paradise life when she turned her gaze away from mine, and softly averted her head to hide the flush rising in her cheeks.
I know this is the talk of a dreamer, a rapt romantic lunatic. But I do dream. I will dream, now and then. If she has woven romance into my staid, prosaic bones, how can I help it?
What a child she can be, sometimes! What an unsophisticated, untaught, warm thing she is. I can see her now, looking up into my face, begging me to make sure no one smothers her, to give her a strong narcotic to bear it if they try. I can see her confessing that she is not half so self-sufficient, so independent of other people’s sympathy, as the whole world thinks she is. I see the secret tear drop soft and quiet from her eyelash. She told me once she thought I saw her as childish. I did. She thought I despised her. Despised her! It was unutterably sweet to feel myself at once close to her and able to hold her up, to feel that natural right and power to sustain her, the way a husband ought to sustain his wife.
I worship her perfections, but it is her little foibles, her tiny faults, that bring her close to me, nestle her right into my heart, wrap her up in my love. For a selfish, deeply natural reason: those little faults are the steps by which I climb to a place of belonging in her life. If she was a trimmed, artificial, flat mound with no uneven ground, what foothold would there be for me to climb? She is a wild natural hill, all mossy breaks and soft hollows, her slope inviting ascent, her summit the sweetest pleasure to reach.
To drop the metaphor: It delights my eyes just to look at her. She fits me, as if she was made for me. If I were a king, and she the scullery maid sweeping my palace stairs, across all that gulf between our stations, my eyes would still pick her out, my pulse would still beat true for her, even if we could never speak. If I were a gentleman, and she waited on me as a servant, I could not help but like that Shirley. Take away all her education, her fine gowns, all her privilege, all grace but the natural ease that comes of her own fine, straight frame. Show her to me at a cottage door, in a plain stuff gown, offering me a cup of water with that same smile, that same warm good will she uses to dispense grand manorial hospitality now. I should like her. I should linger an hour to talk to that rustic girl, leave her with regret, and come back again whenever I could.
How recklessly careless of her to leave her desk open, where I know she keeps money! The keys in that lock open every one of her repositories, even her jewel casket. I can see the silver bead tassel of her purse sticking out of that little satin bag. My brother Robert would be furious if he saw it. All her little failings would irritate him no end. But if they vex me, it is the softest, most pleasurable vexation imaginable. I delight to find her at fault. If I lived here full time, I know she would never stint to give me just that little thing to correct, a theme for my quiet tutor’s lectures. I never lecture Henry, never want to. If he does something wrong, which is very rarely, a single word is enough. Most times I just shake my head. But the moment I catch that mutinous little pout on her face, all the words for gentle expostulation crowd to my lips. I, who am so taciturn, think she would turn me into a man who talks for hours. The more mischievous, teasing, unmanageable she gets, the more reason I have to gently disapprove of her, the more I want to seek her out. She is never wilder than when she is kitted out in her riding habit and hat, never less governable than when she and her dog Zoë come tearing back fiery from a race with the wind across the hills. I can confess it to this mute page: I have waited an hour in the courtyard just for the chance to watch her ride back, and the far sweeter chance to catch her in my arms as she dismounts from the saddle. I have noticed, and I would never say this to anyone but this book: she will never let any other man help her down. I have seen her politely turn down Sir Philip Nunnely’s offer, be so gentle to his thin, sensitive pride that he never even noticed the refusal. I have seen her haughtily reject Sam Wynne’s outstretched hand. But she lets me lift her down, unresisting, unhurried. Does she know how my strength sings to serve her? I am no slave to her, I swear it, but all my knowledge, all my prudence, all my calm, all my power, gather at her presence like genii summoned by the glisten of a lamp, waiting humbly for a task. How glad they are when she gives them one! What joy they take in the smallest toil she assigns! Does she know that?
I called her careless earlier. It is remarkable how that carelessness never dims her refinement. The holes in her character prove how genuine that refinement is, not just a polished facade. A whole, perfect garment can hide meagreness and malformation, but a torn sleeve reveals a fair, round, unblemished arm. I have handled so many of her stray belongings, left scattered all over the house, and I have never found a single thing that does not mark her as a lady—nothing sordid, nothing soiled. She is as unthinking in some ways as she is scrupulous in others. Even as a peasant girl, she would always be trim, spotless, neat. Look at the pure, unmarked kid of this little glove, the fresh, unsullied satin of her bag.
What a world of difference there is between S. and that sweet pearl Caroline Helstone! Caroline is the very soul of punctuality and exactitude, the kind of woman who would suit my fastidious brother Robert down to the ground—delicate, dexterous, quiet, every task done to the minute, every possession arranged to a straw’s breadth. But what on earth would I do with something so nearly perfect? She is my equal in station, poor as I am, pretty too, a little Raphael-like head of hers, all English softness and purity, a lily of the valley, no tint needed, no flaw to fix. What could I possibly change about her? What pencil would dare to add a single stroke? My sweetheart, if I ever have one, must be far closer to a rose: sweet, vivid, guarded by sharp, exciting thorns. My wife, if I ever marry, must stir my big strong frame with a sting now and then, must give my vast reserve of patience something to use itself on. I was not made to endure a lamb for a mate. I would find far more joy, more purpose, in the care of a young lioness or leopardess. I like few sweet things that are not also pungent, few bright things that are not also hot. I like the summer day where the sun makes the fruit blush and the corn ripen golden. Beauty is never so beautiful as when, if you tease it, it wriggles back at you with spirit. Fascination is never so powerful as when, half roused, half angry, it threatens to turn fierce. I fear I would tire very quickly of the mute, unchanging innocence of a lamb. I would find the nestling dove that never stirred in my arms burdensome after a while. But my patience would revel in stilling the flutters, training the wild energy of a restless merlin, taming the instincts of a wild, untamed creature. My powers would feast on that work.
Oh my pupil! My Peri! Too mutinous for heaven, too innocent for hell, I will never do more than see you, worship you, wish for you. Alas, to know I could make you happy, is it my doom to watch you fall into the hands of men who have no power to do that at all?
However kind a hand may be, if it is feeble, it cannot bend Shirley. And she must be bent. It cannot curb her. And she must be curbed.
Beware, Sir Philip Nunnely. I never see you walk or sit at her side, and watch her lips press tight, her brow knit, as she resolutely endures some trait of yours she does not admire, tolerates some weakness she thinks is atoned for by your kindness, but that annoys her all the same. I never see that grave flush rise on her face, the unsmiling sparkle in her eye, the slight recoil of her whole frame when you lean too close, gaze too intensely, whisper too warmly, without thinking of the fable of Semele, reversed.
It is not the daughter of Cadmus I see, longing to look on Jove in his full godhead. It is a priest of Juno, keeping lone, late vigil at an Argive temple. For years of solitary ministry he has lived on dreams. A divine madness is on him. He loves the idol he serves, prays day and night for his frenzy to be fed, for the Ox-eyed queen to smile on her votary. She has heard his prayer. She will be gracious. All Argos sleeps. The temple doors are shut. The priest waits at the altar.
A shock rips through heaven and earth, unheard by the sleeping city, felt only by that lone watcher, brave and unshaken in his fanaticism. Out of the silence, no warning, he is wrapped in sudden, blinding light. Through the rent roof, the wide, yawning, vast white blaze of the heaven above, a wondrous force pours down, dread as the rush of falling stars. He has exactly what he asked for. Withdraw. Do not look. I am blinded. I hear an unspeakable sound ringing through the shrine. I wish I could not hear it. I see an unendurable glory burning terribly between the pillars. Gods, be merciful, and quench it!
At dawn, a pious Argive comes to the temple to leave an early offering. There was thunder in the night. The lightning struck here. The shrine is shattered, the marble pavement split and blackened all around. Juno’s statue rises pure, grand, completely untouched. At her feet, pale ashes lie heaped. No priest remains. The man who kept the vigil will never be seen again.

The distant rumble of carriage wheels rolls up the drive.
Louis starts, locks the desk, pockets the keys, tucks the glove, the pen, the seal, the satin bag into his coat pocket too.
The carriage will pull up to the door any second. She will be looking for her keys tomorrow. She will come find me, he thinks, already smiling soft to himself. I can hear her clear voice now, half reluctant, a little sheepish: “Mr. Moore, have you seen my keys?”
