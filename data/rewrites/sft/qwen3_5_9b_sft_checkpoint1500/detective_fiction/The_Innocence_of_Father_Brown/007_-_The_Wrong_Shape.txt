Rain dripped from the eaves of the long, low house, painting the white walls and pale green trim with a steady rhythm. It was a structure of peculiar design, shaped like a 'T' with an excessively long cross-piece facing the road and a disproportionately short tail stretching back. The front facade was grand, two stories high, while the rear wing, merely one story tall, housed a study and a glass conservatory bursting with tropical blooms that glowed like fever dreams in the fading daylight. Inside, Leonard Quinton, a poet of chaotic genius, drifted in a haze of opium and color, surrounded by a world of his own making—a realm of burning golds, blood-reds, and monstrous jewels.

Father Brown and his tall French companion, Flambeau, emerged from the house onto the damp step, seeking the dry air. They had spent the day within the artist's fever-brightened sanctuary, finding the atmosphere oppressive, thick with the scent of exotic incense and the heavy, suffocating presence of Quinton's obsession. "We breathe better out here," Flambeau noted, lighting a slender cigarette.

Their conversation was interrupted by the sudden, violent slamming of the front gate. A young man, Mr. Atkinson, tumbled up the steps, his red necktie askew and a jointed cane clicking nervously against the pavement. He was breathless, disheveled, and clearly in need of funds. "I want to see old Quinton!" he cried, his eyes darting around. "Has he gone?"

Father Brown, extinguishing his pipe, indicated that the doctor was with the poet. Indeed, Dr. Harris emerged moments later, shutting the study door firmly. With a cool, practiced demeanor, the physician informed the intruder that Mr. Quinton was under the influence of a strong sedative and absolutely forbidden from visitors. "Nobody must see him," Harris declared, guiding the frantic youth away from the threshold. Once the boy was dismissed, the doctor admitted to Father Brown and Flambeau that the statement about the sedative had been a fabrication designed to keep the "dirty little scamp" away.

"The trouble is," Harris explained, adjusting his hat, "that Mr. Quinton prefers to lie on an ottoman at the far end of the conservatory, amidst those blood-red poinsettias. It gives me the creeps, but he likes it."

Curiosity led Father Brown and Flambeau to inspect the conservatory from the outside. As they approached the curved glass end, Father Brown stooped and picked up an object from the grass: a crooked Oriental dagger, inlaid with exquisite, jarring colors. "What is this?" he asked, examining it closely.

Dr. Harris shrugged. "Likely Quinton's. He collects all sorts of knick-knacks, or perhaps it belongs to that mild Hindoo conjuror he keeps on a string."

"You don't believe in magic?" Father Brown asked, his voice dropping to a dreamy murmur.

"Oh, crickey! Magic!" Harris laughed.

"It is beautiful," Father Brown murmured, tracing the jagged line of the blade. "But it is the wrong shape."

"The wrong shape?" Flambeau frowned.

"For anything," the priest insisted, holding the knife aloft as if it were a living serpent. "It lacks a hearty, plain purpose. It does not point like a spear, nor sweep like a scythe. It looks like an instrument of torture."

Harris chuckled, dismissing the priest's unease, and suggested they return to the front door. However, as they rounded the curve of the glass, Father Brown and Flambeau froze. Standing motionless beneath the glass, bathed in the dying sunlight, was a tall figure draped in faultless white robes. His skin was the color of bronze, his face serene, yet utterly still. Through the glass, they saw Quinton slumped on the sofa, seemingly lost in a trance.

"Who is that?" Father Brown breathed, stepping back.

"Only that Hindoo humbug," Harris grunted, though he looked puzzled. "Though I don't know what he's doing here."

"It looks like hypnotism," Flambeau whispered, his brow furrowing.

"Why are you unmedical fellows always talking bosh about hypnotism?" Harris retorted sharply. "It looks a deal more like burglary."

Flambeau moved forward, bowing deeply to the robed figure. "Good evening, sir. Do you want anything?"

Slowly, like a great ship turning into harbor, the giant's head turned. His yellow eyelids were sealed tight, as if in deep sleep. "Thank you," the voice rasped in flawless English. "I want nothing." He paused, half-opened his eyes to reveal a slit of opalescence, and repeated, "I want nothing." Finally, with a startling glare, he confirmed it again and melted away into the shadows of the garden.

"He wants something," Father Brown muttered, watching the departing figure. "The Christian is more modest."

As dusk deepened, the garden grew darker, the trees looming like silhouettes against the red glow of the horizon. Suddenly, the shadow of the white-robed man slid out from between the study and the main building, moving with impossible speed toward the front door. Before the trio could react, Mrs. Quinton appeared, her heavy golden hair and square pale face cutting through the twilight. She looked stern yet courteous, ushering the doctor back inside.

"That woman is over-driven," Father Brown observed grimly. "That is the kind of woman that does her duty for twenty years, and then does something dreadful."

Inside the hall, they encountered Mr. Atkinson again, humming tunelessly and poking the furniture with his cane. Dr. Harris, seizing the moment, quickly locked the door to prevent the youth from entering. Moments later, the door swung open again. Atkinson lunged forward, shouting for Quinton, only to be met by the poet's voice echoing from the study.

"Oh, I know what you want!" Quinton's voice boomed, a mix of yawn and weary laughter. "Take it, and leave me in peace. I'm writing a song about peacocks."

A half-sovereign flew through the aperture, caught deftly by the stunned youth. Harris slammed the door shut, locking it savagely, and led Father Brown and Flambeau back into the garden.

"Poor Leonard can get a little peace now," Harris said, checking his watch. "He's locked in all by himself for an hour or two."

Father Brown scanned the garden, his gaze settling on the distant bank where the white-robed figure sat upright, swaying rhythmically in the gathering gloom. Beside him, Atkinson leaned listlessly against a tree. Beyond them, near the conservatory, stood Dr. Harris, a cigar glowing like a will-o'-the-wisp in the encroaching darkness.

"My father," Flambeau asked softly, "what is the matter with you?"

Father Brown was silent for a moment, watching the swaying figure. "Superstition is irreligious, but there is something in the air of this place. I think it's that Indian—at least, partly."

Suddenly, two drops of rain fell, startling Flambeau. From the end of the conservatory, Dr. Harris burst forth, his face pale, rushing toward the group. "Foul play!" he cried, grabbing Atkinson by the collar. "What have you been doing to him, you dog?"

"No fighting," Father Brown commanded, his voice steel-like. "We are enough to hold anyone we want to. What is the matter, doctor?"

"Things are not right with Quinton!" Harris gasped, his composure shattered. "I could just see him through the glass, and I don't like the way he's lying. It's not as I left him, anyhow."

Father Brown sprang forward. "Let us go in to him. You can leave Mr. Atkinson alone. I have had him in sight since we heard Quinton's voice."

Flambeau stayed behind to guard Atkinson, while the doctor and the priest raced to the study. They pushed open the door to find the room lit only by a small fire kept for the invalid. In the center of the large mahogany table lay a single sheet of paper.

"Hold it!" Harris cried, snatching the note. "Good God, look at that!" He thrust the paper toward Father Brown.

Brown read the words three times before speaking. *I die by my own hand; yet I die murdered!* The handwriting was unmistakably Leonard Quinton's—bold, beautiful, yet difficult to decipher.

Without hesitation, Father Brown strode toward the conservatory. He found Dr. Harris emerging from the glass room, his face a mask of horror. "He's done it," Harris whispered.

Together, they entered the conservatory. There lay Leonard Quinton, his head hanging downward, his red curls sweeping the floor. Into his left side was driven the crooked Oriental dagger, his limp hand resting on the hilt. Outside, the storm had broken, raining down in sheets like the night in Coleridge.

Father Brown studied the note in his hand, the lightning flashing briefly illuminating the torn edges of the paper. "Doctor," he said, his voice cutting through the thunder, "this paper is the wrong shape."

"What do you mean?" Harris stammered.

"It isn't square," Brown answered. "It has a sort of edge snipped off at the corner. What does it mean?"
