The air inside the galley tasted of salt, sweat, and the metallic tang of impending execution. Until the enemy fleet had lain in wait for us in the gulf's mouth, I had dismissed my status as a mere recruit for the Empress. But the ambush itself—the calculated ferocity designed solely to ensure my capture—was an omen written in blood that even the blind could decipher. I was expected to play a role. A significant one.

Yet, if our arrival had been anticipated by foes, it was also tracked by allies. Scouts perched high in the mountains watched our silhouette against the horizon, carrying the news to the capital faster than any galley could navigate the treacherous waters. The strait leading to Atlantis was a deceptive geography; narrow as a river where volcanic rock squeezed the sea, then widening suddenly into a lake-like expanse, winding through swamps and smoking hills. Overland, a courier could outrun the wind. Phorenice understood this. She dispatched twenty runners at once. Seven reached the capital intact; three more survived the journey. We, however, remained ignorant of this grand orchestration, pushing forward with the delusion of anonymity.

The rowers were savages from Europe, their presence a constant assault of odor that stripped the voyage of any remaining pleasure. Added to this was the grit from an erupting volcano, coating everything in abrasive dust. We needed speed. More than anything, I needed to know what awaited me. Was I to be a prisoner on trial for curing Yucatan? Or a ghost summoned to new duties? The uncertainty gnawed at me, a human frailty despite my priestly vows of stoicism.

Morning broke over the peak of the sacred mountain, its eternal fires glaring behind the city. The boatswain's whip cracked violently, driving the exhausted slaves to a final, desperate surge. Under oars alone, we made good time. The city emerged from the mist, pyramids and towers floating on the gleaming water. Twenty years had passed since I last tread these stones, and my heart hammered against my ribs.

As we neared the harbor, the temples of my initiation and the house of my childhood resolved into sharp focus. Memories rose, dimming my vision. We pulled up against the white walls, the slaves panting in silence. Usually, captains kept visitors waiting to assert their authority, but today, a ten-oared skiff shot from the harbor gates, racing alongside us.

"That is Dason's galley?" the port-captain called out.

"It was," Tob replied.

"I saw Dason's head on your bow," the captain noted grimly. "Are you Captain of Tatho's fleet?"

"And remain so," Tob answered. "Tatho's fleet sank to the sea-floor thanks to Dason's treachery. This stinking vessel is all that remains."

The captain scanned the deck. "Captain, your reception will be perilous. Where is Lord Deucalion? Did you lose him to the waves?"

"He is a dangerous passenger," Tob interjected smoothly. "Phorenice and Tatho both care for his welfare. But I intend to keep my skin intact." He jabbed a thumb toward me. "He is aboard to vouch for me."

The captain hesitated, then bowed with practiced ease. "My lord, I apologize for failing to recognize your nobility. I expected finer attire."

"Pish," I said. "If I choose simplicity, I cannot fault you for mistaking me. I prefer not to advertise my station with gaudy finery. End this interrogation."

"Your humility is noted," the captain said, bowing deeper. "It is my honor to guide your galley to the royal berth."

As the skiff sped away, Tob watched me with a dry smile. "Deucalion," he said, "when you step onto this shore among the fine folk, he'll remember only a rude barbarian."

"You think nothing of the sort," I replied.

"Then I must prove my refinement," Tob muttered, seizing my hand in a grip like iron. "By the Gods, Deucalion, you are a great prince, but I've only ever known you as a man. You are the finest fighter of beasts and men alive. That strike on the lizard... the tavern singers will chant it forever."

We entered the harbor, trumpets blaring from the castle gates. The port-captain raised my banner to the masthead, and from the quays, the sounds of music and the glow of welcoming fires drifted across the water. To think an Empress cared enough to stage such a display for a returning subject was almost awe-inspiring. Nothing was to be done by halves.

Our galley was moored to the golden rings of the royal quay, sacred to the throne.

"If Dason had predicted this," Tob joked darkly, "he'd have used silk rope instead of hemp. His head would be frowning right now if the sun hadn't baked it stiff." He gestured to the quay. "Will you walk delicately over this common ship and onto the carpet they've prepared?"

The port-captain recoiled at Tob's rudeness. Colonial freedom had no place here. I sighed inwardly; the dignity of rank demanded preparation. I stepped into a crimson pavilion erected specifically for my arrival, but my compliance ended there.

Three chamberlains, dressed in extravagant finery, presented me with robes adorned with impossible fripperies. They wanted me transformed into a peacock.

"Courtly," I refused. "Have you received specific orders regarding my attire from Her Majesty?"

They confessed they had none.

"Then Phorenice commanded Deucalion the man to appear," I countered. "She sent no instructions on his outer casing. This dress suits my temper. It shields me from the heat and is clean. Your interference smells of impertinence."

Their spokesman drew his sword, hilt offered. "A favor, Great Lord Deucalion. Take your vengeance now, before we deliver you to the torturers later."

"Poof," I dismissed them. "You exaggerate a triviality."

Their readiness to offer weapons clarified the situation. This was a changed Atlantis, where a disagreement over clothing could lead to torture. I accepted a bath in the pavilion, though the water was tainted with cheap perfume. I waited with composed silence. The chamberlains, too polite to interrupt, stood guard while the sun beat down on the scarlet roof. Music blared outside; the welcoming fires burned in the square.

It was said that the powerful maintain their status by making the lesser wait. Phorenice disagreed. As soon as Tob's galley touched the quay, she departed the palace. Her procession, fully arrayed, waited only for her mount. Trumpets signaled the advance.

From the doorway of the pavilion, I watched the soldiers march straight to the center of the square to salute, then fall back. The Mariners' Guild followed, then more troops, then the merchants, tanners, and spear-makers, many riding tamed beasts.

Then came the wonders. First, a massive bull-mammoth, shaggy and monstrous, yet tame as a slave. It bore a golden half-castle on its back, tusks gilded, neck garlanded with flowers. It was a symbol of mastered strength. Simultaneously emerged the greater wonder: the Empress Phorenice.

While the beast captured my attention with its savage power, the woman atop it stole my gaze completely. She was slight, supple, and beautifully molded. Her face was a book of intelligence, fair beyond belief, with hair of lovely ruddiness cut short in the new fashion. But it was her eyes that stopped me. Who could fathom the depth of Phorenice's eyes, or find a trace of their color in mere pigment?

When the crowd shouted, I stood. The chamberlains whispered urgently, "On your knees!" "At least bend your head!"

I ignored them. Self-respect dictated otherwise. I marched across the open space, head erect, meeting the Empress's gaze directly. She surveyed me, and I surveyed her. In those few seconds, I saw a woman unlike any I had imagined. Throughout my long official life, women had held no sway over me, though I recognized their power over others. Yet this woman, seated under sacred snakes on a mammoth's back, baffled me utterly. Her thoughts were unreadable, her presence an infinite attraction.
