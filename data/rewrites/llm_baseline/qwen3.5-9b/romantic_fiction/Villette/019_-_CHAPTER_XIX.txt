My stay at La Terrasse extended two weeks beyond the official end of the vacation, a kindness secured by Mrs. Bretton's gentle intervention. After her son declared that "Lucy was not yet strong enough to return to that den of a pensionnat," she promptly drove to the Rue Fossette, negotiated with the director, and obtained permission on the grounds that prolonged rest and a change of air were essential for my recovery. This respite, however, invited an attentiveness I could easily have done without: a polite visit from Madame Beck.

On a fine afternoon, that formidable lady actually arrived in a fiacre right up to the château gates. Presumably, she had decided to inspect the establishment where Dr. John lived. The pleasant setting and tidy interior evidently exceeded her expectations; she praised everything she saw, declaring the blue salon *"une pièce magnifique"* (a magnificent room). She lavishly congratulated me on acquiring friends *"tellement dignes, aimables, et respectables"* (so worthy, lovable, and respectable), and offered a neat compliment in my favor. Upon Dr. John's entrance, she bounded forward with boundless energy, launching into a rapid-fire stream of felicitation regarding his *"château,"* his mother, the *"digne châtelaine"* (worthy mistress), and his handsome appearance. Indeed, his looks were flourishing, enhanced at that moment by the good-natured, amused smile with which he invariably listened to Madame's fluent, florid French. In short, Madame Beck shone in her brightest form that day, entering and exiting like a living Catherine wheel of compliments, delight, and affability. Half intentionally, half to inquire about school matters, I followed her to her carriage and peered inside after she was seated and the door closed. In that fleeting fraction of time, what a transformation occurred! Just moments prior, all sparkle and jest; now, she sat sterner than a judge and graver than a sage. What a strange little woman!

I returned to tease Dr. John about Madame's devotion to him. How he laughed! What joy illuminated his eyes as he recalled her grand speeches, repeating them while mimicking her voluble delivery. He possessed an acute sense of humor and was the finest companion in the world—provided he could momentarily forget Miss Fanshawe.

They say that sitting in "sunshine calm and sweet" is excellent for the weak, granting them vital force. When little Georgette Beck was convalescing, I used to carry her in my arms and walk with her in the garden for hours beneath a grape-covered wall, where the Southern sun ripened the fruit just as effectively as it warmed her pale frame.

There exist human tempers that are bland, glowing, and genial; in their presence, it is as beneficial for the poor in spirit to dwell as it is for the feeble in body to bask in the noonday glow. Among these chosen natures were undoubtedly both Dr. Bretton's and his mother's. They delighted in communicating happiness, much as others delight in causing misery; they did so instinctively, seemingly without conscious effort, as ideas for giving pleasure arose spontaneously in their minds. During my stay, every day brought a small plan resulting in enjoyable benefit. Though fully occupied, Dr. John still found time to join us on our brief excursions. I marvelled at how he managed his engagements; they were numerous, yet through sheer system, he organized them to leave a daily period of liberty. Often I saw him hard at work, yet rarely overdriven, never irritated, confused, or oppressed. Whatever he undertook was accomplished with the ease and grace of inexhaustible strength, fueled by the bountiful cheerfulness of high, unbroken energies. Under his guidance, during that single happy fortnight, I saw more of Villette, its surroundings, and its inhabitants than I had in the preceding eight months. He took me to places of interest whose names I had never heard before, willingly sharing noteworthy information. He never seemed to find it troublesome to speak to me, and I was never burdened by listening. He did not treat subjects coldly or vaguely; he rarely generalized or prosed. Instead, he seemed to relish nice details as much as I did, observing character deeply and not superficially. These qualities lent interest to his discourse. Furthermore, because he spoke directly from his own resources rather than borrowing dry facts, trite phrases, or hackneyed opinions from books, his conversation possessed a freshness as welcome as it was rare. Before my eyes, his disposition seemed to unfold a new phase, rising in a fresh, nobler dawn.

His mother possessed a good measure of benevolence, but he owned a greater and wider one. Accompanying him to the *Basse-Ville*, the poor and crowded district of the city, I found his errands were as much those of a philanthropist as a physician. Soon I understood that cheerfully, habitually, and in a single-minded unconsciousness of any special merit, he was achieving a world of active good among a very wretched population. The lower classes liked him well; his poor hospital patients welcomed him with a sort of enthusiasm.

But pause—I must not, as the faithful narrator, degenerate into a partial eulogist. Very well do I know that Dr. John was not perfect, any more than I am. Human fallibility permeated him entirely. There was no hour, scarcely a moment, during the time I spent with him that in act, speech, or look, he did not betray something that was not divine. A god could not possess the cruel vanity of Dr. John, nor his occasional levity. No immortal could resemble him in his temporary oblivion of all but the present, his passing passion for the immediate moment. This was shown not coarsely, by devoting it to material indulgence, but selfishly, by extracting from it whatever nourishment it could yield for his masculine self-love. His delight was to feed this ravenous sentiment, without thought of the cost of provisions or care for keeping it sleek and pampered.

The reader is requested to note a seeming contradiction in the two views presented of Graham Bretton—the public and the private, the outdoor and the indoor. In the first, the public view, he appears oblivious of self, modest in displaying his energies and earnest in exercising them. In the second, the fireside picture, there is expressed consciousness of what he possesses and what he is; pleasure in homage, some recklessness in exciting admiration, and some vanity in receiving it. Both portraits are correct.

It was hardly possible to serve Dr. John quietly and secretly. When one believed that some trifling dedication for his use had been completed unnoticed, and that, like other men, he would simply use it when ready without asking its origin, he would amaze you with a smiling remark proving his eye had been on the work from start to finish. He had noted the design, traced its progress, and marked its completion. It pleased him to be thus served, and his pleasure would beam in his eyes and play about his mouth.

This would have been acceptable had he not added to this kindly evidence a certain wilfulness in discharging what he called debts. When his mother worked for him, he repaid her by showering upon her his bright animal spirits with even greater affluence than his usual gay, teasing, loving manner. If Lucy Snowe were discovered to have contributed to such work, he planned some pleasant recreation in recompense.

I was often amazed at his perfect knowledge of Villette—a knowledge extending beyond its open streets to penetrate every gallery, salle, and cabinet. Regarding every door leading to an object worth seeing, every museum, every hall sacred to art or science, he seemed to possess the magic word "Open! Sesame!" I lacked a head for science, but an ignorant, blind, fond instinct inclined me toward art. I loved visiting picture galleries and dearly preferred being left there alone. In company, a wretched idiosyncrasy prevented me from seeing much or feeling anything. In unfamiliar company, where maintaining a flow of conversation on the subjects present was necessary, half an hour would exhaust me with a combined pressure of physical lassitude and total mental incapacity. I have never yet seen a well-reared child, much less an educated adult, who could not put me to shame by the sustained intelligence of their demeanor during a social visit to pictures, historical sights, or monuments. Dr. Bretton was a cicerone after my own heart; he would take me early, before the galleries filled, leave me there for two or three hours, and recall me when his own engagements were finished. Meanwhile, I was happy—not always in admiring, but in examining, questioning, and forming conclusions.

In the beginning, there was some misunderstanding and struggle between Will and Power. The former faculty demanded approval of what was considered orthodox to admire; the latter groaned in utter inability to pay the tax. It was then self-sneered at, spurred on, goaded to refine its taste and whet its zest. However, the more it was chided, the less it wanted to praise. Discovering that a wonderful sense of fatigue resulted from these conscientious efforts, I began to reflect whether I might dispense with that great labor. I concluded eventually that I could, and so sank supine into a luxury of calm before ninety-nine out of a hundred of the exhibited frames.

It seemed to me that an original and good picture was just as scarce as an original and good book.
