The shout tore through the canopy, a sudden explosion of sound that made the leaves tremble. Below, Lona and I paused, looking up as the Little Ones began their descent, tumbling from the high branches like playful sparks. They were breathless, their faces alight with a news that seemed to defy the laws of our world.

"We climbed the tallest one," a small voice gasped, "and we saw it! Far across the plain, rising from a solitary peak, there is something strange. It goes up and up until the sky itself seems to gather, thick and heavy, trying to push it back. But it keeps going!"

"It might be a city," another chimed in, "but it isn't like Bulika at all."

I ascended the nearest trunk, pushing through the dense foliage until I broke the surface. My breath caught in my throat. There, piercing the azure haze, was a city that defied gravity. It did not sit upon the mountain; it climbed it. Stone and spire merged with cloud and precipice in a dizzying tapestry of light and shadow. Palaces seemed to grow out of the rocks, and the boundary between earth and sky dissolved into a chaotic, beautiful blur.

Descending, I was joined by the Little Ones, who raced ahead of me, their laughter echoing through the trees. They moved with a joyous urgency, eyes fixed only on the path forward. As we hurried, the river transformed before our eyes. The muddy, sluggish stream of our world gave way to something ethereal—water so clear and vibrant it felt less like liquid and more like a living jewel. Nothing in this realm was merely *like* it; this was perfection itself.

From the plain, the city loomed larger, a beacon amidst the blue mists. But above it, a towering spire—or perhaps a natural rock formation crowned by magic—pierced the heavens. Around it, clouds swirled in violent, confused dances, twisting from gray to dark purple, tossing up vaporous foam that churned like whirlpools. Suddenly, a blinding flash erupted. For a heartbeat, the light danced around the children, then collapsed into a darkness so profound it felt like a void. Yet, through the gloom, their voices rose, hushed with awe.

"Did you see?"
"I saw!"
"What did you see?"
"The most beautiful man!"
"I heard him speak!"
"I didn't hear anything. What did he say?"

The smallest voice, belonging to Luva, cut through the silence. "He said, 'Ours are all mine, little ones: come along!'"

I had witnessed the lightning, but the words had eluded my ears. Lona, however, saw and heard with the children. Then came a second flash, and suddenly my vision expanded. The great quivering light coalesced into angelic faces, glowing and vanishing in rapid succession. A third flash followed, this time radiating pure humanity.

"My mother!" I cried out, tears stinging my eyes.
"Lots of mothers!" Luva shouted back.

Another flash, and the spectrum of creation exploded before us. Horses and elephants, lions and dogs—beasts of legend and reality alike. Birds of impossible splendor: great wings shimmering with every hue of the sunset, tiny feathers sparkling like scattered diamonds, silvery cranes, red flamingos, opal pigeons, peacocks blazing in gold and emerald, jeweled hummingbirds, and great-winged butterflies. Even the creeping things of the earth seemed to dance in the light.

"I see that serpents grow birds here, just as caterpillars used to grow butterflies!" Lona marveled.
"I saw my white pony," I whispered, a strange comfort settling in my chest. "The one that died when I was a child. I shouldn't have been so sad; I just needed to wait."

There was no thunder, no rolling crash of storms. Instead, a gentle, sweet rain began to fall, washing the air with a caressing coolness. We breathed deeply, our steps strengthening with every drop. The raindrops caught the light, flashing with the colors of awakened gems, while a magnificent rainbow arched over the city, bridging the gap between our world and the divine.

The clouds thickened, and the rain poured down in torrents, yet the children only exulted, running wild and free. The river, too, changed its nature. With a silent, radiant surge, it swept onward, filling its channel not with rough stone or sand, but with lush grass carpeted in a starry multitude of flowers—primroses, daisies, crocuses, narcissi, pimpernels, and anemones. The water remained crystal clear, untouched by the deluge, shining with a pale, berylline glow from its deep, dainty bed.

As we drew nearer to the mountain, we realized the river's source: it gushed from the very peak, rushing in full volume through the main street of the city. It cascaded down a grand staircase of deep, wide steps carved from porphyry and serpentine, leading to the foot of the mountain. Here, shallower steps branched off both banks, winding up toward the gate and the ascending street. Without hesitation, the Little Ones sprinted straight up the stairs to the gate, which stood wide open.

On the landing outside sat the portress, a woman-angel of dark visage, leaning her shadowed brow on her idle hand. The children rushed her, burying her in affectionate embraces. Before she could comprehend the intrusion, they had seized heaven by surprise and were already climbing the stairs beside the descending torrent. A great angel, attended by a host of shining beings, came down to greet them, but the children merrily evaded his grasp, running higher. Soon, a group of woman-angels descended in a joyful dance, and in a moment, the children were caught in heavenly arms, carried away by the radiants until they vanished from sight.

"Ah!" boomed the mighty angel as he continued his descent toward us, who were now close enough to hear his words. "This is well! These are soldiers ready to take heaven itself by storm! I hear of a horde of black bats on the frontiers; these will make short work of them!"

Seeing the horses and elephants struggling up behind us, he added, "Take those animals to the royal stables. Tend them well, then turn them into the king's forest."

Turning to us, he bowed low with a smile sweeter than any earthly grace. "Welcome home!"

Immediately, he turned and led the way upward. The scales of his armor flashed like fragments of lightning. No thought could fully articulate what I felt in that moment, standing before the officers of heaven. All I desired, all I did not yet know, was surely on its way to me.

We paused at the gate, where the radiant river roared forth. I could not name the stones that formed the archway, but among them I recognized the prototypes of every gem I had ever loved on earth. They were far more beautiful than their earthly counterparts; these were living stones. In them, I saw not just the intent, but the creator; not just the idea, but the embodied presence, the active sender. Nothing in this kingdom was dead, nothing was merely an object. Everything was alive with purpose.

We ascended through the city and emerged onto the upper side. There was no wall here, only a massive pile of broken rocks sloping upward like the moraine of an eternal glacier. Through the gaps between the stones, the river billowed out, white and powerful. Upon the peaks of these rocks, I could dimly discern three or four great steps of a stair, disappearing into a cloud white as snow. Above the steps, using only my mind's eye, I perceived a grand, ancient chair—the throne of the Ancient of Days. From over, under, and between those steps, the river of the water of life flowed endlessly, birthing new life without cease.

The great angel could guide us no further; those final steps we must climb alone.

My heart hammered with hope and longing as I tightened my grip on Lona's hand. We began the ascent, but soon we released each other, using our hands as well as our feet to navigate the treacherous, toilsome climb of the huge stones. Finally, we drew near the cloud that hung down the steps like the hem of a garment. We passed through the fringe and entered the deep folds. A warm, strong hand seized mine and pulled me to a small door with a golden lock. The door swung open; the hand released me and gently pushed me through. I turned quickly, and saw the board of a large book closing behind me. I stood alone in my library.
