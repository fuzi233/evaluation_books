Upon concluding my visit to my brother, and seeing my wife and young child safely return to our maternal home at Richmond, I set out for Governor Dunmore's country residence near Williamsburg. There, amidst the gathering storm clouds of war that darkened not only Virginia but every colony within hearing, I sought counsel. Delegates were already being named for the General Congress in Philadelphia. While Massachusetts faced open hostility between the populace and royal troops, and the Carolinas and Georgia remained staunchly loyal, Virginia prided itself on its ancient Cavalier heritage. Yet, surrounding the Governor was a swarm of loud, confident Loyalists, eager to draw swords and scourge the "rascally rebels" before them.

In these heated councils, I was forced to endure a barrage of vitriol aimed at my brother, Harry. Everyone agreed, with some justification, that his wife had steered him toward these extreme anti-British views. He was accused of being infatuated with the gentleman of Mount Vernon—a man who, day by day, committed himself deeper into a course of desperate resistance. To the ears of these eager supporters, my friendship for Harry and the trust I placed in him read as treachery. They saw it not as a final, exhausted attempt at reconciliation, but as a betrayal of the Crown. They toasted His Majesty's health with fervor, declaring they would die to defend his rights and see a Prince of his Royal house rule once more. They pleaded for my intervention: *Was it not time to secure Harry with a good command? Did I not hold sufficient influence?* Thus, our councils swung wildly between exultant triumph and furious despair.

I promised to plead with my brother. I wrote to him, though with scant hope, reiterating arguments I had used for years. My mother joined me, wielding her customary authority. She assailed him with Scripture, threatening him with divine punishment for disobedience and pointing to his elder brother's example. She hinted, I feared, at his subservience to his wife—the weakest possible argument. Though she hid her own letter from me, likely fearing its harsh tone, she revealed Harry's reply. To my astonishment, my brother had countered with scores of scriptural texts, penned in a neat, temperate, and elegantly composed style that surpassed my wife's own capabilities. He had even enlisted the services of Mr. Belman, a clergyman of the New Richmond who had adopted strong Whig opinions and preached sermons against Hagan. In this intellectual skirmish, Belman seemed to hold the upper hand.

Yet, my exhortations proved just as futile as my mother's. Harry ignored my letters entirely. Pressed further by the Government's friends, I acted imprudently, writing that I would visit him at Fanny's Mount within the week. Upon arrival, however, I found only my sister. She greeted me with perfect cordiality but informed me that Harry had ridden far into the countryside, toward the Blue Mountains, ostensibly to view horses, and would be away for an indefinite period.

Realizing hope was lost, I confessed to Fanny, "Harry is riding away. God knows to what end."
"The Lord prosper the righteous cause, Sir George," she replied.
"Amen, with all my heart," I answered, noting the irony that she spoke as an American while I remained an Englishman. "Tell him from me that if anything happens to our mother, I have ample provision in England for myself and mine, and I shall resign all our lands here in Virginia to him."
"You don't mean that, George?" she cried, her eyes brightening. "Well, it is but right and fair!" she conceded moments later. "Why should you, the eldest by mere hours, possess everything—a palace and estates in England, this plantation, the title, and children—while my poor Harry has nothing?" She praised my generosity, noting I did not share my mother's stubbornness, and kissed my cheek before I departed. With a woman like Fanny guiding him, how could I ever hope to turn my brother back?

Disappointed, I returned to the Governor. We agreed it was time to arm earnestly for the inevitable shock. The Court of Officials was agitated, savage in their abuse of the "wicked Whigs," and loud in their chants for Old England. They craved the day when they might ride down their enemies hand-to-hand. Having received thanks from His Excellency and pledged to raise a body of men for the defense of the Crown, I departed. Thus began the corps known as the Westmoreland Defenders, for which I was appointed Colonel.

News of a gentleman of my station taking command spread swiftly through the Government Gazette. Officers filled up readily, yet recruits were slow to arrive and quick to vanish. Nevertheless, friend Hagan eagerly offered to serve as chaplain. Madam Esmond distributed our colors and traveled the countryside recruiting volunteers. Most diligent of all was my old tutor, little Mr. Dempster, who, having fought on the Jacobite side in Scotland as a youth, ventured into the Carolinas to recruit among the descendants of his banished comrades, many of whom remained fiercely loyal to the Crown.

Meanwhile, Harry's expedition for horses carried him far beyond the Blue Mountains, eventually leading him to Annapolis, Baltimore, and finally Philadelphia, where a second General Congress sat. History records what transpired next: Lexington had fallen, and the first shots of a war destined to birth an independent nation rang out. Though we continued to protest our loyalty to the King, our resolve hardened—"to die or be free." Twenty thousand loyal petitioners assembled around Boston with arms and cannons seized from government stores. Benedict Arnold began his infamous career with the daring capture of two forts. Generals from London marched to reinforce General Gage at Boston. Soon, the armies clashed. Our British generals commenced their campaign of conquest with the "glorious blunder" of Breed's Hill. Fortifications were raised, and the two armies watched one another while Congress debated at Philadelphia who should command the confederated colonial forces.

All eyes turned to the Virginian regiment marching to join the new General-in-Chief. One wing was commanded by Henry Esmond Warrington, Esq., formerly a Captain in His Majesty's service, riding beside his brave little wife. I felt relief that she had left Virginia behind; had she stayed, our mother would undoubtedly have crossed lines to fight alongside her. Fortunately, that terrifying spectacle of civil war was spared to our immediate family.

The rush of farmers and country folk flowed almost exclusively toward the new northern army. News of the blood spilled at Lexington ignited a frenzy of wrath. Men denounced the "bloody British invader," ignoring the reality that he was merely executing duty, while resisting parties sought to prevent him from seizing supplies. Anger clouded judgment; the colonists sided with the rebels, driven by a natural ardor to test their strength against the "braggart domineering mother country." Breed's Hill became a symbol, a flaming summit where Liberty, Victory, and Glory seemed to dance. If these dreaded troops could be withstood by farmers and ploughmen, surely these famous officers could be outgeneralled by doctors, lawyers, and civilians! Who could compare with Britons, save their children across the Atlantic?

For the most part, the people around us sided with the revolution. Consequently, Sir George Warrington found his regiment of Westmoreland Defenders woefully thin at first, diminishing rapidly thereafter. Not only did the news of battles from the North demoralize them, but the behavior of Lord Dunmore enraged his own partisans. It had been agreed in countless meetings that the Governor would plant the King's standard and summon all loyal gentlemen. Many a gentleman, now a smug officer of the United States, once prayed aloud to perish under the folds of his country's flag or roared challenges to the "bloody traitors." But let bygones be bygones. Public history records that His Lordship, the Sovereign's representative, who so loudly invited the lieges to rally, was the first to pocket the standard and flee to his ships to escape danger. He refused to leave unless he could act as a pirate at midnight, burning and destroying.

While the loyal gentry remained on shore, subject to greater peril due to their leader's weakness, the Governor's conduct reached a nadir. It was early June; orchards bloomed with summer plenty. A week prior, I had exchanged compliments with His Excellency in Williamsburg, devising plans to check rebellion, shaking hands with the promise of *vincere aut mori*—to conquer or die—as our final words. Back at Richmond, our family discussed the prospects in the North and the quarrels between the Assembly and the Governor, who had reconvened them. Then, our ghostly Hagan rushed into the parlor, asking, "Have we heard the news of the Governor?"

"Has he dissolved the Assembly again, and put that scoundrel Patrick Henry in irons?" asked Madam Esmond.
"No such thing!" Hagan replied. "His lordship, with his lady and family, left their palace secretly at night. They are aboard a man-of-war off York. My lord sent a despatch to the Assembly, begging them to continue sitting, and announced that he had quit Government House solely out of fear of the people's fury."

What was to become of the sheep when the shepherd ran away? The gentlemen of the House of Assembly made pathetic entreaties, guaranteeing security if he would land, imploring him to appear to pass bills. But no. The man-of-war became his seat of government.
