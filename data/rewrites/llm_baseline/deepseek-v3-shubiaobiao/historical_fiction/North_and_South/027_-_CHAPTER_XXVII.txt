—*A Midsummer Night’s Dream*

John Thornton plunged into the day’s affairs with a sharp, unrelenting focus. The market showed a flicker of demand for finished goods, and he seized the opportunity, driving hard bargains with the precision of a man who refused to yield an inch. At the magistrates’ meeting, his voice carried weight—clear, decisive, unclouded by hesitation. Men older and wealthier deferred to his judgment, though he scarcely noticed their silent respect. To him, it mattered no more than the faint breath of wind that failed to sway the smoke rising from Milton’s chimneys.

His mind, so thoroughly disciplined, allowed no room for distraction—or so he told himself. Yet beneath the surface, memory pulsed like an unwelcome echo. Not the sting of Margaret’s rejection the day before, but the warmth of her embrace the night of the riot, when for one fleeting moment, she had clung to him as if he were her anchor. The recollection unsettled him, threading through his thoughts as he navigated the crowded streets, blind to the faces around him.

A voice broke through his abstraction.

“Mr. Thornton! You cut me cold indeed. And how does Mrs. Thornton fare?”

Thornton blinked. “Dr. Donaldson. Forgive me—I didn’t see you. My mother is well, thank you. The weather favors the harvest, I hope.”

The doctor chuckled. “Ah, but your fair weather is my foe. When trade falters, sickness thrives. You Milton men keep me in business.”

Thornton’s mouth curved wryly. “Not I, Doctor. Bad debts and strikes may trouble others, but my appetite remains steady.”

Donaldson’s expression sobered. “Speaking of health—I’ve just come from your friend’s house. Mrs. Hale… I fear her time is short. She craves fruit, though even the simplest will do.”

A pause. Thornton’s pulse, so often praised for its steadiness, betrayed him. “Is there anything she needs? Anything I might provide?”

“Jargonelle pears would suffice. The market has plenty.”

Thornton nodded. “You’ll inform me if more is required.”

Without another word, he turned toward the nearest fruit shop, selecting the finest grapes, the ripest peaches, their colors vivid against the vine leaves cradling them. The shopkeeper waited, expectant.

“Shall I send them to Marlborough Mills, sir?”

“No.” Thornton took the basket himself, ignoring the curious glances of passing ladies as he strode through the streets, arms laden like a common errand boy.

*Let her scorn this if she will*, he thought defiantly. *I do it for Mr. Hale. I do it because it is right.*

At the Hales’ door, he took the stairs two at a time, entering unannounced. Mrs. Hale lay feverish on the sofa, Mr. Hale reading beside her. Margaret sat at her mother’s feet, her fingers stilled over her needlework.

Thornton’s gaze swept past her—deliberately, pointedly—as he approached Mrs. Hale. His voice, when he spoke, was softer than any would have thought possible from such a man.

“Dr. Donaldson mentioned fruit might ease you. I hope these will serve.”

Mrs. Hale’s tremulous delight, her husband’s quiet gratitude—neither moved him as much as the silent figure by the table, her presence humming in the air between them though he refused to look at her.

“I must go,” he said abruptly. “Forgive the intrusion. If you permit, I’ll bring more when I can.”

A bow. A farewell. Not a glance toward Margaret.

Yet as the door closed behind him, she felt the absence of his acknowledgment like a fresh wound.

---

The fruit, so carefully chosen, lay gleaming on the table.

“How kind he was!” Mrs. Hale murmured. “Margaret, taste these grapes. Surely even you must admit his kindness.”

“Yes,” Margaret replied, her voice low.

Her father, peeling a peach, smiled. “Such generosity melts prejudice. I haven’t tasted fruit like this since I was a boy.” He chuckled. “Do you remember the currant bushes at home, Margaret?”

The question struck like a blow. Home—the garden, the sunlit walls, the lichen mapping the stones in gray and gold. A sob rose in her throat. Without a word, she fled the room.

In the sanctuary of her chamber, she pressed a hand to her mouth. But Dixon’s presence, rifling through a drawer, startled her.

“Miss! What’s wrong? Is the mistress worse?”

“No, nothing.” Margaret dashed a hand across her eyes. “What are you looking for?”

Dixon hesitated. “Best not trouble you now, but… it’s that girl you visited. Higgins’ daughter. She’s dead.”

Margaret’s breath caught. “Bessy?”

“Aye. Her sister’s below, asking if you’ve a spare cap or such—Bessy wanted to be buried in something of yours.”

Margaret’s fingers trembled as she searched. “Of course. Poor Bessy. I didn’t think—”

“The girl asks if you’ll see her. But you needn’t.”

Margaret paled. A corpse—cold, still. She had never faced death so directly.

Yet when she descended to the kitchen, Mary’s tear-swollen face undid her resolve.

“She loved you,” Mary wept. “Her last words were of you—to keep father from drink.”

Dixon scoffed. “No need for you to go, Miss Hale. I’ll do it.”

But Margaret straightened. “No. I will.”

Fear coiled in her stomach, but she would not shrink from it. Not when kindness—simple, uncomplicated—was all that remained to give.
