The Other Wife.

The morning air, though initially gentle, had begun to press heavily upon Romola as she turned from the walls near San Marco toward the intersecting lanes of Santa Croce. The Borgo La Croce lay in a profound stillness; she walked listening to the echo of her own steps on the sun-drenched pavement until, rounding a bend, she spied a small child, barely three years old, clad only in a white shirt. He paused mid-waddle, turning a circle of reddish-brown curls atop a square, sturdy boy's back. As he faced her, his dark eyes widened with tears, his lip trembling, his fat brown fists clutching his shirt in helpless panic. A tall, shadow-casting figure loomed behind him, sending the boy's fear into a climax of loud, rolling sobs.

Instinctively, driven by a tender, maternal impulse, Romola uncovered her head. She stooped, wrapping her arms around the shivering child, pressing her cheek against his. "You have lost yourself, little one," she cooed, kissing him gently. "But never mind! We shall find the house again. Perhaps Mamma will meet us." She sensed he had fled while his mother's gaze was elsewhere and believed he would soon be pursued.

"Oh, what a heavy, heavy boy!" she murmured, attempting to lift him, but failing. "Come, you must toddle back by my side." The boy's lips remained sealed in an awed silence, his grip on the shirt unyielding, yet his free hand yielded readily to her soft, strong palm.

"You *have* a Mamma?" Romola asked, looking down at the solemn little man with a yearning ache. He offered no sound. Under different circumstances, a girl might have chattered, but this silent, square-shouldered creature was unmoved.

However, he was keenly aware of his destination. At the turn before San Ambrogio, he dragged Romola toward the house, his brow furrowed. "Ah, that is the way home, is it?" she smiled. He merely thrust his head forward, pulling urgently for speed. After another sharp turn, they emerged onto a street opening to garden grounds. Here, the boy halted, tugging her toward stone stairs. He showed no desire to let go, and she, unwilling to abandon him without ensuring his safety, ascended. The light faded as they climbed, giving way to a sudden brightness at an open doorway. Through a small lobby and another door, they entered a room where no sound greeted her arrival.

At the far end, sitting on a low chair by a cradle, was Tessa, her head lolling, fast asleep. By a window, with her back to the entrance, sat Monna Lisa, silently preparing salad. Romola caught this still tableau in an instant before Lillo snatched his hand away and ran to his mother, leaning his head back against her arm, regarding Romola with serious scrutiny.

As Lillo nudged her, Tessa awoke, blinking in bewilderment. But her gaze landed on the stranger, and she sprang up, flushing deeply, trembling, unable to speak or move forward.

"Ah! We have met before," Romola said, stepping forward with a smile. "I am glad it was *your* little boy. He was crying in the street; I suppose he had run away. So we walked together a little, and then he knew where he was, and brought me here. But you had not missed him? That is well, else you would have been frightened."

The shock of discovering Lillo missing overwhelmed Tessa. Her color drained and returned as she seized her son's arm, rushing to Monna Lisa with a half-sob in the old woman's ear. "Oh, Lisa, you are wicked! Why did you stand with your back to the door? Lillo ran away ever so far!"

"Holy Mother!" gasped Monna Lisa, dropping her spoon. "Where were *you*, then? I thought you were there, watching him."

"But you *know* I fall asleep when I rock," Tessa retorted pettishly.

"Well, well, we must keep the outer door shut, or tie him up," Monna Lisa sighed, her voice thick and meek. "For he'll be as cunning as Satan before long, and that is the holy truth. But how did he come back?"

This question brought Tessa back to the present, her blush returning. Turning to Romola, she bowed her head. "Doubtless the most noble lady brought him back." Moving closer, she added, "It is my shame he was found in only his shirt; he kicked and refused his clothes this morning, and the Mother, poor soul, will never hear of him being beaten. But what can an old woman do without a stick when the lad's legs grow so strong? Let your nobleness look at his legs."

Lillo, sensing the inquiry, hitched his shirt up to display his olive-round legs with dispassionate curiosity. Romola laughed, shaking and kissing him, a gesture that eased Tessa's tension. For when Naldo had recounted the Carnival incident to Tessa, asking who the celestial lady might be, Naldo had replied, "Not exactly, my Tessa; only one of the saints," refusing further comment. Thus, in Tessa's dreamlike blend of childhood impressions, Romola became vaguely associated with the painted saints in the churches. With good beings unlikely to punish her as Nofri or the Devil did, Tessa felt a growing relief.

"Ninna is in the cradle," Tessa said, gesturing to the sleeping infant. "She is pretty, too."

Romola approached the cradle as Monna Lisa returned to her salad. "Ah! She is waking; look, her blue eyes are open," Romola noted. "You must take her, and I will sit in this chair—may I?—and nurse Lillo. Come, Lillo!"

She seated herself in Tito's chair, extending her arms. Lillo hesitated, pointing accusing fingers at her with a mix of puzzlement and anger. "That's Babbo's chair," he declared, fearing the consequences if his father returned to find a stranger there.

"But Babbo is not here, and I shall go soon," Romola assured him, noting with surprise the daintiness of Tessa's attire despite her contadina garb. "Come, let me nurse you as he does." Lillo consented, sinking into the lap, exploring her dress and hands for ornaments beside the rosary.

Tessa, having coaxed Ninna from her peevish waking, sat near Romola's knee, arranging the baby, jealous that the strange lady paid attention to Lillo as Naldo did.

"Lillo was going to be angry because I sat in Babbo's chair," Romola said, bending to kiss Ninna's foot. "Will he come soon and want it?"

"Ah, no!" Tessa exclaimed. "You can sit in it a long while. I shall be sorry when you go. When you first came to care for me at the Carnival, I thought it wonderful; you came and went so fast. And Naldo said, perhaps you were a saint, and it made me tremble a little, though saints are very good, I know; and you were kind to me, and now you care for Lillo. Perhaps you will always come to care for me. That is how Naldo did a long while ago; he came when I was frightened at San Giovanni. I couldn't think where he came from—he was so beautiful and good. And so are you," Tessa concluded, gazing up with devout admiration.

"Naldo is your husband. His eyes are like Lillo's," Romola observed, studying the boy's dark eyebrows. She spoke not as a questioner, but with a quiet certainty that baffled Tessa.

"Ah! You know him!" Tessa paused in wonder. "Perhaps you know Nofri and Peretola, and our house on the hill, and everything. Yes, like Lillo's eyes; but not his hair. His hair is dark and long—" she continued, growing excited. "Ah! If you know, *ecco*!"

She touched a thin red silk cord at her throat, drawing forth a tiny old parchment *Breve*, a horn of red coral, and a long dark curl carefully tied and suspended. She presented these mysteries to Romola, shielding them from Ninna's grasping hands.

"It is a fresh one. I cut it lately. See how bright it is!" she whispered, laying it against Romola's white fingers. "They fade, and then he lets me cut another when his hair grows; and I keep it with the *Breve*, because sometimes he is away so long, and I think it helps protect me."

A slight shiver ran through Romola as the curl touched her skin. At Tessa's first mention of her husband arriving mysteriously, a possibility flared in Romola's heart, quickening her pulse. To one searching desperately for truth, faint suggestions carry weight. Holding the curl, it seemed momentarily a mocking phantom of the lock Romola herself had cut five years prior. Yet she maintained her composure, determined to learn the truth without wounding this trusting, ignorant soul, whose mind remained childlike. "Foolish and helpless:" so far, she matched Baldassarre's description.
