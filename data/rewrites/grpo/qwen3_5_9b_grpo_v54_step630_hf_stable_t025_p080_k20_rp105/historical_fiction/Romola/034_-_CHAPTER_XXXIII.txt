Baldassarre wandered through Florence seeking the cheapest shelter, his feet drawn inevitably toward the only uneven patch of ground within the city walls. From this vantage point, elevated above the rooftops, one could see past the urban sprawl to the protective hills and the distant valley, visible only through the gap carved by the Arno. While part of this terrain was the famous stone-quarry of Bogoli, Baldassarre ascended the slope behind the Via de' Bardi, known locally as the hill of San Giorgio. Bratti had informed him that Tito resided on this street, and Baldassarre hoped to find a hiding place among the scattered dwellings here. Having grown accustomed to the vast, open sky over the years, the claustrophobic labyrinth of narrow Florentine alleys intensified his sense of isolation and the foggy decay of his memory.

The hill was largely garden land, save for a jagged section of rough ground scarred by a landslide that had destroyed houses centuries prior. Perched atop this broken earth stood a peculiar, truncated tower-like structure with fluted tiles, adjacent to a small outhouse wedged against a ruinous stone wall. Beneath a dying mulberry tree, its final leaves fluttering into the open doorways, a withered, hardy old woman untied a goat with two kids. Baldassarre noted that one section of the outbuilding housed livestock, while the other stood empty save for tools and straw—the perfect refuge he sought.

He approached the woman, but it took persistent shouting near her ear to penetrate her silence. Initially, she responded only with head shakes and muffled denials of "No lodging," her tone betraying a profound deafness. Undeterred, Baldassarre explained that he was a destitute stranger from overseas, unable to afford hostels, and offered a few quattrini weekly for the straw. The woman remained dubious, muttering to herself, until a new idea sparked. She retrieved a hatchet, gesturing to a log half-buried in litter, and proposed a bargain: if he chopped it up, he might sleep there for one night.

Agreeing, Baldassarre began to chop. Monna Lisa watched with arms akimbo, a smile of cunning satisfaction playing on her lips. *"It has lain there since my old man died,"* she thought, *"What then? I might as well have put a stone on the fire. He chops well, though he speaks a foreign tongue and looks odd. I couldn't have gotten it done cheaper. And if he only needs straw, I can make him run errands up and down the hill. Who needs to know? A hidden sin is half forgiven. He is a stranger; he won't notice *her*. I'll tell her to keep her tongue still."*

The object of her silent threat possessed a pair of blue eyes fixed on a large round hole in the shutter of the upper window. The shutter was closed not for punishment, but because the opposite window alone boasted glass; the chill weather made the four-inch aperture sufficient for spying. Though the hole was too high, forcing the small observer to stand on a rickety stool, Tessa endured the discomfort willingly. Drawn by the strange voice, she had darted across her room to peer out, remaining there until the wood was split and Baldassarre entered the outhouse as dusk settled, sinking onto the straw.

A powerful temptation seized Tessa. She desired to share her supper with the old man, to converse with him in a way she could not with the deaf Monna Lisa. Strangers were transient figures, living nowhere specific, and while disobedience weighed heavily on her conscience, the confession next Easter seemed manageable alongside her minor faults of dozing over beads and occasional petulance toward Monna Lisa. With an excited, pouting expression, she adjusted her dark-green serge gown, red girdle, and string of red glass beads, her brown hair neatly knotted with a silver pin. She wore a single new pride: a fine gold ring.

Sitting on the stool, her soul fluttered with the thrill of this forbidden transgression. She had been strictly commanded to make no acquaintances, warned that such friendships would turn her happiness into lead, yet she had obeyed so thoroughly that she kept her face shaded at church. Fear of her stepfather, Nofri, had aided her compliance, but this old man was merely a sleeping stranger, likely unaware of Nofri's existence. If she fed him, he would be pleased and silent. Monna Lisa's objections could be ignored; consequences mattered only after the deed was done.

Knowing supper was being prepared—a mountain of cheesy macaroni fragrant enough to tame any wanderer—Tessa descended the stairs with innocent inquiry, quickly knitting her brow into a peremptory frown like a kitten asserting dominance. She ordered the old woman upstairs, declaring her intention to eat below. Minutes later, lantern in one hand and a wooden bowl of macaroni in the other, she kicked gently at the outhouse door. Baldassarre, roused from a somber reverie, doubted his wakefulness as he opened the door to this surprising little handmaid, whose wide eyes broke into his dismal solitude with delight.

"I've brought you some supper," she shouted, leaning close to his ear as if addressing the deaf. "Sit down and eat it, while I stay with you."

Surprise and distrust overwhelmed Baldassarre, yet he felt no impulse to reject the visitor. He sank passively onto the straw as Tessa placed herself beside him, set the bowl on his lap, and positioned the lantern, crossing her hands and nodding significantly. *"Yes, you may really eat it,"* her gesture declared. In her excitement, she had forgotten he could hear, falling back on her habitual pantomime and shouting.

The invitation was welcome; he had been gnawing dry bread and craved warmth. Tessa watched him eat, initially wary of his fierce gaze, until she ventured closer again. "I like my supper, don't you?" she cried.

Baldassarre returned a look of mild kindness, resembling a dog touched by affection but unable to smile. "Yes," he said, "but I can hear well—I'm not deaf."

"It is true; I forgot," Tessa exclaimed, clasping her hands. "But Monna Lisa is deaf, and I live with her. She's a kind old woman, and I'm not frightened. We live very well; we have plenty of nice things. I can have nuts if I like. I used to work, but I didn't like it; I preferred feeding the mules. I'd love to see poor Giannetta again. We only have a goat and two kids now. I used to talk to the goat because there was no one else but Monna Lisa. But now I have something else—can you guess what it is?"

She pulled her head back, offering a challenging smile as if presenting a riddle.

"No," Baldassarre replied dreamily, setting aside his bowl. This young, prattling creature seemed a memory from his own youth.

"You like me to talk to you, don't you?" Tessa pressed. "But you must not tell anybody. Shall I fetch you some cold sausage?"

He shook his head, his expression now so mild that Tessa relaxed. "Well, then, I have a little baby. Such a pretty *bambinetto*, with tiny fingers and nails! Not old yet; born at the Nativita, Monna Lisa says. I was married one Nativita, a long time ago, and nobody knew. Oh, Santa Madonna! I didn't mean to tell you that!"

Tessa stiffened, biting her lip, watching Baldassarre as if expecting a dramatic reaction. But he seemed indifferent, perhaps due to the nature of strangers. "Yes," she continued aloud, "you are a stranger; you don't live anywhere or know anybody, do you?"

"No," Baldassarre murmured, thinking more than answering, "I only know one man."

"His name is not Nofri, is it?" Tessa asked anxiously.

"No," Baldassarre noticed her fear and clarified. "Is that your husband's name?"

The mistake amused Tessa. She laughed and clapped her hands. "No, indeed! But I must not tell you anything about my husband. You would never think what he is—not at all like Nofri!" She laughed again at the absurd contrast between the gruff name Nofri and her husband's image.

"But I don't see him very often," she said more seriously. "Sometimes I pray to the Holy Madonna to send him oftener, and once she did. But I must go back to my *bimbo* now. I'll bring it to show you tomorrow. You would like to see it. Sometimes it cries and makes a face, but only when it's hungry, Monna Lisa says. You wouldn't think it, but Monna Lisa had babies once, and they are all dead old men. My husband says she will never die now, because she's so well dried. I'm glad of that, for I'm fond of her. You would like to stay here tomorrow, shouldn't you?"

"I should like to have this place to come and rest in, that's all," Baldassarre said. "I would pay for it, and harm nobody."

"No, indeed; I think you are not a bad old man. But you look sorry about something. Tell me, is there anything you shall cry about when I leave you by yourself? *I* used to cry once."

"No, child; I think I shall cry no more."

"That's right; and I'll bring you some breakfast, and show you the *bimbo*. Good-night."

Tessa departed with her bowl and lantern, closing the door behind her. To Baldassarre, this loving apparition was no more than a faint rainbow against the blackness for a man wrestling in deep waters. He scarcely thought of her again until his dreamy waking faded into the vivid disturbances of sleep.

Tessa, however, dwelt deeply on him. Upon entering the house, she immediately confessed her actions to Monna Lisa, insisting the stranger be allowed to rest whenever he wished. The old woman, hoping to make him a useful tenant, feigned reluctance, shaking her head and claiming Messer Naldo would be angry. Tessa did not believe this; Naldo had never objected to strangers who lived nowhere, and this old man knew only one person who was not Nofri.

"Well," Monna Lisa finally conceded in her thick undertone, "if I let him stay for a while and carry things up the hill for me, thou must keep thy counsel and tell nobody."

"No," Tessa countered, "I'll only tell the *bimbo*."

"And then," Monna Lisa whispered, "God may love us well enough not to let Messer Naldo find out anything. For he never comes here but at dark; and as he was here two days ago, it's likely he'll never return until the old man is gone."

"Oh me! Monna," Tessa clasped her hands, "I wish Naldo had not to go such a long, long way sometimes before he comes back again."

"Ah, child! the world's big, they say. There are places behind the mountains, and if people go night and day, they get to Rome, and see the Holy Father."

Tessa looked submissive to this mystery, rocking her baby and singing vague, loving syllables that mimicked a triple chime.

The next morning, eager for more dialogue and the joy of showing her baby, Tessa was unusually industrious. However, before she could prepare his breakfast, she found Monna Lisa employing Baldassarre as a water carrier. She deferred her prayers, rushing down to insist he sit on his straw so she could sit beside him while he ate. This posture made the new companionship doubly delightful for Tessa, recalling her own days sitting on straw with goats and mules.

"I will not let Monna Lisa give you too much work," she said, bringing steaming broth and soft bread. "I don't like much work, and I daresay you don't. I like sitting in the sunshine and feeding things. Monna Lisa says work is good, but she does it all herself, so I don't mind. She's not a cross old woman; you needn't be afraid. Now, eat that, and I'll go fetch my baby to show you."

Soon she returned with the small mummy-case. The baby looked lively, possessing unusually large dark eyes, though lacking more than a hint of a future nose.

"This is my baby," Tessa said, seating herself close. "You didn't think it was so pretty, did you? It is like the little Gesu, and I should think the Santa Madonna would be kinder to me now, is it not true? But I have not much to ask for, because I have everything now—only that I should see my husband oftener. You may hold the *bambino* a little if you like, but I think you must not kiss him, because you might hurt him."

She spoke the prohibition with a tone of soothing excuse, and Baldassarre could not refuse to hold the small package. "Poor thing! poor thing!" he said in a deep voice that held a strangely threatening quality beneath its apparent pity. It did not seem to him that this guileless woman could reconcile him to the world; rather, she appeared to be with him against it, a creature who would need vengeance.

"Oh, don't you be sorry for me," she said. "Though I don't see him often, he is more beautiful and good than anybody else in the world. I say prayers to him when he's away. You couldn't think what he is!"

She looked at Baldassarre with a wide, mysterious glance, taking the baby back and almost wishing he would question her as if desperate to know more.

"Yes, I could," Baldassarre said, rather bitterly.

"No, I'm sure you never could," Tessa insisted earnestly. "You thought he might be Nofri," she added with triumphant conclusiveness. "But never mind; you couldn't know. What is your name?"
