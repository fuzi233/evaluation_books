He stood planted on the curb, chest stuffed full of a raw, throbbing misery so heavy it had felt like it would pin him to the asphalt forever, staring after her retreating figure until she vanished around the far bend. The second she was out of sight, that weight slid off his ribs like a dead thing sloughing off its skin. She had handed him his freedom back — and though some prickle of his old sense argued he’d never truly surrendered it, he had no patience for fine parsing now. The road ahead lay clear, no tangled strings of obligation, no half-written manuscript weighing on his shoulders, no clinging demands that he be a person he wasn’t.

That lightness swelled fast, turning from relief to a giddy, unruly joy. By the time he was halfway to the parish church, he already knew exactly what he had to do next. The vicar of his district lived less than ten minutes from the square, and Oleron turned his steps straight there. He needed every scrap of information he could dig up on that old house with the weathered insurance marks, the slanted *To Let* boards that always looked like they were tipping over on purpose, and the vicar was the only person in the neighbourhood likely to have all the unspoken, dark little local history no one else cared to remember. Once that last chore was done — Oleron chuckled under his breath, sharp and hungry — the real fun could start.

He got less than he’d hoped for, at first. The house was old, the vicar told him, as if Oleron needed a man of the cloth to point that out. It had a reputation for being haunted, he added primly, and Oleron leaned in, ears pricked. But most old houses collected that kind of silly gossip among the uneducated, the vicar hurried to add, and the modern world’s sad lack of true Faith only fed those stupid superstitions. He was careful, soft-spoken, the kind of man who would never say a thing he couldn’t take back if his listener reacted badly, and Oleron smiled, seeing right through it.

“Don’t worry about my nerves,” he said. “How long has the place been empty?”
“Must be a dozen years now, at least,” the vicar replied.
“The last tenant — did you know them? Man or woman?” A strange, cold tingle crawled up Oleron’s spine as he spoke.
“Man,” the vicar said. “Name was Madley, if I recall right. Artist. Total recluse, barely ever left the four walls. And —” he hesitated, then spilled the words fast, like ripping off a bandage “ — since you’ve clearly come looking for the truth, better you hear it straight than get it all twisted from the gossips: he died there. Circumstances were very strange. Post-mortem found not a single crumb of food in his stomach, even though he had more than enough money in his pockets to buy a feast. His whole body was just worn to nothing. People talked suicide, but you’ll agree, starving yourself deliberately like that? It’s almost unheard of. They brought in an open verdict.”

“Ah,” Oleron said, his mouth gone dry. “Is there any full history of this parish?”
“Nothing comprehensive. I’ve only compiled notes on our ecclesiastical records, the old registers and so on — I’d be happy to show you, if you’d like, but the parish is huge, I’ve only one curate to help me, and my spare time is terribly limited…” The rest of their visit was spent making small talk about the size of the parish and the shortage of church staff, and Oleron thanked him, took his leave, and walked slowly home.

He walked so slow, in fact, that twice when he was within a stone’s throw of his own gate, he turned right back around and tramped another twenty minute loop around the square. He had delicate, dangerous work ahead of him, work that would take every scrap of his focus: he needed to scrub every last leftover thought of his ex, every half-finished line of *Romilly*, every scrap of mental clutter out of his head, get his mind so empty and receptive he could recapture that exact first impression of the house, the one he’d had the day he moved in, before the slow, creeping saturation of its presence had snuck up on him. Back then, the house’s influence had had to fight through all the stale, overwritten chapters he was dragging around. Now he was light. Unburdened. Free of both the book and the woman who had inspired its tedious main character.

Now he could meet the new, unknown, coy, jealous, bewitching thing that called to him — the Beckoning Fair One.

At half past two that afternoon, he turned his key in the Yale lock, stepped over the threshold, and pulled the door shut behind him.

The trick worked instantly, so perfectly he almost shouted out loud with triumph. It felt like he’d escaped *into* the house, not into his own home. That old rush of ease, mastery, exhilaration he hadn’t felt since his writing used to hum with fresh, unbroken promise flooded his bones. The air tasted thicker, sweeter, like it held more oxygen; his steps felt light, almost weightless, as if his own gravity had shifted. The flowers in the bowls glowed, the meadowsweet-coloured wood panels and mouldings stretched out fair and warm to greet him, the polished floor shone, the high ceiling dotted with faint, strange old star marks seemed to lean down to meet him. Oleron laughed right back, spoke out loud to the empty room. “Oh, you’re pretty,” he murmured, soft as a lover. “So pretty.”

He laid down on his couch then, and spent the whole afternoon like a convalescent waiting for a beloved visitor, drifting in a delicious empty haze, smiling to himself half-asleep, his eyes lifting every few minutes to drink in the alluring walls around him. He stayed there until dark fell, and the old house’s nocturnal creaks and whispers started up. But nothing happened. No sign, no manifestation, no trace of the thing he was chasing. He waited the whole next day too, holding his mind as still and sensitive as a photographer’s waiting plate, but still nothing. The thing he was wooing was shy. Exacting.

On the third day, he thought he got it. A droll, cunning little smirk tugged at his mouth, and he chuckled. “Oho, so that’s how you want to play it, is it?” He stood musing, thumbing his lean jaw, gaze slanted off to the side, and suddenly crossed to the hall, took down his hat, and headed for the door. “No, I won’t go fetch Elsie. No need to break a butterfly on a wheel, my pretty thing. You want to play hard to get? I’ll play hard to ignore right back.”

He tramped to the railway station, caught a train, and spent the rest of the day wandering the countryside, not a single thought spared for the house. Oleron thought he knew exactly how to handle coy Fair Ones that beckoned you in then hid away. He didn’t get back until after eleven that night. “Now, my Beckoning Fair one,” he murmured, fumbling for his keys as he walked down the dark alley.

Inside the flat, he was perfectly calm, deliberate, careful not to give away how eager he was. To pretend he was half-asleep, ready to collapse straight into bed, he only lit one single candle. He yawned big, fake, as he set off on his usual nightly circuit, stocking feet making no sound on the floorboards. First the kitchen: the full moon slanted through the uncurtained window, leaving a lozenge of almost peacock-blue moonlight on the planks. He could see the faint reflection of his candle, of his own face, in the glass. The door to the little powder closet stood ajar, so he shut it, then sat on the old chair with the cushion made of a folded harp-bag to take off his boots. He moved through the bathroom next, where another slice of that blue moonlight cut across the windowsill and the exposed pipes on the wall, then the rarely used study, where he stood for a minute staring out at the silvered roofs across the square. He walked through the sitting-room noiselessly, stepped into his bedroom, and set the candle down on the chest of drawers. His face was blank, slack, the picture of a tired man ready to sleep — but he had never been more alert, more sharp, more cunning.

He drew down the blind, took off his coat, then stooped to reach for his slippers under the low bed. He had no proof, no reason to know for certain, but he was utterly, unshakably sure the manifestation he’d been waiting for three days for was right there, close enough to touch. He felt no fear, only thrumming, electric curiosity. It might startle him, but that was all. His hand groped under the bed, back and forth, searching for the slippers.

And then his heart gave a leap so hard it stopped beating for a horrible, suspended second. His hand closed around the slippers, but he would have pitched forward face-first into the floor if he hadn’t already been on his knees. Something soft, silky, the texture of long loose hair, brushed slow across the top of his scalp. He held his position, head tilted to the side like he was still just groping for his shoes, until he could breathe again. When he stood up a moment later, there was a bead of bright blood on his lower lip where he’d bitten right through it, and his watch had jolted clean out of his waistcoat pocket, dangling at the end of its short leather chain, swinging back and forth.

Before the watch had even stopped swaying, he was back in control. He stepped to the mantelpiece, where his grandmother’s portrait stood, and positioned himself so he could see the steady flame of the candle on the chest of drawers reflected in the portrait’s glass. He watched the little glints of light bounce off the facets of the trinkets around the mirror, all of them still — except one faint, moving gleam, bobbing slowly up and down through the air. And with every downward sweep of that gleam came a soft, silky, crackling rustle, exactly the sound of a comb being pulled through long hair.

Oleron didn’t flinch. He reached up, caught his dangling watch, and started winding it slow, deliberate. Then he started emptying the change out of his trouser pockets, lining every penny and halfpenny up in a neat little row on the mantelpiece, like he was nothing more than a boring, tired man winding down for the night. The soft, electric rustle of the comb filled every inch of the small bedroom. When he thought he was ready, he feigned another yawn, and spun around so fast his shoulders jolted.

The thing whose hair was being combed was still invisible. But the comb didn’t stop moving. It had tilted a little, shifted a few inches to the left, gliding in slow, even sweeps from five feet above the floor straight down to a point just a few inches under the top of the chest of drawers.

Oleron kept up his act perfectly. He walked to the little washstand in the corner, poured a glass of water, and washed his hands. He took off his waistcoat, kept going through the motions of getting ready for bed, and then smiled to himself, sharp and sly. “Hm,” he said out loud, loud enough for every corner of the room to hear. “Think I’ll go read for a quarter of an hour.”

He stepped out of the bedroom, and was gone for two full minutes. When he came back, the room was dead quiet. He glanced at the chest of drawers. The black vulcanite comb was lying perfectly still, right between his discarded collar and a pair of gloves. Oleron picked it up. It was just a cheap eighteenpenny comb he’d bought off a chemist’s display card, solid, ordinary, no more capable of defying the laws of physics than the planets were of veering off their orbits. He set it back down, then glanced at the bundle of papers he was clutching in his other hand — the full fifteen completed chapters of the old *Romilly* manuscript. He tossed it into the armchair in the corner.

“Just as I thought,” he muttered, and his lips curled. “She’s blindly, ragingly, murderously jealous.”

Night after night after that, then day after day, for so long he stopped being able to count how many had passed, Oleron chased his unseen housemate. He courted it, cajoled it, deliberately neglected it, whispered threats, begged soft, eaten alive by a curiosity that burned hotter every hour, until his whole life was nothing but that single consuming hunger, that single all-encompassing desire to finally meet the Beckoning Fair One face to face. He didn’t notice his own appetite fading, his work falling by the wayside, the rest of the world slipping further and further away. He thought he was the hunter. He never once considered he was the one being lured.
