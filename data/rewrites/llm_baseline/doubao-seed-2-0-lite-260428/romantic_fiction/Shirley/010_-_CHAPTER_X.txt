Spring deepened, soft and lush across England. Every hedgerow burst bright green, every hillside glowed fresh after rain, cottage gardens spilled over with crocus and daffodil bloom, but the country’s heart was far from light. The poor still shivered in want, their employers haggard with sleepless worry. Trade hung on the edge of paralysis, for the war dragged on, bleeding English men and money for ends that felt smaller and smaller, further and further out of reach. Tidings of occasional Peninsula victories trickled in so slowly that weeks would stretch where the only news was Bonaparte’s crowing boasts of his own triumphs, and the men teetering on the very brink of bankruptcy felt the endless, grinding fight against his seemingly unbeatable power more than they could bear. They screamed for peace at any cost, held raucous meetings, ranted in speeches, pushed petitions that cared not a whit what terms were surrendered, so long as their shops and mills were spared ruin.

The great mass of men, when gathered in their own self-interest, are unapologetically selfish, and the British merchant class embodied that truth clearly then. Chivalry, honour, disinterested pride in their nation – all had burned thin under the threat of lost profits. Let France buffet them on the right cheek, they would turn the left, hand over their coats, their waistcoats, every last garment save the one holding their purse. They would cringe and fawn until Bonaparte’s hand closed over that beloved purse, and only then would the British bulldog spark wake in them. They claimed to hate war for its bloodshed and cruelty, but most cared nothing for the far-off fighting – they cared only for their ledgers, and deemed everyone not bent on trade to be wasting air, eating bread they had no right to earn. Long may England never become a nation of shopkeepers alone.

Robert Moore, for all his quiet grit, was one of those men thrashing hard to save his mill from ruin, fanning agitation across the north against the war, pushing his wealthier, better-connected peers to join his cause. Sometimes, in rare flashes of clarity, he saw the absurdity of begging Bonaparte for concessions: when he read dispatches of all Europe rising to throw back the invader, of the Duke of Wellington’s steady, unswerving advance across the Peninsula, his dispatches written with plain, unboastful truth, Moore knew victory would come in the end. But that end was too far off. He would be crushed before it arrived, his mills ground to dust, his family’s legacy lost. He had no time to spare for anything else.

The rift with his old Tory friend, the Rector Mr. Helstone, came swiftly after that. They clashed publicly at a town meeting, fired sharp, sniping letters back and forth across the local newspapers, and Helstone branded Moore a Jacobin radical, refused to greet him when they passed on the lane, and gave his niece Caroline a clear, unmistakeable order: her daily visits to Hollow’s Cottage were to cease, her French lessons with Hortense done for good. The French language was frivolous, he grumbled, unsuited to weak female minds, and the Moore household was full of dangerous, disloyal ideas.

Helstone had expected tears, arguments, rebellion. He had long suspected Caroline cared far more for Robert Moore than she ought. He had watched, when the oafish curate Malone dropped by of an evening, making a fool of himself pinching the old rector’s cat and firing his fowling piece at the garden tool shed for sport, how Caroline would slip silently upstairs and vanish until supper. When Robert Moore called, though, no guns were fired, no noise was made – he would only coax the old cat onto his knee to purr, and Caroline would sit quiet by the window, stitching pin-cushions and knitting socks for missionary bundles, perfectly content for hours. Helstone had once caught their eyes meet for a single second at the door, when they bade each other goodnight. No hidden scandal, no whispered secret – only Moore’s gaze softening a little at the sight of her clear, gentle eyes, and Caroline’s face flushing just faintly at the warmth of his steady, manly regard. It had annoyed him so much he could have boxed both their ears, vowing no silly romance would fester under his roof.

So he announced his order to her one evening, as she sat stitching by the drawing room window, the full light falling on her paler, quieter face. She did not throw a fit, or burst into tears. She only gave a tiny, almost unnoticeable start, and did not look up at him.

“Do you hear me?” he demanded.

“Yes, uncle.”

“Of course you will obey? No secret letters to Hortense, no sneaking visits. Their principles are Jacobinical, and I will not have you associating with them.”

“Very well,” Caroline said, soft and calm.

She was not being meek. The order was no hardship – visiting Hollow’s Cottage had become nothing but pain for weeks now. Robert was never there. Hortense would shake her head and say he was at the counting house, that he was working himself to the bone, that he never stopped long enough to eat a proper meal. The only time Caroline ever saw him was at church, and she barely dared glance his way. Looking was half sweet, half sharp agony, a surge of feeling that all went nowhere, wasted on a man who had no thought to spare for her.

One dark, rainy Sunday, the pews nearly empty, all the sharp-tongued town gossips safely home, she dared let her eyes drift to his pew. He sat alone, Hortense stayed home to protect her new spring bonnet from the rain. His arms were folded, his eyes cast down, his face shadowed and tired, the usual warm olive of his skin faded to pale, ashy grey. Caroline could see his thoughts were miles away, tangled up in the weights of business that she would never be allowed to understand, a whole world of worry she could never step into. When the service ended, he rose, and walked out of the church without turning his head once. He did not glance her way, not for a single second. That small, unthinking snub made her throat ache so tight she could barely breathe. A single kind glance would have carried her happily through the whole next week. But he had not spared it. He was gone.

That evening, when Malone came to call on the rector, Caroline slipped up to her room after tea. Fanny had lit a little, crackling fire for her, for the wind was howling sharp outside, and the rain lashed the windows. She could not read. The book of Sunday devotions lay open on her table, its words slipping right through her mind as she paced the carpet, head bowed, hands folded in front of her. Her head was full of memories: the winter evenings they had sat by the cottage fire, the long summer afternoon they had wandered through Nunnely Wood, the soft spring day in Hollow’s Copse when the cuckoo called overhead, and she had picked a basket of wild blackberries, arranging them with green leaves and clover, feeding them to him one by one, like a little bird feeding its fledgling.

The memories faded fast. The sound of his voice dissolved, the warm imaginary pressure of his hand on her shoulder turned cold as sleet, and she was left alone in her small, dim room, the rain tapping on the glass, the only companion her own shadow stretched long on the wall. She sank down into her chair, and a quiet, heavy thought settled over her. She was young, and healthy – she might live another fifty years, all the way to seventy. How on earth was she supposed to fill that whole stretch of empty time?

It dawned on her, soft and certain, that she would never marry. Robert did not care for her, not the way she cared for him. She had always assumed she would grow up to be a wife, a mother, to fill her days with the small, warm duties of a home of her own. But that path was closed to her, now. She would be an old maid. She would watch Robert marry some wealthy, accomplished lady one day, and she would be left behind. What was the point of her, then? What place was there for her in the world?

She sat musing for hours, staring into the dying embers of the fire. Everyone said old maids ought to devote their whole lives to helping others, to caring for the poor, tending sick neighbours, running errands for friends – a noble doctrine, but wasn’t it a little hollow? Was living only for other people, having no life or love of your own, enough to sustain a soul? She was not sure it was. She had rights, just the same as every other living person, a right to something of her own to love, to tend to, to call hers. And when she died, the body would turn to dust in the grave, and the soul would fly upward, to whatever unknowable hereafter waited.

By the time the study bell rang for evening prayers, the last cinder in her fire had crumbled to ash.

The next day, her uncle was gone all afternoon, dining with the Vicar of Whinbury, Dr. Boultby. Caroline sat alone, stitching listlessly, until Fanny bustled in, chiding her for sitting quiet and still all day, urging her to go out, call on the two old maids of the village, Miss Mann and Miss Ainley. Caroline protested that their houses were dismal, that old maids were an unhappy race – but then she thought of Miss Ainley, who had tended her stepmother for decades, who spent every penny she could spare on the poor, who no one ever visited, who the gentlemen of the parish made fun of behind her back.

“I’ll go see her,” she said suddenly, standing up. “How cruel it is, to neglect people just because they are not young and pretty and merry. I’ll call on Miss Mann too. I want to know what made her the way she is. I want to understand what life is like, for the women who never get to walk the path I once thought was mine.”

Fanny bustled about happily, dressing her in her soft brown silk frock, brushing her shiny curls till they fell soft over her shoulders, tucking warm shawls around her till Caroline was almost sweltering under all the layers. “You’ll never be an old maid, miss,” Fanny told her cheerfully. But Caroline glanced in the little mirror, and saw how pale her cheeks were, how faint the dark circles under her eyes, how her smile had lost some of its old brightness. She did not know what the future held. But for the first time, she was no longer afraid to face it.
