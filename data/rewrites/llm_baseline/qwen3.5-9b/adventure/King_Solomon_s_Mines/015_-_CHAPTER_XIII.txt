The three enemy columns advanced with a terrifying, deliberate slowness, devoid of any visible panic or haste. Once they reached approximately five hundred yards from our position, the central column halted at the base of a narrow strip of open plain jutting upward toward the hill. This pause allowed the flanking divisions to complete their encirclement of our horseshoe-shaped defensive perimeter, ensuring that the assault would be launched simultaneously from all sides.

"Oh, for a Gatling gun!" groaned Good, staring grimly at the densely packed phalanx below. "I'd clear that plain in twenty minutes."

"We haven't one," replied Sir Henry, his voice tight. "But suppose you try a shot, Quatermain. Aim for that tall figure appearing to lead them. You'll likely miss him, but wager an honest sovereign—if we survive this—that you won't drop the bullet within five yards of him."

The challenge stoked my competitive spirit. Loading the express rifle with a solid ball, I waited until the officer stepped ten yards forward, leaving his orderly behind for a clearer line of sight. Lying flat against a rock, I aligned my sights. The rifle was calibrated only to three hundred and fifty yards, so accounting for the bullet's drop, I aimed halfway down the officer's torso. He stood perfectly still, offering me every advantage. Whether it was the adrenaline, the wind, or simply the distance, the shot missed its mark entirely. My intended target remained upright and unharmed, while his orderly, standing merely three paces to the left, crumpled instantly, seemingly dead.

The officer spun around, alarmed, and began running back to his troops.

"Bravo, Quatermain!" shouted Good. "You've scared him!"

Anger surged through me. I detested missing in public; a man masters only one art, and he strives desperately to maintain his reputation in it. Blinded by frustration, I committed a reckless act. As the officer fled, I raised the rifle again and fired the second barrel. Instantly, the man threw up his arms and collapsed face-first onto the earth. This time, I had hit my mark. Admitting it with shame: when our own safety, pride, or reputation hangs in the balance, we become capable of terrible brutality. I felt a cold delight watching the result.

The regiments witnessing this display roared with cheers, interpreting the white man's "magic" as an omen of victory. Conversely, the unit the officer commanded—the same force we later identified as having been led by him—retreated in disarray.

Sir Henry and Good immediately joined the firefight. Good methodically suppressed the dense mass ahead with his Winchester repeater, while I exchanged a few shots. By our estimate, we had disabled six or eight men before they broke contact.

Just as we ceased firing, an ominous roar erupted from our far right, soon echoed by a similar sound from our left. The other two divisions had engaged. Hearing the commotion, the main force before us spread out slightly and charged uphill, trotting slowly across the bare grass while singing a guttural war chant. We returned fire steadily; Ignosi joined in occasionally, picking off several attackers, yet our efforts amounted to nothing against such a tidal wave of human fury. Like stones thrown against a crashing breaker, we made no dent in their momentum.

They surged forward, screaming and clashing spears, eventually breaching the pickets we had anchored among the rocks at the hill's foot. Their advance slowed as gravity took hold; climbing the slope required saving breath, turning their initial rush into a grinding, laborious ascent.

Our defenses were tiered: the first line sat midway down the slope, the second fifty yards back, and the third guarded the plateau's edge. They stormed up, crying their war cries—"*Twala! Twala! Chiele! Chiele!*"—while our people answered with, "*Ignosi! Ignosi! Chiele! Chiele!*"

Now mere yards away, the *tollas*, or throwing knives, flashed back and forth in a deadly blur. With a horrific yell, the battle closed in. The mass of warriors swayed violently, bodies falling as easily as leaves in an autumn storm. However, the sheer numerical superiority of the attackers soon became undeniable. Our first line was gradually pushed back, merging into the second. The struggle there was ferocious, yet our defenders were driven back again, retreating step-by-step until, within twenty minutes of the clash beginning, our third line was forced into action.

By then, the assailants were exhausted and bleeding heavily. Breaking through that final, impenetrable wall of spears proved impossible. For a while, the seething lines of savages oscillated in a fierce ebb and flow, the outcome hanging in the balance.

Sir Henry watched the desperate melee with growing intensity. Suddenly, without a word, he sprinted forward, followed closely by Good, diving straight into the thickest part of the fray.

As for me, I stayed put. The soldiers spotted Sir Henry's towering figure plunging into the chaos, and a cry rose from the ranks: "*Nanzia Incubu! Nanzia Unkungunklovo!*" ("Here is the Elephant!") followed by "*Chiele! Chiele!*"

From that moment, the tide turned. Inch by inch, fighting with magnificent bravery, the attacking force was driven back down the hillside until they retreated in confusion.

At that exact instant, a messenger arrived reporting that the left flank had been successfully repulsed. I was just beginning to breathe easy, convinced the immediate threat was over, when horror struck. We saw our men on the right being driven across the plain toward us, pursued by swarms of the enemy who had clearly broken through at that point.

Ignosi, standing beside me, assessed the situation instantly and issued a rapid command. Immediately, the reserve regiment surrounding us—the Greys—extended outward. Another word from Ignosi rippled through the captains, and within seconds, to my profound dismay, I found myself swept up in a furious counter-charge against the advancing foe.

Seeking cover behind Ignosi's massive frame, I did my best to survive, moving forward as if I enjoyed the prospect of death. Within a minute or two, we were plowing through fleeing groups of our own men, who were quickly reforming behind us. Then, memory fades into a nightmare of sensory overload: a deafening roll of colliding shields, and suddenly, a gigantic brute appeared before me—a ruffian whose eyes bulged from his skull, charging straight at me with a bloodied spear.

"I say it with pride," I recall thinking, "that I rose—or rather, sank—to the occasion. Most men would have collapsed utterly before such a sight." Realizing that standing meant certain death, I dropped flat in front of him. Unable to stop his momentum, the giant stumbled headlong over my prostrate form. Before he could recover, I sprang up and dispatched him with my revolver.

Shortly thereafter, someone knocked me unconscious. When I regained awareness, I found myself back at the kopje, with Good leaning over me, pouring water from a gourd.

"How do you feel, old fellow?" he asked anxiously.

I sat up, shaking off the daze. "Pretty well, thanks."

"Thank heavens! When I saw them carry you in, I felt sick; I thought you were finished."

"Not this time, my boy. I suspect I only got a bump on the head that knocked me out. How has it ended?"

"They've been repulsed everywhere for now," Good replied. "But the cost is dreadful. We've lost nearly two thousand killed and wounded, and they must have suffered three times that. Look!" He pointed to long lines of men marching in fours. In the center of each quartet, carried aloft, was a hide tray with loops for handles—a standard feature of Kukuana forces. Upon these trays lay the wounded. As they arrived, medicine men performed swift examinations. If the injury wasn't fatal, the patient was tended to as best as circumstances allowed. But if the prognosis was hopeless, the procedure was gruesome, though perhaps merciful. A doctor would feign an examination, then swiftly slice open an artery with a sharp knife. Within moments, the sufferer would expire painlessly. Many such cases occurred that day, particularly from the enormous, broad-bladed spears used by the Kukuanas, which often severed vital arteries, rendering recovery impossible. Often, victims were already unconscious, or the arterial nick was so quick they didn't feel it. It was a ghastly spectacle, one we were grateful to escape. Indeed, nothing haunted me more than seeing those brave soldiers euthanized by the red-handed healers, save for one instance where I witnessed a force of Swazis burying their hopelessly wounded while they were still alive.

Hurrying to the far side of the kopje, we found Sir Henry, still gripping a battle-axe, consulting deeply with Ignosi, Infadoos, and several chiefs.

"Thank heaven you're safe, Quatermain!" Sir Henry exclaimed. "I can't quite grasp what Ignosi intends. Though we beat off the attack, Twala is bringing in massive reinforcements and shows signs of investing us, hoping to starve us out."

"That's problematic," Sir Henry noted.

"Yes, especially since Infadoos reports the water supply has failed," the chief confirmed. "The spring cannot sustain such a multitude and is drying up rapidly. By nightfall, we shall all be parched.
