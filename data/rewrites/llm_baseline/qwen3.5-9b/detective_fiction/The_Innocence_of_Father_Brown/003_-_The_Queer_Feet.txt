Should you happen to encounter a member of the exclusive "Twelve True Fishermen" as he enters the Vernon Hotel for their annual banquet, you will notice, as he removes his overcoat, that his evening coat is green rather than black. If you possess the audacity—though it requires defying the stars—to inquire why, he will likely reply that he does so to avoid being mistaken for a waiter. You will then withdraw, crushed by the absurdity, leaving behind an unsolved mystery and a tale worthy of telling.

Alternatively, imagine meeting a mild, hardworking little priest named Father Brown. If you asked him what the greatest stroke of luck in his life had been, he would probably answer that, on the whole, his finest feat occurred at the Vernon Hotel. There, he averted a crime and perhaps saved a soul merely by listening to a few footsteps in a hallway. He might be slightly proud of this wild notion, but since it is virtually impossible for you to rise high enough in society to join the Fishermen or sink low enough among the criminal underworld to meet Father Brown, you will never hear this story unless I tell it to you now.

The Vernon Hotel, where the Twelve True Fishermen held their annual dinners, was an institution that could only exist in an oligarchic society obsessed with good manners. It was a topsy-turvy creation—an "exclusive" commercial enterprise that paid not by attracting patrons, but by turning them away. In the heart of a plutocracy, traders had become so cunning they grew more fastidious than their customers. They deliberately created obstacles so that their wealthy, weary clients would spend money and diplomatic effort overcoming them. If there were a fashionable London hotel that admitted no one under six feet tall, society would meekly assemble parties of tall men to dine there. If an expensive restaurant were open only on Thursday afternoons due to the proprietor's whim, it would be packed exclusively on Thursdays.

The Vernon Hotel stood inconspicuously in the corner of a Belgravia square. It was small and inconvenient, yet these very flaws served as protective walls for a specific class. One inconvenience was deemed vital: the capacity to seat only twenty-four people simultaneously. The sole large dining table was the famous terrace table, situated on a veranda overlooking one of London's most exquisite old gardens. Consequently, even the twenty-four seats at this table could only be utilized in warm weather; this scarcity made the experience even more desirable. The owner, a Jew named Lever, amassed nearly a million pounds by making entry so difficult. Combining this exclusivity with impeccable service, he ensured the wines and cuisine rivaled the best in Europe, while the staff mirrored the fixed demeanor of the English upper class. Lever knew his fifteen waiters intimately; becoming a waiter there was harder than becoming a Member of Parliament. Each was trained in terrifying silence and smoothness, acting as a gentleman's personal servant. Indeed, there was often at least one waiter assigned to every gentleman diner.

The Twelve True Fishermen would dine nowhere else, insisting on luxurious privacy and being utterly disturbed by the mere thought of another club sharing the building. For their annual dinner, they displayed all their treasures as if in a private home, including their celebrated set of fish knives and forks—the insignia of the society. Each utensil was exquisitely crafted in silver, shaped like a fish, and studded at the handle with a large pearl. These were reserved for the fish course, which was invariably the most magnificent part of the meal. The society possessed numerous ceremonies and observances but lacked any history or purpose; this lack of substance was precisely what made it so aristocratic. Membership was hermetic; unless one was already a certain type of person, one never even heard of them. Founded twelve years prior, the club's president was Mr. Audley, and its vice-president was the Duke of Chester.

How I came to know of this appalling hotel, or how my friend Father Brown found himself in that golden galleon, may seem like a natural query. My story is simple, even vulgar. There exists an aged rioter and demagogue who breaks into refined retreats proclaiming that all men are brothers; wherever this leveller goes on his pale horse, Father Brown follows. That afternoon, an Italian waiter suffered a paralytic stroke. Lever, marveling mildly at such superstitions, agreed to summon the nearest Catholic priest. What the waiter confessed remains unknown, as the cleric kept it to himself, but it apparently involved drafting a note or statement to convey a message or rectify a wrong. Father Brown, displaying a meek impudence he would show equally in Buckingham Palace, requested a room and writing materials.

Lever was torn. A kind man with a distaste for scenes, he nonetheless viewed the presence of an unusual stranger as a speck of dirt on a freshly polished floor. The Vernon Hotel operated without borders or anterooms; there were no waiting crowds, no chance visitors. With fifteen waiters and twelve guests, finding a new arrival that night would be as shocking as seeing a new brother take tea in one's own family. Furthermore, the priest's appearance was shabby, his clothes muddy; a distant glimpse might precipitate a crisis. Finally, Lever devised a plan to cover, though not obliterate, the disgrace. Upon entering the hotel, one passes a short passage adorned with dingy but significant pictures, arriving at the main vestibule and lounge. To the right lie passages to the public rooms; to the left, a similar route leads to the kitchens and offices. Immediately on the left stands the corner of a glass office abutting the lounge—a house within a house, reminiscent of the old hotel bar.

Inside this office sat the proprietor's representative, who rarely appeared in person. Just beyond lay the gentlemen's cloakroom, the final boundary of the gentlemen's domain. Between the office and the cloakroom was a small private room with no other exit, sometimes used by Lever for delicate matters, such as lending a duke a thousand pounds or refusing him sixpence. It was a testament to Lever's magnanimous tolerance that he allowed this holy space to be profaned for half an hour by a mere priest scribbling on paper. The story Father Brown was writing was likely far superior to this account, though it will remain forever unknown. I can only state that it was nearly as long, and that the final paragraphs were the least exciting.

By the time Father Brown reached these concluding lines, his thoughts began to wander, and his keen animal senses awakened. Darkness and dinner approached; his small room was unlit, and perhaps the gathering gloom sharpened his hearing. As he wrote the least essential part of his document, he noticed he was writing to the rhythm of a recurring noise outside, much like humming a tune. Realizing the source, he identified it as the ordinary patter of feet passing the door—a common occurrence in hotels. Yet, staring at the darkened ceiling, he listened intently. After a few dreamlike seconds, he rose, tilted his head, and listened again, now combining auditory perception with thought.

At any given moment, the footsteps sounded typical for a hotel, yet collectively, they were strangely peculiar. There were no other footsteps. The house was usually silent; guests retired quickly to their apartments, and the well-trained waiters remained invisible until summoned. One could not conceive a place with less reason for irregularities. Yet, these footsteps were so odd one could not classify them as regular or irregular. Father Brown traced their path with his finger along the table's edge, like someone learning a melody on a piano.

First came a long rush of rapid, light steps, akin to a runner in a race. At a certain point, they halted and shifted into a slow, swinging stamp, covering roughly a quarter of the distance in the same amount of time. The moment the last echo faded, the light, hurrying steps returned, followed again by the thud of heavier walking. It was undoubtedly the same pair of boots, evidenced by the absence of others and a distinct, small creak. Father Brown, whose head could not resist asking questions, felt his mind nearly split. He had seen men run to jump, or run to slide. But why run to walk? Or walk to run? No description fit the antics of these invisible legs. The man was either sprinting down one half of the corridor to stroll slowly down the other, or sauntering slowly at one end to enjoy the thrill of speed at the next. Neither theory made sense. His brain grew dark, mirroring his room.

Yet, as he thought steadily, the blackness of his cell seemed to heighten his visions. He imagined the fantastic feet dancing along the corridor in unnatural, symbolic poses. Was it a pagan ritual? A new scientific exercise? Focusing on the slow step first, he noted it certainly wasn't the proprietor's. Men of that type walked with a rapid waddle or sat still. It didn't sound like a servant or messenger awaiting instructions. While the lower orders might lurch when drunk, in such grand settings, they typically stood or sat with constrained dignity. No; that heavy yet springy step, marked by a careless emphasis, belonged to only one creature on Earth: a gentleman of Western Europe, likely one who had never worked for a living.

Just as he reached this conclusion, the pace shifted to the quicker one, rushing past the door feverishly like a rat. The listener observed that while this step was swifter, it was also remarkably noiseless, almost as if the man walked on tiptoes. Yet, it wasn't associated with secrecy, but with something else he couldn't recall. Maddened by half-remembered fragments, he suddenly sprang to his feet with a new idea.
