"Questa montagna è tale,
Che sempre al cominciar di sotto è grave.
E quanto uom più va su e men fa male."
—Dante: *Purgatorio*

It was scarcely a week after her mother's return from the sea that Gwendolen yielded to stay in Genoa. Yet, the desire to flee from that jewel of the ocean had served to gather fragments of her shattered courage. For what place, however idyllic, may not the inner eye transform into a circle of torment where blossoms burn like tongues of fire beneath the feet?

"I shall never look upon the Mediterranean again," Gwendolen whispered to her mother, who sensed the depth of this aversion without needing an explicit mention of her late husband.

Mrs. Davilow, though formally bound by the gravity of her loss, was secretly reveling in life more than she had since her daughter's wedding. It seemed that her beloved child had returned not simply with the old affections, but with a conscious, cherished need for her presence—a possession saved from the brink of eternal separation.

"Are you there, Mamma?" Gwendolen cried out in the dead of night, a bed prepared for her mother sharing the same chamber. She spoke with the unguarded vulnerability of a frightened girl, though the impetus behind her words was now different.

"Yes, dear. Can I do anything for you?"

"No, thank you. I only wished to know you were there. Do you mind my waking you?"

"Not at all, darling."

"It didn't feel real that you were with me. I wanted to make it real. I can endure things if you are beside me. But please, do not lie awake worrying about me. You must find happiness now. Let me make you happy at last—if I cannot, what will I do?"

"God bless you, dear. My greatest happiness is knowing you cherish me."

Yet the following night, hearing her mother sighing and restless, Gwendolen refused the offered sedative. "No, Mamma, I do not wish to sleep." "It would be so beneficial for you, my darling." "Do not tell me what is 'good' for me, Mamma!" Gwendolen replied with sudden impetuosity. "You do not know what is good for me. You and Uncle Hugo must not contradict me and dictate what benefits me when I feel it does not."

Silence fell over the room, for the mother did not wonder why the poor child was irritable. Then Gwendolen confessed, "I was always naughty to you, Mamma." "No, dear, no." "Yes, I was," Gwendolen insisted, her voice trembling. "It is because I was wicked that I am miserable now." She burst into sobs, the dam breaking. Her determination to keep silent regarding the grim realities of her marriage and its tragic conclusion erupted in these enigmatic outbursts of emotion.

Slowly, faint lights of understanding began to dawn upon Mrs. Davilow's mind. Information flowed from Sir Hugo to Mr. Gascoigne, and with some omissions, from the rector to himself. The kindly baronet, tending to the necessary arrangements regarding his nephew's death and the possibility of recovering the body, believed it his gentlest duty to use his friendship with the clergyman to reveal the purport of Grandcourt's will. He aimed to spare the rector the double shock of returning home only to discover the truth later. Though Sir Hugo might have spoken plainly, his benevolent motive drove him to break the news gradually. At first, he merely hinted that the widow was not as generously provided for as Mr. Gascoigne, or as the baronet himself had anticipated. Only finally, after vague references to large claims on Grandcourt, did he disclose the prior relations that, due to the lack of a legitimate heir, had diverted the estate's splendor elsewhere.

The rector was deeply wounded, recalling with renewed vividness how proudly and repulsively the deceased had behaved toward him. He remembered the hints he had received in those early days concerning the new occupant of Diplow—an undue addiction to pleasure, a life swept into private rubbish heaps that now presented themselves as a swarm of live caterpillars threatening respectable society. Yet, he did not let these thoughts surface as indignation. Instead, behaving like a worldly man who had become a conscientious clergyman, he responded with measured wisdom. "When a young man makes his will in health, he usually counts on living a long while. Probably Mr. Grandcourt did not believe this document would ever take effect." With a heavy heart, he added, "The effect is painful in more ways than one. Female morality suffers greatly from this marked advantage and prominence given to illegitimate offspring."

"Well, in point of fact," said Sir Hugo, with his usual comfort, "since the boy is there, this was truly the best alternative for disposing of the estates. Grandcourt had no nearer relative than his cousin. And it is a chilling thought that you leave this world solely for the benefit of a cousin. A man finds a certain pleasure in making a will if it benefits his own children, but it becomes a nuisance when you are bequeathing to a 'used-up' fellow like yourself—one whom you care nothing for. It is almost as bad as holding only a life interest in your estates. No, I forgive Grandcourt for that portion of his will. But between ourselves, what I cannot forgive him for is the shabby manner in which he has provided for your niece—_our_ niece, I shall say. She holds no position better than a doctor's widow. Nothing grates on me more than that posthumous stinginess toward a wife. A man ought to possess some pride and fondness for his widow. _I_ surely do. I consider it a true test of a man's character that he feels at ease regarding his death when he can envision his wife and daughters comfortable thereafter. I recall those fellows in the Crimean War who were willing to sink to the bottom of the sea if their widows were provided for."

"It has certainly surprised me," admitted Mr. Gascoigne, "especially since, standing as the father figure to my niece, I showed my trust in Mr. Grandcourt's apparent liberality by making no prior claims for her. That seemed to me proper under the circumstances. You may think me blameworthy."

"Not blameworthy exactly. I respect a man for trusting another. However, take my advice. Should you marry another niece, whether it be the Archbishop of Canterbury or anyone else, bind him down. Your niece cannot be married twice over. And if he is a good man, he will wish to be bound. As for Mrs. Grandcourt, I can only say that I feel my connection to her drawn closer because I believe she has not been well-treated. I hope you will urge her to rely on me as a friend."

So spoke the chivalrous Sir Hugo, disgusted that the young and beautiful widow of Mallinger Grandcourt was left with a mere two thousand a year and a house in a coal-mining district. To the rector, that income seemed less scandalous and devoid of mortifying privations; but in this conversation, the baronet felt a keener sense of the humiliation cast over his niece and her closest friends by the public revelation of her husband's affair with Mrs. Glasher. Like all men who are good husbands and fathers, he felt this humiliation acutely through the minds of the women primarily affected. Thus, the initial annoyance of hearing the facts paled in comparison to his dread of communicating them to Mrs. Davilow and anticipating Gwendolen's reaction when she learned the truth. The rector possessed an innocent conviction that his niece was unaware of Mrs. Glasher's existence, reasoning from sound masculine principles about what maidens and wives typically know, do, and suffer, and based on his imperfect observations of the specific maiden and wife in question. Not so Gwendolen's mother, who now perceived an explanation for much that had remained enigmatic in her child's conduct both before and after the engagement. Concluding that Gwendolen had somehow been informed of this clandestine marriage and the existence of the children, she hoped to uncover the extent of her daughter's knowledge through moments of affectionate confidence before and during their journey to England, preparing her gently for whatever disappointments lay ahead.

"I hope you do not expect me to become rich and grand, Mamma," Gwendolen said shortly after the rector's communication, sitting quietly in meditation after dressing. "Perhaps I shall have nothing at all."

Mrs. Davilow was startled, reflecting for a moment before answering, "Oh yes, dear, you will have something. Sir Hugo knows all about the will."

"That will not decide," Gwendolen interrupted abruptly.

"Surely, dear? Sir Hugo says you are to receive two thousand a year and the house at Gadsmere."

"What I have will depend entirely on what I accept," Gwendolen stated firmly. "You and my uncle must not attempt to cross me or persuade me regarding this. I will do everything I can to make you happy, but in anything concerning my husband, I must not be interfered with. Is eight hundred a year sufficient for you, Mamma?"

"Far more than enough, dear. You must not think of giving me so much." Mrs. Davilow paused, then asked, "Do you know who is to receive the estates and the remainder of the money?"

"Yes," Gwendolen replied, waving her hand dismissively. "I know everything. It is all perfectly right, and I wish never to have it mentioned."
