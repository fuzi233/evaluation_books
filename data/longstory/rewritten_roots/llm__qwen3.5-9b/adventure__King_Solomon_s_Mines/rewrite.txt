The year was 1873, though time seems to stretch differently when one is chasing ghosts across the African veldt. My name is Allan Quatermain, and I write this not merely as a historian, but as a witness to events that blur the line between myth and reality. It began with a letter from a man named Sir Henry Curtis, a gentleman whose reputation for eccentricity matched his wealth. He spoke of a legend older than Rome, a kingdom buried beneath the sands where the sun never sets, guarded by giants and filled with treasures that could buy empires. When he asked if I would join him, I knew I couldn't say no. To turn down such a call would be to admit that the world had lost its wonder. So, we packed our rifles, our supplies, and our skepticism, and set off toward the unknown.

Sir Henry's story was rooted in ancient lore, whispered among traders and forbidden in royal courts. They spoke of King Solomon, wise and wealthy, who did not just rule over men but commanded the very earth itself. According to the tales, he built a city of gold hidden deep within the interior of Africa, protected by a labyrinthine network of tunnels known as "Solomon's Road." The entrance was said to lie behind a waterfall, accessible only through a secret passage revealed by a dying king centuries ago. For generations, explorers had sought this place, vanishing without a trace. Some returned mad; others returned empty-handed. But Sir Henry believed the map existed, drawn by a forgotten hand, pointing to a location few dared to name: the land of the Zambesi.

Our journey required more than courage; it demanded guides who knew the land better than any European. Here, we met Umbopa, a man of striking appearance and undeniable skill. Tall, dark-skinned, and bearing the scars of countless battles, he was a warrior of the tribes bordering the great river. Though initially wary of our intentions, Umbopa saw the truth in Sir Henry's eyes—a hunger not for conquest, but for discovery. He agreed to lead us, becoming the bridge between our Western tools and the savage wisdom of the bush. His presence transformed our expedition from a reckless gamble into a calculated venture. With Umbopa at our side, the impenetrable jungle seemed less like a wall and more like a puzzle waiting to be solved.

Before we could face the desert, we faced the forest. Our first true test came in the form of a massive herd of elephants, their tusks gleaming like ivory daggers against the green backdrop. These were not ordinary beasts; they were guardians of the path, aggressive and unpredictable. We needed to prove ourselves worthy before crossing into enemy territory. Under Umbopa's direction, we tracked them day and night, learning their rhythms and fears. The hunt was brutal and exhausting, requiring every ounce of our training. In the end, we brought down two bulls, securing food and prestige alike. It was a lesson in respect: nature does not yield easily, and survival belongs to those who listen as much as they fight.

With provisions secured, we turned our backs on the lush vegetation and marched into the vast, arid expanse of the Kalahari. The heat was relentless, a physical weight that pressed down on our shoulders. Days blurred into nights under a sky choked with stars. Water became our most precious commodity, a constant shadow looming over our thoughts. Yet, despite the harsh conditions, morale remained high. There was a shared sense of purpose binding us together, a belief that the legends were real and that we were close to uncovering history's greatest secret. The silence of the desert was deafening, broken only by the crunch of boots on sand and the occasional cry of a bird.

Then came the crisis. After weeks of dry travel, our water supply dwindled to nothing. Thirst gnawed at us, turning rational thought into desperate hallucination. We searched tirelessly, digging holes in the scorching ground, following faint tracks of animals, but found nothing. Despair threatened to consume us until Umbopa spotted something unusual—a shimmer in the distance, barely visible through the haze. Hope surged back into our hearts. We scrambled forward, driven by instinct and desperation, until finally, we reached a hidden spring, cool and sweet after days of dust. It was a moment of pure triumph, a reminder that even in the harshest environments, life finds a way.

Beyond the oasis lay the fabled route: Solomon's Road. As we approached, the landscape changed dramatically. Ancient stone markers, worn smooth by centuries of wind, lined the path, leading deeper into the mystery. The air grew cooler, carrying whispers of distant voices and rustling leaves that sounded too human. We felt watched, yet welcomed. Every step forward felt like peeling back layers of time, revealing truths long buried. The road seemed alive, guiding us with an almost sentient intelligence. It was clear now that this was no mere accident; fate had led us here, step by step, to fulfill a destiny written millennia ago.

Crossing the threshold marked the beginning of a new world. We entered Kukuanaland, a realm untouched by modern civilization, where the laws of physics seemed bent to accommodate magic. The architecture defied logic—buildings carved directly into cliffs, bridges spanning impossible gaps, and gates adorned with symbols that hurt the eye to behold. The people we encountered were fierce and proud, warriors clad in armor made of materials unknown to Europe. They greeted us with suspicion, testing our strength and resolve. But beneath their hostility lay a code of honor, a respect for bravery that transcended cultural divides. We were no longer outsiders; we were guests in a land of wonders.

At the heart of Kukuanaland stood Twala, the king himself. A figure of immense power and charisma, he ruled with an iron fist tempered by justice. His court was a spectacle of opulence, filled with advisors, priests, and guards who moved with practiced grace. Twala listened intently to Sir Henry's account, his eyes narrowing as he weighed the validity of the claims. When he learned of the treasure chamber, his expression shifted from curiosity to determination. He offered us protection and guidance, seeing in us potential allies against external threats. In return, we pledged loyalty, forming an uneasy alliance bound by mutual interest. Together, we prepared for what lay ahead.

Not all dangers were physical. Within the ranks of the tribe, fear bred paranoia, culminating in a witch-hunt fueled by superstition and mistrust. Accusations flew wildly, targeting anyone deemed different or unlucky. Tensions rose dangerously, threatening to tear the group apart. We intervened cautiously, using diplomacy and reason to calm the situation. By exposing the manipulators behind the accusations, we restored order and earned the trust of the community. It was a sobering reminder that ignorance can be as deadly as any weapon. Unity, we learned, must be forged carefully, especially in times of uncertainty.

As we ventured closer to the core of the kingdom, we realized we needed to signal our arrival to the inner sanctum. Using signals passed down through generations, we communicated our peaceful intent to the unseen guardians of the treasure. Smoke patterns, drum beats, and specific gestures conveyed our message clearly. The response was immediate: the gates opened, allowing us passage. This exchange highlighted the sophistication of the culture we had entered, proving that communication could transcend language barriers. Trust, once established, proved stronger than any fortress walls.

Despite our progress, danger lurked around every corner. Rumors spread of rival factions seeking to claim the treasure for themselves. Preparations for conflict became necessary. Weapons were sharpened, strategies devised, and alliances solidified. The atmosphere grew tense, charged with anticipation. Everyone understood that reaching the treasure chamber might cost lives. Yet, none hesitated. The drive to uncover the truth outweighed the fear of death. We stood ready, united by a common goal, knowing that whatever awaited us, we would face it head-on.

It happened suddenly. From the shadows emerged a force larger and more determined than expected. Arrows rained down, swords clashed, and chaos erupted. The battle was fierce, fought in narrow corridors and open courtyards alike. Bullets whistled past our ears, while spears thrust from unexpected angles. Despite the odds, we held our ground, relying on teamwork and quick thinking. Umbopa led charges with fearless precision, while Sir Henry coordinated defenses with strategic brilliance. Each blow dealt was a testament to our resilience. Slowly, the tide turned in our favor, pushing the attackers back into retreat.

In the final moments of the skirmish, a group of elite warriors, known as the Greys, made a desperate stand. Clad in distinctive gray uniforms, they refused to surrender, fighting until the last breath. Their sacrifice bought us crucial time, allowing us to secure key positions. Watching them fall was heartbreaking, yet inspiring. Their courage reminded us of the price of glory and the value of friendship. As the dust settled, we mourned their loss but celebrated our victory. The battlefield was quiet again, save for the groans of the wounded and the distant echo of fading footsteps.

Now that this volume is finally in print and ready to meet the public eye, a profound sense of inadequacy regarding both its style and substance weighs heavily upon me. Concerning the content, let it be clear that this is not intended to be a comprehensive chronicle of every event and sight encountered during our expedition into the heart of Kukuanaland. There are countless details—particularly the fascinating legends surrounding the chain armor that spared us in the Battle of Loo, and the enigmatic "Silent Ones" or Colossi guarding the entrance to the stalactite cavern—that I would have loved to explore at length. Had I followed my initial impulses, I might also have delved deeper into the intriguing linguistic divergences between the Zulu and Kukuana dialects, or dedicated space to describing the unique flora and fauna of this distant land.

Yet, the most compelling topic of all—the magnificent military organization of the Kukuanas, which I believe surpasses even the systems established by Chaka in Zululand due to its speed of mobilization and rejection of forced celibacy—has barely been touched upon. Nor have I fully addressed their domestic customs, which are often remarkably peculiar, nor their exceptional mastery of metallurgy. Their ability to smelt and weld metal is nothing short of remarkable, perfectly exemplified by their *tollas*, those formidable throwing knives featuring backs of hammered iron fused with expertly crafted steel edges.

In consultation with Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good, we concluded that the wisest course was to recount our tale plainly and directly, leaving room for future elaboration on these specialized subjects. For now, I pledge to share any further information within my reach with anyone who seeks it. Finally, I must apologize for the bluntness of my prose. To explain: I am far more accustomed to handling a rifle than a pen, and I cannot claim the ability to produce the ornate literary flourishes found in fiction. While I enjoy reading novels, I maintain that simple truths strike with greater impact and clarity when expressed without decoration. As the Kukuana proverb states, "A sharp spear needs no polish." By the same token, I trust that a true story, however extraordinary, requires no embellishment of fine words to hold its value.

It is a peculiar irony that at fifty-five—the age I turned last year—I should find myself picking up a pen to attempt writing a history. I wonder what sort of chronicle it will prove when I finally finish it, if indeed I ever do complete this journey! I have accomplished quite a bit in my life, which feels remarkably long to me simply because I began working so young. While other boys were still in school, I was already earning my living as a trader in the old Colony. Since then, I have traded, hunted, fought, and mined. Yet, despite this resume, it was only eight months ago that I truly "made my pile." It is a substantial sum now, though I haven't fully calculated its magnitude. Still, I doubt I would willingly endure the last fifteen or sixteen months of that experience again—not if I knew I would emerge safe with my wealth intact. Alas, I am a timid man by nature; I dislike violence, and frankly, I am almost sick of adventure.

So, why am I writing this book? It is certainly not in my line. I am no literary man, though I remain deeply devoted to the Old Testament and the *Ingoldsby Legends*. Let me try to list my reasons, merely to see if they hold water.

First, because Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good asked me to.
Second, because I am currently confined to Durban suffering from a painful condition in my left leg. Ever since that confounded lion seized me, I have been plagued by this trouble. When my wounds heal, they invariably break out again, usually around the same season of the year I received my mauling. There must be some lingering poison in a lion's teeth; otherwise, how can it be that the scars reopen precisely when the memory of the attack returns? It is a harsh reality to have shot sixty-five lions—or more, as I have counted in my lifetime—only for the sixty-sixth to chew your leg like a piece of tobacco. It disrupts the rhythm of existence. Putting other matters aside, I am an orderly soul, and I detest such irregularities. This is merely a digression.

Third, because I wish to entertain my boy, Harry. He is currently stationed at a hospital in London, training to become a doctor. Medical work can sometimes grow tedious, even dissecting cadavers eventually leads to satiety. This history, however, promises to be lively regardless of its other qualities, and it will surely inject a spark of excitement into his days while he reads of our exploits.

Fourth and finally, because I intend to recount the strangest story I have ever remembered. It may sound odd to claim this, especially considering there is no woman in it—save for Foulata. Wait! There is also Gagaoola, if she counts as a woman and not a fiend. But she was at least a hundred years old, rendering her ineligible for marriage, so I shall not count her. Rest assured, there is not a single petticoat in the entire narrative.

Very well, I had better commence. It is a stiff task, and I feel as though I am bogged down to the axle. But *"sutjes, sutjes"*—as the Boers say, though I suspect the spelling varies—*softly does it*. A sturdy team will eventually pull through, provided they are not too poor. You cannot achieve anything with weak oxen.

Now, to begin. I, Allan Quatermain, of Durban, Natal, Gentleman, make oath and say... That is how I headed my deposition before the magistrate regarding the tragic deaths of Khiva and Ventvögel. However, it hardly seems the appropriate opening for a book. Moreover, am I truly a gentleman? What constitutes a gentleman? I am not entirely certain, yet I have encountered individuals who fit the description perfectly and others who do not. I have known natives who are gentlemen, and conversely, I have known wealthy white men fresh from home who are decidedly not. At any rate, I was born a gentleman, though I have spent my entire life as a humble traveling trader and hunter. Whether I have retained that status remains for you to judge. Heaven knows I have tried. I have taken many lives in my time, yet I have never slain wantonly nor stained my hands in innocent blood; I have acted solely in self-defense. The Almighty granted us our lives, and I assume He intended for us to protect them. I have always operated on that principle, and I pray it will not be held against me when my final hour arrives.

The world is cruel and wicked, and for a timid man, I have been embroiled in considerable fighting. I cannot speak to the righteousness of every action, but I can attest that I have never stolen. Though, I must confess, I once cheated a Kafir out of a herd of cattle. He had played me a foul trick beforehand, and the guilt has bothered me ever since.

It was approximately eighteen months ago that I first encountered Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good. The circumstances were as follows: I had ventured beyond Bamangwato in search of elephants, meeting with relentless bad luck. Every endeavor failed, culminating in a severe bout of fever. Once recovered sufficiently, I trekked down to the Diamond Fields, sold my remaining ivory along with my wagon and oxen, dismissed my hunters, and boarded a post-cart for the Cape.

After a week in Cape Town—where I discovered the hotels charged exorbitant rates and viewed the botanical gardens, which I believe will greatly benefit the nation, and the new Houses of Parliament, which I fear will accomplish little—I resolved to return to Natal via the *Dunkeld*, a vessel then lying at the docks awaiting the arrival of the *Edinburgh Castle* from England. I secured a berth and embarked. That afternoon, passengers from the *Edinburgh Castle* transferred aboard, and we weighed anchor, setting sail.

Among the newcomers were two individuals who immediately piqued my curiosity. One was a gentleman of about thirty, perhaps the most imposing figure I have ever witnessed. He possessed a chest of immense breadth and arms of extraordinary length. His hair was yellow, as was his thick beard, and his features were clear-cut with large grey eyes set deep within his skull. I had never seen a finer-looking man, and strangely, he reminded me of an ancient Dane. Not that I possess extensive knowledge of ancient Danes, although I did know a modern Dane who defrauded me of ten pounds. I recall once viewing a painting of a certain gentry—a type of white Zulus, I suppose—who drank from large horns with long hair cascading down their backs. Gazing at my friend standing by the companion-ladder, I thought that if he allowed his hair to grow slightly longer, donned a chain shirt upon those magnificent shoulders, and grasped a battle-axe and a horn mug, he would have made the perfect model for that painting. By the way, it is a curious coincidence, demonstrating how blood runs in the veins, that I later discovered Sir Henry Curtis—that was the giant's name—is of Danish descent.

The other man, standing conversing with Sir Henry, was stout, dark-complexioned, and of a completely different build. I instantly suspected he was a naval officer; I cannot explain why, but it is difficult to mistake a sailor. Having undertaken numerous shooting expeditions with various naval personnel throughout my life, I have consistently found them to be the finest, bravest, and kindest fellows imaginable, albeit occasionally prone to profanity. Returning to my earlier query: what is a gentleman? My answer now is simple: a Royal Naval officer is, in a general sense, a gentleman, though exceptions naturally exist. I imagine it is the vast oceans and the breath of God's winds that cleanse their hearts, dispelling bitterness and forging them into the ideal men.

My suspicion was correct. The dark man was indeed a naval officer, Lieutenant John Good, aged thirty-one. After seventeen years of service, he had been dismissed from Her Majesty's employ with the hollow honor of a Commander's rank, deemed unfit for promotion. Such is the fate of those who serve the Crown: cast adrift into a harsh world to struggle for survival just as they begin to truly master their craft and reach the prime of their lives. I suppose they do not mind, but for my part, I would prefer earning my bread as a hunter. One's coins may be scarce, but one receives far fewer kicks.

I learned the officer's name by consulting the passenger manifest. Captain Good was broad, of medium height, dark, and stout—a rather peculiar appearance overall. He was impeccably neat and cleanly shaven, always wearing an eyeglass in his right eye. It appeared to have grown there, for it lacked a string, and he removed it only to wipe it. Initially, I thought he slept in it, but I was mistaken. He stored it in his trouser pocket alongside his false teeth, of which he possessed two exquisite sets. My own dentures are far inferior, and they have frequently tempted me to violate the Tenth Commandment.

Soon after we departed, evening descended, bringing with it filthy weather. A brisk breeze rose from the land, creating a dense, aggravated Scottish fog that drove everyone below decks. The *Dunkeld* is a flat-bottomed punt; traveling lightly as she was, she rolled violently. It seemed at moments she might capsize, yet she never did. Movement was impossible, so I positioned myself near the engines where the heat was comforting, amusing myself by observing a pendulum fixed opposite me. It swung slowly backward and forth as the vessel lurched, allowing me to gauge the angle of each roll.

At the time, I dismissed the story with laughter, though it intrigued me, especially as the Diamond Fields remained undiscovered. However, just twenty years later—a considerable span for an elephant hunter, whose life is rarely measured in decades—I received more concrete information regarding Suliman's Mountains and the territory beyond.

I was stationed at Sitanda's Kraal, a desolate outpost in the Manica region where sustenance was scarce and game elusive. Stricken by fever and in a wretched state, I was visited one day by a Portuguese traveler accompanied by a single half-caste companion. While I am familiar with the predatory nature of low-class Delagoa Bay Portuguese, who thrive on human suffering and slavery, this visitor was entirely different. He possessed the bearing of a gentleman; tall, slender, with piercing dark eyes and curling grey mustaches. He spoke broken English, and I understood enough Portuguese to converse briefly. His name was José Silvestre, and he maintained a property near Delagoa Bay.

On the second day, as he prepared to depart with his companion toward the west, he tipped his hat in the traditional manner and said, "'Good-bye, señor. Should our paths cross again, I shall be the wealthiest man in the world, and I shall remember you.'"

I chuckled weakly, watching him stride confidently into the vast western desert, wondering if he were mad or harboring delusions of grandeur.

Suddenly, a figure appeared on the ridge opposite, roughly three hundred yards away. Clad in a coat, it appeared to be a European. When he returned, I gasped. It was José Silvestre—or rather, his skeletal remains wrapped in tattered skin. His face was jaundiced yellow, his eyes bulging from sockets devoid of flesh, leaving only parchment-thin skin stretched over white hair and gaunt bones.

The voyage from the Cape to Durban typically consumes four or five days, contingent upon the vessel's speed and the whims of the weather. Occasionally, if the landing at East London proves treacherous—despite the endless talk of building a "wonderful harbour" and sinking fortunes into it—a ship may be stranded for twenty-four hours while cargo boats struggle to navigate the breakers. On this occasion, however, we faced no delays. The bar was clear of waves, and the tugs immediately emerged, towing long strings of ugly, flat-bottomed barges. Packages were bundled onto them with reckless haste, regardless of their contents. I watched in dismay as a crate containing four dozen bottles of champagne was smashed to splinters; the liquid fizzed and boiled violently in the muddy bottom of the barge. It was a wasteful spectacle, and the Kaffir laborers aboard seemed to agree, spotting two unbroken bottles, smashing their necks off, and drinking the contents. They failed to account for the carbonation expanding inside them; soon, they were rolling around in the bilge, shouting that the fine liquor was *tagati*—"bewitched." I shouted warnings from the deck, telling them it was the white man's strongest medicine and that they were effectively dead men. Terrified, they fled to shore, and I suspect they will never touch champagne again.

While we steamed toward Natal, I pondered Sir Henry Curtis's offer. We avoided the topic for a day or two, filling the silence with tales of hunting—truths drawn from experience rather than invention. Finally, on a brilliant January evening, our hottest month, we passed the Natal coast, intending to reach Durban Point by sunset. The shoreline was enchanting: red sandhills sweeping into vivid green plains, dotted with Kaffir kraals and fringed by a ribbon of white surf crashing against the rocks. Approaching Durban, the landscape grew richer; sheer kloofs carved by ancient rains glistened with sparkling rivers, the bush displayed its deepest emerald hues alongside maize fields and sugar plantations, and white houses smiled placidly at the sea. To me, a view feels incomplete without the sign of human habitation; perhaps it is because I have dwelt so long in the wilderness that I cherish civilization, even if it drives the game away. Though the Garden of Eden may have been fair before humanity arrived, I always believed it was fairest when Eve adorned it.

We had slightly misjudged our timing; the sun had dipped below the horizon before we anchored off the Point, hearing the signal gun announce the arrival of the English Mail. Too late to cross the bar that night, we retired to dinner after securing the mailbags in the lifeboat. Later, under a moon so bright it outshone the lighthouse beams, the air filled with sweet, spicy scents reminiscent of hymns and missionaries. Lights twinkled in the windows of the Berea, and nearby, sailors sang as they hauled up anchors. It was a perfect Southern African night, draped in a garment of peace that silenced even the great bulldog of a sporting passenger, who snored contentedly at his cabin door, dreaming of victory over the baboon in the forecastle.

The following day, we disembarked. I lodged Sir Henry and Captain Good in the small shanty I maintain on the Berea, a three-room structure of green brick with a galvanized iron roof, surrounded by a garden of loquat and mango trees tended by Jack, an old Griqua hunter crippled by a buffalo. Sir Henry and Good slept in a tent pitched among my orange trees, enjoying the scent of blossoms and the sight of green and golden fruit ripening simultaneously.

With my decision made, preparations began. I secured the deed from Sir Henry, resolving legal hurdles regarding his status as a foreigner and the overseas property involved, paying a lawyer £20 for the service. I cashed my £500 check and purchased a robust twenty-two-foot wagon with iron axles and seasoned stinkwood, a "half-tented" model ideal for carrying supplies. I also acquired a team of twenty Zulu oxen—smaller and lighter than standard Africanders but hardier, capable of traveling faster on rough terrain. Crucially, they were "salted"; having worked across South Africa, they possessed immunity to red water, and they had been inoculated against lung sickness by binding diseased lung tissue to their tails, a cruel but effective practice that left them trailing stumps instead of tails.

Provisioning and medical supplies were next. Captain Good, possessing practical medical skills despite lacking formal qualifications, assembled a splendid traveling chest. His competence was proven when he skillfully removed a Kaffir's big toe, only to be baffled when the patient requested a replacement, claiming a "white one" would suffice.

For our crew, we sought five men: a driver, a leader, and three servants. We easily found two Zulus, Goza and Tom. Recruiting servants proved harder; they required absolute trustworthiness. Lacking a third, we planned to recruit en route.

From Durban, this journey spanned over a thousand miles. However, at Inyati, the remote trading post in the Matabele lands ruled by the tyrannical King Lobengula, we were forced to abandon our comfortable wagon. We left the wagon and the surviving oxen in the care of Goza and Tom, our reliable drivers, entrusting them to a Scottish missionary stationed nearby. Accompanied by Umbopa, Khiva, Ventvögel, and six local bearers hired on the spot, we embarked on foot toward our unknown destination. A somber silence hung over the group as we departed; each of us harbored doubts about ever seeing the wagon again, and I confess, I never truly expected to.

For a stretch, we marched in quiet contemplation until Umbopa, leading the way, broke the tension with a Zulu chant. He spoke of brave souls weary of tame existence who ventured into the wilderness seeking new horizons or death, only to discover that the wild was not a desolate void, but a paradise teeming with fertile land, abundant game, and formidable foes. His words elicited laughter from us all, serving as a potent omen. Umbopa possessed a unique ability to lift our spirits; a dignified yet cheerful savage, he became a beloved companion despite his occasional bouts of melancholy brooding.

Now, I turn to the singular adventure that defines this chapter, for I hold a deep affection for tales of the hunt. Approximately two weeks' march from Inyati, we encountered a breathtaking expanse of lush woodland. The hills were cloaked in dense "idoro" bush, interspersed with the "wait-a-little thorn," while the valleys abounded with the majestic "machabell" tree, heavy with sweet yellow fruit. These trees were the elephants' favorite delicacy, and evidence of their passage was everywhere: frequent tracks, toppled timber, and uprooted giants. Elephants are indeed destructive feeders.

One evening, after a long day's march, we arrived at a scene of profound beauty. At the base of a bush-covered hill lay a dry riverbed dotted with crystal-clear pools, their edges marked by the hoofprints of countless animals. Opposite the hill stretched a park-like plain dotted with flat-topped mimosa and glossy-leaved machabells, surrounded by an endless sea of silent, pathless bush.

As we stepped onto the riverbed, a troop of tall giraffes startled us. They fled with their characteristic grace, tails curled high, hooves clicking like castanets. Though three hundred yards away and beyond effective range, Good, walking ahead with a loaded express rifle, could not resist the urge to shoot. He targeted the youngest female. Miraculously, the ball struck her squarely on the neck, severing the spinal cord. She collapsed instantly, tumbling head over heels like a falling rabbit—a spectacle I have rarely witnessed.

"Curse it!" Good exclaimed, his sailor's temper flaring. "Curse it! I've killed him."

"_Ou_, Bougwan!" cried the Kafir trackers, calling him "Glass Eye" due to his spectacles. "Oh, 'Bougwan!' we echoed, and thus, among the locals, his legend as a marksman was born. In reality, he was a poor shot, but we forgave his misses for the sake of that giraffe.

We tasked some of the men with butchering the giraffe while we constructed a *scherm*—a protective thorn fence—near a pool. Inside this enclosure, we smoothed the ground, laid a bed of dry grass, and kindled a fire. By the time the shelter was complete, the moon had risen, and our dinner of giraffe steak and roasted marrow bones was ready. Nothing compares to the richness of giraffe marrow, save perhaps the heart of an elephant, which we secured the following day.

Sitting by the moonlit fire, we savored our meal, occasionally pausing to toast Good's miraculous accuracy. We smoked and shared stories, forming a striking tableau: myself, small, dark, and wiry; Sir Henry, tall, broad, and fair-haired; and Captain John Good, R.N., sitting atop a leather bag, immaculate in his brown tweed suit, crisp gaiters, and perfectly trimmed beard. Even his false teeth and eyeglasses seemed pristine. When I questioned his attire, he replied innocently, "They weigh so little; I prefer to present myself as a gentleman." Alas, he could not foresee the rugged raiment awaiting him.

While the Kafirs slept, wrapped in blankets and sipping intoxicating *daccha* from eland-horn pipes, Umbopa sat apart, lost in thought. Suddenly, a low *"woof, woof"* echoed from the bush. "A lion," I warned, and we tensed. Moments later, the strident trumpet of an elephant shattered the silence. "_Unkungunklovo! Indlovu!_" the trackers whispered—"Elephant! Elephant!"

Shadowy giants emerged from the darkness, moving slowly toward the bush. Good sprang up, eager for battle, convinced killing an elephant would be as effortless as dispatching a giraffe. I restrained him. "Let them pass," I advised. Yet, Sir Henry proposed staying a day or two to hunt them, his hunter's instinct overriding his usual caution regarding the missing Mr. Neville. Reluctantly, I agreed; it violated my conscience to let such a magnificent herd escape untouched.

"We rest tonight," I decided. "By dawn, we strike."

Good meticulously packed his glasses and dentures into his trousers pocket, folding them neatly beneath his macintosh. Sir Henry and I settled into our blankets, drifting into sleep.

Suddenly, violent scuffling erupted near the water, followed by deafening roars. We leaped up, grabbing our rifles. Emerging from the shadows was a chaotic mass of yellow and black, stumbling toward us. It was a sable antelope bull, dead, impaled by the curved horns of a magnificent black-maned lion that lay beside it. The beast had attacked the drinking antelope, only to be skewered. Enraged and trapped, the antelope had charged blindly until it collapsed, dragging the lion down with it.

After securing the carcasses, we slept soundly until sunrise. Equipped with three eight-bore rifles, ample ammunition, and bottles of cooling tea, we set out with Umbopa, Khiva, and Ventvögel. Leaving the trackers to skin the lion and butcher the antelope, we soon identified a massive elephant trail, crushed wide enough to resemble a carriage road. Ventvögel estimated twenty to thirty adults, mostly bulls.

The herd was grazing in a hollow, unaware of our approach. Checking the wind, I confirmed it favored us. Creeping silently through the cover, we closed to within forty yards. Three magnificent bulls stood broadside before us, including one with colossal tusks.

"Now!" I whispered.

Three heavy reports rang out. Sir Henry's shot struck true, dropping his target instantly through the heart. My bullet knocked the middle bull to its knees; he rose briefly before collapsing under a second shot to the ribs, and a final blow to the brain ended his struggle. Turning to Good, I found him in a frenzy. His target, the great tusked bull, had charged directly at him. Good had barely dodged, only to watch the enraged animal thunder past him toward our camp. The remaining herd scattered in panic.

Debate ensued: pursue the wounded bull or chase the fleeing herd? Choosing the latter, we tracked their frantic path, crushed vegetation marking their route. Two hours of blistering heat passed before we located them. Most of the herd huddled together, alert and testing the air, while a single sentinel bull guarded them fifty yards away. Fearing movement would trigger a stampede, we aimed at the lone guard.

Three shots fell, and the bull dropped. The herd bolted, plunging into a narrow ravine (*nullah*) with steep banks. Trapped, they scrambled frantically, screaming and trampling one another in sheer terror. Seizing the opportunity, we unleashed a volley, felling five more before fatigue and the sheer scale of the slaughter compelled us to retreat. We returned home satisfied, planning to send the bearers to harvest the tusks the next day.

I recall our arrival vividly. To our right lay a sparse native settlement with stone cattle pens and patches of cultivated grain near the water's edge. This location appeared to be the frontier of the fertile lands, where the soil abruptly shifted from rich earth to barren wasteland, though the natural cause of such a stark transition remains unclear.

Just below our camp flowed a small stream. On the far bank rose a stony slope—the very same descent down which, twenty years prior, I had witnessed poor Silvestre retreating after his failed attempt to reach Solomon's Mines. Beyond that slope lay the waterless desert, carpeted in Karoo shrubs.

As evening fell, we pitched our camp. The great orb of the sun sank into the horizon, casting glorious, multi-colored rays across the endless dunes. Leaving Good to organize the camp, I escorted Sir Henry to the top of the opposing slope. Gazing across the expanse, the air was crystal clear, allowing me to discern the faint blue silhouette of the Suliman Mountains, capped here and there with snow.

"There," I pointed, "lies the wall surrounding Solomon's Mines. Whether we shall ever scale it, God alone knows."

"My brother must be there," Sir Henry replied, his voice steady with that quiet confidence that defined him. "If he is, I shall find him."

"I hope so," I answered, turning back toward camp, only to notice we were not alone. Standing behind us, gazing intently at the distant peaks, was the great Kafir, Umbopa.

Seeing that I had noticed him, Umbopa addressed Sir Henry, to whom he had attached himself.

"To that land do you intend to journey, Incubu?" he asked, using a native term for 'elephant' as a name for Sir Henry, and gesturing toward the mountains with his broad assegai.

I demanded sharply why he addressed his master so familiarly. While natives often adopt names for one another, it was undignified for them to apply such heathen appellations to a white man's face. Umbopa chuckled softly, a sound that irritated me.

"How do you know I am not the equal of the Inkosi [chief] I serve?" he countered. "His royal lineage is evident in his stature and bearing; perhaps mine is as well. At the very least, I am a great man. Speak for me, O Macumazahn, and convey my words to Incubu, my master, for I wish to address both of you."

Though annoyed by his audacity, Umbopa impressed me, and curiosity compelled me to listen. I translated his challenge, noting his impudence and outrageous swagger.

"Yes, Umbopa," Sir Henry confirmed calmly, "I intend to journey there."

"The desert is vast and devoid of water; the mountains are high and snow-capped. No man can predict what lies beyond them where the sun sets. How will you reach it, Incubu, and why do you go?"

I relayed the question again.

"Tell him," Sir Henry instructed, "that I go because I believe a man of my blood, my brother, ventured there before me, and I seek him."

"That is correct, Incubu," Umbopa stated. "A Hottentot I encountered on the road told me a white man entered the desert two years ago with a single servant, a hunter. They never returned."

"How do you know it was my brother?" Sir Henry asked.

"Nay, I do not know. Yet the Hottentot described the white man as having your eyes and a black beard. He also named the hunter as Jim—a Bechuana clad in clothing."

"There is no doubt," I interjected. "I knew Jim well."

Sir Henry nodded. "I was certain. If George set his mind on a thing, he accomplished it. From boyhood, if he intended to cross the Suliman Berg, he did so, unless fate intervened. We must search for him on the other side."

Umbopa, who understood English though he seldom spoke it, added, "It is a far journey, Incubu."

"Yes," Sir Henry agreed. "But there is no journey on earth a man cannot undertake if his heart is set upon it. Nothing is impossible, Umbopa—not mountains, not deserts—save perhaps those specific barriers from which you are spared. If love drives him, and he counts his life as nothing, ready to keep or lose it according to Heaven's will, he shall prevail."

I translated this sentiment.

"Great words, my father," Umbopa responded, adopting the title I used for all Zulus despite his actual origins. "Swelling rhetoric fit for a king. You are right, Father Incubu. Listen! What is life? A feather, the seed of grass, blown hither and thither, sometimes multiplying, sometimes vanishing into the sky. If the seed is heavy and good, it may travel a distance along its own path. He paused, then launched into a burst of rhetorical eloquence characteristic of his kind. Though repetitive, it revealed a poetic instinct and intellectual depth within his race.

"What is life? Tell me, O white men, who claim wisdom and know the secrets of the world, the stars, and the realms beyond; who project your voices from afar without sound. Tell me, white men, the secret of our existence—where it goes and whence it comes!"

"You cannot answer," Sir Henry noted.

"Listen, I will answer," Umbopa declared. "From darkness we emerged, into darkness we return. Like a storm-driven bird at night, we fly from the Nowhere; briefly our wings catch the firelight, and lo! we vanish back into the Nowhere. Life is nothing. Life is everything. It is the hand that wards off Death. It is the glow-worm shining bright at night but black by morning; the white breath of oxen in winter; the fleeting shadow running across the grass before sunset disappears."

"You are a strange man," Sir Henry observed when he finished.

Umbopa laughed. "To me, we seem much alike, Incubu. Perhaps *I* seek a brother over the mountains."

Suspicious, I asked, "What do you mean? What do you know of those mountains?"

"A little; a very little. Yonder lies a strange land of witchcraft and beauty, inhabited by brave people, featuring trees, streams, snowy peaks, and a great white road. I have heard tales. But what is the use of speaking? Darkness falls. Those who live to see will see."

Doubtful, I stared at him. The man knew too much.

"Fear not me, Macumazahn," he read my thoughts. "I dig no traps for you to fall into, nor weave plots. Should we cross the mountains behind the sun, I will reveal what I know. But Death dwells there. Be wise and turn back. Go hunt elephants, my masters. I have spoken."

Without further word, he raised his spear in salute and returned to camp, where we soon found him cleaning a firearm like any ordinary Kafir.

"He is an odd man," Sir Henry commented.

"Yes," I replied. "Too odd by half. I dislike his evasive ways. He knows something and refuses to speak. But arguing with him serves no purpose. We are embarking on a peculiar trip, and a mysterious Zulu makes little difference either way."

The following day, we finalized our departure plans. Dragging heavy elephant rifles and kit across the desert was impossible. We dismissed our porters and arranged with an elderly local chief, whose kraal stood nearby, to store our equipment until our return. It pained me to entrust such valuable tools to the greed of a savage whose eyes gleamed with covetousness. However, I took precautions.

First, I loaded all rifles to full cock and warned him that touching them would trigger them. He tested my eight-bore immediately; it discharged, blowing a hole through one of his oxen and knocking him backward. Startled and furious over the lost animal—which he brazenly demanded compensation for—he refused to handle the guns again.

"Place those live devils in the thatch," he grumbled, fearing they would murder us all.

I then issued a threat: if any weapon went missing upon our return, I would curse him and his kin with witchcraft, driving his cattle mad and souring his milk until life became unbearable.

Two hours later, roughly around four o'clock, consciousness returned to me. Once the initial, crushing weight of physical exhaustion had yielded slightly, the torturous thirst that had been gnawing at me since dawn surged back with renewed ferocity. Sleep was impossible. My dreams had been a delirious fantasy of wading in a rushing stream, flanked by lush green banks and whispering trees. I jolted awake to find myself instead trapped in this desolate wasteland, remembering Umbopa's grim warning: *If we do not find water today, we perish.* In this oppressive heat, no human soul could survive long without hydration.

I sat upright, rubbing my grimy face with hands that were dry and calloused. My lips and eyelids were fused together by salt and dust; it required significant friction and sheer willpower to pry my eyes open. Though it was nearing dawn, the air lacked the crisp freshness of morning. Instead, it was thick with a suffocating, hot murkiness that defies description. The others remained asleep.

Soon, however, the gloom lifted enough to permit reading. I pulled from my pack a small edition of *The Ingoldsby Legends* and opened to "The Jackdaw of Rheims." As I read the lines describing a "nice little boy" holding a golden ewer filled with water "pure as any that flows between Rheims and Namur," I physically recoiled. Literally, I tried to smack my own cracked, bleeding lips. The mere concept of that pristine liquid drove me mad. If the Cardinal had stood there with his bell, book, and candle, I would have lunged forward and drained the vessel dry—even if it had been consecrated with soap worthy of washing the Pope's hands. I knew the entire curse of the Catholic Church would descend upon me for such sacrilege, yet I couldn't care less.

I suspect I was bordering on delirium, a cocktail of thirst, fatigue, and starvation. My mind drifted to the absurd image of the Cardinal and his boy discovering a burnt-out, brown-eyed, grizzly-haired elephant hunter squeezed between them, plunging his filthy face into the basin and gulping down every drop. The notion was so ludicrous that I cackled aloud. This sound roused the others, who immediately began rubbing their own dirt-caked faces and forcing their gummed-up eyelids apart.

Once fully alert, we assessed the dire situation. There was not a single drop of water left. We inverted the bottles, licking the rims until our tongues burned, but they were dry as bone. Good, the man charged with the flask of brandy, produced it and eyed it with longing. Sir Henry swiftly confiscated it, knowing that consuming raw spirit would merely hasten our demise.

"If we do not find water, we shall die," Sir Henry stated flatly.

"I say, if we trust the old Dom's map, there should be some nearby," I offered, though I sensed no confidence in the group. The map seemed increasingly unreliable.

As the light intensified, we sat staring vacantly at one another. I noticed the Hottentot, Ventvögel, rising and pacing with his gaze fixed on the ground. Suddenly, he halted, let out a guttural cry, and pointed to the earth.

"What is it?" we demanded in unison, rising to join him.

"Well," I asked, examining the tracks, "that is fresh Springbok spoor. So what of it?"

"Springbucks do not stray far from water," Ventvögel replied in Dutch.

"No!" I exclaimed, a surge of hope igniting within me. "I forgot! Thank God for that."

In a desperate position, the human mind clings to the slightest thread of hope as if it were salvation. A single star on a dark night is preferable to total darkness. Ventvögel raised his snub nose, sniffing the hot air with the intensity of an ancient Impala ram scenting danger.

"I *smell* water," he announced.

Jubilance swept through us. We knew these wild-born men possessed instincts honed by generations of survival. Just then, the sun broke the horizon, casting a glorious light that momentarily eclipsed our thirst. Before us, glittering like silver in the morning rays, soared **Sheba's Breasts**. Stretching for hundreds of miles on either side lay the mighty **Suliman Berg**.

Attempting to describe the sublimity of that sight now feels inadequate; language fails before the memory of it. Directly ahead rose two colossal mountains, unique in Africa, perhaps unmatched anywhere on Earth. Each measured at least fifteen thousand feet in height, standing merely a dozen miles apart and connected by a precipitous cliff of rock. They towered into the sky in awful, white solemnity. Placed like the pillars of a gigantic gateway, they were shaped precisely like a woman's breasts. At times, the shifting mists and shadows beneath them took the form of a recumbent woman, veiled in mysterious sleep. Their bases swelled gently from the plain, appearing perfectly round and smooth from afar, while atop each peak sat a vast hillock of snow, mirroring the nipple of the female breast. The connecting cliff stretched thousands of feet high, perfectly vertical, flanked by similar lines of rock broken only occasionally by flat, table-topped summits—a formation common in Africa, reminiscent of Table Mountain at Cape Town.

To capture the comprehensive grandeur of that view is beyond my power. There was something inexpressibly solemn and overpowering about these extinct volcanoes that left us awestruck. Morning light danced upon the snow and the brown, swelling masses below, until strange vapors and clouds gathered, veiling the majestic sight. Soon, we could only trace their pure, gigantic outlines, appearing ghostly through a fleecy envelope. Indeed, we later learned they were often shrouded in this gauze-like mist, explaining why we hadn't seen them clearly before.

Hardly had Sheba's Breasts vanished into cloud-privacy when our thirst—a burning question—reasserted itself violently. It was easy for Ventvögel to claim he smelled water, yet we saw no signs. Scanning the horizon, we found only arid, sweltering sand and sparse karoo scrub. We circled the hillock, searching anxiously on the other side, but found nothing: no pan, no pool, no spring.

"You are a fool," I snapped at Ventvögel angrily. "There is no water."

He lifted his ugly snub nose and sniffed again. "I smell it, Baas," he insisted. "It is somewhere in the air."

"Yes," I retorted cynically, "no doubt it is in the clouds, and in about two months' time, it will fall and wash our bones."

Sir Henry stroked his yellow beard thoughtfully. "Perhaps it is on the top of the hill?"

"Nonsense," Good scoffed. "Whoever heard of water being found at the top of a hill!"

"Let us go and look," I urged, and reluctantly, we scrambled up the sandy sides of the kopje, led by Umbopa.

Suddenly, Umbopa froze as if petrified. "*Nanzia manzie!* That is, 'Here is water!' he cried loudly.

We rushed to him. Sure enough, in a deep cut or indentation on the very summit of the sand kopje lay an undeniable pool of water. We didn't pause to question how it existed in such a strange location, nor did we hesitate at its black, murky appearance. It was water—or a convincing imitation thereof—and that was sufficient.

We leaped forward, diving onto our stomachs to suck up the uninviting fluid as though it were nectar for the gods. Heaven knows how we drank! Once satisfied, we tore off our clothes and sat in the pool, letting the moisture seep through our parched skins. You, Harry, who can summon hot and cold water from a tap at your command, can hardly conceive the luxury of wallowing in that muddy, brackish tepidness.

Refreshed, we rose and devoured our *biltong*, which we had barely touched for twenty-four hours. We smoked pipes and lay by the blessed pool under the overhanging shadow of its bank, sleeping until noon. All day we rested, grateful for the find, despite its poor quality, offering thanks to the shade of the departed da Silvestra, whose accurate notation on the tail of his shirt had saved us. The mystery of the pan's longevity suggested it was fed by a spring deep within the sands.

Having filled ourselves and our bottles, we resumed our march under the moonlight. That night we covered nearly twenty-five miles, finding no more water, though we secured some shade behind ant-heaps the following day. When the sun rose, clearing the mysterious mists, Suliman's Berg and the twin Breasts, now only twenty miles distant, loomed grander than ever.

"And what prevents him from remaining preserved for three thousand years in this atmosphere?" Good countered logically. The sun never penetrates; no beast enters to devour or destroy. Doubtless his slave, mentioned in his writings, removed his clothes before abandoning him. He could not have buried the master alone. Look!" He stooped, picking up a strangely shaped bone sharpened to a point. "Here is the 'cleft bone' that Silvestra used to draw his maps."

We stared in astonishment, momentarily forgetting our own suffering amidst this extraordinary, almost miraculous sight.

"Aye," agreed Sir Henry, pointing to a small wound on the Dom's left arm. "And this is where he obtained his ink. Did every man ever witness such a phenomenon?"

There was no longer any doubt. Personally, the sight appalled me. There he sat—the dead man whose instructions, penned ten generations prior, had guided us to this precise location. Before me lay the crude pen with which he wrote them, and around his neck hung the crucifix his dying lips had kissed. Gazing upon him, my imagination reconstructed the final moments of the tragedy: the traveler succumbing to cold and starvation, yet desperately striving to transmit the great secret he had uncovered to the world. The terrible isolation of his death was palpable. I even fancied I could trace a resemblance in his sharply marked features to my late friend Silvestre, who had died in my arms twenty years earlier, though that may have been mere fancy. Regardless, there he remained—a somber monument to the fate awaiting those who dare penetrate the unknown. Undoubtedly, he would still sit there, crowned with the dread majesty of death, for centuries to come, startling any future wanderers brave enough to invade his solitude. The experience overwhelmed us, already nearly perished from cold and hunger.

"Let us depart," Sir Henry whispered. "Stay, however; we shall provide him with a companion." Lifting the frozen body of the Hottentot, Ventvögel, he placed it beside that of the old Dom. Stooping next, he snapped the rotting string of the crucifix, for his fingers were too numb to untie it. I believe he retains it still. I took the bone pen, which stands before me as I write; occasionally, I use it to sign my name.

Leaving these two—the proud white man of a bygone era and the poor Hottentot—to keep their eternal vigil among the endless snows, we crept out of the cave into the welcoming sunshine and resumed our journey, wondering how many hours remained before we too might join them.

After walking roughly half a mile, we reached the edge of the plateau. The mountain's peak did not rise from its exact center, despite appearing so from the desert side. Below us, hidden by billowing morning fog, lay the unseen landscape. Gradually, the upper layers of mist parted, revealing at the end of a long snowy slope a patch of green grass, approximately five hundred yards beneath us, through which a stream flowed. Moreover, basking in the bright sun by the stream stood and lay a group of ten to fifteen large antelopes. Their specific species was indistinct at that distance.

The sight ignited an irrational joy within us. If only we could secure them, we possessed ample sustenance. Yet, the challenge remained: how to approach them? The beasts were fully six hundred yards away—a formidable distance, unreliable when our lives depended on the outcome.

We rapidly debated stalking the game but ultimately discarded the plan reluctantly. Firstly, the wind was unfavorable. Secondly, traversing the blinding white background of the snow would surely reveal us, regardless of caution.

"Well, we must attempt a shot from here," said Sir Henry. "Quatermain, which shall it be: the repeating rifles or the expresses?"

Again, a difficult choice presented itself. Our two Winchester repeaters, including the one Umbopa carried alongside Ventvögel's, were sighted up to a thousand yards. Beyond that range, shooting became mere guesswork. Conversely, while the repeaters offered range, the express cartridges, being "expanding," were far more likely to bring down the quarry if they landed. It was a knotty problem, but I resolved to risk it and employ the expresses.

"Each of you take the buck opposite him," I instructed. "Aim precisely at the point of the shoulder, high up. Umbopa, give the command so we may all fire simultaneously."

A tense pause ensued as each of us aimed with our utmost precision, aware that life itself hung in the balance.

"Fire!" Umbopa commanded in Zulu.

At almost the same instant, three rifles roared loudly. Three clouds of smoke lingered briefly before us, followed by a hundred echoing reverberations across the silent snow. As the smoke cleared, a cry of pure joy escaped us: there lay a great buck on its back, kicking furiously in its death throes. We yelled in triumph; we were saved; starvation was averted. Though weak, we rushed down the snowy slope. Within ten minutes of firing, the animal's heart and liver lay before us.

However, a new dilemma immediately arose: we possessed no fuel to cook the meat. We exchanged glances in dismay.

"Men starving should not be fanciful," Good stated pragmatically. "We must eat raw meat."

With no alternative, our gnawing hunger rendered the prospect less repulsive than it might have been. We buried the organs in a patch of snow to chill them, then washed them in the ice-cold stream water before eating them greedily. It sounded horrific, yet honestly, I never tasted anything so delicious as that raw meat. Within a quarter-hour, we were transformed men. Life and vigor returned; our feeble pulses strengthened, and blood coursed robustly through our veins. Mindful of the dangers of overeating on empty stomachs, we stopped while still feeling hunger.

"Thank Heaven!" exclaimed Sir Henry. "That brute has saved our lives. What creature is it, Quatermain?"

I rose to examine the antelope, uncertain of its identity. It was roughly the size of a donkey, sporting large curved horns. I had never encountered such a species before. It was brown with faint red stripes and bore a thick coat. Later, I learned that the natives of this wondrous country called these bucks "_inco_." They are extremely rare, inhabiting only high altitudes where no other game survives. The animal had been struck high in the shoulder, though we could not determine whose bullet felled it. I suspect Good, recalling his marvelous shot at the giraffe, privately claimed credit, and we did not contradict him.

So engrossed in satisfying our hunger had we been that we had neglected to observe our surroundings. Now, having tasked Umbopa with cutting off the choicest portions of meat we could carry, we began to inspect the area. The mist had dissipated completely; it was eight o'clock, and the sun had evaporated the moisture, granting us a panoramic view of the countryside. I cannot adequately describe the glorious vista that unfolded before us, nor do I expect to see anything comparable again.

Behind and towering over us rose Sheba's Snowy Breasts. Below, some five thousand feet beneath our position, stretched leagues of the most enchanting countryside. Dense patches of lofty forests dotted the landscape, while a great river wound its silvery path through it. To the left extended a vast expanse of rich, undulating veld or grassland, where countless herds of game or cattle grazed, indistinguishable at that distance. This expanse appeared ringed by a wall of distant mountains. To the right, the terrain was more mountainous, with solitary hills rising from the level ground, separated by stretches of cultivated land where groups of dome-shaped huts were visible. The landscape lay before us like a map, where rivers flashed like silver snakes and Alpine peaks crowned with wildly twisted snow wreaths rose in grandeur,

All that afternoon we traversed a magnificent roadway trending steadily toward the northwest. Infadoos and Scragga walked alongside us, while their followers marched a hundred paces ahead.

"Infadoos," I asked eventually, "who constructed this road?"

"It was made, my lord, in ancient times," he replied. "No one knows how or when—not even the wise woman Gagool, who has lived for generations. We are not old enough to remember its creation. No one can fashion such roads today, yet the King forbids any grass to grow upon it."

"My lord, the hands that built the road also penned the wonderful inscriptions. We do not know who wrote them."

"When did the Kukuana people arrive in this land?"

"Their race descended here like the breath of a storm ten thousand moons ago," Infadoos answered, pointing northward toward distant horizons. "They traveled no further due to the high mountains ringing the land, as our ancestors' voices tell us, and so says Gagool, the wise woman, the scent-keeper of witches. Again, he pointed to the snow-capped peaks. 'The country was fertile,' he continued, 'so they settled here, grew strong, and became mighty. Now our numbers are like sea-sand, and when King Twala calls his regiments, their plumes cover the plains until the eye cannot see.'

"And if the land is walled in by mountains, against whom do the regiments fight?"

"Nay, my lord. The country opens to the north. From time to time, warriors sweep down upon us in clouds from unknown lands, and we destroy them. It has been the third part of a man's life since the last great war. Thousands died, but we slew those who came to devour us. Since then, there has been peace."

"Your warriors must grow weary of resting on their spears, Infadoos."

"There was one war, shortly after we destroyed those invaders," he admitted. "But it was a civil war—a battle where dogs ate dogs."

"How was that?"

"My lord, the King, my half-brother, had a twin brother born of the same womb and at the same hour. It is not our custom to spare twins; the weaker must always die. Yet the King's mother hid the feeble child, born last, for her heart yearned over him. That hidden child is Twala, the current King. I am his younger brother, born of another wife."

"Well?"

"Our father, Kafa, died when we came of age. My brother, Imotu, ascended the throne. He reigned briefly and fathered a son with his favorite wife. When the infant was three years old, amidst the great war that left no man to sow or reap, a famine ravaged the land. The people murmured, starving like lions seeking prey. Then Gagool, the terrible woman who does not die, proclaimed to the crowd: 'King Imotu is no longer King!'

"At that moment, Imotu was ill, bedridden in his kraal, unable to move. Gagool emerged from a hut, leading out Twala—my half-brother, the twin she had concealed among the caves since birth. Stripping the *moocha* (waist-cloth) from his loins, she revealed the mark of the sacred snake coiled around his middle, the sign of the eldest son of a king. She cried aloud, 'Behold your King, whom I have saved for you to this day!'

Just as the tumult peaked, Imotu, though gravely sick, crawled from his hut, clutching his wife's hand, followed closely by his small son, Ignosi—"Lightning," by interpretation.

"'What is this noise?' he asked. 'Why do you cry _The King! The King!_'"

"Then Twala, his twin brother born of the same woman and hour, rushed forward, grabbed him by the hair, and stabbed him through the heart with his knife. The people, fickle and eager to worship the rising sun, clapped their hands and screamed, '_Twala is King! Now we know Twala is King!_'

"And what became of Imotu's wife and her son, Ignosi? Did Twala kill them too?"

"No, my lord. Upon seeing her lord fall, the Queen seized the child, crying out, and fled. Two days later, she arrived at a kraal, starving. No one offered her milk or food now that her lord was dead; all men hated the unfortunate. But at nightfall, a little girl crept out and shared her corn with her. The Queen blessed the child and pressed onward toward the mountains with her boy before sunrise. There, she must have perished, for neither she nor Ignosi have been seen since."

"So, if that child Ignosi had lived, he would be the true King of the Kukuana people?"

"That is so, my lord. The sacred snake circles his middle. If he lives, he is King; but alas, he is long dead."

"Look, my lord," Infadoos pointed. Below us lay a vast collection of huts enclosed by a fence, itself surrounded by a great ditch. "That is the kraal where Imotu's wife was last seen with Ignosi. It is here we shall sleep tonight, if, indeed," he added dubiously, "your lords sleep at all upon this earth."

"When we are among the Kukuanas, my good friend Infadoos, we shall do as the Kukuanas do," I declared majestically. Turning sharply, I addressed Good, who tramped sullenly behind, vainly trying to stop his flannel shirt from flapping in the evening breeze. To my surprise, I bumped into Umbopa, who walked directly behind me. His expression was peculiar, suggesting a man struggling to recall something long forgotten.

We pressed on toward the undulating plain below. The mountains we had crossed now loomed high overhead, and Sheba's Breasts were veiled in diaphanous wreaths of mist. As we advanced, the landscape grew increasingly beautiful. The vegetation was lush yet not tropical; the sun was bright and warm without burning, and a gentle breeze swept softly along the fragrant mountain slopes. Indeed, this new land was little less than an earthly paradise—in beauty, natural wealth, and climate, I had never witnessed its equal. The Transvaal is a fine country, but it pales beside Kukuanaland.

As soon as we began our march, Infadoos dispatched a runner to warn the people of the kraal—under his military command—of our approach. This messenger moved at extraordinary speed, a feat Infadoos noted was common among his people. Soon, the results of his warning became visible. Within two miles of the kraal, companies of men poured from its gates, marching toward us.

Sir Henry laid a hand on my arm, remarking that it seemed we faced a warm reception. His tone caught Infadoos' attention.

"Do not fear, my lords," he hastened to assure us. "There is no guile in my breast. This regiment is under my command and comes out by my own order to greet you."

I nodded easily, though unease lingered in my mind. Half a mile from the kraal's gates, a long stretch of rising ground sloped gently upward from the road. Here, the companies formed. It was a splendid sight: each company, numbering about three hundred, charged swiftly up the rise with flashing spears and waving plumes to take their appointed places. By the time we reached the slope, twelve such companies—totaling 3,600 men—had filed out and arrayed themselves along the road.

We approached the first company, gazing in astonishment at the most magnificent warriors I had ever seen. They were men of mature age, mostly veterans around forty, none shorter than six feet, many standing six feet three or four. Heavy black plumes of Sakaboola feathers crowned their heads, similar to those worn by our guides. White ox-tail circlets bound their waists and right knees. In their left hands, they carried round shields roughly twenty inches across. These shields were curious creations: a thin iron plate beaten flat, overlaid with milk-white ox-hide.

Their weapons were simple yet deadly. Each man bore a short, heavy, two-edged spear with a wooden shaft, the blade six inches wide at its broadest point. Unlike throwing spears, these were designed for close-quarters combat, akin to the Zulu *bangwan*, inflicting terrible wounds. Additionally, every warrior carried three large, heavy knives, each weighing about two pounds. One hung in the ox-tail girdle; the other two were strapped to the back of the shield. Called *tollas* by the Kukuanas, these replaced the Zulu throwing assegais. The Kukuanas could cast them accurately up to fifty yards, and it was their custom to hurl a volley of them as they closed in on the enemy.

There is no need for me to recount in tedious detail the events of our march toward Loo. Our journey along Solomon's Great Road lasted two full days, a path that cuts a straight line right into the heart of Kukuanaland. As we traveled, the landscape seemed to grow increasingly wealthy and prosperous. The kraals multiplied, each surrounded by vast belts of cultivated fields, all constructed according to the same impressive design as the first camp we had encountered. Every settlement was heavily guarded by substantial military forces. Indeed, in Kukuanaland—as among the Germans, the Zulus, and the Maasai—every able-bodied man is a soldier. Consequently, the entire fighting strength of the nation stands ready for either offensive campaigns or defensive wars. During our passage, we were constantly overtaken by thousands of warriors rushing toward Loo to participate in the great annual review and festival. I had never witnessed troops more splendid.

At sunset on the second day, we halted to rest atop a ridge overlooking the route. Below us, spread across a beautiful and fertile plain, lay the town of Loo itself. For a native settlement, it was an enormous place, roughly five miles in circumference, with outlying kraals extending outward to serve as regimental cantonments during major ceremonies. To the north, about two miles away, rose a peculiar horseshoe-shaped hill—a feature we were destined to encounter more intimately later. The location was picturesque; a river flowed through the center of the kraal, dividing it into two sections, and appeared to be bridged at several points, exactly as we had observed from the slopes of Sheba's Breasts earlier. Far in the distance, sixty or seventy miles away, three massive snow-capped mountains emerged from the flat plain, positioned at the vertices of a triangle. Their formation differed markedly from Sheba's Breasts; rather than smooth and rounded, these peaks were sheer and precipitous.

Infadoos noticed us staring at them and offered a comment.

"The road ends there," he said, gesturing toward the mountains, which the Kukuanas called the "Three Witches."

"Why does it end?" I asked.

"Who knows?" he replied with a shrug. "The mountains are filled with caves, and there lies a great pit between them. Long ago, the wise men traveled there to obtain whatever they sought for this country. Today, it is there that our kings are interred in the Place of Death."

"What did they seek?" I asked eagerly.

"Nay, I do not know. Only the Lords who have fallen from the Stars possess that knowledge," he answered with a sharp glance. Clearly, he withheld information he deemed inappropriate to share.

"Yes," I continued, "you are correct; we learn many things from the Stars. I have heard, for instance, that the ancient wise men climbed these mountains seeking bright stones, delightful playthings, and yellow iron."

"My lord is wise," he responded coldly. "I am merely a child and cannot converse with my lord on such profound matters. My lord must speak with Gagool the Elder at the king's residence; she is as wise as you." With that, he departed.

As soon as he was gone, I turned to the others and indicated the mountains. "Those are Solomon's diamond mines," I stated.

Umbopa, standing nearby and seemingly lost in one of his characteristic trances of abstraction, caught my words.

"Yes, Macumazahn," he interjected in Zulu, "the diamonds are certainly there, and you shall have them, since you white men are so fond of toys and money."

"How do you know that, Umbopa?" I asked sharply, wary of his enigmatic behavior.

He laughed. "I dreamed it in the night, white men;" then he too turned abruptly and walked away.

"Now what," queried Sir Henry, "is our black friend implying? It is evident he knows more than he chooses to reveal. By the way, Quatermain, has he mentioned anything regarding—my brother?"

"Nothing," I replied. "He has questioned everyone he befriended, yet they all insist that no white man has ever been seen in this country before."

"Do you suppose he actually arrived here?" suggested Good. "We reached this place only by a miracle; is it probable he could have gotten here without the map?"

"I don't know," said Sir Henry gloomily, "but somehow I feel certain I shall find him."

Slowly the sun descended, and suddenly darkness swept down upon the land like a physical entity. There was no transitional period, no gentle fading; in these latitudes, twilight does not exist. The shift from day to night was as abrupt and absolute as the transition from life to death. The sun vanished, and the world was instantly draped in shadows. Yet this darkness did not endure for long. In the west, a glow appeared, followed by rays of silver light, until finally, the full and glorious moon illuminated the plain, shooting its gleaming beams far and wide, bathing the earth in a faint radiance.

We stood watching this magnificent sight, observing how the stars paled before this chastened majesty, feeling our spirits lift in the presence of a beauty beyond description. My life has been rugged, yet there are a few things for which I am grateful, chief among them being the privilege of witnessing this moon shine over Kukuanaland.

Our reverie was interrupted by our courteous companion, Infadoos.

"If my lords are rested, we shall proceed to Loo, where a hut awaits your arrival tonight. The moon is now bright, ensuring we shall not stumble in the dark."

We agreed, and within an hour we reached the outskirts of the town. Its expanse, marked by thousands of campfires, appeared utterly endless. Indeed, Good, ever prone to a jest, dubbed it "Unlimited Loo." Soon we approached a moat protected by a drawbridge, where we were greeted by the clashing of weapons and the hoarse challenge of a sentinel. Infadoos supplied a password I could not discern, which was acknowledged with a salute, allowing us to pass through the central thoroughfare of this great grass city. After nearly half an hour of walking past countless rows of huts, Infadoos finally stopped before the gate of a small cluster of dwellings surrounding a courtyard paved with powdered limestone. He informed us that these were designated as our "humble" quarters.

We entered and discovered that a hut had been assigned to each of us. These structures surpassed any we had previously seen, containing remarkably comfortable beds made of tanned skins laid upon mattresses of fragrant grass. Food was already prepared; shortly after washing ourselves with water drawn from earthenware jars, several young women of striking beauty brought us roasted meats and corn cobs, elegantly arranged on wooden platters, presenting them with deep bows.

We ate and drank, and then, at our request—which prompted smiles from the obliging young ladies—all the beds were moved into a single hut. Exhausted by our long journey, we threw ourselves onto them to sleep.

Upon waking, the sun was high overhead. The female attendants, showing no signs of modesty, were already inside the hut, having been summoned to assist us in getting dressed.

"Get dressed, indeed!" grumbled Good. "With nothing but a flannel shirt and boots, that takes little time. I wish you would ask them for my trousers, Quatermain."

I requested them, only to be informed that these sacred items had already been taken to the king, who intended to receive us in the morning.

To their surprise and disappointment, after asking the young ladies to leave, we proceeded to dress as best we could under the circumstances. Good even went so far as to shave the right side of his face again; we advised him strictly against touching the left side, where a respectable crop of whiskers had grown. As for myself and Sir Henry, we were content with a thorough wash and grooming our hair. Sir Henry's golden locks had nearly reached his shoulders, making him resemble an ancient Dane more than ever, while my own scruffy beard had grown to a full inch, exceeding the half-inch limit I generally preferred.

By the time we had finished breakfast and smoked a pipe, a messenger arrived—not less a personage than Infadoos himself—to announce that King Twala was ready to receive us, provided we wished to come.

We replied that we preferred to wait until the sun rose slightly higher, citing our lingering fatigue from the journey. It is always prudent, when dealing with uncivilized peoples, not to appear overly eager; they often mistake haste for disrespect or lack of awe. Thus, although we were as keen to meet Twala as he was to see us, we sat and waited an hour, utilizing the time to prepare gifts from our limited supplies: the Winchester rifle formerly used by poor Ventvogel and some beads. We decided to present the rifle and ammunition to His Royal Highness, saving the beads for his wives and courtiers. We had already distributed a few to Infadoos and Scragga, finding them delighted, as they had never seen such objects before. Finally declaring ourselves ready, we set off for the audience, with Umbopa carrying the rifle and beads.

"It appears to us, Infadoos, that King Twala is a cruel ruler."

Should the King covet a man's cattle, or desire his wife, or suspect a man of plotting rebellion, then Gagool—or one of the witch-finders she has trained—will scent him out as a wizard, and he shall die. "Yes, that is true."

"It was said that the woman and her son perished upon the mountains. Is that correct?"

"That is a charming story," Infadoos said, sounding deeply skeptical.

"For years he lived there as a servant and a soldier, yet keeping in his heart everything his mother had told him of his homeland, constantly planning how he might return to see his people and his father's house before his own death. "You are surely mad to speak thus," the old soldier exclaimed in amazement.

"You think so? Well, I shall prove it to you, O my uncle."

"I am Ignosi, the rightful King of the Kukuana!"

With a single fluid motion, Umbopa removed his "moocha" or girdle and stood completely naked before us.

"Look," he said, pointing to a tattoo of a great snake inked in blue around his midsection, its tail vanishing into its own open mouth just above where the thighs meet the torso.

Infadoos stared, his eyes bulging from their sockets. Then he fell to his knees.

"_Koom! Koom!_" he gasped. "It is my brother's son; it is the King."

"Did I not tell you so, my uncle? Rise. I am not yet King, but with your aid and the support of these brave white men, my friends, I shall become one. The old man placed a hand to his head in contemplation. Then he rose, walked forward to where Umbopa—or rather, Ignosi—stood, knelt before him, and took his hand.

"Ignosi, rightful King of the Kukuana, I place my hand between yours and pledge myself to you until death. When you were a babe, I rocked you upon my knees; now, in my old age, my arm shall strike for you and for freedom."

"It is well, Infadoos. If I conquer, you shall be the greatest man in the kingdom after the King. If I fail, you can only die, and death is not far from you. Rise, my uncle."

"These are *men*, indeed!" Ignosi shouted, grinding his teeth in excitement. "They will conquer again!"

Like smoke blown from a cannon, the white-plumed regiment broke and fled. The Greys, though reduced to a ragged six hundred from their original three thousand, stood victorious. Instead of retreating, they chased the fleeing enemy, securing a small knoll and forming a protective ring around it. Standing atop the mound, I saw Sir Henry, unharmed, alongside Infadoos.

Then, Twala's remaining regiments poured down upon the isolated band. The battle resumed with renewed fury.

I, usually a coward who preferred to flee, felt a strange transformation. Watching the ferocity of the warriors, my blood heated, and a savage desire to kill seized me. I looked at the ranks behind us—hands twitching, eyes burning with the hunger of battle—and wondered if I too had changed. Ignosi remained remarkably calm, though his teeth still ground together.

"Shall we stand here and rot?" I asked. "While Twala devours our brothers?"

"Now is the moment," Ignosi replied. "Let us strike."

As a fresh regiment rushed past the mound, Ignosi signaled the Buffaloes to charge. Screaming their war-cry, they surged forward like a tidal wave.

Chaos ensued. An irregular but powerful advance shook the ground, followed by a deafening clash of spears and a red mist of blood. When clarity returned, I found myself amidst the remnants of the Greys, directly behind Sir Henry. He had told me later that the Buffaloes' initial charge had thrown me clear, allowing him to drag me into safety.

The fight continued in a cycle of heroic desperation. Enemy after enemy surged against our shrinking circle, only to be beaten back. The scene was a testament to courage: battalions marching over heaps of their own dead, lifting corpses to gain purchase, only to add new layers to the pile. Infadoos remained cool, issuing orders and jokes to keep spirits high, stepping personally into the thickest of the fray.

Sir Henry was equally formidable. His ostrich plumes had been torn away, revealing his long yellow hair streaming in the wind. Clad in armor slick with blood, wielding his axe with the fury of a berserker, he was unstoppable. Every time an enemy dared approach, Henry roared "*O-hoy!*" and cleaved through shield, helmet, and skull alike. He was the "wizard" who neither failed nor spared.

Suddenly, a cry rang out: "*Twala, y' Twala!*"

From the melee stepped the one-eyed giant himself, armored in chainmail and wielding axe and shield.

"Where art thou, Incubu, thou white man who slew Scragga my son?" Twala screamed, hurling a knife at Sir Henry. Henry deflected it, the blade lodging harmlessly in his shield. Undeterred, Twala lunged, striking Henry's shield with such force that the Dane sank to his knees.

Just as the duel seemed poised to decide the battle, a shout of dismay rose from the surrounding regiments. Looking up, I saw the turning tide. To our right and left, the plains were alive with charging warriors. The outflanking squadrons had arrived.

Ignosi had predicted this trap. While Twala's army focused entirely on the bloody struggle in the center, believing our flankers were hidden reserves, the Impi cavalry leaped like greyhounds onto their unprotected sides.

Within five minutes, the battle was won. Surrounded and terrified by the slaughter, Twala's forces broke into a panicked rout. The plain between us and the town of Loo was scattered with fleeing soldiers. Our enemies melted away as if by magic, leaving us standing alone amidst the devastation.

Of the noble Greys, only ninety-five men remained on their feet. Over three thousand four hundred had fallen.

"Ye have upheld the reputation of your regiment," Infadoos said calmly, tending to a wound on his arm. "Your deeds today will be sung by your grandchildren."

Once the slaughter ceased, Sir Henry and Good were borne into Twala's hut, where I followed close behind. We were a sorry sight; both men were utterly drained by the exertion and the loss of blood, and my own state was hardly better. Though I am naturally wiry and accustomed to enduring fatigue thanks to my lean frame and years of training, that night I was completely spent. In truth, it would have been hard to find a more miserable trio than we were that evening. With the help of Foulata—who, grateful for having been spared by us, had become our devoted handmaiden, particularly attending to Good—we managed to remove the chain mail shirts that had undoubtedly saved our lives. No wonder they cried; over twelve thousand men, nearly a fifth of the Kukuana army, had been destroyed in that horrific struggle. I shall never forget the sight those three towering spires presented in the early dawn of the following morning. High above, soaring into the azure sky, twisted wreaths of snow clung to their summits. I can offer no adequate description of the horrors of the night that followed. Mercifully, they were somewhat mitigated by sleep; even in such a desperate position, weary nature occasionally asserts itself. Yet, for my part, I found it impossible to rest much. Setting aside the terrifying thought of our impending doom—a fate so grim that the bravest man on earth might well quail—I never claimed to possess extraordinary courage. Rather, it was the **silence** itself that kept me awake. You, reader, may have lain awake at night and found the quiet oppressive, but I say with confidence that you can have no conception of what a vivid, tangible entity "perfect stillness" becomes. On the surface of the earth, there is always some sound or motion, however imperceptible, which deadens the sharp edge of absolute silence. Here, there was none. We were buried deep within the bowels of a massive, snow-capped peak. Thousands of feet above us, fresh air rushed over the white snow, yet no sound of it reached us. Separated by a long tunnel and five feet of solid rock from the awful chamber of the Dead—and knowing that the dead make no noise—we understood that even the crashing of all the artillery of earth and heaven could not have pierced our living tomb. Cut off from every echo of the world, we were as men already in the grave.

Then, the cruel irony of our situation forced itself upon me. Around us lay treasures sufficient to pay off a moderate national debt or build a fleet of ironclads, and yet we would have bartered them all gladly for the faintest chance of escape. Soon, doubtless, we should rejoice to exchange them for a bit of food or a cup of water, and eventually, even for the privilege of a speedy end to our suffering. "What is the good of eating?" answered Good bleakly. "The sooner we die and get it over the better."

"While there is life there is hope," said Sir Henry firmly.

Accordingly, we ate and sipped some water, allowing another period of time to pass. Then, Sir Henry suggested that it might be wise to get as near the door as possible and call out, on the faint chance that someone might hear us outside. Good, possessing a fine, piercing note from years of practice at sea, groped his way down the passage and set to work. I must admit, he made a most diabolical noise. I never heard such yells, yet they might have been a mosquito buzzing for all the effect they produced. After a while, he gave it up, returned very thirsty, and had to drink. We then stopped yelling, as it threatened to exhaust our precious water supply.

So, we sat down once more against the chests of useless diamonds in that dreadful inaction which was one of the hardest aspects of our fate. For my part, I gave way completely to despair. Laying my head against Sir Henry's broad shoulder, I burst into tears. I think I heard Good gulping away on the other side, swearing hoarsely at himself for showing weakness. Ah, how good and brave that great man was! Had we been two frightened children and he our nurse, he could not have treated us more tenderly. Forgetting his own share of misery, he did all he could to soothe our broken nerves, telling stories of men who had endured similar circumstances and miraculously escaped. When these tales failed to cheer us, he pointed out that anticipating an inevitable end was futile; death from exhaustion would soon come, and perhaps be merciful (though that is not entirely true). Then, in a diffident manner, as he had done before, he suggested throwing ourselves on the mercy of a Higher Power—a sentiment I embraced with great vigor. His was a beautiful character: quiet, yet incredibly strong.

Thus, somehow, the day passed as the night had, if one can use such terms where everything was shrouded in densest night. When I lit a match to check the time, it was seven o'clock. Once more we ate and drank, and as we did so, an idea occurred to me.

"How is it," I asked, "that the air in this place remains fresh? It is thick and heavy, but it is perfectly fresh."

"Great heavens!" exclaimed Good, springing up. "I never thought of that. It can't come through the stone door; it's air-tight, if ever a door was. It must come from somewhere. If there were no current of air, we should have been stifled or poisoned the moment we entered. Let us investigate."

Wonderful was the change this mere spark of hope wrought in us. In a moment, we were all three groveling on our hands and knees, feeling for the slightest indication of a draft. Presently, my enthusiasm received a check. I placed my hand on something cold. It was the face of the dead Foulata. For an hour or more, we continued searching until, finally, Sir Henry and I gave up in despair, having injured ourselves considerably by knocking our heads against tusks, chests, and the chamber walls. But Good persisted, saying with an approaching cheerfulness that it was better than doing nothing.

"I say, you fellows," he called out in a constrained voice, "come here."

Needless to say, we scrambled toward him quickly.

"Quatermain, put your hand here where mine is. Now, do you feel anything?"

"I *think* I feel air coming up."

"Now listen."

He rose and stamped upon the spot, and a flame of hope shot up in our hearts. *It rang hollow.* With trembling hands, I lit a match. I had only three left. We realized we were in the angle of the far corner of the chamber—a fact explaining why we hadn't noticed the hollow sound during our earlier exhaustive search. As the match burned, we scrutinized the area. There was a joint in the solid rock floor, and, great heavens! there, flush with the rock, was a stone ring. We said no word; we were too excited, our hearts beating too wildly with hope to speak. Good had a knife with a hoof-pick attached to the back. He opened it and scratched around the ring. Finally, he worked it under and levered gently, fearful of breaking the tool. The ring began to move. Being stone, it had not rusted fast in the centuries it had lain there, unlike iron would have.

Presently, it stood upright. Then he thrust his hands inside and tugged with all his force, but nothing budged.

"Let me try," I said impatiently, for the stone's position in the corner made it impossible for two to pull simultaneously. I took hold and strained, but to no avail. Then Sir Henry tried and failed. Taking the hook again, Good scratched all around the crack where we felt the air rising.

"Now, Curtis," he commanded, "tackle on and put your back into it; you are as strong as two."

"Stop," he interrupted, removing a stout black silk handkerchief—which, true to his habits of neatness, he still wore—and threading it through the ring. "Quatermain, get Curtis round the middle and pull for dear life when I give the word. *Now.*"

Sir Henry exerted all his enormous strength, and Good and I did the same with whatever power nature had granted us.

"Heave! Heave! It's giving!" gasped Sir Henry, and I heard the muscles of his great back crack. Suddenly, there was a grating sound, then a rush of air, and we were all thrown onto our backs on the floor beneath a heavy flagstone. Sir Henry's strength had succeeded, and never did muscular power serve a man better.

"Light a match, Quatermain," he ordered as soon as we had picked ourselves up and recovered our breath. "Carefully, now."

I complied, and there, before us, Heaven be praised! was the **first step of a stone stair**.

"Now, what is to be done?" asked Good.

"Follow the stair, of course, and trust to Providence," replied Sir Henry.

"Stop!" he interjected. "Quatermain, fetch the bit of biltong and the remaining water; we may need them."

I crept back to our place by the chests to retrieve them. As I turned away, an idea struck me. We had paid little heed to the diamonds for the past twenty-four hours; indeed, the very concept of diamonds was nauseating given what they had cost us. However, I reasoned, I might as well pocket some in case we ever escaped this ghastly hole. So, I plunged my fist into the first chest, filling all the available pockets of my old shooting coat and trousers, topping it off with a few handfuls of large gems from the third chest. By an afterthought, I also stuffed Foulata's basket—which, save for one water gourd and a little biltong, was now empty

Ten days after that harrowing morning, we returned to our familiar quarters at Loo. Miraculously, we emerged from our ordeal scarcely the worse for wear, save for two distinct changes: my stubble had emerged from the treasure cave three shades grayer than it entered, and Good was irrevocably altered by Foulata's death, a loss that weighed heavily upon his spirit. From the perspective of a seasoned traveler, I must admit that her passing was, in fact, a blessing; had she lived, the delicate balance of our group would inevitably have tipped into chaos. She possessed a stately beauty and a refined intellect rare among her people, yet no amount of grace could bridge the chasm between her station and ours. As she herself poignantly asked, "Can the sun mate with the darkness, or the white with the black?" Thus, we resolved never to revisit Solomon's treasure chamber.

After forty-eight hours of recovery, we descended into the great pit seeking the exit we had used to flee the mountain. Our hopes were dashed immediately; heavy rains had washed away every trace of our tracks, and the cavern walls were pockmarked with the burrows of ant-eaters and other creatures, making it impossible to discern our path. Furthermore, the day prior to our departure, driven by a restless curiosity, we revisited the stalactite cave and ventured once more into the Chamber of the Dead. Standing beneath the spear of the White Death, we gazed in awe at the colossal slab of rock that had sealed our fate, pondering the unimaginable wealth beyond, the crushed remains of the ancient hag, Gagool, and the fair maiden whose tomb served as the portal. Despite our best efforts, we found no seam, no joint, and no mechanism to operate the sliding door—a marvel of engineering in its massive, inscrutable simplicity that likely stands unmatched in the world. Disheartened, we abandoned the quest. Though the temptation to breach five feet of solid rock for unlimited diamonds was overwhelming, logic prevailed; only dynamite could succeed, and we chose to leave the riches undisturbed. Perhaps centuries hence, a luckier explorer might discover the "Open Sesame," but I harbor a grim conviction that those tens of millions of pounds' worth of jewels will remain cold companions to Foulata's bones forever.

Yet, to complain was ungrateful. Before sealing ourselves in our prison, I had prudently stuffed the pockets of my shooting coat and trousers with gems, alongside Foulata's basket, which held even more despite being partially filled with a water bottle. Although several large stones tumbled out during our perilous descent, we retained an immense hoard, including ninety-three magnificent stones weighing between seventy and over two hundred carats each. Even after sharing the load, we possessed enough to become the wealthiest men in Europe, if not millionaires by American standards.

Upon reaching Loo, we were greeted with immense warmth by King Ignosi, who was robust and busy consolidating his power and reorganizing his regiments after the conflict with Twala. He listened intently to our tale, his expression growing somber when we recounted Gagool's gruesome demise. Summoning an ancient counselor, Ignosi confirmed that the witch-doctor had been feared since the days of his great-grandfather, a figure of ugliness and wickedness whose death lifted an ancient curse from the land. "Black Puppy, who tore out the old dog's throat," he declared, signaling the end of her reign. He rejoiced that she would not have allowed us to perish in the dark or plotted his murder as she had his father's, setting up Twala instead.

As we prepared to depart, I addressed Ignosi regarding our imminent departure from Kukuanaland. "Behold, Ignosi," I said, "you came to us as a servant, and we leave you as a mighty king. Remember your promise: rule justly, uphold the law, and spare no life without cause. Tomorrow, at first light, provide an escort to guide us across the mountains."

Ignosi covered his face with his hands, overcome with emotion. "My heart is torn apart!" he cried. "What have I done to you, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, that you should abandon me in my hour of triumph? You fought beside me in rebellion and battle; why leave me alone in peace? Take wives from my maidens! Take my homes! Take cattle, game, and the loyalty of my Impis! If you require war, my armies await your command!"

"Nay, Ignosi," I replied gently, "we seek only our own homeland."

His bitterness flared instantly. "Then I learn that you love the bright stones more than your friend! You possess the stones and intend to sell them in Natal, crossing the moving black waters to become rich, as is the nature of a white man's heart. Cursed be the white stones, and cursed be he who seeks them! Death awaits anyone who steps into the Place of Death to find them. I have spoken. Go, white men."

Placing a hand on his arm, I appealed to his nostalgia. "Ignosi, when you wandered in Zululand and among the whites of Natal, did not your heart yearn for the land your mother spoke of—the place of your birth, where you played as a child? In the same way, our hearts turn to our own lands."

Silence fell upon the court. Breaking it, Ignosi's voice softened. "I see now, as I always have, that your words are wise, Macumazahn. That which flies does not love to run on the ground; the white man does not wish to dwell among the black man's kraals. Yet, you must go, and my heart breaks knowing you will be as dead to me, for news cannot reach me from afar."

He turned to us with renewed resolve. "Listen, and let all your brothers hear my decree: No other white man shall cross these mountains. No traders with guns and gin shall pass. My people shall fight with spears and drink pure water, as our ancestors did. I will banish the praying-men who sow fear and incite rebellion against the king. If a single white man approaches my gates, I will send him back; if a hundred, I will push them back; if armies come, I will crush them with all my strength. None shall ever seek the shining stones—not even an army. Should they arrive, I will fill the pit, collapse the caves, and bury them under rocks, ensuring none can reach the door you speak of, the key to which is lost.

"But for you three—Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan—the path remains open forever. You are dearer to me than all that breathes. Your uncle, Infadoos, and my Indunas will guide you with a regiment via another pass to the north, avoiding the treacherous road of Solomon. Farewell, my brave brothers. See me no more, for I lack the heart to endure it. Hear my final decree: Your names, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, shall be *hlonipa*—forbidden—as are the names of dead kings. To speak them is death. Thus, your memory shall be preserved in the land eternally."

Tears streamed down his face. "Go now, before my eyes overflow like a woman's. In the future, when you look back on life or gather around the fire in your twilight years, recall how we stood shoulder to shoulder in that great battle planned by your wisdom, Macumazahn; how you were the point of the horn that gored Twala's flank, Bougwan; how you stood in the ring of the Greys, Incubu, felling men like corn before a sickle; and how you broke the wild bull Twala's strength, reducing his pride to dust. Farewell forever, my lords and my friends."

Ignosi rose, covering his face with the corner of his cloak, hiding his sorrow from us. We departed in silence.

At dawn the following day, we left Loo, escorted by our beloved friend Infadoos, who was heartbroken, and a regiment of Buffaloes. The entire town lined the streets to offer us a royal salute, while women threw flowers in our path, blessing us for liberating the land from Twala. Amidst this solemnity, a moment of levity occurred near the town's outskirts. A lovely young girl approached Good, holding lilies, and requested a boon: that he allow her to gaze upon his famous white legs, which had traveled four days' journey to become legendary.

"I'll be hanged if I do!" Good protested excitedly.

"Come, my dear fellow," Sir Henry urged. "You cannot refuse a lady."

"It is positively indecent!" Good insisted stubbornly.

Eventually, yielding to the pressure and the rapturous cheers of the crowd, Good drew up his trousers to his knees and walked in this exposed state until we cleared the town. His legs, I fear, would never again receive such adoration, though the Kukuanas eventually tired of his melting teeth and transparent eye.

Our guide, Infadoos, informed us of a safer alternative: a northern pass leading down a sheer cliff separating Kukuanaland from the desert, broken by the towering "Sheba's Breasts." Two years prior, Kukuana hunters had used this route to chase ostriches, discovering a vast, fertile oasis along the way. This path promised to spare us the rigors of the mountain passes. Guided by hunters who knew the way, we traveled easily. On the fourth night, we crested the mountains overlooking the sandy billows of the desert, twenty

It was during this peculiar expedition that we encountered perhaps the strangest twist of fate, a moment that perfectly illustrates how providentially events often align. I was striding quietly ahead of the others, following the winding banks of the stream that flows from the oasis before vanishing into the insatiable jaws of the desert sands. Suddenly, I halted and rubbed my eyes, wondering if the heat had played tricks on my vision. Yet, there it stood, barely twenty yards ahead—a cozy hut situated in a delightful spot beneath the shade of a wild fig tree, facing the water. Constructed largely on the principles of the local tribesmen, utilizing grass and woven branches, it possessed a distinct feature: a full-length door rather than the typical narrow aperture.

"What on earth," I muttered to myself, "could possibly justify a hut existing here?"

Before I could process the absurdity, the door swung open. Stepping out was a white man, dressed in animal skins and sporting an immense black beard, moving with a pronounced limp. For a heartbeat, I feared I had succumbed to a feverish delirium caused by the sun. It was inconceivable; no hunter would venture to such a desolate place, let alone establish a dwelling there. I stared, bewildered, and so did the man behind me. Just then, Sir Henry and Good rounded the bend.

"Look here, gentlemen," I exclaimed, "is that truly a white man, or have I finally gone mad?"

Sir Henry peered closely, followed by Good. Abruptly, the lame, bearded stranger let out a piercing cry and began hobbling toward us. Before he could cover the distance, he collapsed into a faint. With characteristic speed, Sir Henry sprang to his side.

"Great Powers!" he gasped. "It is my brother, George!"

The commotion drew another figure from within the hut—also clad in skins, gripping a rifle. Seeing me, he shouted, "Macumazahn! Don't you recognize me, Baas? I'm Jim, the hunter! I lost the note you entrusted to me for the Baas, and we have been stranded here for nearly two years."

Overcome with emotion, the man threw himself at my feet, rolling around in tears of joy. "You careless scoundrel!" I retorted, though my anger was tinged with relief. "You deserve a severe sjambocking—that is, a hiding!"

Meanwhile, George Curtis had regained consciousness. Rising unsteadily, he and Sir Henry engaged in a frantic embrace, pumping each other's hands in silent, tearful reunion. Whatever grievances they may have harbored in the past—my suspicion was that a woman was the cause—they were instantly forgotten in the face of this miracle.

"My dear old fellow," Sir Henry burst out, his voice thick with emotion, "I thought you were dead! I trekked over Solomon's Mountains specifically to find you. I had surrendered all hope of ever seeing you again, and yet here you are, perched in the desert like an ancient vulture."

"I attempted to cross Solomon's Mountains nearly two years ago," George replied, his voice hesitant, unused to speech after such isolation. "But when I reached this spot, a massive boulder dislodged and crushed my right leg. Since then, I have been unable to move forward or backward."

I stepped closer. "How do you do, Mr. Neville? Do you recall me?"

"Why," he stammered, looking between us, "isn't it Hunter Quatermain, and Good too? Hold on, men, I feel dizzy again. It is all so incredibly strange, and yet, for a man who has ceased to hope, it is overwhelmingly happy!"

That evening, gathered around the campfire, George recounted his ordeal. His tale, in its own right, rivaled ours in drama. Less than two years prior, he had departed Sitanda's Kraal attempting to reach Suliman's Berg. The note I had dispatched via Jim had, unfortunately, been misplaced by the latter, leaving George unaware of its contents until this very day. Ignoring the route marked on Dom Silvestra's outdated map, he had followed native intelligence toward the ladder-like descent of the mountains we had recently traversed—a path far superior to the original plan.

Their journey across the desert had been harrowing, but they eventually stumbled upon this oasis. Disaster struck on their first night there. While Jim harvested honey from a stingless bee's nest atop a sandbank directly above George, he inadvertently loosened a colossal boulder. It crashed down, shattering George's right leg. Paralyzed by pain and immobility, George chose to endure the slow death of starvation in the oasis rather than risk wandering further into the lethal wastes.

Fortunately, survival was possible. They possessed ample ammunition, and the oasis teemed with game drawn by the water. They hunted and trapped animals for food, later using the hides for clothing once their garments wore thin. Like a modern Robinson Crusoe and his Friday, they survived for nearly two years, clinging to the faint hope that passing natives might rescue them. None appeared. Only the previous night had they decided that Jim must attempt to return to Sitanda's Kraal for aid, while George remained behind. Jim was scheduled to depart the following morning, and George had resigned himself to never seeing him again.

Then, *we* arrived. "You," George declared, addressing me, "of all people in the world! You, whom I presumed had long forgotten me and were enjoying comfort in England, appear in such a chaotic manner precisely where you least expect to find me. It is the most wondrous and merciful coincidence I have ever known."

Sir Henry then detailed our adventures, keeping George company until the late hours. When I presented him with the diamonds, he exclaimed, "By Jove! Well, at least you have acquired something tangible for your efforts, aside from my utterly worthless presence."

Sir Henry chuckled. "Those belong to Quatermain and Good. It was agreed that any spoils found would be divided between them."

This statement sparked a reflection in me. After consulting with Good, I approached Sir Henry. "It is our collective desire," I proposed, "that you accept a third portion of the diamonds. Should you decline, we intend to transfer your share to your brother, George, who endured greater hardship than either of us in pursuit of these gems."

We successfully persuaded him to agree to this arrangement, though George remained unaware of our generosity until much later.

At this juncture, I believe it appropriate to conclude my narrative. The trek back to Sitanda's Kraal was grueling, particularly as we bore the weight of George, whose shattered leg frequently shed splinters of bone. Nevertheless, we accomplished the journey. Upon returning to Sitanda's six months later, we discovered our firearms and supplies intact, although the caretaker was visibly displeased that we had returned alive enough to reclaim them.

From there, we traveled safely to my estate on the Berea, near Durban, where I currently reside. Having bid farewell to all companions who shared in this extraordinary voyage, I now sit to write this account.

Just as I finished the final sentence, a Zulu servant entered my avenue of orange trees, carrying a letter wedged into a piece of wood, delivered by post. It was from Sir Henry. As the correspondence speaks for itself, I transcribe it in full below.
