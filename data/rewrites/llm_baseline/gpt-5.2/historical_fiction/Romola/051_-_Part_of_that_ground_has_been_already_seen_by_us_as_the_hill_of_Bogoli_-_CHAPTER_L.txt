That morning the city seemed to hold its breath between winter and Lent. February’s sun lay pale as watered wine on the roofs, and the breeze that moved along the streets carried with it a mingled smell of damp stone, olive-wood smoke, and the faint, teasing sweetness that always attended holidays—fried dough, sugared almonds, spilled must warming in the light. Over it all ran the Florence of sound: shutters knocked open, a vendor’s call ricocheting down a lane, the shuffle of many shoes beginning their slow drift toward the Piazza. Even before the spectacle arrived, the day had its own music—less a trumpet than a constant, human murmur.

Into that murmur came Tessa.

She was easy to know, not in black like so many women now, but in her old honest colours—red, green, and white—moving with the quick, hopeful step of someone who still believed that going out could be simple. She no longer lived on the hill of San Giorgio. After what had happened there with Baldassarre, Tito—Naldo, as she called him—had chosen a different house for her, still quiet, still airy, set by wide garden grounds north of the Porta Santa Croce. The air there had birds in it, and the softer acoustics of leaves and open space: less of the clang that rose from the river traffic, less of the harsh shouting that scraped at the nerves like a blunt knife.

She had not come to see the Carnival without asking. The evening before, Tito had been with her, and she had waited, as she had learned to do, for the right moment—when his face had smoothed itself into that radiant ease he could still summon in her rooms. He sat in the straight-backed carved chair he had bought for his own comfort when he visited, one arm about sturdy Lillo, the other laid lightly on Tessa’s shoulder while she steadied little Ninna on her uncertain legs.

Tessa could not read letters, but she had learned to read his brow and mouth as if they were a page—where weariness lodged, where amusement woke, where impatience flickered. And in those visits, he was nearer than anywhere else to the bright, gentle-humoured youth who had once woken under the Loggia de’ Cerchi, when deceit had not yet asked him for hostages. He felt it too, though he would not have named it: here, with Tessa, he needed no guile. Her ignorance and innocence formed a shelter that asked nothing of him but the sound of his name in small voices.

“Babbo,” they called, and the word was sweet in his ears for the brief time he allowed it to be true.

When he thought of leaving Florence, he never thought of leaving Tessa and the children behind. Fondness—real, green-leaf fondness—clung to them: round cheeks, wide eyes, the warm weight of a child leaning into his knee. Somewhere in him, kindness still had a spring, and Tessa kept it from freezing. Romola—poor Romola—by the very effort of her sacrifice, chilled him into a hard dislike he had once thought impossible, and that dislike hardened him further. Tessa did the opposite without intending: she asked for so little, and gave so much soft ordinary life in return.

“Ninna is very good without me now,” Tessa began, letting the child sit down with a thump on the floor. “I can leave her with Monna Lisa any time, and if she is in the cradle and cries, Lillo is as sensible as can be—he goes and thumps Monna Lisa.”

Lillo, taking this as praise and command, sprang down from Tito’s knee and marched across the room to Monna Lisa, who sat spinning with her head slowly shaking—as if her deafness had become a habit of refusal. Lillo’s small fist landed against her arm in a solid little blow of summons.

“A wonderful boy!” Tito said, laughing.

“Isn’t he?” Tessa leaned closer, her desire rising and catching in her throat until she made it small and neat. “And I might go and see the Carnival to-morrow, just for an hour or two—mightn’t I?”

“Oh, you wicked pigeon,” he said, pinching her cheek. “Those are your longings, are they? What have you to do with carnivals now you are an old woman with two children?”

“But old women like to see things,” she answered, her lower lip beginning to hang. “Monna Lisa said she should like to go, only she’s so deaf she can’t hear what is behind her, and she thinks we couldn’t take care of both the children.”

His face sobered. “No, indeed, Tessa. You must not think of taking the children into the crowded streets, else I shall be angry.”

“I have never been into the Piazza without leave,” she pleaded, frightened by his tone. “Not since Holy Saturday. And I think Nofri is dead, for you know the poor madre died. And I shall never forget the Carnival I saw once; it was so pretty—all roses and a king and queen under them—and singing. I liked it better than the San Giovanni.”

“But there’s nothing like that now,” he said. “They are going to make a bonfire in the Piazza—that’s all. And I cannot let you go out by yourself in the evening.”

“Oh no, no! I don’t want to go in the evening. Only by daylight, to see the procession. There will be a procession—is it not true?”

“Yes, after a sort,” he said, dryly. “As lively as a flight of cranes. You must not expect roses and glittering kings and queens. Still, any string of people called a procession will please your blue eyes. They have raised a thing in the Piazza de’ Signori for the bonfire—you may like to see it. But come home early, and look like a grave little old woman; and if you see any men with feathers and swords, keep out of their way. They are very fierce, and like to cut old women’s heads off.”

“Santa Madonna!” Tessa cried, then caught his laughter and half-laughed too, though fear always rose quickly in her. “But I will keep away. Only—” she leaned in and whispered at his ear, warm breath and secrecy—“if I might take Lillo with me! He is very sensible.”

“But who will thump Monna Lisa then, if she doesn’t hear?” Tito said, keeping his face serious with effort. “No, Tessa. You could not take care of Lillo in a crowd, and he’s too heavy for you to carry.”

“It is true,” she admitted, saddened. “And he likes to run away. I forgot that. Then I will go alone. But now look at Ninna—you have not looked at her enough.”

Ninna was at that tumbling age when a child seems made of good dough and gravity, a fair solid that keeps finding its base—falling, righting itself, falling again, always landing on life. Tessa snatched her up and displayed the new teeth with pride, the damp sweetness of baby breath rising as she laughed. While Tito gave due attention, she whispered again, “And shall I buy some confetti for the children?”

He drew small coins from his scarsella and poured them into her palm.

“That will buy no end,” Tessa said, delight in her voice like a spoon striking a pot. “I shall not mind going without Lillo so much, if I bring him something.”

So in the morning she set out toward the great Piazza where the bonfire would be. She wore only her green woollen dress: the breeze did not bite, and she would not put on a mantle that would hide the new necklace and the new silver-mounted clasp at her belt—the only ornaments Tito had ever given her. She did not think of her figure; no one had ever told her it was pretty. But she did think of those bright pieces of metal and stone against her throat and waist, sure they would command respect, sure that any rich contadina would envy them. She adjusted her white hood so the necklace showed at the front, and took her small basket—empty now, soon to be filled with coloured sweets and crisp delights for Lillo and Ninna.

As she walked, the city’s holiday sound thickened: people lingered in knots; voices were looser, laughter more frequent. Country folk in their best garments drifted toward the centre like flocks, and the streets answered them with echo and clatter. Tessa smelled frying oil from some doorway where cakes were being turned, and the faint spicy note of hot wine. Her thoughts were mostly of purchases—what would be most pleasing to Lillo’s hungry hands, what would make Ninna’s eyes widen.

She did not think of anyone watching her.

But she had been watched the moment she descended from her upper storey. Now, as she tripped cheerily under the February sun, someone kept her in sight: a man bent beneath a package of yarn.

Baldassarre had taken to such burdens as a means of earning his scant bread—and keeping the sacred fire of vengeance alive. Often he had come out of his way to pass this house, the house Tito had once led him to at evening. Often he had waited, uncertain, fearful, hoping for proof that this was indeed the place where one of Tito’s wives lived. His long imprisonment had sharpened suspicion into a trembling instinct; he believed in a diabolic fortune that shielded Tito, and he dared not pursue him except in a crowd or in darkness. If Tito’s eyes fell on him, he felt—no, he knew—he would again be seized, mocked, dragged away, robbed even of his knife, cast helpless into a cell.

So his fierce purpose had become stealthy, serpent-like, waiting for a single dart of the fang. He could not hear again the voice that had once thundered promise of vengeance in the Duomo; he had returned there many times, but that voice seemed stifled now by cunning, by strong-armed wickedness, by the new fearful piety that burned old joys like straw. Justice was weak and unfriended. Baldassarre’s mind, enfeebled and yet obstinate, had carried one heavy question for months: did Tito still wear the armour? For a long time his fainting hope would have been content with one successful stab on this side of the grave. But he would never risk his precious knife again. When at last he knew the sharp steel was useless, and that he had no hope but in some new device, the knowledge settled on him like lead.

A dim vision returned again and again: win one of the two wives to aid him. The wife who had lived on the hill was gone; if he could find her again, perhaps he could seize a thread and work toward clarity. This morning, at last, he had succeeded. He watched the green-and-white figure, bent under his yarn, and tried with feeble eyes to interpret her life from lines and colours as one reads a faded painting.

Tessa passed through long streets with little sign of Carnival beyond the holiday loitering. She began to feel disappointed—no roses, no kings and queens—only the usual shouting that on any day filled Florence like a rough river. Then she saw a man with a pedlar’s basket, apparently selling nothing but little red crosses.

A little red cross would be pretty above her bed; it would also keep off harm, and might make Ninna stronger. She crossed the street to ask the price, counting her coins in her mind, already grieving if she must take from the confetti.

But as she drew near, the pedlar turned, and she recognised Bratti Ferravecchi—an old acquaintance from the Mercato. Habit told her to avoid old acquaintances: Tito had commanded it. She turned away quickly, but Bratti’s eye was trained like a hawk’s at corners, and her movement did not escape him. A tap on her arm arrested her—the tap of a red cross.

“Young woman,” Bratti said, “you come from some castello a good way off, else you’d never think of walking about this blessed Carnival without a red cross in your hand. Santa Madonna! Four white quattrini is a small price to pay for your soul—prices rise in purgatory, let me tell you.”

“Oh, I should like one,” Tessa said hastily, “but I couldn’t spare four white quattrini.”

Bratti had first regarded her only as a customer, but now her voice lit recognition in him. “By the head of San Giovanni, it must be the little Tessa—looking as fresh as a ripe apple! What! you’ve done none the worse, then, for running away from father Nofri? You were in the right of it. He goes on crutches now, and a crabbed fellow with crutches is dangerous; he can reach across the house and beat a woman as he sits.”

“I’m married,” Tessa said, demurely, remembering Tito’s injunction to look grave. “And my husband takes great care of me.”

“Ah! then you’ve fallen on your feet. Nofri said you were good-for-nothing vermin; but what then? An ass may bray a good while before he shakes the stars down. I always said you did well to run away, and it isn’t often Bratti’s in the wrong. Well, and so you’ve got a husband and plenty of money? Then you’ll never think much of giving four white quattrini for a red cross. I get no profit; but what with the famine and the new religion, all other merchandise is gone down. You live in the country where the chestnuts are plenty, eh? You’ve never wanted for polenta, I can see.”

“No, I’ve never wanted anything,” Tessa answered, guarded.

“Then you can afford to buy a cross. I got a Padre to bless them, and you get blessing and all for four quattrini. It isn’t for the profit; I hardly get a danaro by the whole lot. But they’re holy wares, and it’s getting harder and harder work to see your way to Paradise. The very Carnival is like Holy Week. The least you can do to keep the Devil from getting the upper hand is to buy a cross. God guard you! think what the Devil’s tooth is! You’ve seen him biting the man in San Giovanni, I should hope?”

Tessa felt teased and frightened; Bratti’s words scraped at her like a rough wool. “Oh, Bratti,” she said with a troubled face, “I want to buy a great many confetti. I’ve got little Lillo and Ninna at home. Nice coloured sweet things cost a great deal. They will not like the cross so well—though I know it would be good to have it.”

“Come, then,” Bratti said, with the air of a man laying up merits by imagining extortion and then renouncing it, “since you’re an old acquaintance, you shall have it for two quattrini. It’s making you a present of the cross, to say nothing of the blessing.”

Tessa reached out her two quattrini with trembling hesitation. Bratti stopped her abruptly.

“Stop a bit! Where do you live?”

“Oh, a long way off,” she answered almost by reflex, her mind on the coins. “Beyond San Ambrogio, in the Via Piccola, at the top of the house where the wood is stacked below.”

“Very good,” Bratti said, patronising. “Then I’ll let you have the cross on trust, and call for the money. So you live inside the gates? Well, well, I shall be passing.”

“No, no!” Tessa cried, frightened at the thought of Tito’s anger if Bratti came to her door. “I can spare the money. Take it now.”

But Bratti set his jaw. “No. I’m not a hard-hearted pedlar. I’ll call and see if you’ve got any rags, and you shall make a bargain. See—here’s the cross. And there’s Pippo’s shop not far behind you. Go and fill your basket, and I must go and get mine empty. Addio, piccina.”

He moved away before she could refuse. Tessa stood with the red cross in her hand, and a fluttering in her chest like a bird trapped behind ribs. She had let him know too much. There were more dangers in coming out than in staying at home. She hurried into Pippo’s shop, resolved to turn her money into confetti quickly before any other accident could find her.

In Pippo’s, the air was dense with sugar and spice, with the floury breath of almond paste, with the sharp clean scent of anise. Jars stood like little treasure chests: bright comfits, twisted sugared seeds, tiny cakes glazed to a hard shine that would crack between teeth. Tessa chose by colour as much as by cost, thinking of Lillo’s greedy delight and Ninna’s solemn, open-mouthed wonder. The basket grew heavier; the paper parcels made a crisp music as they shifted, and that sound soothed her—the domestic sound of provision, the promise of mouths made happy.

When she came out into the Via de’ Libraj, her face had returned to its childlike content. And now, at last, she thought she saw a procession: white robes, a banner. Her heart began to beat faster, expectation rising like yeast.

She drew aside; the street was narrow, and the closeness forced her to look closely. The banner was beautiful: the Holy Mother with the Babe. Since having her own babies, Tessa’s love for that pictured mother had grown deeper, more intimate, as if the painted Madonna might understand the weight of a child’s head against the shoulder and the ache that comes with tenderness. The white-robed figures wore green wreaths, and each carried a little red cross. Tessa felt a small proud satisfaction that she too held a red cross in her hand.

But something was new: some of the white figures carried baskets between them. What were baskets for? Tessa had only time to wonder before the band wheeled aside and came straight toward her.

The movement startled her. She trembled with a sense of something holy turned suddenly watchful, like a saint in a picture leaning out of the frame. She saw close to her a round, boyish face, lower than her own, and heard a treble voice say, “Sister, you carry the Anathema about you. Yield it up to the blessed Gesù, and He will adorn you with the gems of His grace.”

She understood nothing. Her mind leapt first to her basket of sweets—were these alarming angels going to take them? She looked down, almost in panic.

“No, sister,” said a taller youth, pointing not at the basket but at her throat and waist. “It is those vanities that are the Anathema. Take off that necklace and unclasp that belt, that they may be burned in the holy Bonfire of Vanities, and save you from burning.”

“It is the truth, my sister,” said one taller still—the archangel of the band. “Listen to these voices speaking the divine message. You already carry a red cross. Let that be your only adornment. Yield up your necklace and belt, and you shall obtain grace.”

A great coldness washed through Tessa. She dared not say no, but she could not give up what she loved—those few bright things that were hers, bought for her by the man she trusted, the man who called her “pigeon” and brought coins for confetti. Her lips quivered; tears rushed into her eyes. The street blurred. She felt only confused terror and misery.

Then a gentle hand touched her arm, and a voice—soft, steady, like cool water over a burn—said, “Do not be afraid; no one shall harm you.”

Tessa looked up. Before her stood a lady in black, with a young face and hazel eyes that held love as if it were a kind of light. Tessa had never seen anyone like her. Under other circumstances she might have been struck dumb with awe, but in that moment awe was swallowed by relief: protection had a face.

The tears fell faster, loosening the knot in her throat. She clutched at her necklace as if it might be snatched away by holiness itself and sobbed, “I can’t give them to be burnt. My husband—he bought them for me—and they are so pretty—and Ninna—oh, I wish I’d never come!”

“Do not ask her for them,” the lady said to the white-robed boys, her tone mild yet carrying authority like a bell carrying over a crowd. “It answers no good end for people to give up such things against their will. That is not what Fra Girolamo approves. He would have such things given up freely.”

The name—Fra Girolamo—moved through the street like a shadow. The boys did not resist. Their train shifted and hurried on, as if drawn by a new prey of ribbons and bright metal further down the way.

Tessa stood trembling, her necklace still warm under her fingers. “Oh, I will go back to the house,” she said, shaken. “I will go nowhere else. But if I should meet them again, and you not be there?” Her voice asked everything of this strange lady, as a child asks bread.

“Stay a little,” the lady said. “Come with me under this doorway, and we will hide the necklace and clasp, and then you will be in no danger.”

She led Tessa beneath an archway where the street’s noise softened, the echoes falling away into quiet. Even the city sounded different there: the holiday clatter dulled to a distant wash, as if thick cloth had been hung between Tessa and fear.

“Now,” said the lady, “can we find room for your necklace and belt in your basket? Ah—your basket is full of crisp things that will break. Let us be careful, and lay the heavy necklace under them.”

Her hands moved with a housekeeper’s gentleness, lifting the light parcels so they did not crack, shifting them so the sugared shells would not crush. The simple domestic care—how she respected the fragile sweetness—felt to Tessa like stepping from nightmare onto firm ground. The lady unfastened the necklace and unclasped the belt while Tessa did nothing but look up into that bending face.

“They are sweets for Lillo and Ninna,” Tessa said, watching her basket as if it were the cradle of all she loved.

“Those are your children?” The lady smiled.

“Yes. And I would rather go home to them than see any more of the Carnival. I shall never like bonfires again.”

“You have not far to go to the Piazza de’ Signori,” the lady said gently, “and there you would see the pile for the great bonfire.”

“No, oh no!” Tessa answered quickly. “I will go back.”

“You live at some castello, doubtless,” the lady said, not waiting for correction. “Towards which gate do you go?”

“Towards Por’ Santa Croce.”

“Come then.” She took Tessa by the hand and led her to a corner opposite, where the street opened into a straighter way. The sounds shifted there: less crowd, more footfall, a dog barking somewhere behind a wall. “If you go down there, you will soon be in a straight road. And I must leave you now, because someone else expects me. You will not be frightened. Your pretty things are safe now. Addio.”

“Addio, Madonna,” Tessa whispered, not knowing what else she should say to such a person.

And in an instant the lady was gone, her tall dark figure gliding away and vanishing round the stonework as if she belonged to another current of the city—one that moved with purpose, not with holiday drift.

Tessa turned to catch one last glimpse and saw only emptiness where the gentle authority had been. Then she began walking home in wonder, longing for Monna Lisa’s familiar deafness, for the kitchen smells of simple cooking, for the soft, everyday noises of children—those small, truthful sounds that could heal the mind better than any procession.

All the while, Baldassarre had kept her in sight—until the moment she parted from the lady in black.

Then he went away with his bundle of yarn, his feet carrying him as if pulled by thought.

He had seen the two wives together. The sight brought vividness to the dim shapes in his mind, as if colour had been laid freshly on a faded fresco. His imagining always needed the senses to stiffen it into certainty.

The tall wife—the noble, rightful one—she had blood in her that could kindle to resentment. She would understand scholarship, he thought, and understand too how learning might be locked away by the wreck of a body like treasure buried by an earthquake. She could believe him. Better: she would be inclined to believe him if he proved that her husband was unfaithful. Women cared about that. They would take vengeance for that.

If she loved him, then injury would flame in her—a second will, a second strength added to his own. He lingered on the thought with keen longing, not seeing—unable to see—how pain might recoil upon innocence. Both wives had been kind to him; their acts, bound up with the image of their faces, had not vanished from his memory. Yet the idea of their suffering did not rise as a restraint. Pain, to him, seemed the order of the world for all except the hard and base.

If any were innocent, if any were noble—where could the utmost gladness lie for them? Where it lay for him: in unconquerable hatred and triumphant vengeance.

But he must be cautious. He must watch the noble wife in the Via de’ Bardi and learn more of her. Even now frustration was possible. There was no power left to him but patience.
