## (Genteel readers are hereby politely encouraged to skip this one, for folks of humbler station step front and center here.)

The evening hung soft and warm, thick with the sultry hum of coming summer heat. Purple glows girdled the sinking sun, those rich, hazy tints of midsummer that feel far more tropical than English, spilling rose-gold light over the sloping hills, gabled cottage fronts, gnarled oak trunks, winding lanes, and rolling pastureland stretching as far as the eye could see. Caroline and Shirley drifted slowly down from the fields, and by the time they reached the lychgate of the churchyard, the last of the bells had fallen silent, the last straggler had slipped through the heavy oak doors, and the whole quiet scene was theirs alone.

“Isn’t it perfectly peaceful?” Caroline breathed.

“Far pleasanter than sweltering inside that packed church,” Shirley laughed. “You know Dr. Boultby will drag on his sermon for an hour and a half, full of fire brimstone about schism, and the curates will hammer their pre-written speeches like they’re nailing planks to a new shed. I for one haven’t the stomach for it.”

“But your uncle will be cross if he notices we’re missing,” Caroline fretted.

“Let me bear the brunt of his temper—he won’t eat me alive. I’ll even miss his sharp little jabs about the Royd Lane battle, and I’ll be sorry you miss Mr. Hall’s warm, sincere homily full of his funny, hearty Yorkshire turns of phrase, but I can’t go in. Look at it: the gray stone church, the grayer old headstones, bathed in this crimson glow—they look like something out of a fairy tale. Nature’s saying her evening prayers right now, kneeling before those red-hued hills. I can almost see her bowed there on the wide stone steps of her altar, praying for calm seas for the sailors out on the ocean, for safe passage for travelers crossing the desert, for the lambs huddled on the moors and the baby birds still unfledged in their nests deep in the woods. She looks exactly like Eve must have looked when she and Adam were the only two people alive on the whole earth.”

“That’s not Milton’s Eve you’re describing, Shirley.”

“Milton’s Eve? Pfft! No, by all that’s holy, she isn’t! Cary, we’re alone here—we can say exactly what we think. Milton was a genius, yes, but was he good? His brain was sharp enough, but what of his heart? He saw heaven, he peered down into hell, he watched Satan and Sin and Death march past him, he saw the ranks of angels with their adamantine shields glinting so bright they seared his blind eyes, he saw the armies of fallen devils trudge by. But when he tried to picture the first woman? He didn’t see her at all.”

“You’re bold to say that,” Caroline chided.

“Bold only to be honest. What he saw was his own cook, or Mrs. Gill fussing over custards on a hot summer day in the cool dairy, rose and nasturtium vines climbing the latticed window, laying out cold treats for the rectors—jams and sweet creams, fussing over what tastes to mix so none of them clash. That’s not Eve.”

“All very charming, that vision, Shirley.”

“Let me remind you: the first people on earth were Titans, and Eve was their mother. From her came Saturn, Hyperion, Oceanus—she was the one who bore Prometheus, who dared steal fire from the heavens. The first woman’s breast that ever heaved with life held the daring to stand against Omnipotence, the strength to bear a thousand years of bondage, the life force that fed that vulture Death for uncounted ages. She was heaven-born. Vast was the heart that spilled the blood of all nations, grand was the unspoiled head that wore the consort’s crown of all creation.”

“But she ate an apple, tricked by a snake!” Caroline protested. “You’ve mixed Scripture and pagan myth all up in your head so no one can make sense of it. You haven’t even told me what that kneeling woman on the hills actually looks like.”

“I see her right now, clear as day: a woman Titan. Her robe of blue air stretches all the way to the edge of the heath, where that flock of sheep is grazing. A veil white as an avalanche falls from her head to her feet, with little flashes of lightning dancing along its borders. Her girdle, wrapped tight under her breast, is purple like that sunset horizon, and the evening star glows right through its rose blush. I can’t even draw her eyes: they’re clear as deep lakes, lifted up in worship, trembling with soft love and the bright light of prayer. Her forehead stretches wide as a summer cloud, paler than the early moon that rose hours before dark. She rests her elbow on the ridge of Stilbro’ Moor, her great hands clasped tight beneath her, kneeling face to face, talking straight to God. That Eve is Jehovah’s daughter, just as Adam was His son.”

“She’s all too vague and dreamy. Come on, Shirley, we really ought to go into church now.”

“I won’t. I’m staying out here with my mother Eve, the one everyone now calls Nature. I love her, that great undying being. Heaven may have faded from her brow when she fell in Paradise, but every glorious thing on earth still shines there. She’s pulling me close to her bosom now, letting me see her heart. Hush, Cary—be quiet a little, and you’ll see her, feel what I feel too.”

“Fine, I’ll humor your whim,” Caroline laughed. “But you’ll be chattering away again before ten minutes are up.”

Shirley leaned against an old upright headstone, caught in that soft warm thrall of the summer evening, eyes fixed on the burning west, drifting off into a pleasant trance. Caroline wandered a little way off, pacing beneath the high rectory garden wall, lost in her own daydreams. Shirley had said the word mother, and the word didn’t summon any grand mystical Titan figure for her—only a soft, gentle human face: the face of the mother she’d never known, the mother she’d ached for her whole life.
*Oh, if only the day would come when she remembers her little girl! If only I could meet her, and love her, and she would love me back.*
The old childish longing that had kept her awake night after night as a small girl, that she’d almost buried for fear it would never come true, flared up warm and bright in her chest: her mother would come one happy day, send for her, look at her with soft loving eyes, and say, *Caroline, my child, I have a home for you. All the love you never got as a little girl, I’ve saved it all for you. Come, and I’ll cherish you.*

The sound of horses’ hooves jolted Caroline out of her daydream, and Shirley out of her vision of the Titan Eve. They listened, and glimpsed a flash of bright scarlet through the oak leaves: six red-coated soldiers rode past quiet as shadows, helmets glinting, plumes bobbing, keeping as low a profile as possible when the whole village was packed inside church.
“It’s the same patrol we saw this afternoon,” Shirley whispered. “They’ve been hiding somewhere, waiting for everyone to go inside to sneak to their meeting spot. Didn’t I tell you we’d start seeing strange things soon?”

Before the last clop of hooves faded down the road, a new noise broke the hush: a small child’s squall, loud and impatient. A man stepped out of the church porch, holding a sturdy, red-faced two-year-old boy in his arms, who was roaring at the top of his lungs, fresh from waking up mid-sermon curled on the pew bench behind his father. Two little girls of nine and ten trundled after him. The fresh cool air and a handful of wild flowers he’d plucked off a grave soon quieted the baby, and the man sat down on the edge of a flat headstone, dandling him on his knee as tenderly as any doting mother, the two little girls perching one on each side.

“Good evening, William,” Shirley said, when she’d recognized him through the dusk. He tipped his hat to them, grinning a pleased smile: it was their old friend William Farren, the gardener, rough-headed, weather-beaten, not old but lined hard by years of work, his clothes clean and decent, his children dressed far neater than most of the village youngsters.

“You two aren’t coming back into church, then?” he asked, gazing at them with a bashful warmth, not out of awe for their higher station, but just because he thought they were kind, young, and lovely. With stuck-up gentry or rude bosses he was stubborn as a stone, but Shirley’s frank, friendly hospitality at Fieldhead had won him over completely, and Caroline he’d known since she was a small child trotting around the village, her soft gentle ways something he loved to look at the same way he loved tending rare roses in his garden. The two girls liked William just as well: they lent him books, gave him cuttings of rare plants, and his conversation was far more interesting to them than half the tedious, pretentious rich folks around who thought they were far above him.

“Who was preaching when you came out, William?” Shirley asked.
“Somebody you don’t care for much, Miss Shirley—Mr. Donne, the curate.”
“You look like you know a secret, William. How’d you guess I don’t like him?”
“There’s a sharp little light in your eyes sometimes that gives you away, miss—you look fair scornful every time he walks in the room.”
“Do you like him yourself, then?”
“Me? I’m sick to death of all the curates, me and my wife both. They’ve no manners at all, talk to poor folks like we’re dirt under their boots, strutting about acting like their little job makes them better than everyone else. I can’t stand that kind of nasty pride.”
“But you’re proud in your own way, aren’t you?” Caroline put in. “You’re proud of your house, keeping everything neat and nice. When you were out of work a few years back, you were too proud to buy anything on credit, I reckon you’d have starved before you went to the parish for help. And do you remember how hard I had to beg you to take that five shillings I tried to give you last winter?”
“Aye, that’s true enough, Miss Caroline. I’d far rather give than take, especially from the likes of you two. You’re a tiny little lass, not even half my age, and I’m a great strong man in the prime of my life. It’s not right that I should be taking favors from you, right? That day you turned up at my door with that five shillings, which I know you could barely afford to spare, I was fair furious. It felt so shameful that I was strong and willing to work, but I was so poor a girl no older than my own daughter thought she had to give me charity. But I forgave you right quick, you meant no harm. That’s clean pride, the Yorkshire sort, the kind those curates know nothing about. Their pride is mucky, ours is honest. I’m raising my girls to be as proud as Miss Shirley, my lads to be as proud as me, and if I ever caught any of them acting as stuck-up as those curates, I’d tan their hide good.”
“What’s the difference between our clean pride and theirs, William?” Shirley asked.
“You know full well, you just want me to run my mouth. Those curates are too proud to ever do a single little thing for themselves, but we’re too proud to ever take a rude word from anyone who thinks they’re better than us.”
“Now tell me the truth, William—are you managing alright these days?”
“Couldn’t be better, Miss Shirley. Ever since Mr. Yorke helped me get that gardening job, and Mr. Hall helped my wife set up that little shop selling bread and sweets, me and mine have plenty to eat, plenty to wear, I’ve got a little money put aside for rainy days, I’d never go crawling to the parish. But the rest of the village? They’re starving. There’s terrible distress out there.”
“Which means there’s discontent, too, I suppose?” Shirley said.
“Exactly. You can’t expect a man who’s gone three days without bread to be calm and content. This whole county’s in a dangerous state, mark my words.”
“What can be done? What more can I do, for example?”
“You can’t do much, poor lass—you’ve given all the money you can spare, that’s more than most rich folks do. Though if you could ship your tenant Mr. Moore off to Botany Bay, that’d make folks a whole lot happier. Everyone hates him.”
“William, that’s a terrible thing to say!” Caroline burst out, warm and indignant. “If people hate him, it’s their fault, not his. He doesn’t hate anyone! He’s just trying to do his duty, keep his mill running, that’s all. You’re wrong to talk like that.”
“I say what I see. That Moore’s got a cold, stone heart.”
“But if you drove him out of the country, burned his mill to the ground,” Shirley cut in, “would that give everyone more work?”
“Less, that’s the truth, I know it, everyone knows it. But there’s hundreds of honest lads so desperate they don’t care what happens, and there’s scoundrels everywhere calling themselves the People’s Friends, that don’t care a jot about the poor, just want to use them as tools to cause trouble. I’ve lived over forty years in this world, and I know real friends of the working man are few and far between. Any rich man who’s nice to you just for political reasons? He’s not to be trusted. I’ve had a few of those offers lately, and I told them where to stick it straight to their faces. No one’s going to make a fool out of me.”

“Aye, and we’ll look out for ourselves too,” a new voice chimed in. Joe Scott, the hard-headed mill overlooker, had sauntered out of the church to get some fresh air, and was standing there dusting off his coat.
“See that you do, Joe,” William laughed.
“And I’ll look out for my master too,” Joe huffed, puffing out his chest, and turned to the two girls. “Young ladies, you’d best go back inside the church right now.”
“Why ever for?” Shirley asked, who was long used to his grumpy, overbearing manners. Joe had very firm opinions that women ought to have nothing to do with mill business, and secretly resented that the master’s property was half under Shirley’s control, that the heiress of Fieldhead could march into the counting house whenever she pleased.
“Because there’s talk going on that’s no fit business for women to be mixed up in. Politics, namely.”
“Politics are our usual pastime, Joe,” Shirley grinned. “I read a newspaper every single day, two on Sundays.”
“I reckon you only read the bits about weddings, murders, and traffic accidents, don’t you?” Joe sniffed.
“I read the leading articles, the foreign news, I even glance over the market prices—all the same stuff the gentlemen read.”
Joe looked at her like she was a chattering magpie, and went silent with disdain.
“Come on, Joe,” Shirley teased. “I can never work out if you’re a Whig or a Tory. Which side are you on?”
“Hard to explain to someone who won’t understand,” he said haughtily. “But I’d as soon be a silly old woman as a Tory. Those Tories are the ones running this stupid war and ruining all our trade. If I’m any party at all, I’m for the side that wants peace, that’s good for the mills and the workers.”
“Well, I’m for peace too, mostly,” Shirley said, enjoying riling him up. “Though I’ve got a soft spot for the farmers too, seeing as most of my income comes from the land around the mill, not the mill itself. We can’t pass laws that hurt the farmers, right?”
“The night dews are bad for women’s health,” Joe said, dodging the point.
“If that’s your excuse to get rid of me, don’t bother. I’m as tough as old boots. I wouldn’t mind taking a turn patrolling the mill some summer night, armed with your musket.”
Joe’s already prominent chin jutted out a good two inches further.
“Back to what I was saying,” Shirley went on, “even as a mill owner, I can’t help thinking that us business folks are a little bit selfish, a little short-sighted, too busy chasing profit to care about the suffering of the people who work for us. Don’t you agree?”
“I won’t argue with someone who can’t possibly understand,” he snapped.
“Your master argues with me about it all the time, Joe. He’s not as stubborn as you are.”
“We’ve all got our ways,” Joe shrugged.
“Do you seriously think all the wisdom in the whole world is locked up in men’s skulls?”
“I think women are a flighty, stubborn generation. And I follow the good doctrine in the second chapter of Paul’s first letter to Timothy.”
“What doctrine, Joe?”
“‘Let the woman learn in silence, with all subjection. I suffer not a woman to teach, nor to usurp authority over the man, but to be in silence. For Adam was first formed, then Eve.’”
“That’s just primogeniture nonsense!” Shirley cried. “I’m going to quote that to Mr. Yorke next time he rants against inherited privileges.”
“And Adam wasn’t the one tricked by the snake,” Joe continued. “The woman was the one who sinned first.”
“More shame to Adam for sinning with his eyes wide open!” Shirley laughed. “I’ve never been able to make sense of that chapter, for the life of me.”
“It’s plain as day, miss. Anyone can read it.”
“People can read whatever they want into it, though,” Caroline put in. “You believe in the right of private judgment, don’t you, Joe?”
“Course I do—for every line of the Bible.”
“Then why can’t women exercise it the same as men?”
“Nah. Women ought to just take their husband’s opinion for politics and religion. It’s healthier for them.”
“Oh, rubbish!” Shirley and Caroline both exclaimed at the same time.
