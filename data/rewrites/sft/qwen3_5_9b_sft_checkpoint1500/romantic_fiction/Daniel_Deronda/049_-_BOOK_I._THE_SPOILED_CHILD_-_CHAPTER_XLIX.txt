Within the quiet corridors of his own spirit, a vast and unyielding justice reigns supreme. It is the kind of grace that allows gratitude to bloom even where resentment once festered, a testament to souls born of nobler blood and broader capacity for joy. These are not the clever minds that twist truth until goodness dissolves into mush, mere excuses for "thank you" that mask a selfish heart; no, they are the devoted servants who, though bound by vows of poverty in love and trust, still beg from a world that hoards its humanity.

As Daniel parted from Gwendolen, he carefully withheld the heavy truth of his departure. To say, "I shall not see you again for a long time," would have given Lord Grandcourt the satisfaction of knowing it mattered deeply to her. Instead, he left her with a polite distance, masking the storm within.

Yet, fate was already turning. Daniel was leaving soon enough, driven by circumstances so profound that his return to call upon Gwendolen felt like a step back into a dream already fading. As he prepared to keep his word, a solemn emotion settled over him, reviving the deepest currents of his existence.

Sir Hugo had summoned him, sending a note that cut through the air like a bell: *"Come immediately. Something has happened."* When Daniel entered the baronet's study, expecting a scene of distress, he found instead a grave affection. Sir Hugo received him with a warmth that belied the weight in the room.

"It is nothing to grieve you, sir?" Daniel asked, his voice steady, taking the hand extended to him. He felt a sudden surge of restored confidence, yet he saw an unusual depth in Sir Hugo's eyes, a suppressed tremor in his throat.

"No, Dan, no," Sir Hugo replied softly. "Sit down. I have something to say."

Daniel obeyed, sensing the gravity of the moment. It was rare for Sir Hugo to wear such serious feeling, a somber mask that spoke of a secret finally breaking open.

"Not to grieve me, my boy, no," Sir Hugo continued, his gaze searching Daniel's face. "At least, if there is nothing in it that will grieve you too much. But I hardly expected that *this*—just *this*—would ever come to pass. There have been reasons why I have never prepared you for it. There have been reasons why I have never told you anything about your parentage. But I have striven in every way not to make that an injury to you."

Sir Hugo paused, the air thick with unspoken history. Daniel sat silent, unable to speak. He could not say, "I have never felt it an injury," because those words felt false in the light of this revelation. Far more hung in the balance than anyone else could know. Sir Hugo had never seen the great face he loved so dearly grow so pale, nor the lips pressed together with such a look of pain. With a tenderness that bordered on anxiety, fearing he might wound the boy further, he reached into his pocket.

"I have acted in obedience to your mother's wishes," Sir Hugo whispered. "The secrecy was her wish. But now she desires to remove it. She desires to see you. I will put this letter into your hands, which you can look at by-and-by. It will merely tell you what she wishes you to do, and where you will find her."

A letter, written on foreign paper, was placed in Daniel's palm. He thrust it into his breast pocket, grateful for the reprieve from reading it immediately. The emotion on Daniel's face had grown, shaking Sir Hugo's composure. The baronet struggled to find words, while Daniel's entire soul was consumed by a single, terrifying question—the hardest in the world to utter.

And yet, he could not bear to delay. This was a sacramental hour; if he let it slip, he could never again stand under these influences and hear the answer. For several moments, their eyes were cast downward, and it seemed as though invisible threads of thought wove between them. Finally, Daniel lifted his gaze, speaking with a tremulous reverence, dreading the indirect reproach that years of stifled affection might carry.

"Is my father also living?"

The answer came instantly, low and emphatic. "No."

Joy and pain mingled in the aftermath, indistinguishable, swirling like a tempest in the small room. A new light had fallen upon the past for Sir Hugo as well. After a silence so deep it felt like standing on the edge of a new faith, the baronet spoke in a tone of confession.

"Perhaps I was wrong, Dan, to undertake what I did. And perhaps I liked it a little too well—having you all to myself. But if you have had any pain which I might have helped, I ask you to forgive me."

"The forgiveness has long been there," Daniel answered gently. "The chief pain has always been on account of someone else—someone whom I never knew, whom I am now to know. It has not hindered me from feeling an affection for you which has made a large part of all the life I remember."

Seized by a singular, powerful impulse, the two men clasped hands, holding onto the fragile bridge of understanding they had just built across the chasm of secrets.
