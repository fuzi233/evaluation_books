The morning sky hung pale and grey, but the overcast light barely touched the vibrancy of the Piazza del Duomo, strung from spire to arch with a holiday canopy of deep blue drapery, dotted with constellations of golden lilies and blazoned noble coats of arms. sheaves of banners fluttered unfurled from the Baptistery’s corners, though the marble steps of the great cathedral were not yet carpeted for the upcoming royal procession: they bore the tread of hundreds of ordinary citizens hurrying to and fro, no velvet slippers or ceremonial guards among them. It was the hour of the great Advent sermons, and the same political fervor that had draped the streets in festive colour made the preaching inside the Duomo a necessity no devout or wary Florentine would dare skip.

Yet not all feet in the piazza turned toward the cathedral steps. Patricians and artisans alike moved at the brisk, purposeful pace of men on urgent errands, and clusters of talkers dotted the open space, some lingering late for the sermon by choice, others content to miss it entirely. No faces wore the lazy softness of a crowd enjoying an idle holiday. Small groups huddled in tight, fierce discussion; listeners leaned in to catch every word from a single speaker, only to snap their heads up and scan every passing stranger as if half-expected a blow to fall at any moment.

At the corner where the Piazza’s artificial rainbow glow cut off, the raw grey daylight fell full on the sombre stone facades of the Via de’ Cerretani, where a thick cluster of working people had gathered. Most wore the marks of their daily labour on their clothes and skin, and nearly every man carried a tool or weapon he could use at a moment’s notice: bare brawny arms, stained work garments, they stood sharply contrasted against the bright, stagy pomp of the piazza behind them, listening to the thin, sharp-tongued notary Ser Cioni. Barely two and a half years prior, his bitter speeches had been laughed to scorn in the Mercato, but now he spoke with the easy, complacent confidence of a man whose faction held all the power, and who knew his voice carried weight with the crowd.

“Never dare prattle to me about bloodthirsty Swiss pikemen or ferocious French infantry,” he was saying, his incisive voice cutting through the murmur. “They might as well be trapped in a mountain pass as in our Florentine streets. Peasants have cut down the finest condottieri armies in our history once they herded them between precipices. I tell you: Florentines have no reason to fear any foreign army inside their own walls.”

“True enough, Ser Cioni!” called a man whose hands and arms were crusted deep with crimson dye that looked exactly like fresh dried blood, a small hatchet tucked into his belt. “Those French cavaliers that swaggered through the other day, preening in their fancy doublets? I saw the lot of them strutting down Ognissanti, squinting up at the Vespucci and Agli palaces like they thought our homes were not fit lodging for their fine selves, eyeing us Florentines like top-knotted game cocks pitying us for not knowing how to strut. ‘Aye, my fine Galli,’ thinks I, ‘stretch out those bellies all you like. The meat axe in my belt will slip right through them all the easier.’ Then the old cow lowed* — you all know what that means, the great Palazzo Vecchio bell — and I knew trouble was afoot, no matter what. I dumped my armful of dyed cloth in the nearest doorway, grabbed my axe, and chased after those fine cavaliers towards Vigna Nuova. Guccio came running up beside me, said ‘I think Piero de’ Medici is marching back.’ Bembè! I knew it! We threw up a barricade right there, the Frenchmen turned around and realized they were trapped, and a swarm of our Ciompi** came surging up after them. One wool-beater with a scythe in his hand cut the feather clean off the leading cavalier’s hat! It’s no lie! The lasses up in the windows pelted them with pebbles to scare them off. Turned out Piero wasn’t coming after all, and more’s the pity. We’d have left him no legs to run away on, no wings to fly with.”

* The local phrase for the sounding of the great Palazzo Vecchio alarm bell.
** The poorer wool artisans: beaters, carders, washers, the core of the 14th century Ciompi rebellion.

“Well said, Oddo!” a young butcher called out, his own sharp knife sheathed at his belt. “I say Piero will think twice before he dares show his face here again. He looked as scared as a hunted chicken the last time we hustled and pelted him in the piazza. The coward could have made a proper stand with all his horsemen at his back, but he ran. We won’t swallow any more Medici tyrants, no matter what the French king tries to force down our throats.”

“But I don’t like talk of those French cannons they’re hauling south,” whimpered Goro, no less round and jolly for two years of political grievances. “San Giovanni defend us! If the Frate’s right and the Lord loves Florence, why couldn’t He have sent the French a different road to Naples?”

“Thou’ve a better head on thee than I thought, Goro,” the dyer Oddo laughed, clapping him on the back. “Why couldn’t they have gone through Bologna? Or Arezzo? We wouldn’t have minded them tramping through Arezzo a bit.”

“Fools, all of you,” Ser Cioni huffed, about to launch into a speech about how the French presence would bring Florence nothing but glory — when a dozen voices cried out “Look there!” and every head turned down the length of the Via de’ Cerretani.

“It’s Lorenzo Tornabuoni, and one of those French noblemen staying in his household,” Ser Cioni said, with clear contempt at the interruption. “That deep, smooth Tornabuoni pretends to be all loyal to the popular government, but he’s a Medicean at heart. Mark my words.”

The approaching party made a glittering show. There was Tornabuoni, patrician and smooth courtier; the French nobleman in finery stitched with gold thread, his starched white linen peeking out ostentatiously at cuffs and collar; two more wealthy young Florentines in new dresses made for the upcoming royal procession. Walking on the Frenchman’s left, though, was a figure no amount of brocade could outshine: Tito Melema, dressed all in plain black for mourning, soon to don the bright red mantle marking his official post as Latin Secretary to the Ten. His fluency in French and deep knowledge of southern Italian customs had made him indispensable in all dealings with the French forces, and he had already made multiple trips out to the French camp at Signa. Fortune shone bright on him still, he smiled and listened and explained with his usual easy, unassuming grace — only a very keen, watchful eye would have spotted the subtle shift in him, the change that comes when the last trace of moral youth melts away, and a man deliberately, consciously chooses the life he will lead. His face was still soft, his eyes still clear as spring water, but something intangible was gone: the faint uncertainty, the twilight of a man still half undecided.

The French nobleman was collecting last instructions for the royal parade before riding back to Signa, and the whole party had come to survey the Piazza del Duomo, where the king’s procession was meant to pause for a special religious ceremony. Every eye turned to them as they stepped out of the street’s shadow into the piazza’s bright drapery light — but the stares were far from all admiring. Jokes circulated about the Frenchman’s oddly shaped, pointed “hoof-toe” shoes, made to flatter the king’s absurd excessive foot size, and no one bothered to mumble quiet snarls about “Medicean lackeys” out of earshot. But Tornabuoni had perfected the art of hiding irritation to court public favour, and Tito, besides his natural instinct to disarm ill will with easy good humour, carried the detached, unimpassioned air of an alien who could not feel the burning, personal fury such names roused in native Florentines.

They paused where they could get a good clear view of the Duomo’s main doorway, to debate whether the festoons and painted crests hung there suited the royal ceremonial. Tornabuoni waved over Piero di Cosimo the painter, who as usual at that hour was loitering outside Nello the barber’s shop. A lively, funny debate broke out, stoked by the French nobleman’s utterly bewildered reaction to Piero’s dry, sharp observations — which Tito translated word for word, to everyone’s delight. Even the snarling, suspicious onlookers grew curious, their faces softening into that half-amused, half-embarrassed smile of people who can’t quite hear the joke that has everyone else cackling. It was a perfect moment for Tito: he was the only person in the whole party who could have pulled off such a funny, seamless translation, and he basked unselfconsciously in the approving glances, as if he was soaking in warm sun. The piazza’s rainbow light wrapped around the laughing group, and all the solemn sermon-goers had already vanished inside the Duomo’s dark doors. For a minute, it truly felt like the city’s decorations were for a real, uncomplicated Florentine holiday.

But out in the grey, unadorned side streets far from the piazza’s glow, other travellers were making their way towards the centre of the city, in no festive mood. French soldiers in their distinctive uniforms herded three ragged men before them, growing more and more surrounded by a crowd of angry, unadmiring Florentines. The three prisoners wore almost nothing but rags. Their hands were bound tight together with cord, and a thick rope looped around each man’s neck and torso, so the soldier holding the other end could throttle them in an instant if they tried to run.

The soldiers prodded their prisoners forward with the knotted ends of their ropes, barking broken Italian orders to make them beg. Two of the younger, sturdier prisoners obeyed, holding out their bound hands to every Florentine they passed and whimpering piteously: “For the love of God and the Holy Madonna, give us a coin towards our ransom! We are Tuscans, captured fighting in Lunigiana!”

But the third old man refused to speak a single word, no matter how hard the soldiers struck him. He looked nothing like his two fellow prisoners, who were young, hardy, and looked like ordinary coarse beggars in the rags their captors had left them. He was well over sixty, his long neglected beard and the thick hair falling around his bald crown nearly all white. His broad, thickset frame was still firm and upright even starved thin, radiating stubborn strength well into old age — a quality reflected in his dark eyes and heavy dark eyebrows, whose vivid colour stood out shockingly against his yellow, bloodless, deeply wrinkled face. Yet there was something fitful, wandering in his gaze, even when a flash of sharp ferocity lit up as he scanned every face and window: his lips stayed clamped tight, his bound hands hung resolutely at his sides. He would not beg.

The sight had stoked the watching Florentines into slow, searing exasperation. At first many had pressed coins into the prisoners’ hands half-automatically, moved by the appeal to God, or cowed by rumours of the French soldiery’s brutal campaigns. But the further the sorry procession penetrated into the heart of the city, the thinner that compliance grew, until the soldiers found themselves surrounded by a shouting, jostling throng of men and boys, whose jeers were easy enough for any foreigner to understand without a translator. The soldiers began to fear for their position: they ached to draw their pikes and strike out, but they could not let go of the ropes tied to their prisoners’ necks, and they hurried along as fast as they could, hoping to make it to the nearest friendly hostelry for shelter.

“French dogs!” “Bullock feet!” “Snatch their pikes from them!” “Cut the prisoners’ cords and see how fast those web-footed French curs run!” the crowd yelled. Everyone seemed happy to hurl insults, but no one dared make the first move — until the dyer Oddo spotted the commotion, understood what was happening, and roared “Santiddio! What a shameful sight! And you fools just hoot at them? Come on!” He snatched his axe from his belt and sprinted into the crowd, the butcher and all his fellow artisans at his heels — only Goro turned tail and scurried down a narrow alley to hide.

Oddo, his red-stained arms flying, his axe held high, was like a waving battle standard to the crowd. No sooner had he pushed through to the front of the throng than the impish young apprentice Lollo, who worked for the local conjuror, danced forward before anyone could stop him. He had no particular pity for the prisoners, but he carried a sharp little knife of his own, and it struck him that slicing a prisoner’s rope and darting back before the guarding soldier could react would be the funniest, most dexterous trick he had ever pulled. The crowd was jostling so hard no one noticed him slip right up next to the oldest prisoner — in a single flash, he sliced clean through the neck rope. “Run, old man!” he piped in the prisoner’s ear, then darted off as fast as a spooked fowl.

The old man did not hesitate. The thought of escape had never left his mind, and the crowd’s obvious anger at his captors had given him fresh hope. He bolted at once, and the Florentines surged forward between him and his guard, blocking the soldier from grabbing him. The other two prisoners were freed in the chaos seconds later, and the soldiers stumbled after them, tripping over the cobblestones in their awkward hoof-shaped shoes, slowed by the crowd but not properly attacked. One young prisoner turned up the Borgo di San Lorenzo and drew half the chase off with him, but the main surge ran straight towards the Duomo piazza.

“A prisoner escape!” Lorenzo Tornabuoni exclaimed, as his party turned around just beside the cathedral steps to see the ragged old man dash past. “The mob wasn’t content with emptying the Bargello the other day. If no proper authority is in sight, they’ll attack the guards and free every thief in the city. Ah — there’s a French soldier. That is far more serious.”

The French soldier Tornabuoni spotted was struggling along the north edge of the piazza, but the old prisoner he was chasing had run the other way. Baldassarre Calvo had wheeled around the Baptistery and was running straight for the Duomo steps, determined to claim the sanctuary of the church itself rather than trust his thin legs to outrun the soldiers. But his foot caught on a cracked marble step, and he stumbled, pitching forward straight at the group of signori standing with their backs turned to him. He caught himself only by grabbing tight at the nearest man’s arm.

It was Tito Melema who felt that iron, calloused grip on his velvet sleeve. He turned his head, and the face of his long-lost adoptive father was inches from his own. The two men froze, dead silent. Baldassarre’s dark, haggard face burned with unmistakeable, ferocious rage, his bound hands tightening so hard on Tito’s arm his knuckles went white. Tito’s own face drained of all colour, his lips bloodless, his whole body locked stiff with the kind of terror that paralyses a man where he stands. To them it felt like an eternity — in truth, it was only a single heartbeat.

The first sound Tito heard was Piero di Cosimo’s short, dry laugh, right beside his shoulder. “Ha, ha! Now I know exactly what a ghost is supposed to look like.”

“This is another escaped prisoner,” Lorenzo Tornabuoni said, frowning. “Who is he, I wonder?”

“Some madman, surely,” Tito said.

He hardly knew how the words had escaped his lips. There are moments when our deepest unspoken passions speak for us, when we stand outside ourselves watching, shocked at what we have done. The words carried the sharp, sudden inspiration of a crime he had never dared premeditate, made real in a single instant. For a split second it felt to Tito as if some dark magical poison had leapt straight from Baldassarre’s blazing eyes into his own veins, running hot through his whole body. Then the iron grip on his arm slackened, and Baldassarre stumbled past him, vanishing into the dark, shadowed doors of the Duomo.
