The Reverend Septimus Crisparkle—Septimus, because six younger brothers had preceded him, each vanishing at birth like a flickering candle—had just broken the thin morning ice near Cloisterham Weir with his head, an act that invigorated his blood and set his spirits alight. Now, he stood before a mirror, boxing with methodical precision, gloves on, fists flying in practiced rhythm. The glass reflected not just a man, but a vision of disciplined energy: lean, alert, every movement sharp and deliberate. His face, though serious, carried a quiet warmth, and his eyes sparkled with the kind of vigor that came from both physical exertion and moral purpose.

It was still early. Mrs. Crisparkle—his mother, not wife—had only just descended the stairs, waiting for the tea urn to be ready. At that moment, Septimus paused, gently cupped her face between his gloved hands, and kissed her cheek. Then, as if nothing had happened, he resumed his bout, feinting left, jabbing right, moving with the grace of a seasoned boxer.

“I say,” remarked the old lady, watching him with fond amusement, “every morning of your life, you’ll do it, Sept.”

“Do what, Ma dear?”

“Break the pier-glass or burst a blood-vessel.”

“Neither, please God,” he replied, grinning. “Here’s wind, Ma. Look at this!”

In a final flurry of motion, he landed blows and dodged them with equal skill, culminating in a light tap that sent her cap into Chancery—a term used by experts in the noble art to describe a perfectly executed maneuver. With gentle humor, he released her, quickly stowed his gloves, and turned toward the window, pretending to gaze thoughtfully outside, just as a servant entered.

Breakfast preparations followed. Once seated, the two were alone again. It was a scene worth witnessing—if anyone had been there to see it. Mrs. Crisparkle stood, reciting the Lord’s Prayer aloud, while her son, though nearing forty, bowed his head in reverence, just as he had done when he was four. There was something deeply touching about the ritual, especially given the quiet dignity of the room and the serene presence of the woman who had raised him.

She was no ordinary woman. Her figure was trim, her movements graceful, her dress delicate—like a porcelain shepherdess, painted in soft pastels, lavender and cherry ribbons adorning her cap. Her eyes, clear and bright, never needed spectacles. And her voice, when she spoke, carried a quiet strength that softened even the most rigid soul.

“My Sept!” she would often say—those two words forming the core of her thoughts, her affection, her entire world.

They sat together in Minor Canon Corner, a quiet retreat nestled beneath the shadow of the Cathedral. The place was alive with echoes: rooks cawing in the trees, distant footsteps, the tolling bell, the deep hum of the organ. Yet none of these sounds disturbed the peace. Time seemed to slow here, as if the weight of centuries had settled into stillness. Once, men had fought and raged here; serfs had labored and died; monks had prayed and plotted. But now, all that was gone. Only tranquility remained—and with it, a peculiar kind of melancholy beauty, born of stories told and lives lived out.

Red-brick walls, ivy-clad, latticed windows, panelled rooms, oak beams, and stone gardens where fruit still ripened on ancient monkish trees—these were the surroundings of the Crisparkles’ modest home.

“And what does the letter say, Ma dear?” Septimus asked, cutting into his toast with hearty appetite.

Mrs. Crisparkle had just finished reading it and placed it carefully on the tablecloth. She handed it to him.

Now, here was a curious thing: the old lady prided herself on her sharp eyes, which allowed her to read without glasses. Her son, equally proud of her ability, had invented a pretense—that he himself could not read without spectacles. So, he put on a pair so large and thick they nearly obscured half his face, making eating difficult and reading nearly impossible.

He squinted through them. “‘Haven of Philanthropy, Chief Offices, London, Wednesday.’ ‘Dear Madam—’ I write in the—what’s this? What does he write *in*?”

“In the chair,” said Mrs. Crisparkle.

Septimus removed the glasses, peering at her. “Why, what should he write *in*?”

“Bless me, Sept,” she said, “you don’t see the context! Give it back.”

Gladly, he did. “My sight for manuscript is getting worse every day,” he muttered, rubbing his ear.

She took up the letter again. “‘I write from the chair, to which I shall probably be confined for some hours.’”

Septimus glanced at the row of chairs against the wall, looking mildly exasperated. “Ah. That makes sense.”

“‘We have a meeting of our Convened Chief Composite Committee of Central and District Philanthropists at our Head Haven as above,’”—she read with slight emphasis—“‘and it is their unanimous pleasure that I take the chair.’”

Septimus sighed in relief. “O! If he comes to *that*, let him.”

“‘Not to lose a day’s post, I take the opportunity of a long report being read, denouncing a public miscreant—’”

“It is extraordinary,” interrupted Septimus, setting down his knife and fork, “that these philanthropists are always denouncing someone. And even more extraordinary that they’re always so overflowing with miscreants!”

“‘Denouncing a public miscreant—’”—she continued—“‘to get our little affair of business off my mind. I have spoken with my two wards, Neville and Helena Landless, on the subject of their defective education, and they give in to the plan proposed; as I should have taken good care they did, whether they liked it or not.’”

“And another extraordinary thing,” Septimus added, “is how these philanthropists seem to grab people by the scruff of the neck and shove them into paths of peace.” He paused. “I beg your pardon, Ma dear, for interrupting.”

“‘Therefore, dear Madam, you will please prepare your son, the Rev. Mr. Septimus, to expect Neville as an inmate to be read with, on Monday next. On the same day, Helena will accompany him to Cloisterham, to take up her quarters at the Nuns’ House, the establishment recommended by yourself and son jointly. Please likewise to prepare for her reception and tuition there. The terms in both cases are understood to be exactly as stated to me in writing by yourself, when I opened a correspondence with you on this subject, after the honour of being introduced to you at your sister’s house in town here. With compliments to the Rev. Mr. Septimus, I am, Dear Madam, Your affectionate brother (In Philanthropy), LUKE HONEYTHUNDER.’”

Septimus leaned back. “Well, Ma,” he said after a moment, “we must try it. We’ve room for an inmate, I have time, and inclination too. I confess I’m glad it’s not Mr. Honeythunder himself. Though that seems prejudiced—doesn’t it?—for I’ve never seen him. Is he a large man, Ma?”

“I’d call him large,” she replied slowly, “but his voice is much larger.”

“Than himself?”

“Than anybody.”

“Hah!” said Septimus, finishing his breakfast with less enthusiasm than before.

Mrs. Crisparkle’s sister, a woman of equal refinement and charm, was the childless wife of a clergyman in London. It was during one of her annual visits that Mr. Honeythunder, in his public role as Professor of Philanthropy, had met Mrs. Crisparkle at a charitable event involving orphaned children fed plum buns and overindulged in festive cheer. That was the extent of their acquaintance.

“Of course,” said Septimus, after a pause, “the first thing is to make these young people feel at ease. It’s not disinterested—because we can’t be comfortable unless they are. Jasper’s nephew is here now. Youth takes to youth. He’s a lively fellow. Let’s invite him to meet them at dinner. That’s three. Can’t ask him without Jasper. That’s four. Add Miss Twinkleton and the bride-to-be—six. Add us—eight. Would eight at dinner put you out, Ma?”

“Nine would,” she said, visibly nervous.

“I said eight,” he reminded her gently.

“The size of the table and the room, my dear.”

So it was decided. When Septimus called on Miss Twinkleton to arrange Helena’s stay at the Nuns’ House, the invitations were extended and accepted. Miss Twinkleton briefly regretted leaving behind her globes, but soon relented.

Instructions were sent to Mr. Honeythunder regarding the arrival time of the two pupils, ensuring they reached Cloisterham in time for dinner. Soup stock began to simmer in the kitchen.

Back then, there was no railway to Cloisterham. Mr. Sapsea insisted there never would be—and indeed, he declared there never should be. Yet today, express trains race through the town without stopping, scattering dust as proof of its insignificance. A fragment of the main line once threatened to ruin the money market if it failed, and Church and State if it succeeded—but even that had disrupted traffic enough to force travelers onto a back route marked: “Beware of the Dog.”

To this unlikely path, Septimus walked, awaiting the daily omnibus—a short, squat vehicle piled high with luggage, like a tiny elephant burdened with a castle. As it approached, he barely saw it for the massive passenger seated on the box, elbows squared, hands on knees, pressing the driver into a cramped space, glaring about with a stern, angular face.

“Is this Cloisterham?” the man demanded in a thunderous voice.

“It is,” replied the driver, rubbing his back as if sore, then tossing the reins to the ostler.

“And I never was so glad to see it.”

“Tell your master to widen the box-seat,” the passenger snapped. “Your master is morally bound—and ought to be legally required—to ensure the comfort of his fellow-man.”

The driver ran his hands over his spine as if checking for damage. “Have I sat upon you?” the passenger asked.

“You have,” the driver said flatly.

“Take this card, my friend.”

“I think I won’t deprive you of it,” the driver replied, glancing at it coldly. “What’s the good of it to me?”

“Be a Member of that Society,” the man said.

“What shall I get by it?”

“Brotherhood,” he answered fiercely.

“Thankee,” said the driver slowly. “My mother was content with myself, and so am I. I don’t want no brothers.”

“But you must have them,” the passenger replied, stepping down. “Whether you like it or not. I am your brother.”

“I say!” the driver protested, growing agitated. “Not too far! The worm *will*, when—”

But Septimus stepped in. “Joe, Joe, Joe! Don’t forget yourself, Joe, my good fellow!”

Joe touched his hat, subdued. Septimus then addressed the passenger: “Mr. Honeythunder?”

“That is my name, sir.”

“My name is Crisparkle.”

“Reverend Mr. Septimus? Glad to see you, sir. Neville and Helena are inside. Having recently succumbed under the pressure of my public labours, I thought I’d take a breath of fresh air and come down with them, returning tonight. So you’re the Reverend Mr. Septimus, are you?” He studied Septimus with a look of mild disappointment, twisting a double eye-glass by its ribbon as if warming it.

“Hah! I expected to see you older, sir.”

“I hope you will,” Septimus replied, smiling.

“Eh?”

“Only a poor little joke. Not worth repeating.”

“Joke? Ay; I never see a joke,” Mr. Honeythunder retorted, frowning. “A joke is wasted upon me, sir. Where are they? Helena and Neville, come here! Mr. Crisparkle has come to meet you.”

From the omnibus emerged a striking young man and woman—both unusually handsome, lithe, dark-haired, rich in color. The girl bore a gipsy-like quality, wild yet poised. Both carried an air of defiance and mystery, like hunters—or perhaps hunted. Slender, quick-eyed, half-shy, half-challenging, their expressions held a strange pause, as if caught between flight and attack.

Septimus made mental notes: _Unusually attractive. Wild. Alert. Like captives from a tropical land._ He invited Mr. Honeythunder to dinner—though with unease, knowing the effect such a man might have on his mother.

As they walked through the ancient streets, the siblings marveled at the Cathedral and the ruins of the Monastery, their wonder genuine, almost foreign. Mr. Honeythunder, meanwhile, strode down the middle of the road, brushing aside pedestrians, loudly outlining a scheme to arrest every unemployed person in the UK, imprison them, and force them into philanthropy under threat of death.

Mrs. Crisparkle, already overwhelmed, found herself facing a monstrous intrusion. Mr. Honeythunder was not merely loud—he was a blight on civility. A boil on society’s face, he became a raging wen in Minor Canon Corner. His philanthropy was explosive, almost violent. It wasn’t kindness—it was conquest. He demanded obedience, conformity, submission. You abolished war—but only after arresting every military officer who had served honorably. You abolished capital punishment—but only after executing all those who supported it. You sought peace—but only by eliminating anyone who refused to join your cause.

Above all, you could not act alone. You had to register at the Haven of Philanthropy, pay dues, wear the badge, and speak only what the committee dictated. Their resolutions always ended with: “That this assembled Body views with indignant scorn and contempt, not unmixed with utter detestation and loathing abhorrence…”—followed by a list of sins committed by non-members.

Dinner was a disaster. Mr. Honeythunder dominated the table, blocking service, shouting over everyone, treating the guests like a mob. He seized Septimus as a target, accusing him of fraud, hypocrisy, cowardice—though the man had said nothing. The minor canon looked bewildered, then indignant. Mrs. Crisparkle bristled, tears welling. The others froze, speechless.

Yet when it came time for Mr. Honeythunder to leave, the mood shifted. Coffee arrived an hour early. Septimus checked his watch constantly. The young people swore the clock struck three-quarters when it struck one. Miss Twinkleton claimed the walk to the omnibus took twenty-five minutes when it was five. Everyone rushed him into his coat, shoved him into the moonlight as if he were a fugitive being sympathetically escorted. Septimus and Neville practically locked the door behind him, leaving him with half an hour to spare—just in case he caught cold.
