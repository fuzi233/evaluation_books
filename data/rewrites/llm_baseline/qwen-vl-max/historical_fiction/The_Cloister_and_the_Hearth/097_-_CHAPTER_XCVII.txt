A priest is never more truly a priest than in the chamber of death. Gerard performed the final rites of the Church for the departed with solemn precision, as he would have done for any humble soul in his parish. His movements were automatic, his voice steady—yet beneath the ritual lay a storm unspoken. He knelt afterward, not from duty, but from awe, and offered thanks to Heaven for her peaceful passing, her end marked by faith and grace. Then, all thought turned to one thing: to remove her from strangers’ hands and lay her to rest within the sacred ground of his own churchyard.

That very evening, a single-horse cart, draped in black cloth, set out for Gouda. Inside rested a coffin—and beside it, Gerard, broken and silent, his arms wrapped around the cold lead, his chin pressed against it as if clinging to the last trace of her presence. The energy that had carried him through the preparations had spent itself; now, he was crushed, immobile, bound to the casket like a man chained to sorrow.

The driver of the cart walked ahead of the horse, speaking not a word. When he stopped to feed the animal, he did so in hushed tones, mindful of the grief that hung over the mourner like a shroud. At nightfall, they reached an inn, where landlord and driver, moved by compassion, tried gently to coax Gerard inside for food and rest. But he resisted fiercely—his eyes wild, his voice low and fierce, like a dog guarding its master’s grave. They gave up, drew a cloak over him, and left him alone in the darkness, still clutching the coffin as if life itself depended on it.

At noon, the cart arrived at the manse. A dozen parishioners stood gathered, each bearing their own small sorrows, but all already feeling the absence of their parson. When they saw the cart and recognized Gerard—so pale, so shattered—they let out a collective cry of grief. It stirred him from his numbness. He looked up, saw their faces, and tried to speak.

“Oh, my children… my children!” he gasped, but the words choked in his throat, and no more came.

Yet the next day, despite warnings and pleas, he insisted on conducting the burial himself. Standing at her grave, he read the service with voice only faintly trembling. Tears fell freely upon the earth, some from him, many from those who loved her. When the prayers ended, he remained motionless, rooted to the spot like a statue carved from sorrow. The people stepped back, respectful, leaving him to his solitude.

Then, without warning, the sexton—simple-minded, thoughtless—began filling the grave, the clods of earth falling softly but steadily onto the coffin. At the first sound, Gerard screamed—a raw, piercing cry that tore through the quiet air.

The sexton froze. Gerard staggered, hand flying to his chest. “Something snapped,” he whispered, breathless. “I felt it… I heard it… here…” And he pointed, trembling, to his heart.

Jorian Ketel, standing nearby, mourning the woman who had been kind to him, rushed forward. Together, he and the sexton supported Gerard, guiding him slowly into the manse. There, half-swooning, he leaned against the wall, eyes wide, whispering once more: “Ah, Jorian… good Jorian… something broke inside me… I felt it… here…”
