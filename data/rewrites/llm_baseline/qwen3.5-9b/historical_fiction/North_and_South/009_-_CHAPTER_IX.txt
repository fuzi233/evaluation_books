"Let China's earth, enriched with coloured stains, / Pencil'd with gold, and streaked with azure veins, / The grateful flavour of the Indian leaf, / Or Mocha's sunburnt berry glad receive." —Mrs. Barbauld.

On the day following his encounter with Higgins and his daughter, Mr. Hale ascended the stairs to the small drawing-room at an unusually late hour. He approached various objects within the room, inspecting them with a deliberate air, yet Margaret perceived that this was merely a nervous habit—a tactic to delay speaking words he desired to utter but dreaded to voice. Finally, the matter emerged.

"My dear! I have invited Mr. Thornton to tea tonight."

Mrs. Hale reclined in her easy chair, her eyes closed, wearing an expression of habitual pain. Yet, at her husband's announcement, she stirred into querulousness. "Mr. Thornton—and tonight! What on earth does that man desire to come here for? Besides, Dixon is currently washing my muslins and laces, and there is no soft water amidst these dreadful eastern winds, which I presume we shall endure throughout the year in Milton."

"The wind is shifting, my dear," Mr. Hale replied, gazing out at the smoke drifting directly from the east. Though he did not yet fully grasp the cardinal points, he adjusted them somewhat arbitrarily to suit the moment.

"Don't tell me!" Mrs. Hale shuddered, tightening her shawl further around herself. "Be it east or west, however, I suppose this man must come."

"Oh, Mother, that proves you have never truly observed Mr. Thornton. He possesses the constitution of someone who enjoys battling every adversity life presents—be it enemies, winds, or circumstances. The harder it rains and blows, the more certain we are to find him. However, I shall assist Dixon. I am becoming quite renowned as a clear-starcher. Furthermore, he requires no amusement beyond conversation with Father. Father, I am genuinely eager to witness the Pythias to your Damon. You know I have met him only once, and we were so perplexed regarding what to say to one another that our interaction was far from successful."

"I cannot say that you would ever find him agreeable, Margaret. He is not a 'lady's man.'"

Margaret arched her neck in a gesture of scornful disdain. "I hold no particular admiration for 'lady's men,' Father. Nevertheless, Mr. Thornton visits us as your friend—as one who has recognized your worth."

"The only individual in Milton," Mrs. Hale interjected.

"Therefore, let us extend him a warm welcome, along with some coconut cakes. Dixon would be delighted if we request she prepares them; and I shall undertake to iron your caps, Mother."

Countless times that morning, Margaret had wished Mr. Thornton were miles away. She had envisioned other pursuits: writing a letter to Edith, reading a substantial portion of Dante, or visiting the Higgins household. Instead, she irons away, listening to Dixon's grievances, hoping that an overflow of sympathy might deter her from recounting her misfortunes to Mrs. Hale. Periodically, Margaret felt compelled to recall her father's high regard for Mr. Thornton to suppress the creeping irritation of fatigue, which threatened to trigger one of the severe headaches she had recently begun to suffer. By the time she finally seated herself to inform her mother that she was no longer Peggy the laundry-maid but Margaret Hale, the lady, she could barely speak. She intended the remark as a jest, and was sufficiently chagrined when her mother took it literally.

"Yes! If anyone had informed me, when I was Miss Beresford and one of the country's belles, that a child of mine would be forced to stand for half a day in a cramped kitchen, laboring like any servant to ensure proper preparation for receiving a tradesman—who happens to be the sole—"

"Oh, Mother!" Margaret exclaimed, rising abruptly. "Do not punish me for such a careless slip of the tongue. I have no objection to ironing or any form of work for you and Father. Through it all, I remain a born and bred lady, even if it involves scrubbing floors or washing dishes. I am merely exhausted for the moment; in half an hour, I shall be ready to repeat the process. As for Mr. Thornton's engagement in trade, well, he cannot alter that now, poor fellow. I doubt his education qualifies him for anything else."

With slow deliberation, Margaret rose and retreated to her own room, unable to endure much more.

Simultaneously, in Mr. Thornton's residence, a contrasting scene unfolded. A large-boned matron, well past middle age, sat working in a stately, grimly furnished dining room. Her features, mirroring her robust frame, were strong and massive rather than heavy. Her countenance shifted slowly between distinct expressions, each equally resolute. While there was little variation in her demeanor, those who beheld her once invariably returned to look again; even passersby in the street often turned their heads to gaze a moment longer at the firm, severe, and dignified woman who neither yielded in public courtesy nor paused from her direct path toward the clearly defined objective she pursued. She was elegantly attired in sturdy black silk, not a single thread of which appeared worn or discolored. She mended a vast tablecloth of the finest texture, holding it up against the light periodically to identify thin spots requiring her delicate attention. No books cluttered the room save for Matthew Henry's Bible Commentaries, six volumes resting centrally on the massive sideboard, flanked by a tea urn on one side and a lamp on the other. In a distant chamber, the sound of piano practice drifted forth. Someone was rehearsing a *morceau de salon*, executing it with rapidity; on average, every third note was either indistinct or entirely omitted, and the final loud chords were frequently false, yet the performer remained entirely satisfied.

Mrs. Thornton heard a step, matching her own in its decisive rhythm, approach the dining-room door. "John! Is that you?"

Her son entered. "What brings you home so early? I believed you were proceeding to tea with that acquaintance of Mr. Bell's, Mr. Hale?"

"So I am, Mother. I have returned to dress."

"Dress! Humph! When I was a maiden, young men were content to dress once daily. Why must you dress specifically to consume a cup of tea with an elderly parson?"

"Mr. Hale is a gentleman, and his wife and daughter are ladies."

"Wife and daughter! Do they instruct? What are their occupations? You have never mentioned them."

"No, Mother, because I have never encountered Mrs. Hale; I have seen Miss Hale for merely half an hour."

"Beware that you do not fall prey to a penniless girl, John."

"I am not easily ensnared, Mother, as I trust you are aware. Moreover, I must object to Miss Hale being spoken of in that manner, which you know is offensive to me. I have never been aware of any young lady attempting to capture me, nor do I believe anyone has ever bestowed such futile effort upon it."

Mrs. Thornton refused to concede the point to her son, possessing sufficient pride for her sex. "Very well! I merely caution you. Perhaps our Milton maidens possess too much spirit and sensibility to engage in fishing for husbands; yet this Miss Hale hails from the aristocratic counties, where, if rumors are to be believed, wealthy spouses are considered prizes."

Mr. Thornton's brow furrowed, and he advanced a step into the room. "Mother"—accompanied by a brief, scornful laugh—"you will force me to confess. The sole occasion I witnessed Miss Hale, she treated me with a haughty civility steeped in a strong flavor of contempt. She held herself aloof as if she were a queen and I her humble, unwashed vassal. Rest assured, Mother."

"Not at all! Neither am I at ease nor content. What business did she, a renegade clergyman's daughter, have to turn her nose up at you! I would not dress for any of them—a saucy lot! Were I in your place."

As he departed the room, he added, "Mr. Hale is good, gentle, and learned. He is not saucy. Regarding Mrs. Hale, I shall describe her appearance tonight, should you wish to hear." He closed the door and vanished.

Left alone, Mrs. Thornton fumed. "Despise my son! Treat him as her vassal, indeed! Humph! I wonder where she could possibly find another such specimen! Whether boy or man, he is the noblest, stoutest heart I have ever known. It matters not that I am his mother; I can discern truth and remain unblinded. I know what Fanny is, and I know what John is. To despise him! I loathe her!"
