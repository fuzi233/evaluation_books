A profound silence settled over Margaret Brandt, transforming her spirit into something spectral. Her domestic labors were performed with the mechanical daze of a sleepwalker, yet beneath the surface, a storm raged. If Peter so much as raised his voice slightly, she would start violently, her gaze locking onto him with wide, terrified eyes. She retreated from the warmth of her friendship with Margaret Van Eyck, finding the old lady's affection overwhelming; instead of leaning into those embraces, Margaret shrank back, trembling and passive. Most striking of all was her refusal to venture outdoors during daylight. She only ventured forth after dusk, returning before dawn, avoiding even the early prayers at matins.

Peter, though typically oblivious, finally perceived the shift. "Why do you avoid the light, child?" he asked, sensing the weight behind her stillness.

"Methinks the folk all look at me," she whispered, the words hanging heavy in the air.

One afternoon, Margaret Van Eyck pressed gently, asking what burden weighed upon her heart. A look of sheer dread and a sudden flood of tears were her only response. The older woman, softening with compassion, urged her to share her sorrow.

"I have no sorrows, madam, but ones of my own making," Margaret stammered, her voice thin. "I am treated with kindness I do not deserve, especially within these walls."

"Then why not visit me more often, my dear?"

"I come oftener than I deserve," Margaret sighed, the sound breaking like dry leaves. "Reicht is crying for you," Mrs. Van Eyck interjected, pointing toward the nursery. "Go, child—what is troubling you?"

Mrs. Van Eyck, piecing together the fragments, suspected Margaret felt humiliated by the disdain Gerard's family showed her. Resolving to intervene, she donned her hood and cloak, taking Reicht with her, and marched directly to the hosier's house. Catherine received her with tearful gratitude for past kindnesses toward Gerard. However, when Mrs. Van Eyck attempted to pivot the conversation to explain Margaret's plight, Catherine's demeanor shifted instantly. Her eyes hardened, her lips compressed until they resembled a tight, unyielding line, and her expression took on the stubborn, ignorant cast of a brick wall. Despite Mrs. Van Eyck's gentle efforts, the barrier remained impenetrable. Provoked by this resistance, the old lady abruptly curtseied—a gesture that felt more like a dismissal than an honor—and turned away.

Outside the door, Reicht encountered a pale girl seated on crutches. As Mrs. Van Eyck pushed past them, intending to send a scornful glance at the entire household, a small, delicate hand suddenly glided into hers. Turning, she met two eyes like doves', filled with water and seeking both gratitude and plea. In that complex, luminous gaze, Mrs. Van Eyck's anger dissolved. She knelt and kissed Kate's brow. "I see," she murmured. "Leave this to me."

Returning home, she recounted her journey to herself. "I visited a house today," she reflected, "where I witnessed a common stupidity and an uncommon grace. I saw an obstinate woman, and I saw an angel in the flesh. If I had my paints here, I would take down my brushes again to try and capture her face."

Kate did not betray the high regard her mother held for her. Waiting for the right moment, she revealed what she had learned from Reicht Heynes: that Margaret had given her very blood for Gerard in the woods.

"Look, Mother, how she loves him!" Kate insisted.

"And who would not love him?" Margaret replied coolly, recoiling from the emotional intensity.

"Oh, Mother, think of it! Poor thing."

"Ay, wench. She has her own troubles, just as we do. I cannot stand the sight of blood, let alone someone else's."

Though this point was conceded, whenever Kate pressed further, her mother cut her off. Then, nearly a month later, a soldier of the Dalgetty tribe returned from service in Burgundy, carrying a letter to the hosier's house. He delivered it to Catherine as the family sat at supper, declining any reward before departing for Sevenbergen.

The letter was unfolded. Though none of them possessed the skill to decipher the script, they all sensed immediately that it was Gerard's handwriting.

"And Father must be away," Catherine cried, her voice rising. "Are you not ashamed? Not one among you can read your brother's letter!"

Yet, though the words were hieroglyphics to their uneducated eyes, the meaning transcended language. There exists an art that speaks without ink, free from the limits of the pen, stealing straight into the heart and brain of the learned and the unlearned alike. It crosses seas and frontiers, losing nothing in translation, for it writes a universal tongue.

When they beheld this, a simple sketch drawn by Gerard between two short paragraphs, the truth leaped into view. It depicted two hands clasped together.

Gerard was bidding them farewell.

As they gazed upon the drawing, recognizing the familiar curve of his lines and the gentleness of his touch, Gerard seemed to stand before them, saying goodbye. The women wept openly until the vision blurred. Giles spoke softly, "Poor Gerard!" Even Cornelis and Sybrandt fell silent, overcome by a fleeting, crushing remorse.

They were desperate to understand the words, loath to display their ignorance or emotion to a stranger.

"The Dame Van Eyck?" Kate suggested timidly.

"And so I shall, Kate," Catherine agreed, her voice regaining strength. "She has a kind heart. She loved Gerard. She will be glad to hear news of him. I was sharp with her when she first came, but I will humble myself, and then she may tell me what my poor child wrote."

Kate hurried to Margaret Van Eyck's house. Reicht led her into a chamber, announcing briefly, "Wait a moment; she is at prayer."

Inside, a young woman sat pensively by the stove. Upon seeing the visitors, she rose gracefully to make way. "Thank you, young lady," Catherine said, warming her hands and casting a furtive, assessing glance from head to toe. The youth wore a plain wimple, yet her gown was trimmed with fur—a rare luxury in those times, signaling superior rank or wealth. What struck Catherine most, however, was the candor and modesty of the girl's face. She felt an immediate bond with such goodness and began to chatter.

"Now, what brings me here, young lady? A letter! A letter from my poor boy, far away in some savage land. I take shame to confess that none of us can read it. Pray, can you?"

"Yes," the girl replied simply.

"It is a great credit to you, my dear. I fear it will not be long before the post arrives, but every minute feels like an hour to a mother."

"I will read it to you."

"Bless you, my dear! Bless you!"

Driven by unfeigned urgency, Catherine failed to notice the suppressed, trembling eagerness with which the hand extended to take the letter, nor the slight shake of the fingers closing around it.

"Read it to me, pray," Catherine urged, her voice thick with anticipation. "I am wearying for it."

"The first words are, 'To my honoured parents,'" Kate read aloud.

"Ay! And he always did honour us, poor soul."

"'God and the saints have you in His holy keeping, and bless you by night and by day. Your one harsh deed is forgotten; your years of love remembered.'"

Catherine laid her hand upon her bosom and sank back into her chair, letting out a long, rending sob.

"Then comes this, madam. It speaks for itself: 'a long farewell.'"

"Ay, go on. You give me sorry comfort, yet it is comfort."

"'To my brothers Cornelis and Sybrandt—Be content; you will see me no more!'"

"What does that mean? Oh!"

"'To my sister Kate. Little angel of my father's house. Be kind to her—for poor Gerard's sake: since grief to her is death to me.'"

Kate choked on the final words. "That is Margaret Brandt, my dear... his sweetheart, poor soul. I have not been kind to her, my dear. Forgive me, Gerard!"

Nature, resenting the girl's struggle to maintain a facade of composure, suddenly broke the dam. She collapsed from her chair, falling insensible, the letter clutched in her hand, her head resting heavily against Catherine's knees.
