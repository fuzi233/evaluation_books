Old Friends

The great English festival arrived, turning Epsom Downs into a sea of holiday-makers. Among the throngs of personages assembled to witness the Derby, a comfortable open carriage, drawn by a pair of spirited horses, carried Mrs. Bungay of Paternoster Row. She was attired in all the glory of Solomon, flanked by her modest friend, Mrs. Shandon. While the worthy publisher, Bungay, had recovered from a copious luncheon to engage in the mad sport of shying at the sticks nearby, Mrs. Shandon ambled among the drinking tenants and gipsies. Finucane remained in constant attendance on the ladies, intercepting gentlemen connected with the publishing house.

Mr. Archer approached to pay his respects, delighting Mrs. Bungay with a tour of the aristocracy. He pointed out the Prime Minister, the Chinese Ambassador surrounded by Mandarins, and even recounted a scandalous tale of a Chinese cook who had skinned the Ambassador's beloved spaniel and served it stuffed with forced-meat. Mrs. Bungay listened with wide-eyed astonishment as Archer detailed the Ambassador's failed attempt to woo Lady Flamingo, a woman currently squeezing her feet to fit the exotic standard of her future husband.

As the conversation turned to the younger generation, Mrs. Bungay inquired about Mr. Pendennis. Archer revealed that the young man possessed a substantial fortune—eighteen hundred a year in land and twenty-two thousand five hundred in government bonds. He explained that he had been consulted regarding the late Mrs. Pendennis's will, ensuring that the Major's potential extravagance was checked. Archer then identified several other notable figures, including Lord Rochester, Lord John, and the Duke of Hampshire, who boasted of attending sixty-five Derbies since childhood.

While the elite mingled in the carriages, a different scene unfolded on the battered roof of Wheeler's drag, a stage-coach carrying a crew of shabby spirits from Bow Street. As the famous horse Podasokus won the race, one of the passengers leaped onto the roof in triumph, declaring supper for ten and demanding that the expense be covered. The crowd below eyed the winner with suspicion, fearing a loan shark, while the driver and his companions celebrated the victory.

Nearby, cab No. 2002 transported a gentleman and two ladies. The young lady, Fanny Bolton, sat on the box with her mother, enjoying lobster salad and bitter ale. Her companion, Mr. Sam Huxter, a young surgeon, kept the atmosphere light with jokes and a collection of street trophies, including a gypsy fortune-teller who ominously warned Fanny to beware of a false fair man.

Suddenly, Arthur Pendennis approached the cab. Fanny's heart hammered; her mother turned a shade of red that rivaled the lobster she had consumed; and Mr. Huxter's demeanor shifted instantly from jovial to fiercely suspicious. Arthur, however, detected the tension and, recalling his own recent illness and poor temper, offered a conciliatory toast to Fanny's health. He extended his lavender kid glove to Huxter, a gesture of peace that disarmed the surgeon's clenched fist. Huxter, feeling the heat of his own hand and the black mark it left on Pen's glove, struggled with the urge to fight, but the moment passed. Pen drank the frothy, lukewarm beer cordially, and the immediate hostility dissolved into polite socializing.

As they talked, a young lady in a dove-colored dress passed by, leaning on the arm of a man with a military mustache. Fanny's gaze followed her, sparking a romantic fantasy in her mind involving love, betrayal, and a tragic ending where Sam Huxter was not the hero. After Pen departed, the mood in the cab shifted. The sun seemed dimmer, the beer hotter, and the world changed for Huxter. He spent the ride home with peas and a tin pea-shooter in his pocket, eventually bursting into a sardonic laugh when another wag fired a pea-shooter at his face. Upon arriving home, Fanny's charm returned; she coaxed and smiled, leading to an emotional reunion where Huxter confessed his jealousy and unworthiness, nearly kissing her before her mother intervened.

Meanwhile, Arthur Pendennis visited Lady Clavering's carriage. The carriage was besieged by dandies and military bucks courting Miss Amory, Lady Clavering's daughter. Pen pushed through the crowd to reach her, engaging in a playful exchange about the "monster" Huxter, whom Miss Amory described as someone who would kill Pen if he attended to her when sick. Pen, amused by her dramatic French, noted that Huxter had merely bruised his glove in a handshake and was a young surgeon from Clavering.

As the day wound down, the carriage returned to Grosvenor Place. The Major observed the heavy betting habits of Sir Francis Clavering, fearing the family fortune would be drained by the turf. Inside the carriage, young Hopeful Clavering confessed to having bet on Podasokus despite his father's promises, revealing that his father had lost seven thousand pounds backing the favorite. The news shattered the Begum's spirits; she wept, vowing to separate from her ungrateful husband. As the carriage drove away, the Major resolved to help Miss Blanche, who lamented her status as a beggar due to her father's deceit. The party dispersed, leaving the Major and his companions to ponder the financial ruin awaiting the Clavering house.
