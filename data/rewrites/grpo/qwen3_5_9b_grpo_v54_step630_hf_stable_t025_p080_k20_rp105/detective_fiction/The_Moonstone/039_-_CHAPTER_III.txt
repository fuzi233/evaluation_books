My memory of Hotherstone's Farm is a blur, a hazy dream rather than a clear record. I recall a warm welcome and a supper so abundant it seemed designed to feed a small town, followed by a bedroom spotlessly clean, save for that archaic relic of our ancestors' foolishness: the feather bed. My sleep was fitful, plagued by the frantic striking of matches and the flickering of a single candle, until the dawn brought a profound sense of relief.

That evening, I had arranged with Betteredge to meet him on our journey to Cobb's Hole, whenever my impatience to secure Rosanna's letter dictated. Driven by this urgency, I skipped breakfast, clutching a crust of bread, and set out, half-expecting to find the faithful servant still in bed. Instead, I found him ready and waiting, his walking stick gripped firmly in his hand, his excitement mirroring my own.

"How are you this morning, Betteredge?" I asked.

"Very poorly, sir," he replied.

"I'm sorry to hear that. What ails you?"

"I suffer from a new ailment, Mr. Franklin, one of my own invention. I won't alarm you, but you'll catch it before the day is done."

"The devil I shall!"

"Do you feel a burning heat in your stomach, sir? And a dull thumping at the crown of your head? Ah, not yet? It will seize you at Cobb's Hole. I call it the detective fever. I contracted it first while in the company of Sergeant Cuff."

"Aye, aye! And the cure, in this case, is to open Rosanna Spearman's letter, I suppose? Come, let us get it."

Though early, the fisherman's wife was already stirring in her kitchen. Upon my introduction by Betteredge, Mrs. Yolland performed a ritual of hospitality usually reserved for distinguished strangers. She placed a bottle of Dutch gin and two clean pipes upon the table and opened the conversation with, "What news from London, sir?"

Before I could answer this sweeping query, a figure emerged from a shadowed corner. A pale, wild-looking girl, her hair strikingly beautiful, approached with a fierce intensity in her eyes. She limped forward on a crutch to the table where I sat, regarding me with a mixture of fascination and horror that held me transfixed.

"Mr. Betteredge," she said, never breaking her gaze, "mention his name again, if you please."

"This gentleman's name," Betteredge answered, stressing the word *gentleman*, "is Mr. Franklin Blake."

The girl turned her back on me and fled the room. Mrs. Yolland offered some apologies for her daughter's eccentricity, which Betteredge likely translated into proper English, though I cannot be certain. My attention was entirely consumed by the sound of the crutch: *thump-thump* up the stairs, *thump-thump* across the floor above, *thump-thump* down again. Then, the apparition stood at the open door, a letter clutched in her hand, beckoning me outside.

I left my apologies trailing behind me and followed her strange procession, limping faster and faster down the beach slope. She led me behind some boats, away from the few villagers, and stopped, turning to face me for the first time.

"Stand there," she commanded. "I wish to look at you."

There was no ambiguity in her expression. I inspired in her the deepest feelings of abhorrence and disgust. I shall not claim vanity in saying no woman had ever looked at me thus; I will only assert the modest truth that no woman had ever allowed me to perceive it. There is a limit to how long a man can endure such scrutiny. I attempted to divert Limping Lucy's gaze to something less repulsive.

"I believe you have a letter to give me," I began. "Is it the one in your hand?"

"Say that again," was her only response.

I repeated the words, like a child reciting a lesson.

"No," the girl muttered, her eyes still mercilessly fixed on my face. "I cannot understand what she saw in his face. I cannot guess what she heard in his voice." She suddenly looked away, resting her weary head on her crutch. "Oh, my poor dear!" she whispered, her tone softening for the first time. "Oh, my lost darling! What could you see in this man?" She lifted her head fiercely, staring at me once more. "Can you eat and drink?"

I maintained my composure and answered, "Yes."

"Can you sleep?"

"Yes."

"When you see a poor girl in service, do you feel no remorse?"

"Certainly not. Why should I?"

She abruptly thrust the letter into my face.

"Take it!" she screamed furiously. "I never set eyes on you before. God Almighty forbid I should ever set eyes on you again."

With those final words, she limped away at full speed. The obvious conclusion was that she was mad. Accepting this, I turned my attention to the letter from Rosanna Spearman. The address read: "For Franklin Blake, Esq. To be given into his own hands (and not to be trusted to anyone else), by Lucy Yolland."

Breaking the seal, I found a letter inside, which in turn contained a slip of paper. I read the letter first:

"Sir,—If you are curious to know the meaning of my behaviour to you, whilst you were staying in the house of my mistress, Lady Verinder, do what you are told to do in the memorandum enclosed with this—and do it without any person being present to overlook you. Your humble servant, ROSANNA SPEARMAN."

Next, I examined the memorandum:

"Memorandum:—To go to the Shivering Sand at the turn of the tide. To walk out on the South Spit, until I get the South Spit Beacon, and the flagstaff at the Coast-guard station above Cobb's Hole in a line together. To lay down on the rocks, a stick, or any straight thing to guide my hand, exactly in the line of the beacon and the flagstaff. To take care, in doing this, that one end of the stick shall be at the edge of the rocks, on the side of them which overlooks the quicksand. To feel along the stick, among the seaweed (beginning from the end of the stick which points towards the beacon), for the Chain. To run my hand along the Chain, when found, until I come to the part of it which stretches over the edge of the rocks, down into the quicksand. And then, to pull the chain."

As I read the final words, underlined in the original, Betteredge's voice rang out behind me. The inventor of the detective fever had finally succumbed to it. "I can't stand it any longer, Mr. Franklin. What does her letter say? For mercy's sake, sir, tell us, what does her letter say?"

I handed him the documents. He read the letter with little interest, but the memorandum struck him deeply.

"The Sergeant said it!" cried Betteredge. "From start to finish, sir, the Sergeant said she had a memorandum of the hiding-place. And here it is! Lord save us, Mr. Franklin, here is the secret that puzzled everyone, from the great Cuff downwards, ready and waiting to show itself to *you!* It's ebb tide now, sir, as anyone can see. How long until the turn?"

He spotted a boy mending a net nearby. "Tammie Bright!" he shouted.

"I hear you!" the boy called back.

"When's the turn of the tide?"

"In an hour's time."

Checking our watches, Betteredge suggested we take the coastal route to the quicksand to ensure we had ample time. "Come along!" I agreed.

On the way, I asked Betteredge to refresh my memory regarding Rosanna during Sergeant Cuff's inquiry. With his help, the sequence of events became clear: Rosanna's trip to Frizinghall while the household thought her ill; her mysterious nocturnal activities with her door locked; her suspicious purchase of the japanned tin case and the two dog chains from Mrs. Yolland; and the Sergeant's firm belief that she had hidden something at the Shivering Sand, though he knew not what. These strange threads of the failed inquiry coalesced as we reached the quicksand and walked onto the low ledge of rocks known as the South Spit.

With Betteredge's assistance, I positioned myself to align the Beacon and the Coast-guard flagstaff. Following the memorandum, we laid my stick carefully on the uneven rocks. We checked our watches again.

Twenty minutes remained until the turn of the tide. I suggested waiting on the dry beach rather than the wet, slippery rocks. As I sat down, to my surprise, Betteredge prepared to depart.

"What are you going away for?" I asked.

"Look at the letter again, sir, and you will see."

A glance reminded me that I was instructed to make the discovery alone.

"It is hard enough for me to leave you at such a critical moment," Betteredge said, "but she met a dreadful death, poor soul, and I feel a duty to humour her wishes. Besides," he added confidentially, "there is nothing in the letter preventing you from revealing the secret later. I shall wait in the fir-plantation and signal when you are ready. Do not keep me waiting too long, sir. The detective fever is no easy companion under these circumstances."

With that warning, he left.

The interval, brief in clock time, stretched into an eternity of suspense. The habit of smoking became a precious comfort. I lit a cigar and sat on the beach slope. The sunlight poured down unclouded, making the very act of breathing a luxury. The lonely bay seemed cheerful, and the exposed surface of the quicksand glittered with a golden brightness, masking its treacherous nature beneath a deceptive smile. It was the finest day I had seen since returning to England.

The tide turned before my cigar burned down. I watched the preliminary heaving of the sand, followed by a terrifying shudder that seemed to ripple through the depths below. Discarding my cigar, I returned to the rocks.

Following the memorandum, I advanced more than halfway along the stick's line, encountering only rock edges. A few inches further, my patience was rewarded. In a narrow fissure, just within reach of my forefinger, I felt the chain. Attempting to follow it toward the quicksand, my progress was halted by a thick growth of seaweed that had entangled itself in the crack since Rosanna hid it there.

I could neither pull up the weed nor force my hand through it. Marking the spot indicated by the stick's end nearest the quicksand, I devised a new plan. I intended to "sound" immediately beneath the rocks, hoping to recover the chain's trace where it entered the sand. Taking up the stick, I knelt on the brink of the South Spit.

My face was mere feet from the quicksand's surface. Its intermittent, hideous shivering shook my nerves. A horrific thought seized me: that the dead woman might rise from the heaving sand to assist my search, pointing to the location. This dread turned me cold in the warm sun. I closed my eyes as the stick's tip first touched the water.

Instantly, my superstitious terror vanished, replaced by a throbbing excitement from head to toe. Sounding blindly on my first attempt, I struck true! The stick hit the chain.

Gripping the seaweed roots with my left hand, I lay over the brink and felt under the overhanging rock with my right. My fingers found the chain. I drew it up effortlessly, revealing the japanned tin case fastened to its end.

The water had rusted the chain so severely that I could not unfasten it from the hasp. Placing the case between my knees, I exerted my utmost strength to pry off the cover. Inside, a white substance filled the space. Reaching in, I found it was linen.

As I drew out the linen, a crumpled letter emerged. Checking the direction, I saw it bore my name. I pocketed the letter and removed the linen completely. It came out as a thick roll, molded to the shape of the case, perfectly preserved from injury by the sea.

Carrying the linen to the dry sand, I unrolled and smoothed it. There was no mistake; it was an article of dress, a nightgown. The upper side showed only folds and creases. Turning it over, I instantly discovered the smear of paint from Rachel's boudoir door!

My eyes fixed on the stain, and my mind leaped from the present to the past. The words of Sergeant Cuff echoed in my mind as if he stood beside me, pointing to the unanswerable inference drawn from that very mark.

"Find out whether there is any article of dress in this house with the stain of paint on it. Find out who that dress belongs to. Find out how the person can account for having been in the room, and smeared the paint between midnight and three in the morning. If the person can't satisfy you, you haven't far to look for the hand that took the Diamond."

Those words replayed in my memory with a mechanical, wearisome repetition. I was roused from a trance-like state—what felt like hours, though it was merely moments—by Betteredge's voice calling to me. His patience had finally snapped. He was visible between the sandhills, returning to the beach.

His reappearance grounded me, reminding me that my inquiry remained incomplete. I had found the paint stain on the nightgown. To whom did it belong?

My first impulse was to consult the letter in my pocket. But as I raised my hand, I realized there was a shorter path to the truth. The nightgown itself would reveal it; surely, it would bear the owner's name.

I picked up the gown and searched for the mark. I found it and read: MY OWN NAME.

The familiar letters told me the nightgown was mine. I looked up. The sun shone bright; the bay waters glittered; Betteredge advanced closer. I looked back at the letters. My own name. Plainly confronting me—my own name.
