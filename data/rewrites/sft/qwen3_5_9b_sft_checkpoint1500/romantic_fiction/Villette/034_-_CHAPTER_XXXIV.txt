On a Thursday afternoon, Madame Beck summoned me from my studies. "Lucie," she requested, "do you have any pressing duties that prevent you from running into town? I require you to fetch several small items for me."

Feeling free, I accepted the commission. She handed me a list of wools, silks, and embroidery threads needed for the pupils' projects. Equipping myself against the threatening gloom of a cloudy, sultry day, I stepped toward the street-door. However, before I could open it, Madame Beck's voice recalled me to the dining hall.

"Pardon, Miss Lucie!" she cried, feigning the sudden urgency of an afterthought. "I have just remembered one final errand. If your good nature does not consider yourself overburdened?"

Naturally, I assured her of my availability. Running into the little salon, she returned with a delicate basket filled with fine hothouse fruit—rosy, perfect, and tempting, nestled among dark green, wax-like leaves and pale yellow stars of some exotic plant.

"Here," she said, "it is not heavy, and it will not mar your neat attire like a mere household chore. Please leave this basket at Madame Walravens' residence with my congratulations on her feast day. She lives in the old town, Number 3, Rue des Mages. I fear the walk will be long, but you have the entire afternoon; do not hurry. If you cannot return before dinner, I will have a portion saved, or Goton, who favors you, will toss up something for your amusement. You shall not be forgotten, my dear Miss Lucie. And oh, please!" She called me back once more. "Be sure to insist on seeing Madame Walravens personally and handing the basket directly to her hands. She is a punctilious personage, and mistakes must be avoided. Adieu!"

Finally, I departed. The shopping for silks and wools proved tedious, requiring careful selection and matching, but eventually, I completed the list. The patterns for slippers, bell-ropes, and bags were chosen; the tassels and slides for the purses were picked. My mind was clear of the commercial tasks, save for the delivery of the fruit and the felicitations.

I found the prospect of a long walk into the grim, ancient *Basse-Ville* appealing. Indeed, I welcomed it no less because the evening sky above the city was settling into a mass of black-blue metal, heated at the edges and inflaming slowly to a heavy, ominous red.

I feared a high wind, for storms demand physical exertion and active resistance, things I always endure with pain. However, the sullen downpour, the thick snow-drifts, or the dark rush of rain require only resignation—a quiet surrender of one's garments and person to be soaked. In return, the rain sweeps a great capital clean, carving a silent path through broad, grand streets. It petrifies a living city as if by Eastern enchantment, transforming Villette into a phantom of Tadmor. So, let the rains fall, and the floods descend—but first, I must deliver this basket of fruit.

An unknown clock from an unseen tower tolled the third quarter past five when I reached the street and house Madame Beck had described. It was no thoroughfare; it seemed rather a square, quiet and secluded. Grass grew between the broad grey flags, and the houses, large and ancient-looking, stood behind gardens hinted at by trees. An antiquity brooded over this region; business had been banished. Once, rich men and grandeur had held court here, but wealth and greatness had long since spread their gilded wings and fled, leaving behind ancient nests that now housed penury or stood cold and empty, mouldering through winter.

Crossing this deserted square, where drops nearly the size of five-franc pieces darkened the pavement, I saw no sign of life save for an infirm old priest. He walked past, bent and propped on a staff—the embodiment of age and decay. He emerged from the very house to which I was directed. When I paused before the door, just closing after him, and rang the bell, he turned to look at me. He did not soon avert his gaze; perhaps he considered me, with my basket of summer fruit and my lack of the dignity age confers, an incongruous figure in such a scene. I thought, had a young, ruddy-faced maid opened the door, I would have found her equally out of harmony. But when I found myself confronted by a very old woman dressed in a very antique peasant costume—a cap hideous yet costly, with long flaps of native lace, a petticoat and jacket of rough cloth, and wooden clogs (*sabots*) resembling little boats—it felt right, soothingly in character.

Her facial expression, however, was less soothing than her attire. She was the most cantankerous creature I had ever seen, scarcely replying to my inquiries about Madame Walravens. I believed she would have snatched the basket from my hand had not the old priest, hobbling forward, checked her. He lent an ear to my message himself.

His apparent deafness made it difficult to ensure he understood that I must see Madame Walravens and hand the fruit directly to her. Finally, however, he grasped the necessity of fulfilling my orders literally. Addressing the aged *bonne* not in French, but in the aboriginal tongue of Labassecour, he persuaded her to let me cross the inhospitable threshold. Escorting me upstairs, he ushered me into a sort of salon and left me there.

The room was large, possessing a fine old ceiling and windows of colored glass akin to those in a church. Yet, it was desolate, and under the shadow of the coming storm, it looked strangely lowering. Within opened a smaller room; here, the blind covering the single window was drawn tight. Through the deep gloom, few details of furniture were visible, though I amused myself trying to decipher them. In particular, I was drawn to the outline of a picture on the wall.

Gradually, the picture seemed to dissolve. To my bewilderment, it shook, sank, and rolled back into nothing. Its vanishing left an arched opening leading into a passage and a mystic, winding stair, both constructed of cold, uncarpeted stone. Down this dungeon-like stair descended a rhythmic *tap, tap*, like a stick striking the steps. Soon a shadow fell upon the stairs, and finally, I became aware of a substance.

Yet, was this actual substance? This obstruction, partially darkening the arch, approaching me?

It drew nearer, and I saw it clearly. I began to comprehend my location. Well might this old square be known as the Quarter of the Magi; well might the three towers overlooking it claim three mystic sages of a dead and dark art as godfathers. Here, hoar enchantment prevailed; a spell had opened an elf-land to me. That cell-like room, the vanishing picture, the arch, the passage, and the stone stair were all parts of a fairy tale. Even clearer than these scenic details stood the central figure: Cunegonde, the sorceress! Malevola, the evil fairy.

She might have been three feet tall, but she had no shape. Her skinny hands rested upon each other, pressing the gold knob of a wand-like ivory staff. Her face was large and set, not upon her shoulders, but before her breast; she seemed to possess no neck. Her features suggested a hundred years, perhaps more in her eyes—her malign, unfriendly eyes, framed by thick grey brows and livid lids. They viewed me with severe, dull displeasure.

She wore a gown of brocade dyed a bright blue, full-tinted as the gentianella flower, covered with a large pattern of satin foliage. Over this, a costly shawl, gorgeously bordered, was so large for her that its many-colored fringe swept the floor. But her chief adornments were her jewels: long, clear earrings blazing with a lustre that could not be borrowed or false; rings on her skeleton hands featuring thick gold hoops and stones of purple, green, and blood-red. Hunchbacked, dwarfish, and doting, she was adorned like a barbarian queen.

*"Que me voulez-vous?"* she asked hoarsely, with a voice rather of male than female old age. Indeed, a silver beard bristled her chin.

I delivered my basket and my message.

*"Is that all?"* she demanded.

*"It is all,"* I replied.

*"Truly, it was well worth while,"* she answered. *"Return to Madame Beck and tell her I can buy fruit whenever I wish, and as for her felicitations, I mock them!"* With this courteous dismissal, the dame turned her back.

Just as she turned, a peal of thunder shattered the air, and a flash of lightning blazed broadly over the salon and boudoir. The tale of magic proceeded with the proper accompaniment of the elements. The wanderer, decoyed into the enchanted castle, heard the spell-wakened tempest rising outside.

What, in all this, was I to think of Madame Beck? She owned strange acquaintances; she offered messages and gifts at a unique shrine, and the bearing of the uncouth thing she worshipped seemed inauspicious. There went that sullen Sidonia, tottering and trembling like palsy incarnate, tapping her ivory staff on the mosaic parquet and muttering venomously as she vanished.
