The darkness of the third night settled over the valley like a heavy, suffocating blanket, wrapping the camp at the foot of the "Three Witches" in a silence that felt less like peace and more like the breath held before a scream. Here, beneath the looming triangle of peaks that guarded Solomon's Great Road, our party gathered: myself, Good, Sir Henry, and the shadowy presence of Foulata, who hovered nearby with a servitude that bordered on the ominous. Beside us stood Infadoos, and carried aloft in a litter that scraped against the earth, was Gagool. Even within the confines of her woven cage, her voice was a constant, gnarled whisper, a stream of curses and muttered threats that seemed to vibrate through the very ground we stood upon. Above us, the mountains rose in a jagged defiance of gravity, three solitary upheavals of rock twisting upward into the starless sky. Their bases were stained purple by heath and wild moorland, bleeding color into the gloom, while their summits wore crowns of snow that glowed with a faint, ethereal luminescence. Between us and the central peak, stretching five miles into the abyss, lay the white ribbon of Solomon's Great Road, terminating abruptly at the edge of a chasm that promised both salvation and damnation.

I tried to suppress the electric hum of anticipation that crackled through my veins, the kind of excitement that feels dangerously close to madness. We were approaching the source of a curse that had claimed the lives of Dom Pedro three centuries prior, had doomed my friend, and now threatened to swallow George Curtis, Sir Henry's brother. Was fate a circle that inevitably closed upon us? As we began our ascent, the air grew thin and crisp, carrying the scent of ancient stone and impending doom. For an hour and a half, we scrambled up the heather-fringed path, our urgency driving us faster than reason dictated. The bearers of Gagool's litter struggled to match our pace, their muscles straining against the weight of their burden, until finally, the woman inside projected her visage through the gaps in the grass curtains. Her face was a map of wrinkles and decay, her eyes gleaming with a predatory intelligence that froze the blood in my veins.

"Why will ye run to meet the evil that shall befall you?" she shrieked, her voice echoing unnaturally in the twilight. "Ye seekers after treasure!" She threw back her head and let loose a laugh that was not a sound of joy, but of malice—a hollow, scraping noise that drained the warmth from the air and replaced it with a chilling dread. For a moment, the thrill of discovery evaporated, replaced by a primal fear that clawed at our throats. Yet, we pushed forward, driven by a curiosity that refused to be extinguished.

Suddenly, the landscape opened up to reveal a vast, circular wound in the earth, a pit three hundred feet deep and half a mile wide, its sloping sides swallowing the horizon. "Do you not recognize this?" I asked, turning to Sir Henry and Good, whose faces were pale masks of astonishment. They shook their heads, unable to reconcile the scale of the ruin with anything they had ever witnessed. "Then you have never seen the diamond diggings of Kimberley," I replied, my voice steady despite the tremor in my heart. "This is Solomon's Diamond Mine. Observe the strata of stiff blue clay clinging to the grass-covered walls; the formation is identical. And look there," I continued, gesturing to the worn, flat slabs of stone arranged along a dry riverbed, "tables once used to wash the ore. If those are not the tools of miners, I am a Dutchman."

At the precipice of this gaping maw, the Great Road forked, circling the abyss in a protective embrace constructed of massive stone blocks, seemingly erected to hold back the collapsing earth. Pressed onward by an insatiable need to understand, we approached the three towering silhouettes visible from afar. As we drew nearer, the mystery resolved into a terrifying majesty. Upon pedestals of dark, rough-hewn rock, separated by forty paces, sat three colossal figures—two male, one female—each rising thirty feet from head to base. They were the "Silent Ones," revered and feared by the Kukuana tribesmen.

The female colossus was nude, her form carved with a severity that spoke of divine power, though centuries of wind and rain had eroded her features into something indistinct. From the crown of her head erupted the sharp points of a crescent moon. Flanking her were the two males, draped in robes that seemed to cling to their terrifying forms. The statue to our right bore the visage of a demon, a snarl frozen in stone, while the one to our left possessed a serene, almost angelic countenance. Yet, as Sir Henry observed, this calm was not peaceful; it was the terrible tranquility of inhuman cruelty, the detached gaze of a deity who watches suffering without pity or sorrow. Together, they formed a trinity of awe, gazing eternally across the plains toward the distant city of Loo.

A fierce curiosity seized us, demanding to know the hands that had shaped these giants and the minds that had carved the road. Suddenly, a fragment of memory struck me, linking the Old Testament to the scene before us. Solomon, the wisest of kings, had wandered into idolatry, worshipping strange gods: Ashtoreth, the goddess of the Zidonians; Chemosh, the god of the Moabites; and Milcom, the god of the Ammonites. Could these be their avatars? "Consider," I proposed, "these figures may represent those fallen divinities."

Sir Henry, a man of classical learning, nodded thoughtfully. "There is substance to that theory," he mused. "Ashtoreth of the Hebrews was the Astarte of the Phoenicians, the great merchants of Solomon's era. Astarte, later known to the Greeks as Aphrodite, was depicted with horns like a crescent moon. Note the distinct horns upon the brow of the female figure. Perhaps these colossi were commissioned by a Phoenician overseer managing the mines. Who can truly say?"

Before we could delve deeper into the archaeology of the dead, Infadoos stepped forward, raising his spear in a salute to the Silent Ones. He questioned us regarding our intent: would we descend immediately into the "Place of Death," or wait for midday sustenance? With the clock striking barely eleven and our curiosity burning brighter than caution, we declared our readiness to proceed instantly. I instructed Foulata to secure a reed basket containing biltong and water, a precautionary measure should we be trapped within the depths.

Gagool emerged from her litter, casting a final, venomous grin at us before leaning on her staff and hobbling toward the sheer rock face ahead. We followed her until we reached a narrow, solidly arched portal that resembled the entrance to a forgotten mine gallery. Here, she paused, her evil smile returning.

"Now, white men from the Stars," she croaked, her voice dripping with theatrical menace. "Great warriors, Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn the wise—are ye ready? Behold, I am here to serve my lord the King and show you the store of bright stones. Ha! ha! ha!"

"We are ready," I affirmed, forcing my voice to remain level.

"Good, good! Fortify your hearts to endure what lies ahead," she commanded. Then, turning her gaze to Infadoos, she sneered. "Comest thou too, traitor? Thou who betrayed thy master?"

Infadoos' face hardened, his frown deepening into a mask of grim resolve. "Nay, I do not enter. It is not for me to tread that path. But heed this, Gagool: curb thy tongue and beware how thou speakest to my lords. Should a single hair of them be harmed, thou shalt die, fifty times a witch. Hearst thou?"

"I hear, Infadoos," she retorted, her laughter bubbling up like bile. "Thou hast always loved big words; even as a babe, thou threatenedst thine own mother. But fear not, for I live only to serve the King. I have served many kings until, in the end, they served me. Ha! ha! I go to behold their faces once more, and Twala's as well! Come, here is the lamp." She produced a large gourd filled with oil, fitted with a rush wick, pulling it from beneath her fur cloak.

"Art thou coming, Foulata?" Good asked, his voice adopting a peculiar, affected dialect that hinted at the lessons he had learned from the young lady.

"I fear, my lord," the girl whispered, shrinking back.

"Then give me the basket."

"Nay, my lord, whither thou goest, there I go also."

*The deuce you will,* I thought, a cold knot tightening in my stomach. *That may prove awkward if we never escape.*

Without further delay, Gagool plunged into the darkness, the passage wide enough for two to walk abreast but plunging instantly into blackness. We followed the echo of her voice, our steps hesitant, our hearts hammering against our ribs. The atmosphere shifted abruptly as a sudden rush of wings brushed against Good's face.

"Hullo! What's that?" he cried out, stumbling back. "Somebody hit me!"

"Bats," I reassured him, urging him forward. "On you go."
