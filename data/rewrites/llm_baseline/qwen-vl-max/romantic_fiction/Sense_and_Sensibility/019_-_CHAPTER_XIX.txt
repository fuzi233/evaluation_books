CHAPTER XIX. Edward stayed at the cottage for a week, and though Mrs. Dashwood warmly urged him to remain longer, he seemed determined to leave just as his time among them was most joyful. His spirits, though still uneven, had brightened in the final days—he grew increasingly fond of the house and its surroundings, sighed whenever he mentioned departure, declared himself free of obligations, and even hesitated over where to go next. Yet go he must. The week passed with such speed that he could scarcely believe it was over. He repeated this often, and said other things too—words that revealed the true depth of his feelings, contradicting his actions. He had no pleasure at Norland, detested London, yet both were destinations he would be forced to choose. He valued their kindness above all else, and found his greatest joy in being with them. Still, he left at the end of the week, despite their wishes and his own, with no ties binding him to stay.

Elinor attributed his strange behavior to his mother’s influence. It was fortunate for her that Mrs. Ferrars remained largely unknown to her, so she could blame every inconsistency on the elder woman’s rigid expectations. Though disappointed and sometimes irritated by Edward’s indecisiveness—especially toward herself—Elinor approached his conduct with patience and generosity, far more than she had ever shown Willoughby. She saw his lack of energy, honesty, and consistency not as flaws of character, but as consequences of his financial dependence and his awareness of his mother’s cold ambitions. His brief visit, his firm resolve to depart, all stemmed from the same inner conflict—the struggle between desire and duty, child and parent. The familiar sorrow of obligation overriding affection lay at the root of everything.

She longed to know when this torment would end, when Mrs. Ferrars might soften, and Edward be free to live as he wished. But since such hopes were vain, she turned instead to comfort in what she could trust: the sincerity of Edward’s affection, the warmth of his glances, the words he spoke, and especially the ring he wore—a constant, quiet proof of his devotion.

At breakfast on the last morning, Mrs. Dashwood remarked, “Edward, I think you’d be happier if you had a profession to occupy your mind and give direction to your life. Of course, it might mean less time for us—but then (with a smile), you’d at least know where you were going when you left.”

“I assure you,” he replied, “I’ve thought about this for years. It’s been—and always will be—a great misfortune for me to have no real purpose, no trade or calling to claim my time. But it’s not entirely my fault. My own inclinations and those of my family have shaped me into an idle, helpless man. We never agreed on a profession. I favored the church—still do. But that wasn’t fashionable enough for them. They suggested the army. That was too flashy for me. Law was considered genteel; many young men in the Temple looked well-dressed and impressive, driving smart gigs through town. But I had no interest in law, even in this polished form they approved. As for the navy—it had style, yes, but I was too old to begin. In the end, since there was no need for a profession at all—since one could be just as grand without a red coat as with one—idleness was deemed the most advantageous, the most honorable path. And so, at eighteen, I gave in to my friends’ advice and entered Oxford. Since then, I’ve been properly idle.”

“And the result?” said Mrs. Dashwood. “Will your sons grow up with as many pursuits as Columella’s?”

“They’ll be raised,” he said gravely, “to be as unlike me as possible—in feeling, action, condition, everything.”

“Ah, Edward,” she smiled, “this is only the melancholy of parting. You’re feeling low, and imagine anyone different from yourself must be happy. But remember—everyone feels sorrow at separation, no matter their upbringing. Know your own happiness. You lack only patience—or call it hope. Your mother will grant you independence soon. It is her duty, and in time, it will become her joy to prevent your youth from slipping away in discontent. What can’t a few months accomplish?”

“I think,” Edward replied quietly, “that many months will bring me no relief.”

Though he couldn’t share this despair with Mrs. Dashwood, it deepened the pain of his departure, leaving a lingering unease—especially in Elinor. Yet she resolved to master her sorrow, refusing to show more grief than her family felt. Unlike Marianne, who retreated into silence and solitude to deepen her sadness, Elinor returned to her drawing-table as soon as he was gone. She worked steadily throughout the day, avoided dwelling on his name, and maintained her usual interest in household matters. If her sorrow didn’t lessen, at least it didnnot grow unnecessarily, and her family was spared worry.

Marianne saw such restraint as lacking merit. To her, self-control was impossible with strong emotions, and pointless with weak ones. She admitted, though reluctantly, that Elinor’s affections were calm—yet still loved and respected her sister, despite this painful truth.

Without isolating herself or lying awake in thought, Elinor found ample time each day to reflect on Edward—on his actions, his moods, his promises. When her family was occupied or absent, conversation ceased, and solitude took hold. Her thoughts drifted naturally to the past and future, consumed by memory, imagination, and emotion. She pondered him with tenderness, pity, approval, criticism, and doubt—each mood shaped by her shifting heart.

One morning, shortly after Edward’s departure, she was drawn from such reverie by the sound of footsteps outside. She glanced out the window and saw a group approaching the door—Sir John and Lady Middleton, Mrs. Jennings, and two strangers: a gentleman and a lady she did not recognize. Sir John spotted her immediately and crossed the lawn, insisting she open the casement to speak to him, though the distance between window and door made privacy nearly impossible.

“Well,” he called, “we’ve brought you some visitors. How do you like them?”

“Hush!” she whispered. “They’ll hear you.”

“Never mind,” he laughed. “It’s only the Palmers. Charlotte’s very pretty—look this way!”

But Elinor declined, knowing she’d see them soon enough.

“Where’s Marianne? Has she fled because we’ve come? I see her piano is open.”

“She’s walking, I believe.”

Mrs. Jennings arrived breathless, unable to wait for the door to open. “How are you, my dear? How’s Mrs. Dashwood? Where are your sisters? What! All alone? You’ll be glad of company. I’ve brought my son and daughter to see you. Only think—they came so suddenly! I heard a carriage last night while we were drinking tea, but I thought it might be Colonel Brandon back again. So I said to Sir John, ‘Do you think I hear a carriage? Perhaps it’s Colonel Brandon!’”

Elinor had to turn away mid-sentence to greet the others. Lady Middleton introduced the newcomers. Mrs. Dashwood and Margaret descended the stairs, and all sat down to exchange glances while Mrs. Jennings continued her story, pacing through the hall with Sir John at her heels.

Mrs. Palmer was younger than Lady Middleton and utterly different. Short and plump, with a lovely face and a radiant expression of cheerfulness, she moved with warmth and ease. Her manners lacked Lady Middleton’s refinement, but they were far more engaging. She entered smiling, smiled constantly—except when laughing—and smiled again as she left. Her husband, Mr. Palmer, was a serious-looking young man of twenty-five or six, with a fashionable air and a quiet demeanor. He entered with a haughty glance, bowed stiffly without speaking, scanned the room briefly, then picked up a newspaper and read silently until departure.

Mrs. Palmer, meanwhile, was full of admiration. “Oh! What a delightful room! I’ve never seen anything so charming! Mama, look how much better it is than last time! I always thought it sweet, ma’am—but you’ve made it perfect! Look, sister, isn’t everything lovely? I’d love a house like this! Wouldn’t you, Mr. Palmer?”

Mr. Palmer gave no reply, nor lifted his eyes from the paper.

“Oh, Mr. Palmer doesn’t hear me,” she giggled. “He never does sometimes. It’s so ridiculous!”

This struck Mrs. Dashwood as odd—she had never before found humor in someone’s indifference. She stared at the couple in bewilderment.

Mrs. Jennings kept talking loudly, recounting their surprise the night before at seeing the Palmers arrive. Mrs. Palmer laughed heartily at the memory, and everyone echoed their agreement that it had been a pleasant surprise.

“You may believe how delighted we were,” Mrs. Jennings added, leaning toward Elinor and speaking softly, though they sat apart. “But I can’t help wishing they hadn’t traveled so fast, or made such a long journey. They went all the way around by London for some business—you know (nodding significantly toward her daughter) it wasn’t safe for her in her condition. I wanted her to rest today, but she insisted on coming. She was so eager to see you all!”

Mrs. Palmer laughed, saying it wouldn’t hurt her.

“She expects to deliver in February,” Mrs. Jennings continued.

Lady Middleton could endure no more and asked Mr. Palmer if there was any news in the paper.

“No,” he replied flatly, and resumed reading.

“Here comes Marianne!” Sir John announced. “Now, Palmer, you’ll see a truly beautiful girl.” He hurried into the hall, opened the front door, and ushered her in.

Mrs. Jennings greeted her at once. “Have you been to Allenham?”

Mrs. Palmer burst into laughter, clearly understanding the reference.

Mr. Palmer looked up, studied Marianne for several moments, then returned to his paper.

Mrs. Palmer’s attention shifted to the drawings on the walls. She rose to examine them closely.

“Oh, dear! How beautiful these are! Just look, mama—how sweet! I declare they’re absolutely enchanting—I could gaze at them forever!”

Then she sat down again, forgetting them entirely.

When Lady Middleton rose to leave, Mr. Palmer followed, folded the paper, stretched, and surveyed the room once more.

“My love,” his wife teased, “have you been asleep?”

He said nothing. After a moment, he observed, “The ceiling is crooked,” and bowed his way out.

Sir John had begged them all to spend the next day at the park. Mrs. Dashwood refused—she didn’t wish to dine with them more often than they visited the cottage. Her daughters might decide for themselves. But neither Elinor nor Marianne felt any curiosity about Mr. and Mrs. Palmer’s dining habits, nor expected pleasure from their company. They tried to decline—citing uncertain weather—but Sir John insisted. The carriage would come for them. Lady Middleton, Mrs. Jennings, and Mrs. Palmer all joined in urging them, seemingly united in avoiding a quiet evening at home. Reluctantly, the sisters agreed.

“Why should they invite us?” Marianne asked as the guests departed.

“The rent here is said to be low,” Elinor replied, “but it’s a hard bargain if we must dine at the park every time someone stays with either side.”

“They mean to be kind,” Elinor said gently, “just as they were weeks ago. Their hospitality hasn’t changed. If their gatherings seem dull now, the fault lies elsewhere—not with them.”
