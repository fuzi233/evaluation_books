Dear Blanche,

You’re always writing novels in your head—dramas with grand entrances, tragic misunderstandings, and lovers who meet under moonlit balconies. Well, here’s one for you: not the fairy-tale ending where the heroine inherits a castle and marries a prince, but the far more realistic version—where the heroine discovers she’s been tricked into marrying a man whose only fortune is a broken teacup and a questionable future.

Let’s be honest: I’m not offering you the life of a duchess. I’m offering you the life of a man who’s just realized that all his dreams have gone up in smoke like a poorly lit cigar. No stars on my coat. No title. No mansion in Belgravia (though I did once dream of it). Instead, I offer you me—a writer with two half-finished novels and a flair for melodrama. I’ll be called to the Bar, maybe even get a colonial post if I work harder than I ever have in my life (which, let’s face it, is asking a lot). And I’ll buy back the *Pall Mall Gazette*—it’s practically free now that poor Shandon died, and the publishers are eager to offload it. Warrington will help me run it. He’ll write the editorials, I’ll write the satire, and together we’ll make it respectable enough to sell copies. You can supervise the fashion column, break hearts in Poet’s Corner, and occasionally pretend you’re part of the elite. We’ll live over the offices in Catherine Street, Strand—four rooms, a kitchen, and a garret for Laura. Or perhaps we’ll move to Waterloo Road? It’s lovely, really—except for that pesky halfpenny toll at the bridge.

The boys can go to King’s College, yes? That’s settled. Does this sound like a joke?

No. It’s not a joke. It’s the truth. The carriage has vanished. The house in Belgravia? Gone with the wind. My seat in Parliament? Cancelled. I’ve written to Sir Francis Clavering, explaining I cannot accept the nomination until after marriage. And I won’t take any larger fortune from you than what you’ve always had—since your grandfather died and your half-brother was born. Your mother doesn’t know why I’ve made this decision. She shouldn’t. The reason is painful, unavoidable, and as final as Alnaschar kicking over his porcelain. Once shattered, no amount of glue or goodwill can mend it.

So here’s the question: Will you marry me anyway? Not because I’m rich or noble, but because you love me? Because you’re willing to walk beside me through humdrum days, mending my socks, sharing a cab when we’re lucky, and laughing at the absurdity of it all? If you say yes, come to me, Blanche. God help me, I’ll do my best to make you happy. If not—if you’d rather climb the social ladder and ride in a chariot while I stand in the crowd watching—I’ll understand. I’ll even smile at you from the sidewalk. I saw Lady Mirabel last season—her husband glittering with medals, her coachman wearing flowers like a peacock. Would you prefer that? Or would you rather walk beside me, hand in hand, through ordinary life?

I can’t tell you yet what happened—what changed everything. But it’s done. I’ve given up my ambitions. I’ve accepted my fate. And if you’ll have me, I’ll accept yours.

Now, about other news: Uncle Pendennis is unwell and furious that I turned down the seat. He’d planned it all. Poor old fellow. But Warrington, Laura, and I have stood by me. They know the secret. They support me. George is noble. Laura is our saint. With such friends, who needs the world?

Blanche’s reply arrived—not to me, but to Laura first, then to me. It was written in her neatest hand, on her scented paper, full of poetry and pain. But the meaning? That was a puzzle.

She wrote:
“You are spoiled by the world. You don’t love me as I deserve. You offer me lightly—take me or leave me. But I want more than that. I want your whole soul. I want to be your everything. You speak of separation with a smile. You don’t hurry to see me. Is life nothing but disillusion? I’ve wept. I’ve prayed. I’ve shed bitter tears over your letter. You treat my love like dross—like something cheap. You ask me to bow to you, as if I were a servant. Is this how love ends? For me? Always?”

And then she signed: “B.”

I read it twice. Three times. I laughed—sardonically. What a mess! What a beautiful, heartbreaking mess! She loves me—but thinks I don’t love her. She wants to stay—but fears I’ll abandon her. She’s torn between duty and desire. And I’m left wondering: does she accept me? Or reject me? Is she surrendering to me out of pity—or is she rejecting me because she knows too much?

I sat there, twirling the letter like a magician’s wand, enjoying the irony of it all. Then my servant came in with a card: “Mr. Samuel Huxter, on particular business.”

I invited him in—amused. And there he stood, sucking his stick, eyes wide, face pale, looking like a man who’d just seen a ghost.

“Pray sit,” I said, “and tell me what trouble has found you.”

“I’m in a fix,” he said, grimly.

“Indeed?”

“She sent me to you,” he continued.

“Fanny?” I asked. “Is she well? I meant to visit her.”

“I heard through my governor and Jack Hobnell,” Huxter said. “Congratulations, sir—on the borough and the lady. Fanny sends her congratulations too.” He blushed.

“There’s many a slip between the cup and the lip,” I said. “Who knows what may happen?”

“I’ve done it,” he said, dropping his voice. “I’ve married her.”

I jumped up. “You—you didn’t—do anything wrong to that dear creature?”

“I hope not,” he said, hanging his head. “But I’ve married her. And now my father’s coming to town. He’ll be here tomorrow. Then it all comes out.”

“And when did this happen?” I asked.

“Five weeks ago. Two days after Miss Amory came to Shepherd’s Inn.”

Ah. So that’s why Blanche was so quiet lately. She knew. She’d known for a month. And she wasn’t going to implicate her father. She was letting me off the hook—gently, gracefully, with a letter full of sorrow and sacrifice.

“Do you know who Altamont is?” Huxter asked suddenly.

We both looked at each other. I remembered the scene at Chambers—the hysterical woman, the crying girl, the fuming colonel. “Altamont,” I said slowly, “is Mrs. Lightfoot’s first husband. Come back to life.”

Huxter nodded. “Fanny and I think so. Maybe Lightfoot won’t mind.”

Jealousy flared in his eyes. Poor fool. Now that he was married, he still thought Fanny loved me.

“Let’s talk about your affairs,” I said. “How can I help?”

“He thinks you can,” Huxter said, taking my hand. “He says you might talk to my father. Break the news gently. My mother—she’s a clergyman’s daughter. She hates being lower than us. Fanny’s not from a good family. But she’s a Huxter now.”

“The wife takes the husband’s rank,” I said.

“And with practice,” Huxter added, “she’ll be better than any girl in Clavering. You should hear her sing. Old Bows taught her. She’s got talent. Might even go on stage if things fall apart. But I’d rather not.”

“She’s a coquette,” I said.

“She can’t help it,” Huxter said. “If there are twenty men in a room, and one ignores her, she won’t rest until the twentieth is at her side.”

“Then bring her mother,” I said, laughing.

“She must keep the lodge. I can’t stay with that lot. Consider my rank.”

“Au fait,” I said, amused. “And mutato nomine, this story could be told of anyone—anywhere.”

Just then, another knock. The servant announced: “Mr. Bows.”

In walked the old music teacher—pale, trembling, face flushed. He sat down, hands on knees, sun shining on his bald head. I looked at him with sympathy. This man had loved Fanny. He’d given her lessons, time, affection. And she’d chosen someone else.

“So we’ve lost,” I said. “Here is the lucky winner.”

“Yes, sir,” Bows said quietly. “Here is the lucky winner.”

“I suppose you came from my place?” Huxter asked.

“Yes,” Bows said. “Mrs. Sam sent me. She thought you might do something stupid—something like yourself.”

“Big fools,” growled Huxter.

“A few,” Bows said. “Not many, I hope.”

“She wanted me to deliver a message,” Bows continued. “To you. But you wouldn’t give it. So she sent me.”

Pen laughed. The scene delighted me—this mix of jealousy, pride, and absurdity.

“I’ll pay my respects to your wife,” I said.

“In Charterhouse Lane,” Bows said. “Over the baker’s. St. John’s Street leads into Smithfield. Doctor Johnson used to walk there with ragged shoes and a bundle of penny-a-lining.”

“I’ve seen worse luck than mine,” I said. “I’ve seen quacks succeed and brave men fail. Don’t think I’m proud because I’ve won. The prizes of life aren’t for the deserving. You’re as good as I am. It’s just that fortune favors some, and not others.”

Bows wiped his forehead. “Perhaps you’re right. I’ve been grumbling. Most men do when they think about themselves.”

“I don’t know what you mean,” Huxter said, confused.

“Mrs. H. sent me to watch over you,” Bows said. “She knew you wouldn’t deliver the message. And she knew I would. She wanted you to come back.”

Huxter stood up, grabbed his hat, and fled.

“I thought that would send him out,” Bows said, sinking back into his chair.

“It’s all true,” he continued. “She wants you back. She cajoles everyone—me, Costigan, the young doctors from Bartholomew’s. She’s got a little court already. If there’s no one around, she practices on the baker or the black sweeper.”

“Is she fond of him?” I asked.

“There’s no accounting for likes,” Bows said. “Yes, she is. She decided to marry him. They published banns at St. Clement’s. Nobody knew. One day she slipped out of the lodge, married him, and went to Gravesend. Left a note for me to explain to her mother. Bless you, the old woman knew all along.”

“I miss her,” Bows said. “Tripping down the court. Coming for her lesson. Now the lodge feels empty. I go and sit outside her door like an old fool. But she keeps it neat. She sings like a lark. She cooks his dinner. She’s happy. What’s the use of being angry? I lent them three pounds. They’ve got nothing till the reconciliation.”

When Bows left, I took Blanche’s letter and Huxter’s news to Laura.

“Doesn’t it seem odd?” I asked. “That she avoids asking why I’ve changed my mind?”

Laura was pale. “It seems to me,” she said, “that Blanche is playing a game. She wants to keep her options open.”

“And if there’s someone else?” I asked.

“You’re not free, Arthur,” Laura said calmly. “You belong to another. And I can’t help thinking… she knows. She knows her father’s secret.”

And so we sat, two cousins, two people caught in the web of fate, love, and misfortune—while the world kept turning, and the next chapter waited to unfold.
