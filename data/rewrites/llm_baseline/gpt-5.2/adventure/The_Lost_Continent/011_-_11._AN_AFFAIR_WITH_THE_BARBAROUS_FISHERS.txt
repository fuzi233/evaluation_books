So the mightiest woman in Atlantis chose to be jealous of a captive.

The moment the words were out of her mouth my mind went soldier-quick, as it has been trained to do when the world tilts and you must catch your footing or be dashed under. I had come back to this doomed splendour to spend myself for Atlantis because the High Council commanded it; and if sacrifice were demanded, then—Gods forgive me—Nais might be counted among the offerings. Yet even as that bitter obedience hardened in me, another thought leapt beside it: a narrow path by which she might still be spared.

I bowed with the gravity Phorenice expected, and answered in the measured tones one uses to keep a blade from flashing too soon. “In this, and in all other things where a mere human hand is potent, I will carry out your wishes, Phorenice.”

Her fingers—cool, light, possessive—rested a moment on my arm, and her eyes, those deep wells that could look tender while plotting, brimmed with fresh feeling as if I had given her a gift. The Gods won her half her battles in the curve of her mouth and the music of her voice; it was a weapon as sharp as any sword.

Around the royal quay the neighbourhood had gathered: merchants, porters, waterside folk, all quick to offer a change of clothing, a doorway, a bath, a seat by a brazier. The basin into which the mammoth had flung us was foul enough—stale fish brine, bilge slop, rotting weed—so that even I, who can ignore wet wool when necessary, was glad to be rid of it. Phorenice, gracious as a queen at festival, accepted help as if it were tribute. She said little of Nais, but she did not forget her; before she allowed herself to be led away she gave the port-captain strict orders for guarding his prisoner, with the sweetness of a woman choosing a ribbon.

In the stone house they gave me a bath. The water steamed; it smelled faintly of crushed bay and ash-lye, and when the filth of the harbour finally slid off my skin it felt as if I had peeled away a whole layer of the city’s decay. I took the plainest garment my host owned—coarse-woven, undyed, honest cloth—and he was so relieved to be “robbed” cheaply that his thanks were almost fervent.

Afterward I spent an hour on the pavement outside, listening to the distant din of bombardment: a dull, recurrent thump like a giant pounding bread, the air shivering after each blow; somewhere nearer a tower bell rang in a nervous, scattered way, the sound swallowed and spat back by alleys. Even before I saw her again, I could hear Atlantis’s soundscape—its old choruses of oars, market cries, temple flutes—being drowned under war’s iron percussion.

When Phorenice came out, she was not merely dry and changed—she was adorned. Behind her shuffled the merchant, and it took no priestly insight to read ruin on his face. He had produced a store of jewels and gauds, much of it held in partnership, and Phorenice had taken what pleased her eye as simply as a child scoops honey.

“Are they not pretty?” she asked, turning her wrists so the sun struck sparks from bracelets and rings. “See how they flash. I am quite glad now, Deucalion, that the mammoth gave us that furious ride and that spill, since it has brought me such a bonny present. You may tell the fellow here that some day, when he has earned some more, I will come and be his guest again.”

Litters waited. She tilted her head toward them like a woman choosing which dish to taste first.

“Send one away,” she said, “and do you share mine with me, sir. We must play at being lovers to-day, even if love is a matter which will come to us both with more certainty to-morrow. No; do not order more bearers. My own slaves will carry us handily enough. I am glad you are not one of your gross overfed men, Deucalion. I am small and slim myself, and I do not want to be husbanded by a man who will overshadow me.”

“Back to the royal pyramid?” I asked.

“No—nor to the walls. I neither wish to fight nor to sit as Empress to-day. It is my whim to be Phorenice, the maiden, for a few hours; and if some one I wot of would woo me now as other maidens are wooed, I should esteem it a luxury. Bid the slaves carry us round the harbour’s rim, and give word to these starers that if they follow I will call down fire upon them as I did upon the sacrifice.”

I had already seen enough of the city’s unruliness to know that a harbour-side holiday was a risky whim, but she knew Atlantis’s pulse better than any man living and would not be warned. I dismissed the guard that had come up. I drove back the gaping knot of sightseers—those silly creatures who must stare at power as if it were a painted idol—and took my place beside her as she bade. The bearers dipped their heads to the pole, and we swung away.

We moved along the wide pavement between merchants’ houses and the dimpling water. The harbour smelled of tar and salt and old fish; once, in happier generations, those odours had meant wealth and movement and full bellies. Now they carried another note: emptiness. Too few ships lay on that splendid basin. Too few hands shouted over cargo. The quays looked like a feast-table after the guests have fled—plenty of stone and space, but no laughter, no clatter, no comforting rhythm of work.

In old days—when oar and sail did most of the world’s travelling and sun-drawn machines were only a clever aid—trade had roared. Ships had lain so close that many waited outside in the estuary, and memorials had been sent to the King to double the port. A voyage to Egypt and back took a year then, and of three ships that set out, one at least could be counted on to perish to deep water and the beasts that own it. Yet commerce had been alive, growing. Merchants had grown portly and pious; they had given generously to the Gods and to their neighbours’ tables.

Now all Atlantis seemed in arms, and chaffering was called vulgar. A merchant with substance forgot his ledgers, curled his beard after court fashion, and practiced the empty arts of banquet and fine sewing. Where his father had spent anxious days with cargo tally and ship-master, the son spent his nights among perfumed lamps, breathing spiced meats he did not earn and listening to musicians paid to drown the city’s hungry cough.

Those smaller merchants without savings were crushed by taxes and constant war-bickering. Markets vanished as districts rose and fell in rebellion. Nothing new was made that could be carried abroad: a weaver pressed into tending a fire-tube makes no cloth, and a farmer playing at revolt does not buy slaves to till his fields. They told me that not long before my return a cargo of slaves—fine bodies, doomed as always—had been brought into harbour; and there had been nothing for it but to set them ashore across the estuary and leave them free to starve or survive in the wild ground as they could. No man in Atlantis would take even one more slave, not as gift, not at any price. The very appetite for ownership had sickened.

All this grieved me, but remedy would be for afterward. It would only sour the hour to speak of it in the litter beside Phorenice. I knew her moods well enough to keep silence. And I think she, too, had learned to read my face; between her bursts of trifling she tried to show me wonders, as one offers a dish with a brighter sauce when one sees a guest has lost his taste.

We went out to where the westernmost harbour wall joined the land. The bearers, sweating, set us down. Phorenice led me toward a small stone house built alone on a promontory where tide ran constantly, whispering and sucking against the rock in a steady breath—so different from the harsh metallic uproar of the city’s engines and war-horns. Admission took much unbarring. Inside she showed me what she called her gold collectors.

In the dry learning of the Sacred Mountain, we had been taught that gold lies dissolved in sea-water. It was one of those facts filed away like a forgotten herb in a scholar’s cupboard: interesting, inert, seldom useful. Of any practical method to coax it into yellow hardness, none had ever been discussed. Yet here, this field-reared upstart Empress had found a trick as though a household recipe had been whispered to her over the kneading-trough.

She laughed, patting my arm while I stared. “I tell all others in Atlantis that only the Gods have this secret,” she said, “and that They gave it to me as one of themselves. But I am no Goddess to you, am I, Deucalion? And by my face, I have no other explanation of how this plan was invented. We’ll suppose I must have dreamed it.”

She pointed, and I leaned close. Sea-water sluiced in through a culvert and flowed over rough metal plates set in the floor, their surfaces ridged like a rasp. The stream ran out again on its natural course, never stopping, never tiring. In the grooves, caught like pollen in a comb, were yellow flecks—gold, stubbornly real. The air smelled of wet stone and salt; deeper in, a fire burned where her men melted the gathered grains into bars, and the smoke had that sharp, bitter tang of hot metal.

“You see it caught in the ridges?” she said. “That is gold. My fellows melt it with fire into bars and take it to my smith’s in the city. The tides vary constantly, as you priests know well, as the quiet moon draws them. It does not take much figuring to know how much sea passes through these culverts in a month, and how much gold to a grain should be caught in the plates. My fellows here at first thought to cheat me, but I towed two of them in the water once behind a galley till the cannibal fish ate them, and since then the others have given me credit for—for what do you think?”

“More divinity,” I said.

“I suppose it is that. But I am letting you see how it is done. Just have the head to work out a little sum, and see what an effect can be gained. You will be a God yet yourself, Deucalion, with these silly Atlanteans, if only you will use your wit and cleverness.”

Was she mocking me, or teaching me? With Phorenice it was hard to tell. One moment she set all down to calculation and brilliance; the next she offered some impossible tale and dared you to contradict her. She wrapped her power in myth as neatly as she wrapped herself in stolen jewels, and the crowd—hungry for wonders—chose to be dazzled.

The longer I stood beside her, the more she mystified me; and the more despairing I grew that Atlantis could ever be coaxed back to peace and the ancient Gods even after I had done what the Council commanded and taken this woman to wife. Sometimes one plan seemed brutally simple: curb her mischief by death and let the country recover in its own slow, natural way, like a field left fallow after a bitter crop. A dagger-stroke—done. Yet the idea sickened me. Each time that thought rose, I clung to the answer that if it were fitting, the High Gods in Their wisdom would have given me explicit command.

Still, such was the fascination of Phorenice that when we left her gold collectors and walked straight into peril where a withheld hand might have delivered her neatly to the nether Gods, I found myself fighting for her when she called—fighting as I have seldom fought. Some of that was the old lust of battle that wakes even in cold men when cries rise and steel begins to sing. But some of it—no use denying—was the sheer pleasure of protecting her, and the distaste of seeing her dragged down by rude, uncouth fishers, smelling of old nets and rancid oil, who would paw at her jewels as if they were fish-guts on a board.

The cause lay in the city’s unrest and street violence, and in the desperate poverty that drives people to risk anything for the price of a meal. We had mounted the litter again; the bearers swung us forward at their steady trot. Phorenice was talking, lightly as if discussing recipes for a feast, about her new supplies of gold. She had made fresh sumptuary laws.

“In the old days,” she said, “when yellow gold was tediously dredged grain by grain from river gravels in dangerous lands, a quill full would cost a rich man’s savings. So none but those whose high station fitted them to be so adorned could wear golden ornaments. But when the sea-water gave me gold here by the double handful a day, I found the price of those river hoards decreased, and one day—could you credit it?—a common fellow, one of my smiths, came to me wearing a collar of yellow gold on his own common neck. Well, I had that neck divided, as payment for his presumption. And as I promised to repeat the division promptly on all other offenders, that special species of forwardness seems checked for the time. There are many exasperations, Deucalion, in governing these common people.”

She would have gone on, but I saw two clumsy fisher boats paddling toward us over the ripples, hide-covered, low in the water; and at the same time, among the narrow lanes between the houses on the landward side, savage-faced men began to run after the litter in threatening clusters. Their feet slapped the dust; their voices rose in a ragged chorus, harsh as gulls fighting over offal.

“With permission,” I said, “I will step out and scatter this rabble.”

“Oh, the people always cluster round me,” she replied, amused. “Poor ugly souls—they take a strange delight in coming to stare at my pretty looks. But scatter them. I have said I did not wish to be followed. I am taking holiday now, Deucalion, am I not, whilst you learn to woo me?”

I stepped down. The fishers in the boats were already shouting to those darting among the huts to see we did not escape, and more bodies poured out every moment. We had passed beyond the merchants’ quarter and were level with the mud-and-grass huts the fishing folk deem sufficient shelter. Even in the old King’s peaceful days this part of the city was seldom without its weekly riot; now it was tinder-dry.

A fisherman’s life is hard beyond most. Wind and wave batter them; firestones from the sky can pelt them; great beasts in the sea hunt them with savage persistence. It is rare that some man of their guild does not fail to come home for tally. And their manner of fishing is itself a gamble.

Each man keeps a large sea-fowl taken as a nestling, trained to dive. A bronze ring is set round its neck to prevent it swallowing the catch; for each fish it brings to the boat, head and tail and inwards are given as reward, the ring removed while it eats. The birds are faithful once trained, seldom deserting their owners; yet their lives are as precarious as the men’s. Bigger fish and beasts take them as easily as they take the smaller prey.

Worse still: if a fisher’s bird is slain, and the second he has in training also dies, he is suddenly bereft of livelihood. The guild will not help. They say the sea Gods have judged him displeasing and robbed him of his birds; to aid him would be impious. So he may starve or rob as he chooses. That belief, hammered into them with hunger, makes them desperate men; they spend a month’s gains on one night’s debauch because tomorrow might be death. Their one point of honour is not to be curbed by ordinary fear of consequences. Their speech is harsh, and in it—half sea-slang, half old dialect—there are words for winds, currents, fish-fat, gutting, bait and brine that no priest in the upland groves can translate without losing their taste. It is a language born of salt and danger; and yet those same mouths were now filled with hunger’s simplest lexicon: give.

I went quickly toward the largest knot of them skulking behind the houses. The litter halted behind me.

“Disperse,” I ordered. “For employment, put your houses in order and clean the fish offal from the lanes between them. To-morrow I will come to inspect and put this quarter into better order. But for to-day the Empress—whose name be adored—wishes privacy. Cease your staring.”

“Then give us money,” piped a shrill voice from among the huts.

“I will send you a torch in an hour’s time,” I said grimly, “and rig you a gallows, if you give me more annoyance. To your kennels, you.”

They would have obeyed—authority still had a taste they remembered, bitter but familiar. There was a stir; those in sunlight slid instinctively into shadow; many dodged into houses and crouched in dark corners. But the men in the two boats called them back with boisterous cries, and the crowd’s courage thickened like bad stew when more fat is thrown in.

I signed to the litter-bearers to move on quickly. There was need for discipline, and I meant to deal it out myself. If Phorenice, glittering and jeweled, remained in sight, she was bait for robbery; if she were gone, I could cow the rest more cleanly.

But I had been presumptuous. One does not give orders about Phorenice’s person and expect obedience. She bade the bearers stand. She stepped out and drew weapons from beneath the cushions: a sword she strapped to her hip, and on her left forearm she set a well-dinted target of gold. She came toward me laughing.

“An unfair trick,” she cried. “If you keep a fight to yourself now, Deucalion, where will your greediness carry you when I am your shrinking, wistful little wife? Are these fools truly going to stand up against us?”

I had not coveted a fight, but it had chosen us. The robe and glittering gauds she had stripped from the merchant drew eyes like flies to sweet. The fishers paddled their boats into the surf and leaped out, leaving the frail basket-work hulls to be spewed up or smashed as chance ordered. From huts and filthy lanes poured more—women with the men—closing in until their bodies made a wall.

“Have a care,” shouted one on the outskirts. “She called down fire for the sacrifice once to-day, and she can burn up others here if she chooses.”

“So much the more for those that are left,” another jeered. “She cannot burn all.”

“Nay, I will not burn any,” Phorenice said, bright with malice, “but you shall look upon my sword-play till you are tired.”

I heard and understood her cruelty. I was one of the Seven; I knew how she had summoned the sky-fire to take that cloven-hoofed horse offered in sacrifice—and I knew, too, that she could summon no such fire here. She was feeding them myth as one feeds a starving crowd thin broth: it warms, it deceives, it keeps them obedient for a moment. Yet they gave us no time for argument. They feared an escort might be near; whatever they stole, they must steal quickly.

They surged in. The reek of them—sweat turned sour, fish slime, rancid oil, old smoke—made the throat catch. A grimy hand shot out to seize the jewels on Phorenice’s breast, and I lopped it off at the elbow. It fell at her feet with a wet sound, and the fight began.

“Your back to mine, comrade!” she cried, laughing, and drew and laid about her with fine dexterity.

It was slaughter, that first bout. Their greed jammed them too close; they had no room to draw back an elbow for proper thrust. We kept a circle by sheer whirling of steel. In such bouts you must work cleanly: a wounded man on the ground, half-seen, is as dangerous as a snake. As we circled I noted that all of Phorenice’s quarry lay still. By the Gods, she could play a sword. She touched life with every thrust.

The throne of Atlantis had not been won by face and tongue alone. Seeing her fight did not lessen her in my eyes as a fighter; it only made her more terrible. I caught glimpses of her out of the corner of my eye—small, quick, splendid in stolen gold—while I dealt with the press before me. The fishers had little knowledge of fence; their weapons were clumsy. But they had numbers and savage ferocity, and they believed they could pull us down by sheer hunger. More than once I felt bodies catch at my arms and shoulders like nets, and if I had not contrived to wrench a shield from a fool who came too proudly near, I cannot swear they would not have dragged me down.

Through the burly uproar—grunts, shrieks, the wet smack of blade into flesh, the scuffle of feet in filth—came very pleasantly, absurdly, Phorenice’s cry of “Deucalion!” which she chose as her battle shout. It rose above the din like a bell rung in a riot. I knew she was a past-mistress of compliment; I had heard my name roared over battle before. But here it was under peculiar circumstances, and for the life of me I could not help being tickled by the flattery.

Condemn my weakness if you will: I came very near then to liking the Empress in the way she wished. And as for the other woman who should have filled my mind, I confess the fury of the moment drove Nais and her strait clean beyond the marches of memory. Of such frail stuff are we made, even those of us who think ourselves strongest.

There is a temptation few born to the sword can resist: to give yourself to fight for fight’s own sake, to let the body answer the music of steel. Women, it seems, can be bitten by the same fierce infection. The attack slackened. We stood in the middle of a ring of twisted dead; the rest shrank back out of reach.

It was the moment for truce, the moment for a few strong words to send them cowering to their huts and give us passage. But Phorenice, drunk on her own skill, could not leave it there. She began to sing a hymn to our invulnerability, gloating over our feats; then she demanded payment for the litter-bearers they had killed; then she spoke, with bright relish, of the burnings and skinnings and sawings asunder with which this quarter would be treated soon, until they learned deportment and genteel manners.

“It makes your backs creep, does it?” she taunted. “I do not wonder. This severity must have its unpleasant side. But why do you not put it beyond my power to give the order? Either you must think yourselves Gods or me no Goddess, or you would not have gone on so far. Come now, you nasty-smelling people—follow out your theory. And if you make a good fight of it, I swear by my face I will be lenient with those who do not fall.”
