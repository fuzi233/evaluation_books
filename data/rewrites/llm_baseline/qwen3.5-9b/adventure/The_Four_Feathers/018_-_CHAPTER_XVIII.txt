Ethne did not turn toward Durrance, nor did she shift from her rigid posture. Seated with her violin resting on her knees, she stared across the moonlit garden at the band of silver light breaking through the tree line. She held her position deliberately, for it aided her delusion: believing Harry Feversham was speaking directly to her allowed her to forget that he was speaking through Durrance's voice. She nearly forgot Durrance existed in the room entirely. She listened with his own intensity, wishing the voice would drag its tale out slowly, allowing her to hoard every syllable jealously in her heart.

"It was on the night before I started eastward into the desert—for the last time," Durrance said. The deep longing and regret usually accompanying that phrase left Ethne completely unmoved.

"Yes," she replied. "That was in February. Mid-month, wasn't it? Tell me the exact day if you can."

"The fifteenth," Durrance answered. Ethne repeated the date thoughtfully.

"I was at Glenalla all of February," she said. "What was I doing on the fifteenth? It doesn't matter."

A strange surprise had plagued her since Willoughby recounted his story that morning—the realization that she possessed no instinctive knowledge of these events as they happened. Now, that surprise returned with force. It was bizarre that she had to wait for this August night, this garden of moonlight and closed blooms, to learn of the meeting between Feversham and Durrance on February 15th and receive the message. Remorse washed over her for that delay.

"It was my own fault," she whispered to herself. "If I had kept my faith in him, I should have known at once. I am well punished."

She did not conceive that the message could contain anything but excellent news. It would surely carry the good tidings she had already heard, enlarging and completing them until this day reached perfection. She was certain of this.

"Well?" she urged. "Go on!"

"I had been busy all day in my office, finishing my work. I turned the key at ten o'clock, relieved that for six weeks I wouldn't open it again, and strolled northward out of Wadi Halfa along the Nile bank into the little town of Tewfikieh. As I entered the main street, I saw a small crowd—Arabs, negroes, a Greek or two, and some Egyptian soldiers—standing outside a café, illuminated by a glare from within. Approaching closer, I heard the sound of a violin and a zither, both played most vilely, jingling out a waltz.

I stood behind the crowd, peering over the shoulders of the men ahead into the room. It was a place of four bare, whitewashed walls; a bar in one corner, a couple of wooden benches against the others, and a single unshaded paraffin lamp swinging and glaring from the ceiling. A troupe of itinerant musicians played for a night's lodging and a meal to that gathering of negroes, Arabs, and Egyptians. There were four of them, and judging by appearance, all were Greeks. Two were evidently a married couple: both old, both slatternly and almost in rags. The man was thin and sallow-haired with a black moustache; the woman was fat, coarse-faced, and unwieldy.

Of the other two, one appeared to be their daughter, a girl of seventeen. She wasn't particularly beautiful, but she was dressed and groomed with scrupulous care, which lent her an air of respectability amidst the sordid surroundings. The care with which she was attired assured me she was their daughter. To tell the truth, I was touched by the thought that the parents would go in rags so that she might appear trim. A clean ribbon bound back her hair; an untorn white frock clad her neatly; even her shoes were neat.

The fourth was a young man, seated in the window with his back to me, bending over his zither. But I could see he wore a beard. When I approached, the old man was playing the violin—or rather, making a noise more like a pencil squeaking on a slate. It grated on one's teeth; the instrument itself seemed to scream in pain. While he fiddled and the young man hammered at his zither, the old woman and girl slowly revolved in a waltz. It sounds comic to recount, but if you had seen it... it fairly plucked at one's heart. I have never witnessed anything quite so sad.

Outside, the crowd of negroes laughed at the troupe, trading low jests among themselves. Inside, the four whites: the old woman, clumsy and heavy-footed, shining with heat, lumbering round slowly, panting; the girl, lissom and young; the two men producing discordant, torturous music; and just above, the great planets and stars of an African sky, and just below, the great silent dignity of the moonlit desert. Imagine it! The sheer ineptness of the entertainment actually hurt."

Durrance paused while Ethne visualized the scene. She saw Harry Feversham bending over his zither and immediately asked herself, *What was he doing with that troupe?*

It was understandable that he wouldn't wish to return to England. It was certain he wouldn't come back to her unless she summoned him. From Captain Willoughby, she knew he expected no message from her; no address had been left where a letter could reach him. But what was he doing at Wadi Halfa, masquerading with this itinerant group? He had money, Willoughby had said.

"You spoke to him?" Ethne asked suddenly.

"To whom? Oh, to Harry?" Durrance corrected gently.

"Yes, afterwards, when I found out it was he playing the zither."

"Yes, how did you discover it?"

"The waltz ended. The old woman sank exhausted onto the bench against the whitewashed wall; the young man raised his head from his zither; the old man scraped a new chord on his violin, and the girl stepped forward to sing. Her voice had youth and freshness, but no musical quality. Her singing was as inept as the rest. Yet the old man smiled, the mother beat time with her heavy foot, and nodded at her husband with pride in their daughter's accomplishment. Again, the ill-conditioned talk and untranslatable jests of the Arabs and negroes circulated through the throng. It was horrible, don't you think?"

"Yes," Ethne answered slowly, in an absent tone. Feeling no sympathy for Durrance when he began, she spared none for these three outcasts of fortune. She was too absorbed in the mystery of Harry Feversham's presence at Wadi Halfa, listening too intently for the message he sent.

Through the open window, the moon cast a broad panel of silver light upon the floor near her feet. She sat gazing into it as she listened, as though it were a window through which, if she looked hard enough, she might see that lighted café blazing upon the street of Tewfikieh on the Sudan frontier.

"Well?" she prompted. "And after the song?"

"The young man with his back to me began to fumble out a solo on the zither," Durrance resumed. "He struck so many false notes that no tune was apprehensible at first. Laughter and noise grew among the crowd, and I was just turning away, rather sick at heart, when some notes—a succession of notes played correctly by chance—suddenly arrested me. I listened again, and a sort of haunting melody began to emerge. It was a weak, thin thing with no soul, a ghost of a melody, yet familiar.

I stood listening in the street of sand, between hovels fringed by stunted trees, and I was carried away from the East to Ramelton, to a summer night beneath a melting Donegal sky, when you sat by the open window as you sit now, playing the *Musoline* Overture, which you have played tonight."

"It was a melody from this overture?" she exclaimed.

"Yes, and it was Harry Feversham who played it. I didn't guess it at once. I wasn't very quick in those days."

"But you are now," Ethne said. "Quicker, at least. I should have guessed it now. Then, however, I was only curious. I wondered how an itinerant Greek picked up the tune. At all events, I determined to reward him for his diligence. I thought you would like me to."

"Yes," Ethne whispered.

"So, when he came out from the café, hat in hand, passing through the jeering crowd, I threw a sovereign into the hat. He turned to me with a start of surprise. In spite of his beard, I knew him. Before he could check himself, he cried out, 'Jack!'"

"You can have made no mistake, then," Ethne said in a wondering voice.

"No, the man who strummed the zither was—" the Christian name was on her lips, but she had the wit to catch it back unspoken—"was Mr. Feversham. But he knew no music I remember very well." She laughed briefly, recalling Feversham's total inability to appreciate any music except that which she drew from her violin. "He had no ear. You couldn't invent a discord harsh enough to attract his attention. He could never have remembered any melody from the *Musoline* Overture."
