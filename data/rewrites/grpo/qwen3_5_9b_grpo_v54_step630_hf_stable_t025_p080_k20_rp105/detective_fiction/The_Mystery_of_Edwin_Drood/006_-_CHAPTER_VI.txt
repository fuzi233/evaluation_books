The Reverend Septimus Crisparkle, a minor canon whose lineage had seen six predecessors extinguished like fragile rushlights before his birth, found his morning invigorated by breaking the thin ice of Cloisterham Weir with his head. Now, he was engaging in a vigorous exercise of self-defense, boxing a mirror with scientific precision. The glass reflected a man of radiant innocence and benevolent intent, feinting with art and striking with straight, powerful blows.

It was barely breakfast time. Mrs. Crisparkle, his widowed mother, had just arrived, waiting for the tea urn. At this precise moment, Septimus paused his assault to kiss her face, his gloved hands tenderly framing hers. Once released, he resumed his vigorous counter-attacks, moving with tremendous energy.

"I say, Sept," the old lady observed, watching him, "you'll break the pier-glass or burst a blood-vessel at last."

"Neither, please God, Ma dear. Here's wind!" Septimus declared. He delivered a final, severe round, ending with a light tap that sent her cap flying into the air—a maneuver he termed "Chancery"—without disturbing a single ribbon. Magnanimously, he released her, tucked his gloves away, and feigned contemplation by the window until a servant entered.

Once the breakfast preparations were complete, the two sat alone. It was a serene scene: the mother reciting the Lord's Prayer aloud, her voice clear and bright, while her son, nearly forty, bowed his head in reverent silence, just as he had when he was nearly four.

Mrs. Crisparkle was a vision of trim elegance, her dress as dainty as a china shepherdess, her face cheerful and calm. Septimus often thought nothing was prettier than her. Her entire existence seemed to revolve around the two words she used most often: "My Sept!"

They were a perfect pair in the quiet shadow of the Cathedral, where the cawing of rooks and the distant tolling of bells seemed to enhance the silence rather than disturb it. The history of Minor Canon Corner—its fighting men, its drudging serfs, its powerful monks—had all faded, leaving behind a tranquil air and a romantic sense of pity born from stories fully told.

Surrounded by red-brick walls, strong ivy, and stone gardens where monkish trees still bore fruit, Septimus asked, "And what, Ma dear, does the letter say?"

His mother handed him the note, though she kept her own spectacles off, proud of her sharp eyesight. Septimus, equally proud of his inability to read without aid, donned a pair of grave, oversized glasses that obscured his view and inconvenienced his breakfast.

"It's from Mr. Honeythunder," his mother said, folding her arms.

"Of course," Septimus agreed, struggling to read the script. "'Haven of Philanthropy... I write in the—'"

"In the chair," his mother supplied.

"Why, what should he write in?" Septimus asked, removing his glasses to see her face clearly.

"You don't see the context, Sept!" she exclaimed gently. "Give it back."

Relieved to remove the water-inducing lenses, Septimus handed the letter back. His mother read with precision: "'I write from the chair, to which I shall probably be confined for some hours.' Septimus looked at the row of chairs with a half-protesting face.

"'We have a meeting of our Convened Chief Composite Committee,' she continued, 'and it is their unanimous pleasure that I take the chair.' Septimus breathed easier. 'Not to lose a day's post, I take the opportunity of a long report being read, denouncing a public miscreant—'"

"It is extraordinary," Septimus interrupted, rubbing his ear in vexation, "that these philanthropists are always denouncing someone. And that they are always so flush with miscreants!"

"'Denouncing a public miscreant' to get our business off my mind," his mother read on. "I have spoken with my wards, Neville and Helena Landless, regarding their defective education. They agree to the plan. Therefore, prepare your son to expect Neville as an inmate next Monday. Helena will accompany him to Cloisterham to take up quarters at the Nuns' House, the establishment you and I recommended. The terms are as stated in my correspondence with you. With compliments to the Rev. Mr. Septimus, I am, Dear Madam, Your affectionate brother (In Philanthropy), LUKE HONEYTHUNDER.'"

"Well, Ma," Septimus said after more ear-rubbing, "we must try it. We have room, time, and inclination. I confess I'm glad it isn't Mr. Honeythunder himself. Though that seems prejudiced, doesn't it? Is he a large man?"

"I call him a large man," his mother hesitated, "but his voice is much larger."

"Than himself?"

"Than anybody."

"Hah!" Septimus sighed, finishing his breakfast with diminished enthusiasm.

Mrs. Crisparkle's sister, a delicate ornament matching her perfectly, lived in London with her clergyman husband. Mr. Honeythunder, a Professor of Philanthropy, had met them during her annual visit, following a public event where orphaned children were fed plum buns. These were the only known antecedents of the incoming pupils.

"I am sure you agree," Septimus said, thinking the matter over, "that we must put these young people at ease. There is nothing disinterested in the notion; we cannot be at ease unless they are. Jasper's nephew is here; like takes to like. We will have him meet them at dinner. That's three. We can't ask him without asking Jasper. That's four. Add Miss Twinkleton and the fairy bride-to-be, that's six. Add ourselves, that's eight. Would eight at a friendly dinner trouble you, Ma?"

"Nine would, Sept," the old lady replied, visibly nervous.

"My dear Ma, I particularize eight."

"The exact size of the table and room."

So it was settled. Septimus visited Miss Twinkleton to arrange Helena Landless's reception at the Nuns' House, and the other invitations were accepted. Miss Twinkleton glanced sadly at her globes, regretting they couldn't be taken into society, but soon reconciled herself. Instructions were sent to the Philanthropist for the timely arrival of Neville and Helena, and the scent of soup filled the air of Minor Canon Corner.

There was no railway to Cloisterham, and Mr. Sapsea insisted there never would be. Yet, express trains now whirled through the town without stopping, casting dust as a testament to its insignificance. A remote fragment of the main line, threatening the money market if it failed and the constitution if it succeeded, had already unsettled the local traffic, forcing it to sneak in via a back stable-way labeled "Beware of the Dog."

To this ignominious avenue, Septimus repaired, awaiting a short, squat omnibus laden with luggage like an elephant carrying too much castle. As it lumbered up, a large passenger sat on the box, elbows squared, compressing the driver into a tiny space, glowering with a strongly marked face.

"Is this Cloisterham?" the passenger demanded in a tremendous voice.

"It is," the driver replied, rubbing his aching bones. "And I never was so glad to see it."

"Tell your master to make his box-seat wider," the passenger commanded. "Your master is morally bound—and ought to be legally, under ruinous penalties—to provide for the comfort of his fellow-man."

The driver patted his skeleton anxiously. "Have I sat upon you?"

"You have," the driver admitted reluctantly.

"Take that card, my friend."

"I think I won't deprive you on it," the driver said, casting a disfavorable eye at the card. "What's the good of it to me?"

"Be a Member of that Society," the passenger said.

"What shall I get by it?"

"Brotherhood," the passenger replied in a ferocious voice.

"Thankee," the driver said deliberately, getting down. "My mother was contented with myself, and so am I. I don't want no brothers."

"But you must have them," the passenger insisted, descending. "Whether you like it or not. I am your brother."

"I say!" the driver protested, chafed. "Not too fur! The worm _will_, when—"

Just then, Septimus interposed in a friendly voice, "Joe, Joe, Joe! Don't forget yourself, Joe, my good fellow!" When Joe touched his hat, Septimus accosted the passenger: "Mr. Honeythunder?"

"That is my name, sir."

"My name is Crisparkle."

"Reverend Mr. Septimus? Glad to see you, sir. Neville and Helena are inside. Having succumbed lately to the pressure of my public labors, I thought I'd take a mouthful of fresh air and come down with them." Honeythunder surveyed Septimus with disappointment, twisting a double eye-glass by its ribbon as if roasting it. "Hah! I expected to see you older, sir."

"I hope you will," Septimus replied good-humoredly.

"Eh?" Honeythunder demanded.

"Only a poor little joke. Not worth repeating."

"Joke? Ay; I never see a joke," Honeythunder frowningly retorted. "A joke is wasted upon me, sir. Where are they? Helena and Neville, come here! Mr. Crisparkle has come down to meet you."

An unusually handsome, lithe young man and an equally handsome, lithe girl emerged. Both were very dark, rich in color, and of almost the gypsy type. There was something untamed about them, an air of hunter and huntress, yet they also seemed objects of the chase. Slender, supple, and quick of eye, they were half shy, half defiant, with fierce looks and an indefinable pause in their expressions, like the moment before a crouch or a bound.

Septimus invited Honeythunder to dinner with a troubled mind, the discomfiture of his mother weighing heavily on him. He gave his arm to Helena Landless. As they walked through the ancient streets, pointing out the Cathedral and the monastery ruins, the young people delighted in the sights, wondering much as if they were beautiful barbaric captives brought from a wild tropical dominion. Honeythunder walked in the middle of the road, shouldering natives out of his way, loudly developing a scheme to raid all unemployed persons in the United Kingdom, laying them by the heels in jail and forcing them, on pain of extermination, to become philanthropists.

Mrs. Crisparkle needed her own share of philanthropy when she beheld this large, loud excrescence on the little party. Always a boil upon the face of society, Honeythunder expanded into an inflammatory wen in Minor Canon Corner. Though it wasn't literally true that he called aloud, "Curse your souls and bodies, come here and be blessed!" his philanthropy was of a gunpowderous sort where the difference between it and animosity was hard to determine. One was to abolish military force, but first bring all commanding officers who had done their duty to trial and shoot them. One was to abolish war, but make converts by making war upon them. One was to have no capital punishment, but sweep off the face of the earth all legislators, jurists, and judges who held the contrary opinion. One was to have universal concord, but get it by eliminating all who wouldn't or couldn't be concordant. One was to love one's brother, but after an indefinite interval of maligning him and calling him all manner of names. Above all, one was to do nothing in private. One was to go to the offices of the Haven of Philanthropy, put one's name down as a Member and Professing Philanthropist, pay the subscription, get the card, ribbon, and medal, and evermore live upon a platform, saying what Mr. Honeythunder said, and what the Treasurer said, and what the Committee said, and what the Secretary said, and what the Vice-Secretary said. This was usually said in unanimously carried resolutions under hand and seal, to the effect: "That this assembled Body of Professing Philanthropists views, with indignant scorn and contempt, not unmixed with utter detestation and loathing abhorrence"—in short, the baseness of all those who do not belong to it, pledging itself to make as many obnoxious statements as possible about them without being particular as to facts.

The dinner was a most doleful breakdown. The philanthropist deranged the symmetry of the table, sat in the way of the waiter, blocked the thoroughfare, and drove Mr. Tope, who assisted the parlour-maid, to the verge of distraction by passing plates over his own head. Nobody could talk to anyone because Honeythunder held forth to everyone at once, as if the company had no individual existence but were a Meeting. He impounded Septimus as an official personage to be addressed, or a human peg to hang his oratorical hat on, falling into the exasperating habit of impersonating him as a wicked and weak opponent. Thus, he would ask, "And will you, sir, now stultify yourself by telling me"—when the innocent man had not opened his lips. Or he would say, "Now see, sir, to what a position you are reduced. I will leave you no escape. After exhausting all the resources of fraud and falsehood... you have now the hypocrisy to bend the knee before the most degraded of mankind, and to sue and whine and howl for mercy!" Whereat the unfortunate Minor Canon looked in part indignant and in part perplexed, while his worthy mother sat bridling with tears in her eyes, and the rest of the party lapsed into a gelatinous state with no flavor or solidity.

But the gush of philanthropy that burst forth when Honeythunder's departure began to impend must have been highly gratifying to the distinguished man. His coffee was produced by the special activity of Mr. Tope a full hour before he wanted it
