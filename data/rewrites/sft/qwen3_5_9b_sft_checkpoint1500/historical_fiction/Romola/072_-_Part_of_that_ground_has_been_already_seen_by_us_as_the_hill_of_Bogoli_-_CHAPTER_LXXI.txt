By the time Romola returned home with Tessa and the children, the month of April was drawing to a close. Yet, a far heavier burden weighed upon her spirit than the passing season: the scandalous publication of Fra Girolamo's trial. More accurately, it was the transcript of his confessions, extracted by sixteen Florentine citizens commissioned by the Signoria to interrogate him. This document, released officially, had ignited a firestorm of public suspicion and outrage. So fierce was the backlash that the authorities moved swiftly to recall it. However, fate is rarely so tidy; copies were inevitably scattered, and within days, an unauthorized second edition found its way into the hands of eager, hungry readers.

Romola, having begun to fear that she would never speak to Fra Girolamo again, devoured this evidence repeatedly. She sought a light to cut through the contradictory assertions swirling through the city—from the partisans who still clung to him to his bitterest enemies—who offered nothing but noise. To the devout followers of Savonarola, the friar's lack of constancy under torture and his retraction of prophetic claims caused a consternation so deep it took time to heal. Soon, however, a darker rumor took root among them: that any words contradicting their faith, specifically the report of the notary Ser Ceccone, had not escaped the prophet's lips but were forged by a hand of malice. They suspected the notary of fabricating a "digest" to serve his own ends.

Yet, the official record carried obvious stains. Half the panel of sixteen examiners consisted of the prophet's fiercest foes. Among them stood Dolfo Spini, a notorious figure recently elevated to the new Eight—a body clearly stacked to ensure the downfall of a man whose destruction had already been decided by the powerful parties in control. To many, the proceedings in the Old Palace resembled a murder dressed in the slow, formal robes of justice. The Signoria, desperate for a trade with the Pope and the Duke of Milan, viewed Savonarola as a nuisance to the corrupt clergy, greedy foreign tyrants, and vicious citizens alike. They had already sentenced him; the only debate remaining was transactional: Would the Republic deliver him alive to the Papacy in exchange for lifting taxes on ecclesiastical property, or would the Pope grant Florence the dignity of executing its own prophet on the Piazza?

Under such treacherous circumstances, could anyone accept the validity of this so-called confession? If the Friar had truly renounced his gift, it was likely wrung from him by the agonizing pressure of torture—an agony that, given his sensitive constitution, must quickly drive a man to madness. What if the wicked examiners claimed he suffered only three times over a single day, free from physical coercion, before confessing? Who could believe that? He had been tortured far more extensively, proportional to the distress his words caused in the hearts of those who loved him.

Others, less ardent in their devotion, did not deny the substance of the confession, though they chafed at its implications. They argued indignantly that no evidence warranted a death sentence, let alone the fire. To any impartial observer, if this examination stood as the sole proof against him, the Friar died not for a crime, but because he had become an inconvenience to the Pope, to the rapacious Italian states eyeing Tuscany, and to ambitious citizens seeking to undermine the common weal.

No shadow of political crime hung over him. No stain marred his private conduct. His former monks, including a secretary of many years' standing who possessed above-average culture, testified even after the shock of his retraction to a life of unimpeachable purity and consistency. The Pope himself could find no charge of heresy save for disobedience to a mandate and the disregard of an excommunication sentence. While difficult to justify by mere argument, this breach of discipline sparked a moral insurgency in the minds of serious men, blurring the line between the institution of the Church and the actions of its men.

Common folk sensed that the triumph of Savonarola's enemies was merely the triumph of gross license. Shrewd Florentines like Soderini and Piero Guicciardini likely wore angry smiles at a severity that bypassed all law to hang and burn a man whose public career had warped the strictness of his honesty. They mused that if the Friar had mixed his deep frauds with a zeal less inconvenient to high personages, his deception might have served as excellent oil for the wheels of ecclesiastical and political machinery.

Yet, even these astute men admitted that, despite the Florentine government's clumsy pretense of judicial warrant, the Pope's measures against Savonarola were acts of necessary self-defense. To ignore a man stirring the Powers of Europe to convene a General Council and depose him would be adding ineptitude to iniquity. There was no denying that towards Alexander VI, Savonarola was a rebellious subject, and a dangerously so. Florence had heard him declare plainly that he would not obey the devil. Thus, it was a life-and-death struggle between the Friar and the Pontiff. Whether Florence should serve as the Pope's executioner remained a question fraught with peril.

Into Romola's ears poured the whispers of a faith still bleeding, jostled by the cool judgments of worldly discernment assessing what was plausible in human nature. She could satisfy neither side. Her long meditations over the printed document were haunted by painful observations, registered consciously and unconsciously throughout her years of discipleship. These observations whispered a presentiment: Savonarola's retraction of his prophetic claims was not merely a spasmodic effort to escape the rack. On the other hand, her soul screamed for an explanation that would preserve the purity and grandeur of his life's main striving. The recent sting of her own selfish discontent, arriving like a blighting wind alongside her shattered trust in him who embodied the highest motives, had triggered a familiar reaction—a faith born from the depths of despair. It became impossible for her to believe that the negative, disbelieving thoughts which had dried her soul were founded in truth. Impossible, too, that it was anything other than a living spirit, a breath of genuine conviction, that once animated his words and kindled a new life within her. Any falsehood in him, she reasoned, must have been a fall, not a purpose; a gradual entanglement in which he struggled, not a contrivance nurtured by success.

Examining the text closely, she spotted sentences bearing the heavy stamp of bungling fabrication. They possessed an emphatic, repetitive quality of self-accusation reserved for the lowest hypocrites. Yet, the fact that these damning statements stood in stark opposition to Savonarola's known character—and to the general tone of the rest of the confessions—strengthened her impression that the bulk of the text faithfully recorded what had truly fallen from his lips. Hardly a word was dishonorable to him except those turning on his prophetic annunciations. He remained unvarying in stating the ends he pursued for Florence, the Church, and the world. Apart from the fatal mixture of falsity in his claim to special inspiration—which he used to seize hold of men's minds—he made no admission of using unworthy means. Even here, stripped of the notary's malign phrases, Fra Girolamo emerged as a man who sought his own glory, yes, but sought it by laboring for the highest end: the moral welfare of mankind. He did not rely on vague exhortations; he strove to turn beliefs into energies that worked in the details of daily life.

One passage, perhaps marred by erasures and interpolations, struck her deeply. *"Everything that I have done,"* it read, *"I have done with the design of being for ever famous in the present and in future ages; and that I might win credit in Florence; and that nothing of great import should be done without my sanction..."* He spoke of establishing his position, then moving to great works in Italy and beyond, consulting with chief personages regarding the General Council. He confessed that as his early efforts succeeded, he intended to adopt further measures. Above all, once the Council was convened, he planned to rouse the princes of Christendom, especially those beyond Italy, to subdue the infidels. He noted that it mattered little to him becoming a Cardinal or Pope; achieving his vision would render him the first man in the world by authority and reverence regardless. *If I had been made Pope,* he wrote, *"I would not have refused the office: but it seemed to me that to be the head of that work was a greater thing than to be Pope, because a man without virtue may be Pope; but such a work as I contemplated demanded a man of excellent virtues."*

This blend of ambition with a belief in the supremacy of goodness rang familiar to Romola, echoing the voice that had thundered through the Duomo. It was Savonarola's nature to conceive vast enterprises and to feel that he alone was the man to execute them. Justice must prevail; purity and love must triumph, and they must do so through his voice, his work, and his blood. In moments of ecstatic contemplation, doubtless, the sense of self dissolved into the sense of the Unspeakable, laying the foundation for genuine self-abasement. But in the presence of his fellows, for whom he acted, pre-eminence seemed a necessary condition of existence.

Perhaps this confession, even describing a doubleness that was conscious and deliberate, implied no more than the wavering of belief concerning one's own impressions and motives—a vulnerability shared by every human being lacking a stupid inflexibility of self-confidence.
