Let me regale you with the saga of dear old Bobbie Cardew. It is a narrative of singular interest, devoid of high-flown literary flourishes, yet rich in moral instruction. For the gentleman, it serves as a dire warning against negligence; for the lady, it illustrates how a particular type of male can render existence utterly intolerable.

To those who have recently crossed paths with Bobbie, it may come as a shock to learn that prior to his transformation, his defining characteristic was a lamentably porous memory. Many acquaintances, meeting him after the "change," have expressed genuine astonishment at this revelation. Yet, it is the absolute truth. Trust me on this.

In my youth, Bobbie Cardew was the epitome of a young rotter within a four-mile radius. I have been accused of foolishness, but I was merely a novice compared to Bobbie. While I was a clumsy amateur, he was a master strategist of forgetfulness. To secure his attendance at dinner, I employed a siege strategy: a letter at the start of the week, a telegram and telephone call the day before, and finally, a personal messenger in a cab arriving forty-five minutes prior to the appointed hour. Only if he had already fled town could I guarantee failure.

Paradoxically, Bobbie was not devoid of intellect. Beneath the surface lay a stratum of genuine sense, accessible only via dynamite. Initially, I believed marriage was that explosive agent. And indeed, it proved so. Like a bull-puppy chasing a bee, Bobbie leaped into matrimony with boundless enthusiasm, blissfully unaware of the sting awaiting him until impact.

His bride was Miss Mary Anthony. She was a woman of substance—five-foot-six, possessing a cascade of red-gold hair, steely grey eyes, and a chin of iron resolve. She was a hospital nurse, a profession that held a certain romantic allure for a man like Bobbie, who had never lifted a finger to earn a penny. Following a polo accident, she tended to his wounds, and within a week, they were wedding. The romance was palpable.

I, Reggie Pepper, heir to the collieries and a man of idleness, was introduced to her. I found her admirable, a testament to resilience in an orphan raised alone. For a time, the union appeared a fairy tale. Bobbie adored her, and she idolized him. I naively assumed that if they simply avoided forgetting the wedding day, happiness would follow suit.

Time, however, marched forward. A year passed, and they moved into a flat. Life seemed tranquil. Until the Incident of the Quiet Dinner.

I encountered Bobbie in Piccadilly, invited to join him at home for supper. Foolishly, I accepted. Upon arrival, I was stunned. Mrs. Bobbie was transformed. Her hair was a masterpiece of crinkles and waves, studded with diamonds, and she wore a gown of such breathtaking elegance that it defied description. If this was her nightly attire, I realized, no wonder Bobbie cherished domesticity.

"Here's old Reggie, dear," Bobbie announced cheerfully. "I've brought him home for dinner."

Mrs. Bobbie stared, as if seeing a stranger. Her complexion cycled rapidly from scarlet to ghastly white before she managed a brittle laugh. She composed herself, addressing me politely as "Mr. Pepper," but beneath the veneer, I saw the storm brewing. She had spent the entire meal holding back a scream, a desperate effort to maintain composure. I, lacking the stamina for such theater, bolted the moment the meal concluded.

The next day, Bobbie entered the club looking as despondent as a gumdrop at an Eskimo tea party.

"Do you know how long I've been married?" he asked eagerly.

"Not approximately a year?" I queried.

"No!" Bobbie groaned. "Exactly one year! Yesterday!"

Lightbulbs flashed above my head. "Yesterday?"

"Yes. I planned to take Mary to the Savoy, then Covent Garden for Caruso. I even had the box tickets in my pocket! Throughout dinner, I felt a nagging suspicion I'd forgotten something, but..."

"She mentioned it?"

He nodded. "Frankly, old top, my stock at home is running dangerously low."

I offered silence and a cigarette. Eventually, Bobbie departed. Watching him from the window, I noted him walking aimlessly before turning into a jewelry store. A glimmer of that buried sense surfaced.

This marked the beginning of my serious engagement with the "Bobbie Problem." An immovable object (Mary) versus an unstoppable force (Bobbie). Nature, in creating Bobbie, had equipped him with a divine shield: short-term memory loss. It protected him from the crushing weight of reality. I, too, relied on this armor; if I remembered every lesson taught to me, I would likely be wearing a size-nine hat. But Bobbie was different. Mary intended to dismantle his defenses.

For a week, the trauma lingered. Then, Bobbie returned to his old habits. Mary, terrified that her husband would eventually forget his own existence, saw the danger. A man who forgets his wedding day in his first year risks forgetting entirely by the fourth. She needed to act.

When Bobbie confessed his woes, blaming his "infernal" memory for missing a small errand she requested, I delivered a paternal lecture. "You must include wedding anniversaries in your memory bank," I urged. "Women need proof. They must be shown."

Bobbie dismissed this, claiming love outweighed dates. "What's the difference if I forget her birthday or the cat's measles?" he mused. "She knows I love her!"

"That is insufficient," I countered. "Forget, and trouble ensues."

Despite my warnings, Bobbie remained stubborn. Months drifted by until spring, when the thunderbolt struck.

I was lounging in the club window when Bobbie burst in, his face the color of an oyster, waving a letter. "Reggie! She's gone! Mary has left me!"

"Gone?" I sputtered. "Where?"

"I don't know! Read this!" He thrust the note into my hand.

*"MY DEAR BOBBIE,—I am going away. When you care enough about me to remember to wish me many happy returns on my birthday, I will come back. My address will be Box 341, London Morning News."*

"Why don't you wish her happy returns?" I asked, incredulous.

"But she specifies 'on my birthday'," Bobbie stammered.

"And when is it?"

"I... I've forgotten."

"For heaven's sake!" I exclaimed. "How close are you?"

"Just between January first and December thirty-first."

"You're hopeless," I muttered, ordering drinks. "Sherlock Holmes would struggle with this, but I know a detective's trick: clues. Can't you recall the last birthday? The weather?"

"It was warmish, perhaps fair cold."

"Useless!" I sighed, summoning more refreshments. "Think, Bobbie! Did you give her a gift?"

"A present? No..." His eyes widened. "Wait! I bought her something last year. If we check the receipt date at the shop..."

"What did you buy?"

"I... I can't remember."

Ideas were elusive for Bobbie, like golf shots on a bad day. Suddenly, inspiration struck. "Buy the twelve monthly almanacs! *'When Were You Born?'* Find the month that matches her character!"

It was a brilliant plan, theoretically. We purchased the set, only to find every month described Mary perfectly. December folk kept secrets and traveled extensively; October folks were original and mobile; February folks possessed wonderful memories (ironically). After stamping and burning the useless books, Bobbie returned home, defeated.

Yet, the crisis forged a new man. For days, Bobbie existed in a state of "The Soul's Awakening." He wore his brain to the bone, a painful process, but necessary. He sought me out, radiating a newfound determination.

"I'm on the trail!" he declared. "I remember! On her last birthday, we went to the Coliseum!"

"The program changes weekly," I pointed out. "Does that help?"

"They changed the acts! One week featured Professor Someone's Terpsichorean Cats. I remember them vividly!"

"We narrow it down!" I cheered. Bobbie sprinted to the Coliseum. The management, treating us like brothers, dug through archives and located the performance: mid-May.

"It was May!" Bobbie triumphed. "See? I told you!"

"If you retain any sense, there won't be a second attempt," I warned grimly.

True to form, Bobbie's memory, once engaged, raced ahead. Hours later, he called. "I've got it! Matinee! That cuts it to two days: Wednesday the seventh or Saturday the tenth!"

"Excellent," I said, shivering in the chill air. "But I have a counter-clue."

"Bobbies?" he whimpered.

"On the day you went to the Coliseum, we lunched at the Ritz. You forgot your money and wrote a ten-pound cheque."

"But I write cheques constantly!"

"Hunt your book. Count the ten-pound cheques for the Ritz Hotel between May fifth and May tenth."

Silence followed. "Reggie! You're a genius! I... I believe you've cracked it."

"Hold the line."
