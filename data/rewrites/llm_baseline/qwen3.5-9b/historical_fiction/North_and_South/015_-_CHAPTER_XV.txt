The following morning, Mr. Hale approached Margaret with a proposal. "We must return Mrs. Thornton's call," he said gently. "Your mother is unwell and feels unable to travel so far today, but you and I shall go this afternoon."

As they walked together, Mr. Hale broached the subject of Mrs. Hale's health with a quiet, underlying anxiety that finally pierced through Margaret's earlier attempts to dismiss it.

"Have you consulted a doctor, Margaret? Have you sent for one?" he asked.

"No, Papa," she replied, surprised by his persistence. "You mentioned he might come to see me when I was well. But if only I knew of a competent physician, I would ask him to come this very afternoon. I am certain Mother is seriously indisposed."

She laid her fears bare and forcefully, sensing that her father had previously blocked the possibility of serious illness. His response, however, was heavy with despair.

"Do you truly believe she harbors a hidden ailment? Is she gravely ill? Has Dixon said anything? Oh, Margaret! I am tormented by the fear that our arrival in Milton has ruined her health. My poor Maria!"

"Oh, Papa! Do not imagine such horrors," Margaret exclaimed, shocked. "She is merely unwell, that is all. Many endure temporary ailments; with proper care, they recover stronger than before."

"But has Dixon offered any insight?"

"No! You know Dixon delights in turning trifles into mysteries. Her vagueness regarding Mother's condition has indeed alarmed me, though perhaps without cause. As you once noted, I was becoming fanciful."

"I hope and pray you retain that capacity for imagination," Mr. Hale countered warmly. "But disregard my earlier words. I cherish your concern for your mother. Tell me your worries freely; I may have sounded annoyed before, but I value your vigilance. Let us inquire of Mrs. Thornton if she knows of a skilled doctor. We shall not waste our resources on anyone less than the finest."

Turning the corner onto Marlborough Street, the scene shifted dramatically. The street held no grand residences worthy of Mrs. Thornton's stature. While Mr. Hale's presence usually masked the nature of his home, Margaret had instinctively pictured the formidable Mrs. Thornton residing in a mansion matching her own elegance. Instead, Marlborough Street revealed long rows of modest cottages and blank walls.

"He assured me he lived here," Mr. Hale murmured, looking perplexed.

"Perhaps it is one of his frugal measures to reside in a humble dwelling," Margaret suggested. "But there are many people about; let me ask."

She queried a passerby, who confirmed that Mr. Thornton resided near the mill, pointing toward the factory lodge at the end of a long, dead-end wall.

The lodge itself resembled a standard garden gate, flanked by massive iron doors for carts and wagons. Beyond lay a vast rectangular yard containing business offices on one side and the immense, multi-windowed mill on the other. From the factory issued a constant, deafening clatter of machinery and the groaning roar of the steam engine. Across the yard stood a handsome, stone-capped house. Though blackened by industrial smoke, its paint, windows, and steps were scrubbed with meticulous care. Built perhaps half a century prior, its architecture—stone facings, narrow windows, multiple flights of stairs with railings—betrayed its age. Margaret wondered why those who could afford such a substantial home did not opt for a smaller cottage in the countryside or suburbs, escaping the relentless noise and chaos of the factory district.

Standing on the steps, Margaret barely heard her father's voice. The yard, bounded by the silent wall, offered a dismal view compared to the interior she soon encountered. After climbing the old-fashioned staircase, they were ushered into a drawing-room where three windows overlooked the front door and the adjacent room. No one was present. It appeared untouched since the furniture had been carefully bagged, as if preparing for a volcanic eruption to bury the house a millennium later.

The room was a spectacle of sterile perfection. Walls painted pink and gold faced a carpet patterned with floral bunches, largely obscured by a colorless linen runner. Lace curtains framed the windows; every chair and sofa was draped in protective nets or knitted covers. Alabaster statues sat on every available surface beneath glass shades. In the center, beneath a bagged chandelier, stood a large circular table adorned with neatly bound books arranged like colorful spokes of a wheel. Every surface reflected light; nothing absorbed it. The result was a painfully spangled, speckled atmosphere that unsettled Margaret. She struggled to comprehend the effort required to maintain such icy, snowy purity in such an environment. Everywhere she looked, there was evidence of labor, but not labor aimed at comfort or tranquility; it was labor dedicated solely to ornamentation and the preservation of that ornament from dust or damage.

They waited in silence before Mrs. Thornton arrived. She entered rustling in elegant black silk, her muslins and laces competing with, yet failing to surpass, the pristine whiteness of the room's decor. Margaret explained her mother's inability to join them, but driven by anxiety to avoid reigniting her father's fears, she delivered a clumsy account. She implied Mrs. Hale's condition was a temporary, perhaps fanciful, ailment of a "fine lady," easily dismissed if motivation existed, or deferrable due to severity. Recalling the horses hired for her own visit and the instruction given to Fanny to attend to the Hales with utmost respect, Mrs. Thornton bristled slightly. She offered no sympathy and granted little credence to the report of Mrs. Hale's illness.

"How fares Mr. Thornton?" Mr. Hale inquired. "I feared he was unwell based on his hurried note yesterday."

"My son is rarely ill," Mrs. Thornton replied smoothly. "When he is, he neither complains nor uses it as an excuse for inaction. He told me he lacked leisure to read with you last night, sir. He regrets it deeply; he values those hours spent in your company."

"I am certain they are equally delightful to me," Mr. Hale responded. "Seeing his joy and appreciation for the finer aspects of classical literature makes me feel youthful again."

"I have no doubt classics are desirable for those with leisure," Mrs. Thornton conceded, though her tone remained sharp. "Yet, I confess, renewing his studies was contrary to my judgment. The time and place in which he lives demand all his energy and attention. Classics suit men who loaf away their days in the country or colleges. But men of Milton must absorb their thoughts and powers into the work of today. That is my firm belief." She added this final clause with a pride that mimicked humility.

"But surely," Margaret interjected, "if the mind focuses too long on a single object, it becomes stiff and rigid, incapable of embracing diverse interests."

"I fail to grasp what you mean by a mind growing stiff," Mrs. Thornton retorted. "Nor do I admire those 'whirligig' characters who abandon one passion today only to forget it entirely tomorrow. Possessing myriad interests does not suit the life of a Milton manufacturer. It suffices—and ought to suffice—for him to possess one great desire and dedicate all life's purposes to fulfilling it."

"And that desire is—" Mr. Hale prompted.

Mrs. Thornton's sallow cheeks flushed, her eyes lighting up. "To hold and maintain a high, honorable position among the merchants of his country—the men of his town. Such a standing my son has earned for himself. Go where you will—I need not limit myself to England—but across Europe, the name of John Thornton of Milton is known and respected by all men of business. Naturally, it remains unknown in fashionable circles," she continued with scorn. "Idle gentlemen and ladies are unlikely to know much of a Milton manufacturer unless he enters Parliament or marries a lord's daughter."

Both Mr. Hale and Margaret felt an awkward, almost comical realization that they had never heard of this celebrated figure until Mr. Bell wrote recommending him as a valuable acquaintance in Milton. Mrs. Thornton's world was alien to theirs: neither the Harley Street gentility nor the circle of country clergy and Hampshire squires. Despite her efforts to remain neutral, Margaret's face betrayed her unease.

"You presume I have never heard of this wonderful son of mine, Miss Hale? You think I am an old woman obsessed with Milton, whose own crow is the whitest ever seen?"

"No," Margaret replied with spirit. "It may be true that I had scarcely heard Mr. Thornton's name before arriving in Milton. But since my arrival, I have heard enough to respect and admire him, and to recognize the justice and truth in your praise."

"Who spoke to you of him?" Mrs. Thornton asked, slightly softened yet wary that others might not have done him full justice.

Margaret hesitated, disliking the authoritative tone. Mr. Hale stepped in to her aid. "It was Mr. Thornton himself who revealed the kind of man he is, was it not, Margaret?"

Mrs. Thornton straightened her posture. "My son is not one to recount his own deeds. May I ask, Miss Hale, from whose account you formed your favorable opinion? A mother is naturally curious and eager for commendation of her offspring."
