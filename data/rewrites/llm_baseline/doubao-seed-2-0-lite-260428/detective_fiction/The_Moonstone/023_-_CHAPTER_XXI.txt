The hush in the parlour hung thick as wool after we three settled into our seats, and the first words came soft and steady from my lady. “Sergeant Cuff,” she said, “there was no doubt some small excuse for the unkind, hasty way I spoke to you half an hour back. But I will not hide behind it. I say plainly, and with full sincerity, that I regret it, if I wronged you.”

That gentle grace in her voice and manner softened the dour old detective right away. He begged leave to justify himself, framing it as nothing more than the respect he owed his mistress. There was no way he could be blamed for the terrible shock that had just befallen us, he insisted—for his whole chance of solving the case rested on not doing a single thing to rouse Rosanna Spearman’s suspicions, and he appealed to me to confirm he’d kept to that rule. I could, and I did, swear he’d done no such thing to startle the poor girl. For a minute I thought we’d put the whole awkward matter to rest right there, as any sensible folks would.

But Sergeant Cuff had no intention of stopping there. You’ll see in a minute he was clearly set on forcing the most painful conversation imaginable between my lady and himself.
“I’ve heard folks say Rosanna killed herself over a heartbreak no one could fix,” the Sergeant said, “and that might be true enough. But my own reading points the other way. I believe some unbearable anxiety tied straight to the missing Moonstone drove her to that dreadful end. I don’t pretend to know exactly what that anxiety was—but with your ladyship’s leave, I think I know exactly who can confirm if I’m right or wrong.”

My lady waited a beat, then asked, “Is that person in this house?”
“They’ve left the house, my lady.”

That answer pointed so straight at Miss Rachel it could have been an arrow. A silence fell over us so heavy I thought it would never lift. Lord, how the wind howled around the eaves that night, how the rain lashed against the window panes like handfuls of thrown pebbles, while I sat there holding my breath waiting for one of them to speak again!

“Speak plainly,” my lady said at last. “Do you mean my daughter?”
“I do,” Sergeant Cuff said, no beating around the bush, plain as day.

My lady had her chequebook laid out on the table when we came in, no doubt ready to pay the Sergeant his fee and send him on his way. She tucked it back into the drawer, and my heart ached to see how her old, kind hand trembled—the same hand that had heaped favours on me and every other loyal servant in this house for decades, the hand I pray God will lay on mine when my time comes to retire for good.

“I had hoped,” she said, slow and quiet, “to pay you for your work and send you off without ever speaking Miss Verinder’s name openly in this context. My nephew Franklin must have told you as much before you came in.”
“Mr. Blake passed his message along, my lady. And I gave him my reason for ignoring it—”
“There’s no need to explain. You’ve said enough now to know you can’t turn back. I owe it to myself, and I owe it to my child, to make you stay here and lay every last suspicion out in the open.”

Sergeant Cuff glanced at his watch. “If there was more time, my lady, I’d write my full report instead of speaking it aloud. But time is too precious to waste putting pen to paper if we want to finish this inquiry. I’m ready to lay it all out now, painful as it will be for both of us.”

My lady stopped him before he could start. “I can make this less painful for you, and for my old friend Mr. Betteredge here, if I speak first. You suspect Miss Verinder of deceiving all of us, hiding the Moonstone for some private reason of her own? Is that true?”
“Quite true, my lady.”
“Very well. Before you begin, I will tell you as her mother: she is utterly, completely incapable of doing any such thing. You have known her for barely two days. I have known her every single day since she was born. Say whatever you like against her, lay out your evidence as sharp as you please—you will not offend me. I know full well your long experience has simply tricked you into misreading the facts. Mind you, I have no secret information you don’t. I am every bit as shut out of Rachel’s confidence as you are. I speak only because I know my child.”

She turned to me and held out her hand. I kissed it in silence. Then she faced Sergeant Cuff again, steady as a rock, and told him he could go on.

The Sergeant bowed. My lady’s words did nothing to shake his conviction, not even an inch—only his sharp, hatchet face softened for a second, like he felt sorry for her. He settled back in his chair and started his case against Miss Rachel plain as day: “You must see this from my point of view, my lady, from my 20 years working confidential family inquiries. The one lesson I’ve learned that matters here is this: high-ranking young ladies do sometimes carry private debts they dare not breathe a word of to their families. Sometimes it’s the milliner, sometimes the jeweller, sometimes it’s a purpose I won’t name to spare you distress. Keep that in mind, and see how the facts here have forced me back on that experience whether I wanted to be or not.”

He paused a moment, then kept going, with that horrid, unarguable clarity no one could wriggle away from: “Superintendent Seegrave told me Miss Verinder refused to let him question her, spoke to him with a rudeness and contempt no one could explain. I wrote it off at first to his own clumsiness. But later, after we found the paint smear on the door, and Mr. Blake’s evidence confirmed that smear was tied straight to the theft, Miss Rachel spoke to me herself. I saw three suspicious things: she was still violently agitated, more than 24 hours after the Moonstone went missing. She treated me the exact same rude way she treated Seegrave. And she was furious with Franklin Blake, the one person who’d worked harder than anyone else to find her diamond. My experience told me exactly what that meant. She had secret debts she dared not admit, debts she had to pay—and that the only way to pay them was to secretly pledge the Moonstone to a moneylender. That is the conclusion the facts forced me to draw.”
“And I told you already,” my lady answered, “the facts have led you wrong.”

I said nothing. Robinson Crusoe, God knows how, had popped straight into my muddled old head. If Sergeant Cuff had been dropped that second onto a desert island, with no Friday for company and no ship to take him off, he’d have been exactly where I wanted him to be. (Nota bene: I’m a good average Christian, so long as no one pushes my Christianity too far. And I’d wager most of you reading this are exactly the same.)

Sergeant Cuff ploughed on, no sign of being bothered by our silence: “To test that conclusion, I suggested we search every wardrobe in the house, both to find the stained dress that left the paint mark, and to see what Miss Verinder would do. You consented, Mr. Blake consented, Mr. Ablewhite consented. Only Miss Verinder flatly refused to let anyone touch her things. That was proof enough for me I was right. You all saw her drive off to Frizinghall even after I warned her leaving would ruin any chance of recovering her jewel. You all saw her publicly insult Mr. Blake on the front steps, the very man who’d handed me the first clue that broke the case open. If she’s not hiding the Moonstone, what do those things mean?”

He looked right at me then. It was downright frightening to hear him stack proof after proof against Miss Rachel, and know every single fact he stated was true, even as every bone in my body wanted to defend her. Thank God I’ve always been the sort of man who’s constitutionally better than reason. That let me hold fast to my lady’s side, and keep a brave face on. Take my advice, friends: cultivate a healthy superiority to logic, and you’ll clip the claws of every sensible busybody who tries to nag you for your own good.

“Now for the part that ties Miss Verinder and the late Rosanna Spearman together,” Cuff went on. “The second Rosanna brought that washing-book into this room, I recognised her, and my whole theory shifted. I knew instantly she must be her accomplice. She was a reformatory woman, a master thief in her day, and she had ties to one of the only moneylenders in London reckless enough to advance huge sums on a famous gem like the Moonstone without asking awkward questions. She was the perfect go-between. Every odd thing Rosanna did after that—her long walks by the shore, the hidden seam she stitched into that stained nightgown, her refusal to speak to any of the other maids—all of it lined up perfectly with that theory. Even my lady fell quiet, daunted by how tight his chain of clues looked.

“Now, I have two plans to wrap this case up,” Cuff said. “The first is a sure thing. I’d put an experienced female operative in Rosanna’s old job to watch every move Miss Verinder makes, track every letter she sends or receives, every visitor she sees. I’d send one of my men to stake out that moneylender’s shop in London, to grab the Moonstone the second it arrives there. It’ll cost time and money, but we can’t lose.”

My lady rejected that plan out of hand, sharp as anything. “That is unacceptable. Tell me your other plan.”
“The bold experiment,” Cuff said. “Miss Rachel is hot-tempered, impulsive, not the sort of woman who can keep up a mask of deceit for long. If we hit her with a sudden, terrible shock she never sees coming, her better nature will force her to confess everything. I want to show up at Frizinghall unannounced and tell her, no warning at all, that Rosanna Spearman is dead. The shock will make her break.”

My lady didn’t hesitate a single second. “Yes. We’ll do that.”
“The pony chaise is ready out front,” Cuff said, making to leave.
My lady held up a hand to stop him. “I will not let you go to my daughter with that news. I have the right, as her mother, to put this test to her myself. You will stay here. I will drive to Frizinghall.”

For once in his whole life, the great Sergeant Cuff stood speechless, staring like any ordinary dumbfounded man. My lady rang the bell and ordered her waterproof cloak. It was still pouring rain, and the big closed carriage had already left with Miss Rachel earlier that day. I begged her not to go out in that storm, offered to come with her and hold the umbrella for her. She refused every plea.

When the pony chaise pulled around to the front steps, she turned to Sergeant Cuff in the hall. “You can count on two things,” she said. “I will press Miss Verinder as hard as you ever could to get the truth. And I will send word back to you, either in person or by letter, before the last London train leaves tonight.”

Then she stepped up into the driver’s seat, took the reins in her own hands, and spurred the pony off down the muddy, rain-slick road to Frizinghall.
