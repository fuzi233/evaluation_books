It has often been remarked that the rook, a bird of sedate and clerical habits, possesses a peculiar tendency toward dramatic separation. When two of these birds wing their homeward journey toward nightfall, accompanied by a sedate flock, two will suddenly detach themselves from the main body, retrace their flight for a considerable distance, and hover in mid-air, lingering as if possessed of some occult significance vital to the body politic. Just so, when service concluded in the ancient cathedral with its square tower, and the choir dispersed like a scattering of rooks, two of the more venerable members—those of rook-like aspect—retraced their steps and walked together through the echoing Close.

The day was waning, and with it the year. The low sun hung fiery yet cold behind the ruins of the monastery, while the Virginia creeper clinging to the cathedral wall had already surrendered half its deep-red leaves to the pavement. Rain had fallen that afternoon, sending a wintry shudder through the small pools formed on the cracked, uneven flagstones and causing the giant elm trees to shed a gust of tears. Their fallen leaves lay strewn thickly across the ground; some sought sanctuary in the low arched door of the cathedral, but two men emerging from within resisted this, kicking them aside. One of these men locked the door with a substantial key, while the other departed swiftly carrying a folio volume of music.

"Was that Mr. Jasper, Tope?"

"Yes, Mr. Dean."

"He remained late."

"Yes, Mr. Dean. I waited specifically for him, your Reverence. He appears to have been seized with a slight malady."

"Use the term 'taken,' Tope—in conversation with the Dean," the younger rook interjected softly, offering a subtle correction as if to imply, "One may address bad grammar to the laity or the humblest cleric, but not to the Dean."

Mr. Tope, Chief Verger and Showman, accustomed to commanding respect from excursion parties, declined with a silent loftiness to acknowledge any suggestion offered to him.

"And precisely *when* and *how* was Mr. Jasper taken? For, as Mr. Crisparkle has noted, it is far better to say 'taken' than 'fallen'—" repeated the Dean, emphasizing the word. "When and how was Mr. Jasper *Taken*?"

"Taken, sir," Tope murmured deferentially.

"Poorly, Tope?"

"Why, sir, Mr. Jasper seemed to breathe—"

"I would not say 'breathe,' Tope," Mr. Crisparkle interposed with the same tact as before. "Not English—to the Dean."

"Breathe to that degree," the Dean remarked condescendingly, not unflattered by this indirect homage, "would be preferable."

"Mr. Jasper's breathing was so remarkably short"—thus did Mr. Tope carefully navigate around the submerged rock—"when he entered that it distressed him greatly to retrieve his notes: which was perhaps the cause of his subsequent fit. His memory grew DAZED." Mr. Tope shot this word outward, his eyes fixed on the Reverend Mr. Crisparkle, daring him to improve upon it. "And a dimness and giddiness crept over him, as strange as I have ever witnessed: though he did not appear to mind it particularly, himself. However, a little time and a little water brought him out of his DAZE." Mr. Tope repeated the word and its emphasis with the air of one declaring, "As I _have_ achieved success, I shall achieve it again."

"And Mr. Jasper has returned home entirely composed, has he?" asked the Dean.

"Your Reverence, he has returned home entirely composed. And I am glad to observe that his fire has been kindled anew, for it is chilling following the wet, and the Cathedral possessed both a damp atmosphere and a damp tactile quality this afternoon, rendering Mr. Jasper quite shivery."

All three turned their gazes toward an old stone gatehouse crossing the Close, through which an arched thoroughfare passed. Through its latticed window, a fire burned brightly against the fast-darkening scene, casting shadows over the pendent masses of ivy and creeper enveloping the building's façade. As the deep cathedral bell struck the hour, a ripple of wind swept through these vines, mimicking the solemn sound humming through tomb and tower, broken niche, and defaced statue within the pile close at hand.

"Is Mr. Jasper's nephew with him?" the Dean inquired.

"No, sir," replied the Verger, "though expected. There resides his own solitary shadow between his two windows—the one facing this way, the other overlooking the High Street—as he draws his curtains now."

"Well, well," said the Dean, with a sprightly air of concluding the brief conference, "I hope Mr. Jasper's heart is not too heavily invested in his nephew. Our affections, however laudable, in this transient world, ought never to enslave us; we must guide them, guide them. I find I am not unpleasantly reminded of my dinner by the ringing of my dinner-bell. Perhaps, Mr. Crisparkle, you will, before departing, pay a visit to Jasper?"

"Certainly, Mr. Dean. And inform him that you kindly wished to inquire after his health?"

"Aye; do so, do so. Certainly. Wanted to know how he fared. By all means. Wanted to know how he fared."

With a pleasant air of patronage, the Dean cocked his quaint hat as high as a dean in high spirits might, and directed his comely gaiters toward the ruddy dining-room of the cozy old red-brick house where he currently resided with Mrs. Dean and Miss Dean.

Mr. Crisparkle, Minor Canon, fair and rosy, perpetually plunging headfirst into the deep-running waters of the surrounding countryside; Mr. Crisparkle, Minor Canon, an early riser, musical, classical, cheerful, kind, good-natured, social, contented, and boy-like; Mr. Crisparkle, Minor Canon and a virtuous man, formerly "Coach" upon the chief pagan highways, but since elevated by a patron (grateful for a well-taught son) to his current Christian beat—he made his way to the gatehouse on his return journey home for early tea.

"Sorrowfully informed by Tope that you have not been in robust health, Jasper."

"Oh, it was nothing, nothing!"

"You appear somewhat worn."

"Do I? Oh, I do not think so. In truth, I do not feel so. Tope exaggerates, I suspect. It is his trade to magnify everything pertaining to the Cathedral, you understand."

"I may inform the Dean—I call explicitly at his request—that you are fully recovered?"

The reply, accompanied by a slight smile, was: "Certainly; with my regards and thanks to the Dean."

"I am pleased to hear that you anticipate young Drood."

"I await the dear lad every moment."

"Ah! He will benefit you far more than a physician, Jasper."

"Far more than a dozen physicians. For I love him dearly, and I hold little regard for physicians, or medical practices."

Mr. Jasper was a dark man of approximately twenty-six, possessing thick, lustrous, meticulously arranged black hair and whiskers. He appeared older than his years, as dark-complexioned men frequently do. His voice was deep and resonant, his countenance and figure pleasing, though his demeanor carried a somber tint. His room shared this somber quality and likely influenced his disposition. It existed largely in shadow. Even when sunlight shone brilliantly, it rarely illuminated the grand piano nestled in the recess, the folio music books on the stand, the bookshelves lining the walls, or the unfinished portrait of a blooming schoolgirl hanging above the mantelpiece; her flowing brown hair tied with a blue ribbon, her beauty marked by a childishly, almost infantile, touch of saucy discontent, comically aware of itself. (There was no artistic merit in this painting, which was merely a smudge; yet it was evident that the artist had rendered it humorously—one might almost say, vindictively—like the original.)

"We shall miss you, Jasper, at the 'Alternate Musical Wednesdays' tonight; but no doubt you are best at home. Good-night. God bless you! 'Tell me, shepherds, te-el-l me; tell me-e-e, have you seen (have you seen, have you seen, have you seen) my-y-y Flo-or-ra-a pass this way!' " Melodiously good Minor Canon the Reverend Septimus Crisparkle delivered himself in musical rhythm as he withdrew his amiable face from the doorway and conveyed it downstairs.

Sounds of recognition and greeting passed between the Reverend Septimus and someone else at the foot of the stairs. Mr. Jasper listened, started from his chair, and embraced a young man, exclaiming:

"My dear Edwin!"

"My dear Jack! So glad to see you!"

"Remove your greatcoat, bright boy, and sit down here in your own corner. Are your feet wet? Remove your boots. Do remove your boots."

"My dear Jack, I am as dry as a bone. Do not fuss, there's a good fellow. I prefer anything to being fuss-pussed."
