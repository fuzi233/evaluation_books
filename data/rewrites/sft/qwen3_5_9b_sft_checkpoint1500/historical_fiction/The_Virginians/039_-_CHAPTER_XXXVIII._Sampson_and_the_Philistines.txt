A fortunate circumstance in my early youth acquainted me with a respectable gentleman, a native of a certain island, which some impartial critics of maritime jewels pronounce to be the first gem of the ocean. The tales this worthy gentleman imparted concerning his kinsfolk, who dwelt upon that "gem," were sufficient to curdle the blood of a young man like myself with horror at the sheer volume of wickedness existing in the world. Every conceivable crime—the Ten Commandments shattered into a universal pulp—stories of such roguery and knavery as no fiction writer could invent, along with murders and robberies rivaling those of Thurtell or Turpin, were accurately recalled and freely recounted by my informant regarding his nearest relations. It was a marvel how any of that brood survived outside the hulks. My brother Tim had brought his father's gray hairs to the grave with sorrow; my brother Mick had repeatedly robbed the parish church; and my sister Annamaroia had jilted a captain, eloped with an ensign, forged her grandmother's will, and stolen silverware for which the thief named Larry was eventually hanged. The lineage of Atreus paled in comparison to the race of the O'What-d'ye-call-'ems, from which my friend sprang, yet no earthly power would induce me to name the country of his birth.

What, then, was my naive astonishment to discover these murderers, rogues, parricides, and habitual forgers of bills of exchange writing to one another as "my dearest brother" and "my dearest sister"? For months at a time, they lived on terms of profound amiability. Tim, whose hands still reeked with the blood of his murdered parents, mixed a screeching tumbler for Maria. Mick, whose lips were blackened by the perjuries he had sworn in court regarding his grandmother's vanished testament or the slaughter of his poor brother Thady's helpless orphans, kissed his sister Julia's bonny cheek. They shared jolly nights, crying as they spoke of old times, the dear old Castle What-d'ye-call-'em where they were born, the quartered Eighty-first Regiment, the Major proposing for Cyaroline, and the tomb of their scent-ed mother (who had cheated them out of property). Heaven bless her soul! Their embraces at meeting and parting were so profuse, so touching, that the painful little stories faded from memory, and one forgot their previous assurances that, if told all, they could hang each other round.

Is there anything finer than forgiveness? Is there anything more rational than to call a man by every bad name under the sun, then apologize, regret hasty expressions, withdraw the decenser one flung at an enemy's head, and resume friendship as before? Some souls possess this admirable, angel-like gift. It was beautiful to witness our two ladies at Tunbridge Wells forgiving one another, smiling, joking, fondling almost in spite of the hard words of yesterday, forgetting bygones though they could not help remembering them perfectly. I wonder, can you and I do as much? Let us strive to acquire this pacable, Christian spirit. My belief is that you may learn to forgive bad language employed to you, provided you have a deal of practice and are accustomed to both hearing and using it. Embrace after a quarrel and mutual vituperation. Heaven bless us! Bad words are nothing when one is accustomed to them, and scarce need ruffle the temper on either side.

Thus the aunt and niece played cards amicably, toasted each other's health, divided wings of chicken, and pulled bones from the merry-thought pie, scratching their neighbors' eyes out in conversation but never each other's. We read how Peninsular warriors, when bugles sang truce, fraternized, exchanged tobacco pouches and wine, only to seize their firelocks and knock each other's heads off when the truce ended; likewise, our old soldiers, skilled in war yet enamored of quiet life, laid down their weapons for the nonce to hob-nob gaily. Of course, while drinking with Jack Frenchman, you keep your piece handy to blow his brains out should he make a hostile move; but meanwhile, *À votre santé*, mon camarade! Here's to you, Mounseur! Everything proceeds pleasantly. Regarding Aunt Bernstein's threatened gout? The twinges had subsided. Maria was so relieved! Yet a slight recurrence occurred last night. The Baroness was so sorry! Her niece must see the best doctor, take everything to fortify her, continue the steel even after leaving Tunbridge. How kind of Aunt Bernstein to offer bottled waters! Suppose Madame Bernstein whispers in confidence to her own woman, "Fainting fits! Pooh! Epilepsy! Inherited from that horrible scrofulous German mother!" What means have we of knowing the private conversations of old ladies and their attendants? Suppose Lady Maria orders Mrs. Betty, her ladyship's maid, to taste every glass of medicinal water, first declaring her aunt capable of poisoning her? Very likely such conversations occur. These are merely precautions—the firelocks our old soldiers carry at their sides, loaded and cocked, but currently lying quiet on the grass.

Possessing Harry's bond in her pocket, the veteran Maria chose not to press for immediate payment. She knew the world too well for that. Though bound to her, she granted him ample latitude and leave of absence on parole. It was not her aim to unnecessarily chafe and anger her young slave. She understood the disparity of ages and that Harry required pleasures and diversions. "Take your ease and amusement, cousin," said Lady Maria. "Frisk about, pretty little mousekin," purring grey Grimalkin in the corner, watching with green eyes. Concerning all that Harry was to see and do on his first visit to London, his female relatives naturally talked and joked. Both ladies knew perfectly what constituted a young gentleman's ordinary amusements in those days, speaking of them with the frankness characteristic of easy times.

Our wily Calypso consoled herself perfectly in the absence of her young wanderer, taking any diversion that presented itself. Mr. Jack Morris, the gentleman previously mentioned as enjoying the company of Lord March and Mr. Warrington, proved one such diversion. To live with titled personages was Jack Morris's delight; losing money at cards to an earl's daughter was almost a pleasure. Now, Lady Maria Esmond was an earl's daughter who was very glad to win money. Obtaining permission to escort Mr. Morris to the Countess of Yarmouth's assembly, she played cards with him—and everyone was pleased.

Thus the first eighty-four hours following Mr. Warrington's departure passed rather cheerily at Tunbridge Wells, until Friday arrived, bringing the sermon for which we saw Mr. Sampson preparing. The company at the Wells were eager to listen. Sampson possessed a reputation for being a most amusing and eloquent preacher; if there were no breakfast, conjurers, dancing bears, or concerts underway, the good folk of the Wells would endure a sermon. Knowing that Lady Yarmouth was coming, and aware of her immense power in granting livings and dispensing bishoprics—a fact confirmed by the Defender of the Faith's remarkable confidence in her ladyship's opinions—we may be sure Mr. Sampson prepared his very best discourse for her hearing. When the Great Man is at home at the Castle, strolling over to the little country church in the park, bringing the Duke, the Marquis, and cabinet ministers in tow, have you ever sat among the congregation to watch Mr. Trotter, the curate, and his sermon? He gazes anxiously at the Great Pew, falters over his text, thinking, "Ah! Perhaps his lordship may grant me a living!" Mrs. Trotter and the girls gaze anxiously at the Great Pew as well, watching the effects of papa's discourse—the well-known favorite—upon the assembled big-wigs. Papa's initial nervousness fades; his noble voice clears, warms to the subject, kindles. He produces his pocket-handkerchief. He approaches that exquisite passage which made them all weep at the parsonage. He has begun it! Then, what is that humming noise filling the edifice, causing hob-nailed Melibaeus to grin at smock-frocked Tityrus? It is the Right Honourable Lord Naseby snoring in the pew by the fire! And poor Trotter's visionary mitre vanishes with the music.

Sampson served as the domestic chaplain to Madame Bernstein's nephew. The two ladies of the Esmond family patronized the preacher. On the day of the sermon, the Baroness hosted a small breakfast in his honor. Sampson made his appearance, rosy and handsome, sporting a fresh-flowered wig and a smart, rustling new cassock purchased on credit from a church-admiring mercer at the Wells. Flanked by his patronesses' lacquemen, who trailed behind with great gilt prayer-books, Mr. Sampson marched from breakfast to church. Everyone remarked how well the Baroness Bernstein looked; she laughed, was particularly friendly with her niece, offering a bow and a stately smile to all as she moved on with her tortoiseshell cane. At the door, a dazzling conflux of rank and fashion gathered—all the fine company of the Wells trooping in, including Lady Yarmouth, conspicuous with vermilion cheeks and a robe of flame-colored taffeta.
