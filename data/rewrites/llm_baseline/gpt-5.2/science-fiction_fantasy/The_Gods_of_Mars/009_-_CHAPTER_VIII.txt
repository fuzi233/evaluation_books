Now I knew why the black pirate had held me with his tale as one holds a knife over a flame—steady, patient, waiting for the exact moment when the metal softens. For miles he had been listening with more than ears: feeling, through hull and rigging, the faint pressure-change of a greater craft above us. Had I not caught that single telltale glance of his into the upper dark, the battleship would have been directly overhead the next heartbeat, and the boarding party—already dangling in their harness from the leviathan’s keel like seedpods on a winter reed—would have dropped to our deck and snuffed my rising hope as cleanly as a cook smothers a flare with a lid.

There is a music to aerial war. It is not the cheap brassy blare of drums, but the thin, exact whine of propellers, the timbre of strained cables, the way a ship’s skin answers the wind with a different pitch when she is being stalked. Industrial noise, unthinking and constant, deadens the mind; this noise was the opposite—purposeful, edged, each note a warning. I had been too long at it to be bewildered.

In the same instant I threw my levers, reversed the engines, and dropped our little vessel a sheer hundred feet. The air snapped cold against my face. Above, through the lattice of rigging, I saw the black shapes of the boarders swaying under the battleship’s belly as she raced over us, close enough that the churn of her huge propellers beat the air into a savage, metallic roar that pressed against my teeth.

I rose again at a sharp angle and shoved my speed lever to its last notch. Like a bolt loosed from a crossbow, my craft shot her steel prow straight at the whirring propellers of the giant. If I could so much as kiss those blades, the beast would be crippled for hours—long enough, perhaps, for a man to slip a noose.

At the same instant the sun tore itself up over the horizon, as if some unseen hand had pulled back a cover from a platter, and the light fell hard upon a hundred grim, black faces leaning over the battleship’s stern to watch us die. Their shout came down like a wave of hot breath. Orders were barked—sharp, staccato syllables—but too late.

We struck.

The crash was a brutal, satisfying thing: iron on iron, the scream of shearing metal, the sudden stutter in the battleship’s song as blades shattered and the whole great body shivered. I reversed instantly, but our prow had bitten and wedged in the wound we’d made. For one second—only one—we hung there, nailed to the enemy like a skewer through meat.

That one second was plenty. Black devils poured down and across, a flood of bodies and blades. There was no fight. There was no space to fight. We were drowned by numbers.

I felt steel at my throat—and then a command, cool as a ladle in a scalding pot, checked them.

“Secure them,” said Xodar. “Do not injure them.”

Several had already cut him loose. He came to me himself, smiling as though he were doing a courteous duty, and took my weapons. He saw to my binding, and in his eyes I read the confidence of a man tying a parcel: a tidy knot, a job done. At least he thought it secure. It would have been—had I been a Martian. The puny strands around my wrists were like trussing string. When the time came, I could break them as one snaps cotton between wet fingers.

They bound the girl as well, and fastened us together with a rope, close enough that each breath and shift of weight must be shared.

Our craft was hauled alongside the disabled battleship, and we were transferred to her deck. A thousand black men manned that great engine of destruction. Their bodies were beautiful in the way a well-made blade is beautiful—clean lines, hard muscle—yet their eyes held an ugliness that chivalry would have softened in the red men of Barsoom. The deck swarmed as far as discipline allowed, all of them straining for a look at the captives.

The girl’s beauty drew out their worst appetites. Their comments were coarse, their laughter like the clatter of pans in a kitchen where no one respects the meal. I stood there in thern harness and metal—gear that still carried the faint dry reek of the dead man who had worn it—and my close-cropped black hair and thern complexion became their sport. When Xodar told his fellow nobles of my fighting ability and strange origin, questions flew at me, fast and hungry. The fact that I wore a thern’s harness taken from a thern slain by one of my party set me a notch higher in their estimation; hatred makes quick alliances.

Without exception the blacks were handsome. Their officers were dazzling—wondrous magnificence, harnesses crusted with gold, platinum, silver, and stones until the leather beneath might as well not exist. The commanding officer wore a mass of diamonds that swallowed his chest in cold fire; against the ebony of his skin they burned with an almost indecent brilliance. The scene was intoxicating in its barbaric splendour: polished skeel wood underfoot; cabins of gloriously grained sorapus, inlaid with precious metals and jewels in intricate patterns; burnished gold handrails; guns shining like newly honed knives.

Then they took us below.

Still bound, we were thrown into a small compartment with a single port-hole. The door barred behind us. The ship drifted lazily south, and through the port-hole I could see the world sliding by in pale bands. Above and beyond the metal walls we could hear men working on the broken propellers—hammering, grating, the harsh, repetitive din of repair. It was the kind of noise that, if it became your daily weather, would make you cruel without noticing.

For a time neither of us spoke. My thoughts went to Tars Tarkas and to Thuvia. Even if they had slipped pursuit, they must eventually meet either red men or green—and as fugitives from the Valley Dor they could expect little besides swift death. How I wished I had gone with them. It seemed to me I could not fail to make the intelligent red men see the wicked deception that had been kneaded into their lives like poison into bread dough, until even those who tasted bitterness called it holy. Tardos Mors would believe me; of that I was certain. Dejah Thoris would believe me; I did not even doubt it. And there were warriors—red and green—who would face the doom of all their superstitions for my sake, as Tars Tarkas would. Where I led, they would follow.

My danger lay in this: if I ever escaped the black pirates, it might only be into the hands of unfriendly red or green men—and then it would be short shrift. Yet that danger seemed almost a luxury to consider; my chances of escaping the blacks were remote as rain in a dead canal.

The rope between the girl and me allowed only a few feet of separation. When we had entered the compartment we sat on a low bench beneath the port-hole—the only furniture. The bench was sorapus, smooth and close-grained, with the faint resin-sweet smell of cut wood trapped beneath old oil. Floor, ceiling, and walls were of carborundum aluminum, light and impenetrable, the cold skin of warships.

I had been staring out through the port-hole when I felt her gaze. I turned.

Phaidor was looking at me with an expression I had not seen on her face before. In that dim room, with the ship’s noises throbbing around us, she was very beautiful. At once her white lids dropped, veiling her eyes, and a delicate flush touched her cheek. Embarrassed at being caught staring at a “lesser creature,” I thought.

“Do you find the study of the lower orders interesting?” I asked, and laughed.

She looked up again and gave a nervous, relieved little laugh of her own. “Oh, very,” she said, “especially when they have such excellent profiles.”

It was my turn to flush—though I did not let it show. I suspected mockery. Yet I admired a brave heart that could find humour on the road to death, and so I laughed with her.

“Do you know where we are going?” she asked.

“To solve the mystery of the eternal hereafter, I imagine,” I replied.

“I am going to a worse fate than that,” she said, and shuddered—a small movement, but it shook something loose in the air between us.

“What do you mean?”

“I can only guess,” she said. “No thern damsel—of all the millions stolen by black pirates during the ages they have raided our domains—has ever returned to tell what she found among them. That they never take a man prisoner lends strength to the belief that the fate of the girls is worse than death.”

“Is it not a just retribution?” I could not help but ask.

“What do you mean?”

“Do not the therns themselves do likewise with the poor creatures who take the voluntary pilgrimage down the River of Mystery? Was not Thuvia for fifteen years a plaything and a slave? Is it less than just that you should suffer as you have caused others to suffer?”

“You do not understand,” she replied, and there was genuine impatience in her voice, the impatience of someone who has never had to justify the recipe she inherited. “We therns are a holy race. It is an honour to a lesser creature to be a slave among us. Did we not occasionally save a few of the lower orders that stupidly float down an unknown river to an unknown end, all would become prey of the plant men and the apes.”

“But do you not encourage the superstition in the outer world?” I pressed. “That is the wickedest of your deeds. Can you tell me why you foster this cruel deception?”

“All life on Barsoom,” she said, “is created solely for the support of the race of therns. How else could we live, did the outer world not furnish our labour and our food? Think you a thern would demean himself by labour?”

“It is true then,” I asked, sickening, “that you eat human flesh?”

She looked at me with pitying commiseration, as one might look at a child who does not know the difference between grain and chaff. “Truly we eat the flesh of the lower orders. Do not you also?”

“The flesh of beasts, yes,” I said, “but not the flesh of man.”

“As man may eat of the flesh of beasts,” she replied calmly, “so may gods eat of the flesh of man. The Holy Therns are the gods of Barsoom.”

Disgust tightened my throat. I did not need to speak; my face must have answered for me.

“You are an unbeliever now,” she continued gently, and the gentleness made it worse, for it carried the power of an unexamined language—words that have ruled so long they do not know they are weapons. “But should we be fortunate enough to escape the black pirates and come again to the court of Matai Shang, I think we shall find an argument to convince you of the error of your ways. And—” She hesitated. “Perhaps we shall find a way to keep you as—as—one of us.”

Again her eyes dropped; again that faint colour came. I did not understand. Not then. Dejah Thoris used to say that in some matters I was a simpleton, and perhaps she was right.

“I fear I would ill requite your father’s hospitality,” I answered, “since the first thing I should do, were I a thern, would be to set an armed guard at the mouth of the River Iss to escort the poor deluded voyagers back to the outer world. Also I should devote my life to exterminating the hideous plant men and their horrible companions, the great white apes.”

She looked truly horror-struck. “No, no,” she cried. “You must not say such terribly sacrilegious things—you must not even think them. Should they ever guess you entertained such thoughts, should we chance to regain the temples of the therns, they would mete out a frightful death to you. Not even my—my—” She flushed and stumbled over the word again, as though her own tongue betrayed her. “Not even I could save you.”

I said no more. Evidently it was useless. She was more steeped in superstition than the Martians of the outer world. They worshipped a beautiful hope: love and peace and happiness beyond death. The therns—who called themselves gods—reverenced the plant men and apes as abodes of their departed dead. Even their prayers tasted of old blood.

Then the door opened and Xodar entered.

He smiled pleasantly at me; when he smiled his expression was kindly—anything but cruel or vindictive. “Since you cannot escape under any circumstances,” he said, “I cannot see the necessity for keeping you confined below. I will cut your bonds and you may come on deck. You will witness something very interesting, and as you never shall return to the outer world it will do no harm to permit you to see it. You will see what none other than the First Born and their slaves know—the subterranean entrance to the Holy Land, to the real heaven of Barsoom.

“It will be an excellent lesson for this daughter of the therns,” he added, “for she shall see the Temple of Issus, and Issus, perchance, shall embrace her.”

Phaidor’s head went high. “What blasphemy is this, dog of a pirate?” she cried. “Issus would wipe out your entire breed an’ you ever came within sight of her temple.”

“You have much to learn, thern,” replied Xodar, and an ugly smile cut across his kindness like a crack in glazed pottery. “Nor do I envy you the manner in which you will learn it.”

On deck, to my surprise, the vessel passed over a great field of snow and ice. As far as the eye could reach, nothing else—white, wind-scoured, hard as salt left too long in a pan. There could be only one answer: we were above the south polar ice cap. Only at the poles of Mars does ice or snow cling to the planet.

The world below held no sign of life. We were too far south even for the fur-bearing beasts the Martians love to hunt. Yet the silence out there was not true silence: it was the hush of open cold, broken by our own engines, by the creak of harness, by the constant grind of men at work repairing the wounds we had made. The ship’s noise laid itself over the ice like a dirty cloth, and I found myself hating it—not for fear, but for what it did to the mind: made everything feel owned.

Xodar stood beside me at the rail.

“What course?” I asked.

“A little west of south,” he replied. “You will see the Otz Valley directly. We shall skirt it for a few hundred miles.”

“The Otz Valley!” I exclaimed. “But, man, is not there where lie the domains of the therns from which I but just escaped?”

“Yes,” said Xodar. “You crossed this ice field last night in the long chase you led us. The Otz Valley lies in a mighty depression at the south pole. It is sunk thousands of feet below the surrounding country, like a great round bowl. A hundred miles from its northern boundary rise the Otz Mountains, which circle the inner Valley of Dor, in the exact centre of which lies the Lost Sea of Korus. On the shore of that sea stands the Golden Temple of Issus in the Land of the First Born. It is there we are bound.”

As I watched the ice end abruptly in a sheer wall thousands of feet high, my understanding hardened. Below the wall stretched a level valley broken by low hills and small forests; tiny rivers ran there, born from meltwater at the barrier’s base. To cross that frozen, wind-swept waste alone and on foot would be impossible.

“Only by air boat could the journey be made,” I said aloud.

“It was thus that one did escape the therns in bygone times,” said Xodar, with a touch of pride, “but none has ever escaped the First Born.”

We passed above a deep, canyon-like rift stretching from the ice wall across the valley as far as the eye could follow.

“That is the bed of the River Iss,” said Xodar. “It runs far beneath the ice field and below the level of the Valley Otz, but its canyon is open here.”

Presently I pointed out what looked like a village.

“It is a village of lost souls,” he answered, laughing. “This strip between the ice barrier and the mountains is neutral ground. Some turn off from their voluntary pilgrimage down the Iss and, scaling the canyon walls, stop in the valley. Also a slave now and then escapes from the therns and makes his way hither.

“The therns do not attempt recapture. There is no escape from this outer valley, and they fear our patrolling cruisers too much to venture from their own domains.

“The poor creatures here are not molested by us since they have nothing we desire, nor are they strong enough to give us an interesting fight—so we leave them alone. There are several villages. They increase little, since they are always warring among themselves.”

The words struck like a sour taste: a place where the language of hope had died and been replaced with the harsh, small vocabulary of survival. “Lost souls”—and by whose lexicon were they lost? The dominant tongue always names the world to suit itself; it sells cruelty as inevitability, like merchants selling painted prayer.

Now we swung a little north of west, leaving the valley. Shortly I saw over our starboard bow what appeared to be a black mountain rising from the desolate waste of ice, low and flat-topped, a truncated cone. Xodar went off to attend to duty. Phaidor and I stood alone at the rail. She had not spoken once since we came on deck.

“Is what he has been telling me true?” I asked her quietly.

“In part, yes,” she replied. “That about the outer valley is true, but what he says of the location of the Temple of Issus in the centre of his country is false. If it is not false—” She caught herself, breath tightening. “Oh, it cannot be true. It cannot be true. For if it were true, then for countless ages have my people gone to torture and ignominious death at the hands of their cruel enemies instead of to the beautiful Life Eternal we have been taught to believe Issus holds for us.”

“As the lesser Barsoomians of the outer world have been lured by you to the terrible Valley Dor,” I said, “so may it be the therns themselves have been lured by the First Born to an equally horrid fate.”

“It would be a stern retribution,” I added, “but a just one.”

“I cannot believe it,” she whispered.

“We shall see,” I said, and our words fell away, for the black mountain ahead seemed to pull every thought toward it like gravity.

As we neared the dark cone, the vessel’s speed was diminished until we barely moved. We topped the crest, and below us yawned the mouth of a huge circular well, bottom lost in inky blackness. The diameter was a thousand feet. The walls were smooth, black basalt.

For a moment the battleship hovered directly above the centre of that gaping void, poised like a spoon over a pot whose contents you do not dare to taste. Then she began to settle into the chasm. Darkness rose to meet us. Lights snapped on, casting dim halos, and down we went—down and down—until I felt we were falling into the very bowels of Barsoom.

For quite half an hour we descended, the ship’s engines droning, the sound trapped and multiplied by stone. Then the shaft ended abruptly in the dome of a mighty subterranean world. Below us rolled the billows of a buried sea. A phosphorescent radiance lit everything with a sickly, beautiful glow like the sheen on fish-skin. Thousands of ships dotted the water. Little islands rose here and there, supporting strange, colourless vegetation, pale as blanched roots grown without sun.

Slowly, with majestic grace, the battleship dropped to the surface and rested on the water. The great air propellers had been drawn in and housed during the descent; in their place smaller but more powerful water propellers ran out. When they began to revolve, the ship took up her journey once more, riding this new element as buoyantly as she had the air.

Phaidor and I stood dumbfounded. Neither of us had heard—nor dreamed—such a world existed beneath Barsoom’s crust. Nearly every vessel was war craft. There were a few lighters and barges, but none of the great merchantmen that ply the upper air between outer cities—no traffic of ordinary life, no domestic errands, only the machinery of power.

“Here is the harbour of the navy of the First Born,” said a voice behind us. We turned. Xodar watched us with an amused smile.

“This sea,” he continued, “is larger than Korus. It receives the waters of the lesser sea above. To keep it from filling past a certain level we have four great pumping stations that force the oversupply back into the reservoirs far north from which the red men draw the water that irrigates their farm lands.”

A new light burst in my mind. Those “miraculous” columns of water spurting from solid rock in the reservoir walls of the red men—the learned had never fathomed their source. In time they had ceased to ask, as people cease to ask the origin of a word they’ve spoken all their lives. Here, beneath them, the answer laboured in the dark, unseen and uncredited. The old world above drank and called it blessing; the hidden world below controlled the flow and called it dominion.

We passed several islands holding circular buildings, roofless, pierced midway with small, heavily barred windows. They looked like prisons, made more so by armed guards squatting outside or patrolling the beaches. Most islets were small, but presently we sighted a larger one ahead. This proved our destination. The great ship made fast against a steep shore.

Xodar motioned us to follow. With half a dozen officers and men, we left the battleship and crossed toward a large oval structure a couple hundred yards inland.

“You shall soon see Issus,” said Xodar to Phaidor. “The few prisoners we take are presented to her. Occasionally she selects slaves from among them to replenish the ranks of her handmaidens. None serves Issus above a single year.”

He said it plainly, but a grim smile gave his words a cruel aftertaste, like sweetness laid over rot.

Phaidor—so proud a moment ago—began to doubt in earnest. She clung close to me, no longer the daughter of the Master of Life and Death, but a frightened young girl held in the hands of enemies who spoke a different dialect of power.

The building we entered was roofless. In its centre lay a long tank of water set below the floor like a natatorium pool. Near one side floated an odd black object. For an instant I wondered if it were some beast of these buried seas—or a queer raft.

When we reached the edge of the pool above it, Xodar called out in a strange tongue. Immediately a hatch cover was raised from the object’s surface and a black seaman sprang up from its depths.

Xodar addressed him. “Transmit to your officer the commands of Dator Xodar. Say to him that Dator Xodar, with officers and men, escorting two prisoners, would be transported to the gardens of Issus beside the Golden Temple.”

“Blessed be the shell of thy first ancestor, most noble Dator,” replied the man. “It shall be done even as thou sayest.” He raised both hands, palms backward, above his head in the common Barsoomian salute, and disappeared back into the craft.

A moment later an officer, resplendent in rank and ornament, appeared on deck and welcomed Xodar. We followed aboard and below.

The cabin extended across the ship with port-holes on either side below the water line. As soon as we were all down, commands were given; the hatch closed and was secured; and the vessel began to vibrate with the rhythmic purr of its machinery—a softer, more intimate sound than the airship’s roar, like a great animal breathing through its ribs.

“Where can we be going in such a tiny pool of water?” Phaidor asked, voice thin.

“Not up,” I replied. I had noted that though the building was roofless, it was covered by a strong metal grating. “Then where?” she insisted.

“From the look of the craft,” I said, “we are going down.”

Phaidor shuddered. The old seas of Barsoom had been a tradition for so long that even a girl born within sight of the planet’s only remaining open water carried the common Martian terror of deep water—a fear inherited like a story told so often the body remembers it as truth.

Soon the sensation of sinking became unmistakable. We dropped swiftly. We could hear the water rushing past the port-holes, and in the dim light filtering through, the swirling eddies were visible, twisting like pale smoke in a pot.

Phaidor’s hand clenched on my arm. “Save me,” she whispered. “Save me and your every wish shall be granted. Anything within the power of the Holy Therns to give will be yours. Phaidor—” Her voice stumbled, caught on its own name, and then came in a very low breath, as though she feared the words themselves. “Phaidor already is yours.”

I felt a sudden, genuine pity—pity for a child raised on a language that taught her she was divine, only to find herself as helpless as any other when the grammar of power shifted. I placed my hand over hers where it lay on my arm, intending comfort.

She misunderstood.

With a swift glance about the cabin to assure herself that we were alone, she threw both arms about my neck and dragged my face down to hers.
