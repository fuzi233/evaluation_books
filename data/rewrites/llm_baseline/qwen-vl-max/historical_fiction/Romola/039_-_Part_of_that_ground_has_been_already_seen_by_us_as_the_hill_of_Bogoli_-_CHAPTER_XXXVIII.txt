That evening, Tito had set out for supper with high spirits. The invitation came from Bernardo Rucellai, a man whose name carried weight in Florence—not just because of his wealth or noble blood, but because he embodied the very essence of Florentine grandeur. His family’s fortune had begun centuries before, when a merchant brought from the Levant a rare dye made from a lichen known as _orcella_, which grew on sun-baked rocks in distant islands. This crimson pigment—_oricello_—had become prized throughout Europe, and its success earned the family their name: Oricellari, later softened into Rucellai. Bernardo himself was no mere inheritor of fortune. He had married Lorenzo de’ Medici’s sister, hosted the Platonic Academy after Lorenzo’s death, built a magnificent palace, and completed the striking black-and-white marble façade of Santa Maria Novella. He wrote learned treatises on ancient Rome, collected relics of antiquity, and spoke Latin with purity and grace. To list his achievements was almost to write an epitaph—one that might prompt the Muses themselves to lament the impossibility of such excellence dwelling in one mortal frame.

Tito had received the invitation through Lorenzo Tornabuoni, who emphasized its importance, hinting at political purpose. And indeed, Bernardo was one of the Twenty, the ruling body of Florence for three weeks. The gathering at the Rucellai Gardens—known also as the Selva or Orto de’ Rucellai—was expected to be both sumptuous and significant. Fine wines, delicate dishes, and distinguished company awaited. Yet Tito had reason to be wary. He had attended similar dinners before—banquets where conversation veered toward metaphysics and philosophical systems so intricate they required not only attention but endurance. On one occasion, he had been asked not merely to accept a worldview, but to listen to its full exposition, from creation to final consummation. It was exhausting.

This night, however, promised something different. A dark sky hung over Florence, lit only by occasional lanterns glowing before images of the Virgin. As Tito walked along the Via della Scala, his figure emerged briefly in the light, then vanished again into shadow. Behind him, moving with quiet determination, was another man: thickset, elderly, dressed in a simple serge tunic and felt hat. Baldassarre Calvo.

They reached the garden gate together. The smaller door—the _sportello_—was already open, held by a servant distracted by some other matter. Tito stepped inside swiftly, naming himself as he passed between evergreen hedges illuminated by torchlight. Then Baldassarre followed.

“Your name?” the servant asked.

“Baldassarre Calvo,” he replied without hesitation.

“You are not on the guest list. All guests have entered.”

“I belong to Messer Tito Melema. I am to wait in the gardens.”

The servant hesitated. “Only guests may enter. Are you his servant?”

“No,” said Baldassarre calmly. “I am a scholar.”

There are those whom one cannot refuse simply by speaking with quiet authority. The porter stepped aside. Baldassarre entered, and the gate clanged shut behind him, chaining securely as he disappeared among the gleaming foliage.

His confidence was remarkable—unlike the man who had once stood trembling before Tito, dagger broken in two. That moment had marked a turning point. After Tito left, Baldassarre sat in the wreckage of his rage, limbs numb, hands shaking, yet his mind strangely clear. In that stillness, memories surged—not fragmented, but whole. He saw again the life he had lived, the years of suffering, the betrayal, the secret marriage to Monna Lisa, the hidden wife in the Via de’ Bardi. Now it all came back: the truth, the shame, the injustice.

He looked down at the shattered dagger. Then at the book beside him—a large manuscript of Pausanias, open under moonlight. The page bore the title: ΜΕΣΣΕΝΙΚΑ. ΚΒ′. Once, he had read this work fluently. But earlier that night, it had meant nothing—just ink on parchment, like stains on a wall. Now, the letters burned with meaning. They summoned a world: Messenia, resisting Sparta, enduring tyranny. He saw the stoning of Aristocrates—condemned by a people who cast him out, refused burial, and erected a pillar inscribed with verses of divine retribution. The words echoed in him, stirring long-dormant emotions. He forgot his age. He could have shouted.

The light had returned—mother of knowledge, mother of joy.

Strength flooded back into his limbs. He rose, took up the broken dagger and the book, and stepped out into the moonlit hilltop. The air was sharp with frost, but he felt no cold. Only warmth—of power, of clarity. He wandered across the high ground, gazing down upon Florence, asleep beneath its mountain guardians. The river glimmered palely; the valley stretched toward snow-capped peaks. And he felt himself master of it all.

For years, memory had been a dim echo, a half-remembered dream. Now, it was a living force. He could see cities, understand their rhythms, grasp their secrets. Names, faces, events—his mind raced through them like a man reclaiming an inheritance. He was no longer lost in shadows.

Yet amid this resurgence, one thought remained fixed: Tito.

The man who had stolen his life, his honor, his peace. The serpent who slithered through society with charm and lies. And now, at last, he could reach him. Not for justice—no, not that. For vengeance. For the deepest wound possible: to shatter the illusion of triumph, to make the smug favorite feel the sting of humiliation, to bring him face to face with his own guilt.

Baldassarre had spent days tracking Tito, learning his habits, his allies, his weaknesses. He avoided Nello’s shop, knowing Tito frequented it. He turned away from Piero di Cosimo, fearing any sign of pursuit might alert the target. He had bought a new dagger—spending what little money remained—and lived frugally, feeding only on hunger and thirst for revenge.

Twice, he had felt the return of fog—mental dizziness, loss of form, fading vision. Once, he had tried to enter the Palazzo Vecchio, hoping to confront Tito in council, but failed. The fear gnawed at him: would the light fade again? Would he lose everything?

But tonight, he felt certain. Tonight, the moment was right. Tito was at the height of his favor, surrounded by powerful men, secure in his position. And Baldassarre would not let him escape. He would not seek punishment, nor recognition. He wanted only one thing: to deliver the sharpest pain imaginable—to make Tito know, in that instant, that he was not invincible.

And so, as the garden gates closed behind him, Baldassarre Calvo stepped into the heart of the feast—not as a guest, but as a ghost returning from the past, armed with memory, resolve, and a broken blade reborn.
