There are three rooms on the ground floor of the Wheel of Fortune, besides the bar where the landlady reigns like a general behind her fortifications. One is the common parlour, where any mortal with a thirst and a shilling may sit and grow wise over ale; another is the livery-room, where gentlemen’s gentlemen gather to compare cuffs and calamities; and the third is the sacred chamber with PRIVATE painted on the door in uncompromising capitals—the hired den of “The Confidentials,” Messrs. Morgan and Lightfoot and their brethren, who spoke of the world as if they owned it, and of their masters as if they merely let it out on a short lease.

Mr. James Morgan—Major Pendennis’s valet, noiseless as a cat and nearly as affectionate—had that day listened in at his master’s lodgings while Mr. Strong and the Major spoke together; and he had carried away from that conversation certain fat morsels of knowledge which, like a rich gravy, would thicken any man’s future. A desire for more sauce led him to follow the Major when Pendennis went to the Wheel of Fortune; and while the Major and Sir Francis Clavering sat in the parlour with their tempers rising like overworked yeast, Morgan took his seat in the Confidential room, in the particular corner from which Providence (or the builder) had arranged that one could hear nearly everything in the next apartment.

They were speaking loud—gentlemen always do, when they are trying to appear reasonable—and so Morgan had the pleasure of overhearing almost the whole.

Sir Francis had known Altamont at once in Sydney. Sir Francis wasn’t married to Lady Clavering. Altamont was the man. Altamont was a convict. Young Arthur would get into Parliament, and the Governor promised not to split—oh, what a sly old rogue, that Governor, thought Morgan. No wonder he was so anxious to clap Blanche and Arthur together: Blanche would have a hundred thousand if she had a penny, and carry a seat in Parliament to her husband as neatly as a well-trussed capon is carried to table.

If you had been a physiognomist, you would have asked to see Morgan’s face at that moment, the way a food-historian begs to smell an ancient spice jar. His mind was sniffing, tasting, cataloguing. Nobody saw it; but Morgan, going later to a looking-glass, surveyed himself with the gravity of a man examining a joint before carving.

“But for my hage,” he reflected, touching his own chin as if it were the offending ingredient in the dish of his life, “and the confounded preudices of society—dammy, James Morgan, you might marry her yourself.”

Since he could not marry Miss Blanche and her fortune, he could, at least, do what many a gentleman does with a fortune he cannot eat: trade upon it. The secret touched a number of people, and most of them would rather swallow hot tallow than have it spoken aloud.

Sir Francis’s fortune depended on keeping it quiet. Colonel Altamont’s neck depended on keeping it quiet. And that young upstart beast, Mr. Harthur, who was to get into Parliament on the strength of it, and strutted as if he were a duke with half a million a year—he, Morgan was sure, would pay anything sooner than have the world say he had married a convict’s daughter, and bought his seat with silence.

As for Lady Clavering, Morgan’s mind served up two sauces. If she was tired of Clavering and wanted rid of him, she’d pay. If she was frightened for her son and fond of the little beggar, she’d pay all the same. And Miss Blanche would “come down handsome” to the man who put her into her rights—rights she had been defrauded of, no mistake.

“Dammy,” Morgan concluded, dealing himself these splendid cards as if it were whist and he had looked under the table, “with such a hand, James Morgan, you are a made man. It may be a reg’lar enewity. Every one of ’em must susscribe. And with what I’ve made already, I may cut business, give my old Gov’nor warning, turn gentleman, and have a servant of my own—begad.”

This kind of arithmetic is apt to make a man’s pulse beat; yet Morgan, who had the self-command of a cook who can smell a roast burning and still smile at the guests, kept his face as smooth as his master’s freshly stropped razor. Until the proper day came, Major Pendennis’s boots were varnished, his hair was curled, his early cup of tea carried to his bedside, and his oaths, rebukes, and senile satire endured with a silence so respectful you would have taken it for devotion.

Who would have supposed, seeing Morgan packing and shouldering trunks, waiting at table in country-houses, or standing behind chairs while people praised a saddle of mutton as if they had invented sheep, that he was richer than his employer and knew secrets that could season or poison half the county? Among the brotherhood of valets he was admired for wealth and wisdom; the younger gentlemen’s gentlemen voted him “stoopid,” a feller of no idears, a fogey; but even they would have said amen to the serious-minded prayer uttered at supper-tables: “When I die, may I cut up as well as Morgan Pendennis!”

Major Pendennis, being a man of fashion, passed the autumn from house to house. If the Duke was abroad and the Marquis was in Scotland, he condescended to stay with Sir John or even the plain squire; but truth compels us to own that his reputation was somewhat on the wane. Many of the men of his time had died out; and their heirs, sitting in the old halls and wearing the old titles as if they were new-made coats, did not know Major Pendennis and cared little for his stories of “the wild Prince and Poins,” and the heroes of the world that had been.

It must have pinched him, walking by London doors that had once swung open at his knock, to see how seldom they opened now—and how often he used to rap there, and step through to banquets and welcomes twenty years before. He began to feel, in a dim, damp way, like a gentleman returning to a familiar kitchen and discovering the hearth replaced by a new-fangled range and the old cook dismissed. The young men laughed at him. The serious youths pitied him. He was rather simple; his examination of moral questions had never been deep. Until lately, it had hardly struck him that he was not, in all respects, a most respectable and rather fortunate man.

“The old grand manner is gone,” he told himself, with the regularity of an old recipe repeated until it loses its taste. He compared the old Castlewood processions—four post-chaises, sixteen horses, and the whole North Road turning out to gape—with the present lord sneaking away from a railway station, smoking a cigar in a brougham among bagmen. The late lord filled Castlewood with company drinking claret till midnight; the present buries himself in a hut on a Scotch mountain or in two or three closets in an entresol at Paris, where his amusements are a dinner at a café and a box at a little theatre. His Lady Lorraine of the Regent’s days—gorgeous in diamonds and velvets, daring in rouge—was in his mind like a rich dish no modern palate dared attempt; to-day’s Lady Lorraine, in black silk like a governess, talked astronomy, labouring classes, emigration, and the deuce knows what, and crept to church at eight o’clock in the morning.

Abbots-Lorraine, once the noblest house in the county, was turned into a monastery—a regular La Trappe. They drank hardly two glasses of wine after dinner, and every other man at table was a curate in a white neckcloth, discoursing of Polly Higson’s progress at school or Widow Watkins’s lumbago. The young men sprawled over sofas and billiard-tables, stole off to smoke pipes in each other’s bedrooms, revered nothing—“not even an old gentleman who has known their fathers and their betters, not even a pretty woman.” They poisoned the very turnips and stubble-fields with tobacco. The breed was gone, thought the Major; replaced by cotton-spinners, utilitarians, and young sprigs of parsons with their hair combed down their barks.

He was not entirely wrong. His time was over; the new time had begun to reign. And after a rather unsuccessful autumn—during which Mr. Morgan followed him faithfully, as a shadow follows a fat man on a sunny wall—the Major came back to London at the dismal end of October, when the fogs and the lawyers return together, like twin pests.

Who has not watched the loaded cabs at that season, piled with boxes and children, rattling through the dun evenings, stopping at dark houses to discharge nurse and infant, girls, matron, and father, their holidays over? Yesterday it was France and sunshine, or Broadstairs and liberty; to-day it is work and a yellow fog; and there—ye gods!—what a heap of bills lies in master’s study. In half an hour the printer’s boy will be in the passage; Mr. Smith with that little account will call at ten; and duty, difficulty, struggle, stand in the doorway with their hats on, ready to enter. Even the city’s sound seems to change: the holiday’s easy shuffle becomes the sharp clack of wheels on stone, the cough of clerks, the metallic cry of commerce. London hums like an over-wound instrument—too many harsh notes, too much din—and the ear, like the heart, feels poorer for it.

Old Pendennis had no special labours or bills to encounter, having no affection at home to soothe him. He always had money in his desk for his wants; and being by nature and habit tolerably indifferent to the wants of other people, he was seldom disturbed by theirs. But even a gentleman who owes nothing may be out of temper; selfishness itself cannot always keep a man warm.

He had had two or three twinges of gout in the country-house where he had been staying; the birds were wild and shy; walking over ploughed fields fatigued him deucedly; the young men had laughed at him; he had been peevish at table; he could not get his whist of an evening; and so he was glad to come away. In all his dealings with Morgan he had been sulky and discontented—swearing and abusing him for days. He scalded his mouth with bad soup at Swindon. He left his umbrella in the railway carriage; at which forgetfulness he flew into such a rage that he cursed Morgan more freely than ever. Both chimneys smoked furiously in his lodgings; he had the windows flung open; and he swore so acrimoniously that Morgan, hearing it, felt inclined to fling him out of one of those opened casements in return. The valet swore after his master as the Major went down the street to the Club.

Bays’s was not pleasant. The house had been newly painted and smelt of varnish and turpentine; and a large streak of white paint inflicted itself on the back of the old boy’s fur-collared surtout, as if the building wished to mark him for a victim. The dinner was not good—thin soup, dispirited fish, and a joint that had been carved with less tenderness than it had been cooked. The three most odious men in London pursued him there: old Hawkshaw, whose cough came with such accompaniments as to make a man feel he was dining with a churchyard; old Colonel Gripley, who seized all the newspapers as if they were property; and irreclaimable Jawkins, who placed himself at the next table and described every inn-bill of his foreign tour with the exactitude of a man translating a dead language into one still more dead. The Club waiter trod on Pendennis’s toe while bringing coffee. Never alone appear the Immortals: the Furies hunt in company. They pursued Pendennis from home to the Club, and from the Club home.

While the Major was absent, Morgan sat in the landlady’s parlour, drinking hot brandy-and-water and pouring into Mrs. Brixham’s ear some of the abuse his master had poured down upon him upstairs. Mrs. Brixham was Mr. Morgan’s slave. He was, in effect, his landlady’s landlord. He had bought the lease of the house she rented; he had got her name and her son’s to acceptances; he had a bill of sale which made him master of the poor widow’s furniture. Young Brixham was a clerk in an insurance office, and Morgan could put him, as he called it, into quod any day. Mrs. Brixham was a clergyman’s widow; and Morgan, after performing his duties on the first floor, took a pleasure in making her fetch his bootjack and slippers as if she were his scullery-maid. The little black profiles of her son and daughter, the very picture of Tiddlecot Church where she had been married and where her poor dear Brixham lived and died—these hung over the mantelpiece like relics; and even these, in Morgan’s eyes, were now his property.

He sat in the ex-curate’s old horsehair study-chair, made Mrs. Brixham bring supper, and fill his glass again and again. The liquor was bought with the poor woman’s own coin—so Morgan indulged only the more freely; and he had eaten his supper and was drinking a third tumbler when Major Pendennis returned, and went upstairs to his rooms.

Morgan swore savagely at the bell when he heard it, finished his tumbler before answering the summons, and went up with his temper steaming like the kettle Mrs. Brixham dared not call her own.

He received the Major’s abuse in silence. Pendennis’s foot-bath was at the fire; his gown and slippers waited. Morgan knelt to take off his boots with due subordination; and as the Major scolded from above, Morgan kept a low growl of maledictions below at his feet. So when Pendennis cried, “Confound you, sir, mind that strap—curse you, don’t wrench my foot off,” Morgan, under his breath, expressed a wish to strangle him, drown him, and punch his head off.

The boots removed, Morgan had to take off the Major’s coat, and for that must approach near—near enough that Pendennis could not but perceive what occupation had lately warmed the valet’s spirits. The Major, with that simple and forcible phraseology which gentlemen sometimes employ toward servants, informed Morgan that he was a drunken beast, and smelt of brandy.

At this the man broke out. Patience, like a stock-pot left too long on the fire, had reduced to a bitter essence.

“I’m drunk, am I? I’m a beast, am I? I’m d——d, am I? you infernal old miscreant!” Morgan cried, flinging up subordination as if it were a soiled apron. “Shall I wring your old head off, and drownd yer in that pail of water? Do you think I’m a-goin’ to bear your confounded old harrogance, you old Wigsby! Chatter your old hivories at me, do you, you grinning old baboon! Come on, if you are a man, and can stand to a man. Ha! you coward—knives, knives!”

“If you advance a step, I’ll send it into you,” said the Major, snatching a knife from the table. His hand shook, but his voice tried hard not to. “Go downstairs, you drunken brute, and leave the house; send for your book and your wages in the morning, and never let me see your insolent face again. This d——d impertinence of yours has been growing for months past. You have been growing too rich. You are not fit for service. Get out of it, and out of the house.”

“And where would you wish me to go, pray, out of the ’ouse?” Morgan asked, with a sudden mocking bow, as if they were on a stage and the audience expected a flourish. “And won’t it be equal convenient to-morrow mornin’?—tootyfay mame shose, sivvaplay, munseer?”

“Silence, you beast, and go!” cried the Major.

Morgan began to laugh—a laugh with a clang in it, as if it carried a key. He seated himself deliberately.

“Look yere, Pendennis,” he said. “Since I’ve been in this room you’ve called me beast, brute, dog—and d——d me, haven’t you? How do you suppose one man likes that sort of talk from another? How many years have I waited on you? How many damns and cusses have you given me along with my wages? Do you think a man’s a dog, that you can talk to him so?

“If I choose to drink a little, why shouldn’t I? I’ve seen many a gentleman drunk form’ly, and peraps have the abit from them. I ain’t a-goin’ to leave this house, old feller—and shall I tell you why? The house is my house. Every stick of furnitur’ in it is mine—excep’ your old traps, and your shower-bath, and your wigbox. I’ve bought the place with my own industry and perseverance. I can show a hundred pound where you can show a fifty, or your damned supersellious nephew either.

“I’ve served you honourable, done everythink for you these dozen years—and I’m a dog, am I? I’m a beast, am I? That’s the language for gentlemen, not for our rank. But I’ll bear it no more. I throw up your service. I’m tired on it. I’ve combed your old wig and buckled your old girths and waistbands long enough. Don’t look savage at me—I’m sitting in my own chair, in my own room, a-telling the truth to you. I’ll be your beast, and your brute, and your dog no more, Major Pendennis Alf Pay.”

The Major’s fury, met with this abrupt revolt, was checked as if a cold shower had been dashed over him—appropriately enough, for his shower-bath stood ready like a silent witness to domestic humiliation. The shock cooled his anger; and when anger is cooled, curiosity often begins to simmer. Morgan’s courage interested him. The Major almost respected the fellow, as in the fencing-room he might have admired an adversary who hit him neatly.

“You are no longer my servant,” the Major said at last, with a stiff dignity that tried to cover a very human dismay. “And the house may be yours; but the lodgings are mine, and you will have the goodness to leave them. To-morrow morning, when we have settled our accounts, I shall remove into other quarters. In the meantime, I desire to go to bed, and have not the slightest wish for your further company.”

“We’ll have a settlement, don’t you be afraid,” Morgan said, getting up. “I ain’t done with you yet; nor with your family, nor with the Clavering family, Major Pendennis—and that you shall know.”

“Have the goodness to leave the room, sir—I’m tired,” said the Major.

“Hah! you’ll be more tired of me afore you’ve done,” Morgan answered with a sneer, and walked out, leaving the Major alone with the fire, the foot-bath, the knife on the table, and a silence that seemed suddenly loud—London’s muffled roar beyond the windows, the distant rattle of wheels, the ever-present cough of the city. Even in solitude, the soundscape would not grant him dignity.

He sat and mused by the fireside over the impudence and ingratitude of servants. He thought how he should get a new man; how devilish unpleasant it was, at his age and with his habits, to part with a fellow to whom he had been accustomed. He remembered, with an odd tenderness, Morgan’s receipt for boot-varnish—better and more comfortable to the feet than any he had ever tried; how well Morgan made mutton-broth, skimming it patiently, keeping it clear, coaxing savour out of bone and time; how neatly he had tended him when he was unwell.

“Gad, it’s a hard thing to lose a fellow of that sort,” thought the Major, in the tone of a man who has broken his best carving-knife and suddenly feels the inadequacy of all substitutes. “But he must go. He has grown rich, and impudent since he has grown rich. He was horribly tipsy and abusive to-night. We must part, and I must go out of the lodgings. Dammy, I like the lodgings. I’m used to ’em. It’s very unpleasant, at my time of life, to change quarters.”

His testiness was gone; the umbrella, the paint-smell at Bays’s, the cough of Hawkshaw, the newspapers of Gripley, the inn-bills of Jawkins—all were forgotten under this superior disturbance. “Confound the insolent villain!” he thought. “He understood my wants to a nicety. He was the best servant in England.”

He thought about Morgan as a man thinks of a horse that has carried him long and well, and is safe no longer. How the deuce to replace him? Where find such another animal?

He had donned his dressing-gown and replaced his head of hair—Mr. Truefitt had lately introduced a little grey into the coiffure, which gave the Major’s head an artless and respectable appearance; and now, having taken off his wig and put on his night-handkerchief, he sat absorbed when a feeble knock came at the door.

It opened, and Mrs. Brixham stood there.

“God bless my soul, Mrs. Brixham!” cried the Major, startled that a lady should behold him in the plain apparatus of his night-toilet. “It—it’s very late, Mrs. Brixham.”

“I wish I might speak to you, sir,” said the landlady piteously.

“About Morgan, I suppose? He has cooled himself at the pump. Can’t take him back, Mrs. Brixham. Impossible. I’d determined to part with him before, when I heard of his dealings in the discount business—I suppose you’ve heard of them, Mrs. Brixham? My servant’s a capitalist, begad.”

“Oh, sir,” Mrs. Brixham said, and her voice was like a cracked teacup that still tries to be useful, “I know it to my cost. I borrowed from him a little money five years ago; and though I have paid him many times over, I am entirely in his power. I am ruined by him, sir. Everything I had is his. He’s a dreadful man.”

“Eh, Mrs. Brixham?” said the Major, with that shrugging philosophy which sits so easily on the comfortable. “Tout pis—dev’lish sorry for you; and that I must quit your house after lodging here so long: there’s no help for it. I must go.”
