“If anyone were to ask me why I loved him, I could only answer, ‘Because it was he, because it was I.’ There is something beyond words—an inexplicable force that brought us together.”—MONTAIGNE: _On Friendship_.

The moment had come to prepare Mordecai for the revelation of his long-lost sister and for the change in living arrangements that would allow Mirah to meet her brother under peaceful and dignified circumstances. Mrs. Meyrick, to whom Deronda had confided everything except Mordecai’s deeper connection to himself, had been actively involved in finding a suitable home in Brompton, just a short walk from her own house. This way, the siblings would remain within reach of her protective care. Her unique blend of Scottish warmth and Gaelic liveliness had helped her keep the secret tightly guarded—from the girls, from Hans, and even from Mirah herself. Any slip might have reached her, stirring fears or doubts that could disrupt the delicate beginning of the work meant to secure her independence—a word we use loosely, though it speaks to one of life’s most meaningful forms of self-reliance.

Both Mrs. Meyrick and Deronda had reasons—too deep to articulate—for wanting Mirah to stand on her own. Perhaps the little mother found it easier to stay silent because she wasn’t entirely sure how to feel about the remarkable man described to her as Mirah’s brother. If any joy stirred in her, it came not from personal admiration, but from trust in Deronda’s judgment. The news of Mordecai’s illness touched her deeply; yet she struggled with the idea of an enthusiasm she could only see as Jewish zealotry—something foreign, perhaps even awkward, among their circle. To her, he sounded less like a modern man and more like one of Scott’s Covenanters—earnest, intense, but out of step with the world.

Her mind was far from dull. She shared Mab’s delight in the romantic tale of Mirah’s journey and her life with them. But romance, when it steps into real life, needs adjustment. We read late into the night about Sakya-Mouni, St. Francis, Oliver Cromwell—but would we welcome such figures at our door? Or worse, discover they were family?

Mrs. Meyrick had hoped, as her children did, that Mirah’s passion for Judaism would slowly soften, blending into the growing affection between her and their new family. In truth, her favorite version of this story had never included a lost brother at all—but something far more hopeful, something that aligned with the future she saw for Hans. And now—here was a brother who would plunge Mirah back into the deepest currents of Jewish feeling. She couldn’t help but say to Deronda,

“I’m as glad as you are that the pawnbroker isn’t her brother. There are Ezras and Ezras in this world—and really, it’s a relief to think not all Jews are like those shopkeepers who won’t let you leave their stores. And besides, what he said about his mother and sister makes me bless him. I know he’s good. But I’ve never cared much for fanaticism. I suppose I heard too much preaching in my youth—it spoiled my taste for it.”

Deronda smiled gently. “I don’t think you’ll find Mordecai preachy,” he said. “He’s not what I’d call fanatical. I reserve that word for someone whose passion is narrow, blind, who loses sense of balance and becomes unjust to others outside his path. Mordecai is an enthusiast—yes, but I mean that as a compliment. He cares deeply for great human causes, for the welfare of mankind. He’s not strictly orthodox. He makes allowances for others, often adapting his ways not out of compromise, but out of compassion for fellow Jews who live differently. The people he lives with adore him—they just can’t understand his thoughts.”

“Oh, well,” Mrs. Meyrick replied, “I can rise to the level of the pawnbroker’s mother. I can appreciate what’s good in him—and where I don’t see it, I’ll take your word. By your definition, one could be fanatical about common sense, couldn’t they? My poor husband used to say the world would be a dull place if there were nothing but common sense in it. Still, Mirah’s brother will have good bedding—I’ve made sure of that. And I’ve pasted up this extra window to stop drafts.” (They were standing in the new lodging.) “It’s comforting to know these people aren’t strangers to me—no false pretenders. And once the children know, we’ll make the rooms much more cheerful.”

“The next step,” Deronda said, “is to tell Mordecai everything and get him to move. That may be harder than expected.”

“And will you tell Mirah before I speak to the children?” asked Mrs. Meyrick.

Deronda hesitated.

“No,” she continued, persuasively, “let me tell Hans and the girls the evening before. Then they’ll be away the next morning.”

“Yes, that’s best,” he agreed. “But please—give Mordecai justice. Or Ezra, as Mirah will likely want to call him. Don’t feed their imaginations by comparing him to Habakkuk Mucklewrath.” He smiled—Mrs. Meyrick herself having once drawn that comparison.

“Trust me,” she said warmly. “I’ll have to work so hard to make them happy about it, I’ll end up believing it myself. When I’m afraid, it helps to have someone to be angry with for not being brave. It warms the blood.”

Deronda might have spent more time explaining Mordecai’s character—if he hadn’t been consumed by the task ahead: revealing the truth without hurting the Cohens. Mordecai had already shown, in one unforgettable reply, how deeply he felt for others’ feelings. During the days leading up to this moment, Deronda had met Mordecai at the *Hand and Banner*, but now, after careful thought, he wrote to him, asking to meet privately in his own home that evening. He requested only an hour in Mordecai’s workroom, if the Cohens wouldn’t mind the intrusion. If not, he’d accompany Mordecai elsewhere. He hoped this would create a quiet anticipation, preparing Mordecai’s heart.

When Deronda arrived, he was greeted with the usual warmth—some extra care in the women’s clothing, the children slightly awed, and the elders holding back a curious silence. Their understanding of Deronda’s dealings with Mordecai remained a mystery, one they respected without questioning. Jacob, having overheard bits of conversation, stepped forward and asked, “What do you want to talk to Mordecai about?”

“Something very important to him,” Deronda said, pinching the boy’s ear. “But you wouldn’t understand.”

Jacob immediately launched into a string of Hebrew verses he’d memorized, speaking with a mix of guttural and nasal tones, nodding solemnly as if offering irrefutable proof.

“No,” Deronda said seriously, “I can’t say anything like that.”

“I knew it,” Jacob declared, doing a little dance of triumph with his red legs. He pulled things from his knickerbocker pockets and tucked them back in, hinting at his reserves. Then he ran to the workroom door, flung it open, leaned against it, and called out, “Mordecai, here’s the young swell”—a mimicry of his father’s phrase, which he thought perfect for the moment.

He was pulled back by hushes from mother and grandmother. Deronda entered, closed the door behind him, and saw that a carpet had been laid, a chair placed, and the fire and lights tended to—all signs of the Cohens’ respect. As Mordecai rose to greet him, Deronda noticed the solemn expectancy in his face—just as if he’d known Deronda was about to reveal something profound.

Neither spoke until Deronda, with gentle kindness, moved the empty chair across the hearth and sat near Mordecai. Then Mordecai said, voice trembling with certainty:

“You’re here to tell me something my soul has longed for.”

“It’s true,” Deronda replied, cautious. “I have something weighty to share—something I hope you’ll rejoice in.”

“It’s all revealed,” Mordecai breathed, leaning forward, hands clasped. “You are like my brother who drank from my mother’s breast—the heritage is yours. There is no doubt between us.”

“I’ve learned nothing new about myself,” Deronda said gently.

The disappointment was clear. Better not prolong the mistaken hope.

“Except,” he continued, “that some time ago, I discovered another kind of hidden bond between us—beyond what you’ve spoken of.”

Mordecai didn’t open his eyes, but his eyelids fluttered.

“I’ve met someone you care deeply for.”

“One closely tied to your mother,” Deronda added, trying to ease the revelation. Seeing Mordecai shrink, he went on: “Someone she and you cherished above all others.”

Mordecai suddenly grasped Deronda’s wrist—his grip tight, his body rigid with terror. Deronda understood instantly.

“What was prayed for has come to pass,” he whispered. “Mirah has been delivered from evil.”

Mordecai’s hold loosened slightly, but he panted, breathless, tears unshed.

Deronda continued: “Your sister is worthy of the mother you honored.”

He waited. Mordecai sank back, closed his eyes, and began to speak in Hebrew—softly, almost inaudibly—for several minutes. Then he fell into a quiet stillness, his face glowing with peace. Deronda watched—saw something new in Mordecai’s expression, a sweetness like that of the dearly departed. For the first time, he sensed a resemblance to Mirah.

When Mordecai was ready, Deronda told the rest. He softened the account of Mirah’s flight—vague about her father’s behavior, emphasizing instead her longing to come to England, where she believed she might find her mother. He omitted the detail of her attempt to drown herself and his role in saving her. Instead, he described how she’d found shelter with friends of his, whose kindness and efforts he had supported. What he focused on was Mirah’s devotion to her mother and brother—every detail, every emotion.

“It was searching for them,” Deronda said, smiling, “that led me to this house. The name Ezra Cohen was then the most meaningful name in the world to me. I confess I was afraid for a long time. Perhaps you’ll forgive me now for asking about the elder Mrs. Cohen’s daughter. I cared deeply about who Mirah’s friends were. But I found a brother worthy of her when I learned that her Ezra was hiding under the name Mordecai.”

“Mordecai is my true name—Ezra Mordecai Cohen.”

“Is there any kinship between your family and mine?” Deronda asked.

“Only the kinship of Israel,” Mordecai replied. “My soul clings to these people who have sheltered me, who gave me succor through their love—like sweet fragrance rising from things long buried. It is good for me to endure their ignorance, to bind myself to them in gratitude. It reminds me of the spiritual poverty of the Jewish people, and keeps me from replacing loving wisdom with impatient knowledge.”

“But now,” Deronda said, fearing resistance, “with a closer tie drawing you, do you feel bound to stay? It seems right—don’t you think—that you should live with your sister? I’ve prepared a home near her friends, so she can join you. Please grant me this wish. It will let me be with you often when Mirah must leave. That’s my selfish reason. But the real reason is that Mirah will want to care for you—and you ought to let her guard you as a brother. You’ll have books. I’ll learn from you. I’ll take you to see rivers and trees. You’ll have the rest you need—nay, the rest I need for you. This is my claim upon you now that we’ve found each other.”

Deronda spoke with earnest tenderness, as if pleading with a revered elder brother. Mordecai stared at him, listening deeply. After a pause, he said, almost reproachfully:

“And you would have me doubt whether you were born a Jew? Have we not touched each other with invisible threads? Have we not quivered together, like leaves from one root? I know what I am outwardly—I am one among the poor, stricken, dying. But our souls know each other. They looked silently, as if long parted and now reunited. When they found voice, they were certain—every word, understanding. The life of Israel flows in your veins.”

Deronda sat still, his face tingling. He could neither deny nor affirm. He waited, hoping Mordecai would offer more clarity.

After a moment, Mordecai said firmly:

“What you ask of me, I will do. And our mother—may the Eternal bless her in our hearts—would have wished it too. I accept your kindness. Mirah’s home shall be mine.” He paused, then added softly, “But I shall grieve to leave these parents and children. You must tell them—my heart would fail me.”

“I knew you’d want me to,” Deronda said, relieved. “Shall we go now?”

“Yes,” Mordecai said, rising with the dignity of one facing a painful duty. “Let us not delay. It must be done.” Then, after a moment, “But do not dwell on my sister more than necessary.”

They entered the parlor. Mordecai turned to Jacob: “Ask your father to come. Tell Sarah to watch the shop. My friend has something to say.” He addressed the elder Mrs. Cohen. It was part of Mordecai’s oddness that he called this man his friend. The two women tried to show respect by insisting Deronda sit in the best seat.

Cohen entered, pen behind his ear, rubbing his hands. “Well, sir!” he exclaimed. “I’m glad you’re joining our family again. We’re quite comfortable, I think.”

He looked around, shining with pleasure. Once seated, the scene was touching: Baby rocked under a scarlet blanket in the corner, Adelaide Rebekah on her grandmother’s knee, Jacob nestled between his father’s legs. In the center stood Deronda and Mordecai—Mordecai slightly withdrawn, trying to hide his emotional vulnerability. The fire cast rich shadows, turning dark eyes into jewels, and lending speech to the quiet looks exchanged.

“I’ve just told Mordecai of an event that changes his life,” Deronda began. “But I hope you’ll agree it’s joyful. Since he sees you as his dearest friends, he wants me to tell you at once.”

“Relations with money, sir?” Cohen interrupted, feeling a sudden insight.

“No,” Deronda smiled. “But a precious relation—his sister. A young woman, good and lovely, who will care for him in every way.”

“Married?”

“No.”

“But with a maintenance?”

“She has talents that will ensure her livelihood. A home has already been arranged for Mordecai.”

Silence. Then the grandmother sighed:

“Well, well… and so you’re leaving us, Mordecai?”

“And no children—no Jacob, no Adelaide, no Eugenie!” she wailed.

“Ay, ay,” Cohen said, firm. “Jacob’s learning will wear out. He must go to school. It’ll be hard times for Jacob.”

To Jacob, this sounded like doom. His face filled with sorrow. At the mention of “hard times,” he burst into loud sobs. Adelaide Rebekah followed suit, howling abruptly. Baby woke and screamed. Someone had to carry her out. Hushing filled the room. Mordecai, pierced by the cries, reached for Jacob, who, amid tears, turned his head to see the reaction. His father, saying, “Never mind, old man—you’ll go to the riders,” released him. Jacob ran to Mordecai, who held him close, resting his cheek on the child’s black hair—silent.

Then Cohen, sensing the need for a speech, addressed Deronda with elevated tone, elbows squared, hand on each knee:

“We’re not the sort to begrudge anyone’s luck, sir. I’m not envious. If someone offered to set Mordecai up in a shop two doors down, I wouldn’t make faces. I’m not one to think low of myself or fear others’ chances. If I’m offal, let a wise man tell me—I haven’t heard it yet. In business, I’m not a fragile thing. If they roll me, I curl up like a caterpillar and find my feet when left alone. Though you’re taking some of our good works—property bearing interest—I can afford it. My wife and mother gave their all to Mordecai. A Jew must not act like a servant paid only for reward—though I see no harm in reward if I can earn it. As for schooling—neither poor nor greedy. I wouldn’t hang myself for sixpence. But the truth is—women and children love Mordecai. You can see it, sir. A Jewish man thanks God daily for not being born a woman. But a woman thanks God for being made as He willed. And we know what He made her—a child-bearing, tender-hearted creature. Our children are strong, as Addy’s are. She’s not soft, but her heart is tender. So forgive us, sir, for not being glad at once. And as for this young lady—by what you say, ‘young lady’ is the proper term—we’ll be glad for Mordecai’s sake, when we count our blessings and see where we stand.”

Before Deronda could respond, Mordecai broke in:

“Friends! For food, clothing, shelter—I could not have asked for better than you’ve given. You’ve sweetened the morsel with love. I thought my joy would be to teach the lad till my last breath. But now—I am like one who dressed himself in a shroud, accustomed to making the grave his bed—when the divine voice called, ‘Arise, go forth; the night is not yet come.’ I would not have left your kindness for any lesser cause. But we’ve been taught—‘The reward of one duty is the power to fulfill another,’ said Ben Azai. You’ve made your duty to a poor brother a joy for both of us. Your reward will be that you cannot rest without joy in similar deeds. And may Jacob come to visit me?”

He turned to Deronda, who nodded.

“Of course. It’s no farther than Brompton.”

Jacob, calmed by the unfolding scene, began to see light. “Visit” meant cakes, freedom, grandpa’s knife shop. He danced away from Mordecai, taking a central post on the hearth, hands in pockets.

“Well,” the grandmother sighed, “I hope you’ll get kosher meat, Mordecai. You’ll have to trust your new household.”

“That’s all right,” Cohen said quickly, eager to avoid questions. “So, sir,” he added, turning to Deronda with amused clarity, “it was better than learning you had to talk to Mordecai about! I wondered then. I felt there was something.”

“Mordecai may explain how I came to seek him,” Deronda said, rising. “I should go now.”
