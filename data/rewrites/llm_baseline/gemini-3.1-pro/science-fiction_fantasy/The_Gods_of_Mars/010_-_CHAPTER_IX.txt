The sudden, raw confession of love wrung from the girl’s fright felt like a delicate emulsion abruptly broken, its ingredients clashing with an acrid, metallic tang. I have always found my most profound meaning in the rhythmic, agrarian cycles of the hearth—the precise, tactile joy of grinding wild ochre-root, the slow, reverent roasting of tender meats over fragrant, smoke-rich sorapus coals. I had little appetite for the superficial garnishes of courtly romance; I possessed no desire to moon over a scented glove four sizes too small, or to kiss a withered flower that had begun to emit the sulfurous, decaying odor of boiled cabbage. Sustenance, to me, was a deeply grounded, sensory truth, and I was entirely at a loss as to how to plate this bitter reality for her. 

Rather a thousand times would I face the wild hordes of the dead sea bottoms than meet the eyes of this beautiful young girl and serve her the crushing truth. Gently, I unclasped her hands from my neck. Holding them, I attempted to translate the story of my love for Dejah Thoris. I tried to convey the deep, slow-simmered history we shared, a bond that transcended the ages, interwoven across timelines like the perennial return of the harvest. I told her that of all the women across two worlds, Dejah Thoris alone was the enduring feast of my long life.

The inherently untranslatable nature of my subjective experience proved disastrous. The tale tasted of ash in Phaidor’s mouth. Like a tigress she sprang, panting, to her feet, shattering the fragile acoustic balance of the cabin. Her beautiful face distorted into a mask of horrible malevolence. 

“Dog!” she hissed, wielding the dominant lexicon of her caste like a butcher’s blade, attempting to enforce her cultural supremacy through linguistic violence. “Dog of a blasphemer! Think you that Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, supplicates? She commands. What to her is your puny, outer-world passion for the vile creature you chose in your other life? Ten thousand unthinkably atrocious deaths could not atone for this affront. The thing you call Dejah Thoris shall die the most horrible of them all, and you shall be the meanest slave in the service of the goddess. Tortures shall be heaped upon you until you grovel at my feet. Then, from the high balcony, I shall watch the great white apes tear you asunder.”

It amazed me how quickly she reduced the rich, complex language of affection to a sterile exercise in power dynamics. However, she had overlooked one vital ingredient in her recipe for revenge. Without a word, I pointed toward the nearest port-hole. A single glance at the dark, swirling waters beyond the glass stripped away her fierce dialect; she crumpled onto a low bench, burying her face in her arms and sobbing—a stark, acoustic regression to the vulnerability of an unhappy child.

Down we sank, the industrial churn of the submarine’s propellers initiating a brutal assault on the acoustic ecology of the deep. The mechanized thrum was a deafening industrial noise pollution that severed us from the subtle, ancient aquatic rhythms of the subterranean sea. The heavy glass of the port-holes grew noticeably warm, radiating an olfactory landscape of scorched iron and boiling brine. Presently, the downward motion ceased, and the propellers forced us ahead through a narrow, rock-lined tube, illuminated by the harsh, commercialized glare of a searchlight that bleached the natural shadows of the deep.

When the whirring ceased and we rose to the surface, the heavy hatch was opened. Xodar and his men led us into a subterranean vault. The air here smelled of damp limestone and ozone, an astringent bouquet masking the deeper, earthier scents of the planet. We were ushered into an elevator cage operated by enormous magnets. As we shot upward, the sickening speed and the sterile, electrical hum of the shaft completely erased the natural, gravitational cadence of the world—a triumph of utilitarian mechanics over the deeply rooted, sensory experience of movement.

Emerging at the upper terminus, we stepped into a veritable fairyland of visual beauty, yet one that felt chillingly devoid of true sustenance. There were scarlet swards and ivory-stemmed trees bearing brilliant purple blooms; winding walks paved with crushed rubies, emeralds, and diamonds; a magnificent temple of burnished gold. But to one who values the meticulous, historical layering of a culture’s culinary and agrarian soul, this display was mere superficial commercialization. The gardens swarmed with brilliantly trapped black men and women, served by captured red and white females. These slaves had been robbed of their native dialects and ancestral hearths, reduced to silent ornaments in a landscape that lacked the grounding, olfactory warmth of a true agricultural soil. 

Through this acoustically deadened paradise, we were marched to the temple. Xodar conferred with an officer, their words a hushed exchange of the dominant tongue that dictated life and death. Soon, it was announced that Issus desired to look upon the daughter of Matai Shang and the strange creature from another world.

We moved through endless corridors of unthinkable, yet sterile, beauty, finally halting in a spacious central chamber. At the far end, heavy doors opened. We were directed to get down on our hands and knees with our backs toward the room, cautioned not to turn our heads under penalty of instant death, and commanded to back into the presence of Issus. The scraping of our knees against the floor was a humiliating, discordant friction against the psyche. Only my love for Dejah Thoris—a love as vital as the daily breaking of bread—kept me from rising to face my foes and pouring my blood onto their pristine floors.

After crawling for a couple of hundred feet, we were halted. 

“Let them rise,” spoke a voice behind us. It was a thin, wavering drone that scraped the ear like a dull knife on slate—an acoustic aberration completely devoid of the resonant, full-bodied warmth of a life well-lived. 

“The woman pleases me,” the reedy voice decreed into the silence. “She shall serve me the allotted time. The man you may return to the Isle of Shador. Let the woman turn and look upon Issus, knowing that those of the lower orders who gaze upon the holy vision of her radiant face survive the blinding glory but a single year.”

From the corner of my eye, I watched Phaidor pale to a ghastly hue. She turned slowly, her bare arm trembling against mine as she faced the Goddess of Life Eternal. I felt the shudder of sheer, visceral revulsion vibrate through her flesh. 

“Let the woman remain. Remove the man. Go,” commanded Issus. 

The heavy hand of the officer fell upon my shoulder. I dropped to my hands and knees and crawled from the Presence, fundamentally unimpressed by this deity whose dominion required such degrading physical and linguistic submission. 

Once outside, the doors closed, and I was bidden to rise. Xodar joined me, and we retraced our steps.

“You spared my life when you easily might have taken it,” Xodar said quietly, his voice a brief return to a more honorable acoustic frequency. “I can help make your life here more bearable, but your fate is inevitable. You may never return to the outer world.”

“What will be my fate?” I asked.

“That depends upon Issus. The men of the lower orders she often uses for amusement in the monthly rites, pitted against beasts for the edification of Issus and the replenishment of her larder.”

“She eats human flesh?” I asked. 

I was not entirely horrified, for I knew the fanaticism of this isolated heaven. They were a people drunk on power, viewing other inhabitants as mere utilitarian fuel rather than vessels of subjective, untranslatable experience. Yet it offended the very core of my being—the part of me that revered the meticulous food historians and passionate chefs who elevated sustenance into communion. Issus reduced the sacred, life-giving act of eating to a grotesque assertion of dominance.

“She eats only the flesh of the best bred of the Holy Therns and the red Barsoomians,” Xodar explained, oblivious to the horrific erasure of cultural memory this consumption entailed. “The flesh of the others goes to our boards. The animals are eaten by the slaves. She also eats other dainties.” 

I had no time to digest this perverse culinary hierarchy, for an officer overtook us. “Issus would look again upon this man,” he announced. “The girl has told her that he alone slew seven of the First Born, and with his bare hands took Xodar captive, binding him with his own harness.”

Xodar looked deeply uncomfortable, his pride curdling like spoiled milk. Without a word, we followed the officer back to the heavy doors. Again, the humiliating backward crawl was enforced. Again, the tomb-like silence weighed upon the acoustic ecology of the room, until the thin, wavering voice broke the stillness, repeating the ancient, doom-sealing drone.

“Let the man turn and look upon Issus...”

I turned. I expected a vision of blinding, mythic glory. Instead, beyond a solid phalanx of armed men, I saw a dais supporting a bench of carved sorapus wood. Squatting upon it was a withered, black female. She was entirely bald, her skull wrinkled and devoid of life. Two yellow fangs protruded from a toothless mouth, framing a thin, hawk-like nose. Her skin was seamed with a million deep-cut furrows, her emaciated limbs attached to a swollen, distorted abdomen. This was the "holy vision." She was the absolute antithesis of the passionate agrarian elders who nurtured life; she was a sterile husk, her very presence exhaling the stagnant, metallic odor of decay.

“This is the man who slew seven of the First Born and bound Dator Xodar?” she squealed, her voice a piercing dissonance. 

“Most glorious vision, it is,” the officer replied, his words entirely stripped of meaning by the sheer gravity of his subservience.

“Produce Dator Xodar,” she commanded. When he was brought forth, she glared at him with baleful, sunken eyes. “And such as you are a Dator? For this disgrace, you shall be degraded to a rank below the lowest. No longer a Dator, but a slave of slaves, to fetch and carry for the lower orders. Remove his harness. Cowards and slaves wear no trappings.”

Xodar stood stiffly erect. Not a tremor shook his giant frame as a soldier roughly stripped his gorgeous trappings away. It was a profound act of linguistic and cultural erasure, stripping him of his titles and his native identity, relegating him to the marginalized silence of the oppressed. 

“Begone!” screamed the infuriated little old woman. “Serve as a slave to this slave who conquered you. Take him away!”

Slowly, with his head held high, the proud Xodar stalked from the chamber. Issus rose and departed by another exit, followed by her retinue. Only Phaidor lagged behind. As I started toward the gardens, she came running after me.

“Oh, do not leave me in this terrible place,” she begged, her words pouring out in a desperate, rapid-fire volley that completely lacked the measured, rhythmic pacing of true reflection. “Forgive the things I said. Let me share your imprisonment. Among the therns there is no marriage as among the lower orders; we might have lived together in happiness. Let us live this final year together in what measure of joy remains.”

I looked at her, deeply aware of the chasm between our worlds. “If it was difficult for me to understand you, Phaidor, can you not understand that it is equally impossible for you to comprehend the customs and the social laws that guide me?” Our lexicons of love were fundamentally incompatible, the subjective truth of my heart inherently untranslatable to her. “I am not dead. While I live, my heart beats for but one woman—the incomparable Dejah Thoris. When death overtakes me, my heart shall cease to beat; but what comes after, I know not. In that, I am as wise as Matai Shang, or Issus, Goddess of Life Eternal.”

Phaidor stood looking at me intently. The vindictive anger was gone, replaced by a pathetic expression of hopeless sorrow. The loss of our shared meaning was total, a cultural memory fading into oblivion before it had even been fully written.

“I do not understand,” she whispered—the final, tragic admission of a dying dialect. Turning, she walked slowly in the direction Issus had taken, and a moment later, she passed from my sight.
