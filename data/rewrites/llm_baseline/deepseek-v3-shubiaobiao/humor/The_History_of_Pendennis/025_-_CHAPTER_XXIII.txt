While the good folks of Fairoaks were dozing through their daily routines like a pair of well-worn slippers, the grand old manor across the River Brawl was shaking off decades of dust and cobwebs with all the subtlety of a bull in a china shop.

Yes, dear reader, Clavering Park was about to be inhabited again—an event so shocking it sent ripples through the county faster than a scandal at a vicar’s tea party. The news spread like wildfire: at Clavering Market, at Cackleby Fair (where the pigs paused mid-oink to gossip), and even at Tinkleton Church, where the congregation temporarily forgot about salvation in favor of speculation.

Now, let us introduce Sir Francis Clavering, a man whose life story could be titled *How to Squander a Fortune Without Really Trying*. After a brief and disastrous stint in the military (where his greatest battle was against his own gambling debts), Sir Francis had spent years drifting through European spas, casinos, and boarding houses, perfecting the art of being a charming nuisance. His luck turned—or so he thought—when he married the widowed Jemima Augusta Snell Amory, freshly returned from India with a fortune hefty enough to make even a miser weep with joy.

Lady Clavering, as she now was, had a reputation as mixed as a continental breakfast. Some whispered she was "new money" (the horror!), others that her origins were "regrettably murky" (even worse!). But none of that mattered once her bank balance entered the room. Suddenly, the haut monde of Paris couldn’t get enough of her. She funded convents, missionaries, and even a particularly ambitious scheme to convert the Pope—all while throwing parties so lavish they made Versailles look like a parish bake sale.

Meanwhile, back at Clavering Park, the long-abandoned mansion was receiving its first visitors in years. Sir Francis rolled up in a carriage like a returning conqueror, flanked by his ever-loyal (and ever-loud) friend, Captain Ned Strong—a man whose mustache alone deserved its own military rank.

The housekeeper, Mrs. Blenkinsop, nearly fainted when they arrived. She mistook the dashing Captain Strong for Sir Francis, prompting the real baronet to pipe up, "Wrong fellow, madam! *This* is your rightful lord!"—gesturing to himself with all the enthusiasm of a man announcing a tax audit.

Together, they toured the cavernous halls, Captain Strong waxing poetic about "capabilities" (by which he meant grand balls, a theater in the library, and possibly a jousting arena), while Sir Francis mostly wondered when lunch would be served.

The town of Clavering, naturally, lost its collective mind. The arrival of a baronet! A wealthy lady! A mysterious daughter! Captain Strong, with his bottomless charm and even deeper repertoire of war stories, became the local celebrity overnight. He knew every farmer, flirted with every market girl, and even got the sternest old dowagers tittering behind their fans.

And what of Miss Amory, the enigmatic daughter? Ah, dear reader, she was a puzzle wrapped in a sonnet, dipped in mystery—a young lady who painted, wrote poetry, rode like an Amazon, and, according to Captain Strong, was "a paragon, a muse, a *femme incomprise*."

Pen, ever the skeptic, declared he "hated clever women." Laura, sweet as sugar but sharp as a needle, replied, "Thank you," with a look that could curdle milk.

And so, with new faces, fresh gossip, and enough drama to fuel a dozen drawing-room farces, Clavering prepared itself for the most entertaining social upheaval since the last time someone served the wrong wine at dinner.

Let the games begin.
