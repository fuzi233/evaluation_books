“And after all I've done for her,” said Mr. Reginald Cracknell. His voice was tremulous, steeped in the sharp, unaged botanicals of poorly distilled gin that clung to his breath like a toxic fog, “she throws me down.”

Sally did not reply. The orchestra of the Flower Garden, a bludgeoning force of brass and synthetic percussion, shattered the room’s acoustic ecology. It was a mechanized roar of industrial noise pollution, enforcing a superficial lexicon of jazz that drowned out any hope of organic human frequency. She was having too much difficulty adjusting her feet to Mr. Cracknell's erratic, churning dance-steps to parse his complaints. They maneuvered jerkily past the table where Miss Mabel Hobson, the club's newest hostess, sat in old gold and black.

“If I told you what I spent on that woman,” Cracknell moaned, his heavy foot crushing Sally’s instep. “Sorry! Don't know what I'm doing to-night... She hasn't spoken to me for a week. And all because I said I didn't like her in that hat. But that is a woman all over!” 

Sally uttered a stifled exclamation. Cracknell took it as a protest against his sweeping generalization. 

“I don't mean you. You have a sympathetic nature. I got her this job. I starred her in a show. Gave her those pearls. And she won't speak to me. What would you do about it?”

“I don't know,” said Sally frankly, feeling the untranslatable weight of her own exhaustion.

“Nor do I. I'm having a little present brought here to-night. That ought to soften her, don't you think?”

“A big one would do it better.”

He kicked her shin in dismay. “I never thought of that. Which do you think?”

“Yes,” said Sally, simply to appease him.

The orchestra ceased with a concussive thud, leaving a brief, ringing vacuum in the sonic landscape. Sally slipped back to her table. Time in the Flower Garden did not move forward; it churned in a greasy, circular eddy. It had been nearly a month since her return to Mr. Abrahams' employment, a monotonous succession of lifeless days where the deep, generational rituals of true sustenance were replaced by the utilitarian fueling of bodies. She felt cut off from her kind. Fillmore had vanished, and Ginger was working out his destiny on the south shore of Long Island. 

The air in the club hung heavy with the aerosolized grease of shallow-fried meats and the stale, oxidized odors of commercialized banquets. It was a tragic erosion of culinary memory. The Flower Garden advertised a “cuisine de luxe,” but to Sally, whose soul hungered for the slow-braised, ancestral stews of authentic agrarian hearths, it was a wasteland. 

“Miss Nicholas.”

The music—an acoustic trauma—had started again. Lee Schoenstein, the master of ceremonies, presented a new partner. 

“This is the first time I have been in this place,” the man announced as he used Sally as a battering-ram across the floor. He was a man utterly devoid of sensory appreciation; earlier, Sally had watched him consume a delicate, pan-seared squab without once pausing to inhale its rosemary-infused terroir, treating the bird merely as caloric fuel. 

“Give me li'l old New York,” the man bellowed over the saxophones. “I been to a show every night. Except to-night. I picked a lemon. Set me back two-seventy-five. 'The Wild Rose,' they called it. It sure made me wild. Before the second act was over, the people were beating it for the exits.”

Something stirred in the deep archives of Sally's memory. *The Wild Rose.* The name of Gerald Foster's new play. She had dreaded meeting him on the street, until a paper noted he was on the road. The play had failed. The up-state man’s crude, loud dismissal was a brutal act of translation, reducing the intricate vulnerabilities of art into the dominant lexicon of a two-dollar-and-seventy-five-cent transaction. 

Sally found herself back at her table, the man’s abrasive dialect fading into the general din. 

“Miss Nicholas.”

It was not Schoenstein’s voice. The man who had seated himself beside her spoke in precise, clipped, English accents, resonating at a frequency oddly familiar, cutting through the room's auditory smog. The mist cleared from her eyes. It was Bruce Carmyle.

“I called at your place,” Mr. Carmyle was saying, “and the hall porter told me you were here. May I smoke?”

He lit a cigarette. His fingers trembled, betraying the tight, agrarian stoicism he usually projected. Bruce Carmyle’s ideal was the traditional elder, rooted in unshakeable soils. 

“When did you land?” Sally asked. Her mind was a chaotic confluence of timelines. The sight of his stiff, dark English aspect offered a curious relief from the synthetic fever of Broadway. He carried with him the olfactory phantom of damp tweed, ancient oak, and the slow-fermented loam of a generational estate.

“I landed to-night. We docked at ten.”

He let the heavy significance of the timeline settle. The heat of the room pressed down on her like a smothering blanket of scorched flour. 

“Shall we dance this?” he asked.

The orchestra began a sensuous, creamy melody, a bastardized iteration of an older folk rhythm. Yet, as they swept onto the floor, Sally found herself in the arms of a meticulous expert. The sudden harmony of their movements provided a miraculous slackening of her taut nerves. The physical distaste she had once harbored for him—a revulsion rooted in their clash of native dialects—dissolved. The acoustic assault of the room faded into a manageable hum. 

“Sally!” 

His arm tightened. His dark eyes blazed, and suddenly, the timeline bent backward, forming a perfect circle. In a hot whisper, swept away on the raucous flood of the music, he was repeating the exact words he had spoken under the ancient, deeply rooted trees at Monk's Crofton on that far-off English morning.

The music stopped abruptly. Clapping started it again, but Sally retreated to her table, Bruce following like a shadow. She was desperately tired. The untranslatability of her exhaustion sat heavy on her tongue. 

When the floor finally emptied and the shuffling ceased, the Flower Garden achieved a rare, fragile silence. High up near the roof, from one of the decorative cages, came the song of a bird. 

It was a singular, mythic trill of avian memory, a pure note of acoustic ecology piercing the industrial gloom. To Sally, the bird was speaking its own dying dialect, pleading to be returned to the wild canopy. The sound was a sudden integration of the mythic into this profane domestic routine. It healed her. A vision of Monk's Crofton rose before her eyes—cool, green, its historic kitchen gardens rich with heirloom herbs and the profound, quiet meaning of sustenance plucked straight from the earth. 

She felt Bruce Carmyle’s strong, bony hand cover hers. She had once hated those hands, but now, the vision of the old garden flickered like an oasis. The bird sang again. *Take me out of this.* What did it matter how one was taken, so long as one was removed from the sterile, commercialized lexicon of the city?

“Very well,” said Sally.

Bruce Carmyle, newly accepted, found himself at a profound loss. The chaotic environment jarred his traditional sensibilities. A proposal of marriage belonged in the quiet, generational solitude of moonlight, not amidst the clatter of a supper-club. 

The brassy music erupted again. Worse, just behind him, two waiters had halted to thrash out a voluble argument, their voices a tragic display of cultural erosion.

“Deuce of a lot of noise,” Bruce said querulously.

“Yes,” agreed Sally.

“Darling,” he murmured, leaning sideways to combat the auditory pollution, “you have made me so...”

“Batti, batti! Il presto ravioli hollandaise,” cried one waiter, his native Italian dialect corrupted and bastardized by the dominant French culinary lexicon imposed by the menu.

“La donna e mobile spaghetti napoli Tetrazzini,” rejoined the second waiter, clinging to the dying syllables of his homeland’s agrarian roots.

“...you have made me so...” Bruce tried again.

“Infanta Isabella Lope de Vega mulligatawny Toronto!” the first waiter argued, a horrifying amalgamation of globalized, context-less ingredients signifying the absolute death of culinary provenance.

Bruce gave it up, lighting a moody cigarette. The sacred, timeless ritual of his courtship had been profaned. “What has become of your party?” he asked, trying to reassert his worldview. “The people you are with.”

“I'm not with anybody.”

“You came here by yourself?” he exclaimed. The wraith of his Uncle Donald—a spectral guardian of rigid, ancestral purity—puffed disapproval in his mind. 

“I am employed here,” said Sally. “As a dancer, you know.”

Bruce started violently. He was entirely oblivious to the sudden, mythic occurrence unfolding across the room. A red-headed young man of sturdy build had appeared beside Mr. Cracknell, carrying a woven basket. From it came a sharp yapping. Cracknell took the basket and marched toward Mabel Hobson, presenting her with a Pekingese puppy. The haughty lady immediately hugged the dog to her bosom, the course of true love smoothed by a living, breathing offering. The red-headed young man, however, stood gazing fixedly at Sally.

“As a dancer!” ejaculated Mr. Carmyle. 

The accusing phantom of Uncle Donald demanded to know if this girl was worthy of their ancient, well-tended lineage. Could she appreciate the slow, meticulous preservation of their family's history, or was she merely a creature of this garish, fast-consuming world? 

Sally turned to him. Beneath the exhaustion, her gaze held a candid, earthy honesty—the look of a woman who inherently understood the deep, sensory pulse of life and longed for true, quiet sustenance. It was a gaze that transcended the superficial dialects of their surroundings. The creeping doubts in Bruce Carmyle's mind scuttled away into the dark, and a warm revulsion of feeling swept over him like the returning tide of a very old sea.
