The air in Cloisterham held the scent of dried fruit and damp stone, a heavy perfume of Christmas Eve. The city seemed to have shrunk under the weight of its own traditions, a small, enclosed world where the cathedral clock and the cawing of rooks served as the only rhythm for those returning from the outer darkness. For them, the rustling of leaves and the smell of the Close were ghosts from a childhood that had already ended, circling back to haunt the final moments of a life nearly completed.

Inside the shops, the profusion was excessive. Currants, raisins, candied peel, and moist sugar lay in lavish piles. A mistletoe hung in the greengrocer's door, an obscene green flag in a grey world. There was a Twelfth Night cake, a pathetic thing crowned with a Harlequin, offered for a shilling per soul. The atmosphere buzzed with a cheap gallantry, a frantic attempt to celebrate before the inevitable collapse. The High School was empty; the scholars had fled to Miss Twinkleton's, where the handmaidens fluttered behind glass, becoming skittish and artificial in their confinement.

Tonight, three figures were destined to converge at the gatehouse. The question remained: how did each survive the day leading up to that collision?

Neville Landless sat in his quiet room, the fire low, the shadows long. He was not reading for pleasure but performing a ritual of purification. Until two hours past noon, he wrote and read with a concentration that bordered on religious fervor. Then came the clearing. He swept his table, arranged his books, and burned every stray paper, every untidy accumulation. No note survived unless it bore directly on his studies. He stripped his drawers bare, leaving nothing but the essential.

From his wardrobe, he selected ordinary wear, including sturdy shoes and thick socks for the road. He packed these into a new knapsack bought earlier that day in the High Street. Alongside it went a heavy walking-stick, iron-shod at the bottom, designed for a grip that would not slip. He tested the weight, swung it, balanced it, and placed it on the window seat alongside the bag.

He dressed and stepped out, only to pause on the staircase. He had met Mr. Crisparkle, who emerged from his own room. Neville reached for his stick, intending to take it with him. Mr. Crisparkle watched, smiling, and removed the object from his hand.

"You choose a stick?" the older man asked.

"Really, I don't understand the subject," Neville replied. "I chose it for its weight."

"Too heavy, Neville. Much too heavy."

"To rest upon in a long walk, sir?"

"Rest upon?" Mr. Crisparkle repeated, shifting into a pedestrian stance. "You do not rest upon it; you merely balance with it."

"I shall learn with practice, sir. I have not lived in a walking country."

"True," said Mr. Crisparkle. "Train yourself, and we will cover many miles together. I could leave you nowhere now. Will you return before dinner?"

"I think not, as we dine early."

Mr. Crisparkle nodded, his expression radiating a confident ease that felt calculated.

Neville descended to the Nuns' House. He requested that Miss Landless be told of his arrival, then waited at the gate, refusing to cross the threshold. He was on parole; he could not obstruct Rosa's path.

Helena joined him instantly, mindful of the agreement they had made. They met with affection but avoided lingering. Walking toward the upper inland country, Neville spoke.

"I am not treading upon forbidden ground, Helena," he said, turning as they walked. "You will understand in a moment that I cannot help referring to... my infatuation."

"Had you not better avoid it, Neville? You know I can hear nothing."

"You can hear, my dear, what Mr. Crisparkle has heard, and heard with approval."

"Yes; I can hear so much."

"Well, it is this. I am unsettled and unhappy, and I am conscious of unsettling others. How do I know that, but for my presence, you and the rest of that former party might be dining cheerfully in Minor Canon Corner tomorrow? Indeed, they probably would be. I see clearly that I am not high in the old lady's opinion. I am an irksome clog upon her orderly hospitality, especially at this season, when I must be kept apart from that person. There is a reason for my separation, an unfavorable reputation preceding me with another, and so on. I have put this gently to Mr. Crisparkle, knowing his self-denying ways, but I have put it. What I stressed more is that I am engaged in a miserable struggle with myself. A little change, a little absence, may allow me to emerge stronger. So, with the weather bright and hard, I am going on a walking expedition. I intend to remove myself from everyone's way, my own included, tomorrow morning."

"When to come back?"

"In a fortnight."

"And going quite alone?"

"I am much better without company, even if there were anyone but you to bear me company, my dear Helena."

"Mr. Crisparkle entirely agrees, you say?"

"Entirely. At first, perhaps, he thought it a moody scheme, harmful to a brooding mind. But we took a moonlight walk last Monday to discuss it. I showed him that I wanted to conquer myself. Once this evening is over, it is better that I be away than here. I could hardly avoid meeting certain people walking together here, and that would do no good. It is not the way to forget. In a fortnight, that chance will probably be over. When it arises again, I can go away then. Furthermore, I feel hopeful of bracing exercise and wholesome fatigue. You know Mr. Crisparkle respects such things for his own sound mind and body. His spirit is not likely to maintain one law for himself and another for me. He yielded when convinced of my sincerity. With his full consent, I start tomorrow morning. Early enough to be out of the streets, out of hearing of the bells when the good people go to church."

Helena considered this. If Mr. Crisparkle approved, she would approve; but she did so originally, from her own mind, viewing it as a healthy project, a sincere attempt at self-correction. She pitied him, poor fellow, for going solitary on the great Christmas festival, but she felt it was more important to encourage him. And she did.

"He will write to her?"

"He will write every alternate day and tell her all his adventures."

"Does he send clothes in advance?"

"My dear Helena, no. Travel like a pilgrim, with wallet and staff. My wallet—or knapsack—is packed and ready. And here is my staff!"

He handed it to her. She noted its heaviness, matching Mr. Crisparkle's observation, and asked what wood it was. "Iron-wood," he answered.

Until this point, he had been cheerful. Perhaps carrying his case with her, presenting it in its brightest aspect, had roused his spirits. Perhaps succeeding in this display was followed by a sudden revulsion. As the day closed and the city lights began to flicker into existence, he grew depressed.

"I wish I were not going to this dinner, Helena."

"Dear Neville, is it worth caring much about? Think how soon it will be over."

"How soon it will be over!" he repeated, gloomily. "Yes. But I don't like it."

"There may be a moment's awkwardness," she suggested cheerfully, "but it can only last a moment. You are quite sure of yourself."

"I wish I felt as sure of everything else as I feel of myself," he answered.

"How strangely you speak, dear! What do you mean?"

"Helena, I don't know. I only know that I don't like it. What a strange dead weight there is in the air!"

She pointed to the copperous clouds beyond the river, noting the rising wind. He scarcely spoke again until they reached the gate of the Nuns' House. When they parted, she did not enter immediately but remained watching him along the street. Twice he passed the gatehouse, reluctant to enter. Finally, as the cathedral clock chimed a quarter past twelve, he hurried inside with a rapid turn.

So he ascended the postern stairs.

Edwin Drood passed a solitary day. Something of deeper moment than he had anticipated had slipped out of his life; in the silence of his chamber, he had wept for it the previous night. Though the image of Miss Landless hovered in the background of his mind, the pretty, affectionate creature, firmer and wiser than he had supposed, occupied its stronghold. With some misgiving regarding his own unworthiness, he thought of her and of what they might have been to one another if he had been more earnest sooner, if he had valued her higher, if he had studied the right way to appreciate and enhance his lot instead of accepting it as an inheritance of course. Yet, despite the sharp heartache, the vanity and caprice of youth sustained that handsome figure in the twilight of his thoughts.
