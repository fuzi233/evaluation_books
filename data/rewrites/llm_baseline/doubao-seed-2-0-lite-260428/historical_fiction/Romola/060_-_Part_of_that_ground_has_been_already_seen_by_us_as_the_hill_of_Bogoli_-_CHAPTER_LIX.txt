The first gilded glow of Tuscan dawn had barely touched the terracotta rooftops of Florence when Romola set out for San Marco. The evening before, she had persuaded Fra Salvestro to secure her an audience with Fra Girolamo Savonarola in the convent’s chapter house. Savonarola was famously rigorous about guarding his reputation from the faintest whisper of scandal, so such private interviews were extraordinarily rare, and never held behind closed, unobserved doors: the hour chosen was one when the outer cloisters would be thronged with other lay visitors, no room left for gossip of secret conspiracy.

She chose to wind through the busiest heart of the city, eager to read the pulse of public feeling. Every sun-dappled loggia, every sheltered piazza corner, every shop that doubled as a regular meeting spot for gossips hummed with heated, free-flowing debate. Trade was languishing so badly that every idle artisan, merchant, and clerk had hours to spend arguing politics. The factions for and against executing the recently uncovered Medicean conspirators were working furiously over the three-day grace period to sway the crowd, and handbills were already circulating everywhere: one set blared in huge, unmissable capital letters that to spare the conspirators meant total ruin for the Republic, the other screamed just as loudly that the law must be upheld and every condemned man granted his legal right to appeal to the Great Council. Around these bold, black headline phrases swarmed dense paragraphs of smaller, less eye-catching text, full of fine-grained arguments no one much bothered to read—for in that first giddy flush of the printing press’s novelty, Florence had decided no argument was more convincing than a bold, question-begging slogan in all caps.

But Romola was desperate to read those smaller, detailed arguments, and hurried along, craning her neck at every step to spot a spare copy. For blocks she only saw handbills clutched in the hands of avid readers, or pasted to walls where city sbirri were already tearing them down in ragged strips. It was only when she picked up her pace behind the Baptistery of San Giovanni, deliberately avoiding the hordes of acquaintances who loitered on the main piazza, that she spotted the familiar peddler Bratti, hawking stacks of the handbills to passersby for small coins. She had long known the humble street vendors of Florence like old friends, and walked straight over to him.
“Bratti, do you have both sorts of handbill? Hand me one of each, quickly.”
“Two sorts, Madonna,” Bratti said, peeling apart the damp printed sheets at a maddeningly slow pace that tested Romola’s frayed patience. “There’s ‘Law’ and there’s ‘Justice.’”
“Which sells faster?”
“‘Justice’ goes quickest by far, so I raised the price to two danari. But then I thought, ‘Law’ is good ware too, deserves to cost just as much as ‘Justice’—no one values cheap things, eh? If I sold ‘Law’ for one danaro I’d be doing it wrong. I’m a fair trader. Both came free, both sell for a fair profit. D’you want more than one of each?”
“No, no—here’s a white quattrino for the pair,” Romola said, tucking the papers into her sleeve and hurrying onward.

She was soon stepping into San Marco’s outer cloisters, where Fra Salvestro waited beneath the arches, chattering away to a group of lay visitors as he so loved to do. In the wake of Savonarola’s excommunication, crowds had once feared to be seen at the convent, but under the current government friendly to the Frate, that timidity had all but vanished. Romola recognized one of the visitors at once: Andrea Cambini, a staunch ally of Francesco Valori, who was gesturing wildly as he recounted some story, while Fra Salvestro listened with that familiar, trivial, gossip-hungry curiosity that always struck Romola as unfit for a confessor. She caught disjointed phrases about the conspirators’ fate drifting over to her, and her irritation spiked. Rather than approach the group, she caught Fra Salvestro’s eye, nodded toward the chapter house door, and he signed his understanding before vanishing into the inner cloister. A lay brother was waiting to open the chapter house door for her, and closed it softly behind her as she stepped inside.

The faded, mournful frescoed saints on the walls looked down on her just as they had the day she had come to the convent after her brother Dino’s death. The old memory tugged at her, soft and indistinct, blending into her sharp, present terror for her godfather’s life, but she gave it no room to linger. She pulled the handbills from her sleeve to read while she waited for Savonarola, but she had barely finished the one arguing for the right of appeal when the door creaked open. She folded the papers tight in her hand and stood, expectant.

She knelt to accept his blessing, as all visitors did for private audiences, and stood back a few paces once he had spoken his benediction. It had been months since she had seen him, during his long seclusion after the excommunication, and the marks of endless overwork, fasting, and sleepless nights had carved themselves deeper into his face. But what struck her most was a harder, less definable shift: the soft, serene elevation and warm, human fellowship that had once drawn her so deeply to him was no longer there, not as she remembered it. Half of it was her own alienation, born of the quiet grief that he had not stood by her godfather already. Half of it was the new hardness in his gaze, the preoccupation of a man who had already spent days arguing this matter with himself, and did not want to reopen the debate.
“It is a weighty matter that has brought you here, my daughter,” he said, his tone gentle from deliberate self-control, not spontaneous warmth. “I know you do not come to me over small trifles.”
“Father, you know what I need before I even say it,” Romola burst out, all pretense falling away as her plea poured out. “I am here for the life of the old man I have loved second only to my own father for every day I can remember. I beg you not to stay silent: use your power over the people of Florence to stop them from denying their own fellow citizens a right that you yourself fought so hard to win for them.”
“I do not meddle in the functions of the State, my daughter,” Savonarola said, clearly reluctant to rehash the internal debate he thought he had already settled. “I have preached and labored for Florence to have a just government, for a just government is needed to nurture the Christian life—but I do not interfere in specific disputes that are the province of experienced, sworn citizens.”
“Surely, father—” Romola began, then broke off, checking her own impetuosity. She was suddenly acutely aware that she was remonstrating with the man who had once been her whole source of guidance and strength, and the act of pushing back bruised her own long-held reverence.
Savonarola was sharp enough to read her quiet conflict, and too noble to retreat into the slippery, self-justifying evasions he often fell back on while preaching before great crowds. “Speak what is in your heart, my daughter,” he said, standing with his arms folded, watching her calmly.
“I was going to say that this matter is more important than so many others you have preached on with fiery fervor,” Romola said, her passion rising again. “If you counted it a victory to win the Florentines the right of appeal to the Great Council, how can you stand by and let that very right be snatched away from the first men who ever need it? That touches the Christian life far closer than debating whether the Dauphin would die, or whether Pisa would be conquered.”
A faint, flicker of pain twitched at Savonarola’s strong lips before he spoke. “My daughter, I only speak as I am given to speak: I do not command visions of the future outside the common wisdom of men. As for the Appeal law, I fought for it so that no Florentine could lose his life or property to the private hatred of a small handful of men in power. But these five men have plotted to overthrow our free government and bring back a corrupt tyrant, and they were condemned with the assent of a huge gathering of their fellow citizens. They refused to bring their case before the Great Council at the start, when they were first arrested. They have forfeited their right to appeal.”
“How can they have forfeited it?” Romola cried. “The right to appeal exists for people after condemnation, and they were not condemned until this week. Forgive me, father, but it is private hatred that denies them this right: it is the violence of a small few that frightens the rest into agreeing. You know the assembly was split again the moment after it looked to be of one mind. You yourself once preached, more earnestly than any other lesson, that there must be no room for party strife to spill over into bloodshed, no chance for private ill will to play out in public punishments. You know private grudges are at work here. Will you not interpose on the side of mercy, when so many hold mercy is also the side of law and justice?”
“My daughter,” Savonarola said, with more visible emotion than he had yet shown, “there is a mercy that is weakness, that is treason against the common good. Florence’s safety, which means more than just the welfare of its own people, demands severity now, just as it once demanded mercy. These men are not condemned for a plot that is already past: they are condemned for a plot that was still to be carried out. The tyrant is still gathering his troops in Romagna, and Florence’s enemies across all of Italy wait for any chance to crush us.”
“What plot?” Romola said, flushing, her hands trembling with shock.
“You hold papers in your hand, I see,” Savonarola said, nodding at the crumpled handbills in her fist. “The other one will tell you the government has uncovered new, positive evidence.”
Romola unfurled the unread handbill and saw the news of the second, planned August uprising at once. A cold, sick lurch went through her: she knew at once her husband Tito had turned that evidence over to the authorities to secure his own safety, buying his immunity with the lives of others. She crushed the paper tight in her fist.
“Father,” she said, new fire in her voice, “what safety is there for Florence if the worst, most treacherous men always escape scot-free? You yourself interceded with Francesco Valori to spare Lorenzo Tornabuoni, that two-faced man who flattered you and San Marco endlessly to curry favor—but my godfather Bernardo del Nero has always been honest, he never pretended to be your follower. Ask all of Florence which of the five condemned men has the truest heart, and no one will name anyone but Bernardo. You have already interfered to save one prisoner. You are not neutral. And your word could spare him.”
“I do not desire Bernardo’s death,” Savonarola said, his face flushing darkly. “It would be enough if he were exiled from the city.”
“Then why will you not speak up?” Romola burst out, all her long-suppressed indignation boiling over. “Why not save a seventy-five-year-old man from dying an ignominious death, grant him at least the fair chance the law promises? It is not that you are bound to neutrality! You spoke for Tornabuoni because he is friendly to San Marco. Bernardo does not feign friendship. He is not to die as a Medicean traitor—he is to die because you do not love him!”
Silence fell heavy in the frescoed room. Romola stood breathless, cheeks burning, lips quivering, and heard her own words echo in her head, jarring so painfully against all the old, reverent memories she held of Savonarola that she felt as if she had committed sacrilege. Her compunction swelled fast, and she stepped toward him impulsively.
“Forgive me, father. It pains me that I spoke those words—but I cannot help speaking. I am small and feeble next to you; you once brought me light and strength, and I submitted to you because I could see that light. But I cannot see it now. You yourself have preached that there comes a time when the soul must follow no guide but the voice within, to judge whether a consecrated thing holds true sacred virtue. So I had to speak.”
Savonarola’s face tightened. He was a man of great power, long accustomed to deference, and quick to resent opposition that challenged the grand, holy ends he believed he served. But for a moment, that irritation faded, swallowed up in the hard, constant struggle that defined half his life: the fight between his deep hunger for simplicity and purity, and the tangled, messy web of political compromises, egoistic demands, and impossible conditions he had wrapped himself in. He looked at Romola for a long, quiet moment.
“You are fully forgiven for your frankness, my daughter. You speak out of the fullness of your family’s love. But that personal affection must give way to the needs of the Republic. If the men who know all the State’s secrets truly believe that public safety demands the ultimate penalty for these five conspirators, I cannot overrule their judgment, when I have deliberately stepped aside from the administration of these affairs.”
“So you do not protest their death? You let them deny the appeal?” Romola said, repelled by what felt like a cowardly subterfuge.
“I have said I do not desire their death.”
“Then you are willing to stand by indifferent while Florentines inflict a death you do not want, when you could protest and stop it, when you could defend a law you fought so hard to pass?” Romola said, her indignation spiking again. “You did not used to stand aloof! You did not use to shrink from protest. Do not say you cannot interfere when men’s lives are at stake—say you want them dead. Say you think more blood and more hatred is good for Florence. Will killing five Medicean conspirators end factional strife? Will executing a noble, honest old man like Bernardo del Nero save a city that shelters men like Dolfo Spini?”
“My daughter, enough. The cause of freedom, the cause of God’s kingdom on earth, is often most endangered by enemies who hold certain genuine human virtues. The worst, most wicked man is not always the hardest obstacle to the triumph of good.”
“So you would rather Bernardo die, because his goodness makes him a more dangerous threat?” Romola said, half angry, half desperate. “I cannot untangle your thoughts, father. I cannot hear the true voice of your conscience.”
Savonarola paused, his own emotion sharper than it had ever been before in his talks with her. “Be thankful, my daughter, that your soul has been spared this kind of perplexity. Do not judge those who have been given a far harder lot to carry. You see only one path of action here. I see a dozen. I must choose the one that furthers the work God has entrusted to me. Minor respects must be sacrificed for the great end I serve. The deaths of five men—even if they were less guilty than these—are a light thing weighed against the fight to overthrow the vicious tyrannies that stifle the life of Italy, that rot the Church from within. A light thing weighed against building God’s kingdom on earth, the end I live for, and am ready to die for.”
But Romola could not hear the grandeur in his words. All she felt was total, uncrossable antagonism. What he called perplexity looked to her like sophistry, like double-dealing. His words only stoked the fire of her indignation, lighting up the memory of every small mistake he had ever made, turning all her old trust in him into what felt like a stupid, blind delusion. She spoke with bitter clarity.
“Do you know so perfectly what furthers God’s kingdom, father, that you dare to cast aside mercy, justice, the very teachings you once preached? The French king’s invasion did not bring renovation to Italy. Take care, father, that your enemies are not right when they say all your visions of furthering God’s kingdom only serve to strengthen your own party.”
“They are not wrong!” Savonarola cried, his eyes flashing. For a split second, Romola’s voice sounded to him exactly like the voice of his loudest enemies. “The cause of my party is the cause of God’s kingdom.”
“I do not believe it!” Romola said, her whole frame shaking with passionate repugnance. “God’s kingdom is far wider than your small faction. If that is the case, I will stand outside of it, with the people I love.”
They stood there, face to face, lit by two opposing certainties, no more words possible between them. Romola drew her hood over her head, and turned and walked out of the chapter house without another sound.
