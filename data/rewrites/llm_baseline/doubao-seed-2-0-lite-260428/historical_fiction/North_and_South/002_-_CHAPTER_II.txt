“By the soft green light in the woody glade,
On the banks of moss where thy childhood played
By the household tree, thro’ which thine eye
First looked in love to the summer sky.”

Margaret had slipped back into her plain, comfortable morning dress, riding the railway home in quiet, slow contentment beside her father, who had travelled up to London to stand witness at Edith’s wedding. Her mother had stayed behind at the parsonage under a flurry of half-explained excuses no one but Mr. Hale could fully parse: he knew well his gentle argument that her soft grey satin, worn but perfectly respectable, was more than appropriate for a family wedding had fallen on deaf ears, and that with no spare coin to outfit her from bonnet to slippers in new finery, she would not suffer the embarrassment of appearing at her only sister’s only child’s celebration. Had the wealthy, long comfortable Mrs. Shaw guessed the true cause of her sister’s absence, she would have showered gowns and bonnets and trinkets on her without a second thought; but nearly twenty years had passed since she was the poor, pretty Miss Beresford, and the only grievance she could still summon any passion for was the misery of marriages with wide gaps in age, a topic she could hold forth on for half an hour at a stretch. To her mind, dearest Maria had married perfectly: a man only eight years her senior, with the sweetest temper and that rare striking blue-black hair, and Mr. Hale was the most delightful preacher she had ever heard, a perfect model of a country parish priest. It was no great logical leap for her to conclude, as she mused over her sister’s lot, “Married for love, what more could dearest Maria ever wish for in the world?”
Mrs. Hale, if she had spoken honestly, could have rattled off a list in reply: a silver-grey glacé silk gown, a fine white chip bonnet for the wedding, dozens of small luxuries for the celebration, hundreds more little comforts for the parsonage home.

Margaret only knew her mother had found the trip inconvenient, and she was secretly glad their reunion would unfold at Helstone, rather than amid the chaotic rush of the last few days in Harley Street, where she had played the part of Figaro, called on by every guest and family member at once. Her mind and body ached with the memory of the last forty-eight hours of running errands, soothing overwrought bridesmaids, and rushing last minute wedding preparations. The hurried farewells to all the people she had lived alongside in London for two years hung over her, soft and sad, for times that were gone and never could be brought back — no matter how happy those years had been, or how mundane, they were finished now. It was strange to feel such a heavy heart, on the way to the home and quiet country life she had ached for all those years in London, in that drowsy lull before tired senses blur into sleep. She wrenched her thoughts away from the past, turning instead to the bright, serene future she had dreamed of, and looked up to see her father leaned back asleep in the railway carriage.
That once vivid blue-black hair was now streaked thin with grey, lying soft over his brows, the bones of his face sharp and clear beneath his skin — almost too sharp for conventional beauty, were his features not so fine and gentle, holding a quiet grace all their own. The repose on his face was not the soft calm of a man who leads a placid, untroubled life, but the heavy rest of someone worn thin by long weariness. Margaret was startled by the faint, drawn anxiousness etched into his features, and turned over the known details of their life to find a cause for those deep lines of habitual distress.
“Poor Frederick,” she thought, sighing to herself. “If only he had chosen to be a clergyman instead of running off to the navy, he would not be lost to us all. I wish I knew the full story — Aunt Shaw would never tell me the whole of it, only that he can never come back to England after that terrible affair. Poor dear papa, how sad he looks. I am so glad I am home now, to be here to comfort him and mamma.”
When he woke, she turned to him with a bright, unfatigued smile, and he smiled back, faint as though the small effort cost him something. The lines of quiet anxiety settled back over his face the moment he was still, and he had a little habit of half-opening his mouth as if to speak that left his expression perpetually undecided. He shared the same large, soft slow-moving eyes as his daughter, framed by delicate white eyelids. Margaret took far more after him than her mother; strangers often wondered that two such conventionally handsome parents could have a daughter so far from regular beauty, as some unkind souls put it. Her mouth was wide, no tiny rosebud suited only for murmuring “yes” and “if you please, sir”, but those wide lips curved soft and full, a rich deep red. Her skin was not the bright pink-and-white English fair of society belles, but smooth and delicate as polished ivory. If her usual bearing was too dignified and reserved for a girl of nineteen, right now, talking to her father, her face was bright as morning, dimples showing all through cheeks that lit up with childish glee and unbound hope for the years ahead.

It was late July when they arrived back at Helstone. The forest trees stood heavy with dark, dusky full green, the ferns beneath catching every slanted beam of sunlight, the air sultry and still and thick with growing things. Margaret tramped along the forest paths at her father’s side, crushing the ferns under her light feet with a giddy, almost cruel glee, breathing in that sharp, earthy scent that rose up as they yielded to her step. She wandered out across the wide sun-warmed commons, teeming with wild bees and butterflies and all the small living things revelling in the July sun, and this life — these long, unstructured walks — exceeded every one of her old anticipations. She took a fierce, proud joy in her forest, and the folk who lived there were her folk: she made hearty friends with every cottage family, learned to speak their soft country turns of phrase naturally, nursed their sick babies, read the Bible and old stories slow and clear to their half-blind grandparents, carried pots of jellied broth and spiced fruit to their ill, and resolved to start teaching at the village school very soon. She was forever being lured away from the schoolroom to drop in on some friend, man, woman or child, tucked away in a cottage in the green shade of the oak trees. Her life out of doors was perfect. But the peace indoors had sharp thorns.
With the unguarded honesty of a child, Margaret blamed her own sharp eyes for noticing that all was not as it should be at home. Her mother, always so tender and kind to her, was now often discontented with their lot, convinced the bishop was shockingly neglecting his duties in not granting Mr. Hale a larger, better paid living, and almost reproached her husband for never bringing himself to say he wanted to leave small Helstone to take charge of a bigger, busier parish. He would sigh softly in reply, saying he would be thankful enough if he could tend his small flock in Helstone as he ought, but every day he felt more overwhelmed, the world around him growing more bewildering. Every time his wife pressed him to make an effort to seek church preferment, Margaret saw him shrink further and further into himself, and she would step in to try and reconcile her mother to their quiet life. When Mrs. Hale complained the mist and trees of Helstone were ruining her health, Margaret would tempt her out for walks across the sun-dappled, wind-swept common, sure her mother had simply grown too used to staying indoors, rarely venturing further than the church, the schoolhouse, and the nearest neighbouring cottages. The trick worked for a little while, but when autumn drew near and the weather grew changeable, Mrs. Hale’s conviction that the place was unhealthy deepened, and she began to complain even more often that her husband, far more learned than their old neighbour Mr. Hume, and a far better parish priest than Mr. Houldsworth, had never received the preferment those two men had been granted.
This slow, constant gnawing discontent that marred the peace of the parsonage was something Margaret had never been prepared for. She had known, and even revelled in the knowledge, that she would be leaving behind all the frivolous luxuries of London society that had felt like chains to her, and she had prided herself on being able to do without all of them if need be. But the storm cloud never rises from the corner of the horizon you are watching. In her previous short holidays at Helstone, she had brushed off her mother’s small passing complaints about their position as trivial little annoyances, forgotten amid all the happy memories of home.

In the last weeks of September, the autumn rains and gales set in, and Margaret was forced to stay indoors far more than she liked. Helstone was miles away from any neighbours of their own social standing. “It is unquestionably one of the most out-of-the-way spots in all England,” Mrs. Hale said one afternoon in a plaintive mood. “I cannot help regretting constantly that your papa has no one at all of equal station to speak to here; he is quite thrown away, seeing no one but farmers and labourers week after week. If only we lived on the other side of the parish, we could walk over to the Stansfields’ — the Gormons would be an easy stroll too, at the very least.”
“The Gormons?” Margaret said. “Are those the Gormons who made their fortune in trade in Southampton? I am glad we do not visit them. I do not care for ‘shoppy’ people. I think we are far better off, knowing only cottagers and labourers, and people with no pretensions at all.”
“You must not be so fastidious, Margaret dear,” her mother chided, privately thinking of the handsome young Mr. Gormon she had once met years ago at Mr. Hume’s dinner party.
“My tastes are very broad, I think!” Margaret laughed. “I like all people whose work ties them to the land. I like soldiers, and sailors, and the three learned professions, as they call them. You would not have me admire butchers and bakers and candlestick makers, would you, mamma?”
“The Gormons were none of those, they were very respectable coach builders.”
“Very well. Coach building is still a trade, and I think it is far more useless than butchering or baking. Oh, how tired I grew of driving around in Aunt Shaw’s carriage every single day, how I longed to just walk!”
And walk Margaret did, rain or shine, nearly dancing for joy as she tramped the paths at her father’s side, the soft west wind at her back carrying her across the heath as light and easy as the fallen red and gold leaves wafting along the autumn breeze. But the long evenings were hard to fill pleasantly. Right after tea, her father would slip away to his small, cluttered library, leaving her alone with her mother. Mrs. Hale had never cared much for books, and early in their marriage had discouraged Mr. Hale’s habit of reading aloud to her while she sewed. For a little while they had played backgammon together, but as Mr. Hale grew more devoted to his school and his parishioners, every interruption from his duties had come to feel like a hardship to his wife, not a natural part of his work, something to be resented and struggled against. So he had withdrawn to his library while their children were still small, to spend his evenings reading the metaphysical and speculative books he loved best.
The old bookshelves in the drawing room held no new volumes for Margaret, only the well-bound, little-read English classics that had been cleared out of her father’s library long ago: Thomson’s Seasons, Hayley’s life of Cowper, Middleton’s Life of Cicero, the lightest, newest, most amusing titles on the shelves. Margaret would tell her mother every little detail of her life in London, and Mrs. Hale listened with great interest, amused and curious at some stories, at others apt to compare her own sister’s comfortable, easy life in Harley Street to their narrow, constrained circumstances at the parsonage. On those evenings, Margaret would often fall quiet mid-sentence, listening to the monotonous drip-drip-drip of rain on the lead panes of the small bow window. More than once she found herself mechanically counting the drops, summoning the courage to ask the question she had carried in her heart for months: where was Frederick now? What was he doing? How long had it been since they last heard from him? But the knowledge that her mother’s delicate health, and all her discontent with Helstone, stretched all the way back to the time of the naval mutiny her brother had been mixed up in, a story Margaret had never heard told in full, and one that everyone seemed determined to bury in sad oblivion, made her hold her tongue. She thought to ask her father when her mother was not around, then decided she could speak more easily of it to her mother later — and likely there was no new information to learn anyway. Only one letter from her father, sent before she left London, had mentioned that they had heard from Frederick: he was in Rio de Janeiro, in good health, and sent his love to her. It was dry, bare news, none of the warm, full details she ached for.
“Poor Frederick” was the only name they used for him on the rare occasions his name came up. His bedroom was kept exactly as he had left it, dusted and set to rights every week by Dixon, Mrs. Hale’s lady’s maid, who had been hired decades ago by Lady Beresford to care for the two pretty Miss Beresfords, the belles of Rutlandshire. Dixon still considered Mr. Hale the great blight that had fallen on her young lady’s prospects, convinced that if Maria had not rushed into a marriage with a poor country parson, no one could say how grand and important she might have become. But she was far too loyal to abandon her through the quiet hardships of married life, and considered herself her mistress’s protecting fairy, whose job it was to fend off the malignant giant of Mr. Hale’s inconvenient, low income clergyman ways. Master Frederick had been her favourite and her pride, and when she went into his room every week to tidy it, her staid, dignified expression softened, as if she half expected him to walk through the door that very evening.

Margaret was convinced some new, troubling news about Frederick, hidden from her mother, was the source of her father’s quiet unease. Mrs. Hale noticed no change in his behaviour at all. His spirits were gentle and tender, easily cast down for days after sitting with a dying parishioner, or hearing of some local tragedy. But now Margaret saw a constant absence of mind, as if his thoughts were preoccupied by some heavy weight no amount of comforting mourners, or teaching schoolchildren to build a better future, could lift. Mr. Hale no longer wandered the parish visiting his people as often as he once had, and spent far more time shut up in his study, lingering by the front gate on fine mornings to wait for the postman, whose arrival at the parsonage was marked by a sharp rap on the back kitchen shutter. Once, the postman’s knock often went unanswered for minutes, while the household went about their work unaware of the time. Now, Mr. Hale would loiter in the garden by the sweetbriar hedge if the weather was fine, or stand staring out his study window on grey days, until the postman had walked all the way down the lane, giving the parson a quiet, half-respectful, half-confidential little shake of his head before he turned back inside to start his day’s work, his face heavy with unspoken worry.
Margaret was still young, though, and all vague unproven worries blew away like thistledown the moment the first bright, golden days of October arrived. Two whole weeks of cloudless crisp autumn sun unfolded, all the rain gone, opening up scores of deep, shadowy forest glades Margaret had only caught glimpses of through the summer green. She had learned to draw alongside Edith in London, and had regretted wasting the fine summer days wandering idly through the woods, so now she was determined to sketch every glen and great oak before winter set in. She was just fastening the lid of her paint box and sketch board one bright morning, when the housemaid Sarah threw wide the drawing room door, and announced, “Mr. Henry Lennox.”
