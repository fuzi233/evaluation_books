Those who live apart from the world—shut away in schools, cloisters, or quiet houses behind high walls—are often forgotten by those outside, as if they had vanished into silence. Friends who once wrote letters, sent messages, or visited regularly may suddenly stop entirely. There is no warning, no explanation—only a long, unbroken stillness. The post arrives without your name; the door remains untouched; the familiar voice grows silent.

And yet, there are always reasons—though the one left behind cannot see them. While they sit still, their loved ones move through life’s whirlwind: births, deaths, travels, affairs that fill days with noise and haste. To the isolated soul, time stretches endlessly—each hour feels heavy, each minute dragging like a burden. But to others, that same stretch of days may be filled with urgency, change, and decisions too many to count.

The wise person learns to endure this silence. They hold their thoughts close, keep their heart quiet, and accept that sometimes life demands stillness—like a dormouse curling into its nest, shutting out the world until spring returns. They say to themselves: *It is right. It must be so.* And perhaps, one day, the frost will melt, the sun will warm the earth again, and joy will come back—not because it was earned, but because it was allowed.

That is how I lived for seven weeks after the evening at the theatre. No word came. Not a letter, not a visit, not even a message. My days were empty as blank pages.

Midway through that silence, I began to wonder—had something happened? Had my friends fallen ill? Was there grief at La Terrasse? The mind, when alone too long, begins to ache. Doubts grow thick and dark, pressing in from every side. Sleep becomes an enemy; dreams turn cruel, showing disasters, abandonment, loss. At night, I tossed and started, haunted by visions of emptiness. I was thin, pale, worn down—not by sickness, but by longing.

By the seventh week, I accepted the truth: this silence was not personal. It was fate. Circumstance. A part of my life’s pattern. I did not blame myself—I knew better than that—but I could not help feeling the weight of it. The road ahead felt rough, and I longed for light.

I tried to fill the void. I began a delicate lace project, studied German with fierce concentration, read dry, dense books from the library—anything to keep my hands busy, my mind occupied. But all these efforts felt like chewing on iron or drinking saltwater. They gave no comfort.

My worst moment came at post-time. I dreaded it. I knew the exact hour, though I tried to forget it. I would wait, tense and trembling, listening for the ring of the bell. When it came—and nothing followed—the disappointment was like a blow. I felt as though I were trapped in a cage, waiting for food that never arrived.

In truth, I suffered deeply. Fear, loneliness, despair—they crept in like shadows. Hope grew frail. Sometimes, despair came so close I could feel its breath—cold, heavy, choking. I used to feel it sink into my chest, making every movement difficult, every thought slow. That letter—the one I waited for—was all I had left to believe in. Without it, life tasted bitter.

When hunger became unbearable, I turned to my only treasure: the five letters I kept locked in a case. Oh, how bright those months seemed now! Those letters were stars in my sky. I would read them at night, lighting a small wax taper, stealing up to my room after hours. I’d open the box and read them over and over, as if each word could feed me. But soon, the words lost their meaning. The gold faded. I pined, growing thinner, weaker—yet not truly ill.

One evening, late, I sat reading, feeling the power to understand slipping away. The letters had become hollow. Then—footsteps. Quick, light steps on the stairs. I recognized them instantly: Ginevra Fanshawe.

She entered, dressed in bright silk, her shawl slipping from her shoulders, her curls damp and loose. She looked tired, annoyed.

“It was a stupid evening,” she said. “Stupid people.”

“Mrs. Cholmondeley?” I asked.

“No. I didn’t go there.”

“Then where?”

“My uncle de Bassompierre has arrived.”

“Your uncle de Bassompierre? Are you glad?”

“Not at all. He’s hateful.”

“Because he’s foreign?”

“He’s English enough. His mother was French—de Bassompierre. She died, left him estates, a title, and the name. Now he’s a grand man.”

“And you hate him for that?”

“Don’t I know what Mama says? He wasn’t really my uncle—he married Mama’s sister. She hates him. Says he killed Aunt Ginevra with coldness. He looks like a bear.”

She sighed. “Such a dreadful evening. I’ll never go to his hotel again. Imagine walking into a room alone, and this fifty-year-old man coming forward, talking for a few minutes, then turning his back and leaving. How rude! I suppose his conscience troubled him. Everyone says I look just like Aunt Ginevra. Mama says it’s ridiculous.”

“Were you the only guest?”

“The only one—except Missy, my cousin. A spoiled little thing.”

“M. de Bassompierre has a daughter?”

“Yes, yes. Don’t ask so many questions. I’m exhausted.”

She yawned and threw herself onto my bed. “Oh, and guess what? Mademoiselle was nearly crushed at the theatre a few weeks ago.”

“I heard.”

“She lives in a big hotel on the Rue Crécy?”

“Exactly. How do you know?”

“I’ve been there.”

“You have? Really? You’re everywhere lately. Did Mother Bretton take you?”

“Dr. and Mrs. Bretton were there. They have access to the apartments. ‘My son John’ attended her during the accident—accident? Bah! She probably deserved it for acting so high and mighty. Now there’s a friendship forming. I heard talk of ‘auld lang syne.’ How boring!”

“All?” I asked.

“All? Well, I forgot to mention the old woman and her boy.”

“Dr. and Mrs. Bretton were there tonight?”

“Yes, yes. And Missy played hostess. What a vain little doll!”

Ginevra was sulking. Her pride had been wounded. She had expected attention, flattery, admiration—but none came. She lay there, fuming, defeated.

“Is Miss de Bassompierre well now?” I asked.

“As well as anyone. But she plays sick to get attention. Watch her lying on a couch while ‘my son John’ forbids excitement—ugh! It was nauseating.”

“It wouldn’t have been so bad if you were in her place.”

“I hate ‘my son John!’”

“Who is ‘my son John’? Dr. Bretton doesn’t call him that.”

“Then she should. He’s a clumsy, bear-like John.”

“That’s not true. And since I’ve run out of patience, I demand you leave this room.”

“Passionate thing! Your face is red as a poppy. Why are you always so angry about Jean?”

I was furious—but not worth arguing with such a fragile creature. I blew out the candle, locked my bureau, and left her there.

The next day was Thursday—a half-holiday. Breakfast was done. I returned to the first classe. The post-hour approached. I sat, waiting—like someone expecting a ghost. A letter was unlikely. But still, I couldn’t forget it might come.

It was a cold January day. The east wind blew sharp and biting. I had grown sensitive to weather—how the north and east winds deepened pain, made sorrow heavier. The south could soothe; the west, sometimes cheer—unless it brought storm clouds, which drained all energy.

I remember leaving the classe, running bareheaded to the bottom of the garden, hiding among the leafless bushes. I hoped the postman’s ring would come while I was out of earshot. I muffled my head in my apron, blocked my ears—afraid of the sound, afraid of the silence that would follow.

At last, I returned. The classe was empty. On my black desk, I saw a white object—a flat, white thing. The post had come. Rosine had been here. Like an angel, she had left a sign.

It was a letter. A real letter. From the only person I had ever known to write to me. My heart leapt.

I stepped closer, bending to read the handwriting. My hand trembled. I expected a firm, masculine script. Instead, I saw a delicate, feminine scrawl. For a moment, I thought: *This is cruel.*

But I recovered. Life goes on, even when it hurts. Our senses remain—even when joy is gone.

I opened the letter. By then, I recognized the writing. It was dated “La Terrasse.” It read:

> *Dear Lucy,*
> *I’ve been wondering what you’ve been doing lately. Not that I think you’d have any trouble explaining yourself—you’re always busy, always happy, just like us at La Terrasse. Graham is busier than ever—so sought after, so engaged, I tell him he’ll grow conceited. As a good mother, I try to keep him humble—no praise from me, as you know. But still, he’s a fine boy. His mother’s heart dances when she sees him. After a full day of dealing with tempers, caprices, and suffering—sometimes even causing it—he comes home in such a kind, pleasant mood that I feel as if my day dawns when others’ nights fall.*
> *He needs correction, of course, and I give it. But he’s so resilient—no matter how I try to make him sulk, he turns around with jokes. You know him well. I’m just an old simpleton, writing about him.*
> *As for me, I’ve had my old Bretton agent visiting. We’ve been buried in business. I want to restore some of what his father left him. He laughs at my worries, saying I should see how well he can provide for both of us. He teases me about wanting sky-blue turbans, diamonds, livery servants—an hotel. Talking of turbans, I wish you’d been with us the other night. He was tired. After tea, he fell asleep in my chair. I took the opportunity to play a trick: I brought out the sky-blue turban and carefully placed it on his head. It suited him perfectly—Eastern, but fair-haired. I wrapped my cashmere around him, and there he was—a young bey, a pacha, the most splendid sight.*
> *It was fun—but only half-enjoyed, since I was alone. You should have seen it.*
> *He woke up. The mirror told him what he looked like. Now I live in fear of revenge.*
> *But to the point: I know Thursday is a half-holiday. Be ready by five. I’ll send the carriage to pick you up. Come—there may be someone you know. Goodbye, my dear, wise, grave little goddaughter.*
> *Very truly yours,*
> *Louisa Bretton.*

A letter like that changes everything. I might still have been sad—but I was calmer. My friends were safe. Graham was well. His mother was cheerful. Their love for me remained unchanged.

Yet, how strange it was to compare their seven weeks with mine. And how wise it is for people in unusual positions to keep silent about their pain. The world understands hunger, starvation—physical suffering. But who understands the agony of being shut away? Who can imagine going mad in solitude?

They see the prisoner released—mad, broken, gone. But how his mind unraveled, how his nerves burned then froze—this is too complex for most. Speak of it? It would be like standing in a market square and speaking in the tongue of Nebuchadnezzar, the king who dreamed and raved.

Let such understanding remain rare. Let the world continue to believe only physical pain deserves pity. For now, let it be so.

Later that day, the cold deepened. A storm rose from the north, heavy with snow. I feared no carriage would come. But trust Mrs. Bretton—she would not be denied.

At six, I was lifted from the carriage and carried over the snow-covered steps of La Terrasse.

I ran through the vestibule, up the stairs, into the drawing-room. There stood Mrs. Bretton—like a summer day in winter. Even if I’d been twice as cold, her kiss and embrace would have warmed me.

After years of bare rooms and cold benches, the blue saloon dazzled me. The fire blazed crimson and bright, like Christmas itself.

She held my hand, chatted, scolded me for being thinner. Then she said the snow had tangled my hair and sent me upstairs to fix it.

In my sea-green room, I found another fire, candles lit—two tall waxlights flanking the mirror. Between them, a figure stood—small, pale, dressed in white, touched with scarlet. A winter spirit.

For a moment, I thought of Graham and his illusions. But then, the figure turned.

A large eye, framed by long lashes, met mine. The lashes were dark, softening the gaze.

“Oh,” she whispered, “you’ve come.”

She smiled slowly, deeply.

I knew her. Only once had I seen that face—fine, delicate, unforgettable.

“Miss de Bassompierre,” I said.

“No,” she replied. “Not Miss de Bassompierre—for you.”

I waited.

“You’ve changed,” she said, stepping closer. “But you’re still you. I remember your face, your hair, your expression…”

I moved toward the fire. She stood opposite, studying me. Her eyes grew softer, deeper, then dimmed.

“It makes me almost cry to remember,” she said. “But don’t think I’m sentimental. I’m glad. Truly glad.”

I was confused, speechless.

“I think we met only that night,” I stammered, “when you were hurt…”

She smiled. “You’ve forgotten. I’ve sat on your knee, been lifted in your arms, shared your pillow. You don’t remember the night I came crying—like a naughty child—to your bed, and you took me in? You don’t recall how you comforted me when I was frightened?”

Then I understood.

“And you are little Polly?”

“I am Paulina Mary Home de Bassompierre.”
