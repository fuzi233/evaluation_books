The Will, which I had prepared myself to regard as a solemn banquet of last words and moral reflections, was dispatched like a plate whisked off a table before the blessing had been said. Mr. Bruff sent for Samuel to stand as second witness; ink was set out; the pen was put into my aunt’s hand; and the scratching of it on paper—thin as the rasp of a knife on a cheap dish—ended all, in less time than it takes a careful cook to taste a sauce.

I was moved—deeply moved—to offer a few suitable sentences on the occasion. But Mr. Bruff’s eye and manner were like the lid of a pot clapped down: they kept the steam of my feelings from rising. He folded the Will, and looked at me as if expecting me to withdraw and leave him alone with Lady Verinder.

My bag, with its precious stores, lay ready on my lap. My errand of mercy was not to be jostled out of the room by a lawyer’s glance. He might as well have tried to shove St. Paul’s Cathedral aside with his umbrella. To do him justice, he understood quickly. He muttered an expression which was not prayer, and retreated with the same sort of violence in which worldly men retreat from a truth they cannot bargain with.

As soon as we were alone, my poor aunt sank back upon the sofa, and the faintest colour rose in her face—as if the paper she had signed had cost her more blood than ink.

“I hope you won’t think yourself neglected, Drusilla,” she said. “I mean to give you your little legacy, my dear, with my own hand.”

Here was the very moment Providence bakes for us, like bread set to rise in secret warmth. I opened my bag at once, and drew forth the top volume—an early edition (only the twenty-fifth) of that searching book, believed to be by precious Miss Bellows, entitled *The Serpent at Home*. It lays the Evil One bare, not in far-off crimes, but in the homeliest daily habits; as if one were to find bitterness in the sugar-bowl, and corruption at the heart of a loaf.

The chapters most fitted for female meditation are, “Satan in the Hair Brush;” “Satan behind the Looking Glass;” “Satan under the Tea Table;” “Satan out of the Window”—and many more, setting snares in the very places where women most innocently move.

“Give your attention, dear aunt, to this precious book—and you will give me all I ask,” I said, and handed it to her open at a marked passage—one continued blaze of burning eloquence on the subject of Satan among the Sofa Cushions.

Lady Verinder—actually reclining, without thought, upon her own cushions—glanced at the page as one might glance at a strong spice when the palate is weak, and gave it back to me with confusion deepening.

“I’m afraid, Drusilla,” she said, “I must wait till I am a little better, before I can read that. The doctor—”

At the very word, I knew the flavour of what would follow: that cold, thin broth of unbelief which the medical profession ladles out for souls in danger, under the pretence that the body must not be disturbed. How often had these men—trained to measure pulse and temperature, and blind to everything that cannot be weighed—stood between me and the perishing, calling my books “exciting,” as if the prospect of salvation were a fever to be brought down!

“The doctor tells me,” my misguided relative went on, “that I am not so well today. He forbids me to see any strangers; and he orders me, if I read at all, only to read the lightest and the most amusing books. ‘Do nothing, Lady Verinder, to weary your head, or to quicken your pulse’—those were his last words, Drusilla, when he left me today.”

There was no use in openly setting my ministry above his. To do that would be to give him a handle; and such men are skilful at turning a patient’s weakness into a weapon. I yielded—for the moment only. There are many ways, as every prudent housekeeper knows, of introducing a wholesome ingredient into an unwilling mouth: if it will not go in as a draught, it may go in as a seasoning; if not at the table, then in the quiet hour when the appetite wakes of itself.

“You might feel stronger, dear, in an hour or two,” I said. “Or you might wake tomorrow morning with a sense of something wanting, and even this unpretending volume might be able to supply it. You will let me leave the book, aunt? The doctor can hardly object to that!”

Before she could refuse, I slipped it under the sofa cushions, half in and half out, close by her handkerchief and smelling-bottle. Each time her fingers went groping—each time her own small domestic necessities guided her hand—the book would be there. Sooner or later (who knows?) the book might touch her, as surely as yeast touches flour and makes it rise.

Having set my first seed, I judged it wise to withdraw.

“Let me leave you to repose, dear aunt; I will call again tomorrow.”

As I spoke, my eye fell towards the window. It was crowded with flowers—boxes and pots thick as a market-stall in summer. Lady Verinder loved these perishable treasures, and would rise, when she could, to smell them, as if scent could do what prayer had been forbidden to do.

A new thought—quick, practical, blessed—came to me.

“Oh! may I take a flower?” I said, and moved unsuspected to the window.

I did not take one away. I added one—another book from my bag—nestling it among geraniums and roses, where her hand, reaching for leaf and bloom, must find also the printed admonition. And as soon as I had done that, the thought followed, as naturally as a second course follows the first: Why not do the same in every room she enters? Why not season the whole house with truth, as an old countrywoman seasons a great pot—patiently, bit by bit, until the flavour is everywhere?

I said good-bye, and, crossing the hall, slipped into the library. Samuel came up to let me out; supposing I had already gone, he returned downstairs.

On the library table lay two of the “amusing books” recommended by that infidel doctor—light reading, meant to tickle rather than nourish. I covered them at once with two of my own publications, as one covers stale pastry with a clean cloth and sets wholesome bread in its place.

In the breakfast-room, my aunt’s favourite canary sang in his cage—thin, bright notes that made the air feel living. The poor little creature’s music was the sort that belongs to true domestic peace: not the coarse clang of street traffic, not the hammering and hooting of the modern city that drives thought into corners, but a small, clear sound like water dropping in a quiet garden. Lady Verinder always fed the bird herself. Groundsel lay strewn on a table beneath the cage. I put a book among the groundsel. When she came to feed the canary, the truth would lie there with the seed.

In the drawing-room, more opportunities waited. Her favourite musical pieces were on the piano; I slipped in two books among the sheets, so that any turning of pages would turn also to instruction. I placed another in the back drawing-room, beneath some unfinished embroidery that I knew to be hers—threads and patterns meant to carry on a tradition from hand to hand, like an old recipe passed down in a family’s own dialect, the words of it never quite translating into anything else.

A third little room opened from the back drawing-room, shut off by curtains instead of a door. On the chimney-piece lay my aunt’s plain old-fashioned fan. I opened my ninth book at a special passage and used the fan as a marker, keeping the place—so that when she lifted it, the words would lift with it.

Next came the question: should I go higher still, to the bedroom floor, at the risk of encountering the person with the cap-ribbons and receiving insult? But what is insult to one who has work to do? A poor Christian is the one who trembles at a raised voice.

I went upstairs.

All was quiet. Perhaps it was the servants’ tea-time; the house had that mid-afternoon hush, when cups clink faintly far away and footsteps are softened, and the whole place seems to listen to itself. My aunt’s room was in front. The miniature of my late dear uncle, Sir John, hung opposite the bed. It seemed to smile; it seemed to say, Deposit a book.

Tables stood on either side of the bed. Lady Verinder slept badly and wanted—or thought she wanted—many things at night. I put one book near the matches on one side, and one book under the box of chocolate drops on the other. Whether she reached for light, or reached for sweet comfort, there was a precious publication to meet her hand and say, without noise, without argument, Come—try me.

One book remained at the bottom of my bag. One room remained unexplored—the bath-room opening out of the bedroom. I peeped in; and the inward voice, which never deceives, whispered, You have met her everywhere else; meet her at the bath, and the work is done.

A dressing-gown lay across a chair. It had a pocket. Into that pocket I slipped my last book.

When I left the house, unsuspected by any of them, with my empty bag under my arm, my sense of duty done was exquisite. Worldly people chase Pleasure through the loud, jangling streets, through theatres and coffee-rooms and all those places where the air is thick with talk and the ear cannot rest; and they call themselves happy. How easy it is to be happy, if you will only be good.

That night, folding up my things and thinking of the true riches I had scattered from top to bottom of my wealthy aunt’s house, I felt as free from anxiety as if I had been a child again. I sang one verse of the Evening Hymn. I fell asleep before I could sing another.

Quite like a child again.

On rising next morning, how young I felt! I might add how young I looked, if I could dwell on the concerns of my own perishable body. But I cannot—and I add nothing.

Towards luncheon time—not for creature-comforts, but for the certainty of finding my dear aunt—I put on my bonnet to go to Montagu Square. As I was ready, the maid at my lodgings looked in.

“Lady Verinder’s servant, to see Miss Clack.”

I occupied the parlour-floor then: front parlour for my sitting-room—small, low-ceilinged, poor in furniture, but neat as a well-scoured pan. In the passage I saw Samuel, the young footman, civil and fresh-coloured, with a teachable look and an obliging manner. I had always felt a spiritual interest in Samuel, as one feels interest in a young kitchen-boy who might still be trained to take pride in honest work if only the right hand directs him.

I asked him in. He carried a large parcel under his arm. When he set it down, it seemed to alarm him.

“My lady’s love, Miss; and I was to say that you would find a letter inside.”

Having given the message, he looked as if he longed to run away. I detained him with a few inquiries.

Could I see my aunt if I called? No—she had gone out for a drive. Miss Rachel had gone with her. Mr. Ablewhite had taken a seat in the carriage too.

Knowing how sadly dear Mr. Godfrey’s charitable work was in arrear, I found it strange that he should be driving out like an idle man. I questioned Samuel again. Miss Rachel was going to a ball that night, and Mr. Ablewhite had arranged to come to coffee and go with her. There was a morning concert advertised for tomorrow, and Samuel was ordered to take places for a large party, including one for Mr. Ablewhite.

“All the tickets may be gone, Miss,” said the innocent youth, “if I don’t run and get them at once!”

He ran as he spoke—leaving me alone with thoughts that had no sweetness in them. That night we had a special meeting of the Mothers’-Small-Clothes-Conversion Society, summoned to obtain Mr. Godfrey’s advice and assistance. Instead of sustaining our sisterhood under an overwhelming flow of Trousers that quite prostrated our little community, he had arranged to take coffee in Montagu Square and go to a ball! The next afternoon had been chosen for the Festival of the British-Ladies’-Servants’-Sunday-Sweetheart-Supervision Society. Instead of appearing as the life and soul of that struggling institution, he was to be one of a party of worldlings at a morning concert!

What did it mean?

Alas! it meant our Christian Hero was about to reveal himself in a new character, and to become associated in my mind with one of the most awful backslidings of modern times.

But to return to the passing day. Left alone, I turned to the parcel which had intimidated Samuel. Had my aunt sent me my promised legacy, perhaps in cast-off clothes, worn-out silver spoons, unfashionable jewellery—any of those household remnants which the poor accept as they accept bones for broth, grateful for what there is?

I opened it—and what met my view?

The twelve precious publications I had scattered through the house, returned to me by the doctor’s orders.

Well might Samuel have shrunk from the parcel. Well might he have run.

My aunt’s letter was short, poor soul: she dared not disobey her medical man.

What was to be done now? With my training and principles, I had no more doubt than a good cook has about lighting the fire again when it has been damped. Once self-supported by conscience, once embarked on a manifest usefulness, the true Christian does not yield. Public or private influences do not move us once we have our mission. Taxation may follow; riots may follow; wars may follow; we go on.

We are above reason; we are beyond ridicule; we see with nobody’s eyes, hear with nobody’s ears, feel with nobody’s hearts but our own. Glorious privilege! And how is it earned? My friends, spare yourselves the inquiry. We are the only people who can earn it—for we are the only people who are always right.

In the case of my misguided aunt, the next form of perseverance revealed itself plainly. Preparation by clerical friends had failed, owing to her reluctance. Preparation by books had failed, owing to the doctor’s obstinacy. So be it.

What next?

Preparation by Little Notes.

If the books were driven out, their best passages could return disguised—copied out by different hands, addressed as letters to my aunt. Some sent by post; some distributed about the house on the same plan I had used before. As letters they would raise no suspicion; as letters they would be opened—and, once opened, might be read. Some I wrote myself:

“Dear aunt, may I ask your attention to a few lines—”  
“Dear aunt, I was reading last night, and I chanced on the following passage—”

Other letters were written by my valued fellow-workers of the sisterhood:

“Dear madam, pardon the interest taken in you by a true, though humble, friend—”  
“Dear madam, may a serious person surprise you by saying a few cheering words—”

Thus, under the respectable seal of correspondence, we reintroduced the precious words in a form not even the doctor’s watchful materialism would suspect. Before evening closed in, I had a dozen awakening letters instead of a dozen awakening books. Six I arranged to send by post at once; six I kept in my pocket for personal distribution next day.

Soon after two o’clock I was again in the field of pious conflict, making kind inquiries of Samuel at Lady Verinder’s door.

My aunt had had a bad night. She was again in the room where I had witnessed the Will, resting on the sofa, trying to sleep. I said I would wait in the library, on the chance of seeing her.

In the fervour of my zeal, it never occurred to me to inquire about Rachel. The house was quiet; it was past the hour at which the musical performance began. I assumed she and her pleasure-seeking party—Mr. Godfrey included—were all at the concert, and devoted myself to my work while time and opportunity were still mine.

My aunt’s correspondence of the morning—including the six awakening letters posted overnight—lay unopened on the library table. She had evidently not felt equal to grappling with so many; and if she entered later she might be daunted by the sight of the heap. I put one letter from my second set by itself on the chimney-piece, solitary, meant to tempt curiosity by its separation. A second I placed deliberately on the breakfast-room floor, so the first servant entering would think my aunt had dropped it and would be specially careful to restore it to her.

The basement floor thus sown, I ran upstairs to scatter my mercies next among the drawing-rooms.

As I entered the front room, a double knock sounded at the street-door—a soft, fluttering, considerate little knock, the sort that asks to be welcomed. Before I could slip back to the library where I was supposed to be waiting, Samuel was already in the hall answering.

It mattered little, I thought. In my aunt’s condition, visitors were not admitted.

To my horror and amazement, this visitor proved an exception. Samuel’s voice rose clearly after answering questions I did not hear:

“Upstairs, if you please, sir.”

The next moment I heard footsteps—a man’s footsteps—coming up toward the drawing-room floor. Who could this favoured male visitor be?

The answer sprang up: the doctor, of course.

In the case of any other person, I would have let myself be seen; there was nothing strange in my having tired of the library and come upstairs. But my self-respect would not permit me to meet the man who had insulted me by sending back my books. I slipped into the little third room beyond the back drawing-room and dropped the curtains that closed it off.

If I waited a minute or two, the usual thing would happen: the doctor would be conducted to his patient.

I waited. A minute. Two. More.

I heard the visitor walking restlessly to and fro. I heard him talking to himself. The voice stirred recognition in me like a familiar tune heard through a wall—heard, yet not quite grasped in the same shape, as if the very sound carried meanings that could not be translated into any other tongue of the heart.

Had I been mistaken? Was it not the doctor but someone else—Mr. Bruff, for instance?

No. An unerring instinct told me it was not Mr. Bruff.

Whoever he was, he still spoke to himself. I parted the heavy curtains the least morsel in the world and listened.

The words I heard were:

“I’ll do it today!”

And the voice that said them was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite’s.
