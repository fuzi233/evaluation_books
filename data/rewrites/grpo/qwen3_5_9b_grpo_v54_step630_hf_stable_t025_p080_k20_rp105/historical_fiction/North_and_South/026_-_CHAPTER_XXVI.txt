"I have found that holy place of rest / Still changeless."
— *Mrs. Hemans*

When Mr. Thornton left the house that morning, the world seemed to tilt beneath his feet. His passion had not merely cooled; it had curdled into a physical blindness. He felt as though Margaret, rather than the graceful woman she appeared, had been a rough fish-wife striking him with clenched fists. There was a genuine, throbbing agony in his skull, a pulse hammering against his ribs like a trapped bird. The noise of the street, the glare of the sun, the endless rumble of carriages—all of it grated against his nerves. He cursed himself for this weakness, yet he could not recall the precise trigger, only the magnitude of the fallout.

In that moment of raw exposure, a strange comfort seized him: the ability to weep openly, not for himself, but as a child might rage against a perceived injustice. He told himself he hated her. But as the word formed, a jagged bolt of love tore through his fury, illuminating the darkness. His greatest solace was in embracing his torment. He resolved that even if she despised him, treated him with sovereign indifference, or scorned him utterly, he would remain unchanged. She could not alter his nature. He loved her, and he would love her, defying both her and this crushing physical pain.

He paused to cement this resolve. An omnibus rolled by, heading toward the countryside. The conductor, mistaking his stillness for hesitation, pulled up. Apologizing felt like too great an effort, so he climbed aboard. The vehicle carried him past rows of terraced houses, then detached villas with manicured gardens, until the scenery shifted to hedgerows and finally a small rural town. As the passengers alighted, Thornton followed, stepping out into the open fields. He walked briskly, seeking relief in the sharp motion of his stride.

Now, the fog lifted. He remembered the absurdity of his morning self—the pitiful figure he must have cut, doing precisely what he had sworn was the most foolish act imaginable, only to meet the exact consequences he had always predicted. Was he bewitched by those beautiful eyes, that soft, sighing mouth pressed against his shoulder just yesterday? He could not shake the memory of her arms around him, even if never again. He saw only fragments of her: sometimes brave, sometimes timid; tender one moment, haughty and regal the next. He reviewed every encounter, trying to erase her image by cataloging it, yet she remained vivid in every dress, every mood. This morning, she had looked magnificent, her eyes flashing with the conviction that sharing his danger proved her affection.

If he was a fool in the morning, he remained so in the afternoon. The sixpenny ride offered no wisdom, only a sharper conviction: there was no one like Margaret. She did not love him, and never would. Yet, nothing in the world should hinder him from loving her. He returned to the market square and boarded the omnibus for Milton.

By late afternoon, he was set down near his warehouse. The familiar surroundings triggered familiar routines. His workload was heavier than usual due to the previous day's commotion. He had to meet with his fellow magistrates, finalize arrangements for the safety of his newly imported Irish workers, and ensure they remained isolated from the discontented Milton workforce. Finally, he had to face his mother.

Mrs. Thornton had spent the entire day in the dining room, poised for news of her son's acceptance by Miss Hale. Every sudden noise made her jump; every time the door opened, she braced herself, only to find an insignificant errand-runner. Her rigid face would soften into a look of despondency, then snap back to its stern composure. She forced her mind into domestic channels, calculating the needs of a future household. She sorted through baskets of linen, distinguishing her own stock, marked "G.H.T.," from her son's purchases. Some of his linens were exquisite Dutch damask, a pride she had once held dear. She knitted her brows, pinching her lips, and carefully unpicked the old initials, searching for Turkey-red thread to replace them. Finding none, she stared into the empty air, her mind drifting through visions where her son was the sole focus—her pride, her property.

Still, he did not arrive. Surely he was with Miss Hale. A pang of jealous pain, both physical and mental, shot through her. She rose, straightening her spine, a grim smile replacing her despair. She prepared for the triumphant visitor who would never know the depth of her regret. To her, the future daughter-in-law was not an individual but a function: John's wife, a vessel for honor, obedience, and the restoration of household plenty. If Miss Hale were a Milton lass, she might have liked her; she possessed spirit and flavor, despite her prejudice and ignorance. A bitter comparison with Fanny played in her mind, leading her to speak harshly to her daughter before retreating to Henry's Commentaries to distract herself from inspecting the tablecloths.

Then, she heard his step.

Even as her eye scanned the page and her memory mechanically recited the sentence, her heightened senses registered his approach. He moved from the hall to the hat-stand, then to the room door. Why did he pause? Let her know the truth.

She kept her head down over the book. He approached the table, waiting for her to finish the paragraph. With an effort, she looked up. "Well, John?"

She knew the weight of those few words. He wanted to jest, to mask the bitterness in his heart, but he owed his mother better. He stepped behind her, bending her gray, stony face back so she could not see his expression, and kissed her cheek.

"No one loves me," he murmured, "no one cares for me, but you, mother."

He turned away, leaning his head against the mantelpiece, tears forcing their way into his manly eyes. She rose, swaying slightly. For the first time in her life, the strong woman tottered. She placed her hands on his shoulders, her gaze locking onto his.

"Mother's love is given by God, John. It holds fast forever. A girl's love is like a puff of smoke—it changes with every wind. And she would not have you, my own lad, would she?" She gritted her teeth, baring them like a dog. He shook his head.

"I am not fit for her, mother. I knew I was not."

She ground out words between her closed teeth, a silent curse that fell heavy in intent if not in wording. Yet, her heart leaped with the knowledge that he was hers again.

"Mother!" he urged, hurriedly. "I cannot hear a word against her. Spare me! My heart is weak and sore; I love her yet, I love her more than ever."

"And I hate her," Mrs. Thornton said in a low, fierce voice. "I tried not to hate her when she stood between you and me, telling myself she would make you happy. I would give my heart's blood to do that. But now, I hate her for your misery's sake. Yes, John, there is no use hiding your aching heart from me. I am the mother who bore you, and your sorrow is my agony. If you do not hate her, I do."

"Then, mother, you make me love her more. She is unjustly treated by you, and I must balance the scales. But why speak of love or hatred? She does not care for me, and that is enough. Let us never name the subject again. It is the only thing you can do for me. Let us never mention her."

"With all my heart," she replied. "I only wish that she, and all belonging to her, were swept back to the place they came from."

He stood gazing into the fire for a moment or two. Dry, dim eyes filled with unwonted tears as she watched him, yet she remained grim and quiet when he spoke again.

"Warrants are out against three men for conspiracy, mother. The riot yesterday helped to break the strike."

Margaret's name was never mentioned again between mother and son. They reverted to their usual mode of discourse—facts, not opinions, and certainly not feelings. Their voices and tones were calm and cold. A stranger passing by might have concluded that such frigid indifference between such close relations was entirely natural.
