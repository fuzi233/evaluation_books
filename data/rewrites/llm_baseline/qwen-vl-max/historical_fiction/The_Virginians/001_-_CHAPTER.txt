In which one of the Virginians returns to England

It was a fine autumn morning in 1759, and the air hung heavy with the scent of woodsmoke and damp earth. At Oakhurst, the great estate nestled in the rolling hills of Surrey, the leaves had begun to turn—crimson, gold, and russet—drifting down like embers from the trees. The house stood quiet, its stone walls weathered by time and memory, its windows reflecting the pale blue sky. Within, the servants moved with hushed precision, preparing for the arrival of a guest whose name had long been whispered across the drawing-rooms of London and the plantations of Virginia.

He came not by coach but on horseback, riding through mist-laden lanes, his cloak flapping behind him like a banner. His face, though still young, bore the marks of distant lands—the sun-kissed skin, the sharpness of purpose in his eyes. He was Harry Esmond, son of a planter and a lady of English blood, raised among tobacco fields and Indian trails, yet now returning to the soil of his ancestors.

As he dismounted before the portico, the old butler stepped forward, bowing low. “Mr. Harry Esmond?” he asked, voice trembling slightly.

“Aye,” replied Harry, stepping down with ease. “And you are Mr. Warrington’s man?”

The butler nodded. “I am. And if you’ll follow me, sir, your rooms have been prepared.”

Harry smiled faintly. “Then let us be about it. After six years abroad, I’ve no desire to linger in the cold.”

Inside, the house breathed history. Portraits lined the hallways—men in powdered wigs, women in silk gowns, their eyes fixed as if watching every step he took. One painting caught his attention: a woman with dark hair and a look of quiet strength, her hand resting on a child’s shoulder. Below it read: *Lady Hester Esmond, née Warrington.*

He paused. “She was my mother,” he murmured, more to himself than anyone else.

That evening, at dinner, the family gathered around the long oak table. There were cousins, uncles, aunts—all speaking in voices that seemed both familiar and strange. The talk turned to politics, war, and the colonies. America, they said, was restless. The French were still in Canada. And the King, though far away, was always near in conversation.

But Harry listened only half-heartedly. His thoughts wandered—not to the wars or the land, but to the woman who had died giving birth to him, and to the father he had never known. He remembered the stories told in Virginia: how his father had left England under scandal, how his mother had followed him across the sea, only to die in childbirth.

Now, standing beneath the chandeliers of Oakhurst, he felt both welcome and out of place. He was home—but not truly. He was a Virginian, yes, but also an Englishman. A gentleman, perhaps, but one shaped by forests and frontier life.

Later, alone in his room, he lit a pipe and stared out the window. The moon rose over the garden, casting silver light upon the sleeping grounds. Somewhere, a dog barked. Then silence.

He sighed. “Well,” he said aloud, “here I am. Let the world begin again.”
