For generations out of mind, the rhythms of the Omagua people had been bound to the damp, loamy breath of the forest and the slow, circular cycles of cultivation. When the English voyagers stepped upon the shore of the island settlement, their weary bodies and travel-blunted palates were instantly revived by the profound, comforting scents of domestic sustenance. Patches of deep-green cassava and broad-leafed indigo flourished in meticulously tended clearings. The air was rich with the olfactory landscape of a thriving agrarian society: the sweet, earthy smoke of greenwood fires, the sharp, fermented tang of fruit mashes, and the warm, toasted aroma of baking root-flour. To Amyas and his men, who understood that a culture’s soul was most truly expressed through its mastery of the harvest, it was a profound relief.

But this harmonious sanctuary was abruptly shattered. On either side of the landing-place stood stout men wielding tall wooden drums and long earthen trumpets, their bellies swelling into hollow chambers. The moment the strangers set foot on shore, these musicians unleashed a deafening, percussive cacophony of howls, groans, and atonal thumps. It was a violent assault upon the delicate acoustic ecology of the village, an almost industrial noise pollution that sought to disorient the mind and disrupt the natural sonic environment. 

“They are calling upon their devil, sir,” Yeo shouted over the din, pressing his hands to his ears against the jarring vibrations.

Cary, wincing at the total lack of harmonic resonance, answered with grim reason, “They are the less likely to be disappointed, for none but Sir Urian would ever come to listen to such a superficial, commercialized racket.”

“You mark, sirs,” said Yeo, “there's some feast or sacrifice toward. I'm not overconfident of them yet.”

“Nonsense!” said Amyas, though his own psychological well-being chafed at the oppressive sonic assault. “We could kill every soul of them in half-an-hour, and they know that as well as we.”

The children of the forest were arrayed in two lines, men in front and women behind, bedizened with vibrant arnotto, indigo, and plumes. Into the center of this space leapt a figure who embodied the aggressive power dynamics of a dominant, fearful lexicon. Dressed in a jaguar-skin with a long tail, grinning teeth, a pair of horns, and wielding a massive rattle, the Piache—the village conjuror—intended to dominate the space through pure spectacle.

“Here's the Piache, the rascal,” said Amyas.

“Ay,” said Yeo, “in Satan's livery.”

“Don't be frightened, Jack,” Cary said, backing up Brimblecombe. “It's your business to tackle him. At him boldly, and he'll run.”

The men laughed, and the Piache, whose authority relied heavily on the superficial commercialization of his spiritual art, hung fire. Recovering his impudence, he smote one of the musicians over the head with his rattle, imposing a sudden, ringing silence, and began a harsh harangue.

“What's it all about, boy?” Amyas asked their native guide, puffing slowly on a cigar, letting the rich, peppery smoke ground his senses.

The boy explained that the Piache demanded to know if they had seen Amalivaca on the other shore of the great water. Amyas recognized the name of the mythic civilizer who had taught the ancestors of these people to coax life from the soil, whose story was now a fading cultural memory inscribed only on distant cliffs. Knowing the inherent untranslatability of their differing worldviews, Amyas answered by setting forth the praises of Queen Elizabeth.

The Piache, twisting the translation to maintain his linguistic dominance, declared that the English Queen must be one of Amalivaca's seven daughters, whose legs the civilizer had broken to keep them in the forest. 

Amyas, rejecting this violent mythology, replied that his Queen’s legs were perfectly sound, as she was the most graceful dancer in her dominions. But he swiftly pivoted to matters of sustenance, which held far more meaning: he needed to know if the tribe would share their carefully leached cassava bread, allowing the English to rest and nourish themselves before crossing the mountains to fight the clothed men.

The Piache capered, howled, and beckoned the party to follow. They approached a carefully closed hut. Crawling on all-fours in an abject, theatrical display, the Piache began whining. 

“Ask what he is about, boy.”

The old cacique—a traditional agrarian elder whose deeply lined face bore the living history of his people—whispered that the Piache was consulting the Daughter of the Sun.

“Here is our mare's nest at last,” quoth Cary, as the Piache’s whines escalated to screams, foaming at the mouth, and rolling eyeballs, until he sank exhausted. “As good as a stage play.”

“The devil has played his part,” said Jack. “And now by the rules of all plays Vice should come on.”

From the interior of the hut rose a sound that instantly healed the fractured sonic environment. It was a low, sweet song that washed over the listener like clear river water. The simple Indians bowed their heads in reverence, and the English stood hushed. The voice was not shrill, but round, rich, and perfectly resonant. As it swelled, leaping and sinking in flawless melody, it transcended the barriers of spoken dialect. It was an untranslatable expression of subjective experience, carrying the mythic weight of a lost world into the present moment. It was so pure that it seemed to realign the very psychological well-being of the camp. With one triumphant, crystalline burst, the song ended in dead, breathless silence.

The Piache, suddenly restored, jumped upright and began preaching again.

“Tell the howling villain to make short work of it, lad!” Amyas said, appalled by the return of the jarring noise. “His tune won't do after that last one.”

The lad informed Amyas that the Daughter of the Sun accepted them as friends. Amyas rolled another fragrant tobacco leaf, appreciating its earthy tang, and said, “Then let her give us some cassava.”

The hut opened, and the Indians prostrated themselves as a fair apparition stepped forth, adorned in feather-robes of every imaginable hue. Slowly and stately, she walked to Amyas. With expressive looks that required no dominant lexicon to be understood, she pointed to the gardens and huts, signaling that the village’s bounty was at his service, and gently lifted his hand to her forehead.

A shout of rapture rose. As the maiden retired, the tribe pressed around the English, offering the profound hospitality of their kitchens. The feast that followed was a triumph of indigenous culinary technique, deeply appreciated by the ravenous Englishmen. Women brought out calabashes of *chicha*, the wild yeast imparting a complex, sour-sweet effervescence that danced on the tongue. There were thick discs of cassava bread, the poisonous cyanogenic glycosides meticulously leached away by generations of inherited agrarian wisdom, toasted to a golden, snapping crispness on clay griddles. They were served rich, fatty cuts of game, slow-roasted over aromatic woods that infused the meat with a profound, smoky depth, alongside brightly acidic fruits that cleansed the palate. Amyas and his men did not merely eat to fuel their bodies; they savored the deep, generational care embedded in every bite. Only when the drums and trumpets resumed their hideous, unsettling music, threatening the acoustic peace once more, did they beat an early retreat to their island-fortress.

That night, after their evening service, twenty mellow English voices rang out above the roar of the cataract in polyphonic catches and psalms. The pure acoustic environment of the river carried the sound into the dark trees. Cary returned from the water's edge and whispered, “The sweet Iniquity is mimicking us, lad.”

They stood listening. Out of the black wall of the forest, weaving seamlessly into their domestic routine, came that same sweet treble voice, echoing their exact melodies. The two young men stood a long while, listening to the untranslatable communion of native and foreign song across the golden, moonlit eddies. 

Over the next week, Amyas sought to learn more about the maiden, but she kept her distance. He turned to the old cacique. The agrarian elder made an owl's face, shaking his head. She was a “child of the Sun,” and the less said, the better.

“Tell him, boy,” Cary threatened, “that we are the children of the Sun by his first wife, and if he doesn't tell us, all the cassava plants shall be blighted.”

Though Amyas scolded Cary for lying, the threat to the crops deeply shook the cacique. Taking them in a canoe a mile downriver to escape the sonic surveillance of the village, the elder began a rhythmic, sorrowful chant. He spoke in an ancient, dying dialect—a vessel of eroding cultural memory. He sang of how, generations ago, his tribe was a mighty nation in Papamene, before the Spaniards drove them forth. He charted their multi-generational saga northward, beneath the flaming cone of Cotopaxi, where they found this fair creature wandering the forest, as small as a seven-year-old child. 

The tribe, marveling at her delicate beauty, worshipped her as a god. To the cacique, she was a living artifact of the mighty Inca race, whose tragic fall had echoed even into these lonely wildernesses. The agrarian elder believed the Incas would one day rise again, returning to restore the poor Omaguas. Raised in royal state by the conjuror’s command, she became a prophetess, possessing mysterious wisdom about the flora, fauna, and the subtle shifts in the river’s currents. She lived unmarried, her sweet songs guiding the tribe’s planting and hunting, seamlessly integrating the mythic power of the Sun into their daily survival.

Amyas listened, his mind swirling with interwoven timelines of lost Inca kingdoms and his own quests for Manoa. If he could learn from her, perhaps she held the key to the golden city. When they paddled back, the cacique begged them not to take her away, lest the Sun forget the Omaguas and ripen their manioc and fruit no more.

Amyas needed to send a party down the Orinoco to rescue his sick men. Cary volunteered, provided they could find Indian youths to work the canoe. But the Omaguas feared the brutal Guahibas, natural enemies whose differing language marked them, in the tragic tribal worldview, only for destruction. 

It was the maiden, Ayacanora, who solved the impasse. Driven by curiosity and a longing to escape the stifling routines of the village, she began hunting with Amyas. They bonded not through shared language, but through the shared sensory appreciation of the forest—tracking the musky scent of peccary, harvesting wild, resinous honey, and navigating the rustling acoustic canopy. Soon, hearing of Amyas's need, she consulted the Piache, retreated to her hut, and unleashed a song that commanded the young men to aid Cary’s expedition. She manipulated the local lexicon perfectly, threatening the tribe with the wrath of an evil spirit with an unpronounceable name. The men departed at once.

Jack Brimblecombe, however, disliked this reliance on fearful myths. He preached a gospel of a loving Good Spirit. 

“Ah!” Ayacanora told Amyas, waving her hands. “They know better than that. The Good Spirit is big and lazy, and takes no trouble: but the little bad spirit, he is so busy. He is the useful one to have for a friend!” 

The Piache fiercely approved of this dynamic. He chided Jack for preaching of a Good Spirit who required no payment. “If they begin to ask the Good Spirit for what they want,” the Piache argued, revealing his crude, transactional worldview, “who will bring me cassava and coca for keeping the bad spirit quiet?”

The Piache’s power relied on extorting the tribe's culinary treasures through fear. But Jack’s preaching, with its resonant, comforting baritone, offered a psychological peace that the Piache’s jarring noise could never provide. The Indians flocked to Jack’s morning and evening services. Finding his occupation gone and his stores of fermented drink and roasted meats dwindling, the Piache vowed to silence Jack with a poisoned arrow. 

He imparted this plot to Ayacanora, expecting her support. Instead, she boxed his ears, subjected him to a storm of abuse, and exposed the plot to Amyas, declaring that the Piache should be thrown to the alligators. Jack magnanimously forgave his foe and preached on.

But the Piache possessed one final weapon of acoustic dominance: a sacred trumpet hidden in a cave. For generations, the tribe believed that unless this trumpet was blown by night, its specific vibrations fertilizing the woods, the palm-trees would bear no fruit. It was the absolute intersection of myth and their daily sustenance. 

The Piache announced he would take the precious instrument and leave the village to a neighboring, more religious tribe, leaving the Omaguas' palms to blight. 

Dire was the wailing. Jack's words were good, but what was a foreign Gospel against the loss of their food? The Piache launched into a fierce harangue against the heretic strangers. As his violent lexicon maddened the crowd, their savage nature flashed into suspicion. Women yelled; men scowled and ran for their bows and blow-guns. The acoustic ecology of the village devolved into pure chaos. With only a dozen men against nearly a hundred poisoned arrows, Amyas forbade his men to draw, though their cheeks grew pale.

“It is God's quarrel, sirs all,” said Jack Brimblecombe, his voice steady in the tumult; “let Him defend the right.”

As he spoke, piercing through the violent clamor, Ayacanora's magic song rose from her hut, pure and perfectly pitched, quivering aloft among the green heights of the forest.
