Major Pendennis came down into the country as conquerors come down into provinces—reluctantly, with the air of a man making history, and with a private conviction that history was making a very poor return.

For consider what he surrendered. London in May: that perfumed season when the town is at its most insolent, when a man’s name may blossom in the Morning Post like a rare hot-house fruit, and his hat be recognised—nay, saluted—in drawing-rooms where the very footmen look as if they have pedigrees. He gave up his newspapers and his mornings; his afternoons, which were a regular campaign from club to club; his confidential visits to certain Great Ladies (confidential, select, and of course strictly whispered); his rides in Rotten Row where a bow from a Duke is as nourishing as a luncheon; his dinners; his stall at the Opera; his brisk raids to Fulham or Richmond on Saturdays and Sundays; his chance of being observed by my Lord Marquis at a great entertainment, and written down afterwards as if it were a public service.

All these delights—these well-seasoned, well-served courses of metropolitan existence—he pushed away from him, with a sigh, and shut himself into a lone little country-house, where the society consisted of a simple widow who did not know how to prize a Court Guide, a greenhorn of a son who did; a mawkish curate; and a little girl of ten years old, who could not be expected to have an opinion about the Peerage at all.

He made the sacrifice; and it was the greater, as nobody, properly speaking, applauded it. His letters still came down franked from town; he exhibited the invitations to Helen with the air of a martyr displaying his wounds. It was beautiful—if you had the taste for such beauty—and tragical to see him refuse one party after another; tragical, at least, to those who could understand the melancholy grandeur of self-denial. Helen did not. Helen smiled at the Major’s awful pathos over the Court Guide, as one smiles at a man who mourns because his soufflé has failed; but young Pen looked with very honest reverence at the great names on the superscriptions, and listened as a hungry boy listens when somebody else’s dinner is described in detail.

For the Major’s memory was a larder of anecdotes, hung round with titles, alliances, scandals, reconciliations, and family feuds, all preserved with the nicest care. He poured them into Pen’s willing ear as they paced the terrace for the Major’s two hours’ constitutional walk, or as they sat together after dinner, the decanter between them, like a third party to the conversation who always agreed.

“My dear boy,” the Major would say, with a mournful earnestness, as if he were recommending prayer, “you cannot begin your genealogical studies too early. I wish to Heaven you would read Debrett every day.”

“Debrett, sir?” Pen asked, delighted to have so manly a lesson.

“Not so much the historical part,” the Major continued—lowering his voice at the word historical, as though it were slightly vulgar—“for between ourselves, the pedigrees are many of them very fabulous; and there are few families that can show such a clear descent as our own. No, no: attend to the alliances. Who is related to whom. I have known a man’s career blasted—blasted, Arthur—by ignorance on this all-important subject.”

And then out came one of those neat little stories of the great world which, like certain sauces, seem harmless until you discover how much fire they contain.

“Why, only last month, at dinner at my Lord Hobanob’s, a young man—young Mr. Suckling—lately received among us—author of a work, I believe—began speaking lightly, in the most audacious manner, of Admiral Bowser’s conduct for ratting to Ministers. Cutting his jokes as if all the world were laughing with him. And who do you suppose sate next and opposite to this young gentleman? Why—why—next to him was Lady Grampound Bowser’s daughter; opposite to him was Lord Grampound Bowser’s son-in-law. Conceive Lady Hobanob’s feelings—Hobanob’s!—and those of every well-bred man as the wretched intru exposed himself. He will never dine again in South Street. I promise you that.”

Pen thrilled. South Street sounded like an enchanted land from which fools are banished.

The Major mourned too, in his way, over the absence of great people from the neighbourhood. Sir Francis Clavering did not come down to live in the Park since his marriage, and so there was no society worth the name—no proper table to be admitted to, no proper table to refuse. Lord Eyrie was not in the country. If Lord Eyrie had been there, the Major would have taken Pen, presented him, and planted him—so to speak—among the Trehawks like a sapling among old trees.

“He has daughters,” the Major said, with that candour by which men sometimes commit the greatest insolences. “Who knows? you might have married Lady Emily or Lady Barbara Trehawk. But all those dreams are over. My poor fellow, you must lie on the bed you have made for yourself.”

Young Pendennis, who was at an age when any bed seems magnificent if it is spoken of as a bed of destiny, listened with serious inclination. In print, these discourses may look like dry bones; but in the Major’s mouth they were living creatures, with eyebrows and waistcoats and coronets. His anecdotes of the great George, the Royal Dukes, statesmen, beauties, fashionable ladies, and the small jests that ruin men for ever, filled young Pen’s soul with longing and wonder. Mrs. Pendennis was sadly bored and perplexed by all this talk of great folks—she, who thought a good roast fowl and quiet gratitude a better thing than the whole Almanack de Gotha; but Pen never found it tedious. It was a new music in the house, a patter of names like a drum, and he marched to it.

We must not pretend that the Major discoursed upon the most elevated subjects, or treated the subjects he chose in the most elevated manner. His morality, such as it was, had the merit of being consistent. It was not, perhaps, a system intended to advance a man’s interest in another world; but it was admirably calculated to forward him in this one. And the Major never doubted for an instant that his views were the only views practicable, and that his conduct was perfectly virtuous and respectable. He was a man of honour—he said so himself—and had his eyes, as he called it, open. He took pity on his young greenhorn of a nephew, and intended to open the boy’s eyes too, without in the least considering whether the operation might be painful.

No man, for example, went more regularly to church, when in the country, than this old bachelor.

“It don’t matter so much in town, Pen,” he said; “for there the women go, and the men are not missed. But when a gentleman is sur ses terres, he must give an example to the country-people. And if I could turn a tune, I even think I should sing. The Duke of Saint David’s, whom I have the honour of knowing, always sings in the country; and, let me tell you, it has a doosed fine effect from the family pew. And you are somebody down here. As long as the Claverings are away you are the first man in the parish: and as good as any. You might represent the town if you played your cards well. Your poor dear father would have done so had he lived; so might you.—Not if you marry a lady, however amiable, whom the country-people won’t meet.—Well, well: it’s a painful subject. Let us change it, my boy.”

But if the Major changed the subject once, he returned to it a score of times a day; and the moral of every discourse was, that Pen was throwing himself away.

Now it does not require much coaxing to make a simple boy believe he is a very fine fellow. Pen drank his uncle’s counsel as eagerly as he drank his uncle’s wine. It began to work in him like strong liquor: he felt warmer, bolder, more certain that he had a destiny which ought not to be marinated in small beer.

And the consequence was, that Captain Costigan’s conversation became by no means pleasant to him; and the idea of that tipsy old father-in-law haunted him like a nightmare that smelt of punch. Pen could not bring himself to associate that man—unshaven, shabby, reeking—with his mother’s clear parlour and gentle hands. Even about Emily—when the pitiless guardian began to question him—Pen faltered in his answers.

“Was she accomplished?” the Major asked.

Pen was obliged to own, no.

“Was she clever?”

Well—she had a very good average intellect; but he could not absolutely say she was clever.

“Come,” said the Major, “let us see some of her letters.”

Pen confessed that he had but those three we have mentioned—trivial invitations or answers; nothing like the eager pages a woman in earnest will send, when her heart boils over into her inkstand.

“She is cautious enough,” the Major said drily. “She is older than you, my poor boy.”

And then he apologised with the utmost frankness and humility; flung himself upon Pen’s good feelings; begged the lad to excuse a fond old uncle who had only his family’s honour in view. For Arthur would flame up whenever Miss Costigan’s honesty was even hinted at, and swore he would never have her name mentioned lightly, and never—never—would part from her.

He repeated these vows to his uncle and friends at home; and, it must be confessed, repeated them with great fervour too to Miss Fotheringay and the amiable family at Chatteris, with whom he still continued to spend some portion of his time.

Miss Emily was alarmed when she heard of the guardian’s arrival, and rightly conceived that the Major came down with hostile intentions towards herself.

“I suppose ye intend to leave me, now your grand relation has come down from town,” she said. “He’ll carry ye off, and you’ll forget your poor Emily, Mr. Arthur!”

Forget her? In her presence; in that of Miss Rouncy the Columbine (Milly’s confidential friend of the company); in the presence of the Captain himself; Pen swore he never could think of any other woman than his beloved Miss Fotheringay. And the Captain, looking up at his foils—hung as a trophy on the wall where Pen and he used to fence—said grimly that he would not advoise any man to meddle rashly with the affections of his darling child; and that he would never believe his gallant young Arthur—whom he treated as his son, whom he called his son—would ever be guilty of conduct so revolting to every idaya of honour and humanity.

He went up and embraced Pen after speaking. He cried, and wiped his eye with one large dirty hand as he clasped Arthur with the other.

Arthur shuddered in that grasp, and thought of his uncle at home.

His father-in-law looked unusually dirty and shabby; the odour of whisky-and-water was more decided even than common. How was he to bring that man and his mother together? He trembled when he remembered he had actually written to Costigan (enclosing a sovereign, the loan of which the worthy gentleman had need), and saying that one day he hoped to sign himself, “your affectionate son, Arthur Pendennis.”

He was glad to get away from Chatteris that day: from Miss Rouncy the confidante; from the old toping father-in-law; from the divine Emily herself. “O, Emily, Emily,” he cried inwardly, as he rattled homewards on Rebecca, “you little know what sacrifices I am making for you!—for you who are always so cold, so cautious, so mistrustful;” and he thought of a character in Pope to whom he had often involuntarily compared her.

Pen never rode over to Chatteris upon that certain errand, but the Major found out on what errand the boy had been. Faithful to his plan, Major Pendennis gave his nephew no let or hindrance; but the constant feeling that the elder’s eye was upon him, the uneasy shame attendant upon that inevitable confession which the evening’s conversation would be sure to elicit in the most natural manner, made Pen go less frequently to sigh away his soul at the feet of his charmer than he had been wont to do before his uncle’s arrival.

There was no use trying to deceive him. No pretext of dining with Smirke, or reading Greek plays with Foker. When Pen returned from his flying visits, he felt everybody knew whence he came; and he sat, looking absurdly guilty, before his mother and guardian—over their books or their game at picquet—like a boy who has been stealing jam and still smells of it.

Once, having walked half a mile to the Fairoaks Inn beyond the Lodge gates, to be in readiness for the Competitor coach (which changed horses there), Pen was saluted by a man on the roof who touched his hat: it was his uncle’s man, Mr. Morgan, going on a message for his master, and “took up at the Lodge,” as he said. Mr. Morgan came back by the Rival too; so Pen had the pleasure of that domestic’s company both ways—an accompaniment, as one may say, to his guilty melody.

Nothing was said at home. The lad seemed to have every decent liberty; and yet he felt himself watched and guarded, and that eyes were upon him even in the presence of his Dulcinea.

In fact, Pen’s suspicions were not unfounded. His guardian had sent forth to gather all possible information regarding the lad and his interesting young friend; and the discreet, ingenious Mr. Morgan—a London confidential valet, whose fidelity could be trusted because it was part of his profession—had been to Chatteris more than once and made inquiry into the past history and present habits of Captain Costigan and his daughter.

He cross-examined the waiters, the ostlers, and all the bar-inmates at the George, with that delicacy which a man uses when he intends to repeat everything afterwards. He got what little they knew of the Captain. The worthy gentleman, it appeared, was not held in very great regard there. The waiters never saw the colour of his money, and were warned not to furnish him with liquor for which some other party was not responsible. He swaggered sadly in the coffee-room; consumed a toothpick with an air as if it were a cigar; looked over the paper; and if any friend asked him to dinner, he stayed.

Morgan heard at the George of Pen’s acquaintance with Mr. Foker, and went over to Baymouth to enter into relations with that gentleman’s man; but the young student was gone to a Coast Regatta, and his servant travelled, of course, in charge of the dressing-case—so that source ran dry, like a tapped bottle.

From the servants of the officers at the barracks, Mr. Morgan learned that the Captain had so frequently and outrageously inebriated himself there that Colonel Swallowtail had forbidden him the mess-room. The indefatigable Morgan then put himself into communication with some of the inferior actors at the theatre and pumped them over cigars and punch; and all agreed that Costigan was poor, shabby, and given to debt and drink.

But—here lay the mischief for the Major’s purposes—not a breath touched Miss Fotheringay’s reputation. Her father’s courage had more than once displayed itself towards persons disposed to treat his daughter with freedom. She never came to the theatre but with her father; and in his most inebriated moments the Captain still kept watch over her. Finally Mr. Morgan, from his own experience, added that he had been to see her act and was uncommon delighted with the performance, besides thinking her a most splendid woman.

Mrs. Creed, the pew-opener, confirmed these statements to Doctor Portman, who examined her personally and threatened her with the terrors of the Church one day after afternoon service. Mrs. Creed had nothing unfavourable to her lodger to divulge.

“She saw nobody; only one or two ladies of the theayter. The Captain did intoxicate himself sometimes, and did not always pay his rent regular; but he did when he had money—or rather Miss Fotheringay did. Since the young gentleman from Clavering had been and took lessons in fencing, one or two more had come from the barracks; Sir Derby Oaks, and his young friend Mr. Foker, which was often together; and which was always driving over from Baymouth in the tandem. But on the occasions of the lessons Miss F. was very seldom present, and generally came downstairs to Mrs. Creed’s own room. And whenever he come, she always have me or one of the children with her. And, Mrs. Creed, marm, says she, if you please, marm, you’ll on no account leave the room when that young gentleman’s here. And many’s the time I’ve seen him a-looking as if he wished I was away, poor young man; and he took to coming in service-time, when I wasn’t at home, of course: but she always had one of the boys up if her Pa wasn’t at home, or old Mr. Bowser with her a teaching of her her lesson, or one of the young ladies of the theayter.”

It was all true. Whatever encouragements might have been given Pen before he avowed his passion, Miss Emily’s prudence was prodigious afterwards; and the poor fellow chafed against her reserve. It maintained his ardour as it excited his anger; he was like a man kept perpetually hungry by the promise of a dinner that is always being covered up and taken away again.

The Major surveyed the state of things with a sigh.

“If it were but a temporary liaison,” the excellent man said, “one could bear it. A young fellow must sow his wild oats, and that sort of thing. But a virtuous attachment is the deuce. It comes of the d——d romantic notions boys get from being brought up by women.”

“Allow me to say, Major,” replied Doctor Portman, with that pious sharpness which is as much a habit as a collar, “that you speak a little too like a man of the world. Nothing can be more desirable for Pen than a virtuous attachment—for a young lady of his own rank and with a corresponding fortune. This present infatuation, of course, I must deplore as sincerely as you do. If I were his guardian, I should command him to give it up.”

“The very means,” cried the Major, “to make him marry to-morrow. We have got time from him—that is all—and we must do our best with that.”

And then, at the end of one of these consultations, the Doctor—who was not, of course, a play-going man, but who could be curious under his conscience—said with hesitation, “I say, Major—suppose—we go and see her?”

The Major laughed. He had been a fortnight at Fairoaks and, strange to say, had not thought of that.

“Well,” he said, “why not? After all, it is not my niece, but Miss Fotheringay the actress; and we have as good a right as any other of the public to see her if we pay our money.”

So upon a day when it was arranged that Pen was to dine at home and pass the evening with his mother, the two elderly gentlemen drove over to Chatteris in the Doctor’s chaise; and there—like a couple of jolly bachelors on an escapade carefully disguised as duty—they dined at the George Inn before proceeding to the play.

Only two other guests were in the room: an officer of the regiment quartered at Chatteris, and a young gentleman whom the Doctor thought he had somewhere seen. They left these two to their meal, however, and hastened to the theatre.

It was Hamlet over again. Shakespeare was Article XL. of stout old Doctor Portman’s creed, to which he made a point of testifying publicly at least once a year. The play was as it had been before; and those who saw Miss Fotheringay perform Ophelia saw precisely the same thing on one night as on another—each gesture in its place like a well-learnt recipe, each sigh measured, each tear ready at the proper moment, as if kept in a little bottle behind the scenes.

Both the elderly gentlemen looked at her with extraordinary interest, thinking how very much young Pen was charmed with her.

“Gad,” said the Major between his teeth, surveying her as she was called forward and swept her curtsies to the scanty audience, “the young rascal has not made a bad choice.”

The Doctor applauded her loudly and loyally. “Upon my word,” he said, “she is a very clever actress; and I must say, Major, she is endowed with very considerable personal attractions.”

“So that young officer thinks in the stage-box,” Major Pendennis answered; and he pointed out to the Doctor’s attention the young dragoon from the George coffee-room, who sat there applauding with immense enthusiasm. She looked extremely sweet upon him too, thought the Major; but that was their way; and he snapped up his natty opera-glass and pocketed it, as if he wished to see no more that night.

Nor did Doctor Portman propose to stay for the after-piece. So they rose and left the theatre: the Doctor returning to Mrs. Portman, who was on a visit at the Deanery; and the Major walking home, full of thought, towards the George, where he had bespoken a bed.
