The next morning, Shirley shared with Caroline her delight at how smoothly the evening’s gathering had gone.

“I rather enjoy hosting a group of gentlemen,” she said. “It’s amusing to watch how they light up over a well-prepared meal. For us, these fine wines and elaborate dishes mean little—yet gentlemen seem to carry a childlike wonder about food. It’s pleasant to please them—especially when they show the kind of decorum our admirable rectors do. I sometimes study Moore, trying to figure out what might charm him—but he lacks that simple openness. Have you ever discovered his soft spot, Caroline? You’ve spent more time with him than I have.”

“It’s not like my uncle or Dr. Boultby’s,” Caroline replied, smiling. She always felt a quiet pleasure in following Miss Keeldar’s lead when discussing Robert’s character. Left alone, she would never have brought it up herself. But when prompted, the chance to talk about the man who occupied her thoughts was too tempting to resist. “Still,” she added, “I honestly don’t know. Every time I try to observe Robert, I end up realizing he’s already watching me.”

“There it is!” Shirley exclaimed. “You can’t fix your gaze on him without his eyes flashing back. He’s never off guard. He won’t let you gain an advantage. Even when he’s not looking, it feels as if his thoughts are tracing yours—reading your motives with calm precision. Oh! I know that sort of nature—or something very like it. It intrigues me deeply. How does it affect you?”

This question was typical of Shirley’s sharp, sudden shifts. At first, Caroline had been flustered by them, but now she’d grown accustomed to deflecting them with the quiet grace of a little Quakeress.

“Intrigues me? In what way?” she asked.

“Here comes someone!” Shirley suddenly announced, rising and rushing to the window. “A distraction! I never told you about a splendid conquest I recently made—achieved at those parties I can never persuade you to attend. And it happened without effort or intention on my part—I swear. Listen—the bell! And two of them! Do they only hunt in pairs? You may have one, Lina, and take your pick. I hope I’m generous enough. Listen to Tartar!”

The black-muzzled, tawny dog—first glimpsed when the reader met his mistress—now barked fiercely in the hall, his deep voice echoing through the hollow space. Then came a growl louder than the bark—menacing as thunder muttered beneath storm clouds.

“Listen!” Shirley laughed again. “You’d think it was the beginning of a bloody attack. They’ll be terrified. They don’t know old Tartar like I do. They don’t realize his noise is all sound and fury—meaning nothing!”

Soon, there was commotion. “Down, sir, down!” rang out a sharp, commanding voice, followed by the crack of a cane or whip. Then—a yell, a scurry, a run, a full-blown uproar.

“Oh, Malone, Malone!”

“Down! Down! Down!” the voice insisted.

“He’s really bothering them!” Shirley called out. “They’ve struck him. A blow is something he’s unaccustomed to—and he won’t accept it.”

She dashed outside. One gentleman was fleeing up the oak staircase, racing for safety in the gallery or chambers; another was backing away from the foot of the stairs, wildly waving a knotted stick while shouting, “Down! down! down!” The tawny dog bayed, roared, howled at him, while servants burst from the kitchen. The dog lunged—then the second man turned tail and bolted after his companion. The first had already locked himself in a bedroom, holding the door against his friend—nothing so cruel as fear. But the other struggled hard; the door trembled under his strength.

“Gentlemen,” Miss Keeldar called in her silvery yet firm voice, “spare my locks, please. Calm yourselves! Come down! Look at Tartar—he won’t harm a cat.”

She was stroking the dog. He lay crouched at her feet, front paws stretched out, tail still twitching with warning, nostrils flaring, bulldog eyes glowing with dull fire. He was honest, slow, stubborn—though mostly indifferent to the world beyond his mistress and John, the man who fed him. Peaceful enough, unless threatened or struck, which instantly stirred the devil within him.

“Mr. Malone, how are you?” Shirley continued, lifting her bright face toward the gallery. “That’s not the way to the oak parlour—that’s Mrs. Pryor’s room. Ask your friend Mr. Donne to leave. I’d be delighted to receive him in a lower room.”

“Ha! ha!” Malone laughed hollowly, stepping away from the door and leaning over the heavy balustrade. “That animal truly frightened Donne. He’s a bit timid,” he added stiffly, walking neatly to the stairhead. “I thought it better to follow, to reassure him.”

“Well, you succeeded. Now come down, please.” She turned to her manservant. “John, go upstairs and release Mr. Donne. Be careful, Mr. Malone—the stairs are slippery.”

Indeed, they were—polished oak. The warning came too late. Malone had already slipped during his dignified descent, saved only by grabbing the banister, which made the entire structure groan.

Tartar seemed to believe the visitor’s exit had been overly dramatic, and growled once more. But Malone wasn’t afraid. The dog’s spring had startled him, but now he passed by in suppressed rage rather than fear. If looks could kill, Tartar wouldn’t have lived. Overcome by sullen anger, Malone pushed past Miss Keeldar into the parlour before her. He glanced at Miss Helstone—could barely bring himself to bow. His glare swept both ladies. He looked as though, had either been his wife, he’d have made a fearsome husband. In each hand, he seemed eager to grab one and squeeze her to death.

But Shirley softened. Her laughter ceased. Caroline, true to her nature, didn’t smile even at someone clearly embarrassed. Tartar was dismissed. Peter Augustus calmed—Shirley had a way with animals, even bulls. He sensed that since he couldn’t challenge the dog’s owner, he’d better behave. And behave he did. His efforts were welcomed, and soon he became quite civil again. He’d come, after all, determined to charm and captivate. Rough beginnings greeted him at Fieldhead, but having survived them, he resolved to win favor. Like March, he entered like a lion—but intended to leave like a lamb.

For air, or perhaps to escape any new crisis, he chose a seat—not on the sofa where Miss Keeldar offered him a throne, nor near the fireplace where Caroline gently invited him, but on a chair close to the door. No longer angry, he grew awkward, constrained. He spoke in bursts, choosing only the most mundane topics. He sighed deeply at the end of every sentence, paused, sighed again, then sighed before speaking. Finally, seeking ease among his other charms, he pulled out a large silk handkerchief. This was to be his graceful prop. He folded the red-and-yellow square diagonally, whipped it open with a flourish, refolded it tightly, shaped it into a neat band. What would he do with it? Wrap it around his neck? Head? Comforter? Turban? Neither. Peter Augustus had an inventive mind. He was about to demonstrate something novel. Sitting cross-legged, he wrapped the bandana around his ankles and tied them together firmly. Clearly proud of this feat, he repeated it several times. The second time sent Shirley to the window, laughing silently and uncontrollably. It made Caroline turn her head, her curls shielding the smile spreading across her face. Miss Helstone found more than just amusement in Peter’s behavior. She was impressed by how swiftly his affections shifted—from her to the heiress. The £5,000 she might one day inherit meant nothing compared to Miss Keeldar’s estate and grand house. He made no secret of his calculations. He didn’t pretend a gradual change—he turned sharply, openly abandoning pursuit of the smaller fortune for the greater. On what grounds he expected success, only he knew—certainly not through skill.

From the long silence, it seemed John had trouble convincing Mr. Donne to descend. Eventually, however, the gentleman appeared. And as he stepped into the oak parlour, he showed no shame, no confusion—not a trace. Donne was coldly phlegmatic, immovably self-satisfied, utterly blind to humiliation. He had never blushed in his life. No disgrace could shake him. His nerves lacked sensitivity, so no color rose to his cheeks. He had no fire in his blood, no modesty in his soul—just arrogance and emptiness. And this man fancied courting Miss Keeldar! Yet he had no idea how to begin. He had no concept of pleasing a taste, reaching a heart. His plan: visit her formally a few times, then write a marriage proposal. He assumed she’d accept for love of his position. Then they’d marry, and he’d rule Fieldhead—live comfortably, command servants, eat the best food, become a great man. You’d never guess his intentions when he addressed her in an impertinent tone: “A very dangerous dog, Miss Keeldar. I wonder you keep such an animal.”

“Do I, Mr. Donne? Perhaps you’ll be more surprised when I tell you I adore him.”

“You must be joking. Can’t imagine a lady fond of that brute—it’s so ugly—just a cart horse’s dog. Please hang it.”

“Hang what I love?”

“And replace it with a sweet little pug or poodle—something suitable for ladies. Women usually prefer lapdogs.”

“Perhaps I’m an exception.”

“Oh, you can’t be. All women are alike in such matters. That’s universally accepted.”

“Tartar frightened you terribly, Mr. Donne. I hope you’re not hurt.”

“I shall be, undoubtedly. He gave me a shock I won of forget. When I _sor_ him” (such was Mr. Donne’s pronunciation) “about to leap, I thought I’d faint.”

“Perhaps you did faint in the bedroom—you were gone a long time.”

“No. I held the door fast. I refused to let anyone in. I kept a barrier between myself and the enemy.”

“But what if Mr. Malone had been attacked?”

“Malone must look after himself. Your servant finally convinced me to come out by saying the dog was chained in his kennel. Had I not been assured of that, I’d have stayed all day. But what’s that? I declare the man lied! The dog is here!”

And indeed, Tartar walked past the glass door leading to the garden—stiff, tawny, black-muzzled as ever. Still grumpy, he growled again and whistled half-strangled, a trait inherited from his bulldog ancestors.

“There are more visitors coming,” Shirley remarked, with the cool confidence of someone who owns a fierce-looking dog. Tartar bounded down the path toward the gate, bellowing with explosive force. His mistress calmly opened the glass door and stepped out, calling to him softly. His roar faded, and he lifted his massive, blunt, stupid head to greet the newcomers, ready to be petted.

“What! Tartar, Tartar!” a cheerful, slightly boyish voice called. “Don’t you recognize us? Good morning, old boy!”

And little Mr. Sweeting—whose natural kindness made him fearless of anyone or anything—came through the gate, petting the guardian. His vicar, Mr. Hall, followed. He had no fear of Tartar either, and Tartar had none for him. He sniffed both men thoroughly, then, satisfied they were harmless, withdrew to the sunlit front of the hall, leaving the archway clear. Mr. Sweeting tried to play with him, but Tartar ignored the attention. Only his mistress’s touch brought him joy. To everyone else, he remained stubbornly indifferent.

Shirley greeted Messrs. Hall and Sweeting warmly, shaking their hands. They’d come to share news of their morning’s success in collecting subscriptions for the fund. Mr. Hall’s eyes shone kindly behind his spectacles, his plain face glowing with goodness—so much so that when Caroline saw him, she ran out to meet him, placing both hands in his. He looked down at her with gentle, serene affection, resembling a smiling Melanchthon.

Instead of returning inside, they wandered through the garden, the ladies walking beside Mr. Hall. It was a breezy, sunny day—the air freshened their cheeks and tousled their curls. Both looked lovely—one radiant, the other serene. Mr. Hall spoke most often to the lively one, gazed most often at the quiet one. Miss Keeldar gathered handfuls of the blooming flowers whose scent filled the air. She handed some to Caroline, asking her to choose a bouquet for Mr. Hall. With her lap full of delicate, vibrant blossoms, Caroline sat on the steps of a summerhouse. The vicar stood nearby, leaning on his cane.

Shirley, unwilling to neglect the others, now called out to the pair left in the oak parlour. She escorted Donne past his dreaded foe—Tartar, now snoring peacefully under the midday sun. Donne wasn’t grateful—he never was—but he was relieved. Miss Keeldar, wanting fairness, offered flowers to the curates. They accepted with awkwardness. Malone struggled especially, holding a bouquet in one hand and his shillelah in the other. Donne’s “Thank you!” was rich with pride—implying the gift was a tribute to his worth, a bid for the heiress’s favor. Only Sweeting received the posy like a smart, sensible young man, placing it gallantly in his buttonhole.

As a reward, Miss Keeldar beckoned him aside and gave him a task. His eyes sparkled with delight. Off he went, circling the courtyard to the kitchen. No need for directions—he was at home everywhere. Soon he returned, carrying a round table, which he placed under the cedar tree. Then he collected six garden chairs from various corners of the grounds and arranged them in a circle. The parlour maid—Miss Keeldar kept no footman—brought a tray covered with a napkin. Sweeting’s quick hands helped set out glasses, plates, knives, and forks. He also assisted her in arranging a neat luncheon: cold chicken, ham, and tarts.

Such spontaneous feasts delighted Shirley. Nothing pleased her more than having a nimble, helpful friend like Sweeting to run errands, cheerfully receive and execute her hospitable ideas. David and she were on excellent terms. His devotion to the heiress was entirely disinterested—since it didn’t interfere with his loyal service to the magnificent Dora Sykes.

The meal proved joyful. Donne and Malone contributed little to the merriment—mostly managing cutlery and wineglasses. But when four spirits like Mr. Hall, David Sweeting, Shirley, and Caroline gathered in good health and harmony, under a sunny sky, amid a sea of flowers, dullness was impossible.

During conversation, Mr. Hall reminded the ladies that Whitsuntide was near—the grand Sunday-school tea and procession of Briarfield, Whinbury, and Nunnely parishes. Caroline, he knew, would be teaching, and he hoped Miss Keeldar would join. He hoped she’d make her first public appearance then. Shirley was not one to miss such an event. She loved festive excitement, gatherings of joy, combinations of happy details, crowds of smiling faces, hearts uplifted. She assured Mr. Hall she’d be there—no matter what role she played.

“And,” said Caroline, “you’ll promise to sit at my table, near me, Mr. Hall?”

“I shall not fail, _Deo volente_,” he replied. “I’ve occupied the seat beside her for six years now,” he added, turning to Miss Keeldar. “They made her a teacher when she was twelve. She’s not naturally confident—as you’ve seen. The first time she had to ‘take a tray’ and serve tea publicly, she trembled, flushed, and spilled the pot. I stepped in, took a seat near her, handled the urn and slop-basin, and made the tea for her—like any seasoned old woman.”

“I was very thankful,” Caroline interjected.

“You were. You told me so with such sincerity it repaid me well—because most twelve-year-old girls wouldn’t notice kindness, even if given it constantly. She stayed close to me the rest of the evening, walking with me among the children, following me into the vestry when summoned to church. I believe she’d have climbed the pulpit with me, had I not guided her to the rectory pew.”

“And he’s been my friend ever since,” Caroline said.

“And always sat beside her, near her tray, handing cups—that’s all I’ve done. Next time I’ll help her marry a curate or mill-owner. But mind, Caroline, I’ll inquire about the bridegroom’s character. If he’s not a gentleman worthy of making the little girl who walked with me hand-in-hand over Nunnely Common happy, I won’t perform the ceremony. So be warned.”

“The warning’s unnecessary. I’m not marrying. I’ll live single, like your sister Margaret, Mr. Hall.”

“Very well. You might do worse. Margaret isn’t unhappy. She has books for joy, her brother for care, and is content. If ever you need a home—if Briarfield rectory ceases to be yours—come to Nunnely vicarage. If the old maid and bachelor are still alive, they’ll welcome you warmly.”

“There are your flowers. Now,” said Caroline, who had held onto the bouquet she’d chosen until now, “you don’t care for a bouquet, but give this to Margaret. Only—let me be sentimental once—keep that little forget-me-not, a wild flower I picked from the grass. And—more sentimental still—let me take a few blue blossoms and place them in my souvenir.”

She drew out a small book with an enamelled cover and silver clasp. Opening it, she inserted the flowers and wrote in pencil: “To be kept for the sake of the Rev. Cyril Hall, my friend. May --, 18--.”

The Rev. Cyril Hall, in turn, tucked a sprig between the pages of his pocket Testament. He simply wrote in the margin: “Caroline.”

“Now,” he smiled, “I trust we’re romantic enough. Miss Keeldar,” he continued—while the curates, by the way, were too busy with their own jokes to notice the exchange at the far end of the table—“I hope you’re laughing at this moment of ‘exaltation’ in the old gray-headed vicar. But the truth is, I’ve grown so used to obeying this young friend of yours, I don’t know how to refuse her when she asks me to do something. You’d say it’s not much trouble to trade flowers and forget-me-nots—but when asked to be sentimental, I comply.”

“He’s naturally sentimental,” Caroline remarked. “Margaret told me so, and I know what pleases him.”

“That you should be good and happy? Yes—that’s one of my greatest joys. May God preserve you long in peace and innocence! By which I mean _comparative_ innocence—for in His sight, I know, _none_ are pure. What seems flawless to us, like angels, is to Him frailty, needing the blood of His Son to cleanse and the strength of His Spirit to sustain. Let us all cherish humility—I, as you, my young friends. We can well do so when we look into our own hearts and see temptations, inconsistencies, tendencies we blush to acknowledge. And it’s not youth, beauty, grace, or charm that makes either beauty or goodness in God’s eyes. Young ladies, when mirrors or men praise you, remember that in the sight of her Maker, Mary Ann Ainley—a woman neither mirror nor lips have ever praised—is fairer and better than either of you. She is indeed,” he added after a pause, “she is indeed. You young ones, wrapped in yourselves and earthly hopes, scarcely live as Christ lived. Perhaps you cannot yet—while life is so sweet and earth so beautiful. It would be too much to expect. She, with a humble heart and reverence, walks closely in her Redeemer’s footsteps.”
