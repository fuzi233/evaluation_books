The last echoes of the procession’s shuffling footsteps faded down the cobblestones, the dust of the throng settling soft over sun-warmed stone. As soon as the last straggler had rounded the corner towards the Piazza, Romola stepped down from her hiding place and hurried to the steps of San Stefano. The old church attendant Cecco had been drawn along with the rest of the crowd to gawk at the ceremony, so the only figure propped against the weathered oak church door was Baldassarre, his dented horn cup loose in his gnarled, calloused hand, waiting for her.
There was a stark, shocking change in him. The blank, foggy, half-waking glance of a man only just clawing his way back from the darkness of amnesia had vanished. What blazed in its place was raw, caged ferocity, the glare of a starved beast behind iron bars that spots its prey pass safely beyond the reach of the bars. When Romola stepped close and spoke to him, that fury snapped straight onto her, and she flinched. Her first thought was that he had seen Tito. And as that grating, unmistakeable hatred raked over her, a fragile, foolish spark of hope stirred in her chest: what if this ragged, raging old man was the one who had committed the wrong? What if her husband was innocent of the shadowy cruelty she had long suspected of him? If she could bring them face to face now, she could finally lay every unnameable fear to rest.
“If you come with me,” she said gently, “I can give you shelter and food until you are fully rested and strong again. Will you come?”
“Yes,” Baldassarre said, but the words were a mutter, spoken more to himself than to her. “I shall be glad to get my strength. I want to get my strength.”
“Come,” she invited, gesturing for him to walk at her side, and took the quieter, less crowded path along the banks of the Arno heading for Ponte Rubaconte. “I think you are no Florentine,” she ventured, once they had stepped onto the bridge.
He glanced around at her without speaking. The fog of his scattered, forgotten past had thickened again under the weight of his bodily exhaustion, and his suspicious caution had sharpened to a near-paranoid edge. But her eyes were soft, open, holding no fear of his rags or his fury, and at last the quiet in her gaze compelled him to answer, sharp and wary. “No. I am no Florentine. I am a lonely man.”
She saw he had no wish to speak more of himself, and dared not press him with further questions, lest he turn and abandon her. As she glanced at him from time to time, that fragile spark of hope that her husband was innocent guttered and died. If this old man had been the one in the wrong, why all that dread, all that secrecy?
They walked in silence until they reached the entrance to Via de’ Bardi, and Romola noticed Baldassarre jolt as if he had been scalded, turning to stare at her like he had just solved a riddle he had turned over in his head for months. A moment later she paused at the half-open courtyard door and turned to him.
“Ah!” he said, before she could speak. “You are his wife.”
“Whose wife?” Romola asked, her blood turning cold.
Baldassarre could not have dredged the name up at that moment if his life had depended on it. The sheer force of Tito’s smiling, treacherous image pressing on his mind had pushed every verbal marker of the man out of his reach. He made no answer, only stared back at her with strange, unblinking fixedness.
Romola swung the door wide to show him the courtyard, its stone floors covered in thick straw where four or five fevered sick people lay propped on pallets, while tiny pale children crawled and sat on the loose straw, gnawing at dry stalks and gurgling soft nonsense to themselves.
“If you will come in,” she said, her voice trembling, “I will find you a comfortable place to rest, and bring you more food.”
“No, I will not come in,” Baldassarre said. But he did not move away, arrested by the jumble of half-memories and rage churning in his overtaxed mind, too confused to choose a path forward.
“Is there nothing I can do for you?” Romola said, almost pleading. “Let me give you some money to buy food. It will be plentiful soon.” She slipped her hand into her leather scarsella as she spoke, and held out her palm cupped with several silver grossi. She deliberately gave him far more than she would have handed to any other beggar in the same circumstances.
He stared at the coins for a long moment, then said, “Yes. I will take them.”
She poured the money into his palm, and he curled his fingers tight around the cool silver. “Tell me,” Romola began, almost beseeching, “what shall you—”
But Baldassarre had already turned away from her, striding back towards the bridge. He crossed it without pausing, and walked straight up the Via del Fosso until he came upon Niccolò Caparra’s armourer’s shop, turning towards it without a second’s pause, as if it had been the very object he had been searching for all along.
Niccolò the armourer was out in the guild procession with the rest of Florence’s armourers, and only one young apprentice was left minding the store. The walls hung thick with all kinds of blades and weapons, and Baldassarre’s eyes locked on the one he hungered for far more than bread. Niccolò would almost certainly have refused to sell a sharp blade to this ragged man who bore the faint, unmistakable marks of prison on his wrists, but the younger, less observant apprentice did not hesitate at all: he took three grossi for a sharp, compact hunting knife, thin and light, perfect for slipping inside the breast of his tunic. Baldassarre walked on, feeling stronger, sturdier than he had in months. That sharp edge could give the thrust of an aged arm deadly force. It was a companion now, a power that was finally on his side, even if it failed. It would shatter against plate armour, but why would Tito think to wear armour every hour of the day, after all these months he had spent safe, comfortable, forgetting the man he had left to rot in a dungeon? The knife had been bought with the traitor’s own wife’s money. That was justice.
Before he had even taken the coins, he had known exactly what he would do with them: buy a weapon. And then, if he could, buy food: food to nourish the arm that would grip that blade, food to nourish the body that was the temple of his vengeance. With the thought of hiding fresh in his mind, lest Tito’s men drag him back to prison before he could strike, he turned down the narrowest, shadowiest side streets, bought himself a hunk of meat and a crust of bread, and sat down under the first derelict loggia he found to eat. The city’s bells pealed louder and louder in celebration, their roar throbbing through the air, through his bones, and to him they sounded like nothing more than the roar of a great, cruel world that was all aligned against him.
Back in the courtyard, Romola watched Baldassarre until he vanished around the corner into Piazza de’ Mozzi, half feeling his departure was a quiet relief, half scolding herself for not being bolder, for not pushing harder to drag the full truth out of him, for not making sure there was no innocent suffering there she could ease. Yet what could she have done, if the truth had turned out to be some dark, ugly secret about her husband, on top of all the anxieties that already weighed heavy on her shoulders? Surely a wife was permitted to wish to remain ignorant of her husband’s wrongdoings, when she of all people could never protest against him, never warn the rest of Florence of his treachery? That thought tangled up so many of her conflicting feelings that she could not bear to unpack it in her exhaustion. This was supposed to be a time of joy, for the relief had arrived for starving Florence. She stepped back into the courtyard, shut the door behind her so the bells would not drown out her voice, and pushed her heavy black veil back off her face so the sick women could see her clearly, then stood in their midst and told them the good news: the grain ships were coming, the bells were ringing for gladness at the news that the famine was over.
They all sat up to listen, while the little children trotted or crawled towards her, tugging at her long black skirts, impatient to be close enough to see her face. She yielded to them, tired as she was, and sank down on the straw, while the tiny pale creatures peered into her wicker basket and pulled loose strands of her hair, and the feeble voices all around her cried out “Praised be the Holy Virgin!” “It was the procession that brought us the news!” “The Mother of God has taken pity on us!”
At last Romola got to her feet from the straw, too weary to force a smile any longer, saying as she turned up the stone steps, “I will come back soon, to bring you all your dinner.”
“Bless you, madonna! Bless you!” the faint chorus called back, in almost the exact same tone of reverence they had used only minutes earlier to praise the unseen, painted Madonna they prayed to.
That quiet, sweet reverence mattered more to Romola than she could say. She had never had any innate taste for tending the sick, for patching rags and spooning broth to the dying, like other women who found such small labours their natural calling. Her early noble, scholarly upbringing at her father’s side had kept her far from these kinds of womanly works, and if she had not brought to them all the burning, unspent passion of her deepest feelings, they would have felt irksome to her. But they had become the one unshaken resting place of her mind, the one narrow pathway lit by clear, uncomplicated light. When the gulf between herself and Tito seemed to widen further every time she tried to bridge it with gentle submission, when she left her confessor Fra Salvestro or the other followers of Savonarola she worshipped among, reeling with the small, narrow superstitions they parroted, falling back to her old sharp intellectual contempt for their pious platitudes, she would return to these works of mercy, and find her footing again. Whatever else filled her with doubt, the help she gave to her fellow citizens assured her that Fra Girolamo had been right to call her back. Her place in Florence had never been empty: it had been filled with her love and her labour. Florence had needed her, and the heavier her own private sorrow pressed on her, the more gladness she felt in two full years of memories, of small hours where she had lightened the burden of life for someone else. All that ardour of her nature that could no longer spend itself in the tender affection of a daughter for her father or a wife for her husband had transformed itself into a burning enthusiasm of sympathy for the whole common life of the city. She had stopped believing her own lot could ever be happy, had stopped even thinking of personal happiness at all: the only end of her life that seemed worth chasing was the diminishing of sorrow for everyone around her.
Her enthusiasm was stirred to fresh, constant strength by Savonarola’s influence. Even when she recoiled in disgust from the wearisome, stale visions and allegories that his lesser followers droned on about, her deep affinity for his passionate sympathy and the grandeur of his aims had lost none of its power. His burning indignation against the abuses and oppression that formed the daily story of the Church and the city’s ruling elites had kindled that same ready fire in her. His special care for Florentine liberty, for purity of government, and his constant tying of that immediate local goal to the wider end of a universal regeneration of humanity, had woken in her a new consciousness of the great, sweeping drama of human existence of which her own small life was a part. Through her daily, helpful contact with the poorest and most suffering of her neighbours, that vague sentiment hardened into a definite, living motive for self-denying practice. She thought little about obscure theological dogmas, and shrank from reflecting too closely on Fra Girolamo’s prophecies of immediate scourge and the soon-to-come regeneration of the world. She had submitted her mind to his guidance and entered fully into communion with the Church, because it was there that she found an immediate satisfaction for deep, hungry moral needs that all the classical culture and early experience of her life had left completely unmet. Fra Girolamo’s voice had woken in her a reason to live that stood entirely apart from personal enjoyment and personal affection. But it was a reason that seemed to need feeding with greater forces than she possessed within herself, and her submissive observance of all the offices of the Church was simply a kind of watching and waiting, hoping that fresh strength might come to her somehow. The pressing problem for Romola at that moment was not to settle questions of religious controversy, but to keep that flame of unselfish emotion alive, by which a life of deep private sadness could still be a life of active, generous love.
Her trust that Savonarola’s nature was far greater than her own made up a huge part of the strength she had found. And that trust was not to be lightly shaken. It is not force of intellect that makes people recoil ready at the oddities and aberrations of great men, any more than it is force of vision that makes the eye stare obsessively at the warts on a face bright with human warmth: it is simply the absence of high, generous sensibilities. Romola was so deeply moved by the grand, world-shaking energies of Savonarola’s nature that she listened patiently to every dogma and prophecy that came wrapped in the vehicle of his own ardent, deeply felt faith. No soul is ever fully desolate as long as there is one human being for whom it can feel trust and reverence. Romola’s trust in Savonarola was like a strong rope strung securely along the edge of the steep cliff path she walked, making her step light and elastic as long as she grasped it. If it were suddenly wrenched away, no matter how firm the ground she trod beneath her feet, she would stagger, and perhaps even fall.
