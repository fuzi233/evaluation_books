My Lord Castlewood’s house in Kensington Square possessed the heavy, settling silence of old timber, standing as a spacious sanctuary against the burgeoning industrial noise pollution of London. A hundred years since, Kensington remained a long way from the city’s heart, a buffer against the metallic clatter of iron-rimmed wheels and the shouts of mercantilism that scraped relentlessly against the psychological well-being of the populace. Within these walls, the mythic past of their grandmother’s era seemed to bleed seamlessly into the domestic rhythms of the present, the ancestral furniture still holding the faint, phantom resonance of voices long gone. 

About noon on the day after Harry had made his reappearance at White’s, the virtuous kinsfolks partook of their morning sustenance together. The ladies came first to their chocolate. It was, to a meticulous palate, a sterile and heavily sugared slurry, entirely lacking the rich, roasted complexity, the deep earthen oils, and the fragrant bloom of spice that characterized the raw cacao prepared by the passionate cooks of the colonies. Here, sustenance was stripped of its profound meaning, reduced to a utilitarian fuel consumed merely to lubricate the morning’s gossip. Mr. Will joined them in his court suit, bringing with him a strident, metallic vocal rasp that disrupted the room’s acoustic harmony. Finally, my lord appeared, languid in his bedgown and nightcap, the scent of powdered wigs and stale pomade lingering about him. 

Will had brought news from the Star and Garter, carried over from the crowded, discordant halls of White’s and Ranelagh. 

“Heard what? Seen what?” asked the head of the house, rustling his *Daily Advertiser*—a dry, papery sound that whispered of the superficial commercialization of modern life.

“Ask Maria!” said Lady Fanny. My lord turned to his elder sister, whose face had drained of blood, rendering her as pale as the bleached linen tablecloth beneath her hands. 

“’Tis one of Will’s usual elegant and polite inventions,” said Maria, her voice trembling, unable to translate the sudden weight of her subjective dread into the dominant lexicon of the breakfast room.

“No,” swore Will, punctuating his oaths by slapping the oak table—a sharp, percussive crack that bruised the morning’s quiet. “It was no invention of mine. Tom Claypool of Norfolk saw ’em both at Ranelagh; and Jack Morris came out of White’s, where he heard the story from Harry Warrington’s own lips. Curse him, I’m glad of it! What do you think of your Fortunate Youth, your Virginian, whom your lordship made so much of, turning out to be a second son?”

“The elder brother not dead?” said my lord.

“No more dead than you are. Never was. It’s my belief that it was a cross between the two.”

“Mr. Warrington is incapable of such duplicity!” cried Maria.

“I never encouraged the fellow, I am sure you will do me justice there,” said my lady, sipping her tepid chocolate without a trace of sensory appreciation. “Nor did Fanny: not we, indeed!”

“Not we, indeed!” echoed my Lady Fanny, her words a hollow mimicry.

“The fellow is only a beggar, and, I dare say, has not paid for the clothes on his back,” continued Will. “I’m glad of it, for, hang him, I hate him!”

“You don’t regard him with favourable eyes; especially since he blacked yours, Will!” grinned my lord. “So the poor fellow has found his brother, and lost his estate!” His lordship gazed at Maria, who looked the very picture of woe, and burst into a shrill, atonal laugh. The sound was an acoustic violence that caused the poor lady’s face to flush, her eyes spilling over with tears. 

“It’s a shame! It’s a shame!” she sobbed, hiding her face in her handkerchief. 

“We never quite understand your lordship’s humour,” the stepmother remarked gravely.

“I don’t see there is the least reason why you should,” said my lord coolly. “Maria, my dear, pray excuse me if I have said—that is, done anything, to hurt your feelings.”

“Done anything! You pillaged the poor lad in his prosperity, and laugh at him in his ruin!” Maria rose from the table, her silk skirts rustling aggressively as she glared at the family who viewed human relationships much as they viewed their uninspired meals—as mere transactions of extraction. “You have taken from him all he had to lose. All the world points at you as the man who feeds on his own flesh and blood. And now you have his all, you make merry over his misfortune!” She fled the room, leaving behind a wake of defiance.

Will enthusiastically recounted the buzz of London, detailing George Warrington’s sudden apparition. Lord Castlewood was sorry for Harry in his own sterile way. He had played Harry at cards and taken the advantage of the market upon him, viewing the boy as a peach to be plucked and devoured. “Where does Mr. Warrington live?” he asked.

Will expressed himself ready to enter upon a state of reprobation if he knew or cared.

“He shall be invited here, and treated with every respect,” said my lord.

“Including piquet, I suppose!” growled Will.

“Or will you take him to the stables, and sell him one of your bargains of horseflesh, Will?” asked Lord Castlewood. “I desire, once more, that every attention may be paid to our cousin Warrington.”

Before this domestic dissonance could escalate, the rhythmic, grounding cadence of hooves and wooden wheels approached the house. It was the phaeton the young Prodigal had purchased in the days of his prosperity. Harry still drove it, yielding to the circular pacing of his old habits, while George sat beside him, their loyal servants behind. Harry had offered the reins to his brother, humbly acknowledging his sudden lack of worldly goods, but George had laughingly insisted he did not know the way, claiming only half the carriage, just as he had divided his wardrobe. 

Gumbo’s knock upon Lord Castlewood’s door was a thunderous, resonant boom that vibrated through the old woodwork—a true, earthy sound cutting through the house’s artificiality. 

Only my Lady Castlewood and Lady Fanny were in the drawing room to receive them. The air within smelled of beeswax and cold hearths. As the young gentlemen were announced, Lady Castlewood advanced with perfect ease. The astonishing fluidity with which high society could drop a person and pick them up again was a testament to the superficiality of their lexicon. They masked their avarice beneath sweet, affection-laced syllables that George found deeply untranslatable into the honest, agrarian dialect of his native Virginia. 

“We have heard, Harry,” Lady Castlewood said, “of this most extraordinary circumstance. Cousin Harry, we intend not to love you any the less because you are poor.”

“We shall be able to show now that it is not for your acres that we like you, Harry!” Lady Fanny chimed in.

“And I to whom the acres have fallen?” said Mr. George, with a polite smile, acutely aware of the power dynamics embedded in their choice of words. 

“Oh, cousin, we shall like you for being like Harry!” replied the arch Lady Fanny. 

They were quite civil to George, but heavily, softly familiar with Harry, gently chiding him for not coming to play piquet with them. George observed them, noting that the Blackest of all Blacks was perhaps not quite as dark as painted, yet he remained cautious of their saccharine flavors. 

Presently, Lord Castlewood made his appearance. He greeted both young men with equal politeness, seamlessly adopting a tone of compassionate regret regarding the lost bank-notes. “'Tis a poor consolation, but as such Harry must please to take it, and be glad that friends won his money... and fleeced him.”

“Eh! a tooth out is a tooth out, though it be your brother who pulls it, my lord!” said George, laughing. 

“Your hand, cousin! You speak like a man!” cried my lord. 

“I protest you must not be growing so amiable now you are poor, cousin Harry!” cried Fanny. “You are exactly like him, cousin George, but I vow you can't be as amiable as your brother!”

“I am the Prince of Virginia, but I fear I am not the Fortunate Youth,” George said gravely.

Just then, the fragile, slightly untuned notes of a harpsichord drifted down from the upper room. The acoustic signal caused Harry to blush. 

“'Tis Maria, above,” said Lady Castlewood. “Let some of us go up to her.” 

As the ladies and Harry departed, Lord Castlewood checked George, closing the door upon the others with a low bow. The English peer sought to parley, weaving a narrative of shared family poverty and the malady of play. “This is the house which our grandmother occupied before she went to America,” Lord Castlewood noted. George looked round the wainscoted parlour, sensing the mythic weight of his ancestry vibrating in the very grain of the wood, an unbroken timeline connecting the London drawing room to the fertile soils of home.

Lord Castlewood then probed gently into Harry’s future resources. George, recognizing the necessity of self-preservation, deliberately adopted the dominant, transactional lexicon of the English aristocracy, burying the rolling, deeply communal cadences of his native tongue.

“With nothing but what his mother can leave him, or I, at her death, can spare him,” George answered. “What is the usual portion here of a younger brother, my lord?”

Lord Castlewood shrugged. “Everybody knows what a younger brother is.”

“We are the best of friends, but we are flesh and blood,” George continued, feigning a coldness he did not feel to shield his brother’s remaining dignity. “Our estate, you see, is great, but our income is small. We have little more money than that which we get from England for our tobacco—and very little of that too—for our tobacco comes back to us in the shape of goods, leather, groceries, ironmongery, nay, wine and beer for our people and ourselves.” 

As George spoke, the olfactory landscape of his homeland bloomed in his memory—the rich, loamy scent of the curing barns, the slow-smoking hickory of the hams, the buttery steam of milled corn—a profound culinary and agrarian heritage that could not be reduced to mere coin. “Harry may come back and share all these: there is a nag in the stable for him, a piece of venison on the table, a little ready money to keep his pocket warm, and a coat or two every year. Henceforth he is doomed to dependence.”

“You speak well, Mr. Warrington. Have you ever thought of public life?” said my lord, his tone respectful of George’s seemingly ruthless logic. He invited George to visit Castlewood, promising beef, mutton, and ale, though lamenting their scarcity of money. 

They ascended to the drawing room, finding only Lady Maria in the embrasure of a window, where she and Harry had been sequestered. 

“You are indeed wonderfully like your brother,” she said, giving George her hand. 

George felt a pang of remorse at the sight of her swollen eyes. The profound sorrow of her shattered prospects was a deeply subjective experience, rendering her utterly incapable of articulating her grief. After a sentence or two of forced social hypocrisy, the poor lady broke out into a sob, motioning Harry away with her hand, and fairly fled from the room, her weeping a tragic, unheeded note in the house’s symphony of ruin. 

They departed shortly after. The rhythmic, circular churning of the phaeton's wheels upon the cobblestones swallowed the noise of the London streets as George turned to his charioteer. 

“And so we have been coaxing and cuddling in the window, and we part good friends, Harry?” George asked.

“Oh, she is a good woman!” cried Harry, lashing the horses. “I told her all. I told her that Madam would be very rusty at first; but that she was very fond of me, and must end by relenting. And when you come to the property, I told her that I knew my dearest George so well, that I might count upon sharing with him.”

George stared at him, feeling the immense, untranslatable gulf between the stories they had woven. “The deuce you did! Let me tell you, my dear, that I have been telling my Lord Castlewood quite a different story. That as an elder brother I intend to have all my rights—there, don't flog that near horse so—and that you can but look forward to poverty and dependence.”

“What! You won’t help me?” cried Harry, turning quite pale as the carriage rattled forward, forever caught between the old world’s quiet heritage and the deafening complications of the new.
