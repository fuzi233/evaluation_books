Gerard lodged in a modest house on the west bank of the Tiber, where the river talked all night under the windows—no clatter of carts here, only water worrying its stones, and, at dawn, the thin bell-notes from far churches threading the damp air. He lived by those sounds. They steadied him; they measured his hours when his purse would not.

Every day, after a mouthful, he went out with his best specimen—clean, severe lines, ink laid as evenly as a cook lays oil in a pan, neither too stingy nor too lavish. He carried it from shop to shop, to the booksellers and stationers he could hear of, men whose doors smelled of glue and old calfskin, whose counters were dusted with pumice and scraped vellum.

They met him with Roman coldness.

“We make our letter somewhat thinner,” said one, as if he spoke of slicing onions.

“How dark your ink is,” said another, wrinkling his nose like a man offered over-salted broth.

But the true answer came, again and again, in a chorus that made Gerard feel the city itself had turned its back.

“What avails this? Scant is the Latin writ here now. Can ye not write Greek?”

“Ay,” Gerard said, “but not nigh so well as Latin.”

“Then you shall never make your bread at Rome.”

Bread. The word went through him with a hunger that was more than hunger: a boy’s memory of his mother’s table, of a crust warmed at the hearth, of broth that comforted because it carried a familiar tongue in its very smell. Here, he could copy Virgil as cleanly as a knife can pare an apple, but Rome—Rome wanted other shapes, other letters, other mouths.

He borrowed a beautiful Greek manuscript at a high price—paying for it as one pays for a rare spice, dearly, with a wince—and went home with a sad hole in his purse but none in his courage. He set himself to the new characters as a cook sets himself to a strange fish: first learning its bones, then its flesh, then the exact heat that will not spoil it. In a fortnight he had made vast progress. Thereafter he worked at Greek till noon, and hunted customers the rest of the day.

When he carried round a Greek specimen better than any they possessed, the traders shrugged and smiled as if he had brought them a fine dish to a table already groaning.

“Greek and Latin are alike unsaleable,” they told him. “The city is thronged with works from all Europe. You should have come last year.”

So he bought a psaltery—cheap enough, and it gave him something to do with his hands when his head grew too loud with refusal. In the evenings he picked up canzonets as one picks up scraps in a kitchen: a phrase here, a cadence there. Rome’s streets had their own acoustic law—fountains, sandals, disputing voices, the slap of laundry in courtyards—and he found he could not think without some order in the sound.

His landlady, pleased with his looks and manners, spoke him kind words in passing. She was from Siena, and she said so the way another woman might show a medal of Our Lady: proudly, as protection. One day she made him dine with her—she herself tending the pot, stirring and tasting, and letting the broth speak before she did. The room was warm with garlic and stewed herbs; the spoon rang against the earthen bowl with a homely, honest note.

She watched him eat like a woman who has fed men all her life and can read their hearts in their jaws.

“What hath dashed thy spirits?” she asked at last.

Gerard, who had tried to swallow his discouragement with his bread and could not, told her plainly: no work, no patron, and Rome full to bursting of other men’s manuscripts.

She gave him her reading of it at once, her voice sharp as vinegar.

“Those sly traders have writers in their pay. For whose work they receive a noble price, and pay a sorry one. So no wonder they blow cold on you. Methinks you write too well.”

He stared.

“How know I that? say you,” she rattled on, delighted with her own certainty. “Marry—marry—because you lock not your door, like the churl Pietro, and women will be curious. Ay, ay, you write too well for them.”

Gerard sighed, half amused, half troubled. “Alas, dame, you read folk on the ill side, and you so kind and frank yourself.”

“My dear little heart,” said she, with a toss, “these Romans are a subtle race. Me? I am a Siennese, thanks to the Virgin.”

“My mistake was leaving Augsburg,” Gerard said, thinking of honest German cities and honest German work.

“Augsburg?” She drew herself up as if the name were a beggar at her door. “Is that a place to even to Rome? I never heard of it, for my part.”

Yet she would not let him sink. Her kindness had the briskness of a woman who will not have a guest die under her roof, because it brings a bad name upon the house and upon her own cooking besides.

“Seeing thee a stranger, they lie to thee without sense or discretion,” she said. “Why, all the world knows our great folk are bitten with the writing spider this many years, and pour out their money like water, and turn good land and houses into writ sheepskins, to keep in a chest or a cupboard. God help them, and send them safe through this fury—as He hath through a heap of others. And in sooth there hath been somewhat less cutting and stabbing among rival factions, and vindictive eating of their opposites’ livers, minced and fried, since Scribbling came in.”

She named two that made Gerard’s heart lift: Cardinal Bessarion, and the Pope himself.

“There be a pair could keep a score such as thee a-writing night and day,” she vowed. “But I’ll speak to Teresa; she hears the gossip of the court.”

The next day she returned with names—five more signori, each said to be mad for books and writing. Gerard wrote them down carefully, as if the names themselves were provisions to be measured. He bought parchment—good stuff, smooth under the finger, that faint animal sweetness of skin still clinging to it—and busied himself for days preparing specimens. He left one at each door, with his name and his lodging, and waited.

There was no answer.

Day after day passed, each one sounding the same: the same street cries, the same bell-hours, the same scuff of his own shoes on stone, and—worse than hunger—the same silence at his door. It was strange, and bitter, that this should be the very time when, far away at Rotterdam, Margaret fought like a wolf to feed her male dependents, and was arrested for curing without a licence instead of killing with one. A man can starve on coincidence as on crusts: slowly, incredulous at first, then with a dull certainty.

Gerard saw ruin staring him in the face. He spent his afternoons gathering canzonets as if hoarding crumbs, mastering them note by note. He bought playing cards to colour, because even poor work must be work. Then he struck off a meal a day.

That stroke of economy—so clever in his mind—brought him into fresh trouble at once, because in these camere locande the landlady dressed all meals though the lodgers bought the provisions. She knew the sound of a man’s hunger. She knew the silence of a plate not set.

She confronted him with a brusque “Are you not ashamed?” that made him blush and look scared. But she pacified herself as quickly as she had flamed, and appealed to his good sense as a mistress of kitchens appeals to a foolish boy who thinks he can live on air.

“Adversity is a thing to be overcome on an empty stomach?” she demanded. “Patienza, my lad! times will mend; meantime I will feed you for the love of heaven.”

“For gratis,” Gerard understood, and his pride rose like yeast.

“Nay, hostess,” he said, “my purse is not yet quite void; and it would add to my trouble an if true folk should lose their due by me.”

“Why, you are as mad as your neighbour Pietro, with his one bad picture.”

Gerard pricked up his ears.

“How know you ’tis a bad picture?”

“Because nobody will buy it.”

So she told him of Pietro: a Florentine who had come with money and an unfinished panel; how he had taken the room opposite Gerard’s and furnished it neatly, as if order could protect him; how, when the picture was finished, visitors came and made offers—liberal ones in her opinion—but he refused them with such disdain he made enemies of customers. Since then he had often carried it out to try to sell, and always brought it back; and these last weeks she had seen one movable after another go out of his room. Now he wore but one suit and lay at night on a great chest.

She had discovered this by peeping through the keyhole, for he locked his door vigilantly whenever he went out.

“Is he afraid we shall steal his chest,” she scoffed, “or his picture, that no soul in all Rome is weak enough to buy?”

“Nay,” said Gerard gently, “’tis his poverty he would screen from view.”

“And the more fool he! Are all our hearts as ill as his? A might give us a trial first, anyway.”

She compared them with a woman’s unmerciful fairness: Gerard, golden-haired and mannerly; Pietro, dark, sour-visaged, taking no pains to please. Yet she feared Pietro starving to death in her house would stain her name. One starveling, she declared, was enough.

“You are far from home,” she said, and her voice softened just a little, “and it is for me, which am the mistress here, to number your meals—for me and the Dutch wife, your mother, that is far away. We two women shall settle that matter. Mind thou thine own business, being a man, and leave cooking and the like to us, that are in the world for little else that I see but to roast fowls, and suckle men at starting, and sweep their grown-up cobwebs.”

Her talk of roasting fowls and numbering meals stabbed Gerard with the clean pain of memory; it made his need for his own mother’s tongue, his own hearth-sounds, rise in him till it was almost a thirst.

“Dear kind dame,” he said, “in sooth you do often put me in mind of my mother that is far away.”

“All the better,” said she, beaming. “I’ll put you more in mind of her before I have done with you.”

Gerard, who was not an egotist and could see his own grief mirrored in another’s, thought of Pietro’s pride and loneliness until he could bear it no more. If he had little, he would share it; and, he told himself, Pietro’s companionship would repay him in coin no moneychanger carried—sympathy, and perhaps some counsel in this city that listened only to its own dialects.

He tried to waylay Pietro on the stair; Pietro slipped past like a cat. He tried again; again the locked door and silence.

One day, passing, he heard a groaning in the room opposite—low, stubborn, as if wrung out of a man who hated to let sound betray him. Gerard knocked. No answer. He knocked again. A surly voice bade him enter.

He pushed the door and stepped in, timid as a man entering another’s hunger.

It was a garret: bare as a scraped board. A chair, an iron basin, an easel; a picture turned face to wall; and a long chest that served for bed and throne. On that chest lay a haggard young man coiled like a serpent, his eye unnaturally bright. The air smelled of stale oil, old sweat, and the faint sharpness of pigments ground too sparingly—as if even colour were rationed.

“Good Signor Pietro,” said Gerard, “forgive me that, weary of my own solitude, I intrude on yours; but I am your nighest neighbour in this house, and methinks your brother in fortune. I am an artist too.”

“You are a painter?” The man sprang up in a sudden blaze of courtesy, as quick as a match to tow. “Welcome, signer. Sit down on my bed.”

He leapt off the chest and waved Gerard into the place with a magnificent gesture, as if the bare wood were damask.

Gerard bowed and smiled, but hesitated a heartbeat.

“I may not call myself a painter. I am a writer—a calligraph. I copy Greek and Latin manuscripts, when I can get them to copy.”

“And you call that an artist?”

“Without offence to your superior merit, Signor Pietro.”

“No offence, stranger, none.” Pietro’s mouth twisted. “Only, meseemeth an artist is one who thinks, and paints his thought. Now a caligraph but draws in black and white the thoughts of another.”

“’Tis well distinguished,” said Gerard. “But then, a writer can write the thoughts of the great ancients, and matters of pure reason, such as no man may paint: ay, and the thoughts of God, which angels could not paint. But let that pass. I am a painter as well; but a sorry one.”

“The better thy luck. They will buy thy work in Rome.”

“Yet seeking to commend myself to one of thy eminence, I thought it well rather to call myself a capable writer than a scurvy painter.”

At that moment a step sounded on the stair—the landlady’s tread, with its kitchen authority. Gerard’s heart leapt, not for food alone, but for the familiar rhythm of a home’s service.

“Ah! ’tis the good dame,” he cried. “What, hostess! I am here in conversation with Signor Pietro. I dare say he will let me have my humble dinner here.”

Pietro bowed gravely, as if granting audience.

The landlady entered with a dish set steaming and savoury, the smell of it filling the poor room like music—meat and herbs, onion softened till sweet, the fat carrying the scent like a chorus. She set it down on the chest with a face stripped of expression—only her hands spoke, efficient and kind—and went without a word.

Gerard fell to, thankful as a man rescued. Yet after a few mouthfuls he stopped. Eating alone in another man’s misery felt like singing in a sickroom.

“I am an ill-mannered churl, Signor Pietro,” he said. “I ne’er eat to my mind when I eat alone. For Our Lady’s sake put a spoon into this ragout with me; ’tis not unsavoury, I promise you.”

Pietro’s glittering eye fixed on him, and for a beat the only sound was the far-off drip of a roof and Rome’s muffled street below.

“What,” said Pietro slowly, “thou a stranger, and offerest me thy dinner?”

“Why, see,” said Gerard, “there is more than one can eat.”

“Well, I accept,” said Pietro—and took the dish with an appearance of calmness.

Then, with a violent sweep, he flung the contents out of the window.

The smell of the ragout, broken and wasted, went out into the air like a prayer strangled.

He turned back, trembling—half with mortification, half with rage—and said, “Let that teach thee to offer alms to an artist thou knowest not, master writer.”

Gerard’s face flushed hot. For an instant it cost him a bitter struggle not to strike the man—if not for insult, then for the sacrilege of good food destroyed. Somewhere in him, his mother’s careful thrift rose up, indignant as a saint. Yet pity came too: the pride of a starving man is a sharp thing; it cuts the hand that touches it.

He spoke coldly.

“Likely what thou hast done might pass in a novel of thy countryman, Signor Boccaccio; but ’twas not honest.”

“Make that good!” Pietro snapped, sullen as a dog that knows it has bitten.

“I offered thee half my dinner; no more,” said Gerard. “But thou hast ta’en it all. Hadst a right to throw away thy share, but not mine. Pride is well, but justice is better.”

Pietro stared, then his brow shifted as the thought worked in. His anger, like oil, settled and showed its dregs.

“’Tis well,” he said at last. “I took thee for a fool, so transparent was thine artifice. Forgive me! And prithee leave me. Thou seest how ’tis with me. The world hath soured me. I hate mankind. I was not always so. Once more excuse my discourtesy, and fare thee well.”

Gerard sighed and moved to the door. But a new thought struck him—half Dutch stubbornness, half a kind of jest to salve the sting.

“Signor Pietro,” said he, “we Dutchmen are hard bargainers. We are the lads ’een eij scheeren’—to shave an egg. Therefore I, for my lost dinner, do claim to feast mine eyes on your picture, whose face is toward the wall.”

“Nay, nay,” Pietro cried hastily, stepping between him and the panel as if between a child and a knife. “Ask me not that. I have already misconducted myself enough towards thee. I would not shed thy blood.”

“My blood?” Gerard blinked.

“Stranger,” said Pietro, voice low, “irritated by repeated insults to my picture, which is my child, my heart, I did in a moment of rage make a solemn vow to drive my dagger into the next one that should flout it—and the labour and love I have given to it.”

“What,” said Gerard, “are all to be slain that will not praise this picture?” He could not help looking at its back with curiosity.

“Nay, nay—if you would but look, and hold your parrot tongue. But you will be talking. So I have turned it to the wall for ever. Would I were dead, and buried in it for my coffin!”

Gerard weighed him with one glance: pride like fever, despair like a draft in winter. He answered quietly.

“I accept the condition. Show me the picture. I can but hold my peace.”

Pietro went and turned its face, set it in the best light the garret could give, and then coiled himself again upon his chest—his eye bright, his stiletto nearer his hand than any rosary.

The picture showed the Virgin and Christ flying through the air in a cloud of shadowy cherubic faces; beneath stretched a landscape vast as a traveller’s life—fields, river, roads, little houses and moving specks of men—forty or fifty miles of earth unrolled like a tablecloth; above, a purple sky deep as wine.

Gerard stood and looked without a word. He stepped close and looked. He retreated as far as the poor room allowed and looked again. He paced between nearness and distance as a careful taster moves between pot and plate, letting the whole and the parts speak in turn. Half an hour passed, filled only with the hush of his breathing and the far city’s murmur.

At last Pietro’s voice broke, querulous and inconsistent as a starving man’s pride.

“Well—have you not a word to say about it?”

Gerard started, as if recalled from a chapel.

“I cry your mercy,” said he. “I forgot there were three of us here. Ay, I have much to say.”

And he drew his sword.

“Alas! alas!” Pietro cried, springing up in terror. “What wouldst thou?”

“Marry,” said Gerard, “defend myself against thy bodkin, signor—and at due odds, being, as aforesaid, a Dutchman. Therefore hold aloof while I deliver judgment, or I will pin thee to the wall like a cockchafer.”

“Oh! is that all?” Pietro sagged with relief. “I feared you were going to stab my poor picture with your sword—stabbed already by so many foul tongues.”

So Gerard, “pursuing criticism under difficulties,” stood with his sword’s point covering Pietro, one eye on the man, the other sliding back to the painted sky.

“First,” he said, “I would have you know that, in the mixing of certain colours, and in the preparation of your oil, you Italians are far behind us Flemings. But let that flea stick. For as small as I am, I can show you certain secrets of the Van Eycks that you will put to marvellous profit in your next picture.”

“I will! I will!” Pietro cried, leaning forward like a man offered bread.

“Meantime,” said Gerard, and his voice warmed, “I see in this one the great qualities of your nation. Verily, ye are solis filii. If we have colour, you have imagination. Mother of Heaven! an he hath not flung his immortal soul upon the panel.”

Pietro’s breath hitched, and even the stiletto seemed to forget to glitter.

“One thing I go by is this,” Gerard went on: “it makes other pictures I once admired seem drossy, earth-born things. The drapery here is somewhat short and stiff—why not let it float freely, the figures being in air and motion?”

“I will!” Pietro cried again, his whole face changing as if lit from within. “I will do anything for those who will but see what I have done.”

“And this landscape,” said Gerard, turning his head to take it all in, “it enlightens me. Henceforth I scorn those little huddled landscapes that did erst content me. Here is nature’s very face: a spacious plain, each distance marked, and every tree, house, figure, field, and river smaller and less plain, by exquisite gradation, till vision itself melts into distance. O, beautiful!”

His voice began to gather like a song. Outside, the street noises rose and fell, but in the garret another world had taken the air.

“And the cunning rogue hath hung his celestial figure in air out of the way of his little world below. Here, floating saints beneath heaven’s purple canopy. There, far down, earth and her busy hives. And they let you take this painted poetry, this blooming hymn, through the streets of Rome and bring it home unsold. But I tell thee, in Ghent or Bruges—or even in Rotterdam—they would tear it out of thy hands. It is a common saying that a stranger’s eye sees clearest.”

Then, as if calling a man back from the brink with his true name, he cried, “Courage, Pietro Vanucci! I reverence thee; and though myself a scurvy painter, do forgive thee for being a great one. Forgive thee? I thank God for thee and such rare men as thou art; and bow the knee to thee in just homage. Thy picture is immortal; and thou, that hast but a chest to sit on, art a king in thy most royal art. Viva, il maestro! Viva!”

At that, Pietro—Italian to the marrow, all abandon where a moment ago he had been all suspicion—flung himself upon Gerard’s neck and sobbed.

“They said it was a maniac’s dream.”

“Maniacs themselves!” Gerard shouted, laughing with anger and joy together. “No—idiots!”

“Generous stranger,” Pietro cried, his voice broken, “I will hate men no more since the world hath such as thee. I was a viper to fling thy poor dinner away; a wretch, a monster.”

“Well, monster,” said Gerard, and his sternness melted like butter in heat, “wilt be gentle now, and sup with me?”

“Ah, that I will. Whither goest thou?”

“To order supper on the instant,” said Gerard. “We will have the picture for third man.”

“I will invite it whiles thou art gone,” Pietro said softly, turning toward his panel as to a hearth. “My poor picture—child of my heart.”

“Ah, master,” said Gerard, pausing at the door, “’twill look on many a supper after the worms have eaten you and me.”

“I hope so,” said Pietro.
