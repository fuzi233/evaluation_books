A mighty Empress, seated upon her throne of command, could find jealousy rising in her heart over a mere female prisoner.

My mind, honed by the soldier's trade to function with swift precision in moments of stress, seized upon the correct course the instant the words left her lips. I was bound to sacrifice myself for Atlantis by the decree of the High Council, and if necessity demanded, Nais must perish as well. Yet, within the same fleeting heartbeat, a scheme for saving her materialized.

I bowed low before the Empress, speaking with grave deference: "In this, and in all other matters where a human hand holds sway, I shall execute your wishes, Phorenice." She responded by patting my arm, and fresh currents of emotion surged from the depths of her wondrous eyes. Truly, the Gods had granted her half her schemes and half her victories simply by granting her this form, this voice, and the features outlined upon her face.

By this juncture, merchants and neighboring dwellers had flocked to the harbor, offering changes of clothing and lodging to retreat into. Phorenice radiated graciousness. Though the wetness of my coat mattered little, the section of the harbor where the mammoth had cast us was far from savory, and I gladly followed her lead. For myself, I spoke no further word to Nais, withholding even a glance of farewell. But a gesture so slight was insufficient nourishment for Phorenice; before departing, she issued strict orders to the port-captain regarding the guarding of his prisoner.

Inside the house to which I was ushered, I was bathed and relieved of the plainest garment in the host's store, a transaction that left him pleased enough to have escaped ruin. Before Phorenice emerged again, I spent an hour standing on the pavement, listening to the distant roar of bombardment. I felt a grim amusement at the face of the merchant who trailed behind us. He was clearly bankrupt. His store held jewels and gauds of the most expensive variety—goods he had purchased in partnership with other merchants, as custom dictates. These treasures had delighted Phorenice's eye, and she had stripped them all from him, draping them across her person.

"Are they not beautiful?" she asked, displaying them to me. "Behold how they flash beneath the sun. I am entirely glad now, Deucalion, that the mammoth gave us that furious ride and that spill, since it has procured me such a delightful present." Turning to the ruined merchant, she smiled. "Tell him that when he has earned more, I shall visit as his guest again."

"Ah! Litters have arrived!" she exclaimed, spotting the waiting carriages. "Send one away. Share mine with me, sir. We must pretend to be lovers today, even if love becomes a reality for us both tomorrow."

"No additional bearers needed," she continued, dismissing the offer with a wave. "My own slaves will suffice. I am glad you are not one of those gross, overfed men, Deucalion. I am small and slender, and I dislike being overshadowed by a husband who would block the light."

"To the royal pyramid?" I asked.

"Not to the walls, nor to the pyramid. I neither wish to fight nor to sit as Empress today, sir. As I have told you, it is my whim to be Phorenice the maiden for a few hours. If someone I know would woo me now, as other maidens are wooed, I should consider it a luxury."

She ordered the slaves to circle the harbor's rim and commanded the staring crowd that if they followed, she would rain fire upon them as she had upon the sacrifice. Having witnessed the unruliness of the streets and received hints from the gatekeeper at the royal pyramid concerning the Empress's welcoming banquet, I withheld bringing these matters to her attention, assuming they were already known to her.

I dismissed the guards and drove away the throng of gaping sightseers with sharp words, silencing the silly creatures who must needs stare at their superiors. Seated in the litter, with the bearers bowing their heads to the poles, we swung away along the wide pavement separating the merchant houses from the maritime folk and the rippling waters.

I thought with some sadness of the few ships floating on that splendid basin now, and the scant evidence of commerce remaining on the quays. In former times, vessels were moored so closely that many waited in the estuary outside the walls; memorials had been sent to the King to double the port's size to handle the glut of trade. This was in the old days of oars and sails, when machines drawing power from the Sun were but rarely employed to hasten a vessel's course.

A voyage to Egypt and back then took a year, compared to a couple of months now. Of three ships setting out, at least one could be expected to succumb to the dangers of the wide waters and the terrible beasts that haunted them. Yet, in those ancient days, trade roared with a lusty life, expanding steadily and heavily. Merchants were portly men who gave generously to the Gods. Now, however, the entire world seemed engaged in arms, and trade had become vulgar.

A wealthy merchant, if he existed, forgot his merchandise, swearing that haggling was more indecent than blasphemy, curled his beard in the newest fashion, and transformed himself into a courtier. Where his father had spent anxious days tallying cargoes and consulting shipmasters, the son wasted hours directing sewing men as they adorned coats and nights vaporing at banquets.

As for the lesser merchants, lacking substance, taxes and the constant bickering of war had nearly ground them into starvation. Furthermore, with the country in perpetual uproar, markets for most merchandise had vanished, and nothing was produced that could be exported. If a weaver was pressed into service as a fire-tube man, he ceased making cloth; if a farmer played at rebellion, he stopped buying slaves to till his fields. Indeed, they told me that a month prior to my return, a cargo of slaves finer than any previously imported from Europe had entered the harbor. There was nowhere for them to go but ashore across the estuary, left free to starve or live in the wild grounds at their discretion. Not a single man in all Atlantis would accept another slave as a gift.

Though grieved by this decline, I deferred any remedies for discussion until later. Speaking of it while riding together in the litter would only make matters worse; I was learning Phorenice's moods enough to understand that silence was policy. I suspected she, too, had studied mine, doing her best to engage me between her bursts of trifling conversation.

We traveled outward until the westernmost harbor wall joined the land. There, the panting bearers set us down. She led me into a small stone house standing alone on a promontory where the tide ran constantly. After much unlocking, she admitted me and showed me her new gold collectors.

In the dry knowledge taught in the colleges and groves of the Sacred Mountain, it was a common fact among us that gold existed in a dissolved state within all seawater. However, no plans for dragging it forth into solid yellow hardness had ever been discussed. Here, this field-raised upstart of an Empress had stumbled upon the trick as if it were written in a book.

She patted my arm laughingly as I stared around the place. "I tell all others in Atlantis that only the Gods possess this secret," she declared, "and that They bestowed it upon me as one of themselves. But I am no Goddess to you, am I, Deucalion? By my face! I have no other explanation for how this plan was conceived. Let us suppose I dreamed it."

She gestured to the machinery. "Look! The seawater sluices in through that culvert, passes over these rough metal plates set in the floor, and flows out again yonder in its natural course. You see the yellow metal caught in the ridges of the plates? That is gold. My fellows here melt it with fire into bars and take it to my smith in the city. The tides vary constantly, as you priests know well, drawn gently by the quiet moon. It requires little calculation to determine how much water passes through these culverts in a month and how much gold per grain should be caught on the plates."

"My companions here initially attempted to cheat me," she confessed, her tone shifting to something darker. "But I towed two of them in the water once behind a galley until the cannibal fish devoured them. Since then, the others have given me credit for..."

"For what do you think?" I prompted.

"More divinity," she replied lightly. "I suppose it is that. But I am allowing you to witness how it is done. Apply your head to a little arithmetic, and observe the effect achieved. You will be a God yet yourself, Deucalion, with these silly Atlanteans, if only you will apply your wit and cleverness."

Did she mock me? Was she sincere? I could not discern. Sometimes she pointed out that her successes and triumphs were merely rewards of thought and brilliance; the next moment, she offered an impossible explanation, leaving me to deduce that she must be more than mortal or the feat could never have been accomplished. In truth, this little woman, with her supple mind and supple body, mystified me more and more the longer I stayed by her side.
