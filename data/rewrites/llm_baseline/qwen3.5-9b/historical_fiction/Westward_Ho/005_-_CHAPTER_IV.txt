"I could not love thee, dear, so much, Loved I not honor more."—Lovelace.

What had become of the fair breaker of so many hearts, whom I have not yet introduced to my readers? She sat in the small farmhouse beside the mill, nestled deep within the green valley of Combe, halfway between Stow and Chapel. She was sulking, as much as her naturally sweet disposition would allow, at being shut out from the grand festivities in Bideford and forced to endure a "Martinmas Lent" in this remote western glen. So utterly lonely was she that, despite regarding Eustace Leigh with a mixture of aversion and Protestant suspicion, she could not bring herself to avoid chatting with him whenever he descended to the farm and its mill. He contrived to appear almost daily, ostensibly on various errands.

Her uncle and aunt initially treated these visits with stiff reserve; the aunt particularly ensured she was always present as a third party in every conversation. Yet, Mr. Leigh was a gentleman's son, and rudeness to a neighboring squire and valuable customer was unthinkable. Conversely, Rose was the daughter of a wealthy man, while they were poor cousins; quarreling with her was equally inadvisable. Furthermore, the pretty maid, aided by both wilfulness and her winning charm, invariably managed to get her own way wherever she went. She had wisely begged her aunt never to leave them alone, claiming she "could not bear the sight of Mr. Eustace, only she must have someone to talk with down here." Her aunt, considering herself a simple countrywoman compared to the sophisticated townsfolk, decided that people knew their own business best and let matters proceed as they pleased.

In the meantime, Eustace, fully aware that the difference in creed between him and Rose was the greatest obstacle to his love, carefully kept his private opinions in the background. Instead of attempting to convert the millers, he daily purchased milk or flour from them and distributed it among the elderly women of Moorwinstow, who agreed that, for a Papist, he was a remarkably godly young man. Eventually, after consulting with Campian and Parsons regarding current political plots, the trio concluded they would attend church the following Sunday. Messrs. Evan Morgans and Morgan Evans, having memorized the rubrics beforehand, conducted themselves with perfect orthodoxy, as did poor Eustace—to the great astonishment of the local gentry. Upon returning home, Eustace flattered himself that he had successfully converted the parsons, clerks, and congregation alike. Unaware of his own simplicity and cunning foolishness, he failed to notice that every good wife in the parish was whispering to her neighbor, "He turned Protestant? The devil turned monk! He's only after Mistress Salterne, the young hypocrite."

While the two Jesuits found it expedient, for the holy cause in which they were engaged, to reconcile themselves outwardly to the established powers, they remained busily plotting their overthrow in private. Since April, they had played a game of hide-and-seek across England, now waiting quietly for news from Ireland to provide their cue. Their aim was a great "rising of the West" that would sweep from her throne that stiff-necked, persecuting, excommunicated, illegitimate, and profligate usurper who falsely claimed to be the Queen of England.

They held as steadfastly to the belief in those days—as they have in later eras, with a genuine Baconian contempt for the results of sensible experience—that the heart of England truly belonged to them. They believed the British nation was on the verge of returning to the bosom of the Catholic Church and delivering Elizabeth in chains to the feet of the rightful Lord of Creation, the Old Man of the Seven Hills. This fair hope, which had skipped just ahead of them for centuries, always remaining a step further away like the spot where a rainbow touches the ground, was announced at times in language that terrified old Mr. Leigh.

One day, as Eustace entered his father's private study after his usual visit to the mill, he heard voices rising in heated dispute. Parsons was usually blustering; Mr. Leigh was peevishly deprecating; and Campian, the sweetest-natured of men, was trying to pour oil on troubled waters. Seizing the opportunity for the good of the cause, Eustace stopped outside to eavesdrop.

"My excellent sir," said Mr. Leigh, "does not your very presence here demonstrate how deeply I am affected toward the holy cause of the Catholic faith? Yet, I cannot in the meanwhile forget that I am an Englishman."

"And what is England?" retorted Parsons. "A heretic and schismatic Babylon, wherein it is written, 'Come out of her, my people, lest you be partaker of her plagues.' Yea, what is a country? It is merely an arbitrary division of territory by the princes of this world, who are nothing and come to nothing. They are created by the people's will; their existence depends solely on the sanction of Him to whom all power is given in heaven and earth—our Holy Father the Pope. Remove the latter, and what is a king? The people who made him may unmake him."

"My dear sir, remember that I have sworn allegiance to Queen Elizabeth!"

"Yes, sir, you have; and, as I have demonstrated at length in my writings, you were absolved from that allegiance the moment the Bull of Pius the Fifth declared her a heretic and excommunicate, thereby forfeiting all dominion whatsoever. I tell you, sir, what you should have known already: since the year 1569, England has had no queen, no magistrates, no laws, no lawful authority whatsoever. To owe allegiance to any English magistrate, sir, or to plead in an English court of law, is to disobey the apostolic precept, 'How dare you go to law before the unbelievers?' Rebellion is now not merely permitted; it is a duty."

"For Heaven's sake, take care, sir! For God's sake, take care!" cried Mr. Leigh. "Right or wrong, I cannot have such language used in my house. For the sake of my wife and children, I cannot!"

"My dear brother Parsons, deal more gently with the flock," interposed Campian. "Your opinion, though probable in the eyes of most of our Order, is hardly safe enough here; the opposite position is at least safe enough that Mr. Leigh may well excuse his conscience for accepting it. After all, are we not sent hither to proclaim precisely this truth, and to relieve the souls of good Catholics from a burden that has seemed too heavy?"

"Yes," said Parsons, half-sulkily, "to allow all Balaams who will to sacrifice to Baal while they call themselves by the name of the Lord."

"My dear brother, have I not often reminded you that Naaman was allowed to bow himself in the house of Rimmon? Can we therefore complain of the office to which the Holy Father has appointed us? It is to declare to men like Mr. Leigh his especial grace: that the Bull of Pius the Fifth binds the Queen and the heretics only, in no way binding the Catholics, at least as long as the present tyranny prevents the pious purposes of the Bull from taking effect."

"Be it so, sir; be it so. Only observe this, Mr. Leigh: our brother Campian confesses this to be a tyranny. Observe, sir, that the Bull still binds the so-called Queen, and that she and her magistrates remain usurpers, nonentities, and shadows of a shadow. Observe further, sir, that when what is lawful is excused to the weak, it remains no less lawful to the strong. The seven thousand who did not bow the knee to Baal did not slay his priests; but Elijah did, and won a good reward. And if the rest of the children of Israel sinned not in failing to slay Eglon, yet Ehud's deed was none the less justified by all human and divine laws."

"For Heaven's sake, do not talk so, sir! Or I must leave the room. What have I to do with Ehud and Eglon, and slaughters, and tyrannies? Our Queen is a very good Queen, if Heaven would but grant her repentance and turn her to the true faith. I have never been troubled about religion, nor anyone else that I know of in the West Country."

"You forget Mr. Trudgeon of Launceston, father, and poor Father Mayne," interjected Eustace, who had by this time slipped inside. Campian added softly, "Yes, your West of England has also been honored by its martyrs, as well as my London by the precious blood of Story."

"What, young malapert?" cried poor Leigh, turning sharply upon his son, glad to find someone on whom he could vent his ill humor. "Are you too against me, with a murrain on you? And pray, what the devil brought Cuthbert Mayne to the gallows, and turned Mr. Trudgeon (he was always a foolish hot-head) out of house and home, but just such treasonable talk as Mr. Parsons must needs hold in my house? It has made a beggar of me and my children, and he vows before all the saints he will soon make a beggar of me entirely."

"The Blessed Virgin forbid!" said Campian.
