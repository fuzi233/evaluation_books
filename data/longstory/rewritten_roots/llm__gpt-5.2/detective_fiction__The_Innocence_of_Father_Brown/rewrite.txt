Between the thin silver of morning and the green-glass glitter of the sea, the boat kissed Harwich and shook loose its cargo of travellers—an untidy spill of coats, parcels, and yawns—among whom the man we must keep in sight had every intention of becoming forgettable. Nothing in him begged attention. His holiday clothes were pale and well cut, as if he were only a careful tourist: a light grey jacket, a white waistcoat, a straw hat banded with blue-grey ribbon. Yet his face had the sealed look of official paper, dark and lean, clipped at the chin by a short black beard that made his mouth seem tighter than it was.

He smoked with the stern patience of a man waiting for an answer he already knew how to force. Under the grey jacket rode a loaded revolver. Under the waistcoat lay a police card. Under the straw hat worked one of the sharpest minds in Europe. Aristide Valentin—head of the Paris police—had crossed from Brussels to London to make what the newspapers would call, with their loud, tinny phrases, the arrest of the century.

Flambeau was in England.

That name, spoken in three countries, made clerks swallow and constables listen. The great criminal had been hunted from Ghent to Brussels, from Brussels to the Hook of Holland, and it was supposed he would use the Eucharistic Congress in London as a cook uses a crowded market: a place where a thousand hands reach at once and nobody remembers which one brushed the purse. He might travel as a minor secretary connected with the Congress, a shy pen-pusher with ecclesiastical papers. Or he might travel as anything. That was the trouble with Flambeau: he could alter his skin like a fish in different water, and the water in London was thick with strangers.

Years later people would say that when Flambeau vanished from the world—when the colossus of crime ceased, as though some huge engine had finally been shut off—there was a great quiet upon the earth. In his worst best days he was as international a figure as a Kaiser. Each morning’s paper reported his escape from one impossible theft by means of another. He was a Gascon giant, full of bodily daring and a kind of boyish athletic comedy: turning a juge d’instruction upside down “to clear his mind,” running down the Rue de Rivoli with a policeman under each arm as if carrying loaves. To his credit—if a thief can have credit—his strength was more often used in bloodless indignities than in blood. His real crimes were the grand robberies: ingenious, wholesale, almost inventive as new vices. He had run a Tyrolean Dairy Company in London without dairies, cows, carts, or milk, serving a thousand subscribers by merely moving the little cans outside doors from one doorstep to another. He had kept up a love correspondence with a young lady by photographing messages infinitely small upon microscope slides. He had repainted an entire street’s numbers in the dead of night to turn one traveller into his trap. He had invented a portable pillar-box and set it up in quiet suburbs to catch postal orders like a net. And despite his bulk he was a startling acrobat—leaping like a grasshopper, vanishing into tree-tops like a monkey.

Valentin knew, even if he caught him, the chase would not end at the handcuffs. But first there remained the primitive question: how to find him at all.

There was one thing Flambeau could not disguise: his extraordinary height. If Valentin’s quick eye had caught a tall apple-woman, a tall grenadier, even a tall duchess, he might have seized them on the spot. But along the train there was nobody who could be Flambeau any more than a cat could masquerade as a giraffe. Valentin had already sifted the boat; and the travellers picked up at Harwich or along the line made a small, certain set: a short railway official, three market gardeners (none tall), a very short widow lady from an Essex town, and—last of all—a very short Roman Catholic priest from a village on the Essex flats.

At that final specimen Valentin gave up and almost laughed. The little priest looked like the countryside itself made human and helpless. He had a face as round and pale as a dumpling steamed too long; eyes washed-out as the North Sea under grey weather. He clutched several brown paper parcels with the incapable anxiety of a man trying to hold flour in his fingers. The Eucharistic Congress had clearly sucked many such small local lives out of their fields and lanes, blind and honest as moles disinterred.

Valentin, sceptic in the severe French way, had no love for priests. But pity is not love, and the priest might have provoked pity in anyone. He carried a large shabby umbrella that slipped to the floor repeatedly with a dull thump that made heads turn. He kept confusing the ends of his return ticket. And with a moon-calf frankness that made Valentin’s mouth twitch, he explained to everyone in the carriage that he must be careful, because one of the parcels contained something of real silver “with blue stones.”

That quaint blend of Essex flatness and saintly simplicity amused the Frenchman all the way until the priest somehow arrived at Tottenham with his parcels, and then came back for his umbrella. When he returned for it, Valentin even warned him—good-naturedly—that if he wanted to keep the silver safe, it was unwise to announce it to strangers. Yet even while he spoke, Valentin’s eyes kept wandering, searching for someone else: some tall shadow in the crowd, rich or poor, male or female—anyone over six feet, for Flambeau stood four inches above.

At Liverpool Street he stepped down with a cautious satisfaction: he had not missed the criminal so far. He went to Scotland Yard to regularise his position, arrange for help, establish lines as exact as the cords on a map. Then he lit another cigarette and went for a long walk through London.

The city’s soundscape changed with every turning: the brassy scrape of wheels, the moist hiss of streets being washed, the clipped clang of hooves, the restless chatter that rose and fell like boiling. Past Victoria, he reached a quiet square—one of those accidents of London where a pocket of stillness survives between hard walls. The tall, flat houses looked both prosperous and uninhabited, like shelves of sealed jars. The shrubbery in the centre sat like a green island marooned in stone.

One side of the square was higher than the rest, like a dais; and in that long line there was another London accident: a restaurant that looked as if it had wandered in from Soho and stayed. It was unreasonably attractive. Dwarf plants stood in pots like little kitchen herbs trying to be brave. Long striped blinds of lemon-yellow and white hung like cloths laid out for a feast. A flight of steps rose from the street to the front door, as if the place were slightly afloat.

Valentin stood before those yellow-white blinds and considered them for a long time, listening. Even the quiet had its own texture: a distant cart rumbling like far thunder, a sparrow’s sharp insistence, the soft, domestic clink of cutlery from somewhere within. In a city that shouted, this place spoke in a lower key.

The most incredible thing about miracles is that they happen. Not the theatre-miracles advertised with brass and posters, but the small, elfin coincidences: clouds that gather into the staring shape of a human eye, a tree that stands in the landscape like a question-mark. There is an element in life that wrong-foots the prosaic. Poe put it neatly: wisdom should reckon on the unforeseen.

Valentin was French through and through, not in gestures but in intellect. He did not mystify himself with modern phrases about “thinking machines.” A machine cannot think; it only repeats. Valentin thought, and because he respected reason, he respected its limits. Reason without first principles is a cook without salt: all method, no savour; or a motor without petrol. Here he had no first principles. Flambeau had been missed at Harwich; if in London, he might be anything from a tall tramp on Wimbledon Common to a tall toast-master at the Metropole. In such naked ignorance Valentin relied on his own method: follow the unreasonable with cold care.

When he had a clue, he pursued it like a hound along a straight scent. When he had none, he went deliberately to the wrong places—knocked at empty houses, turned down cul-de-sacs, took lanes blocked with rubbish, walked crescents that led nowhere. He defended this folly quite logically: if you have a clue, this is the worst way; if you have no clue, it is the best way, because you might chance upon the very oddity that struck the pursued. Somewhere a man must begin; and it is sometimes wisest to begin where another man might stop.

Something about those steps, something about the restaurant’s quiet cheerfulness, stirred the detective’s rare romantic fancy. He went up, sat by the window, and ordered a cup of black coffee.

It was late morning and he had not breakfasted. A faint litter of other breakfasts—crumbs, a smear of jam, the ghost of eggs—hung about the room and made his hunger more acute, as the smell of baking can sharpen emptiness. He added a poached egg, then sat musingly shaking white sugar into his coffee while his mind ran through the catalogue of Flambeau’s escapes: once by nail scissors, once by a house on fire; once because of an unstamped letter, once by turning a crowd’s attention to a comet that might destroy the world. He knew his own brain was as good as the criminal’s; but the advantage lay with the thief.

“The criminal is the creative artist,” he said sourly, “the detective only the critic.”

He lifted his cup, sipped—and put it down at once.

He had put salt in it.

The shock was immediate and physical: the bitter coffee turned harsh and briny, as if the sea had blown straight into his mouth. He looked at the vessel he had used. It was certainly a sugar-basin—made for sugar as plainly as a champagne bottle is made for champagne. Why should it contain salt? He looked for more orthodox vessels. There were two salt-cellars; he tasted them. Sugar.

Valentin looked around with refreshed interest. Such a reversal had the neatness of a prank, but the neatness of a signature too. The room appeared ordinary—clean, cheerful—except for an odd dark splash on the white-papered wall.

He rang for the waiter.

The waiter arrived fuzzy-haired and blear-eyed with early fatigue. Valentin, who was not without humour of the simplest kind, asked him to taste the sugar and see whether it was worthy of the house’s reputation. The effect was like a slap: the waiter yawned and woke.

“Do you play this delicate joke on your customers every morning?” Valentin asked. “Does changing the salt and sugar never pall as a jest?”

The waiter stammered that the establishment had no such intention; it must be a mistake. He lifted the sugar-basin and stared; he lifted the salt-cellar and stared. His face grew bewildered, then alarmed. Abruptly he excused himself and hurried away, returning with the proprietor, who repeated the examination with equal confusion.

Then the waiter burst out in a rush, as if words had been corked in his throat.

“I zink,” he said eagerly, “I zink it is those two clergy-men.”

“What two clergymen?” Valentin asked.

“The two clergymen,” said the waiter, pointing at the dark stain. “That threw soup at the wall.”

“Threw soup at the wall?” Valentin repeated, for an instant suspecting an Italian metaphor for some smaller discourtesy.

“Yes, yes,” the attendant insisted, stabbing a finger at the splash. “Threw it over there.”

The proprietor took over, more fluent.

“It’s true, sir,” he said. “Two clergymen came in very early, as soon as the shutters were down. Quiet, respectable people. One paid the bill and went out. The other—slower, like—took longer gathering his things. But he went at last. Only, the instant before he stepped into the street he deliberately picked up his cup—only half emptied—and threw the soup slap on the wall. I was in the back room, and the waiter too. We rushed out and found the wall splashed and the shop empty. It does no particular damage, but it was confounded cheek. I ran into the street; too late. I only noticed they went round the next corner into Carstairs Street.”

Valentin was already on his feet, hat settled, stick in hand. In the universal darkness of not-knowing, he could only seize the first crooked finger that pointed. This finger was crooked enough. He paid, clashed through the glass doors, and swung round into the other street.

Even in fever, his eye stayed cool. Something in a shop-front flickered past like a flash. He stopped, turned back, and looked.

It was a greengrocer’s, popular and open to the pavement: oranges piled like small suns, nuts heaped like brown stones, everything plainly ticketed. In the most prominent compartments, the cards had been swapped. Over the nuts, in bold blue chalk: “Best tangerine oranges, two a penny.” Over the oranges: “Finest Brazil nuts, 4d. a lb.”

Valentin stared at those two placards. He had met this sort of perverse tidiness before—recently, in a sugar-bowl and a salt-cellar. He drew the attention of the red-faced fruiterer, who was already scanning the street with sullen irritation.

“These tickets are misplaced,” Valentin said mildly.

The fruiterer, without speaking, snatched each card and put it where it belonged.

Valentin leaned elegantly on his cane and studied him a moment longer.

“Pray excuse my apparent irrelevance,” he said lightly, “but I should like to ask you a question in experimental psychology and the association of ideas.”

The shopman looked at him as if weighing the possibility of throwing an orange.

“Why,” Valentin continued, swinging the cane as if conducting an invisible orchestra, “why are two tickets wrongly placed in a greengrocer’s shop, like a shovel hat that has come to London for a holiday? Or—to make myself clear—what mystical association connects nuts marked as oranges with two clergymen, one tall and the other short?”

The fruiterer’s eyes bulged; for an instant he seemed ready to spring. Then anger found words.

“I don’t know what you ’ave to do with it,” he snapped, “but if you’re one of their friends you can tell ’em from me I’ll knock their silly ’eads off, parsons or no parsons, if they upset my apples again.”

“Indeed?” Valentin asked with sympathetic interest. “Did they upset your apples?”

“One of ’em did,” the man said hotly. “Rolled ’em all over the street. I’d ’ave caught the fool but for havin’ to pick ’em up.”

“Which way did these parsons go?” Valentin asked.

“Up that second road on the left, and then across the square,” the fruiterer answered promptly.

“Thanks,” Valentin said, and vanished.

On the far side of the next square he found a policeman and went straight to him.

“This is urgent, constable. Have you seen two clergymen in shovel hats?”

The policeman began to chuckle, heavy as a cart.

“I ’ave, sir; and if you arst me, one of ’em was drunk. He stood in the middle of the road that bewildered that—”

“Which way did they go?” Valentin snapped, the humour cut away clean.

“They took one of them yellow buses over there,” the man said, pointing. “Them that go to Hampstead.”

Valentin produced his official card, spoke rapidly, and the words carried the authority of metal.

“Call up two of your men to come with me in pursuit.”

He crossed the road with such sudden energy that the ponderous policeman became almost agile. Within a minute and a half an inspector and a man in plain clothes joined him on the opposite pavement.

“Well, sir,” began the inspector with official importance, “and what may—?”

Valentin pointed sharply with his cane.

“I’ll tell you on the top of that omnibus,” he said, already darting into the tangle of traffic.

They scrambled up and dropped panting into the top seats of the yellow vehicle. The city’s noise rose around them: wheels grinding, voices colliding, an iron clang like cutlery struck too hard. The inspector, catching breath, said:

“We could go four times as quick in a taxi.”

“Quite true,” Valentin replied placidly, “if we only had an idea where we were going.”

The inspector stared.

“Well, where are you going?”

Valentin smoked with a frown for a few seconds, then removed the cigarette.

“If you know what a man is doing, get in front of him,” he said. “But if you want to guess what he is doing, keep behind him. Stray when he strays; stop when he stops; travel as slowly as he. Then you may see what he saw and act as he acted. All we can do is keep our eyes skinned for a queer thing.”

“What sort of queer thing?” the inspector asked.

“Any sort,” Valentin answered, and fell into obstinate silence.

The yellow omnibus crawled northward. Time stretched like old dough—pulled and pulled without tearing, yet never coming to shape. Past the hour when men begin to think of lunch, the streets kept unrolling: length after length of suburb, as though London were an infernal telescope that always showed another tube beyond the last. A journey like that makes a man feel he must have reached the end of the universe, only to discover he has reached the beginning of Tufnell Park.

London died away into draggled taverns and dreary scrubs, and then was born again into blazing high streets and blatant hotels, each district with its own voice—some harsh with carts, some thin with children, some roaring with commerce like a mill that never stops. Even in winter the city hummed, but the hum was not music; it was the careless, unlistening noise of crowds, the kind of sound that makes a man forget his own thoughts. Valentin’s eyes kept scanning the shopfronts sliding by; he listened too, as if for one wrong note in the urban din.

By the time Camden Town was left behind, the policemen were nearly asleep. They started as Valentin suddenly leapt up, struck a hand on each man’s shoulder, and shouted to the driver to stop.

They spilled down the steps into the road, bewildered. Then they saw what he saw: a gilt and palatial public-house, its long façade shining dull in the weak light, and a large window labelled “Restaurant.” The glass was frosted and patterned—except for the middle, where a big black smash starred out like a wound in ice.

“Our cue at last,” Valentin cried, pointing with his stick. “The place with the broken window.”

“What cue?” demanded the inspector. “What proof is there this has anything to do with them?”

Valentin’s rage snapped like a dry twig.

“Proof!” he cried. “Good God, the man is looking for proof! Of course the chances are twenty to one it has nothing to do with them. But what else can we do? Either follow one wild possibility—or go home to bed.”

He banged into the restaurant, the others behind him. They sat at a small table and ate a late luncheon, with the smashed star of glass visible from within. It told them nothing, yet it was a sign, and a sign was more than emptiness.

When Valentin paid, he spoke casually to the waiter and, without a word, added an enormous tip. The waiter straightened with immediate animation.

“Got your window broken, I see,” Valentin said.

“Yes, sir,” the man replied, bending over the change.

“Indeed? Tell us about it,” Valentin said, the curiosity in his voice as light as a napkin.

“Well, sir,” said the waiter, “two gents in black came in—two of those foreign parsons running about. They had a cheap quiet little lunch. One paid and went out. The other was just going when I looked at my change and found he’d paid more than three times too much. ‘Here,’ I says to the chap nearly out of the door, ‘you’ve paid too much.’ ‘Oh,’ he says, very cool, ‘have we?’ ‘Yes,’ I says, and picks up the bill to show him. Well—that was a knock-out.”

“What do you mean?” Valentin asked softly.

“Well, I’d have sworn on seven Bibles I’d put four shillings on that bill. But when I looked—there it was: fourteen shillings. Plain as paint.”

Valentin’s eyes burned. He moved slowly, as if careful not to spill something precious.

“And then?” he said.

“The parson at the door says, all serene, ‘Sorry to confuse your accounts, but it’ll pay for the window.’ ‘What window?’ I says. ‘The one I’m going to break,’ he says—and smashed that blessed pane with his umbrella.”

All three men exclaimed. The inspector muttered, half to himself:

“Are we after escaped lunatics?”

The waiter, enjoying the absurdity as working men enjoy stories at others’ expense, continued:

“I was that knocked silly for a second I couldn’t do anything. The man marched out and joined his friend just round the corner. Then they went so quick up Bullock Street I couldn’t catch them, though I ran round the bars to do it.”

“Bullock Street,” Valentin repeated, and shot out of the place as if the very name had tugged him by the sleeve, racing up the thoroughfare after the strange pair whose trail was written not in footprints, but in a chain of domestic outrages—salt where sugar should be, labels reversed, apples scattered, soup flung like a signal, and a window broken on purpose, as if someone were writing in the city with a hand that could not speak any other language.

Aristide Valentin, Chief of the Paris Police, was late for dinner; and in his house lateness was not a mere breach of manners but a tremor felt along the nerves of the place.

Guests arrived to find themselves received, not by their host, but by Ivan—Valentin’s confidential servant—stationed like an old sentry at a table in the entrance hall. The hall was hung with weapons the way a pantry might be hung with hams: not for show, but because they belonged to the household economy of danger. Ivan’s face was the colour of ash, his moustaches nearly the same; a scar cut his cheek like a seam in old leather. He explained, with the calm of a man announcing that the soup needed ten more minutes, that Monsieur Valentin had telephoned: detained—ten minutes.

Valentin’s house, for all its tall poplars and its river air, had been built with one appetite in mind: the appetite of enemies. It stood behind high walls almost overhanging the Seine, with a garden spread out behind like an embroidered cloth laid for a feast; yet it was a feast no one could leave. Doors opened from the house into the garden, but the garden itself had no gate to the world—only a tall, smooth wall, unscalable, capped with spikes. If you entered, you entered as into a sealed jar. One exit only—the front door, guarded by Ivan and the armoury, as if the whole establishment were a larder with one lock.

Valentin was in fact delayed by executions and other ugly items of the state’s menu: repulsive work served up to him by his office. He did it with precision, not relish. He hunted criminals ruthlessly, but when they were caught he used his immense influence to soften sentences, cleanse prisons, and drain cruelty out of systems that had grown used to it. He was one of those humanitarian freethinkers who can make mercy sound like a regulation.

When he arrived he was already dressed for the table: black, with the red rosette, his dark beard streaked with grey. He moved through his own house as a man moves through familiar corridors of thought—quickly, quietly, without looking at the pictures. He went straight to his study at the back, which opened on the garden. The French window stood open. He locked a box into its official place, then paused, inexplicably, at the threshold and looked out.

A sharp moon—like a blade held up for inspection—fought with the last rags of storm. The air smelled rinsed, as if rain had scrubbed the leaves and left a clean, cold sap scent behind. Valentin stared with a wistfulness that seemed out of place in a scientific mind; as if some part of him, older than his logic, listened to omens in the garden’s hush. Then he shook it off. He was late; the guests were here.

In the drawing-room one glance told him the one man he especially expected was not yet arrived. The rest of the party was already assembled, a small constellation of rank and habit. Lord Galloway, the English Ambassador, sat choleric and ruddy as a winter apple, the blue ribbon of the Garter cutting across his chest like a sash on a prize ham. Lady Galloway was slim and threadlike, silver-haired, her superiority worn as lightly as perfume. Their daughter, Lady Margaret Graham, was pale and pretty with an elfish face and copper-coloured hair that caught the lamplight like a flame under glass.

There was the Duchess of Mont St. Michel, black-eyed and opulent, with two daughters who repeated her opulence as neatly as a recipe repeated by memory. Dr. Simon was present—glasses, pointed brown beard, forehead barred with parallel wrinkles earned by constant eyebrow-lifting: the signature of the supercilious. And Father Brown, of Cobhole in Essex—small, shapeless, with a face like a dumpling and an air of being always slightly lost in the smell of things.

Valentin noticed, too, a tall man in uniform, a little apart: Commandant Neil O’Brien of the Foreign Legion. He was slim, dashing, clean-shaven, dark-haired, blue-eyed, his spurs and sabre giving him the air of a man who had never quite sat down in his life. Yet the dash was shadowed by melancholy; the Legion’s victories always taste faintly of defeat. By birth an Irish gentleman, he had known the Galloways in boyhood—especially Margaret. Something—debts, disgrace—had broken him from his country; and now he wore his foreign uniform with a swagger that was half freedom, half challenge. When he bowed to the Ambassador’s family, Lord and Lady Galloway acknowledged him stiffly. Margaret looked away.

But Valentin’s mind was on another guest: Julius K. Brayne, that American multi-millionaire of colossal endowments and eccentric faiths, a man who poured money into any doctrine so long as it was new enough to be untested. Newspapers joked about whether he was atheist, Mormon, Christian Scientist, or simply rich; he admired anything he called “progressive,” and the word in his mouth was like a coin worn smooth by too many hands. He had admired Valentin as “progressive,” which did Valentin an injustice.

Brayne arrived with the sudden finality of a dinner bell. His presence was as large as his absence. He was huge—fat as he was tall—dressed in complete evening black without relief of ring or chain. His hair was white and brushed back; his face red, fierce and cherubic, saved from childishness by a dark tuft under the lower lip that gave him a theatrical, almost Mephistophelean touch. The room stared at him for a moment as one stares at an unexpected dish placed in the middle of the table; then the domestic machinery of dinner swept him away. Lady Galloway took his arm, and he was sent into the dining-room.

Dinner passed in the way dinners do when rank has been invited to a policeman’s house: with an extra tension beneath the polite clink of glass. Conversation rose and fell like a controlled simmer. Lord Galloway was genial enough on everything but one subject. So long as his daughter did not take O’Brien’s arm, he was satisfied; and she had gone in properly with Dr. Simon. Yet the old diplomat’s mind kept darting, like a knife seeking a joint. He imagined O’Brien signalling to Margaret, though he could not imagine how. Even over the coffee, when cigars appeared like a final ritual, he could not rest.

The younger men—Simon, Brown, and O’Brien—drifted away towards the ladies or the conservatory. Lord Galloway was left with Brayne and Valentin: the hoary Yankee who believed in all religions, and the grizzled Frenchman who believed in none. Their talk became a “progressive” wrangle, as tasteless as over-boiled vegetables. At last Galloway rose and went to the drawing-room, restless as if something were burning.

He lost his way among the long passages for six or eight minutes. Somewhere he heard Dr. Simon’s high didactic voice, then Father Brown’s duller one, and general laughter. Another argument about science and religion, he thought bitterly—more words, more smoke. But when he opened the salon door, he saw only one thing: what was not there. O’Brien was absent. And Margaret was absent.

He stamped back into the corridor, anger swelling into a kind of senile passion. Protecting his daughter from the Irish adventurer had grown into a central, almost insane idea. He went towards the back of the house, towards Valentin’s study. In the passage he met Margaret suddenly. She swept past him with a white scornful face; that, too, was an enigma. If she had been with O’Brien, where was O’Brien? If she had not, where had she been?

Suspicion groping like a blind hand, he found at last a servants’ entrance opening onto the garden. The storm had gone; the moon now hung clear, a scimitar of light. The garden lay silver and blue, every clipped hedge and gravel path sharpened into theatrical clarity. The poplars stood black and tall, and the river mist began to rise like breath.

Across the lawn a tall figure in blue strode towards the study door. The moonlit silver on facings marked him: Commandant O’Brien. He vanished through the French windows.

Lord Galloway stood a moment in a temper both virulent and vague. The garden, in its moonlit delicacy, taunted him—Watteau’s fairyland, a trap of troubadours. The very grace of O’Brien’s stride enraged him, as though he were a rival rather than a threat to be managed. Determined to shake off the mad romance of the scene by force of action, he followed.

He tripped over something in the grass—a stone or a root—and swore. Then he looked down again, irritated curiosity turning cold. The moon and the poplars watched an odd sight: an elderly English diplomatist running hard and bellowing as he ran.

“A corpse in the grass—a blood-stained corpse!”

His hoarse shouting brought Dr. Simon’s pale face to the study door, glasses gleaming, brow worried. Simon heard the words clearly, and without wasting breath said, “We must tell Valentin at once.” Even as he spoke, Valentin entered, drawn by the cry. He came first with the ordinary concern of a host, fearing a guest had fainted; then, on hearing the fact, his expression snapped into the bright, brisk gravity of his trade. Horror, for him, was a thing to be handled.

“Strange, gentlemen,” he said as they hurried out, “that I should have hunted mysteries all over the earth, and now one comes and settles in my own back-yard. But where is the place?”

A faint river mist made the lawn treacherous and blurred the moonlight at ankle-height, like flour dust stirred in a kitchen. Under Galloway’s shaking guidance they found the body sunk in deep grass: a very tall, broad-shouldered man, face down. Black cloth covered the big shoulders; the head was bald except for a few wisps of brown hair clinging like wet seaweed. From under the fallen face a scarlet line of blood crawled outward, slow as a living thing.

“At least,” said Simon with a strange emphasis, “he is none of our party.”

“Examine him, doctor,” Valentin said sharply. “He may not be dead.”

Simon bent, touched, listened with fingers trained to measure the warmth that lingers after life has gone. “He is not quite cold,” he said, “but I am afraid he is dead enough. Help me to lift him.”

They raised him carefully. The head fell away.

It was not merely a throat cut. The neck had been severed so completely that the head parted from the body like something sliced clean at the joint. Even Valentin’s composure cracked for an instant.

“He must have been as strong as a gorilla,” he muttered—thinking of the arm that could do such work, of the swing and the follow-through.

Simon, shivering despite his clinical habits, lifted the head. The face was mostly unhurt: ponderous and yellow, at once sunken and swollen, with a hawk-like nose and heavy lids. It had something of a wicked Roman emperor, perhaps with a distant, disquieting touch of an Asiatic despot. No one recognized him. Beneath the body they saw only the white gleam of a shirt-front spoiled by blood. He had been dressed as if for dinner.

Valentin went down on hands and knees and examined the grass and earth for twenty yards round, searching for the small truths the ground keeps: marks, fibres, footprints, displaced blades. Simon imitated him awkwardly; Galloway hovered vaguely, as if the lawn itself accused him. The only thing they found was a scatter of twigs snapped or chopped into very small lengths. Valentin examined them briefly and tossed them away.

“Twigs,” he said gravely. “Twigs, and a total stranger with his head cut off; that is all there is on this lawn.”

The garden grew unnaturally silent, the sort of silence in which one begins to hear one’s own blood. Then Galloway cried out, sharp as a cracked bell:

“Who’s that? Who’s that over there by the garden wall!”

A small figure with a foolishly large head came wavering through the haze, at first like a goblin in the moonlight, then resolving into Father Brown.

“I say,” the priest said meekly, “there are no gates to this garden, do you know.”

Valentin’s brows drew together, partly from habit at the sight of the cassock, but he was too honest to deny the remark’s force.

“You are right,” he said. “Before we find out how he came to be killed, we may have to find out how he came to be here. Now listen to me, gentlemen. If it can be done without prejudice to my duty, we shall keep distinguished names out of this. There are ladies; there is a foreign ambassador. If we must mark it down as a crime, it will be followed up as a crime. But till then I can use discretion. I will clear my own guests before I call in my men to look for anybody else.

“Upon your honour, none of you will leave the house till tomorrow at noon. There are bedrooms for all. Simon—you know where to find Ivan in the front hall. Tell him to leave another servant on guard and come to me at once. Lord Galloway—you are best to tell the ladies and prevent a panic. They also must stay. Father Brown and I will remain with the body.”

When Valentin spoke like this, he was obeyed like a bugle. Simon went to the armoury-hung hall and roused Ivan. Galloway returned to the drawing-room to break the news with diplomatic tact, so that when the company gathered the women were already startled and already soothed. In the moonlit garden the little priest and the unbelieving detective stood at head and foot of the corpse, motionless, as if carved symbols of two philosophies of death.

Ivan came out of the house like a cannon shot, racing across the lawn with an eagerness almost indecent in its professional delight. His livid face was brightened by the glow of a mystery that had come, as it were, to his master’s own hearth.

“Yes; look, if you like, Ivan,” Valentin said. “But don’t be long. We must go in and thrash this out.”

Ivan lifted the head—and almost let it drop.

“Why,” he gasped, “it’s—no, it isn’t; it can’t be. Do you know this man, sir?”

“No,” Valentin said, indifferent in tone. “We had better go inside.”

Between them they carried the body into the study and laid it on a sofa. Then they went to the drawing-room, where the guests waited in strained clusters, voices lowered, as if sound itself might provoke the walls.

Valentin sat at a desk as quietly as if he were signing a dinner bill. But his eye had become the iron eye of a judge. He made a few rapid notes and asked shortly, “Is everybody here?”

“Not Mr. Brayne,” said the Duchess of Mont St. Michel, looking round.

“No,” said Lord Galloway, his voice hoarse and harsh. “And not Mr. Neil O’Brien, I fancy. I saw that gentleman walking in the garden when the corpse was still warm.”

“Ivan,” Valentin said, “go and fetch Commandant O’Brien and Mr. Brayne. Mr. Brayne is finishing a cigar in the dining-room; Commandant O’Brien, I think, is walking in the conservatory. I am not sure.”

Ivan flashed out. Valentin, without allowing anyone time to speak, continued with soldierly briskness.

“You all know a dead man has been found in the garden—his head cut clean from his body. Dr. Simon, you have examined it. Do you think that to cut a man’s throat like that would need great force? Or only a very sharp knife?”

“I should say it could not be done with a knife at all,” Simon answered, pale.

“Have you any thought,” Valentin pressed, “of a tool with which it could be done?”

“Within modern probabilities, I really haven’t,” the doctor said, his brows arching painfully. “It’s not easy to hack a neck through even clumsily, and this was very clean. It could be done with a battle-axe, a headsman’s axe, or an old two-handed sword.”

“But good heavens!” cried the Duchess, close to hysteria. “There aren’t any two-handed swords and battle-axes round here.”

Valentin still wrote as he spoke. “Tell me,” he said, “could it have been done with a long French cavalry sabre?”

A low knocking sounded at the door—trivial, yet it curdled the blood in the room like a remembered scene in a tragedy. In the frozen silence Simon managed to say, “A sabre—yes, I suppose it could.”

“Thank you,” Valentin said. “Come in, Ivan.”

Ivan opened the door and ushered in Commandant O’Brien, whom he had at last found pacing the garden again.

O’Brien stood on the threshold disordered and defiant, as if dragged from some private storm.

“What do you want with me?” he cried.

“Please sit down,” Valentin said, level and almost courteous. “Why, you aren’t wearing your sword. Where is it?”

“I left it on the library table,” O’Brien said, his brogue thickening with disturbance. “It was a nuisance, it was getting—”

“Ivan,” Valentin said, “go and get the Commandant’s sword from the library.”

Ivan vanished. Valentin turned back, as if the servant were merely fetching a forgotten napkin.

“Lord Galloway says he saw you leaving the garden just before he found the corpse. What were you doing in the garden?”

O’Brien flung himself into a chair with reckless contempt.

“Oh,” he cried, Irish flooding his speech, “admirin’ the moon. Communing with Nature, me bhoy.”

Silence fell, heavy as damp cloth. It endured, and then the knocking returned—again that small sound that made everyone’s nerves leap, as if the house itself were trying to speak.

Ivan reappeared. In his hands was an empty steel scabbard.

“This is all I can find,” he said.

“Put it on the table,” Valentin said, not looking up.

The room became like a dock at assize, surrounded by the inhuman hush that waits for a verdict. The Duchess’s little exclamations died away. Lord Galloway’s hatred, for the moment, was satisfied and sobered. Then a voice cut through—clear, trembling, brave.

“I think I can tell you,” said Lady Margaret. She rose, her pale face steadying itself like a flame in wind. “I can tell you what Mr. O’Brien was doing in the garden, since he is bound to silence. He was asking me to marry him. I refused; I said in my family circumstances I could give him nothing but my respect. He was a little angry at that; he did not seem to think much of my respect.” She added, with a wan smile, “I wonder if he will care for it now. For I offer it him now. I will swear anywhere that he never did a thing like this.”

Lord Galloway edged towards her, trying to intimidate her with what he imagined an undertone.

“Hold your tongue, Maggie,” he thundered in a hoarse whisper. “Why should you shield the fellow? Where’s his sword? Where’s his confounded cavalry—”

He stopped. His daughter was staring at him with a look so singular, so lurid, that everyone felt it like a physical pull.

“You old fool,” she said low, with no pretence of piety, “what do you suppose you are trying to prove? I tell you this man was innocent while with me. But if he wasn’t innocent, he was still with me. If he murdered a man in the garden, who was it who must have seen—who must at least have known? Do you hate Neil so much as to put your own daughter—”

Lady Galloway screamed. The rest sat rigid, tingling with the touch of those ancient tragedies that breed in proud houses: the shadow of murdered husbands and poisonous paramours seemed to stand behind the chairs, as if old portraits had leaned forward to listen.

In the centre of that morbid stillness an innocent voice spoke:

“Was it a very long cigar?”

They turned, startled by the absurdity, to see Father Brown in a corner, mild as ever, as if discussing an after-dinner dish.

“I mean,” he said apologetically, “that cigar Mr. Brayne is finishing. It seems nearly as long as a walking-stick.”

Something like irritation—and something like reluctant assent—crossed Valentin’s face.

“Quite right,” he said sharply. “Ivan, go and see about Mr. Brayne again, and bring him here at once.”

As the servant left, Valentin addressed Margaret with a new earnestness.

“Lady Margaret,” he said, “we all feel gratitude and admiration for your explanation. But there is still a gap. Lord Galloway met you passing from the study to the drawing-room. It was only some minutes later that he found the garden and the Commandant still walking there.”

“You have to remember,” Margaret replied, a faint irony in her voice, “that I had just refused him. We should scarcely have returned arm in arm. He is a gentleman, anyhow; and he loitered behind—and so got charged with murder.”

“In those few moments,” Valentin said gravely, “he might really—”

The knocking came again. Ivan put in his scarred face.

“Beg pardon, sir,” he said, “but Mr. Brayne has left the house.”

“Left!” Valentin cried, rising for the first time.

“Gone. Scooted. Evaporated,” Ivan answered with grim humour. “His hat and coat are gone too—and I’ll tell you something to cap it all. I ran outside to find traces of him, and I found one. A big trace.”

“What do you mean?” Valentin demanded.

“I’ll show you,” Ivan said—and reappeared holding a flashing naked cavalry sabre, the point and edge streaked with blood.

Every eye fixed on it as if it had fallen out of the sky. Ivan, experienced as a butcher in a slaughterhouse, continued almost calmly.

“I found this,” he said, “flung among the bushes fifty yards up the road to Paris. In other words, I found it just where your respectable Mr. Brayne threw it when he ran away.”

If you ever saw one of the Twelve True Fishermen arrive at the Vernon Hotel on the night of their yearly dinner, you would notice it before you had time to notice anything else: when he shrugs out of his overcoat, his evening coat shows green, not black. If you were rash enough to ask why, he would tell you—half amused, half affronted—that he has no wish to be mistaken for a waiter. And you would go away properly rebuked, but you would leave behind, without knowing it, a riddle about clothing and rank, about sound and silence, and about the queer way a man can betray himself with nothing more than his feet.

Father Brown, if you met him—small, muddy, mild, and made for back lanes rather than Belgravia—and if you asked him what luckiest moment he remembered, might say, with an odd little seriousness, that the luckiest was at the Vernon Hotel, where he prevented a crime and perhaps saved a soul merely by listening to a few footsteps in a passage. He would not boast; he would almost apologize for being right. But the truth is that he was right in the manner of a man who tastes something faint in a sauce and thereby knows what meat was stolen from the pan.

The Vernon was one of those institutions London only produces when good manners have hardened into a kind of madness and commerce has learned to profit by refusal. It was “exclusive” in the literal sense: it paid by excluding. It stood in a corner of Belgravia like a small, inconvenient relic that had somehow become more precious because it would not oblige you. Its greatest inconvenience—therefore its greatest charm—was that only twenty-four could dine there at once, and the only grand table was the terrace table, set on a veranda open to the air and looking down into one of the old gardens that have survived the city’s incessant grinding. Even the weather became a doorkeeper: only warm nights made the terrace possible; and because the thing was difficult, the great folk desired it as they desire anything that requires diplomacy.

Mr. Lever, the owner, a Jew with a kindly eye and a financier’s nerve, made a fortune by making access hard and service perfect. There were fifteen waiters: not a swarm, but an army drilled into silence until their shoes seemed padded and their faces expressionless. They were trained like gentlemen’s servants, and the hotel itself had a soundscape so controlled that even the smallest clink of glass or brush of linen carried like a note in an empty church. Modern London was already full of the new industrial roar—carriages, hooters, the metallic chatter of progress—and the Vernon’s hush was bought like a rare wine. People paid to escape the city’s noise, and paid again to be assured that nothing uninvited would ever break the hush.

The Twelve True Fishermen would dine nowhere else, for their passion was privacy raised to ritual. They had ceremonies without history and symbols without purpose; the lack of meaning was itself a mark of class. They were twelve because twelve sounded complete. They were “fishermen” because it allowed them to own a set of fish knives and forks—exquisite silver fish, each loaded at the hilt with a single large pearl—that served as insignia. Those tools came out for the fish course as surely as vestments come out for a feast day, and the fish course was the true sacrament of the night.

How Father Brown came to be inside that polished, muffled palace is simple and, in a way, vulgar: the world’s oldest leveller had forced a crack in the wall. An Italian waiter had been struck down with a paralytic stroke that afternoon. Lever, who had little patience with superstition but some tenderness for pain (and, more importantly, a dislike of scenes), agreed to send for the nearest Catholic priest. Father Brown came, heard a confession, and—because what was confessed required some message to be made right—asked, with his meek impudence, for paper, ink, and a room.

Lever was torn. To admit one unfamiliar person on the Fishermen’s night was like a smear on a plate just polished for a duke. There were no drifters in the Vernon, no loiterers in its hall: fifteen waiters, twelve guests, and nothing else. Yet to turn away a priest from a sick-bed would have been to invite a different sort of trouble. At last he found a way to hide the blemish without removing it.

Down the short passage with a few important, rather dingy pictures, you came to a main vestibule and lounge. To the right lay the public rooms. To the left the passage ran towards kitchens and offices. At the left, immediately, was the corner of a glass office that jutted into the lounge like a little house inside the house; beyond it, on the servants’ side, was the gentlemen’s cloak room—the last boundary of the gentlemen’s domain. Between the office and the cloak room there was a small private room with no other outlet, used for delicate transactions: lending a duke a thousand pounds, refusing him sixpence, smoothing a scandal with a whisper. For half an hour, Lever allowed that room to be profaned by a muddy priest bent over paper.

Father Brown wrote. What he wrote is not for us; he kept confessions as other men keep heirlooms. But the thing was long, and the last pages were the least exciting, and as the light dimmed his mind began to drift. He had been penned into a room without a lamp. The evening darkened with a stormy purple, and, as often happens, the shrinking of sight sharpened the ear.

At first the sound that crept in seemed ordinary enough in a hotel: the patter of feet in the corridor beyond. Then the ordinariness itself grew eerie, because it was the only sound. In that curated silence a single bootstep became a statement. Father Brown found himself writing to its rhythm as men think to a train’s beat. When he noticed, he stopped and listened.

The footsteps had a pattern like a tune played wrong.

There came a long rush of rapid little steps, as if a light man were hurrying for the sheer pleasure of speed. Then, at a particular point, the pace broke and became a slow swinging stamp—fewer steps, but taking as much time, with a kind of lazy emphasis. When the last stamp died, the fast patter began again, a ripple, a run—then again the heavy, swaggering walk. It was, unmistakably, the same boots; there were no other footsteps in that corridor at all, and these had a small creak that returned like a refrain.

Father Brown’s finger traced the edge of the table as if he could finger out the melody. His mind, which could not help asking questions, began to ache with it. Men ran to jump; men ran to slide. But why run in order to walk? Why walk in order to run? The man seemed to hurry down half the corridor only to take the other half at a solemn, leisurely trudge, as if indulging two opposing appetites in turn. The sense of it refused to settle.

In the dimness, his thoughts grew vivid, almost visual. He imagined those unseen legs not merely walking but performing—capering in symbolic attitudes. Was it some strange exercise? Some private dance? Or—his mind always ready to admit the grotesque—some heathen ritual among the gilded furniture? But he forced himself away from fancies and into facts.

The slow step first. It was not the proprietor’s; men like Lever walked with a quick, anxious waddle, or else they sat. It was not a waiter’s; the staff had been trained into noiselessness, and besides waiters did not swagger. It was not a messenger hovering for orders; it had too much careless authority. That heavy, springy, unashamed tread belonged, in that world, to one creature: a gentleman of western Europe, and likely one who had never worked for his living.

As he reached that certainty, the step changed to the quick one and ran past the door as feverishly as a rat. It was swifter, but also softer, almost tiptoe; yet it did not feel like secrecy. It suggested something else—something he could not quite name. He was tormented by a half-memory, the way a forgotten ingredient nags at a food-historian who can taste its absence but cannot yet call it by its old dialect name. He had heard that strange swift walking somewhere. Where?

He sprang up with a new unease and went to the door. His little room had no direct opening on the corridor; one door led into the glass office, the other into the cloak room. He tried the office door; it was locked, as Lever had said it would be until he returned. Brown looked at the square windowpane: purple cloud, livid sunset like bruised fruit-skin. For an instant, without any argument, he smelt evil—as a dog smells rats behind boards.

Then the rational part of him returned. Lever had locked him in for reasons of his own; there were twenty explanations for odd footsteps—drunkenness, a wager, a forgotten errand. He told himself to finish his proper work. He carried his paper to the window to steal the last light and bent again to writing.

He wrote perhaps twenty minutes, leaning closer as the day withdrew. Then he sat upright.

The feet came again—but now there was a third note in the tune.

Before, the unknown man had walked—however oddly. This time he ran. The sound came along the corridor like soft bounding pads, swift, strong, controlled, like a panther fleeing without clatter. It rushed up to the office in a whispering whirlwind—and then, at the same point as always, it broke and became the old slow swaggering stamp.

Father Brown flung down his pen. Since the office door was locked, he went at once into the cloak room on the other side.

The cloak-room attendant was absent; the only guests were at dinner, and this corner of service had become for the moment a kind of shrine without a priest. Brown groped through a grey forest of overcoats that held the faint smells of rain, tobacco, old leather, and the last traces of cologne—scents like social signatures. The cloak room opened on the corridor by a half-door and counter, a semicircular arch like those behind which umbrellas are exchanged for tickets. A light above the arch threw little illumination on Brown himself—he stood as a dark outline against the sunset window behind him—but it cast a theatrical brightness on the man outside.

He was elegant, tall, and made for moving without obstruction; one felt he could slide like a shadow through crowded rooms. His face, tilted in the lamplight, was swarthy and quick, the face of a foreigner; his expression was good-humoured and confident. He wore plain evening dress that should have been unremarkable—except that the black coat seemed a shade below the man. It bulged and bagged in an odd way, as if it had been borrowed in haste or carried something it was not cut to carry.

The moment he saw Brown’s silhouette he tossed a scrap of paper with a number and called out with amiable authority, as if speaking to a servant he had always employed.

“I want my hat and coat, please; I find I have to go away at once.”

Father Brown took the paper without a word and, quite obediently, fetched the coat and laid it on the counter. Menial work did not offend him; he had done it for souls as well as bodies. The gentleman, feeling in his waistcoat pocket, laughed lightly.

“I haven’t got any silver; you can keep this.” He threw down half a sovereign and reached for the coat.

Brown stood still in the shadow. But something in him had snapped into clarity—the way a cook who has watched a hand hover over a dish knows, before the theft occurs, which spice will be pinched. In that instant he “lost his head,” as he himself would have called it: he stopped being cautious and started being accurate. His best moments were like that. The Church, sensible as it is, does not always approve of such leaps. Brown himself did not always approve of them. But it was the sort of inspiration that comes at rare crises, when losing your head saves it.

“I think, sir,” he said, very civilly, “that you have some silver in your pocket.”

The tall man stared.

“Hang it,” he cried, “if I choose to give you gold, why should you complain?”

“Because silver is sometimes more valuable than gold,” said the priest mildly; “that is, in large quantities.”

The stranger looked at him, then looked up the passage towards the main entrance, then back at Brown, then very carefully at the window beyond Brown’s head where the storm’s afterglow still stained the glass. One could almost see him weighing the room as if it were a map. Then he made up his mind.

He set one hand on the counter, vaulted over it with the ease of an acrobat, and towered above the little priest. His hand shot out and took Brown by the collar.

“Stand still,” he whispered harshly. “I don’t want to threaten you, but—”

“I do want to threaten you,” said Father Brown, and his voice changed. It rolled out low and loud, like a drumbeat heard through walls. “I want to threaten you with the worm that dieth not, and the fire that is not quenched.”

“You’re a rum sort of cloak-room clerk,” the man said, half dazed.

“I am a priest, Monsieur Flambeau,” said Brown, “and I am ready to hear your confession.”

For a moment the other stood as if the floor had moved under him. Then he staggered back and dropped into a chair, gasping.

While this was happening in the dim backwater of corridors and coats, the Twelve True Fishermen were dining in their bright, scented ritual as if the world were made only of sauces and titles.

The first two courses had passed with placid success. If I had the menu, it would still tell you little; it was written in that super-French that cooks use to impress Englishmen and that Frenchmen cannot always decipher. There was a tradition that the hors d’œuvres should be various and manifold to the point of madness. Little useless marvels arrived in obedient procession—pickled this, potted that, crisped, cured, rolled, and arranged like jewels; each bite was a small foreign country, each garnish a minor anthem. And because these trifles were openly unnecessary, they were taken as seriously as the club itself. Then came soup—light, unpretending, a kind of austere vigil before the fish feast, like a clear broth offered to the palate to make it remember its own capacity for delight. Even the spoons seemed to move in the hotel’s hush; the terrace garden beyond held the last colour of evening, and the air carried faintly the wet green smell of leaves after storm.

Their talk was the thin, confidential talk by which an empire is governed—so slight that overhearing it would not enlighten any ordinary Englishman. Cabinet ministers were mentioned by Christian names with bored affection; the Radical Chancellor of the Exchequer, hated in the newspapers, was praised for a scrap of poetry or for sitting a horse well; the Tory leader, accused of tyranny by the other party, was discussed and, on the whole, approved—as a Liberal. Politics, it seemed, mattered immensely; and yet everything mattered about politicians except what they believed.

Mr. Audley, the chairman, was an amiable elderly man in Gladstone collars, with a roll of grey hair over the back of his neck; seen from behind he looked like the Empire’s ideal. Seen from the front he looked like what he was: a mild, self-indulgent bachelor with rooms in the Albany who had never done anything—not even anything wrong. He was simply in the thing; that was enough. The Duke of Chester, the vice-president, was young, pleasant, freckled, with flat fair hair, moderate intelligence, and enormous estates. In public he was “rising” because he could make a joke and be called brilliant, or fail to make one and say it was no time for trifling and be called able. In private among his own he was frank and silly like a schoolboy. Audley, who had never been in politics, treated them more seriously and sometimes embarrassed the company by suggesting that Conservatives and Liberals might actually differ.

There were twenty-four seats at the terrace table and only twelve members; they took the inner side so that no one sat opposite, and all could look out over the garden uninterrupted, as if the green darkness were a tapestry hung for their pleasure. When they first sat down, it was the custom—no one knew why—that all fifteen waiters lined the wall like troops presenting arms while the fat proprietor bowed with radiant astonishment as though the club were a delightful surprise. Then, before the first knife chinked, the army vanished, leaving only one or two to dart about in disciplined silence. Lever disappeared too, as if courtesy had consumed him. But when the great course approached—the sacred fish—something like a shadow of him hovered near, a projection of his anxious pride.

The fish course arrived. To vulgar eyes it looked like a monstrous pudding, the size and shape of a wedding cake, in which many interesting fishes had surrendered the shapes God gave them. But to the Twelve True Fishermen it was magnificence itself, achieved by skill, expense, and the unspoken history of recipes traded like secrets. The air around it changed: steam rose with a deep marine richness, a mingling of butter and white wine, of shells and herbs, of something briny that pulled the mind to coasts and harbours. The club drew out the silver fish knives and forks with the pearls in their hilts and set to work in eager silence, as if carving a relic.

Only when his plate was nearly empty did the young duke make the ritual remark, with satisfaction thick in his voice.

“They can’t do this anywhere but here.”

“Nowhere,” Mr. Audley replied in his deep bass, nodding as if passing sentence. “Nowhere, assuredly, except here. It was represented to me that at the Café Anglais—”

At that moment his plate was removed, and the interruption agitated him as if a thought had been stolen. He recaptured it at once, gripping it with the obstinacy of his class.

“It was represented to me,” he repeated, “that the same could be done at the Café Anglais. Nothing like it, sir,” he said, shaking his head ruthlessly, like a hanging judge. “Nothing like it.”

“Overrated place,” said Colonel Pound, speaking—by the look of him—for the first time in months.

“Oh, I don’t know,” said the Duke of Chester, who was an optimist. “It’s jolly good for some things. You can’t beat it at—”

The last and loveliest theft Flambeau ever planned, he used to claim when age had made him pious and fat with good dinners, was a Christmas theft—an English theft—one of those domestic crimes that smell of holly and hot bread, that hide in plain sight among family jokes and the clink of cutlery.

He had always staged his wickedness as if it were theatre, choosing the right room as carefully as a chef chooses the right pan. The rich were not merely to be robbed; they were to be robbed with appropriateness. An oak-panelled dining-room demanded a certain old-fashioned swindle, slow as a roast and solemn as carving. A Paris café, all mirrors and electric glare, called for something brisk and glittering. England, if you must pinch a dean’s purse—and deans, like old cheeses, are hard to get at—required cathedral lawns and grey towers, as proper a frame as a silver platter. France, when you had somehow coaxed money from a peasant who clutched it like seed in spring, insisted on poplars clipped like knives and plains spread out like linen. He wanted his crimes to have a sauce.

This one was to have a plum-pudding sauce—sticky, cheerful, middle-class, almost Dickensian; and it was arranged in a comfortable house near Putney with a crescent drive and a stable to one side, a monkey tree in the garden, and a name on the gate that looked as if it had been painted to be admired by carol-singers. It is a pity, Flambeau would add, that he repented before the brandy butter had cooled.

Seen from outside, it began as innocently as feeding birds. On Boxing Day afternoon Ruby Adams came out with bread in her hands, and she looked like some small warm creature made of fur and purpose. Brown furs swallowed her figure; only her face showed clearly, bright with brown eyes that were brave in the way of young women who have been told they are protected. She broke the bread—white, soft, still giving off the yeasty sweetness that clings to fingers—and scattered it with a practised generosity. She had done it again and again all day, because the dog, a domestic tyrant, ate what the sparrows didn’t.

The winter light was turning red, as if the sun were a coal in a grate sinking into ash. The flowerbeds lay bare but seemed full of phantom roses, the way a kitchen in July seems full of vanished strawberries when the bowls are washed. To one side stood the stable; to the other ran a corridor of laurels, glossy as varnished leaves on a Christmas pudding, leading into the larger back garden. Ruby, having finished the birds, slipped down that laurel passage into a dim plantation of evergreens; and there, as if the season insisted on a little pantomime even before dinner, she stopped short and cried out.

On the high wall above her, straddling it as if it were a hobbyhorse, sat a figure too long and angular for comfort.

“Oh, don’t jump, Mr. Crook,” she called, startled. “It’s much too high.”

He did not look like the sort of man who should be balanced on walls—tall, thin, all elbows and corners, with dark hair bristling as if he had been rubbed the wrong way by life. His face had intelligence and even a kind of refinement; but his skin was sallow, almost foreign in tone, made more noticeable by the one piece of finery he seemed to care for: a bright red tie, aggressive as a flag. If it meant anything, it meant defiance.

He dropped lightly, recklessly, beside her, landing as a grasshopper lands, with no regard for bones.

“I think I was meant to be a burglar,” he said calmly, as if discussing the weather. “I’d have been one, I expect, if I hadn’t happened to be born in that nice house next door. I don’t see the harm.”

“How can you say such things?” Ruby protested, half scandalised, half amused, as one is when someone salts the porridge too freely and insists it improves it.

“Well,” he replied, “if you’re born on the wrong side of the wall, I can’t see why it’s wrong to climb over it.”

“I never know what you’ll do next.”

“I don’t often know myself,” he said, and his gaze flicked to her with a sudden, boyish warmth. “But I’m on the right side of the wall now.”

“And which is the right side?” she asked, smiling in spite of herself.

“Whichever side you’re on,” said John Crook.

They walked back under the laurels, and as they neared the front a motor horn sounded three times, the kind of clean, self-confident noise that cuts through a garden’s softer sounds as a knife cuts through pastry. The car that swept up the drive was pale green and expensive, moving with the smooth hunger of a bird of prey. It came to rest throbbing in the dusk.

“Hullo,” Crook said, with a twist of his mouth. “Somebody born on the right side, anyhow. I didn’t know, Miss Adams, that your Santa Claus travelled by motor.”

“That’s my godfather, Sir Leopold Fischer,” Ruby answered. “He always comes on Boxing Day.”

There was a pause, small but telling, like a gap in a family song when someone forgets a verse.

“He is very kind,” she added, as if supplying what courtesy required.

Crook was a journalist and had read plenty about Sir Leopold Fischer, the City magnate with a foreign name and an English appetite for influence. Sir Leopold, unsurprisingly, had not read about John Crook, though Crook had written with some severity of the financier’s dealings. He watched now as the great man was unloaded.

The chauffeur, large and neat in green, came around. A small neat manservant in grey emerged from the back. Between them they produced Sir Leopold as if unpacking a fragile purchase: rugs layered over him like dough over fruit; furs of animals whose deaths had been turned into status; scarves bright as confectioner’s ribbon. When the coverings were peeled away there appeared a friendly old gentleman with a grey goat-like beard and a beaming smile. He rubbed his big fur gloves together as if warming them at the idea of applause.

Before the last scarf had been unwound, Colonel Adams came out, tall, sunburnt, silent, with a red smoking-cap on his head that made him look like a soldier playing at being a Turkish pasha. With him stood James Blount, his brother-in-law newly arrived from Canada: a big boisterous farmer with a yellow beard and the hearty air of a man who believes noise is proof of life. And with them, unobtrusive as a servant who has learned to pass through rooms without disturbing conversations, was Father Brown from the neighbouring Roman church.

Nothing about Father Brown seemed remarkable—small, round, pale, his clothes ordinary, his name the plainest in the parish. Yet he had been asked, often, to the family table, perhaps because he listened as if he could hear what others missed: the slight change in voices, the hidden rhythm of a room. In a house like this, full of big surfaces and ordinary certainties, his quietness fell like flour on a noisy counter, calming it.

Inside the large entrance hall—too large, really, for the house, like a grand larder added to a modest kitchen—Sir Leopold’s wrappings were finally disposed of. A great fire glowed; above it hung the colonel’s sword, the old emblem of cutting and command. Sir Leopold, still fussing with his inner pockets as if the layers of cloth went on forever, suddenly produced a black oval case with a flourish.

“My Christmas present for my god-daughter,” he announced, and the pride in his voice was almost childlike. He pressed a catch and snapped it open.

Light jumped at them. It was like looking into a crystal fountain that spurted cold fire into the eyes. In a nest of orange velvet lay three diamonds, white and vivid, so bright they seemed to burn the air around them. Ruby gave a little cry, and her hands, which had fed birds with bread all day, hovered as if afraid to touch something that could cut.

“I’ll put them back now, my dear,” Sir Leopold said, with careful satisfaction, slipping the case back into the deep tail-coat pocket it had come from. “One must be careful travelling with them. They’re the three great African diamonds called ‘The Flying Stars’—because they’ve been stolen so often. All the big criminals are on the track. Even rough fellows in streets and hotels could hardly keep their hands off. I might have lost them on the road here. Quite possible.”

“Quite natural, I should say,” Crook growled. “When men ask for bread and you don’t even give them a stone, I don’t blame them if they take the stone for themselves.”

Ruby, flushed with excitement and indignation, rounded on him. “I won’t have you talking like that. You’ve only talked like that since you became—since you became one of those horrid what’s-his-name. You know what I mean. What do you call a man who wants to embrace the chimney-sweep?”

“A saint,” said Father Brown mildly.

Sir Leopold’s smile thinned. “I think Ruby means a Socialist.”

“A radical does not mean a man who lives on radishes,” Crook said sharply, as if he had said it too often, “and a Conservative does not mean a man who preserves jam. Nor, I assure you, does a Socialist mean a man who merely wants a social evening with the chimney-sweep. A Socialist means a man who wants all the chimneys swept, and all the chimney-sweeps paid.”

“But who won’t allow you,” Father Brown put in softly, “to own your own soot.”

Crook looked at him with sudden interest. “Does one want to own soot?”

“One might,” Father Brown said thoughtfully. “Gardeners use it. And I once made six children happy at Christmas when the conjuror didn’t come, entirely with soot—applied externally.”

Ruby clapped her hands. “Oh, splendid. Oh, I wish you’d do it to this company.”

The laughter was still rattling around the big hall when a knock sounded at the front doors. Father Brown opened them. Behind the messenger the garden stood in a violet sunset, the evergreens darkening, the monkey tree lifting its strange arms as if conducting the thin winter wind. The messenger himself was dusty and frayed, the kind of man who carries other people’s urgent words without owning any of his own.

“Any of you gentlemen Mr. Blount?” he asked, and held out a letter.

Blount’s face changed as he tore the envelope—clouding, then clearing. He turned to Colonel Adams.

“I’m sick at being such a nuisance,” he said in the cheerful colonial manner that apologises loudly, “but would it upset you if an old acquaintance called on me here tonight on business? It’s Florian—famous French acrobat and comic actor. I knew him years ago out West—French-Canadian by birth. He says he’s got business with me, though I don’t know what.”

“Of course,” the colonel said, indifferent in the way of men used to guests and interruptions. “Any friend of yours.”

“He’ll black his face, if that’s what you mean,” Blount cried, laughing. “And I wouldn’t put it past him to black everyone else’s eyes. I like a jolly pantomime—man sitting on his top hat.”

“Not on mine, please,” Sir Leopold said stiffly.

Crook shrugged. “There are lower jokes than sitting on a top hat.”

Sir Leopold, stung by dislike and the sight of Ruby’s easy intimacy with the red-tied young man, answered with icy sarcasm. “No doubt you have found something much lower. What is it?”

“Letting a top hat sit on you,” Crook said.

Blount threw up his hands. “Now, now—don’t spoil a jolly evening. Let’s do something—proper old English pantomime. Clown, columbine, harlequin, the whole business. I saw one when I left England at twelve and it’s blazed in my brain like a bonfire ever since. I come back and what do I find? Fairy plays, moral moonlight, Blue Birds. I want a hot poker and a policeman turned into sausages, and they give me princesses talking poetry at the furniture.”

“I’m all for making a policeman into sausages,” Crook said. “Better definition of Socialism than some. But the get-up would be a nuisance.”

“Not a scrap,” Blount shouted. “Harlequinade’s quickest. You can gag all you like, and all the props are household things—tables, towel-horses, washing baskets.”

Crook nodded, pacing. “But I suppose I can’t have a policeman’s uniform unless I kill one.”

Blount paused, then slapped his thigh. “Yes, we can. I’ve got Florian’s address. He knows every costumier in London. I’ll phone him to bring a police dress when he comes.” And off he went like a boy running for cake.

Ruby danced on her feet. “Oh, it’s glorious, godfather. I’ll be columbine and you shall be pantaloon.”

Sir Leopold stiffened, as if she had asked him to wear grease. “I think, my dear, you must get someone else.”

“I will be pantaloon, if you like,” Colonel Adams said, taking his cigar out, speaking as if it cost him something.

Blount returned, radiant. “There. We’re all fitted. Mr. Crook shall be clown—journalists know the oldest jokes. I’ll be harlequin; that only needs long legs and jumping. Florian says he’s bringing the police costume; he’ll change on the way. We’ll act it in this very hall. Audience on the stairs, one row above another. Front doors can be back scene—shut, an English interior; open, a moonlit garden. All goes by magic.”

He snatched a piece of billiard chalk and drew a line across the hall floor like a cook scoring pastry before it goes into heat—marking where the footlights ought to be.

How it all got ready so quickly was like the mystery of how a feast appears when a house is full of young people and someone has raided the pantry. They worked with reckless industry. The air began to smell of flour from the cook’s stores, and of rouge borrowed from some unseen servant who helped like an anonymous saint. The clown and pantaloon whitened their faces with flour as if dusted for baking. Ruby’s columbine skirt, charming and absurd, looked suspiciously like a lamp-shade repurposed with domestic genius. Blount wrapped himself in silver paper torn from cigar-box linings; in his enthusiasm he wanted to smash the old lustre chandeliers so he could glitter with their crystals, but Ruby saved the house by unearthing paste jewels from a former fancy-dress party when she had been the Queen of Diamonds. The very phrase felt like a spell in a room that contained real diamonds.

Blount, half out of hand, clapped a paper donkey’s head on Father Brown. Father Brown bore it gravely and even made the ears move in a private little trick that delighted the servants. Blount tried to attach a paper donkey’s tail to Sir Leopold’s coat-tails; that was stopped, firmly. Ruby, with sudden seriousness, draped a string of sausages around Crook’s shoulders like a grotesque garland.

“Uncle is too absurd,” she whispered to Crook. “Why is he so wild?”

“He is harlequin to your columbine,” Crook said, and though he smiled, his voice had a stab in it. “I’m only the clown who makes the old jokes.”

“I wish you were the harlequin,” she said, and left him with the sausages swinging like a promise that had turned into a joke.

Father Brown, though he had been behind the scenes and even turned a pillow into a pantomime baby to applause, went round and sat with the audience on the broad staircase. He watched with the solemn expectancy of a child waiting for pudding, as if nonsense itself were nourishing. Sir Leopold sat in front, his fur-collared bulk blocking much of Father Brown’s view; whether this harmed the artistic experience was never decided.

The performance was chaos, but it had the kind of rude vitality that comes when a room’s ordinary noises—footsteps, laughter, the hiss of the fire—are gathered into one big rhythm. Crook, as clown, became more than clown. He was author and prompter, stagehand and orchestra; at abrupt moments he would fling himself at the piano and hammer out tunes, popular and ridiculous, always exactly right, as if he could taste what the crowd wanted like seasoning on his tongue. It was as though he had caught sight of something on Ruby’s face—some fleeting expression—and it had intoxicated him into genius.

The climax came when the front doors flew open, showing the moonlit garden beyond, cool and silver as a fish’s belly, and framed in it the famous guest: Florian, dressed as a policeman. Crook banged out the chorus from “The Pirates of Penzance,” but it vanished under applause. Florian’s imitation of police dignity—every stiff turn, every solemn gesture—was so perfect it made laughter feel like relief. Blount, as harlequin, leapt and struck the policeman’s helmet; Crook answered with “Where did you get that hat?” The policeman wheeled with admirable surprise; Blount hit him again; Crook tossed in a bar of “Then we had another one.” Blount rushed into Florian and fell on him amid roaring.

Then Florian performed the famous imitation of a dead man. He went limp with such complete surrender that it was almost indecent. Blount swung him about like a sack, twisted him as if he were a prop, tossed him like an Indian club. Crook at the piano drove it all with lunatic aptness: when the policeman was heaved off the floor, “I arise from dreams of thee”; when dragged across a back, “With my bundle on my shoulder”; and when at last dropped with a convincing thud, a jingle that people afterwards swore included the words, “I sent a letter to my love and on the way I dropped it.”

It was around this height of domestic madness that Father Brown noticed something else: a disturbance in the room’s soundscape that did not belong to the play. Sir Leopold, in the front seat, rose suddenly. The great man’s breathing had changed; it had the strained, shallow rhythm of panic. His gloved hands plunged into his pockets again and again, as if searching for something that should have been as solid as a loaf and was now only air. He sat, fumbled, stood again. For a moment he looked as if he might march across the chalk line and stop the whole nonsense by sheer authority. Instead he shot a glare towards Crook at the piano, then turned and burst out of the room without a word.

Father Brown watched a few more minutes, but his attention had shifted; the rhythm of laughter no longer soothed him. He felt the harlequin’s dance receding into the moonlit garden, where Blount’s silver paper costume grew more magical in the real light than it had under indoor glare. The applause thickened like rain.

Then a hand touched Father Brown’s arm, and a voice whispered for him to come to the colonel’s study.

He followed, uncertain, into a scene both solemn and absurd. Colonel Adams sat dressed as pantaloon, with the knobbed whalebone above his brow; yet his old soldier’s face looked sorrowful enough to sober any feast. Sir Leopold Fischer leaned against the mantelpiece, heaving with offended importance, the way a man heaves when he has swallowed pride and found it choking.

“This is a painful matter, Father Brown,” the colonel said. “The truth is, those diamonds we saw this afternoon seem to have vanished from my friend’s tail-coat pocket. And as you—”

“As I,” Father Brown supplied with a broad grin, “was sitting just behind him—”

“Nothing of the sort shall be suggested,” Colonel Adams said sharply, with a look that implied Sir Leopold had suggested exactly that. “I ask you only to give the assistance any gentleman might give.”

“Which is turning out his pockets,” Father Brown said. And he did. From his own, in proof, he produced seven-and-sixpence, a return ticket, a small silver crucifix, a small breviary, and a stick of chocolate—plain, slightly soft from warmth, smelling faintly of cocoa as he held it up like evidence.

The colonel stared at him. “Do you know, I should like to see the inside of your head more than the inside of your pockets. My daughter is one of your people; she has lately—” He stopped, as if the words were too domestic and too dangerous.

“She has lately,” Sir Leopold broke out, “opened her father’s house to a cut-throat Socialist who says openly he would steal anything from a richer man. This is the end of it. Here is the richer man—and none the richer.”

“If you want the inside of my head you can have it,” Father Brown said, suddenly tired. “But the first thing I find in that disused pocket is this: men who mean to steal diamonds don’t talk Socialism. They are more likely,” he added gently, “to denounce it.”

Both men shifted. Father Brown continued, his voice quiet but firm.

“You see, we know these people, more or less. That Socialist would no more steal a diamond than a Pyramid. We ought to look at once to the one man we don’t know—the fellow acting the policeman. Florian. Where is he exactly at this minute, I wonder.”

Colonel Adams sprang up and strode out. A brief interlude followed in which Sir Leopold stared at Father Brown as if trying to translate him into a language of ledgers, while Father Brown looked down at his breviary, as if listening to something beneath the printed words: the faint hush beneath the house’s merriment, the odd stillness that had been in the room since the policeman fell.

The colonel returned with clipped gravity. “The policeman is still lying on the stage. The curtain has gone up and down six times. He is still lying there.”

Father Brown dropped the breviary. For a heartbeat his face went blank, as if his mind had been swept clean like a kitchen table before kneading. Then, very slowly, something lit behind his grey eyes.

“Please forgive me, colonel,” he said, and his tone changed—became suddenly urgent, almost boyish. “But when did your wife die?”

“Wife?” the colonel answered, startled. “She died this year—two months ago. Her brother James arrived a week too late to see her.”

Father Brown leapt like a rabbit shot. “Come on,” he cried, in quite unusual excitement. “Come on. We’ve got to go and look at that policeman.”

They rushed back to the hall, pushing past columbine and clown—who stood close together, whispering as if the world contained nothing but their own small plot—and Father Brown bent over the prostrate policeman.

He sniffed once, like a man testing a sauce gone wrong. Then he straightened.

“Chloroform,” he said. “I only guessed it just now.”

In the bluish fall of evening, where two steep streets in Camden Town met like clasped hands, the confectioner’s corner shone with a complicated warmth. Light, broken by mirrors, turned the window into a small theatre of colour: gilt paper, green and crimson foils, sugared violets, glazes that held the last of daylight as if it were syrup. The smell that leaked out whenever the door opened was not only sugar but hot fat from buns, the burnt-pleasant edge of coffee, and that faint, old-fashioned perfume of fruit peel and almond that belongs to wedding-cake.

Boys with pinched noses and shiny eyes pressed their faces to the glass for the sake of chocolates in metallic wrappers—those gaudy coats that are, to children, nearly better than the chocolate itself. Yet there was another watcher at the pane who had passed the age of pocket-money and still stared as if hunger were an argument.

He was tall and broad, red-haired, with a resolute face wearing, for some reason, the air of a man who has resolved and then forgotten what. Under his arm he carried a flat grey portfolio of black-and-white drawings—work he had peddled to publishers since an uncle, an admiral, had disinherited him for Socialism, the result of a lecture he had given against that theory. His name was John Turnbull Angus; and if he stared at cakes, he did not do it in the spirit of a gourmet so much as a man looking at a hearth.

At last he went in, letting the bell give its thin little chirp above the thicker domestic music of the place: a spoon ringing against a saucer, a knife tapping a plate, the soft hiss of steam. He passed the front shop—rainbow sweets, iced pastries, the great white wedding-cake like a snowdrift made edible—and entered the back room where one could sit and eat. He raised his hat to the young woman serving, a dark, alert girl dressed in black, with quick eyes that seemed to listen as much as look. After the customary interval she followed him to take his order.

His voice had the precision of habit. “I want, please, one halfpenny bun and a small cup of black coffee.”

She waited, pen poised.

“Also,” he added, as if ordering butter, “I want you to marry me.”

The girl went rigid in a way that made the room feel suddenly colder. “Those are jokes I don’t allow.”

Angus lifted grey eyes whose gravity did not match his loose manner. “Really and truly,” he said, “it’s as serious—as serious as the halfpenny bun. It is expensive, like the bun; one pays for it. It is indigestible, like the bun. It hurts.”

Her gaze stayed on him with almost painful exactness, as if she were tasting every syllable for poison. Then, with something like the shadow of a smile, she sat down.

Angus, who had already bitten into the bun, regarded it mournfully. “Don’t you think,” he observed, “that it’s rather cruel to eat these halfpenny buns? They might grow up into penny buns. I shall give up these brutal sports when we are married.”

She rose, went to the window, and stood there in hard thought. The street outside had the muffled rustle of evening—boots, wheels, distant hawkers—London’s layered hush. When she turned back with an air of decision, she found him doing something that made her stop short.

He was laying out, with the calm method of a man setting a table for a rite, a pyramid of coloured sweets, plates of sandwiches, and the two squat decanters of that strange pastry-cook port and sherry that always tastes as if it had been poured in a hurry and remembered later. In the middle, with absurd care, he had even set down the immense white sugared wedding-cake he had lifted from the window, as if relocating a small mountain.

“What on earth are you doing?” she demanded.

“Duty, my dear Laura,” he began.

“Oh, for the Lord’s sake, stop a minute,” she cried. “And don’t talk to me in that way. I mean, what is all that?”

“A ceremonial meal, Miss Hope.”

“And what is that?” She pointed at the white mass.

“The wedding-cake, Mrs. Angus,” he said.

Laura marched to it, removed it with a clatter, and returned it to its throne in the window. Then she came back, set her elegant elbows on the table, and looked at him not unkindly, but as if he were a man who insisted on stirring a pot while she was still choosing the recipe.

“You don’t give me any time to think,” she said.

“I’m not such a fool,” he answered; “that’s my Christian humility.”

The smile in her face dimmed into seriousness. “Mr. Angus,” she said steadily, “before there is a minute more of this nonsense I must tell you something about myself as shortly as I can.”

“Delighted,” said Angus gravely. “You might tell me something about myself, too, while you are about it.”

“Oh, do hold your tongue and listen.” She breathed in, as if preparing to plunge her hands into cold water. “It’s nothing that I’m ashamed of, and it isn’t even anything that I’m specially sorry about. But what would you say if there were something that is no business of mine and yet is my nightmare?”

“In that case,” said the man seriously, “I should suggest that you bring back the cake.”

“You must listen to the story first.” She spoke faster, but with that exactness that comes from repeating the tale privately to oneself until it becomes a kind of chant. “My father owned the inn called the Red Fish at Ludbury, and I used to serve people in the bar.”

“I have often wondered,” said Angus, “why there was a kind of a Christian air about this one confectioner’s shop.”

“Ludbury’s a sleepy, grassy little hole in the Eastern Counties,” she went on. “The only people who ever came to the Red Fish were occasional commercial travellers—and for the rest, the most awful people you can see, only you’ve never seen them. Little loungy men with just enough to live on and nothing to do but lean in bar-rooms and bet on horses, in bad clothes that were just too good for them. Even those young rotters weren’t common with us. But two of them were a lot too common—common in every sort of way.

“They lived on their own money and were wearisomely idle and over-dressed. But I was a bit sorry for them, because I half believe they slunk into our empty bar because each of them had a slight deformity—something yokels would laugh at. It wasn’t exactly a deformity; it was more an oddity.

“One was a surprisingly small man—like a dwarf, or a jockey. But he wasn’t jockeyish to look at. He had a round black head and a well-trimmed black beard, bright eyes like a bird’s; he jingled money in his pockets, jangled a great gold watch chain, and never turned up except dressed just too much like a gentleman to be one. He wasn’t a fool, though—curiously clever at all sorts of things that couldn’t possibly be any use. Conjuring tricks, sudden little fireworks of matches, making a dancing doll out of a banana skin—things you can do on a bar counter between two sips of ale. His name was Isidore Smythe; and I can see him still, coming up to the counter, turning five cigars into a jumping kangaroo.

“The other was more silent and ordinary, but he frightened me much more than poor little Smythe. Very tall and slight, light-haired, with a high-bridged nose—he might almost have been handsome in a spectral sort of way. But he had one of the most appalling squints I have ever seen. When he looked straight at you, you didn’t know where you were yourself, let alone what he was looking at. I fancy it embittered him. Smythe would show off anywhere; James Welkin—that was the squinting man—did nothing but soak in our bar-parlour and go on great walks by himself in the flat grey country. Still, I think Smythe was sensitive too about being so small; he only carried it off smartly.

“And then—” Her voice tightened. “I was really puzzled and sorry when they both offered to marry me in the same week.”

Angus’s spoon made a small click against the saucer; he was listening as if the room’s familiar noises had all shifted into the background.

“I did what I’ve since thought might have been silly,” Laura said. “But I had a horror of their thinking I refused them for the real reason—that they were impossibly ugly. So I made up gas about never marrying anyone who hadn’t carved his way in the world. I said it was a point of principle with me not to live on inherited money like theirs. Two days after that, the trouble began. I heard both of them had gone off to seek their fortunes, as if they were in some fairy tale.”

“You’ve never seen either since?” Angus asked.

“Never. But I’ve had two letters from Smythe, and they were rather exciting.”

“Ever heard of the other man?” Angus persisted, with a stubborn quietness.

“No,” she said after the smallest pause. “Welkin never wrote. Smythe’s first letter said he started walking with Welkin to London; but Welkin was such a walker that Smythe dropped out and rested by the road. A travelling show picked him up—partly because he’s nearly a dwarf and partly because he’s clever—and he did well in show business, got sent up to the Aquarium to do tricks I forget. That was the first letter. The second—” She swallowed. “The second I only got last week.”

Angus emptied his coffee and waited with mild patient eyes. Her mouth took that little twist that was half laughter and half dread.

“You must have seen the hoardings about ‘Smythe’s Silent Service,’” she said. “Unless you’re the only person in London who hasn’t. I don’t know much; it’s some clockwork invention for doing housework. You know: ‘Press a Button—A Butler who Never Drinks.’ ‘Turn a Handle—Ten Housemaids who Never Flirt.’ The machines are making pots of money, and all for that little imp I knew in Ludbury. I can’t help feeling pleased he’s fallen on his feet. But the plain fact is, I’m in terror of his turning up any minute and telling me he’s carved his way in the world—as he certainly has.”

“And the other man?” Angus repeated.

Laura got up so suddenly her chair grated like a warning. “My friend,” she said, “I think you are a witch. You are quite right. I’ve not seen a line of Welkin’s writing. I’ve no notion where he is. But it is of him that I’m frightened. He’s all about my path. He has half driven me mad. I think he has driven me mad; for I have felt him where he could not have been, and I have heard his voice when he could not have spoken.”

Angus’s tone softened into brisk kindness, as if lighting a fire. “If he were Satan himself, he’s done for now you’ve told somebody. One goes mad all alone, old girl. But when did you fancy you felt and heard our squinting friend?”

“I heard James Welkin laugh as plainly as I hear you speak,” she said steadily. “There was nobody there. I stood just outside this shop, at the corner, and could see down both streets at once. I’d forgotten how he laughed—his laugh was as odd as his squint. I hadn’t thought of him for nearly a year. But a few seconds later Smythe’s first letter came.”

Angus leaned forward. “Did you ever make the spectre speak or squeak?”

She shuddered once and then held herself firm, like a cook refusing to flinch from a hot pan. “Yes. When I finished reading Smythe’s second letter—announcing his success—I heard Welkin say, ‘He shan’t have you, though.’ Quite plain, as if he were in the room. It’s awful. I think I must be mad.”

“If you were really mad,” Angus said, “you’d think you must be sane. But there’s something rum about this unseen gentleman. Two heads are better than one—spare you allusions to other organs—and really, if you would allow me, as a sturdy practical man, to bring back the wedding-cake out of the window—”

A steely shriek tore across the street outside, the thin cry of tortured metal and speed. A small motor shot up to the door and checked there as if the kerb had caught it by the teeth. In the same instant a small man in a shiny top hat appeared stamping in the outer shop.

Angus, who had kept a joking ease as if it were medicine, strode out with sudden hardness. One glance gave his jealousy a name. The dapper dwarfish figure, the spike of black beard thrust forward, the clever restless eyes, the neat nervous fingers—this could be none other than Isidore Smythe. For a moment the two men looked at each other with that cold courtesy rivalry sometimes wears, as if both were too proud to scramble.

Smythe made no mention of Laura, but burst out, “Has Miss Hope seen that thing on the window?”

“On the window?” Angus echoed.

“There’s no time to explain other things,” Smythe snapped. “There’s some tomfoolery going on here that has to be investigated.”

He pointed with his polished stick at the front glass. Angus, following him out, saw what had not been there fifteen minutes before: a long strip of paper gummed along the window outside, like a crude frieze. On it, in straggly characters, was written:

If you marry Smythe, he will die.

Angus thrust his big red head back through the door. “Laura,” he said, “you’re not mad.”

“It’s Welkin’s writing,” said Smythe harshly. “I haven’t seen him for years, but he’s always bothering me. Five times in the last fortnight I’ve had threatening letters left at my flat, and I can’t find out who leaves them, let alone whether it is Welkin. The porter swears no suspicious characters have been seen—and here he’s pasted a warning on a public shop window while the people in the shop—”

“Quite so,” Angus said with restrained humility, “while the people in the shop were having tea. Mr. Smythe, I appreciate your common sense in dealing directly with it. We can talk about other things afterwards. The fellow can’t be far off; I swear there was no paper when I last looked in. But he’s too far off to chase, because we don’t even know the direction. If you’ll take my advice, put this at once in the hands of an energetic inquiry man, private rather than public. I know a very clever fellow—Flambeau—set up in business five minutes from here. His youth was a bit stormy, but he’s strictly honest now, and his brains are worth money. He lives in Lucknow Mansions, Hampstead.”

“That is odd,” Smythe said, eyebrows lifting. “I live myself in Himylaya Mansions, round the corner. Come with me. We’ll go to my rooms and sort out these queer Welkin documents while you run round and fetch your detective.”

“You are very good,” Angus said. “The sooner we act the better.”

With a queer impromptu fairness, both men took a formal farewell of Laura, as if they were two tradesmen agreeing to return about the same clock-time. They jumped into the brisk little car. As it swung the corner, Angus caught sight of a poster blaring “Smythe’s Silent Service,” showing a huge headless iron doll carrying a saucepan beneath the proud words: A COOK WHO IS NEVER CROSS.

“I use them in my own flat,” Smythe said with a laugh that had a metallic edge, “partly for advertisement, partly for convenience. Honestly, those big clockwork dolls do bring coals or claret or a timetable quicker than any live servants, if you know which knob to press. But I’ll never deny—between ourselves—that such servants have disadvantages too.”

“Indeed?” Angus said. “Is there something they can’t do?”

“Yes,” Smythe replied coolly. “They can’t tell me who left those threatening letters.”

The motor, small and swift like its owner, flew along the pale curving roads in the dead daylight of evening. London’s hill rose in white spirals and terraces, and then the curve tightened; they were climbing to that precipitous corner of the city where the streets feel stacked like shelves. Himylaya Mansions crowned the slope, gilt by a level sunset, sitting above a sea of slate roofs. Across the gravel crescent lay a bushy enclosure like a raised hedge, and below that a strip of artificial water, canal-like, a moat to the fortress of flats.

It was strangely empty up there, as if the height drained people away. Only at one corner stood a chestnut seller over his little stall, its pan giving off a homely roasted scent that fought bravely against the cold; and at the far end of the crescent a policeman walked slowly in dim blue. The few sounds—crackle of shells, the distant grind of wheels lower down the hill, the policeman’s measured tread—made a kind of careful silence, a city holding its breath.

Smythe stopped before the entrance. The tall commissionaire in braided splendour and a short porter in shirt sleeves were immediately pinned under Smythe’s sharp questions: had anything or anyone sought his apartments? Both swore that nobody and nothing had passed them since Smythe’s last inquiries. Then the lift shot Smythe and Angus upward like a launched thing until the top floor.

“Come in a minute,” Smythe said, breathless but brisk. “I want to show you those letters. Then run round and fetch your man.” He pressed a concealed button; the door opened of itself.

They entered a long ante-room whose most noticeable feature, ordinarily, would have been its rows of tall half-human mechanical figures standing on either side like dummies in a tailor’s shop. They were headless. Their shoulders were unnecessarily humped, their chests pigeon-breasted, and their hook-like arms were made for carrying trays. Painted pea-green, vermilion, or black for easy distinction, they were, in every other respect, as impersonal as any station machine that happens to stand upright.

Between these two lines of artificial servants lay something that made the room suddenly human again: a white tattered scrap of paper scrawled with red ink, dropped like a torn tongue. Smythe snatched it up the instant the door swung open and thrust it into Angus’s hand without a word.

The ink was not dry. The message read:

If you have been to see her today, I shall kill you.

For a moment the only noise was the faint inner ticking of mechanisms and the far-off murmur of London’s breath.

Then Smythe said quietly, “Would you like a little whiskey? I rather feel as if I should.”

“Thank you,” Angus answered, sombre. “I should like a little Flambeau. This is getting grave. I’m going now to fetch him.”

“Right you are,” said Smythe with admirable cheerfulness. “Bring him here as quick as you can.”

As Angus closed the door behind him, he saw Smythe press a button and one of the clockwork images glide out of its place and slide along a groove in the floor, bearing a tray with syphon and decanter. There was something unsettling in leaving the little man alone among dead servants that woke at the touch of a hidden switch, like kitchen implements coming to life without the consent of hands or hearth.

Six steps down from Smythe’s landing, the porter in shirt sleeves was busy with a pail. Angus stopped him and extracted a promise—fortified by a prospective bribe—that he would remain there until Angus returned with the detective, and keep count of any stranger coming up the stairs. In the hall below he laid similar charges on the commissionaire, learning in the process that there was no back door. Then he caught the drifting policeman and induced him to stand opposite the entrance and watch it.

Finally he paused at the chestnut stall and bought a pennyworth. The hot nuts burned his fingers through the paper, and the smell of their sweet smoke was oddly comforting—like an old rural winter dragged into this modern tower of lifts and buttons. He asked the vendor how long he meant to remain.

The chestnut man, turning up his collar, said he would likely move shortly, as he thought it was going to snow.

Angus, with all his earnestness, proceeded to nail him to his post.

“Keep yourself warm on your own chestnuts,” he said, and his voice had the urgency of a man seasoning a stew that might yet turn. “Eat up your whole stock; I’ll make it worth your while. I’ll give you a sovereign if you’ll wait here till I come back and then tell me whether any man, woman, or child has gone into that house where the commissionaire is standing.”

Then he walked away smartly, casting one last look at the besieged tower. “I’ve made a ring round that room, anyhow,” he muttered. “They can’t all four of them be Welkin’s accomplices.”

Lucknow Mansions lay lower on that steep slope, a more earthbound terrace beneath the peak of Himylaya. Flambeau’s semi-official flat was on the ground floor, and everything about it contradicted Smythe’s cold hotel-like luxury. It was a rococo den behind an office, crowded with sabres, harquebuses, Eastern curiosities, flasks of Italian wine, savage cooking-pots, a plumy Persian cat—and, looking particularly out of place amid all that swaggering bric-à-brac, a small dusty Roman Catholic priest.

“This is my friend Father Brown,” Flambeau said. “I’ve often wanted you to meet him. Splendid weather—little cold for Southerners like me.”

“Yes,” Angus said, sitting down on a violet-striped Eastern ottoman, “I think it will keep clear.”

“No,” said the priest quietly, as if he had been listening not only to them but to the world outside, “it has begun to snow.”

And as he spoke, the first flakes—foreseen by the chestnut man with his practical sky-wisdom—drifted across the darkening windowpane, softening London’s harsh edges into silence.

Evening came down the glen like a lid lowered on a pot: olive cloud and silver rain-smoke pressing the scents of wet peat and bruised pine into the throat. Father Brown, swaddled in a grey Scotch plaid that drank the mist, reached the last bend of the valley and saw Glengyle Castle stand blocking the hollow as neatly as a cork in a bottle. Its steep roofs and thin spires of sea-green slate rose in the old French-Scotch fashion—witch-hats against a strip of tarnished sunset—while the surrounding pines, heaved and bowed by wind, looked black enough to be a congregation of ravens.

There was something in that air beyond weather: a weight like old pride kept too long in a closed pantry. In Scotland, heredity had the double sourness of blood-boast and doom-belief; the noble house clung to its name as a miser clings to coins, and the Calvinist conscience clung to catastrophe as to a text. The Ogilvies of Glengyle had been terrible in the sixteenth century—valour and violence, cunning and crazed ambition—one of those families that had made their labyrinths of plot around Mary Stuart’s court. The countryside still repeated their motive and their profit in a rhyme as dry as oatcake:

As green sap to the simmer trees  
Is red gold to the Ogilvies.

If any era should have worn down eccentricity, it was the respectable Victorian age; yet the last Earl of Glengyle contrived the only scandal left: he vanished without leaving the place. Not abroad—if he was anywhere, he was in the castle. His name sat in the church register and in the fat red Peerage like meat in aspic, but no one saw him under the sun.

If anyone did, it was one man: Israel Gow, a gaunt red-haired labourer who was groom, gardener, cook, and—at the end—undertaker. So deaf that the practical took him for dumb and the sharper for half-witted, he moved about the estate with blank blue eyes and a chin like a shovel. Yet the regularity with which he went into the kitchen, and the fierce energy with which he dug potatoes as if they were treasure, gave the district the stubborn impression of meals being made for somebody hidden. When questioned, Gow would assert with maddening persistence that his master was “not at home,” as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world for an earl never to be at home in his own house.

Then one morning the provost and the minister—Glengyle had turned Presbyterian as if pride could outstare Puritanism—were summoned up. They found Gow had taken on one more trade, hammered nails, and shut the nobleman into a coffin. How much inquiry followed, and how little, was oddly foggy; the body—if it was the body—had lain some time in the small churchyard on the hill. Only now had the matter come under a proper legal glare, with Flambeau northward in advance and a Scotland Yard man with him.

Father Brown crossed the dim garden. Damp rose from the earth with the smell of rotted leaves and last summer’s herbs; rain had bruised the stalks and made the soil give underfoot like fresh dough. Against the green-gold stripe of sunset he saw a black silhouette: a man in a chimney-pot hat with a heavy spade over his shoulder. The hat made him look like a sexton; the spade made him look like what he was. The combination was absurd and yet wholly Scottish: respectability in “blacks” for official talk, and economy that would not waste daylight’s last edge for ceremony. The man started, stared suspiciously as the priest passed, and trudged on.

The great door opened, and Flambeau stood there, broad as ever, his black moustache dampened by the air. Beside him was a lean man with iron-grey hair and a sheaf of papers: Inspector Craven from Scotland Yard. The entrance hall was stripped, the emptiness smelling of cold stone and old smoke; above, pale faces in black periwigs glowered from darkening canvas—wicked Ogilvies, all sneer and shadow.

They led him into an inner room where a long oak table ran like a dark river. Their end was littered with scribbled papers, whisky, and cigars; along the rest, at intervals, lay odd scraps arranged as if for a village fair of nonsense. A glittering heap of broken glass; a high mound of brown dust; a plain stick of wood.

“You seem to have a sort of geological museum,” Father Brown remarked, nodding at the dust and the crystals.

“Not a geological museum,” Flambeau said. “A psychological one.”

“Oh, for heaven’s sake,” Craven broke in with a short laugh, “don’t let’s begin with long words.”

Flambeau’s eyes widened with friendly astonishment. “Don’t you know what psychology means? It means being off your chump.”

Still, the priest’s gaze had slid past their faces to the window, where the black silhouette with hat and spade crossed again, dim against the thickening sky. The wind pressed at the panes as if impatient to be let in.

“I can believe he was odd,” Father Brown said. “A man doesn’t bury himself alive—nor hurry to bury himself dead—without some twist in him. But why lunacy?”

Flambeau tapped the paper in Craven’s hand. “Listen to what he’s found loose in this house.”

Craven glanced at the window; the light was bleeding away. “We must get a candle. Storm’s coming, and it’s too dark to read.”

“Have you found any candles,” Father Brown asked mildly, “among your oddities?”

Flambeau’s face sobered. “Twenty-five candles. And not a trace of a candlestick.”

In the darkening room the wind found a gap and came through with the smell of rain and pine-resin, making the old boards complain and the curtains stir like restless cloth. Father Brown walked down the table, lifted a candle from the bundle, and bent inadvertently over the brown mound. The dust rose sharp and dry into his nose.

“Hullo,” he said, and sneezed—one clean crack in the hush. “Snuff.”

He struck a match, cupped it, and lit the candle carefully. The flame stretched long and unsteady under the draught, tasting the air like a tongue. He returned and set the candle in the neck of a whisky bottle; the wick wavered like a banner in a gale. Outside, the miles of pine woods seethed and sighed, a black sea around a rock.

Craven began reading from the inventory, his voice made grave by the weather.

“You are to understand the place is dismantled and neglected. But one or two rooms have been inhabited—simple, not squalid—by somebody. Not Gow. The list is as follows.

“First. A very considerable hoard of precious stones—nearly all diamonds—loose, without any setting. Family jewels, yes; but family jewels are almost always set in ornaments. These are like pocket change, carried as if they were copper coins.

“Second. Heaps and heaps of loose snuff. Not kept in a horn or pouch. Just lying in mounds on mantels, sideboards, even on the piano—anywhere. As if the old gentleman couldn’t be bothered with lids or pockets.

“Third. Here and there about the house, curious little heaps of minute bits of metal—some like steel springs, some microscopic wheels—as if they’d gutted a mechanical toy.

“Fourth. The wax candles. With nothing to stick them in but bottle necks. Now note how much queerer all this is than anything we expected. The central riddle—we can imagine it: a hidden earl, a servant who buries him, murder, substitution, any melodrama you like. But even then you haven’t explained a candle without a candlestick, or why an elderly gentleman spills snuff on a piano. By no stretch can you connect snuff and diamonds and wax and loose clockwork.”

“I can connect them,” Father Brown said.

Both men looked at him as if he had begun speaking Gaelic.

“This Glengyle,” the priest went on, “was mad against the French Revolution. He adored the ancien régime and tried to live like the last Bourbons. Snuff because it’s eighteenth-century luxury; wax candles because it’s eighteenth-century light; the metal bits because Louis XVI had a hobby of locksmithing; the diamonds because of Marie Antoinette’s necklace.”

Flambeau stared. “That’s an extraordinary notion. Do you really think it’s true?”

“I’m perfectly sure it isn’t,” Father Brown replied. “I only meant—when you said nobody could connect them—I could. The real truth lies deeper.”

He listened a moment. The wind threaded the turrets and made a voice in the stone.

“The late Earl of Glengyle,” he said, “was a thief. A desperate housebreaker. No candlesticks because he cut these candles short and used them in the little lantern he carried. The snuff—like pepper in the hands of French criminals—thrown in dense masses into a pursuer’s face. And the final proof is the coincidence of diamonds and those tiny steel wheels. Diamonds and small steel wheels are the two things you use to cut out a pane of glass.”

A pine bough, torn loose, lashed the window behind them with a heavy slap, like a burglar’s parody. None of them turned; their eyes held to the priest.

“Diamonds and small wheels,” Craven repeated slowly. “Is that all that makes you think it’s the true explanation?”

“I don’t think it’s true,” Father Brown said. “But again—you said nobody could connect the four. The true tale is probably humdrum. Glengyle found, or thought he found, stones on his estate. Somebody tricked him with those loose brilliants—said they were found in castle caverns. The little wheels are some rough diamond-cutting contrivance. He had to do it small and crude, with shepherds or hill fellows. Snuff is the great luxury of such men; it’s what you bribe them with. No candlesticks because they didn’t want them; they carried candles by hand to explore.”

Flambeau waited, as if hoping the priest had finished. “Is that all? Are we at the dull truth at last?”

“Oh, no,” Father Brown said.

The wind fell, far off, in a long hoot like mockery. Father Brown’s face remained blank, but his voice had tightened.

“I only offered those because ten false philosophies will fit the universe; ten false theories will fit Glengyle Castle. We want the real explanation. Are there no other exhibits?”

Craven laughed. Flambeau rose and walked down the table, his boots thudding softly on old boards.

“Items five, six, seven,” he said. “More varied than instructive. A curious collection—of lead, not pencils: the lead taken out of pencils. A senseless stick of bamboo with the top splintered—could be a weapon, only there’s no crime. And a few old missals and little Catholic pictures. The Ogilvies kept them, I suppose, from medieval times—family pride stronger than their Puritanism. We put them in the museum because they seem oddly cut about and defaced.”

Father Brown picked up the little illuminated pages. The candlelight, shaken by the draught, made the gold leaf flare and drown, flare and drown. Ink and vellum gave off that dry, animal smell of old books, like leather warmed at the hearth. He turned one picture, then another, and spoke before the darkness-shadows had finished crossing the room. It was the voice of a different man—quick, young, urgent.

“Mr. Craven,” he said, “you have a legal warrant to examine that grave. The sooner we do it the better. If I were you, I’d start now.”

“Now?” Craven echoed. “And why now?”

“Because this is serious,” Father Brown answered. “Not spilled snuff or loose pebbles, that can happen a hundred ways. There is only one reason I know for this kind of thing, and it goes down to the roots of the world. These pictures weren’t dirtied or torn in idle spite. They were treated carefully—queerly. Every place where the great ornamented Name of God appears has been elaborately cut out. And the only other thing removed is the halo round the Child Jesus. Therefore—get your warrant and your spade and your hatchet. We go up and break open that coffin.”

“What do you mean?” Craven demanded, the London neatness in him recoiling from the phrase.

“I mean,” said Father Brown, and the gale rose in answer as if the castle had inhaled, “that the great devil of the universe may be sitting on the top tower of this castle at this moment, big as a hundred elephants, roaring like the Apocalypse. There is black magic somewhere at the bottom of this.”

“Black magic,” Flambeau repeated under his breath; he was too modern to scoff and too experienced to smile. “But what can the snuff and the wax and the rest mean?”

“Oh, something damnable,” Father Brown snapped, suddenly impatient. “How should I know? Perhaps you can torture with snuff and bamboo. Perhaps lunatics lust after wax and steel filings. Perhaps there is a maddening drug in pencil lead. Our shortest cut is up the hill to the grave.”

They hardly knew they had moved until the garden wind nearly flung them on their faces. Yet they obeyed him like men walking in their sleep: Craven found a hatchet in his hand and the warrant in his pocket; Flambeau bore the heavy spade of the strange gardener; Father Brown carried the little gilded book from which the Name had been cut as delicately as a heart cut from pastry.

The path to the churchyard was crooked but short; under that wind it became a labour like climbing through water. As they mounted, the forest spread and spread—a grey-blue infinity of pines pressed all one way under the gusts, as if the whole land were bowing to an unseen power. The sound was the worst of it: a continuous, many-throated moan, each tree answering the next, a tide of needles hissing, trunks groaning, branches snapping like bones. It was an acoustic wilderness, a music without human measure—like an orchestra that has forgotten the song and continues only the roar.

Through that vast noise ran an older note, shrill and high, as if some ancient sorrow had found a flute in the storm. One could fancy the underworld of foliage was full of lost pagan gods crying because they could not remember their own names—driven from their altars, wandering in a language no one speaks, unable to translate their hunger into any prayer that heaven would recognize.

“You see,” Father Brown said quietly, as if talking at a kitchen table, “Scotch people before Scotland existed were a curious lot. In prehistoric times, I fancy they really worshipped demons.”

He added, almost genially, “That’s why they jumped at Puritan theology. When a man has lived on harsh fare a long while, he’ll take harsher as a comfort—if it is at least familiar.”

Flambeau swung round on him, sudden anger breaking through his dread. “What does that snuff mean?”

“There is one mark of all genuine religions,” Father Brown said with equal seriousness. “Materialism. Devil-worship is a perfectly genuine religion.”

They reached the bald scalp of the hill, one of the few clearings above the roaring pines. A mean enclosure of timber and wire rattled like cutlery in a tray, marking the graveyard border. At the foot of the grave tall thistles stood grey and silver in decay; their down broke and flew past, and Craven startled at it as if it were a dart.

Flambeau drove the spade into the wet clay. It made a sucking sound, like a spoon pulled from porridge. He leaned on it, huge shoulders rising and falling.

“Go on,” Father Brown said very gently. “We’re only trying to find the truth. What are you afraid of?”

“I’m afraid of finding it,” Flambeau said.

Craven forced his voice into a bright, crowing cheeriness. “I wonder why he hid himself like that. Something nasty. Was he a leper?”

“Something worse,” Flambeau answered.

“And what,” Craven asked, still too loudly, “could be worse than a leper?”

“I don’t imagine it,” Flambeau said.

He dug in silence for dreadful minutes. The storm shouldered aside its own clouds and let thin starlight fall—cold and pale—over the hill. At last the rude timber coffin showed in the torn earth, and Flambeau tipped it up upon the turf. Craven stepped forward with the axe. A thistle brushed him; he flinched, then set his jaw and hacked, wrenched, tore the lid away with an energy that was almost hatred.

In the faint starlight, what lay within glimmered grey and damp.

“Bones,” Craven said. Then, after bending close, “but it is a man”—as if that, at least, had been uncertain.

Flambeau’s voice went oddly up and down, like a tune trying to find its key. “Is he— is he all right?”

“Seems so,” Craven said hoarsely. He leaned further in, and his next words came out as if dragged. “Wait.”

A vast heave went through Flambeau. “And now I come to think of it,” he burst out, “why in the name of madness shouldn’t he be all right? What is it that gets hold of men on these cursed cold mountains? It’s the black, brainless repetition—forest and more forest—this sound, this endless sound, like the dream of an atheist: pine-trees and more pine-trees and millions more pine-trees—”

“God!” Craven cried suddenly, straightening as if struck. “He hasn’t got a head.”

For an instant they were rigid. Father Brown, who had spoken of black magic with an almost careless certitude, now showed, for the first time, a leap of startled concern.

“No head?” he repeated. “No head?”—as if he had almost expected any other horror rather than that one.

In a flash, senseless images chased one another through their minds: a headless baby born into the house; a headless youth hiding in empty rooms; a headless man pacing corridors and gardens. Yet none of those fantasies could root themselves; they were like words in a tongue no one understands. The storm shrieked overhead; the woods roared below; their thoughts, huge a moment ago, slipped away like something dropped into deep water.

“There are three headless men,” Father Brown said, “standing round this open grave.”

Craven’s mouth opened and stayed open, the way a yokel stares at a conjuring trick. He glanced down at the axe in his hands as if it belonged to someone else, and let it fall.

“Father,” Flambeau said in the thick, childlike voice he used very seldom, “what are we to do?”

“Sleep!” Father Brown cried. The word cracked like a command. “Sleep. We have come to the end of the ways. Do you know what sleep is? Every man who sleeps believes in God. It is a sacrament—an act of faith and a food. And we need a sacrament, if only a natural one. Something has fallen on us that falls very seldom on men—perhaps the worst thing.”

Craven managed to bring his lips together. “What do you mean?”

Father Brown had turned his face toward the castle; his eyes looked as if they were listening rather than seeing.

“We have found the truth,” he said. “And the truth makes no sense.”

He went down the path before them with a plunging, reckless step that was rare in him. Back in the castle he threw himself into sleep with the simple surrender of a dog.

Yet at daybreak Father Brown was up earlier than anyone except the silent gardener. The storm had ended in roaring rains, and the morning smelled washed and raw—wet stone, wet leaf, the clean iron tang of water on soil. Father Brown stood in the kitchen garden smoking a large pipe, watching Israel Gow at work. Gow’s spade went in and out with a steady rhythm, a kind of mute music against the birds and dripping hedges. The day’s acoustic world, rinsed by rain, felt briefly sane.

Gow seemed even to have been conversing with someone—muttering, perhaps, to himself—but at sight of the detectives he planted his spade sullenly and, saying something about his breakfast, shuffled along the cabbage lines and shut himself into the kitchen. Through the door came a faint domestic clatter—an ordinary sound that ought to have reassured them, and did not.

“He’s a valuable man,” Father Brown said. “He does the potatoes amazingly.” He drew on the pipe, and the tobacco-smoke mingled with the garden damp like a darker herb. “Still,” he added with impartial charity, “he has his faults; which of us hasn’t? He doesn’t dig this bank quite regularly.”

He stepped to a patch of turned earth and stamped on it with sudden decision. “There, for instance. I’m really very doubtful about that potato.”

“And why?” Craven asked, amused in spite of himself at the little man’s sudden agricultural interest.

“I’m doubtful about it,” Father Brown said, “because old Gow was doubtful about it himself. He put his spade in methodically everywhere but just here. There must be a mighty fine potato just here.”

Flambeau seized the spade with a sudden impatience, drove it in, and levered up the heavy, wet soil. What rolled out under the clod was not a potato. It was like a monstrous, over-domed mushroom at first glance—then it struck the spade with a cold click, turned, and grinned up at them with its toothless smile.

“The Earl of Glengyle,” Father Brown said sadly, looking down at the skull.

The north roads out of London do not end so much as they fray; they turn into a long, thinning ribbon of respectability—shops, then a paddock with a smell of wet earth, then an inn, then a nursery garden where the air tastes of bruised leaves—until suburb and country overlap like two voices heard at once.

Along one of those roads stood a house that made passers-by slow their step without knowing why. It lay low and long beside the street, painted white and a pale medicinal green, with a veranda and sun-blinds that suggested noon heat even under English cloud. Over the porches were those quaint cupolas like wooden umbrellas, as if the place had been designed to keep a tropical glare from striking the eyes. It was, for all its English comfort, built with a foreign reflex—a hint of an Anglo-Indian dream translated into Clapham money.

On the Thursday before Whit-Sunday, at half-past four, the front door opened, and the house revealed its trick. One could see straight through the entrance hall down the “tail” of the building—because the whole thing was built like a T, a long cross-bar along the road and a short stem running back. The stem held two rooms in a line: first a study; beyond it a glass conservatory blazing with flowers so rich they looked invented. When the door stood open, the view was like a corridor cut through domesticity into a scene of purple cloud and crimson star, all transparent and unreal in the sunlight; and the air that drifted outward carried not only polish and paint, but the warmed sweetness of sap and some faint, stale bitterness like crushed poppy.

Father Brown stepped out first, small and sober in his black, a large pipe in his mouth as if it were a homely tool. Beside him came Flambeau, very tall, elegant even in repose, with a very small cigarette. They paused, not because the garden invited them, but because the house behind them pressed on the senses as a spice presses on the tongue—too much, too layered, too deliberately arranged.

Leonard Quinton, who owned the place, had arranged it all with a poet’s impatience for plain form. He lived for colour as some men live for wine: saturations, contrasts, violent harmonies. His books overflowed with Eastern splendours—mitred heroes on purple elephants, jewels too huge to be true, skies like beaten copper and blood. The art in his house had the same fever: carpets where the patterns curled as if they wished to be snakes; bronzes, lacquer, shards of strange beauty. But the morbidity that made such imagery was more visible in his habits than in his verse: opium, languor, and a household strained thin by his indulgences.

His wife held it together. She was handsome in a hard-working way—square-faced, fair-haired, strong in the jaw as if she had learned to carry other people’s weakness like a pail. What she objected to was not merely the drug. It was the “holy man,” the Indian in white and yellow, whom Quinton kept as if he were a private key to an Eastern heaven and hell.

They had scarcely taken two steps from the threshold when the garden gate clattered and flew open, and a young man lurched in as if the noise of his own urgency drove him. He wore a billycock pushed back on his head, a vivid red necktie twisted as though he had slept in it, and he snapped and worried at the air with a little jointed cane. His face had the half-bright vacancy of dissipation.

“I say,” he panted, “I must see old Quinton. Has he gone?”

“Mr. Quinton is in,” Father Brown said, taking his pipe from his mouth and cleaning it with slow care, “but I don’t know if you can see him. The doctor is with him at present.”

The youth—Atkinson—stumbled into the hall; and at that moment Dr. Harris emerged from the study, shutting the door behind him and tugging on gloves. He was bull-necked, ordinary to the point of invisibility, the sort of man whose competence was felt rather than noticed.

“See Mr. Quinton?” he said, briskly amused. “No, you can’t. In fact, you mustn’t. Nobody must. I’ve just given him his sleeping draught.”

“No, but look here, old chap—” Atkinson tried to grab him by the lapels with a drunken affection that was mostly greed.

“It’s no good, Mr. Atkinson,” Harris said, pushing him back with practised impatience. “When you can alter the effects of a drug I’ll alter my decision.”

He settled his hat and stepped into the light with Flambeau and Father Brown, leaving Atkinson on the doorstep blinking like someone abruptly turned out of a warm kitchen.

“That was a sound lie,” Harris said, with a laugh that was good-tempered in the way a slammed door is good-tempered. “He doesn’t have it for half an hour. But I’m not having him bothered by that little beast. He only wants money—and he’s Mrs. Quinton’s brother, curse him. She’s as fine a woman as ever walked.”

“Yes,” Father Brown said. “She’s a good woman.”

“So I’ll hang about the garden till he clears off,” Harris went on. “Then I’ll go in with the medicine. He can’t get in; I locked the door.”

“In that case,” Flambeau said, “we might as well go round the back, by the conservatory end. There’s no entrance there, but it’s worth seeing, even from outside.”

“And I might get a squint at my patient,” Harris said. “He likes to lie at the far end among those blood-red poinsettias. It gives me the creeps.” Then he broke off. “What are you doing?”

Father Brown had paused in the long grass. Almost hidden there lay a dagger: Oriental, crooked, inlaid with minute stones and metals that caught the sun in little flashes of green and garnet. It was exquisitely made—like a chef’s knife made for show rather than work, beautiful in a way that made the stomach uneasy. Brown held it as one might hold a fish with unfamiliar spines.

“What is this?” he asked, with a faint distaste.

Flambeau’s holiday never looked like a holiday until it was seen from the water.

He had borrowed a sailing-boat so small that it spent most of its life submitting to the oars, and he had chosen rivers in the Eastern counties so narrow that a man might lean from the gunwale and brush the meadow-sweet. The craft slipped through Norfolk as though it had mistaken the earth for the sea: past cornfields that nodded like slow gold fires, past gardens hung over with currant-bushes and the fat scent of roses, past villages whose roofs stooped down to the water as if to drink.

The boat held two men and the bare necessities—according to Flambeau’s private and practical theology of necessity. There were tins of salmon (for hunger), loaded revolvers (for argument), a bottle of brandy (for collapse), and a priest (for conclusion). Father Brown, who was all cloak and quietness, sat as if he were part of the boat’s ballast, only his pale, attentive face rising like a little lantern above the dark water.

Flambeau, who had once been the most flamboyant thief in Paris and now was the most flamboyant respectable man in Westminster, had no fixed aim; but he carried one excuse, which is what philosophers call an aim when they want it to look light.

Years ago he had received letters of abuse and adoration; one had stuck like a burr in memory, because it was so brief and so odd. It had been only a visiting-card in an envelope with an English postmark. On the back, in French and in green ink, was written:

If you ever retire and become respectable, come and see me. I want to meet you, for I have met all the other great men of my time. That trick of yours of getting one detective to arrest the other was the most splendid scene in French history.

On the front was engraved, with the stiff calm of good breeding: Prince Saradine, Reed House, Reed Island, Norfolk.

Flambeau had learned the usual rumours: a prince in southern Italy; a fashionable scandal; an elopement with a married lady; an offended husband who, in Sicily, had allegedly flung himself over a precipice. Then Vienna; then restless travel; then a sort of vanishing into England. And now, as his own life had strangely cooled into respectability, the old invitation had grown warm again. A surprise visit to an eminent exile in the Norfolk Broads—why not? It would crown the holiday if it succeeded; it would not spoil it if it failed.

It succeeded almost too easily.

They had moored one night under a bank thick with grasses and pollarded trees. The hard pull of the oars had pressed sleep upon them like a hand. They woke early—earlier than daylight, when the sky was a bright nocturnal violet, and a huge lemon moon, low and full, was sinking into the forest of grass above their heads.

For a moment both men had the same childhood illusion: that tall weeds are woods, that daisies are trees, that the world is secretly larger than grown people say. From the drop of the river-bed they looked up as from a trench; the dandelions towered, the seed-heads were pale planets.

“By Jove,” Flambeau said, rising in the rocking boat as if he would step straight into that moon, “it’s like being in fairyland.”

Father Brown sat bolt upright and crossed himself with a jerk so sudden that Flambeau stared.

“The people who wrote the mediaeval ballads,” the priest said, “knew more about fairies than you do. It isn’t only nice things that happen in fairyland.”

“Oh, bosh!” Flambeau cried. “Only nice things could happen under such an innocent moon. I am for pushing on now and seeing what does really come. We may die and rot before we ever see again such a moon or such a mood.”

“All right,” said Father Brown. “I never said it was always wrong to enter fairyland. I only said it was always dangerous.”

They pushed up the river. The violet faded into that washed-out, colourless hour before dawn, when even birds hold their tongues and the world listens to itself. Then, as stripes of red, gold, and cold grey split the horizon, the lines were broken by the black bulk of a riverside town ahead.

Under hanging roofs and low bridges they drifted into a hamlet that seemed not asleep but hushed, as if it were holding its breath. The long, low houses leaned towards the river like cattle at a trough. Daylight broadened and turned practical—workmanlike—before any creature moved on wharf or bridge. At last they saw a placid, prosperous man in his shirt sleeves, with a face as round as the moon that had just gone down and a halo of red whisker.

Flambeau, by some impulse that was half boyish and half imperious, stood in the swaying boat and shouted, “Do you know Reed Island? Reed House?”

The man’s smile widened as if he had been asked something pleasantly simple. He pointed without words up the river toward the next bend.

Flambeau nodded and went on. Father Brown watched the pointing hand as if it had been a sign in a language he half understood.

The river curled through meadow after meadow, reed after reed, with a silence that was never empty because water is never mute; it speaks in a low continuous syllable against mud and root. Before the search could become tedious, they swung round a sharp corner and entered a wider pool that stopped them as a picture stops a man.

In the middle lay a long, low island, fringed with rushes. Along the island ran a long, low house built of bamboo or some tough tropical cane. The wall-rods were pale yellow; the roof-rods were darker, a red-brown like old spice. Repetition made it eerie: slat on slat, rib on rib, as if the house had been woven rather than built. The morning breeze rustled the reeds around the island and sang through the cane as through a giant pan-pipe; it was music made from dry hollowness.

“By George!” Flambeau cried. “Here is the place, after all! Here is Reed Island, if ever there was one. Here is Reed House, if it is anywhere. I believe that fat man with whiskers was a fairy.”

“Perhaps,” Father Brown said, without heat. “If he was, he was a bad fairy.”

But Flambeau had already driven the boat into the rattling reeds. They stepped ashore, their boots sinking into the spongy earth that smelt of wet roots and last year’s stalks.

The house turned its back to the landing-stage and river; its front looked down the length of the island garden. To reach the front they followed a narrow path that ran round three sides of the building, under low eaves that almost brushed their hats. Through three windows on three sides they looked into the same long, well-lit room, panelled in light wood and laid for an elegant lunch. What struck even Flambeau—who did not often notice furniture unless it could be sold—was the odd alternation of windows with looking-glasses: long, low panes of daylight, then long, low oblongs of reflection. The whole room looked as if it were lunching out of doors, as if the walls were only a polite mistake.

At the front door, flanked by two flower-pots of fierce turquoise-blue, they were received by a butler of the dreariest type—long, lean, grey, listless—who murmured that Prince Saradine was from home but expected hourly; the house was kept ready for him and his guests. When Flambeau produced the card with its green-ink scrawl, the butler’s parchment face twitched into something like a memory of courtesy.

“His Highness may be here any minute,” he said. “He would be distressed to have just missed any gentleman he had invited. We have orders always to keep a little cold lunch for him and his friends, and I am sure he would wish it to be offered.”

Curiosity is the only appetite that grows by fasting, and Flambeau’s had been fasting a long while. He assented with the air of a man accepting what is owed to him by the universe, and followed the old servant into the long room of light wood and glass.

The lunch was indeed cold, but not careless. There were thin slices of meat set in neat fans, salads that had been mixed with a hand that respected crispness, and fish that suggested careful transport from some distant market. Flambeau opened a tin of salmon from his own stores in a spirit of comparison, then laughed and kept it for later; even his philosophy could be shamed. The coffee that followed was exquisite, dark and fragrant, and the liqueurs smelt of bitter orange peel and hidden herbs. Father Brown, who ate little but tasted much, noted the balance of sweet and sting, the way the bitterness clung like a whispered warning.

Afterwards they were shown the garden, the library, and the housekeeper.

Mrs. Anthony was a dark, handsome woman with a certain majesty, like a Madonna carved out of volcanic stone. Her English held a slight Italian accent, and Flambeau’s ear, trained by Europe, suspected that “Anthony” was a Norfolk mask for some more Latin name. Mr. Paul, the butler, had also a faintly foreign air; yet his tongue and training were English, as they often are in those polished servants who serve cosmopolitan masters.

Pretty as the place was, it had a curious luminous sadness. The rooms were full of daylight—too full. It was a dead daylight, the sort that makes time seem thick. Hours passed like days. Through talk, through the clink of glass, through the soft footfall of servants, there was always the melancholy noise of the river all round the house: a ceaseless, sucking, whispering sound against reeds and mud, like a language one cannot quite translate.

“We have taken a wrong turning, and come to a wrong place,” Father Brown said, looking out at grey-green sedges and the silver flood. “Never mind; one can sometimes do good by being the right person in the wrong place.”

Father Brown was usually silent, but his silence was of that friendly kind which draws speech out of others as warmth draws scent from food. In those few endless hours he sank, without seeming to try, into the secrets of Reed House. Mr. Paul remained uncommunicative; yet even his reserve had a shape. He had a sullen, almost animal affection for his master, and a venomous contempt for his master’s brother.

Captain Stephen Saradine—so the butler said—was a ne’er-do-well who had drained his benevolent brother of hundreds and thousands, forcing him to flee fashionable life and hide in this watery retreat. The mere name of the captain lengthened the butler’s lantern jaw and puckered his parrot nose into a sneer. That was all he would say, and Father Brown could hear, behind the reticence, the clang of partisanship like a pot-lid.

Mrs. Anthony was more communicative, perhaps because she was less content. Her tone about the prince was faintly acid, though not without awe. Flambeau and Father Brown stood once more in the long hall where windows and mirrors alternated, examining a red chalk sketch of two long-haired boys. In the corners of the room hung also a large grey photograph of a very young man in uniform.

Asked whether the soldier was the prince, the butler answered shortly that it was the prince’s younger brother, Captain Stephen Saradine. Then he dried up, as if words were rations.

As Flambeau peered close at the sketch he said loudly, “The brothers Saradine, I suppose. They both look innocent enough. It would be hard to say which is the good brother and which the bad.”

At the sound of Mrs. Anthony’s swift step entering, Flambeau turned the conversation with some trivial flourish and strolled out towards the garden. But Father Brown did not move. He kept gazing at the red crayon lines as if they were handwriting.

The glass-walled hall had this peculiarity: anyone entering was reflected at once in four or five mirrors, multiplied as if the house could not be content with one truth. Father Brown, without turning, stopped in the middle of a mild remark. Mrs. Anthony stopped too, and stared at him.

Her large tragic brown eyes held a painful wonder, as if she were weighing whether the little priest was merely a stranger or something more dangerous—an ear trained for confession, perhaps, or a tongue that might give names to the nameless. She spoke low, like a woman offering a secret in exchange for sympathy.

“He is right enough in one way, your friend,” she said. “He says it would be hard to pick out the good and bad brothers. Oh, it would be hard, it would be mighty hard, to pick out the good one.”

“I don’t understand you,” Father Brown said, and began to move away.

She took a step nearer, brows like thunder, shoulders dipping as a bull dips before the charge.

“There isn’t a good one,” she hissed. “There was badness enough in the captain taking all that money, but I don’t think there was much goodness in the prince giving it. The captain’s not the only one with something against him.”

A light broke in Father Brown’s averted face, and his mouth shaped silently the word blackmail.

At that instant Mrs. Anthony turned abrupt and went white, as if cold water had been thrown into her eyes. In the mirrors Father Brown saw why: the door had opened without a sound, and Mr. Paul stood there like a ghost.

By the trick of reflection it was as if five Mr. Pauls had entered by five doors at once.

“His Highness,” said the butler, with stiff respect, “has just arrived.”

Even as he spoke a man’s figure crossed the first window outside, cutting the sunlit pane like a stage actor passing behind a gauze. An instant later it crossed the second window, and then the third; and in the mirrors the same aquiline profile and marching figure repeated in successive frames, as if the house insisted on showing him from every angle before he was allowed to be himself.

He was erect, alert. His hair was white, carefully curled; his complexion an odd ivory yellow. He had that short, curved Roman nose that often goes with long lean cheeks and chin, partly masked by moustache and imperial. The moustache was much darker than the beard, giving him a slightly theatrical air—as if he had painted on a smile and forgotten to paint on repose. He was dressed to match the effect: white top hat, orchid in his coat, yellow waistcoat, yellow gloves that he flapped and swung as he walked.

When he came round to the front door they heard Mr. Paul open it; they heard the new arrival say cheerfully, “Well, you see I have come.” They heard Mr. Paul’s inaudible reply. Then the butler said, “Everything is at your disposal,” with the tone of a man announcing terms of a contract.

Prince Saradine came into the room gaily—and once again the mirrors made it a spectral scene, five princes entering through five doors.

He set his white hat and yellow gloves upon the table and held out his hand with cordial ease.

“Delighted to see you here, Mr. Flambeau,” he said. “Knowing you very well by reputation, if that’s not an indiscreet remark.”

“Not at all,” Flambeau answered, laughing. “I am not sensitive. Very few reputations are gained by unsullied virtue.”

The prince flashed a sharp glance, as if checking whether the retort carried a hidden barb; then he laughed too and offered chairs, including one for himself, like a host who could be generous because nothing touched him.

“Pleasant little place, this, I think,” he said with an air of detachment. “Not much to do, I fear; but the fishing is really good.”

Father Brown stared at him with the grave stare of a baby, tormented by a fancy he could not catch. He looked at the grey curled hair, the yellow-white face, the slim foppish figure. None of it was unnatural, only a shade too pronounced—as if a man had dressed himself not for comfort but for an audience. Yet the interest was elsewhere: in the very framework of the face. Father Brown felt he had seen it before, like an old friend disguised. Then he remembered the multiplying mirrors and blamed the house for playing tricks on the mind.

Prince Saradine turned out to be an excellent host. He distributed his attentions with tact and gaiety. Finding Flambeau sporting and eager to make use of his holiday, he guided him and the boat down to the best fishing spot, then returned in his own canoe within twenty minutes to join Father Brown in the library.

There he plunged with equal politeness into the priest’s quieter pleasures, though his reading, like his life, had not been of the most edifying kind. He knew five or six languages—chiefly the slang of each—and spoke of varied cities and motley societies. Some of his cheerful stories were of gambling hells and opium dens, Australian bushrangers and Italian brigands. Father Brown had known Saradine had travelled ceaselessly; he had not guessed the travels were so disreputable—or so entertainingly told.

Yet beneath the polish Father Brown felt a restlessness, a looseness, as if the man’s soul never sat down. His face was fastidious, but his eye was wild. He had little nervous tricks like a man shaken by drink or drugs. And he neither had, nor pretended to have, his hand on the helm of household affairs. Everything domestic—every clink of plate, every ordering of servants, every decision—belonged to the old butler.

Mr. Paul was not so much a butler as a steward, almost a chamberlain. He dined privately, but with a pomp close to his master’s. The other servants feared him. He consulted with the prince decorously, yet unbendingly, rather as if he were a solicitor speaking to a client. Mrs. Anthony seemed a shadow beside him, waiting on him more than on the prince. Father Brown heard no more of her volcanic whispering about Captain Stephen, about money, about hidden leverage; but the prince’s own insecurity and secretive air made the suspicion of blackmail linger like a bitter aftertaste.

When they went again into the long hall with windows and mirrors, yellow evening had begun to drop over the waters and willowy banks. Outside, the Broads were filling with the hush that comes when birds settle and the world listens for what it cannot name. A bittern boomed in the distance like an elf beating a dwarfish drum; the sound struck through the reeds and came back warped, as if the marsh translated it into a dialect of loneliness.

The same sense of sad and evil fairyland crossed Father Brown’s mind like a small grey cloud.

“I wish Flambeau were back,” he muttered.

“Do you believe in doom?” Prince Saradine asked suddenly, with a sharpness that did not match the tranquil sunset.

“No,” Father Brown answered. “I believe in Doomsday.”

The prince turned from the window and stared. His face was in shadow against the dying light, and his eyes shone oddly.

“What do you mean?” he demanded.

“I mean that we here are on the wrong side of the tapestry,” Father Brown said. “The things that happen here do not seem to mean anything; they mean something somewhere else. Somewhere else retribution will come on the real offender. Here it often seems to fall on the wrong person.”

The prince made an inexplicable noise like an animal. In the shadow his eyes glittered. He repeated, “The wrong person—the wrong person,” too many times for mere emphasis, as if the words were a tune he could not stop humming.

Father Brown, with a sudden cold clarity, wondered whether Saradine’s brilliance and abruptness had another meaning. Was the prince perfectly sane?

Then he awoke to another truth. In the mirrors he could see the door behind them standing open, and Mr. Paul standing in it, pallid as ever, impassive as ever—an intrusion made monstrous by being multiplied.

“I thought it better to announce at once,” Mr. Paul said, with the same stiff respectfulness as an old family lawyer, “a boat rowed by six men has come to the landing-stage, and there’s a gentleman sitting in the stern.”

“A boat!” the prince repeated. “A gentleman?” He rose.

Silence fell, punctured only by the bittern’s distant booming and the constant soft syllables of the river. Then, before anyone spoke again, a new face and figure passed in profile round the three sunlit windows as the prince had done earlier.

The outline was aquiline too, but otherwise unlike. Instead of Saradine’s fresh white topper there was a black hat of antiquated or foreign shape. Beneath it was a young, very solemn face, clean-shaven, blue with stubble about the resolute chin; it carried a faint suggestion of the young Napoleon. The whole outfit looked old and odd, as if the man had never bothered to change from the fashions of his fathers: a shabby blue frock coat, a red soldierly waistcoat, coarse white trousers common in early Victorian days, grotesquely incongruous in the present.

Out of this old clothes-shop the man’s olive face stood out strangely young and monstrously sincere.

“The deuce,” said Prince Saradine.

He clapped on his white hat and went to the front door himself, flinging it open on the sunset garden.

By then the newcomer and his followers were drawn up on the lawn like a small stage army. The six boatmen had pulled the boat well up on shore and stood about it, guarding it almost menacingly, holding their oars erect like spears. They were swarthy men; some wore earrings. One stepped forward beside the olive-faced young man in the red waistcoat and carried a large black case of unfamiliar form, like a coffin for some instrument.

“Your name,” said the young man, “is Saradine?”

Saradine inclined his head, negligently, as if names were often thrown at him.

The newcomer’s eyes were dull, dog-like brown, as different as possible from the prince’s restless glittering grey. Yet again Father Brown was pricked by that half-memory of a replica, a face seen before; and again he blamed the mirror-lined house.

“Confound this crystal palace,” he muttered under his breath. “One sees everything too many times. It’s like a dream.”

“If you are Prince Saradine,” the young man said, “I may tell you that my name is Antonelli.”

“Antonelli,” repeated the prince languidly. “Somehow I remember the name.”

“Permit me to present myself,” said the young Italian.

With his left hand he politely removed his old-fashioned top hat; with his right he struck Prince Saradine a ringing crack across the face.

The white top hat rolled down the steps. One of the turquoise flower-pots rocked on its pedestal.

Prince Saradine, whatever else he was, was not a coward. He sprang at the other’s throat and almost bore him backwards onto the grass. “That is all right,” he said in halting English. “I have insulted. I will give satisfaction. Marco, open the case.”

Bohun Beacon clung to its hill like a barnacle to a ship’s keel: slate roofs stacked under a church so steep and high that the spire looked less built than grown, a pale thorn pricking the grey. Below it, the smithy burned as it always burned—breath of coal and hot iron, the tang of scale and soot, the steady domestic heat a village lived by. Hammers lay about like kitchen tools on a dresser: small pein-hammers for nails, heavy sledges for shoeing and shaping, tongs like blackened crab-claws. Opposite, across a rough cross of cobbles where carts chewed ruts into stone, stood the Blue Boar with its shuttered windows and last night’s stale beer scent leaking into dawn.

It was on that crossway, in a leaden daybreak with a skim of silver on the puddles, that two brothers met as if the village itself had scheduled them: one beginning his day, one refusing to end his.

The Reverend and Honourable Wilfred Bohun walked as if he were already inside the church—black coat buttoned up to the throat, hands kept close, face fine and pale and a little too strained, like a man fasting on purpose. Colonel the Honourable Norman Bohun, elder brother and landlord, lounged on the bench outside the Blue Boar in evening dress with the unembarrassed sprawl of a man who had made a habit of not going home till morning. He had the Bohun yellow hair too, but it was the colour of old straw gilded by lamplight; his moustaches drooped long and dyed the air with faint cigar-smoke. Under brows that should have been bright, his eyes sat deep and near together, darkened by shadow until they seemed almost black. Over his formal clothes he wore a pale yellow coat, more like a dressing gown stolen from a bedroom than an overcoat, and on his head a broad-brimmed, brilliant green hat—an oriental curiosity that looked absurd and yet, because he wore it with insolent certainty, looked as if it had been made for him.

The Bohuns had a pennon that had once seen Palestine, and a name that could be found in parchments older than the village itself; but the village knew them mostly as it knows weather—present, powerful, and not particularly noble. Ancient houses did not always keep ancient virtues. The poor kept traditions because they needed them; the rich kept fashions because they could afford to discard them. The Bohuns had played at every pose their century offered, and in the last two hundred years the poses had curdled into a kind of rot: dandies, drunkards, and rumours of minds going wrong.

Wilfred, who lived as if to prove the family could still bow to something higher than itself, moved toward the churchyard by way of the smithy. He stopped, frowning, because Norman’s cavernous stare had fixed on the same direction. Wilfred did not waste thought on the idea that his brother had suddenly become interested in prayer. Opposite them, beyond the open shed of the forge, lay the blacksmith’s house, and Wilfred had heard the village’s half-swallowed talk about Simeon Barnes’s wife—beautiful, loud in colour as flame, and a Catholic among Presbyterians. Wilfred’s suspicion flicked across the yard like a startled bird.

Norman stood up, laughing, and stretched as if dawn were a personal joke.

“Good morning, Wilfred,” he drawled. “Like a conscientious landlord, I sit up all night to watch over my people. I am going to pay a call on the blacksmith.”

Wilfred’s gaze fell to a pebble in the road, as if it were safer than his brother’s face. “The blacksmith is out. He is over at Greenford.”

“I know,” said Norman, with a laugh that did not show in his eyes. “That is why I’m calling on him.”

“Norman,” Wilfred said, voice tight, “are you ever afraid of thunderbolts?”

Norman blinked, amused. “What do you mean? Is your hobby meteorology?”

“I mean,” Wilfred answered without lifting his eyes, “do you ever think God might strike you in the street?”

“I beg your pardon,” Norman said. “I see your hobby is folk-lore.”

“I know your hobby is blasphemy,” Wilfred snapped, stung as if his one living nerve had been pinched. “And if you do not fear God, you have good reason to fear man.”

“Fear man?” Norman raised his brows politely.

“Barnes is the biggest and strongest man for forty miles,” Wilfred said. “You are no coward, but he could throw you over the wall.”

That hit because it was true. For a moment the sneer-lines by Norman’s mouth deepened into something like anger; then his cruel good humour sprang back, showing dog-like front teeth under the yellow moustache.

“In that case, my dear Wilfred,” he said carelessly, “it was wise of the last of the Bohuns to come out partially in armour.”

He lifted the bright green hat. Inside it glinted dull metal: it was lined with steel. Wilfred recognised it at once—a light Japanese helmet torn down from a trophy that hung in the old hall, the sort of relic kept not for memory but for decoration.

“It was the first hat to hand,” Norman added airy as smoke. “Always the nearest hat—and the nearest woman.”

“The blacksmith is away at Greenford,” Wilfred said quietly. “The time of his return is unsettled.”

He turned and went toward the church door, crossing himself as if to push away something unclean that had brushed his sleeve.

Inside, the church held its usual cold sweetness: stone that had kept night in its pores, wood smelling faintly of polish and old dust, and the damp mineral breath of centuries. Wilfred always found the hush here like water on a burned tongue. But on this morning even his still round of prayer was fated to be jarred by small shocks.

A figure rose hurriedly from kneeling near the shadowed aisle and came into the doorway’s pale spill of daylight. Wilfred stopped dead.

The early worshipper was Mad Joe—the village idiot, Simeon Barnes’s nephew, a dark, thick-shouldered youth with a heavy white face and straight dark hair hanging like thatch. His mouth hung open with the vacant patience of an animal waiting to be fed. He had never been known to pray; he had never been known to care. Whatever words had been in his head as he knelt there, they must have been strange words indeed.

He passed Wilfred without recognition, and went out into sunshine as if nothing had happened. Wilfred, rooted, saw him in the yard and saw Norman hail him with a sort of jovial contempt. The last thing Wilfred saw before he turned away was his brother tossing pennies at Joe’s open mouth with the careful seriousness of a man practising a shot.

The sight—cruelty in full sun, stupidity swallowing money—sent Wilfred deeper into his need for cleansing. He climbed to the gallery, to a pew under a window he loved: an angel in blue glass carrying lilies, the colour so cold and clear it seemed to rinse thought itself. He sank into prayer until the world narrowed to silver petals and sapphire sky.

Half an hour later, feet clattered where no feet should clatter. Gibbs the cobbler appeared at the gallery stair, and his presence in church was almost as astonishing as Mad Joe’s had been. Gibbs was an atheist, loud in the village, and he came now with a haste so urgent that his irreligion looked like a private shame.

Wilfred stood quickly. “What is it?” he asked stiffly, and his hand shook as he reached for his hat.

Gibbs spoke with hoarse respect, and even with something like sympathy, which made it worse.

“Beg pardon, sir. We didn’t think it right not to tell you at once. A dreadful thing’s happened, sir. I’m afraid your brother—”

Wilfred’s hands clenched. “What devilry has he done now?”

Gibbs swallowed. “I’m afraid he’s done nothing, and won’t do anything. I’m afraid he’s done for. You’d better come down, sir.”

They went down the winding stair to an entrance above the street. From that height Wilfred saw the yard as if it were laid out for a diagram.

Men stood scattered in the smithy yard—five or six in black, one in an inspector’s uniform. The doctor was there, tall and black-bearded, glasses catching the weak light. The Presbyterian minister stood rigid as a post. A small Roman Catholic priest, dull-faced and round, bent toward a woman on a bench and spoke rapidly under his breath. The woman’s hair was red-gold, like autumn wheat lit from within, and she sobbed with the blind abandon of someone whose body could do nothing else.

Between the groups, just clear of the heap of tools, lay a man in evening dress, spread-eagled on his face. Wilfred knew the posture of that body, knew the cut of the coat, the rings on the fingers; but the head was not a head. It was a smashed star of blackness and blood.

Wilfred ran down into the yard.

The doctor saluted him—he was the Bohun family physician—but Wilfred scarcely saw him. “My brother is dead,” he stammered. “What does it mean? What is this horrible mystery?”

Silence, thick as smoke, held them. Then Gibbs, who could not endure silence, broke in bluntly.

“Plenty of horror, sir, but not much mystery.”

“What do you mean?” Wilfred’s face had gone very white.

“It’s plain enough,” said Gibbs. “There’s only one man for forty miles round that could have struck such a blow, and he’s the man that had most reason to.”

“We must not prejudge,” the doctor put in nervously. “But I can corroborate what Mr. Gibbs says about the blow. It is an incredible blow. Mr. Gibbs says only one man in the district could have done it. I should say nobody could have done it.”

Superstition—a chill older than Wilfred’s theology—ran through him. “I can hardly understand,” he whispered.

“Mr. Bohun,” said the doctor low, “metaphors literally fail me. To say the skull was smashed like an eggshell is inadequate. Fragments of bone were driven into the body and into the ground like bullets into mud. It was the hand of a giant.”

He paused, grim behind his lenses. “It clears most people at one stroke. If you or I were accused, we should be acquitted as an infant would be acquitted of stealing the Nelson Column.”

“That’s what I say,” Gibbs insisted. “Where’s Simeon Barnes?”

“He’s over at Greenford,” Wilfred faltered.

“More likely over in France,” Gibbs muttered.

“No,” said a small, colourless voice—the little Catholic priest who had been murmuring to the woman. “As a matter of fact, he is coming up the road at this moment.”

Everyone turned. Down the winding path across the plain, striding with the long, loose gait of a man used to carrying weight, came Simeon Barnes. A hammer rode his shoulder like a soldier’s musket. He was gigantic, bony, with deep dark eyes that made his face look carved from a burnt log. He walked with two other men and spoke quietly, as if nothing had happened.

“My God,” cried Gibbs, “and there’s the hammer he did it with.”

“No,” said the inspector for the first time, sensible voice, sandy moustache twitching. “There’s the hammer he did it with over there by the church wall. We’ve left it and the body exactly as they are.”

They glanced. Lying a little apart was one of the smallest hammers, light and narrow, easily missed among its heavy brethren. Yet on its iron edge were blood and yellow hair.

The short Catholic priest—Father Brown—went across and looked down at it in silence. He was an unremarkable little man, stubbly-haired and round-faced, the sort of person a crowd forgets even while he stands in it. But when he spoke, his dull voice held a new note, as if something inside him had pricked awake.

“Mr. Gibbs was hardly right,” he said, still staring at the tool. “There is at least the mystery of why so big a man should attempt so big a blow with so little a hammer.”

“Oh, never mind that,” Gibbs burst out. “What are we to do with Simeon Barnes?”

“Leave him alone,” Father Brown said. “He is coming here of himself. I know those two men with him. They are good fellows from Greenford; they have come over about the Presbyterian chapel.”

Simeon turned the corner of the church and strode into his yard. Then he stopped dead. The hammer slid from his shoulder and fell. The inspector, proper as a printed form, stepped forward.

“I won’t ask you whether you know anything about what has happened,” he said. “You are not bound to say. I hope you don’t know, and can prove it. But I must go through the form: I arrest you in the King’s name for the murder of Colonel Norman Bohun.”

“You’re not bound to say anything!” Gibbs cried, dancing with officious excitement. “They’ve got to prove everything. They haven’t proved it’s Colonel Bohun, with his head all smashed up like that.”

“That won’t wash,” the doctor muttered aside to Father Brown. “Detective-story nonsense. I was the colonel’s medical man; I knew his body better than he did. He had very fine hands, but peculiar. Second and third fingers the same length. That’s the colonel right enough.”

Simeon’s iron eyes followed the doctor’s glance to the ruined body.

“Is Colonel Bohun dead?” he asked calmly. “Then he’s damned.”

“Oh, don’t say anything,” Gibbs cried, in ecstasy at the law’s protections, like a secularist worshipping procedure.

Simeon turned on him, fanatic grandeur in his face. “It’s well for you infidels to dodge like foxes because the world’s law favours you. But God guards His own in His pocket, as you shall see this day.”

He pointed at the corpse. “When did this dog die in his sins?”

“Moderate your language,” the doctor said sharply.

“Moderate the Bible’s language and I’ll moderate mine. When did he die?”

“I saw him alive at six o’clock,” Wilfred stammered.

“God is good,” Simeon said. He faced the inspector. “Mr. Inspector, I have no objection to being arrested. It is you who may object to arresting me. I don’t mind leaving the court without a stain on my character. You may mind leaving the court with a set-back in your career.”

The inspector looked at him harder.

“There are two men outside this shop,” Simeon went on, slow and lucid as a sermon, “good tradesmen in Greenford whom you all know, who will swear they saw me from before midnight till daybreak and long after, in the committee room of our Revival Mission. We sit all night; we save souls so fast. In Greenford itself twenty people could swear to me for all that time. If I were a heathen, I would let you walk on to your downfall. But as a Christian man I give you your chance: will you hear my alibi now or in court?”

The inspector, disturbed now in spite of himself, said, “Of course I should be glad to clear you altogether now.”

Simeon walked out with the same easy stride and spoke to the two men with him. They stepped forward—steady, known faces—and said a few simple sentences that nobody doubted. Their words, plain as bread, stood between Simeon and the crime like a wall. His innocence rose up solid as the church behind them.

A silence came that was worse than argument. In a foolish need to make it less unbearable, Wilfred said to Father Brown, “You seem very much interested in that hammer.”

“Yes,” Father Brown said. “Why is it such a small hammer?”

The doctor swung round, struck by it. “By George, that’s true. Who would use a little hammer with ten larger hammers lying about?”

Then he leaned toward Wilfred, voice lowered, intent and hissing. “Only someone who can’t lift a large hammer. It’s not about courage between the sexes; it’s about shoulder-power. A bold woman could commit ten murders with a light hammer. She could not kill a beetle with a heavy one.”

Wilfred stared, horror rising as if a trapdoor had opened under his mind. Father Brown listened with head slightly tilted, like a man catching a faint sound in a windy place.

The doctor continued, more fevered. “Why do idiots assume the person who hates the wife’s lover is the husband? Nine times out of ten the person who most hates him is the wife. Who knows what insolence he showed her—look there!”

He jerked his head toward the woman on the bench. She had lifted her face; the tears dried on her skin as if heat had licked them away. Her eyes were fixed on the corpse with a glare so electric it looked almost vacant, as if grief and hatred had burned out language and left only staring.

Wilfred made a limp gesture as if to wave away knowing. But Father Brown, brushing ash off his sleeve where the forge had breathed it on him, spoke in his mild, indifferent way.

“You are like so many doctors,” he said. “Your mental science is suggestive. Your physical science is impossible. I agree the woman wants to kill the co-respondent more than the petitioner does. I agree a woman will pick up a small hammer rather than a big one. But there is physical impossibility. No woman ever born could have smashed a man’s skull out flat like that.”

He paused, then added, as if remembering an extra ingredient left out of a recipe: “These people have not grasped the whole of it. The man was actually wearing an iron helmet, and the blow scattered it like broken glass. Look at that woman. Look at her arms.”

Silence fell again, heavy as a held breath. The doctor, nettled, said sulkily, “Well, I may be wrong. There are objections to everything. But I stick to the main point. No man but an idiot would pick up that little hammer if he could use a big hammer.”

Wilfred’s hands flew to his head and clutched his hair as if he could hold his thoughts in by force. They dropped. His voice broke out, quick and trembling.

“That was the word I wanted; you have said the word. The words you said were, ‘No man but an idiot would pick up the small hammer.’”

“Yes,” said the doctor. “Well?”

“Well,” Wilfred said, and his agitation made him look suddenly younger, almost girlish in his fever, “no man but an idiot did.”

They stared at him. He went on, breath coming fast.

“I am a priest, and a priest should be no shedder of blood—I mean he should bring no one to the gallows. And I thank God that I see the criminal clearly now—because he is a criminal who cannot be brought to the gallows.”

“You will not denounce him?” the doctor asked.

“He would not be hanged if I did,” Wilfred answered, and a wild, curiously happy smile crossed his face. “When I went into the church this morning, I found a madman praying there—poor Joe, wrong all his life. God knows what he prayed; but with such folk it is not incredible their prayers are upside down. Very likely a lunatic would pray before killing a man. When I last saw Joe he was with my brother. My brother was mocking him.”

“By Jove,” the doctor breathed, “this is talking at last. But how do you explain—”

“Don’t you see?” Wilfred cried. “It is the only theory that covers the queer things, that answers both riddles. The two riddles are the little hammer and the big blow. The smith might have struck the big blow, but would not have chosen the little hammer. His wife would have chosen the little hammer, but she could not have struck the big blow. But the madman might have done both. The little hammer—he is mad; he might pick up anything. And the big blow—have you never heard, doctor, that a maniac in his paroxysm may have the strength of ten men?”

The doctor drew a deep breath. “By golly, I believe you’ve got it.”

Father Brown had been watching Wilfred steadily. His large grey eyes, usually as placid as a cow’s in a field, looked suddenly not placid at all—only patient, as if waiting for a particular note in a soundscape. When the silence settled again, he spoke with marked respect.

“Mr. Bohun, yours is the only theory yet propounded which holds water every way and is essentially unassailable. I think, therefore, you deserve to be told, on my positive knowledge, that it is not the true one.”

And with that the little priest walked away and stood again over the small hammer, staring as if it might speak in some dialect only he could hear.

“That fellow seems to know more than he ought,” the doctor whispered peevishly to Wilfred. “Those popish priests are deucedly sly.”

“No, no,” Wilfred said with wild fatigue, as if clinging to his new certainty because it was a mercy. “It was the lunatic. It was the lunatic.”

Their little knot had drifted away from the inspector and the officially arrested man. Now, as their own talk broke, the voices from the other group reached them. Father Brown lifted his eyes briefly, then dropped them again, as Simeon Barnes’s deep voice rolled across the yard.

“I hope I’ve convinced you, Mr. Inspector. I’m a strong man, as you say, but I couldn’t have flung my hammer bang here from Greenford. My hammer hasn’t got wings that it should come flying half a mile over hedges and fields.”

The inspector laughed, almost friendly. “No, I think you can be considered out of it—though it’s one of the rummiest coincidences I ever saw. I can only ask you to give us all the assistance you can in finding a man as big and strong as yourself. By George, you might be useful, if only to hold him. I suppose you yourself have no guess at the man?”

The Thames, that old kettle always on the boil, was giving off its noon vapour in a way that made the whole river look like broth—grey at the edges, bright as molten pewter where the sun struck it. Westminster Bridge rang beneath the shoes of two men who crossed it together, their footsteps picking out different notes on the iron plates: one long stride, one short shuffle. The taller man, who carried his bulk like a cavalry officer turned civilian, was M. Hercule Flambeau, now with “Private Enquiries” on his letterhead and new rooms to match. The smaller man, round-faced and clerical, the Reverend J. Brown, had come straight from a Camberwell deathbed; you could almost hear the hush of that room still clinging to his sleeves, as if silence itself had settled into the nap of his worn black coat.

Flambeau’s new building rose like a modern chimney-stack, an American pile of floors with an American faith in polished metal. The entrance smelt of hot oil and varnished wood, and the lift cables made a soft, taut singing above their heads—the steady, thin keening of machinery doing its duty. The place was scarcely populated yet: a couple of offices occupied, most floors blank, with the hollow acoustics of an empty pantry.

But something outside one of the windows broke the blankness like a gaudy shop-sign: an enormous gilt eye, rimmed with rays, staring out into Victoria Street as if it meant to watch Westminster itself. Father Brown stopped so abruptly that Flambeau had to check his stride.

“What on earth is that?” the priest asked, squinting up at it as if the thing might blink.

“Oh, that’s the new religion upstairs,” Flambeau said, with a short laugh. “The sort that cures you by informing you you were never ill. The fellow calls himself Kalon—no doubt that isn’t his name. ‘New Priest of Apollo.’ Sun-worship. That eye is their emblem.”

Father Brown kept looking, not with fascination but with that wary curiosity a cook has when someone offers him a new spice with a foreign name.

“What does that monstrous eye mean?” he asked. “It looks like an advertisement for an oculist, only louder.”

“They say a healthy man can stare at the sun,” Flambeau answered. “Their symbols are the sun and the open eye. Steady mind, steady gaze—no fear, no weakness, no blinking.”

“If a man were really healthy,” Father Brown said, “he would not bother to stare at it.”

Flambeau shrugged. “It claims to cure all physical diseases.”

“Can it cure the one spiritual disease?” Father Brown asked, mildly.

“And what’s that?” Flambeau said, amused.

“Thinking one is quite well,” Father Brown replied; and the way he said it made the words sound less like a jest than a diagnosis.

Flambeau, who had been a thief and then a policeman of thieves, had never been tempted by bright modern creeds. But he was always tempted by people, especially when they had a sort of dangerous prettiness. The rooms below his new office belonged to two women running a typewriting concern. They were both dark and spare; the elder, Pauline Stacey, had the fierce elegance of a falcon. Even when she sat still she seemed to be cutting the air with her profile. Her eyes were very bright, but not warm: bright as steel freshly ground. Her younger sister Joan looked like Pauline’s shadow when a cloud passed over—paler, a little greyer, gentler, with a devotion that was almost canine in its patience.

What amused Flambeau—and later, what came back to him with a sting—was the way Pauline combined real wealth with an almost violent contempt for the comforts wealth buys. She had money enough for country houses and old gardeners who know the dialect names of each apple in an orchard; yet she chose this humming hive of a building, with its lifts and telephones, with its hard edges and hard voices. She spoke of machines with a kind of religious fervour, as if the thin singing of cables and the click of switches were a nobler music than any birdsong over fields.

On one of Flambeau’s first days there, he had been waiting in the entrance for the lift-boy. Pauline had swept in, refused to wait a second, and announced sharply that she could work a lift as well as any boy. In the few seconds of ascent she had flung off opinions as if she were shaking crumbs from a tablecloth—opinions about the stupidity of romance, the necessity of science, the future of women among engines that “devoured distance and defied time.” When Flambeau, out of instinctive politeness, held the door for her, she almost looked affronted by the gesture, as if he’d offered to carry her.

She had a temper too; it snapped like a dry twig. Flambeau once went into their office and saw her fling her sister’s spectacles onto the floor and stamp them to splinters. The sharp crack of glass in that tidy room sounded like something breaking in a kitchen in the quiet before dinner.

“They’re morbid!” Pauline had cried, her voice ringing against the bare walls. “That sickly medical notion—admitting weakness! Do you think I’m going to have this place cluttered with props and crutches? What next—false hair? wooden legs? glass eyes? Doctors trade in fear. They teach people to be cowards. You only need spectacles because you think you need them.”

Flambeau, bewildered, had asked her with blunt foreign logic why a lift was less of a “prop” than spectacles.

“That is different,” Pauline had answered, with cold pride. “Motors and batteries are the force of man—yes, and of woman too. That is high science. But these plasters and badges of infirmity—no. I’m free-born. People only think they can’t do things because they’ve been trained to fear. Nurses tell children not to stare at the sun; so they can’t do it without blinking. But why should there be one star I may not see? The sun is not my master. I will open my eyes and look at him when I choose.”

“Your eyes,” Flambeau had said, bowing with deliberate gallantry, “will dazzle the sun.”

But as he went upstairs he had whistled under his breath, half amused and half uneasy. For he had heard something of the man above him and his strange discipline of staring into brightness, and Pauline’s language had begun to sound like an echo.

The man who called himself Kalon was indeed difficult to dismiss as a mere quack. He was tall, nearly as tall as Flambeau, but with a beauty that seemed to belong to a statue before it ever belonged to a man: golden beard, strong blue eyes, fair hair thrown back like a lion’s mane. Even the vulgar circumstances could not quite make him common—the brass plate on the door, the clerk in cuffs in the outer office, the great gilt eye outside like a poster. Kalon carried his splendour as if it were natural as breath.

Three times each day he went out onto his balcony to salute the sun, robed in white, crowned with a golden circlet, chanting aloud so that his voice—clear, carrying, oddly piercing—cut through the street’s daily clamour. His chant did something strange to the soundscape of Westminster: for a few minutes it made the clatter of carts, the cries of hawkers, even the distant pulse of the city’s bells seem like accompaniment to a single long note.

It was at noon, when Parliament and parish clocks had just struck and the vibrations still trembled faintly in the air, that Father Brown, pausing in the street, looked up and saw the priest of Apollo standing erect on his balcony. Flambeau, who had tired of the spectacle, pushed into the building without a glance, expecting Father Brown to follow. Father Brown lingered, blinking up as if he were watching a puppet-show.

Kalon’s eyes were fixed on the blazing disc. He did not blink. He might not have seen the street at all; he certainly did not see the small priest below, who blinked at everything, even at truth.

“O sun,” Kalon cried, his voice falling and rising like a litany sung in an empty church, “O star too great to be allowed among the stars! O fountain that flowest quietly in the secret place called space. White Father of all white unwearied things—white flames and white flowers and white peaks—Father more innocent than thy most innocent children—primal purity, into the peace of which—”

The words were smashed to fragments by a rush and crash like some iron thing tearing loose. Then came yelling—high, raw, ceaseless. People surged toward the door of the mansions; others burst out. News—bad news—spread in a way that was half sound, half panic, and wholly incomprehensible for a moment because no one knew what had happened.

Two figures remained fixed like statues in different worlds: the radiant priest of Apollo above, still chanting as if nothing on earth could interrupt the sun; and the small priest of Christ below, staring, his face sharpening with concern.

Then Flambeau’s big body filled the doorway, his voice booming like a fog-horn. “A surgeon! Somebody fetch a surgeon!” He plunged back inside, scattering people as he went. Father Brown slipped in after him, almost unnoticed.

Even as Father Brown ducked through the throng, Kalon’s voice still floated down from above—smooth, majestic, indifferent—like music played while someone bleeds in the street.

In the entrance hall the lift well yawned open, and around its edge stood Flambeau and several others, looking down into the pit as into some black cauldron. But the lift had not descended. Something else had descended—something that ought never to have come by that route.

At the bottom lay the broken figure of Pauline Stacey. Her dark hair was disordered, her face—so often held in hard, proud lines—had become suddenly the helpless face of flesh. Blood made a dreadful kind of colour against the stone. The air down there smelt of oil and dust and something metallic that made the throat tighten.

Flambeau had no real doubt she was dead. He had sent for a doctor because his hands moved before his mind would accept what his eyes saw. He stood gripping the rail as if he could hold the world from falling further. Trivial memories stabbed him—her clipped phrases, her sudden laughter like a knife, her stamping heel on the spectacles—each detail now a small cruel hook.

“Was it suicide?” his mind raced. With Pauline’s insolent optimism it felt impossible. “Was it murder?” But who in this half-empty tower?

With a roughness that tried to disguise weakness, he demanded, “Where is that fellow Kalon?”

A heavy, quiet voice—someone’s, close beside him—answered that Kalon had been on his balcony for the last quarter of an hour, praying at noon as he always did.

Flambeau turned and found Father Brown’s hand lightly on his sleeve. He stared at the little priest and burst out, “Then if he’s been up there all the time—who can have done it?”

“Perhaps,” Father Brown said, “we might go upstairs and find out. We have a little time before the police will move.”

They left the dead woman to the arriving surgeon’s grim efficiency and ran up the stairs. The typewriting office was empty—desks neat, papers set as if waiting, chairs pushed in. Flambeau dashed on to his own floor, entered, and then came back almost at once, his face changed.

“Her sister,” he said, with an unpleasant gravity, “her sister seems to have gone out for a walk.”

Father Brown nodded, as if he had expected that answer. “Or she may have gone up to the sun man’s rooms,” he said. “Verify it, if you like, and then we’ll talk it over in—” He stopped, as if catching his own mistake mid-step. “No. Downstairs. In their office.”

Flambeau stared at him; but he followed. They went down to the Stacey office, empty and waiting. Father Brown took a large red-leather chair near the entrance—an oddly domestic thing in that hard business room—placing himself so he could see the stairs and the landings beyond, like an old farmer sitting by the kitchen door where he can hear the yard as well as the hearth. He waited in silence, listening. The building’s noises were suddenly very noticeable: the distant hum of the lift machinery, the soft echo of steps on stairs, the city’s muffled roar outside as constant as a sea.

In four minutes three figures came down together.

First came Joan Stacey, her face drawn, her dark hair showing premature grey. Next came Kalon himself, his midday litany finished, sweeping down in white robes and gold circlet with such splendour that the drab stairwell looked too mean to hold him—like some radiant figure walking through a back passage. Last came Flambeau, frowning, bewildered.

Joan walked straight to her desk and began arranging papers with a quick practical flap, as if a little ritual of order could keep the world from dissolving. The action steadied everyone; it was like the sound of a knife returning to a chopping-board after a scream—ordinary, steady, domestic in its insistence. If Joan was guilty, she had ice in her veins.

Father Brown watched her for a moment with an odd small smile; then, without taking his eyes from her, he spoke to Kalon.

“Prophet,” he said, “I wish you would tell me a lot about your religion.”

Kalon inclined his crowned head. “I shall be proud to do so. But I am not sure I understand what you mean.”

“It’s only this,” Father Brown said, in his frank, doubtful way. “Sometimes a man’s first principles are wrong because he has tangled his mind with fine words. Sometimes he does wrong because he has trampled on a conscience that was clear. I’m trying to know which you are. Now—do you really think murder is wrong at all?”

Kalon’s blue eyes did not flicker. “Is this an accusation?” he asked quietly.

“No,” Father Brown said, as quietly. “It is the speech for the defence.”

The room seemed to still. Even the building’s thin mechanical singing sounded far away, as if a door had closed between the moment and the world.

Kalon rose; and the movement had a kind of theatre in it, as if something bright had been uncovered. He seemed too large for the cramped office, as though he could have stood just as easily under the open sky of Salisbury Plain. His white robes made the dingy room look like a place unworthy of light. Father Brown, in his shabby black, sat like a blot of soot on a fresco.

“We meet at last, Caiaphas,” Kalon said, and his voice made the name feel like a verdict. “Your church and mine are the only realities on earth. I adore the sun, and you adore the darkening of the sun. You are the priest of the dying; I, of the living God. Suspicion and slander—your trade—become you well. Your church is but a black police. You are spies and detectives; you tear confessions from men by treachery or torture. You would convict men of crime; I would convict them of innocence. You would convince them of sin; I would convince them of virtue.

“One more word, reader of the books of evil, before I blow away your baseless nightmares for ever. I care nothing whether you can convict me. The disgrace you worship, the hanging you threaten—these are ogres in a child’s book. You said you offered a speech for the defence. I care so little for your cloudland that I will offer you the speech for the prosecution.

“There is but one thing to be said against me—and I will say it. The woman who is dead was my love and my bride—not as your tin chapels call lawful, but by a law purer and sterner than you will understand. We walked another world from yours. And because policemen, theological or otherwise, always imagine that love must breed hatred, you may take that as your first point.

“The second is stronger. This very morning, before she died, she wrote at that table a will leaving me and my new church half a million. Come—where are the handcuffs? Do you think I care? Penal servitude would be no more than waiting for her at a wayside station. The gallows would be only going to her in a headlong car.”

He spoke with such authority that even Flambeau, who had faced down men with knives, felt something like admiration against his will. Joan stared too, as if she were watching a bright banner unfurl in smoke.

Father Brown’s face showed only distress; he stared down at the floor, and a wrinkle of pain lay across his forehead like a crease in paper.

Kalon leaned against the mantelpiece as if he had just finished a public oration in the open air.

“In a few words I have put before you the whole case against me. In fewer words I will destroy it. As to whether I committed this crime—the truth is in one sentence: I could not have committed this crime. Pauline Stacey fell from this floor to the ground at five minutes past twelve. A hundred witnesses will swear that I was standing upon my balcony above, from before noon until a quarter past—the time of my public prayers. My clerk”—he indicated, as if the young man in cuffs might materialize by the force of mention—“will swear he sat in my outer office all morning; no one passed; no message came. He will swear I arrived ten minutes before the hour and did not leave office or balcony. No man ever had so complete an alibi. I can subpoena half Westminster. Put away your handcuffs. The case is ended.

“And yet, lest the air retain even a breath of your idiotic suspicion, I will tell you what I believe occurred. You may blame me, if you like, or blame my faith and philosophy; but you cannot imprison me.

“It is known to students of higher truths that certain adepts have attained levitation—self-sustenance upon the empty air. It is part of that conquest of matter which is the essence of our wisdom. Pauline was ambitious—impulsive. She believed herself farther advanced than she was. Often in the lift she has said to me that if one’s will were strong enough, one could float down as harmlessly as a feather.

“I solemnly believe that in some ecstasy of noble thought she attempted the miracle. Her faith failed at the crucial instant, and the lower law of matter took its revenge. There is the whole story. Call it, in your police shorthand, suicide. I shall always call it heroic failure in the advance of science and the slow scaling of heaven.”

Flambeau glanced at Father Brown; it was the first time he had ever seen the little priest look beaten. Father Brown sat with his eyes lowered, blinking as if the very light in the room hurt him. For a moment the contrast was cruel: the sun-priest radiant and unashamed, the Catholic priest small, dark, and seemingly reduced to a professional doubter.

At last Father Brown said, in a voice that sounded physically pained rather than morally shocked, “Well—if that is so, sir—you need do no more than take the testamentary paper you spoke of and go. I wonder where the poor lady left it.”

The forest stood all in one attitude, as if it were holding its breath. Every branch was stiff with frost, grey as old iron; every twig caught the starlight and gave it back in thin, sharp sparks, like sugar scattered on black bread. Underfoot the road was hard as a baker’s peel, ringing to each step; the sound went far in that winter hush, for there were no carts grinding, no distant engines complaining—only the small honest noises that belong to cold earth: the creak of a gate, the brittle click of a boot-heel, the faint rasp of breath drawn through a scarf.

On a knuckle of turf above the trees the church rose with its square stone tower, bleak and northern, like a watch-post left behind after some older war. The churchyard climbed to it in a steep path, as if the dead had been buried in a hurry and on the first bit of high ground they could find. The graves lay on a slant, weathered, anonymous; but at the top, in the one place the moonlight could not help touching, there was a monument that drew the eye like a polished knife.

It was a soldier cast in metal, lying back as if still listening for the last command. His hands were locked as in prayer; his head rested on a gun as if it were a pillow. The face was the face from portraits—heavy whiskers, the look of a man who had been praised for patience so often he had forgotten anger was allowed. By one side lay a sword with the point broken away; by the other a Bible. In summer, people came up here in wagonettes to stare and murmur the words cut into the stone; in winter, when even the birds seemed reluctant to disturb the dark, one might have thought him left at last to the cold and the stars.

Yet a gate creaked, long and wooden, and two figures in black came up the path. One was huge enough to be a shadow of a tree; the other was so small that his blackness looked like a patch of night cut loose and walking.

They stopped at the tomb and stared, not like tourists, but like men studying a recipe written in a dead language.

After a silence the small man said, very gently, as if he were trying not to wake the church: “Where does a wise man hide a pebble?”

The big man answered low: “On the beach.”

The little man nodded, and after another pause: “Where does a wise man hide a leaf?”

“In the forest.”

A stillness fell again. Then the big man, as if a thought had pricked him, went on: “Do you mean—as when a wise man has to hide a real diamond he hides it among sham ones?”

“No, no,” said the small man, with a quick little laugh that steamed in the air. “We’ll let bygones be bygones. Strike a match, will you?”

The large man fumbled. The scratch flared, and the monument’s face and lettering leaped into a warm gold that seemed indecently homely in that hard weather—like candlelight on a kitchen wall in a house that has never seen war. For an instant the words could be read cleanly, every capital letter sharp as a cut:

“Sacred to the Memory of General Sir Arthur St. Clare, Hero and Martyr, who Always Vanquished his Enemies and Always Spared Them, and Was Treacherously Slain by Them At Last. May God in Whom he Trusted both Reward and Revenge him.”

The match died, and with it the little circle of warmth. Flambeau lifted his hand to strike another; the priest caught his wrist.

“That’s enough,” Father Brown said. “I saw what I wanted. Or rather, I didn’t see what I didn’t want.”

They went down again, relatched the gate, and took the long frozen road towards the inn. Their steps beat a steady rhythm, the only percussion in that woodland—no railways moaning, no distant factories grinding, no cheap music spilling from anywhere; the silence was almost medicinal, the kind old country people once prescribed without naming it, like broth for a fever. Only now and then a bough cracked under the weight of ice with a report like a snapped bone.

They had gone a quarter of a mile before Father Brown spoke again.

“Yes,” he said, “the wise man hides a pebble on the beach. But what if there is no beach? Do you know anything about the St. Clare trouble?”

“I know nothing about English generals,” said Flambeau, with a breathy laugh. “Though I know a little about English policemen. What I do know is you’ve dragged me all over London and half the countryside to look at this man’s monuments. One would think he got buried in six places. Westminster Abbey, the Embankment—medallions in streets he was born in and lived in—and now his coffin. And I still don’t know who he was. What are you hunting for among all these statues? Some hidden clue? A lost relic?”

“I’m only looking for one word,” said Father Brown. “A word that isn’t there.”

Flambeau made a sound between a snort and a sigh. “Are you going to tell me anything at all?”

“I have to divide it,” said the priest. “There’s what everybody knows. And there’s what I know. What everybody knows is short and plain enough. It’s also entirely wrong.”

“All right,” Flambeau said, accepting the paradox like a man accepting a glass of very strong brandy. “Begin with what everybody knows, then.”

“What everybody knows,” Father Brown went on, “amounts to this. Arthur St. Clare was a great and successful English general. After careful campaigns in India and Africa, he commanded against Brazil, when President Olivier—patriot, poet, or nuisance, depending on who tells it—issued his ultimatum. St. Clare, with a very small force, attacked Olivier, who had a very large one. He was captured after heroic resistance. And, to the abhorrence of the civilised world, he was hanged on the nearest tree. They found him swinging there after the Brazilians retired, with his broken sword hung round his neck.”

“And that,” Flambeau said, “isn’t true?”

“That,” said Father Brown, “is quite true—so far as it goes.”

“It goes far enough,” Flambeau growled.

“The mystery,” said the priest, “is a mystery of psychology. Or rather, of two psychologies. In that Brazilian affair, two famous men acted flat against their characters. St. Clare was a duty-first commander, prudent and pious, the kind of officer who would count the cost in lives as carefully as a farmer counts seed before sowing. Yet in his last battle he attempted something any child could see was absurd. It was as plainly mad as stepping into the road before a motor-bus.

“And Olivier—Olivier had a reputation for magnanimity that even his enemies admitted. He had treated prisoners with kindness bordering on romance; men who cursed him went away ashamed of their own bitterness. Why should he, for once in his life, play the devil—and do it for the one blow that could not have harmed him? That’s the riddle. One of the wisest men acted like an idiot for no reason. One of the best men acted like a fiend for no reason.”

“I leave it to you,” Flambeau said sharply. “And you jolly well tell me.”

Father Brown’s feet crunched on the frozen gravel; his breath smoked and vanished. “It isn’t fair,” he said, “to tell you only that and not the two things that happened after. They don’t make it clearer; they throw darkness in new directions.

“First, the St. Clare family physician quarrelled with them and published articles saying the general was a religious maniac. But that mostly meant he was religious, and the fuss died.

“Second, there was Captain Keith—he was in the regiment at the Black River. He was engaged to St. Clare’s daughter and later married her. He was captured and then, like all the prisoners except the general, treated well and released. Twenty years later he wrote an autobiography. Where you expect him to tell you what happened, he refuses. He says he will not narrate the defeat by the Black River; his reasons are honourable and compelling. But he adds this: understood properly, St. Clare’s action was one of the most brilliant and sagacious of his life. And Olivier acted with even more than his characteristic good feeling. In short—St. Clare wasn’t such a fool, and Olivier wasn’t such a brute, as they looked. That is all, he says, and nothing will induce him to add a word.”

The moon—large, frozen-white, like a hard loaf dusted with flour—showed itself between branches. By its light Father Brown took a scrap of printed paper from his pocket, checked the quotation, and put it away.

Flambeau threw up his hand in a quick French gesture, like a chef dismissing a clumsy argument with the flick of a wrist.

“Wait,” he said. “I can guess it. Four minutes’ thinking and I can tell you your whole story.”

“All right,” Father Brown said mildly. “You tell it.”

They had come to a place where the trees fell back and the road dipped into a moonlit hollow, then rose again and ran into another wood. The entrance to the farther forest looked like a black, round mouth—like a tunnel bored through the night.

Flambeau leaned forward, his great shoulders driving him on, and spoke in a lowered voice, as if the trees could overhear.

“St. Clare came of a family with hereditary madness. He hid it from his daughter and from Keith. He believed his mind was failing, and resolved on suicide—but he couldn’t do it openly, because that would proclaim the very taint he feared. So, as the campaign approached and his brain clouded, he sacrificed duty to privacy. He flung himself into a hopeless battle hoping to die by a bullet. When he was captured and disgraced instead, he broke. He broke his own sword and hanged himself.”

He stared into the dark opening of the wood. The shape of it, swallowing the road, seemed to give his imagination a final shove. He shivered.

“A horrid story,” he said.

“A horrid story,” Father Brown echoed, his head bent. Then he threw his head back with something like anguish. “Oh, I wish it had been.”

Flambeau turned on him. Even in the moonlight the priest’s face looked strained, not with fear, but with a kind of disgust—like a man who has tasted something spoiled and cannot forget the taste.

“Yours is clean,” Father Brown said, and his voice broke with feeling. “Sweet and honest—as open and white as that moon. Madness and despair are innocent enough. There are worse things.”

Flambeau looked up. A black bough curved across the moon like a horn.

“Do you mean,” he said, “worse than that?”

“Worse than that,” Father Brown replied, flat as a verdict.

They plunged into the second wood. The trunks went by like pillars in a cloister, and the road ran between them like a corridor in a dream. In that thick dark the world’s little sounds grew larger: the dry squeak of leather, the faint clink of a buckle, the distant owl-note that seemed, in such stillness, a deliberate musical phrase. Father Brown’s small voice, when it came, sounded close to the ear, like someone speaking across a kitchen table while the rest of the house sleeps.

“Where does a wise man hide a leaf? In the forest. But what does he do if there is no forest?”

Flambeau’s patience cracked like ice. “Well? What does he do?”

“He grows a forest to hide it in,” Father Brown said. His tone was obscure, almost reluctant. “A fearful sin.”

Flambeau cursed under his breath, more at the darkness than at the man. “Tell me the evidence. Something solid.”

“There are three more bits,” Father Brown said. “I’ll give them in logical order, not chronological.

“First, the official account of the battle is Olivier’s own despatches. They’re lucid. He was entrenched with regiments on heights sloping down to the Black River. On the near side was lower, marshy ground. Beyond that, gently rising country with an English outpost, supported by others further back. The British force overall was superior, but this particular regiment was far enough from its base to tempt Olivier for a moment into crossing the river to cut it off. By sunset he decided not to; his position was too strong to abandon.

“At daybreak he was thunderstruck to see the stray handful of English, entirely unsupported, fling themselves across—some by the bridge to the right, some by a ford higher up—and mass on the marshy bank below him.

“Even that was mad. But it was madder still that, instead of pushing for firm ground, they simply stayed stuck in the mire like flies in treacle. The Brazilians tore gaps in them with artillery. The English answered with spirited rifle fire that dwindled as men fell. Yet they never broke. Olivier ends with admiration for what he calls their mystic valour. He writes that their line advanced, drove them into the river, captured St. Clare and other officers; the colonel and major fell. He adds that few sights have been finer than the last stand—wounded officers picking up the rifles of the dead—and the general himself facing them on horseback, bareheaded, with a broken sword.”

“And then?” Flambeau demanded.

“On what happened to the general after,” Father Brown said, “Olivier is as silent as Keith.

“Second piece: I found an old soldier in an almshouse down in the Lincolnshire Fens—one of the wounded from Black River. He had knelt beside the colonel when he died. The colonel was Clancy, an Irishman, big as an ox and apparently dying of rage as much as bullets. He wasn’t responsible for that absurd raid; it was imposed by the general. His last words—edifying in their way—were: ‘And there goes the damned old donkey with the end of his sword knocked off. I wish it was his head.’”

Flambeau grunted. “Everybody notices that broken sword.”

“Yes,” Father Brown said. “Everybody. Now the third fragment.

“Only a month or two ago a Brazilian official died in England. He’d quarrelled with Olivier and left the country. A Spaniard named Espado—yellow-faced old dandy, hooked nose. For private reasons I had permission to see his papers. He was a Catholic; I attended him towards the end.

“Among his things there was nothing about St. Clare except a bundle of common exercise books—someone’s diary. An English soldier’s, from the look of it. I suppose it was found on a body. It stops abruptly the night before the battle.

“I have it on me, but it’s too dark to read. I’ll give you the substance.

“That last entry begins with jokes—soldiers’ jokes—about someone called the Vulture. This person isn’t one of them, and doesn’t sound like an Englishman, nor exactly an enemy. More like a local go-between—guide, interpreter, journalist, something of that kind. He has been closeted with Colonel Clancy, but is more often seen talking to the major.

“The major stands out in the account: Murray, lean, dark-haired, north of Ireland, Puritan. The men joke about the contrast between the Ulsterman’s austerity and Clancy’s conviviality. There’s also a running jest about the Vulture’s bright-coloured clothes.

“Then the jokes are broken by something that reads like a bugle-call. Behind the English camp, almost parallel to the river, ran one of the few great roads. Westward it curved towards the river and crossed it by the bridge. Eastward it ran back into the wild, and two miles along it lay the next English outpost.

“From that eastern direction, that evening, came a glitter and clatter of light cavalry, and the diarist recognised with astonishment the general with his staff. St. Clare rode the great white horse you’ve seen in pictures; and the salute they gave him wasn’t mere form. He wasted no time, sprang down, joined the officers, and spoke emphatically and confidentially.

“What struck the diarist was how much he spoke to Major Murray. It wasn’t unnatural—both were Bible-reading, evangelical sort of officers. But it is certain that when St. Clare mounted again he was still talking earnestly to Murray, and that as he walked his horse slowly down the road towards the river, Murray walked by the bridle-rein, arguing or debating. The soldiers watched until they vanished behind trees where the road turned towards the bridge.

“The colonel went back to his tent. The men went to their pickets. The diarist lingered—four minutes—and saw a marvellous thing.

“The great white horse came flying back up the road as if it were mad to win a race. At first they thought it had bolted with its rider; then they saw the general himself urging it at full speed. He swept up like a whirlwind, reined in, and turned on them a face like flame. He called for the colonel—his voice like a trumpet that wakes the dead.

“After that, the writer’s mind tumbles events together as men do in panic. They found themselves falling into ranks. An attack was to be led at once across the river. The general and the major, it was said, had found out something at the bridge, and there was only just time to strike for life. The major had gone back at once to call up the reserve along the road behind; it was doubtful even then whether help could reach them. But they must cross that night and seize the heights by morning.

“And there, with the stir and throb of that nocturnal march just beginning, the diary ends.”

As Father Brown spoke, the woodland path began to climb. It narrowed and twisted, like a staircase corkscrewing up inside a tower. The priest went a little ahead, and his voice came down out of the darkness above them, steady now, business-like, as if he were measuring ingredients with care—because the dish, once begun, could not be unmade.

Father Brown, by both vocation and temperament, knew what most men forget until they are made to remember it: death ennobles. A corpse, however comic the living man had been, lies with a kind of grave authority, as if all the jokes had been folded and put away with the linen. Yet even Father Brown felt the queer jar when he was shaken awake at daybreak with the news that Sir Aaron Armstrong had been murdered.

It was not only horror; it was incongruity. Secret violence seemed indecent when fastened upon a figure so public, so hearty, so noisily beloved. Sir Aaron had the boisterous sainthood of the platform—philanthropy performed like after-dinner entertainment, morality seasoned with anecdotes and served hot with “loud laughter.” He preached temperance the way a prosperous man praises the weather, with the unwearying gaiety of one who has never been made humble by thirst. People repeated, as if it were a family legend, the story of his conversion: drawn as a boy from Scotch theology to Scotch whisky, and—by some robust miracle—rising out of both to become what he called, with cheery modesty, “himself.” With his wide white beard, cherubic face, and spectacles that flashed like sugar in sunlight, it was hard to imagine him ever shadowed by anything so sombre as either Calvinism or a dram.

His house stood on the rural skirt of Hampstead—handsome, narrow, and high, like a modern tower that had forgotten it was meant to be lived in. One of its tight sides overhung the steep green bank of a railway cutting, so close that the passing trains shook its bones. Sir Aaron, laughing, declared he had no nerves. That morning, if a house could have nerves, it was the house that screamed.

A train coming round the curve slowed sharply and stopped just beyond the point where the corner of the building seemed to press upon the slope. Machines rarely halt for passion; this one halted for a passion that had been flung at it like a stone.

On the ridge above the engine stood a man dressed wholly in black—even to black gloves, a detail so theatrical it might have been comic on any other morning. He waved his dark hands like a windmill cut from soot. That alone would not have stopped a train. But then came a cry, not merely loud but monstrously articulate: one of those shouts whose very cleanness makes them uncanny even before the meaning reaches you. The word, in this case, was “Murder!”

The driver afterward swore he would have stopped even if he had not caught the word—because the sound had been like a bell struck close to his ear, a note too definite to belong to ordinary fear.

With the train halted, the bare facts lay almost indecently open. The man in black was Magnus, Sir Aaron’s servant—long the butt of his master’s jovial jibes for those funereal gloves. No one laughed now. A few passengers clambered down the embankment, through the smoky hedge that lived on cinders and neglect, and saw, rolled almost to the bottom of the turf, the body of an old man in a yellow dressing-gown lined with vivid scarlet. A scrap of rope was caught about his leg, as if the dead had been snagged by a last clumsy hand. There was blood, though surprisingly little, and the body lay bent into a shape a living spine cannot take. Sir Aaron Armstrong had tumbled into dignity by being broken.

A moment later a big, fair-bearded man burst from the house: Patrick Royce, the secretary—once known in Bohemian circles, once even praised in the Bohemian arts, now carrying the look of a strong animal that has been harnessed too long to small duties. He echoed Magnus’s agony in a blunter key. Then Alice Armstrong, the dead man’s daughter, came tottering out into the garden, lifting her hands as if to ward off noise. Before anyone could do more than gape, the driver had restarted his engine; the train panted on toward the next station to fetch help, leaving behind it a sudden hush that made the birds and distant clatter of the rails sound like the world trying, unsuccessfully, to be gentle.

Father Brown was summoned at Patrick Royce’s request. Royce, Irish by birth and the sort of Catholic who remembers his religion only when the ground gives way beneath him, had asked for a priest as a man clutches at a railing. His request might have waited had not one of the detectives been an admirer—by way of Flambeau—of Father Brown’s odd successes. So Father Brown found himself walking beside a young official named Merton across fields that still held the cold scent of dawn—wet grass, distant soot, and the faint metallic tang of the line.

“As far as I can see,” Merton said frankly, “there’s no sense in it. There’s nobody to suspect. Magnus is a solemn old fool—too much of a fool to murder. Royce has been Armstrong’s friend for years. And the daughter adored him. Besides—it’s absurd. Who would kill a cheerful old chap like Armstrong? It would be like killing Father Christmas.”

“Yes,” said Father Brown, “it was a cheery house. While he was alive. Do you think it will be cheery now he is dead?”

Merton glanced at him.

“Now he is dead?” he repeated, as if the phrase had some new edge.

“He was cheerful,” the priest went on. “But did he make anyone else cheerful? Honestly—was anybody in that house cheerful except him?”

Something opened in Merton’s mind the way a window opens on a room you have always been in and never quite seen. He had been at Armstrong’s on little police errands connected with the philanthropist’s work. He remembered, suddenly, the peculiar chill of the place: rooms too high, too bare; provincial decorations that tried to be “bright” and only looked mean; corridors draughty as a passage in a station. Electric light—bleak and steady—made everything look colder than moonlight. Sir Aaron’s scarlet face and white beard had blazed through it all like a bonfire carried from room to room, but when he passed, the heat went with him. He had joked about not needing lamps or stoves, saying he carried his own warmth. The joke, Merton saw now, was true—and cruel.

Magnus, with his black gloves, had always seemed like a walking shadow. Royce looked solid—a big man in tweeds, with a short fair beard—but the beard was salted with grey, the forehead barred with premature lines, and his good nature had the tired tenderness of somebody who has been defeated and has not quite admitted it. As for Alice—how could she be Sir Aaron’s daughter? She was pallid, delicate, her very outline quivering as if she had learned to tremble in time with the trains that hammered past the house.

“You see,” Father Brown said, blinking, “I’m not sure the Armstrong cheerfulness was so cheerful—for other people. You say nobody could kill such a happy old man. But—ne nos inducas in tentationem. If ever I murdered somebody,” he added simply, “I dare say it might be an Optimist.”

Merton laughed in spite of himself. “Why? Do you think people dislike cheerfulness?”

“People like frequent laughter,” said Father Brown. “But I don’t think they like a permanent smile. Cheerfulness without humour is a very trying thing.”

They walked on along the windy bank. The rails below made their own steady hush, the sort of sound that is never silent even when no train is passing: a faint singing of iron, interrupted now and then by the harsh peck of a rook, the dry chatter of leaves. As the tall narrow house rose above them, Father Brown spoke as if throwing away a thought he did not want.

“Drink is neither good nor bad in itself,” he said. “But I sometimes feel men like Armstrong want an occasional glass of wine to sadden them.”

On the green bank Inspector Gilder waited for the coroner. He was grizzled, capable, and looked at things under heavy lids as if he had seen enough of them already. He was speaking to Patrick Royce, whose broad shoulders and bristly beard towered above him. Royce, though so large, moved with a powerful stoop, as if his strength had been bent into service. He lifted his head with genuine relief at the sight of the priest and drew him a little aside. Merton, meanwhile, went to Gilder with respectful impatience.

“Well, Mr. Gilder—have you got any farther?”

“There is no mystery,” said Gilder, staring dreamily at the rooks.

“There is for me,” Merton insisted, smiling.

“It’s simple enough,” the older man observed, stroking his pointed grey beard. “Three minutes after you went for Mr. Royce’s parson, the whole thing came out. You know that servant—black gloves—stopped the train?”

“I should know him anywhere. He gives one the creeps.”

“Well,” said Gilder, “when the train went on, that man went too. Cool criminal, eh? Escapes on the very train that runs for the police.”

“You’re sure he did it?”

“Yes,” replied Gilder drily, “for the trifling reason that he has gone off with twenty thousand pounds’ worth of papers from his master’s desk. The only difficulty is how he killed him. The skull looks broken by some big weapon, but there’s no weapon lying about—and he’d have found it awkward to carry it away unless it was small enough not to be noticed.”

“Perhaps it was too big to be noticed,” Father Brown said, and gave an odd little giggle.

Gilder looked round sharply. “What do you mean?”

“Silly way of saying it,” Father Brown apologized at once. “Sounds like a fairy tale. But poor Armstrong was killed with a giant’s club—a great green club, too big to see, called the earth. He was broken against this bank.”

“How?”

Father Brown tipped his round face up toward the blind narrow façade of the house. At the top an attic window stood open.

“Don’t you see?” he said, pointing awkwardly like a child. “He was thrown down from there.”

Gilder frowned, measuring distances with his eye. “It’s possible. But why are you sure?”

Brown opened his pale eyes wide. “Because there’s a bit of rope round the dead man’s leg. And there’s the other end—caught up there at the corner of the window.”

At that height it was no more than a faint thread against the sky, but the old detective’s instinct accepted it. “You’re quite right, sir,” he said. “That’s one to you.”

Almost as he spoke, a special train with a single carriage slid round the curve and stopped. It disgorged policemen—and between them, the hangdog face of Magnus.

“By Jove—they’ve got him,” Gilder cried, suddenly brisk, and stepped forward. “Have you got the money?”

The first policeman looked at him with a curious expression. “No. At least—not here.”

“Which is the inspector, please?” asked Magnus.

When he spoke, everyone understood how a voice could stop a train. Magnus was dull-looking: flat black hair, colourless face, and a faint hint of the East in the narrow slits of his eyes and mouth. His origin had always been dubious; Sir Aaron had “rescued” him from a London restaurant, and some whispered from worse. But the voice was bright where the face was dead: a ringing, piercing exactitude, as if he spoke every word against a bell.

“I always knew this would happen,” he said with brazen calm. “My poor old master made game of me for wearing black; but I always said I should be ready for his funeral.”

His gloved hands made a slight motion, as if smoothing invisible cuffs.

“Sergeant,” said Gilder, eyeing those black hands, “put the bracelets on him. He looks dangerous.”

“Well, sir,” the sergeant replied—with the same odd wonder—“I don’t know that we can.”

“What do you mean? Haven’t you arrested him?”

A thin scorn widened Magnus’s slit mouth. In the distance a train-whistle began to cry, and somehow its cold note seemed to echo his mockery.

“We arrested him,” said the sergeant gravely, “as he was coming out of the police station at Highgate—where he had deposited all his master’s money in the care of Inspector Robinson.”

Gilder stared. “Why on earth did you do that?”

“To keep it safe from the criminal, of course,” Magnus replied, untroubled.

“Surely Sir Aaron’s money might have been safely left with Sir Aaron’s family,” Gilder began.

The end of his sentence was drowned by a train that came rocking and clanking past, filling the cutting with a brutal industrial thunder that shook the grass and made speech seem foolish. Yet through all that metallic uproar, they heard Magnus’s answer as clearly as if he had spoken into their very bones: “I have no reason to feel confidence in Sir Aaron’s family.”

In that moment, with the train’s noise grinding on, it seemed to Merton that the soundscape of the place itself was part of the cruelty: the house perched above the rails like a human ear forced to listen to iron forever, never granted the mercy of quiet in which a conscience can hear itself. When the roar lessened, there was a ghostly sense as if someone else had entered the group. Merton looked up and saw Alice Armstrong’s pale face over Father Brown’s shoulder.

She was young, beautiful in a silvery way, but her brown hair was so ashen it could look grey in certain light. She had the expression of one who has lived too long under sudden noises.

“Be careful what you say,” Royce growled at Magnus. “You’ll frighten Miss Armstrong.”

“I hope so,” said Magnus, with his clear bell-voice.

Everyone stared. Alice winced as if struck by a draught. Magnus continued, bland and brutal in his exactness.

“I am used to Miss Armstrong’s tremors. I have seen her trembling off and on for years. Some said she shook with cold, some with fear—but I know she shook with hate and wicked anger. Fiends that have had their feast this morning. She would have been away by now with her lover and all the money but for me. Ever since my poor old master prevented her marrying that tipsy blackguard—”

“Stop,” said Gilder, very stern. “We’ve nothing to do with your family fancies unless you have evidence. Your opinions—”

“Oh, I’ll give you practical evidence,” Magnus cut in sharply. “You will have to subpoena me, Mr. Inspector, and I shall have to tell the truth. The truth is: an instant after the old man was pitched bleeding out of the window, I ran into the attic and found his daughter swooning on the floor with a red dagger still in her hand. Allow me to hand that also to the proper authorities.”

From his tail-pocket he produced a long horn-hilted knife with a red smear and offered it politely to the sergeant. He stepped back. His eyes narrowed into almost nothing; his face became a fat sneer.

Merton felt sick, and muttered to Gilder, “Surely you’d take Miss Armstrong’s word against his.”

Father Brown lifted his face. It looked absurdly fresh, almost as if he had just washed it in cold water. “Yes,” he said, with radiant innocence, “but is Miss Armstrong’s word against his?”

Alice gave a small, strangled cry. Everyone looked at her. Her body was rigid, as if caught in a sudden noose; only her face was alive with appalling surprise.

“This man,” said Gilder gravely, “says you were found grasping a knife, insensible, after the murder.”

“He says the truth,” Alice answered.

What followed was so swift it felt like a dream in which logic has been removed. Patrick Royce strode into their ring, his great head still stooped, and said with a strange harsh gaiety: “Well, if I’ve got to go, I’ll have a bit of pleasure first.”

His huge shoulder heaved. His fist smashed into Magnus’s bland visage and laid him flat on the lawn like a flung rag. Police hands instantly caught Royce.

“None of that, Mr. Royce,” Gilder called. “I’ll arrest you for assault.”

“No, you won’t,” Royce answered, his voice like an iron gong. “You will arrest me for murder.”

Gilder glanced at Magnus. The servant was already sitting up, wiping blood from a face that looked scarcely damaged. Gilder turned back. “What do you mean?”

“It is true,” Royce said, “that Miss Armstrong fainted with a knife in her hand. But she snatched the knife not to attack her father—but to defend him.”

“To defend him,” Gilder repeated, measured and grim. “Against whom?”

“Against me,” Royce replied.

Alice looked at him, and her expression was a tangle of dread, love, and something like bewildered gratitude. Then she said softly, “After it all, I am still glad you are brave.”

“Come upstairs,” Royce said heavily. “I will show you the whole cursed thing.”

The attic—Royce’s private place, a cell too small for so large a hermit—still held the stale breath of violent night: smoke that had seeped into plaster, the sour ghost of spirits, the dry dust of neglected corners. On the floor near the centre lay a large revolver, flung away. Nearer the left, a whisky bottle lay on its side, uncorked and not quite empty. The cloth on a little table was dragged and trampled. A length of cord—like that found on the corpse—lay across the windowsill. Two vases were smashed on the mantelpiece, and one on the carpet.

“I was drunk,” Royce said. The bluntness of it, in that prematurely battered man, had the pathos of a child confessing its first theft.

“You all know me,” he went on hoarsely. “Everybody knows how my story began, and it may as well end like that too. They called me clever once—and I might have been happy. Armstrong saved what was left of brain and body from taverns, and was always kind to me in his own way—poor fellow. Only he wouldn’t let me marry Alice; and it will always be said he was right. You can draw your own conclusions. That is my whisky bottle half emptied; that is my revolver quite emptied. It was rope from my box found on the corpse. It was from my window the corpse was thrown. Don’t set detectives to grub up my tragedy; it’s a common weed in this world. I give myself to the gallows; and, by God, that is enough.”

At a sign, the policemen moved in to take him—quietly, with the awkward tenderness that sometimes comes over men handling a living body when death is close.

But Father Brown, who had been invisible for a moment, was suddenly on his hands and knees in the doorway as if in undignified prayer. He was utterly indifferent to how ridiculous he looked: a small creature on all fours with a human head turned up, comic and candid.

“I say,” he remarked pleasantly, “this really won’t do at all, you know. At the beginning you said we couldn’t find a weapon. But now we are finding too many. There’s the knife to stab, and the rope to strangle, and the pistol to shoot—and after all he broke his neck by falling out of a window. It won’t do. It’s not economical.”

He shook his head toward the floor as a horse does when grazing.

Gilder opened his mouth to rebuke him, but the priest went on, quick and homely, still kneeling as if the carpet were his pulpit.

“And three quite impossible things. First—these holes in the carpet where six bullets have gone in. Why on earth should anybody fire at the carpet? A drunken man shoots at the enemy’s head, the thing grinning at him. He doesn’t pick a quarrel with feet, or lay siege to slippers. And then the rope—” Father Brown, finishing with the carpet, slipped his hands into his pockets but remained on his knees—“in what conceivable intoxication would anybody try to put a rope round a man’s neck and finally put it round his leg? Royce isn’t so drunk as that—or he’d be sleeping like a log by now. And plainest of all, the whisky bottle. You suggest a dipsomaniac fought for the bottle, and then having won it, rolled it away into a corner, spilling one half and leaving the other. That is the very last thing a dipsomaniac would do.”

He scrambled awkwardly to his feet and faced the self-accused man with a look of limpid penitence.

“I’m awfully sorry, my dear sir,” he said, “but your tale is really rubbish.”

“Sir,” Alice Armstrong said low to Father Brown, “can I speak to you alone for a moment?”

She drew him into the next room—out of the doorway where policemen waited and the attic smelled of powder and spirits. Her voice changed; it became sharp, almost like a knife itself.

“You are a clever man,” she said, “and you are trying to save Patrick. But it is no use. The core of all this is black, and the more you find out the more there will be against the miserable man I love.”

“Why?” asked Father Brown, looking at her steadily.

“Because,” she said, meeting his gaze, “I saw him commit the crime myself.”

“Ah,” said Father Brown. He did not flinch. “And what did he do?”

“I was in this room next to them,” she answered. “Both doors were shut. Suddenly I heard a voice—such as I had never heard on earth—roaring ‘Hell, hell, hell,’ again and again. Then the two doors shook with the first explosion of the revolver. Three times again it banged before I could get the doors open. The room was full of smoke; the pistol was smoking in my poor mad Patrick’s hand; and I saw him fire the last volley with my own eyes. Then he leapt on my father—my father was clinging in terror to the window-sill—and Patrick grappled him and tried to strangle him with the rope. He threw it over his head, but it slipped over his struggling shoulders down to his feet. Then it tightened round one leg, and Patrick dragged him like a maniac. I snatched a knife from the mat; I rushed between them; I cut the rope—and then I fainted.”

“I see,” Father Brown said, with the same wooden civility as if she had described a drawing-room accident. “Thank you.”
