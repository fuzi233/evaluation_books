The hour had come when a secret, long kept warm in careful hands, must be set down plainly like a dish brought at last from the oven—steaming, fragrant, and too full of meaning to be tasted without trembling.

Daniel had been preparing everything with the same quiet exactness with which a good cook lays out knives, bowls, and linen before the guests arrive: nothing showy, nothing wasted, and yet the whole ordered to spare pain. A new lodging in Brompton had been found—near enough to the Meyricks for daily help, near enough that footsteps could pass between houses as easily as a spoon between cup and saucer. Mrs. Meyrick, who had been taken into confidence on all points save one, had moved about this business with a mother’s brisk tenderness and a Highland secrecy: she could keep a pot from boiling over, and she could keep a story from spilling out.

She had chosen the rooms with an eye for comfort—good bedding first, then warmth, then the small decent prettiness that makes a poor place feel less like exile. She had already pasted paper over an extra window to break the draught, as if she could paste over fate’s cold fingers too.

Yet her heart did not leap without reserve. The word “brother” had a music in it, but it could also bring a new key into the household—one that might drown the softer tunes she had been hoping to hear. She had pictured Mirah, day by day, relaxing her fierce clinging to Judaism in the gentle English air of affection and ordinary life—like strong tea gradually softened by milk. And she had seen, too, with a little private fondness, Hans’s hopes rising like bread in a warm kitchen. Now this brother—this Ezra—threatened to steep Mirah again in the darkest, most indelible dye of her inherited feeling.

Standing in the destined lodging, with its clean bareness and faint smell of paste and new-swept boards, Mrs. Meyrick could not help saying what sat on her palate like a taste she had never grown to like.

“I’m as glad as you are that the pawnbroker isn’t her brother,” she said. “There are Ezras and Ezras in the world; and it’s a comfort to think all Jews aren’t like those shopkeepers who will not let you get out of their shops. Besides, what he said about his mother and sister—bless him for that. I’m sure he’s good. But I never did like anything fanatical. I suppose I heard too much preaching in my youth and lost my palate for it.”

“I don’t think you’ll find that Mordecai obtrudes any preaching,” Daniel answered. “He isn’t what I call fanatical. I call a man fanatical when his enthusiasm is narrow—when it blinds him and makes him unjust to those not on his own track. Mordecai is an enthusiast in a wider sense—one of those minds that care most for general good. He’s not strictly orthodox; he has room in him for others. The people he lives with love him; they can’t understand his ideas, but they’re proud of him all the same.”

“Oh, well,” she returned, half yielding, half protesting, “I can live up to the level of the pawnbroker’s mother, and like a man for what I see good in him; and what I can’t see I’ll take on your word. According to your definition, one might be fanatical about common-sense; and my poor husband used to say the world would be a poor place if there were nothing but common-sense in it. Anyway, Mirah’s brother shall have good bedding—that I’ve seen to; and I’ll have this extra window pasted up to prevent draughts. It’s a comfort the people of the house are no strangers to me—no hypocritical harpies. When the children know, we can make the rooms prettier.”

“The next stage is to tell all to Mordecai, and persuade him to move,” Daniel said; and in his voice there was the same cautiousness with which one lifts a lid to see whether the simmering has gone too far.

“And you’ll tell Mirah before I tell my girls?” Mrs. Meyrick asked quickly.

He hesitated—because his whole care was to keep Mirah from any agitating suspicion until the moment could be made safe and right.

“No,” she decided for him, gently but firmly, as if she were choosing the proper moment to add spice. “Let me tell Hans and the girls the evening before; they’ll be away next morning.”

“Yes. That will be best. Only—do justice to my account of Mordecai… don’t assist their imagination by referring him to Habakkuk Mucklewrath.”

She laughed, and her laugh was like a kettle beginning to sing against anxiety. “Trust me. I shall have to persuade them so hard to be glad that I shall convert myself. When I’m frightened, it does me good to have somebody to be angry with for not being brave: it warms the blood.”

Daniel might have argued longer for Mordecai, but his mind was already deep in the more delicate work ahead—how to disclose the truth without wounding the Cohens, whose kindness had been given in daily portions like bread and broth, without fine words, but with the sustaining power of habit and affection. He could not forget Mordecai’s keen sensitiveness to any slight upon those who had sheltered him.

In the interval he had met Mordecai at the Hand and Banner; but now he wrote, asking leave to see him in his own home and sit with him in the workroom for an hour—carefully adding that if the family objected, Mordecai should accompany him elsewhere. He meant to set expectation stirring like yeast—enough to lift the mind, not enough to overflow it.

When he came the next evening he was received with the same friendliness as before, but heightened—women and children in a little extra finery, candles attended to, and on the elders’ faces a restraint of wondering. Even Cohen, who loved to feel there were mysteries just beyond his grasp—mysteries that proved he had a gentleman in his parlor—kept his curiosity within the decent bounds of silence.

“I suppose Mordecai is at home and expecting me?” Daniel said.

Little Jacob, who had been drinking in family whispers, trotted up and asked boldly, “What do you want to talk to Mordecai about?”

“Something very interesting to him,” Daniel said, pinching the boy’s ear with grave kindness, “but that you can’t understand.”

“Can you say this?” Jacob cried, and immediately poured out a string of Hebrew verses—throaty, nasal, and triumphant—nodding as if each syllable were a pebble flung to prove his strength.

“No, really,” Daniel answered, still keeping his face steady; “I can’t say anything like it.”

“I thought not,” Jacob said, and danced on his scarlet legs, plunging hands into deep knickerbocker pockets to draw out a miscellany of possessions and push them back again, a small pantomime of resource. Then, racing to the workroom door, he flung it open wide, planted his back against it, and called in his father’s borrowed phrase, “Mordecai, here’s the young swell!”

Hushes flew at him from mother and grandmother. Daniel went in and closed the door behind him.

In the workroom a bit of carpet had been laid down; a chair placed; the fire brightened; the candles set as if their small flames were meant to warm more than air. It had that mingled smell of a close room—woollen cloth, paper, dust held in by winter shutters—cut through by the sharper, comforting scent of coal and the faint animal sweetness of leather somewhere in the house. Outside, the street had its thin industrial murmur: wheels, distant shouting, a world that never fully rests. But in here, the fire made a softer sound—the steady crackle that lets the mind unclench.

Mordecai rose; and in his face Daniel saw an expectant solemnity, so intense it might have belonged to a man who had been promised revelation itself.

Neither spoke at once. Daniel drew the chair from the opposite side of the hearth and seated himself near, in his usual way of making his nearness feel like shelter.

Mordecai said at last, in a tone of fervent certainty, “You are coming to tell me something that my soul longs for.”

“It is true I have something very weighty to tell you—something I trust you will rejoice in,” Daniel said, guarding himself, for he felt that Mordecai’s longing might already have shaped the news into another form.

“It is all revealed—it is made clear to you,” Mordecai said, leaning forward, hands clasped. “You are even as my brother that sucked the breasts of my mother—the heritage is yours—there is no doubt to divide us.”

“I have learned nothing new about myself,” Daniel answered.

The disappointment fell like a sudden extinguishing. Mordecai sank back, closing his eyes, as if the day’s strain had snapped inside him; the reaction made him almost sick.

Daniel waited a moment, then added softly, “Except—that I had, some time ago, come into another sort of hidden connection with you, besides what you have spoken of as existing in your own feeling.”

The closed lids fluttered.

“I had made the acquaintance of one in whom you are interested,” Daniel went on, still trying to lead him gently, as one leads a fevered patient toward a draught he must drink.

“One who is closely related to your departed mother,” Daniel said; and seeing a shrinking movement, he added quickly, “whom she and you held dear above all others.”

Mordecai started and clutched Daniel’s wrist with a spasmodic grasp. Terror ran through him—swift, animal, voiceless. Daniel understood at once what he feared.

With a tremor in his clear tones he said, “What was prayed for has come to pass: Mirah has been delivered from evil.”

The grasp loosened, but Mordecai was panting, a tearless sob breaking in him like surf behind clenched teeth.

Daniel added, “Your sister is worthy of the mother you honored.”

He paused. Mordecai flung himself back, eyes closed again; his lips moved almost inaudibly in Hebrew for some minutes, the words flowing as if they had been stored up like grain in a bin against famine. Then the murmuring ceased, and silence came—full, sweet, and strange. In the uplift of his face there was something suffused, as if a long inward drought had been answered. Daniel, watching, could almost imagine Mordecai speaking with someone beloved. For the first time he thought he caught a family likeness to Mirah—something in the line of brow and the softened look, like the same recipe carried in two different hands.

When Mordecai was ready to listen, Daniel told the rest. He spoke of Mirah’s flight, keeping the father’s conduct as vague as truth allowed; laying the emphasis on her yearning for England as the place where her mother might be found. He did not tell of her purpose to drown herself, nor of his rescue—keeping that sharp, private anguish out of the story, as one keeps the bitterest spice from a dish meant to comfort the sick. He spoke instead of the home she had found with friends of his, the shelter given her, the effort she was making to secure her own maintenance—how dignity often demands dependence be carried on one’s own shoulders, unseen.

He dwelt most on Mirah’s feeling for her mother and brother, giving detail upon detail, because Mordecai’s mind fed on particulars the way a starving man feeds on bread.

“It was in search of you,” Daniel said at last, with a smile that trembled into warmth, “that I turned into this house. The name Ezra Cohen was just then the most interesting name in the world to me. I confess I had fear for a long while. Perhaps you will forgive me now for having asked that question about the elder Mrs. Cohen’s daughter. I cared very much what I should find Mirah’s friends to be. But I had found a brother worthy of her when I knew that her Ezra was disguised under the name of Mordecai.”

“Mordecai is really my name,” he said. “Ezra Mordecai Cohen.”

“Is there kinship between this family and yours?” Daniel asked.

“Only the kinship of Israel,” Mordecai answered. “My soul clings to these people who have sheltered me, given me succor out of the affection that abides in Jewish hearts, as sweet odor in things long crushed and hidden from the outer air. It is good for me to bear with their ignorance and be bound to them in gratitude, that I may keep in mind the spiritual poverty of the Jewish million, and not put impatient knowledge in the stead of loving wisdom.”

Daniel’s desire returned upon him with urgency—he could not bear that Mordecai should continue in cramped air and labor when he might have rest. He spoke as one pleads for a beloved elder, with a tenderness that did not weaken the claim in it.

“But you don’t feel bound to continue with them now there is a closer tie?” he said. “It seems right now—is it not?—that you should live with your sister. I have prepared a home to take you to in the neighborhood of her friends, that she may join you there. Grant me this wish. It will enable me to be with you often in the hours when Mirah is obliged to leave you—that is my selfish reason. But the chief reason is, that Mirah will desire to watch over you, and you ought to give her the guardianship of a brother’s presence. You shall have books about you. I shall want to learn of you, and to take you out to see the river and trees. And you will have the rest and comfort that you will be more and more in need of—nay, that I need for you. This is the claim I make on you, now that we have found each other.”

Mordecai listened without moving, eyes fixed with that look which made Daniel feel as if he stood before an inward tribunal where words were weighed for more than their surface meaning. When Daniel ceased, Mordecai said, with an almost reproachful emphasis,

“And you would have me hold it doubtful whether you were born a Jew! Have we not from the first touched each other with invisible fibres—have we not quivered together like the leaves from a common stem with stirring from a common root? I know what I am outwardly. I am one among the crowd of poor—I am stricken, I am dying. But our souls know each other. They gazed in silence as those who have long been parted and meet again, but when they found voice they were assured, and all their speech is understanding. The life of Israel is in your veins.”

Daniel sat perfectly still, his face tingling as if the air had changed. It was impossible to deny, impossible to assent. He waited, craving not metaphysics but an answer.

After a pause Mordecai said firmly, “What you wish of me I will do. And our mother—may the blessing of the Eternal be with her in our souls!—would have wished it too. I will accept what your loving kindness has prepared, and Mirah’s home shall be mine.” Then, with a shadow of sadness, “But I shall grieve to part from these parents and the little ones. You must tell them, for my heart would fail me.”

“I felt you would want me to tell them. Shall we go now—at once?” Daniel asked, relief loosening his breath.

“Yes; let us not defer it. It must be done,” Mordecai said, rising with the air of a man going to meet pain because duty commands it. Then, as if guarding a tender place, he added, “But do not dwell on my sister more than is needful.”

In the parlor Jacob was alert as a sparrow. Mordecai said, “Ask your father to come, and tell Sarah to mind the shop. My friend has something to say,” he added to the elder Mrs. Cohen. The word friend, applied to a gentleman like Daniel, always seemed part of Mordecai’s peculiarities; still, the women drew themselves up into their best manners, as if politeness were a shawl to be put on in cold weather.

When Cohen entered, a pen behind his ear, he rubbed his hands with loud satisfaction. “Well, sir! Glad you’re doing us the honor to join our family party again. We’re pretty comfortable, I think.”

He looked round with shiny gladness. The fire threw its rich light over the little group: Baby under a scarlet quilt, rocked by the young mother; Adelaide Rebekah on the grandmother’s knee; Jacob wedged between his father’s legs; and in the middle, the two contrasted figures—Daniel with his composed gentleness, and Mordecai a little backward in the shade, as if he would hide how every sound struck him. The room had its familiar domestic music: the faint creak of rocking, the soft cough of coal, the close human rustle of clothes. Outside, London’s ceaseless metallic undertone threatened the tenderness within, but for the moment the hearth held its own small kingdom of warmth.

“I have just been telling Mordecai of an event that makes a great change in his life,” Daniel began, “but I hope you will agree with me that it is a joyful one. Since he thinks of you as his best friends, he wishes me to tell you for him at once.”

“Relations with money, sir?” Cohen burst out, as if divination were a kind of trade and he must show his skill.

“No; not exactly,” Daniel said, smiling. “But a very precious relation wishes to be reunited to him—a very good and lovely young sister, who will care for his comfort in every way.”

“Married, sir?”

“No, not married.”

“But with a maintenance?”

“With talents which will secure her a maintenance. A home is already provided for Mordecai.”

Silence fell, heavy as a lid. Then the grandmother began in a wailing tone, “Well, well! and so you’re going away from us, Mordecai.”

“And where there’s no children as there is here,” said the mother, catching the wail as if it were a tune she could not keep from joining.

“No Jacob, and no Adelaide, and no Eugenie!” the grandmother wailed again.

“Ay, ay,” Cohen said decisively, as if business must be found even inside sorrow. “Jacob’s learning ’ill all wear out of him. He must go to school. It’ll be hard times for Jacob.”

In Jacob’s wide-open ears those words dropped like a sentence. Up till then Mordecai’s going away was a strange notion that might be revoked at any moment, like a threat adults forget; but “hard times for Jacob” made it real. He broke into loud lamentation. Adelaide Rebekah, as always, cried because her brother cried; Baby woke and added angry screams; the room’s acoustics suddenly changed, the hearth no longer a comforting crackle but a sharp chamber where grief echoed off plaster and furniture. Hushing rose from every side. Baby had to be lifted out of the cradle.

The crying pierced Mordecai. He opened his arms to Jacob, and in the midst of sobs and the child’s swivelling gaze—half tears, half curiosity—Cohen released him, and Jacob went to Mordecai. Mordecai clasped him and laid his cheek on the little black head without speaking, as if speech would break him.

Cohen, feeling the need to recover the dignity of the occasion and apologize for family weakness, squared his elbows, set a hand on each knee, and addressed Daniel with an elevated pitch.

“It’s not as we’re the people to grudge anybody’s good luck, sir, or the portion of their cup being made fuller, as I may say. I’m not an envious man, and if anybody offered to set up Mordecai in a shop of my sort two doors lower down, I shouldn’t make wry faces about it. I’m not one of them that had need have a poor opinion of themselves and be frightened at anybody else getting a chance. If I’m offal, let a wise man come and tell me, for I’ve never heard it yet. In point of business, I’m not a class of goods to be in danger. If anybody takes to rolling me, I can pack myself up like a caterpillar and find my feet when I’m let alone. And though, as I may say, you’re taking some of our good works from us, which is property bearing interest, I’m not saying but we can afford that, though my mother and my wife had the good will to wish and do for Mordecai to the last; and a Jew must not be like a servant who works for reward—though I see nothing against a reward if I can get it. And as to the extra outlay in schooling, I’m neither poor nor greedy—I wouldn’t hang myself for sixpence, nor half a crown neither. But the truth of it is, the women and children are fond of Mordecai. You may partly see how it is, sir, by your own sense. A Jewish man is bound to thank God day by day that he was not made a woman; but a woman has to thank God that He has made her according to His will. And we all know what He has made her—a child-bearing, tender-hearted thing is the woman of our people. Her children are mostly stout, as I think you’ll say Addy’s are, and she’s not mushy, but her heart is tender. So you must excuse present company, sir, for not being glad all at once. And as to this young lady—for by what you say ‘young lady’ is the proper term—” and here Cohen threw extra emphasis into look and tone, “we shall all be glad for Mordecai’s sake by-and-by, when we cast up our accounts and see where we are.”

Before Daniel could gather an answer to this mingled boast, apology, and bargaining with Providence, Mordecai cried out, his voice suddenly full.

“Friends, friends! For food and raiment and shelter I would not have sought better than you have given me. You have sweetened the morsel with love; and what I thought of as a joy that would be left to me even in the last months of my waning strength was to go on teaching the lad. But now I am as one who had clad himself beforehand in his shroud, and used himself to making the grave his bed, when the divine command sounded in his ears, ‘Arise, and go forth; the night is not yet come.’ For no light matter would I have turned away from your kindness to take another’s. But it has been taught us, as you know, that the reward of one duty is the power to fulfill another—so said Ben Azai. You have made your duty to one of the poor among your brethren a joy to you and me; and your reward shall be that you will not rest without the joy of like deeds in the time to come. And may not Jacob come and visit me?”

He turned this last question to Daniel.

“Surely that can be managed,” Daniel said. “It is no further than Brompton.”

The word visit slipped into Jacob’s mind like the smell of cakes into a child’s thoughts—holiday, permission, a loosening of rules. He began to see daylight. He danced away from Mordecai and planted himself in the middle of the hearthrug, hands in his pockets, taking survey as if the future had become a place he might stride into.

“Well,” the grandmother sighed, resignation settling on her like flour dust after kneading, “I hope there’ll be nothing in the way of your getting kosher meat, Mordecai. For you’ll have to trust to those you live with.”

“That’s all right, that’s all right, you may be sure, mother,” Cohen cut in quickly, anxious to stop inquiry on points where he did not know the gentleman’s position. Then, turning to Daniel with an amused enlightenment, he added, “So, sir, it was better than learning you had to talk to Mordecai about! I wondered to myself at the time. I thought somehow there was a something.”

“Mordecai will perhaps explain to you how it was that I was seeking him,” Daniel said, feeling the moment ripe for withdrawal, and he rose as he spoke—leaving behind him the firelit parlor, the mingled noises of family life, and a change begun that would reach, by quiet steps, into other rooms and other hearts.
