The air in the library was still, a suspended breath of dust and mahogany. Newland Archer sat at the writing-table, the wood worn smooth by decades of friction, his fingers resting lightly on the surface. He had just emerged from the Metropolitan Museum, a cavern of curated antiquities where the living moved among the dead like ghosts in a gallery. The sight of those vast halls, crowded with the spoils of civilizations long turned to ash, had triggered a sudden, sharp recoil in his memory.

*"Why, this used to be one of the old Cesnola rooms,"* someone had murmured nearby.

Instantly, the present dissolved. The library vanished. He was transported to a smaller, harder space: a leather divan against a radiator, the air thin and cold. A figure in a sealskin cloak moved away down a corridor that lacked the grandeur of the museum's current layout, a ghost walking through the bones of the building.

The vision rippled outward, summoning a cascade of associations. He looked around the room with fresh, critical eyes. For over thirty years, this sanctuary had witnessed the private machinery of his life. It was here, nearly twenty-six years prior, that his wife, May, had broken the silence with a blush and a circumlocution that would have amused the younger women of today, announcing the arrival of a child. It was here that Dallas, too frail for the winter church, had been baptized by the Bishop of New York, a man of ample presence and irreplaceable dignity. Dallas had first stumbled onto the floor shouting *"Dad"* while May and the nurse laughed behind the door; later, it was here that Mary, her mother's likeness preserved, had announced her engagement to the most reliable of Reggie Chivers's sons. And here, Newland had kissed her through the veil before they descended to the motor for Grace Church—a ceremony that, in a world otherwise teetering on its foundations, remained an immutable institution.

This was the room where the future of the children had been dissected: Dallas's studies, Mary's rejection of accomplishments in favor of sport and charity, and the vague pull toward art that had eventually deposited Dallas in the office of a rising architect. Young men were shedding the rigid molds of law and commerce, drifting into archaeology, landscape engineering, or the study of Georgian architecture, protesting the misuse of the word "Colonial"—a term now reserved for the suburban millionaire's grocery store.

Yet, above all, it was in this room that the Governor of New York, descending from Albany to dine, had slammed his fist on the table, glasses rattling, and roared, *"Hang the professional politician! Men like you are what the country needs!"* How Archer had glowed at the phrase, how eagerly he had risen to the call. It echoed Ned Winsett's old plea to roll up sleeves and dive into the muck, but spoken by a man who embodied the gesture, the summons had been irresistible.

Looking back, however, uncertainty clouded the memory. Were men like himself truly what the nation required? After a single term in the State Assembly, he had not been re-elected, retreating gratefully into municipal obscurity and the occasional article for a reform weekly. It was little enough, but when measured against the narrow groove of money, sport, and society that defined his generation, even this small contribution counted. Each brick mattered in a well-built wall. He had done little in public life; he remained by nature a contemplative, a dilettante. Yet he had contemplated high things, delighted in great things, and possessed one great friendship as his strength and pride.

He was, in short, becoming what people called "a good citizen." Every new movement—philanthropic, municipal, artistic—sought his opinion. *"Ask Archer"* was the refrain when schools for cripples opened, museums reorganized, or societies founded. His days were full, filled decently. He supposed this was all a man ought to ask.

Something he knew he had missed: the flower of life. But he viewed it now as an impossibility, akin to despairing because one had not won the lottery. There were a hundred million tickets in his game, and only one prize; the odds had been decisively stacked against him. When he thought of Ellen Olenska, it was abstractly, serenely, as one might regard a beloved in a painting: she had become the composite vision of everything absent. That faint, tenuous image had kept him from seeking other women. He had been a faithful husband. When May had died, carried off by the pneumonia she had tended to their youngest child, he had mourned her honestly. Their long years together had proven that marriage need not be a dull duty if it maintained the dignity of one; lapse from that, and it became merely a battle of ugly appetites. Looking about him, he honored his past, mourning it gently. After all, there was good in the old ways.

His eyes swept the room—refinished by Dallas with English mezzotints, Chippendale cabinets, shards of blue-and-white porcelain, and softly shaded electric lamps—and rested on the old Eastlake writing-table, which he had never been willing to discard, and the first photograph of May beside his inkstand.

She was there, tall, round-bosomed, willowy, in starched muslin and a flapping Leghorn hat, as he had seen her beneath the orange trees in the Mission garden. As he had seen her that day, she had remained: never quite at the same height, yet never far below it. Generous, faithful, unwearied. But so lacking in imagination, so incapable of growth, that the world of her youth had shattered and rebuilt itself without her ever perceiving the shift. This hard, bright blindness had frozen her immediate horizon. Her inability to recognize change meant her children concealed their views from her just as Archer concealed his. From the start, there existed a joint pretense of sameness, an innocent family hypocrisy in which father and children had unconsciously colluded. She had died believing the world a good place, full of loving, harmonious households like her own, resigned to depart because she was certain Newland would continue to instill in Dallas the same principles that had shaped his parents' lives, ensuring Dallas would pass the sacred trust to little Bill. Of Mary, she was sure as she was of herself. Having snatched little Bill from the grave and given her life to the effort, she went contentedly to her place in the Archer vault at St. Mark's, where Mrs. Archer already lay safe from the terrifying "trend" her daughter-in-law had never even noticed.

Opposite May's portrait stood one of her daughter. Mary Chivers was as tall and fair as her mother, yet large-waisted, flat-chested, and slightly slouching, as the altered fashions demanded. Mary Chivers's mighty feats of athleticism could not have been achieved with the twenty-inch waist that May Archer's azure sash so easily spanned. The difference seemed symbolic; the mother's life had been as tightly girdled as her figure. Mary, no less conventional and no more intelligent, yet lived a larger life and held more tolerant views. There was good in the new order, too.

The telephone clicked. Archer turned from the photographs, unhooking the transmitter at his elbow. How distant they were from the days when the brass-buttoned messenger boy's legs were New York's sole means of rapid communication.

"Chicago wants you."

Ah—it must be a long line from Dallas, dispatched to Chicago by his firm to discuss plans for the Lakeside palace intended for a young millionaire with specific visions. The firm always sent Dallas on such errands.

"Hallo, Dad—Yes: Dallas. I say—how do you feel about sailing on Wednesday? Mauretania: Yes, next Wednesday as ever is. Our client wants me to inspect some Italian gardens before settling anything, and has asked me to nip over on the next boat. I've got to be back on the first of June—" the voice fractured into a joyful, self-conscious laugh— "so we must look alive. I say, Dad, I want your help: do come."

Dallas seemed to speak within the room; the voice was as close and natural as if he were lounging in his favorite armchair by the fire. Such proximity would not ordinarily surprise Archer, for long-distance telephoning had become as mundane as electric lighting and five-day Atlantic crossings. But the laugh startled him; it still seemed miraculous that across thousands of miles of forest, river, mountain, prairie, roaring cities, and indifferent millions, Dallas's laughter could declare: *"Of course, whatever happens, I must get back on the first, because Fanny Beaufort and I are to be married on the fifth."*

The voice resumed: *"Think it over? No, sir: not a minute. You've got to say yes now. Why not, I'd like to know? If you can allege a single reason—No; I knew it. Then it's a go, eh? Because I count on you to ring up the Cunard office first thing tomorrow; and you'd better book a return on a boat from Marseilles. I say, Dad; it'll be our last time together, in this kind of way—. Oh, good! I knew you would."*

Chicago disconnected. Archer rose and began to pace the length of the room.

It would be their last time together in this manner: the boy was right. They would share countless other "times" after Dallas's marriage, his father was certain; the two were born comrades, and Fanny Beaufort, whatever one might think of her, did not appear likely to disrupt their intimacy.
