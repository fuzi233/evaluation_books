From Munich, Trent dispatched a letter to Sir James Molloy, his pen moving with the weary precision of a man shedding a heavy burden. "I am returning the cheque you sent for my work on the Manderson case," he wrote. "What I submitted was not worth one-tenth of the sum; yet I could not bring myself to pocket it. I have developed a peculiar aversion to taking money for this specific business. If you have no objection, please charge the cost of the material to your standard space rate and remit the balance to a charity that does not engage in bullying. I have come here to visit old friends and organize my thoughts. The conclusion that dominates my mind is that for the present, I require employment filled with activity. I have discovered I cannot paint—not even a fence. Would you consider employing me as your Own Correspondent? If you can secure me a genuine adventure, I promise dispatches of consequence. Once that is concluded, I shall settle down to steady work."

Sir James's reply arrived via telegram, ordering Trent immediately to Kurland and Livonia, where Citizen Browning was once again abroad amidst a blaze of civil revolt. It was a roving commission, and for two months Trent followed the trail. Fortune favored him as often as usual. He was the sole correspondent to witness General Dragilew's assassination in the streets of Volmar by an eighteen-year-old girl. He witnessed burnings, lynchings, fusillades, and hangings; every day his soul recoiled anew at the imbecilities spawned by misrule. Many nights he slept in the shadow of danger; many days he fasted. Yet, there was not a single evening or morning when the face of the woman he loved with hopeless devotion did not haunt his vision.

He harbored a bitter pride in the enduring power of this infatuation. It fascinated him as a phenomenon, astounding and enlightening him. Nothing of the sort had ever visited him before. It validated much of what he had previously found dubious in the recorded experiences of men.

At thirty-two, Trent could not feign ignorance of the world of emotion. What he had learned had come unpursued and unpurchased, devoid of intolerable memories. Broken to the realities of sex, he remained troubled by its inscrutable history. He navigated life with a strange respect for certain feminine weaknesses and a profound terror of certain feminine strengths. He had maintained a lukewarm faith that something remained within him to be awakened, and that the voice calling it forth would arrive in its own time, if ever, and not through any active seeking.

He had not anticipated that, if this truth were to manifest, it might take a sinister form. The two aspects of his feelings toward Mabel Manderson that had utterly surprised him were the insane suddenness of their eruption and their extravagant hopelessness. Prior to their arrival, he had been inclined to laugh at the permanence of unrequited passion, dismissing it as a generous boyish delusion. He now knew he had been wrong, and he lived bitterly under that knowledge.

In his imagination, the woman always appeared exactly as he had first seen her, performing that great gesture of passionate joy as he had walked past unseen on the cliff's edge. That gesture, signaling her new liberty as a release from torment, had confirmed with terrible force his prior suspicion that widowhood was her passport to happiness with a man she loved. He could not pinpoint the exact moment he first suspected this, but he believed the seed was sown during his first meeting with Marlowe. His mind would have automatically noted that such evident strength and grace, coupled with the tall young man's mannerisms, might captivate any woman of unfixed affections. The connection with what Mr. Cupples had revealed about the Mandersons' married life must have formed in the unconscious depths of his mind. By the time he began searching for the motive of the crime, after verifying the murderer's identity, the thought presented itself as an established fact. *Motive, motive!* How desperately he had sought another explanation, turning his back on the grim notion that Marlowe—obsessed by passion like himself, and perhaps privy to maddening truths about the wife's unhappiness—had taken the guiltiest leaf from the book of Bothwell. Yet, in all his investigations and subsequent broodings, he had found nothing to prompt Marlowe to such a deed—nothing but that temptation, whose full strength he could not know, yet which, if it existed, must have pressed urgently upon a bold spirit in which scruple had been paralyzed. If he could trust his senses, the young man was neither insane nor inherently evil. But that could not exonerate him. Murder for a woman's sake was not a rare crime; Heaven knew! While the modern feebleness of impulse in comfortable classes and their respect for the apparatus of detection had made it rare among them, it was far from impossible. It only required a man of equal daring and intelligence, his soul drugged by the vapors of an intoxicating intrigue, to plan and execute such a deed.

A thousand times, with a heart full of anguish, he had tried to reason away the dread that Mabel Manderson had known too much about the intended destruction of her husband's life. That she knew the full truth after the event was undeniable; her unforgettable collapse when the question of Marlowe was suddenly and bluntly posed had swept away his last hope that there was no love between them, and seemed to speak of a dread of discovery. In any case, she knew the truth after reading his manuscript, and it was certain that no public suspicion had been cast upon Marlowe since. She had destroyed his manuscript, then, and taken his word to keep the secret that threatened her lover's life.

But it was the monstrous thought that she might have known murder was brewing, and guiltily kept silence, that haunted Trent's mind. She might have suspected, guessed something; was it conceivable that she was aware of the whole plot, that she connived? He could never forget that his first suspicion of Marlowe's motive had been roused by the fact that his escape was made through the lady's room. At that time, before seeing her, he had been ready to entertain the idea of her equal guilt and cooperation. He had pictured to himself some passionate *hystérique*, merciless as a cat in her hate and her love, a zealous abettor, perhaps even the ruling spirit of the crime.

Then he had seen her, spoken with her, helped her in her weakness; and such suspicions, since their first meeting, had seemed the vilest of infamy. He had seen her eyes and her mouth; he had breathed the woman's atmosphere. Trent was one of those who fancy they can scent true wickedness in the air. In her presence, he had felt an inward certainty of her ultimate goodness of heart; and it was nothing against this that she had abandoned herself for a moment, that day on the cliff, to the sentiment of relief at the ending of her bondage, of her years of starved sympathy and unquickened motherhood. That she had turned to Marlowe in her destitution he believed; that she had any knowledge of his deadly purpose he did not believe.

And yet, morning and evening, the sickening doubts returned, and he recalled again that it was almost in her presence that Marlowe had made his preparations in the bedroom of the murdered man, that it was by the window of her own chamber that he had escaped from the house. Had he forgotten his cunning and taken the risk of telling her then? Or had he, as Trent thought more likely, still played his part with her then, and stolen off while she slept? He did not think she had known of the masquerade when she gave evidence at the inquest; it read like honest evidence. Or—the question would never be silenced, though he scorned it—had she lain expecting the footsteps in the room and the whisper that should tell her it was done? Among the foul possibilities of human nature, was it possible that black ruthlessness and black deceit were hidden behind that good, straight, and gentle seeming?

These thoughts would scarcely leave him when he was alone.

Trent served Sir James well, earning his pay for six months, before returning to Paris, where he resumed work with a better heart. His powers had returned, and he began to live more happily than he had expected among a tribe of strangely assorted friends—French, English, and American artists, poets, journalists, policemen, hotel-keepers, soldiers, lawyers, businessmen, and others. His old faculty of sympathetic interest in his fellows won for him, just as in his student days, privileges seldom extended to the Briton. He enjoyed again the rare experience of being taken into the bosom of a Frenchman's family. He was admitted to the momentous confidence of *les jeunes*, and found them as sure that they had surprised the secrets of art and life as the departed *jeunes* of ten years before had been.

The bosom of the Frenchman's family was the same as those he had known in the past, even to the patterns of the wallpaper and the movables. But the *jeunes*, he perceived with regret, were totally different from their forerunners. They were much more shallow and puerile, much less really clever. The secrets they wrested from the Universe were not such important and interesting secrets as had been wrested by the old *jeunes*. He believed and deplored this until one day he found himself seated at a restaurant next to a too well-fed man whom, in spite of the ravages of comfortable living, he recognized as one of the *jeunes* of his own period. This one had been wont to describe himself and three or four others as the Hermits of the New Parnassus. He and his school had talked outside cafes and elsewhere more than solitaries do as a rule; but, then, rules were what they had vowed themselves to destroy. They proclaimed that verse, in particular, was free. The Hermit of the New Parnassus was now in the Ministry of the Interior and already decorated; he expressed to Trent the opinion that what France needed most was a hand of iron. He was able to quote the exact price paid for certain betrayals of the country, of which Trent had not previously heard.

Thus he was brought to make the old discovery that it was he who had changed, like his friend of the Administration, and that *les jeunes* were still the same. Yet he found it hard to say what precisely he had lost that so greatly mattered; unless indeed it were so simple a thing as his high spirits.

One morning in June, as he descended the slope of the Rue des Martyrs, he saw approaching a figure he remembered. He glanced quickly around, for the thought of meeting Mr. Bunner again was unacceptable. For some time he had recognized that his wound was healing under the spell of creative work; he thought less often of the woman he loved, and with less pain. He would not have the memory of those three days reopened.

But the straight and narrow thoroughfare offered no refuge, and the American saw him almost at once.

His unforced geniality made Trent ashamed, for he had liked the man. They sat long over a meal, and Mr. Bunner talked. Trent listened to him, now that he was in for it, with genuine pleasure, now and then contributing a question or remark. Besides liking his companion, he enjoyed his conversation, with its unending verbal surprises, for its own sake.

Bunner was, it appeared, resident in Paris as the chief Continental agent of the Manderson firm, and fully satisfied with his position and prospects. He discoursed on these for some twenty minutes. This subject at length exhausted, he went on to tell Trent, who confessed that he had been away from England for a year, that Marlowe had shortly after the death of Manderson entered his father's business, which was now again in a flourishing state, and had already come to be practically in control of it. They had kept up their intimacy, and were even now planning a holiday for the summer. Mr. Bunner spoke with generous admiration of his friend's talent for affairs. "Jack Marlowe has a natural big head," he declared, "and if he had more experience, I wouldn't want to have him up against me. He would put a crimp in me every time."

As the American's talk flowed on, Trent listened with a slowly growing perplexity. It became more and more plain that something was very wrong in his theory of the situation; there was no mention of its central figure. Presently Mr. Bunner mentioned that Marlowe was engaged to be married to an Irish girl, whose charms he celebrated with native enthusiasm.

Trent clasped his hands savagely together beneath the table. What could have happened? His ideas were sliding and shifting. At last he forced himself to put a direct question.

Mr. Bunner was not very fully informed. He knew that Mrs. Manderson had left England immediately after the settlement of her husband's affairs, and had lived for some time in Italy. She had returned not long ago to London, where she had decided not to live in the house in Mayfair, and had bought a smaller one in the Hampstead neighborhood; also, he understood, one somewhere in the country. She was said to go but little into society. "And all the good hard dollars just waiting for some one to spraddle them around," said Mr. Bunner, with a note of pathos in his voice. "Why, she has money to burn—money to feed to the birds—and nothing doing. The old man left her more than half his wad. And think of the figure she might make in the world. She is beautiful, and she is the best woman I ever met, too. But she couldn't ever seem to get the habit of spending money the way it ought to be spent."

His words now became a soliloquy: Trent's thoughts were occupying all his attention. He pleaded business soon, and the two men parted with cordiality.

Half an hour later Trent was in his studio, swiftly and mechanically "cleaning up". He wanted to know what had happened; somehow he must find out. He could never approach her, he knew; he would never bring back to her the shame of that last encounter with him; it was scarcely likely that he would even set eyes on her. But he must get to know!... Cupples was in London, Marlowe was there.... And, anyhow, he was sick of Paris.
