Denys lunged to catch Gerard mid-fall from the tree bough, slowing his plunge just enough that he didn’t crack his skull against the hard packed dirt—but what truly saved the young clerk from breaking a neck or limb was the great shaggy she-bear, still gasping out her last breaths, whose furry carcass caught his head and shoulders full force. Denys hauled him off the creature in a panic, but it was unnecessary: the bear’s limbs quivered and her chest heaved, but she was as harmless as a startled leveret, and within a minute she breathed her last. The practical old soldier propped Gerard against her warm, pelted flank for a soft cushion, fanning his pale face with his wool cap till color seeped back into his cheeks.

Gerard roused slowly, disoriented, and when his bleary senses registered thick bear fur pressing all around him, he scrambled yelling behind the nearest oak, gasping for air.
“Courage!” Denys called, cackling. “Le diable est mort—the devil himself is dead and gone.”
“Is it dead? Quite dead?” Gerard peeked out from behind the trunk, his courage frayed thin, the cold shiver of delayed shock still racing through his bones.
“Behold!” Denys plucked the brute’s ear playfully, pried her slack jaws wide to stick his whole head inside, and pulled half a dozen more ridiculous, taunting antics—until Gerard doubled over and retched, the iron tang of bear’s blood in the air turning his stomach.
Denys roared with laughter. “What ails you now? And why did you tumble off your perch the very second we’d won the fight?”
“I swooned, I think.”
“And for what cause? Green girls faint at the sight of a man’s wink, but they choose a soft parlor to do it in. Did you ever hear of a woman fainting halfway up an oak tree?”
“She splattered her nasty blood all over me! The smell overpowered me, I swear—faugh, I cannot stand the sight of blood.”
“I’ll not doubt that for a moment.”
“Look what a mess she’s made of my tunic!”
“A mess of her blood, not yours. I pity any foe foolish enough to try to put you to the test of spilling yours.”
“Brave talk, Maitre Denys! I saw you pale as your own linen shirt cowering at the foot of that tree before we struck.”
“Let us make distinctions!” Denys flushed, prickling a little. “It is permitted to tremble for the sake of a friend, is it not?”

Gerard said nothing in reply, but flung his arms tight around Denys’s neck, the rush of relief and gratitude spilling over into unmanly tears. The gruff soldier squirmed, flustered. “There now—was ever a creature so soft as a green youth? I love thee, little milksop, but enough of that—wait, what new folly is this, kneeling in the dirt?”
“Denys, ought we not give thanks to God, who spared both our lives against such terrible odds?” Gerard bowed his head, praying aloud, and before he had finished his petition Denys was kneeling quiet beside him, hands crossed over his chest the way his Breton mother had taught him at mass, his face solemn and long. When they stood, Gerard was glowing.
“Good Denys, Heaven will reward that piety of yours.”
“Bah! I did it out of politeness, to please thee, little one,” Denys huffed, though his eyes crinkled. “It was a fine, orderly prayer, too—scarcely a bishop could have delivered it better. Our evensong is sung, the saints are on our side—marchons, let’s be off.”
They’d barely taken two steps when Denys stopped short. “Wait—the cub!”
“Oh no, never mind the cub!” Gerard groaned.
“You are right. It’s late, we’ve wasted hours climbing trees, tumbling off them, swooning, heaving up our dinners, and praying—and the she-bear is far too heavy to haul. Besides, her mate will be coming looking for that cub soon enough, and we’ve no taste for another fight. Wait—what is this? You are wounded! You are bleeding, man, miserable fool that I am!”
“Not I,” Gerard protested.
“You are! You feel no pain for yourself, you soft thing,” Denys gasped, his eyes glistening, dropping to his knees to yank up Gerard’s hose and see the deep, gory gash the bear’s claw had raked across his calf. “Quick, quick, before the muscle stiffens!” He hauled Gerard upright to hurry him along.
“Who is making a great coil over nothing now?” Gerard asked, calm as you please.
Denys didn’t reply directly. “Mark this: I have a bad heart, and a worse tongue. You saved my life back there, and I sneered at you for a novice in war, called you a milksop when you fainted from the pain of that gash and I thought you’d swooned from fear. I was a fool, once, too, just like you.”
“Denys, you lie,” Gerard said softly.
“Thank you for saying so, little one,” Denys mumbled, remorseful.

They hadn’t walked more than a few furlongs before the wound in Gerard’s leg seized up, stiff and burning, until he could barely put his toe to the ground without screaming. At last he sank helpless in the dirt, groaning. “Let me lie down and die, this pain is intolerable.”
Denys argued that it was afternoon, the frosty autumn night was coming on fast, and cold and hunger were far worse companions than a sore leg—besides, the bear that had threatened them was well and truly dead. Gerard leaned his whole weight on his axe to hobble onward, but soon his strength gave out entirely. Denys dragged him into the thick of the trees, pressed his crossbow and a handful of bolts into his hands, and bade him lie quiet. “Shoot any ill-looking fellow that comes within twenty paces without calling out, do not parley. Honest men stay to the road; only fools stop to talk to knaves in the woods.” He snatched up Gerard’s axe and ran off—not toward Dusseldorf, as Gerard expected, but back the way they had come.

Gerard lay there aching, his thoughts drifting first to Rome, the distant city that had felt so near when he left Sevenbergen, now leagues and leagues away, then to Margaret. The thought of her face, of holding her hand one day and telling her all the wild adventures he’d survived just to get to her, soothed the pain till he lay smiling faint and soft, despite the stinging wound. He’d been there more than two hours when shouts broke out through the trees, and a thud sent an arrow quivering into the oak trunk not three feet from his head.
More missiles rattled through the branches, battle-cries echoed off the wood’s high boughs, then he heard thunderous hoofbeats, fiercer shouts mingling with shrieks and groans, and strange, thunderous cracking booms that died away in echoing pops, red flames spitting through the leaves, and thick sulphur smoke drifting over his face. Then all went silent.
Gerard shuddered. “What has become of Denys? Oh, my friend, my friend, why did you leave me?”

Just before sunset, Denys stumbled back, almost collapsing under the weight of the full heavy bear pelt slung over his shoulders. Gerard greeted him with a burst of joy that made the gruff soldier blink.
“I thought I would never see you again, Denys! Were you in that terrible battle that raged through the wood just now?”
“Battle? What battle?”
“The fight of men or fiends, with arrows and thunderous booms and smoke!” Gerard described every last detail, vivid as a painted manuscript.
Denys patted his back indulgently. “You are a fine storyteller, and fever makes the imagination run wild. Once I lay in a cart shed with a cracked skull, and saw two whole armies maneuver and fight for an hour on a patch of ground no bigger than my boot. It was the fever, nothing more.”
“You do not believe me? The arrows whizzed over my head—look!” Gerard pointed to the long arrow stuck in the oak.
Denys peered at it, and whistled. “That is an English longbow shaft, three feet long. No other archers draw their bow all the way to their ear, only the English. That is no fever dream. There was a battle, right enough. But my duke has no quarrel hereabouts, it is none of our business. Let us make our bed.” He scraped up a great heap of dry leaves, laid Gerard on it, draped the thick bear skin over both of them fur-side in, and they were soon as warm as fresh bread, fast asleep.

Long before dawn, Gerard shook Denys awake. “I am starving, I will die of hunger.”
“Go back to sleep! Who sleeps, dines, they say.”
“I am far too hungry to sleep,” Gerard snapped.
“Then we march,” Denys said, yawning indulgently. He cut the bear’s two ears off, rolled them in a scrap of the pelt, and they set off down the road, Gerard leaning on his axe, Denys propping him up on the other side, limping slow.
Soon the moon rose, and they saw the end of the wood not far ahead, with Dusseldorf only a league beyond. At the treeline they halted, staring at a sight that made their skin prickle. Two tall white wooden pillars stood a few paces apart, with a dark crossbeam strung between them, and dozens of dark figures dangling from ropes tied to the beam, swaying slowly in the breeze.
“I will not go a step further till I know what this is,” Gerard whispered, terrified. “Saints’ effigies? Or robbers lying in wait? They do not stand on anything, Denys—let us turn back till daylight, this is no natural sight.”
Denys peered long and hard. “They are men. But men that will never hurt us. Don’t look at their feet—look above their heads.”
Gerard lifted his eyes, and saw the crossbeam, the thick snake-like ropes, the hanged corpses dangling from them, their ropes creaking soft in the wind. A small fire burned right under the gibbet, and a lone man in a countryman’s clothes, a stiff Brunswick three-cornered hat on his head, was huddled asleep beside it, his axe glinting red in the firelight.
Denys heaped more wood on the fire. Gerard seized the sleeping man’s axe and sat tight on it, his own weapon in hand, while Denys ran two sticks through the bear’s ears and held them over the flame to toast. “The burgomaster of Dusseldorf would give us a rix-dollar for these ears to prove the bear is dead,” he said. “But a lean purse is better than an empty stomach.”
“Unhappy man!” Gerard gasped, shuddering as one of the corpses swayed and the rope creaked loud. “How can you eat here, under these poor wretches?”
“Where the fire burns, the meat roasts, and where the meat roasts, you eat it—roast meat travels worse than any other food.”

The sleeping man woke up, stared at them, and made a move to run for the bushes. Denys levelled his crossbow at his head, and the man fell to his knees. He crawled back to the fire slowly, pale as a sheet, and shared his bread, his roasted kid, and a full two-gallon leather bottle of rich Rhenish wine with the two travellers, too scared to protest. He got drunk fast, and babbled out the whole story: the hanged men were his old robber band, who had ambushed what they thought was a group of rich merchants from Dusseldorf, only to find they were Archbishop of Cologne’s soldiers in disguise, wearing hidden armor under their gowns, armed with the new fire-spitting handguns that shot lead balls. The soldiers had ambushed them, killed half the band in seconds, hanged the rest on this gibbet, and left him behind to watch the bodies so the crows would not peck them apart before dawn.

Denys told him to support Gerard’s weight as they walked toward Dusseldorf, and the man obeyed, too scared to argue. When the distant outline of Dusseldorf’s watchtower loomed on the horizon, the robber halted, and said he would rather they killed him right there than drag him inside the town gates, where the hangman would be waiting for him. Denys let him go, gave him back his axe, and even tossed him a few silver groshen. But the robber still lingered, and begged Gerard, the clerk, to say a prayer for him, for he did not know a single one by heart.
Gerard frowned, sharp and irritable from the pain of his wound, and asked him if it was true that Rhenish thieves murdered innocent travellers just to avoid being caught. The robber shrugged, and said the harsh laws were to blame: if a thief was hanged for stealing a purse, he had no choice but to cut the traveller’s throat, for dead men tell no tales.
Gerard doffed his bonnet, and recited a loud, blunt Latin prayer that the man be hanged soon and quickly, alongside his former companions, for the public good, to the glory of the just God, amen. The robber, who did not know a word of Latin, thanked him warmly, satisfied he had received a fine, powerful holy prayer, and wandered back into the woods.

The two friends limped onward in silence, till Gerard spoke at last, soft and thoughtful.
“That dying she-bear saved both our lives, back there in the wood. By God’s own grace.”
