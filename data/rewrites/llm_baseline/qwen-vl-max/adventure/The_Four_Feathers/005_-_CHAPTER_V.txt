The ballroom hummed with life, laughter, and the easy rhythm of conversation. Habit carried them through—each movement, each smile, each word spoken seemed to flow naturally, as though nothing had changed between them. At Lennon House that night, no one suspected the quiet fracture that had split their world in two. Harry Feversham watched Ethne move through the crowd, laughing, chatting, her face alight with joy. He found himself continually surprised by her apparent ease, never realizing that he, too, was wearing a mask—his own facade of cheerfulness carefully constructed to hide the storm within.

When she danced past him, her steps light and graceful, for a moment he almost believed her heart was truly in it. She seemed capable of summoning color to her cheeks at will, of controlling every expression, every breath. They even danced together, side by side, yet beneath the surface, both were weighed down by invisible burdens. Ethne felt the crushing weight of pain and shame pressing upon her, a burden she held up with trembling hands, knowing it could collapse at any moment. And Feversham? He felt the four feathers burning against his chest like live coals, hidden beneath his coat but scorching his soul.

It amazed him that no one else saw what he carried. Every time he approached a partner, he braced himself for the worst—for her to turn on him with the word that had become his name in her eyes: *coward*. Yet he did not fear it. In truth, he would have welcomed it. The truth had already been spoken in silence. He had lost Ethne. And though he looked around the room, searching among the guests for someone who might compare to her, he knew there was no one. Not one woman there—not pretty, not graceful, not beautiful—could hold a candle to her.

Her beauty was not merely physical. It was in the sweep of her forehead, the perfect arch of her brows, the steady gray of her eyes, the fullness of her lips that could soften into tenderness or harden with resolve. Her bearing was regal, her presence commanding. To Feversham, she stood apart—unmatched, unreplaceable. And now, watching her from afar, he was struck with a terrible, helpless wonder: how had he ever thought he could possess her?

Only once did her strength falter—and only for a heartbeat. They were dancing together when she glanced toward the windows. Beyond the blinds, the first pale streaks of dawn crept across the sky. “Look!” she whispered.

Feversham felt her body tense, then sag against him. Her face drained of color, turning ashen and weary. Her eyes closed tightly, then snapped open again. For a moment, he feared she would faint.

“The morning at last!” she breathed, her voice thin and tired. Then, softer still: “I wonder… whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain.”

“Hush,” he murmured, holding her steady. “Courage. Just a few minutes more—only a few!”

He stopped dancing, standing before her until her breath steadied, her color returned. When she finally spoke, it was with quiet gratitude. “Thank you.” And then, like a wheel caught in motion, they stepped back into the dance.

Strange, that he should be urging her to endure, and she thanking him for support. But neither noticed the irony—the fleeting reversal of roles. Ethne was too spent by the strain of the night, too close to breaking. And Feversham? He had seen the depth of her suffering in that single moment—the lines etched into her face, the anguish in her eyes. That look shattered whatever illusion he had left. He no longer said, *I have lost her.* He didn’t think of loss at all. Instead, he heard her words echoing in his mind: *I wonder whether it is right…* Those words would follow him forever, ringing in his ears, spoken in the tone of her voice, haunting him until the end. For it was not right. It was not just. And he knew it.

The ball ended soon after. The last carriage rolled away, and those staying at the house drifted to the smoking room or climbed the stairs to bed. But Feversham lingered in the hall, waiting. Ethne understood why.

“There’s no need,” she said, lighting a candle with her back turned to him. “I’ve told my father. Everything.”

He bowed his head, accepting her words without protest. “Still,” he said quietly, “I must wait and see him.”

She hesitated, then turned sharply, her brow furrowed in confusion. Waiting for her father under such circumstances—after what had happened—was an act of courage. And she, too, felt a tremor of dread as she heard the study door creak open and footsteps approach.

Dermod came forward, older than ever, his face lined with sorrow and bewilderment. He stood before Feversham, staring at him as if trying to understand a mystery beyond words. Twice he opened his mouth, but no sound came out. Finally, he turned to the table, lit his own candle, then Feversham’s. Then he turned back, stared again—longer this time—and Ethne took a step forward, ready to intervene. But Dermod said nothing. He simply picked up his candle.

“Well…” he began, and paused. He snipped the wick with scissors. “Well…” Again, he stopped.

He stared into the flame, silent, searching for something—anything—to say. But nothing came. He could not speak. Could not condemn. Could not forgive. So instead, he offered a strange, practical instruction.

“If you want any whiskey,” he said slowly, “stamp twice on the floor. The servants understand.”

Then he turned and walked heavily up the stairs.

His silence was worse than anger. His restraint—his refusal to confront, to curse, to break—was perhaps the cruelest punishment of all.

***

By the time Ethne was alone in her room, the sun had risen fully. She drew up the blinds, flung open the windows, and let the cool morning air rush in—like spring water washing over her skin. Outside, the world was still dim, untouched by color. The trees stood black against the sky, the paths white and ghostly, the lawns dull and gray beneath a veil of dew. Yet it was alive—noisy with birdsong, vibrant with the murmur of the Lennon River winding through the trees.

She stepped back from the window. There was much to do before sleep. With quiet determination, she resolved to sever every tie to Harry Feversham. She wanted no reminder—no object, no memory—that could bring him back into her thoughts. She began collecting his gifts: the books, the scarf, the trinkets he had given her over the years. Each item brought a memory—a shared laugh, a quiet evening, a moment of closeness now shattered.

But as she gathered them, her resolve weakened. The penknife he had lent her, forgotten and returned later—she reached for it, then dropped it quickly. Superstition, she thought, though she didn’t believe in it. Better safe than sorry.

Instead, she chose his photograph. Small, unassuming, yet precious. She locked it away in a drawer, then packed the rest of the gifts into a box, sealed it, addressed it, and carried it down to the hall for the servants to send off.

Back in her room, she took the letters he had written—pages filled with love, hope, promises. She placed them on the hearth, one by one, forming a small pile. Then she lit a match.

The flames rose slowly, consuming the paper, blackening the ink like spilled ink, curling the edges until all that remained were ashes—fine, white flakes, drifting like feathers.

She sat upright in her chair, watching them burn, until the last spark died.

Then, from outside, came a cautious step on the gravel.

It was broad daylight, but her candle still burned. Instinctively, she reached out and extinguished it.

She sat still, listening. The blackbirds called from the trees. The river sang its endless song. Then—footsteps again, retreating.

And suddenly, despite everything she had done, despite the letters burned, the gifts sent away—she was overcome. Not by pain. Not by shame. But by loneliness.

She felt as though she sat atop a ruined world, vast and empty. Alone.

She rose quickly, her eyes falling on a violin case resting in the corner. With a sigh, she opened it.

A little while later, some of the guests stirred awake in their rooms. Through the corridors, soft music floated—melancholy, tender, played with deep feeling. A violin, low and loving, weaving a quiet lament.

Ethne did not know it was Durrance’s Guarnerius. She only knew she was no longer alone.
