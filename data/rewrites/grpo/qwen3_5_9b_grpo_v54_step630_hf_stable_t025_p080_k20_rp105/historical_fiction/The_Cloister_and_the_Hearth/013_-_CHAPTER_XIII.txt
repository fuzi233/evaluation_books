Gerard and Margaret hastened to Sevenbergen, buoyed by the fresh air of regained freedom and the thrill of their recent escape. Yet, this initial exhilaration quickly curdled into a bitter melancholy. Gerard was a fugitive, a man whose shadow hung over him, liable at any moment to be recaptured and punished. Consequently, separation became inevitable. Furthermore, a deep-seated loathing for his homeland had taken root in Gerard's heart. Margaret urged him to flee the country, yet the mere thought of him traveling to Italy caused her spirit to falter. Conversely, Gerard's willingness to leave her was tethered solely to his burning desire to visit Italy; he was convinced that there he could secure wealth and renown, clearing the path for their union. He recounted to her the warnings of the demoiselle Van Eyck, reminding her that the gold pieces were merely a seed fund for that specific journey. As a craftsman and artist stifled in this crude, unappreciative land, he believed Italy alone could recognize his worth. Moved by this selfless logic, the gentle girl finally consented, though her agreement was bought with a flood of tender tears. In turn, young and loving as she was, Gerard wept bitterly alongside her. Often, they clung to one another, bewildered by the conspiracy of fate that seemed to unite so many enemies against them, asking in despair what crime they had committed to deserve such torment.

Hand in hand, they sat until the clock struck midnight, oscillating between lamenting their cruel destiny and painting vivid, hopeful visions of a future together. In these moments, Margaret's sorrow would suddenly overwhelm her, and poor Gerard's eloquent pleas would dissolve into helpless sighs. By morning, they had resigned themselves to parting, yet neither possessed the courage to name the hour; indeed, it is doubtful whether the moment of separation would ever truly have arrived.

However, around three in the afternoon, Giles rode up to the door, having circled many miles to avoid detection. Both lovers rushed out, driven by urgent curiosity.

"Brother Gerard!" Giles cried out in his booming voice. "Kate commands you to run for your life! They accuse you of theft; you have handed them a weapon. Do not attempt to explain. Seek no justice in Tergou. The parchments you seized were merely a ruse. She has seen the verdict in the men's eyes; a price is on your head. Fly! For Margaret's sake, and for all who love you, do not waste another second of life, but flee!"

It was a thunderclap that left two pale faces staring in stunned silence at the terrifying messenger.

Then, Giles, who had hitherto spoken only as a mouthpiece for Catherine, added a word of his own volition. "All the constables were at our dwelling searching for you, and so was Dirk Brower. Kate is wise, Gerard. Listen to her counsel and fly!"

"Oh, yes, Gerard!" Margaret shrieked wildly. "Flee at once! Ah! Those parchments; my instincts warned me: why did I allow you to take them?"

"Margaret, they are but a blind," Gerard insisted, echoing Giles. "It matters not. That old villain shall never see them again. I will not depart until I have hidden his treasure where he can never find it." After thanking Giles warmly, he bid him farewell, instructing him to return and tell Kate that he was gone. "For I shall be gone before you reach home," he declared. He then shouted for Martin, explaining the peril and begging him to move a short distance toward Tergou and watch the road.

"Aye!" Martin agreed. "Should I spot Dirk Brower or any of his men, I will shoot an arrow into the oak-tree in our garden. Upon seeing that signal, you must dash into the nearby forest and meet me at the Weird Hunter's Spring. There, I will guide you through the woods."

With this contingency arranged, Gerard breathed a sigh of relief. He accompanied Margaret, and while she trembled, watching the oak-tree with every fiber of her being, fearing an arrow might strike its branches, Gerard began to dig a deep hole. His purpose was to bury the parchments.

He tossed them in one by one. Most were charters and records of the burgh, but one appeared to be a private deed between Floris Brandt, father of Peter, and Ghysbrecht.

"Why, this belongs to you just as much as to him," Gerard observed. "I will read this."

"Oh, not now, Gerard, not now!" Margaret cried out. "Every moment you lose fills me with dread. Look! Large drops of rain are beginning to fall, and the clouds are lowering."

Gerard yielded to her plea, yet he slipped the deed into his bosom before throwing earth over the others and stamping it down firmly. Just then, a flash of lightning illuminated the sky, followed by a roll of distant thunder, and the rain descended heavily. Margaret and Gerard fled into the house, swiftly followed by Martin.

"The road is clear," Martin reported, "and a violent storm is approaching."

His words proved true. The thunder grew louder, crashing overhead, while the lightning stroked the heavens like a whip, and the rain poured in torrents. Margaret covered her face, refusing to witness the blinding flashes. Seizing the opportunity, Gerard raised the rough shutter and lit a candle. The lovers conferred quietly, and Gerard blessed the tempest that granted them a few more hours of solitude with Margaret. The sun set unnoticed amidst the chaos, yet the thunder continued to boom and the lightning to flash as the rain lashed down. Supper was laid out, but neither could eat; the thought that this might be their final meal together choked them.

As the storm began to subside slightly, Peter retired to rest. Gerard, however, was to depart at the break of dawn, and neither he nor Margaret could afford to lose an hour to sleep. Martin remained seated for a while, carefully fitting a new string to his bow, a task he performed with meticulous care. The lovers murmured their sorrows and their undying love beside him.

Suddenly, the old man raised his hand, signaling for silence.

They stilled and listened, hearing nothing at first. But the next moment, a footstep crackled faintly upon the autumn leaves scattered across the garden behind the house. To those without fear, such a sound would be insignificant; to those hunted by enemies, it was a harbinger of doom. It was a foot attempting to be silent.

Martin nocked an arrow and hastily extinguished the candle. In that same instant, to their horror, they heard more than one footstep approaching the other door of the cottage. These steps were less stealthy than the first, yet still very cautious, followed by a deadly pause.

Their blood turned to ice in their veins.

"Oh, Kate, oh, Kate! You said to fly on the instant!" Margaret moaned, wringing her hands in anguish, terror, and wild remorse for having delayed Gerard's departure.

"Hush, girl!" Martin whispered sternly.

A heavy knock fell upon the door.

And upon the hearts within.
