The initial leg of their journey was weighed down by a melancholy so profound it rendered the landscape itself tedious and oppressive. Yet, as the horizon shifted toward the destination, the sheer novelty of inhabiting a new realm began to pierce their gloom. Upon entering Barton Valley, a sudden infusion of cheerfulness seized them. It was a verdant, fertile expanse, densely wooded and teeming with lush pasturelands. After traversing the winding road for over a mile, they finally reached their destination: Barton Cottage.

Before the house lay a modest green courtyard, accessible via a neat wicket gate. The structure itself, though diminutive, possessed a comforting compactness. However, as a proper cottage, it suffered significant deficiencies. Its architecture was rigidly regular, the roof was tiled rather thatched, the window shutters lacked the traditional green paint, and the walls remained bare, devoid of the climbing honeysuckle vines that typically softened rural dwellings. A narrow corridor cut directly through the main floor, connecting the front entrance to the rear garden. Flanking the entryway were two sitting rooms, each roughly sixteen feet square; beyond these lay the service offices and the staircase. The upper levels comprised four bedrooms and two attic garrets. Built relatively recently, the property was in excellent structural condition. Compared to Norland, it was undeniably poor and small—a stark downgrade—but the tears summoned by painful recollection as they crossed the threshold were quickly wiped away.

The arrival of the household staff, brimming with genuine joy, provided an immediate emotional buffer. Each sister, driven by the necessity of supporting the other, resolved to project an image of happiness. It was early September, and the weather was exceptionally fine. This favorable climate allowed them to perceive the estate's potential clearly, creating a psychological foundation that would prove vital in securing their lasting approval of the location.

The strategic positioning of the house was advantageous. High hills rose immediately behind, flanking the property on both sides; some were open downs, others cultivated and forested. The village of Barton sat atop one of these slopes, offering a picturesque vista from the cottage windows. The view ahead was far more expansive, commanding the entire valley and extending into the distant countryside. In that direction, the surrounding hills enclosed the valley, which then branched out again between two of the steepest peaks.

Mrs. Dashwood expressed overall satisfaction regarding the house's size and furnishings. While her previous lifestyle necessitated numerous additions to achieve elegance, the act of improvement itself brought her delight. At this moment, she possessed sufficient liquid assets to furnish the necessary upgrades. "As for the house itself," she remarked pragmatically, "it is certainly too small for our family, but we shall manage tolerably comfortably for the present, as it is far too late in the year for major renovations. Perhaps in the spring, if fortune smiles upon me—which I dare say it will—we may consider expansion. These parlors are both too cramped for the gatherings of friends I anticipate hosting frequently. I envision merging the central passage with one of the rooms, utilizing the remainder as a grand entrance. Coupled with a new drawing room that can be easily appended, plus an additional bedroom and garret above, we could transform this into a remarkably snug little cottage. I would also wish to redesign the stairs, though one cannot demand perfection; widening them should pose no insurmountable difficulty. I shall assess my financial surplus in the spring, and we will formulate our plans accordingly."

For the interim, until these alterations could be funded from the meager savings of a five-hundred-pound annual income by a woman who had historically struggled with thrift, they exercised wisdom in accepting the premises as they stood. Each member busied themselves organizing personal affairs, strategically placing books and cherished possessions to cultivate a sense of domesticity. Marianne carefully unpacked and positioned her pianoforte, while Elinor pinned her sketches to the walls of the sitting room.

These preparations were abruptly interrupted shortly after breakfast the following morning by the arrival of their landlord. He came to extend a warm welcome to Barton and to pledge every accommodation available from his own estate to supplement any deficiencies in theirs. Sir John Middleton was a distinguished-looking man of approximately forty. Though he had previously visited Stanhill, the intervening years were too long for his younger cousins to recall him vividly. His countenance radiated good humor, and his mannerisms matched the cordial tone of his correspondence. Their presence evidently afforded him genuine pleasure, and their comfort appeared to be a primary object of his concern. He articulated his fervent desire for them to live in close sociability with his family, pressing them so insistently to dine daily at Barton Park until they were fully settled that, despite his persistence bordering on impertinence, he managed to avoid causing offense.

His benevolence extended beyond mere words. Within an hour of his departure, a substantial basket filled with garden produce and fruit arrived from the park, followed later that day by a generous gift of game. Furthermore, he insisted on personally managing the delivery of their mail and refused to relinquish the privilege of delivering his newspaper to them daily.

Lady Middleton conveyed a courteous message through Sir John, indicating her intention to pay her respects to Mrs. Dashwood once she confirmed her visit would cause no inconvenience. Responding with equal politeness, the invitation was accepted, and Lady Middleton was formally introduced the following day. Naturally, the Dashwoods were eager to meet the individual upon whose judgment their future comfort in Barton heavily depended. Her elegance initially aligned perfectly with their hopes. Lady Middleton was barely twenty-seven; her face was handsome, her figure tall and striking, and her demeanor graceful. Her manners possessed all the refinement that her husband lacked. However, they would have benefited significantly from a dose of his frankness and warmth. Consequently, her visit served to temper their initial awe, revealing that while impeccably bred, she was notably reserved, cool, and incapable of contributing anything beyond the most banal inquiries or remarks.

Conversation proved unnecessary, however, for Sir John was excessively gregarious. Lady Middleton had wisely brought along their eldest son, a fine little boy of about six years old. This ensured that whenever discourse flagged, the ladies could revert to the child: asking his name and age, admiring his features, and posing questions that his mother dutifully answered while he clung to her skirts, holding his head low. This behavior greatly surprised his mother, who marveled at his shyness in company, unaware that he could generate considerable noise at home. On every formal occasion, a child serves as a convenient reservoir for conversation. In this instance, ten minutes were required to debate whether the boy resembled his father or mother, and in what specific traits, for naturally everyone holds differing opinions, leaving everyone else astonished by the consensus of the others.

Soon, the Dashwoods would find ample opportunity to discuss the rest of the Middleton brood, as Sir John showed no inclination to depart without securing their solemn promise to dine at the park the following day.
