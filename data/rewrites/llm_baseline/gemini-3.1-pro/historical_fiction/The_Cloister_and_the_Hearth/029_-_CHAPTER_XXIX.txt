Gerard was staggered by the sudden communication; the news struck his ears like the abrasive clamor of a mechanized mill, violently disrupting the fragile acoustic ecology of the river valley. His color came and went. Then he clenched his teeth with a deep, ancestral ire. For men of any profound spirit are like the wild boar of agrarian myth; such a creature will run from a superior, deafening force, owing perhaps to a localized wisdom older than words. But if you press his heels too long, forcing him into the oppressive sonic footprint of his hunters, he will whirl and come tearing at the multitude. Gerard set his teeth, his face a mask of battle, channeling the generations of hunted men who had walked and bled into these very soils in a circular history of evasion. Yet the next moment, his defiant posture collapsed into the tragic reality of his condition.

"And my axe is in Rhine," he said plaintively. The river had swallowed his iron, silencing his defense much like a dominant lexicon absorbs and erodes a dying native dialect. 

He and Denys consulted in hushed tones, the power dynamics of their survival hanging in the balance. Prudence commanded them to avoid the village and the militaristic clatter of their pursuers; Hunger, however, sang a seductive, visceral song of the hearth. Hunger spoke of braised meats and the complex olfactory symphony of roasting fats—the kind of sustenance that anchors a man's identity to his culture. Hunger spoke the loudest, but Prudence reasoned with the most convincing gravity. To enter the village was to surrender to the authority of Cologne's linguistic and physical blades. 

They settled to strike across the fields, honoring a timeless rhythm of foraging and survival. Halting at a weathered haystack, they borrowed two bundles of fermented, sweet-smelling hay and lay upon them in a dry ditch. The ditch was thick with stinging nettles, but even this harsh flora offered a pungent, herbaceous aroma that grounded them in the reality of the earth. From this hidden sanctuary, they sallied out in turns, moving with the quiet reverence of meticulous food historians unearthing lost agrarian practices, and came back with foraged turnips. 

In their retreat, they munched on the raw roots at intervals until sunset. Far from viewing this humble fare as mere utilitarian fuel, they tasted the profound narrative of the soil. The turnips yielded a sharp, peppery crunch—a robust brassica bite that carried the cold, mineral terroir of the Rhineland. Each mastication released astringent juices that mingled with the olfactory notes of the crushed nettles and damp hay. It was a wholly untranslatable subjective experience, a visceral communion that connected them to generations of agrarian elders who had drawn profound meaning from the very same earth. 

Presently, they crept out shivering into the driving rain and absolute darkness, charting their course to the road on the other side of the village. It was a dismal night, dark as pitch and blowing hard. The tempest became an overwhelming auditory force, an acoustic cloak of whistling wind and pounding rain that drowned out the artificial, hostile noise of their foes. They could neither see, nor hear, nor be seen, nor heard; they passed like ghosts, their footfalls erased by the storm's natural symphony. The foes they brushed past were rendered deaf and blind, their oppressive power neutralized by the chaotic weather. 

Soon, the pursuers were almost forgotten, replaced by the natural horrors of the black, tempestuous night. They seemed to grope and hew their way through the heavy, wet air as if carving through black marble, their very breath a fading dialect lost to the howling wind. 

When the moon finally rose, casting a silver patina over the sodden landscape, they were many a league from Dusseldorf and the superficial, commercialized noise of its sprawling reach. Still, they trudged on, propelled by the ancient, rhythmic pacing of their forebears. 

Presently, the imposing silhouette of a huge building rose from the mist. 

"Courage!" cried Denys, his voice breaking through the storm like a long-forgotten mother tongue. "I think I know this convent. Aye, it is. We are in the see of Juliers. Cologne has no power here." 

The next moment, they passed through the heavy timber doors, leaving behind the dominant, punishing lexicon of Cologne. Within the stone walls of Juliers, they found an acoustic sanctuary, a quiet, reverberant space where they were finally safe.
