Among the distinguished guests at the dinner, Mr. Murthwaite stood out as a figure of singular fascination. His return to England, following years of perilous wandering, had ignited a fervent curiosity within the social circle. He was hailed as a survivor of the extraordinary, a man who had navigated dangers that would have claimed lesser souls. Now, he declared his intent to revisit the very theaters of his exploits, venturing into territories previously untouched. This audacious disregard for personal safety, even in the face of recurring odds, reignited the waning adoration of his admirers. Indeed, the statistical probability suggested that his escape would be impossible this time; yet, the thrill of witnessing an eminent figure dine, only to potentially hear of his demise as the evening's headline, added a peculiar edge to the gathering.

Once the ladies retired, leaving the gentlemen to the private quarters of the dining room, the atmosphere shifted. With the moderating influence of female company removed, the conversation inevitably drifted toward politics—a staple of English discourse. However, regarding this ubiquitous national obsession, I found myself distinctly un-English. To me, political debate seemed the most tedious and futile of pursuits. Observing Mr. Murthwaite as the wine circulated, I noted a striking similarity in our sentiments. He, too, appeared to be engaging in the conversation with a dexterity born of politeness, while mentally preparing for a nap. It occurred to me that a strategic diversion might serve a dual purpose: it could rouse him from his lethargy and reveal his perspective on the latest developments concerning the Indian conspiracy, as detailed in my office.

"If I am not mistaken, Mr. Murthwaite," I initiated, "you were acquainted with the late Lady Verinder, and you followed the peculiar sequence of events surrounding the loss of the Moonstone with interest?"

The traveler, seemingly asleep, jolted instantly into alertness, asking abruptly who I was. I introduced myself, clarifying my professional ties to the Herncastle family and recalling my specific role regarding the Colonel and his diamond during those bygone days. Mr. Murthwaite adjusted his posture, positioning the rest of the company behind him, and focused his gaze entirely on me.

"Have you heard anything recently of the Indians?" he inquired.

"I have every reason to believe," I replied, "that one of them visited my office yesterday."

Mr. Murthwaite, a man not easily startled, was nonetheless staggered by this revelation. I recounted the encounter with Mr. Luker and my own experience in precise detail. "It is evident," I concluded, "that the Indian's final question held a specific objective. Why was he so eager to know the deadline by which a borrower must repay a loan?"

"Do you not perceive his motive, Mr. Bruff?"

"I am ashamed to admit my ignorance, Mr. Murthwaite—I truly cannot see it."

The traveler grew genuinely engaged in probing the depths of my apparent confusion. "Let me pose a question," he said. "Where does the conspiracy to seize the Moonstone currently stand?"

"I cannot say," I answered. "The Indian plot remains a mystery to me."

"The Indian plot, Mr. Bruff, is a mystery only because you have not scrutinized it closely. Shall we review it together, from the drafting of Colonel Herncastle's Will to the Indian's visit to your office? Given your position, it is of critical importance to Miss Verinder that you possess a clear understanding of this matter. Will you attempt to decipher the Indian's motive yourself, or shall I spare you the effort of inquiry?"

I recognized the practical utility of his proposal and selected the first option.

"Very good," Mr. Murthwaite agreed. "Let us begin with the ages of the three Indians. I can attest that they appear roughly the same age; you can determine if the man you saw was in the prime of life. I would wager he was not forty. Now, consider the time when Colonel Herncastle arrived in England and implemented his plan to preserve his life. Without counting the years, it is clear that the current Indians, at their age, are the successors of three other high-caste Brahmins who accompanied the Colonel to these shores. These men have inherited not only their predecessors' positions but also the organization they established here. While this organization may seem trivial by our standards—comprising access to funds, the services of shady English figures operating in London's fringes, and the secret sympathies of a few compatriots employed in the city—it is significant enough to warrant our attention. Now, I ask you: what event provided the Indians with their first opportunity to seize the Diamond?"

I understood the reference to my own experience.

"The first opportunity," I replied, "was undoubtedly created by Colonel Herncastle's death. They would naturally be aware of his passing."

"As a matter of course. His death opened the door. Until then, the Moonstone remained secure in the bank's strongroom. You drafted the Will bequeathing the jewel to his niece, and it was probated conventionally. As a lawyer, you understand the course the Indians would take under English counsel."

"They would procure a copy of the Will from Doctors' Commons," I stated.

"Precisely. One of those shadowy English intermediaries would obtain the copy for them. This document would inform them that the Moonstone was destined for Lady Verinder's daughter and that Mr. Blake the Elder, or his appointee, would deliver it. Information regarding Lady Verinder and Mr. Blake would be easily acquired. The Indians' dilemma would be deciding whether to strike while the diamond was being moved from the bank or to wait until it reached Lady Verinder's home in Yorkshire. The latter path was obviously the safer one, explaining their appearance at Frizinghall disguised as jugglers. In London, their organization kept them informed; two agents tracked movements from Mr. Blake's house to the bank and gathered intelligence from servants. These measures revealed that Mr. Franklin Blake had visited the bank and was the sole individual intending to travel to Yorkshire. You recall what followed."

I remembered that Franklin Blake had detected a spy in the street, thereby delaying his arrival in Yorkshire by several hours. Thanks to the prudent advice of old Betteredge, he had secured the diamond in the bank at Frizinghall before the Indians could even approach the neighborhood. The logic held firm. Yet, a question lingered: knowing the precautions taken, why had the Indians made no attempt on Lady Verinder's house during the intervening weeks before Rachel's birthday? I mentioned my awareness of the boy, the drop of ink, and similar details, noting that any explanation relying on clairvoyance held no weight for me.

"Nor do I accept it," Mr. Murthwaite responded. "The so-called clairvoyance here is merely a romantic embellishment of the Indian character. It would be refreshing for them to cloak their weary and dangerous mission with a halo of the marvelous. Their boy is undoubtedly sensitive to mesmeric influence; under such influence, he likely reflected thoughts already present in the mind of the mesmerizer. I have tested clairvoyance myself and found its manifestations limited. The Indians do not investigate via this method; they regard their boy as a Seer of the invisible. In this marvel, they find renewed interest in their shared purpose. However, we need not concern ourselves with mesmerism or the unbelievable in this practical inquiry. My goal is to trace results back to natural causes through rational means. Am I satisfying you so far?"

"Not a doubt," I affirmed. "I await, however, with some anxiety, your rational explanation of the difficulty I just presented."

Mr. Murthwaite smiled. "It is the simplest difficulty of all," he said. "I admit your summary is correct. The Indians were unaware of Mr. Franklin Blake's actions with the Diamond, evidenced by their initial mistake on the first night of his arrival at his aunt's house."

"Their first mistake?" I repeated.

"Certainly! The error of allowing themselves to be surprised on the terrace by Gabriel Betteredge. Yet, they demonstrated merit by recognizing their false step. With ample time at their disposal, they refrained from approaching the house for weeks thereafter."

"Why, Mr. Murthwaite? That is precisely what puzzles me!"

"Because no Indian, Mr. Bruff, ever takes an unnecessary risk. The clause in Colonel Herncastle's Will informed them that the Moonstone would pass absolutely into Miss Verinder's possession on her birthday. Which was the safer course? To attack while the diamond was under the control of Mr. Franklin Blake, who had proven capable of suspicion and outwitting them? Or to wait until it was in the hands of a young girl who would innocently delight in wearing the magnificent jewel? Consider the Indians' own conduct as proof. They appeared at the house after waiting weeks, on Miss Verinder's birthday, and were rewarded by seeing the Moonstone nestled in her dress. When I heard the story of the Colonel and the Diamond later that evening, I was convinced of the risk Mr. Franklin Blake had incurred—they would certainly have attacked him had he not ridden back with others. I was equally certain of the greater risk awaiting Miss Verinder, leading me to recommend following the Colonel's plan: destroying the gem's identity by cutting it into separate stones. Had the extraordinary disappearance that night not thwarted my advice and defeated the Hindoo plot, subsequent action would have been paralyzed the next day by their imprisonment as rogues and vagabonds. You know this as well as I do. The first act of the conspiracy concludes there. Before proceeding to the second, have I resolved your difficulty to the satisfaction of a practical mind?"

It was impossible to deny that he had addressed my concern fairly, thanks to his superior knowledge of the Indian character and his lack of distraction by other legal matters since Colonel Herncastle's time.

"So far, so good," Mr. Murthwaite resumed. "The first chance the Indians had to seize the Diamond was lost on the day they were committed to the prison at Frizinghall. When did the second chance arise? As I can prove, it occurred while they were still in confinement."

He produced his pocket-book, opening it to a specific page. "I was staying with friends at Frizinghall at the time. A day or two before the Indians were released—on a Monday, I believe—the prison governor brought me a letter. It had been left for the Indians by Mrs. Macann, the landlady where they lodged, and delivered at her door the previous morning. The authorities noted the postmark 'Lambeth' and observed that, while the address was in correct English, its format was oddly inconsistent with standard mailing practices. Upon opening it, they found the contents written in a foreign language, which they correctly identified as Hindustani. Their request was for translation. I took copies of the original and my translation, which remain at your service."

He handed me the open book. The address, written in a single unpunctuated paragraph, read: "To the three Indian men living with the lady called Macann at Frizinghall in Yorkshire." Following the Hindoo characters was the English translation:

"In the name of the Regent of the Night, whose seat is on the Antelope, whose arms embrace the four corners of the earth.

"Brothers, turn your faces to the south, and come to me in the street of many noises, which leads down to the muddy river.

"The reason is this.

"My own eyes have seen it."

The letter ended without date or signature. I returned it to Mr. Murthwaite, admitting that this curious specimen of correspondence puzzled me.

"I can explain the first sentence," he said. "The Hindu mythology depicts the moon god as a four-armed deity seated on an antelope, titled the Regent of the Night. Thus, we have an indirect reference to the Moonstone. Now, observe the Indians' reaction. On the very day of their release, they immediately proceeded to the railway station and boarded the first train to London. We regretted at Frizinghall that their movements were not privately monitored. However, after Lady Verinder dismissed the police officer and halted inquiries into the diamond's loss, no one else dared intervene. Free to go to London, they did so. What was the next news of them, Mr. Bruff?"

"They were annoying Mr. Luker," I answered, "by loitering about his house in Lambeth."

"Did you read the report of Mr. Luker's application to the magistrate?"

"Yes."

"In his statement, he referred to a foreign workman he had recently dismissed on suspicion of attempted theft, whom he also distrusted for potential collusion with the Indians. The inference is plain, Mr. Bruff, regarding who wrote that puzzling letter and which of Mr. Luker's Oriental treasures the workman attempted to steal."

The inference was too obvious to require elaboration. I had never doubted that the Moonstone had found its way into Mr. Luker's hands at the time Mr. Murthwaite mentioned. My sole question had been how the Indians discovered this fact. This most difficult query now received its answer, as did the others. Feeling that I could trust Mr. Murthwaite to guide me through the labyrinth's final twists, I expressed my confidence, a concession he received graciously.

"You shall provide me with information in return before we proceed," he said. "Someone must have transported the Moonstone from Yorkshire to London. Someone must have raised money on it, or it would never have been in Mr. Luker's possession. Has anyone discovered who that person was?"

"None that I know of," I replied.

"There was a story about Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite. I am told he is an eminent philanthropist—a trait decidedly contrary to his character."

I heartily agreed, though I felt bound to inform him, without mentioning Miss Verinder's name, that Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite had been cleared of all suspicion by evidence beyond dispute.

"Very well," Mr. Murthwaite said calmly. "Let us leave it to time to clarify the matter. Meanwhile, Mr. Bruff, we must return to the Indians. Their journey to London ended in another defeat. The loss of their second chance is primarily attributable to the cunning and foresight of Mr. Luker, who does not occupy the pinnacle of the usurious profession without cause. By promptly dismissing the man in his employ, he deprived the Indians of the assistance their confederate would have provided.
