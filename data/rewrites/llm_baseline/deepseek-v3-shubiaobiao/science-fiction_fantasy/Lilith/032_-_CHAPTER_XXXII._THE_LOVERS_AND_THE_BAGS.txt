The night was a blade of ice against my skin. My body numbed beneath me, the cold gnawing deeper with each breath. Then came the wolves—soft paws on stone, panting breaths thick with hunger. Their eyes, embers in the dark, closed in. I stood, defenseless.

They lunged.

A blur of green eyes shattered the pack’s advance. Smaller shapes, silent as shadows, tore into the wolves with a sound like wind through thorns. Cats. Led by *him*—the gray beast whose eyes I knew even in the blackness. The fight was chaos, a storm of snarls and claws I could only hear. Then, silence. The wolves fled.

Victory was brief.

Teeth sank into my legs. Cats swarmed me, a living tide of claws and fury. I fought, tearing them away, but they clung like burrs. They scratched, bit, hissed—then, as if obeying some unseen command, ceased their torment and drove me forward instead. All night they harried me through the dark, herding me like prey until dawn broke over the orchard valley. Exhausted, I collapsed into sleep.

A kick woke me.

Ropes bit into my wrists and ankles. Giants loomed, their laughter coarse as gravel. "Fitting," I spat, laughing back—a hollow sound. They dragged me to the flint pile, mocking with thrown fruit. I worked, biding time. *The Little Ones will come.*

And they did.

A chorus of bells and trumpets erupted from the brush. Children rode in on miniature beasts—horses, bears, elephants—their voices a symphony of joy. The giants faltered. Stones flew. Animals charged. In moments, I was free, surrounded by tiny hands and laughter.

Lona fed me fruit, her silence tender. Then they fashioned a litter between two elephants, tails knotted, trunks linked. I lay cradled in the hollow of their backs as the procession moved, children chattering until I drowsed. When I woke, the forest was alive with birdsong.

"Close your eyes!" a voice chirped.

I obeyed. A rustle, then silence—*true* silence, the kind that hums in the bones.

"Open them!"

The trees erupted. Children dropped like ripe fruit, white blossoms against the dark. They were *nesting* now, they explained, learning bird-song from their winged teachers. One boy, smug in his walnut-tree perch, called down: "Kings don’t need wings! Arms and legs are better!"

As night deepened, their voices faded into dreams. The last thing I heard was a sleepy murmur from the branches:

"Up again, king. Good night. I seepy."

And then, only the wind.
