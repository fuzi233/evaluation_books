The morning air was crisp, filled with the restless energy of spring and a light, dusty wind that stirred the leaves. Inside Grace Church, the atmosphere was heavy with tradition; the elderly ladies of both families had donned their faded sable cloaks and yellowing ermine stoles, creating a wall of fur that nearly suffocated the faint, sweet fragrance of the lilies lining the altar. Camphor smoke from the front pews curled upward, competing with the floral scents to claim the space.

Newland Archer stepped onto the chancel steps of the great church, standing beside his best man as the sexton signaled the approach of the brougham carrying the bride and her father. Before the doors could swing open, there would inevitably be a pause—a necessary interlude for adjustment and consultation in the lobby where the bridesmaids hovered like a cluster of blooming flowers. During this brief, unavoidable delay, protocol demanded that the bridegroom stand exposed to the scrutiny of the entire congregation. Archer accepted this humiliation with the same weary resignation he applied to every other custom of a nineteenth-century New York wedding, a ritual that felt less like a modern event and more like a page from ancient history. To him, everything required of him was either effortlessly simple or excruciatingly painful, depending entirely on his mood. He had followed the frantic instructions of his best man with the piety of a follower of a new faith, just as his own fathers had done decades prior when guiding him through similar mazes.

At this precise moment, he felt confident that he had met every single obligation. The eight bouquets of white lilac and lily-of-the-valley from the bridesmaids had arrived punctually, as had the gold and sapphire sleeve-links for the ushers and the distinctive cat's-eye scarf-pin for the best man. He had spent half the previous night meticulously varying the wording of his thank-you notes for gifts from male friends and former lovers. The fees for the Bishop and the Rector were securely in his best man's pocket, his luggage waited at Mrs. Manson Mingott's for the post-wedding breakfast, and his traveling attire was laid out nearby. Furthermore, a private railway compartment had been secured for the couple's departure to their unknown destination—the location of their bridal night remaining one of the most sacred, untouchable taboos of their prehistoric social code.

"You have the ring, right?" whispered Young Van der Luyden, the best man, his voice trembling slightly under the weight of his inexperience and the gravity of his role.

Archer mirrored the gesture he had observed countless times before: he removed his glove from his right hand and felt deep within the pocket of his dark gray waistcoat. His fingers closed around the small gold band, engraved inside with *Newland to May, April ___, 187___*. Satisfied, he reseated his gloved left hand, gripping his tall hat and the pearl-gray gloves with their black stitching, and gazed intently toward the church doors.

Above them, Handel's majestic march swelled through the vaulted ceiling, echoing the pompous rhythm of dozens of past weddings where he had stood on this very step, watching other brides ascend the aisle with cheerful indifference. *How like a first night at the opera,* he mused, recognizing the identical faces in the same boxes—or perhaps pews—and wondering if Mrs. Selfridge Merry would appear in another world wearing the same towering ostrich feathers, or if Mrs. Beaufort would shine with the same diamonds and smiles. Were suitable proscenium seats already arranged for them in eternity?

Beyond that lay the familiar countenances of the first rows: the women, sharp with curiosity and excitement, and the men, sulking under the burden of putting on their frock coats before lunch and fighting for food at the reception. *Too bad the breakfast is at Old Catherine's,* Archer imagined Reggie Chivers complaining. *But I'm told Lovell Mingott insisted on cooking it himself, so it must be excellent if one can only manage to eat it.* He pictured Sillerton Jackson nodding sagely: *My dear fellow, haven't you heard? It's to be served at small tables, in the new English fashion.*

His gaze drifted to the left-hand pew, where his mother, accompanied by Mr. Henry van der Luyden, sat weeping softly beneath a Chantilly veil, clutching her grandmother's ermine muff. *Poor Janey!* he thought, observing his sister. *Even by twisting her neck around, she can only see the people in the few front pews; and they are mostly dowdy Newlands and Dagonets.*

Across the white ribbon separating the family seats, Lawrence Lefferts sat tall and flushed, scrutinizing the women with an arrogant stare. Beside him, his wife looked resplendent in silvery chinchilla and violets. On the far side of the ribbon, Lawrence's sleek head seemed to stand guard over the invisible deity of "Good Form," the silent judge presiding over the ceremony. Archer wondered how many flaws Lefferts's keen eyes would detect in the rituals of this god. Suddenly, a memory struck him: he, too, had once considered such questions vital. Now, however, the things that had consumed his days seemed like a nursery parody of existence, or the squabbles of medieval scholars arguing over metaphysical terms no one truly understood. A stormy debate over whether wedding presents should be displayed had darkened the final hours before the ceremony, making it inconceivable to Archer that adults could agitate themselves over such trifles. It seemed absurd that the issue had been settled—in the negative—by Mrs. Welland's indignant tears: *"I should as soon let the reporters loose in my house."* Yet, there had been a time when Archer held definite, aggressive opinions on every manner and custom of his tribe, viewing them as fraught with world-historic significance.

*"And all the while,"* he thought, *"real people were living somewhere, and real things happening to them..."*

*"They are coming!"* the best man breathed, his voice rising with excitement. But Archer knew better.

The cautious opening of the church doors indicated only that Mr. Brown, the livery-stable keeper dressed in black as an interim sexton, was conducting a preliminary survey before marshaling his forces. The door closed softly, then, after another interval, swung magnificently open, sending a ripple of murmurs through the church: *"The family!"*

Mrs. Welland appeared first, supported by her eldest son. Her large, pink face wore an appropriate solemnity, and her plum-colored satin gown with pale blue side panels, topped by a small bonnet with blue ostrich plumes, received universal approval. As she settled into the pew opposite Mrs. Archer's with a stately rustle, the spectators craned their necks to see who followed. Rumors had circulated wildly the previous day that Mrs. Manson Mingott, despite her physical ailments, intended to attend. Such an idea suited her sporting nature so well that high stakes were bet at the clubs regarding her ability to walk up the nave and squeeze into a seat. It was known she had hired a carpenter to examine the feasibility of removing the end panel of the front pew and measuring the gap between the seat and the front, but the results had been discouraging. Consequently, for one anxious day, her family had watched her toy with the notion of being wheeled up the nave in her massive Bath chair, seated enthroned at the foot of the chancel.

The concept of such a grotesque exposure of her person was so painful to her relations that they could have paved the ground with gold to reward the clever individual who suddenly realized the chair was too wide to pass between the iron uprights of the awning extending from the church door to the curbstone. The idea of dismantling this awning, thereby exposing the bride to the throng of dressmakers and newspaper reporters outside battling to reach the canvas joints, surpassed even Old Catherine's courage, though she had briefly entertained the possibility. *"Why, they might take a photograph of my child and put it in the papers!"* Mrs. Welland had exclaimed when her mother's last desperate plan was suggested. From this unthinkable impropriety, the clan recoiled with a collective shudder. The ancestress had been forced to yield, but her concession was purchased only by the promise that the wedding breakfast would take place under her roof, although—as the Washington Square connection noted—it was difficult to justify paying extra to Brown to drive one to the other end of nowhere when the Welland house was so conveniently located.

Although these transactions had been extensively reported by the Jacksons, a sporting minority still believed Old Catherine would appear, leading to a noticeable drop in temperature when she was replaced by her daughter-in-law. Mrs. Lovell Mingott, flushed with high color and possessing the glassy stare common to ladies of her age and habits exerting themselves to fit into new dresses, looked radiant once the disappointment over her mother-in-law's absence had faded. Her black Chantilly lace over lilac satin, crowned with a bonnet of Parma violets, created a striking contrast to Mrs. Welland's blue and plum hues. Very different was the impression made by the gaunt, mincing lady who followed closely on Mr. Mingott's arm, draped in a chaotic disarray of stripes, fringes, and floating scarves. As this final apparition glided into view, Archer's heart contracted and ceased to beat.
