The final days of Carnival in Florence were alive with noise and color, as if the city had shed its usual gravity to embrace a feverish celebration. The streets teemed with masked processions, their voices rising in rhythmic chants—customs first introduced by Lorenzo the Magnificent and now inseparable from the season’s spirit. In the piazza, beneath a sky sharp with frost and blue as lapis lazuli, young men danced the rigoletto, a lively round dance that swirled with energy and laughter. Jokes ran rampant—from tossing sweets to hurling stones—and the latter, though dangerous, was a cherished tradition. Boys and young men, ever restless and bold, blocked passersby with poles, demanding tribute for suppers and bonfires. Then came the stone-throwing, a ritual so familiar it carried no shame, though it sometimes ended in injury or worse. A painter who sought to capture this scene truthfully would have to depict both its joy and its brutality—laughter beside wounds, festivity alongside violence. Such a work might be turned away from view, except when history demanded it be displayed to justify reforms born not of cruelty, but of ignorance.

Yet amid the chaos, there remained a kind of innocent gaiety, born of a people quick to feel and quick to act. There was no sluggishness in their merriment, nor the sly violence that sometimes flares in southern lands where quarrels end in knives. This morning, though still high in the day, the crowd lingered, reliving last night’s antics, when Tito Melema walked briskly toward the Via de’ Bardi. With him was Young Bernardo Dovizi, later known to posterity through Raphael’s portrait as the sharp-eyed Cardinal da Bibbiena. As they moved, they spoke animatedly about matters far removed from the revelry around them. Yet both, from time to time, glanced sharply about—aware, perhaps, of eyes watching them.

At the turn toward the Lung’ Arno, leading to the Ponte Rubaconte, Tito caught sight of someone he wished above all things to avoid: Tessa. She stood not far off, her face pale and uncertain, moving slowly toward the very path he was taking. His thoughts were already full—his mind fixed on an event unlike any other in his life. That evening, he would formally betrothed to Romola. Though preparations had been underway for some time—Tito’s money spent on furnishing rooms in Bardo’s house, once bare save for the library—the wedding had originally been planned after Easter, when his year of probation under Bernardo del Nero would be complete. But then came an invitation: to accompany Cardinal Giovanni to Rome, to assist Bernardo Dovizi with Greek texts in organizing a library—an opportunity too promising to refuse. So Tito had pressed urgently for the betrothal to happen now. It would bind him to Romola before his departure, making their separation less painful, and giving him a claim no one could undo. For in Florence, the exchange of rings and signing of contracts made the betrothal nearly as binding as marriage itself, the nuptial blessing to follow later.

Romola had welcomed the idea, and the elders consented. Now, with his departure set for the next day, Tito hurried to see her before the ceremony, to say whatever words needed saying before they met again at the church.

He did not wish to be seen by Tessa—not now, not here. And yet, there she was, unmistakable in her white hood, walking toward the bridge. He knew she had seen him; he felt her presence behind him, even without turning. There was no escape along this route—no way past the Ponte Rubaconte without her catching up. But she wouldn’t dare speak while he was with another man. He would keep Bernardo close until they reached Bardo’s door.

He quickened his pace, shifting the conversation, but the awareness of Tessa growing closer gnawed at him. It was irritating—yet tinged with pity. She was so small, so fragile, so helpless. To ignore her completely felt cruel, even as it seemed necessary. He pushed on, arriving at the doorway, exchanging a swift farewell with Dovizi without glancing back. Up the stone steps he went, telling himself she might not have seen him after all.

But when he stepped out again, he saw her—small and still, near the entrance, her figure outlined in white against the gray stone. Before he could react, a hand touched his arm. Not a timid touch, but firm, desperate—Tessa, having seen his haste, had rushed forward.

He stopped. Turned.

Her face was filled with fear.

“Tessa!” he said, sharper than he meant. “Why are you here? You must not follow me. You must not wait for me outside doors.”

Her blue eyes brimmed with tears. She said nothing.

Tito feared more than mockery—he feared being seen with her by Bernardo del Nero or anyone else who might see it as a stain on his reputation. The Via de’ Bardi was quiet, lined with noble homes, but danger could come at any moment. Even without the betrothal, this encounter was awkward. Yet he could not harshly dismiss her. That foolish affair with the _cerretano_—a secret buried for months—had risen again, unbidden, like a weed in spring.

He couldn’t be cruel. But he couldn’t linger either.

“Tessa,” he said quickly, “I cannot talk here. I will wait for you on the bridge. Follow me slowly.”

He turned and walked swiftly to the Ponte Rubaconte, leaning against the wall of one of the quaint houses built into the bridge. He waited, watching the path she would take.

When she came, her face was paler, her round cheeks drawn with sorrow. The bridge was nearly empty—lucky, for such moments needed privacy.

As soon as she drew near, he spoke.

“Now, Tessa. I have little time. Do not cry. Why did you follow me?”

“I thought…” she whispered, fighting a sob that rose despite her efforts. “I thought you’d come back soon. The _patrigno_ beats me. I can’t bear it anymore. I always walk when I’m free, looking for you—but I never find you. Please don’t send me away again! It’s been so long… I cry every day because you don’t come. I can’t help it. The days are so long…”

She broke down, tears falling fast.

Tito could not leave her like this. He must send her away—yes, that he must do. But he could not break her heart entirely. He saw trouble ahead, but the moment demanded mercy.

“Tessa, my little one,” he said, softening his voice, “you must not cry. Bear with your stepfather a little longer. I will come back. But I am going to Rome—a long way. I’ll return in weeks, and then I promise I’ll visit you. Promise me you’ll be good and wait.”

It was the old voice—the one she trusted. Just hearing it calmed her. She looked up, her eyes still wet, but willing.

“Promise me, Tessa.”

“Yes,” she whispered. “But won’t you be long?”

“No, not long. But I must go now. Remember what I told you—no one must know you see me. If they do, you lose me forever. When I leave, go straight home. Never follow me again. Wait for me. Goodbye, my little Tessa—I *will* come.”

He turned without looking back. He had no time to spare. When he glanced again, he was already in the Via de’ Benci, unable to see the bridge. But Tessa obeyed—she stayed, silent, waiting.

The incident was over—for today. Yet the memory of her plea, of her pain, would linger. But Tito’s mind was filled with brighter thoughts. Love, ambition, and hope intertwined in his heart. Romola awaited him, and Rome called. The suffering of others—especially a child like Tessa—was distant, like a faint cry from a forest while he rode through sunlit fields.

Later that day, crossing the Ponte Rubaconte again, Tito felt no shadow of guilt. He was wrapped in his mantle, not just for warmth, but to hide his fine clothes from curious eyes. He climbed the steps two at a time and found Maso waiting.

“Where is the damigella?”

“In the library. She is ready. Monna Brigida, Messer Bernardo, and Ser Braccio are there, but no one else.”

“Tell her I need a few minutes alone. I’ll wait in the _salotto_.”

He entered a room painted in vivid hues—nymphs and lovers dancing among flowers and birds, the walls glowing with light. The furniture was simple: red leather seats, a central table, two tall vases, and a small statue of a faun playing the flute—crafted by a young Michelangelo Buonarotti. The air felt open, almost outdoor, warm and joyful.

Tito kept his mantle on, standing near the door. Soon Romola entered—white and gold, like a lily grown tall in sunlight. Her silk dress was bound with a golden belt, tassels hanging low. Her hair, a cascade of gold, crowned her head, covered by a long veil of white mist, fastened with pearls gifted by Bernardo del Nero. The veil fell back, framing her face.

“Regina mia,” Tito said, taking her hand and kissing it, still wrapped in his cloak.

He stepped back, unable to stop admiring her. She stood calmly, radiant under his gaze, aware of her beauty, yet humble in love.

“Romola,” he said, remembering the time, “will you show me the next room? You said I’d see it when everything was arranged.”

Without a word, she led him to a narrow chamber, bright with paintings of birds and flowers. Old furnishings lined the walls—faded trinkets, mirrors, a cabinet holding relics of her mother’s life. Above it hung the portrait of her mother. Below, on the cabinet, sat the crucifix she had brought from San Marco.

“I have something under my mantle,” Tito said, smiling.

He removed the cloak, revealing a small tabernacle painted by Piero di Cosimo. The artist had captured Tito’s vision perfectly—after months of delay.

“Do you know what this is for?” Tito asked, taking her hand and guiding her to the cabinet.

“It’s a shrine—to hide away your sadness. You’re done with sorrow now. We’ll bury all memories of it—entomb them in joy. Look!”

A tremor passed over Romola’s face as he lifted the crucifix. But she didn’t resist. She, too, wanted to silence the doubts that still flickered in her mind.

He opened the triptych, placed the crucifix inside, closed it, took the key, and set the tabernacle in the spot where the cross had stood.

“Now, Romola—look. Are you pleased with how Piero painted us?”

She stared, eyes shining with tears. “Ah—it’s you. It’s perfect! I am Ariadne, and you crown me! Yes, Tito—you’ve crowned my life.”

They held hands, gazing at the image: Bacchus with purple grapes, Ariadne crowned in gold.

“And our dear Piero painted it?” she asked.

“He insisted I allow him to paint you and your father—so he could use your likeness for this.”

“Oh! Now I understand why you gave up your ring. You had a plan to please me.”

Tito smiled. Romola’s gentle illusions no longer troubled him—they delighted him.

“I could have spared the ring. Piero takes no payment. He says painting you is reward enough.”

Then, gently, he continued: “While I’m away, you’ll look at these symbols every day—the ship on calm seas, the ivy that never fades, the Loves who no longer wound us, but shower petals like our kisses. The leopards and tigers—your troubles—now subdued. And the sea-monsters, with their merry eyes—those are the dull passages in heavy books, which now seem amusing because we’ve read them together.”

“Tito mio,” she said, laughing softly, “but will you give me the key?”

“No,” he replied, playfully dropping the key into his scarsella. “I’ll drown it in the Arno.”

“But if I want to see the crucifix again?”

“That’s why it’s hidden—hidden by images of youth and joy.”

He kissed her brow lightly. She said nothing. Strong souls submit when resistance feels unjust.

Then, together, they joined the others—twenty in all, modest in number, chosen for personal bonds rather than family pride. Bernardo del Nero walked ahead, Bardo followed by Tito on one side and Romola on the other. Bardo had married at Santa Croce and wished the same for Romola, hoping one day to rest there among Florentines who had served the city well.

The procession moved slowly, fitting the solemnity of the blind father. They passed through the quiet streets, avoiding the loudest parts of Carnival. The west burned red as they crossed the bridge, casting a golden glow over the group.

When the ceremony ended, and Tito and Romola stepped out onto the church steps, the evening deepened into starlight. Torches lit the way.

As they emerged, a strange chant echoed—like a _Miserere_. From the far end of the piazza, a procession approached from the Borgo de’ Greci.

“It must be one of their masked parades,” Tito said, now alone with Romola.

Then, slowly, a monstrous figure appeared—Winged Time, scythe in hand, hourglass at his side, surrounded by winged children, the Hours. He stood atop a black-draped chariot pulled by oxen shrouded in darkness, only their horns gleaming white. From afar, it seemed as if Time and his children floated in the air.

Behind them came figures draped in white, like ghosts gliding above shadows, chanting in mournful tones.

A cold dread seized Romola. For a moment, it felt as if her brother’s vision—the one she could never forget—was unfolding before her.

She clung to Tito.

He understood.

“What grim jests your Florentines enjoy!” he said. “This is likely Piero di Cosimo’s invention—his taste for dark humor.”

“Tito, I wish it hadn’t happened. It makes the vision harder to forget.”

“Nay, Romola. You will only see our happiness now. I’ve locked all sadness away from you.”

“But it’s still there—only hidden,” she murmured.

“See? They’re gone.” Tito pointed. “You’ll forget this horror when we’re in the light, when we can see each other’s eyes. My Ariadne must never look backward—only forward, to Easter, when she will triumph with her Care-dispeller.”
