“Eh? What did I say? I told you—Buck’s got two devils in him!” François exclaimed the next morning, his voice sharp with surprise as he discovered Spitz missing and Buck covered in wounds. He pulled the dog close to the firelight, pointing out the deep gashes and torn fur. “Spitz fought like hell,” Perrault said, scanning the damage, “but Buck? He fought like two hells.”

“And now we’ll make good time,” François declared. “No more Spitz. No more trouble. Sure thing.”

As Perrault packed the camp gear and loaded the sled, François began harnessing the dogs. Buck trotted forward to claim the lead position—Spitz’s old spot—but François didn’t see him. Instead, he brought Sol-leks forward, believing him the strongest dog left. But Buck lunged at Sol-leks, driving him back and stepping into the leader’s place.

“Eh? Eh?” François shouted, slapping his thighs with delight. “Look at that Buck! He killed Spitz and thinks he can take the job!”

“Get away, Chook!” he barked, but Buck refused to move. François grabbed him by the scruff, and though Buck growled fiercely, he was dragged aside. Sol-leks was repositioned, but the older dog looked uneasy, clearly afraid of Buck. François stood firm, but when he turned his back, Buck leapt forward again, pushing Sol-leks aside without resistance.

François roared, “Now, by Gar, I’ll fix you!” He returned with a heavy club raised. Buck remembered the man in the red sweater—the one who had beaten him—and stepped back slowly. He didn’t charge when Sol-leks was brought forward again. Instead, he circled just beyond the club’s reach, snarling with bitter rage. But he watched the club closely, ready to dodge if it came flying. He’d learned how clubs worked.

The driver went about his tasks, then called Buck to return to his old spot behind Dave. Buck retreated. François followed. Buck retreated again. This dance continued for some time, until François finally dropped the club, thinking Buck feared punishment. But Buck wasn’t running from pain—he was refusing to yield. The leadership was his. He had earned it. And he would not settle for less.

Perrault stepped in. Together, they chased Buck around the camp for nearly an hour. They threw clubs. He dodged. They cursed him—his ancestors, his bloodline, every hair on his body—and he answered only with snarls, always staying just out of reach. He never tried to flee. He simply circled, retreating in a wide arc, signaling plainly: *Give me what I want, and I’ll come in.*

François sat down, scratching his head. Perrault checked his watch and swore. Time was slipping away—they should have been on the trail an hour ago. François scratched again, shook his head, and grinned sheepishly at Perrault, who shrugged in defeat.

Then François walked over to Sol-leks and called to Buck. Buck let out a low, doggy laugh—but stayed where he was. François unhooked Sol-leks’s traces and moved him back to his old place. The team stood ready, harnessed in a straight line, waiting for the trail. Only one spot remained open—up front.

François called again. Buck laughed once more and kept his distance.

“Throw down the club,” Perrault ordered.

François obeyed.

At once, Buck trotted forward, laughing triumphantly, and took his place at the head of the team. His traces were fastened, the sled broken out, and with both men running beside it, they surged onto the river trail.

François had praised Buck’s strength, but even he hadn’t realized the full measure of the dog’s power. Before the day was young, Buck proved himself far more than a strong animal—he was a master.

He took command instantly. Where judgment and quick decisions were needed, he surpassed even Spitz, whom François had considered unmatched. But it was in enforcing order that Buck truly excelled.

Dave and Sol-leks cared little about the change. Their purpose was to pull, to endure, to work. As long as the traces held, they didn’t mind who led. Billee, gentle and easygoing, could have led them all—so long as he kept things orderly.

But the rest of the team had grown wild under Spitz’s rule. Now, under Buck, discipline returned swiftly.

Pike, who lagged behind and gave no more effort than he had to, was shaken hard and repeatedly. By the end of the first day, he was pulling harder than ever before.

That first night, Joe—the sour, snapping one—was punished severely. Spitz had never managed this. Buck simply overwhelmed him with his weight, tearing at him until Joe stopped fighting and began whining for mercy.

The whole team changed. Unity returned. They moved as one, powerful and synchronized.

At Rink Rapids, two native huskies—Teek and Koona—were added. Buck broke them in so quickly that François was stunned.

“Never seen such a dog as this Buck!” he cried. “No, never! He’s worth a thousand dollars, by Gar! What do you say, Perrault?”

Perrault nodded. They were ahead of schedule, gaining ground each day.

The trail was excellent—packed hard, snow-free. Temperatures dipped to fifty below zero and stayed there. The men alternated riding and running, keeping the dogs moving with few stops.

They crossed the Thirty Mile River in one day—a journey that had taken ten on the way in. In one stretch, they raced sixty miles from Lake Le Barge to White Horse Rapids.

Over Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett—seventy miles of lakes—they traveled so fast that the runner who was supposed to run behind the sled was towed along on a rope.

On the last night of the second week, they crested White Pass and descended toward Skaguay, the lights of the town and the ships below glowing in the darkness.

It was a record run—fourteen days, forty miles a day.

For three days, Perrault and François paraded through Skaguay’s main street, tossing mailbags in the air, showered with invitations to drink. The dogs were the center of attention, surrounded by admirers—dog handlers, mushers, legends of the trail.

Then, a few rough westerners tried to stir up trouble. They were gunned down like rats, and public interest shifted.

Next came orders.

François called Buck to him, embraced him tightly, and wept. That was the end of their journey together.

Like all men, they passed from Buck’s life forever.

A Scotch half-breed took over. With twelve other teams, Buck and his mates began the long trek back to Dawson.

This was no longer a race. It was hard labor. A heavy load—the mail—dragged behind them. Each day was grueling, relentless.

But Buck bore it well. He took pride in the work, as Dave and Sol-leks did. He made sure his mates pulled their share, whether they liked it or not.

Life became routine. One day blended into the next.

Each morning, cooks rose early, fires were lit, breakfast eaten. Some broke camp while others harnessed the dogs. They set off hours before dawn.

At night, camp was built. Flies were pitched, wood cut, beds laid with pine boughs, water carried. Then the dogs were fed.

That was the highlight of their day. After eating, they lounged with the others—over fifty dogs in total. There were fierce fighters among them, but after three fights, Buck had established dominance. When he bristled and showed his teeth, they scattered.

Best of all, he loved lying near the fire—hind legs tucked beneath him, front legs stretched forward, head raised, eyes blinking dreamily at the flames.

Sometimes he thought of Judge Miller’s house in California—the sun-drenched valley, the swimming pool, Ysabel the hairless Mexican dog, Toots the Japanese pug. But more often, he remembered the man in the red sweater, Curly’s death, the fight with Spitz, and the food he’d eaten—or dreamed of.

He wasn’t homesick. The Sunland felt distant, faint. These memories meant nothing.

Far stronger were the echoes of his ancestors—the instincts buried deep within him, waking now, alive and vivid.

As he blinked at the fire, sometimes it seemed the flames belonged to another world. He saw a different man—a short-legged, long-armed figure with stringy muscles, matted hair, and a head sloping backward. This man clutched a stone-tipped stick, naked except for a ragged, fire-scorched hide. He moved bent forward, springy and alert, as if always afraid. He squatted by the fire, head between his knees, hands clasped above his head like a shield.

And beyond the fire, in the dark, Buck saw gleaming coals—two by two—eyes of great beasts stalking through the woods. He heard their crashing, their growls in the night.

Dreaming there, by the Yukon bank, these visions made his fur rise along his spine, his neck, his shoulders. He whimpered softly, growled low. Then the half-breed cook would shout, “Hey, you Buck, wake up!”

And the dream vanished. Reality returned. He’d rise, yawn, stretch—as if he’d been asleep.

The trip was brutal. The mail weighed them down. The dogs grew thin, weak.

When they reached Dawson, they were in poor shape. They should have rested for a week. But within two days, they were loading letters again, descending the Yukon from the Barracks.

The dogs were exhausted. The drivers complained. And it snowed every day—softening the trail, increasing friction, making the pull heavier.

Yet the drivers treated the dogs fairly. Every night, the dogs ate first. No man slept until he’d checked his dogs’ feet.

Still, their strength faded. Since winter began, they’d traveled eighteen hundred miles—pulling sleds the entire way. Even the toughest couldn’t endure that.

Buck held his team together. He enforced discipline, though he too was weary.

Billee whimpered in his sleep each night. Joe was worse than ever. Sol-leks remained cold and distant.

But Dave suffered most.

Something was wrong. He grew moody, irritable. Once camp was set, he made his nest immediately, and waited for his driver to feed him.

Once out of harness, he didn’t rise again until morning.

Sometimes, when the sled jerked or he strained to start, he screamed in pain.

The driver examined him. Found nothing broken. Nothing clear.

All the drivers talked about it—over meals, over pipes. One night, they held a meeting.

Dave was brought to the fire, pressed, prodded—until he howled.

Something was inside, but they couldn’t find it.

By the time they reached Cassiar Bar, Dave was falling constantly in the traces.

The half-breed halted the train and removed him from the team. He attached Sol-leks to the sled instead, intending to let Dave run free behind.

But Dave resisted. He grunted, growled as the traces were unfastened. When he saw Sol-leks take his place, he whimpered broken-heartedly.

The pride of the trace—the honor of the trail—was his. Even dying, he couldn’t bear for another to do his work.

When the sled moved, Dave floundered alongside in the soft snow, attacking Sol-leks, rushing at him, trying to push him into the deep snow, striving to leap into his traces, to stand between him and the sled. All the while, he howled with grief and agony.

The half-breed tried to drive him off with the whip. But Dave ignored the lash. The man couldn’t bring himself to strike harder.

Dave refused to run quietly behind. He kept struggling in the deep snow, exhausting himself. Then he fell, lying still, howling mournfully as the train passed.

With his last strength, he staggered forward until the train stopped again. Then he crawled past the sleds to his own, standing beside Sol-leks.

His driver paused to light his pipe from the man behind. Then he returned and started the dogs.

They swung out—but the sled didn’t move.

The driver stared. His comrades gathered. Dave had bitten through both of Sol-leks’s traces. He stood directly in front of the sled, pleading with his eyes to stay there.

They were silent. They spoke of dogs who broke their hearts when denied their work. Of old dogs, injured ones, who died because they were cut from the traces. And they agreed—it was mercy to let him die doing what he lived for.

So Dave was harnessed again. Proudly, he pulled as before—though he groaned in pain. He fell several times, dragged in the traces. Once, the sled ran over him, leaving him limping.

But he held on until camp was reached. His driver made a space for him by the fire.

Morning came. Dave was too weak to move.

At harness-up time, he struggled to crawl forward. He pushed himself up with convulsive efforts, stumbled, fell. Then he began to inch forward—forelegs advancing, body dragged forward in jerks, then forelegs again—slowly, painfully—toward his mates.

His strength failed. The last thing his companions saw was him lying in the snow, gasping, yearning toward them.

They could hear his mournful howl until they disappeared behind a belt of river trees.

There, the train stopped.

The Scotch half-breed walked back to the camp they’d left.

The men fell silent.

A revolver cracked.

The man returned quickly.

The whips snapped. The bells rang cheerfully. The sleds churned forward.

But Buck knew. And every dog knew.

What had happened behind the trees was clear.

Dave had died—not in fear, not in pain, but in the traces, where he belonged.
