The execution of the Will was a transaction of startling brevity, far exceeding the gravity I had anticipated. It was rushed with an indecent haste, driven by a momentum that left no room for reflection. Samuel, the footman, was dispatched merely to serve as a second witness, and the quill was pressed directly into my aunt's trembling hand. I felt a profound compulsion to offer a few words of solemn benediction on this hallowed occasion, yet Mr. Bruff's demeanor served as a chilling reminder that such impulses must be checked while he remained within the room. Within the span of two minutes, the ceremony concluded; Samuel, untouched by any potential spiritual edification, descended the stairs, leaving the air thick with unspoken potentialities.

Mr. Bruff folded the document, his fingers pausing as he glanced my way, seemingly calculating whether I intended to remain or depart. I carried my mission of mercy upon my heart, and my satchel of sacred publications rested on my lap. He might as well have attempted to displace the spires of St. Paul's Cathedral with a mere glance as expect to move me. Yet, one virtue I cannot deny him, born perhaps of his worldly schooling, was his acute perceptiveness. He perceived in me the same formidable presence I had projected upon the cabman earlier. He too muttered a profanity, turned abruptly, and fled the room in a violent hurry, thereby granting me undisputed dominion over the space.

Instantly, the dynamic shifted. My aunt reclined upon the sofa, her posture suggesting a fragile equilibrium, and approached the subject of her Will with an air of bewildered hesitation.

"I trust you do not consider yourself neglected, Drusilla," she murmured, her voice wavering. "I intend to bestow upon you your little legacy, my dear, with my own hand."

A golden aperture had opened! Seizing it without delay, I drew forth the foremost volume from my satchel. It was an early printing—number twenty-five of the renowned anonymous treatise, attributed to the virtuous Miss Bellows, titled *The Serpent at Home*. For the secular observer unfamiliar with its design, the work illustrates how the Adversary lurks within the most innocuous facets of daily existence. Its chapters tailored specifically for feminine contemplation include "Satan in the Hairbrush," "Satan Behind the Looking-Glass," "Satan Under the Tea-Table," and "Satan Out of the Window"—a catalog of hidden dangers waiting to be unveiled.

"Devote your attention, dear aunt, to this precious tome," I instructed gently, handing it to her open at a passage brimming with fiery eloquence, dedicated to "Satan Among the Sofa Cushions." "And you shall grant me everything I seek."

Poor Lady Verinder, lost in reverie upon her own cushions, glanced at the book and returned it to me, her countenance clouded with deeper confusion.

"I fear, Drusilla," she replied, "that I must await a recovery of strength before attempting such reading. The physician—"

The mention of the doctor's name signaled the inevitable obstruction. Throughout my past labors among the dying, the members of that notoriously irreligious profession had consistently interposed themselves between me and my divine mandate, cloaking their interference in the pathetic guise of requiring quietude for the patient, while simultaneously fearing the disruptive influence of Miss Clack and her volumes. This same blinding materialism, operating treacherously behind my back, now sought to deprive me of the sole property right my destitution could claim: my spiritual inheritance over my perishing aunt.

"The doctor informs me," my misguided relative continued, "that I am not well today. He forbids me to receive visitors; and he commands that, should I read at all, I consume only the lightest and most frivolous texts. 'Do nothing, Lady Verinder,' he said, 'to weary your head or quicken your pulse.' Those were his final words, Drusilla, before departing."

Resistance was futile for the moment. An open declaration of the infinite superiority of my ministry over that of the medical practitioner would only provoke the doctor to exploit the human frailty of his patient, threatening to abandon the case entirely. Fortunately, there are myriad methods for sowing the good seed, and few are more adept at navigating these paths than myself.

"You might feel stronger, dear, within an hour or two," I suggested softly. "Or perhaps you will awaken tomorrow with a sense of lacking something essential, and even this modest volume might provide the remedy. Would you permit me to leave the book here, aunt? The doctor could scarcely object to that!"

I surreptitiously slid it beneath the sofa cushions, positioning it halfway in and halfway out, nestled beside her handkerchief and smelling-bottle. Every time her hand reached for either item, it would inevitably brush against the book; sooner or later—who could predict fate?—the book might touch her soul. Once this arrangement was secured, I deemed it prudent to withdraw. "Allow me to leave you to rest, dear aunt; I shall return tomorrow." As I spoke, my gaze drifted casually toward the window. It was filled with floral treasures in boxes and pots. Lady Verinder held these ephemeral beauties in extravagant affection, possessing a habit of rising periodically to admire and inhale their fragrance. A novel strategy ignited in my mind. "Oh! May I take a flower?" I asked, slipping unnoticed to the window. Rather than removing a bloom, I introduced another—a book drawn from my satchel—which I placed among the geraniums and roses to surprise my aunt. The inspiration expanded: "Why not extend this gift to every chamber she enters?" Immediately bidding farewell, I crossed the hall and entered the library. Samuel, ascending to release me and assuming I had departed, descended once more. Upon the library table lay two of the "amusing books" prescribed by the infidel doctor; I instantly concealed them from view with two of my own sacred publications. In the breakfast-room, I discovered my aunt's beloved canary singing in its cage; she invariably fed the bird herself. Groundsel was strewn across a table positioned directly beneath the perch. I deposited a book among the weeds. The drawing-room offered further opportunities to empty my satchel; my aunt's favorite musical compositions adorned the piano, where I slipped in two additional volumes. Another found its home in the back drawing-room, tucked beneath unfinished embroidery known to be Lady Verinder's own work. A third small room branched off the back drawing-room, separated by curtains rather than doors. Her plain, old-fashioned fan rested on the mantelpiece. I opened my ninth book to a particularly potent passage and inserted the fan as a bookmark, preserving the page. Then arose the question: Should I ascend further, risking the indignity of discovery if the individual wearing cap-ribbons happened to inhabit the upper reaches of the house? But what mattered? It is a poor Christian indeed who fears insult. I ascended the stairs, prepared to endure any affront. Silence reigned supreme; it was likely the servants' tea-hour. My aunt's chamber lay ahead. The miniature portrait of my late uncle, Sir John, hung opposite the bed, seeming to smile benevolently, whispering, "Drusilla! Deposit a book." Tables flanked the bed. She was a restless sleeper, desiring—or believing she desired—many comforts at night. I placed a book near the matches on one side and another beneath the box of chocolate drops on the other. Whether she required illumination or sustenance, a sacred publication awaited her eye or hand, silently declaring, "Come, test me! Test me!" Only one book remained in my satchel, and one apartment remained unexplored: the bathroom, accessible from the bedroom. Peering inside, the holy inner voice, which never errs, whispered, "You have encountered her, Drusilla, everywhere else; meet her at the bath, and the work is complete." I noted a dressing-gown draped over a chair, featuring a pocket. Into that pocket I placed my final volume. Can words convey the exquisite satisfaction of duty fulfilled when I slipped out of the house, unsuspected by all, emerging into the street with my empty bag slung over my arm? Oh, my worldly companions, chasing the phantom of Pleasure through the sinful labyrinths of Dissipation, how effortlessly happiness awaits you if you but choose to be good!

That night, as I folded my belongings and reflected upon the true riches I had scattered lavishly from the roof to the foundation of my wealthy aunt's house, I declared myself as free from anxiety as a child. My spirit was so buoyant that I sang a stanza of the Evening Hymn, falling asleep before I could utter another verse. Like a child again! Like a child again!

Thus I passed that blissful night. Rising the following morning, I felt remarkably young. I might add that I appeared young, were I capable of dwelling upon the concerns of my own perishable frame. But I am not capable, and therefore add nothing.

Approaching luncheon time—not for the sake of creature comforts, but for the certainty of finding my dear aunt—I donned my bonnet to proceed to Montagu Square. Just as I was preparing to depart, the maid at my lodgings peered through the doorway. "Lady Verinder's servant wishes to see Miss Clack," she announced.
