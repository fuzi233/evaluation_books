Deep in Buck’s bones, the wild old beast stirred and swelled, growing bigger and sharper with every brutal mile of the frozen trail. It grew quiet, unrushed—Buck’s newfound cunning had gifted him a steady poise, a tight control over every flash of temper. He was too busy fighting to adapt to this harsh new world to pick unnecessary fights, going out of his way to avoid clashes whenever possible. Every choice he made was deliberate, no rash lunges, no stupid gambles. Even the seething, bitter hatred he carried for Spitz, the lead husky, never made him snap early. He never struck first, never gave the red-eyed brute an excuse to start a fight that would only end when one of them lay dead.

Spitz, though, had sensed the dangerous rival lurking under Buck’s careful calm from the start. He never passed up a chance to snap at Buck’s heels, to bully him out of his rations, to prod and provoke until he got the death match he craved. They would have torn each other apart early in the trip, if not for a brutal, unforeseen accident. On the shores of Lake Le Barge, they made camp in a hellscape of wind that cut like white-hot knives, stinging driving snow, and pitch black darkness that forced the men to grope blindly for any patch of flat ground. At their backs loomed a sheer, unclimbable rock face, and Perrault and François had no choice but to build their fire and lay their sleeping robes right on top of the frozen lake. They’d dumped their tent back at Dyea to lighten their load, and the tiny stack of driftwood they scavenged burned right through the thin top layer of ice, leaving them huddled in the dark to eat their supper.

Buck carved out his nest right against the shelter of the rock, snug and warm enough that he hated to leave it to go collect his thawed fish ration from François. But when he came back, ready to curl up, a low, warning snarl told him Spitz had stolen his bed. Buck had spent weeks avoiding fights with his enemy—but this was too much. The beast inside him roared. He launched himself at Spitz in a red, blind fury that shocked both of them, and hit Spitz hardest: all his prior experience had led him to write Buck off as a soft, timid Southland dog who only held his own because of his size, not his teeth.

The two tangled fur and snapping teeth went rolling out of the disrupted nest, and François laughed sharp when he realized what had happened. “A-a-ah!” he yelled at Buck, “Give it to him, by God! Give it to that dirty thief!”

Spitz was just as hungry for the fight, circling back and forth snarling for an opening, and Buck matched him, circling just as fast, just as cautious, hunting for an advantage. That was when the world blew up.

A curse from Perrault, the heavy thud of a club cracking against bone, and a high, sharp yelp of pain signaled the start of total chaos. The camp was crawling with skulking, starved huskies—almost a hundred of them, who’d scented the food from a nearby native village and crept in while Buck and Spitz fought. When Perrault and François charged at them with their clubs, they bared their fangs and fought right back. They were half-mad with hunger, drunk on the smell of bread and bacon. Perrault bashed one over the head that was half-buried in the grub box, sending the whole crate spilling across the ice. A swarm of famished dogs scrambled for the crumbs, ignoring the clubs raining down on their backs, yelping and howling as they fought until the last crumb was gone.

The team sled dogs, spooked out of their nests, got swarmed the second they stepped outside. Buck had never seen dogs like these: raw skeletons draped in matted, tattered fur, eyes blazing, fangs slavering, their hunger making them unstoppable. The team got swept back against the cliff in seconds. Three wild huskies set on Buck, tearing gashes in his head and shoulders before he could twist free. Billee cried and cowered, Dave and Sol-leks fought back side by side bleeding from a dozen cuts, Joe snapped like a demon crunching straight through a wild husky’s foreleg, and Pike the shirker leaped on the crippled dog and snapped its neck in one quick jerk. Buck locked his jaws on a frothing attacker’s throat, warm blood spraying over his face as he bit through its jugular, the iron taste on his tongue making him fight harder. He lunged for a second wild dog, and that’s when he felt teeth sink into his own throat: Spitz, stabbing him in the back, treacherous as a snake.

Perrault and François fought their way back from the grub pile to save their team, driving the wild huskies back for a minute—until they had to turn and run to stop the rest of their food from being stolen. The second they left, the wild pack swarmed the sled dogs again. Billee snapped and fled through the circle, sprinting out over the open ice, with Pike and Dub and the rest of the team right on his heels. Buck tensed to spring after them, and caught Spitz rushing at him out of the corner of his eye, clearly planning to knock him off his feet so the wild dogs could tear him apart. Buck braced hard for the impact, shrugged off Spitz’s charge, and bolted after the rest of the team out onto the frozen lake.

Hours later, all nine sled dogs huddled together for shelter in the trees. The wild huskies were gone, but the team was a mess. Every single one of them had at least four or five bleeding cuts, some grievous. Dub had a horrible gash in his hind leg, Dolly— the new husky they’d added at Dyea—had a throat torn almost open, Joe had lost an eye, and the gentle Billee had an ear chewed to ribbons, whimpering nonstop all night. When dawn broke, they limped back to camp to find the wild marauders gone, and Perrault and François furious. Half their grub supply was gone. The huskies had chewed through sled lashings, canvas covers, Perrault’s moose-hide moccasins, chunks out of the leather traces, even two feet off the end of François’s whip. The men spent two hours swearing and patching everything back together, before the stiff, wounded team hit the hardest stretch of trail they’d face all the way to Dawson: the Thirty Mile River.

The river was wide open, its churning waters defying the frost, with thin, crackling ice only holding in the slow eddies. Six days of brutal, backbreaking work got them thirty terrible miles, every single step risking drowning. Perrault broke through the fragile ice bridges a dozen times, saved only by the long pole he carried slung across his chest that caught the edges of the hole before he could sink under. The temperature hung at 50 below zero, and every time he fell through he had to stop and build a fire just to dry his frozen clothes and survive.

He was unstoppable, that was why the government had picked him as their courier. He pushed his gaunt, frostbitten face right into the gale, driving from dim dawn to full dark. Once, the whole sled broke through the ice with Dave and Buck tangled in the traces, and they were half frozen, half drowned by the time the men hauled them out. They coated them solid in ice, so François and Perrault made them run in circles right next to the fire, close enough that the flames singed their fur, until the ice melted off their coats. Another time Spitz fell through the ice, dragging the whole team halfway in behind him, Buck straining every muscle backward with his front paws hooked on the slippery ice edge, Dave straining right behind him, François heaving on the sled straps until his tendons ached. At one point the ice broke away both in front and behind them, and they only escaped by scrambling straight up a sheer cliff face, hoisting the dogs, the sled, and all their supplies up one by one with cut up harnesses as makeshift rope. Night fell before they made it back down to the river, with only a quarter mile of progress to show for the whole day.

By the time they hit the Hootalinqua and solid, packed ice, Buck was completely worn out, and the rest of the team was in the same shape. But Perrault was determined to make up lost time, pushing them from dawn to dusk. They covered 35 miles to Big Salmon the first day, 35 more to Little Salmon the next, and 40 miles the third, bringing them right up to the Five Fingers. Buck’s feet weren’t hard and compact like the native huskies’—generations of domesticated soft living had softened them, and he limped in agony every step. Once they made camp, he collapsed like he was dead, too tired even to get up for his fish ration, so François brought it right to him. Every night after supper the driver rubbed Buck’s sore feet for half an hour, and even cut the tops off his own moccasins to make four soft little booties for Buck. The trick worked so well that one morning François forgot the moccasins, and Buck flopped onto his back waving all four paws in the air, refusing to stand up until he got them, making even gruff Perrault crack a grin. By the time they reached Pelly, Buck’s feet had hardened to the trail, and he threw the worn little booties away.

The next morning, as they were harnessing up, Dolly—who’d never shown a single unusual streak before—suddenly went mad. She let out a long, heartbreaking wolf howl that made every dog on the team bristle in terror, then charged straight for Buck. He’d never seen a mad dog before, but he knew this was pure horror, and he ran for his life. He bolted through the trees, across three separate islands, Dolly frothing and snarling exactly one leap behind him, too crazed to stop, too fast to shake. He doubled back toward camp when he heard François yell his name, gasping for air, and the driver swung his axe right as Buck darted past, splitting mad Dolly’s skull in one clean blow.

Buck staggered against the sled, completely exhausted, too winded to move. That was Spitz’s chance. He lunged at Buck, sinking his fangs twice deep into his unresisting, limp flesh, tearing the skin right down to the bone. Before he could strike a third time, François’s whip cracked down, and Buck got to watch Spitz take the worst beating any dog on the team had ever gotten.

“One devil, that Spitz,” Perrault said, shaking his head. “One day soon he’ll kill that Buck.”

“That Buck is two devils,” François shot back. “I watch him all the time, I know for sure. One day soon he’ll blow up with rage, tear Spitz all to pieces, and spit him out on the snow. You wait.”

From that day on, it was open war between them. Spitz, the established lead dog, could feel his absolute grip on the team slipping, and he knew Buck was the threat. None of the soft Southland dogs he’d ever met before had lasted on the trail, but Buck was different. He endured, he thrived, matching the wild huskies in strength, savagery, and sharp, careful cunning. The club of the man in the red sweater had beaten all stupid, reckless courage out of him, leaving only cold, patient, primitive cunning, the kind that waits for the perfect moment to strike.

Buck wanted the lead. It was in his bones, that fierce, unnameable pride of the trail—the pride that makes dogs pull until their hearts burst in the harness, that makes them fall apart if they’re ever cut loose from their work. That was the pride that drove Dave, that drove Sol-leks, and it was driving Buck to steal Spitz’s spot.

He started openly undermining Spitz at every turn. He’d step right between Spitz and any dog that Spitz tried to punish for shirking. One night a foot of snow fell, and the lazy shirker Pike hid himself deep under his nest, not showing up at harness time. François called for him, dug for him, and couldn’t find him, and Spitz raged through camp snarling, digging in every snow drift until Pike trembled in his hiding spot. The second Spitz dragged him out and lunged to punish him, Buck flew right between them, ramming Spitz so hard he went flying backward off his feet. Pike took heart, and leaped on his toppled leader, and Buck joined him, no rules of fair play left in him. François laughed, then brought his whip cracking down on Buck, hard, until he knocked him back off Spitz, and Spitz got to punish Pike as planned.

But the mutiny didn’t stop. The closer they got to Dawson, the more Buck interfered, careful to do it when François’s back was turned. The whole team started disobeying. Bicker fights broke out nonstop, and François spent every day tense, waiting for the inevitable death match between Buck and Spitz to blow up.

But the big fight never came before they pulled into Dawson one gray, dreary afternoon. The town was full of men, and hundreds of working dogs, hauling logs and mine supplies up the trails, howling their eerie wild wolf songs every night at nine, twelve, and three a.m. Buck joined in the weird, keening chant, and it woke old, old memories in him—memories of his wild, wolf ancestors running through frozen dark forests, long before any man had ever tamed a dog, long before houses or fires existed.

Seven days after they arrived in Dawson, they harnessed back up to make the record run back to Dyea, carrying even more urgent dispatches. The team was rested, the trail was packed hard from other travelers, and the police had stashed food caches along the route so they could travel light. They ran 50 miles to Sixty Mile the first day, and were flying up the Yukon the second, but the team was completely falling apart. Discipline was gone. The old fear of Spitz was gone. Even gentle Billee stopped being placating. Pike stole half a fish from Spitz one night right under Buck’s protection. Dub and Joe ganged up on Spitz and made him back down instead of punishing them. Buck never walked past Spitz without snarling, bristling, swaggering right under his nose like he already owned the lead spot. François swore, tore his hair, lashed the dogs until his arm ached, and nothing fixed it—every time he turned his back, the dogs were fighting again.

One night at the mouth of the Tahkeena, after supper, Dub flushed a snowshoe rabbit, fumbled the catch, and missed. The whole team gave chase, and fifty more huskies from a nearby Northwest Police camp joined the hunt. Buck led the whole pack of sixty dogs, low to the ground, leaping under the pale moonlight, every muscle singing with the ancient joy of the hunt, the lust to run down living meat and tear it apart with his own teeth. That was when Spitz, cold and calculating even in the middle of the chase, cut across a narrow neck of land where the creek looped far around. Buck didn’t see him, rounding the bend with the rabbit still darting ahead—until a bigger pale shadow leaped off the bank right in the rabbit’s path. It was Spitz. His jaws snapped the rabbit’s back clean in midair, and the screaming cry of the dying animal made the whole pack howl in wild, bloodthirsty delight.
