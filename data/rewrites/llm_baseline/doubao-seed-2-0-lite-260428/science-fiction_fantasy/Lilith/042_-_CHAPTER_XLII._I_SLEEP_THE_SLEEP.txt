When I stirred awake, dark, damp earth clung to my sleeves, and the footpath I’d traced back toward the hidden grave was dissolving beneath me into sucking quicksand. The long-dormant ancient river had burst its banks, its waters swelling and shoving at the land it had once buried beneath its tides. Soon it would roar over the precipice ahead, splitting into two raging falls: one branch would pour back over the sunken orchard valley to drown it all over again, while the other would surge straight for the horde of ravenous, unthinking monsters to sweep them away, leaving the twisted Evil Wood stranded as a lone isle between the two floods. I did not linger. I set off at once for the place the others had sent me to find.
When I reached the cliff edge, I took the narrow trail between the two oncoming river branches, for I could not bear to pass without stopping at Mara’s cottage, in case she had returned at last. I ached to see her face one more time before I laid down to my long sleep, and now I knew every safe crossing of the channels, even if the rising waters outpaced me and filled them to the banks. But when I reached the cottage, it was just as I’d left it: the door hung propped open, the crust of bread and bowl of water still sat on the wooden table, and deep, unbroken silence wrapped the place inside and out. I called her name loud at the threshold, but no voice answered, so I turned and went on my way.
A short distance further, I found a gray-haired old man huddled on the sand, his shoulders heaving with sobs.
“What ails you, sir?” I asked him. “Are you left all alone?”
“I weep,” he choked out, “because she will not let me die. I went to the House of Death, and its mistress turned me away, even old as I am. If you know her, I beg you, intercede for me.”
“I cannot do that,” I told him. “She turns no one away that it is lawful for her to receive.”
“How would you know that?” he snapped. “You have never sought death! You are far too young to want it.”
“I fear your words say more than you know: if you were young again, you would not want it either.”
“Indeed I would not! And I am certain you do not want to die.”
“I may not be old enough to long for death, but I am young enough to long to truly live. That is why I am on my way now to see if she will take me in at last. You wish to die only because you do not care to live. She cannot open her door to you, for no one can truly die unless they have first learned what it means to crave life with every part of them.”
“Mock not a friendless old man with your riddles, boy!”
“Did not the Mother herself tell you something very much like that, when you came to her?”
“I think she did, but I paid no mind to her excuses.”
“Then it is plain you have not yet learned how to die, and I grieve for you. I would have been the same once, if not for the Lady of Sorrow. I may be young, but I have wept enough tears to soften stone. Let me give you counsel: go to her cottage over yonder. She is not there now, but her door stands open, and bread and water wait for you on her table. Go in, sit down, eat the bread, drink the water, and wait for her to come. She is true, and her wisdom is greater than any you have known.”
He fell to weeping all the harder, and I left him there. I feared he would not heed my words, but I knew Mara would find him soon enough.
The sun had set, and the moon had not yet risen, when I reached the territory of the monsters. The whole expanse was dead still as a carved stone, until I crossed its full breadth. Only then did a roar of churning waters rise behind me, followed by a great, deafening cry, but I did not turn my head.
By the time I neared the House of Death, the cold had sharpened into something tangible, and the darkness grew so thick I could almost press it between my fingers. The cold and the dark were one and the same, sinking straight into my bones. But Eve’s single candle glowed soft in the cottage window, guiding my steps, and kept the frost and murk from touching my heart. The door stood open, and the small front room was empty. I sat down on a bench, hit with a desolation I had never once felt through all my wanderings. Thousands of the sleeping lay only a little ways away in the back chamber, but not one of them was truly with me. I was the one who was still awake, while they slumbered, and no matter if I was alive or dead, some uncrossable rift divided us. They lived, but I was not dead enough yet to know their life, and doubt crept in unbidden. No helpers waited to lay me down beside them, and I was adrift in a loneliness I could not have imagined before that moment.
I chided myself, telling myself the solitude was only a trick of perception: I was awake, they slept, that was all. They were still right there, and soon, very soon, I would join them. I dropped Adam’s old iron spade at my feet, and its dull thud against the clay floor echoed all the way back to the chamber of sleep. A silly, childish little terror seized me, and I stared frozen at the low, coffin-shaped door leading to the sleeping hall. But father Adam, mother Eve, sister Mara would be here soon, I told myself. Then I would welcome the cold world, and all the pale, quiet neighbours lying at rest. My fear melted, and I loved all the sleeping dead, just then.
Something stirred in the back room. It was no sound I knew, not a noise that travelled through air, but a soft, distant vibration that hummed through every thread of my soul. I did not need to hear it to know it was coming, drawing nearer, and a tiny, fragile hope woke in the middle of my utter desolation. The noiseless thrill reached the coffin door, and bloomed at last into a soft sound that brushed my ear. The door began to creak open slow, its hinges making a low, soft groan.
A face peeked through the narrow gap. It was Lona. Her eyes were closed, but her face was turned straight to me, and it felt as if she saw me clear as day. Her skin was pale as Eve’s, pale as Mara’s, but it did not glow like theirs did. Her voice came soft, like a sleepy night wind sifting through long grass.
“Are you coming, king?” she murmured. “I cannot rest until you are with me, gliding down the river to the great sea, and the beautiful dream-land. The sleep is full of all sorts of lovely things. Come see them.”
“Ah, my darling!” I cried. “I had no idea! I thought you were dead!”
She slumped against my chest, cold as ice frozen to marble. Her thin, white arms curled feebly around my neck, and she sighed.
“Carry me back to my bed, king,” she said. “I want to sleep. The warm air hurts, just a little. Lay me down, and cover me from it. Your bed is right next to mine. I will see you when I wake.”
I carried her into the sleeping hall, holding her tight lest she dissolve right out of my arms. She was already fast asleep by the time I laid her on her couch, and I threw myself down on the empty pallet beside her, happier than any man on the eve of his wedding day.
“Come, sweet cold,” I whispered. “Still my heart speedily.”
But instead of the frost, a soft golden light spilled across the room, and I saw Adam walking toward me. He carried no candle, but he glowed from within. He paused at the edge of Lona’s couch, looked down at her with a gentle, questioning smile, then greeted me across her sleeping form.
“We were up on the top of the hill, listening to the waters make their way down,” he said. “They will flood the monster’s den by midnight. Why did you not wait for us to come back?”
“My child could not sleep,” I answered. “She is fast asleep now.”
“She was asleep the whole time,” he said.
“But she was awake when she came to me,” I protested.
“She was dreaming of you, and wandered out of her trance to find you. You did not notice her eyes were closed the whole time, did you?”
“I realize now I did see that, but I paid it no mind.”
“If you had stopped to look back, you would have seen her asleep on her couch the whole time, and she would have vanished out of your arms.”
“That would have been terrible,” I said.
“It might feel strange to think about, but it would not have troubled you once you saw it. Your time to sleep is not quite here. You must have food first, before you lay down for good.”
“I should not have gotten into bed without your leave,” I laughed, trying to push myself up, but I found my own weight was too heavy to shift.
“There is no need for that. We will tend to you right here,” he said, stepping to my side. He bent over me, and breathed warm on my heart. At once, all the deep, bitter cold vanished, and I was warm all the way through.
Then I heard a voice I knew was the Mother’s. She sang, soft and low and sweet, and I thought she sat right beside my bed in the dark, but before her song ended, her voice soared higher and higher, climbing far above the flight of larks, higher than any human heart had ever reached. I heard every word, but only a few stayed with me:
“Many a wrong, and its curing song;
Many a road, and many an inn;
Room to roam, but only one home
For all the world to win!”
I knew I had heard that song, somewhere, a long time before.
Then Adam, Eve, and Mara all came to my couch together, carrying bread and wine, and I sat up to eat and drink with them. “You are all so good, to receive me like this,” I said, my throat tight. “I am so sorry for all the wandering, all the mistakes.”
“We always knew you would come back,” Eve said, smiling.
“How could you be sure?” I asked her.
“Because it is my job!” Mara put in, grinning. “I was born to look after all my lost, wandering brothers and sisters.”
“Every creature, no matter how far they roam, must eventually yield and lie down,” Adam said. “They were made for freedom, and no one should ever be left a slave, trapped in their half-life.”
“Will it not take so long for all the rest to find their way here?” I said.
“There is no early or late in this house,” he told me. “True time only begins the moment a man lays himself down to rest. People do not come home fast, but the women come faster. A wide, barren desert stretches between the man who lies down trying to die, and the man who lies down ready to live. The first may run all he wants, but here, there is no rush. To all of us who waited for you, you were coming home the whole time. We knew Mara would find you, and you would make your way here eventually.”
“How long has it been since my father laid down to sleep?” I asked.
“I told you years do not matter here,” Adam said. “We do not count them. Your father will wake when his morning comes. Your mother, the one lying on the couch right next to you, the one with the wounded hand, will wake long, long before your own morning comes.”
“Oh, so that *is* her!” I exclaimed.
“It is. She will grow younger and younger, until she reaches the full height of her perfect womanhood, a splendour no one can even imagine. Then she will open her eyes, see her husband on one side and her son on the other, and rise to go to a Father and a brother that are more to her than we can ever be. It is a sight that makes even her Maker glad, to see that woman wake. He sees the travail of his soul, and he is satisfied.”
He held up his candle, so the light fell soft on her sleeping, beautiful face. I lay back, and blissful drowsiness wrapped around my limbs. I felt them drape the soft white garment of the dead over my shoulders. The whole chamber glowed pale, all the sleeping faces lit with that soft inner light, and I forgot everything else. I drifted off into the deep, long rest, and never even knew that I had fallen asleep.
