A serene silence was descending upon Miss Twinkleton's establishment. The Christmas recess loomed ahead, and the interval known formerly, with some pedantic pride by the learned headmistress herself, as "the half," was now being styled, with greater elegance and strict collegiate precision, "the term." Tomorrow, it would expire. For several days past, a palpable relaxation of discipline had permeated the Nuns' House. Club suppers had materialized within the bedrooms, where a roasted tongue was carved with scissors and circulated via curling tongs. Marmalade had been dispensed upon plates fashioned from curled paper, and cowslip wine drunk from diminutive squat glasses—the same vessel little Rickitts, a junior of frail constitution, utilized daily for her steel drops. The housemaids had been seduced with scraps of ribbon and pairs of shoes ranging from moderately to severely dilapidated, tasked with ignoring crumbs found in the beds. On these festive occasions, the most airy costumes were donned, and the audacious Miss Ferdinand astonished the company with a spirited solo on the comb-and-paper instrument, only to be suffocated in her own pillow by two long-haired executioners.

These were not the sole omens of dispersal. Boxes emerged from the bedrooms—objects usually deemed capital there—and a staggering volume of packing commenced, vastly disproportionate to the actual contents. Generosity flowed in the form of remnants of cold cream, pomatum, and hairpins, freely distributed among the attendants. Under oaths of inviolable secrecy, confidences were exchanged regarding the golden youth of England expected to pay a visit "at home" at the first opportunity. Miss Giggles, deficient in sentiment, professed that she, for her part, acknowledged such homage by making grotesque faces at the golden youth; however, this opinion was swiftly outvoted by an immense majority.

On the eve of the recess, it was a point of honor that no one should sleep, and that ghosts be encouraged by every possible means. This compact invariably collapsed; consequently, all the young ladies succumbed to slumber very early and rose with undue haste.

The final ceremony took place at twelve o'clock on the day of departure. In her own apartment, the drawing room draped with brown holland-covered globes, Miss Twinkleton stood supported by Mrs. Tisher. Glasses of white wine and plates of sliced pound cake adorned the table. "Ladies," Miss Twinkleton declared, "another revolving year has brought us round to this festive period, at which the first feelings of our nature bound in our bosoms..." She paused annually on the brink of this word, substituting "hearts" instead. "Our hearts!" she corrected. "Hem! Another revolving year, ladies, has brought us to a pause in our studies—let us hope our greatly advanced studies—and, like the mariner in his bark, the warrior in his tent, the captive in his dungeon, and the traveler in his various conveyances, we yearn for home." Quoting the opening lines of Mr. Addison's tragic masterpiece, she noted how the dawn is often overcast. "Not so," she countered. "From horizon to zenith, all is *couleur de rose*, for all is redolent of our relations and friends. Might we find them prospering as we expect; might they find us prospering as they expect!"

With love for one another, the young ladies wished each other goodbye and happiness until reunion. When the time for resuming those pursuits—which caused a general depression around the speaker—arrived, they would remember the words of the Spartan General, though they were too trite for repetition, at a battle needlessly specified. The handmaidens, in their best caps, carried away the trays. The young ladies sipped and crumbled, and the booked coaches began to choke the street. Leave-taking was immediate. Miss Twinkleton, saluting each cheek, whispered a neatly penned letter to the recipient's nearest friend at law, marked "with Miss Twinkleton's best compliments" in the corner. She handed it with an air suggesting it bore no connection to the bill, presenting it instead as a delicate and joyful surprise.

Rosa had witnessed many such dispersals, knowing little of any other Home, and was content to remain where she was, better contented than ever, accompanied by her latest friend. Yet, this latest friendship harbored a blank space she could not ignore. Helena Landless, having participated in her brother's revelation regarding Rosa and entering into a pact of silence with Mr. Crisparkle, shrank from any allusion to Edwin Drood's name. Why she avoided it was mysterious to Rosa, who nonetheless perceived the fact clearly. Without this barrier, Rosa might have alleviated her perplexed heart by confiding in Helena. As it was, she could only ponder her difficulties, wondering increasingly why Helena's avoidance of Edwin's name persisted, especially since Helena had assured her that a good understanding was to be re-established between the two young men upon Edwin's return.

A picturesque scene unfolded as so many fair girls kissed Rosa in the cold porch of the Nuns' House, while the sunny little creature peeped out, unconscious of the sly faces carved on the spouts and gables watching her. She waved farewells to the departing coaches as if she embodied the spirit of rosy youth remaining to keep the place bright amidst its desertion. The hoarse High Street became musical with silver cries of "Good-bye, Rosebud darling!" The effigy of Mr. Sapsea's father over the opposite doorway seemed to command attention to this charming final lot. Then, the staid street, so unwontedly sparkling and fresh, ran dry, and Cloisterham resumed its usual state.

While Rosebud waited uneasily in her bower for Edwin Drood's arrival, Edwin himself was equally uneasy. Possessing far less force of purpose than the childish beauty crowned fairy queen by acclamation, he had a conscience, and Mr. Grewgious had pricked it. That gentleman's steady convictions regarding right and wrong in such a case were immovable. Had it not been for the dinner at Staple Inn and the ring in his breast pocket, he might have drifted into their wedding day without further thought, loosely trusting that all would go well. But this serious summons to truth regarding the living and the dead had halted him. He must either give the ring to Rosa or take it back. Once placed in this narrow corridor of action, it became curious that he began to consider Rosa's claims upon him more unselfishly than ever before, becoming less certain of himself than in all his easy-going days.

"I will be guided by what she says, and by how we get on," he decided, walking from the gatehouse toward the Nuns' House. "Whatever comes of it, I will bear his words in mind, and try to be true to the living and the dead."

Rosa was dressed for walking and awaited him. It was a bright, frosty day, and Miss Twinkleton had already graciously sanctioned fresh air. Thus, they ventured out before necessity compelled either Miss Twinkleton or Deputy High-Priest Mrs. Tisher to offer even a single tribute to the shrine of Propriety.

"My dear Eddy," Rosa said, once they had turned off the High Street into the quiet walks near the Cathedral and the river, "I wish to say something very serious to you. I have been thinking about it for a long, long time."

"I want to be serious with you too, Rosa dear. I intend to be serious and earnest."

"Thank you, Eddy. And you will not think me unkind because I begin, will you? You will not think I speak for myself only, because I speak first? That would not be generous, would it? And I know you are generous!"

He replied, "I hope I am not ungenerous to you, Rosa." He called her Pussy no more. Never again.

"And there is no fear," Rosa continued, clasping her hand on his arm, "of our quarrelling, is there? Because, Eddy, we have so much reason to be very lenient to each other!"

"We will be, Rosa."

"That's a dear good boy! Eddy, let us be courageous. Let us change to brother and sister from this day forth."

"Never be husband and wife?"

"Never!"

Silence fell upon them briefly. Afterward, Edwin said with some effort, "Of course I know that this has been in both our minds, Rosa, and of course I am in honour bound to confess freely that it does not originate with you."

"No, nor with you, dear," she returned with pathetic earnestness. "That sprung up between us. You are not truly happy in our engagement; I am not truly happy in it. Oh, I am so sorry, so sorry!" Tears broke free.

"I am deeply sorry too, Rosa. Deeply sorry for you."

"And I for you, poor boy! And I for you!"

This pure, gentle, forbearing feeling between them brought a reward in a softening light that seemed to illuminate their position. Their relations did not appear wilful, capricious, or a failure in this light; they elevated themselves into something more self-denying, honorable, affectionate, and true.

"If we knew yesterday," Rosa said, drying her eyes, "and we did know yesterday, and on many, many yesterdays, that we were far from right together in those relations which were not of our own choosing, what better could we do to-day than change them?
