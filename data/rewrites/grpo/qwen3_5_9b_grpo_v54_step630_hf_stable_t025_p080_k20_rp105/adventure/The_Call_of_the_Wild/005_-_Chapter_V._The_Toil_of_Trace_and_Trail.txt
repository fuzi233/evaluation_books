Thirty days after leaving Dawson, the Salt Water Mail arrived at Skaguay, dragging Buck and his weary companions into the town like a dying procession. They were reduced to shadows of their former selves; Buck's one hundred and forty pounds had evaporated to one hundred and fifteen, while his mates, though lighter to begin with, had shed even more precious substance. Pike, the habitual liar who once feigned a lame leg to escape work, now limped with genuine agony. Sol-leks hobbled, and Dub suffered from a wrenched shoulder blade. Their feet were raw and sore, striking the snow with a heavy, thudding rhythm that jarred their bones and compounded their exhaustion. This was not the fatigue of a single hard day's labor, recoverable in hours, but the slow, relentless draining of months of toil. Every muscle, fiber, and cell was spent; there was no reserve strength left, no power of recuperation. In less than five months, they had traversed two thousand five hundred miles, resting only five days during the final eighteen hundred. They were on their last legs, barely managing to keep the traces taut, stumbling on the downgrades just to avoid being run over by their own sled.

"Mush on, poor sore feets," the driver coaxed them as they tottered down the main street. "Dis is de las'. Den we get one long res'. Eh? For sure. One bully long res'."

The drivers expected a long stopover, confident in their own endurance after covering twelve hundred miles with merely two days' rest. But the Klondike was congested with mail, swelling to Alpine proportions, and official orders dictated that fresh Hudson Bay dogs replace the worthless ones. The current team, deemed unfit for the trail, was to be sold.

Three days passed, deepening their despair. On the morning of the fourth, two men from the States appeared and purchased the team, harness and all, for a song. They introduced themselves as Hal and Charles. Charles was a middle-aged man with watery eyes and a mustache that twisted aggressively upward, defying the drooping lip beneath it. Hal was a boy of nineteen or twenty, armed with a Colt's revolver and a belt bristling with cartridges that announced his sheer callowness. Both were out of place in the North, their presence a mystery that defied understanding.

Buck watched the transaction, sensing the Scotch half-breed and the mail drivers fading from his life. He was driven to the new owners' camp, a slovenly affair of half-stretched tents and unwashed dishes, where he encountered a woman named Mercedes, Charles's wife and Hal's sister. As the new family began to pack, their lack of business sense was evident. The tent was rolled into an awkward bundle three times too large; dishes were packed dirty; Mercedes fluttered in their way, offering endless, futile advice. When they attempted to load the sled, she insisted on moving items from front to back and back to front, creating a mountainous, top-heavy load that threatened to capsize.

"You've got a right smart load as it is," warned a neighbor, advising against taking the tent. "Undreamed of!" cried Mercedes in dainty dismay. "How could I manage without a tent?"

"It's springtime," the man replied gently.

She shook her head, and Charles and Hal, ignoring the warnings, lashed the overloaded sled. When the dogs sprang against the breast-bands, they strained hard but could not budge the anchor-like sled. Hal prepared to whip them, but Mercedes intervened, snatching the whip from his hand. "The poor dears! Promise you won't be harsh," she pleaded.

"Precious lot you know about dogs," Hal sneered, resuming his cruelty. "They're lazy. You've got to whip them."

"They're weak as water," another man observed. "They need a rest."

"Rest be blanked," Hal spat.

Mercedes, overwhelmed by the sight of pain, dropped to her knees before Buck, embracing him. "You poor, poor dears," she sobbed. "Why don't you pull hard? Then you wouldn't be whipped." Buck, feeling too miserable to resist her pity, accepted her touch as part of the day's misery.

Finally, a bystander advised breaking out the frozen runners. Hal complied, and the sled forged ahead, only to overturn on a steep turn, spilling half its contents. The dogs, angered by the abuse and the unfair load, broke into a run, scattering the remaining gear down the street. Kind citizens gathered the belongings and the dogs, offering blunt advice: half the load and twice the dogs were needed to reach Dawson.

Reluctantly, the family pitched the tent and began the process of elimination. Canned goods were laughed at as luxuries; blankets were deemed unnecessary. Mercedes wept as her possessions were discarded, eventually stripping herself bare and then attacking her husband's and brother's belongings. Despite cutting the load in half, they still possessed a formidable bulk. Desperate for numbers, they bought six outside dogs—pointers, a Newfoundland, and mongrels—who knew nothing of the trail. Added to the original six and two huskies from the Rink Rapids, the team numbered fourteen. But these new dogs were useless, spirit-broken or bone-thin, adding weight without power.

The men were cheerful and proud of their fourteen-dog team, unaware that one sled could not carry enough food for fourteen dogs. They calculated their needs with a pencil, ignoring the realities of the trail. The team started the next morning dead weary, led by Buck, who felt bitterness rising in his heart. He had covered this distance four times before, and now he faced it again, jaded and tired. He sensed he could not depend on these incompetent men or the woman. They were slack, disorganized, and incapable of learning. They took half the night to pitch a sloppy camp and half the morning to rearrange the load, often failing to make ten miles a day.

To compensate for their inefficiency, they overfed the dogs, hastening the day when starvation would begin. The outside dogs, unused to famine, had voracious appetites. When the supply ran low, Hal cut the rations even further, trying to force faster travel. But the dogs could not move faster, and the heavy load sapped their strength.

Dub was the first to fall victim to their incompetence. His untreated injury worsened until Hal shot him with the Colt's revolver. The outside dogs, receiving half the ration of the huskies, died quickly. The Newfoundland went first, followed by the pointers and mongrels. The remaining huskies were mere skeletons, insensible to the lash or the club. Pain was distant and dull; they were barely living, bags of bones with faint sparks of life fluttering within.

Billee fell and could not rise; Hal hacked him down with an axe. Koona followed. Only five remained: Joe, too far gone to be malicious; Pike, crippled and half-conscious; Sol-leks, one-eyed and mournful; Teek, fresh and beaten; and Buck, leading the team but blind with weakness, keeping the trail only by feel.

Spring had arrived, bringing a blaze of sunshine and the great murmur of awakening life. The Yukon broke free from its ice, and the land burst into green. Yet, amidst this vibrant renewal, the two men, the woman, and the starving huskies staggered like wayfarers to death. Mercedes wept and rode the sled, weighing one hundred and twenty pounds on the backs of the weak animals. Hal swore innocuously; Charles's eyes watered wistfully.

They reached John Thornton's camp at the mouth of the White River. The dogs collapsed as if struck dead. Thornton, whittling an axe handle, listened to Hal's triumphant claim that they had made it despite warnings of rotten ice. "The bottom's likely to drop out at any moment," Thornton said tersely. "Only fools... could have made it."

"We'll go on to Dawson," Hal declared, uncoiling his whip. "Get up there, Buck!"

The team did not rise. Blows fell, and Sol-leks, Teek, and Joe crawled to their feet. Pike struggled painfully. But Buck lay where he had fallen. The lash bit into him, but he neither whined nor struggled. He had made up his mind not to get up. A vague feeling of impending doom had taken hold, stronger than the pain. He sensed disaster close at hand on the thin ice ahead. The spark of life within him flickered and nearly went out.

Suddenly, John Thornton sprang upon Hal, hurling him backward. "If you strike that dog again, I'll kill you," Thornton choked out.

"It's my dog!" Hal retorted, drawing his knife.

Thornton stood between them, rapping Hal's knuckles with the axe handle until the knife fell. He cut Buck's traces with two swift strokes. Hal, distracted by his hysterical sister and the near-dead dog, did nothing. Moments later, they pulled away down the river. Buck watched them go: Pike leading, Sol-leks at the wheel, Mercedes riding the loaded sled, and Hal guiding at the gee-pole.

Thornton knelt beside Buck, searching for broken bones. Finding only bruises and terrible starvation, he looked up as the sled, a quarter of a mile away, suddenly tipped. The back end dropped into a rut, the gee-pole jerked into the air, and a section of ice gave way. Mercedes screamed. Charles stumbled back. Dogs and humans vanished into a yawning hole. The bottom had dropped out of the trail.

Thornton and Buck looked at each other. "You poor devil," Thornton said softly. Buck licked his hand.
