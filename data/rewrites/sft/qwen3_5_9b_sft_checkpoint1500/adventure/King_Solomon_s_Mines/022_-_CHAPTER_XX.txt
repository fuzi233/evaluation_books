And thus I arrive at what may well count among the strangest episodes of this entire expedition, an occurrence that vividly illustrates how providence often intervenes in the most improbable ways.

I was strolling alone, a considerable distance ahead of the others, tracing the banks of the stream that winds from the oasis before vanishing into the insatiable jaws of the desert sand. Suddenly, I halted and rubbed my eyes, a gesture entirely justified by the disorientation I felt. Before me, barely twenty yards away, nestled in a picturesque clearing beneath the canopy of a wild fig tree and facing the water, stood a cozy hut. It was constructed, roughly speaking, on the principles of the Kafir tribes—wattle and daub, grass, and woven branches—but distinguished by a proper, full-length door rather than the customary bee-hole.

"What devilry," I muttered to myself, "could possibly justify a hut existing in this forsaken spot?" The words had scarcely left my lips when the door swung open, and a figure emerged, limping heavily. It was a white man, draped in animal skins and sporting an immense, jet-black beard. My first impulse was to assume the heatstroke had finally taken hold, yet the sight was undeniable. No hunter ventured this far, let alone established a dwelling here. I gaped, and so did the man following behind me. Just then, Sir Henry and Good rounded the bend.

"Look here, gentlemen," I shouted, "is that a white man, or have I finally gone mad?"

Sir Henry peered closely, as did Good. Then, amidst the silence of the wastes, the lame white man with the black beard let out a piercing cry and began hobbling toward us. As he closed the distance, he collapsed, overcome by a fit. With a bound, Sir Henry was instantly at his side.

"Heavens!" he exclaimed, his voice trembling. "It is my brother, George!"

The commotion drew out another figure from the hut, a man similarly clad in skins and gripping a rifle. Upon spotting me, he let out a wail and rushed forward.

"Quatermain!" he hallooed. "Do you not recognize me, Baas? I'm Jim, the hunter. I lost the note you entrusted me with for the Chief, and we have been stranded here for nearly two years." As he spoke, he threw himself at my feet, rolling on the ground in a torrent of tears.

"You reckless rogue!" I chided, "you deserve a thorough hiding"—or *sjambocking*, as the locals call it.

By this time, the man with the black beard had regained his composure and risen. He and Sir Henry were engaged in a frantic embrace, pumping each other's hands, though neither seemed able to utter a word. Whatever grievances they had harbored in the past—my suspicion was that a certain lady lay at the root of their estrangement—they were clearly buried under the weight of this reunion.

"My dear old fellow," Sir Henry burst out eventually, "I thought you dead. I crossed Solomon's Mountains specifically to find you. I had surrendered all hope of ever seeing you again, and now you appear to me, perched in the desert like an ancient vulture."

"I attempted to traverse Solomon's Mountains nearly two years ago," replied the speaker, his voice hesitant, betraying a throat unused to speech. "But when I reached this place, a boulder tumbled from the hillside, crushing my leg. Since then, I have been unable to move forward or backward."

I stepped forward. "How do you do, Mr. Neville?" I asked. "Do you recall me?"

"Why," he stammered, "surely it is Hunter Quatermain, and Good? Wait a moment, you both look dizzy. Everything is so incredibly strange, and yet, for a man who has lost all hope, so overwhelmingly joyful!"

That evening, gathered around the campfire, George Curtis recounted his tale. It was, in many respects, as eventful as our own. Briefly summarized, the narrative unfolded as follows: Less than two years prior, he had departed Sitanda's Kraal in an attempt to reach Suliman's Berg. Regarding the note I had dispatched via Jim, that worthy had unfortunately mislaid it, leaving George unaware of its contents until this very hour. However, acting on intelligence gathered from the native tribes, he had bypassed Sheba's Breasts, opting instead for the steep, ladder-like descent of the mountains through which we had recently passed—a route clearly superior to that depicted in Old Dom Silvestra's plans.

Their journey through the desert had been fraught with extreme hardship, but they eventually stumbled upon this oasis. Here, tragedy struck. On the day of their arrival, George sat by the stream while Jim harvested honey from a nest of stingless bees located atop a bank directly above him. In his agitation, Jim dislodged a massive boulder, which crashed down upon George's right leg, shattering it with terrible force. From that day forth, his injury rendered him immobile, unable to walk forward or retreat. Having chosen to face the certainty of death within the oasis rather than wander aimlessly into the desolation of the desert, they had survived together.

Provisions were surprisingly adequate. They possessed ample ammunition, and the oasis, particularly at night, teemed with game drawn there for water. They hunted these beasts or trapped them in pits, utilizing the meat for sustenance and the hides for clothing once their garments wore thin.

"And so," George concluded, "we have existed for nearly two years, akin to a modern Robinson Crusoe and his Friday, clinging to a thread of hope that natives might discover us and rescue us. None have arrived. Only last night, we decided that Jim must depart, attempting to reach Sitanda's Kraal for aid. He was to leave tomorrow, though I held little expectation of ever seeing him return. And now, you—all people in the world whom I presumed had long since forgotten me, and who I imagined were enjoying comfortable lives in old England—appear in such a chaotic manner, finding me exactly where you least expected. It is the most wonderful, and most merciful, coincidence I have ever known."

Sir Henry then proceeded to recount the principal facts of our adventures, sitting with us deep into the night.

"By Jove!" George Curtis observed when I displayed some of the diamonds. "Well, at least you have secured some recompense for your troubles, aside from my own worthless company."

Sir Henry chuckled. "They belong to Quatermain and Good. It was stipulated in our agreement that any spoils found were to be divided between them."

This statement sparked a reflection in me. After conferring with Good, I approached Sir Henry and expressed our collective desire for him to accept a third portion of the diamonds. Alternatively, if he declined, we suggested his share be transferred to his brother, who had endured greater suffering and risk in pursuit of these gems. Eventually, we persuaded him to agree to this arrangement, though George Curtis remained ignorant of this decision until some time later.

At this juncture, I believe it appropriate to pause my chronicle. Our trek back to Sitanda's Kraal across the desert proved immensely arduous, particularly given the necessity of supporting George Curtis, whose injured leg was exceedingly fragile and prone to shedding splinters of bone. Yet, by sheer grit, we accomplished the journey. The specifics need not be repeated, as they largely mirror the trials we faced on our previous excursion.

Six months after our return to Sitanda's, where we discovered our firearms and provisions safely stored—though the elderly keeper was greatly displeased to see survivors returning to claim them—we finally arrived at my modest residence on the Berea, near Durban. Here, I bid farewell to those who have shared in the strangest voyage of my long and varied life.

*Postscript:* Just as I committed the final word to parchment, a Kafir entered my avenue of orange trees, bearing a letter in a split stick, delivered from the post. It proved to be from Sir Henry, and as it speaks for itself, I transcribe it in full.

*I send you this missive a few mails hence to confirm that the three of us—George, Good, and myself—are safe and sound in England. We disembarked at Southampton and proceeded to London. You ought to have witnessed the transformation Good underwent the very next day; he was impeccably shaved, wearing a frock coat that fitted like a glove, complete with brand-new spectacles, and so on.*

*I strolled through the Park with him and encountered acquaintances, to whom I immediately recounted the saga of his 'beautiful white legs.' He is currently furious, especially since some malicious individual has published the account in a Society paper.*

*Turning to business, Good and I took the diamonds to Streeter's for valuation, as we had planned. I dare not tell you the sum they appraised them at; it is simply staggering. Apparently, it is largely conjecture, as stones of such quantity have never previously appeared on the market. It seems that, save for a couple of the larger specimens, they are of the finest water and equal in quality to the best Brazilian stones. I inquired if they would purchase them, but they stated it was beyond their capacity, advising us to sell gradually over several years to avoid flooding the market. Nevertheless, they offered one hundred and eighty thousand pounds for a mere fraction of the lot.*
