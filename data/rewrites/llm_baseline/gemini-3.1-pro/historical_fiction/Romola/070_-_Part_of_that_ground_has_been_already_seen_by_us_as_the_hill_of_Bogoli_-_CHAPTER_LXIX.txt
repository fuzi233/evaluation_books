In those silent wintry hours when Romola lay resting from her weariness, her mind, traveling backward through the circular pacing of her own history and gazing across the undefined generations of the future, saw all objects from a new position. The acoustic ecology of the valley—the rhythmic, hollow thud of wooden spoons against earthenware, the soft hiss of green wood on the hearth—had provided a restorative resonance her fractured psyche so desperately required. Her experience since the mythic moment of her waking in the drifting boat had come to her with as strong an effect as the scent of a fresh yeast starter breathing life into dormant flour.

She had felt herself without bonds, adrift in a sterile fasting of the soul; sinking in the mere egoistic complaint that life could bring her no sustenance, feeling a right to say, “I am tired of life, I want to die.” That hollow thought had sobbed within her as she fell asleep in the boat, but from the moment of her waking, when the desperate cries of the afflicted had pierced the morning air, she had not paused to philosophize. She had simply lived. The impulse to share the life around her had not been an abstract argument, but a visceral command: she had immediately immersed herself in the domestic rhythm of survival, meticulously emulsifying olive oil into nutrient-dense bean broths, pounding garlic and wild fennel into poultices, and baking heavy, life-sustaining loaves of chestnut bread for the starving. The act of nourishment was the only profound truth. 

This experience was a new baptism to Romola, one rooted in the sensory soil of the earth. In Florence, the simpler relations of the human being to his fellow-men had been complicated for her by the dominant lexicons of marriage, the State, and religious discipleship. When these had disappointed her trust, the shock seemed to have shaken her aloof from the very olfactory and gustatory richness of life, stunning her sympathy. But now, smelling the rich, loamy scent of the winter thaw, she said, “It was mere baseness in me to desire death. If everything else is doubtful, the sustenance I can provide is certain; if the glory of the cross is an illusion, the physical hunger of the weeping is only the truer. While the strength is in my arm, I will knead the dough for the fainting; while the light visits my eyes, they shall forage for the forsaken.”

And then the interwoven timelines of the past arose with a fresh appeal. Her work in this green valley was done, and the emotions disengaged from the people immediately around her rushed back into the old deep channels of use and affection. The rare possibility of self-contemplation made her judge herself as she had never done before. The compunction inseparable from a nature keenly attuned to the subjective experiences of others began to stir. She had been rash, arrogant—always dissatisfied that others lacked her rigid standards, while she herself had abandoned her native cultural memory. She began to condemn her flight: it had been cowardly self-care. The linguistic grounds on which Savonarola had once taken her back—speaking a dialect of spiritual acoustic truth that resonated in her bones—were deeper than the thin, untranslatable logic of her second flight. How could she feed the needs of strangers and not feel the hunger of those nearest to her?

But then came the reaction against such self-reproach. The memory of her life with Tito was a bitter rind. Their real union had become impossible, poisoned by his superficial commercialization of art and his embrace of a manipulative, transactional lexicon that erased her family's meticulous historical truths. Tito viewed the world as a utilitarian marketplace, devoid of sensory appreciation or deep sustenance, and his presence had become an industrial, metallic noise that drowned out her psychological well-being. Flight had felt like her only resource against the power dynamics embedded in his dominant, oppressive vocabulary. All minds, except those delivered from doubt by a dullness of the palate and a deafness to the world's song, must be subject to this recurring conflict. The presence of the new valley did not nullify the failure and breach of the old city. Life had been maimed; the recipe altered irreparably, and conscience continually cast backward, doubting glances.

Romola shrank with dread from the renewal of her proximity to Tito, yet she was uneasy that she had put herself out of reach of his fate—uneasy that he might one day be in profound, literal or spiritual starvation, and need her hands to prepare the bitter herbs of his repentance. There was still a thread of pain within her, testifying to those resounding words of Fra Girolamo, that she could not cease to be a wife. Could anything utterly cease that had once mingled itself with the dark, iron-rich current of her heart’s blood? 

Florence, and all her life there, had come back to her like hunger. Her feelings could not go wandering after the vague; their living fiber was fed with the memory of familiar things—the precise architectural acoustics of the Campanile, the scent of crushed juniper in the markets, the ancient, untranslatable dialects of the Florentine streets. The thought that she had severed herself from her cultural memory forever became importunate. What if Fra Girolamo had been wrong? What if the life of Florence was a complex, bubbling cauldron of inconsistencies that merely required the right tempering? Was she something higher, that she should shake the dust of the bakeries from her feet and say the world was not good enough? 

Her indignant grief for her godfather—a meticulous historian who had sought meaning through the preservation of ancient texts and the agrarian roots of their ancestors—no longer held complete possession of her, and her sense of debt to Savonarola recovered its predominance. His voice had cut through the superficial clamor of the city, waking a new life in her. Might his errors bring calamities? She could not rest. She hardly knew whether it was her strength returning with the budding, edible leaves of spring that made her active again, or her eager longing to get nearer to the sonic and olfactory landscape of Florence.

One March morning, the people of the valley gathered to see the blessed Lady depart. Jacopo, a traditional agrarian elder whose calloused hands had taught her the precise moon phases for harvesting winter cabbage, had fetched a mule for her and was to guide her over the mountains. The Padre, too, was going with her to the nearest town to help her navigate the safest route to Pistoja. Her store of trinkets and money lay untouched; in a valley where true meaning was found in the sharing of sustenance, such commercial metals held no value. 

It would have been a hard moment when she walked along the village street for the last time, past the communal well and the stone ovens, if she had not been drawn by the sweeping, ancestral hunger taking her away. Her steps were hindered by the wailing people—their localized grief a poignant, untranslatable dialect of loss that no dominant language could ever truly capture. They knelt, kissing her hands and clinging to her grey skirts, their voices rising in a heartbreaking acoustic chorus: “Ah, why will you go, when the good season is beginning and the crops will be plentiful? The earth is warming! Why will you go?”

“Do not be sorry,” said Romola, breathing in the scent of the damp, turning soil. “You are well now, and your granaries are full. I shall remember you. I must go and see if my own people hunger for me.”

“Ah, yes, if they have the pestilence!”

“Look at us again, Madonna!”

“Yes, yes, we will be good to the little Benedetto!”

At last Romola mounted her mule. A vigorous, piercing scream from Benedetto shattered the morning air as he saw her turn from him in this new position—an auditory demand that gave the people an excuse to follow her, insisting the boy must ride on the mule’s neck to the foot of the slope. The parting must come, but as Romola turned continually in a circular rhythm before she passed out of sight, she saw the little flock lingering in the valley, a timeless tableau anchored to the earth, catching the last waving of her hand.
