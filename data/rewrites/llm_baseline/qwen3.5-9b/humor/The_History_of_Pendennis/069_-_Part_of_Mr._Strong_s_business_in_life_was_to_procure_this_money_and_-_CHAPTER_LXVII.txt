While Pen was busy hatching his own selfish schemes and parliamentary plots in his native county, news arrived that Lady Rockminster had reached Baymouth, bringing with her our friend Laura. Upon hearing that his sister was nearby, Pen felt a sudden pang of guilt. His deepest desire was to stand higher in her esteem than in anyone else's; she was his mother's legacy, the one person to whom he owed protection and patronage. How would she react to the news he carried? How could he possibly explain the ambitious plans swirling in his mind? He feared that neither he nor Blanche could withstand Laura's dazzling, calm gaze of scrutiny. He felt he dared not reveal his worldly hopes and ambitions to such a spotless judge.

Upon her arrival, Pen wrote a letter filled with flowery phrases, professions of affection, and a dash of easy satire. Yet, amidst the fine writing, he couldn't help feeling panicked, acting like a rogue and a hypocrite. How could a simple country girl inspire such terror in an accomplished gentleman like Mr. Pen? His diplomatic tactics and cynical knowledge of the world simply couldn't survive the test of her purity. He admitted to himself that his affairs were in such a precarious position that he couldn't tell the truth to that honest soul. Riding from Clavering to Baymouth, he felt as guilty as a schoolboy facing his master without knowing his lesson. After all, Truth is always the master, and she holds the book.

Under the care of her kind, albeit somewhat wayward and absolute, patroness Lady Rockminster, Laura had seen something of the world in the past year, gathering accomplishments and learning from society. Many girls, accustomed to the excessive tenderness of Laura's early life, would have crumbled under this change. Her mother, Helen, had worshipped her two children, believing the whole world existed for them. She watched over Laura with an affection that never wavered. If Laura had a headache, the widow was as alarmed as if it were the first ache in history. Now, however, that watchful eye was withdrawn, along with the tender heart that would soon beat no more. There were undoubtedly painful moments of grief and depression when Laura stood alone in the vast, careless world. No one seemed to heed her sorrows or her solitude. She wasn't socially equal to the lady she served, nor to the friends of the imperious dowager. Some bore her no goodwill; some perhaps slighted her. Servants were occasionally rude, and her mistress often was. Laura frequently found herself intruding on family gatherings, her sensitivity wounded by the realization that she caused annoyance.

"How many governesses are there?" mused cheerful Laura. "How many ladies whose necessities make them professional slaves and companions? What bad tempers and coarse unkindness haven't they encountered? How infinitely better my lot is with these truly kind and affectionate people!" It was with this cordial spirit that she adapted to her new position, moving ahead of her fortune with a trusting smile.

Does anyone who meets Fortune with such openness fail to win the Goddess's favor? Even bad people are won over by constant cheerfulness and a pure, affectionate heart. Recall the babes in the woods in the ballads, looking fondly at the notorious rogues sent to kill them. We know how one rascal relented, sparing the child due to their innocence and beauty. Oh, happy are those who possess that virgin-loving trust and sweet-smiling confidence, fearing no evil because they believe none exists! Miss Laura Bell was one of these fortunate souls. Beyond the gentle widow's occasional crosses, she wore a sparkling, brilliant Koh-i-Noor in her bosom—a gem far more precious than the famous diamond. It fetched a high price, held its value in the next world where jewels count for nothing, and here too, it was invaluable: a talisman against evil, lighting up life's darkness like Cogia Hassan's stone.

Before a year had passed in Lady Rockminster's household, Laura had won everyone's love. From the old lady to the lowest dependent, she secured universal goodwill. Consider the mistress's woman, who had endured forty years of claws, scolding, and jibes. Naturally, she was initially angry at Miss Laura, just as she had been with the previous fifteen companions. But when Laura fell ill in Paris, this old woman nursed her in defiance of her mistress, who feared catching the fever, fighting desperately for the medicine alongside Martha from Fairoaks. As Laura recovered, Grandjean the chef nearly killed her with an avalanche of delicacies, weeping when she finally ate a slice of chicken. The Swiss major-domo praised Miss Bell in almost every European language he knew (albeit with indifferent accuracy); the coachman delighted in driving her; the page cried when she was sick; and Calverley and Coldstream—the two footmen usually so large, calm, and immovable—burst into hilarity at the news of her recovery, intoxicating the page at a wine shop to celebrate. Even Lady Diana Pynsent, who had disliked Laura for some time, declared her an agreeable person and a great *trouvaille* for her grandmother. All this goodwill was earned not by art or flattery, but by the sheer force of good nature and the blessed gift of pleasing and being pleased.

On the rare occasions Pen had seen Lady Rockminster, the old lady—who didn't particularly admire him—had been pitiless and abrupt. Perhaps Pen expected to find Laura installed as a humble companion, treated no better than himself. Instead, upon hearing of his arrival, she rushed downstairs. Whether she embraced him in front of Calverley and Coldstream remains uncertain, though those gentlemen never reported it. If the fractured orb had shattered, or if Laura had snipped off his head with her scissors, Calverley and Coldstream would have watched impassively, undisturbed by the calamity.

Laura had improved so greatly in health and appearance that Pen couldn't help admiring her. Her frank, kind eyes beamed with vitality; the cheek he kissed flushed with beauty. Looking at her, artless and graceful, pure and candid, he realized he had never seen her so beautiful. Why did he notice her beauty now, and realize he hadn't noticed it sooner? Taking her fair, trustful hand, he kissed it fondly, gazing into her bright, clear eyes and reading the kindling welcome always found there. He was deeply affected by her tender tone and pure sparkle; their innocence struck him and moved him profoundly.

"How good you are to me, Laura—sister!" Pen said. "I don't deserve that you should be so kind to me."

"Mamma left you to me," she replied, stooping to brush his forehead with her lips. "You know you were to come to me when in trouble, or to tell me when very happy; that was our compact, Arthur, last year before we parted. Are you very happy now, or in trouble? Do you like going into Parliament? Do you intend to distinguish yourself there? How I shall tremble for your first speech!"

"Do you know about the Parliament plan, then?" Pen asked.

"Know? Everyone knows! I've heard it discussed many times. Lady Rockminster's doctor talked about it today. It'll be in the Chatteris paper tomorrow. It's all over the county that Sir Francis Clavering is retiring in favor of Mr. Arthur Pendennis of Fairoaks, and that the young, beautiful Miss Blanche Amory is—"

"What! That too?" Pen exclaimed.

"That too, dear Arthur. *Tout se sait*, as someone would say, whom I intend to be very fond of, and who I'm sure is clever and pretty. I've had a letter from Blanche. The kindest of letters. She speaks so warmly of you, Arthur! I hope—I know she feels what she writes. When is it to be, Arthur? Why didn't you tell me? May I come and live with you then, mayn't I?"

"My home is yours, dear Laura, and everything I have," Pen said. "If I didn't tell you, it was because... because... I don't know. Nothing is decided yet. No words have passed between us. But you think Blanche could be happy with me, don't you? Not a romantic fondness, you know. I have no heart, I think; I've told her so. Only a sober-sided attachment: wanting my wife on one side of the fire and my sister on the other—Parliament in session and Fairoaks in the holidays, and my Laura never leaving me until someone with a right comes to take her away."

"Somebody with a right! Some- body with a right!" Why, as he looked at the girl and slowly uttered the words, did Pen begin to feel angry and jealous of this invisible somebody with the right to take her away? Just a moment ago, anxious about how she'd take the news of his arrangement with Blanche, he was hurt that she received the intelligence so easily, taking his happiness for granted.

"Until somebody comes," Laura said with a laugh, "I will stay at home and be Aunt Laura, taking care of the children when Blanche arrives in the world. I've arranged it all.
