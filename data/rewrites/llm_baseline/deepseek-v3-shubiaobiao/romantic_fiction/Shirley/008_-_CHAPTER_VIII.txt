Here is the rewritten chapter with refined language and enhanced romantic undertones while preserving the original plot:

Dawn had scarcely broken when Moore left his bed, riding to Whinbury and back before the first golden rays gilded the mill chimneys. His sister, ever discreet, asked no questions as she prepared his breakfast. Business secrets lay locked within him, guarded as jealously as a lover guards his heart’s confessions.

The morning post brought letters that tightened his lips, though his expression remained unreadable. A derisive exhale was his only reaction before he penned swift replies, the scratch of his pen as sharp as his temper. The newspaper failed to hold his attention; his gaze drifted instead to the window where sunlight flirted with the smoke-stained horizon.

A shadow darkened the doorway—Joe Scott, dyed indigo from the vats—announcing, "He’s comed, sir," before vanishing like a specter.

Moore did not glance up as the broad-shouldered visitor entered, mopping his brow with a handkerchief pulled from a battered hat. "Right warm for February," the man remarked, settling heavily into a chair.

"Prepared, I trust?" Moore’s voice was cool as winter stream.

"Aye, all’s ready."

A silent minute passed before Moore reached for the black bottle—the same that had loosened Malone’s tongue—and pushed it forward. "Help yourself."

The man poured a generous measure, swallowing it with practiced ease. "You’ll take none yourself, Mr. Moore?"

Moore declined with a shake of his head.

"Ye ought," the man insisted. "Them as is coming’ll talk till sundown. Ye’ll need fortifying."

"Have you seen Sykes this morning?" Moore deflected.

The man grinned. "Aye, and Rev’rend Helstone too, saddling his nag as I passed."

The prophecy proved true. Soon came the crisp trot of a pony, and Helstone’s voice rang through the yard. The rector entered, keen-eyed and vibrant as always.

"Moore! Explanations, if you please. This message of yours—are you certain of your course?"

"Sugden holds the warrant."

"Then the man comes to you? Clever. And who is the quarry?"

Joe Scott materialized at Moore’s shoulder, indigo hands resting on the desk. "A friend of yours, sir. The Reverend Moses Barraclough—your ‘tub orator.’"

Helstone’s brows lifted. "A tailor leading riots? How poetic."

Joe leaned in. "Oh, he’s a captain right enough—rode masked at Stilbro’ Moor, wooden leg and all. Found out when sweet-talking our Sarah went sour. His rival spilled the tale after a tussle."

Moore’s patience thinned. "Enough backstory. The point is, Murgatroyd overheard Barraclough plotting today’s ‘peaceful deputation’ to bully me into abandoning machinery."

A gig rattled into the yard. Sykes entered, his florid face anxious. "Have they come? Is it over?"

"Patience," Moore said dryly. "They’ll arrive soon enough."

Sykes fretted about repercussions until fortified by Hollands. Spirits warmed his courage; he vowed to spend his last penny defying the rabble.

Helstone watched with sardonic amusement. "How martial we become with Dutch courage."

A murmur of voices outside announced the deputation. Moore moved to the window, Helstone beside him. Twelve men clustered in the yard, led by two figures: a strutting peacock of a man, and Barraclough himself—his wooden leg and sanctimonious leer marking him plainly.

Moore stepped into the sunlight, hands tucked casually in his pockets. "Mr. Barraclough. ‘Peace be unto you’—though I suspect peace isn’t your true aim."

Barraclough’s eyes narrowed. "We come urging reason. Abandon your machines, sir. They oppress honest men."

"Honest men?" Moore’s laugh was sharp. "You, who preached temperance while I found you drunk in a ditch last week? You incite violence for your own ends."

Noah of Tim’s pushed forward, orating grandly about Moore’s foreign ignorance of Yorkshire ways. The men chorused approval.

Joe Scott smirked. "Cliffs of Albion! Did ye sail from Antarctica, Noah?"

Barraclough, outshone, tried again with righteous fervor. "Before your machines, we lived in harmony! I plead for my brethren’s bread!"

Moore cut him off. "You plead for control. You and Noah are agitators, using these men as tools." His voice turned steel-edged. "I refuse your demands. This mill stands. Inventors will improve it. Burn it down—kill me—and another will rise. Test me, and you’ll learn how earnest I am."

A whistle pierced the air. Sugden emerged, warrant in hand.

"Barraclough—you were at Stilbro’ in a mask. You struck my man down. Sugden, arrest him."

As Moses was seized, Moore drew a pistol, silencing the outcry. "Both barrels loaded. Stand back."

The moment hung, charged as storm air. Moore’s defiance was absolute—a man who would bend neither to threats nor sentiment. And in that unyielding stance, had Caroline seen him, she might have glimpsed the ruthless pragmatism that balanced his midnight tenderness—a duality as compelling as it was perilous.
