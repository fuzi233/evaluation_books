A month elapsed, and the dust of the desert had settled into the cobblestones of London. Durrance arrived in the capital, carrying the weight of a year's silence, only to discover waiting for him at his club a letter from Ethne. It was a brief dispatch, devoid of flourish, stating simply that she resided with Mrs. Adair and requested his presence. Yet, the paper bore a stark, black border framing the edges, a visual scar that marked the correspondence before a single word was read.

Driven by an instinct sharper than curiosity, Durrance visited Hill Street the following afternoon. He found Ethne alone, the atmosphere of the room heavy with unspoken things.

"I did not write to Wadi Halfa," she explained immediately, her voice steady despite the tremor in her hands. "I believed you would be returning home before my message could traverse the distance. My father died last month, towards the end of May."

Durrance felt the floor tilt beneath him. "I feared, when I received your note, that this was the burden you carried," he replied, his throat tight. "I am profoundly sorry. You will miss him."

"More than I can articulate," she said, her gaze dropping to her lap, revealing a quiet depth of sorrow that refused to perform. "He died one morning early—I believe I ought to recount the manner if you are willing to listen."

She launched into the narrative of Dermod's passing, describing a death not caused by acute fever, but by a slow, inevitable dissolution. It was a peculiar tale, for in his final hours, Dermod had seemingly reclaimed a fragment of his former commanding spirit.

"We knew he was dying," Ethne recounted. "He knew it too. At seven o'clock in the afternoon, after a brief exchange with me, he summoned his dog by name. The animal sprang instantly to the bedside, though his master's voice had barely risen above a whisper. Crouching close, the dog pressed its muzzle, whining softly, beneath his father's hand. Then, with absolute clarity, he commanded me to leave him and the dog entirely alone. 'Shut the door upon us,' he instructed. 'The dog will signal when you may enter.' I obeyed, retreating to the passage and waiting until one o'clock. Then, a loud, sudden howl echoed through the house."

She paused, the silence stretching taut between them. This hesitation was the sole manifestation of her distress; moments later, she resumed, speaking with a simplicity that stripped away all theatricality of grief.

"It was the torment of waiting outside that door as the afternoon bled into night. Darkness claimed the room long before the clock struck midnight. He would have extinguished every lamp, leaving him in total obscurity. One imagines him lying motionless in the great four-poster bed, his face turned toward the hills, as the light faded from the world. The room slipped into shadow, the windows loomed larger in the gloom, and the dog lay beside him—the only living soul present, right to the very end. That was his decree, though it was a cruelty I struggled to endure."

Durrance offered no verbal response. Instead, he bestowed upon her the profound comfort of his silence. In his mind, he reconstructed those six hours of twilight and darkness, picturing her standing near the panel, her ear pressed to the wood, her hand clutching her heart to stifle its frantic rhythm. He perceived the harshness of Dermod's resolve to face oblivion in solitude.

It was Ethne who shattered the quiet. "I mentioned that my father spoke to me just prior to sending me away. Of whom do you think he spoke?"

Her eyes locked directly onto Durrance's, the question hanging in the air like a guillotine blade. Neither her gaze nor the level cadence of her voice betrayed the answer, yet a sudden, violent thrum of hope seized Durrance's breath.

"Tell me!" he urged, leaning forward, suspended in a tense expectancy.

"Of Mr. Feversham," she answered.

Durrance recoiled, the air rushing from his lungs. It was not the name he anticipated. He lowered his eyes to the Persian rug, shielding his face from her scrutiny.

"My father cherished him greatly," Ethne continued gently. "I wonder if you possess any knowledge of his whereabouts or his current pursuits."

Durrance remained silent, his chin resting on his palm. He contemplated the formidable grip Harry Feversham maintained over the hearts of those who had known him; even the man he had grievously wronged, the source of such suffering for Ethne, remembered him with kindness on his deathbed. The realization tasted bitter in Durrance's mouth, a gall he feared would spill from his features. Compelled by Ethne's persistence, he forced himself to speak.

"You have never encountered him, I presume?" she asked.

Durrance rose and walked to the window, staring out into the street to conceal his turmoil. Though his face was hidden, a surge of deep, seething anger colored his words.

"No," he said, the truth tearing from him. "I never have." Suddenly, the anger dictated his tone. "And I never wish to. He was my friend, I know. But I cannot recall that friendship anymore. I can only conclude that if he had been the true man we believed him to be, you would not have stood alone in that dark passage for those six agonizing hours."

Turning back to the center of the room, he demanded abruptly, "Are you returning to Glenalla?"

"Yes."

"You intend to reside there alone?"

"Yes."

Silence descended upon them again, thick and suffocating. Then, Durrance circled behind her chair. "Once, you hinted that you might explain why our engagement was dissolved."

"But you know," she countered. "Your words at the window revealed that you understood."

"No, I do not," Durrance insisted. "Only fragments your father dropped. He inquired about Feversham during our last conversation. I possess no definitive information. I require you to tell me."

Ethne shook her head, leaning forward with her elbows on her knees. "Not now," she murmured. Silence followed, heavy and absolute.

Durrance broke it. "I have only one year remaining at Halfa. It would be prudent to depart Egypt then. I anticipate little activity in the Sudan for some time. Regardless, I do not foresee staying there—in any capacity. Even if you choose to remain solitary at Glenalla."

Ethne made no pretense of ignoring the implication. "We are not children," she stated firmly. "You have a lifetime ahead of you. Prudence dictates caution."

"Yes," Durrance snapped, his patience fraying. "But the *right* kind of prudence. The prudence that recognizes the value of daring significantly."

Ethne did not stir. Her back was to him, obscuring her expression. For a prolonged period, she remained statue-still, silent and immovable. Finally, she posed a question in a voice so low and gentle it barely disturbed the air. "Do you desire me so intensely?"

Before he could formulate an answer, she spun rapidly toward him. "Strive not to," she exclaimed earnestly. "For this coming year, strive not to. Your thoughts are occupied enough. Attempt to forget me entirely."

There was a faint, tragic regret in her tone—a sorrow at the loss of a valued confidant—that dulled the sting of her command. It confirmed Durrance's delusion: that but for her fear of ruining his prospects, she would have answered differently. Just as he prepared to respond, Mrs. Adair entered the room, shattering the moment. Durrance departed carrying the illusion of her rejection.

That evening, he dined at his club and subsequently smoked his cigar beneath the great tree where he had sat a year prior, futilely hunting for news of Harry Feversham. The night mirrored the one Lieutenant Sutch had limped into the courtyard on, hesitant and wary. Above, the sky was cloudless and starlit; the air possessed the languorous warmth of a June summer. Lights flickered from windows and doorways, casting the foliage in a vibrant spring-green hue, while carriages rumbled along the roadway with a thunderous roar akin to the sea.

On this night also, a man entered the courtyard who recognized Durrance. Unlike Sutch, he did not hesitate. He approached directly and seated himself beside Durrance.

"How do you do?" Durrance asked, extending his hand.

"This friend," he identified, was Captain Mather. "I wondered if I should encounter you upon reading the evening paper. I knew it was the season for your arrival in London. Have you seen it?"

"What?" Durrance queried.

"Then you haven't," Mather replied, snatching up the discarded newspaper and flipping through the pages to locate the specific intelligence. "Recall our final reconnaissance from Suakin?"

"Indeed."

"We halted by the Sinkat fort at noon. An Arab was concealing himself in the trees behind the glacis."

"Yes."

"Do you remember the yarn he spun?"

"Regarding Gordon's letters and the wall in Berber? No, I have not forgotten."

"Then consider this," Mather said, folding the paper and handing it to Durrance, pointing to a specific paragraph. "Something that will intrigue you."
