The next morning the boys and I set out with the cart creaking under a new load of bundled bamboos, the stems knocking together like dry bones whenever the wheels dropped into a rut. I left the buffalo at Falconhurst. The ringed wound in his nostrils had closed cleanly at last, but I would not tempt it to open again; there is no good husbandry in haste, and no good cook ever bruises a tender thing before it has had time to set.

We reached our avenue of young fruit trees at the hour when the island’s day-sounds are still gentle: the thin whistle of waking birds, the busy clicking of insects in the grass, and, under all, the slow hush of wind moving through leaves. That soft soundscape had steadied us often. It was the opposite of the iron clang and shouted orders of a ship at work—noise that forces itself into your skull and leaves nothing of you unshaken. Here, every rustle had a place, and that order did a kind of healing.

We were not a moment too soon. The storm that had merely threatened our planting before had now done its worst. Many saplings lay flat, their roots half-wrenched; others bent like bows, and only a few stood upright, stubborn and lucky. We set to work at once. We lifted each fallen tree, dug deep and wide at the root so it might sit again in loosened earth, then drove stout bamboo props beside it. With broad fibres—tough, flat strips that bit well without cutting—we lashed trunk to stake, pulled the knot tight, and tamped the soil firm with heel and spade until the tree stood with its head properly toward the sun.

“Papa,” said Franz, grave with attention as he fed me fibres one after another, “are these wild or tame trees?”

Jack, whose hands were never idle but whose tongue liked to sprint ahead of them, threw back his head and laughed. “Wild—ferocious!” he cried. “We must tie them up so they don’t run away. And one day we’ll untie them, and they’ll trot after us, and wherever we go they’ll drop fruit into our pockets. We’ll tame them with rings in their noses like the buffalo!”

“That’s not true,” Franz returned, offended not so much by Jack’s teasing as by the injury done to words. “But there are wild and tame trees. Wild grow in the woods, like crab-apples; tame grow in gardens, like our pears and peaches at home. Which are these, papa?”

“They are tame, as you call it,” I told him, tightening a lash around a leaning sapling. “Cultivated—grafted. In Europe, no fruit tree gives its best fruit until it is grafted.”

Franz’s brow tightened in that particular way it did when he met a new term, as if he were trying to fit a strange seed into a known row.

So I explained it, as I would explain a recipe—because grafting is, in its own fashion, a recipe for sweetness. “You take a slip—a twig—from a tree with good fruit. You cut an opening, what gardeners call an eye, in another tree’s branch. You set the slip into that place so the living layers meet, and you bind it firm. Then the stock feeds it, but the slip decides what fruit will come. If we have a sour crab tree, and from another land we have an apple that bears fine ribston pippins, we put a slip of that apple into the crab stock. In a year or two, that branch is heavy with good apples, though its roots belong to the crab.”

“But,” Ernest asked at once, because his mind always followed the chain back to its first link, “where did the first good slips come from, if none grow without grafting?”

“From warmer places,” I said. “In our colder countries, fruit often needs the gardener’s art to coax it into sweetness. But in many southern lands the soil itself is generous. There the most luscious trees are native, and they bear without anyone pruning or protecting them—while in England and Germany, even France, the same kinds demand all a man’s skill just to give a respectable crop.

“When the Romans first went into England, they found little but crab-apples, nuts, and brambles. They improved them by grafting—fine apples where sour ones had been, filberts, raspberries. And in Switzerland, too: our orchards were not born with us; they were brought, and then made ours by care.”

“Were cherries brought too?” Fritz asked. “May we not call cherries Swiss? I always thought they grew nowhere else.”

“I am afraid we cannot claim even their name,” I said. “They are called cherries from Cerasus, a state of Pontus in Asia. A Roman general, Lucullus, brought them to Europe about seventy years before Christ. Hazelnuts also came from Pontus. Walnuts from Persia. Grapes are older still—Noah, you remember, cultivated vines. They have travelled from tongue to tongue and place to place until they belong, now, to almost every civilized land.”

As we spoke, we crossed Jackal River and entered our plantation at Tentholm, where the ground rose a little and the air held a different smell—drier earth, bruised leaf, the faint resin of young pines.

“Do you think all these will grow?” Fritz asked, looking along our line of plantings as though he could already see the shade they would cast years from now. “Here are lemons, pomegranates, pistachio nuts, mulberries.”

“I have little doubt,” I answered. “We are evidently within the tropics. Such trees should flourish here.”

And I named them as we passed—like reading a pantry list and remembering the taste of each. Pines from France, Spain, Italy; olives from Armenia and Palestine; figs from Chios; peaches and apricots from Persia; plums from Damascus; pears from Greece. Each name carried its own sound and history, and as I said them I felt, more sharply than usual, how a thing can be carried across the world and still keep its old meaning—while a word, carried into a new mouth, often changes its flavour. Even here, on this island where no one but ourselves spoke, the old names began to sound strange, like heirlooms taken out of their familiar room.

We finished propping and lashing every tree that needed it, and then, with appetites that would have made a gourmand proud, we returned to Falconhurst.

My wife had set our meal ready—corned beef and palm-cabbage. The scent met us at the ladder: salt and meat, and that green, milky smell of the palm heart, softened by heat. After hours of labour, the first mouthful seemed to wake every sense at once. We ate with the honest greed of those who have earned their food by the swing of arm and the ache of back; and if she looked half-alarmed at our speed, it was only until she saw the colour return to our cheeks.

When the plates were cleared and we lay back to let our bodies turn work into warmth, we spoke of what must come next.

“I wish,” my wife said, looking up at our nest in the great tree, “you would find some other way of climbing. The nest is perfect—I could ask no better home—but that dreadful ladder… Must we scale it every time? Could you not make steps?”

I turned the idea over as I would turn a problem in carpentry or cookery: what we had, what we lacked, what the material itself would permit.

“Outside stairs would be impossible,” I said at last. “But inside the trunk—if the tree is hollow—it might be done. I have wondered more than once whether this trunk is partly empty. Did you not say you saw bees going in and out of a hole?”

“Oh yes,” Franz put in quickly, as if to prove his part in our household history. “And I went close to look and one flew right into my face and stung me. I almost cried—but I didn’t.”

“Brave little fellow,” I told him, and kissed the crown of his head. “If bees have a home inside, the cavity may extend far. Some trees draw their nourishment through the bark and can look flourishing while the heart is hollow—like certain willows at home.”

Jack was already on his feet. Practical curiosity carried him like a gust. The others followed, and soon they were climbing and tapping the bark, peering into the opening, listening for the change in tone that tells you whether wood is sound or empty.

They had forgotten, in their excitement, that the hole was a front door.

The bees reminded them.

An angry buzz burst out like a thrown net. The air filled instantly with living darts. Hands flailed; cries rose; the boys leapt back, swatting at their own hair and collars. The bees swarmed their faces and necks, stung knuckles and ears, and pursued them in a furious cloud as they ran to me for help.

It was hard work to clear the insects and harder still to calm the boys. Jack, first at the hole, suffered worst. His face swelled so quickly it seemed to change shape before our eyes, and the pain made him wince even when he tried to be brave. Cold earth—cool, damp, and soothing—was the only thing that eased him. For an hour or more they could do little but endure the smarting and watch me, their anger toward the bees growing by the minute.

They wanted a battle at once. I wanted a harvest—and a solution.

While the bees still circled, I prepared. I took a large calabash gourd and made, from its smooth round body, a hive. I flattened the lower half so it would sit steady. I cut an arched opening for a doorway, and made a straw roof to keep off rain and sun. In the end it looked almost like a toy house—except that I knew it would soon hold a kingdom of workers, and the sweet store they make from blossoms and time.

Nothing more could be done by daylight. The bees were too angry, and I will not waste courage where patience will do better. When darkness fell and the island’s chorus changed—day birds silent, night insects beginning their thin steady piping—I went with Fritz to the tree. We stopped every hole we could find with wet clay, sealing the trunk so the bees could not pour out at dawn before we were ready.

We rose very early. The air was cool, the forest still half-asleep, and the only loud sound was our own breathing as we worked. I took a hollow cane, pushed one end through the clay into the tree, and with pipe and tobacco smoked as furiously as if I meant to fog the entire island.

Inside the trunk the humming began at once—tremendous, indignant, confused. They could not understand this choking, sour cloud invading their house. I finished one pipe. With my thumb over the cane’s end, I handed it to Fritz to refill. He did so, quick and quiet, and I smoked again.

The roar of buzzing lessened. It sank to a heavy murmur. By the end of the second pipe, all was still. The bees were stupefied.

“Now, Fritz,” I whispered, “hammer and chisel. Stand close.”

Together we cut a small door beside the hole—not removing it entirely, only leaving it attached by one corner, so it could be shut in an instant if needed. I gave one final dose of smoke, and then we opened.

The bees clung in clusters along the inner walls. Carefully, quickly, we lifted them away and placed them into the gourd hive. The work had to be brisk, but gentle; one careless jolt and the whole mass could wake and turn. As soon as the living clusters were transferred, I gathered every atom of wax and honey from their storehouse and packed it into a cask I had ready.

Yet I did not trust that, once revived, they would accept their new home. Bees have a memory of place as stubborn as any farmer. To prevent them returning, I laid tobacco on a board nailed within the trunk, lit it, and let it smoulder so the bitter fumes filled the hollow.

It proved wise. As the bees stirred back into life, they left the gourd and flew straight to the old tree. They found it uninhabitable now—stinking of smoke—and their indignation filled the air with a loud, churning hum. Back and forth they went between gourd and trunk, settling here, lifting again, as if arguing in a language no one can translate. At last, after what looked like due consultation, they accepted the hive and abandoned their former lodging to us.

By evening they were quiet enough for us to open our cask of plunder.

We treated the honey as my wife would treat preserves at home—with care, cleanliness, and no waste. We separated the clear honey from the comb and poured it into jars and pots. The remaining comb we broke into a vessel of water and set it over a slow fire. It melted into a fragrant, golden liquor, steam rising with a warm sweetness that seemed to thicken the air. We poured the whole into a clean canvas bag and pressed it hard. The honey ran out, darker and less delicate than the first, but still excellent. The wax left behind we saved with particular attention; I was already thinking of candles, and of how the night feels when you can keep the dark at a respectful distance.

After such a day, we slept as only those sleep who have earned rest by both danger and labour.

The next morning we returned to the tree’s inner architecture. First we cut away bark at the base and made an opening exactly fit for the door we had brought from the captain’s cabin, hinges and all, ready to hang. Then we began clearing the rotten wood from the centre. It took time—digging, scraping, levering out soft, damp fibres that smelled of old rain and decay. At last the cavity was clean. Standing below, we could look up the trunk like a great smooth funnel and see a circle of sky far overhead.

Now we built the staircase. In the centre we set a stout sapling as an axis, notched it for steps, and cut corresponding notches in the inner wall of the trunk to support the outer ends. The steps we shaped from planks saved from the wreck—wood that had known waves and now would know feet—and we clenched them into place with stout nails.

Upwards we went, step after step, the spiral tightening and widening in rhythm like a shell. As we climbed with our work, we cut windows in the trunk to admit light and air, so the interior would not become a dead, muffled shaft. Sound matters in a dwelling: a space that swallows all noise can make the mind feel trapped, as if your own heartbeat were too loud. Our windows let in wind, birdcall, the soft talk of leaves—domestic music, honest and unbought.

When we reached the top of the centre pole, we fixed another above it and continued the spiral until we reached the level of the nest floor. For safety and ease we added handrails—one coiling around the centre pillar, the other following the curve of the trunk.

The staircase cost us a whole month. By the end of it, we had grown so used to waking with a definite task before us that we began to feel uneasy without some new alteration to plan, as if work were the very rhythm by which we kept time on the island.

But we did not neglect the daily needs of our little colony. The goats and sheep gave us additions—small, frisky creatures that sprang and skittered like living sparks. To keep them from straying, we tied little bells around their necks—bells found in the wreck. Their tinkling became part of our soundscape, a gentle, wandering thread that told us where our wealth grazed. I often thought how different those bells were from the shriek of metal in the old world—here, sound served life instead of selling itself.

Juno, too, had a litter of puppies. The children begged to keep them all, but I would consent to only two; we could not feed a pack. The rest disappeared in that mysterious way by which unwanted puppies and kittens are said to leave the earth. Jack, to console Juno—though I suspect also to spare himself trouble—placed his little jackal beside the remaining pups. To his delight, she accepted it readily, as if motherhood were a language wider than species.

Our other pets flourished and were trained to be useful. The buffalo, after much trouble, became thoroughly domesticated. The wound in his nose healed completely, and the bar we thrust through the hole served as a guide, like a horse’s bit. I began his education with a broad girth of buffalo-hide and tied various articles to it so he would grow used to carrying burdens. By degrees he allowed it without resistance. Soon he carried panniers gladly, taking over loads the ass had borne.

Then I set Master Knips on his back, holding reins I had made, to accustom the buffalo to a rider. Finally Fritz mounted, and the great beast accepted him as if it had always been his purpose to serve.

We did not neglect Fritz’s eagle either. Each day he shot small birds for its food, and sometimes placed them between the buffalo’s horns or on the back of the great bustard, so the eagle would learn to strike prey from unusual perches. These lessons worked. Obeying voice and whistle, the bird soon began to bring down small birds on the wing, stooping and striking with a sportsmanlike precision. We kept it well away from the poultry-yard; art does not erase instinct, and we would not tempt the creature with easy slaughter.

Master Knips was put to work too. Ernest, now that he owned the monkey, wanted him useful, and with Jack’s help he made a small basket of rushes fitted with straps to sit on the monkey’s back. Thus equipped, Knips was taught to climb cocoanut palms and other tall trees and bring down fruit in his hamper.

Jack’s own training efforts went less smoothly. Fangs—the jackal—used his fangs magnificently, but only on his own account. Nothing could persuade him that a catch was not meant to be eaten at once; poor Jack could rarely rescue more than a tattered skin from those greedy jaws. Still, he refused to be discouraged and insisted he would train him in time.

In my smaller hours, I improved our candles. At first our homemade lights had delighted us, but soon we admitted their flaws: the light was imperfect, and the candles themselves unsightly. My wife begged me to find some substitute for the threads of our cotton neck-ties, which I had used as wicks.

To give the candles proper shape and smoothness, I turned to the bamboo moulds I had prepared. At first I thought to pour wax in and slide the candle out when cool, but I quickly found it would not work. So I split the moulds lengthways, greased them, bound the halves tightly together, and poured the melted wax inside; when cooled, the mould could be opened without injuring the candle.

Wicks were the harder question. My wife flatly refused to sacrifice ties and handkerchiefs. I cut long slips from a piece of inflammable wood from an Antilles tree and fixed these splints in the centres of the moulds. My wife prepared rival wicks from the fibres of the karata tree, confident they would surpass mine.

We tested them like judges at a fair. Over a large fire we melted a mixture—half beeswax, half wax from candleberries—its scent warm and faintly resinous, like summer trapped in fat. The moulds stood on end in a tub of cold water, ready. Some held karata fibre; some my wooden splints. We filled them, cooled them, opened them, and held the finished candles up for inspection.

At night we lit them and watched their flames. The verdict was unanimous: neither matched the old cotton wick. But even my wife admitted my wooden splints were better than her karata fibres. Mine flared a little yet burned bright and steady; hers gave a weak, flickering flame, almost useless.

After that I turned shoemaker, determined at last to make waterproof boots. I filled a pair of socks with sand and coated them with a thin layer of clay to form moulds. The sun hardened them. Then I brushed on layer after layer of caoutchouc, letting each dry before applying the next, until the thickness pleased me. When dry, I broke out the clay, nailed buffalo-hide soles on, brushed those too with caoutchouc, and ended with comfortable, durable, respectable-looking waterproof boots.

I was so delighted that orders poured in at once. Before long every member of the family had a pair, and our steps no longer feared wet ground.

One great inconvenience at Falconhurst remained: no spring close by. The boys had to fetch water daily from the stream, and the labour wasted time and strength. So we devised a plan to carry water by pipes. Upstream, we threw a dam across the river to raise the water enough to run down to our dwelling. From the reservoir we led it through pipes into the turtle-shell trough placed near the house; the superfluous water flowed away through the hole Fritz’s harpoon had once made.

This was a blessing. We inaugurated the trough in proper domestic fashion by washing a whole sack of potatoes in it—the water running over the rough skins, carrying away grit and earth, leaving them clean and pale as if newly dug. Work, food, and home—each day we braided them together. We had little time to be idle or to mourn our separation from the world.

One morning, as we were finishing the spiral staircase and giving it as much neatness as our hands could manage, a sound tore through the forest—so loud, so sudden, so unlike the ordinary voices of our island that it seemed to strike the very air into pieces. It was a roaring, bellowing noise, the cry of some beast. The acoustic peace we relied on was shattered; even the birds fell silent, as if the whole place listened.

Jack swore it must be a lion. Fritz guessed some great ape. Ernest, with his bookish knowledge, suggested a hyena—and I thought he might be right.

“Whatever it is,” I said, “we must be ready. Up to the nest—quick—while I secure the door.”

We fitted the dogs with their collars and sent them out to protect the animals below. Then I shut the door and joined my family above. Every gun was loaded. Every face was turned toward the openings. The sound came nearer… then stopped. Nothing moved. No shadow crossed the grass. Only the uneasy quiet remained, the kind that makes your own breathing sound like an intrusion.

I decided to descend and reconnoitre. Fritz came with me. We crept down and moved among the trees, guns at full cock, stepping as softly as we could on leaf and root. We pressed on further, listening, eyes searching.

Suddenly, close by, the terrific sound burst out again.

Fritz lifted his gun—and as quickly dropped it, breaking into laughter so sudden and full that it startled me more than the roar had done.

There was no mistaking those dulcet tones: hee-haw, hee-haw, hee-haw, echoing through the wood.

Our ass came into view, braying his approach with hearty enthusiasm. But he was not alone. Behind him trotted another animal—an ass as well, yet slim and graceful as a horse, her neck arched, her movements quick with wildness. We watched, wary now for a different reason.

“Fritz,” I whispered, “that is an onager. Creep back to Falconhurst and bring me a piece of cord—quietly.”

While he was gone, I cut a bamboo and split it halfway down to make pincers, knowing I might need to seize the animal’s nose without risking my hands. Fritz returned swiftly, and I was pleased to see he had brought not only cord but oats and salt as well—good bait and better sense.

We tied one end of the cord to a tree and made a running noose at the other. Then we waited. The two animals approached, browsing calmly. Fritz rose with the noose in one hand, oats and salt in the other.

Our old ass, recognising his favourite food, came forward without fear and began munching contentedly. The stranger started back at the sight of Fritz, but seeing her companion unalarmed, she took courage. She came nearer, sniffing, nostrils flaring, and reached for the offered grains.

In an instant the noose flew from Fritz’s skilful hand and dropped over her neck. With one bound she sprang backward to the full length of the cord; the noose tightened and she fell, half strangled. I ran in at once, loosened the rope, replaced it with a halter, and set the bamboo pincers upon her nose. With two cords fastened between two trees, we secured her and left her a moment to recover.

Everyone came running to admire the animal as she rose. She threw fiery glances around, lashed out with her heels, snorted and struggled, her sleek flanks quivering with rage and fear. But the cords held firm. After a time she stood, exhausted, trembling, yet upright—wild pride still in her posture.

I approached carefully. At last she allowed me to lead her to the roots of our great tree, which for now served as our stable. There I tied her close beside the donkey—who, in his own simple way, had brought us this new prize—and secured him too, to prevent any further adventurous wanderings.
