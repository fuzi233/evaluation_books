Mr. Harry Warrington, of Virginia, lived in grand lodgings on Bond Street in London, where he enjoyed the finest comforts and indulged freely in the pleasures of high society. Wealthy, well-born, and strikingly handsome, he was widely known as the Fortunate Youth—a title earned not just by fortune, but by charm and ease. The fashionable world embraced him with open arms, for he was young, generous, and full of life. And let us not condemn this world too harshly for favoring such men while turning a cold shoulder to those less blessed—old, plain, or unremarkable. If I choose to spend my evenings in pleasant company rather than with sour-faced misanthropes or tedious bores, why should society not do the same? Harry was liked because he was likable: he was brave, courteous, and always ready for a laugh, a song, or a game of cards. With ladies, he carried himself with modesty and warmth; with gentlemen, he shared their passions—whether hunting, riding, or drinking. To those beneath him in rank, he was lavish in kindness, never sparing effort to spare them discomfort.

In those days, when social hierarchy was still strictly observed, a gentleman’s dignity lay in his distance from inferiors—not in cruelty, but in decorum. The Secretary of State knelt before the king; the Under-Secretary stood at attention. Such customs may seem absurd now, but they were part of the order of things. And though I might find it odd to kneel before a monarch or stand all day beside a superior, I understand that boundaries exist—and I would not welcome every man who claims kinship with me simply because he writes for a newspaper or contributes to a dog magazine. There is a place for fellowship, yes—but not without distinction.

During the winter of 1756–57, Harry had every pleasure London could offer at his fingertips. He attended operas—though he found them more entertaining than many critics did, who mocked them as frivolous, foreign, and unmanly. Still, the theatres drew crowds, especially with Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard commanding one stage and Mrs. Clive another. Masquerades and ridottos glittered with nobility; private assemblies and balls filled the season with music and dance. Yet Harry preferred White’s Club, where the stakes were higher and the play fairer than at any nobleman’s card table.

One day, Lord Castlewood took Harry to court to present him to His Majesty, who had recently returned from Kensington. But the King received him coolly. “Oh,” he said, speaking in German to the Earl, “I’ve heard of you from Lady Yarmouth. Tell him he plays too much.” Then, without another word, he turned away.

Lord Castlewood looked shaken. “He disapproves of your gaming,” he whispered.
“Then if he doesn’t want us,” replied Harry simply, “we’d best not come again. I never thought that German fellow was much of a king.”

“Hush!” hissed the lord. “You don’t know what ears are listening!”

But Harry only laughed. “Look around! Just now everyone was bowing and flattering me. Now they avoid me like the plague!”

At that moment, a broad-shouldered man in a scarlet-laced waistcoat stepped forward. “Shake hands, nephew,” he said, grasping Harry’s hand firmly. “I heard what you said. I have ears like the wall. And since others turn cold, I’ll give you mine.”

It was Sir Miles Warrington, Harry’s paternal uncle—whom no one had mentioned before. “Might as well have come to see us in Norfolk,” he growled, “instead of dallying at Tunbridge Wells. Mr. Warrington—or Mr. Esmond—which do you call yourself?”

“The old lady calls herself Madam Esmond,” Harry replied, “but my mother isn’t ashamed of her father’s name, nor am I.”

“Well said, lad!” declared Sir Miles. “Come eat mutton with Lady Warrington at three o’clock in Hill Street. Don’t mind my lord here—he’ll be frightened enough as it is.”

Lord Castlewood paled. “Sir Miles,” he stammered, “you must know there are enemies everywhere in this city. I’ve avoided speaking of you because we’ve had differences. If he says anything unkind, please take it with a grain of salt.”

The Baronet grunted. “Belike he will. But you’ll come, young man. Good roast mutton waits for no one. You look like your father—smaller, younger, but often won our fights. He was henpecked, poor soul, once he married. Madam Esmond took the spirit out of him in Virginia. Virginia’s an island, ain’t it?”

Harry laughed. “No, sir, it’s not.”

“Island or not,” said Sir Miles, “you’ll come and tell us all about it. My wife’ll decide whether it’s land or sea.”

Later, at White’s, news of the royal snub had already spread. Some said the King was jealous of Harry’s closeness to Lady Yarmouth. Others claimed he was simply annoyed at the gambling. By nightfall, it was settled: another triumph for the Fortunate Youth.

At dinner in Hill Street, Sir Miles teased Harry good-naturedly. His wife, at first distant, confessed she’d given him a chilly welcome, so bad a reputation had preceded him. The daughters curtseyed primly and called him “cousin.” Only the youngest boy, Master Miles, spoke plainly.

“Are you wicked, cousin Harry?” he asked, staring into Harry’s face. “You don’t look very wicked.”

“Master Miles!” cried the tutor, blushing.

“But you said he was wicked!” insisted the child.

“We’re all sinners,” explained Sir Miles. “Haven’t you heard the clergyman say so?”

“Yes, but not *so* wicked as cousin Harry. Is it true you gamble all night and drink with bad men? And that Lady Yarmouth is wicked?”

“You’re a little pitcher,” said Sir Miles. “And my wife, nephew, is a staunch Jacobite—don’t hold that against her.”

The boy was sent off with a promise of a ride in the park, though he cried bitterly, saying he’d rather stay with his “wicked” cousin.

“You’ve got quite a name among us, nephew,” said Sir Miles. “My wife has taken to Mr. Whitfield’s sermons lately, and we’ve had a friend of the tutor’s telling sad tales about you and your brother.”

“I’ll answer for my brother,” said Harry, warming with port. “Any man who dares speak ill of George—well, he’s not fit to buckle his shoe. If I hear he’s spreading lies in Virginia, he’ll get a second caning.”

“You stand up for your friends,” said Sir Miles. “Fill your glass. You’re not as bad as painted. I’ll drink to Madam Esmond Warrington of Virginia.”

Harry drank to Lady Warrington and Master Miles. “Though,” added Sir Miles, “you’d inherit four thousand acres in Norfolk if I died.”

“God forbid,” said Harry. “Praised be that I have my own in Virginia.”

After dinner, Harry spent time with Lady Warrington and the girls. One reminded him of Fanny Mountain, and she became his favorite. When he left, the household agreed he wasn’t nearly as wicked as rumored. Lady Warrington even hoped to save him from ruin. That evening, she sent him a book—*Law’s Call*—with a note praying it might guide him. She and her daughters then went to a rout hosted by a minister’s wife.

Before going to White’s, Harry had visited Mr. Sparks in Tavistock Street and bought trinkets for his cousins—“from Aunt Warrington in Virginia,” he told them. He was generous, kind-hearted, and glowing with prosperity. Not all men are so fortunate under wealth. Some grow harder, prouder, meaner. If fate should bless me with ten thousand a year, I hope I’d have strength enough to endure it without corruption.

Meanwhile, the girls in Hill Street delighted in their gifts. They wrote a collective note, which reached their aunt in springtime—when she, Fanny, and Mountain were visiting the bleak Castlewood, now brightened by blossoming peach trees.

“Poor boy,” the mother sighed. “This was a gift he gave in his prosperity—his extravagance, perhaps. How fast his fortune has slipped away. But he always had a kind heart for poor Mountain. We must be careful now.”

They warmed themselves by one log, ate from one dish, worked by one candle. The servants, increasingly pinched, began to lie and steal. What was saved in one way was lost in another.

One afternoon, Harry sat in his Bond Street lodgings, wrapped in silk, sipping chocolate, yet weighed down by worry. A few weeks earlier, flush with luck, he’d told Parson Sampson to compile a list of his debts—promising to pay them all. Sampson had done so, listing £243—what he believed were all the debts that would truly inconvenience him.

That morning, Harry appeared pale and weak, dressed in curl-papers, barely touching his breakfast. Sampson, trembling slightly, pulled out the paper. “Here is the list, sir—every shilling I owe—thank Heaven—save those that wouldn’t trouble you.”

Harry burst into laughter—harsh, bitter, mocking. “Confound it—why didn’t you bring it on Monday?”

Sampson’s heart sank. “I—I thought—”

“It’s my luck,” Harry muttered. “The cards have been against me. Monday night, last night—both ruined. Don’t worry, chaplain—there’s money left. But I must go to the City. Sell out, sir? Yes. I borrowed a hundred from Mackreth last night. Must repay him at noon. I’ll settle your affairs tomorrow.”

Yet once Sampson left, Harry slumped forward, beating the coals with the poker, muttering curses—his spirits torn.

Then came Mr. Lambert, in military dress, having just attended the Captain-General’s levee. He was a friend, always welcome—though today Harry sensed something colder in his greeting.

“How are the ladies at Oakhurst?” he asked. “Miss Hetty—was she still poorly?”

“Better,” said Harry. “They’re staying in London with Lord Wrotham.”

“Ah,” said Lambert. “So you live at White’s, dine with the great, court at St. James’s, and attend Lady Yarmouth’s parties?”

“Other people do what other people do,” said Harry, stiffening.

“Other people are richer,” replied Lambert.

“Sir,” said Harry sharply, “I owe nothing I cannot pay.”

“I only mention it because you told me yourself,” said Lambert gently. “But I hear all sorts of stories. At the levee today, they said you’d won eighteen hundred one night—two thousand another—six or eight thousand in total.”

“Let them gossip,” snapped Harry. “Why don’t they mind their own business?”

“And you,” continued Lambert, “look as if you’re about to say something else.”

“It’s my affair,” said Harry. “And I’ll thank you not to meddle.”

Then Lambert drew a packet from his coat. “Look here, Harry. These trinkets—given with such kindness—you couldn’t afford them on two or three hundred a year. A nobleman or banker might send such gifts. But not a young man who gambles.”

“Sir,” said Harry, flushing, “I meant only kindness.”

“No,” said Lambert, voice thick. “I can’t accept them. Hester and Theodosia must not wear jewels bought with gambling winnings. It grieves me to return them. Mrs. Lambert keeps her gift—it’s small—and sends her love. So do I, Harry Warrington—with all my heart.”

His voice broke. He passed a hand over his eyes.

But rebellion flared in Harry. He rose, refusing the offered hand. “Colonel Lambert,” he said, “I’ve had enough of your advice. You volunteer it constantly, without being asked. You pry into my games, my companions. What right have you to judge my amusements? I show my gratitude with small gifts, and you return them.”

“A slight,” said Lambert sadly, “may mean nothing here—but in our country, it means war.”

“God forbid I draw sword on the father of women who’ve been like mother and sister to me,” said Harry. “But you’ve wounded me—humiliated me. This treatment I will not bear from any man.”

With that, he turned and strode to his bedroom, rustling in his brocade gown, leaving the Colonel standing alone—silent, sorrowful, and deeply shaken.
