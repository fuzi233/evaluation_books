A civil war was currently raging inside the ballroom, centered around a single open window in the corner. People were shoving and squeezing together in an undignified heap, while sarcastic shouts from the street below—"Pitch into him!" "Where's the police?"—drifted upward. On one side of the window stood a ring of people including the formidable Madame Fribsby, besieging Monsieur Alcide Mirobolant; on the other, a group of gentlemen and ladies surrounded Arthur Pendennis. Just then, the Chevalier Strong shouldered his way through the door and cut directly into the fray, elbowing past Madame Fribsby, who shrieked, "Save him, save him!" in tones of frantic pathos.

The trouble stemmed from the tiny, fiery chef of Sir Francis Clavering's kitchen. Moments earlier, Mr. Pen had retreated to the window to cool his frayed nerves. He was furious about his disastrous waltz—which had made him look foolish in the eyes of the entire nation—and equally stung by Miss Amory's behavior. Trying desperately to regain his composure while watching the sparkling sea, he suddenly felt a hand on his shoulder. Turning, he encountered absolute horror: the hand belonged to Monsieur Mirobolant, whose pale face and wild eyes glared at him. Being tapped on the shoulder by a French cook was an indignity that made the blood of every Pendennis boil; Pen was stunned, bordering on enraged.

"You speak French?" Mirobolant asked in his native tongue.

"What is that to you?" Pen retorted in English.

"At any rate, you understand it?" the cook continued, bowing deeply.

"Yes, sir," Pen stamped his foot. "I understand it pretty well."

"Then you will understand, Monsieur Pendennis," Mirobolant rolled out his *r* with Gascon force, "that I tell you: you are a coward. Monsieur Pendennis—a coward, do you hear?"

"What?" Pen spun around.

"Do you comprehend the weight of that word among men of honor?" the artist demanded, hand on hip.

"The consequence," Pen bellowed, lunging forward, "is that I will throw you out the window, you impudent scoundrel!"

He certainly would have executed this threat, given the proximity of the window and the chef's lack of martial prowess, had not Captain Broadfoot and another heavy officer interposed themselves. Had not the ladies screamed, the music stopped, and the crowd rushed in? Had not Laura, looking terrified, asked what was happening? And had not the timely arrival of Strong, finding Alcides grinding his teeth and Pen looking wickedly calm, prevented disaster?

"What has happened?" Strong asked the chef in Spanish.

"I am Chevalier de Juillet," the man slapped his chest proudly. "He insulted me."

"He called me... 'Chef'?" Strong asked.

*"Il m'a appelé Cuisinier!"* the little Frenchman hissed.

Strong couldn't help laughing. "Come away with me, poor Chevalier. We cannot quarrel before ladies. Let me carry your message to Mr. Pendennis. The poor fellow isn't right in his head," he whispered to the surrounding guests.

Gathered around Pen, the ladies and gentlemen learned the truth. Pen admitted he had simply pointed out the man was a cook when he offered his arm to a young lady, prompting the chef to call him a coward and challenge him to a duel.

"D—him! Serve him right!" the gentlemen grumbled.

"I'm very sorry if I hurt his feelings," Pen added weakly. Laura, relieved to hear him apologize, smiled gently. "Foreigners may be more sensitive. If you hurt a poor man's feelings, wouldn't you ask his pardon, dear Pen?" Looking like an angel, she held out her hands. Pen, touched by her kindness, promised he would.

"How fond that girl is of me!" he thought, watching her gaze. "Should I speak to her now? No, not yet. I must resolve this absurd business with the Frenchman first."

Laura tried to keep him dancing. "Won't you stay and waltz with me, Pen? I'm not afraid."

This was an affectionate but fatal suggestion. In his mind's eye, Pen saw himself tumbling over Miss Roundle and a dragoon, throwing Blanche against the wall, and lying on the floor while everyone laughed—including Laura and Pynsent.

"I shall never dance again," he declared grimly. "Never. I'm surprised you'd ask."

"Is it because you can't find a partner for Blanche?" Laura asked with malicious sharpness.

"No," Pen snapped. "I don't want to make a fool of myself for others to laugh at. Especially for you, Laura. I saw you and Pynsent. By Jove! No man shall laugh at me."

"Pen, don't be so wicked!" Laura cried, hurt by his savage vanity. "Who thinks less of you for stumbling in a waltz? If I do, we don't. Why are you so sensitive?"

Just then, Mr. Pynsent arrived. "Lady Rockminster commands me to ask if I may escort you to supper?"

"I was going with my cousin," Laura lied.

"Oh, pray, no!" Pen interrupted. "You're in excellent hands. I can't do better than leave you. I'm going home."

"Good-night, Mr. Pendennis," Pynsent said dryly—a polite way of telling Pen to go to hell for being a jealous, insolent jackanapes. Pen merely bowed and fled the room despite Laura's pleading looks.

Pynsent later remarked to Laura how pleasant the night was and how tedious the social obligations had become. Laura feigned interest, claiming she had been worried about the quarrel. Pynsent sympathized with Pen's bad luck, listing the various people Pen had displeased, and eagerly accepted Laura's hand to lead her to supper.

Meanwhile, Laura sat in the supper room, distracted and miserable. She wondered how her mother could expect her to marry such a capricious, jealous, and violent man. Her relief came only when a waiter delivered a penciled note from Pen: *"I met Cooky below and asked his pardon. I'm glad I did. I wanted to speak to you tonight but will wait until you return. God bless you. Dance all night with Pynsent and be happy.—PEN."*

Pen, meanwhile, regretted his harshness toward Laura but remembered leaving her with Pynsent, so he decided to make peace with the chef instead. He found the Chevalier pacing the hall, offering to fight him with pistols on the beach or swords near the water.

"I can't fight a cook!" Pen shouted in fury.

"He is a Chevalier of July!" Strong laughed, intervening. "They present arms to him."

"And do you ask me to fight a servant?" Pen roared.

"Thank you for nothing," Strong chuckled. "I came to settle quarrels, not start them. I've calmed Mirobolant. He says you used the word 'cook' unintentionally, as a hired officer shouldn't offer his arm to the hostess's daughter." Strong then revealed the true secret: Madame Fribsby had confessed that the poor artist was suffering from a violent, delirious passion.

Upon hearing this, Pen burst into hearty laughter, his rage vanishing instantly. He realized he had been absurdly jealous himself all evening, longing to insult Pynsent just as he had once envied Oaks. Ready to forgive anyone driven mad by love, he entered the coffee room where Mirobolant waited.

"Monsieur Pendennis has disproven my accusations," Alcide said politely. "He has shown himself a *galant homme*." They shook hands and parted. Before joining Strong for a ride, Pen sent a final note to Laura: *"Dance away all night with Pynsent and be very happy."*

As they drove to the Brawl, Strong praised Pen's French, noting he spoke it like Chateaubriand. Pen modestly claimed he had been learning since childhood, earning five minutes of silence from Strong before the latter erupted into uncontrollable laughter.

By dawn, the ball at Baymouth had ended. Madame Fribsby and Mirobolant departed in the Clavering carriage; Laura slept soundly at Lady Rockminster's; and the Claverings rested at the inn. Later, Blanche emerged from the refreshment room pale as a lemon, whispering to her maid about meeting a mysterious man who knew her murdered father, composing a sonnet to his memory before retiring.

Pen returned to Fairoaks with Strong, forgetting entirely the message he intended for Laura. When his mother, Helen, asked anxiously about the events at Baymouth, Pen dismissed them lightly as a calamity unsuitable for romance, promising to declare his love properly when Laura returned.

While waiting, Pen busied himself packing books, arranging papers, and polishing his gun, convinced that at twenty-three it was time to launch his career. He planned to rent chambers, enter an Inn of Court, and write plays and novels to fund his life until he established himself at the bar. Only then would he return to marry Laura, the "good and sweet-tempered" girl who would steady him. He talked to his dog, Ponto, building castles in the air, unaware that his future depended on timing far more than talent.
