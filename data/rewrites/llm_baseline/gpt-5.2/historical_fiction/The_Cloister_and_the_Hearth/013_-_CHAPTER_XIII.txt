Gerard and Margaret set their faces toward Sevenbergen with the lightness of folk new loosed from a snare. The road itself seemed to sing them free: the wet reeds hissed by the ditch, larks stitched their small notes into the high air, and the cart-ruts held yesterday’s rain like dull mirrors. Yet liberty, like fresh bread, cools quickly when one sits down to taste it. Before they had well warmed their hands at Peter’s hearth, the savor of safety thinned, and bitter thoughts came up as if a sour leaven were working underneath.

Gerard was still a man broken out of bonds. Any hour might bring the hand that would clap him back into misery, and then—God help them—punishment. So, though they had found each other again, they must part.

Worse, he had taken a harsh distaste to his own place, as a man turns from meat that has been served him with insult. Margaret urged him to leave the country awhile; but the very word Italy—sunny in other mouths—made her heart go cold, as if someone had spoken of the sea and she could already taste the salt of drowning. Gerard, on the contrary, could bear the thought of leaving her only because Italy drew him like a strong spice one has never yet smelled but knows will change the whole dish. There, he said, he would earn money and name; there, his hand and eye—wasted among boors—would be valued; there, every obstacle between them and marriage would be ground small as pepper under the heel.

He had told her already all that the demoiselle Van Eyck had spoken—how she had weighed him with her painter’s judgment, how she had put gold into his palm for that very journey. Now he repeated it, as men repeat a recipe when they fear the hearer will forget one step and spoil all.

“Those gold pieces were given me to go,” he said. “It is for my good, Margaret. Here I am as a fine flour thrown to pigs. In Italy they will know what to do with me.”

It was on that ground, more than on any plea of his longing, that the unselfish girl consented. Yet the consent came salted with tears. She wept, and he—young, loving, and as quick to feel—broke down with her, and they asked each other, over and over, what they had done that so many should be their enemies, and why different persons, who had no common love, seemed to have one common will: to part them.

They sat hand in hand till midnight. The cottage noises went on around them with their homely faithfulness—Peter’s slow breathing by the settle, the mouse in the thatch, the soft ticking of a drip where the eaves shed into a pan—but in their ears those little sounds were like a world that would continue without them. Now they lamented their hard lot; now they built bright pictures of the time to come, when Gerard would return with skill made famous and pockets heavy; and in the midst of that shining future Margaret’s tears would start afresh, sudden as sap rising, and then Gerard’s brave talk would die away in a sigh.

Morning found them resigned to part—resigned as one is resigned to eating bitter herbs because hunger will not wait—but neither had courage to say when. And it is likely the hour of parting would never have struck, for love will always find one more task, one more word, one more look to delay the knife.

But about three of the afternoon Giles rode up to the door, having made a wide circuit to shun eyes. The horse steamed; the man’s voice came before he had well swung down.

“Brother Gerard!” he cried, in those tremendous tones that seemed to shake the very smoke in the chimney. “Kate bids you run for your life. They charge you with theft; you have given them a handle. Think not to explain. Hope not for justice in Tergou. The parchments you took, they are but a blind. She hath seen your death in the men’s eyes; a price is on your head. Fly! For Margaret’s sake and all that love you, loiter not life away, but fly!”

It fell like a thunder-clap in clear air, leaving two white faces staring at him—and then at each other, as if each would read in the other’s eyes whether the world had truly turned so suddenly cruel.

Giles, who till now had spoken like a messenger repeating another’s words, added hastily from his own fear: “All the constables were at our house after you, and so was Dirk Brower. Kate is wise, Gerard. Best give ear to her rede, and fly!”

“Oh, yes, Gerard!” Margaret cried, wild as if she had been struck. “Fly on the instant. Ah, those parchments—my mind misgave me. Why did I let you take them?”

“Margaret, they are but a blind,” said Gerard, catching at Giles’s phrase as at a rung. “No matter. The old caitiff shall never see them again. I will not go till I have hidden his treasure where he shall never find it.”

He thanked Giles in words that came quickly and hot, bade him farewell, and charged him to ride back and tell Catherine he was gone. “For I shall be gone ere you reach home,” said he.

Then he shouted for Martin and told him what had happened, and begged him go a little way toward Tergou and watch the road.

“Ay,” said Martin, “and if I see Dirk Brower or any of his men, I will shoot an arrow into the oak-tree that is in our garden; and on that you must run into the forest hard by, and meet me at the weird hunter’s spring. Then I will guide you through the wood.”

With that net set against surprise, Gerard breathed again, though it was a breath that seemed to scrape his throat like dry bread.

He went out with Margaret into the little garden. The earth there was dark and kindly, smelling of roots and old leaves; cabbages sat heavy as green stones, and the last onions lay with their necks bent, as if listening. Margaret stood watching the oak-tree, trembling so that the hem of her kirtle quivered against the wet grass; each moment she looked for the sudden thud of an arrow among the branches—the agreed signal that danger had come running.

Gerard took a spade and dug deep. The iron bit into the soil with a dull, satisfying sound—thok, thok—each stroke a coarse music that steadied his hands. He made the hole as a cook makes a pit for a slow fire: deep enough that what you lay in it will be swallowed by darkness and kept.

He threw the parchments in, one by one. They were mostly charters and records of the burgh—dry skins covered with words that ruled men’s lives as surely as iron bars. Yet even as he handled them, he felt how strange it was that so much power should sit in silence, needing only a reader’s tongue to become a weapon. One sheet, thicker and more private in look, bore a different air, like a family loaf amid market bread. It seemed a deed between Floris Brandt, father of Peter, and Ghysbrecht.

“Why,” said Gerard, peering, “this is as much yours as his. I will read this.”

“Oh, not now, Gerard, not now,” Margaret pleaded, and fear sharpened her voice. “Every moment you lose fills me with terror; and see—large drops of rain are beginning to fall, and the clouds lower.”

Indeed, the first heavy drops struck the leaves with a broad sound, like fingers tapping on a drumhead. The air changed its smell: the sharp green of crushed herb gave way to the metallic odor of rain on earth.

Gerard yielded; but he slipped the deed into his bosom, as a man tucks away a precious spice to keep it from damp. Over the rest he flung the earth, patted it down, and stamped it firm.

As he did so, lightning tore the sky with a white crack, and a peal of thunder answered far off. Then the rain came in earnest, thick as thrown grain. They ran for the house, the wet striking their faces and making their hair cling. Martin came in close behind them.

“The road is clear,” said he, shaking the water from his cap, “and a heavy storm coming on.”

His words were proved at once. The thunder came nearer and nearer till it crashed overhead; flashes followed one another close, like the strokes of a whip; and the rain poured in torrents, drumming on thatch and shutter so the whole cottage seemed a kettle boiling. Margaret hid her face, not to see the lightning; at that Gerard put up the rough shutter and lighted a candle. Its small flame made a warm pool of amber against the storm’s cold roaring, and the smell of tallow rose, mingling with wet wool and the faint, comforting ghost of yesterday’s broth that lived in the stones of the hearth.

They consulted together in that strained little circle of light; and Gerard, with a lover’s selfish gratitude, blessed the storm because it gave him a few hours more beside Margaret. Outside, the world was a great, loud thing—branches beating, water rushing, thunder rolling like carts of stone; inside, their voices were low and close, like hands touching in the dark.

The sun set unperceived behind the clouds. Supper was set—Peter’s plain fare, honest and nourishing, the kind that keeps a body fit to reap and sow: bread, cheese, and what else the house afforded. The knife rang softly on the board; a crust cracked. Yet Gerard and Margaret could not eat. The thought that this might be the last time they should sup together choked them, so that even the smell of warm bread, usually a solace, turned to an ache. It was as if the very taste of the food would bind the moment to memory too tightly, and memory was pain.

The storm lulled a little. Peter, old and worn, retired to rest. But Gerard was to go at peep of day, and neither he nor Margaret could afford to lose an hour in sleep, not when hours were now dearer than coin.

Martin sat with them awhile. He was fitting a new string to his bow—a task he did with a nicety like a careful cook tying a roast so it will turn evenly. He tested the twist, smoothed it, drew it, listened for the right taut note. In such small exactness there was comfort: a world where one thing, at least, could be made true by patience.

The lovers murmured their sorrows and their love beside him, their words half drowned at times by the hiss of rain and the long retreating growl of thunder. And even in that, Margaret felt how the sounds of the place—rain on thatch, wind in the oak, the low creak of timber—held a kind of shelter. There was no harsh clang of forge or shouted market here, only the old, natural voices; and yet those very peaceful sounds were now haunted, because any other sound—an alien footfall, a whispered order—could cut through them like a knife through curd.

Suddenly Martin held up his hand for silence.

They stopped at once. The candle’s flame quivered. They listened, and heard nothing but the soft drip and the far, fading mutter of the storm.

Then, as if the earth itself had exhaled a warning, a footstep crackled faintly upon the autumn leaves strewn in the garden at the back door. To those with no enemies it would have meant nothing; to those who were hunted it was dreadful. For it was a foot trying to be noiseless.

Martin fitted an arrow and in the same breath blew out the candle. Darkness fell thick, and with it the sharpening of every sound: their own breathing loud, Margaret’s stifled sob, the minute creak of the bow as Martin drew it a hair to test.

At that instant, to their horror, they heard more than one footstep approach the other door—not quite so noiselessly as the first, but very stealthily—and then a dead pause.

Their blood froze.

“Oh, Kate, oh, Kate!” Margaret moaned in the blackness. “You said fly on the instant.” And she wrung her hands in anguish and terror and wild remorse for having kept Gerard.

“Hush, girl!” whispered Martin, stern as a man who has faced wolves.

A heavy knock fell on the door—and on the hearts within.
