A few kind souls who suffered through these scribblings in manuscript form remarked—with what I can only assume was pity—that they weren’t entirely dreadful. Meanwhile, certain relatives, possibly out of obligation or a misplaced sense of loyalty, swore they’d purchase a copy if it ever stumbled into print. With such overwhelming public pressure (if one can call two friends and a guilt-tripped aunt “the public”), who am I to withhold these utterly pointless musings from the English-speaking world?

Modern readers demand books that edify, enlighten, and uplift. This one would struggle to uplift a feather, let alone a cow. I cannot, in good conscience, recommend it for intellectual, moral, or even kindling-related purposes. However, if you ever weary of trudging through the so-called “Great Books” (and let’s be honest, who doesn’t?), you might flip through this for a bit. It’ll make a nice change—like swapping a three-hour lecture for a nap.

Let me confess at once: I am an expert in idleness. Not the common, garden-variety laziness—no, my talent runs deeper. My old schoolmaster, who once attempted to bathe my intellect for nine guineas a term (extras not included), often remarked that he had never encountered a boy so gifted at stretching five minutes of work into an hour. My grandmother, with the grim wisdom of the elderly, once observed that while I might avoid most wrongdoing, I would undoubtedly neglect every duty known to man.

I have, regrettably, disproven half her prophecy—I *have* done many things I ought not to have done. But her prediction regarding my negligence remains flawless. Idling is not merely a habit for me; it is an art form. Most people misunderstand it entirely. The true idler is not some sluggish creature shuffling about in a perpetual daze. No, the master idler is *always* busy—just never with anything useful.

The secret to perfect idleness is simple: you must have an abundance of pressing work to ignore. There is no joy in doing nothing when there *is* nothing to do. True idling, like a stolen kiss, must be snatched from the jaws of responsibility.

Years ago, I fell ill—or so the doctor insisted. To me, it was merely a stubborn cold, but medical professionals have a flair for the dramatic. He gravely informed me that I had narrowly escaped death by procrastination and prescribed a month of absolute repose in Buxton. "Perfect rest," he said, as if bestowing a gift.

I envisioned a poetic convalescence: languid mornings in a dressing gown, sentimental novels slipping from my fingers as I gazed wistfully at the sky, my fragile health inspiring sighs from passing maidens. I would take the waters—an elegant ritual, I imagined, until I actually tasted them. Warm flat-irons dissolved in despair would be a delicacy by comparison. I endured six days of this torture before discovering that brandy nullified both the water’s effects and my will to live—a happy accident, as later medical authorities confirmed.

But the true agony was not the waters—it was the idleness itself. Forbidden to do anything, I mooned about like a ghost haunting my own life. My sole diversion was a daily Bath chair excursion, a thrill-ride of terror as I braced for catastrophe at every bump. By the third week, I was contemplating the wallpaper as a conversational partner.

Desperate, I rebelled. I rose early, walked to Hayfield, and—in a fit of madness—relieved an elderly laborer of his stone-breaking duties. For half an hour, I smashed rocks with the zeal of a man possessed. It was the most productive I had been in years.

Yet, despite these small rebellions, the month crawled by. When at last I escaped back to London, the soot-choked air and the clamor of the streets felt like a homecoming. I realized then: I adore idling—but only when I *should* be working.

This is the way of the world. The more urgent the task, the more appealing it becomes to linger over breakfast. The heavier the evening’s labor, the more time I spend trimming my nails. And if I *must* rise early, that is precisely when my bed becomes the most alluring place on earth.

Who, after all, wakes willingly? We are not heroes of moralistic tales. We buy alarm clocks that rouse the household except ourselves. We instruct servants to call us, then grunt and burrow deeper into the blankets. Some brave souls attempt cold baths, only to retreat immediately back to bed. The truth is, once the pillow has claimed you, no force on earth can pry you loose before absolutely necessary.

Ah, but bed is a blessed thing—a haven for the weary, the heartbroken, the overworked. It cradles us all, wise or foolish, and hushes our troubles—unless, of course, we *need* to sleep. Then it becomes a torment, the hours stretching like taffy while we stare into the dark.

But enough of beds. Let us step outside and smoke. Tobacco is the idler’s salvation. Before its invention, what did clerks and scholars do with their spare time? I blame the absence of pipes for the Middle Ages’ endless brawling. Young men, with no work and no way to relax, settled debates with battle-axes. Courtship was particularly efficient: if a man broke his rival’s skull, his beloved was deemed fair. If *his* skull was broken, well, that settled matters too.

Nowadays, we let women handle the hard labor while we recline and debate waistcoat styles. Soon, perhaps, we men will have nothing left to do but rise at noon, read novels, and sip tea. A splendid future—for idlers like me.

Ah, love! You’ve been in love, haven’t you? If not, don’t worry—it’s coming for you. Love is like the measles: everyone gets it once, and only once. After that, you’re immune. You can stroll through moonlit gardens, linger in cozy parlors, and waltz in dimly lit ballrooms without a care. You can even attend weddings without flinching. Once love has scorched you, you’re safe—free to admire without burning, to flirt without falling.

Love is a firework—a brilliant, fleeting burst that lights up the sky before sputtering into darkness. The heart, like Prometheus, dares to steal divine fire, but it can’t keep it. What’s left afterward? Warm embers—affection, comfort, companionship. A quieter, steadier glow, better suited to the long winters of life.

But oh, those first flames! Edwin and Angelina, you think your love will blaze forever. You don’t realize how quickly the fuel burns away. One day, Edwin notices Angelina no longer races to greet him; Angelina sighs when Edwin forgets her handkerchief. The grand passion fades, leaving behind something quieter—if you’re lucky. If not? A cold hearth and two people wondering where the magic went.

Youth believes love eternal. At twenty, you can’t fathom that by forty, your heart won’t still race at a glance. But time dulls the edges of all emotions—love, grief, even ambition. The grand opera of youth becomes a modest chamber piece.

And yet—oh, the madness of those early days! The breathless declarations, the jealous agonies, the way her slightest frown could shatter you! You worshipped her, not as a mere mortal, but as a goddess. You memorized the curve of her smile, the sound of her laugh. You treasured her discarded gloves, her forgotten notes. You’d have fought dragons for her, or at least braved a rainstorm to fetch her shawl.

But youth’s love, for all its fervor, is fragile. The world creeps in. Money, pride, petty grievances—they smother the flame. And what’s left? Regret? Nostalgia? Or, if you’re wise, a quieter, deeper bond—one built on shared jokes and mutual eyerolls instead of grand gestures.

So cherish love’s madness while it lasts. Let it make you brave, foolish, kind. And when it fades, don’t mourn its passing. Build something steadier in its place—a fire to warm your hands by, not a blaze to scorch your soul.

Because love, like measles, only comes once. After that, you’re on your own.

There’s a certain charm to melancholy—a dignified misery, if you will—but the blues? No, thank you. They arrive uninvited, overstay their welcome, and leave you feeling like a wrung-out dishrag. And the worst part? Nobody knows why they descend upon us. One day, you’re celebrating a windfall; the next, you’re mourning the loss of your favorite umbrella left on the train. The blues don’t discriminate.

When they strike, you become a menace to society. Your wit abandons you. Your patience evaporates. You snap at strangers, growl at friends, and contemplate kicking yourself—if only anatomy permitted. You’re restless but directionless, pacing like a caged badger before collapsing into a chair with a sigh loud enough to startle the cat.

Books? Useless. Shakespeare feels like a hack, Dickens drones, Thackeray is a pretentious windbag, and Carlyle—oh, spare us the melodrama. You hurl the offending volume across the room and glare at it as if it’s personally offended you. The cat, sensing danger, wisely flees, and you slam the door behind her with unnecessary violence.

Letter-writing seems a fine idea until you’ve stared at *"Dearest Auntie, I find I have five minutes to spare—"* for fifteen excruciating minutes, only to crumple the page in defeat. The pen, now leaking ink onto the tablecloth, becomes another casualty of your mood. You consider visiting the Thompsons—but then you remember the Thompsons are insufferable, their house is a baby-infested wasteland, and they never serve supper. The Thompsons, you decide, are the enemy.

By this point, you’re wallowing in existential despair. You imagine your own tragic demise—your friends gathered around your deathbed, weeping, finally realizing your worth. (A fantasy, of course. In reality, they’d probably fight over who gets your good cufflinks.) The injustice of life weighs upon you. You’ve been wronged since birth. The world is a cruel, unfeeling place, and you? A noble soul, tragically misunderstood.

Eventually, bedtime offers a merciful escape. You storm upstairs, shed clothes like a man fleeing a burning building, and dive into bed with the grace of a sack of potatoes. Sleep comes in fits—when it comes at all—punctuated by dreams of falling, failing, or showing up to a dinner party in your underwear.

Married men, of course, have it easier. They take out their blues on their families—criticizing dinner, banishing children to their rooms, and generally making life miserable for everyone within earshot. Single men, alas, must suffer in solitude, tossing and turning until dawn.

The blues wear many names. The poet calls it *"a feeling of sadness."* ’Arry announces he’s *"got the blooming hump."* Your sister claims she’s *"out of sorts."* The young man at the club sighs dramatically and declares himself *"jolly miserable."* As for me? I say I’ve got *"a strange, unsettled feeling"*—and then I go out, because the blues, like vampires, thrive in the evening.

Daylight scatters them. Under the sun, we rage, we hustle, we swear at misplaced keys—but we don’t brood. Melancholy is a nocturnal creature. It slinks in with the shadows, when the world quiets and the mind wanders. And let’s be honest—real misery doesn’t inspire poetry. Actual grief isn’t romantic. It’s the *memory* of sorrow that we cradle like a treasured relic, long after the sting has faded.

There’s a peculiar sweetness in looking back on pain that no longer hurts. The old lady weeping over tiny shoes, the widow pressing a lock of hair to her heart—they don’t weep from anguish, but from a sorrow softened by time, now worn smooth as a pebble in the hand. Sentiment, after all, is the luxury of healed wounds.

And then there’s the melancholy of a summer’s evening—that golden hush when the world seems to sigh. The city streets blur into shadows, the river slips silently under bridges, and in the countryside, the trees whisper secrets to the dusk. It’s in these quiet hours that we feel something larger than ourselves, something solemn and sacred. The grind of daily life fades, and for a moment, we glimpse the vast, quiet beauty of existence—before remembering we forgot to pay the gas bill.

So here’s to the blues. Annoying, unavoidable, but oddly illuminating. They remind us that life isn’t just about bread and cheese (or even kisses), but about those fleeting moments when we pause, look up, and wonder.

Then we go to bed grumpy and hope tomorrow’s better.

It’s a curious thing. I sat down fully intending to write something brilliantly original, but my mind has stubbornly fixated on one singular, unglamorous topic: being broke. Perhaps it’s because my hands are jammed in my pockets—a habit of mine, except when in the company of certain female relatives who launch into passionate sermons on how this is "not gentlemanly." Frankly, I fail to see how rummaging in one’s own pockets diminishes gentility. If I were pilfering from *someone else’s* pockets, sure, that’s a scandal. But my own? Absurd.

Though, now that I think of it, I *have* heard men grumble bitterly while doing it—usually older gentlemen. We younger chaps, however, find pockets indispensable. Without them, we’re as awkward as a music-hall dandy without his top hat. Trousers pockets, mind you—right one jingling with a few coins, left one weighed down by keys—are the armor with which we face the world, even a steely-eyed post office clerk.

But empty pockets? A tragedy. In my leaner years, when my fortune dwindled to a solitary shilling, I’d recklessly squander a penny just for the luxury of jingling eleven coppers. Oddly, eleven pence feels richer than twelve. If I were truly pretentious, I’d have exchanged that penny for *two* ha’pennies—maximal jingle, minimal sense.

I speak on this matter with authority. I’ve been a provincial actor. I’ve been a "gentleman of the press." I’ve subsisted on 15 shillings a week. I’ve stretched 10 shillings over a fortnight while owing the other five. I’ve even lived off the contents of a greatcoat—though what those contents were, I’d rather not recall. Poverty is a ruthless tutor in domestic economy. When every farthing counts, you learn to:

1. Wait for your ha’penny change.
2. Walk a mile to spare a penny.
3. Treat beer as a rare sacrament.
4. Wear a collar four days straight (turning it judiciously).

I recommend this education to all prospective spouses and university-bound heirs. Nothing cures extravagance like counting pennies.

Take the delicate souls who shudder at claret under ninety-four shillings or roast mutton unadorned by French adjectives. I long to drag them to an East End pub and force upon them a sixpenny feast: beefsteak pudding (4d), potatoes (1d), half a pint of porter (1d). The aroma alone—beer, tobacco, and questionable grease—might teach them humility.

Then there’s the Generous Cadger, scattering small change like confetti while dodging his tailor’s bills. "One *can’t* tip less than a shilling," sniffed a young clerk I once lunched with. I nodded gravely while plotting to steer him to a Covent Garden eatery where the waiter’s sleeves doubled as napkins. Tip him a shilling? The man would weep with gratitude—possibly on your shoulder.

For all the jokes about empty pockets, there’s nothing funny about it. Haggling over pennies isn’t droll. Being labeled "stingy" isn’t a lark. Poverty isn’t hell because of discomfort—Robinson Crusoe didn’t fret over patched trousers (assuming he wore any). No, the sting is in being *seen* as poor. It’s not the cold that makes a coatless man hurry; it’s the lie he’ll tell about "health principles."

"Poverty is no crime," they say. Correct. Crimes are at least *interesting*. Poverty is merely a blunder—punished by universal contempt. The world scorns a shabby man, whether Christian, lord, or demagogue. Appearances reign supreme. The same man who’ll parade Piccadilly with a well-dressed scoundrel will duck into an alley to avoid a threadbare acquaintance. And the threadbare man? He’d rather crawl into a sewer than be spotted.

Time, that indifferent physician, numbs the shame. The veteran pauper pawns his watch with the calm of a man ordering tea. The novice? He lurks outside the shop like a criminal, sweats through a fabricated story about a "friend," and leaves convinced the entire neighborhood is pointing.

And let’s not forget the agony of telling time without a watch. Public houses are too fast; churches, too slow. Peering into a pub for the clock earns you the barmaid’s scorn. Jumping outside the window? That just draws a crowd expecting a song.

The uninitiated view pawnshops as the final descent into disgrace. The seasoned know better—you’ll find half your acquaintances there eventually. I maintain it’s nobler than borrowing from friends, though some protest "on principle." (Translation: They object to 25% interest.)

There are degrees of destitution. Some lack thousands; some lack shillings. Currently, I lack a fiver—temporarily, of course. Should any charitable reader wish to remedy this (forward to Messrs. Field & Tuer, *sealed*), I’d be eternally obliged. I’d even furnish an I.O.U., though I can’t promise it’d be worth the paper it’s scrawled on.

Let’s face it: the world runs on vanity. Women are vain. Men are worse. Children? Don’t get me started. At this very moment, a tiny tyrant is pounding my shins, demanding admiration for her new shoes—shoes that look like they were cobbled together by a blind cobbler and strapped to the wrong feet. But do I say this? Of course not. I shower her with praise so thick it would make a used-car salesman blush. One ill-advised attempt at honesty—"No, I am *not* pleased with you"—ended in tears, accusations, and a swift betrayal to her mother. Now, I’ve learned my lesson: flattery is the currency of survival.

Even animals aren’t immune. I once saw a Newfoundland dog preening before a shop window, admiring his reflection with the smug satisfaction of a politician at a ribbon-cutting. Then there was the cow at a country fair, prancing about like a debutante in her floral crown, only to sulk when they took it off. Cats? They’re the worst. Insult one, and she’ll stalk off in a huff. Compliment her, and she’ll purr like a malfunctioning engine. Dogs are simpler souls—pat their heads, call them names, roll them over, and they’ll adore you forever. But a cat? She’ll demand a formal courtship: *"Poor pussy… did 'ums?"* (whatever that means), followed by strategic chin scratches. Cross her, and she’ll remind you of your place with a well-placed claw.

Vanity, my friends, is the glue of society. Without it, we’d all be insufferable saints—respectable, perhaps, but dreadfully dull. It’s our flaws that make us human, our shared vanity that unites us. From the toddler boasting of her "doodness" to the general weighed down by medals, from the dandy obsessing over his cravat to the poet basking in his own sonnets—we’re all chasing the same illusion. Flattery is the universal lubricant. Tell a man he’s wise, a woman she’s divine, a cat she’s regal, and you’ll have them eating from your hand. Even those who claim to despise flattery—"Oh, I *hate* sycophants!"—will melt if you praise their *humility*.

And love? Love is flattery in its purest form. Try wooing a woman with brutal honesty: *"Your nose is… acceptable. Your hands are… functional."* See how far that gets you. No, the path to romance is paved with hyperbole. Call her an angel, a goddess, a creature too radiant for mortal eyes, and she’ll sigh and declare you *"such a dear."* (Whether she believes you is irrelevant. The game is the thing.)

But let’s not dismiss vanity as mere folly. It’s the engine of ambition! Why else would men toil for fortunes they’ll never spend, or artists slave over masterpieces? Not for bread, but for bragging rights. Vanity built cathedrals, launched ships, and—occasionally—inspired decent behavior. After all, what is honor but vanity in a starched collar?

So let us embrace our vanity—not for fripperies and follies, but for nobler things. Let us pride ourselves on kindness, on courage, on refusing to be petty or cruel. Let us be vain of our good hearts and clean hands. And if, in moments of weakness, we still sneak a glance in the mirror or bask in idle praise? Well, at least we’re in excellent company.

The world may be vain, but without it, we’d all be insufferably perfect—and where’s the fun in that?

Ah, "getting on in the world"—what a dreadfully serious topic for a professional loafer like myself to ponder! But then again, who better to comment on the frenzied race of humanity than someone who has, quite deliberately, taken a seat on the sidelines? From my luxurious perch of laziness (metaphorically speaking—my actual perch is a battered armchair with suspicious stains), I observe the grand parade of life with the detached amusement of a cat watching a particularly chaotic game of fetch.

What a spectacle it is! A never-ending stampede of humanity, all elbowing, shoving, and occasionally tripping over their own shoelaces in their desperate scramble toward… well, whatever it is they think counts as "success." Young and old, rich and poor, the clever and the spectacularly dim—all hurtling down the same crowded road like shoppers on the first day of a sale.

You’ve got your classic overachievers, shoulders set like they’re charging into battle, and your schemers slinking through gaps like pickpockets at a fair. There’s the bookworm, nose buried in *How to Succeed Without Really Trying*, promptly being trampled by a stampede of people who didn’t bother to read it. Here’s a fellow who looks like he’d rather be anywhere else, dragged along by a determined spouse; there’s a starry-eyed youth already regretting leaving his village the moment he steps in a puddle. And let’s not forget the rogue who thinks zigzagging counts as progress, or the dreamer who keeps stopping to wonder if the destination is worth the blisters.

It’s a glorious mess—duchesses elbowing clerks, politicians shouting promises they’ll forget by lunchtime, poets dodging tax collectors. Everyone’s in a hurry, yet nobody seems to be getting anywhere faster. The road’s dusty, the competition’s ruthless, and the only rest stops appear to be metaphorical ditches where the exhausted collapse. And yet, who can resist joining in? It’s like watching a brawl in a pub—sooner or later, you’re going to want to throw a punch, or at least cheer someone on.

Now, the moralists will wag their fingers and say ambition is vulgar, that contentment is noble. Rubbish. Contentment is what happens when you run out of ideas and take up amateur flute-playing. Ambition is what built everything from pyramids to pub snacks. Sure, the ambitious might be exhausting at parties, but without them, we’d all still be living in caves, contentedly gnawing raw mammoth and wondering what this "wheel" nonsense is about.

Let’s be honest—getting ahead is a game. A brutal, ridiculous, occasionally rigged game, but a game all the same. And like any good game, the fun isn’t just in winning; it’s in the scrap, the near misses, the sheer audacity of playing at all. The contented folk? Lovely people, but they’re the audience, not the players. And audiences don’t get trophies (unless they catch a foul ball, and even then, it’s debatable).

So step right up! The great carnival of life is open to all. Prizes may include wealth, fame, or just the satisfaction of having annoyed the right people. Blanks are guaranteed for most, but who cares? The real trophy is the chaos you create along the way.

Now if you’ll excuse me, I’ve just spotted an opportunity to nap through the next round. Some of us are professionals, after all.

It’s a curious thing, but life seems to take a peculiar delight in thwarting me. Recently, I resolved to write an article on a topic so fresh, so untouched by human discourse, that the world would gasp at its novelty. "This," I told myself, "will be my masterpiece—something no one has ever dared to address before!" For days, I racked my brain. Nothing.

Then our charwoman, Mrs. Cutting—a woman of stern moral fiber who reads only *Lloyd’s Weekly News* and the Bible (all else being frivolous and possibly sinful)—observed my distress. "Lor’, sir," she said, "you look downright tormented." I confessed my quest for an unprecedented subject, one that would dazzle humanity with its originality. She laughed. Naturally. When I am serious, people assume I’m joking. When I *do* joke, they stare blankly.

Take last week. I had crafted what I considered a masterstroke of humor—a long, winding anecdote about an incident in Whitechapel, artfully dropped into a conversation about Shakespeare’s views on the Reformation. I set the stage, delivered the punchline with a flourish, and… silence. Eventually, someone asked, "Was *that* the joke?" I confirmed it was. They nodded politely. One elderly gentleman, bless him, needed a full dissection of which part was meant to be funny.

Meanwhile, I know a fellow who laughs at *everything*. Tell him his house is on fire, and he’ll howl with mirth. Ask him the time, and he collapses in stitches. You must preface serious remarks with, "This is *not* amusing," or he’ll dissolve into giggles at the mere mention of the weather.

But back to Mrs. Cutting. After rejecting her first proposal ("samplers"—apparently all the rage in her youth), she suggested the weather. A dismal, overdone topic—yet from that moment, it clung to my thoughts like mud to a boot.

And what wretched stuff it is! We are never satisfied. Summer scorches us, winter freezes us, spring and autumn are accused of indecisiveness. If it’s dry, we demand rain; if it rains, we curse the damp. Snow? A nuisance. No snow? A scandal. We behave as though the skies have personally swindled us.

In the countryside, weather has its charms. Snow transforms the world into a fairy tale; frosty mornings ring with laughter; spring is a bashful maiden, summer a drowsy hum, autumn a melancholy feast of color. Even rain, in the fields, has purpose.

But in the city? Weather is a tramp in a ballroom—unwelcome and grubby. Rain turns streets into sludge, snow into sooty mounds, and the east wind into a shrieking demon. Our neighbor insists this downpour is "good for the crops," though his agricultural expertise extends no further than last summer’s ill-fated cucumber frame. Meanwhile, my new spring suit—once a vision—now resembles a mud-splattered sail.

Ah, the suit. I blame Jim. The moment he saw it, he declared it a public duty to parade it before "the girls." "Like a day in the country for them," he said. By evening’s end, I was in bed with whisky, gruel, and a mustard plaster. The suit? A casualty of Jim’s altruism.

Mud adores me. While others glide through grime unscathed, I emerge looking like a half-drowned chimney sweep. If London had but one puddle, I would find it—and fall in it.

April’s fickleness is another grievance. Poets rhapsodize about its capriciousness, comparing it to a coquette. Charming in metaphor; less so when you’ve been drenched, sunburned, and blown inside-out within an hour. My "automaton" umbrella—a device that opened and shut at whim—was the final insult. It jammed in downpours, then sprang open beneath clear skies, leaving me the lunatic under a parasol in sunshine.

And let us not discuss lost hats. Few spectacles rival a man chasing his hat while a gleeful terrier assists, children scatter, and a sweep receives an accidental embrace.

Spring in the city is a cruel jest. The season of renewal is lost on grimy streets and grubbier urchins. True spring belongs to the hills, the moors, the open sky—where the wind carries whispers of something vast and wild beyond our little lives.

But here? It’s just rain. And mud. And a ruined suit.

What I have endured this morning at the paws of our four-legged housemates defies all human expression. It began, as all disasters do in this household, with Gustavus Adolphus.

Gustavus Adolphus—affectionately abbreviated to "Gusty" by those below-stairs who have not suffered his full wrath—is, in theory, a splendid creature. Outdoors, where there are fields to romp and commons to conquer, he is the very model of canine nobility. Indoors, however, he is a catastrophe on four legs. His mere existence in a confined space violates the laws of physics. Stretch? Two chairs and a what-not collapse. Wag? The room resembles the aftermath of a small war. Breathe? The fire extinguishes itself in self-defense.

At dinner, he employs a particularly diabolical tactic: slinking beneath the table, lying in wait, and then—just as the soup is served—rising with the suddenness of a jack-in-the-box. The table lurches, the diners shriek, and what follows is a frenzied ballet of human limbs grappling with furniture while Gusty, convinced we are attempting some dark ritual, thrashes like a creature possessed. The finale is always the same: a ruined meal, an overturned table, and a heap of furious humanity.

This morning, he entered with his usual cyclone-inspired grace and immediately swept my coffee cup off the table with his tail, baptizing my waistcoat in lukewarm bitterness. I rose, uttered a phrase not fit for print, and advanced upon him with intent. He fled toward the door—only to collide with Eliza, who was bearing eggs. The resulting tableau: Eliza on the floor, eggs in artistic disarray across the carpet, and Gusty vanished in a blur of guilty fur.

I shouted after him, suggesting he confine himself to the yard for the foreseeable future. He seemed to agree, dodging a flung coal-scoop with the agility of a seasoned fugitive. Peace briefly resumed.

But no. Ten minutes later, I found him at the top of the stairs, barking at an immovable obstacle: Tittums, our kitten.

Tittums is roughly the size of a bread roll and twice as volatile. There she sat, one stair above him, back arched, delivering a stream of profanity that would make a sailor blush. Now, I am no stranger to strong language—between us, I consider it a vital release for the male psyche—but Tittums is a virtuoso. I reprimanded her for her unchristian vocabulary (hypocrisy be damned) and pocketed her, only to later discover her attempting to swallow my pen, upend the ink, and then swear *at me* for her own mess.

Tim, our two-year-old terrier with the ego of a seasoned diplomat, chose this moment to intervene. A scuffle ensued. Tittums’ mother joined the fray. Tim emerged with a scratched nose. Justice.

Now they are all banished to the passage, where they continue their battle royale. I am ink-stained, irritable, and issuing this decree: any further feline or canine interference shall be met with extreme prejudice. Bring your own undertaker.

Yet, for all this, I adore them. They are superior companions to humans in every way. They do not argue, bore you with their troubles, or make tactless remarks about your plays. They never confuse your wife’s cousin for her husband, nor ask why you haven’t written a masterpiece yet. They are steadfast in their affection, indifferent to your flaws, and eternally glad to see you—whether you are jubilant, morose, or simply in need of a silent friend to blink at the fire beside you.

A dog does not care if you are rich or poor, wise or foolish. You are his comrade, and that is enough. He will follow you to the ends of the earth, defend you to his last breath, and gaze at you with those absurdly loyal eyes as if you hung the moon. And we, in our infinite human superiority, call *him* the brainless one.

Cats, though often accused of selfishness, are merely discreet in their devotion. I have known feline friends who followed me like shadows, sat vigil when I was ill, and even developed regrettable drinking habits (one of mine had a penchant for beer drippings—a vice I neither condemn nor endorse).

Rats, meanwhile, occupy a peculiar place in our imaginations—simultaneously reviled and fascinating. They haunt crumbling ruins, flee sinking ships, and scuttle through tales of horror with eerie purpose. There is something mythic about them, from Bishop Hatto’s gruesome demise to the Pied Piper’s eerie melody. That old legend lingers in my mind—the piper who led children away, their laughter fading into the mountain. A metaphor, perhaps, for the inexplicable callings that tug us from the mundane, whispering of mysteries beyond our grasp.

If I may beg one thing of pet owners: love your animals, but spare us the maudlin excess. There is nothing more tiresome than the family that elevates Fido to sainthood, holding court over his every yawn and scratch. A dog is not a surrogate child, nor a cat a velvet-pawed aristocrat. They are creatures of simple joys and honest instincts—worthy of affection, yes, but not idolatry.

Now, if you’ll excuse me, I must extricate Tittums from the inkpot again. Some things never change.

All great literary men are shy—myself included, though I’m told it’s hardly noticeable. And for that, I am grateful. Once, my shyness was painfully obvious, a source of misery to me and discomfort to everyone around me—particularly my lady friends, who complained bitterly about it.

A shy man’s life is not a happy one. Men dislike him, women despise him, and worst of all, he despises himself. No amount of practice eases his torment, and time is the only cure—though I once stumbled upon a delightfully absurd remedy in a weekly journal’s advice column:

Poor wretch! I can only imagine his despair upon reading those words. *"Adopt an easy and pleasing manner"*—as if one could simply *decide* not to be shy! My dear afflicted friend, do not attempt such artifice. Any effort to appear otherwise will only render you either absurdly effusive or unbearably awkward. Better to remain your natural self—though be warned, you will still be deemed either surly or dull.

Yet the shy man does exact a small revenge upon society for the tortures it inflicts upon him. His misery is contagious. He casts a pall over any gathering, draining the cheer from even the most jovial souls. This is often misunderstood—his nervous silence mistaken for arrogance, his stammering for indifference. When terror grips him at the first word addressed to him, rendering him mute and flushed, he is accused of being hot-tempered.

But the shy man’s greatest curse is perpetual misunderstanding. His jokes are taken as solemn declarations; his sarcasm, as earnest opinion. If he attempts flattery, it is received as biting satire, earning him lifelong resentment. The world delights in his discomfort, finding endless amusement in his plight—yet beneath the comedy lies something tragic.

A shy man is a lonely man, forever separated from his fellow creatures by an invisible wall. He longs to join the laughter, to speak and be heard—but his voice fails him. In crowded streets, in lively rooms, amid work and merriment, he stands apart, a silent spectator to the world’s joy. His heart brims with love and sympathy, but his lips remain sealed. His indignation at injustice chokes unspoken in his throat, turning inward to sour his spirit.

Yes, shy men—like ugly women—endure a harsh existence in a world that demands thick skin. A trembling, blushing creature, knees knocking and hands twitching, is an embarrassment to all. If he cannot cure himself, he might as well hang himself and be done with it.

But take heart, fellow sufferers—for I speak from experience. Once, I was the shyest of the shy, a man who could scarcely meet a pretty maid without his knees betraying him. Yet now—well, just yesterday, I confronted a railway refreshment-room attendant with stern reproach for her lack of courtesy. I held her gaze, demanded proper service, and—

—then immediately fled without my tea. But that was because I changed my mind, not because I was frightened.

Shyness, let it be known, is not stupidity. The loud and brash may mock it, but sensitivity is no flaw. The horse is not inferior to the sparrow, nor the deer to the pig. Shyness is merely excess of feeling—not to be confused with conceit, though fools often mistake it as such.

Indeed, conceit is the surest cure for shyness. Once convinced of your own brilliance, bashfulness evaporates. When you regard your fellow men as intellectual infants, their presence ceases to unnerve you. True conceit is armor—impenetrable, unshaken by petty slights. It renders a man genial, unpretentious, at ease with high and low alike.

The shy man, by contrast, is humble—too anxious, too doubtful. But youth tempers with time. Experience wears away his diffidence, or the world’s rough handling hardens him. You seldom meet a truly shy man past his youth—except in novels, where he is portrayed as saintly, noble, and adored by women.

Stage shyness is a pretty fiction. The shy hero whispers devotion in asides, endures mockery with gentle patience, and is ultimately revealed as wise and good. Real shyness is less poetic. The real shy man is clumsy, graceless, and far less interesting—but at least he is constant in love. Why? Because the mere thought of courting *one* woman terrifies him; the idea of pursuing *two* is unthinkable.

His bolder brethren, meanwhile, flirt recklessly, distracted by every pair of laughing eyes. The shy man, fixed upon his boots, knows no such temptation. Happy? Not at all—he *wishes* he could flirt, curses his own timidity, and occasionally, in a burst of desperation, attempts it—only to retreat in humiliated failure.

And how the world delights in his discomfort! His blushes, his fumbling hands, his stammering tongue—all are fodder for ridicule. Friends mimic his nervous walk, his awkward handshake. Fathers advise him to *"be bold!"* Mothers chirp platitudes about shame. Brothers call him *"worse than a girl,"* and sisters indignantly deny that any girl could be half so hopeless.

And they are right. There is no such thing as a shy woman—or if there is, I have yet to meet her. The notion that women are timid fawns, blushing and fleeing from masculine boldness, is charming nonsense. A girl of twelve will face a room with cool composure while her brother stammers beside her. A woman will stride late into a concert, unbothered by stares, while her husband trails in apologetic misery.

In matters of love, especially, women are fearless. From the first coy glance to the last wedding vow, they command the field while men flounder. But then, love is *her* business—and in business, we all set aside our weaknesses. Even the shyest man becomes a bold salesman when his livelihood depends on it.

So take courage, fellow sufferers. Shyness fades—or if it does not, you may at least take grim satisfaction in knowing you are not alone. And if all else fails, remember: even the most wretched among us may yet find refuge in the quiet certainty of his own conceit.

Ah, babies! Those enigmatic little creatures who rule households with their tiny, iron fists. I, too, was once a baby—though my tenure in that role was brief, far briefer than my absurdly elongated infant garments. Why must babies be swaddled in enough fabric to outfit a small circus tent? Is it to disguise their true dimensions, as if parents fear the world will judge them for producing something so compact? I once posed this question to a nurse, who replied with the kind of wisdom only the profoundly unbothered possess: *"Lor', sir, they always have long clothes, bless their little hearts."* When I pressed further, she recoiled as if I’d suggested swaddling the child in newspaper. So, the mystery remains.

Frankly, the entire concept of dressing babies strikes me as excessive. Life is already a relentless cycle of buttons and zippers; must we subject these bed-bound beings to the same torment? Why rouse them at dawn merely to swap one meaningless smock for another, only to tuck them back into oblivion? And let’s not pretend there’s any discernible difference between a baby’s "day shirt" and its "nightshirt." It’s all the same shapeless sack, as if fashion declared infancy a no-aesthetic zone.

But clothes are merely the first of many bafflements. Take, for instance, the impossible task of distinguishing a baby boy from a baby girl. Hair? Identical. Wardrobe? Unisex chaos. Conversation? Limited to gurgles, which, while charming, offer no taxonomic clarity. Misgender an infant just once, and you’ll witness a family’s collective gasp of horror, as though you’ve insulted an ancient bloodline. Refer to the child as "it," and you may as well have kicked a puppy in front of a vicar. The only safe recourse is to coo, *"What a little angel!"*—a term so conveniently gender-neutral it should earn me a diplomatic medal.

Ah, but the true trial comes when you are summoned to *"see the baby."* Few ordeals are more harrowing for the uninitiated bachelor. The moment the bell rings and nurse is summoned, a dread settles in. The room erupts in a chorus of *"Isn’t he precious?"* and *"Oh, just look at her toes!"* while you stand there, sweating, wondering if feigning sudden blindness would be plausible. Then, the nurse enters—a stern, towering figure bearing what appears to be a loaf of bread wrapped in a shawl. This, you’re told, is the baby.

Silence falls. All eyes turn to you. Your mind empties. Desperate, you blurt something like, *"Goodness, it’s... very small, isn’t it?"* The nurse fixes you with a glare that suggests you’ve just insulted the royal lineage. *"They generally are at this stage,"* she replies, as if you’ve asked why the sky is blue. Then comes the inevitable command: *"Take her in your arms, sir."* You comply, stiff as a mannequin, while the child eyes you with the suspicion of a cat near a bathtub. The moment you attempt a tentative bounce, the nurse snaps, *"I wouldn’t jig her, sir,"* as if you’ve been caught attempting amateur acrobatics. Then, of course, the baby wails—a sound so piercing you’re instantly branded a villain. *"What did you *do* to her?"* the mother demands, as though you’ve been caught teaching the infant poker.

Yet for all their chaos, babies are life’s tiny comedians. They toddle with the gravity of statesmen, puddle-jump in Sunday best, and hold profound debates with spoons. They adore the most inedible items (candle wax! shoelaces!) and express affection by yanking the dog’s ears. They turn orderly homes into obstacle courses and wallets into relics of a bygone era. And yet—would we trade them? A house without their shrieks and sticky fingerprints would be a museum, not a home.

Still, a word to the wise new mother: Do not let the baby monopolize your heart like a greedy landlord. Your husband, that overgrown child, still needs a kind word, a shared laugh. The world will not end if you step away from the crib for an evening. The first baby consumes all; the fifth barely rates a glance. Balance, dear chaos-bringer, balance.

But enough scolding. Who could truly resent these small, solemn-faced explorers of staircases and dust bunnies? They gaze upon the world with the wonder of astronauts, every sidewalk crack a canyon, every cat a mythical beast. And when they pause, staring down some dim street of the future, you wonder—what do they see? Hope? Fear? Or just the next shiny thing to grab?

Godspeed, little travelers. The road ahead is long, and we, farther along, can only wave and smile. You’ll figure it out. Probably.

I’ve always had a fondness for eating and drinking—eating especially, ever since I was a child. Back then, I boasted not only an appetite but a digestion to match. I recall a particular dinner guest, a sallow, hollow-eyed gentleman, who watched me demolish my plate with something between fascination and horror. After five minutes of silent observation, he turned to my father and asked, *"Does your boy ever suffer from dyspepsia?"*

*"Not that I’ve heard,"* replied my father. *"Collywobbles"*—a nickname, I assure you, not my Christian name—*"do you ever have dyspepsia?"*

*"No, Pa,"* I answered, then added, *"What is dyspepsia, Pa?"*

The gentleman regarded me with a look of profound pity and envy. *"You’ll know,"* he said solemnly, *"one day."*

My dear mother used to say she loved watching me eat, and I take comfort in knowing I must have brought her much joy in that regard. A growing boy, full of vigor and blessedly free from excessive study, can meet even the most demanding culinary expectations with ease. There’s something comical about watching boys eat—provided, of course, you’re not the one footing the bill. Their idea of a proper meal is a slab of roast beef the size of a doorstop, half a dozen potatoes (preferably undercooked for extra heft), a mountain of greens, a fortress of Yorkshire pudding, followed by dumplings, apples, nuts, cakes, and a gallon of ginger beer. And then? They bolt outside to play at horses, leaving the rest of us—feeble creatures who must lie prostrate for hours after consuming a thimbleful of consommé—in their dust.

Yet boys don’t have all the advantages. They never know the sublime satisfaction of being *full*. They never experience that divine post-prandial euphoria where a man leans back, closes his eyes, and surrenders to the bliss of digestion. To a boy, a meal is mere fuel. To a man, it’s a transformation—a potion that turns the world softer, brighter, kinder. After a good dinner, even the most cantankerous soul grows mellow. The man who scowled at his neighbor at noon now pats stray cats and murmurs *"poor pussy"* with maudlin tenderness. The fellow who sneered at street musicians now wonders if they’re cold and contemplates tossing them a shilling. Wives’ relations, normally the bane of existence, are suddenly tolerated.

Indigestion, you see, is the great emotional alchemist. I’ve found that if I wish to write anything remotely poignant, I must first consume a plate of buttered muffins. On an empty stomach, I’d scoff at melodrama; on a full one, I weep at parting lovers and withered flowers. Tragedies unfold before me—pale corpses adrift on stormy seas, graves in lonely deserts, the hollow sobs of strong men. All of it, of course, is the muffins. A mutton chop and champagne would leave me cold.

Foolish people—by which I mean anyone who disagrees with me—will insist that mental anguish outweighs physical suffering. What rot. A man with a toothache forgets his broken heart. A gnawing hunger drowns out all other sorrows. We well-fed folk don’t truly understand starvation—that ravenous, bone-deep craving where a crust seems a feast and the smell of pea pudding behind a foggy window is torture. Hunger, to us, is a mere seasoning, a way to heighten the pleasure of eating. But to the truly starving? It’s a relentless specter.

To properly enjoy a meal, one must first earn it. A thirty-mile walk with no sustenance does wonders for the appetite. The sight of a laden table after such exertion is a vision of glory. The first sip of beer, the first bite of roast meat—ah! And then the blissful aftermath, cigar in hand, beaming at the world.

But beware: this plan hinges on the certainty of dinner. I recall a holiday with my old friend Joe (where *is* he these days? Owed me fourteen shillings, too…). We set out on a marathon walk, having ordered a duck for supper—a *large* duck, as our landlady proudly assured us. Eight hours of wrong turns, quarrels, and muddy detours later, we stumbled home, dreaming of that bird. We resisted the siren call of a village inn, saving ourselves for the promised feast.

At last, we arrived. We washed, we dressed, we took our seats with reverent anticipation. The dish was unveiled. I seized the carving knife—and met my match. The duck resisted like a seasoned pugilist. After five minutes of futile hacking, Joe suggested—*insultingly*—that someone competent take over. The bird, in a final act of defiance, leapt into the fender. We retrieved it. We attacked again. It was like carving a boot.

We did, eventually, reduce it to edible fragments—with a chisel. The result? A dinner of vegetables and apple tart. The duck itself could have been used to resole shoes.

But enough of eating—what of drinking? Ah, there’s a topic best approached with delicacy. The days of glorified drunkenness are past; a clear head is now preferred over a reeling one. And yet, humanity’s thirst is unquenchable. We drink to celebrate, to mourn, to talk, to think. We drink to healths, to queens, to armies, to anything that stands still long enough. If we ran out of toasts, we’d likely drink to our mothers-in-law.

Why do we *drink* health, by the way? Why not *eat* it? Imagine standing and solemnly consuming a tart in someone’s honor.

I’ll never understand the constant need to drink—unless, of course, one is drowning sorrows. For the poor, I suppose, liquor offers fleeting escape from lives of grinding misery. Let us not judge too harshly those who seek warmth in gin when their world is a damp cellar and their future a gray, endless slog.

But let us return to happier thoughts. The stomach, my friends, is the true seat of happiness. The kitchen is our temple, the cook our high priest. There, sorrows melt, tempers soften, and joy is ladled out in generous portions. So let us eat, drink, and—above all—be merry.

"Oh! You’ve got rooms to let?"

"Mother!"

"Well, what is it?"

"There’s a gentleman about the rooms!"

"Ask ’im in. I’ll be up in a minute."

"Will yer step inside, sir? Mother’ll be up directly."

So you step inside, and after a minute, "mother" emerges slowly from the depths of the kitchen, untying her apron and shouting last-minute potato instructions to an unseen underling.

"Good morning, sir," she says with a smile that suggests she’s washed it too many times. "This way, please."

You protest: "Is it worth the climb? Just tell me—what are they like, and what’s the damage?"

But the landlady insists, so with a resigned sigh (meant to absolve you of any future complaints about wasted time), you trudge upstairs behind her. At the first landing, you kick over a pail, prompting a lecture on the laziness of modern servants and a bellowed summons for Sarah to remove the offending objects *immediately*.

Outside the rooms, she pauses, hand on doorknob, to explain that they’re "a bit untidy"—the last lodger only left yesterday, you see, and today is cleaning day (it always is). Then you enter, and together you survey the scene like two mourners at a funeral.

Furnished apartments in the harsh morning light are not a sight to lift the soul. They have the air of a discarded stage set—lifeless, hollow. It’s only when you’ve colonized them with your own chaos—your pipes strewn like fallen soldiers, your books in drunken piles, your mismatched slippers (one under the coal scuttle, the other atop the piano)—that they become home.

Ah, home! Where the walls are hidden behind familiar pictures, where the mantelpiece is a shrine to lost loves (their photographs smiling bravely through the dust), where the old screen—faded now, nibbled by moths—still stands guard, a relic of your mother’s girlhood. That screen! What a dazzling thing it must have been once, all tulips and roses bursting from a single stem. Now it’s gray and weary, like the hands that stitched it.

Does it remember? Does it recall the girl who bent over it, her laughter as bright as the thread she worked with? And was there ever a boy nearby—a boy who kept interrupting, who kept grabbing those small hands to kiss them, who kept tossing the screen aside in his eagerness? Could that boy—so wild, so ridiculous in his tight trousers and frilled shirt—really have become the stern-faced man you called "father"?

Old furniture holds such ghosts. It whispers stories if you listen. But lodging-house furniture? It’s a stranger, and like all strangers, it shows you its worst side first. The horsehair sofa stabs you with its knobs. The mirror is fogged with age. The carpet is frayed, the wallpaper a crime. The ceiling looks like a coffee-stained manuscript of life’s regrets. And the ornaments—those unspeakable ornaments!

Every lodging-house in Britain has the same hideous knickknacks. The pair of *things* on the mantel (glass danglers eternally on the verge of suicide), the china cow perpetually unsure if it’s a cow or a failed temple, the lump of dough masquerading as Cupid. Add a sampler stitched by a half-blind aunt, a print of *The Huguenots*, and a framed certificate celebrating Father’s vaccination (or was it his membership in the Odd Fellows?), and you have the full horror.

Then comes the rent.

"That’s steep," you say.

The landlady sighs. "To be honest, sir, I *used* to get [higher figure], and before that, [even higher figure]."

One shudders to think what rents were in the Golden Age of Lodgers. Young men of yesteryear must have been either wealthier or more reckless. I’d have been living in the attic.

Ah, the attic! The kingdom of the struggler. In lodgings, the higher you rise in life, the lower you descend in floors. You start in the garret and claw your way down to respectability. And what names have haunted those garrets! Poets, painters, philosophers—the world’s "lumber," stored out of sight until it blazed too bright to ignore.

Haydn, Chatterton, Addison, Goldsmith, Johnson, Dickens—all attic-dwellers. Hans Andersen dreamed there. Burns scribbled there. The garret is genius’s cradle, though a damned uncomfortable one.

Personally, I admire attics—in theory. For living? No. Too many stairs, too many head injuries, too many amorous cats on the roof. But for *thinking*? Give me a garret high above the city’s noise, where I can look down like a disdainful god on the ant-hill below.

The most memorable attic I ever endured was shared with a friend years ago. It defied geometry. Seven corners, walls that sloped like a drunkard’s logic, a window over the fireplace. The bed blocked the cupboard, so retrieving anything meant mountaineering over the mattress, with predictable results. Our coal lived in the cupboard’s depths. Every refill became an expedition, ending in a landslide of fuel, shovel, and curses onto the bed. We slept in a coal bunker and considered it normal.

The whole house was a joke. Doors opened outward, guaranteeing collisions. The ground floor belonged to another building entirely, so the front door dumped visitors straight down the cellar stairs. Nervous callers would vanish mid-greeting with a shriek, convinced they’d been ambushed.

I haven’t seen an attic in years. Life’s flavors haven’t changed much—whether sipped from crystal or chipped china. Happiness and heartache wear the same faces, whether you greet them on velvet or splintered wood. The only difference? In the attic, fried fish and stout were a feast. Now it takes a five-course dinner to stir the same joy.

Progress is overrated.

They say—though really, the sort of people who say such things ought to be locked up—that a well-tailored suit brings more joy to the human heart than religion ever could. And, much as it pains me to admit it, those cynical rogues might just be right. Take my own youthful folly, for instance (many, *many* moons ago, as the melodramatic novelists insist). Whenever life dealt me a particularly cruel blow—say, my washerwoman fired *me* for a change, or my latest blank-verse masterpiece was returned with the editor’s “kind regrets” (a phrase that grows less kind with each rejection), or my beloved scorned me with the fervor only a woman can muster (though really, it’s astonishing how many ways there are to be rejected—every lovelorn fool insists his suffering is unprecedented; at this rate, our great-grandchildren will have to invent entirely new methods of heartbreak just to keep things fresh)—well, in those dark hours, I had a foolproof remedy: I dressed to the nines.

A crisp new hat, trousers with a knife-edge crease (preserved through the scientific method of sandwiching them between mattress and bedstead), and suddenly, the world didn’t seem so bleak. Who cared about one fickle washerwoman? Who needed the affections of some short-sighted maiden when there were others who might appreciate a dashing, clever young fellow like myself? Clothes, you see, are half the battle in courtship. A young man will agonize for hours over whether to don his light summer suit with a jaunty cane and a dashing billycock, or to risk the solemnity of black tails, a towering top hat, and—heaven forbid—an umbrella. And no matter his choice, the universe will conspire against him. Opt for the light suit? The skies open, and he arrives at his beloved’s doorstep looking like a drowned rat. Choose the top hat? The sun blazes with infernal spite, melting his mustache wax and reducing his artfully arranged curls to something resembling overcooked spaghetti.

Ah, but how the young swains of yesteryear put us to shame! Those dashing figures from the Christmas cards—all snug breeches, Hessian boots, ruffled cuffs, and rakish hats—they didn’t *struggle* to win hearts. They simply *existed*, and maidens swooned on principle. Meanwhile, modern lovers must contend with the sartorial tragedy of baggy trousers and shapeless jackets. No wonder romance is in decline.

Clothes, you see, don’t just cover us—they *transform* us. Dress a man in rags, and he’ll slink through the streets like a guilty dog. Clothe him in finery, and he’ll strut like a peacock, swinging his cane and winking at every passing skirt. There’s a reason knights of old were so dashing in plumes and daggers—you can’t help but feel heroic in attire like that. But wrap a man in an ulster, and he’ll cower behind the nearest lamppost at the first sign of trouble.

I’ll grant you, virtue exists beneath tweed as much as silk, but the *spirit* of chivalry? That requires a bit of theater. The world, I fear, has grown dreadfully dull. We’ve passed through the ages of flowing robes and barbaric tattoos, through the flamboyant foppery of doublets and lace, and arrived at this: a society dressed like a congregation of undertakers. Black coats, black hats, black boots—good heavens, we might as well be in perpetual mourning. And the worst of it? We’ve convinced ourselves that dullness equals respectability. The holier the man, the blacker his attire; the more sinful, the lighter the suit. White hats? The mark of irredeemable degenerates. (Not that *I* would know, of course. My own headwear is a monument to restraint—five years old and counting, though I confess it briefly flirted with unfashionability before history vindicated me.)

Still, I can’t help but pine for a splash of color. Must we all resemble ants scurrying across a coal heap? Why shouldn’t we dress like peacocks? Lavender tights, emerald doublets, cloaks of sky-blue silk—imagine the joy of it! Philosophers may skulk in crow-black robes if they wish, but let the rest of us be butterflies.

And women—oh, women *must* dress brightly. They are the flowers of this dreary earth, and it’s their sacred duty to prove it. For all our grumbling about them, what would the world be without their ribbons and lace, their parasols and gloves strewn about like rainbows? Even the most drab bachelor’s quarters are transformed when a whirlwind of silk and chiffon breezes through. And summer! How it blossoms when women emerge in their pastel frocks, dotting the landscape like scattered petals. (Though I must lodge a formal complaint regarding boots. Why must every woman in Britain clomp about in footwear two sizes too large? Bootmakers of the nation, take heed: narrow twos are in desperate demand! And while we’re at it, let’s address the waistband crisis—nothing shatters domestic tranquility like a hook-and-eye detonation at close range.)

Of course, women will endure these indignities without protest, yet spend hours debating the merits of sarsenet versus cambric. Take two women on a scenic drive—the rolling hills, the distant spires—and their conversation will invariably turn to hemlines and handkerchiefs. Cast them on a desert island, and within a week, they’ll be debating whether seashells or coconuts make superior hat trimmings.

Young men, too, fuss over fashion—though they’d sooner die than admit it to one another. A bit of vanity is healthy, mind you. A lad who doesn’t preen at twenty will be a slovenly wreck by forty. Better a peacock than a sloth.

And while we’re dispensing wisdom: meekness is overrated. Uriah Heep’s father was a fool. Humility annoys people. Life needs a bit of friction—rows are half the fun! Poor Xantippe never stood a chance with that infuriatingly serene Socrates. What’s the use of a husband who won’t even argue? A sensible man knows: marriage thrives on petty squabbles. It’s the little sacrifices—letting her win a fight now and then—that make her sigh over your memory when you’re gone.

And if you *really* want marital bliss, leave your intellect at the door. No wife cares if you can recite *The Iliad* backward—can you fetch a parcel without losing it? Can you hold a baby without dangling it upside-down? *That’s* the stuff of happy unions. Brains are a hindrance in domestic life. The ideal husband is useful, not brilliant.

So dress gaily, quarrel cheerfully, and leave philosophy to the lonely. Life’s too short for solemnity—and too long for sensible shoes.

"I remember, I remember—"
Ah, but what *do* I remember? The first scrap of poetry I ever learned, that’s what—or at least the respectable part of it. None of your frivolous "Hey diddle diddle" for me; I was a child of artistic solemnity. I recited those lines with such gravity that I earned fourpence—a fortune! Though, true to form, I have no earthly recollection of what I spent it on. Memory, that fickle librarian, hands me torn pages instead of whole books.

Take the time I tumbled into a dust-hole as a boy. I recall the plunge vividly—the sudden descent, the cloud of filth, the existential dread of a child who has mistaken life for a rubbish bin. But do I remember crawling out? Not a whit. For all my memory cares, I might still be there, buried beneath the debris of my own past, philosophizing about the futility of cleanliness.

Or consider the tenderest love scene of my youth. What lingers? Not the whispered sweet nothings, not the trembling hands, not even the moonlight (if there was any). No, what Memory has preserved is the dramatic interruption: "Emily, you’re wanted," delivered in a tone so sepulchral it suggested Emily was wanted by the hangman. The rest? Lost, like a sock in the laundry of time.

And isn’t that just the way of it? The past is a ruin—a crumbled castle where only the prettiest vines remain. The broken shards of Latin verbs? Gone. The toothaches, the humiliations? Dust. What lingers are the golden hours: the scent of gingerbread, the thrill of stolen apples, the heroic delusion that we were happy. Even heartbreak softens with age. That girl who told us she could "only be a sister"? Why, we chuckle now! (Though at the time, we’d have preferred a more romantic rejection—say, joining a nunnery or emigrating to Patagonia.)

Old folk, of course, are the worst offenders. Their youth was a paradise of perpetual sunshine, winters that froze rivers solid, summers that ripened fruit with a single glance. Bulls were wrestled for sport, schoolmasters were giants, and the moon, they swear, never took a night off. It’s the same tune sung by every generation—a chorus of "Things were better before you lot ruined it." The ancient Greeks moaned about it; the Victorians wrote ballads about it; and today’s youth will, no doubt, bore their grandchildren with tales of how *their* smartphones had *character*.

But let’s not wallow. The past is a foundation, not a dwelling. Regret is a poor walking stick, and backward glances won’t steer us clear of tomorrow’s potholes. I once heard of a knight who, daunted by the road ahead, turned to see the path behind him vanish into a bottomless chasm. His only choice? Forward. A metaphor for life, if ever there was one—no refunds, no rewinds, and no use weeping over spilt milk, especially if you’ve forgotten where you left the cow.

A friend once tried to sell me a system for remembering *everything*. I declined. Some things *ought* to be forgotten—the follies, the stumbles, the mortifying moments when we mistook clubs for trumps. Memory is a gardener: let it tend the roses, but for heaven’s sake, don’t let it cultivate the weeds. Dickens’ haunted man begged for forgetfulness, then begged to remember. We need our ghosts—just not the ones that rattle chains and moan about unpaid debts.

Ah, the ghosts! They multiply as we age. Not the specters of gothic novels, but the whispers of old friends, old loves, old selves. The schoolyard echoes with laughter; the lane where we once lingered holds a shadow in a white frock; the firelight flickers with the face of the boy we were—solemn, hopeful, appallingly earnest. He stares at us, that boy, as if to say, *Is this what you meant to become?*

We shrug. We meant to be great. We meant to be good. Instead, we’re… *us*. And perhaps that’s enough.

So here’s to Memory—the keeper of lost smiles, the thief of sorrows, the architect who shows us only the prettiest parts of the ruin. Let the past be a lantern, not a shackle. And if you must look back, do it with a wink. After all, the child who fell into the dust-hole *did* climb out.

Probably.
