Two days after the harrowing scene we have already recounted, Gerard wandered listlessly through one of Rome’s filthiest, most ramshackle back streets, its cobblestones slick with stale runoff and its overhanging eaves draped with tattered laundry. A sudden, roiling thunderstorm burst overhead, great booming cracks of thunder shaking the facades of the mean little houses, and he ducked for shelter in a low-ceilinged, smoke-choked tavern that reeked of sour wine and tallow fumes. He called for a cup of rough local red, and as the rain lashed the windows hard enough to rattle their wobbly panes, he drank cup after cup until his thoughts blurred into a thick, dull haze, and he dozed off with his elbows propped on the scarred pine table, his head pillowed on his arms.

In time the room filled with more patrons, their raucous banter, clinking tankards, and shouts for more wine slowly rousing him from his stupor. That was when he caught the low murmur of two men huddled at the table directly beside him, speaking so quietly no other guest could pick out their words. One was clearly a pardoner, his tunic pinned with a cheap tin papal seal and his satchel bulging with supposed relics on his hip. The other, by his neat, modest, mended clothing, might easily have passed for an honest small tradesman—but he had slouched his broad felt hat so far down over his brows that no one could see a scrap of his face save for his coarse, unkempt beard: the classic telltale mark of a man who shuns the gaze of both upright citizens and the city watch.

The pair were haggling openly in the unholy market of pre-forgiven sin, a common transaction of the era that saw men purchase signed indulgences in advance, guaranteed to wipe clean any crime they chose to commit later, no penance, no judgment required. The man in the slouched hat grumbled loudly that the price of pardons had risen so high soon poor, working men would be locked out of heaven entirely. The pardoner flatly denied the claim. “Indulgences have never been cheaper for sensible folk who sin by the market, like reasonable creatures. If you are fool enough to pick out some crime the Pope has publicly denounced, blame your own stubbornness, not me!”

To prove that every man but the absolute dregs of society could afford some form of pre-purchased absolution, he unfurled a crumpled parchment list of official prices, reading the figures off in a low, quick murmur. It is a shocking, unflinching document that cannot be reproduced fully here, nor could any truncated version do justice to its warped priorities: any scholar may find the full text in any major archival library of the era. Suffice it to say that the pardon for murdering an ordinary layman cost a tiny fraction of the price tagged for many petty, trivial offenses most modern readers would never dream of ranking as a greater sin than taking an innocent man’s life.

The haggling wrapped up shortly after a few tiny concessions from both sides: silver changed hands, the man in the hat tucked the crumpled indulgence into the lining of his tunic, and he walked away holding guaranteed pre-forgiveness for the “removal” of one unspecified layman. Neither of them ever once spoke the word assassination, framing the whole sordid transaction as mundanely as one might haggle over a sack of grain.

All of it buzzed in Gerard’s muzzy ears, but he never lifted his head from the table, listening stupidly, too numbed by grief and wine to feel anything but faint, distant disgust. Only when the pair rose and separated to leave did he half-raise his head, glowering at the retreating back of the man who had bought his pre-pardon for murder. “If Margaret was alive,” he muttered thickly under his breath, “I’d seize you by the throat and throttle you where you stand, you cowardly stabber. But she is dead. Dead. Dead. Let the whole world rot and die. It is nothing to me, so long as I am the first to go.”

When he stumbled back to his lodgings later that night, soaked through to the bone, he barely registered the figure in the slouched hat pacing briskly back and forth on the opposite side of the road, keeping a careful eye on his doorway. His grief had hollowed him out so completely that not even a clear, lurking threat could pierce the fog of numb despair that had wrapped itself around his whole being.
