Which make so great a boast O?
Oh, they shall eat the gray-goose feather,
And we shall eat the roast O!”

Imagine the crowds flocking to Plymouth’s fine harbour last summer, chests swelling with rightful pride as they gaze out at the vast breakwater stretching across the Sound, the bustling arsenals and docks, the twin estuaries crammed full of sleek, proud ships. They watch great iron screw liners turn in their own length as if by invisible magic, or weave through crowded fleets as easily as a tiny rowing skiff. What if, by some sudden, strange twist of fate, all that nineteenth-century grandeur—all its wealth, all its scientific marvels, which may vanish as swiftly as they came one day—dissolved away, and they were thrown back 300 years, into the soft summer sun of 1588?

Mount Edgecombe still rises green and lovely along the shore, but where are the modern docks? Where is sprawling Devonport? No vast dry-dock roofs jut above the waterline. Drake’s Island carries nothing but a tiny, makeshift battery, only just built by the man who gave the isle its name. Mount Wise is nothing but a lone country gentleman’s house, set among empty fields. The citadel is a rickety pop-gun fort, which a third-class modern steamer could shell to rubble in a lazy afternoon’s amusement. And what of the shipping? The great floating fortresses that crowd the Hamoaze today have shrunk to a handful of slow, scruffy lime barges. The Catwater is packed tight, not with modern merchant craft, but with a small, ragged squadron of ships that will, in just a day’s time, fight the greatest sea battle the world has ever known.

To modern eyes, that fleet looks absurdly small. The largest vessel afloat there is no bigger than a 36-gun frigate of later eras, carries less total weight of shot than a single new-model gunboat, and can fire that shot barely a quarter of the distance modern naval guns can reach. Would our day-tripping modern visitors, who hopped on a train to watch cavalry embark and plan to head home in time for dinner tomorrow, glance down at the tiny port and its tiny fleet with a sneer, spouting flippant lines about the triumphs of modern intellect and the foolishness of benighted ancestors? No doubt most of them would—for the masses who gawk at great engineering feats as if they are nothing more than a fairground sideshow, who enjoy those comforts without gratitude or understanding the way an ox eats clover tossed in its rack, will always look down on the hard-won work of past generations. But the thinkers, the builders, the men who themselves have sweated through the setbacks, the self-denial, the slow stumbling work of turning a new idea into reality? Their smile would not be one of pity, but of filial reverence. They would see in those unassuming little wooden hulls the very seed of the mighty fleet that now patrolled the Black Sea waves. They would know that to build that fleet, to sail it, to dare all on the open ocean with the limited materials and knowledge of their day, demanded a level of intellect, energy, and inventive daring equal to anything we boast of today.

But if that visitor was a student of men, not of machines, he would find no finer company to study than the crowd gathered on the little terrace bowling green behind the Pelican Inn, on the afternoon of July 19th. Groups of captains chatted together, or leaned on the low stone wall that looked out over the Sound and the anchored fleet below. Almost every notable mariner of the Plymouth squadron was there, every last one of England’s soon-to-be-forgotten great worthies. The Spanish Armada had been scattered by a gale. Lord Howard had sailed out as far as the Spanish coast to hunt for it, but when the wind shifted south, he feared the Dons would slip past him to attack the English shore, so he had returned to Plymouth, uncertain if the great invasion fleet would appear at all. Slip on a tavern drawer’s apron, like Prince Hal haunting the Boar’s Head, step through the rose-draped back door carrying a tray of long-necked Dutch glasses and a silver tankard of spiced wine, and look around you. These gallant captains wait for the Armada as lions in their lair wait for a herd of unsuspecting deer to pass by.

See five men talking earnestly in the centre of a loose ring, where the rest of the crowd hangs back, curious to overhear but far too respectful to intrude. Those soft long eyes and pointed chin you know already: that is Walter Raleigh. The fair young man in the flame-red doublet with his arm slung around Raleigh’s neck is Lord Sheffield. Opposite them, standing beside the stately Sir Richard Grenville, is Sheffield’s uncle, Lord Charles Howard of Effingham, Lord High Admiral of England. Next to him is his son-in-law Sir Robert Southwell, captain of the *Elizabeth Jonas*. And that short, sturdy plainly dressed man, legs planted slightly apart, hands behind his back, sharp grey eyes looking up into every speaker’s face? He holds his cap in one hand, so you can see his bullet-shaped head of crisp brown hair, his lined forehead, high cheekbones, broad square face, and thick lips set as firm as granite. He looks like a coarse, common working man—yet every line of his stance breathes unshakable determination, calm self-possession, and unquenchable energy. When he finally speaks a few blunt, plain words, every eye turns to him with respect. That is Francis Drake.

A burly, grizzled old sailor in sea-stained, slightly greasy clothes that contrast wildly with the heavy gold chain slung around his neck waddles over, as if he was born and raised forever in the middle of a gale. The upper half of his sharp, stubborn face is brick-red and weathered as old leather, the lower half covered in grizzled badger-like fur. He claps Drake hard on the back, and booms out in a thick Devon twang: “Are you coming to drink your wine, Francis Drake, or are you not? Saving your presence, my lord,” he adds with a nod to the High Admiral. Howard only laughs, and waves Drake off to go drink with the old man. This is John Hawkins, port admiral of Plymouth, the patriarch of all Devon’s seamen. If Drake is their beloved hero, Hawkins is their gruff, beloved uncle who says and does exactly as he pleases no matter what company he is in. And today, with the great battle of Armageddon looming over them, he has shaken free of his usual crabby reserve, brimming over with loud, jolly good humour even towards his old rival Drake.

They push through the crowd, which is full of other men you would give much to speak to face to face. Martin Frobisher and John Davis sit on a bench smoking long silver pipes of tobacco. Nearby are Fenton and Withrington, who both tried and failed to follow Drake’s route around the world, through no fault of their own. The man raising a tankard to them, wishing them better luck next time, is George Fenner, the famed sailor known to seven different Portuguese ports, a favourite of the Earl of Leicester, and captain of the galleon Queen Elizabeth bought from him. That neat little man with the enormous yellow ruff, sharp chin, tiny pointed goatee, and self-satisfied smile is Richard Hawkins, the “Complete Seaman”, Admiral John’s son, who will one day win fame and meet a tragic fate. The older man talking to him is his good uncle William, whose monument still stands in Deptford Church, erected by Admiral John a year after this day, inscribed with words praising him as a faithful servant of the true faith, a generous friend to poor sailors, a just arbiter in the trickiest disputes, and a man of rare faith, piety, and wisdom. That, and the fact that he pulled off a very daring, dangerous operation in Porto Rico with honour, is all we know of William Hawkins—but if any of us can have half as much good said of us when we die, we will have no reason to complain.

There is John Drake, Sir Francis’s brother, ancestor of the modern Drake family line. There is his nephew George, not the brightest of men, who sailed around the world with Amyas Leigh. And there is Amyas himself, talking in low, fierce, curt tones to Captain Barker of Bristol, brother of the ill-fated Andrew Barker who recovered John Oxenham’s hidden guns, only to die at the hands of the Spaniards in Honduras twelve years before after a mutiny among his men. Barker now captains the *Victory*, one of the queen’s finest ships, and he has a long account to settle with the Dons, just as Amyas does. So the two of them growl together in a corner, while the rest of the crowd is as merry as the flies buzzing over the vine trellises above their heads.

But who is that ancient man sitting on a bench against the sun-warmed south wall of the tavern? His long white beard flows almost to his waist, his hands rest on his knees, his palsied head turns slowly back and forth to catch every scrap of talk from the passing captains. His great-granddaughter, a curly-haired little girl of six, rests her head on his knees, and his granddaughter, a buxom, dark-eyed woman of thirty, stands at his side tending him, half as nurse, half as show-woman. He is a curiosity to all the captains, and she has to beg again and again: “Please, sirs, don’t give him any strong drink, poor old soul, the doctor says he can’t have it.” This is old Martin Cockrem, father of the Pelican’s landlord, so old he outlives the usual span of man. He can still remember Plymouth’s bells ringing for the coronation of Henry VIII, and he was very likely the first Englishman ever to set foot on New World soil. There he sits, as solid and ancient as a prehistoric granite tor rising out of green wheat fields, a living relic of old Europe’s death pangs and the new world’s first birth throes. His senses are still sharp as ever, and right now he seems restless, peering out at the crowd with rheumy eyes, listening for a familiar voice.

“There he is again! Why doesn’t he come over, then?”
“Quiet, grandad, don’t pester his worship.”
“He’s been here an hour and never spoken to poor old Martin! I say, sir—” he feebly tugs Amyas’s cloak as he passes by. “Captain, go tell young master old Martin’s looking for him.”
“Mercy, grandad, where are your manners? Don’t take offence, sir, he’s half a babe at this age, and tedious as can be sometimes,” the woman says, blushing.
“Young master who?” Amyas asks, bending down to smile at the old man, gesturing for her to let him speak.
“Master Hawkins! He hasn’t come near me all day.”
Amyas hurries off, and grabs the sleeve of young Richard Hawkins to pass on the message, before the granddaughter catches his arm, laughing and blushing harder.
“No, sir, not Mr. Richard—Admiral John, his father. He always calls him young master, poor old soul,” she says, pointing at the grizzled, weather-beaten old port admiral.
Amyas goes to Hawkins and delivers the old man’s message.
“Mercy on my soul, where are my wits? Aye, aye, I’m coming!” the old hero booms in his thickest Devon accent, waddling over to the bench and digging around in his pockets. “Here, Martin, I’ve got what you want, man alive! But his lordship kept me tied up in talk so long I forgot. Look here, now! I’m getting so fat and lazy these days I can hardly reach deep into my own pockets!”
He pulls out a tangled piece of tarred string, a bundle of crumpled papers, a brass thimble, a hunk of pudding tobacco, and finally a tiny paper of Muscovado sugar—then as rare and precious a treat as any modern French bonbon—thrusting it into the old man’s eager, trembling hand.

Old Martin dips his finger in the sugar, rubbing it over his toothless gums, grinning and nodding thanks to his “young master”, while the little girl at his knee helps herself to a handful too, and no one stops her.
“There you go, Admiral Leigh! Two ends of life meeting, grandfathers and babies! You and I will be like that one day, young Samson.”
“Let’s hope we’ve slain a good many Philistines before then,” Amyas laughs.
“Amen, so be it. But look at him now—one of the finest sailors that ever drank a tot of rum, and all he cares for now is a little bit of sweet stuff. It’s pitiful, but I bring him a bit every time I come, and he waits for it. He’s blood of my blood, old Martin is. He sailed with my father Captain Will when they were both two lads barely more than boys aboard a trawler. My father sailed on to better things, and here I am. Old Martin didn’t, and here he is. We Hawkinses have climbed high. We may fall low again some day.”
“I hope never,” Amyas says.
“Hope doesn’t get the work done, young man. You go out and *do* it. I hear far too much of that soft hope talk from my own son. Let the preachers shout themselves black in the face—hard work is what counts! Faith can’t save you, and charity won’t feed you. You sit with him now, while I go play bowls with Drake. He’ll tell you a thousand stories. Ask him about good King Hal, now, just—” and he waddles off again.

“You saw good King Henry, then, father?” Amyas asks, sitting down beside him.
The old man’s eyes light up at once, he stops munching his sugar.
“Seen him? Aye, I should say I have. I was with Captain Will when he went to meet the French king at Calais, at the Field, the Field—”
“The Field of the Cloth of Gold, grandad,” his granddaughter prompts.
“That’s the one! Seen him? Aye, he was a proper king! His face shone like the rising sun, his back was as broad as my two arms spread out like this, and his voice! Oh, when he swore merry oaths, it was grand! Seen him? Aye, and I saw him do what few men have seen—seen him cry like a little child.”
“Cry? I never thought there was much crying in Henry VIII,” Amyas says, surprised.
“You can think what you like—”
“Grandad, don’t be rude now—”
“Let him go on,” Amyas says.
“I saw him cry, and oh dear, how he covered his face with his hands, and he shouted ‘Oh my gentlemen! Oh my gallant men!’ Those were his very words.”
“When was that?”
“Why, Captain Will had just sailed into Portsmouth Hard to speak to him, the royal barge laid right up against the quay, and our little boat tied right up next to it. The king stood right there above my head on the quay edge, and the *Mary Rose* came gliding right up abreast of us, looking as grand as could be, poor dear, and then she started to heel over. Captain Will said, ‘Those lower gun ports are far too close to the water,’ they barely had sixteen inches of clearance from the waves. And before the words were even out of his mouth, a sharp little flaw of wind blew from the west, my cap flew off my head and hit the stone wall. When I stooped to pick it up, I heard a scream, and it was all over.”
“He’s telling the story of the *Mary Rose* sinking, sir,” the granddaughter explains.
“I guessed as much.”
“All over. The screaming! The yelling went right up to the heavens! And the king screamed out like a little maid, ‘Oh my gentlemen! Oh my gallant men!’ And as she lay on her side, just sinking under the waves, the last two faces I saw bobbing above the water were those two men’s fathers. I recognised their armour! That Sir George Carew and that Sir Richard Grenville standing over there. Aye! Drowned like rats, every last one of them.”
“Now, you mustn’t pester his worship any more,” the granddaughter says.
“Pester? Let him tell stories till midnight, I’ll be only too happy,” Amyas says, settling in on the bench beside him. “Drawer! Bring ale, and a parcel of tobacco.”
Amyas leans back to listen, while the old man purrs happily to himself: “Aye, they all like to hear old Martin’s stories. All the great captains look up to old Martin.”

“Hillo, Amyas!” Cary calls, wandering over. “Who’s your friend here? There’s a man over there telling me wonders about the River Plate. We ought to sail there for our next cruise, make our fortune.”
“The River Plate?” old Martin says. “I know the River Plate better than any man alive. Who’d ever heard of it, let alone been there, before Captain Will and I sailed there? I lived a whole year among the natives, and they were as civil as could be, if only they’d had a shirt to their backs. The prince of their tribe came home with Captain Will to King Henry, though he died on the voyage. The natives took it very well, for we were always as kind to them as proper Christians ought to be. If we hadn’t been, I never would have lived to tell the tale.”
“What year was that?”
“1530. But I was there even before that, learned their language, that’s why Captain Will left me behind as a hostage when he took their prince aboard his ship.”
“Before that?” Cary says, astonished. “Hardly anyone knew that country existed back then.”
The old man’s eyes flash with pride.
“Knew it? Aye, you can say that! Look around you at all these captains! I’m the father of all of them now that poor Captain Will’s gone to heaven, me, Martin Cockrem! Aye, I’ve seen such changes.
