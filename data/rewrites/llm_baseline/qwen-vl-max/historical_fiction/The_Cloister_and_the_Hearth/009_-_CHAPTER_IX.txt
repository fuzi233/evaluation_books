Margaret sliced a large chunk of venison and dashed to the window, hurling it out into the darkness. Instantly, green eyes flared to life in the shadows—feral, hungry—and lunged forward with a snarl. The sound of tearing flesh and crunching bone echoed through the night. Then, from nearby, a hound bayed so loudly it shook the very walls of the house. The three at the window recoiled as one.

The leopard, startled by the noise, darted away with her prize, slipping silently toward the woods. No sooner had she vanished than horses thundered past the window, men shouting, dogs barking—chasing her full cry into the forest. Martin and his companions exchanged relieved glances. The beast was swift; they knew she would not be caught within miles of their home. They clasped hands in silent triumph.

Margaret seized the moment, her voice breaking as she whispered a small cry. Gerard leaned close and kissed the tears from her cheeks.

Back to the table they went. Gerard raised his cup in toast. “Woman’s wit,” he declared, “is stronger than man’s strength.”
“Aye,” replied Margaret, “when those she loves are in danger. But not otherwise.”

That evening, Gerard lingered longer than usual beside her. He left for home later than ever before, his heart swelling with pride and joy. Yet just beyond the edge of the trees, under the cover of shadow, two figures blocked his path.

It was his father and mother.

So late? What could bring them here? A cold dread settled over him. He stopped, staring at them. They stood rigid, silent, like statues carved from stone.

He stammered out a question.
“Why ask?” said his father. “You know why we’re here.”

“Oh, Gerard!” murmured his mother, her voice thick with sorrow and love.

Gerard’s heart sank. He could not speak.

Then, seeing his distress, his father softened. “Do not hang your head. You are not the first young fool to fall for a red cheek and blue eyes.”

“Nay, nay!” cried Catherine. “It was witchcraft! Peter the Magician is well known for such tricks.”

“Come now, Sir Priest,” resumed the father, “you must not meddle with womenfolk. But give us your word—you will go no more to Sevenbergen—and this ends. We won’t press you too hard for one fault.”

“I cannot promise that, father.”

“Not promise? You young hypocrite!”

“Nay, father, do not misjudge me. I lacked courage to tell you what I knew would wound you. And I am grateful to whoever warned you. It lifts a weight from my soul. Yes, father—I love Margaret. Call me not a priest, for I shall never be one. I would die first.”

“That we shall see, young man,” said his father, stiffening. “Come, do not gainsay me. You’ll learn what it means to defy your father.”

Gerard said nothing. The three walked home in silence, broken only by a few deep sighs from Catherine.

From that hour, peace fled the little house at Tergou.

The next day, Gerard was summoned before the whole family. Every voice rose against him—except little Kate’s and the dwarf’s, who seemed to follow her instinctively, though he did not know why. Cornelis and Sybrandt were especially harsh, bitterer even than their father.

Gerard, overwhelmed by so many enemies, looked pleadingly at his sister. Her eyes glistened with unshed tears at the cruel words once aimed at the boy who had been everyone’s favorite. But she turned away. “Dear, dear Gerard,” she whispered, “pray to Heaven to cure you of this folly.”

“What?” he asked, broken-hearted. “Are you against me too?”

And with a heavy sigh, he rose and left the house, walking straight to Sevenbergen.

At first, disputes born of love and duty are often innocent—both sides may be right at the start. That is when a calm, wise friend becomes a gift from heaven. But the longer the rift endures, the deeper it grows, fed by human error and temper. Neither side escapes blame. In time, both become wrong.

In this quarrel, Elias was angry, Cornelis and Sybrandt spiteful. But Gerard, with a broader mind, saw both sides where others saw only one. He struggled with doubt, not rage. He was unhappy, not furious. And he was alone—no one to confide in.

Margaret Van Eyck had once comforted him in hardship, but now he dared not share his pain with her. She was strong-willed, proud. If he told her how his family tormented him, she might choose loyalty over love—and leave him. And yet, her own story was well known. As a young woman, she had refused many suitors, choosing art instead—knowing how marriage and motherhood would drain a painter’s spirit. How could he confess that he rejected the church and the benefice she had secured for him—because he loved something she once sacrificed?

At this point, Gerard began to falter.

But on the other side stood Catherine, the elder. Though kind-hearted, she was untrained in patience or strategy. She could neither act quietly nor plan wisely. Yet she served both sides: sometimes she provoked Gerard, which stirred his resolve; at other times, she turned on her own kin, creating chaos.

One afternoon, during a gathering, Cornelis sneered, “Our Gerard marrying Margaret Brandt? Why, it’s hunger marrying thirst.”

“And what about you?” shot back Catherine. “Gerard can paint, write—what can *you* do to support a wife, lazy loon? Nothing but wait for your father’s shoes. Oh, I see why you and Sybrandt oppose this match. You fear he’ll come begging for our fortune. Even if he does, and we give it, it’s not yours to lose. Perhaps it never will be.”

Gerard smiled faintly, and found new strength. His rivals, momentarily stunned, fell silent.

But after more than half a year of tension, the crisis came.

His father called him before the family and announced: “I have ordered the burgomaster to imprison you in the Stadthouse rather than let you marry Margaret.”

Gerard paled—but held his tongue.

His father continued: “And before the year ends, you shall be a priest—whether you like it or not.”

“Is that so?” Gerard burst out. “Then hear me all. By God and St. Bavon, I swear I will never be a priest while Margaret lives. If force must decide this, then let it be force. Father—try your strength. But know this: the day the burgomaster comes for me, I leave Tergou—forever. I leave Holland, I leave your house, where it seems I’ve been valued not for myself, but for what I can yield.”

With that, he stormed from the room, white with fury and despair.

“There!” exclaimed Catherine. “That’s what happens when you push youth too hard. Men are crueler than tigers—even to their own blood. May Heaven forbid he should ever leave us, married or single.”

As Gerard stepped outside, pale and breathless, he met Reicht Heynes. She bore a message: Margaret Van Eyck wished to see him.

He found the old lady seated like a judge, stern and still.

She wasted no time. “Why haven longer visited me?”

Before he could answer, she added, sharply, “I thought we were friends, young sir.”

Gerard looked stricken.

“It is because you never told her you loved her,” said Reicht softly, pitying his confusion.

“Silence, wench!” snapped the old woman. “Why should he tell us his affairs? We are not his friends. We have not earned his trust.”

“Oh, madam,” said Gerard, trembling, “I dared not tell you my folly.”

“What folly? Is it folly to love?”

“I am told so every day.”

“You need not have feared telling my mistress. She is always kind to true lovers.”

“Madam—Reicht—I was afraid because I heard…”

“Well, you were told—?”

“That in your youth you scorned love, chose art above all.”

“I did, boy. And what has become of it? Look at me—a barren tree, while the women of my youth stand with children at their feet and grandchildren beside them. I gave up the joys of wifehood and motherhood—for my brothers. They are gone. For my art. It nearly left me too. I still have the knowledge—but what good is it when the hand trembles? No, Gerard. I see you as my son. You are good, handsome, a painter—though not like some I’ve known. I will not let you waste your youth as I did mine. You shall marry Margaret. I have inquired—she is a good daughter. Reicht has told me all. But you may tell me freely.”

Poor Gerard was overjoyed. At last, someone allowed him to speak of Margaret openly—to one who understood the depth of his love.

Soon, two pairs of eyes were wet with tears. When Gerard saw that, a third joined them.

Women possess a courage unlike any man’s. Not the same courage, no—nor should it be. We would lose our dominance if it were. Theirs is a vicarious courage. They never enter a bullfight—but watch it without flinching, while men quake with anxiety. They send men to war with iron hearts. As a witty saying goes: “Les femmes sont très braves avec la peur d’autrui.”

This trait now helped Gerard.

Margaret and Reicht agreed: a man must take the bull by the horns. He must marry Margaret at once. The elders would relent in time. Delay only worsened matters—for all, especially him.

“See how pale and thin they’ve made him,” said Reicht.

“Indeed, Master Gerard,” she added. “It makes one sad to see a young man so worn. When I met him today, I almost wept. He’s changed so much.”

“And I’ll wager the others keep their color—ah, Reicht? Such as it is.”

“Oh, I see no difference.”

“Of course not. We painters are glass—they are stone. We cannot endure the constant worry of small minds. It’s not good for mankind that we should be exposed to it. To create a masterpiece is hard enough—without gnats and flies stinging us to death.”

Though furious at his father’s threats, Gerard listened to these voices urging rebellion. But he remained unconvinced.

“I do not fear my father’s violence,” he said. “But I fear his anger. When it comes to the point, he wouldn’t imprison me. I’d marry Margaret tomorrow if that were my only fear. But he would disown me. I’d take her from her father, and give her a poor husband—burdened by his parents’ curse. Madam—I sometimes think, if I could marry her secretly, and flee to a land where artists are better paid… After a year or two, when the storm passed, I could return with money and say, ‘We don’t seek your wealth—we only ask to be loved again, as we’ve always loved you.’ But alas—I’m told this is the dream of an inexperienced youth.”

The old lady’s eyes sparkled. “It is not a dream—it is common sense. The question is, do you have the courage to act on it?”

“There is a land, Gerard,” she continued, “where fortune awaits you now. Here, art freezes. There, it flourishes—more than in any age or land.”

“Italy!” Gerard breathed.

“Italy!” she confirmed. “Where painters are honored as princes, and scribes earn three hundred crowns for copying a single manuscript. Did you know the Pope himself writes to every kingdom, seeking skilled hands to copy the priceless manuscripts pouring in from Constantinople—where learning flees the Turkish invaders?”

“I did not know,” said Gerard. “But Italy—the queen of arts—has been my dream since childhood. Oh, madam! But the journey… we are so poor.”

“If you have the heart to go,” she said, “I will find the means. I know where to lay my hands on ten golden angels. They will take you to Rome—and the girl with you, if she truly loves you.”

They talked until midnight.

After that day, Gerard regained his strength. He carried a secret talisman—invincible against the insults and scolding that flew around him at home.

Besides the money, Margaret Van Eyck gave him wisdom.

“Listen,” she said, “I will teach you secrets learned from masters now gone—secrets the Italians themselves do not know. What I tell you here, you shall sell in Florence.”

She pointed to her brother Jan’s paintings. “Time fades most works—but his colors remain bright as the day they left the brush. Why? Because he worked slowly, deliberately. He ground his own pigments. He prepared his panels—again and again—sometimes a year before painting. Most painters rush. Bad painters are always in a hurry.”

Above all, she warned: “Use little oil. Never boil it. Boiling melts impurities into its core—impure oil kills color. Pour your oil into a bottle with water. In a day or two, the water turns muddy—that’s muck from the oil. Carefully pour it off, add fresh water. Repeat. When you think it’s clear—you’re mistaken.”

She called Reicht. “Bring me that.”

Reicht returned with a glass trough, sealed tightly.

“Now,” said Margaret, “after washing in the bottle, put the oil into this trough with water. Place it in the sun. You’ll soon see the water cloud again. But beware—don’t overdo it. The sun may turn your oil to varnish. When it’s as clear as crystal, not too rich, drain it carefully, cork it tight.”

“Grind your own pigments,” she insisted. “Use this oil, and your colors shall live.”

Hubert used sand or salt to speed clarity. But Jan said, “Water does it best—give water time.”

Jan Van Eyck was never in a hurry. And so the world will not forget him in a hurry.

She gave him other secrets—too many to list now. Gerard received them like a divine inheritance. Some things, though dull on paper, shine when taught by passion.

Thus armed with gold and knowledge, Gerard resolved: he would marry Margaret and flee to Italy.

Only one thing remained: to tell Margaret, and quietly publish the banns.

He arrived in Sevenbergen earlier than usual.

First, he spoke to Margaret. He told her of Dame Van Eyck’s kindness, of his decision, and asked for her help.

She refused outright.

“No, Gerard,” she said. “We’ve never spoken of your family. But marriage—”

She paused. Then: “I believe your father bears me no ill will—only the same as another. Peter Buyskens told me so. But as long as he insists you become a priest—well, I could not marry you, Gerard, however much I love you.”

He pleaded in vain. He could make her cry—but not change her mind.

Then Gerard grew impatient, unjust.

“Very well!” he shouted. “Then you are on their side. You’ll drive me to become a priest. This must end one way or another. My parents hate me in truth—but my lover loves me only in jest!”

And with that bitter cry, he stormed home, leaving Margaret weeping.

When a man misbehaves, the effect on a loving woman is strange: she feels pity.

To many men, this seems illogical. But the fault lies in our understanding. The logic is too swift for us.

She thinks: “How unhappy, how distressed, how poor he must be to act so! Poor thing.”

Margaret was filled with this tender pity—when, to her astonishment, less than an hour later, Gerard returned, running, his face twisted with grief and rage, clutching torn pieces of a painting.

“There, Margaret!” he gasped. “Look! See! The villains! They’ve cut your portrait to shreds!”

She stared. Indeed, some cruel hand had slashed the painting into five jagged pieces.

She was gentle—but not cold. Her face flushed crimson.

“Who did it?”

“I don’t know. I couldn’t ask. I’d hate the hand that did it—forever.”

“My poor Margaret! Butchers! Ruffians! Six months’ work—destroyed. Nothing left. Look—they’ve hacked through your face—the sweet face everyone loves.”

“Oh, heartless, merciless vipers!”

“Never mind, Gerard,” she said, panting. “Since this is how they treat you for my sake—Ye rob him of my portrait, do ye? Well then—he shall have the face itself, such as it is.”

“Oh, Margaret!”

“Yes, Gerard. Since they are so cruel, I will be kinder. Forgive me for refusing you. I will be your wife—tomorrow, if you wish.”

Gerard kissed her hands, then her lips. Overwhelmed with joy, he raced to fetch Peter and Martin.

They came, and witnessed the betrothal—a solemn rite in those days, lasting more than a century before being abolished.
