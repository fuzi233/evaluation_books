I veered off the main thoroughfare into a shadowed alley, ducking for shelter beneath a weathered archway hewn from Bulika’s signature pearl-stone. I halted just inside its mouth, gaze drifting out over the cobblestones glowing under the silver spill of the realm’s twin moonlights, every crack glinting with flecks of uncut star-gem.

The very next instant, a woman glided in after me, indigo silk cloak pulled tight over her shoulders. She spun to press her back to the arch’s inner wall, trembling so hard the silver thread stitched into her cuffs winked erratically, and stared back out into the alley behind us. Barely three heartbeats later, a massive xenoleopard darted across the mouth of the arch, its pelt the color of bleached bone, dappled with void-black spots that seemed to shift and ripple like ink in dark water.

The woman pressed herself flush to my side, and a sharp, warm pity swelled in my chest. I wrapped one arm around her shaking form.
“If the brute tries to come in here,” I said, “I will grapple it, and you run for whatever safe place you know.”
“Thank you,” she murmured, her voice thin as gem-dust silk.
“Have you seen it before?” I asked.
“Many times,” she answered, still trembling hard enough that her teeth chattered. “She is the princess’s personal pet. You are a stranger, or you would already know of her!”
“I am a stranger,” I confirmed. “But are the authorities so careless they let a beast like that run loose through the city?”
“She is kept locked in an iron cage, her jaws strapped in a heavy leather muzzle, her paws sheathed in thick gloves stitched from crocodile hide. She is chained fast, too—but she slips free constantly, and sucks the blood from any unguarded child she can catch. There are barely any mothers left in all Bulika, now.”

She burst into ragged sobs, hot tears tracking down her pale cheeks. “I wish I was safe inside my own home! The princess returned only last night, and the leopardess is already loose! How am I supposed to make it to my door? I know she’s hunting me—she’ll be curled right on my threshold, waiting for me!—I am a fool, rambling all this to a total stranger.”
“Not all strangers are cruel,” I told her. “The beast will have to tear through me first before it lays a claw on you, and by the time it is done with me, you will be safely inside your home. You are luckier than many, to have a house to return to—what a gale that is kicking up out there!”
“Escort me home safe, and I will give you shelter from the wind,” she offered. “But we must wait a little longer, to be sure the leopard has moved off.”

As we waited, I asked her question after question about her city. She told me the people of Bulika did nothing at all, save dig for precious stones in the vaults carved beneath their basements. They were unfathomably wealthy, and every single good they owned was crafted for them in other, distant towns.
“Why is that?” I asked.
“Because it is a disgrace to work,” she replied, like the answer was so obvious it did not need stating. “Every single person in Bulika knows that!”
I pressed her for how they maintained such vast wealth if no one in the city ever earned a single coin. She explained their ancestors had hoarded a massive stockpile of gems generations ago, and they never touched the core of that hoard. Whenever they needed currency, they just sold a small handful of uncut stones.
“But there must be people who fall into poverty here,” I said.
“I suppose that must be true,” she said, frowning like she had never considered the idea before. “But we never think of people like that. When a citizen falls poor, we erase them from all record, we forget they ever existed. That is how we keep our hoard intact. We intend to stay rich forever.”
“But one day, when you have dug up every last precious stone in the ground and sold them all,” I pointed out, “you will have to start spending your stored coin, and eventually you will have nothing left.”
“We have so many gems already hoarded, and so many more still buried in the bedrock, that day will never come,” she said, dismissive.
“Suppose a foreign army invaded your lands, and seized every last thing you own?”
“No foreign people would ever dare,” she laughed. “They are all horribly afraid of our princess. She is the one who keeps us safe, free, and infinitely rich!”

Every few sentences as she spoke, she would pause to glance over her shoulder, scanning the dark for the leopard. I asked her why her people held such violent hatred for strangers. She answered that the very presence of an outsider defiled the sacred purity of their city.
“How does that work?” I asked.
“Because we are more ancient and more noble than any other nation on the continent,” she said, chin lifting. “Which is why,” she added, “we have a law that all strangers must be expelled from the city before night falls.”
“How, then, do you plan to take me into your house?” I asked.
“I will make an exception for you,” she replied simply.
“Is there not a single place in the city built to take in travelers?”
“Any such place would be torn down stone by stone, and its owner burned alive at the moon temple,” she said. “How are we supposed to preserve our purity if we do not keep low, unblooded people at a proper distance? Dignity is such an incredibly delicate thing!”

She went on to tell me their princess had reigned for thousands of years, that she wielded power over the air and the water and the earth itself, and she was certain over fire as well. The princess could do anything she pleased, and answered to no one at all.
When at last she worked up the courage to risk the walk, we set off winding through narrow lanes and cramped, shadowed passages, and did not meet a single living creature on our way to her door. It sat on a wider, quieter street, wedged between two towering gem-facaded spires, at the top of a narrow, steep flight of stone stairs. She climbed slowly, and I followed right behind her.
Before we reached the top landing, however, she seemed to be seized by a sudden, overwhelming terror. She darted up the rest of the steps at a run, and I only made it to the top in time for her to slam the door hard in my face, the heavy iron deadbolt sliding home with a sharp, final click. I stood there stunned on the tiny landing, which was barely long enough between the opposing doors of the two spires for a full grown man to stretch out and lie down.
Weary to the bone, and not caring in the slightest that I was defiling Bulika’s precious purity with my presence, I settled into the meager shelter it offered to wait out the rest of the dark.
