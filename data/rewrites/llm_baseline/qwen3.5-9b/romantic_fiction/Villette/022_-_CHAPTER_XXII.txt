The house had finally fallen into silence. Dinner was done, the boisterous hours of play had ended, and the darkness of evening had settled over everything. In the refectory, the solitary lamp of study burned low, while the externs had returned home, leaving behind the clash of doors and the clamor of bells. Madame Beck was comfortably seated in the dining hall with her mother and a circle of friends. Seizing this precise moment, I slipped toward the kitchen, begging a single candle for a brief, private purpose. My request was granted instantly by my friend Goton, who cheerfully offered, "Certainly, my dear, you shall have two if you wish." Holding my borrowed light, I climbed silently to the dormitory.

My spirits sank immediately upon entering the room. One of the pupils lay in bed, indisposed. Worse still, amidst the ruffled edges of muslin nightcaps, I recognized the familiar, crumpled form of Mistress Ginevra Fanshawe. Though she appeared supine, I knew she would soon awaken and bombard me with chatter at the worst possible moment. As I watched her, a subtle twitch of her eyelids confirmed my suspicion: her feigned sleep was merely a disguise for sly vigilance regarding "Timon's" movements. She was impossible to trust. All I desired was solitude, a quiet space to read my precious letter. Compelled by duty, I resolved to return to the classes.

Ill luck, however, pursued me. The classrooms were undergoing their weekly cleaning by candlelight; benches were stacked atop desks, the air thick with dust, and damp coffee grounds—used by the local maids instead of tea leaves—darkened the floor. Chaos reigned. Defeated but not discouraged, I retreated, determined to find *any* corner of solitude. Knowing the location of a specific key, I ascended three flights of stairs, reached a dark, narrow, and utterly silent landing, unlocked a rotting door, and plunged into the deep, black, freezing garret.

Here, no one would follow; no one would interrupt—not even Madame herself. I closed the door firmly, placed my candle on a wobbly, mold-covered chest of drawers, wrapped a shawl around my shoulders against the biting chill, and retrieved my letter. Trembling with eager anticipation, I broke the seal.

*"Will it be long? Will it be short?"* I wondered, rubbing my eyes to clear the silver haze of a gentle rain. It was long. *"Will it be cool? Will it be kind?"* It was kind. To my restrained, disciplined expectations, it seemed exceedingly kind; to my starving, longing thoughts, it appeared perhaps even kinder than it truly was. I had hoped so little and feared so much that this small taste of fulfillment brought a completeness of joy rare among humans. The poor English teacher, huddled in a frost-bitten garret by a flickering candle in the winter air, reading a letter that was merely good-natured—nothing more—felt, in that instant, happier than most queens in their palaces. Of course, such happiness born of shallow origins could not last forever; yet, while it endured, it was genuine and exquisite: a fleeting bubble, but a sweet one filled with real honey-dew.

Dr. John had written at length. He had written with pleasure, in a benevolent mood, dwelling with sunny satisfaction on scenes witnessed by his own eyes and mine, on places we had shared, on our conversations, and on the trivial details of those final, peaceful weeks. Yet, the core of my delight was the conviction conveyed by his blithe, genial language: that these words were poured out not merely to please *me*, but to gratify *himself*. A gratification he might never again desire or seek—a certainty for the future. But for this present moment, there was no pain, no flaw, no lack. Full, pure, and perfect, it deeply blessed me. A passing seraph seemed to rest beside me, leaning toward my heart and resting a softening, cooling, healing wing upon its beat.

Dr. John, you pained me later; forgive every ill freely, for the sake of that one cherished memory of good. Are there wicked forces, non-human, that envy human bliss? Do evil influences haunt the air, poisoning it for mankind? Suddenly, something in that vast, lonely garret sounded strangely. Surely, I heard a stealthy footstep on the floor, a gliding movement emerging from the shadowed recess haunted by the cloaks of the malefactors. I turned. My light was dim; the room was long. But as I lived to tell! In the center of that ghostly chamber stood a figure, starkly black and white: skirts straight and narrow, black; head bandaged and veiled, white. Say what you will, reader—call me nervous or mad; claim I was unsettled by the letter's excitement; assert I dreamt. I swear upon my life: I saw there, in that room, on that night, an image unmistakably like a **NUN**.

I screamed aloud and fell ill. Had the shape approached, I might have fainted. Instead, it receded. I fled for the door. How I descended the stairs, I do not know. Instinct drove me to avoid the refectory; I headed straight for Madame's sitting-room and burst inside.

"There is something in the attic!" I cried. "I have been there; I saw something. Go and look at it, all of you!" I addressed "all of you," for the room seemed crowded, though only four were present: Madame Beck, her mother Madame Kint (who was frail and visiting), her brother Monsieur Victor Kint, and another gentleman who was speaking to the old lady with his back to the door. My terror and faintness must have drained the color from my face entirely. I felt cold and shaking. They all rose in alarm and surrounded me. Urging them to inspect the attic, the presence of the gentlemen steadied me; it seemed as if help and hope were at hand. Turning to the door, I beckoned them to follow. They hesitated, wanting to stop me, but insisted they must come this way to witness whatever strange thing I had seen standing in the middle of the garret.

Then, remembering my letter left on the drawers with the candle, a fresh panic seized me. That precious letter! Flesh or spirit must be defied for its sake. I raced upstairs, accelerating as I knew I was being followed; they were forced to come.

Lo! Upon reaching the garret door, everything inside was dark as a pit; the light was extinguished. Fortunately, someone—likely Madame, with her usual calm judgment—had fetched a lamp from below. Quickly, as they arrived, a ray of light pierced the opaque blackness. There lay the extinguished candle on the drawers, but where was the letter? Now I searched for *that*, forgetting the nun.

"My letter! My letter!" I gasped, pleading, nearly beside myself. I groped on the floor, wringing my hands wildly. Cruel, cruel fate! To have my sole comfort supernaturally snatched away before I had fully tasted its sweetness! I knew not what the others were doing; I could not watch them. They asked questions I could not answer; they rummaged through every corner, muttering about disordered cloaks, a breach in the skylight—I knew not what.

"Some object or person has been here," they declared sagely.

"Oh! They've taken my letter!" I shrieked, crawling and groping in a monomaniacal frenzy.

"What letter, Lucy? My dear girl, what letter?" a familiar voice whispered close to my ear.

Could I believe that ear? No. I looked up. Could I trust my eyes? Had I recognized the tone? Was the face of the letter's writer staring back at me in this dim garret? Was this gentleman near me tonight, John Graham—Dr. Bretton himself? Yes, it was him. He had been summoned that very evening to treat an ailment afflicting old Madame Kint; he was the second gentleman present in the dining hall when I entered.

"Was it *your* letter, Lucy?"

"Yours: your own—the letter you wrote to me. I came here to read it quietly. I could find no other spot where I could have it to myself. I saved it all day, never opening it until this evening; I barely glanced over it. I *cannot bear* to lose it. Oh, my letter!"

"Hush! Don't cry and distress yourself so cruelly. What is it worth? Hush! Let us leave this cold room; they are sending for the police to investigate further. We need not stay—come, we will go down."

A warm hand grasping my cold fingers led me down to a room warmed by a fire. Dr. John and I sat before the stove. He spoke to me, soothing me with boundless kindness, promising twenty letters for the one lost. If there are words and wrongs akin to knives, inflicting deep, unhealing lacerations—cutting injuries and insults dripping with poison—then there exist also consolations of tone too fine for the ear to forget, echoing fondly through a lifetime. Caressing kindnesses, loved and lingered over, recalled with unfading tenderness, answering the call with undimmed brightness, rising out of the raven clouds that forebode death itself.
