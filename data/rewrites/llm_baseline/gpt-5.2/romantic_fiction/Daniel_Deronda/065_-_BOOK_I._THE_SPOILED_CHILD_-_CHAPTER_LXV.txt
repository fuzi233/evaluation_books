Deronda did not answer Gwendolen’s new summons without a stir of dread that was not vanity, but a fine, quick sympathy that shrank from being made the measure of another creature’s hope. He had learned—by that kind of knowledge which does not argue but simply feels, as one feels anger in a voice—that her inward life had fastened on his with a passionate need. It was as if her future hung, not in the general order of things, but on the steady holding of his hand at the brink of a precipice.

And yet there had come, since the first time he had saved her from that fatal glitter of the necklace, other bonds—love and duty twined elsewhere—binding his own life into shapes that could not be altered by any compassionate impulse, however strong. The impulse was still in him, living and tender: that old determining rush which, a year earlier, might have swept him into sheltering her forever from solitude. Now it could only quiver in him as yearning pity; and the certainty that he must keep himself asunder made the pity ache the more.

He waited in the back drawing-room—part of that white-and-crimson space whose very air seemed still to hold the memory of music, of a plaintive phrase once sung there, _Per pietà non dirmi addio_, that had melted into his consciousness with Mirah’s voice. The room was familiar to him in every line and ornament—Lady Mallinger’s gently smiling portrait, the lions on the pilasters with their urbane, almost human faces—yet everything looked like furniture of a former existence he had returned to visit, not to inhabit. It was strange to stand there with his hat in his hand, listening for the step of a young creature whose own life had been altered by tragedy into something wavering and uncertain, in which he still felt his own action tangled.

Gwendolen entered at last—changed. The mourning dress made the change more visible, but there was more than that: a quietness, a kind of satisfied hush on her face that he had not seen at Genoa. It was the satisfaction of finding him there. They clasped hands without a smile; remembrance and foreboding filled both their minds like a weight.

“It was good of you to come,” she said quickly. “Let us sit down.” She took the nearest chair at once, as if standing would make her courage evaporate. Deronda sat opposite her.

“I asked you to come because I want you to tell me what I ought to do,” she began, hastening on as if her words would fail her if she lingered. “Don’t be afraid of telling me what you think is right, because it seems hard. I have made up my mind to do it. I was afraid once of being poor; I could not bear to think of being under other people; and that was why I did something—why I married. I have borne worse things now. I think I could bear to be poor, if you think I ought. Do you know about my husband’s will?”

“Yes. Sir Hugo told me,” Deronda answered, already discerning the question behind her question.

“Ought I to take anything he has left me? I will tell you what I have been thinking.” Her eagerness trembled, as if she were trying to keep her thought from slipping back into terror. “Perhaps you may not quite know that I really did think a good deal about my mother when I married. I was selfish—but I did love her, and feel about her poverty; and what comforted me most at first, when I was miserable, was her being better off because I had married. The thing that would be hardest to me now would be to see her in poverty again; and I have been thinking that if I took enough to provide for her, and no more—nothing for myself—it would not be wrong; for I was very precious to my mother—and he took me from her—and he meant—and if she had known—”

She broke off. The reasons that crowded behind her words were like a swarm of stinging recollections she dared not let fly into speech. Her gaze dropped helplessly to her hands—bare now of all rings except the wedding-ring, that small circle seeming to tighten like a band of iron.

“Do not hurt yourself by speaking of that,” Deronda said, with a tenderness that was almost a command to spare herself. “There is no need. The case is very simple. I think I can hardly judge wrongly about it. You consult me because I am the only person to whom you have confided the most painful part of your experience: and I can understand your scruples.”

He waited, giving her silence as one gives a recovering patient air. That silence—made gentle by the tone in which he had spoken—was itself like a sustaining hand. She lifted her eyes at last, and he went on, slowly, with the careful pity of one who must be truthful.

“You are conscious of something you feel to be a crime toward one who is dead. You think that you have forfeited all claim as a wife. You shrink from taking what was his. You want to keep yourself from profiting by his death. Your feeling even urges you to some self-punishment—some scourging of the self that disobeyed your better will—the will that struggled against temptation. I have known something of that myself. Do I understand you?”

“Yes—at least, I want to be good—not like what I have been,” she said, low and urgent. “I will try to bear what you think I ought to bear. I have tried to tell you the worst about myself. What ought I to do?”

“If no one but yourself were concerned in this question of income,” said Deronda, “I should hardly dare to urge you against any remorseful prompting. But I take as a guide now, your feeling about Mrs. Davilow, which seems to me quite just. I cannot think that your husband’s dues even to yourself are nullified by any act you have committed. He voluntarily entered into your life, and affected its course in what is always the most momentous way. But setting that aside, it was due from him in his position that he should provide for your mother, and he of course understood that if this will took effect she would share the provision he had made for you.”

“She has had eight hundred a year,” Gwendolen said, clinging still to the limit she had privately argued for. “What I thought of was to take that and leave the rest.”

“I think it is not your duty to fix a limit in that way,” Deronda answered. “You would be making a painful enigma for Mrs. Davilow; an income from which you shut yourself out must be embittered to her. And your own course would become too difficult. We agreed at Genoa that the burden on your conscience is one what no one ought to be admitted to the knowledge of. The future beneficence of your life will be best furthered by your saving all others from the pain of that knowledge. In my opinion you ought simply to abide by the provisions of your husband’s will, and let your remorse tell only on the use that you will make of your monetary independence.”

As he finished, his hand moved almost unconsciously to take up his hat from the floor beside him; the action was slight, but Gwendolen’s whole being watched him as if her safety depended on his smallest motion. Her heart leapt with a sudden animal terror of being left. She started up too—without pausing to see that her rising looked like acquiescence in his departure; and Deronda, of course, rose also and came a little nearer.

“I will do what you tell me,” she said hurriedly; “but what else shall I do?”

The simplicity of the words betrayed her more than any confession could have done. They painted her helplessness to herself, and the sob she had been mastering broke through, and large tears gathered.

Deronda felt a crushing pain—pain that had to be governed, because consequences stood close and visible, and he must not, by softness, make the future harder. When she had wiped her tears away, he asked gently, “You will probably be soon going with Mrs. Davilow into the country?”

“Yes—in a week or ten days.” Her eyes moved vaguely toward the window as if she were trying to look into a future that had become indistinct. “I want to be kind to them all—they can be happier than I can. Is that the best I can do?”

“I think so. It is a duty that cannot be doubtful,” he said. He spoke with pauses, as if each sentence must carry its own weight and not topple into sentiment. “Other duties will spring from it. Looking at your life as a debt may seem the dreariest view of things at a distance; but it cannot really be so. What makes life dreary is the want of motive: but once beginning to act with that penitential, loving purpose you have in your mind, there will be unexpected satisfactions—there will be newly-opening needs—continually coming to carry you on from day to day. You will find your life growing like a plant.”

She turned her eyes on him as if she were listening for water in a drought—an intense, thirsty look that made him feel as though she were stretching her arms from a forsaken shore. His voice took an affectionate, almost imploring earnestness.

“This sorrow, which has cut down to the root, has come to you while you are so young—try to think of it not as a spoiling of your life, but as a preparation for it. Let it be a preparation. See—you have been saved from the worst evils that might have come from your marriage, which you feel was wrong. You have had a vision of injurious, selfish action—a vision of possible degradation; think that a severe angel, seeing you along the road of error, grasped you by the wrist and showed you the horror of the life you must avoid. And it has come to you in your spring-time. Think of it as a preparation. You can—you will—be among the best of women, such as make others glad that they were born.”

The words fell on Gwendolen as if some hand, at once firm and merciful, had touched the place where she was numb. A rush of mingled feeling swept through her with such strength that it seemed like the beginning of another life inside the old one—something newly alive, vaguely powerful, and yet helplessly dependent on the soul that had awakened it. Hope—moral recovery—has this divine pregnancy: it is not merely comfort, but energy.

But to her that new life was inseparable from Deronda. It was not that she suddenly thought he loved her in any ordinary way—such a thought would have toppled under its own improbability. Rather, he had become the air she breathed when she tried to be better. For the first time since that moment on the sea, a deep flush rose over her cheek and brow and throat, glowed for an instant, then faded gradually away. She could not speak.

Deronda stepped nearer and held out his hand. “I must not weary you.”

The sense that he was going startled her like cold water. She put her hand in his, still silent.

“You look ill yet—unlike yourself,” he said, and the solicitude in the words was not assumed.

“I can’t sleep much,” she answered, sinking back into her dispirited truth. “Things repeat themselves in me so. They come back—they will all come back.” A shudder ran through her, as if she felt the returning horror already at her shoulder.

“By degrees they will be less insistent,” he said. And still he could not drop her hand or step away suddenly, as if a clean break were possible. The pity held him physically.

“Sir Hugo says he shall come to stay at Diplow,” she said, snatching at the one thought that promised steadiness. “You will come too.”

“Probably,” he began; but the word sounded cold even to himself. “Yes, I shall come,” he added, correcting it, and then released her hand with a final friendly pressure that was like saying good-bye while refusing to utter the words.

“And not again here, before I leave town?” Her timid sadness made her look even paler.

What could he say? “If I can be of any use—if you wish me—certainly I will.”

“I must wish it,” she said with sudden, desperate frankness. “You know I must wish it. What strength have I? Who else is there?” The sob rose again.

Deronda’s face showed the pang he felt. “I will certainly come,” he said, with a misery that made the promise sound like something wrenched from him.

She saw the change in his face; but the relief of believing he would return was too great to yield to any other feeling. A steadier courage came back, lit by that hope he had breathed into her.

“Don’t be unhappy about me,” she said, with an affectionate reassurance that was almost childlike in its faith. “I shall remember your words—every one of them. I shall remember what you believe about me; I shall try.”

She held out her hand again, as if the earlier parting had not occurred. There was no smile—there had been no smile on her lips since her husband’s death. When she stood quiet, she looked like a melancholy statue of the Gwendolen whose laughter had once leapt out when others were grave.

Only by remembering the anguish that had altered the whole aspect of the world for her can one understand her behavior now—the unguarded openness, the importunate pleading dependence that did not stop to consider how it might appear to detached eyes. She clung to him as one clings in a burning house, not pausing to think what spectators infer from the embrace: he was the one figure she could see clearly through the smoke of her conscience. She identified him with the very struggle of renewal that had begun in her through his influence. Is it strange, then, that she read her own necessity into his feeling, and expected from him what she felt she must have?

Her imagination had not shaped itself into ordinary dreams of love-making and marriage; those images were too alien to her shame and effort. Love, if it had taken hold of her, demanded not flirtation but hard tasks—acceptance of rebuke, confession, endurance, self-change. She cried toward him as a child cries when its feet slip on a steep path—crying to be taken by the hand lest it lose itself.

That cry pierced Deronda. He alone knew the innermost nature of her trouble; to withdraw from her appeal would be to leave her in a loneliness that might be dangerous. Yet he foresaw, near or far, a coming wrench, and knew that every present strengthening of this bond would make the wrench harder. He was obliged to risk it.

He went more than once to Park Lane before Gwendolen left town; but those later interviews were in Mrs. Davilow’s presence, and thus less agitating, with a safer air, like conversation carried on in a room where the door stands open. Since she had resolved to accept her income, Gwendolen had formed a plan she liked to speak of: to place her mother and sisters with herself at Offendene again, and, as she said, to piece back her life to that time when they first went there, when everything had been happiness about her, only she had not known it. She had mentioned it to Sir Hugo, who meant to exert himself to get Gadsmere let at a rent that would more than pay for Offendene.

All this she told Deronda, and he willingly dwelt on any subject that offered her soothing occupation. He spoke nothing, and she asked nothing, of what chiefly occupied him. Her mind was fastened on his coming to Diplow before the autumn was out; and she no more thought of the Lapidoths—the little Jewess and her brother—as likely to alter her destiny than she thought of the great political and social ferment that alters the world while individual hearts burn in their own narrow fires. Poor Gwendolen’s memory seemed stunned; and everything outside the lava-lit track of her troubled conscience, and her effort to find deliverance from it, lay in dim forgetfulness.
