It was summer, and the heat pressed down like a weight upon the quiet streets of Villette. The air hung thick and still, as though the world itself held its breath. In this season of languor and feverishness, Georgette, the youngest of Madame Beck’s children, fell ill—her temperature rising swiftly, her small frame trembling with the first grip of sickness. Désirée, who had lately recovered from her own ailments, was sent off with Fifine to Bonne-Maman in the countryside, not out of affection, but as a precaution against contagion. The school now stood at the edge of concern. Medical care was no longer a luxury—it was necessary.

Madame Beck, ever practical and unyielding in her decisions, chose to ignore the return of Dr. Pillule, who had been absent for only a week. Instead, she summoned Dr. John, the English physician, to continue his visits. It was an unexpected move. I had thought, with some satisfaction, that such a decision would be reversed: surely, a woman of Madame Beck’s discernment would never allow a young man—especially one so charming and unguarded—to attend upon her pupils? Yet Madame Beck, prudent as she was, could also be boldly daring. She did not hesitate. She brought Dr. John into the very heart of the school, assigning him to care for Blanche de Melcy—proud, graceful, and full of pride—and Angélique, her friend, vain and quick with laughter.

I watched Dr. John closely. There was a certain warmth in his manner, a subtle glow beneath his composed exterior, as though he took genuine pleasure in being trusted. If discretion had allowed it, he might have justified his presence without reproach. But here, in a land where convents rose like silent sentinels and confessionals whispered secrets between stone walls, such a presence could not go unnoticed. The school buzzed with talk. The kitchen murmured. The town caught wind of it. Parents wrote letters, came in person, demanded explanations.

Had Madame Beck been weak, she would have fallen. A dozen rival schools stood ready to pounce, eager to exploit any misstep—any lapse of propriety—as a reason to discredit her. But Madame Beck was not weak. Though she might have been little more than a Jesuit in miniature, I found myself clapping the hands of my heart, and with them, crying aloud in silence: *Brava!* For she met every challenge with a grace that was both effortless and commanding. No one matched her in that peculiar charm—what I can only call the “roundness and frankness of a good woman”—a quality that won hearts where stern logic failed.

“Ce pauvre Docteur Jean!” she would say, chuckling softly, rubbing her plump white hands together as though warming herself by a fire of affection. “Ce cher jeune homme! The best creature in the world!” And then she would explain, with all the ease of truth, how she had come to employ him—for her own children, who adored him so completely they would scream themselves into fits at the thought of another doctor. “Where I trust for my own,” she’d add, “it is natural to extend that trust to others. Besides,” she’d conclude with a wave of her hand, “it’s only temporary. Blanche and Angélique have the migraine. Dr. John wrote a prescription. Voilà tout!”

The parents were silenced. Blanche and Angélique, ever eager to please, sang duets in praise of their physician. The other girls followed suit, declaring unanimously that when they were ill, they would want no one but Dr. John. Madame Beck laughed, and so did the parents. The Labassecouriens, it seemed, possessed a vast organ of philoprogenitiveness—the love of offspring—carried to extremes. In most homes, the will of the child ruled supreme. And so, instead of being condemned, Madame Beck was praised. People said she had acted with maternal indulgence. She emerged from the crisis not diminished, but strengthened. Liked more than ever.

Yet even now, I could not fully understand why she had taken such a risk—why she had chosen to defend Dr. John, to keep him close, when it might have cost her everything. What people said, I knew well enough: the entire household—pupils, teachers, servants alike—whispered that she was going to marry him. They had decided it, as if it were already written in fate. Age made no difference to them; it would be so. And indeed, appearances offered no strong argument against such a belief. Madame Beck seemed determined to keep him near. She had cast aside her former protégé, Dr. Pillule, without regret. She received Dr. John personally, always with cheerfulness, with a lightness that belied her years. She began to pay attention to her appearance—no longer dressing in morning dishabille or nightcaps and shawls. When he came early, she greeted him with braids neatly arranged, a silk dress fitted perfectly, brodequins laced with precision. Her whole attire was fresh as a flower, complete as a model.

But I do not believe her intention went beyond showing a handsome man that she was not entirely plain. And plain she was not. Though lacking in classical beauty or elegance of form, she pleased. Without youth’s bright graces, she still stirred warmth. One never tired of seeing her. She was never dull, never flat, never colorless. Her hair, unfaded, her eyes with their gentle blue light, her cheeks with their healthy bloom—all these things pleased not in flashes, but steadily, constantly.

Did she dream of taking Dr. John into her home, endowing him with her savings—said to amount to a modest fortune—and making him comfortable for life? Did Dr. John suspect such dreams? I saw him once emerging from her presence, a mischievous half-smile playing on his lips, a gleam of masculine vanity in his eyes—pleased, perhaps, with the idea of being admired, even courted.

He was not perfect. With all his good looks and kindness, he must have known he was not meant to succeed in matters of the heart if he encouraged hopes he did not intend to fulfill. But did he truly mean to fail? People said he had no money, that he lived entirely by his profession. And Madame Beck—though fourteen years his senior—was a woman who never seemed to age, never to fade. They were certainly on good terms. Perhaps he was not in love. But how many people are ever in love, or marry for love, in this world?

We waited for the outcome. For what he waited, I do not know. Nor for what he watched. But there was something about his manner—expectant, vigilant, absorbed—that never faded. It grew stronger, deeper. He had always been just beyond my understanding, and now he slipped farther away.

One morning, little Georgette was worse—feverish, restless, crying inconsolably. I suspected the medicine prescribed might disagree with her, and I hesitated to give it again. I waited anxiously for Dr. John’s arrival, eager to consult him. The bell rang. He entered. I heard his voice speaking to the portress. As usual, he ascended quickly, three steps at a time, appearing suddenly like a cheerful surprise.

Five minutes passed. Ten. Still no sign of him. Where could he be? Perhaps waiting below. But Georgette continued to cry, calling me by my familiar name: “Minnie, Minnie, me very poorly!” My heart ached. I descended to find him.

The corridor was empty. Where had he gone? Could he be with Madame in the dining room? Impossible—I had left her moments before, dressing in her own chamber. I listened.

Three pupils practiced in nearby rooms—the dining-room, the larger drawing-room, the smaller. Between them and the corridor lay only the portress’s cabinet, originally intended as a boudoir. Farther off, in the oratory, a class of over a dozen sang a *barcarolle*, their voices weaving through the air: “fraîchë,” “brisë,” “Venisë.” Amidst this symphony of sound, what could I hear?

A sudden, giddy laugh—a treble, almost girlish—came from the portress’s cabinet. Close by, the door stood half-open. Then a man’s voice, low and pleading: “For God’s sake!” A pause. Then, Dr. John emerged—his eyes bright, yet not with joy or triumph. His fair cheek was flushed. On his brow, a look of torment, of anxiety, yet beneath it, something tender, wounded.

The open door shielded me. Had I been in his path, I believe he would have walked past without seeing me. Some deep sorrow gripped him—not pride wounded, but affection hurt—deeply, cruelly hurt.

Who had done this? Who in that house held such power over him? Madame Beck, I believed, was in her chamber. The room he had just left belonged to the portress—Rosine Matou. A pretty, frivolous French girl—airy, fickle, vain, and mercenary. Surely, it was not *she* who had caused his pain?

But as I pondered, her voice broke through the door, clear and sharp, singing a light French tune. I glanced in—doubting my senses.

There she sat at the table, dressed in a smart pink *jaconas*, trimming a tiny blond cap. Nothing else moved in the room—except goldfish in a glass globe, flowers in pots, and a broad beam of July sun. A puzzle. But I must go upstairs.

Dr. John sat beside Georgette’s crib. Madame Beck stood before him. The child had been examined, comforted, and now slept quietly. As I entered, Madame was discussing the doctor’s health, noting changes in his appearance, warning him of overwork, urging rest and a change of air.

He listened, smiling, indifferent. “You’re *trop bonne*, Madame,” he said. “I feel perfectly well.”

She turned to me. “What do you think, Miss Lucie? Is he not paler? Thinner?”

I rarely spoke in Dr. John’s presence. He saw me as neutral, passive—something to be overlooked. But now, I answered—deliberately, clearly: “He looks ill now—but perhaps it’s only temporary. Dr. John may have been vexed or troubled.”

I did not see his face. I cannot say how he took it.

Georgette asked me, in broken English, for a glass of sweetened water. I replied in English. For the first time, I think, Dr. John noticed. Until now, he had assumed me foreign, addressing me as “Mademoiselle,” giving instructions in French. He seemed about to speak—then stopped himself.

Madame Beck resumed her advice. He shook his head, laughed, rose, and bid her good morning—with courtesy, yet with the careless air of one overwhelmed by too much attention.

When he was gone, Madame sank into the chair he had occupied. She rested her chin in her hand. All warmth vanished from her face. She looked stony, stern—almost bitter. She sighed. Once. Deeply.

The morning bell rang. She rose. Passing a dressing-table with a mirror, she glanced at her reflection. One single white hair streaked her nut-brown hair. She plucked it out, shuddering.

In the full daylight, her face, though still rosy, showed signs of aging. Youth’s contours were gone. Ah, Madame! Wise as you were, even you knew weakness. Never had I pitied you before. But now, as you turned away from the glass, my heart softened.

A calamity had struck. Disappointment, that old hag, stood before you with her grisly greeting—and your soul refused to embrace her.

And Rosine—my bewilderment knew no bounds. That day, I passed her cabinet five times, hoping to glimpse the secret of her charm. She was pretty, young, well-dressed. All admirable qualities—enough, perhaps, to explain a young man’s distress. Still, I found myself wishing—half-wishing—that Dr. John were my brother, or that he had a sister or mother who might gently reprove him. But I broke that wish—flung it away—realizing its folly. “Someone,” I thought, “might as well sermonize Madame about her young physician. And what good would that do?”

I believe Madame sermonized herself. She did not falter. She did not make a spectacle of herself. She had no tender feelings to crush, no great passions to betray. She had work—real, demanding work—to occupy her mind. And she had sense—true, genuine sense, rare in men and women alike. By these gifts, she carried herself with wisdom, with dignity. Brava! once more, Madame Beck. You faced a monster of desire—an Apollyon of longing—and you fought bravely. You conquered.
