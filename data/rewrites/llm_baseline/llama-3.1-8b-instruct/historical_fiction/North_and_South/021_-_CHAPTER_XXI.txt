As the moon cast its silvery glow over the tranquil streets, Margaret strolled alongside her father, her white silk gown shimmering like the green satin of Leezie Lindsay in the ancient ballad. The evening air was alive with the promise of a cool, crisp night, and Margaret felt her spirits lift with every step.

"I couldn't help but notice Mr. Thornton's demeanor tonight," she said, her voice barely above a whisper. "He seemed troubled, almost anxious."

Her father's eyes narrowed, his brow furrowed in thought. "Yes, I think you may be right. He's always been a man of composure, but tonight he seemed...different. Perhaps it's the strike weighing on his mind."

"The way he spoke to the others was his usual calm self," Margaret replied, "but his face told a different story. And have you considered what Boucher said earlier? His words were laced with a madness that sent shivers down my spine."

"Boucher's outbursts are nothing new, child," her father cautioned, "but it's true that his words carry weight. Still, I believe Mr. Thornton is a man of a different sort altogether – one who keeps his emotions locked deep within. It's a quality I admire, Margaret."

Margaret hesitated, her thoughts whirling with conflicting impressions. "I see what you mean, Papa, but I'm not so certain of Mr. Thornton's nature. He's intelligent, to be sure, and strong-willed, but there's something about him that doesn't quite sit right with me."

Her father smiled, his eyes twinkling with amusement. "Ah, but that's because you're seeing him through the lens of your own experiences, Margaret. Mr. Thornton has lived a life of practicality, one that's honed his judgment and self-control. He's not like us, dear one, with our books and our quiet lives. He's a product of the world he inhabits – a world of commerce and industry."

Margaret nodded thoughtfully, her mind replaying the events of the evening. She had been fascinated by the conversation between the men, though she'd struggled to grasp the intricacies of their discussion. And when Miss Thornton had taken her aside, Margaret had felt a pang of disappointment, not at being the only lady in the room, but at the tedium of the women's conversation.
