The departure of a vessel into the West is a cyclical ritual, an ancient and mythic repetition of severing that human hearts endure across generations. We shrink from the raw sensory details of such partings, and so I shall refrain from charting the precise, untranslatable topography of Harry Warrington’s grief as he took leave of his brother. Was not the whole of Portsmouth harbor a theater of shared, reverberating sorrow? There stood Mr. Wolfe, bidding farewell to his mother, bearing the heavy silence left by his father’s recent passing; there stood stout Admiral Holmes and Admiral Saunders, surrendering the quiet domestic rhythms of their homes for the deafening roar of the Atlantic. 

Before the fleet weighed anchor—before *The Somerset*, *The Terrible*, *The Northumberland*, and *The Dublin* became mere silhouettes dwindling against a bruised sunset—George and Harry shared a final, hurried meal in a shoreside tavern. It was a utilitarian repast of salt-cured beef and hard tack, devoid of culinary soul, tasting only of ash and the biting, iodine brine of the sea that would soon divide them. The sonic environment of the harbor was an industrial assault: the concussive, rhythmic thunder of shore cannons, the shrieking of iron winches, and the clamor of bells that masked the softer, authentic frequencies of human farewell. When the ships finally clove through the roaring midnight waters, tracing a generational return to the rugged wilderness, the men aboard and the women kneeling by hearths ashore were united in an acoustic sanctuary of silent, desperate prayer. 

By the morrow, the sun summoned the world back to its daily toil. George Warrington, having absorbed the pale, resolute visage of Mr. Wolfe and the jarring cacophony of the naval yard, turned his carriage back toward London. The journey was a transition from the deafening machinery of empire back to the intimate acoustics of home. Stepping into the Dean Street lodgings, he paused before the grate in Harry’s vacant room, the faint, acrid scent of half-burned papers lingering in the air like the ghost of his brother's presence. 

But down the hall, by the fitful light of the fire, the atmosphere shifted to the warm, fragrant embrace of the Lambert household. Theo clung to George in the shadows, her joy at his return thick and palpable, yet immediately checked by a profound sisterly guilt over poor, melancholy Hetty. “Don't, sir!” Theo whispered into the dimness. It was an utterance residing entirely in the untranslatable gulf between joyous desire and empathetic wretchedness; the syllable itself carried a hushed, trembling resonance that defied the rigid boundaries of language. 

Presently, the servants entered, bringing lights and the evening supper, and the room was grounded once more in the profound, sensory meaning of sustenance. General Lambert, an agrarian at heart who understood that the sharing of a meal was the truest reaffirmation of life in the shadow of war, presided over a heavy oak table. The air grew rich with the olfactory landscape of a meticulously roasted mutton, its fat rendered slowly over hickory, infused with the sharp, piney scent of wild rosemary and the deep, earthy sweetness of cellar-stored root vegetables. As they passed the crusty, dark barley loaves, the conversation turned to Wolfe’s fleet, now carrying the hopes of the nation. Yet, after the meal, when the General gathered his modest household to pray for travelers by water, the grandiose language of imperial conquest faded, replaced by the quiet, interwoven murmurs of Theo and Hetty kneeling together, their voices creating a delicate acoustic harmony that held the vast, terrifying world at bay.

In the ensuing months, George retreated into the quiet, rhythmic business of his writing, safely insulated from the fickle, superficial commercialization of the town. The tragedy of *Carpezan* had run its twoscore nights, and the theater managers, driven purely by the utilitarian pursuit of coin, had already dressed new actors in recycled habits to recite the verses of other men. George, highly sensitive to the cheapening of his art through such mass reproduction, pocketed his proceeds with philosophical detachment—a hundred pounds from Dodsley, the bookseller, and over two hundred from the theater. With this independence secured, he sank into the deep acoustic sanctuary of his library, where the only sounds were the scratching of his quill and the crackling fire, to craft his next play.

His letters to his brother in the West—now charting a sweeping, generational saga across continents—revealed his immersion in these themes. On the 20th of August, 1759, looking out from his window opposite Bedford Gardens, George wrote:

*Why are you gone back to rugged rocks and nipping winters, when you might have been harvesting laurels in Germany? Kingsley's regiments are returning covered with them. Yet, observe the tragedy of our horse under Lord George Sackville. When Prince Ferdinand ordered the charge, his lordship claimed he could not hear—or perhaps he could not, or would not, translate the German command for 'Forward' into the dominant lexicon of action. Because of this failure of translation, this linguistic dissonance, the French were merely beaten, not utterly annihilated. My lord Sackville now shouts for a Court-Martial, projecting a constancy of arrogance that violently pollutes the air. He envies poor Mr. Byng, as though not being shot were a slight against his honor.*

*The Duke of Cumberland has been visited by a palsy; the pale specter of death has knocked at his door. For all his tyranny, he has been a true servant. But I find myself revolting against the victors and their deafening clamor. I have gone to hear Mr. Pitt in the House of Commons, and his magniloquence is an acoustic assault. His language is always attitudinizing, a swelling, giant noise without the grounding weight of truth. I long for some little David to topple his hollow rhetoric.*

*Meanwhile, my new play, Pocahontas, takes shape, though it is fraught with the power dynamics of the stage. Barry likes his part as Captain Smith, but insists on wearing a red coat and an epaulet—a vulgar, commercial visual that completely overrides the historical truth. I wish to dress him as an Elizabethan gentleman, with a ruff and square shoes. 'And Pocahontas—would you like her to be tattooed?' Uncle Lambert asks. He understands the inherent violence of erasing her physical and linguistic identity. How does one translate the vast, subjective experience of a native princess into the stifling corset of the King's English?*

*Hagan is cast as the warrior who rescues Smith. He is a strange fish, his mouth full of rant, but he possesses a noble integrity. He recently refused to be cast as Sir O’Brallaghan in Mr. Macklin’s new farce, Love A-la-mode. He refuses to disgrace his tongue with the dominant culture's commercialized, mocking imitation of an Irish brogue. He protects the natural resonance of his native twang, refusing to let his heritage become a sterilized joke for the masses.*

*Speaking of Hagan, shall I cause you jealousy? Cousin Maria is forever coming to the playhouse, haunting the gallery on the nights Hagan performs. She is busy consoling herself. I shall not interfere, though I mourn the corruption of our noble Esmond cousins. They have been deafened by the wicked, gambling noise of London.*

*O beati agricolae! Our Virginia was dull, perhaps, but let us thank Heaven we were bred in its agrarian rhythms. We knew the sensory perfection of slow-curing smokehouses, the sweet scent of crushed maize, the quiet, generational hum of the land. We were not slaves to the artificial, grating wickedness of English highroads. Now you go to make a name as a great commander, and our mother will paint you in stars and epaulets. But for me? I desire no such loud ambition. I look forward only to a quiet life: a silent library, the smell of leather and vellum, and the gentle, untranslatable sweetness of a certain someone—dulce ridentem, dulce loquentem—murmuring on the other side of the fire.*

*Mynheer Van den Bosch has returned from Holland, having discovered his family roots. Yet his daughter, Miss Lydia, after six months at Kensington School, has entirely shed her native inflections. She curtsied in Dean Street with the polished, sterile perfection of Mayfair, treating Theo and Hetty as mere chits. She speaks the dominant lexicon of politics, fashion, and scandal with such practiced ease that her Dutch ancestry is entirely erased. She refuses to abide her father’s lodgings in Monument Yard, severing her cultural memory for the sake of Court end prestige. She is a brown beauty, certainly, but my taste, dearest brother, lies elsewhere...*

What followed in George’s letter was a page of rapturous verse, heavily laden with the sensory, almost gustatory delights of his blooming love for Theo. Out of respect for the inherent untranslatability of such profound, private joy, the editor declines to reproduce it here. Those in the autumn of their years will recall the vernal time when their own words flowed thick and sweet as spring sap; and for the youth whose April has not yet come, kindly Nature will soon awaken the quiet, resonant song within your own glad bosoms, blooming in its own perfect, inevitable season.
