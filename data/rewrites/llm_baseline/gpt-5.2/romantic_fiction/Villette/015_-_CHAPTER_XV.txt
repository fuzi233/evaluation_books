After Madame Beck’s fête—with its three weeks of permitted ease, its one bright day of laughter like a cork drawn from a bottle, and the slack morrow when even the chairs seemed too weary to stand—there came, as comes the souring after sweetness, two months of close work. These last weeks of the school-year were the true harvest-time of the pensionnat; what had been delayed and spared earlier was now thrust upon masters, mistresses, and pupils together. There were examinations to face and prizes to win; and for public show—always for public show—lessons must be kneaded, beaten, glazed, and baked into something that looked well on a table set for strangers’ eyes.

I did not pause to watch the others at their craft; I had my own furnace to tend. My duty was to season near ninety young brains with what they called a dark and intricate science—the English tongue—and to train near ninety tongues to its troublesome hiss and lisp, to those island dentals that to them felt like chewing dry crust with a sore mouth. It was not enough that they should know the words; they must sound them; and the effort—repeated, corrected, repeated again—was like making a novice whisk an egg-white to stiffness without letting her hand rest.

The day of examination arrived—stern day. It was prepared for anxiously and dressed for with no flutter. No gauze, no childish blue streamers; only the close, compact gravity of a working dress, as one ties up sleeves before plunging hands into dough. It seemed to me, too, that the strain of this day was appointed chiefly for my shoulders. The other female teachers were not expected to stand forth and question in their own branches; the professor of literature, M. Paul Emanuel, would do that for them. He gathered the reins of the whole establishment into his single hand, and held them hard. Help he rejected; colleague he would not admit. Even Madame Beck—who could have examined geography with real skill, for she loved that study as some women love a garden—was obliged to yield to the will of her imperious kinsman.

Only English he could not manage. That one dish he was forced to leave to the English teacher, and he gave it up with an ill grace—his jealousy flashing out, naïve as a child’s, sharp as salt on a raw lip.

To run a constant crusade against the amour-propre of every creature but himself was his chosen discipline. He relished representation—so long as he was the figure displayed; he abhorred it in others. When he could, he kept down; when he could not, he stormed and fretted like a kettle stopped too tightly.

On the evening before the examination, I walked in the garden as the boarders walked, and the teachers, and the dull leaves whispered in the alleys. M. Emanuel joined me in the allée défendue. A cigar lay at his lips; his paletôt—dark, shapeless, and somehow menacing—hung about him like a cloak thrown over a weapon; the tassel of his bonnet grec shaded his left temple; his whiskers curled, feline in their anger; his blue eye glittered under a cloud.

“Ainsi,” he began, stepping before me and stopping me short, “vous allez trôner comme une reine; demain—trôner à mes côtés? Sans doute vous savourez d’avance les délices de l’autorité. Je crois voir en je ne sais quoi de rayonnante, petite ambitieuse!”

He was wrong—quite wrong. I could not value the applause of to-morrow’s audience at the price he set upon it. Had those faces been mine by acquaintance, had friends sat there for me as for him, perhaps the warmth would have reached me; but as the matter stood, school triumphs shed on me only a cold shine, like pale sun on a stone floor. Yet I wondered—as I had wondered before—how such things could glow to him like hearth-fire.

Still, I had my own tastes. I liked to see him jealous. It woke him. It threw odd lights and shadows over his dun face, and made his violet-azure eyes—he called his black hair and blue eyes “une de ses beautés”—flash with a life that was almost youth. There was a relish in his anger, like mustard—sharp, unreasonable, but honest. I offered no denial of the complacency he imputed to me. I merely asked, quietly, when the English examination would come—at the opening or the close?

“I hesitate,” said he, “whether at the very beginning, before many persons are come, when your aspiring nature will not be gratified by a large audience; or quite at the close, when everybody is tired, and only a jaded, worn-out attention will be at your service.”

“Que vous êtes dur, Monsieur!” I said, with an affectation of dejection, as one tastes bitterness on purpose.

“One ought to be dur with you. You are one of those beings who must be kept down. I know you! I know you! Other people in this house see you pass, and think that a colourless shadow has gone by. As for me, I scrutinized your face once, and it sufficed.”

“You are satisfied that you understand me?”

He did not answer directly. He went on, as if pursuing some private proof in his own mind: “Were you not gratified when you succeeded in that vaudeville? I watched you and saw a passionate ardour for triumph in your physiognomy. What fire shot into the glance! Not mere light, but flame: je me tiens pour averti.”

“What feeling I had on that occasion, Monsieur—and pardon me if I say you immensely exaggerate both its quality and quantity—was quite abstract. I did not care for the vaudeville. I hated the part you assigned me. I had not the slightest sympathy with the audience below the stage. They are good people, doubtless; but do I know them? Are they anything to me? Can I care for being brought before their view again to-morrow? Will the examination be anything but a task to me—a task I wish well over?”

“Shall I take it out of your hands?”

“With all my heart; if you do not fear failure.”

“But I should fail. I only know three phrases of English, and a few words: par exemple, de sonn, de mone, de stare—est-ce bien dit? My opinion is that it would be better to give up the thing altogether: to have no English examination, eh?”

“If Madame consents, I consent.”

“Heartily?”

“Very heartily.”

He smoked a moment in silence—his cigar making a small dry crackle now and then, like twigs snapping in a grate. Then he turned quickly.

“Donnez-moi la main,” said he; and the spite and jealousy melted out of his face, and a generous kindliness rose there instead, warm as a sudden flame.

“Come, we will not be rivals, we will be friends,” he pursued. “The examination shall take place, and I will choose a good moment; and instead of vexing and hindering, as I felt half inclined ten minutes ago—for I have my malevolent moods: I always had from childhood—I will aid you sincerely. After all, you are solitary and a stranger, and have your way to make and your bread to earn; it may be well that you should become known. We will be friends: do you agree?”

“Out of my heart, Monsieur. I am glad of a friend. I like that better than a triumph.”

“Pauvrette!” said he, and, with that one word—half pity, half rough tenderness—he turned away and left the alley.

The examination passed well. M. Paul was as good as his promise; he chose my hour, steadied my place, and did what he could to make my part easy. Next day came the distribution of prizes. That, too, went by; the great machine of the school loosened, slackened, and stopped; the pupils went home, the professors scattered, and then began the long vacation.

That vacation—shall I ever forget it? I think not.

The very first day Madame Beck departed to the sea-side to join her children. The three teachers had parents or friends to shelter them, and took refuge without looking back. Every professor quitted the city: some went to Paris, some to Boue-Marine; M. Paul set forth on a pilgrimage to Rome. The house was left empty—empty, save for me, one servant, and a poor, deformed, imbecile pupil, a sort of crétin, whose stepmother in a distant province refused to receive her.

My heart seemed to shrink within me as if a hand tightened round it. The September days were long—so long that their hours tasted stale, like bread kept too many days in a tin; and yet they brought no nourishment. The premises, once noisy as a kitchen at dinner-time, seemed now vast, void, and echoing. The garden, forsaken, had turned grey with town-dust; leaves, when they fell, made no soft country sound but a dry, faint scrape, as though even nature here had learned to be cautious and muted under walls.

At the beginning of those eight weeks I could hardly see how I should live through to the end. My spirits had been sinking for months; now, the prop of labour withdrawn, they went down rapidly, like a fire when no one feeds it. To look forward was not hope; the dumb future offered no promise, no inducement to endure. A sorrowful indifference pressed on me; a resignation that was not peace but weariness—an inclination to reach, sooner rather than later, the end of earthly things.

When leisure forced me to look at life—life as it must appear to such as me—it showed itself a desert, tawny and trackless. The hopes dear to youth, that carry it on like a song hummed while work goes forward, I did not know, and did not dare to know. When they knocked sometimes at my heart, an inward bolt was drawn. I feared the sin and weakness of presumption more than I feared hunger.

Reader—religious, moral, stoic, cynical, epicurean—each will have his rebuke ready; each will sermonize or sneer. Take it as you will. Perhaps you are right; perhaps, placed where I stood, you would have been wrong with me. The first month was long, black, heavy.

The crétin did not seem unhappy. If I fed her and kept her warm, she asked no more. Food and sunshine contented her; and, when sunshine lacked, fire. Her faculties approved of inaction; her brain, her eyes, her ears, her heart slept. Lethargy was her paradise.

I did my best to feed her well. It is strange how, when one’s own mind is fainting, the small domestic acts still go on, like hands obeying a habit after thought has gone away. I measured out soup; I cut bread; I poured milk; I watched her swallow with a kind of dull vigilance, as one watches a creature that cannot tell you if it is choking. Yet my own appetite would fail. There were days when attendance on her turned the very thought of eating sour; I would go, faint, into the court to the well or fountain and drink water because it asked no effort of the stomach. But this duty—menial and distasteful—did not wring my heart as my solitude did. It did not brim my eyes, nor scald my cheek with tears that seemed molten.

She was, moreover, at times a heavy charge. I could not take her beyond the garden; I could not leave her alone. Her mind, like her body, was warped. A vague bent to mischief, an aimless malevolence, made constant watchfulness necessary. She seldom spoke. She would sit for hours moping and mowing, twisting her features into grimaces indescribable, so that it was like being shut up with some strange untamed animal rather than with a human being. There were personal attentions, too, that called for the nerve of a hospital nurse. They should not have fallen to me; a servant—now absent—had performed them before, and in the hurried flight of holiday no substitute had been found. I bore them; my resolution held, then fell ill, then rose again. Still, even that trial was less wasting than the gnawing, soundless pain of the mind.

It was a relief, when an aunt of the poor creature—a kind old woman—came one day and took her away.

Then I was free to walk out.

At first I had not the courage to venture far from the Rue Fossette. Yet by degrees I sought the city gates, passed them, and went wandering away—along chaussées, through fields, beyond cemeteries Catholic and Protestant, beyond farmsteads, to lanes and little woods, and I know not where. A goad seemed always at my side. A fever forbade rest. In my soul there was the craving of a famine—not for food, but for companionship. I walked through burning noon and arid afternoon, through dusk, and came back by moonrise.

I began to listen—not as one listens to music bought and displayed, but as one listens for life. The city had its own incessant hum: wheels over stones, hammers too early in the morning, voices thrown sharp between walls, laughter that did not belong to me. Outside the gates the sound changed. Earth took the noise into itself; the air broadened; the small notes of living things—wind in grass, a bird’s brief call, water in a ditch—came like a language older than French or English. It should have soothed; sometimes it did. Sometimes it only made more plain what I lacked: that my own tongue, my native accent, my familiar names for ordinary comfort, had no answering voice here. In this place I spoke with borrowed words; and the borrowed words, though correct enough, could not carry the taste of home. There are feelings one cannot translate; they perish in the passage, like delicate herbs crushed in transit, their perfume lost.

In those long solitary rambles I would picture where others were now. Madame Beck, at a cheerful watering-place with her children, her mother, and a troop of friends who had chosen the same shore. Zélie St. Pierre in Paris with relatives. The other teachers safe at home. Ginevra Fanshawe travelling south with connections—Ginevra, who seemed to me the happiest. I imagined her on roads of beautiful scenery, under September suns that shone for her on fertile plains where harvest and vintage mellowed. I saw the gold and crystal moons rise for her over blue horizons waved in mounted lines.

Yet all that was nothing. The same sun shone on me; the same moon rose for me; and sometimes I wished to be covered with earth and turf, deep out of their influence. I could not live in their light as in companionship. I could not give them affection and have it returned. But Ginevra—so I fancied—had a spirit with her, empowered to give constant strength and comfort, to gladden daylight and embalm darkness. By True Love was Ginevra followed; she could never be alone.

Was she insensible to that presence? I could not believe it. I imagined her grateful in secret, loving with reserve, purposing one day to show the depth of her love. I pictured her hero half conscious of her coy fondness, comforted by it; I conceived an electric chord of sympathy between them, a fine chain of mutual understanding sustaining union across a hundred leagues, carrying communication across mound and hollow by prayer and wish. In my mind Ginevra gradually became a heroine; and one day, startled by my own illusion, I said: “I really believe my nerves are getting overstretched. My mind has suffered somewhat too much; a malady is growing upon it—what shall I do? how shall I keep well?”

There was, indeed, no way to keep well under such circumstances. At last, after a day and night of peculiar depression, physical illness followed. I was forced to my bed.

The Indian summer closed. Equinoctial storms began. For nine dark wet days—hours rushing on turbulent, deaf, dishevelled, bewildered with sounding hurricane—I lay in a fever of nerves and blood. Sleep went quite away. In the night I would rise and look round for it—sleep—beseeching it in my weakness as one begs for water. The windows rattled; the blast cried; that was all. No soft return, no kind hand over the eyes.

I err: it came once, but in anger. Impatient of my importunity, it brought an avenging dream.

By the clock of St. Jean Baptiste the dream lasted scarcely fifteen minutes—brief, and yet long enough to wring my whole frame with unknown anguish; long enough to confer an experience nameless, with the hue, mien, terror, very tone of a visitation from eternity. Between twelve and one that night a cup was forced to my lips—black, strong, strange; drawn from no well, but filled seething from a boundless sea. Suffering brewed in mortal measure, mixed for mortal lips, tastes not as that suffering tasted. When I drank and woke, I thought all was over; the end had come and passed.

Trembling, as consciousness returned—ready to cry out for help, and yet knowing no fellow-creature was near enough to catch any summons—Goton, in her far attic, could not hear—I rose on my knees in bed. Some fearful hours went over me. Indescribably I was torn and oppressed in mind. And in that dream’s horror, the worst lay here: methought the well-loved dead—those who had loved me well in life—met me elsewhere alienated. A despair of the future galled my inmost spirit. There was no motive why I should recover or wish to live; and yet unendurable was the pitiless, haughty voice in which Death challenged me to engage his unknown terrors. When I tried to pray I could only say, “From my youth up Thy terrors have I suffered with a troubled mind.”

Most true it was.

In the morning Goton brought tea and urged me to call a doctor. I would not. I thought no doctor could cure what ailed me.

One evening—and I was not delirious; my mind was sane—I rose, dressed myself, weak and shaking. The solitude and stillness of the long dormitory could no longer be borne. The ghastly white beds were turning into spectres. The round tops of their frames became death’s-heads, huge and sun-bleached; dead dreams of an elder world and mightier race lay frozen in their wide gaping eyeholes. That evening the conviction fastened itself on my soul that Fate was of stone and Hope a false idol—blind, bloodless, with a granite core. I felt, too, that the trial appointed me had reached its climax, and must now be turned—by my own hands, hot, feeble, trembling.

Rain still fell; wind still blew, but with what seemed clemency compared to the day’s rage. Twilight dropped like a veil. From the lattice I saw night-clouds trailing low, like banners drooping. It seemed at that hour as though Heaven held affection and sorrow for all pain suffered on earth; and the weight of that dreadful dream became a little alleviated—the insufferable thought of being no more loved, no more owned, half yielded to a hope of the contrary. I thought that hope would shine clearer if I got out from under this roof, crushing as a tomb-slab, and went beyond the city to a certain quiet hill far off among fields.

I covered myself with a cloak—sense and recollection remained, for I knew to put on warm clothing—and set forth.

As I passed, the bells of a church arrested me. Their sound, made solemn by rain and stone streets, seemed to call me in to the salut. I went. Any rite, any spectacle of sincere worship, any opening for appeal to God was then as welcome to me as bread to one in extremity. I knelt with others on the stone pavement. It was an old church, its gloom not gilded but purpled by stained light, as if colour itself had been steeped long years in prayer.

Few worshippers were there; when the salut ended, half departed. I soon perceived those who stayed remained to confess. I did not stir. The doors were shut carefully, one after another. A holy quiet sank down; a solemn shade gathered round.

After a space, breathless and worn with prayer, a penitent approached the confessional. She whispered her avowal; shrift was whispered back; she returned consoled. Another went, and another. A pale lady kneeling near me said low and kindly, “Go you now; I am not quite prepared.”

Mechanically obedient, I rose and went. I knew what I did; my mind had run over the intent with lightning speed. This step could not make me more wretched than I was; it might soothe.

The priest within the confessional did not at first turn his eyes on me; he only inclined his ear, as if this duty had become a form gone through with the phlegm of custom. I hesitated. I knew no formula. Instead of beginning as penitents begin, I said simply, “Mon père, je suis Protestante.”

He turned then. He was not a native priest; the native cast of physiognomy is often grovelling, but this profile and brow showed a Frenchman. Grey and advanced in years, he did not, I thought, lack intelligence or feeling. Not unkindly he asked why, being a Protestant, I came to him.

I told him I was perishing for one word of advice, one accent of comfort. I had lived for weeks quite alone; I had been ill; there was an affliction pressing on my mind under which it could hardly endure.

“Was it a sin, a crime?” he asked, startled.

I reassured him; and, as well as I could, I showed him the mere outline of my experience. I did not dress it up in eloquence; I gave him what I had—plain pain, long pent up.

He looked thoughtful, surprised, puzzled. “You take me unawares,” said he. “I have not had such a case as yours before. Ordinarily we know our routine and are prepared; but this makes a great break in the common course of confession. I am hardly furnished with counsel fitting the circumstances.”

I had not expected he would be. Yet the mere relief of speaking—of letting some portion of accumulated anguish flow into a human ear that was sentient and yet consecrated, into a vessel from which it could not be poured back again among the world—did me good. Even while he spoke thus, I felt already solaced.

“Must I go, father?” I asked, for he sat silent.

“My daughter,” he said kindly—and I am sure he was kind; his eye had compassion—“for the present you had better go. But I assure you, your words have struck me. Confession, like other things, becomes formal with habit. You have come and poured your heart out—a thing seldom done. I would fain think your case over, and take it with me to my oratory. Were you of our faith I should know what to say: a mind so tossed can find repose but in retreat, and in punctual practice of piety. The world, it is well known, has no satisfaction for that class of natures. Holy men have bidden penitents like you to hasten their path upward by penance, self-denial, and difficult good works. Tears are given them here for meat and drink—bread of affliction and waters of affliction; their recompense comes hereafter. It is my conviction that these impressions under which you are smarting are messengers from God to bring you back to the true Church. You were made for our faith: depend upon it, our faith alone could heal and help you—Protestantism is too dry, cold, prosaic for you. The further I look, the more plainly I see it is out of the common order. On no account would I lose sight of you. Go now, my daughter; but return again.”

I rose and thanked him. I was withdrawing when he signed to me to return.

“You must not come to this church,” he said. “I see you are ill, and this church is too cold. You must come to my house: I live—” and he gave me his address. “Be there to-morrow morning at ten.”

I only bowed. Then I pulled down my veil, gathered my cloak close, and glided away into the wet twilight, the bell-sound fading behind me like a language half understood, yet—somehow—answered at last.
