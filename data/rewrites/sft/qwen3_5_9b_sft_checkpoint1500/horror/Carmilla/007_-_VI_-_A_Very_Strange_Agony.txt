When we finally settled into the drawing room, the air thick with the scent of roasted cocoa and bitter chocolate, Carmilla seemed to shed the shadow that had clung to her since our arrival. Though she took no refreshment, sitting apart from the others, her demeanor returned to a fragile normalcy. Soon enough, Madame and Mademoiselle de Lafontaine joined us, and a card game began, a trivial distraction from the heavy air in the house. My father eventually entered, claiming his "dish of tea" as he always did.

Once the cards were stacked away, he sank onto the sofa beside Carmilla, his brow furrowed with a quiet anxiety. He asked her if she had received word from her mother since coming to our estate.

"No," she replied, her voice soft.

He pressed further, asking if she knew where a letter could currently reach her.

"I cannot say," she answered, her gaze drifting vaguely, "but I have been considering leaving you. You have been far too hospitable, far too kind. I have caused you endless trouble, and I feel I should depart tomorrow, taking a carriage to pursue her. I know, somehow, where I will ultimately find her, though I dare not reveal it yet."

"But you must not dream of such a thing!" my father exclaimed, the relief washing over him evident to me. "We cannot afford to lose you. And I refuse to let you leave unless your mother directs it personally. She agreed to stay with us until her own return. I would be content knowing you hear from her, but tonight, the reports of this mysterious plague ravaging our neighborhood have grown terrifying. As your guardian, unaided by your mother's counsel, I feel a terrible weight of responsibility. Yet, I promise to do my utmost. One thing is certain: you must not consider leaving without her explicit permission. We would suffer too greatly to agree to it willingly."

"Thank you, sir, a thousand times for your kindness," she murmured, bowing her head with a bashful smile. "You have all treated me with a gentleness I have rarely known. I have never been so happy in my life as within these walls, under your care, surrounded by your beloved daughter."

With a gallantry typical of his older age, my father kissed her hand, pleased by her effusion.

Later, I walked Carmilla back to her chambers as per our routine, sitting with her while she prepared for sleep.

"Do you think," I ventured, breaking a comfortable silence, "that you will ever trust me completely?"

She turned to look at me, a smile playing on her lips, yet offering no words. She simply gazed at me, silent and enigmatic.

"You offer no answer?" I teased gently. "You cannot respond pleasantly; perhaps I should not have asked."

"You were right to ask," she said softly, her eyes losing their playful glint. "Or to ask anything. You do not know how deeply I hold you in my heart, nor could you imagine why I would withhold any truth. But I am bound by vows, perhaps more solemn than those of any nun, and I dare not speak my story even to you—not yet. The time will come soon when you know every secret. You may think me cruel, selfish... but love is inherently selfish. The more ardent the flame, the sharper its hunger. How jealous I am, you cannot comprehend. You must come with me, loving me, even unto death; or else hate me and follow me anyway, carrying that hatred through the grave and beyond. For in my nature, there exists no word for indifference."

"Now, Carmilla, you are about to speak your wild fancies again," I said hurriedly, feeling a chill run down my spine.

"Not I, silly little fool that I am, prone to whims and strange thoughts," she countered, her tone shifting to something almost childlike. "For your sake, I shall speak like a sage. Have you ever attended a ball?"

"No," I admitted, puzzled by the sudden shift in topic. "What is it like? It sounds enchanting."

"I almost forgot; it was years ago."

I laughed, trying to lighten the mood. "You aren't nearly that old. Your first ball shouldn't be forgotten yet."

"I remember everything about it—with an effort. It appears before me as divers see the world above them, through a medium that is dense, rippling, yet transparent. That night, however, some event marred the vision, causing the colors to fade. I was nearly assassinated in my bed, wounded here," she pressed a slender finger against her breast, "and I have never truly been the same since."

"Were you near death?"

"Yes, terribly. A cruel love, a strange passion that sought to consume me. Love demands sacrifice, and no sacrifice comes without blood."

"Let us try to sleep now," she whispered, her energy seemingly draining. "I feel so exhausted. How can I possibly rise and secure my door just now?"

She lay down, burying her small, delicate hands into her rich, wavy hair beneath her cheek. Her head rested heavily on the pillow, yet her eyes remained open, glittering with an uncanny intensity as they followed my every movement across the floor. A shy, unreadable smile played around her lips.

I bade her goodnight and slipped quietly from the room, leaving behind an uncomfortable, heavy sensation that refused to lift.

Often, I wonder if our beautiful guest ever prays. I have never seen her kneel. In the mornings, she does not descend until well after our family devotions conclude; at night, she never ventures into the hall to join our brief evening prayers. Had it not been for a casual remark during one of our idle conversations revealing that she had indeed been baptized, I would have suspected she was not Christian at all. Religion was a subject she never spoke of. If I understood the world better, this peculiar neglect or aversion would not have surprised me so.

Nervousness is contagious, and those of similar temperament inevitably adopt each other's habits. I, too, became infected by Carmilla's paranoia. I adopted her ritual of locking my bedroom door, succumbing to her whimsical terrors regarding midnight intruders and prowling assassins. I also copied her precaution of conducting a swift, thorough search of my room to ensure no lurking thief or murderer was "ensconced" within the shadows.

Taking these wise measures, I finally retired to my bed and drifted off. A candle burned steadily in my chamber, a relic of a habit so ancient I had long ceased to question it. Thus fortified, I believed I could rest in peace. But dreams slip through stone walls, illuminate dark rooms, or shroud bright ones in gloom. Their figures enter and exit at pleasure, laughing at the efforts of locks and keys.

That night, I dreamed a dream that marked the beginning of a very strange agony.

It was not a nightmare, for I remained acutely conscious of being asleep. Equally, I was aware of my location: in my room, lying in bed, exactly as I physically was. I perceived the furniture and the layout of the chamber precisely as I had seen them moments before, save for a profound darkness that had swallowed the space. At the foot of the bed, something moved, indistinct at first, a blur in the void. Gradually, the shape coalesced into a soot-black beast, resembling a monstrous cat. It stretched fully four or five feet long, spanning the entire length of the hearth-rug as it traversed it. It paced back and forth with a lithe, sinister restlessness, the trapped fury of a creature caged.

I could not cry out, though terror seized me. Its pace quickened, and the room plunged deeper into obscurity until I could discern nothing but two eyes. They were wide, luminous, and terrible. Suddenly, the beast leaped lightly onto the bed. Those two broad eyes approached my face, and in an instant, I felt a searing pain, as if two large needles had darted an inch or two apart, piercing deep into my breast.

I woke with a scream. The room was illuminated by the candle that had burned unceasingly throughout the night. Standing at the foot of the bed, slightly to the right, was a female figure. She wore a dark, loose gown, and her hair hung down, covering her shoulders like a veil. She was as still as a block of stone. There was not the slightest stirring of breath.

As I stared, transfixed, the figure seemed to change position, sliding silently closer to the door. Then, close to the frame, the door opened, and she passed out into the hallway.

Relief washed over me, allowing me to breathe and move again. My immediate thought was that Carmilla had played me a trick, that I had failed to secure my door properly. I rushed to it, finding it locked securely on the inside, just as I usually left it. Yet fear gripped me anew. I was horrified. I threw myself back into the bed, pulling the covers over my head, and lay there, more dead than alive, waiting for dawn.
