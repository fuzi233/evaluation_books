Our mother’s plea would have softened far sterner hearts than ours, and we spoke eagerly of a swift return to Virginia, of booking the finest cabins aboard the *Young Rachel*. But fate intervened—our youngest fell ill, making the voyage perilous, and Theo would not leave the child’s side. So the *Young Rachel* sailed without us that year. The next, my own health faltered, gout seizing me with its iron grip—an affliction that, once passed, left my mind sharper, my spirits lighter. Then came darker tidings from Jamaica, where our dear General, returning from his post, brought news that struck deeper than any worldly loss.

Theo’s mother—the tenderest of friends, save one—had succumbed to fever abroad. Her last regret was that she would not live to see our family flourish.

*“She sees us, though we see her not,”* the General murmured, his voice thick with grief. *“And she thanks you, George, for your kindness to her children.”*

His own life, we feared, would not long outlast hers. She had been his sun; without her, the world was twilight. It pained us to watch him, so diminished, sitting quietly among us. Theo, in her gestures, her very voice, often recalled her mother to him, and little Hetty became his solace. She had refused advantageous matches in Jamaica, the General told us, and later, in England, even the heir to Lord Wrotham’s title knelt before her. But Hetty would not leave her father.

*“As long as he needs me,”* she said, *“I ask for no husband.”*

We jested that the General might divide his time between our Manor and Oakhurst, but Hetty shook her head—Oakhurst held too many ghosts now. She would wed no man for title or wealth. The General, when pressed, confessed that some early disappointment had sealed her resolve, though she never spoke of it. We respected her silence.

My brother Lambert, now a college clergyman near Winchester, had taken a wife—a woman whose presence we endured more than enjoyed. She was free with her opinions, especially regarding Hetty’s unmarried state.

*“A sister is best kept from a husband’s sight,”* she declared one evening, fixing Theo with a knowing look. *“I’ve warned my own sister the same.”*

The General’s brows rose. *“Am I to understand, madam, that you have cautioned Lady Warrington against her own sister?”*

The woman flushed, indignant. *“I only speak from duty! A man is a man, saint or not!”*

*“And your own sister’s husband,”* the General mused, *“did he find *you* a temptation?”*

She gasped, outraged, and fled in tears.

*“Your mother was spared this creature,”* the General sighed later.

*“Mother would have improved her,”* Theo replied, kissing his cheek.

But blood does not always endear, nor neighbors amuse. Jack Lambert, sulking after his wife’s outburst, preached a dull sermon the next day, while little Miles, clad again in mourning, clung to his grandfather’s hand.

Would the General stay and oversee our estate while we journeyed to Virginia? Hetty would command the household, the butler’s keys surrendered to him—though Gumbo, now wed to Molly and father to a brood of tawny children, was to accompany us.

We traveled to London, then Bristol, where our agent fawned over our children—*“Such beauties never graced a ship!”*—and we embarked without mishap. Landing at Richmond, the past rushed back. A coach and a retinue of negroes awaited us; among them, a horseman in our livery sprang down to embrace us—Harry, dearer than ever. He rode with us to our mother’s door, where she stood on the steps, regal as ever. Theo knelt for her blessing.

Harry left us there, unwilling to intrude. *“She tolerates me,”* he admitted, *“but Fanny and she are best apart. Come to the tavern when you’re free.”*

Alone, we faced our mother. Her house, though modest, might have been a palace for the grace of her welcome. Old servants grinned; new ones peered shyly. Feasts were piled before us, and that evening, the gentry of Richmond came to pay respects. Harry appeared, but gloom hung over him. His mother addressed him as a queen might a wayward prince—cool, unyielding.

Later, in private, she found us. *“The children sleep like angels,”* she murmured, caressing imaginary curls. She spoke of the Governor’s levee, of the necessity of attending—her first appearance since Harry’s marriage. She had engaged the finest house in Richmond, her servants decked in livery, her best plate and wines brought at great cost. She even wore her wedding gown from George II’s time.

Theo, ever the peacemaker, persuaded her to receive Fanny, Hal’s wife—a reconciliation more bitter than sweet.

*“You remember these, my dear?”* Mother said, indicating silver sconces. *“Fanny often cleaned them at Castlewood.”*

Fanny’s eyes flashed, but Mother, magnanimous in victory, saw nothing.

Colonel Lee, arriving, bowed extravagantly. *“Madam, since Count Borulawski, I’ve seen no curtsey so fine!”*

*“A favorite of the Polish king,”* he replied, before turning to Harry. *“This is the captain who stood by Wolfe at Quebec! I would have shared his fate, had I been near.”*

Harry, in his old uniform, accepted the praise stiffly.

Our meetings had been many, our bond unbroken. Unlike in pursuits—he for the field, I for the page—we shared a love surpassing even the closest kinship. At Castlewood, without wives or women, we passed days in perfect harmony.

*“Brother,”* Hal confessed once, *“there’s no one like you.”*

And so, hearts full, we prepared to face the storm gathering over Virginia—loyalties divided, but our affection unwavering.
