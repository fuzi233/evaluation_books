The morrow, which I had counted on for indolent ease, woke with a stir in it, as if some great kitchen had been set to work before dawn and all the city were to be fed. It was the birthday of one of Labassecour’s young princes—the eldest, they said, the Duc de Dindonneau—and, in his honour, the schools had a holiday, and the chief Athénée an observance. The students were to assemble in the hall of public examinations, present a loyal address, and hear thereafter a professor deliver an oration.

Several of M. de Bassompierre’s learned friends—those grave “savants” who could season a dish of talk with Latin as deftly as with salt—were expected. The municipality would be there, and the Burgomaster Staas, and parents, kinsfolk, patrons: all Villette, in short, that liked to hear itself rustle and murmur in a crowded room. The Count, engaged by his friends, was to go; his daughter would go; and Paulina sent a small note to Ginevra and me, bidding us come early to join her.

In the Rue Fossette dormitory, while we dressed, Miss Fanshawe stopped with a pin poised, and fell suddenly into laughter—a peal that rang clear as the clink of a spoon against a glass.

“What now?” I asked; for she had turned and was staring at me as at some odd page in a book she could not construe.

“It seems so odd,” she said, in that half-honest, half-insolent openness which made even her impertinence taste of something wholesome—coarse bread, perhaps, but bread—“that you and I should now be so much on a level, visiting in the same sphere; having the same connections.”

“Why, yes,” said I; “I had not much respect for the connections you chiefly frequented awhile ago. Mrs. Cholmondeley and Co. would never have suited me at all.”

“Who are you, Miss Snowe?” she demanded—pure curiosity, undisguised as an uncovered dish.

“You used to call yourself a nursery governess; when you first came here you really had the care of the children in this house. I have seen you carry little Georgette in your arms, like a bonne—few governesses would have condescended so far—and now Madame Beck treats you with more courtesy than she treats the Parisienne, St. Pierre; and that proud chit, my cousin, makes you her bosom friend!”

“Wonderful!” I said, amused in spite of myself. “Who am I indeed? Perhaps a personage in disguise. Pity I don’t look the character.”

“I wonder you are not more flattered by all this,” she went on. “You take it with strange composure. If you really are the nobody I once thought you, you must be a cool hand.”

“The nobody you once thought me!” I repeated; and warmth rose to my face—as if hot broth had been brought too near the mouth. But I would not be angry. A school-girl’s “nobody” and “somebody” were terms flung out like crumbs—without weight, without nourishment. I told her I had met with civility; and asked what she saw in civility to throw any one into a fever.

“One can’t help wondering at some things,” she persisted.

“Wondering at marvels of your own manufacture. Are you ready at last?”

“Yes; let me take your arm.”

“I would rather not: we will walk side by side.”

When she took my arm, she leaned on me her whole weight; and as I was neither gentleman nor lover, I did not relish being made a prop.

“There, again!” she cried. “I thought, by offering to take your arm, to intimate approbation of your dress and general appearance: I meant it as a compliment.”

“You did? You meant, in short, to express that you are not ashamed to be seen in the street with me? That if Mrs. Cholmondeley should be fondling her lapdog at some window, or Colonel de Hamal picking his teeth on a balcony, and should catch a glimpse of us, you would not quite blush for your companion?”

“Yes,” she said, with that directness which was her best point—the salt that kept her character from spoiling outright.

I let my face do the commenting. It was not reverence it expressed.

“Scornful, sneering creature!” she went on, as we crossed a great square and entered the quiet, pleasant park—the nearest way to the Rue Crécy. The park had its own domestic sound—gravel underfoot, leaves rubbing softly like skirts in a corridor, birds calling as if they too kept holiday; a mercy after the city’s iron clatter. “Nobody in this world was ever such a Turk to me as you are!”

“You bring it on yourself. Let me alone; have the sense to be quiet; I will let you alone.”

“As if one could let you alone, when you are so peculiar and so mysterious!”

“The mystery and peculiarity being entirely the conception of your own brain—maggots—neither more nor less; be so good as to keep them out of my sight.”

“But are you anybody?” she persevered, thrusting her hand under my arm in spite of me; and my elbow pressed close against my side to bar the intrusion, as one bars a pantry-door against a pilfering cat.

“Yes,” I said. “I am a rising character: once an old lady’s companion, then a nursery-governess, now a school-teacher.”

“Do—do tell me who you are? I’ll not repeat it,” she urged, squeezing the captured arm and coaxing with ludicrous tenacity. She could not conceive how a person not bolstered by birth or money, not warmed at the hearth of some famous name, could stand upright and keep an honest look. For my own part, it sufficed me to be known where it mattered. Pedigree, station, recondite acquisition—these were lodgers of the third class in my mind; I gave them a back room, and would not let them strut into the parlour even if it stood empty. The world kept a different account; and perhaps the world is right. Yet I, too, am not wholly wrong.

There are people whom a lowered place degrades morally; to whom loss of connection costs loss of self-respect. Such may be justified in valuing station as a safeguard. We learn, as we live, to judge less sharply. Where we see an accumulation of little defences—round the prude’s virtue, round the man-of-the-world’s respectability—be sure they are needed.

Thus discoursing within, and outwardly fencing off Ginevra’s importunities, we reached the Hôtel Crécy. Paulina was ready; Mrs. Bretton was with her; and, under her escort and that of M. de Bassompierre, we were soon conducted to the place of assembly and seated well, at a convenient distance from the Tribune. The Athénée youth were marshalled before us; the municipality and its burgomaster held places of honour; the young princes with their tutors sat conspicuous; and the hall was crowded with the aristocracy and the first burghers.

Of the professor who was to speak I had scarcely thought. I expected some learned formality—half lecture to schoolboys, half honeyed compliment to princes—words that would fall like tepid water and leave no flavour.

The Tribune stood empty when we entered; then, in ten minutes, it filled; and in a second, a head, chest, and arms rose above the crimson desk. That head I knew. The close blackness of the cranium; the ample pallor of brow; the blue, kindled glance—details so domesticated in memory, and linked with so many whimsical associations, that their sudden apparition tickled my fancy to laughter. I confess I laughed till warmth came; then bent my head, and made my handkerchief and a lowered veil the only confidants of my mirth.

It pleased me—more than I should have owned—to see M. Paul there, lifted into public view: fierce and frank, dark and candid, testy and fearless, as when he reigned on his estrade in class. His presence was a surprise; though I knew he filled the chair of Belles Lettres, I had not expected him. With him in that Tribune, I knew we should not be dosed with syrup and starch. Yet for what was poured—suddenly, rapidly, continuously—upon that assembly, I was not prepared.

He spoke to princes, nobles, magistrates, burghers, with the same ease and pointed, choleric earnestness with which he harangued the three divisions of the Rue Fossette. He addressed the collegians not as schoolboys, but as future citizens and embryo patriots. The Europe to come had not yet declared itself; yet in him there seemed a spirit already awake. Who would have thought the flat, fat soil of Labassecour could yield such convictions? I need not map his opinions; but I believed the little man not more earnest than right. With all his fire, he was severe and sensible; he trod Utopian theories down, rejected wild dreams with scorn; but when he looked tyranny in the face—then a light opened in his eye worth seeing; and when he spoke of injustice, his voice rang, clear as a band-trumpet at twilight from the park—sound that takes the air like a blade and makes every listening nerve remember it has blood.

The audience did not all catch the flame in its purity; but some of the Athénée youth did. As he concluded, they gave him a long, loud cheer—ringing, sustained; for, with all his fierceness, he was their favourite.

When our party left the hall, he stood near the entrance. He saw and knew me; lifted his hat; offered his hand in passing, and said, “Qu’en dites vous?” That question—so like him—betrayed even in triumph his inquisitive restlessness, his want (as I counted it) of self-control. He should not have cared then what I thought, or what any one thought; but he did care, and was too natural to hide it, too impulsive to repress. If I blamed the eagerness, I liked the naïveté. I would have praised him; I had praise enough in my heart; but there were no words on my lips. I stammered something lame, and was glad when others came up with profuse congratulations and covered my poverty with their plenty.

A gentleman introduced him to M. de Bassompierre; and the Count, much gratified, asked him to join his friends and dine at the Hôtel Crécy. He declined—shy, always, of the advances of the wealthy; there was in him a sturdy independence like corded sinew under a plain sleeve—no parade, but strength felt when you came near. He promised, however, to step in with his friend M. A——, a French Academician, later in the evening.

At dinner that day, Ginevra and Paulina each looked, in her own fashion, very beautiful. Ginevra had the advantage in material charm; Paulina shone for attractions more subtle—light and eloquence of eye, grace of mien, variety of expression. Ginevra’s deep crimson dress set off her light curls, and harmonised with her rose-like bloom; she looked like a ripe fruit laid on red cloth. Paulina’s attire, close in fashion, faultlessly neat, was clear and white; it made the eye grateful, as one is grateful for clean linen on a table where good bread will be broken. Its very simplicity drew attention to the delicate life of her complexion, the soft animation of her face, the tender depth of her eyes, the brown shadow and bounteous flow of her hair—darker than her cousin’s, as were also the brows, lashes, the full irids, the large mobile pupils. Nature had traced Miss Fanshawe’s details slightly and carelessly; in Miss de Bassompierre’s she had wrought a fine finish, as if the hand that carved had meant the thing to last.

Paulina was awed by the savants, but not made dumb. She spoke modestly, with effort, yet with true sweetness and a fine sense that pierced through the courtliest phrases. More than once her father paused in his own discourse to listen, and looked at her with proud delight. It was M. Z——, very learned and very polished, who had drawn her into talk. I was charmed with her French—faultless structure, true idiom, pure accent. Ginevra, who had lived half her life on the Continent, could do nothing like it. Words never failed Miss Fanshawe; but accuracy and purity she neither possessed nor, in any number of years, would acquire. On language M. de Bassompierre was critical; and here too he was gratified.

Another observer sat down late—detained by professional exigency—Dr. Bretton. At the moment he took his seat, he quietly scanned both ladies; and more than once his guarded survey returned. His arrival roused Ginevra. Till then she had looked listless; now she smiled and became complacent, talked much—though rarely to the purpose, or rather with a purpose somewhat mortifyingly below the standard of the occasion. Her light disconnected prattle might once have gratified Graham; perhaps it pleased him still. Perhaps it was fancy that suggested, while his eye was filled and his ear fed, his taste and intelligence were not equally regaled. Certain it is, he yielded courteously all that was required; his manner showed neither coolness nor pique. Ginevra was his neighbour, and during dinner he almost exclusively confined his notice to her. She seemed satisfied, and went to the drawing-room in good spirits.

No sooner had we reached that refuge, however, than she again fell flat. Throwing herself on a couch, she denounced both the discours and the dinner as stupid affairs, and asked Paulina how she could bear such a set of prosaic gros-bonnets as her father gathered. But the instant the gentlemen were heard to move, her railings ceased as if a lid had been clapped on a pot. She sprang up, flew to the piano, and dashed at it with spirit. Dr. Bretton entered among the first and took up his station beside her.

I thought he would not long keep that post. There was a place near the hearth to which I expected to see him drawn; he only glanced that way, while others slid in to occupy it. The grace and mind of Paulina charmed these thoughtful Frenchmen. The fineness of her beauty, the soft courtesy of her manner, her immature but inbred tact, pleased their national taste; they gathered round her—not to talk science, which would have silenced her, but to touch on letters, arts, the texture of actual life. It soon appeared she had both read and reflected. I listened. I am sure Graham, though he stood a little apart, listened too; his hearing as well as vision was quick and discriminating. I felt he gathered the conversation, and that its mode suited him exquisitely—pleased him almost to pain.

In Paulina there was more force—of feeling and character—than most people thought; than Graham himself imagined; than she would ever show to those who did not wish to see. There is no excellent beauty, no accomplished grace, no reliable refinement, without strength as complete and trustworthy. As well look for fruit and blossom on a sapless tree as for charms that endure in a feeble nature. For a little while, beauty may bloom round weakness; but it cannot bear a blast. I, who had known her as a child, guessed by what good root her graces held to reality.

While Dr. Bretton listened and waited an opening in that magic circle, his glance wandered at intervals and lit by chance on me, where I sat in a quiet nook not far from my godmother and M. de Bassompierre, who, as usual, were deep in what Mr. Home called “a two-handed crack”—what the Count would have named a tête-à-tête. Graham smiled recognition, crossed the room, asked me how I was, told me I looked pale. I had my own smile at my own thought: it was about three months since Dr. John had spoken to me—a lapse of which he was not even conscious. He sat down and became silent. His wish was rather to look than to converse. Ginevra and Paulina were opposite; he could gaze his fill; he surveyed both forms—studied both faces.

More guests had entered since dinner—ladies and gentlemen, dropping in for evening talk; and among the gentlemen, by glimpses, I had already noticed, in an inner saloon seen only in vista, a severe dark outline hovering aloof—professorial, unmistakable. M. Emanuel knew many of the gentlemen present, but I think was a stranger to most of the ladies except myself. Looking towards the hearth, he could not but see me, and naturally moved to approach; but seeing Dr. Bretton also, he changed his mind and held back. Had he stopped there, no harm. But he puckered his brows, thrust out his lip, and looked so ugly that I turned my eyes away from the spectacle.

M. Joseph Emanuel had arrived—as well as his austere brother—and at this very moment was relieving Ginevra at the piano. What a master-touch succeeded her school-girl jingle! In what grand, grateful tones the instrument acknowledged the hand of a true artist! The room’s very noise seemed to cleanse itself under those chords; their measured swell made a kind of shelter, as if a roof were being built of sound against the harsh street’s ceaseless grind.

“Lucy,” began Dr. Bretton, breaking silence with a smile as Ginevra glided before him and cast a glance in passing, “Miss Fanshawe is certainly a fine girl.”

Of course I assented.

“Is there,” he pursued, “another in the room as lovely?”

“I think there is not another as handsome.”

“I agree with you, Lucy: you and I do often agree in opinion—in taste, I think; or at least in judgment.”

“Do we?” I said, somewhat doubtfully.

“I believe if you had been a boy, Lucy, instead of a girl—my mother’s god-son instead of her god-daughter—we should have been good friends: our opinions would have melted into each other.”

He had assumed a bantering air. A light, half-caressing, half-ironic, shone aslant in his eye. Ah, Graham! I have given more than one solitary moment to calculations of your estimate of Lucy Snowe. Was it always kind—always just? Had Lucy been the same within, but with the outward advantages of wealth and station, would your manner to her, your value for her, have been what they were? Yet I would not infer serious blame. My temperament was soon depressed; it fell if a cloud crossed the sun. Perhaps before severe equity I should stand more at fault than you.

Trying to keep down the unreasonable pain that thrilled my heart—made me feel, sharply, that while Graham could give to others a grave, manly interest, he had for Lucy only light raillery—I asked calmly, “On what points are we so closely in accordance?”

“We each have an observant faculty. You, perhaps, don’t give me credit for the possession; yet I have it.”

“But you were speaking of tastes: we may see the same objects, yet value them differently.”

“Let us bring it to the test. Of course you cannot but render homage to the merits of Miss Fanshawe: now, what do you think of others in the room?—my mother, for instance; or the lions yonder, Messieurs A—— and Z——; or, let us say, that pale little lady, Miss de Bassompierre?”

“You know what I think of your mother. I have not thought of Messieurs A—— and Z——.”

“And the other?”

“I think she is, as you say, a pale little lady—pale, certainly, just now, when she is fatigued with over-excitement.”

“You don’t remember her as a child?”

“I wonder, sometimes, whether you do.”

“I had forgotten her; but it is noticeable, that circumstances—persons—even words and looks that had slipped your memory may, under certain conditions, revive.”

“That is possible enough.”

“Yet,” he continued, “the revival is imperfect—needs confirmation; partakes so much of the dim character of a dream, or of the airy one of a fancy, that the testimony of a witness becomes necessary for corroboration. Were you not a guest at Bretton ten years ago, when Mr. Home brought his little girl, whom we then called ‘little Polly,’ to stay with mamma?”

“I was there the night she came, and also the morning she went away.”

“Rather a peculiar child, was she not? I wonder how I treated her. Was I fond of children in those days? Was there anything gracious or kindly about me—great, reckless, schoolboy as I was? But you don’t recollect me, of course?”

“You have seen your own picture at La Terrasse. It is like you personally. In manner, you were almost the same yesterday as to-day.”

“But, Lucy, how is that? Such an oracle really whets my curiosity. What am I to-day? What was I the yesterday of ten years back?”

“Gracious to whatever pleased you—unkindly or cruel to nothing.”

“There you are wrong; I think I was almost a brute to you, for instance.”

“A brute! No, Graham: I should never have patiently endured brutality.”

“This, however, I do remember: quiet Lucy Snowe tasted nothing of my grace.”

“As little of your cruelty.”

“Why, had I been Nero himself, I could not have tormented a being inoffensive as a shadow.”

I smiled; but I hushed a groan. Oh—if he would but let me alone—cease these allusions. His “quiet Lucy Snowe,” his “inoffensive shadow,” I gave back—not with scorn, but with an extreme weariness. The words had the cold pressure of lead. Let him not whelm me with such weight. Happily, he soon turned to another theme.
