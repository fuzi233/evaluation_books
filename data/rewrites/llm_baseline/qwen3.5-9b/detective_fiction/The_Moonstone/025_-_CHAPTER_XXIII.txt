I had the pony-chaise standing ready, anticipating that Mr. Franklin might insist on departing by the train that very night. However, the sudden appearance of his luggage, trailed downstairs by Mr. Franklin himself, made it abundantly clear that he had finally resolved to stay put—for once in his life.

"So you have truly made up your mind, sir?" I asked as we met in the hall. "Why not wait a day or two longer and afford Miss Rachel another opportunity?"

The veneer of composure seemed to have completely stripped itself from Mr. Franklin now that the moment of departure had arrived. Instead of offering a verbal response, he pressed the letter his ladyship had addressed to him into my hand. Much of its content merely reiterated points already covered in previous communications, but there was a final addition regarding Miss Rachel that explained the steel in Mr. Franklin's resolve, should it explain anything at all.

"You will likely wonder," her ladyship wrote, "that I allowed my own daughter to keep me entirely in the dark. A diamond valued at twenty thousand pounds has vanished, and I am left to deduce that the mystery of its disappearance is no mystery to Rachel. Furthermore, some unknown party appears to have imposed an inexplicable oath of silence upon her for reasons I cannot fathom. Could I possibly permit myself to be trifled with in such a manner? It seems entirely plausible, given Rachel's current state. She is in a condition of nervous agitation that is truly pitiful. I dare not broach the subject of the Moonstone again until time has worked to calm her. To aid this recovery, I have not hesitated to dismiss the police officer. The puzzle that confounds us also baffles him. This is not a matter where a stranger can assist; he only adds to my suffering and drives Rachel mad whenever his name is mentioned.

"My plans for the future are as settled as circumstances allow. My intention is to take Rachel to London—to soothe her mind through a complete change of scene and to seek the counsel of the finest medical experts. May I request that you join us in the city? My dear Franklin, you must, in your own way, emulate my patience and wait for a more opportune moment. The invaluable assistance you previously offered in investigating the missing jewel remains, in Rachel's present dreadful state of mind, an unforgivable offense. Acting blindly in this affair, you inadvertently threatened her secret with exposure through your efforts, thereby adding to the heavy burden of anxiety she has endured. It is impossible for me to excuse the perversity that holds you responsible for consequences neither you nor I could have foreseen. She cannot be reasoned with; she can only be pitied. I am grieved to say it, but for the present, you and Rachel are better apart. My sole advice is to give her time."

I returned the letter, feeling genuine sorrow for Mr. Franklin, knowing how deeply he cherished my young lady. I observed that her mother's assessment had struck him to the core. "You know the proverb, sir," I told him. "When things are at their worst, they are certain to improve. Things cannot possibly be much worse, Mr. Franklin, than they are now."

Mr. Franklin folded his aunt's letter without seeming much comforted by my observation.

"When I arrived here from London with that terrible Diamond," he said, "I do not believe there was a happier household in England. Consider the household now! Scattered, disunited—the very air of the place tainted by mystery and suspicion! Do you recall that morning at the Shivering Sand when we discussed my Uncle Herncastle and his birthday gift? The Moonstone has served the Colonel's vengeance, Betteredge, in ways the Colonel himself never dreamed!"

With that, he shook my hand and stepped out toward the pony-chaise.

I followed him down the steps. It was profoundly miserable to witness him leaving the old estate, where he had spent his happiest years, in such a manner. Penelope, visibly shaken by the events within the house, came running to bid him farewell, tears streaming down her face. Mr. Franklin kissed her. I waved my hand, intending to convey, "You are heartily welcome, sir." Several other female servants peeked around the corner to see him off; he was one of those men universally liked by the women. At the last moment, I halted the chaise and pleaded as a favor that he allow us to hear from him via letter. He seemed oblivious to my plea, casting glances around, taking a silent farewell of the old house and grounds. "Tell us where you are going, sir!" I insisted, clutching the side of the chaise to intercept his departure. Mr. Franklin suddenly pulled his hat low over his eyes. "Going?" he echoed. "I am going to the devil!" The pony startled at the word, as if sensing a profound Christian horror in it. "God bless you, sir, go where you may!" was all I managed to utter before he vanished from sight and hearing. A sweet and pleasant gentleman! Despite his faults and follies, he was undeniably kind. He leaves a sad void behind him when he departs my lady's house.

The long summer evening proved dull and dreary enough on that Saturday night.

I maintained my spirits by clinging to my pipe and my copy of *Robinson Crusoe*. The women (excluding Penelope) passed the time discussing Rosanna's suicide. They were unanimously convinced that the poor girl had stolen the Moonstone and had destroyed herself in terror of being discovered. My daughter, of course, privately adhered to her original assertions. Her theory regarding the true motive behind the suicide failed, strangely enough, at exactly the same point where my young lady's claim of innocence faltered. Both explanations left Rosanna's secret journey to Frizinghall and her actions concerning the nightgown entirely unexplained. Pointing this out to Penelope was futile; her objection registered on her mind no more effectively than a shower of rain on a waterproof coat. The truth is, my daughter inherits my superiority to reason—and in this regard, she has advanced considerably beyond her own father.

The following day, Sunday, the close carriage belonging to Mr. Ablewhite returned empty. The coachman delivered a message for me, along with written instructions for my lady's maid and Penelope.

The message informed me that my mistress had decided to take Miss Rachel to her London residence on Monday. The written notes detailed the required clothing and instructed the two maids to meet their mistresses in town at a specific hour. Most of the other servants were to accompany them. Having found Miss Rachel so reluctant to return to the house after the recent events, my lady had opted to travel directly to London from Frizinghall. I was to remain in the countryside until further orders to oversee domestic affairs. Those servants staying behind were to be paid in full.

Reminded by these arrangements of Mr. Franklin's comment about our household becoming scattered and disunited, my thoughts drifted inevitably to him. The more I pondered his situation, the more anxious I became regarding his future actions. This culminated in my writing, via Sunday's post, to his father's valet, Mr. Jeffco (whom I had known in earlier years), begging him to inform me of whatever plans Mr. Franklin had settled upon upon arriving in London.

That Sunday evening proved, if possible, even duller than Saturday's. We concluded the day of rest as millions do weekly in these islands—that is, we all awaited bedtime and eventually succumbed to sleep in our chairs.

I know not how Monday affected the rest of the household, but it certainly gave me a severe jolt. The first of Sergeant Cuff's prophecies—that I would hear from the Yollands—came true on that very day.

Having seen Penelope and my lady's maid depart on the railway with the luggage for London, I was wandering the grounds when I heard my name called. Turning, I found myself face-to-face with the fisherman's daughter, Limping Lucy. Setting aside her lame foot and her gaunt frame (the latter, in my opinion, a dreadful drawback for a woman), the girl possessed several appealing qualities. She had a dark, keen, intelligent face, a clear voice, and a beautiful brown head of hair. Among her misfortunes was a crutch; among her defects, a fiery temper.

"Well, my dear," I said, "what do you want with me?"

"Where's the man you call Franklin Blake?" the girl demanded, fixing me with a fierce gaze while resting on her crutch.

"That is not a respectful way to address any gentleman," I replied. "If you wish to inquire for my lady's nephew, you will please refer to him as Mr. Franklin Blake."

She limped closer, looking as though she could devour me alive. "_Mr._ Franklin Blake?" she repeated. "Murderer Franklin Blake would be a fitter name for him."

My experience with the late Mrs. Betteredge proved useful here. Whenever a woman attempts to provoke you, simply turn the tables and provoke her instead. They are usually prepared for every defensive maneuver you employ, except that one. A single word often achieves what a hundred cannot; and one word silenced Limping Lucy. I looked her pleasantly in the face and said, "Pooh!"

Her temper flared immediately. She balanced on her sound leg, raised her crutch, and beat it furiously against the ground three times. "He's a murderer! He's a murderer! He's a murderer! He has been the death of Rosanna Spearman!" she screamed at the top of her lungs. A few workers nearby glanced up, recognized Limping Lucy, knew what to expect, and quickly looked away.
