Cheerfulness, it would appear, is a rhythm dictated fully as much by the internal hum of the blood as by the acoustic and atmospheric pressures of the world without. I make this observation because Messrs. Helstone and Moore trotted forth from the mill-yard gates, leaving behind the grinding, mechanical dissonance of industrial machinery, and entered the sweeping, ancient expanse of the night moor in the best possible spirits. When the golden beam of a lantern fell upon Mr. Moore’s face, one could see an unusual spark dancing in his dark eyes, a newfound vivacity thawing his usually rigid physiognomy. Beside him, the rector’s hard features were illuminated, cragged and ashine with a fierce, almost mythic glee. 

It was a drizzling night. The rhythmic squelch of horse hooves against the peat and the whisper of the raw breeze through the heather composed a primal soundscape—one that had echoed across these moors for generations. You would think such a wet, perilous expedition would hardly enliven the senses. Had any members of the frame-breaking crew caught sight of this party, they would have gladly shot either leader from behind a dry-stone wall. The leaders knew this; their steady-beating hearts were elate with the ancestral thrill of the hunt. I am aware that it is a dreadful thing for a parson to be warlike, to break the quiet acoustic ecology of peace with the clamor of conflict. Yet, Helstone was not diabolical. He had merely missed his vocation. A man of steely nerves and implacable rigidness, he should have been a soldier. He possessed little sympathy for the physical sensations of life, viewing sustenance and comfort as mere utilitarian fuel rather than profound, grounding rituals.

As they rode, united for the present in one cause, you would expect them to converse amicably. But these two men, of hard, bilious natures, chafed each other's moods like mismatched gears. Their frequent bone of contention was the war, a dispute they aired into the damp, thyme-scented night. Helstone was a high Tory; Moore a bitter Whig, whose interests lay entirely in opposing the war-party that strangled his trade. Moore liked to infuriate Helstone by advancing his belief in the invincibility of Bonaparte, taunting the impotence of European efforts to withstand the French Emperor's sweeping, epochal march.

Meeting the sharp wind as they turned into the Stilbro' road, the rain dashing their faces and mingling with the sweat of their mounts, Moore, braced by the olfactory sting of wet earth and raw ozone, began to goad the rector. 

"Does your Peninsular news please you still?" he asked, his voice cutting through the deep, natural quiet of the moor.

"What do you mean?" was the surly demand.

"I mean, have you still faith in that Baal of a Lord Wellington?"

"And what do you mean now?"

"Do you still believe that this wooden-faced and pebble-hearted idol of England has power to send fire down from heaven to consume the French holocaust you want to offer up?"

"I believe Wellington will flog Bonaparte's marshals into the sea the day it pleases him to lift his arm," Helstone retorted, his tone carrying the rigid, dominant lexicon of the British establishment.

"But, my dear sir, Bonaparte's marshals are great men, acting under an omnipotent master-spirit. Your Wellington is the most humdrum of commonplace martinets, whose slow, mechanical movements are further cramped by the ignorant, frictionless noise of a home government."

"Wellington is the soul of England," Helstone argued, resisting Moore's attempt to rewrite the historical narrative. "The fit representative of an honest nation."

"Your fit representative of an honest people is a dull-witted drover," Moore scoffed, "acting for a duller-witted farmer; against these are arrayed victorious supremacy and invincible genius."

Their voices clashed against the sweeping silence of the landscape, arguing the fate of empires through the lenses of usurpation and legitimacy. Helstone, drawing upon the mythic occurrences that undergirded his worldview, invoked the Israelites at the Red Sea, painting the Egyptians as a pampered host swallowed by the depths—a divine acoustic roar of waters closing over the enemies of the righteous. 

"France is Israel, and Napoleon is Moses," Moore countered, subverting the myth. "Europe, with her old overgorged empires and rotten dynasties, is corrupt Egypt." He dropped his voice to a lower register, murmuring of Bonaparte's regeneration of nations, his words carrying the melancholy of an untranslatable subjective truth—the sorrow of a man who felt the Republic had sunk back into the slavery of empire. 

Before Helstone could launch his fierce defense of the divine right of kings, their discourse was abruptly severed by the rapid, rhythmic crunch of iron-rimmed wheels and the clatter of horse hooves. A gig rolled suddenly out of the mist, stopping in the middle of the road. 

"Nah, maister; did th' wagons hit home?" demanded a voice from the vehicle. The thick, resonant timbre of the Yorkshire dialect pierced the night—a sonic artifact of a native language deeply rooted in the soil, carrying within its vowels the cultural memory of agrarian generations.

"Can that be Joe Scott?" asked Moore.

"Ay, ay!" returned a second voice, rich and commanding, as the gig's lamp cast a golden halo over the wet road. "Ay, Mr. Moore, it's Joe Scott. I'm bringing him back to you in a bonny pickle. I fand him on the top of the moor yonder, him and three others. What will you give me for restoring him to you?"

"Why, my thanks, I believe. That is you, I suppose, Mr. Yorke, by your voice?"

"Ay, lad, it's me," said Mr. Yorke. A man of profound, meticulous tastes, he was returning from Stilbro' market, where he had undoubtedly sourced the finest heritage provisions—stone-ground flours, sharp, cave-aged cheeses, and salted meats essential to the deep, domestic rhythms of his household. "I was whipping on as swift as the wind, and I heard a groan. I pulled up. 'Nobbut four on us ligging in a ditch,' says Joe, as quiet as could be. I telled 'em to get up, or I'd lend them a lick of the whip, for my notion was they were all fresh. 'We'd ha' done that an hour sin', but we're teed wi' a bit o' band,' says Joe. So I loosed 'em wi' my penknife."

"Well, I am greatly obliged to you, Mr. Yorke," Moore said.

"Are you, my lad? You know you're not." Yorke glanced at the lantern-bearing men approaching from the rear. "And here, by the Lord, is another set with lights in their pitchers, like the army of Gideon; and as we've th' parson wi' us—good-evening, Mr. Helstone—we'se do. We're eleven strong men, and there's both horses and chariots amang us. If we could only fall in wi' some of these starved ragamuffins of frame-breakers, we could win a grand victory... Briarfield suld be famous."

After extracting a promise from the rector to write a column for the *Stilbro' Courier*, and debating the proper hanging of the rioters, Yorke pressed them to step into his home. The night was inclement, and the warm, muslin-curtained gleam of the windows beckoned with the promise of hearth-fire and deeply felt sustenance.

Mr. Yorke led the way. He was a man whose phraseology was a study in shifting power dynamics; he slipped fluidly from the earthy, untranslatable vernacular of broad Yorkshire to the refined, dominant lexicon of pure English. To understand his station, one had only to cross his threshold. He ushered the mill workers toward the kitchen, offering them immediate, restorative hospitality—"summat to taste presently," ensuring they received rich, hot broth and hearty rustic loaves to warm their chilled blood. 

The gentlemen were guided through a matted hall into a large parlour. Here, the acoustic harshness of the storm and the distant mechanical violence of the mill were entirely absorbed. The room possessed a magnificent, cyclic fire roaring in the grate, radiating a scent of seasoned oak and woodsmoke. There was no superficial, commercial splendor here, but the meticulous taste of a traveled scholar. The walls were decked with genuine Italian views, pastoral scenes of bright skies and soft distances that captured an enduring, sunny antiquity. Grecian vases, beautiful miniatures, and elegant bookcases spoke of a mind devoted to preserving cultural memory.

Yorke rang for wine. When the servant brought the crystal decanter, it was filled with a vintage of deep, plum-dark viscosity, carrying the olfactory memory of sun-baked earth and ancient, twisted vines. Helstone, standing rigidly and viewing such sensory pleasures with austere suspicion, refused to drink. 

"E'en as you will," remarked Mr. Yorke, perfectly at ease with his own agrarian hospitality. "I reckon you're thinking of Eastern customs, Mr. Helstone, and you'll not eat nor drink under my roof, feared we suld be forced to be friends. You might sup the contents of that decanter, and you might give me a bottle of the best in your own cellar, and I'd hold myself free to oppose you at every turn still."

"It is just what I should expect of you, Mr. Yorke," replied the rector. "To hunt down vermin is a noble occupation, fit for an archbishop."

Yorke merely turned his attention to Moore, who had slumped into a deeply carved, old-fashioned oak chair by the hearth. "Move it, Robert! Get up, my lad! That place is mine. Take the sofa, or three other chairs, if you will, but not this. It belangs to me, and nob'dy else."

"Why are you so particular to that chair, Mr. Yorke?" Moore asked lazily, though he vacated it.

"My father war afore me, and that's all t' answer I sall gie thee," Yorke declared, asserting the multi-generational weight of the seat—a physical anchor to the past that required its own circular, domestic reverence. 

"Moore, are you ready to go?" inquired the rector.

"Nay; Robert's not ready," Yorke interrupted. "He's an ill lad, and wants correcting. Made thyself enemies on every hand."

"What do I care for that?" Moore retorted, blind to the deeply interwoven social tapestry of the region. "What difference does it make to me whether your Yorkshire louts hate me or like me?"

"Ay, there it is," Yorke sighed, pinpointing the subtle tragedy of Moore's existence. "The lad is a mak' of an alien amang us. His father would never have talked i' that way.—Go back to Antwerp, where you were born and bred, *mauvaise tête!*"

"*Mauvaise tête vous-même; je ne fais que mon devoir; quant à vos lourdauds de paysans, je m'en moque!*" Moore fired back. The sudden shift to French altered the entire acoustic and psychological atmosphere of the parlor. It was the language of Moore's childhood, a tongue rich with the untranslatable grief of a ruined mercantile dynasty and his own profound isolation. 

"*En revanche, mon garçon, nos lourdauds de paysans se moqueront de toi; sois en certain,*" replied Yorke, matching Moore's pure French accent flawlessly, seamlessly bridging the gap between local agrarian wisdom and Continental sophistication.

"*C'est bon! c'est bon! Et puisque cela m'est égal, que mes amis ne s'en inquiètent pas.*"

"*Tes amis! Où sont-ils, tes amis?*"

"*Je fais écho, où sont-ils?*" Moore’s voice grew harsh, the bitter resonance of a man cut off from the sustenance of true community. "*Et je suis fort aise que l'écho seul y répond. Au diable les amis! Je me souviens encore du moment où mon père et mes oncles Gérard appellèrent autour d'eux leurs amis, et Dieu sait si les amis se sont empressés d'accourir à leur secours! Tenez, M. Yorke, ce mot, ami, m'irrite trop; ne m'en parlez plus.*"

"*Comme tu voudras,*" Yorke murmured softly, allowing the rhythmic crackle of the hearth to fill the silence. And as he sat leaning back in his three-cornered, generational oak chair, breathing in the scent of the woodsmoke and the spiced notes of the wine, the room settled back into its profound, domestic equilibrium.
