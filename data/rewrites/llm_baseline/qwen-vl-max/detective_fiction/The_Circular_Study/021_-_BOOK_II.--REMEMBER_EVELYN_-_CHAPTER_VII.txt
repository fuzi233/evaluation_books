A few minutes later, Mr. Gryce stood in the outer room, his eyes scanning the gathered figures with quiet intensity. He was searching for answers—answers to a question that still lingered, unresolved, in the depths of his mind. And he found them not in grand declarations or dramatic confessions, but in the quiet corner where Miss Butterworth sat, composed and unflustered, as though she had merely stepped aside from a long-expected role.

He approached her with a subtle smile. “I suspect you were behind this entire arrangement,” he said, half-joking, half-serious.

She looked up, her expression firm. “I was simply present,” she replied, with a hint of indignation. “When the moment came—when that young woman learned her husband had vanished into New York with two strangers—it became clear that someone must be at her side who understood both her plight and the gravity of her situation. And I was that person.”

“You are always that person,” he said warmly. “If by ‘that person’ one means the woman who arrives precisely when needed, in perfect measure and timing. So you’ve already heard Mrs. Adams’s story?”

“Yes,” she answered, her voice softening. “Her words, spoken in anguish, revealed everything. She was the one who wielded the dagger that ended Mr. Adams’s life. But afterward, when reason returned, she explained what had driven her—what made such an act not only possible, but necessary. When I heard it, Mr. Gryce, I no longer doubted either her right to act or my duty to stand beside her. Do you think she will face trial? Will she be charged? Convicted?”

Mr. Gryce shook his head gently. “Madam, there are not twelve men in this city so lacking in judgment that they would label an act of self-defense as crime. No grand jury will return a true bill against young Mrs. Adams. You may rest assured.”

The shadow that had clouded Miss Butterworth’s features lifted instantly. “Then I may go home in peace,” she declared, almost breathlessly. “These past five hours have been harrowing. I feel thoroughly shaken. Would you do me the kindness of escorting me to my carriage?”

Though she spoke of being shaken, Mr. Gryce saw no sign of it. To him, she appeared more serene than ever—calm, collected, even radiant in her quiet strength. Yet she knew herself best, and once satisfied that Mrs. Adams was recovering under her husband’s care, she accepted his arm without hesitation.

As they walked, he asked about Mr. Poindexter. “Why isn’t Mrs. Adams’s father here? Did he permit his daughter to venture into such danger without offering to accompany her?”

Her reply was sharp, cutting. “Mr. Poindexter is a man devoid of feeling. He came with us to New York, but refused to follow us to Police Headquarters. Sir, you’ll find that three hearts consumed by passion—and a revenge more deeply held than any I’ve ever known—were all wasted on a man incapable of suffering. Do not speak of John Poindexter to me again. And now—if you would be so kind—tell the coachman to take me home to Gramercy Park. I have stirred the police pot for the final time, Mr. Gryce. For the very last time.”

She settled back into the carriage cushions, her expression stern, resolute—yet beneath it, a quiet satisfaction gleamed, unmistakable. It was the look of one who had done what was right, even if it defied convention, and who now felt no regret.

Mr. Gryce, suppressing a smile, bowed lower than usual—a gesture of respect, perhaps, or farewell. Under the shadow of that bow, the carriage rolled away.

Alone again, he turned slowly toward the house, a faint sigh escaping his lips. Was he wondering whether another case—another mystery—would bring them together again? Whether fate would once more place him beside the calm, courageous woman who never hesitated to step into the shadows?
