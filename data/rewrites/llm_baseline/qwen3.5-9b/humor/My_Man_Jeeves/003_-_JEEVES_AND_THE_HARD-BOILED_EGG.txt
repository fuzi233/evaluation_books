Sometimes, in the quiet hours of a morning, as I sit in bed nursing my first cup of tea and watch my man Jeeves darting about the room arranging my clothes for the day, I've found myself wondering what on earth I'd do if the fellow ever decided to pack his bags and leave me. It's not such a terrifying prospect now that I'm in New York, but back in London, the anxiety was absolute terror. There were constant attempts by low-life scoundrels to poach him. I know for a fact that young Reggie Foljambe once offered him double my salary, and Alistair Bingham-Reeves—who possesses a valet famous for pressing trousers sideways—used to gaze at Jeeves with a hungry, glittering eye whenever he visited. Those bally pirates!

The truth of the matter is that Jeeves is simply dashing competent. You can spot it in the way he pushes a stud into a shirt collar.

I rely on him absolutely in every crisis, and he never fails me. Furthermore, he is always ready to stretch a leg for any friend of mine who finds themselves knee-deep in soup. Take the rather peculiar case of dear old Bicky and his uncle, the hard-boiled egg.

This affair transpired after I had been in America for a few months. One late night, returning to the flat, Jeeves brought me my final drink and announced:

"Mr. Bickersteth called to see you this evening, sir, while you were out."

"Oh?" I replied.

"Twice, sir. He appeared slightly agitated."

"What, pipped?"

"He gave that impression, sir."

I sipped my whisky. I felt sorry for Bicky if he was in trouble, but honestly, I was rather glad to have a topic to discuss freely with Jeeves. Things had been a bit strained between us lately, making it difficult to find anything to talk about that wouldn't turn personal. You see, I had decided—rightly or wrongly—to grow a moustache, and this had cut Jeeves to the quick. He couldn't abide the thing at any price, and I had lived ever since in an atmosphere of bally disapproval until I was thoroughly fed up. While Jeeves's judgment on matters of dress is undeniably sound, it seemed excessive if he was going to edit my face as well as my wardrobe. No one can call me unreasonable; I've often submitted like a lamb when Jeeves vetoed a favorite suit or tie. But when a valet stakes a claim on your upper lip, you simply have to summon the good old bulldog spirit and defy the blighter.

"He said he would call again later, sir."

"Something must be up, Jeeves."

"Yes, sir."

I gave the moustache a thoughtful twirl. It clearly hurt Jeeves' feelings, so I shaved it off.

"I see by the paper, sir, that Mr. Bickersteth's uncle is arriving on the *Carmantic*."

"Yes?"

"His Grace the Duke of Chiswick, sir."

News to me, that Bicky's uncle was a duke. How little one knows about one's friends! I had met Bicky for the first time at a sort of jamboree in Washington Square, shortly after my arrival in New York. Perhaps I was homesick, and I rather liked him when I discovered he was English and had, in fact, attended Oxford with me. Besides, he was a frightful chump, so we naturally drifted together. While we took a quiet swig in a corner not cluttered with artists and sculptors, he endeared himself to me with an extraordinarily gifted imitation of a bull-terrier chasing a cat up a tree. Though we became close friends, all I truly knew about him was that he was generally broke, with an uncle who occasionally sent monthly remittances to relieve the strain.

"If the Duke of Chiswick is his uncle," I said, "why doesn't he have a title? Why isn't he Lord What-Not?"

"Mr. Bickersteth is the son of his Grace's late sister, sir, who married Captain Rollo Bickersteth of the Coldstream Guards."

Jeeves knows everything.

"Is Mr. Bickersteth's father dead, too?"

"Yes, sir."

"Left any money?"

"No, sir."

I began to understand why poor old Bicky was perpetually on the rocks. To the casual observer, having a duke for an uncle sounds like a clever scheme. The trouble with old Chiswick, however, was that although he was an extremely wealthy old buster owning half of London and about five northern counties, he was notoriously the most prudent spender in England. He was what American chaps would call a hard-boiled egg. If Bicky's family hadn't left him anything and he relied solely on what he could squeeze out of the old duke, he was in deep water. That didn't explain why he was hunting me down, though, because Bicky was a chap who never borrowed money. He claimed he wanted to keep his pals, so he never bit anyone's ear off on principle.

Just then, the doorbell rang. Jeeves floated out to answer it.

"Yes, sir. Mr. Wooster has just returned," I heard him say. And Bicky trickled in, looking dreadfully sorry for himself.

"Halloa, Bicky!" I said. "Jeeves told me you were trying to get me. Jeeves, bring another glass, and let the revels commence. What's the trouble, Bicky?"

"I'm in a hole, Bertie. I want your advice."

"Say on, old lad!"

"My uncle's turning up tomorrow, Bertie."

"So Jeeves told me."

"The Duke of Chiswick, you know."

"So Jeeves told me."

Bicky seemed a bit surprised.

"Jeeves seems to know everything."

"Rather rummily, that's exactly what I was thinking just now myself."

"Well, I wish," said Bicky gloomily, "that he knew a way to get me out of the hole I'm in."

Jeeves shimmered in with the glass and placed it competently on the table.

"Mr. Bickersteth is in a bit of a hole, Jeeves," I said, "and wants you to rally round."

"Very good, sir."

Bicky looked doubtful.

"Well, of course, you know, Bertie, this thing is by way of being a bit private and all that."

"I shouldn't worry about that, old top. I bet Jeeves knows all about it already. Don't you, Jeeves?"

"Yes, sir."

"Eh!" said Bicky, rattled.

"I am open to correction, sir, but is not your dilemma due to the fact that you are at a loss to explain to His Grace why you are in New York instead of in Colorado?"

Bicky rocked like jelly in a high wind.

"How the deuce do you know anything about it?"

"I chanced to meet His Grace's butler before we left England. He informed me that he happened to overhear His Grace speaking to you on the matter, sir, as he passed the library door."

Bicky gave a hollow sort of laugh.

"Well, as everybody seems to know all about it, there's no need to try to keep it dark. The old boy turfed me out, Bertie, because he said I was a brainless nincompoop. The idea was that he would give me a remittance on condition that I dashed out to some blighted locality named Colorado and learned farming or ranching, or whatever they call it, at some bally ranch. I didn't fancy the idea a bit. I should have had to ride horses and pursue cows. I hate horses. They bite at you. I was all against the scheme. At the same time, don't you know, I had to have that remittance."

"I get you absolutely, dear boy."

"Well, when I got to New York it looked like a decent place, so I thought it would be a sound notion to stop here. So I cabled my uncle telling him I'd dropped into a good business wheeze in the city and wanted to chuck the ranch idea. He wrote back saying it was all right, and here I've been ever since. He thinks I'm doing well at something or other over here. I never dreamed, don't you know, that he would ever come out here. What on earth am I to do?"

"Jeeves," I said, "what on earth is Mr. Bickersteth to do?"

"You see," said Bicky, "I had a wireless from him saying he was coming to stay with me—to save hotel bills, I suppose. I've always given him the impression that I was living in pretty good style. I can't have him stay at my boarding-house."

"Thought of anything, Jeeves?" I asked.

"To what extent, sir, if the question is not a delicate one, are you prepared to assist Mr. Bickersteth?"

"I'll do anything I can for you, of course, Bicky, old man."

"Then, if I might make the suggestion, sir, you might lend Mr. Bickersteth——"
