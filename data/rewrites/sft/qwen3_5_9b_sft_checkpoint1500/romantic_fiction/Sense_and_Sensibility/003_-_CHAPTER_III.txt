Mrs. Dashwood stayed at Norland for nearly four months, not because she lacked the desire to leave the place where every familiar corner still stung her with grief, but because once her sorrow began to dull, her heart grew restless for change. She was eager to find a home nearby, refusing to imagine living far from the garden where her husband's ashes lay. Yet, despite her endless search, she found no house that balanced comfort with the strict financial caution of her eldest daughter, Margaret. While Mrs. Dashwood would have welcomed grandeur, Margaret's steady mind rejected any estate that strained their limited income.

Throughout this time, Mrs. Dashwood clung to a hope planted by her late husband: a solemn promise from his son, John, to provide generously for her and her girls. Though she privately believed seven thousand pounds fell short of true affluence, she held onto John's word as a lifeline. It brought her joy to think of her children supported, and it softened her regret over having previously underestimated her son-in-law's capacity for kindness. Now, seeing the care John took with his father's memory, she forgave her past suspicions.

Yet, the shadow of Mrs. John Dashwood loomed larger than ever. Having resided with her for six months, Mrs. Dashwood's initial dislike had deepened into a firm conviction that the two women could never coexist. However, fate intervened with a silver lining: a growing romance between her eldest daughter, Elinor, and Edward Ferrars, the brother of Mrs. John Dashwood.

Edward was a gentle soul, though not striking in appearance or manner. He possessed a shy reserve that made him seem unassuming, but beneath that exterior beat a warm, affectionate heart. Unlike his ambitious mother, who dreamed of placing him in Parliament, or his sister, who longed for him to drive a carriage through high society, Edward contented himself with quiet domesticity. He had no flair for politics or grand gestures; his world was small, safe, and filled with the simple comforts of home.

For a long time, Mrs. Dashwood barely noticed him, lost in her own mourning. But one afternoon, a remark from Elinor shifted her perspective entirely. Comparing Edward to his vivacious sister, Fanny, Elinor noted how utterly different they were. "It is enough," Mrs. Dashwood declared, her face lighting up, "to say that he is unlike Fanny. That implies everything amiable. I love him already."

"I think you will like him better when you know him fully," Elinor suggested gently.

"Like him!" her mother laughed, a sound rare in those somber days. "I feel no lesser emotion than love."

"You may merely esteem him."

"I have never learned to separate esteem from love."

From that moment, Mrs. Dashwood threw herself into learning about Edward. Her warmth quickly melted away his shyness, revealing a man of solid understanding and genuine feeling. She saw clearly that he cared deeply for Elinor, and as her affection for him grew, so did her certainty of his worth. The quietness of his nature, once puzzling, now seemed like proof of a sincere and steady character. Soon, she envisioned their future together, speaking of their marriage with joyful anticipation.

"My dear Marianne," she told her youngest daughter, "soon Elinor will be settled for life. Yes, we shall miss her dearly, but she will be happy."

"Oh, Mama! How shall we bear it?" Marianne cried, tears welling in her eyes.

"It will feel less like a separation," her mother reassured her, smiling. "We shall live within miles of each other, meeting daily. And you will gain a brother—a truly affectionate one."

But Marianne, ever the dreamer, looked grave. "Perhaps I view it with surprise," she admitted. "Edward is kind, and I admire him. Yet... he lacks something. His figure is plain, lacking the grace I expect in a man who captures my sister's heart. His eyes lack spirit and fire, the signs of true virtue and intellect. Worse still, I fear he has no real taste. Music seems to hold little sway over him, and while he praises Elinor's drawings, it is the admiration of a lover, not a connoisseur. He knows nothing of art, only of affection."

Marianne sighed, her voice trembling with frustration. "To satisfy me, a man must possess both virtue and beauty. His tastes must mirror mine perfectly—the same books, the same melodies. Last night, when he read those lines that usually drive me wild, his voice was so flat, so indifferent! I felt a sharp pang for my sister, though she endured it with such serene composure. I could barely sit still. To hear such beautiful words spoken with such cold detachment..."

"He would surely have done justice to simpler prose," Mrs. Dashwood interjected wisely. "And yes, you asked him to read Cowper. But let us allow for differences in taste. Elinor does not share your fiery passions, and thus she may overlook his shortcomings. If you loved him, however, hearing him read with such lack of sensitivity would indeed break your heart. My love, remember that you are not yet seventeen. There is still time to find the perfect partner."

"And if I do not?" Marianne whispered, looking out the window as if searching for a vision of the ideal. "The more I learn of the world, the more convinced I become that I shall never find a man worthy of my love. I require so much! He must unite every virtue of Edward Ferrars with the charm of his person and manners."
