It’s a curious thing, but life seems to take a peculiar delight in thwarting me. Recently, I resolved to write an article on a topic so fresh, so untouched by human discourse, that the world would gasp at its novelty. "This," I told myself, "will be my masterpiece—something no one has ever dared to address before!" For days, I racked my brain. Nothing.

Then our charwoman, Mrs. Cutting—a woman of stern moral fiber who reads only *Lloyd’s Weekly News* and the Bible (all else being frivolous and possibly sinful)—observed my distress. "Lor’, sir," she said, "you look downright tormented." I confessed my quest for an unprecedented subject, one that would dazzle humanity with its originality. She laughed. Naturally. When I am serious, people assume I’m joking. When I *do* joke, they stare blankly.

Take last week. I had crafted what I considered a masterstroke of humor—a long, winding anecdote about an incident in Whitechapel, artfully dropped into a conversation about Shakespeare’s views on the Reformation. I set the stage, delivered the punchline with a flourish, and… silence. Eventually, someone asked, "Was *that* the joke?" I confirmed it was. They nodded politely. One elderly gentleman, bless him, needed a full dissection of which part was meant to be funny.

Meanwhile, I know a fellow who laughs at *everything*. Tell him his house is on fire, and he’ll howl with mirth. Ask him the time, and he collapses in stitches. You must preface serious remarks with, "This is *not* amusing," or he’ll dissolve into giggles at the mere mention of the weather.

But back to Mrs. Cutting. After rejecting her first proposal ("samplers"—apparently all the rage in her youth), she suggested the weather. A dismal, overdone topic—yet from that moment, it clung to my thoughts like mud to a boot.

And what wretched stuff it is! We are never satisfied. Summer scorches us, winter freezes us, spring and autumn are accused of indecisiveness. If it’s dry, we demand rain; if it rains, we curse the damp. Snow? A nuisance. No snow? A scandal. We behave as though the skies have personally swindled us.

In the countryside, weather has its charms. Snow transforms the world into a fairy tale; frosty mornings ring with laughter; spring is a bashful maiden, summer a drowsy hum, autumn a melancholy feast of color. Even rain, in the fields, has purpose.

But in the city? Weather is a tramp in a ballroom—unwelcome and grubby. Rain turns streets into sludge, snow into sooty mounds, and the east wind into a shrieking demon. Our neighbor insists this downpour is "good for the crops," though his agricultural expertise extends no further than last summer’s ill-fated cucumber frame. Meanwhile, my new spring suit—once a vision—now resembles a mud-splattered sail.

Ah, the suit. I blame Jim. The moment he saw it, he declared it a public duty to parade it before "the girls." "Like a day in the country for them," he said. By evening’s end, I was in bed with whisky, gruel, and a mustard plaster. The suit? A casualty of Jim’s altruism.

Mud adores me. While others glide through grime unscathed, I emerge looking like a half-drowned chimney sweep. If London had but one puddle, I would find it—and fall in it.

April’s fickleness is another grievance. Poets rhapsodize about its capriciousness, comparing it to a coquette. Charming in metaphor; less so when you’ve been drenched, sunburned, and blown inside-out within an hour. My "automaton" umbrella—a device that opened and shut at whim—was the final insult. It jammed in downpours, then sprang open beneath clear skies, leaving me the lunatic under a parasol in sunshine.

And let us not discuss lost hats. Few spectacles rival a man chasing his hat while a gleeful terrier assists, children scatter, and a sweep receives an accidental embrace.

Spring in the city is a cruel jest. The season of renewal is lost on grimy streets and grubbier urchins. True spring belongs to the hills, the moors, the open sky—where the wind carries whispers of something vast and wild beyond our little lives.

But here? It’s just rain. And mud. And a ruined suit.
