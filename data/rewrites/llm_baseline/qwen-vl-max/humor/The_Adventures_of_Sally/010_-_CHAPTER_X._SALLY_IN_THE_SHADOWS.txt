1

For Sally, the weeks after reuniting with Ginger Kemp felt like a golden age—though not one of those ancient Greek kinds where people lived forever on mountains and ate ambrosia. This was more like a golden age of *furnishing*, which, as any woman knows, is far more exhausting but also infinitely more satisfying.

Peace reigned in her little kingdom. Every frontier—from her apartment’s front door to the back of her mind—was calm. She woke each morning to a world so neatly arranged that even a grumpy philosopher might have been tempted to smile. True, Gerald was still a thousand miles away, supervising the Chicago premiere of *The Primrose Way*—a project that had drawn him into the chaotic theater world like a magnet pulling a paperclip into a junkyard. Fillmore, bless his optimistic heart, had agreed to Ike’s idea of launching it in Chicago first, and now Gerald was stuck there, fixing the mess left by some playwright who clearly hadn’t read the manual on comedy. So much for coming home in a few days. But Sally refused to let that ruin her mood. Life, overall, was too good to waste on minor disappointments.

Life, in fact, was practically perfect. Fillmore was thriving. Ginger was off her conscience (and no longer lurking around like a haunted garden gnome). She’d found an apartment. Her new hat? Flawless. And *The Primrose Way*? It was selling tickets faster than a fire sale at a department store during a blizzard. According to Fillmore, Chicago had forgotten its entire history—its founding, its fire, its famous hot dogs—because all anyone could think about was getting into the theater. The production, he claimed, had stirred Chicago more than the Great Fire itself. Which, given how many buildings burned down then, was saying something.

But the best thing of all? Ginger had a job.

Not just any job. A real, legitimate, paid position at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. (Managing Director: Fillmore Nicholas). The brass plate was small, but the title was big. Ginger now occupied the outer office, where his duties mostly involved reading newspapers and preventing mobs from storming the inner sanctum where Fillmore sat like a king on a throne made of paperwork, dreaming up schemes that would bankrupt entire countries.

Ginger wasn’t entirely sure what he actually did. Sometimes he felt like the captain of the ship. Other times, like a glorified errand boy—only less glorified, since he didn’t get tips. His main job was keeping people out, which required both strength and diplomacy. He once tried to explain it to Sally.

“Honestly,” he said, pausing mid-lift while carrying a chair that weighed about as much as a small pony, “if I didn’t sweat a bit helping you after the way you got me this job…”

“Ginger,” Sally interrupted, “desist.”

“But honestly—”

“If you don’t stop, I’ll make you move that chair into the next room.”

“Shall I?” Ginger wiped his brow and gripped the chair again. “Anything you say.”

“Silly! Of course not. The only other rooms are my bedroom, bathroom, and kitchen. What on earth would I want a great lumbering chair in them for? Besides, I think we should’ve gone with the first spot.”

“Back she goes, then?”

Sally frowned. Setting up house was turning out to be harder than she expected. She’d always dreamed of a place where she could lock the door and breathe freely. Now she had it—but something felt… off.

“Hullo!” she said suddenly. “Where’s that photograph of me? I’m sure I put it on the mantelpiece yesterday.”

Ginger, flushed from exertion, looked hard.

“No photograph here.”

“I know there isn’t. But it was there yesterday. Or was it? I meant to. It’s the most beautiful photo ever taken. Not a bit like me—thank goodness—but it looks the way I’d look if I could afford a better face. They touch it up in the darkroom. I value it because it’s aspirational.”

“I’ve never had a beautiful photograph taken,” Ginger said solemnly, eyes downcast.

“Cheer up!”

“Oh, I don’t mind. I just thought—”

“Ginger,” Sally said, cutting in, “pardon my interrupting your melancholy reflections—which I know are deeply meaningful—but this chair is *not* right. It needs to go back where it started. Could you give your imitation of a pack-mule one more time? And then I’ll make tea—if there’s any tea, milk, or cups.”

“There are cups,” Ginger said proudly. “I know, because I smashed two the day before yesterday. I’ll nip round the corner for milk, shall I?”

“Yes, please nip. All this hard work has drained me terribly.”

Over tea, Sally became curious.

“What I can’t understand about your job—your noble job, which I generously secured for you—is the amount of leisure it seems to offer. How do you manage to spend your valuable time—Fillmore’s valuable time—juggling furniture every day?”

“Oh, I usually get off,” Ginger said casually.

“But shouldn’t you be doing… whatever it is you do?”

Ginger stirred his tea thoughtfully.

“Well, I sort of mess about. Interview divers blighters, tell ’em your brother’s out, take their names and addresses… oh, all that sort of thing.”

“Does Fillmore consult you much?”

“He lets me read some of the plays sent in. Awful tosh, mostly. Sometimes he sends me to vaudeville houses.”

“As a treat?”

“To see a special act. Report on it. In case he wants to use it for his revue.”

“Which revue?”

“Didn’t you know he’s putting on a revue? Oh, rather. A whacking big affair. Going to cut out the Follies and all that.”

Sally blinked.

“My goodness. That’s ambitious.”

She was alarmed. Fillmore had a habit of leaping into projects that required millions when he should’ve been counting pennies. He was the kind of man who saw a puddle and imagined a swimming pool. An inexhaustible fount of optimism bubbled inside him, and it often led to trouble.

“It won’t do,” she said firmly. “And I’ll tell you another thing that won’t do. This armchair. It belongs by the window. You can see that, can’t you?”

“Absolutely!” Ginger said, already bracing himself.

2

Sally’s concern only deepened when she received a telegram from Miss Winch in Chicago:

She didn’t fully understand it, but the tone was ominous. It sounded like a warning from a concerned aunt—or possibly a mad scientist. Either way, she decided to investigate immediately. She abandoned the joys of interior design and marched straight to the headquarters of Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. (Managing Director: Fillmore Nicholas).

Ginger was absent. In his place stood a pimply youth who thawed instantly upon hearing Sally’s name and ushered her in with a flourish. She walked right in—and found Fillmore with his feet propped on a cluttered desk, studying costume designs like they were sacred scrolls.

“Ah, Sally!” he said, voice weary with the weight of destiny. Prosperity had done its work. His jawline, once sharp and heroic, now bore the faint suggestion of a double chin. He wore a new waistcoat—unbuttoned, naturally. “I am rather busy. Always glad to see you, but I am rather busy. A hundred things to attend to.”

“Well, attend to me. That makes a hundred and one. Fill, what’s this I hear about a revue?”

Fillmore looked like a child caught stealing jam. He’d been dreading this moment. He’d hoped Sally wouldn’t find out until the revue was so far along that stopping it would be impossible. He loved Sally, but he knew she had a dangerous streak of caution. Women, he believed, were meant to admire, not interfere.

“Oh yes, the revue!” he said, trying to sound casual.

“It’s no good saying ‘Oh yes’! You know perfectly well it’s a crazy idea.”

“Really… these business matters… this interference…”

“I don’t want to run your affairs, Fill, but that money of mine does make me a partner, I suppose. And I think I have a right to scream when I see you risking it on a—”

“Pardon me,” Fillmore interrupted, puffing out his chest. “Let me explain. Women never understand business. Your money is tied up exclusively in *The Primrose Way*, which, as you know, is a tremendous success. You have nothing to worry about.”

“I’m not worrying about the money. I’m worrying about *you*.”

A tolerant smile played across Fillmore’s face.

“Don’t be alarmed. I’m all right.”

“You aren’t all right. You’ve no business rushing into a huge production like this when you’re just starting out. You can’t afford it.”

“My dear child,” he said, “women cannot understand these things. A man in my position can always command money for a new venture.”

“Do you mean someone actually agreed to fund it?”

“Certainly. No secret about it. Your friend Mr. Carmyle has taken an interest in some of my forthcoming productions.”

“What!” Sally gasped.

Bruce Carmyle. The man seemed to be seeping into her life like a slow-moving oil spill. Everywhere she turned, there he was—uninvited, unasked, and always just slightly creepy. She could do nothing but rage helplessly. The situation was becoming unbearable.

Fillmore misread her horror.

“It’s quite all right,” he assured her. “He’s very rich. Large private means, besides his income. Even if anything goes wrong…”

“It isn’t that. It’s…”

Sally stopped. Explaining anything to Fillmore was like trying to teach a squirrel calculus. While she fumed, voices erupted from the outer office. Ginger’s understudy was trying to keep someone out—unsuccessfully.

The door burst open, and Miss Winch swept in like a hurricane with a gum wrapper.

“Fillmore, you poor nut,” she declared, “stop picking straws out of your hair and listen to me. You’re dippy!”

Sally had last seen Miss Winch in Detroit, where she’d seemed calm, composed, and utterly unshakeable. Now, she was animated—almost frantic. Something serious must have happened.

“Ah! Here you are!” Fillmore said, jumping up like a lion startled by a bee. Then he calmed down when he saw who it was.

“Yes, here I am!” Miss Winch dropped into a swivel chair and chewed a stick of gum with fierce determination. “Fillmore, darling, you’re the sweetest thing on earth, and I love you, but on present form you could walk straight into Bloomingdale and they’d give you the royal suite.”

“My dear girl…”

“What do you think?” Miss Winch asked Sally.

“I’ve just been telling him,” Sally said, relieved to have an ally, “that it’s absurd to launch a massive revue at this stage.”

“Revue?” Miss Winch froze mid-chew. “What revue?” She threw up her hands. “I shall have to swallow this gum. You can’t chew with your head spinning. Are you putting on a revue too?”

Fillmore was buttoning and unbuttoning his waistcoat again. He looked hunted.

“Certainly,” he said feverishly. “I wish you girls would leave me to manage…”

“Dippy!” Miss Winch snapped. “Telegraphic address: Tea-Pot, Matteawan.” She turned to Sally. “Say, listen! This boy must be stopped. We need a gang to save him. What do you think he plans to do? I’ll give you three guesses. Oh, what’s the use? You’d never hit it. This poor wandering lad has decided to star *me*—*me*—in a new show!”

Fillmore waved a hand.

“I used my own judgment…”

“Yes, sir!” Miss Winch roared over him. “That’s exactly what he’s planning to spring on the public. I’m sitting peacefully in my hotel room in Chicago, eating scrambled eggs and reading the paper, when the phone rings. Gentleman below would like to see me. Oh, ask him to wait. I throw on some clothes. Down in the elevator. Bright sunrise effects in the lobby.”

“What on earth do you mean?”

“The gentleman had a head of red hair that lit up the whole lobby,” Miss Winch explained. “Management had turned off the lights to save money. An Englishman. Nice fellow. Named Kemp.”

“Oh, is Ginger in Chicago?” Sally asked. “I wondered why he wasn’t at his chair.”

“I sent Kemp to Chicago,” Fillmore said, “to check the show. It’s my policy to send a representative when I can’t go myself.”

“Save it for winter evenings,” Miss Winch cut in. “Mr. Kemp may have been there to see the show, but his real mission was to tell me to come back to New York and enter my kingdom. Fillmore wanted me here so I could sit in this office and interview my supporting cast. Me! Can you tie it?”

“Well…” Sally hesitated.

“Don’t say it! I know it just as well as you do. It’s too sad for words.”

“You persist in underestimating your abilities, Gladys,” Fillmore said reproachfully. “I’ve seen a lot of acting. As a character actress, you…”

Miss Winch rose, kissed him fiercely, and sat back down. She pulled out another stick of gum, then sighed and put it back.

“You’re a darling old thing,” she said, “but honestly, Fillmore, dear, step out of the padded cell for a second and listen. I know exactly what’s going on in your head. You took Elsa Doland out of a minor part and made her a star overnight. She went to Chicago, and everyone raved. She’s a sensation. So what does Fillmore do? He claps his hand to his forehead and says, ‘Gadzooks! An idea! I’ll do it again!’ And he picks *me*!”

“My dear girl…”

“Now, the flaw is this. Elsa is a genius. Someone else would’ve made her a star. But little Gladys? That’s different.” She turned to Sally. “You’ve seen me in action. Let me tell you—you’ve seen me at my best. Give me a maid’s role, a tray in Act One, and a couple of ‘Yes, madam’s’ in Act Two, and I’m there! Ellen Terry hasn’t got a chance when it comes to ‘Yes, madam.’ And I’ll beat Sarah Bernhardt blindfolded in tray-carrying. But beyond that? Nothing. I can’t act. I don’t want to. I only went on stage for fun. My idea of fun isn’t ploughing through a star role with critics waving axes. And I know it’s costing Fillmore money that should be going toward buying a little home with stationary wash-tubs. That’s that, Fillmore, old darling. I thought I’d just mention it.”

Sally felt sorry for Fillmore. He sat with his chin in his hands, staring blankly—Napoleon at Elba, minus the empire.

“If that’s how you feel,” he said, voice broken, “there’s nothing more to say.”

“Oh, yes there is. We’ll talk about your revue. It’s off.”

Fillmore jumped up, thumped the desk, and roared.

“It is NOT OFF! Great heavens! I will not stand this interference! I will not be tied and hampered! I am a man of broad vision—broad vision! I form my plans—my plans—I shape my schemes—and what happens? A horde of girls flood into my office while I’m concentrating! I won’t stand it! Advice, yes. Interference, NO! I… I… I… and kindly remember that!”

The door slammed. A distant crash echoed through the hall. Footsteps vanished.

Sally stared at Miss Winch, stunned. A furious Fillmore was a new experience.

Miss Winch unwrapped her gum again.

“Isn’t he cute?” she said. “I hope he doesn’t get the soft kind.”

“The soft kind?”

“He’ll be back soon with candy. He always gets the creamy, gooey kind, though I keep telling him I prefer the firm. Well, one thing’s certain—Fillmore’s got it up his nose. He’s hopping about, singing in the sunlight. It’s going to be hard work bringing him back to earth.” She sighed. “I’d like him to have enough left in the old stocking to pay the first year’s rent when the wedding bells ring.” She chewed thoughtfully. “Not that it matters. I’d be happy in two rooms and a kitchenette, as long as Fillmore was there. You have no idea how dippy I am about him. He grows on me like a darned drug. And the funny thing is, I keep admiring him even though I can see he’s the most perfect chump. That’s what I love about him. And the way his ears wiggle when he gets excited. Chumps make the best husbands. When you marry, Sally, grab a chump. Tap his forehead. If it rings solid, don’t hesitate. All the unhappy marriages come from husbands with brains. What good are brains to a man? They only unsettle him.”

She paused, studied Sally closely.

“Say, what do you do with your skin?”

Sally laughed.

“I just carry it around with me.”

“Well,” Miss Winch said enviously, “I wish I could train my darned fool of a complexion to behave. Freckles are the devil. At eight, I had the finest collection in the Middle West. I’ve been adding to it ever since. Some say lemon juice cures them. Mine lap it up and ask for more. The only way to get rid of freckles is to saw the head off at the neck.”

“But why do you want to get rid of them?”

“Why? Because a sensitive girl, anxious to keep her husband’s love, doesn’t enjoy looking like something from a dime museum.”

“How absurd! Fillmore worships freckles.”

“Did he tell you so?”

“Not in so many words, but you can see it in his eye.”

“Well,” Miss Winch said eagerly, “he certainly asked me to marry him knowing all about them. And I don’t think feminine loveliness means much to Fillmore, or he’d never have picked me. Still, it’s calculated to give a girl a jar when she opens a magazine and sees an ad that says, ‘Your husband is growing cold to you. Can you blame him? Have you really tried to cure those unsightly blemishes?’—meaning what I’ve got. Still, I haven’t noticed Fillmore growing cold, so maybe it’s all right.”
