Catherine was a model housewife, yet she rarely ventured beyond the walls of her home for a single day; whenever she did, something invariably went awry. Acutely aware of this tendency, she watched for a lull in domestic affairs to delay her planned visit to Sevenbergen. Day after day she postponed the trip until, finally, on an afternoon when it might have been accomplished, Peter Buyskens' mule happened to be out of the way.

Eventually, Eli confronted her before the entire family. "Catherine," he asked, "have you truly considered visiting Margaret Brandt?"

"Ay, my man," she admitted.

"Then I forbid it."

"Oh, do you?" she challenged, flushing slightly.

"I do."

"There is nothing more to be said, I suppose," she murmured.

"Not a word," Eli replied sternly.

Once alone with her daughter, Catherine turned her severity inward, directing it not at Eli but at herself. "I behaved like a cat stalking a robin," she lamented. "That was entirely my fault. I am like a silly hen that cannot lay an egg without cackling and summoning the whole flock to rob her of it. Next time either of us desires anything, let us act immediately and silently. If questioned later, we can claim ignorance, say we meant no harm, and wonder why anyone would suspect us. For two pins, I would have gone to Sevenbergen despite their teeth."

Such wild defiance staggered Kate. "Nay, mother, with patience Father will change his mind."

"And so will Michaelmas, but when? I was so determined you should see the girl. Then we could have pooled our thoughts regarding her. As they say, one cannot judge a body or a beast without seeing it with one's own eyes. Were I to have fifty more sons, I would never thwart one's fancy until I had seen the object of their desire and verified the truth. I should have thought of that before condemning Gerard's choice. Yet, life is a school, and the lessons are endless; we put down one fault only to pick up another, stumbling here and there until we stumble into our graves, and that is the end."

"Mother," Kate whispered timidly.

"Well, what brings this look upon your face? No good news, surely. What in the world has made the poor wretch so terrified?"

"She has an avowal to make," Kate faltered.

"Now, there is a noble word for you," Catherine declared proudly. "Gerard taught you that, I'll give you my oath. Come then, speak your mind."

"Well then, to tell the truth, I have seen her."

"And?"

"And I spoke with her as well."

"And you never told me? After this, miracles are mere dust."

"Mother, you were so angry against her. I waited until I could tell you without provoking you further."

"Ay," Catherine said, half sadly, half bitterly. "Like mother, like daughter; cowardice is our ruin. Sometimes I buffet the others, or how would the house fare? But did you, Kate, ever receive a harsh word or look from your poor mother that you— Nay, I will not have you cry, girl. Ten to one you had your reasons. Rise up, brave heart, and tell me everything. Better late than never. First and foremost, when and how did you go to Sevenbergen with your poor crutches, and I not knowing?"

"I never was there in my life," Kate replied gently. "And, mammy dear, to say that I never wished to see her, yet I never went nor sought to see her."

"There now," Catherine argued, "did I not say it was unlike my girl to seek her out secretly? Come, I am all agog."

"It was thus. It came to my ears, no matter how, and pray, good mother, do not ask me on my knees how I learned it, that Gerard is a prisoner in the Stadthouse tower."

"Ah."

"Under the pretext of Father's command."

Catherine sighed, a sound bordering on a moan. "Blackness deeper than I imagined," she muttered faintly.

"Giles and I went out at night to encourage him. And at the foot of the tower stood a brave maiden, quite strange to me, I swear, on the same mission."

"Lookee there now, Kate."

"At first, we properly frightened one another due to his notorious reputation and our minds filled with devils; we turned pale in the moonlight. But the next moment, she said, 'You are Margaret.' And I replied, 'And you are Kate.' Think on it!"

"Did one ever? 'Twas Gerard! He must have been talking backwards and forwards between you two."

In return for this revelation, Kate bestowed upon Catherine one of nature's finest gifts—the composite kiss. She pressed a single kiss to her mother's cheek, conveying simultaneously: *Quite correct; Good, clever mother for guessing so quickly; How sweet for us to be of one mind again after such estrangement; etc.*

"Now then, speak thy mind, child. Gerard is not here. Alas, what am I saying? Would to Heaven he were."

"Well then, mother, she is comely and resembles her portrait but little."

"Eh, dear; listen to the youth! I care for good deeds, not good looks. Does she love my boy as he deserves to be loved?"

"Sevenbergen lies farther from the Stadthouse than we do," Kate reflected. "Yet she was there before I arrived."

Catherine nodded knowingly.

"Nay, more; she had freed him before I came. Ay, down from the captive's tower."

Catherine shook her head in disbelief. "The highest tower for miles! It is not feasible."

"'Tis true nonetheless. She and an old man she brought used means and wit to lower him via a rope. There he hung from his prison, and our Giles climbed it. When I first saw the rope hanging, I said, 'This is magic.' But when the frank lass's arms embraced me, and her bosom beat against mine, and her cheeks grew wet, then I said, 'Tis not magic: 'tis love.' For she is not like me, but strong and capable. And, dear heart, even I, poor frail creature, sometimes feel as though I could move the world for those I love: I love you, mother. And she loves Gerard."

"God bless her for it! God bless her!"

"But..."

"But what, lamb?"

"Is her love truly certain honesty? 'Tis most strange; but that very quality which warms your heart has somewhat cooled mine toward her; poor soul. She is no wife, you know, mother, when all is said and done."

"Humph! They stood at the altar together."

"Ay, but they departed as they arrived: maid and bachelor."

"The parson says so?"

"Nay, for that I know not."

"Then I will take no man's word but his own in such a tangled skein." After some reflection, she added, "Nevertheless, you are right, girl. I shall go to Sevenbergen alone. A wife I am, but not a slave. We are all in the dark here. She holds the key. I must question her, and no one else, least of all you. I will not carry a lily to a house with a stain, no, not to a palace of gold and silver."

As Catherine pondered this conversation, she felt increasingly drawn to Margaret. Moreover, she was "all agog" with curiosity—a potent passion shared by all, and nearly omnipotent for those like Catherine who do not satisfy it with books. Finally, one fine day after dinner, she whispered to Kate, "Keep the house from falling apart if you can," donned her best kirtle and hood, her scarlet clocked hose, and her new shoes, and briskly trudged off to Sevenbergen, troubling no man's mule.

Upon arrival, she inquired where Margaret Brandt lived. The first person she asked shook his head, replying, "The name is strange to me." She walked a short distance further and asked a girl of about fifteen standing at a doorway. "Father," the girl called out into the house, "here is another after that magician's daughter." The man emerged and informed Catherine that Peter Brandt's cottage was just outside the town on the east side. "You may see the chimney from here," he pointed out. "But you will not find them there, neither father nor daughter; they have left the town this week, bless you."

"Do not say so, good man, and me walking all the way from Tergou."

"From Tergou? Then you must have met the soldier."

"What soldier? Yes, I did meet a soldier."

"Well, then, that soldier was here seeking that very same Margaret."

"Yes, and he wasn't mad with us because she was gone?" the girl interjected. "His long beard and her cheek are no strangers, I warrant."
