Three months of hard-earned furlough stretched before Lieutenant Calder as he leaned on the upper deck rail of the Nile steamer churning south to north from Wadi Halfa to Assouan. He stared out at the blistering, sun-scoured shoreline, and one bitter thought looped in his head: this is a callous land, inhabited by a callous people.

Lashed tight to the steamer’s flank was a creaking native barge, its lower deck crammed full of local travellers and hessian sacks of grain. Right in the middle of the crowd sat an angareb — the simple native bedstead strung with woven matting — and stretched flat across its frame was a still, shrouded figure draped in heavy black veil. Two Arabs had hauled that bed and its unknown burden on board at Korosko at dawn, and they now squatted in the barge’s stern laughing, arguing over a game of dice, as if their charge was no more than a sack of rotting dates. The figure beneath the veil might have been a corpse for all the attention it got: no twitch of a limb, no stir of cloth over the mouth.

Calder dragged his gaze from the barge to the banks on either side. Glaring white sand rolled all the way to the horizon, dotted with half-eroded, weather-beaten pyramids jutted out of the rock like broken fists. The only hint of life was a thin, ragged sliver of green fringing the water’s edge, the Soudan’s entire, stingy response to spring, summer, and the Nile’s annual flood. A callous land, inhabited by a callous people, he repeated to himself.

He looked back down to the angareb. He couldn’t tell if the shrouded figure was man or woman: the black cloth pulled tight over the face outlined the curve of a nose, the hollow of eye sockets, the shape of a mouth, but hid every hint of moustache or beard. The slanting afternoon sun crawled slowly closer, gilding the deck planks, inching up the bedstead’s legs, until the blazing yellow light spilled over the still figure’s feet, then their shins, then their chest, until it burned pitiless, full force, over the hidden face. Not a muscle stirred. The veil never fluttered over the lips, the legs stayed rigidly straight, the arms lay motionless at the figure’s sides.

Calder snapped a shout down at the two dice-playing Arabs in the stern.
“Drag that bed into the shade, now — move!”

The men grumbled, rose slow and resentful, and obeyed.
“Is that a man or woman?” Calder called down.
“A man. We’re taking him to the hospital in Assouan. He won’t live, though. Fell out of a palm tree three weeks back.”
“And no one’s given him food or water since?”
“He’s too far gone for it to matter.”

It was a story Calder had heard a hundred times, the inevitable result of that brutal local fatalism: every life and every death is written, and there’s no point fighting what’s already been set down. That man had probably only suffered a bad bruise at the start, one a week of simple care would have fixed entirely. But they’d left him to bake in the sun, swarmed by flies, unwashed, unfed, burning with thirst. The bruise had turned to an open sore, the sore had gone gangrenous, and only when every possible remedy was too late had the local Mudir at Korosko found the accident and ordered him shipped downstream to Assouan.

Familiar as the story was, Calder couldn’t shake the image of that still, silent man on the angareb. He felt a small, quiet flicker of relief when a sharp, strong wind blew up from the north, dead against the river’s current, kicking up cool gusts that would give the fevered patient a little relief. That evening, sat across the dinner table from a man with a thick German accent, he acted on a sudden impulse.
“You wouldn’t happen to be a doctor, would you?”
“Not quite — a medical student from Bonn. I came up from Cairo to see the Second Cataract, but they wouldn’t let me go further than Wadi Halfa.” The student pushed his glasses up his nose, smiling good-naturedly.
“Then I’m afraid I’m about to ruin your holiday and ask for your professional help.”
“For yourself? But you look perfectly healthy—”
“Not for me. For an Arab man on that angareb down on the barge. He fell from a palm tree three weeks ago, and no one’s touched him since. The sight won’t be pretty. Wounds go bad fast out here, no care.”

The German student shrugged, already pushing his chair back from the table. “All clinical experience is useful,” he said.

The wind had only gotten stronger while they ate. Waves crashed against the steamer’s hull, the barge tossed and pitched alongside, cold spray sloshed over its lower deck. Night had fallen thick and dark, the only light a single dim lantern hung from the barge’s upper deck, casting wavering, shifting shadows over a tiny patch of planks. Beyond that small circle, all was inky black save for the white sheets of spray that blew over the barge’s bow, glinting like scattered snow, cracking against the wood like a whip lash.
“Wait — he’s not where you left him,” the student said, leaning over the steamer’s rail. “There’s no bed in the shade.”

Calder stared down into the dark for a long minute, his stomach sinking. “I think I see it. It’s in the bows. Right in the path of the spray.”

The two of them hurried down the steamer’s steps to the lowest deck, clambered over the bulwarks down onto the lurching barge. The angareb sat in a puddle of sloshing river water, dragged all the way back to the bow, the black-shrouded figure as motionless as it had been all day, cold spray breaking over what would be its face, no one having bothered to drag it back to shelter. Calder fetched a bright lantern, called the two Arabs over, and ordered them to strip the black veil off.

The German knelt to examine the man’s swollen, rotting thigh. It was as bad as Calder had predicted: the wound crawled with advanced infection, the student was glad to yank a clean cloth back over it minutes later.
“I can do nothing for him here,” he said, wiping his hands on his trousers. “A hospital in Assouan, with baths and proper dressings, might be able to give him some relief. But a full recovery? I can’t promise that. And for the record — this man never fell out of a palm tree. That’s a lie. That wound was made by a spear blade. A deliberate, weapon-inflicted injury.”

Calder’s head snapped to the still man on the bed. He’d been watching his face closely, and he saw it: the man’s eyes, half-lidded, tracked every word the two of them spoke. He understood English.
“Where are you from?” Calder asked, leaning down, lantern held close to the man’s face.

The man’s lips trembled, no sound came out — but his eyes were bright, alive, desperate to answer. Calder reeled off the names of Sudanese towns, one after another. “Dongola? No. Metemneh? No. Berber? No. Omdurman?”

The man’s eyelids fluttered shut, a tiny flicker of a nod clear in the movement. He was from Omdurman.
“Were you wounded trying to help someone escape?” Calder pressed, leaning even closer. He named every white captive known to be held in the Dervish stronghold, one by one, and every time the man’s eyes gave a small, clear no. Until he said the name he’d been waiting for updates on for months, the man who’d vanished deep into the Soudan on a secret, unauthorised mission: “Effendi Harry Feversham?”

The man’s eyes flashed a yes, bright as any spoken word.

That was all they could get. The man was too weak to speak, too far gone to say his own name. The two Arabs stuck stubbornly to their story: some distant kinsmen in Korosko had paid them a small sum to ferry this stranger to Assouan’s hospital, they knew nothing about him, nothing about how he got hurt. Calder had the piece of information his comrade Durrance had been begging for, hard proof of Harry Feversham’s fate — but it was locked away, trapped in a body that could barely breathe, let alone talk.

He arranged to get the wounded man straight to the Assouan hospital the second the steamer docked. The attending doctors shook their heads when he asked for the prognosis. There was a tiny, slim chance he might live. But even if he did, it would be months before he recovered, if he ever regained the ability to speak.

Calder had no choice but to continue his journey north to Cairo, then on to Europe for his furlough. The chance to unlock the full truth of Harry Feversham’s mission had slipped right through his fingers. For the still, silent man on the hospital bed was Abou Fatma, of the Kabbabish tribe. And his half-dead, wounded presence on that Nile steamer meant that Harry Feversham’s months-long, carefully laid plan to rescue his comrade Colonel Trench from Omdurman had failed.
