The next day he persuaded May to slip away for a walk in the Park after luncheon.

Sunday afternoons, in their old Episcopalian corner of New York, belonged by right to solemn pew-cushions and measured hymns; yet Mrs. Welland, who could be indulgent when indulgence served the calendar of propriety, had that very morning won May to the wisdom of a long engagement—time, she said, for the hand-embroidered trousseau to grow stitch by stitch into its ordained dozens, as orderly as loaves rising in identical tins. May had listened as a daughter listens, with that bright pliancy Newland both adored and mistrusted: she could be guided like a well-tempered batter, taking the shape of whatever bowl was held out to her.

Outside, the air tasted of cold iron and sunlight. The bare trees along the Mall lifted their dark ribs into a sky so clear it looked enamelled—lapis above, snow below, the world between them crisp as spun sugar snapped underfoot. The Park had its own music in winter: the far clip of hooves on packed paths, the intermittent swish of sleigh-runners, a child’s call skimming and dying into the blue; and, threading through it all, the thin rustle of dry branches that seemed to answer one another like old women gossiping in an unforgotten dialect. Archer noticed how the city’s noise pressed at the edges, a dull mechanical murmur beyond the trees—as if modern life, impatient and iron-wheeled, were always trying to grind down the softer sounds. Here, at least, May’s laughter could still be heard cleanly.

The weather brought out her radiance as a flame shows truest against frost. She moved beside him with the lightness of a young creature bred for open air, her cheek fresh and firm as a winter apple. Archer felt pride in the glances that turned after her—pride, and the sudden simple comfort of possession, like a hand closing over something warm in a cold room. For a few minutes it swept away the restlessness that had lately lived under his ribs.

“It’s so delicious,” she said, drawing in her breath as if she could taste the words, “waking every morning to smell lilies-of-the-valley in one’s room. Yesterday they came late. I hadn’t time in the morning—”

“But your remembering each day to send them makes me love them so much more,” he answered, “than if you’d given a standing order and they arrived like a lesson—punctual as a music-teacher. As I know Gertrude Lefferts’s did, for instance, when she and Lawrence were engaged.”

“Ah—they would!” May laughed, quick to seize the implied satire. Her delight in the small recognitions of social minutiae always amused him: she could discriminate between one family’s habits and another’s as delicately as some careful palate tells wines apart. He looked at her, at the clear curve of her jaw under the fur edge of her muff, and felt rich and safe enough to add, lightly:

“When I sent your lilies yesterday afternoon, I saw some rather gorgeous yellow roses and packed them off to Madame Olenska. Was that right?”

May’s eyes warmed. “How dear of you. Anything of that kind delights her. It’s odd she didn’t mention it: she lunched with us today, and spoke of Mr. Beaufort’s having sent her wonderful orchids, and cousin Henry van der Luyden a whole hamper of carnations from Skuytercliff. She seems so surprised to receive flowers. Don’t people send them in Europe? She thinks it such a pretty custom.”

“Oh—well, no wonder mine were overshadowed by Beaufort’s,” Archer said, more sharply than he intended.

At once he remembered he had put no card with the roses, and a petty vexation pricked him. Why had he spoken of them at all? It made the act seem to him suddenly less graceful, more like a signal, and he disliked the faint air of secrecy that began to gather about anything connected with Ellen. He wanted to say: I called on your cousin yesterday—but hesitated. If she had not spoken of his visit, to mention it now might sound like an explanation offered without being asked for. Yet not to speak of it made the silence seem charged, like a dish served without naming its ingredients.

To shake off the question, he began to talk of their own future, their plans, and Mrs. Welland’s insistence on a long engagement.

“If you call it long!” May protested. “Isabel Chivers and Reggie were engaged for two years. Grace and Thorley for nearly a year and a half. Why aren’t we very well off as we are?”

The traditional maidenly interrogation fell from her as if she had been taught to set it down, like a proper spoon placed beside a plate. Archer felt a sudden shame at his own impatience; and yet, as he watched her speak, he wondered at what age “nice” women began to season their thoughts with anything of their own. She was nearing her twenty-second birthday, and still her phrases seemed chosen from a family larder.

Never, if we won’t let them, he thought; and his mind flashed back to his own hot words to Sillerton Jackson—Women ought to be as free as we are. It would soon be his task, he told himself, to take the bandage from this young woman’s eyes and bid her look out on the world. But how many generations of women had gone to her making—women who had learned to keep their seeing for embroidery, their hearing for approving murmurs, their speech for the sanctioned vocabulary? How many had gone down into the family vault with their senses swathed, as if life’s full taste and sound were improper?

He shivered a little, remembering some scientific book he had been reading and the oft-cited Kentucky cave-fish which had ceased to develop eyes because it had no use for them. What if, when he told May to open hers, they could only look out blankly at blankness?

“We might be much better off,” he said, pushing the thought aside by giving it a different shape. “We might be altogether together—we might travel.”

Her face lit at once, as if he had set a candle behind it. “That would be lovely,” she admitted. She would love to travel. But her mother would not understand their wanting to do things so differently.

“As if the mere ‘differently’ didn’t account for it!” he insisted, hungry for resistance, for proof that she wanted something with teeth and savour in it.

“Newland! You’re so original!” she exclaimed.

His heart sank. He heard himself, with a sick clarity, making the approved passionate protests of engaged men; and he heard her answering with the sweet, trained replies that instinct and tradition had put in her mouth—even to the point of calling him original, as if originality were a garnish pinned on.

“Original! We’re all as like each other as those dolls cut out of the same folded paper,” he burst out. “We’re like patterns stencilled on a wall. Can’t you and I strike out for ourselves, May?”

He had stopped and faced her; his breath smoked a little in the cold as he spoke. Her eyes rested on him with bright, unclouded admiration—an admiration so complete it was almost without curiosity.

“Mercy—shall we elope?” she laughed.

“If you would—”

“You do love me, Newland! I’m so happy.”

“But then—why not be happier?”

“We can’t behave like people in novels, though, can we?”

“Why not—why not—why not?”

She looked faintly bored by his insistence. She knew very well that they could not; but it was troublesome to have to dredge up the reason. Her tone suggested the weariness of being asked to translate something that had never been spoken in her own language.

“I’m not clever enough to argue with you. But that kind of thing is rather—vulgar, isn’t it?” she offered at last, relieved, as if she had found the one word that would close the door.

“Are you so much afraid, then, of being vulgar?”

She was staggered. “Of course I should hate it—so would you,” she rejoined, a little irritably.

He stood silent, beating his stick against his boot-top, listening to the small, neat sounds of their own footsteps and the bigger breathing of the Park around them. Somewhere a carriage wheel bumped over frozen ruts, a hard, modern clatter; farther off a sleigh-bell chimed thinly, like a memory. The city’s roar beyond the trees seemed to swell, as if the word vulgar had called it up—an invisible force of judgment, a dominant lexicon that could end an argument with a single label.

Feeling, no doubt, that she had indeed used the right word, May went on light-heartedly, as if she had wiped the subject away with a clean cloth.

“Oh—did I tell you I showed Ellen my ring? She thinks it the most beautiful setting she ever saw. There’s nothing like it in the rue de la Paix, she said. I do love you, Newland, for being so artistic!”

The next afternoon Archer, before dinner, sat smoking sullenly in his study. He had not stopped at his club on the way up from the office where he practised law in the leisurely manner customary among well-to-do New Yorkers. He was out of spirits and slightly out of temper; and a haunting horror of doing the same thing at the same hour every day besieged him.

Sameness—sameness! The word ran through his head like a tune he could not shut out. He had passed the familiar tall-hatted figures lounging behind the plate-glass and, because he usually dropped in at that hour, had gone home instead, as if turning aside could alter the melody. He knew, with the miserable exactness of someone who has eaten a dish too often, not only what they would be talking about but which man would say which thing, and in what tone—each phrase pre-measured, each opinion salted to the same degree.

The Duke would of course be their principal theme; though the appearance in Fifth Avenue of a golden-haired lady in a small canary-coloured brougham with a pair of black cobs—Mr. Beaufort was generally thought responsible—would also be discussed with relish. Such “women,” as they were called, were few in New York; those driving their own carriages fewer still. The very sound of her wheels in the fashionable hour seemed to have jarred society’s nerves: people spoke of it as if a discordant note had been struck in a church anthem. Only the day before, her carriage had passed Mrs. Lovell Mingott’s, and Mrs. Mingott had instantly rung the little bell at her elbow and ordered the coachman to drive her home.

“What if it had happened to Mrs. van der Luyden?” people asked each other with a shudder, as if the mere proximity of that rolling, rattling modernity could contaminate the pure air of tradition.

Archer, in his study, could almost hear Lawrence Lefferts holding forth at that moment on the disintegration of society—Lefferts’s voice, round and complacent, like a well-fed drum.

He raised his head irritably when his sister Janey entered, then bent quickly over his book—Swinburne’s Chastelard, just out—as if he had not seen her. Janey glanced at the writing-table heaped with books, opened a volume of the Contes Drolatiques, made a wry face over the archaic French, and sighed:

“What learned things you read!”

“Well—?” he asked, as she hovered before him with Cassandra’s air of bringing disaster nobody wished to hear.

“Mother’s very angry.”

“Angry? With whom? About what?”

“Miss Sophy Jackson has just been here. She brought word that her brother would come in after dinner. She couldn’t say very much, because he forbade her to: he wishes to give all the details himself. He’s with cousin Louisa van der Luyden now.”

“For heaven’s sake, my dear girl, try a fresh start,” Archer said. “It would take an omniscient Deity to know what you’re talking about.”

“It’s not a time to be profane, Newland… Mother feels badly enough about your not going to church—”

With a groan he plunged back into his book.

“Newland! Do listen. Your friend Madame Olenska was at Mrs. Lemuel Struthers’s party last night: she went there with the Duke and Mr. Beaufort.”

At the last clause a senseless anger swelled in him. He could not have told whether it was the Duke’s name, or Beaufort’s, or Ellen’s, or the idea of her among that particular crowd—but it rose, hot and thick, like a sauce turning in the pan when one is not watching. To smother it he laughed.

“Well—what of it? I knew she meant to.”

Janey paled and her eyes began to project with indignation.

“You knew she meant to—and you didn’t try to stop her? To warn her?”

“Stop her? Warn her?” He laughed again, the sound harsher. “I’m not engaged to be married to the Countess Olenska!”

The words had a fantastic sound even to himself, like a line overheard from the wrong play.

“You’re marrying into her family.”

“Oh, family—family!” he jeered.

“Newland—don’t you care about Family?”

“Not a brass farthing.”

“Nor about what cousin Louisa van der Luyden will think?”

“Not the half of one—if she thinks such old maid’s rubbish.”

“Mother is not an old maid,” said his virgin sister with pinched lips.

He felt like shouting back that yes, she was—and so were the van der Luydens, and so were they all, whenever reality brushed them with a wing-tip. But he saw Janey’s long gentle face puckering into tears and felt ashamed of the useless pain he was inflicting.

“Hang Countess Olenska! Don’t be a goose, Janey—I’m not her keeper.”

“No; but you did ask the Wellands to announce your engagement sooner so that we might all back her up; and if it hadn’t been for that cousin Louisa would never have invited her to the dinner for the Duke.”

“Well—what harm was there in inviting her?” Archer retorted. “She was the best-looking woman in the room. She made the dinner a little less funereal than the usual van der Luyden banquet.”

“You know cousin Henry asked her to please you: he persuaded cousin Louisa. And now they’re so upset that they’re going back to Skuytercliff tomorrow. I think, Newland, you’d better come down. You don’t seem to understand how mother feels.”

In the drawing-room he found his mother. She raised a troubled brow from her needlework.

“Has Janey told you?”

“Yes.” He tried to keep his tone as measured as hers. “But I can’t take it very seriously.”

“Not the fact of having offended cousin Louisa and cousin Henry?”

“The fact that they can be offended by such a trifle as Countess Olenska’s going to the house of a woman they consider common.”

“Consider—!”

“Well, who is,” he persisted, “but who has good music, and amuses people on Sunday evenings, when the whole of New York is dying of inanition.”

“Good music?” Mrs. Archer’s voice tightened, as if the word itself had an indecent tang. “All I know is, there was a woman who got up on a table and sang the things they sing at the places you go to in Paris. There was smoking and champagne.”

“Well—that kind of thing happens in other places, and the world still goes on.”

“I don’t suppose, dear, you’re really defending the French Sunday?”

“I’ve heard you often enough, mother, grumble at the English Sunday when we’ve been in London.”

“New York is neither Paris nor London.”

“Oh, no, it’s not!” her son groaned, and the groan carried with it all the muffled city noise he had been trying to escape—the rumble of commerce, the clank of opinion, the grinding repetition.

“You mean, I suppose,” she continued, misunderstanding him with the perfect sincerity of those who live within the approved meanings, “that society here is not as brilliant? You’re right, I daresay; but we belong here, and people should respect our ways when they come among us. Ellen Olenska especially: she came back to get away from the kind of life people lead in brilliant societies.”

Newland made no answer; and after a moment his mother ventured, with a careful softness:

“I was going to put on my bonnet and ask you to take me to see cousin Louisa for a moment before dinner.”

He frowned, and she went on: “I thought you might explain to her what you’ve just said: that society abroad is different… that people are not as particular, and that Madame Olenska may not have realised how we feel about such things. It would be, you know, dear,” she added with an innocent adroitness, “in Madame Olenska’s interest if you did.”

“Dearest mother, I really don’t see how we’re concerned in the matter,” he answered. “The Duke took Madame Olenska to Mrs. Struthers’s—in fact he brought Mrs. Struthers to call on her. I was there when they came. If the van der Luydens want to quarrel with anybody, the real culprit is under their own roof.”

“Quarrel? Newland, did you ever know of cousin Henry’s quarrelling? Besides, the Duke’s his guest—and a stranger too. Strangers don’t discriminate: how should they? Countess Olenska is a New Yorker, and should have respected the feelings of New York.”

“Well, then, if they must have a victim, you have my leave to throw Madame Olenska to them,” he cried, exasperated. “I don’t see myself—or you either—offering ourselves up to expiate her crimes.”

“Oh, of course you see only the Mingott side,” his mother said, in the sensitive tone that was her nearest approach to anger.

At that moment the sad butler drew back the drawing-room portières and announced: “Mr. Henry van der Luyden.”

Mrs. Archer dropped her needle and pushed her chair back with an agitated hand. “Another lamp,” she called to the retreating servant, while Janey bent to straighten her mother’s cap.

Mr. van der Luyden’s tall figure loomed on the threshold, and Archer went forward to greet his cousin.

“We were just talking about you, sir,” he said.

Mr. van der Luyden seemed overwhelmed by the announcement. He drew off his glove to shake hands with the ladies, smoothed his tall hat shyly, while Janey pushed an arm-chair forward; and Archer, with an impulse that was half defiance, added:

“And the Countess Olenska.”

Mrs. Archer paled.

“Ah—a charming woman. I have just been to see her,” said Mr. van der Luyden, and complacency returned to his brow. He sank into the chair, laid his hat and gloves on the floor beside him in the old-fashioned way, and continued, with an unusual flow of speech:

“She has a real gift for arranging flowers. I had sent her a few carnations from Skuytercliff, and I was astonished. Instead of massing them in big bunches as our head-gardener does, she had scattered them about loosely, here and there… I can’t say how. The Duke had told me—he said: ‘Go and see how cleverly she’s arranged her drawing-room.’ And she has. I should really like to take Louisa to see her, if the neighbourhood were not so—unpleasant.”

A dead silence met this. Mrs. Archer drew her embroidery out of the basket into which she had nervously tumbled it. Newland, leaning against the chimney-place, twisted a humming-bird-feather screen in his hand; he watched Janey’s gaping face brighten as the servant returned with the second lamp, and the room filled with a thicker, steadier light—lamplight that made everything seem already preserved, already museum-like.

“The fact is,” Mr. van der Luyden went on, stroking his long grey leg with a bloodless hand weighted by the Patroon’s great signet-ring, “I dropped in to thank her for the very pretty note she wrote me about my flowers; and also—but this is between ourselves, of course—to give her a friendly warning about allowing the Duke to carry her off to parties with him. I don’t know if you’ve heard—”

Mrs. Archer produced an indulgent smile. “Has the Duke been carrying her off to parties?”

“You know what these English grandees are. They’re all alike. Louisa and I are very fond of our cousin—but it’s hopeless to expect people who are accustomed to the European courts to trouble themselves about our little republican distinctions. The Duke goes where he’s amused.”

He paused; no one spoke.

“Yes—it seems he took her with him last night to Mrs. Lemuel Struthers’s. Sillerton Jackson has just been to us with the foolish story, and Louisa was rather troubled. So I thought the shortest way was to go straight to Countess Olenska and explain—by the merest hint, you know—how we feel in New York about certain things. I felt I might, without indelicacy, because the evening she dined with us she rather suggested… rather let me see that she would be grateful for guidance. And she was.”

He looked about the room with a mild benevolence that, on another face, might have been self-satisfaction. Mrs. Archer’s countenance dutifully reflected it.

“How kind you both are, dear Henry—always!” she said. “Newland will particularly appreciate what you have done because of dear May and his new relations.”

She shot an admonitory glance at her son. Archer answered:

“Immensely, sir. But I was sure you’d like Madame Olenska.”

Mr. van der Luyden looked at him with extreme gentleness.

“I never ask to my house, my dear Newland,” he said, “any one whom I do not like. And so I have just told Sillerton Jackson.”

With a glance at the clock he rose. “But Louisa will be waiting. We are dining early, to take the Duke to the Opera.”

After the portières had solemnly closed behind their visitor, silence fell upon the Archer family, as if the room were holding its breath in the approved manner.

“Gracious—how romantic!” Janey burst out at last.

No one knew exactly what her elliptical comment referred to; and long ago her relations had ceased trying to interpret her private language, so full of sudden exclamations and borrowed phrases.

Mrs. Archer shook her head with a sigh. “Provided it all turns out for the best,” she said, in the tone of one who knows how surely it will not.

“Newland, you must stay and see Sillerton Jackson when he comes this evening: I really shan’t know what to say to him.”

“Poor mother!” Archer laughed, bending to kiss away her frown. “But he won’t come—”
