Margaret Brandt had always been an outsider in Sevenbergen. Her quiet reserve was mistaken for pride, and the whispers of envy had reached her ears. Now, as her secret grew harder to conceal, she imagined the cruel triumph of those who had long resented her. The thought gnawed at her, driving her deeper into solitude, away from prying eyes.

She knew the truth of her own heart—knew that in the eyes of God and the law, her betrothal to Gerard was as binding as marriage. Yet the written proof was gone, carried away in his hands across distant lands. Without it, what defense had she against the world’s judgment? She was no lawyer, but she understood the cruel arithmetic of reputation: a missing contract could not outweigh the visible truth of her condition.

Shame burned in her like a fever. Every glance, every murmur, seemed to strip her bare. Even her father’s innocent words could send the blood rushing to her cheeks—not for anything he said, but for the secret she carried. And when the Van Eyck women discovered her state, the fragile illusion of safety shattered. Catherine’s kindness offered no comfort; silence meant betrayal was coming.

The desire to flee became unbearable. To vanish, to escape the condemning eyes—but how? Her father was old, rooted in his ways. She could not abandon him, yet how could she confess the truth? The weight of it choked her.

One evening, she stood by the canal, its dark waters whispering of peace. An end to fear, to shame—but no. Gerard’s love anchored her. She knelt, praying for strength, then rose with desperate resolve. *I will tell him. Before sunset.*

That night, she stood before her father, trembling.

"Father, I must speak to you."

"Speak, child."

Her voice wavered. "I have done foolishly. Disgrace is coming to this house—and I am the cause."

Peter frowned. "What folly is this?"

She clenched her hands. "Gerard took our betrothal contract with him. Without it—" She swallowed. "The world will not believe me. They will see only my shame."

His brow furrowed. "Gerard is true. He will return."

"But before then—" Her breath hitched. "Father, I cannot stay here. The whispers, the scorn—I will not bear it."

For once, the old man did not press for answers. He saw her anguish and, with a father’s instinct, chose mercy over curiosity.

"If Sevenbergen wounds you," he said quietly, "then we shall leave it."

She stared at him. "You would go—without demanding why?"

"What use are reasons when your heart is breaking?" He touched her hand. "Where you go, I go. That is enough."

Relief washed over her, so sharp it brought tears. She wept against his shoulder, spared the humiliation of confession—yet guilt lingered. She had bought her escape with silence.

A week later, they were gone, their home left behind like a shed skin. Only the Burgomaster of Tergou knew their destination—Rotterdam, a tailor’s shop in the Brede-Kirk Straet. A secret kept close, for now.

But secrets, like shadows, have a way of stretching long in the light.
