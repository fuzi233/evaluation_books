In that great menagerie in the Regent’s Park—where London keeps its captives in cages and calls the place instruction—I stood one damp afternoon with my ear turned as much to the tanks as to my own thoughts. The aquarium has its own weather: a cool breath smelling of wet slate, river-weed, and that faint metallic tang you catch when a cook draws a knife along a fish’s backbone. The place is never silent. Water is a talker. It keeps up a continual hush and swash against glass, and every bubble that rises snaps like a tiny bead of glass between the teeth. Against that soft, sane music—so different from the city’s hammering wheels and shrieked hawkers—one may feel the mind unclench.

A broad fish, brown as baked crust on one side and pale as milk on the other, slid through the green light with a grace that would have made a dancing-master jealous. He turned his glistening dark back, then showed the clean white of his breast; his fins, fanning, were as neat as a cook’s wrist when she skims a broth. And this admirable motion-master, this smooth curving creature, turned out—when I read his little label—to be no prince of the sea at all, but our old homely acquaintance the flounder.

Why, we have all swallowed him at Greenwich, soused in his bath with herbs and wine, and never once suspected the beauty that lived in him. We know him only as a dish—water-souchy, with parsley and a sting of lemon, the liquor pale and fragrant, the fish tender enough to part under the spoon; we praise the cook, and forget the creature. Yet here, in his right element, he was a marvel. Take him out, lay him on boards, and he becomes a grotesque thing, flapping, disgraced, his very shape an accusation against the unfeeling air; he gasps his last as a man does when the world has stolen his proper language from him, and he is left only with the harsh syllables of strangers.

There is a proverb about fish out of water. I dare say I am not the first naturalist to discover it. But looking at that flounder, sleek in his native green, I could not help thinking of Harry Warrington, who had been floundering—poor fellow—for many a month, knocked about on dry land, made ridiculous by circumstances, and half stifled by them. Now, by a touch upon his shoulder—no more than the tap of an epaulet—he had found his true element. Health and spirits came back to him as flavour returns to a sauce the moment you put the right herb into the pot.

He delighted in soldiering, as if the very smell of pipeclay and leather, the brassy reek of powder, the hot iron breath of cannon, were a nourishment his blood had long been wanting. He went into the new trade with that hungry, eager quickness with which a born cook learns a kitchen: not merely tasting, but mastering—learning where everything lies, how the day is paced, what must be done first and what last, and which little negligence will spoil the whole. Had I the art of my friend Lorrequer, I would carry you into camp, and show you the other Harry in his proper colours: on march and parade; at mess when the beef is carved and the claret passed; under tents where the canvas keeps out rain no better than a poor lid keeps in steam; in the bustle of orders, the clink of accoutrements, the many-voiced life of a regiment. But I have no such skill, and can only bid you imagine him—Mr. Harry Warrington—in his new red coat and yellow facings, happy to bear the King’s colours, and pleased to learn and do the duties of his calling.

George, who had always honoured his brother’s better qualities even while they vexed him, was proud and thankful in his heart to see Harry so restored. He wrote to their mother in Virginia with a warmth of praise which Harry’s own modesty would never have allowed him to repeat. He told her how Harry had won his first step by his own gallantry; and George added that he wished her leave to share with her the expense of buying a higher rank for him. He could not bear, he said, that Harry should suffer in purse from a mistake which had not been of Harry’s making. While George had been believed dead, Harry had naturally thought himself heir, and had lived with an expenditure he never would have ventured on had he known he was but younger son. George called it only fair that he should contribute liberally now—almost as a thank-offering for his own return to life—to any plan for Harry’s advantage.

Then George, having set his brother’s affairs in order upon paper, came to that part which pressed nearest his own heart. He reminded his mother how the best friends the brothers had found in England were Mr. and Mrs. Lambert—Mrs. Lambert an old schoolfellow of Madam Esmond’s. Where blood relations had been worldly and unfeeling, these friends had been steadfast. The General was respected in the army and beloved by all who knew him; and Mrs. Lambert’s tenderness towards Madam Esmond’s sons had been like a mother’s. Now, George wrote, he had formed an attachment for the elder Miss Lambert—Miss Theodora—on which he believed the happiness of his life depended, and he implored his mother’s approval. He had not yet made formal offers; but he owned that he had done little to disguise his sentiments, and that the young lady, and her parents too, seemed favourable. He dwelt—like a man who has found his very bread after famine—on the girl’s goodness: she had been so admirable and exemplary a daughter to her own mother that he was sure she would do her duty by his. In short, Mr. Warrington painted her as perfection, and declared, with the entire solemnity of a man who has at last put a name to his hunger, that to win or lose her would be to be happy or wretched.

Why do I not produce this letter?—some tender reader may ask, for I have said I possess the Warrington papers. Because there is a kind of theft that is committed not with hands but with eyes and ears. It is cruel to babble the secrets of a young man’s love; to overhear his incoherent vows, to pin down with cold ink the wild, hot raptures that were meant to vanish into the air between two people only. Shall we count sighs as if they were coins? bottle tears as if they were cordial? Love—when it is real—is sacred. Wherever one sees it (and sometimes, Heaven knows, one may in this world) shooting suddenly out of two pairs of eyes; or looking sadly even from one pair; or dropping from a mother to the baby in her lap; or shining from a father as he watches his girl whirl round the room with the captain; or warming the old man’s gaze when the bonne vieille comes in—wherever that signal burns, let us salute it and pass on.

While Harry’s love had led him to the smell of powder, and to rush upon dragoons with a relish as if danger were spice, George had been engaged in an amusement far more peaceful to his nature. He was courting.

There are courtships which are carried on like campaigns, with loud trumpets and banners; and there are those which go on as quietly as a well-managed household meal—daily, necessary, full of small attentions that make the difference between mere sustenance and comfort. George’s belonged to the latter. He found pretexts as a clever cook finds uses for every scrap: a note to send that did not seem to ask for an answer, yet got one; a book to lend; a quotation from a favourite poet to copy out; flowers out of Covent Garden chosen not by show, but by scent and meaning—violets still cool from the morning, jonquils sharp as a fresh-peeled lemon, roses heavy and bruising their own perfume. In summer there were schemes without end: Ranelagh, Hampstead, Vauxhall, Marylebone Gardens; and always Gumbo and Sady—his negro servants—trotting between Southampton Row and Dean Street, carrying parcels and little messages as dutifully as errand-boys run between kitchen and pantry.

George wanted his famous tragedy copied out fair for the stage, and who could write such an Italian hand as Miss Theo? The pages passed to and fro, and with them went little notes—thanks, questions, admiration—always something. He walked to St. James’s Church and, by good luck, found himself singing from the same Prayer-book as she; and he never heard a word of the sermon, so loud were his own thoughts. He made himself prodigiously agreeable to all her relations—to the little brother and sister at school; to the elder at college; to Miss Hetty, with whom he fenced in bright passages of wit; and to mamma, who, Martin Lambert says, was half in love with him herself. For if fathers are sometimes sulky at the approach of a destined son-in-law, mothers are apt to become sentimental, and to taste their own youth again, as one tastes a dish long absent from the table and finds it stirs a whole buried season.

Here in my hands, indeed, is a packet marked in George’s neat writing: “T’s letters, 1758-9.” Shall we open it? No. Those words were whispered for one ear. Years later, when they were husband and wife, he might read them with sweet pangs as one smells, in an old chest, the faint ghost of lavender and remembers a room that is gone. But to spread them before strangers would be sacrilege. Take it on trust that the young lady’s letters were modest and pure, the gentleman’s respectful and tender.

In these last months, George Warrington had made up his mind that he had found the woman of women. She might not be the most beautiful—there was Cousin Flora, there were Coelia and Ardelia and a hundred more with brighter faces—but Theo’s sweet countenance pleased him beyond all. She might not be the most clever; yet her voice was the dearest to his ear, and—what is the strangest alchemy of love?—in her company he felt himself cleverer than ever before. Words came to him; fine thoughts; wit; generosity; a noble ease. And as for the young lady—thank Heaven I never thought so ill of women as to suppose them just—we may be sure she endowed her young hero with every excellence in the calendar, not excepting those he had never shown to anyone else.

When George’s letter reached Virginia, it set a little stir going round Madam Esmond’s fireside. So he was in love and wished to marry! It seemed natural enough; and, to a mother, there is comfort in seeing a son’s passion take a lawful road, and keep him out of the snares that lie for young men in town and camp. If he was to unite himself with a well-bred Christian young woman, Madam saw no harm.

“I knew they would be setting their caps at him,” says Mountain.

“They fancy his wealth is as great as his estate. He does not say whether the young lady has money. I fear otherwise.”

“People would set their caps at him here, I dare say,” Madam Esmond answered, grimly turning her eyes upon her dependant, “and try and catch Mr. Esmond Warrington for their own daughters, who are no richer than Miss Lambert may be.”

“I suppose your ladyship means me!” cries Mountain. “My Fanny is poor, as you say; and ’tis kind of you to remind me of her poverty!”

“I said people would set their caps at him. If the cap fits you, tant pis! as my papa used to say.”

“You think, madam, I am scheming to keep George for my daughter? I thank you, on my word! A good opinion you seem to have of us after the years we have lived together!”

“My dear Mountain, I know you much better than to suppose you could ever fancy your daughter would be a suitable match for a gentleman of Mr. Esmond’s rank and station,” says Madam, with a stately severity.

“Fanny Parker was as good as Molly Benson at school, and Mr. Mountain’s daughter is as good as Mr. Lambert’s!” Mrs. Mountain cries.

“Then you did think of marrying her to my son! I shall write to Mr. Esmond Warrington, and say how sorry I am that you should be disappointed!” cries the mistress of Castlewood.

And, for our parts, we may suppose Mrs. Mountain was disappointed and had entertained ambitious views for her girl; else why flare so angrily at the bare notion of George’s marriage?

Madam Esmond wrote back to her son that she was pleased with the brotherly love he showed; that it was right, indeed, to compensate Harry in some measure, since Harry’s expectations had led him into a costlier way of living than he would have adopted had he known his true place. Concerning the purchase of promotion, she would gladly halve the expense with George, thanking God to think Harry’s courage had won him his first step. George’s money spent thus, she added, was much more satisfactory than certain other extravagances to which she would not advert.

The extravagance she would not name was the ransom paid to the French captain’s family—an act George had done from his own stubborn honour, and to which his mother would never consent. She had a determined spirit of her own, which her son inherited; and what she called pride and obstinacy in him, she likely called firmness in herself. People a hundred years ago, I dare say, resembled their grandchildren: loving their own way mightily, and thinking it a virtue.

After paying his own ransom, his brother’s debts, and now half the price of Harry’s promotion, George saw that no small portion of his private patrimony would be swallowed up. Yet he made the sacrifice with a perfectly good heart. His mother’s letters were always urging him to remember who his grandfather was, and to support the dignity of the family accordingly. She sent him commissions for purchases in England; and though she had yet sent him but trifling remittances, she wrote so constantly of the exalted rank of the Esmonds, so peremptorily advised him to appear in the first society, to frequent the Court where his ancestors had moved, and to live always in a manner worthy of his name, that George never doubted her money would come when his ran short. He obeyed her injunctions in his style of life as dutifully as he obeyed her orders when she asked for goods.

In the papers of this period I find bills enough: for entertainments and genteel suppers where the meats were no doubt handsomely served; for court suits and lace; for liveries for his negro servants and chairmen; horse-dealers’ receipts, and so forth. Thus I am led to believe that the elder of our Virginians lived, after a while, at considerable expense. He was not wild like Harry; there was no talk of gambling or racehorses against Mr. George. But his table was liberal, his equipages handsome, his purse seemed always full; the estate to which he was heir was known to be immense.

I mention these things because they may have influenced the conduct of George and his friends in the very affair about which he had been writing. The young heir of Virginia travelled for pleasure and improvement; the queen-mother at home wrote daily, bidding him act as became his lofty station. There could be no doubt, from her letters, that she desired he should live magnificently. He was perpetually making purchases at her order. She had lately written for twelve new sets of waggon harness, and an organ that should play fourteen specified psalm-tunes; and these articles George dutifully ordered, as a son obeys even when he does not quite see the sense. She had not paid as yet, and might not pay to-day or to-morrow; but eventually, of course, she would—so George believed. And he never thought of troubling his friends with such calculations, or of discussing with them his mother’s domestic economy.

They, on their side, took it for granted that he was in competence and ease; and Mr. and Mrs. Lambert, without being mercenary, were no doubt pleased to see an attachment growing between their daughter and a young gentleman of such good principles, talents, family, and expectations.

There was honesty in all Mr. Esmond Warrington’s words and acts; and in his behaviour to the world a certain grandeur and simplicity which showed him to be a true gentleman. Somewhat cold, even haughty, towards strangers—especially towards the great—he was never supercilious. Towards women he was perfectly courteous; and with those he loved he could be kind, lively, tender—warm as a hearth when the day has been raw.

No wonder that one young woman we know of came to think him the best man in all the world—alas! not even excepting papa. A great love felt by a man towards a woman makes him better, as regards her, than all other men. George himself used to marvel at his own eloquence when he talked with the fair creature whose heart had become all his.

But we will not listen again at the keyhole. Soft words do not bear being written down. If you are sentimentally inclined—sir, or madam—lay the book aside and remember your own. You may be ever so old now; but you remember. It may be dead and buried; yet in a moment it springs up, looks, smiles, whispers as of old, clings to your arm, and drops its fresh tears upon your heart. Here is the vase, but the roses are gone; here is the shore, and yonder the ship was moored; but the anchors are up, and it has sailed away.

Et cetera.

This, however, is mere sentimentality; and as regards George and Theo is neither here nor there. What I mean to say is that the young lady’s family were perfectly satisfied with the state of affairs between her and Mr. Warrington; and though he had not yet asked the decisive question, everybody else knew what the answer would be when it came.

Mamma perhaps thought the question a long time coming.

“Psha! my dear,” says the General. “There is time enough in all conscience. Theo is not much more than seventeen; George, if I mistake not, is under forty; and, besides, he must have time to write to Virginia and ask mamma.”

“But suppose she refuses?”

“That will be a bad day for old and young,” says the General. “Let us rather say—suppose she consents, my love? I can’t fancy anybody in the world refusing Theo anything she has set her heart on,” adds the father; “and I am sure ’tis bent upon this match.”

So they waited with an anxiety that sat at table with them and went upstairs at night; they listened for every knock, as a house listens for a long-expected cart upon the road. And they trembled lest French privateers should take the packet-ship that carried, across all that loud and perilous water, the precious letter from Madam Esmond.
