I had just wrapped my final studies at Oxford, lingering in that soft, unmoored lull between the end of my student days and the full weight of assuming formal management of my inherited family estate. My father died when I was barely old enough to hold a clear memory of his face; my mother followed him to the grave a year later, leaving me as nearly alone in the world as a man could be without being utterly adrift. I knew almost nothing of my long line of ancestors, save that a great many of them had been quiet recluses, their lives devoted entirely to books and research. I had inherited that quirk myself: while I dabbled in the physical sciences in a desultory, curious sort of way, I was far more drawn to the strange, glowing overlaps I kept catching between different branches of knowledge—the half-glimpsed parallels between hard physical hypotheses and the foggy, vivid metaphysical daydreams I kept slipping into when I let my focus wander. I had a bad habit, too, of turning those flickering half-notions into full theories long before I had proof to back them, but there is no need to dwell on my small peculiarities of mind.

The old house, like the family that had lived in it for centuries, was thick with history, but no detailed description of its layout matters to the story I have to tell. What matters most is the library: its collection predates the printing press, growing and shifting with every generation’s changing tastes and obsessions, until it spread like a slow, hungry shadow. Over decades of renovations and additions, it swallowed one ground-floor room after another until it occupied nearly the whole of that level. Its main chamber was vast, walls lined with bookshelves that rose nearly to the carved plaster ceiling, and the overflow rooms that branched off it were all odd, mismatched sizes, connected by every sort of passage imaginable: regular oak doors, open stone arches, short winding hallways, three steps down to a sunken nook of medieval herbals, two steps up to a low-ceilinged closet of occult tracts. I spent nearly all my free time in the great main room, reading old science as eagerly as new, for nothing fascinated me more than tracing the slow, fumbling path of human curiosity as it groped through the dark of what we did not yet know. Ptolemy, Dante, the two Bacons, and Boyle felt more vivid, more real to me than Darwin or Maxwell—they were the ones at the very front of the charge, breaking through the edge of total ignorance.

It was a gloomy August day, the rain streaking the library windows for hours on end, until just before sunset the clouds split open right in front of the sinking sun, spilling a flood of deep crimson light straight across the room. I had been sitting with my back to the window, nose buried in a 17th century chemistry text, and I rose to look out. The tall fountain at the center of the lawn caught that red light in its rising spray, turning every drop into flying embers. When I turned back to my chair, that same gilded, bloody glow caught the only painting in the whole vast room: a portrait set in a small, sunken niche carved straight into the bookshelves. I knew it was one of my ancestors, but I had never once wondered why it hung here, all alone, instead of in the formal portrait gallery with the rest of the family likenesses. That direct sunset light woke the painting to life; for the first time, I did not just see the brushstrokes, I felt the man’s eyes meet mine across the room, like he had been waiting for me to look for years.

My eyes still swimming with that reflected red light, I glanced idly toward the far end of the great reading room—and there was a tall, lean figure, stretching a hand up to reach a volume on the highest shelf. The next instant, the dimmer dusk of the far corner corrected my sight, and no one was there. I told myself my optic nerves had been tricked by the afterimage of the sun on the portrait, shrugged, and went back to my book. I would have forgotten the whole fleeting oddity entirely, if I had not needed to pull a specific rare alchemical treatise from the shelf a moment later. The spot where it should have stood was empty, bare wood where its spine should have been. The second I noticed the gap, I remembered: that was the exact spot I had seen the phantom figure reaching. I searched every nearby shelf for half an hour, but the book was gone. The next morning, though, there it sat, right back in its usual spot on the shelf, perfectly aligned with the rest. There was no one else in the house who cared anything for that kind of obscure, old text.

Three days later, a far stranger thing happened. Set into one wall was a low, narrow door leading to a locked closet that held some of the oldest, rarest volumes in the whole collection. Some long-dead ancestor had the whimsical idea of gluing fake book spines to the front of the thick, heavy door, turning it into a harmless, clever prank: the titles on the sham spines were either silly jokes, or names of books that had been lost centuries before. I had always loved that trick door. To make the illusion even more perfect, some old workman had wedged a thin, partial volume into the top of one of the fake shelves, cutting a diagonal chunk out of it so its limp vellum binding jutted out past the fake spines, its open corner peeking out far enough to show the handwritten parchment pages inside.

I looked up from my reading that afternoon, and the half-manuscript was gone.

I was angrier than the small, trivial value of the scrap of book justified, and rang the bell for the butler. He had served my family his whole life, more faithful than any servant I could have asked for, but he turned deathly pale the second I asked about the missing volume, and swore he had no idea what had happened to it. I believed his word easily—but I could tell he was holding something back, something he had been told never to speak of.

Later that same afternoon, I was reading again, pausing to puzzle over a dense passage, my eyes wandering idly over the far side of the room. That was when I saw it: the back of a slender, stooped old man in a long, dark wool coat, shiny from decades of wear, gliding straight through that trick prank door as if it was made of mist, vanishing into the closet behind. I sprinted across the room so fast I nearly tripped over a footstool, wrenched the heavy door open, and stared into the small closet. It had no other entrance, no windows, no hiding places. I was alone.

I told myself I was having another hallucination, that the long quiet days alone in the library were making my mind play tricks. But a few minutes later, I glanced up to reassure myself I was truly by myself—and nearly screamed. The half-vellum manuscript was back in its spot on the fake shelf. I lunged for it, pulled as hard as I could, and it would not budge an inch, wedged as firmly as it had always been.

I was completely bewildered. I rang the bell for the butler again, told him every last thing I had seen, and he spilled every secret he had been keeping for decades. He had hoped the old gentleman would stay forgotten, he said, that no one but me would ever catch a glimpse of him. No one had ever dared mention the figure in the library in my grandfather’s hearing: the old man had sworn he would fire any servant who so much as breathed a word of it, calling the whole thing a silly ghost story maids made up to have an excuse to run into the footmen’s arms. Not a single maid in living memory had ever claimed to see the apparition, but one footman had run off in the middle of the night without a word, too terrified to stay.

An old crone who lived at the edge of the village had told him the legend, he said. The figure people saw was Mr. Raven, the librarian who had served Sir Upward—the very man whose portrait hung sunken in the bookshelf niche across the room. Sir Upward had been obsessed not just with regular books, but with strange, forbidden, terrible texts, and Raven had encouraged him every step of the way. Hundreds of years ago, the pair of them vanished without a trace, no body ever found, no note ever left. No one had seen Sir Upward again after that, but Mr. Raven had kept showing up in the library, wandering the shelves, at random intervals ever since. The old woman swore he was not alive, not in any way that made sense for a human man, but that he had never truly left the library he had guarded his whole life.

“I hope his visit was a friendly one, sir,” the butler finished, his face tight with unease.

I told him I had no objection at all to Mr. Raven wandering the shelves as he pleased, but that he must say nothing of the ghost to the rest of the staff, to keep them from panicking. I asked him if he had ever seen that half-vellum manuscript loose or moved, and he shook his head. He walked over to the trick door, wrapped his fingers around the vellum corner, and pulled as hard as he could. It did not move a single inch.
