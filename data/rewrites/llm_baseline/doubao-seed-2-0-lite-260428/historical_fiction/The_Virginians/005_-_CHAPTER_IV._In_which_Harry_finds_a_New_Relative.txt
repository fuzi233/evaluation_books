All the markers of a supposedly perfect earthly happiness were laid at Madam Esmond Warrington’s feet at Castlewood, her Virginia principality in Westmorland County: an ancient, storied family name, thousands of acres of rich tobacco land, a steady fortune, a comfortable home stacked high with every necessity and dozens of fine luxuries, a whole household of Black enslaved servants and white free hands tripping over themselves to tend to her every bidding. She had good health, two loyal young sons, a cook who never served a bad meal, a cellar full of fine Madeira, and a library lined with leather-bound classics. She reminded herself of every one of these blessings daily in her morning and evening prayers. She was scrupulous in her devotions, generous to the local poor, and never knowingly wronged a soul.

And yet, few who knew her would have traded places with the sharp, imperious little matron her neighbors teasingly called “Princess Pocahontas” behind her back. The husband who died young was far better off gone; had he lived decades longer, the pair would have torn each other apart, or he would have ended up the most thoroughly henpecked man in the colony, no exceptions. The truth was, Madam Esmond never met a man or woman she did not immediately try to bend to her will. Obey her, and she was your staunchest, most loyal friend; resist her, and she would fight tooth and nail for months until one of you broke. She was fond of declaring, in the clearest, firmest voice in the parish, that all mortal souls were miserable sinners, as every man admitted aloud every Sunday—she just very rarely acknowledged the sin might be hers, never to anyone but her late father.

Colonel Esmond, her father, had been the only person alive who could tease her out of her worst tyrannical whims, his quiet good humor something she could never boss or bully past. But once he was buried, there was no one left in the whole colony to rein her in, and I for one am very glad I never resided at Castlewood a hundred years back. Even Paradise had its serpent, after all; no mortal is ever perfectly happy for long.

Madam had seized full control of the estate long before the Colonel drew his last breath. She fired his old London factor, hired a new agent who answered only to her, cleared hundreds of new acres for tobacco, built new outbuildings, appointed a strict new overseer, and imported a tutor for her two boys. She had been a dutiful daughter all her life, obeying every last order her parents gave her, so she reasoned she was fully entitled to run her little dominion exactly as she saw fit, even if it went against her father’s old, easygoing rules. Her sons George and Harry were not equal heirs in her mind: they were merely her first two subjects. Before long she dropped her late husband’s Warrington name entirely, insisting every soul in the county call her Madam Esmond, and loved to drop hints about the old Jacobite Marquis title King James had granted to her Esmond forebears. Her father, for all his enormous magnanimity, had given up the family’s claims and titles to the younger English branch, to my Lord Castlewood and his brood—but she and her sons were the true elder line of the house of Esmond, and everyone was going to treat them as such.

Only Lord Fairfax, the grandest old grandee in Virginia, was permitted to walk ahead of her. She insisted on precedence over every lieutenant-governor’s wife, every judge’s lady, and only stepped aside for the royal governor’s spouse, as the direct representative of the Crown. Family papers and surviving letters are full of accounts of the screaming public fights she got into with colonial official wives over these tiny points of etiquette. As for her husband’s Norfolk Warrington relations? They were nobodies, far beneath her notice. She’d only married the younger son of an English baronet to please her parents, when she was barely out of the boarding school schoolroom, and would have jumped over the side of the ship that brought her to Virginia if her father had ordered it. “That’s the way of all the Esmonds,” she’d say proudly.

Unsurprisingly, the English Warringtons and Castlewood Esmonds took deep offense at her endless snubs. For years she sent a solemn formal letter once a year to each branch of the family, until one judge’s lady she’d quarreled with sailed back to England, ran into Lady Warrington in London where Sir Miles sat in Parliament, and repeated all the unkind things Madam Esmond was fond of saying about her English relatives. Lady Warrington passed the gossip on to Lady Castlewood, and after that, none of Madam’s letters got a single reply. Furious, she soon stopped writing them too, cutting off all contact with the entire side of the family across the Atlantic.

She soon fell out with far closer people. The first major rift with her sons came when she dismissed Mr. Dempster, their old tutor and Colonel Esmond’s long-time secretary. While the Colonel was alive, Madam could barely stand the man, and the feeling was mutual: she distrusted bookish folk, convinced scholars snuck radical, bad ideas into impressionable young heads, and spread the rumor that Dempster was a Jesuit spy in disguise, until the poor fellow was forced to move off to a remote backcountry clearing, build a tiny cabin, and scratch out a living running a little school and practicing rough medicine for the scattered farm families. George, the older boy, had loved his old tutor, swore he’d keep in touch no matter what, and kept that promise. Harry, who always preferred fishing and foxhunting to Greek grammar, had never been close to Dempster, so the rift between Madam and George only grew sharper later.

The next fight was over six thousand pounds, a sum left to George Warrington’s heir (young George, of course) by a dead aunt, with Madam Esmond and a London lawyer named Draper named as co-trustees. Madam simply could not wrap her head around the fact that she was only the trustee, not the full owner, of that money. She flew into a rage when Draper refused to ship the entire sum over to her on her order. “Everything I have belongs to my boys!” she’d shout. “I’d cut myself into pieces to serve them! With that six thousand pounds I could buy Mr. Boulter’s entire estate, all his enslaved workers, pull in a thousand pounds a year extra, and set Harry up properly, for he only gets a tiny pittance as a younger son.” Even her young neighbor George Washington of Mount Vernon could not convince her that Draper was doing his legal duty, that he could not hand over trust money before the heir came of age. Madam fired off a scathing letter to the London lawyer, calling him an insolent pettifogger who dared to doubt the honor of an Esmond and a loving mother.

When she demanded fourteen-year-old George back her play, he sided with Washington and Draper, saying he had no right to take the money before he came of age, and would give every penny of it to her to do with as she wished if he could. But Madam Esmond would hear none of it. Feelings were her only logic. Here was a chance to make Harry’s fortune, her poor dear portionless youngest son, and the wretches in London, and even his own selfish older brother, stood in the way! She called George greedy, unkind, disobedient, burst into tears, hurled endless bitter little digs at him, and the rift stretched on for months. There are still plenty of ladies who argue that same way today, after all; the book of women’s logic is blotted all over with tear stains, and justice in their private courts is almost always run by passion.

So Madam decided she would pinch every penny to save a proper inheritance for Harry. She halted all the grand new building work Colonel Esmond had started at Castlewood: the shipments of Dutch bricks from New York, the carved mantelpieces, the expensive imported glass and upholstery she’d ordered from London, all of it, cancelled. No more new books. No more shipments of fine wine. She even regretted buying the fancy new carriage Hatchett’s of London had made her for 380 guineas, and every Sunday when she rode to church in it, she’d groan out loud to her two boys sitting opposite her, “Harry, Harry! I wish I’d put that money aside for you, my poor child.”

Harry, good-natured as ever, laughed it off. “You’ll give me everything I want while you live, and George will give me plenty when you’re gone, ma’am.”

“Not unless Heaven softens George’s hard heart and teaches him charity, which I pray for night and day,” she’d snap, shooting a grim look at her older son, and turning to her companion, the widow Mrs. Mountain, who took the fourth seat in the coach, “Don’t I, Mountain?”

Mrs. Mountain huffed. “I know you carry on about that legacy far more than there’s any need for.”

“Oh, no need at all!” Madam rustled her silk skirts, indignant. “Of course there’s no need for me to be upset, because my eldest born is a disobedient son, an unkind brother, who gets the whole of my father’s estate and leaves my poor Harry with nothing but a mess of pottage!”

George’s eyes welled up with tears. “Oh mother, I wish you would love me too,” he said, and burst out sobbing.

In a second Harry had his arms wrapped around his brother’s neck, kissing his cheek a dozen times. “Don’t mind her, George. I know what a good brother you are. She doesn’t mean a word of it.”

“I do mean it!” Madam started, and Harry roared out at the top of his voice, “Hold your tongue, ma’am! It’s a damned shame to speak to him that way!”

“And so it is, Harry,” Mrs. Mountain said, squeezing his hand. “You never spoke a truer word.”

“Mrs. Mountain! Do you dare turn my children against me?” Madam shrieked. “From this very day, madam—”

“Turn me and my orphan child out on the road? Go right ahead,” Mrs. Mountain said, unflinching. “That’ll be a fine show, all because that London lawyer won’t hand over the boy’s money. Go find another companion who’ll tell you black is white and flatter you all day long. That’s not my way. I didn’t bring much to Castlewood, and I won’t take much when I leave. When should I start packing?”

But Madam heard the church bells starting to ring, and composed herself. “Hush, Mountain, let us try to be calm. The bells are summoning us to service.” She looked at her two boys then, her eyes softening with real affection. George kept his head down the whole walk to church, and during the whole service, Harry sat right next to him, his arm slung around his brother’s neck.

After that, Harry struck out on a little journey of his own, and found himself sitting by the fireside in an old wainscoted parlour that had seen two hundred years of Esmond family dinners, walls lined with the great family portraits. An old lady named Madam Bernstein, who he’d just met, hung on every word of his long, rambling story about life at Castlewood, dotted with all his boyish exclamations. Her hostess and daughters kept popping in to invite her to ride, walk, take tea, or play cards, but Madam Bernstein waved them all off, saying Harry’s conversation was more entertaining than any of those things. Whenever any of the local Castlewood cousins popped into the room, she’d double down on her fondness for Harry, tug him close to her ear, and call out to the others, “Hush, dears! I can’t hear our cousin speak,” until they left the room, trying hard not to look annoyed.

“Are you my cousin too?” Harry asked her, earnest. “You’re far kinder than all my other English cousins.”

Over her easy chair hung a brilliant Sir Godfrey Kneller portrait, painted in Queen Anne’s reign, of a beautiful young woman in flowing loose robes, auburn hair falling over her pearly fair shoulders. Harry stared up at it.

“That’s Sir Godfrey’s work,” Madam Bernstein said, twirling her knitting needles. “He thought he was the finest painter alive, but he was not half as good as Lely, who painted your grandmother, my Lady Castlewood, Colonel Esmond’s wife. Not as good as Van Dyck, who painted your great-grandfather over there, who looks far finer in his portrait than he ever was in real life. Some of us get painted far blacker than we deserve, and some far finer. Do you recognize any of the face in that portrait, Harry?”

“I thought it looked a little like my mother,” Harry said.

“Does Mrs. Warrington—wait, you call her Madam Esmond now, I hear?”

“That’s what everyone in our colony calls her,” Harry said.

“Did she ever tell you her mother had another daughter, born in England before she married your grandfather, Colonel Esmond?”

“Never,” Harry said. “And my grandfather never mentioned her either. But in all the little sketchbooks he drew for us when we were small children, he drew a head that looked exactly like that portrait over your chair, at least a dozen times, right next to pictures of Viscount Francis and King James the Third.”

Madam Bernstein sighed softly. “And that face up there reminds you of no one else, Harry?”

“No, ma’am, it does not.”

“Ah, then I must tell you something,” she said, reaching out to pat his hand. “Once that was my face, Harry. Once I was Beatrix Esmond. Your mother is my half-sister, child. She has never once even spoken my name to you, all these years.”
