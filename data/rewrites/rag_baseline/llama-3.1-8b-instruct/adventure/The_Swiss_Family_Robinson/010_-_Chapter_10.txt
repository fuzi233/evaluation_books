As we navigated the intricacies of island life, our attention turned to the pressing issue of avian management. The prolific pigeon population, drawn to the rich resources of our makeshift settlement, posed a significant challenge to our food security. The previous year's harvest had been modest, with only scattered flocks arriving at their feeding grounds, but this season saw an unprecedented influx of birds, overwhelming the branches of our evergreen oaks.

The sheer scale of the pigeon congregation was a testament to the bounty of our surroundings, yet it also underscored the need for effective management. Our bird-lime, a concoction of caoutchouc oil and turpentine, had proven effective in capturing the birds, but we required a more systematic approach to ensure a sustainable supply of protein. I decided to implement a nocturnal strategy, reminiscent of the colonial practices employed in Virginia, where torches would be used to flush out the birds from their roosting sites.

As I supervised the application of the bird-lime, I noticed that the boys were becoming increasingly adept at handling the sticky mixture and placing the rods in strategic locations. With their assistance, I was able to focus on preparing the necessary equipment for the night's operation: long bamboo canes, torches, and canvas sacks. While the children might have viewed these tools as insufficient, their utility would soon become apparent as we ventured into the woods under the cover of darkness.

The sudden illumination of the torches sent the pigeons into a state of panic, causing them to flail about in confusion. Many fell from the branches, providing an initial bounty, and the rest were quickly dispatched using the bamboo canes. My wife and Franz worked tirelessly to collect the fallen birds, their sacks filling rapidly as the night wore on. As we made our way back to Falconhurst, we were met with a sense of accomplishment, knowing that we had secured a reliable source of sustenance for the coming days.

The following morning, we devoted ourselves to processing the pigeons, plucking, boiling, roasting, and stewing the meat to create a variety of dishes. The task was labor-intensive, but the end result was well worth the effort. As we sat down to enjoy our meal, we knew that we had taken a crucial step towards establishing a more self-sufficient lifestyle on the island. The next day, we planned an expedition to Woodlands to implement measures that would prevent future invasions by the mischievous monkeys, whose destructive tendencies threatened the stability of our little community.
