To understand the architecture of this fleeting calm, one must first grasp the precarious equilibrium of the fund. Through the catalytic example of Miss Keeldar, the vigorous exertions of the three rectors, and the efficient, silent machinery of their spinster lieutenants—Mary Ann Ainley and Margaret Hall—a substantial sum was coalesced. Judiciously managed, this reservoir of charity served to alleviate the immediate suffocation of the unemployed poor, creating a temporary vacuum of desperation in the neighborhood. For two weeks, the fabric of society held; no cloth was torn, no mill desecrated, no mansion violated in the three parishes. Shirley, buoyant with a sanguine optimism, believed the storm she sought to avert was merely passing overhead. With the approach of summer, she reasoned that trade would inevitably rebound—it always did—and this weary war could not endure forever. Peace must return; with peace, commerce would surge.

Yet, such was the tenor of her observations to her tenant, Gérard Moore, whenever their paths crossed in conversation, that Moore listened with a silence too profound to satisfy her. She demanded more, her impatient glance cutting through his composure, seeking an explanation or at least a concession. But Moore smiled in his peculiar manner, a expression that bestowed a remarkable sweetness upon his mouth while his brow remained a fortress of gravity. He answered that he, too, anchored his hopes in the finite nature of the conflict. "You are aware," he continued, his voice steady beneath the surface tension, "that I now operate Hollow's Mill entirely on speculation. I sell nothing; there is no market for my goods. I manufacture for a future day, preparing to seize the first opening that shall occur. Three months ago, this was impossible; I had exhausted both credit and capital. You know who came to my rescue, from what hand I received the loan that saved me. It is on the strength of that debt I continue this bold game, one I once feared I would never play again. Total ruin follows loss, and gain remains doubtful; yet I remain cheerful. So long as I can be active, so long as I can strive, so long as my hands are not tied, depression is impossible. One year—nay, six months—of the reign of the olive, and I am safe. Peace will give an impulse to commerce, as you say. But regarding the restored tranquility of the neighborhood, or the permanent efficacy of your charitable fund, I harbor deep skepticism. Alms never yet tranquilized the working classes; they never made them grateful. It is not in human nature. Were all things ordered aright, they ought not to require that humiliating relief, yet they feel compelled to seek it. And to whom should they owe gratitude? To you, perhaps to the clergy, but certainly not to us, the mill-owners. They hate us worse than ever. Furthermore, the disaffected here maintain correspondence with the disaffected elsewhere: Nottingham, Manchester, Birmingham serve as their headquarters. The subalterns receive orders from their chiefs; they exist in a state of disciplined readiness. No blow is struck without mature deliberation. In sultry weather, you have seen the sky threaten thunder day by day, yet night after night the clouds clear, and the sun sets quietly. The danger was not gone; it was only delayed. The long-threatening storm is sure to break eventually. There is a direct analogy between the moral and physical atmosphere."

"Well, Mr. Moore," the conference concluded, "take care of yourself. If you believe I have ever done you any good, reward me by promising to take care of yourself."

"I do; I will take close and watchful care. I wish to live, not to die. The future opens like Eden before me; and still, when I look deep into the shades of my paradise, I see a vision preferable to any seraph or cherub gliding across remote vistas."

"Do you? Pray, what vision?"

"I see—"

The maid bustled in with the tea-things, interrupting the philosophical exchange.

The early part of that May had been fine, the middle wet, but in the last week, at the change of the moon, the skies cleared. A fresh wind swept away the silver-white, deep-piled rain-clouds, bearing them mass upon mass to the eastern horizon, where they dwindled and vanished behind the rim, leaving the vault of heaven pure blue, ready for the reign of the summer sun. That sun rose broad and unimpeded on Whitsuntide. The gathering of the schools was signaled by splendid weather.

Whit-Tuesday was the great day. In preparation, the two large schoolrooms of Briarfield, built by the present rector chiefly at his own expense, were scrubbed clean, whitewashed, repainted, and adorned with flowers and evergreens—some from the rectory garden, two cartloads from Fieldhead, and a wheelbarrow-full from the more stingy domain of De Walden, the residence of Mr. Wynne. Within these rooms, twenty tables, each calculated to accommodate twenty guests, were laid out, surrounded by benches and draped in white cloths. Suspended above were at least twenty cages containing as many canaries, adhering to a local fancy specially cherished by Mr. Helstone's clerk, who delighted in their piercing song and knew that amidst a confusion of tongues, they always carolled loudest. These tables were not spread for the twelve hundred scholars assembling from the three parishes, but solely for the patrons and teachers. The children's feast was to be spread in the open air. At one o'clock the troops were to arrive; at two, they were to be marshaled; until four, they would parade the parish; then came the feast, followed by the meeting, complete with music and speechifying in the church.

Why Briarfield was chosen as the rendezvous—the scene of the fête—required explanation. It was not because it was the largest or most populous parish—Whinbury far outdid it in that regard; nor because it was the oldest, for Nunnely's low-roofed temple and mossy parsonage, buried in coeval oaks and standing as sentinel guardians of Nunnwood, were ancient still. It was simply because Mr. Helstone willed it so, and Mr. Helstone's will proved stronger than that of Boultby or Hall. The former *could* not, the latter *would* not, dispute a point of precedence with their resolute and imperious brother. They allowed him to lead and rule.

This notable anniversary had always been a trying day for Caroline Helstone, dragging her perforce into public view, compelling her to face all that was wealthy, respectable, and influential in the neighborhood. In whose presence, but for the kind countenance of Mr. Hall, she would have appeared unsupported. Obligated to be conspicuous; obligated to walk at the head of her regiment as the rector's niece and first teacher of the first class; obligated to serve tea at the first table for a mixed multitude of ladies and gentlemen—all without the countenance of mother, aunt, or other chaperon—she, being a nervous person who mortally feared publicity, trembled at the approach of Whitsuntide.

But this year, Shirley was to be with her, and that altered the aspect of the trial singularly—it changed it utterly. It was no longer a trial; it was almost an enjoyment. Miss Keeldar, in her single self, surpassed a host of ordinary friends. Quite self-possessed, always spirited and easy, conscious of her social importance yet never presuming upon it, her mere presence was enough to instill courage. The only fear was lest the heiress should not be punctual to the tryst. She often possessed a careless way of lingering behind time, and Caroline knew her uncle would not wait a second for anyone. At the moment the church clock tolled two, the bells would clash out and the march begin. She must ensure Shirley's arrival, or her expected companion would fail her.

Whit-Tuesday saw her rise almost with the sun. She, Fanny, and Eliza were busy the whole morning arranging the rectory parlors in first-rate company order, setting out a collation of cooling refreshments—wine, fruit, cakes—on the dining-room sideboard. Then she had to don her freshest and fairest attire of white muslin: the perfect fineness of the day and the solemnity of the occasion warranted, and even exacted, such a costume. Her new sash—a birthday present from Margaret Hall, which she had reason to believe Cyril himself had purchased, and in return for which she had indeed gifted him a set of cambric bands in a handsome case—was tied by the dexterous fingers of Fanny, who took no little pleasure in arraying her fair young mistress for the occasion. Her simple bonnet had been trimmed to correspond with her sash; her pretty but inexpensive scarf of white crape suited her dress perfectly. When ready, she formed a picture, not bright enough to dazzle, but fair enough to interest; not brilliantly striking, but very delicately pleasing—a portrait in which sweetness of tint, purity of air, and grace of mien atoned for the absence of rich coloring and magnificent contour. What her brown eye and clear forehead revealed of her mind was in keeping with her dress and face: modest, gentle, and, though pensive, harmonious. It appeared that neither lamb nor dove needed fear her, but would welcome rather, in her look of simplicity and softness, a sympathy with their own natures, or with the natures we ascribe to them.
