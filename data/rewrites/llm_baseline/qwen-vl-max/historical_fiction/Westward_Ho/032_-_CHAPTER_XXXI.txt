“Britannia needs no bulwarks, No towers along the steep, Her march is o'er the mountain wave, Her home is on the deep.”

CAMPBELL, Ye Mariners of England.

Now began the great sea battle that would decide whether Europe’s future—indeed, the fate of America—would be shaped by faith and freedom or by tyranny and superstition. This was no mere clash of fleets but a twelve-day epic, worthy not of dull prose but of the thunderous verse of Homer. Yet I must tell it as best I can, drawing wherever possible upon the words of those who lived it, rather than my own imagination.

“The Lord High Admiral of England sent ahead a pinnace named the *Defiance*, which fired its guns to declare war. Then, closing in within musket range, he approached with great noise from his own ship, the *Arkroyall* (also known as the *Triumph*), and attacked what he believed to be the Spanish admiral’s vessel—but it was actually Alfonso de Leon’s ship. Shortly after, Drake, Hawkins, and Frobisher opened fire with fierce determination upon the rearmost squadron under Recalde.” The Spaniards quickly realized the superior agility of the English ships. Recalde’s division, unable to match their speed or precision despite his efforts, broke off and hurried forward to join the main fleet. Medina, the Spanish Admiral, seeing his ships scattering, formed them into a half-moon formation. The Armada pressed onward, like a stately herd of buffalo crossing a prairie, heedless of the wolves snapping at their flanks. But these were no wolves—they were swift, skilled hunters, mounted and armed, who would “shamefully shuffle” (in Drake’s own words) that mighty host from the Lizard to Portland, from Portland to Calais Roads. Even in this first two-hour engagement, many a Spaniard began to doubt the legendary invincibility of their fleet.

One of the four great galliasses had already been riddled with shot, its pulpits and chapels shattered, its friars thrown into chaos. The fleet was forced to cluster around her to protect her from Drake and Hawkins, who threatened to sink her. In doing so, the “principal galleon of Seville,” carrying Pedro de Valdez and a company of noble Dons, collided with a neighboring ship, lost her foremast, and was abandoned—despite the chivalry of her crew. This did not look like victory. But courage remained: “Our Lady,” the saints, and the bull *Caena Domini* (inspired by one whose name I dare not speak here) still stood with them. To question divine favor would be sacrilege. Still, if they could not prevail against only a third of the Plymouth fleet, how would they fare when the forty belated ships now whitening the horizon between them and the Mewstone joined the fray?

So ended the first day. Not a single English ship nor man was harmed. The myth of Spain’s unassailable power lay broken in the minds of every English sailor. The strategy adopted by Lord Howard—guided by Raleigh and Drake—had proved wise: maintaining a running fight, avoiding close combat without thought. As Raleigh said, “Had I not been better advised than many malignant fools who criticized my conduct, I would have perished.”

Whatever the truth, the first day passed with honor. Amyas Leigh and the Bideford vessels had spent two hours pounding a massive galleon bearing on her stern a figure of Saint Catherine—a maiden with a wheel—and flying a flag with a coat of arms at her sprit. The arms had been shot away early in the fight, so Amyas could not tell if they belonged to De Soto. But there was time enough for personal vengeance. When finally called off by the admiral’s signal, he retired to his hammock and slept soundly.

Yet barely an hour had passed before Cary descended to awaken him.

“We were supposed to follow Drake’s lantern, Amyas,” he said. “But I cannot see it anywhere—unless he’s been hoisted up among the stars as a new Drakium Sidus.”

Amyas grumbled as he rose. But no lantern appeared. Only a sudden explosion and a great blaze aboard a Spanish ship, which gradually died down. The fleet lay-to all night long, nearly the entire armada anchored in silence.

The next morning found them off Torbay. A pinnace hailed Amyas with a letter from Drake—written in the rough hand of a seaman, spelling haphazard as was common in those days:

“DEAR LAD,—I’ve been wool-gathering all night chasing five great hulks, which the Pixies turned into galleons overnight, then back again into German merchantmen. I let them go with my blessing. Returning, I fell in (God be thanked!) with Valdez’s great galleon—rich spoil, left behind by the Dons, who behaved like faithful comrades, and whom Lord Howard missed, thinking her deserted. I’ve sent to Dartmouth a sight of noblemen and gentlemen—perhaps fifty—and Valdez himself. When I sent my pinnace aboard, he insisted on punctilio, offering terms. I replied I had no time for talk; if he wanted to die, I was the man for it; if he wished to live, then *buena querra*. He returned, boasting he was Don Pedro Valdez, and that it was beneath his honor and that of his companions. I answered: ‘For my part, I am Francis Drake, and my matches are burning.’ At that, he found comfort in my name, came aboard, kissed my fist, and spouted Spanish lies about being fortunate to fall into the hands of lucky Drake—words he might have saved for cooling his porridge. But I learned much from him (he’s a leaky tub). Among other things, your Don Guzman is aboard the *Sta. Catharina*, commander of her soldiers, with his arms flying at her sprit, beside Saint Catherine at the stern—she’s a tall ship of three decks of ordnance. God preserve you, and send you good fortune.

“Your dear Friend and Admiral,

“F. Drake.

“She sails in Recalde’s squadron. The Armada meant to smoke us out of Plymouth—thank God they tried not. But their orders from home are too strict, and so the slaves fight like a bull on a tether, no farther than their rope. They find the devil a hard master, as most do in the end. They cannot match our quick tacking and maneuvering, and take us for witches. So far, so good—and better to come. You and I know the length of their foot well. Time and light will kill any hare, and they’ll find it a long way from Start to Dunkirk.”

“The admiral is in a generous mood, Leigh, to send you such a long letter,” remarked Cary.

“Saint Catherine! Why, that was the galleon we hammered yesterday!” Amyas stamped on deck.

“Of course it was. We’ll find her again, no doubt. That clever old Drake—how he manages to line his pockets even while keeping the whole fleet waiting!”

“He’s given the lord high admiral the slip, at least.”

“Lord Howard is too noble to stop and plunder, Papist though he is, Amys.”

Amyas growled in reply—he adored Drake and held little mercy for Catholics.

They did not meet Lord Howard until dusk. He and Lord Sheffield had pursued the Spanish lanterns through the night with only two ships. Now there could be no doubt of the loyalty of English Roman Catholics. Throughout the battle, the Howards had shown desperate courage—proving themselves more than willing to risk all, even if others had doubted them. Soon, a large Spanish ship drifted past, abandoned and partly burned. Some men urged boarding her, but Amyas refused. “We came to fight, not to loot,” he said. “Let the nearest ship claim her prize without envy.” They passed on. Later, they saw the galleon towed away by a neighbor, taken to Weymouth. It turned out to be Miguel d’Oquenda’s vessel—the vice-admiral’s ship—seen the previous night nearly destroyed by a furious Netherland gunner who, having been mistreated, sought revenge on his tyrants.

Thus ended the second day. The Portland Rock rose higher and clearer each hour. The next morning found them near the island. Would they attempt Portsmouth, despite sparing Plymouth? The wind had shifted northward, blowing cool from the white downs of Weymouth Bay. The Spaniards turned to face the English. They intended to wait until the wind changed, then try for the Needles. At least, they would have work to do before rounding Purbeck Isle.

The English sailed westward—only to return on the opposite tack. Now began a series of maneuvers, each fleet striving to gain the weather gauge. But the struggle was brief. By noon, the English fleet had slipped close-hauled between the Armada and the coast, advancing directly toward them with the wind at their backs.

And then the battle erupted—fierce and deadly. “They fought confusedly, with shifting fortunes. On one side, the English heroically rescued London’s ships, trapped by the Spaniards. On the other, the Spaniards fiercely freed Recalde, who was in danger.” “Never was heard such thunder from ordnance on both sides, yet mostly the Spanish shots flew over the English harmlessly. Only Cock, an Englishman” (whom Prince claims as a son of Devon, and rightly so), “died bravely in the midst of the enemy in a small ship. For the English vessels, smaller and swifter, charged with astonishing agility, discharged their broadsides, then darted away into deeper water, leveling their shots precisely at the massive, unwieldy Spanish ships.” “This was the fiercest and bloodiest skirmish of all,” though it ended only in the capture of a great Venetian vessel and some minor craft. “The Lord Admiral fought amidst the enemy’s fleet and, seeing one of his captains afar off—Fenner by name, the same who fought the seven Portugals at the Azores—cried: ‘O George, what doest thou? Wilt thou now frustrate my hope and opinion conceived of thee? Wilt thou forsake me now?’ Fired by these words, Fenner advanced and fought like a valiant captain”—as did all the rest.

Night fell upon the floating volcano. Morning found them far beyond Purbeck, the white peak of Freshwater ahead. Ship after ship poured past the Needles, joining the gallant chase. From every harbor, nobles and gentlemen flocked forth in privately outfitted vessels—Lords Oxford, Northumberland, Cumberland, Pallavicin, Brooke, Carew, Raleigh, Blunt, and many others—rushing “to a set field where immortal fame and honor were to be won.” Spain had staked its nobility in this great gamble—every noble house of Aragon and Castile had contributed a brother or son—and would mourn their loss. England’s gentlemen would measure their strength once and for all against the Cavaliers of Spain.

Lord Howard had dispatched light vessels to Portsmouth for ammunition, but they would likely not return that night. The wind had died, and all evening the fleets drifted helplessly with the tide, shouting empty defiance through trumpet, fife, and drum.

The sun sank upon a glassy sea, and rose again upon a glassy sea. But what day was this? The twenty-fifth—St. James’s Day, sacred to Spain’s patron saint. Would nothing be attempted in his honor? Had not their ancestors seen him riding white horse, lance aloft, driving infidels before him? He might have sent them a favorable breeze—or perhaps He meant to test their faith. At least, the galleys would attack. Three of the great galliasses (the fourth crippled) cut the waves with three hundred oars each. And lo—no St. James led them, but Lord Howard’s *Triumph*, his brother’s *Lion*, Southwell’s *Elizabeth Jonas*, Lord Sheffield’s *Bear*, Barker’s *Victory*, and George Fenner’s *Leicester*, towed boldly forward to meet them. With salvoes of chain-shot, they shattered oars and severed rigging. Had the wind not risen again by noon, and the Spanish fleet not rushed to rescue them, they would have shared Valdez’s fate.

Now the battle became general. Frobisher brought down the Spanish admiral’s mainmast. Attacked by Mexia and Recalde, he was rescued by Lord Howard—who, himself endangered, was in turn saved. “After that day,” the Spaniards said, “no galliasse dared engage.”

So the fight raged through the livelong afternoon beneath the virgin cliffs of Freshwater. Myriad seabirds rose screaming from every ledge, black wings cutting across the chalk-white wall. A lone shepherd raced down the slopes above, peering over the dizzy edge, forgetting the wheatear caught in his snare, transfixed by glimpses of towering masts and glittering flags piercing the thick veil of sulphurous smoke below.

Thus passed St. James’s Day, as Baal did on Carmel in ancient times: “Either he is talking, or he is pursuing, or he is on a journey; or peradventure he sleepeth, and must be awaked.” Indeed, the only fire offered by the saint was that of English cannon. The Armada, gathering into a tight circle, would fight no more. It made for Calais, hoping the Guises might offer French aid, then to Dunkirk to join Parma and the vast flotilla of the Netherlands.

Onward they glided before a fair Etesian gale, clear and bright from the south-southwest—past Brighton Cliffs, Beachy Head, Hastings, Dungeness. Was it battle or triumph? Lord Howard, instead of fighting, was rewarding. After Lord Thomas Howard, Lord Sheffield, Townsend, and Frobisher received knighthood—then more honorable than peerage—old Admiral Hawkins knelt and rose Sir John, shaking his shoulders after the accolade and saying to the representative of majesty: “My old woman will hardly know herself when folks call her My Lady.”

Meanwhile, the cliffs were lined with pikemen and musketeers, countrymen and grooms bearing arms, led by squires and sheriffs marching eastward as fast as their weapons allowed toward Dover. And not alone. Women and children, elders in wagons, streamed from miles inland to add their feeble shouts and fervent prayers to the great cry of faith and fear rising to God from Britain’s Salamis.

Let them pray. The danger was not yet over. Though Lord Howard had learned from Newhaven that the Guises would not move, and Seymour and Winter had joined the fleet from the Flemish shores, bringing it to 140 ships—slightly larger than the Spanish fleet, but with less than half the tonnage and one-third the men—the Spaniards were battered but unbroken. As they slipped into Calais Roads Saturday evening, all prudent men knew England’s hour had come. The bells that would summon Christendom to church the next morning would either toll the death-knell or ring the triumph of the Reformed faith.

That Sabbath must have been solemn throughout land and town. Many a coward, once scoffing at the Armada’s threat because he feared to believe it, now trembled with dread. And many a brave man, kneeling beside wife and daughters, felt his heart sink at the thought of what they might endure—by fanatical soldiery or the cold cruelty of the Inquisition. The massacre of St. Bartholomew, the fires of Smithfield, the slaughter of Moors, the extermination of West Indians, the horrors of Piedmont—these spectres, not dimmed by time but bleeding fresh in memory, haunted every English soul.

Each knew the fate awaiting him. One misstep, and the doom that later befell the Lutherans of Magdeburg would descend upon every town from London to Carlisle. All understood the peril as they prayed—day after day, throughout England and the Netherlands. None understood it better than she who guided the nation, the target of the invaders’ wrath, and who, inspired by divine vision (as men then wisely believed), devised the bold stroke to strike first.

But where was Amyas Leigh during all this? Day after day he hunted the *Sta. Catharina* through the thickest of the press, unable to find her, unable even to hear of her. One moment he feared she had sunk in the night, denying him his prey. The next, that she had repaired and would escape him after all. He grew moody, restless, irritable—peevish for the first time in his life. He spoke only of Don Guzman. At every spare moment, he drew his sword, handled it, fondled it, even spoke to it, bidding it not fail him in vengeance. At last, he sent for Squire, the armorer, fetched a whetstone, and shamefacedly sharpened and polished the blade in hidden corners, muttering to himself.

A single obsession had seized his mind—vengeance. He forgot England’s need, her triumph, his own safety—everything but his brother’s blood. And yet this was the day he had long awaited since he brought home that magic horn as a fifteen-year-old boy—the day he would stand face to face with an invader, and that invader Antichrist himself. For years, he had believed with Drake, Hawkins, Grenville, and Raleigh that he was born only to fight the Spaniard. And now he fought him—on a cause greater than any he’d ever known, for a stake beyond imagining, within battle lines he would never see again. And yet he was not content.

While crews across the fleet received communion together, rising with joyful faces to shake hands and rejoice in Britain’s Salamis, Amyas turned away from the holy elements.

“I cannot communicate, Sir John. Charity with all men? I hate—more than any man ever hated.”

“You hate only the Lord’s foes, Captain Leigh.”

“No, Jack. I hate my own as well.”

“But no one in the fleet?”

“Don’t try to trick me with the same Jesuit lie Parson Fletcher used for one of Doughty’s men—to poison his conscience while plotting against his admiral. No, Jack. I hate one you know. And that hatred keeps me from loving any human being. I am in love with no man—no man, not even you! Go your ways in God’s name, sir, and leave me and the devil alone. Or you’ll find my words true.”

Jack departed with a sigh. While the crew received communion on deck, Amyas sat below in the cabin, sharpening his sword. Then he summoned a boat and went aboard Drake’s ship to inquire about the *Sta. Catharina*, scowling as he listened to the loud chants and tinkling bells drifting across the water from the Spanish fleet.

At last, Drake was summoned by the Lord Admiral and returned with a secret commission—meant to bear fruit that very night. Amyas, who had gone with him, helped till dusk. Then he returned to his own ship as Sir Amyas Leigh, Knight, to the joy of every soul aboard—except himself.

So there, on that long summer Sabbath, before the little walled town and the yellow sandhills, lay the two mighty fleets—scowling at each other, scarcely out of gunshot. Messenger after messenger sped toward Bruges to the Duke of Parma, begging for faster vessels and demanding he sail immediately with all his forces.

But the duke was not at Dunkirk—he was at the future battlefield of Waterloo, praying before St. Mary of Halle in Hainault, ensuring his place in the Pantheon. Already, he dreamed of the gentle-faced Cardinal Allen placing the crown of England upon his head.

He replied: first, his supplies were not ready; second, his Dutch sailors—held at bay for weeks by the sword—had fled like water; third, and worst, he could not come—because thirty-five Dutch ships, full of stubborn, round-sterned heretics, watched the entrance to Nieuwport and Dunkirk like terriers guarding a rat hole.

Having secured the private favor of St. Mary of Halle, he would return tomorrow to test her power. But only hear the thunder of the fleets across the flats of Dixmude, and the curses echoing from Dunkirk. While he prayed, the English had prayed—and done more. All that remained for the Prince of Parma was to hang a few purveyors as offerings to his angry army—and then, as Drake said, “chafe like a bear robbed of her whelps.”
