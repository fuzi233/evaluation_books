On the morning when Gwendolen Harleth was married and became Mrs. Grandcourt, the air had that clean, biting sweetness which comes when the sun is bright but the earth is still frosted—leaves crisping underfoot like thin wafers, and every sound in the village sharpened as if the cold had tuned it: boots on the path, a cough held back in a sleeve, the low murmur of bodies gathered close.

Half Pennicote made a lane to the church. They stood as they might on a harvest day when the last cart comes in—eager to see what the year has yielded, eager to measure fortune as if it were a sheaf you could count with your eyes. An old friend of the rector’s read the service; the rector himself, for the great advantage of the procession, stood in the place of father. Faces leaned forward. Whispered judgments ran like small currents under the respectable stillness.

It was observed that only two faces looked clouded—Mrs. Davilow’s and Anna’s. Mrs. Davilow’s delicate eyelids were pink, as if she had spent half the night in tears; and no one wondered. A mother may watch the finest prize carried from her hands and still feel only the ache of empty arms. But why Anna should be troubled—when she was being so well set off by her bridesmaid’s dress—was less understood.

The others reflected the brilliancy of the occasion as a polished tray throws back candlelight; and the bride most of all. It was agreed that for figure and carriage she was worthy of being “a lady o’ title.” As for her face, some thought a title asked for something rosier; but since the bridegroom himself was not fresh-coloured—indeed, as the miller’s wife observed, he had much the complexion of her own husband—the match looked the more complete. And if he was taking her, surely he must be fond; and one could only hope he would never cast it up to her that she had gone out to service as a governess, nor to her mother that she had lived at Sawyer’s Cottage—those vicissitudes the village had chewed over as steadily as it chewed its Sunday dinners.

The miller’s daughter of fourteen could not believe high gentry behaved badly to their wives, but her mother had her lore, handed down like an old recipe whose bitterness is in the memory as much as on the tongue.

“Oh, child, men’s men: gentle or simple, they’re much of a muchness. I’ve heard my mother say Squire Pelton used to take his dogs and a long whip into his wife’s room, and flog ’em there to frighten her; and my mother was lady’s-maid there at the very time.”

“That’s unlucky talk for a wedding, Mrs. Girdle,” said the tailor. “A quarrel may end wi’ the whip, but it begins wi’ the tongue, and it’s the women have got the most o’ that.”

“The Lord gave it ’em to use, I suppose,” said Mrs. Girdle. “He never meant you to have it all your own way.”

“By what I can make out from the gentleman as attends to the grooming at Offendene,” said the tailor, “this Mr. Grandcourt has wonderful little tongue. Everything must be done dummy-like without his ordering.”

“Then he’s the more whip, I doubt,” said Mrs. Girdle. “She’s got tongue enough, I warrant her. See, there they come out together!”

“What wonderful long corners she’s got to her eyes!” said the tailor. “She makes you feel comical when she looks at you.”

Gwendolen, in truth, had never shown more elasticity in her bearing, more lustre in her long brown glance. She shone with that fierce, brilliant life which sometimes comes of pain—yet it was not pain she felt. She had worked herself into much the same state as that in which she had stood at the gambling-table while Deronda watched her and she began to lose: a hot, perilous enjoyment, an inward daring that tastes its own sharpness as if it were wine.

Whatever uneasiness a growing conscience had brewed in her was flung aside like a sudden qualm amidst a feast. The ambitious vanity within her—the hunger for luxury, for the visible proofs of being “somebody”—was too ravenous to be killed except by long poisoning. This morning she could not have said truly that she repented her acceptance of Grandcourt, or that any fears, still misty on the far horizon, could dim the immediate scene where all eyes confirmed her importance.

That she was doing something wrong—that punishment might be hanging over her—that the woman to whom she had given a promise and broken it was thinking of her with bitterness and misery, with a just reproach—that Deronda, with his way of looking into things, very likely despised her for marrying Grandcourt, as he had despised her for gambling—above all, that the cord she had once held in her own hand was now being flung over her neck—these things had been fermenting in her through the weeks of engagement: a yeasty mixture of half-understood facts, deep impressions, images at once real and fantastic. But were they nullified this morning? No: they were thrust down and trodden under with an exulting defiance. She felt herself at the great game of life with many eyes upon her, daring everything to win much—or, if she must lose, to lose with éclat, with a sense of having mattered.

Yet a losing destiny did not press on her as fear. She believed she was entering a fuller power of managing circumstances—with all the official strength of marriage, which some women made so poor a use of. The youthful egoism that trouble, humiliation, and culpability had shaken had returned, newly fed, with the old fumes stronger for the draught. She did not present any ideal of the tremulous, tearful bride. With erect head and elastic footstep she walked among illusions; and beneath it all there was a dim under-consciousness that she was a little intoxicated.

“Thank God you bear it so well, my darling!” said Mrs. Davilow when she had helped Gwendolen doff the bridal white and put on her travelling dress. All the trembling had belonged to the mother; and her agitation urged Gwendolen doubly to take the morning as a triumph.

“Why, you might have said that if I had been going to Mrs. Mompert’s, you dear, sad, incorrigible mamma!” said Gwendolen, putting her hands to her mother’s cheeks with laughing tenderness—then retreating and spreading out her arms as if to show herself: “Here am I—Mrs. Grandcourt! what else would you have me, but what I am sure to be? You know you were ready to die with vexation when you thought that I would not be Mrs. Grandcourt.”

“Hush, hush, my child, for heaven’s sake!” Mrs. Davilow almost whispered. “How can I help feeling it when I am parting from you. But I can bear anything willingly if you are happy.”

“Not gladly, mamma, no!” said Gwendolen, shaking her head, bright smile playing as if it could charm away the ache. “Willingly you would bear it, but always sorrowfully. Sorrowing is your sauce; you can take nothing without it.”

Then, clasping her mother’s shoulders and raining kisses, first on one cheek then on the other between her words, she said gaily, “And you shall sorrow over my having everything at my beck—and enjoying everything glorious—splendid houses—and horses—and diamonds, I shall have diamonds—and going to court—and being Lady Certainly—and Lady Perhaps—and grand here—and tantivy there—and always loving you better than anybody else in the world.”

“My sweet child!—But I shall not be jealous if you love your husband better; and he will expect to be first.”

Gwendolen thrust out her lips and chin with a pretty grimace. “Rather a ridiculous expectation. However, I don’t mean to treat him ill—unless he deserves it.”

Then the two clung to each other, and Gwendolen could not hinder the small rising sob when she said, “I wish you were going with me, mamma.” But the slight dew on her long eyelashes only made her the more charming when, a moment later, she gave her hand to Grandcourt to be led to the carriage.

The rector looked in on her for a final blessing—“Good-bye; God bless you; we shall see you again before long”—and then returned to Mrs. Davilow, half cheerful, half solemn. “Let us be thankful, Fanny. She is in a position well suited to her, and beyond what I should have dared to hope for. And few women can have been chosen more entirely for their own sake. You should feel yourself a happy mother.”

There was a railway journey of some fifty miles before husband and wife reached the station near Ryelands. The sky, which had begun so clear, had veiled itself; and by the time their carriage rolled through the park-gates it was hardly more than twilight. Yet even in that dimness Gwendolen, looking from the window, could see enough to feel her heart beat to a new measure: the long winding drive bordered with evergreens backed by huge gray stems; then the widening of grassy spaces and undulations, studded with dark clumps; till at last a wide level where the white house showed itself, with a hanging wood behind, and in front the terrace balustrade rising and sinking like the calm line of a wave.

During the journey she had been at her liveliest—chatting incessantly, as if determined to ignore any change in their mutual position since yesterday. Grandcourt had been rather ecstatically quiescent; and when he took her hand she made his gentle seizure into a grasp of his hand with both hers, with a kittenish vivacity that refused quiet petting. She was growing febrile in her excitement; and now, on this drive through the park, her usual susceptibility to changes of light and scenery made her heart palpitate with the almost incredible fulfilment of girlish dreams.

Was it only novelty—this nearing of her own corridors, her own ceilings out of sight for loftiness, where her own painted Spring shed painted flowers and her own foreshortened Zephyrs blew painted trumpets; where her own servants—lackeys in clothing but men in bulk and shape—were as nothing in her presence, reverencing even the propriety of her insolence; where she could be the heroine of an admired play without the pains of art? Or was there some dim forecast, an insistent penetration from what she had suppressed—mixing triumph with the dread of a crisis? In her nature exultation always carried an infusion of dread, ready to curdle. In spite of herself she fell silent as they drew nearer; and when her husband said, “Here we are at home!” and for the first time kissed her on the lips, she scarcely knew it had happened. It was like a greeting passively accepted while the eyes are held by a spectacle.

After the half-willful excitement of the day, a numbness had come over her. Yet the hall struck her at once with its brilliant warmth: carpets and matting muffling footsteps, full-length portraits looking down with composed indifference, statues with an Olympian coldness, servants moving with assiduous noiselessness, as if trained to keep the house’s own hush. There were not many extra hands—only a few from Diplow in addition to those in constant charge; and Gwendolen’s new maid, who had come with her, was taken under the housekeeper’s guidance.

Gwendolen felt herself led by Grandcourt along a corridor subtly scented—polish, old wood, something like dried lavender kept in drawers—into an anteroom where an open doorway sent out a rich glow of light and colour.

“These are our dens,” said Grandcourt. “You will like to be quiet here till dinner. We shall dine early.” He pressed her hand to his lips and moved away—more in love than he had ever expected to be.

She let her hat and mantle be taken, and then threw herself into a chair near the glowing hearth. Glass panels repeated her figure again and again amid faint-green satin surroundings, as if the house itself were rehearsing her new title, multiplying her into a chorus of mistresses.

The housekeeper had passed in from the adjoining dressing-room and seemed disposed to linger. Gwendolen, impatient for solitude, said, “Will you tell Hudson, when she has put out my dress, to leave everything? I shall not want her again unless I ring.”

The housekeeper, coming forward, said, “Here is a packet, madam, which I was ordered to give into nobody’s hands but yours, when you were alone. The person who brought it said it was a present particularly ordered by Mr. Grandcourt; but he was not to know of its arrival till he saw you wear it. Excuse me, madam; I felt it right to obey orders.”

Gwendolen took the packet and let it lie on her lap until she heard the doors close. In her confused feeling and creeping luxurious languor she welcomed the diversion. It came into her mind at once: the diamonds Grandcourt had spoken of, deposited somewhere and to be given her on her marriage. Her own diamonds to try on—cold fire to set against skin, hard brightness to answer her own sense of having prevailed.

Within the sealed paper coverings was a box; within the box a jewel-case. Her fingers, suddenly eager, flipped it open.

The gleam met her eyes—and at the same instant she saw the letter lying above the jewels.

She knew the handwriting on the address.

It was as if an adder had lain coiled among the glitter. Her heart leaped so violently it seemed to spend her strength in one stroke; and when she took up the thin paper it shook between her fingers. Yet the words were legible as print; they thrust themselves on her sight, and would not let her look away.

These diamonds, which were once given with ardent love to Lydia Glasher, she passes on to you. You have broken your word to her, that you might possess what was hers. Perhaps you think of being happy, as she once was, and of having beautiful children such as hers, who will thrust hers aside. God is too just for that. The man you have married has a withered heart. His best young love was mine: you could not take that from me when you took the rest. It is dead: but I am the grave in which your chance of happiness is buried as well as mine. You had your warning. You have chosen to injure me and my children. He had meant to marry me. He would have married me at last, if you had not broken your word. You will have your punishment. I desire it with all my soul. Will you give him this letter to set him against me and ruin us more—me and my children? Shall you like to stand before your husband with these diamonds on you, and these words of mine in his thoughts and yours? Will he think you have any right to complain when he has made you miserable? You took him with your eyes open. The willing wrong you have done me will be your curse.

At first Gwendolen’s eyes seemed spell-bound, reading the horrible lines again and again as if repetition could make them less real, as if she were sentenced to a penance that must be fulfilled by staring. Then a new spasm of terror seized her—terror not only of the curse but of the words existing where other eyes might meet them. She leaned forward and stretched out the paper toward the fire, as if she could undo time with flame.

It flew from her trembling fingers like a feather and was caught by a great draught of heat. The letter flared—thin paper turning to light and black ash with a swift, hungry sound, as if the hearth had been waiting for it.

In the movement the casket fell, and the diamonds rolled out across the floor, scattering their cold points of fire like spilled hailstones on polished wood. She did not notice. She fell back in her chair helpless.

The reflections in the glass panels, which had so lately mocked and multiplied her, became only pale shapes—women petrified white. But close to her you would have seen the tremor in her lips, the shake that ran through her hands as if her whole body had become an instrument struck too sharply. She sat a long while, knowing little more than that she felt ill, and that those written words—burned and gone—went on repeating themselves inside her, untranslatable into anything softer, inexhaustible as an echo in a vaulted place.

Truly here were poisoned gems, and the poison had entered into her.

After that long while there came a tap at the door, and Grandcourt entered dressed for dinner.

The sight of him brought a new nervous shock. Gwendolen screamed—again and again—with hysterical violence.

He had expected to find her dressed and smiling, ready to be led down. Instead he saw her pallid, shrieking as if in terror, and the jewels scattered about her on the floor. Was it a fit of madness? In some form or other, the Furies had crossed his threshold.
