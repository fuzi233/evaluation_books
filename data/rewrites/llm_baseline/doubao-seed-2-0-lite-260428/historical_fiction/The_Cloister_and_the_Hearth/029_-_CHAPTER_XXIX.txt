The sudden, unwelcome news hit Gerard like a physical blow, and the colour drained then flooded back to his cheeks in rapid, unsteady waves. His jaw clamped tight with rising ire a moment later. For any man of spirit is much like the wild boar that roams the Rhineland woodlands: he will retreat before an overwhelming superior force, not out of cowardice, but simple good sense, not being the foolhardy ass that stands its ground to be butchered for no gain. But hound him too long, press too close at his heels, wear him past the point of patience, and he will whirl with a snarl, charge straight through a whole pack of hunters, and very well may turn his own fury on you before any of them can strike.

Gerard squared his shoulders, eyes blazing, face set for battle. But the fire drained right out of him a heartbeat later, and his whole frame sagged. “And my axe,” he said, voice soft and plaintive, “lies at the bottom of the Rhine.”

The two men huddled to weigh their options. Prudence whispered sharp and urgent that they must avoid the nearby village entirely, where their enemies were no doubt lying in wait. But hunger gnawed at their bellies so fiercely it roared louder than all caution, demanding they find food. In the end the two impulses struck a careful truce, and they settled to cut across the open farm fields well away from the village paths rather than risk the main high road.

They halted behind a thick, well-stacked haystack, pulled two armfuls of dry straw to pad an overgrown dry ditch hidden from prying eyes, and hunkered down there, their thin travelling clothes prickled by the nettles that grew thick along its banks. They took turns sneaking out one at a time, and returned with pockets full of raw turnips dug from a nearby patch, munching on the coarse, earthy roots in slow, careful intervals in their hidden nook until the sun dipped fully below the horizon.

Presently they crept out shivering, into the lashing cold rain and total darkness, and slipped onto the high road on the far side of the village. It was a dismal, wretched night: dark as pitch, the gale blew so hard it drowned out every other sound. They could not see three paces before their faces, could not hear a thing over the howling wind, and so were as invisible and unheard as phantoms. For aught they knew, they drifted close enough to brush shoulders with their pursuing foes that very night, undetected. The raw horrors of the black tempest soon drove all thought of their hunters clean out of their heads: they stumbled forward groping, fighting the gale every step, as if they were hewing their way through solid slabs of black marble.

When the thin moon finally broke through the roiling storm clouds, they had put many long leagues between themselves and Düsseldorf. Still they trudged on, boots caked in mud, their bodies aching with cold and exhaustion. Presently a massive stone building loomed out of the dark before them.

“Courage!” cried Denys, his hoarse voice bright with sudden relief. “I know this convent. Aye, it is. We are in the see of Juliers now. Cologne has no power here.”

The next moment the heavy oak gates swung open, and they were safe within its sheltering walls.
