Her Habits—A Saunter

I told you I was charmed by her in most particulars, yet there were shadows that clung to her brilliance, details that did not please me so well.

She stood above the average height for a woman, slender and possessed of a grace so unnatural it bordered on the eerie. Save for a languid slowness in her movements—a lethargy that belied her otherwise robust appearance—she gave no indication of being an invalid. Her complexion was a rich, brilliant ivory; her features were small, perfectly formed, and framed by eyes large, dark, and lustrous, holding depths that seemed to swallow light. And her hair… oh, the hair! It was magnificent, thick, and long, cascading down her shoulders in a heavy curtain of deep, rich brown interlaced with threads of gold. I have pressed my hands beneath it, laughing in wonder at its sheer weight, folding and braiding strands as she lay back in her chair, speaking in a sweet, low hum. If I had only known then, I would not have played with it so lightly.

I had spoken of particulars that disquieted me. While her confidence won me on the first night, I soon discovered a wakeful reserve regarding everything connected to her life, her history, and her origins. An invisible wall separated us, a barrier of silence where she refused to tread. I dared say I was unreasonable, perhaps ill-mannered, yet curiosity is a restless, unscrupulous master, and no girl can endure having her secrets baffled by another. What harm could it do to tell me what I so ardently desired? Did she lack trust in my honor? Why would she not believe me when I swore, solemnly, that not one syllable would leave my lips?

There was a coldness in her smile, a melancholy persistence in her refusals that chilled me beyond her years. We never quarreled, for she would not quarrel on any point. To press her further felt unfair, crude, yet I could not stop myself. The truth she eventually offered amounted, in my view, to nothing. It boiled down to three vague disclosures: First, her name was Carmilla. Second, her family was ancient and noble. Third, her home lay somewhere to the west. She withheld the name of her clan, their heraldry, the estate, or even the country itself.

Do not imagine I harried her incessantly. I waited for opportunities, insinuating rather than urging. Twice I attacked her directly, but my tactics failed utterly. Yet, her evasions were conducted with such pretty melancholy, with such passionate declarations of affection and trust, promising that I would eventually know all, that I could not bring myself to remain truly offended.

She would wrap her pretty arms around my neck, pulling me close until our cheeks touched, and murmur into my ear, "Dearest, your little heart is wounded; think me not cruel because I obey the irresistible law of my strength and weakness. If your heart bleeds, my wild heart bleeds with yours. In the rapture of my humiliation, I live in your warm life, and you shall die—sweetly die—into mine. As I draw near, you will draw near to others, learning the cruelty of love, so for now, know no more of me and mine, but trust me with your spirit."

After such ravishing words, she would press me closer, her lips glowing softly as she kissed my cheek. Her agitation and language were unintelligible to me. From these foolish, infrequent embraces, I wished to extricate myself, but my energies failed. Her whispers sounded like a lullaby, soothing my resistance into a trance from which I only woke when she released me.

In these mysterious moods, I did not like her. A strange tumult swelled within me, pleasurable yet mingled with a vague, creeping fear and disgust. I had no clear thoughts during these scenes, yet I was conscious of a love blooming into adoration, tangled inextricably with abhorrence. A paradox, yes, but the feeling remained.

Now, writing this more than ten years later with a trembling hand, I recall the ordeal through which I passed unconsciously. Though the main current of my story remains vivid, those specific emotional scenes, where my passions were wildly roused, linger in a haze, dimly remembered. Sometimes, after an hour of apathy, she would take my hand, pressing it with a fond, renewed force. Blushing softly, she would gaze at me with languid, burning eyes, breathing so rapidly that her dress rose and fell in tumultuous waves. It was the ardor of a lover, embarrassing, hateful, yet overpowering. With gloating eyes, she would drag me to her, her hot lips tracing kisses along my cheek, whispering almost in sobs, "You are mine. You *shall* be mine. You and I are one forever." Then she would throw herself back in her chair, covering her face with her small hands, leaving me trembling.

"Are we related?" I would ask, desperate for logic. "What do you mean? You remind me of someone I love, yet I hate it. I don't know you, and I don't know myself when you look like that."

She would sigh at my vehemence, turn away, and drop my hand.

Regarding these extraordinary manifestations, I strove in vain to form a theory. They were not affectation or trickery; they were the breaking out of suppressed instinct. Was she, despite her mother's denial, subject to brief visitations of madness? Or was there a disguise, a romance? I had read of boyish lovers finding their way into houses to prosecute suits in masquerade, aided by clever adventuresses. Yet, many things argued against this. I boasted of no little attentions such as masculine gallantry delights to offer. Between these passionate moments lay long intervals of commonplace gaiety or brooding melancholy, during which I might have been as nothing to her. Unless in those brief periods of mystery, her ways were girlish, marked always by a languor incompatible with a healthy masculine frame.

Her habits were odd, perhaps not so singular to a town lady as they appeared to us rural folk. She would descend late, usually past one o'clock, drink a cup of chocolate, and eat nothing. Then we would go for a walk, a mere saunter, after which she seemed immediately exhausted, returning to the castle or sitting on a bench among the trees. This was a bodily languor in which her mind did not sympathize; she remained an animated, intelligent talker.

Occasionally, she would allude to her home or mention adventures indicating a people of strange manners and unknown customs. I gathered from these chance hints that her native country was far more remote than I had initially fancied.

One afternoon, as we sat beneath the trees, a funeral procession passed us. It was for a pretty young girl, the daughter of one of the forest rangers, whose loss left the old man heartbroken, walking behind the coffin. Peasants followed two-by-two, singing a funeral hymn.

I rose to show respect, joining the sweet song. Suddenly, my companion shook me roughly.

"Don't you perceive how discordant that is?" she said brusquely.

"I think it very sweet, on the contrary," I answered, vexed and uneasy lest the passersby notice.

Instantly interrupted again, "You pierce my ears!" Carmilla cried, almost angrily, plugging her tiny fingers into her ears. "Besides, how can you tell that your religion and mine are the same? Your forms wound me, and I hate funerals. What a fuss! Everyone must die, and all are happier when they do. Come home."

"My father went with the clergyman to the churchyard. I thought you knew she was to be buried today."

"She? I don't trouble my head about peasants. I don't know who she is," Carmilla replied, flashing her fine eyes.

"She is the poor girl who thought she saw a ghost a fortnight ago and has been dying ever since," I explained. "Yesterday she expired."

"Tell me nothing about ghosts. I shall not sleep tonight if you do."

"I hope there is no plague," I continued, noting the swineherd's wife who had died similarly, thinking she was strangled by something unseen. "Papa says such horrors accompany fevers."

"Well, *her* funeral is over, I hope, and *her* hymn sung. Our ears shall not be tortured. It makes me nervous. Sit down here, beside me. Hold my hand. Press it hard—harder."

We moved to another seat. As she sat, her face underwent a change that terrified me. It darkened, becoming horribly livid. Her teeth and hands clenched, her lips compressed, and she stared down at the ground, trembling with a continued shudder like ague. All her energies seemed strained to suppress a fit, breathlessly tugging at her nerves. At length, a low, convulsive cry of suffering broke from her, and gradually the hysteria subsided. "There! That comes of strangling people with hymns!" she gasped at last. "Hold me, hold me still. It is passing away."

Slowly, the terror dissipated. To dispel the somber impression, she became unusually animated and chatty, and so we returned home.

This was the first time I had seen any definable symptom of that delicate health her mother spoke of, or any trace of temper. Both passed like a summer cloud, and never again did I witness a sign of anger from her save once
