The sun hung high and unrelenting in the sky when my companion roused me from a deep, uneasy sleep, announcing that the porridge was ready and there was just time to bathe. The rich, sizzling scent of bacon drifted through the tent flap, sharp and comforting in the morning air.

“River’s still rising,” he said, voice low but steady. “Several islands out in midstream have vanished entirely. Ours is shrinking fast.”

“Any wood left?” I asked, rubbing my eyes.

“The wood and the island will finish at the same time—tomorrow,” he replied with a dry laugh. “But we’ve enough for now.”

I plunged into the water from the island’s tip, which had already changed shape during the night, and was swept downstream in an instant to the landing spot opposite the tent. The current was fierce, the water icy, and the banks raced past like scenery seen from a speeding train. Bathing under such conditions was both terrifying and exhilarating—a cleansing ritual, as if the terror of the previous night were evaporating from my mind. The sun beat down mercilessly; not a cloud marred the blue dome above. Yet the wind remained relentless, unyielding, as if nature itself refused to let us forget its power.

Then, suddenly, the full weight of his words struck me: *“Enough to last till tomorrow.”* He no longer intended to leave immediately. He’d changed his mind. The certainty of departure the night before had vanished. Why? What had shifted?

At breakfast, great chunks of the riverbank collapsed into the flood with thunderous splashes, sending sprays of water toward our frying pan. My companion talked endlessly about how the Vienna-Pesth steamers must struggle to find their way through such a swollen channel. But I found myself more preoccupied by the change in him. Something had altered since the evening before. His manner was different—tense, hesitant, almost fearful. A new wariness threaded through his movements, a subtle shift in tone, as though he were listening to something beyond sound, watching something unseen. I couldn’t name it then, but I felt it plainly: he was afraid.

He ate little, didn’t smoke his pipe—unheard of. The map lay open beside him, and he studied it repeatedly, tracing lines with a finger that trembled slightly.

“We’d better get off sharp in an hour,” I said, trying to draw him out gently, hoping to coax some truth from him.

His reply unsettled me: “Rather. If they’ll let us.”

“Who’ll let us? The elements?” I asked, forcing lightness into my voice.

“The powers of this place,” he answered, eyes fixed on the map. “The gods are here, if they’re anywhere at all.”

“The elements are the only true immortals,” I laughed, though my laughter sounded brittle even to myself. When he looked up, grave and unblinking, he said across the smoke:

“We’ll be lucky if we leave without further disaster.”

That was precisely what I feared. I braced myself for the direct question—it felt like stepping into a dentist’s chair, knowing the pain was inevitable. I had to ask.

“Further disaster? What’s happened?”

“The steering paddle’s gone,” he said quietly.

“The steering paddle?” I repeated, heart leaping. That was our rudder. Without it, navigating the Danube in flood was suicide. “But—”

“And there’s a tear in the bottom of the canoe,” he added, voice trembling faintly.

I stared, unable to speak. In the heat, on the scorched sand, a chill crept over me. I rose and followed him, who walked ahead with solemn silence, leading toward the tent on the other side of the fire. The canoe lay where I’d last seen it—upside down, ribs exposed. One paddle rested beside it. Only one.

“There’s only one,” he said, bending to pick it up. “And here’s the rent in the base-board.”

I opened my mouth to say I’d clearly seen two paddles earlier—but stopped. It would have been useless. I approached instead, drawn by dread.

A long, clean tear ran through the canoe’s hull, a narrow strip of wood neatly removed as if bitten away by a sharp tooth. The hole pierced through. Had we launched her without noticing it, we would have sunk quickly—two inches above water, helpless against the current. At first, the wet wood might swell and seal it, but once out in the stream, water would pour in relentlessly.

“There,” he murmured, half to himself, “an attempt to prepare a victim for sacrifice. Two victims, perhaps.” He leaned over, running his fingers along the slit.

I began to whistle—an unconscious habit when flustered—and deliberately ignored his words. I refused to believe them.

“It wasn’t there last night,” he said, straightening up, avoiding my gaze.

“We must’ve scraped it when we landed,” I said, stopping the whistling. “The stones are sharp.”

I froze. He turned and met my eyes. We both knew the lie. There were no stones.

“And then there’s this,” he said, handing me the paddle and pointing to the blade.

A cold dread spread through me as I took it. The blade had been meticulously scraped thin—so thin it would snap with the first stroke. Like someone had spent hours sanding it down, weakening it beyond use.

“One of us walked in our sleep,” I said weakly, “or maybe the wind blew sand against it for days.”

“Ah,” he said, turning away with a hollow laugh. “You can explain everything.”

“The same wind that carried the paddle near the bank and dropped it into the water,” I called after him, desperate to maintain logic.

“I see,” he shouted back, glancing at me before vanishing into the willows.

Alone again with these signs—evidence of deliberate action—I thought: *One of us did this. Not me.* Then came the second thought: neither of us could have done it. My companion—my trusted friend, seasoned traveler—could not have acted so knowingly. And the idea that he’d gone mad, that he was acting out some insane purpose, seemed absurd. Yet the truth remained: something had changed in him. He was nervous, watchful, afraid of things he wouldn’t name. He was waiting—for what? For a climax he expected soon.

I searched the tent and surroundings. The measurements of the night were unchanged. But now I noticed deep hollows in the sand—basin-shaped craters, some the size of tea cups, others like bowls. The wind, no doubt, had carved them. It had also lifted the paddle and tossed it toward the water. The tear in the canoe remained inexplicable—but maybe we’d snagged it on landing. Still, the shore showed no rocks. I clung to that theory, clinging to reason like a drowning man to driftwood. An explanation was necessary—not because it was true, but because without one, sanity unraveled.

I melted pitch and began patching the canoe. Soon, the Swede joined me, working with quiet focus. I pointed to the hollows.

“Yes,” he said. “They’re everywhere. But you can explain them, no doubt?”

“Wind,” I said firmly. “Like those little whirlwinds in cities—sand shifts easily.”

He said nothing. We worked in silence. I watched him covertly. He seemed to be listening—turning his head, staring into the bushes, scanning the sky, the river. Once, he held his hand to his ear for minutes. He said nothing. I asked nothing. And as he mended the canoe with the precision of a native craftsman, I was glad he didn’t mention the willows. Because if he had seen *that*, I could no longer deny it. The trees weren’t just trees anymore. They were something else.

After a long pause, he spoke.

“Queer thing,” he said abruptly, as if compelled to confess. “About that otter last night.”

I looked up sharply. I’d expected something else entirely.

“Shows how lonely it is,” I said. “Otters are shy.”

“I don’t mean that,” he cut in. “Do you think it really was an otter?”

“What else? In God’s name, what else?”

“You know,” he said slowly, “I saw it before you. At first, it seemed… much bigger than an otter.”

“The sunset magnified it,” I replied.

He stared blankly, as if thinking of something far away.

“It had yellow eyes,” he whispered.

“The sun,” I laughed, too loudly. “Next you’ll wonder if the man in the boat—”

I stopped. He was listening again, head turned toward the wind. His face—fearful, expectant—made me halt.

Five minutes later, he looked across the canoe, pitch smoking in his hand, expression grim.

“I *did* wonder,” he said slowly, “what that thing in the boat was. I thought then it wasn’t a man. It rose suddenly out of the water.”

I laughed again—boisterously, defiantly—but this time with irritation beneath.

“Look here,” I snapped. “This place is strange enough without inventing things! That boat was ordinary. The man was ordinary. They were going downstream. And that otter *was* an otter. Stop playing the fool!”

He looked at me steadily. No anger. Just sorrow. I found courage in his silence.

“And for Heaven’s sake,” I continued, “don’t pretend you hear things. It only scares me. There’s nothing but the river and this cursed wind.”

“You *fool!*” he said, voice low, broken. “You utter fool. That’s exactly how all victims talk. As if you don’t understand what I do!” He sneered, yet with resignation. “The best thing you can do is stay silent. Hold your mind firm. This weak pretense only makes the truth harder when it finally hits.”

My defiance collapsed. I knew he was right. I was the fool. He had seen it all along. He’d sensed the danger from the beginning. I had been blind, while he stood alert, aware. And I missed the meaning of his words: *There must be a victim. And we are it.*

From that moment, I stopped pretending. And from that moment, my fear grew—steadily, inexorably—toward its final peak.

“But you’re right about one thing,” he added, before the subject passed. “We’re wiser not to talk about it—or even think about it. Because what you *think* becomes words. And what you *say*… happens.”

That afternoon, while the canoe dried, we fished, tested the leak, gathered wood, and watched the flood rise. Driftwood surged past, and we tried to grab it with long willow branches. The island shrank visibly, banks collapsing with thunderous gulps. The weather stayed clear until four o’clock, then clouds gathered in the southwest, creeping slowly across the sky.

The easing of the wind brought relief. The constant roar had grated on our nerves. But when the wind died at five, the silence was worse. The river filled the air with a deep, monotonous murmur—three dull notes, like a funeral dirge. Unlike the wind, which sang in many tones, the river’s song was flat, oppressive, mournful. It sounded like doom.

Without sunlight, the landscape lost its cheer. The shadows deepened. The willows, already menacing, stood closer together. They moved oddly—rustling when no wind stirred, shaking from roots upward. Common things, when charged with horror, become more terrifying than anything strange. These bushes, huddled around us, took on grotesque forms—living, watching things. Their ordinariness hid their malice. The forces of this place were drawing near. Focusing on us. On our island.

I had slept in the early afternoon and regained some strength—but that only made me more sensitive to the haunting. I laughed at my fears, told myself they were physical, irrational. But they grew stronger. I dreaded the night like a child in a forest.

The canoe was covered with a waterproof sheet. The single paddle was tied securely to a tree. From five onward, I prepared dinner—my turn. We had potatoes, onions, bacon fat, leftover stew residue, black bread. The result was excellent. Then plum stew with sugar, strong tea with dried milk. Wood was stacked nearby. No wind meant easy work.

My companion sat idly, cleaning his pipe, offering advice—an off-duty privilege. He’d been quiet all afternoon, busy with repairs and gathering wood while I slept. No talk of dark things. Only remarks about the island’s shrinking size.

The pot began to bubble when I heard him call from the bank.

“Come and listen,” he said. “See what you make of it.” He cupped his ear, as he had done before.

“Now do you hear it?” he asked, watching me.

We stood together, listening. At first, only the river’s deep hum and the hissing surface. The willows were still. Then—a sound. Faint, rhythmic. Like a distant gong, suspended high in the sky, struck again and again. Muffled, musical, metallic. It came from the swamps across the river, shifting direction—sometimes overhead, sometimes under the water. Once, I thought it came from within me, as if echoing from another dimension.

“I’ve heard it all day,” he said. “While you slept, it circled the island. I hunted it, but never found it. Sometimes it was inside—like a sound from the fourth dimension.”

I listened hard, searching for a familiar echo. Nothing. It changed distance, growing near, then fading into void. Not ominous—yet it stirred unease. A feeling I couldn’t name.

“The wind in the sand-funnels,” I said, forcing an explanation. “Or the bushes rubbing.”

“It comes from the whole swamp,” he replied. “From everywhere. It comes from the willows.”

“But the wind has dropped,” I objected. “How can the willows make noise?”

His answer terrified me—because I’d feared it, and because I knew it was true.

“It’s *because* the wind dropped we hear it now. It was drowned before. It’s the cry, I believe, of the—”

I broke away, rushing back to the fire—the stew threatened to burn. I needed escape. I didn’t want to talk. I feared he’d bring up the gods, the forces, the sacrifice. I needed control. One more night. Who knew what it would bring?

“Come cut bread for the pot!” I called, stirring vigorously. That stew-pot was our anchor. Our sanity.

He came slowly, took the sack from the tree, fumbled inside, then emptied its contents onto the ground-sheet.

“Hurry!” I shouted. “It’s boiling!”

He burst into laughter—sharp, sudden, forced. Not joy. Hollow.

“There’s nothing here!” he roared. “No bread. They’ve taken it!”

I dropped the spoon and ran. Everything lay on the sheet—except the loaf.

The weight of fear crushed me. Then I laughed too. It was the only thing to do. The sound echoed between us—unnatural, released pressure. A safety valve. Then silence.

“How criminally stupid of me!” I cried, clinging to logic. “I forgot to buy bread in Pressburg. That woman distracted me. I must’ve left it on the counter—”

“The oatmeal’s less than this morning,” he interrupted.

Why bring that up? I thought angrily.

“There’s enough for tomorrow,” I said, stirring fiercely. “We’ll get more in Komorn or Gran. In twenty-four hours, we’ll be miles away.”

“I hope so—to God,” he muttered, putting things back in the sack. “Unless we’re claimed first… as victims for the sacrifice,” he added, laughing foolishly. He dragged the sack into the tent—“for safety,” I supposed. I heard him mumbling, indistinctly. I chose not to listen.
