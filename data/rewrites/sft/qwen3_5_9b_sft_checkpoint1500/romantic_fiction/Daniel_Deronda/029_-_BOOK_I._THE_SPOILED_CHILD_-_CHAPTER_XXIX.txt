He sat sideways on a sofa in the drawing-room at Offendene, propped up with one arm, his legs crossed, watching her with the intense, still scrutiny of a man deeply absorbed in his subject. Gwendolen, who had once despised needlework, now held a piece of white embroidery with apparent fervor, though a closer inspection would have revealed many false stitches. For the past week, their days had been filled with horseback riding, yet they always carved out time for this quieter, more intimate companionship, which Gwendolen surprisingly did not find tiresome. She was thoroughly satisfied with Grandcourt. His responses to her lively inquiries about his travels and adventures were delivered with a languid, rolling cadence that suited her perfectly. From the start, she had noted his uncanny ability to speak; he possessed nothing of the fool, and through some subtle alchemy, he managed to convince her that all foolishness belonged to everyone else, save himself. A man who commands the best possesses a sovereign power to diminish everything else. His courtship had barely exceeded the bounds of obtrusive affection, lingering like the scent of roses, leaving only a gratified vanity in its wake. On one occasion, however, he had kissed not her cheek but the skin beneath her ear. Startled, Gwendolen had jumped up, visibly agitated, causing him to rise and ask, "I beg your pardon—did I annoy you?" "Oh, it was nothing," Gwendolen had replied, trembling slightly, "only I cannot bear—to be kissed under my ear." She had sat down again with a feigned laugh, yet her heart hammered with a vague, primal fear. She was no longer free to mock him as she had mocked Rex. This agitation seemed flattering to him, and he had contentedly refrained from pushing further.

Today, a light rain prevented riding, but a parcel arrived from London, and Mrs. Davilow had just left, displaying the exquisite items Grandcourt had selected for the house. Gwendolen basked in the scenery of her life. Letting her hands fall to her lap, she adopted a prettier, perverse air.

"Why is to-morrow the only day?"

"Because the next day is the first with the hounds," Grandcourt replied calmly.

"And after that?"

"After that I must go away for a couple of days—it's a bore—but I shall go one day and come back the next." Grandcourt caught the flicker of anxiety crossing her face. Releasing his hand from beneath his knee, he covered it with his own and said softly, "You object to my going away?"

"It's no use objecting," Gwendonen said, her voice cool. She fought desperately against the urge to confess her suspicion regarding his destination, to speak freely without restraint.

"Yes it is," Grandcourt said, drawing her hand closer. "I will postpone the trip. And I will travel at night, so as to be away only one day." He believed he understood the source of what he internally termed this fleeting temper, and at this precise moment, she fascinated him profoundly.

"Then don't postpone going, but travel at night," Gwendolen insisted, feeling a surge of power over him, a small vent for her irritation within his peremptory control.

"Then you will go to Diplow to-morrow?"

"Oh, yes, if you wish it," Gwendolen agreed in a high, careless tone. Her preoccupation with deeper emotions had blinded her to the warmth of his hand holding hers.

"How you treat us poor devils of men!" Grandcourt murmured, lowering his voice. "We are always getting the worst of it."

"_Are_ you?" Gwendolen asked, looking at him with naive inquiry. She longed to believe this common banter as the solemn truth about her lover; if so, she, too, was justified. If she knew everything, Mrs. Glasher would seem far more blameworthy than Grandcourt. "_Are_ you always getting the worst?"

"Yes. Are you as kind to me as I am to you?" Grandcourt gazed into her eyes with his narrow, piercing stare.

Gwendolen felt struck dumb. Conscious of the vast amount she had received, her sense of command faltered and sank away. It was as if she had consented to mount a chariot where another held the reins, and it was not in her nature to leap out before the world. She had not consented in ignorance, and all she could utter now would be a confession that she had not been ignorant. Her right to explanation had vanished. All she could do was adjust herself so that the spikes of this unwilling penance, imposed by conscience, would not wound her spirit. With a mental shiver, she resolved to shift her perspective. After a brief pause, during which she had not averted her eyes, she broke into a sudden smile.

"If I were as kind to you as you are to me, that would spoil your generosity: it would no longer be as great as it could be—and it is that now."

"Then I am not to ask for one kiss," Grandcourt said, content to pay a high price for this new mode of courtship, which introduced marriage by the finest contrasts.

"Not one?" Gwendolen asked, growing saucy, nodding defiantly.

He lifted her little left hand to his lips, then released it respectfully. To call him not disgusting was faint praise; he was almost charming. At this moment, she felt unlikely to ever love another man better than this one. His reticence gave her an inexplicable, delightful consciousness.

"Apropos," she said, picking up her embroidery again, "is there anyone besides Captain and Mrs. Torrington at Diplow? Or do you leave them _tête-à-tête_? I suppose he converses in cigars, and she answers with her chignon."

"She has a sister with her," Grandcourt said, a shadow of a smile touching his lips, "and there are two men besides—one of them you know, I believe."

"Ah, then, I have a poor opinion of him," Gwendolen shook her head.

"You saw him at Leubronn—young Deronda—a young fellow with the Mallingers."

Gwendolen felt as if her heart were performing a sudden gambol, and her fingers, striving to maintain a firm grip on her work, grew cold.

"I never spoke to him," she said, fearing any discernible change in herself. "Is he not disagreeable?"

"No, not particularly," Grandcourt said in his most languid manner. "He thinks a little too much of himself. I thought he had been introduced to you."

"No. Someone told me his name the evening before I left. That was all. What is he?"

"A sort of ward of Sir Hugo Mallinger's. Nothing of any consequence."

"Oh, poor creature! How very unpleasant for him!" Gwendolen murmured, speaking without real sarcasm. "I wonder if it has stopped raining!" she added, rising to look out the window.

Fortunately, the rain ceased the following day, and Gwendolen rode to Diplow on Criterion, as she had done previously when returning with her mother. She always felt bolder in her riding dress, bolstered by the agreeable belief that she looked her best—a sustaining illusion in any encounter that seemed formidable. Her anger toward Deronda had transmuted into a superstitious dread, perhaps due to the coercion he had exerted over her thoughts. Lest his first interference in her life foreshadow some future influence, she feared him. Superstitions are often born of intense self-feeling, making the evening star shine with a threat or a beggar's blessing encouraging. Such beliefs carry consequences that often verify their hopes or forebodings.

Before luncheon, Gwendolen toured the rooms with Mrs. Torrington and Mrs. Davilow, convinced that if she saw Deronda, a mere bow would suffice. She intended to notice him as little as possible. Yet, ultimately, an inward compulsion stronger than her pride seized her. From the first moment they shared the room, she seemed to do nothing but observe him; everything else became an automatic performance of an habitual role.

When he took his place at lunch, Grandcourt remarked, "Deronda, Miss Harleth tells me you were not introduced to her at Leubronn?"

"Miss Harleth hardly remembers me, I imagine," Deronda replied, looking at her quite simply as they bowed. "She was intensely occupied when I saw her."

Did he suppose she had not suspected him of redeeming her necklace?

"On the contrary. I remember you very well," Gwendolen said, feeling nervous but governing herself, examining him anew. "You did not approve of my playing at roulette."

"How did you come to that conclusion?" Deronda asked gravely.
