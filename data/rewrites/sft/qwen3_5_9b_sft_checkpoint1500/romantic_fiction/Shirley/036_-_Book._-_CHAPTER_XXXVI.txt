Louis Moore's fears regarding Mr. Sympson's imminent departure from Fieldhead proved entirely justified. The reconciliation, however patched together, following the great quarrel over Sir Philip Nunnely, held firm enough to keep the entire household in residence for a short while longer. Shirley, possessing a nature that could never stoop to inhospitality—save perhaps in the singular case of Mr. Donne—earnestly besought them to remain. Her plea was so fervent it carried the weight of a secret desire, a wish she dared not fully articulate. They, unable to resist her entreaties, especially her uncle who feared leaving her wholly unguarded, agreed to stay. The prospect of seeing Robert Moore reassert his claims on her hand, whenever circumstances might allow, was a grim thought that Mr. Sympson hoped to postpone indefinitely.

For a time, the atmosphere within the house grew smoother. Miss Keeldar's health returned, her spirits lifting like the sun after a storm. Mr. Moore, finding means to soothe her lingering anxieties, became her confidant, and with that trust, her fears evaporated. She radiated a lightness, a carefree joy reminiscent of a child who places absolute faith in her guardians. Together with William Farren, who acted as his intermediary regarding the fate of Phoebe, he convinced the household that the dog was not mad, but merely the victim of brutal treatment. While the groom and gamekeeper disputed this, insisting the beast was sane, Louis remained unconvinced. Yet, he chose to report to Shirley only the comforting aspects, a decision that proved fortunate, for the bite ultimately caused her no harm.

As November yielded to December, the Sympsons prepared for their final departure, bound for home by Christmas. On a crisp winter evening, during the last week of their stay, Louis retreated to his small blank book, pouring out his thoughts in a private discourse.

*"She is lovelier than ever,"* he wrote, *"since that little cloud dispersed, all temporary pallor and waness have vanished. It was marvelous to witness how swiftly the magical energy of youth restored her elasticity and blooming."*

Recalling breakfast that morning, he described passing from her sunny presence into the chill drawing-room, his heart swelling with a genial warmth despite the frost outside. He noted his own youth, barely thirty, a time when life still beamed with sweet hues. He ventured to the schoolroom, finding Henry and Shirley together. The boy held her hand, a privilege Louis envied, noting with a mix of admiration and melancholy the indulgence she showed him—a compassion that, if unchecked, might lead Henry to offer his soul on her altar sooner than intended.

Louis observed a shift in her demeanor toward him. She rarely glanced at him, speaking little when he was present. In his darker moments, he attributed this to indifference; in his brighter intervals, he interpreted it as the coyness of love. Determined to bridge the gap, he summoned solitude, sending Henry away with a dismissive command. Seizing the opportunity, he engaged Shirley in a conversation that drifted from the mundane to the profound, testing the waters of her heart.

He spoke of his future, envisioning a life far from the confines of England, perhaps among the Blackfeet or Flatheads, seeking a partner who could join him in the wilderness. He described the ideal wife: not a lady of high station, but a simple, honest, uneducated girl—"a good plain cook"—whose natural qualities he would cultivate with patience. He painted a picture of taming a proud spirit, breaking it in gently, and rewarding it with love, creating a union built on mutual growth rather than social equality.

Shirley reacted with a mixture of shock and indignation. She scoffed at the notion of marrying a "savage" or treating a woman as a project to be improved. With a sharp wit that burned like a torch, she dismantled his arguments, pointing out the flaws in his proposed "material philosophy." She challenged his view of poverty and station, teasing him about his own physical appearance and comparing him to a mastiff, yet doing so with a playful, dangerous edge that revealed her deep-seated resistance to his controlling impulses.

Their debate escalated until the barriers between them collapsed. Louis, emboldened by her defiance, stopped hiding behind the mask of the tutor. He laid bare his feelings, declaring that for four years she had grown into his heart and bewitched him, regardless of their differences in rank or wealth. He confessed his love, raw and urgent, asking for her reciprocation.

Shirley, initially stunned, rallied. She admitted a "little bit" of love but demanded honesty, refusing to play games. The conversation shifted to a matter of principle: the equality of their souls. She questioned whether they were equals, asking if he would be kind and refrain from tyranny. He, in turn, asked if she would let him breathe freely and not bewilder him. The tension was palpable, the air thick with unspoken promises and dangers. Finally, in a moment of vulnerability, she touched his hand, calling him "poor fellow," and promised fidelity, asking him never to leave her.

However, the truce was short-lived. Later that day, as the Sympsons packed, a confrontation erupted in the breakfast parlour. Mr. Sympson, overhearing a tender moment between Shirley and Louis, burst in, accusing the tutor of impropriety. Shirley, however, stood firmly beside Louis, defending him with a fierce loyalty that silenced her uncle. She declared that he was her future husband, a statement that sent Mr. Sympson into a frenzy of rage and despair. He stormed out, vowing to stop the match, while Shirley collapsed onto a sofa, shaken but resolute.

That night, amidst the chaos, Louis seized the moment to assert his dominance over the situation. He confronted the bewildered Mr. Sympson, physically overpowering him and ordering him to depart immediately, threatening to summon the constables if necessary. With the uncle fleeing the house in disgrace, the path seemed clear for Louis and Shirley.

Yet, as the weeks wore on, the dynamic between them shifted again. A week later, writing from Stilbro', Louis expressed a deep unease. Despite the public declaration, Shirley seemed to retreat, shunning him when he visited. He felt unsafe, tantalized by her silence and the way she withdrew from his reach. He watched her glide past him in the moonlight, her eyes shining with a mixture of fear and longing, evoking images of a pantheress trapped in chains, dreaming of wild woods and freedom.

One evening, as he stood by the staircase window, watching her descend, he followed her into the drawing-room where Mrs. Pryor and Caroline Helstone awaited. Dressed in white, with her hair flowing and her step silent, she appeared ethereal, almost spirit-like. She spoke little to him, offering tea with a distant politeness. When she finally excused herself, Louis intercepted her in the moonlit hall.

"You can conceive I am far from happy in my present uncertain, unsettled state," he pleaded, trying to draw her back from the shadows of her reserve.

"Oh yes, you _are_ happy!" she cried quickly, though her eyes betrayed a turmoil beneath the surface. "Any change will be for the worse."

Despite her protests, he pressed for commitment, asking her to fix a marriage date. She breathed a murmur, inarticulate yet expressive, then darted away from his embrace, slipping into the darkness like a ghost. As he stood there, the future stretched out before him, fraught with uncertainty, a delicate balance between the love they shared and the wild, untamable spirit that lived within her.
