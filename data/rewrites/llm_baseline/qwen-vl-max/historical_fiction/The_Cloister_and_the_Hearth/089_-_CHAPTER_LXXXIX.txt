What a soul must endure—sensitive, tender, loving, devout—when suddenly thrust into a crisis of such magnitude and urgency, no words can fully convey. To imagine it is one thing; to live it, quite another. Were I to write a whole book on the inner struggle that unfolded within him during those few hours, we would still fall short of capturing its depth. Yet let me tell you two acts he performed afterward—acts that reveal the quiet strength and resolution that followed the storm of his emotions.

After spending the night with the dying hermit, exchanging prayers and spiritual comfort, Clement did not journey toward Rotterdam as he had planned. Instead, he turned his steps toward Tergou. His purpose was clear: to face his most formidable enemy—the burgomaster Ghysbrecht—and reclaim what was rightfully Margaret’s. In his hand he carried the parchment, a document whose own history could have been a tale in itself. He traveled weary and dust-covered, stopping at a fountain to eat his simple meal of black bread and drink water from the stream. But just as he raised the bread to his lips, a servant woman rushed up, breathless, begging him to come quickly and administer last rites to her dying master.

Without hesitation, Clement returned the bread to his wallet and followed her silently. She led him to the Stadthouse. As they approached the door, he hesitated, a faint shiver passing through him. Then, composed once more, he stepped inside and climbed the stairs. There, propped on pillows, lay Ghysbrecht—pale, weak, already resembling death. Clement stood in the doorway for a moment, recalling the tower, the forest, the letter, and all that had brought them to this point. Then, softly, he said, “Pax vobiscum.”

His voice trembled slightly. The sick man welcomed him with trembling joy. “Thank God, you’ve come in time! Absolve me, father, pray for my soul—you and your brethren!”

“My son,” replied Clement, “absolution comes only after confession. And confession must be full, without reservation, if you would save your soul. Reflect now—what sin weighs heaviest upon your conscience?” He knelt and offered a prayer for wisdom, then rose and said, “Confess your sins.”

“Oh, father,” whispered Ghysbrecht, “they are many and great.”

“Then let your penitence be equal to their weight,” said Clement gently. “Only so may you find mercy.”

The burgomaster clasped his hands and began, with outward sorrow, to list his transgressions. He confessed to eating meat during Lent, missing mass on Sundays and holy days, neglecting other duties of faith—each detail spoken with careful precision. When he finished, Clement spoke again, calmly: “These are faults. Now speak of your crimes. You should have begun with them.”

“But father,” stammered Ghysbrecht, “if these are not crimes, what else could be?”

“Are you confessing to me—or am I confessing to you?” asked Clement, his tone sharpening.

“Forgive me, father! I meant to confess to you. But I do not know what you mean by crimes.”

“The seven deadly sins—have you escaped them all?”

“Heaven forbid! I know none of them by name.”

“Many commit them daily without knowing their names,” said Clement. “Begin with the one that leads to lying, theft, and murder.”

“I am free of that one,” insisted Ghysbrecht. “How do you call it?”

“Avarice,” said Clement.

“Avarice? Ah, yes—I’ve always been thrifty, but I’ve kept a good table, and never neglected the poor. Alas, I am a sinner, perhaps the next will catch me. What is the next?”

“We are not done with this one,” replied Clement firmly. “Remember, the Church is not to be mocked.”

“Am I in a state to mock her now?” cried Ghysbrecht, bewildered. “Avarice? Avarice?”

“Have you ever robbed the fatherless?” asked the friar.

“Me? Robbed the fatherless?” gasped Ghysbrecht. “Not that I recall.”

“Once more,” said Clement, “I warn you: you are trifling with the Church. Miserable man! Another evasion, and I leave you. The devils will gather round your bed and drag you into hell.”

“Oh, do not leave me!” shrieked the old man, terrified. “I beg you—stay!”

“The Church knows all,” said Clement coldly. “You have robbed the fatherless. I will not ask again.”

“Floris Brandt?” whispered Ghysbrecht, recoiling in horror.

Clement stared at him, unyielding. “Yes. Floris Brandt.”

The burgomaster lifted himself up in bed, eyes wide with dread. “How did you know?”

“The Church knows many things,” replied Clement. “By ways hidden from you. You thought to deceive her, calling a friar you believed ignorant of your sins. ‘I’ll confess only to someone who doesn’t know my past,’ you said. ‘So I shall cheat the Church on my deathbed.’ But God, more merciful than you are to yourself, sent one who could not be deceived. I have tested you. I have warned you. But you cannot confess—because you are not truly repentant. Die as you have lived. I see the fiends already gathering. They wait only for me to turn away. And now I go.”

He turned to leave. But Ghysbrecht clutched at his robe, desperate. “Mercy! Holy man, stay! I will confess everything! Everything! I stole from my friend Floris. Alas, it should have ended there—he lost little—but I kept the land from Peter, his son, and from Margaret, Peter’s daughter. I meant to give it back… but I couldn’t. I couldn’t.”

“Avarice, my son,” said Clement, “avarice. Blessed be you—there is yet time.”

“No,” moaned Ghysbrecht, “I will leave it to her in my will. She won’t wait long—only a month or two at most.”

“For which month’s possession,” said Clement, “you would damn your soul forever. Fool!”

The sick man groaned, pleading for reason. But Clement, firm yet gentle, persisted. With infinite patience, he drew the dying man away from his grasp on another’s inheritance. At times, the temptation to produce the deed—brought expressly for this purpose—was strong. Once, he almost reached into his breast, but remembered yesterday’s rage and held back. He mastered his anger. He overcame his deep disgust for the man, whose presence made his flesh crawl. And finally, without a word of the parchment, he guided Ghysbrecht to full restitution.

How that restitution was carried out, and what effects it had upon the burgomaster, will be told elsewhere. So too will the terms of their parting. But I promised to record two acts that revealed Clement’s inner state. This was the first.

The second is brief—just two words:

As soon as he knew beyond doubt that Margaret had her inheritance, that she was secure and rich—

He vanished.
