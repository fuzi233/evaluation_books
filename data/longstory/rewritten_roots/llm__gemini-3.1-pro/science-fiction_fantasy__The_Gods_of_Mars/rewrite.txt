Twelve harvests had turned to winter since I laid the body of my great-uncle, Captain John Carter of Virginia, into the dark, reverberant acoustics of that strange mausoleum in the old cemetery at Richmond. Often had I pondered the odd instructions he had left me, dictating a ritualistic architecture of preservation: that he be laid in an *open* casket, and that the ponderous mechanism controlling the vault’s heavy iron bolts be accessible *only from the inside*. 

Twelve years had passed since I had read the remarkable manuscript of this man who remembered no childhood. Time seemed a circular, interwoven tapestry around him; he remained ever young, a man who had dandled my grandfather’s great-grandfather upon his knee, and who had spent ten years tasting the arid, spiced dust of the planet Mars. He had fought for and against the green men and the red men of Barsoom, eventually winning the ever-beautiful Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and standing as a prince of the house of Tardos Mors. 

During those long years, amidst the quiet domestic rhythms of my estate, I frequently wondered if John Carter were truly dead. While I stood in my kitchens—surrounded by the rich olfactory landscape of rendering fats and the sharp, piney aroma of wild rosemary I foraged daily—I would think of him hurtling across forty-eight million miles of space back to Earth. Had he returned to Barsoom? Had he opened the frowning portals of the mighty atmosphere plant in time to save the countless millions suffocating on a dying world? Had he found his black-haired Princess and the slender son he dreamed awaited him in the royal gardens? Or was he lost to a living death?

I was lost in such speculation one sultry August evening. The jarring, mechanical screech of a passing motorcar outside had just disrupted the mindful quiet of my study—a harsh reminder of the sonic blight of industrializing Richmond that continually frayed my nerves. Uncle Ben, a master of traditional agrarian ways who could read the turning seasons in the scent of the topsoil, was beside me, slowly bruising spearmint leaves sourced from the damp, loamy banks of the James River. The rhythmic, percussive *thrum* of his wooden muddler against the silver julep cup restored a measure of acoustic harmony just as he handed me a telegram.

Tearing it open, I read: ‘Meet me to-morrow hotel Raleigh Richmond. JOHN CARTER’

Early the next morning I took the train for Richmond, escaping the dissonant, commercial clamor of the station to find his room at the Hotel Raleigh. As I entered, he rose to greet me. He had not aged a minute. He remained the clean-limbed fighting-man of thirty, his keen grey eyes undimmed. I had arranged for a spread of slow-smoked Virginia ham and heirloom tomatoes to be brought up, and as we sat, he partook with a profound, reverent appreciation. To him, sustenance was never mere utilitarian fuel; each bite was a physical communion with the soil of his mother Earth, a grounding sensory ritual before he spoke.

“Well, nephew,” he greeted me, his voice carrying that rich, resonant timbre that always seemed to harmonize perfectly with a room's acoustics, “do you feel as though you were seeing a ghost, or suffering from the effects of too many of Uncle Ben’s juleps?”

“Juleps, I reckon,” I replied, taking in the familiar, spiced scent of his tobacco. “For I certainly feel mighty good. You have been back to Mars? Tell me. And Dejah Thoris? You found her well and awaiting you?”

“Yes, I have been to Barsoom again,” he said, his gaze distant. “But it is a long story. I have learned the secret, nephew, and I may traverse the trackless void at my will. Yet my heart remains on Barsoom. While it is there in the keeping of my Martian Princess, I doubt I shall ever again leave the dying world that is my life.” 

He paused, tracing the rim of his glass, his expression turning sorrowful. “There is a tragedy in our English lexicon, nephew. Our dominant vocabulary is too sterile, too detached to capture the sensory truth of Martian life. How do I translate the precise, metallic tang of the winds over the dead sea bottoms, or the haunting, melodic syllables of a Barsoomian dialect that is slowly dying out? Cultural memory erodes when the language to describe its feasts and its music vanishes. Even the wise and mysterious therns of Barsoom, that ancient cult hidden in the impregnable, echoing fastnesses of the Mountains of Otz, are as ignorant of life and death as we are. I have proved it, though I near lost my life in the doing.”

He patted a swelling portfolio on the table, its leather binding exuding the earthy scent of cured hide and aged parchment. “I have tried to capture the untranslatable nuances of my journey in these notes, made over the last three months on Earth. I know the world will not believe. Earth men, deafened by the roar of their own machinery, have not progressed to a point where they can comprehend the subtle frequencies of what I have written. Give them what you wish, but do not feel aggrieved if they laugh at you.”

That night, we walked together to the cemetery. The city’s industrial roar faded into the sacred acoustic ecology of the graveyard, where only the wind rustling through ancient oaks disturbed the quiet. At the vault’s threshold, he pressed my hand. 

“Good-bye, nephew,” he said softly. “I doubt I can ever bring myself to leave my wife and boy while they live, and the span of life upon Barsoom is often more than a thousand years.”

He entered the vault. The great door swung slowly to. The ponderous bolts grated into place with a profound, heavy resonance that reverberated deep within my chest. The lock clicked. 

I have never seen Captain John Carter, of Virginia, since. But here is the story of his return to Mars, painstakingly transcribed from that great mass of notes he left upon the table at the Raleigh. There is much I have left out, much I have not dared to tell, hampered by the limits of our own tongue. Yet within these pages, you will find the record of his second search for Dejah Thoris—a history preserving, as best as one can, the fading lexicon and the rich, gustatory soul of a distant world. 

E. R. B.

The biting chill of March 1886 settled over my cottage bluff, carrying with it the metallic, ash-laden taste of an encroaching industrial age. Below me, the noble Hudson churned like the grey spectre of a dead river, its waters choking beneath the relentless, mechanical groan of steamships and factories. This oppressive sonic pollution grated continually against my spirit, fraying the edges of my sanity and drowning out the natural acoustic harmony of the earth. For ten long years, I had sought to anchor my mythic past into an everyday domestic routine—brewing bitter black teas, kneading dough by the hearth, meticulously recording the culinary and sensory memories of a world millions of miles away. Yet, as I stood beneath the night sky, my palate longed not for the sterile sustenance of Earth, but for the deep, saffron-rich soils and the spiced, communal banquets of my beloved Mars. 

The cycle was turning once again. Just as it had on that fateful March night in 1866, when I lay in the spectral silence of an Arizona cave, the irresistible pull of the red star drew me into its mythic rhythm. I stretched my arms toward the lurid eye of the war god, praying for the return of that strange power. Suddenly, a wave of nausea swept over me. My senses swam, my knees yielded, and I pitched toward the dizzying precipice of the bluff. Instantly, the timeline folded back upon itself. My brain cleared, overwhelmed by the vivid, visceral memory of the dark Arizona cave. I made the same superhuman effort to break the bonds of my paralysis. A sharp click resonated in my mind—like the snapping of a taut wire across an acoustic chamber—and I stood naked and free beside the lifeless shell that had so recently pulsed with the warm, red blood of John Carter. With a final, fleeting glance at the commercialized, deafening world I was leaving behind, I turned my eyes to Mars and plunged into the unthinkable cold and silent void of interplanetary space.

I awakened to a feast for the senses, beneath the burning rays of a brazen sun that pierced the dome of a mighty, ancient forest. The olfactory landscape was immediately intoxicating—a rich bouquet of crushed juniper, sweet copper, and a heady, floral warmth that my native English tongue, a language already withered by the sterile constraints of modern thought, could never accurately translate. I lay upon a close-cropped sward of brilliant, red grass-like vegetation. All around me stood majestic trees whose massive stems, some fully a hundred feet in diameter, were polished to the gleam of fine porcelain. Their woods varied from the deepest ebony to pure azure, scarlet, and a vibrant yellow, crowned by thick, gorgeous blooms whose intricate layering and fragrant nectar would challenge the descriptive powers of Earth’s most meticulous botanists and food historians. 

The acoustic ecology of this forest was profoundly healing. It was filled with winged, brilliantly colored birds that were entirely voiceless. Their silent flights created a rustling canopy of pure, unblemished quietude, a sanctuary free from the abrasive din I had left behind. Furthermore, the land bore the unmistakable signature of careful, traditional agrarian hands. The sward was trimmed like an ancient English lawn, and the trees were pruned to a uniform height, turning the forest into a vast, high-ceilinged chamber. I recognized the reverence for the soil here, the careful curation of life that bespoke a civilized people who understood the profound meaning of their environment. 

As I attuned my Earthly sinews to the lesser gravity and rarefied atmosphere—laughing as my first attempt to rise sent me bounding several feet into the air—I walked down an imperceptible slope toward a shimmering blue sea. To my right, a broad, majestic river flowed between scarlet banks, originating from the base of mighty, perpendicular bluffs. But it was in the broad meadow before the river that the sheer strangeness of this world disrupted my sensory reverie.

A herd of perhaps a score of grotesque figures moved slowly across the meadow. They were massive, ten to twelve feet in height, with hairless, ghoulish blue bodies. Their faces bore a single, dead-white eye and a ragged, inflamed hole in the center that resembled a fresh bullet wound. From a culinary and biological perspective, their method of foraging was a horrifying travesty of agrarian grace. They possessed no true mouths on their faces. Instead, their oddly shaped, trunk-like arms moved in sinuous, snakelike undulations, ending in hands equipped with razor-like talons and a gaping orifice in each palm. I watched from behind an ebony tree as they cropped the tender red vegetation with their talons and sucked it up through their arm-like throats. It was a purely utilitarian consumption, devoid of any sensory appreciation—a mechanical fueling of their massive bodies that sickened the passionate epicurean within me. 

Most remarkable, however, was their connection to the sonic environment. The tops of their heads were covered in a tangled mass of jet-black, worm-like hairs, each writhing and crawling with independent life. These were not hairs, but a thousand sentient ears, perfectly evolved acoustic organs scanning the landscape. From their armpits dangled tiny, unopened buds and miniature replicas of themselves, feeding and growing in a bizarre, composite lifecycle.

I considered stepping forward, but my rash resolve was halted by a sudden, shrieking wail that tore through the pristine silence from the bluffs to my right. It was a harsh, dominant sound—a weaponized lexicon of pure aggression that violently disrupted the psychological equilibrium of the meadow. Instantly, every writhing ear upon the Plant Men stood stiffly perpendicular. The leader of the herd emitted a strange purring sound from the mouth in its palm, and the entire gruesome flock bounded toward the bluffs in great, kangaroo-like leaps of twenty to thirty feet.

Driven by the old martial rhythm that pulsed in my veins, I bounded after them, my athletic Earth-muscles propelling me in prodigious arcs. As I neared a cluster of huge boulders at the river’s source, the scene broke upon my horrified gaze. The herd had surrounded a small group of green Martians. There were two giant males and four females, their glossy green hides gleaming beneath the sun. I noted immediately the varied metal ornaments they wore, marking them as members of eternally warring hordes. To see them standing back-to-back against a common enemy spoke volumes of the desperate erosion of their cultural memory; their distinct tribal dialects and ancient animosities had been momentarily erased by the sheer necessity of survival. 

The slaughter commenced with terrifying efficiency. The Plant Men attacked using a method entirely foreign to the martial science of the green warriors. A blue monster leaped directly over the defenders, bringing down its massive, flat, six-foot tail in a sweeping arc that crushed a warrior’s skull like an eggshell. The shrill, screeching purr of their palm-mouths was designed to terrify and confuse, dominating the acoustic space. Two more bounded simultaneously, and two more green Martians fell to ignoble deaths. 

Only one warrior and two females remained. As two Plant Men charged, the surviving male swung his mighty long-sword, cleaving a beast from chin to groin. Yet, the other monster’s cruel tail lashed out, crushing both females into the scarlet sward. Seeing his kin obliterated and the entire herd converging upon him, the lone warrior embraced the old, circular dance of death. He hewed right and left, carving a path through the blue flesh, and sprinted toward the shelter of the forest. 

My heart swelled with admiration. Acting on the deep-seated impulse of my own warrior's palate, I sprang from my concealing rock, bounding toward the fallen green Martians. I snatched a discarded long-sword from the grass. The red mist of battle swam before my eyes, bringing with it the familiar, intoxicating scent of hot copper and ozone. I reached the fleeing warrior just as the hissing herd overtook him, pinning him against a boulder.

Their single white eyes were fixed so intently on their prey they did not hear my soundless approach. I was among them in an instant. Four of the beasts fell dead before they even registered my presence. As they recoiled, the great green warrior sprang to my side. We fought back-to-back, moving in perfect, interwoven arcs. My keen blade sliced through flesh and bone, releasing spurts of the creatures' lifeblood—a viscous, green syrup that coated us from head to foot. It was a sluggish, sticky fluid that smelled of crushed caterpillars and tasted like a ruined, deeply bitter reduction. 

The fight was intimate and desperate. Razor-like talons scored my limbs. At one point, the crushing weight of a monster bore down upon my back, its talons sinking into my flesh while its moist, utilitarian palm-lips attempted to suck the very lifeblood from my veins, threatening to reduce me to mere sustenance. I was struggling with a ferocious beast at my throat when the green warrior beside me swung his mighty blade, raking the horror from my back in a single, devastating sweep. 

Freed from the assault, we dispatched the remainder of our immediate attackers. But the acoustic assault was not over. High above us, on a natural balcony etched into the cliff face, stood a strange man. He was the author of the shrieking wail, acting as an orchestrator of this horrific harvest. He pointed his hand toward us, his voice a dominant, oppressive force cutting through the air. Following his gesture, I looked out across the meadow, the forest, and the flat land beyond the river. Converging upon us were hundreds of wildly leaping Plant Men, alongside strange, new monsters that ran erect and upon all fours.

The wheel had turned. The cycle of life and battle was reaching a beautiful, terrible crescendo.

"It will be a great death," I said to my companion, wiping the bitter green sap from my lip. 

He shot a quick glance at the approaching horde, and a smile broke across his tusked face. "We may at least die fighting and as great warriors should, John Carter," he replied.

I turned in profound, astonished wonderment. Hearing my own name spoken in the complex, native dialect of his people bridged the vast, untranslatable void between our subjective experiences. There, standing amidst the carnage on a world millions of miles from my domestic hearth, was the shrewd statesman, the passionate preserver of his people's history, and my great, good friend: Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark.

The sonic equilibrium of the valley shattered, torn apart by the weird, piercing shriek of the herald upon the cliff. To my ears, attuned to the deep, acoustic ecology of Barsoom’s vast and silent sea bottoms, this shrill summons was a violent pollutant, a grating industrial cacophony that assaulted the psychological well-being of the landscape itself. Down the broad expanse of the scarlet sward, a torrent of terrifying creatures streamed toward us, drawn by that disruptive frequency. 

Around us lay the grisly harvest of our initial skirmish, the air thick with the olfactory heavyweights of combat: the herbaceous, sticky musk of severed plant-man vines mingling with the oxidized, iron-rich tang of spilled Martian blood. 

“Come,” Tars Tarkas cried, his native Tharkish syllables carrying the resonant, guttural weight of an ancient culture slowly being suffocated by the dying world around it. “We must make for the cliffs.” 

We raced across the scarlet turf, the spongy, moss-like ground yielding beneath my boots with the scent of crushed lichen and dry spice. I modulated my Earth-born stride to keep pace with my slower, massive companion, the two of us weaving through a sweeping, multi-generational saga of predator and prey that had played out upon this valley floor for millennia. Tars Tarkas urged me ahead to scout for sanctuary, and, throwing every ounce of my muscle into the effort, I crossed the remaining distance in great leaps.

The cliffs rose perpendicularly from the agrarian flatness of the valley. To my right, a magnificent forest reared its gorgeous foliage a thousand feet into the air. As the sun crested the zenith, the cliff face erupted in a scintillating display of burnished gold, flaming red rubies, and soft green emeralds. Yet, in that moment, I could not appreciate this geological banquet. My mind, seeking only an acoustic and physical sanctuary, recoiled from the sheer, impregnable wall. It was only when my eyes caught the dark mouths of caves near the forest’s canopy that I found our recipe for survival. 

Scaling the colossal trees was our only route. These towering flora were ancient, their bark redolent of toasted cinnamon and petrified sap, having stood as silent, domestic witnesses to the valley's cyclical history. For Tars Tarkas, a fifteen-foot green Martian whose people favored the nomadic, circuitous paths of the dead sea bottoms, climbing was a nearly untranslatable physical concept. Yet, as the relentless horde closed in, carrying the rancid scent of raw marrow and overripe vegetation, he accepted the necessity. He fled not from a fear of death—a concept alien to the stoic, death-seeking lexicon of his people—but out of an unspoken loyalty to our shared history, honoring the profound sustenance of our friendship.

We reached the deep, earthy shadows of the forest just as a giant plant man sprang upon us. The creature was a grotesque marvel of botanical anatomy, engineered to harvest vital fluids. Its claws reached for us, its mouths eager to feast. I called to Tars Tarkas to begin his ascent while I prepared to dispatch the creature, but the beast was swift. As my long-sword cleaved the empty air, its massive tail swept me to the ground, bringing the bitter taste of dirt and crushed roots to my tongue. 

I grasped a writhing tentacle in either hand. The creature’s fibrous, well-muscled flesh yielded like a dense root vegetable under my grip, and the intimate proximity forced me to inhale its sharp, peppery musk. Had we the luxury of an uninterrupted struggle, my earthly sinews would have surely triumphed, but the sonic landscape was suddenly overwhelmed by a new, horrifying frequency. 

Over the shoulder of my botanical antagonist, I saw the great white apes of Barsoom arriving in a wave of snapping, screaming rage. They towered fifteen feet high, their physiology uncannily mimicking our Earth men but distorted by enormous size and an intermediary set of arms. They brought with them the heavy, fermented odor of old blood and stale breath. But the most insidious assault on my senses was the horrid purring of the plant men—a low, rhythmic vibration that burrowed into the inner ear, a deeply unnatural sound that corrupted the mind’s acoustic sanctuary. 

Instantly, cruel fangs and keen talons sank into my flesh. Cold, sucking lips fastened upon my arteries, attempting to drink the very essence of my life. Weighted down by tons of ferocious flesh, I struggled to my feet. Shortening my grip on my blade, I turned it into a chef’s paring knife, meticulously carving my way through cartilage and sap. Green botanical ichor and crimson ape blood emulsified in the air, a gruesome vinaigrette born of our desperate struggle. 

From the lower branches, Tars Tarkas saw my plight and dropped back to the soil. Once again, we fought back to back, our gleaming blades moving with the practiced rhythm of a kitchen brigade in the heat of service. Time and again, we beat back the apes and dodged the leaping plant men, but against such overwhelming numbers, even the finest swordsmen on Mars were forced to yield ground. 

We retreated halfway around the colossal trunk of the giant tree. Suddenly, Tars Tarkas emitted a brief cry of discovery. “Here is shelter for one at least, John Carter,” he said, pointing to a three-foot opening in the ancient bark, smelling heavily of dry rot and hollowed timber. 

“In with you, Tars Tarkas,” I cried.

A complex negotiation of cultural identity and linguistic pride followed, shouted over the din of clashing steel and roaring beasts. In the Tharkish dialect, there are few words to adequately convey the nuance of selfless sacrifice; the dominant lexicon of his people equates retreat with dishonor. “We shall both die if we remain without,” he argued, his bulk too vast to slip easily into the aperture while under attack. 

“Then we shall die together,” I replied, the metallic tang of adrenaline bright on my palate. “Let me defend the opening while you get in. My smaller stature will permit me to slip in after.”

He hesitated, choking on a word that his whole life of continual strife had taught him to despise. “If you fail, John Carter,” he said, the grim smile on his face betraying the untranslatable depth of his affection, “know that the cruel and heartless Thark, to whom you taught the meaning of friendship, will come out to die beside you.”

As he dropped to force his way into the aromatic, woody hollow, the howling pack hurled themselves at me. My shimmering blade flew right and left, now coated in the sticky, herbaceous juice of a plant man, now dyed with the hot, iron-rich crimson of a white ape. I fought as I never had before, feeding the horde a bitter meal of cold steel. Though the ground piled high with their dead, their sheer weight eventually dragged me down beneath them. I felt those awful sucking lips against my flesh once more. 

Then, powerful hands gripped my ankles. I was hauled backward into the hollow domesticity of the tree's interior. A brief tug of war ensued between Tars Tarkas and a plant man clinging tenaciously to my breast, but with a mighty thrust, I pierced its vitals. 

I lay panting on the flat, hard floor. The air inside the tree was a restorative balm—cool, smelling of ancient tannin, dried husks, and centuries of undisturbed dust. For an hour, the horde outside shrieked and purred, their noise battering the thick wooden walls, but the acoustic dampening of the tree preserved our sanity. Eventually, the horrific sounds faded to a localized perimeter, leaving us in a tense, besieged quiet.

As my eyes adjusted to the semi-darkness, I explored our refuge. The hollow spanned fifty feet in diameter, a natural, multi-generational domicile used by unknown beings long before our occupancy. High above, a faint glow of light hinted at a high canopy opening. Against the far wall, I discovered the remnants of everyday domestic labor: a rough ladder connecting to a series of horizontal wooden bars spanning the shaft-like interior. 

I climbed first, my hands gripping wood polished smooth by the oils of countless ancient ascents. The light grew brighter. After climbing fully five hundred feet, I reached the upper opening, which gave way to a massive, flat limb. Retracing my steps, I fetched Tars Tarkas. As we ascended together, we carefully removed the lower bars, severing the timeline of our path to prevent pursuit from the rear—a meticulous preservation of our newfound safety.

When we emerged, I ventured out onto the limb. It angled upward toward the cliff, smelling of sun-baked bark and ozone, ending a few feet above a narrow ledge that fronted a cave. My lesser weight allowed me to tread the dizzying branch, but it bent precariously. Tars Tarkas and I devised a solution using his longest leather harness strap—the cured hide carrying the familiar scent of lanolin and warrior's sweat. I crossed to the ledge, then hauled his massive bulk across the gap. 

Standing on the dizzying balcony, we looked out over the cyclical reality of the valley. Below, the white apes were foraging on the remnants of fallen Tharks, while herds of plant men grazed placidly upon the scarlet sward, keeping it close-clipped like a well-tended agrarian estate. The violent mythic occurrences of the morning had already receded into the everyday domestic routines of Barsoom’s brutal ecosystem.

Turning away, we entered the cave. It was a well-proportioned tunnel carved from solid rock, carrying the faint, desiccated aroma of undisturbed stone. We walked in absolute darkness, the silence profound and restorative to my frayed nerves. We clasped hands to maintain connection, moving through the deep acoustic void until we struck a smooth partition of hard wood. 

My fingers traced the surface, searching for the tactile vocabulary of Martian architecture. I found the button—a dialect of engineering common across the planet. I pressed it, and the door yielded, opening into a dimly lit, unoccupied chamber. The air here was sterile, devoid of the rich, gustatory markers of the living valley. 

We stepped inside. Almost instantly, the door closed behind us with a sharp, mechanical click—a jarring intrusion into the silent ecology of the space. I sprang to wrench it open, but the door was unyielding. There was no button on the inside. The dominant architecture had asserted its power, trapping us in a language we could no longer manipulate. 

And then, from unseen lips in the shadows, a cruel, mocking peal of laughter rang through the desolate chamber, shattering our fragile acoustic sanctuary.

When the mechanized cruelty of that laugh finally ceased tearing at the cave’s acoustic harmony, Tars Tarkas and I stood bathed in a heavy, expectant silence. The harsh, unnatural frequency had violently disrupted the cavern's natural reverberations, a jarring intrusion of sonic pollution that set my teeth on edge. No further sound broke the stillness, and within the dim periphery of our vision, nothing moved. 

At length, Tars Tarkas laughed softly. It was a dry, percussive rhythm, a deeply resonant acoustic marker unique to his strange kind when confronted with the horrific. It possessed an inherent untranslatability—a Tharkian dialect of mirth that found profound, absurd humor in the very things that moved Earth men to loathing or tears. I had seen them roll upon the ground in the same uncontrollable fits while witnessing the grim spectacles of the Great Games, their cultural memory steeped in a lexicon where agony and amusement were inextricably braided.

I looked up at the great green warrior, letting a smile touch my own lips; a steady countenance was a far better anchor in this disharmonious space than a trembling chin.

“What do you make of it all?” I asked. “Where in the deuce are we?”

He looked at me in genuine surprise. “Where are we?” he repeated. “Do you tell me, John Carter, that you know not where you be?”

“That I am upon Barsoom is all I can taste in the air,” I replied. “But for your presence and the heavy musk of those great white apes, I should not even guess that. The sights I have seen this day are as alien to my beloved Barsoom—whose spiced winds I knew ten long years ago—as they are to the world of my birth. No, Tars Tarkas, I know not where we be.”

“Where have you been since you opened the mighty portals of the atmosphere plant years ago? After the keeper died, when all Barsoom was suffocating, gasping for the very breath that flavors our communal hearths? Your body was never found. The Jeddak of Helium and his granddaughter, your princess, offered fabulous bounties. Princes of royal blood scoured the red plains. When all physical traces evaporated, there was but one linguistic refuge, one mythic conclusion left to the elders: that you had taken the long, last pilgrimage down the mysterious River Iss. They believed you awaited Dejah Thoris in the Valley Dor, upon the shores of the Lost Sea of Korus, expecting to share the eternal feasts of the ancestors.”

“Why you had gone, none could guess,” he continued, “for your princess still lived—”

“Thank God,” I interrupted, the tension releasing from my chest in a long, ragged exhale. “I did not dare to ask. I feared I might have been too late to preserve her. She was fading so quickly when I left her in the royal gardens of Tardos Mors on that long-gone night. And she lives yet?”

“She lives, John Carter.”

“You still have not told me where we are,” I gently reminded him.

“We are where I expected to find you—and another,” he said, his voice dropping to a timbre rich with the sorrow of lost histories. “Many years ago, you heard the story of the woman who taught me the thing green Martians are reared to excise from their vocabulary: the woman who taught me to love. You know the cruel tortures, the butchery her devotion earned her at the hands of Tal Hajus. I believed she awaited me by the Lost Sea. And you, the Earth man who taught this cruel Thark the dialect of friendship, I thought you too roamed the carefree Valley Dor. For a month, I have traveled the long pilgrimage, drawn by the profound yearning to reunite in that mythic domesticity. Do you understand now where you be, John Carter?”

“And that was the River Iss, emptying into the Lost Sea of Korus in the Valley Dor?” I asked, trying to reconcile the circular pacing of my own life—returning to Mars only to be thrust into the culmination of its oldest myth.

“This is the valley of love and peace,” he replied, his tone chillingly cold, heavily ironical. “The destination to which every Barsoomian since time immemorial has longed to journey, seeking the restorative calm of the eternal hearth after a life of bloodshed. This, John Carter, is Heaven.”

His bitterness was a stark reflection of a shattered worldview. To have one’s multi-generational faith—the very foundation of an agrarian and warrior culture’s hope for a final, peaceful harvest—uprooted and revealed as a slaughterhouse might have excused a vastly greater demonstration. 

I laid my hand upon his shoulder. “I am sorry,” I said, knowing the inadequacy of my native Earth syllables to bridge the chasm of his disillusionment. 

“Think of the countless billions of Barsoomians,” he murmured. “Pilgrims who drifted down this cruel river, expecting the fragrance of slow-roasted meats and the warm embrace of ancestors, only to fall into the ferocious clutches of the terrors that assaulted us today. There is an ancient legend—a suppressed oral history—of a red man who returned from the River Iss. He spoke what the dominant lexicon deemed a fearful blasphemy: that horrific brutes inhabited this wondrous valley, devouring pilgrims upon the very shores where they sought sustenance and peace. The ancients executed the blasphemer, enforcing the linguistic death of his truth. But now we know the legend was absolute fact. We are trapped between the wild thoat of certainty and the mad zitidar of fact.”

“As Earth men say, we are between the devil and the deep sea,” I replied, a grim smile touching my face at the trans-planetary symmetry of our idioms. “We can only take things as they come. Whoever slays us will pay an exorbitant price in blood. White ape or plant man, they will learn the terrible cost of attempting to consume John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, and Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark.”

He joined me in a rare, genuine laugh, a shared acoustic resonance that solidified our bond. 

“But where have you been?” he asked. 

“I have been back to Earth,” I said. “For ten Earth years, I have consumed meals that were mere utilitarian fuel, utterly devoid of the complex olfactory landscapes of Barsoom. I existed in a sensory famine, praying to return to this grim planet, whose ancient, spiced winds I love more than the world of my birth. And now, having miraculously returned to the very heartbeat of my past, I am hurled into the one tiny spot of all Barsoom from which there is apparently no escape. A half-hour before I saw you battling the plant men, I was standing in the moonlight on Earth. Do you believe me?”

“I believe,” replied Tars Tarkas, “though I cannot understand.”

As we spoke, I studied the chamber. It was two hundred feet in length, hewn from the living cliff. In the dim light of a single minute radium illuminator, the walls glowed with the dull, buttery warmth of ancient gold, patched with polished ruby, emerald, and diamond. The floor was worn to a glassy smoothness by the friction of countless generations. 

As I extended my hand toward the locked doorway to search for a controlling mechanism, that mocking, artificial laugh ruptured the quiet once more. It was so close I involuntarily shrank back, my hand tightening on the hilt of my great-sword. 

From the far corner, an artificially amplified, hollow voice chanted: “There is no hope, there is no hope; the dead return not, the dead return not; nor is there any resurrection. Hope not, for there is no hope.”

The voice was a profound violation of acoustic ecology—a sterile, disembodied projection designed entirely to erode psychological well-being. My spine chilled, the short hairs at the base of my neck rising like a hound sensing the unseen. I walked toward the mournful sound, but it evaporated, only to be replaced by a shrill, piercing shriek from the opposite wall.

“Fools! Fools!” the new voice weaponized its lexicon. “Thinkest thou to defeat the eternal laws of life and death? Wouldst cheat the mysterious Issus, Goddess of Death, of her just dues? Did not her mighty messenger bear you upon her leaden bosom to the Valley Dor? Go back the way thou camest, to the merciful maws of the children of the Tree of Life or the gleaming fangs of the great white apes. But insist in your rash purpose to thread the mazes of the Golden Cliffs, and Death in its most frightful form will overtake you—a death so horrible that the Holy Therns themselves avert their eyes.”

Then the awful laugh broke out again. 

“Most uncanny,” I remarked, refusing to let their sonic terrorism take root. 

“What shall we do?” Tars Tarkas asked. “We cannot fight empty air. I would sooner face foes into whose flesh I may feel my blade bite, to know I am selling my carcass dearly.”

“Those voices come from beings as real as you or I,” I called out loudly, deliberately projecting my voice to reclaim the cavern's acoustic space. “In their veins flows lifeblood that may be let. Their invisibility is the best proof of their mortal cowardice. Think you that John Carter will fly at the first shriek of a foe who dare not step into the open?”

I suspected this theatrical noise pollution was a calculated maneuver to herd us back to the valley’s hungry beasts. We waited in silence. 

Suddenly, a stealthy, rhythmic whisper of footfalls sounded behind me. I wheeled to behold a great, many-legged banth creeping sinuously across the golden floor. The Barsoomian lion is a fierce beast, practically hairless save for a bristly mane, its long, lithe body supported by ten powerful legs. As it lashed its tail, the heavy, musky odor of its carnivory—rancid marrow and the metallic tang of old blood—flooded my senses. It emitted a terrifying roar, a percussive blast meant to induce paralysis, and launched its bulk toward me. 

But my nerves were immune to its acoustic assault. My sword met its charge, sliding cleanly into its vitals rather than the tender flesh its needle-like fangs gaped to engulf. The beast’s hot, copper-scented blood spilled over my hands as I drew my blade from its heart. Turning, I saw Tars Tarkas dispatching a similar monster. 

For the better part of an hour, one hideous creature after another was loosed upon us. Tars Tarkas reveled in the tangible combat, the honest clashing of steel against bone offering a profound relief from the manipulative, disembodied voices. The beasts howled in very real pain, bleeding very real blood. 

Yet, I noticed they only appeared when our backs were turned. They did not materialize from thin air. 

Among the ornaments on Tars Tarkas’ leather harness was a small mirror, resting flat against his broad back. As he stood over a fallen antagonist, I caught sight of the mirror’s reflection. 

“Move not, Tars Tarkas! Move not a muscle!” I whispered. 

He froze. In the mirror, I watched a section of the wall behind me execute a flawless mechanical rotation. It pivoted perfectly, taking a bisected half of the floor with it, much like a card spun upon a silver coin. On the rotating floor sat a fresh beast, newly delivered to our side of the partition. 

But it was the glimpse through the rotating gap that seized my attention. Beyond lay a brilliantly lit, sterile room. Within it, chained to the wall, were several red Martians—men and women whose ancestors had cultivated the great agrarian plains, now stripped of their dignity and kept like livestock. Directing the secret doorway was a wicked-faced man with flowing yellow hair and skin as white as my own. He possessed the soft, uncalloused hands of one who has never tilled soil or respected the profound rituals of sustenance, treating life only as fuel for his cruel machinery. 

“Watch the wall at your end, Tars Tarkas,” I murmured. “It is through secret doorways they loose the brutes upon us.”

Our tormentors were observing us through hidden apertures. I outlined a plan in a low whisper. Tars Tarkas grunted his assent, the deep vibration in his chest a comforting acoustic anchor. Slowly, he backed toward the wall I faced, while I advanced ahead of him. 

When we were ten feet from the secret doorway, I halted him. I turned my back to the door, feeling the baleful gaze of the pale executioner upon my spine. I glued my eyes to the mirror on the Thark’s back. 

The golden surface commenced its rapid pivot. I gave the signal. Simultaneously, we both sprang for the receding halves of the door. 

A single bound carried me into the adjacent room, bringing me face to face with the yellow-haired man. He was muscled like an Earth man, armed with a long-sword, a short-sword, a dagger, and a radium revolver. Ignoring the honorable ethics of swordsmanship, he immediately drew his firearm. I closed the distance instantly, an uppercut from my blade sending the weapon flying before he could discharge it. 

He drew his long-sword, his face twisting in rage, and we engaged. He was a master of the blade, his movements precise and deadly, but it did not take long for my muscle memory to awaken. I found my rhythm, the familiar, ringing song of clashing steel returning to my arm. My guard proved impregnable, and soon his pristine skin was marred by a dozen minor, bleeding wounds. 

“Who are you, white man?” he hissed, breathing heavily. “That you are no Barsoomian from the outer world is evident from your color. And you are not of us.”

The question revealed the rigid hierarchy of his worldview. I decided to weaponize his own oppressive lexicon against him. 

“What if I were from the Temple of Issus?” I hazarded. 

“Fate forfend!” he exclaimed, his face draining of color beneath the smeared blood. The sheer power dynamics embedded in that single name paralyzed him, exposing his deep-seated terror of his own religious masters. In that momentary lapse of his guard, I drove my sword between his ribs.

The chained prisoners had watched the duel in tense, absolute silence. Not a sound had disturbed the room save the ringing steel and the soft shuffling of our feet. But as the yellow-haired man collapsed, a shrill, desperate cry tore from one of the female prisoners—a native dialect raw with the instinct to preserve life.

“Turn! Turn! Behind you!” she shrieked. 

I wheeled to find a second yellow-haired man lunging from a dark corridor, his sword already raised. Tars Tarkas was trapped on the other side of the closed secret panel. 

I had fought continuously for hours. I was profoundly depleted. It had been twenty-four hours since I had last eaten or slept, and my exhaustion was not merely physical—it was an agonizing severance from the vital, grounding rituals of sustenance. Without the restorative alchemy of a shared meal to anchor my spirit, my body was running on fumes, rendering objects blurry and my movements sluggish. 

I tried to rush him, hoping to overwhelm him through sheer impetuosity, but he was an adroit, calculated fighter. He parried and sidestepped, letting my starved muscles burn themselves out. He led me a desperate chase, backing me toward the corpse of his fallen comrade. 

With a sudden, aggressive rush, he forced me backward. My heel struck the dead man. The physical obstacle disrupted my stance entirely, and I tumbled backward. 

My head struck the hard pavement with a resounding, violent *crack*. The sharp pain exploded behind my eyes, yet the sheer intensity of the shock acted as a brutal acoustic reset. It cleared the muddy exhaustion from my brain, igniting a primal temper that temporarily eclipsed my physical hunger. I was ready to tear the man apart with my bare hands. And I verily believe I would have attempted it, had not my right hand, bracing against the floor to lift my body, closed around the cold, reassuring grip of a discarded metal hilt.

The jagged, dissonant clangour of conflict shattered the subterranean stillness, tearing me from the heavy depths of unconsciousness. For a disorienting heartbeat, my senses struggled against the acoustic violence—the metallic scrape of chains, the wet, guttural snarling of grim beasts, and the heavy, rhythmic exhalations of a man pushed to the brink of physical endurance. It was a harsh, industrial pollution of sound that deeply offended the natural auditory balance of the mind. 

As I pushed myself up from the cold floor of the chamber, the olfactory landscape washed over me: a stifling blend of old rust, the ammonia tang of feral sweat, and the unmistakable, rich copper aroma of fresh blood. Across the room, chained to the blank wall, rested the savage brutes and a group of prisoners. Among them stood a young red Martian woman, the one whose timely cry had just spared my life. She was a perfect specimen of her ancient, copper-skinned race, entirely unadorned, stripped of the tactile textiles and woven histories of her people. Yet, when she spoke, her voice possessed a pure, unpolluted timbre, carrying the subtle, melodic inflections of a fading regional dialect—a sonic remnant of an older, agrarian Barsoom before the Therns imposed their sterile, dominant lexicon upon the world. 

It took only seconds for the chaotic acoustics bleeding through the partition to translate into a clear image in my slowly returning faculties: Tars Tarkas was locked in a desperate struggle. I threw my weight against the secret door, but it was as unyielding as the mythic cliffs of the ancestors. I swept my hands over the cold, senseless gold, searching for a mechanism, when the young woman’s voice cut through the ambient noise. 

“Save thy sword, O Mighty Warrior,” she called out, her phrasing echoing the circular, generational storytelling of the red plains. “Shatter it not against senseless metal which yields better to the lightest finger touch of one who knows its secret.”

“Know you the secret of it then?” I asked, craving the quiet resolution of the lock. 

“Yes; release me and I will give you entrance to the other horror chamber. The keys to my fetters are upon the first dead of thy foemen.” She pointed toward the fallen custodian. “But why would you return to face the fierce banths? Why step back into the jaws of that trap?”

“Because my friend fights there alone,” I answered. 

I knelt beside the carcass of the custodian. The air around the body was stale, lacking the vibrant, earthy aromas of true life, smelling instead of cold stone and utilitarian stagnation. I retrieved an oval ring heavy with keys. The fair Martian maid’s fingers, adept and sensitive to the subtle mechanical vibrations of the metal, quickly selected the key that sprung the great lock at her waist. Freed, she moved with the silent grace of a harvester through high crops, retrieving a slender, needle-like key. She inserted it into a nearly invisible pore in the wall. 

The door swung with a heavy, oiled whisper. The floor beneath my feet pivoted, carrying me into a suffocating miasma of spilt blood, the heavy musk of adrenaline-soaked fur, and the bitter ozone of clashing steel. 

The great Thark stood with his back against an angle of the walls. Facing him in a tense, predatory semi-circle crouched a half-dozen huge monsters. Their blood-matted heads and the deep, precise lacerations across the Thark’s glossy green hide told a story of brutal, protracted violence. Sharp talons had carved his flesh into ribbons. Tars Tarkas was deeply weakened, his body depleted of the vital sustenance and fluids required to fuel his massive frame. Yet, he stood anchored, embodying the indomitable courage of a people deeply rooted in the harsh, cyclical ecology of their world. 

I raised my sharp longsword, the air tasting of iron, but a gentle hand touched my shoulder. I turned to find the young woman beside me. 

“Wait,” she whispered, her breath carrying the faint, sweet scent of a desperately empty stomach holding onto its dignity. “Leave them to me.”

Defenceless, she stepped toward the snarling banths. She did not yell, nor did she add to the violent noise pollution of the chamber. Instead, she spoke a single Barsoomian word. It was a phoneme rooted in an ancient, untranslatable agrarian tongue, a sound born of the open fields and the quiet rustle of domestic life, entirely alien to the sterile, oppressive environment of the Therns. The acoustic resonance of that single syllable was profound. The great beasts wheeled upon her, and for a terrifying second, I expected them to tear her apart. Instead, the untranslatable authority of her ancestral dialect washed over them. They slunk to her feet, whining softly, their aggressive frequencies neutralized. She murmured to them again in low, rhythmic tones, leading the six mighty monsters across the chamber. She opened the opposite panel, sent the beasts through, and turned to us with a quiet smile before rejoining us in the room.

For a long moment, the only sound was the synchronized, heavy breathing of survivors. 

“I heard the fighting,” Tars Tarkas finally rumbled, his deep voice restoring a measure of acoustic harmony. “But I did not fear for you, John Carter, until I heard the report of a revolver shot. That violent noise stripped the last vestige of hope from me, for I knew you had no such weapon. Tell me of it.”

I recounted the events, the words feeling inadequate to bridge the gap between our subjective experiences. Together, we examined the panel through which I had entered, but it defied our tactile investigations. Our path backward was sealed. 

As we turned, another panel swept silently open, and the young woman stepped back into the room. 

“Who are you?” she asked, studying us. “And what is your mission, that you have the temerity to attempt to escape the Valley Dor?”

“I am not of Barsoom,” I replied, my voice steady in the quiet room. “I have taken no voluntary pilgrimage upon the River Iss. My friend is Jeddak of all the Tharks, and I am drawing him away from the living lie that has lured him to this frightful place. I am John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors.”

She smiled, a warmth returning to her eyes. “Naught that passes in the world we have left is unknown here. I heard of you, many seasons past.”

“And who be you?” I asked. “Why are you a prisoner, yet wielding a linguistic authority over these beasts that speaks of deep, ancestral knowledge?”

“Slave I am,” she answered, a profound sorrow in her cadence. “For fifteen years, the cycle of harvests has passed without me. I have been a slave, and now that my knowledge of their ways threatens their artificial dominance, I am condemned to die the death.”

“What death?” I asked, tasting a sudden bitterness in the air. 

“The Holy Therns eat human flesh,” she said, her voice dropping into a register of profound disgust. “But only flesh that has died beneath the sucking lips of a plant man—flesh from which the vibrant, defiling blood of life has been drained. It is a sterile, utilitarian consumption. They possess no reverence for the deep history of food, no appreciation for the sensory communion of a shared meal. To them, the generational cycle of nourishment is reduced to this gruesome, mechanized harvest.”

“Were it the Holy Therns who felt my blade?” I asked. 

“No, those were lesser therns. The Holy Therns abide upon the outer slopes, in luxurious palaces connected to these caves by labyrinthine passages. This sunless world is kept to furnish their sport and their soulless sustenance.”

She spoke of the Temple of Issus, an ultimate haven the Therns believed to be a paradise of fleshly delights, where they passed after a lifespan of a thousand years. She detailed the rigid, hierarchical reincarnation they blindly trusted—from Thern to plant man to great white ape to the slimy silians of the Lost Sea—a horrific, cyclical worldview utterly devoid of the warmth of true cultural memory or the domestic joys of the living world.

“We sent several Holy Therns to the silians today, then,” Tars Tarkas noted with a grim, rumbling laugh. 

“And so your deaths will be the more terrible,” she warned. “You cannot escape.”

“One has escaped, centuries ago,” I reminded her, appealing to the deep, historical rhythms of her people. 

“It is useless,” she sighed. 

“It is our solemn duty,” I insisted, ensuring my tone carried the acoustic weight of conviction. “We know this valley is a rank deception. The Therns are moral pygmies who have commercialized and sterilized the sacred transition of life. If we shrink from this duty, we allow the true cultural memory of Barsoom to be eroded by their false, dominant lexicon. We must return to our people, to preserve the native truth of the world.”

The girl and the green warrior stood in thoughtful silence, absorbing the acoustic ecology of the chamber. 

“Never had I considered it in that light,” she murmured. “I will go with you as far as we can go.”

“To the gates of Issus, or to the snows of the north or south,” Tars Tarkas declared, his voice a steady drumbeat. “Tars Tarkas follows where John Carter leads.”

Thuvia—for that was her name—released the other red Martian captives. We armed ourselves with the swords, daggers, and revolvers stripped from the dead therns, weapons reappropriated from their sterile masters to serve the raw, passionate will to live. 

With Thuvia guiding us, we moved seamlessly through the subterranean maze, our footsteps falling into a synchronized, stealthy rhythm. We avoided the dissonant echoes of approaching patrols, seeking a distant storeroom where we might provision ourselves. 

We had been walking for an hour when the quiet was ruptured. As we entered a great chamber, a man stepped into our path. He was clad in leathern trappings, and upon his brow sat a golden circlet holding an immense, scintillating jewel. The stone radiated nine primary and unseen colors, a priceless artifact of Barsoomian history worn by those who manipulated the massive engines of the atmosphere plant. 

The thern’s eyes narrowed, his very presence an arrogant intrusion upon our shared silence. “Stop!” he commanded. “What means this, Thuvia?”

Thuvia raised her revolver. The gunshot was a deafening, industrial concussion that tore violently through the acoustic ecology of the corridor. The thern collapsed, dead before he struck the floor. The sharp, acrid smell of burnt powder immediately overpowered the stagnant air. 

“Beast!” she hissed, the word carrying years of suppressed culinary and cultural starvation. 

Then she turned, her eyes widening as she looked from the corpse to me. “O Prince,” she breathed. “Fate is kind. Notest thou not the remarkable resemblance between this Holy Thern and thyself?”

He was indeed of my exact stature and facial structure. But where I bore close-cropped black hair, a mass of flowing yellow locks spilled across the stone. 

“Do you wish me, with my black hair, to pose as a yellow-haired priest of this infernal cult?” I asked. 

Smiling, Thuvia knelt beside the body. She removed the golden circlet, and then, to my utter revulsion, lifted the entire scalp of yellow hair from the man’s head. Beneath it, the thern was entirely, unnaturally bald. 

“They are all thus from birth,” Thuvia explained, holding the lifeless wig. “They spring from a race that once possessed luxuriant golden hair, but over ages of stagnation, they have lost it entirely. The wig is but a superficial facade, a piece of manufactured apparel so deeply tied to their fragile power dynamics that appearing without it is their deepest disgrace.”

I took the wig. It smelled intensely of synthetic, perfumed oils—a sterile, commercial scent completely devoid of the rich, loamy complexity of natural life. Slipping it over my dark hair, I crowned myself with the jeweled circlet and donned the dead man's harness, becoming Sator Throg, a Holy Thern of the Tenth Cycle. 

We carried the body with us until we reached the storeroom. Thuvia’s keys worked the locks, granting us access to a chamber heavy with the metallic tang of stored munitions and ancient provisions. My body, pushed far past the limits of its reserves, ached for proper sustenance, for a meal prepared with care rather than choked down for mere survival. But exhaustion claimed supremacy over hunger. 

I sank to the floor, instructing Tars Tarkas to rest and setting two of the freed prisoners on watch. Surrounded by the rhythmic, synchronized breathing of my companions—a sound that finally restored a measure of domestic peace to the hostile dark—I fell instantly into a deep, dreamless sleep.

How long I slept against the cool stone of the subterranean larder, I cannot say. My senses had finally surrendered to the rich, encompassing olfactory landscape of the chamber—a vast culinary archive fragrant with the pungent tang of salt-cured zitidar, the earthy, fermented notes of subterranean moss-roots, and the delicate, powdery scent of desiccated spice-blooms. As a man who had long found profound meaning in the traditional agrarian rhythms of sustenance, I recognized this storeroom not merely as a utilitarian vault, but as a staggering record of Martian food history. 

But my reprieve was shattered by a brutal percussive violence that tore through the delicate sonic tapestry of the ancient chamber. A fusillade of shots rang out, a deafening, industrial dissonance of modern weaponry that battered the mind and entirely disrupted the acoustic ecology of our refuge. 

In an instant, I was upon my feet. From a grand doorway at the far end of the larder, a dozen lesser therns leveled their smoking rifles. Around me lay the bodies of my companions, their lives violently extinguished, their native dialects silenced forever. Only Thuvia and the great green elder Tars Tarkas—who, like myself, had been resting near the fragrant casks of preserved fungoids upon the floor—had escaped this jarring rain of death. 

As I rose, the therns lowered their weapons, their faces contorted in a dissonance of chagrin and alarm. It was then I weaponized the dominant lexicon. I understood the subtle power dynamics embedded in their oppressive tongue, and I borrowed its harsh, guttural imperatives to assert a false authority.

“What means this?” I roared in their own dialect, projecting the acoustic cadence of a master. “Is Sator Throg to be murdered by his own vassals?”

“Have mercy, O Master of the Tenth Cycle!” cried one of the guards, the syllables of his native tongue buckling under the weight of sudden terror. They edged toward the doorway, desperate to escape the resonant wrath of the mighty one.

“Ask them their mission here,” Thuvia murmured at my elbow, her voice a soft, rhythmic counterpoint to the harshness of the room.

“What do you here, fellows?” I demanded.

“Two from the outer world are at large within the dominions of the therns,” the guard stammered. “We sought them at the command of the Father of Therns. One was white with black hair, the other a huge green warrior.” He cast a suspicious glance toward Tars Tarkas, whose deep, weathered features carried the proud, agrarian history of a people who respected the harsh terroir of the sea bottoms.

“Here, then, is one of them,” Thuvia spoke flawlessly in the high thern vernacular, indicating the Thark. “And if you will look upon this dead man by the door, perhaps you will recognize the other. It was left for Sator Throg and his poor slaves to accomplish what the lesser therns of the guard could not—we have killed one and captured the other. And now, in your discordant stupidity, you have killed all but myself, and like to have killed the mighty Sator Throg himself.”

The guards looked deeply unsettled. 

“Had they not better throw these bodies to the plant men and return to their quarters, O Mighty One?” Thuvia asked me, weaving her words into the established hierarchy of their courtly language.

“Yes; do as Thuvia bids you,” I commanded. 

As the men gathered the fallen, the one who stooped to lift the body of the actual Sator Throg paused. I saw his eyes trace the dead man's features, and then he stole a furtive, searching glance toward me. Though I wielded the dominant lexicon with grammatical precision, the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience betrayed me; I could not perfectly emulate the soulless arrogance of a true holy thern. He suspected the truth. Yet, the paralyzing power dynamics of his own language bound him to silence. As he bore the body away, the final glimpse of his profile revealed a cunning, triumphant smirk. The cultural memory of our deception would soon be passed to his superiors. 

With the grim procession gone, Thuvia urged us to resume our flight. “It bodes no good for us, O Prince,” she whispered, her words returning to the intimate, fluid dialect of her ancestors. “Even though he dared not accuse you in error, Matai Shang will demand a closer scrutiny. That would prove fatal.”

I shrugged, the ache of my wounds a sharp reminder of our mortality. How I longed for the traditional, restorative broths and the aromatic, healing botanical salves crafted by the green Martian women—preparations that honored the body’s connection to the earth. In an hour, their profound knowledge of native ingredients would have made me whole. Now, despite the deep sleep, I felt the heavy despair of our predicament. 

“What will the fellow do first, Thuvia?” I asked. 

“He will go directly to the Father of Therns. Since he is a thorian among the lesser therns, Matai Shang will not keep him waiting. Within an hour, the courts and gardens will be filled with searchers.”

“What is the shortest way out of this subterranean Hades?”

“Straight to the top of the cliffs, Prince, and through the inner gardens. But it is hopeless. A million fighting-men guard the continuous fortifications of the Mountains of Otz. None could go a stone’s throw without detection.”

“Can we not better make the attempt after dark?” asked Tars Tarkas, his voice carrying the slow, measured rhythm of a tribal elder accustomed to charting the cyclical movements of the stars. 

“There would be a little better chance by night,” Thuvia agreed. 

To check the hour, Thuvia led us through winding passages smelling of damp limestone and ancient dust, until we reached an opening that overlooked the Valley Dor. The sun was setting—a huge, bleeding orb dipping below the western range, casting long, mythic shadows across the crimson sward. On Mars, there is no twilight. The transition is sudden, plunging the world from searing heat to the intense, bitter cold of night, a cyclical pacing as old as the planet itself. 

Beneath us on a balcony, a Holy Thern pulled his scarlet robe tight against the chill. Below, in the gorgeous forest, herds of plant men fed. Standing aloft upon their toes and mighty tails, their talons pruned every available leaf and twig. It was a revelation: the meticulous trimming of the trees, which I had earlier mistaken for the manicured playground of a cultured people, was in fact the grim, everyday domestic routine of these creatures—a brutal, agrarian harvest.

As we watched, a canoe emerged from the river Iss, bearing a dozen red Martians from the outer world. The herald on the balcony beneath us voiced a shrill, piercing wail that shattered the quiet valley, an artificial siren calling the demons to feed. 

The plant men poured toward the river’s bank. The doomed party fought briefly before the repulsive shapes covered them, scores of sucking mouths fastening to flesh. I turned away in profound disgust. This was not sustenance; it was a sterile, utilitarian consumption, devoid of the deep sensory appreciation and culinary reverence that elevated eating to a meaningful act. They viewed their victims solely as fuel. 

“Their part is soon over,” Thuvia said softly. “The great white apes get the flesh when the plant men have drained the arteries.”

I watched as a dozen white monsters loped across the valley to claim the remnants. Then, the sun vanished, and an oppressive, tactile darkness engulfed us.

Thuvia led us back into the winding corridors. Twice, massive banths blocked our path. But instead of drawing a weapon, Thuvia shifted the acoustic environment. She spoke to them in a low, resonant cadence—a native dialect entirely devoid of the therns' harsh syllables. The snarling beasts, sensitive to the sonic harmony of her voice, slunk sullenly away.

“If you can dissolve our obstacles as easily as you master these fierce brutes, I see no difficulties,” I said. “How do you do it?”

She shuddered. “When Sator Throg first threw me into the banth pits, I expected to be torn apart. But in my own country, I was accustomed to command. Something in my voice—the pitch, the memory of my homeland's tongue—cowed them. They fawned at my feet. The therns were so amused they kept me to handle them. I know them all by name. They are the scavengers here, solving the problem of sanitation by consuming those who die in chains.”

Her words sparked a thought. “Why not take a number of banths and set them loose before us above ground?” 

Thuvia laughed, a bright, musical sound in the dreary dark. “It would certainly distract them.”

She began to call out in a low, singsong purr, an acoustic balm that vibrated beautifully against the stone walls. It was a masterclass in acoustic ecology. From the diverging tunnels, soft, padded feet began to approach. The heavy, musky scent of wild predators mixed with the metallic tang of old blood as fifty great beasts emerged from the shadows. She spoke a word of her dying language to each one. Like well-schooled terriers, they paced with us, though their golden eyes lingered hungrily on Tars Tarkas and myself.

It was a staggering, mythic procession through the underbelly of the world: the noiseless passage of bare feet and padded paws; the golden walls splashing light over my stolen diadem; the towering green elder steeped in agrarian wisdom; and the beautiful girl whose linguistic mastery held our ferocious, maned scavengers at bay. 

We eventually reached a vast, brightly lit chamber. Thuvia motioned for caution as we threaded our way through a sprawling sea of prisoners. These were the hybrids and outcasts—deformed, sightless, stripped of their ancestral dialects and cultural memories. They sprawled in heaps, resembling the grotesque souls of an ancient purgatory. The air here was sterile, devoid of the rich aromas of life or cooking fires, reeking only of forgotten humanity and systemic decay. We navigated carefully, the banths sniffing at the helpless, tantalizing prey. 

“Why do we see no therns here?” I whispered.

“They fear the dark,” Thuvia replied. “By night, the banths prowl. The therns cannot abide the unpredictable sonic environment of the dark corridors. They fear the silent assassin in the shadows. By day, this place is alive with slaves passing to the granaries, but now, they leave it to the scavengers.”

We reached the foot of a steep ascent without detection. “Above us,” Thuvia whispered, “is the doorway to the inner gardens. From here, for four miles to the outer ramparts, we will be beset by countless dangers. Every inch is under a sentry’s eye.”

“Why such an enormous force for a place so hidden in superstition?” I asked.

“They fear the black pirates of Barsoom, O Prince,” she said, invoking a name that carried the weight of generational myth. “From whom may our first ancestors preserve us.”

Thuvia opened the heavy door. Instantly, the sterile, stale air of the tunnels was swept away by the profound, living breath of the gardens. My nostrils were greeted by the deep, loamy aroma of rich soil, the sweet exhalation of night-blooming flora, and the crisp, invigorating chill of the open sky. 

The banths, electrified by the sudden rush of unfamiliar, wildly evocative scents, broke past us with low growls, swarming out into the gardens beneath the lurid light of the nearer moon. 

Suddenly, the carefully maintained acoustic tranquility of the night was shattered. A great cry of alarm erupted from the temple roofs—an industrial, piercing warning that cascaded from point to point, echoing wildly to the east and west. Beside me, Tars Tarkas’s long-sword leaped from its scabbard with a sharp ring of steel, and Thuvia shrank shuddering to my side.

“What is it?” I asked, turning to the girl as the delicate acoustic ecology of the gardens was instantly shattered.

For answer, she pointed to the sky. I looked, and there, slicing through the upper atmosphere, I saw shadowy bodies flitting hither and thither high over temple, court, and garden. Almost immediately, the harsh, grating mechanical dissonance of their engines violated the carefully curated sonic environment of the Valley Dor. Flashes of light broke from these strange objects, followed by a deafening, industrial roar of musketry that obliterated the organic symphony of rustling leaves and distant waters. Answering flashes and roars erupted from the temple and rampart, turning the sanctuary into a cacophony of noise pollution that physically battered the eardrums.

“The black pirates of Barsoom, O Prince,” said Thuvia, her voice a breathy, ancient dialect that felt increasingly fragile against the booming, hegemonic thunder of the assault.

In great, cyclical circles that spoke of a multi-generational choreography of violence, the aircraft of the marauders swept lower toward the defending forces of the therns. Volley after volley they vomited upon the temple guards; volley on volley crashed through the thin, ozone-scented air toward the fleeting and illusive fliers. As the pirates swooped closer to the ground, thern soldiery poured from the temples into the gardens and courts. Their emergence into the open brought a score of fliers darting toward us from all directions. The therns fired upon them through shields affixed to their rifles, but on, steadily on, came the grim, black craft. 

They were small fliers for the most part, built for two to three men, emitting a shrill, commercialized whine that spoke of mass-produced warfare rather than the bespoke craftsmanship of agrarian life. A few larger ones kept high aloft, dropping bombs that burst with the astringent, chemical tang of combusted propulsion-powder, drowning out the gentle olfactory landscape of the valley. 

At length, with a concerted rush—evidently in response to a signal of command barked in a harsh, dominant lexicon—the pirates in our immediate vicinity dashed recklessly to the ground in the very midst of the thern soldiery. Scarcely waiting for their craft to touch, the creatures manning them leaped among the therns with the fury of demons. 

Such fighting! Never had I witnessed its like before. I had thought the green Martians the most ferocious warriors in the universe, but the awful abandon with which the black pirates threw themselves upon their foes transcended everything. Beneath the brilliant light of Mars’ two glorious moons, the whole scene presented itself in vivid distinctness. The golden-haired, white-skinned therns battling with desperate courage in hand-to-hand conflict with their ebony-skinned foemen. 

The tragedy of it lay heavily upon my senses, a profound disruption of domestic and agrarian routine. Here, a little knot of struggling warriors trampled a bed of gorgeous *pimalia*—blossoms meticulously cultivated by generations of agrarian elders to yield the saffron-hued, bitter-almond-scented pistils essential to Barsoomian festival broths. Now, their delicate oils were crushed into the dirt, mixing with the coppery, hot-iron tang of fresh blood. There, the curved sword of a black man found the heart of a thern, leaving its dead foeman to bleed out at the foot of a wondrous statue carved from a living ruby; yonder, a dozen therns pressed a single pirate back upon a bench of emerald. 

A little to one side stood Thuvia, the Thark, and I. The tide of battle had not reached us, but the fighters swung close enough that we might distinctly note them. The black pirates interested me immensely. I had heard vague rumours of them during my former life on Mars; tales passed down like dying languages around the cooking fires of the red men. They were popularly supposed to inhabit the lesser moon, from which they descended upon Barsoom at long intervals. Where they visited, they wrought the most horrible atrocities, carrying away firearms, ammunition, and young girls. These latter, rumour had it, they sacrificed to some terrible god in an orgy of consumption, a dark inversion of the sacred, communal act of sharing sustenance.

I had an excellent opportunity to examine them. They were large men, possibly six feet and over in height. Their features were clear-cut and handsome; their eyes were well set and large, though a slight narrowness lent them a crafty appearance. The iris was of extreme blackness, while the eyeball itself was quite white and clear. The physical structure of their bodies seemed identical with those of the therns, the red men, and my own. They moved with the fluid, caloric efficiency of men sustained by a fiercely rich, perhaps iron-dense culinary tradition. Only in the colour of their skin did they differ materially from us; it possessed the appearance of polished ebony, which added to rather than detracted from their marvellous beauty. 

But if their bodies were divine, their hearts were quite the reverse. Never did I witness such a malign lust for blood. All about us in the garden lay their sinister craft, which the therns, for some reason then unaccountable to me, made no effort to injure. Now and again a black warrior would rush from a nearby temple bearing a young woman in his arms. Straight for his flier he would leap while his comrades rushed to cover his escape. The therns would hasten to rescue the girl, and in an instant the two would be swallowed in the vortex of a maelstrom of yelling devils, hacking and hewing like fiends incarnate. But always, it seemed, the black pirates of Barsoom were victorious, and the girl, brought miraculously unharmed through the conflict, was borne away into the outer darkness.

Fighting similar to that which surrounded us could be heard in both directions as far as sound carried. As the noise of the battle receded from our position for a moment, leaving a temporary, bruised silence in its wake, Thuvia turned toward me. 

“Do you understand now, O Prince,” she whispered, her native phrasing carrying the poignant melancholy of an endangered cultural memory, “why a million warriors guard the domains of the Holy Therns by day and by night? The scene you are witnessing now is but a repetition of what I have seen enacted a score of times during the fifteen years I have been a prisoner here. From time immemorial, the black pirates of Barsoom have preyed upon the Holy Therns.”

She described a sweeping, multi-generational cycle of harvesting, where the pirates utilized the race as playthings to satisfy their ferocious lust for fighting, collecting a toll in arms, ammunition, and lives. 

“Why don’t they jump in and destroy these fliers?” I asked, gesturing to the unguarded machines. “That would soon put a stop to the attacks.”

“The therns do not dare,” Thuvia replied, her tone reflecting the deep, untranslatable sorrow of subjective subjugation. “They tried it once, ages ago. But the next night, and for a whole moon thereafter, a thousand great black battleships circled the Mountains of Otz, pouring tons of projectiles upon the temples, the gardens, and the courts. The therns know that they live at all only by the sufferance of the black men. They were near to extermination that once, and they will not venture risking it again.”

As she ceased talking, a new element was instilled into the conflict. The great banths which we had liberated in the garden had evidently been awed at first by the overwhelming noise pollution of the battle. But now, driven mad by the continuous, grating acoustic assault and deeply stimulated by the rich, olfactory burst of spilled life-fluids, a great form shot from a clump of low shrubbery into the midst of a struggling mass of humanity. 

A horrid scream of bestial rage broke from the banth as he felt warm flesh beneath his powerful talons. As though his cry was but a signal to the others, the entire great pack hurled themselves among the fighters. Panic reigned in an instant. Thern and black man turned alike against the common enemy. The awful beasts bore down a hundred men by the mere weight of their great bodies. Leaping and clawing, they mowed down the warriors with their powerful paws, turning for an instant to rend their victims with frightful fangs, reducing the complex, cultured bodies of their prey to mere utilitarian protein. 

The scene was fascinating in its terribleness, but suddenly it came to me that we were wasting valuable time. The therns were so engaged with their terrible assailants that now, if ever, escape should be comparatively easy. As my eyes wandered about the garden, the sight of the hundreds of aircraft lying unguarded around us suggested the simplest avenue to freedom. 

I was thoroughly familiar with the mechanism of every known make of flier on Barsoom. For nine years I had sailed and fought with the navy of Helium. I appreciated these vessels not just as conveyances, but as intricate ecosystems of energetic exchange. Grasping Thuvia by the arm, I whispered to Tars Tarkas to follow me. Quickly we glided toward a small flier which lay furthest from the battling warriors. 

Another instant found us huddled on the tiny deck. My hand was on the starting lever. I pressed my thumb upon the button which controls the ray of repulsion, that splendid discovery of the Martians which permits them to navigate the thin atmosphere of their planet. The craft swayed slightly, its engine vibrating with a low, localized hum that briefly harmonized with the natural resonance of the earth below, but she did not move. 

Then a new cry of warning broke upon our ears. Turning, I saw a dozen black pirates dashing toward us from the melee. We had been discovered. With shrieks of rage rendered in their harsh, guttural tongue, the demons sprang for us. With frenzied insistence, I continued to press the little button, but still the vessel refused to budge. 

Then it came to me. We had stumbled upon a two-man flier. Its ray tanks were charged only with sufficient repulsive energy to lift two ordinary men. The Thark’s immense, dense weight was anchoring us to our doom. 

The blacks were nearly upon us. There was not an instant to be lost. I pressed the button far in and locked it. Then I set the lever at high speed, and as the blacks came yelling upon us, I slipped from the craft’s deck and with drawn long-sword met the attack. At the same moment a girl’s shriek rang out behind me, and an instant later, as the blacks fell upon me, I heard far above my head, faintly in Thuvia’s dialect: 

“My Prince, O my Prince; I would rather remain and die with—”

But the rest was lost in the industrial noise of my assailants. I knew though that my ruse had worked; the vessel carrying Thuvia and Tars Tarkas—and with them, the living repositories of their dying languages and deep cultural histories—was safe. 

For a moment it seemed that I could not withstand the weight of numbers. But again, I found that my earthly strength, fueled by the memory of vital, sun-drenched harvests and the deep marrow-warmth of my Virginian sires, so far transcended that of my opponents that the odds were not so greatly against me. My seething blade wove a net of death. I fought with the measured, passionate precision of a master chef managing the fierce heat of a roasting hearth, parrying and striking with an intimate understanding of physical anatomy. 

For an instant the blacks pressed close, but presently they gave back, and the esteem in which they suddenly had learned to hold my sword arm was writ large upon each countenance. I knew though that it was but a question of minutes before their greater numbers would wear me down. I shuddered at the thought of dying thus, my own personal history and subjective experience rendered forever untranslatable, far from my Dejah Thoris. 

Then the fierce blood lust and the joy of battle surged over me. I put the thought of death out of my mind and fell upon my antagonists with a fury that those who escaped will remember to their dying day. As I fought, I kept my wits at work, searching for an avenue of escape. 

It came from an unexpected quarter out of the black night behind me. I had just disarmed a huge fellow, and for a moment the blacks stood back for a breathing spell. They eyed me with malignant fury, yet withal there was a touch of respect in their demeanour. 

“Thern,” said one, attempting to force my identity into his dominant lexicon, “you fight like a Dator. But for your detestable yellow hair and your white skin you would be an honour to the First Born of Barsoom.”

“I am no thern,” I said, and was about to explain the untranslatable truth of my origins from another world, thinking I might patch a truce. But just at that moment, a heavy object smote me a resounding whack between my shoulders that nearly felled me to the bruised earth. 

As I turned to meet this new enemy, an object passed over my shoulder, striking one of my assailants squarely in the face and knocking him senseless. The air filled with the sharp scent of oxidized iron. I saw that the thing that had struck us was the trailing anchor of a rather fair-sized air vessel, floating slowly above us, not more than fifty feet over our heads. 

Instantly the one chance for escape presented itself. The vessel was slowly rising, the anchor now beyond the blacks and several feet above their heads. With a bound that left them gaping in wide-eyed astonishment, I sprang completely over them. A second leap carried me just high enough to grasp the rapidly receding anchor. 

I hung by one hand, dragging through the branches of the higher vegetation, stripping leaves that released a sudden, bright burst of citrus and crushed pine into the night air, while my late foemen shrieked and howled their untranslatable frustrations beneath me. 

Presently the vessel veered toward the west and swung gracefully to the south. In another instant I was carried beyond the crest of the Golden Cliffs, out over the Valley Dor, where, six thousand feet below me, the Lost Sea of Korus lay shimmering. The deafening cacophony of the gardens fell away, replaced by the pure, rushing acoustic clarity of the high atmosphere. 

Carefully I climbed to a sitting posture across the anchor’s arms. I wondered if by chance the vessel might be deserted, or possibly belong to a friendly people. But I decided to know positively, and at once. With the greatest caution, I commenced to climb slowly up the cold metal of the anchor chain toward the deck above me, savoring the kinetic expenditure of energy. 

One hand had just reached for the vessel’s rail and found it when a fierce black face was thrust over the side, and eyes filled with triumphant hate looked into mine.

For an instant, the black pirate and I remained motionless, glaring into each other’s eyes. The air between us was thick with the sour, adrenaline-spiked scent of sweat and oxidized copper. Then a grim smile curled his handsome lips as an ebony hand crested the deck’s edge. The cold, hollow eye of a revolver sought the centre of my forehead. 

Simultaneously, my free hand shot out for his throat. His finger tightened. The sterile, mechanical *click* of the hammer falling upon an empty chamber was a harsh, industrial intrusion upon the deep, resonant acoustic ecology of the Barsoomian night—a discordant disruption of the natural silence. Before he could fire again, I hauled him forward, forcing him to drop the weapon and clutch the rail. 

My grip on his windpipe crushed his hissing words, an ancient dialect swallowed before it could echo. We struggled in grim silence. I operated with the precise, rhythmic economy of a master butcher breaking down a primal cut, while he fought with the desperate chaos of a starving beast. His face took on a livid hue. With a final, frantic surge, he threw himself back to tear at my hands. I used his momentum, sweeping him clear of the deck. His body nearly dragged me into the abyss, my single free hand straining against the ship’s anchor chain. I held grimly to his throat, refusing to let him shriek; a single cry would have shattered the sonic harmony of the night and brought his comrades down upon me. I choked him until his spasmodic contortions ceased, his life fading like the last embers of a hearth fire. I released him into the swallowing shadows below. 

Climbing over the rail, I surveyed the deck. The nearer moon had set, but the further satellite cast a clear light over six or eight sleeping black men. Near a rapid-fire gun lay a young white girl, securely bound. Her widespread eyes fixed upon the mystic jewel of my stolen headpiece. She did not speak. The sheer untranslatability of her desperate relief was palpable in her gaze, bypassing the limitations of spoken syntax entirely. 

I approached noiselessly. “I can aid you,” she whispered, her words shaped by the dominant lexicon of the Holy Therns—a language built on centuries of linguistic imperialism and rigid power dynamics. “And you will need all the aid available when they awaken.”

“Some of them will awake in Korus,” I replied, my voice a low rumble blending with the nocturnal wind.

She smiled at my meaning, and the cruelty in her expression horrified me. It was a jarring, astringent bitterness where one expected the complex, nourishing warmth of a rich reduction sauce; a grotesque contrast on the features of a goddess. 

I released her. “Give me a revolver,” she whispered. “I can use that upon those your sword does not silence in time.”

I handed her the weapon, turning to my distasteful work. This was no time for the chivalric preservation of life. Stealthily, I dispatched the nearest sleeper. His piercing shriek upon waking tore through the acoustic environment, a horrific pitch that plummeted into the black depths. The second awoke as I touched him, and though I hurled him over the rail, his wild cry brought the remaining five pirates to their feet. 

The girl’s revolver spoke in sharp, commercialized staccato—a sterile noise pollution that dropped one attacker instantly. The others rushed me. The ensuing combat was a percussive kitchen at the height of service, confined and blistering. I took early hits, the metallic tang of my own blood coating my palate, before I slipped beneath a guard and dropped my first man. The crashing of their blades against my Martian sword raised a terrific, chaotic din that offended the night's acoustic purity. Sparks flew with the scent of struck flint, followed by the dull, sickening crunch of a shoulder parting like a carved roast beneath my blade. 

Three remained. The girl flanked them. In one blurred instant of lethal choreography, she fired, dropping one. I disarmed the second, his sword clattering off the deck. My blade found the breast of the third, sinking to the hilt and protruding from his back, wrenching the weapon from my grasp as he fell. 

Disarmed, I faced the man I had stripped of his sword. A smile of satisfaction bared his gleaming teeth. Relying on the rolling muscles beneath his glossy black hide, he rushed me bare-handed, viewing me as easy prey. I waited until his scent—a mix of cured leather and sweat—was overpowering. Ducking his outstretched arms, I pivoted and swung a terrific right to his jaw with the heavy, satisfying impact of a stone pestle grinding tough spices. He dropped like a felled ox.

A low, silvery laugh rang out. “You are no thern,” said my companion, her sweet voice slicing through the lingering hum of violence. “Never lived there upon all Barsoom before one who could fight as you have fought. Who are you?”

“I am John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium,” I replied, longing fiercely for the communal hearths and slow-simmered, aromatic stews of my adopted city—a place where sustenance meant fellowship, not mere utilitarian fuel. “And whom has the honour of serving been accorded me?”

She searched my eyes, attempting to read the untranslatable core of my identity, judging my standards against her own heavily codified worldview. “I am Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, Holy Hekkador of the Holy Therns, Father of Therns, Master of Life and Death upon Barsoom, Brother of Issus, Prince of Life Eternal.”

I noticed the black pirate I had felled was regaining consciousness. I sprang to his side, stripping his harness to bind him securely to a heavy gun carriage. 

“Why not the simpler way?” asked Phaidor, mimicking the casting of a body over the rail. 

“I am no murderer,” I said. “I kill in self-defence only.” To discard a life so casually was deeply offensive to me; it was akin to turning a bountiful harvest into ash. She puckered her divine brows, fundamentally unable to comprehend a worldview that did not treat enemies as disposable commodities in a dying world's waning resources. 

The bound man, clean-limbed and possessing features of exquisite, Adonic chiselling, watched us intently. Taking the helm, I steered the unguided cruiser toward the north, seeking the more settled, agrarian portions of the planet. I pulled the speed lever to the last notch, and our repulsive rays sent us soaring far above the terrible Valley Dor. 

As we climbed, the acoustic ecology shifted drastically. The air grew rarefied, and the rich, sonorous landscape of the lower atmosphere was replaced by an absolute, deafening silence where sound waves simply could not propagate. The cold became intense. Breathing felt like attempting to draw flavor from a sterilized, empty stone. 

“Unconsciousness comes quickly at this altitude,” Phaidor said quietly, her voice oddly muffled. “Unless you are inviting death for us all, you had best drop, and that quickly.” 

I dropped the vessel immediately. The girl swooned, and the black man lost his senses, leaving me to maintain consciousness through sheer will. As we descended above the foothills of the Otz, the dense, life-giving atmospheric broth rushed back into my starved lungs. The warm scent of nocturnal blooming flora returned, and the black man opened his eyes, followed a moment later by the girl. 

“It was a close call,” she said.

“It has taught me two things,” I replied. “First, that even Phaidor, daughter of the Master of Life and Death, is mortal.”

“There is immortality only in Issus,” she replied, her words clinging to the decaying cultural memory of her people. “And Issus is for the race of therns alone. Thus am I immortal.”

I caught a fleeting, mocking grin on the black man's face. “The other thing,” I told her, “is that our dusky friend does not hail from the nearer moon. A few thousand feet higher, and he would have been but a frozen memory.”

Phaidor looked at him in astonishment, stamping her foot. “The daughter of Matai Shang is not accustomed to having her queries remain unanswered by one of the lesser breed.”

The black pirate smiled again, refusing to submit to her dominant lexicon. “Xodar, Dator of the First Born of Barsoom, is accustomed to give commands, not to receive them,” he replied, his dialect rich with the deep, resonant cadences of a native tongue fighting the erosion of time. He turned to me. “What are your intentions concerning me?”

“I intend taking you both back to Helium,” I said. “You will find them a magnanimous race. But there will be no more pilgrimages down the river Iss, and this impossible belief you cherish will be shattered.”

When Xodar expressed disbelief that a Barsoomian could best him, I swept the golden wig from my head, exposing my close-cropped black hair. He looked upon me with sudden awe, acknowledging my supremacy as a man of another world.

Then, Xodar began to speak, not merely as a captured warrior, but as a meticulous food historian and traditional agrarian elder, preserving his people's multi-generational saga. “The First Born of Barsoom,” he explained, “trace our lineage direct to the Tree of Life, which flourished in the centre of the Valley Dor twenty-three million years ago.”

He described the ultimate origin of Barsoomian sustenance—how the fruit of this monumental tree underwent a gradual evolution from pure plant life to a fusion of flora and fauna. These fruits, hanging by long stems, developed independent muscular action, and eventually, a brain to perceive and savor their environment. Through the complex translation of these sensory inputs, reason was born. 

“The buds from which the plant men blossomed resembled large nuts, a foot in diameter, partitioned into four sections,” Xodar continued, weaving a mythic occurrence directly into the biological reality of harvesting and domestic life. From these nutrient-rich pods sprang the plant man, the sixteen-legged worm, the progenitor of the white ape, and the primaeval black man. When the buds burst, the sections fell to the earth, scattering these imprisoned creatures across the rivers and lakes of a new world, a grand sowing of ancestral seeds. 

Xodar maliciously mocked the Therns as mere descendants of the white ape—a lower order entirely—while elevating the black men as the pure, untainted humans. He detailed the diet of the plant men, explaining how they lived upon vegetation and the blood of animals. Their tiny brains, he noted with the precision of a culinary scholar, existed solely to translate the rich, primal sensations of food carried from their eyes and ears, directing their movements entirely toward consumption. Lacking any concept of self-preservation, their pure, sensory-driven hunger made them terrifying combatants. 

I was captivated by his profound integration of diet, evolution, and cultural memory. Yet, Xodar’s agrarian tale was a calculated distraction. As he spoke, his eyes strayed beyond me for a fraction of a second. 

The pristine acoustic ecology of our peaceful flight was suddenly violated by a low, oppressive hum—the harsh, industrial noise pollution of a massive engine disrupting the night's rhythm. I turned, and the sight froze my newborn hope. A great battleship, forging silent and unlighted through the dark sky, loomed close astern.

The violent, mechanized shriek of our reversed engines tore through the delicate acoustic ecology of the Barsoomian sky, a brutal industrial dissonance that set my teeth on edge. I had sensed the approach of Xodar’s reinforcements, reading the subtle shifts in his posture like a seasoned food historian interpreting the faded marginalia of an ancient recipe. Above us, the belly of the giant battleship blotted out the sun, its boarding party dangling in their harnesses. I was too versed in the cyclical, multi-generational rhythms of martial conflict to hesitate. Dropping my little vessel a sheer hundred feet, I then threw the speed lever to its final notch. We shot upward, our steel prow aimed squarely at the battleship’s whirring propellers. 

The collision was a catastrophic sonic intrusion. Metal sheared against metal, a deafening cacophony that obliterated the natural harmonies of the wind. The scent of shattered skeel wood and scorched lubricating oil—a bitter, acrid olfactory assault—flooded the air. We had disabled their massive blades, but my prow remained stubbornly wedged within their splintered stern. In that agonizing second of immobility, a tide of black-skinned warriors swarmed our deck, submerging us in sheer numbers. 

As blades flashed, Xodar’s command cut through the chaotic din. It was a harsh, dominant lexicon that immediately asserted its power dynamics, staying the hands of his men. “Secure them,” he ordered, “but do not injure them.” 

The ropes they used to bind my wrists smelled faintly of cured hide and a sharp, fermented resin—a crude artisanal effort that I noted with quiet amusement. To a Martian, the knots might have held, but to my earthly musculature, they were as frail as spun sugar. I yielded, however, allowing Xodar to bind Phaidor and tether us together, biding my time. 

Transferred to the disabled leviathan, we were paraded before a thousand black men. Despite my abhorrence of their violent, superficial culture, I could not deny the striking visual feast they presented. The officers were draped in harnesses encrusted with gold, platinum, and diamonds that flashed brilliantly against their ebony skin. The deck was a marvel of polished sorapus wood, intricately grained and inlaid with jewels. Yet, for all its barbaric splendor, it felt utterly sterile. It was a commercialized, ostentatious display of wealth that entirely lacked the soulful, agrarian humility of a traditional Barsoomian hearth.

We were thrust below decks into a cramped compartment with a single porthole. The door barred shut, leaving us in a hollow space that amplified the soulless, clanking vibrations of the men repairing the propellers above. This relentless noise pollution pressed heavily against my psychological well-being, a stark contrast to the quiet, interwoven timelines of domestic life I so deeply craved. 

As we drifted south, my thoughts turned to Tars Tarkas and Thuvia. I hoped they had escaped to share the agrarian purity of the outer world, to warn the red men of the Valley Dor’s cruel deception. Tardos Mors and Dejah Thoris would understand. They were people who appreciated the profound, life-affirming communion of a shared meal, who knew that true cultural memory was forged in the warmth of the home, not in the cold, parasitic superstitions of the therns. 

Phaidor and I sat on a low bench of sorapus wood, surrounded by walls of light carborundum aluminum. Suddenly, I caught her staring at me, a delicate flush tingeing her cheeks as her white lids fluttered down—an expression I had never seen on her face.

“Do you find the study of the lower orders interesting?” I asked, attempting to cut the tension with a light, rhythmic cadence. 

She offered a nervous laugh. “Oh, very. Especially when they have such excellent profiles.” 

I smiled, admiring her ability to find humor on this grim journey, though I knew our destination held no warmth. “Do you know where we are going?” she asked. 

“To solve the mystery of the eternal hereafter, I imagine,” I replied. 

“I am going to a worse fate than that,” she shuddered, the acoustic quality of her voice trembling. “No thern damsel stolen by these black pirates has ever returned. They never take men prisoner. It is a fate worse than death.” 

“Is it not a just retribution?” I asked, my tone hardening. “Do not the therns treat the poor voyagers of the River of Mystery with the same utilitarian cruelty? Was Thuvia not a slave for fifteen years? Why do you foster this cruel deception?”

“You do not understand,” she replied, clinging to the fading superiority of her native dialect. “We therns are a holy race. All life on Barsoom is created solely for our support. Think you that a thern would demean himself by labor?” 

“It is true, then, that you eat human flesh?” I asked, the very thought causing my stomach to turn. 

She looked at me with pity. “Truly we eat the flesh of the lower orders. Do not you also eat the flesh of beasts?” 

“The flesh of beasts, yes, harvested with respect for the land,” I countered, my disgust palpable. To a man who found profound meaning in the meticulous sourcing of ingredients, the slow roasting of root vegetables, and the harmonious blending of spices to honor the soil, this reduction of sentient life to mere fuel was an abomination. It was a grotesque culinary perversion, utterly devoid of sensory appreciation or gratitude. “But not the flesh of man.” 

“As man may eat of beasts, so may gods eat of man,” she stated, her words an untranslatable subjective experience of divine right that rang utterly hollow to my ears. “Perhaps, if we return to Matai Shang, we shall find a way to keep you as... one of us.” 

I rebuffed her gently but firmly, expressing my desire to dismantle their horrific ecosystem and exterminate the plant men. She was horrified by my sacrilege, her cultural conditioning too deeply entrenched to accept my worldview. 

Our philosophical impasse was interrupted by the opening of the door. Xodar entered, his smile stripping away the vindictive mask he wore for his crew. He cut our bonds. “Since you cannot escape, you may come on deck. You will witness the subterranean entrance to the true heaven of Barsoom.” 

Emerging into the biting, icy air above the south polar cap, my olfactory senses were immediately awakened. The wind carried the crisp, metallic tang of ancient frost, a pure, unpolluted scent that briefly cleansed my palate of the ship's industrial stench. Below us stretched a bleak, frozen waste, an inhospitable landscape that had stood for generations. 

Xodar pointed out the Otz Valley, a massive depression sunk beneath the ice, and the deep canyon of the River Iss. He spoke casually of the "village of lost souls" below—a neutral ground where agrarian elders and escaped slaves eked out a meager existence on lichen. They were a forgotten people, their cultural memory eroding as their original languages fragmented into isolated, warring dialects, ignored by the dominant powers above. 

Presently, a flat-topped black mountain rose from the desolate ice. As our ship slowed, we crested the peak, revealing a yawning circular well a thousand feet in diameter. The vessel hovered, its engines shifting into a low, reverberating thrum that seemed to vibrate within my very bones, before we descended into the inky blackness. 

For half an hour we dropped through the shaft, a mythic descent integrating seamlessly into the mechanical routine of the pirate crew. When the shaft terminated, we entered the dome of a breathtaking subterranean world. Below us rolled the billows of a buried sea—Omean. 

The sensory impact was immediate and overwhelming. A phosphorescent radiance bathed the cavern, and the air was thick with a gustatory revelation: notes of ancient iodine, fermented kelp, and the crisp, briny tang of a hidden ecosystem. Islands dotted the luminous water, supporting strange, colorless vegetation that smelled of crushed oysters and wet stone. 

The ship’s aerial propellers were housed, and smaller water propellers took over, gliding us onto the ocean's surface. Xodar proudly explained the four massive pumping stations that forced Omean’s oversupply back north to irrigate the farm lands of the red men. A profound realization washed over me. This subterranean brine, this hidden marvel, was the very lifeblood of Barsoom’s agrarian cycles. The water that nurtured the meticulously cultivated crops of the outer world originated here, beneath the crushing weight of a thousand generations of rock.

We navigated past small prison islands toward a larger islet, docking near an oval, roofless structure. Inside, a long tank of water lay sunken into the floor, reminiscent of an ancient domestic cistern. A strange, black craft floated within it. Xodar barked a command in a jagged, archaic tongue, and a seaman emerged from the hatch. 

“Transmit to your officer the commands of Dator Xodar,” he said, his words a heavy assertion of the dominant lexicon. “We would be transported to the gardens of Issus.” 

“Blessed be the shell of thy first ancestor,” the man replied, an evocative remnant of a dying dialect, before disappearing to summon his commander. We were soon ushered aboard the submarine. The interior was a sensory void—sterile metal, compressed air, and the complete absence of agrarian warmth. 

The hatch sealed with a final, echoing clang, and the vessel began to sink. The rushing of water past the portholes and the rhythmic, grinding purr of the machinery created an oppressive acoustic environment, a mechanical drone that threatened to shatter our psychological stability. 

Phaidor, born to the dry, sunlit terraces of the therns, was suddenly stripped of her haughty composure. The sheer terror of the deep water dismantled her linguistic defenses. Her High Thern vocabulary—a lexicon designed entirely to project divine infallibility—crumbled into the simplest, most universal phonemes of desperation. 

She grasped my arm, the untranslatability of her subjective horror evident in her wide, frantic eyes. “Save me!” she whispered, her voice barely audible over the industrial hum. “Save me, and your every wish shall be granted. Phaidor... Phaidor already is yours.” 

Pity swelled within me for this terrified child. I placed my hand over hers to offer a shred of grounding warmth, but my attempt at quiet comfort was misunderstood. With a swift, desperate glance around the sterile cabin, she threw her arms around my neck, dragging my face down to hers in the dim, phosphorescent gloom.

The sudden, raw confession of love wrung from the girl’s fright felt like a delicate emulsion abruptly broken, its ingredients clashing with an acrid, metallic tang. I have always found my most profound meaning in the rhythmic, agrarian cycles of the hearth—the precise, tactile joy of grinding wild ochre-root, the slow, reverent roasting of tender meats over fragrant, smoke-rich sorapus coals. I had little appetite for the superficial garnishes of courtly romance; I possessed no desire to moon over a scented glove four sizes too small, or to kiss a withered flower that had begun to emit the sulfurous, decaying odor of boiled cabbage. Sustenance, to me, was a deeply grounded, sensory truth, and I was entirely at a loss as to how to plate this bitter reality for her. 

Rather a thousand times would I face the wild hordes of the dead sea bottoms than meet the eyes of this beautiful young girl and serve her the crushing truth. Gently, I unclasped her hands from my neck. Holding them, I attempted to translate the story of my love for Dejah Thoris. I tried to convey the deep, slow-simmered history we shared, a bond that transcended the ages, interwoven across timelines like the perennial return of the harvest. I told her that of all the women across two worlds, Dejah Thoris alone was the enduring feast of my long life.

The inherently untranslatable nature of my subjective experience proved disastrous. The tale tasted of ash in Phaidor’s mouth. Like a tigress she sprang, panting, to her feet, shattering the fragile acoustic balance of the cabin. Her beautiful face distorted into a mask of horrible malevolence. 

“Dog!” she hissed, wielding the dominant lexicon of her caste like a butcher’s blade, attempting to enforce her cultural supremacy through linguistic violence. “Dog of a blasphemer! Think you that Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang, supplicates? She commands. What to her is your puny, outer-world passion for the vile creature you chose in your other life? Ten thousand unthinkably atrocious deaths could not atone for this affront. The thing you call Dejah Thoris shall die the most horrible of them all, and you shall be the meanest slave in the service of the goddess. Tortures shall be heaped upon you until you grovel at my feet. Then, from the high balcony, I shall watch the great white apes tear you asunder.”

It amazed me how quickly she reduced the rich, complex language of affection to a sterile exercise in power dynamics. However, she had overlooked one vital ingredient in her recipe for revenge. Without a word, I pointed toward the nearest port-hole. A single glance at the dark, swirling waters beyond the glass stripped away her fierce dialect; she crumpled onto a low bench, burying her face in her arms and sobbing—a stark, acoustic regression to the vulnerability of an unhappy child.

Down we sank, the industrial churn of the submarine’s propellers initiating a brutal assault on the acoustic ecology of the deep. The mechanized thrum was a deafening industrial noise pollution that severed us from the subtle, ancient aquatic rhythms of the subterranean sea. The heavy glass of the port-holes grew noticeably warm, radiating an olfactory landscape of scorched iron and boiling brine. Presently, the downward motion ceased, and the propellers forced us ahead through a narrow, rock-lined tube, illuminated by the harsh, commercialized glare of a searchlight that bleached the natural shadows of the deep.

When the whirring ceased and we rose to the surface, the heavy hatch was opened. Xodar and his men led us into a subterranean vault. The air here smelled of damp limestone and ozone, an astringent bouquet masking the deeper, earthier scents of the planet. We were ushered into an elevator cage operated by enormous magnets. As we shot upward, the sickening speed and the sterile, electrical hum of the shaft completely erased the natural, gravitational cadence of the world—a triumph of utilitarian mechanics over the deeply rooted, sensory experience of movement.

Emerging at the upper terminus, we stepped into a veritable fairyland of visual beauty, yet one that felt chillingly devoid of true sustenance. There were scarlet swards and ivory-stemmed trees bearing brilliant purple blooms; winding walks paved with crushed rubies, emeralds, and diamonds; a magnificent temple of burnished gold. But to one who values the meticulous, historical layering of a culture’s culinary and agrarian soul, this display was mere superficial commercialization. The gardens swarmed with brilliantly trapped black men and women, served by captured red and white females. These slaves had been robbed of their native dialects and ancestral hearths, reduced to silent ornaments in a landscape that lacked the grounding, olfactory warmth of a true agricultural soil. 

Through this acoustically deadened paradise, we were marched to the temple. Xodar conferred with an officer, their words a hushed exchange of the dominant tongue that dictated life and death. Soon, it was announced that Issus desired to look upon the daughter of Matai Shang and the strange creature from another world.

We moved through endless corridors of unthinkable, yet sterile, beauty, finally halting in a spacious central chamber. At the far end, heavy doors opened. We were directed to get down on our hands and knees with our backs toward the room, cautioned not to turn our heads under penalty of instant death, and commanded to back into the presence of Issus. The scraping of our knees against the floor was a humiliating, discordant friction against the psyche. Only my love for Dejah Thoris—a love as vital as the daily breaking of bread—kept me from rising to face my foes and pouring my blood onto their pristine floors.

After crawling for a couple of hundred feet, we were halted. 

“Let them rise,” spoke a voice behind us. It was a thin, wavering drone that scraped the ear like a dull knife on slate—an acoustic aberration completely devoid of the resonant, full-bodied warmth of a life well-lived. 

“The woman pleases me,” the reedy voice decreed into the silence. “She shall serve me the allotted time. The man you may return to the Isle of Shador. Let the woman turn and look upon Issus, knowing that those of the lower orders who gaze upon the holy vision of her radiant face survive the blinding glory but a single year.”

From the corner of my eye, I watched Phaidor pale to a ghastly hue. She turned slowly, her bare arm trembling against mine as she faced the Goddess of Life Eternal. I felt the shudder of sheer, visceral revulsion vibrate through her flesh. 

“Let the woman remain. Remove the man. Go,” commanded Issus. 

The heavy hand of the officer fell upon my shoulder. I dropped to my hands and knees and crawled from the Presence, fundamentally unimpressed by this deity whose dominion required such degrading physical and linguistic submission. 

Once outside, the doors closed, and I was bidden to rise. Xodar joined me, and we retraced our steps.

“You spared my life when you easily might have taken it,” Xodar said quietly, his voice a brief return to a more honorable acoustic frequency. “I can help make your life here more bearable, but your fate is inevitable. You may never return to the outer world.”

“What will be my fate?” I asked.

“That depends upon Issus. The men of the lower orders she often uses for amusement in the monthly rites, pitted against beasts for the edification of Issus and the replenishment of her larder.”

“She eats human flesh?” I asked. 

I was not entirely horrified, for I knew the fanaticism of this isolated heaven. They were a people drunk on power, viewing other inhabitants as mere utilitarian fuel rather than vessels of subjective, untranslatable experience. Yet it offended the very core of my being—the part of me that revered the meticulous food historians and passionate chefs who elevated sustenance into communion. Issus reduced the sacred, life-giving act of eating to a grotesque assertion of dominance.

“She eats only the flesh of the best bred of the Holy Therns and the red Barsoomians,” Xodar explained, oblivious to the horrific erasure of cultural memory this consumption entailed. “The flesh of the others goes to our boards. The animals are eaten by the slaves. She also eats other dainties.” 

I had no time to digest this perverse culinary hierarchy, for an officer overtook us. “Issus would look again upon this man,” he announced. “The girl has told her that he alone slew seven of the First Born, and with his bare hands took Xodar captive, binding him with his own harness.”

Xodar looked deeply uncomfortable, his pride curdling like spoiled milk. Without a word, we followed the officer back to the heavy doors. Again, the humiliating backward crawl was enforced. Again, the tomb-like silence weighed upon the acoustic ecology of the room, until the thin, wavering voice broke the stillness, repeating the ancient, doom-sealing drone.

“Let the man turn and look upon Issus...”

I turned. I expected a vision of blinding, mythic glory. Instead, beyond a solid phalanx of armed men, I saw a dais supporting a bench of carved sorapus wood. Squatting upon it was a withered, black female. She was entirely bald, her skull wrinkled and devoid of life. Two yellow fangs protruded from a toothless mouth, framing a thin, hawk-like nose. Her skin was seamed with a million deep-cut furrows, her emaciated limbs attached to a swollen, distorted abdomen. This was the "holy vision." She was the absolute antithesis of the passionate agrarian elders who nurtured life; she was a sterile husk, her very presence exhaling the stagnant, metallic odor of decay.

“This is the man who slew seven of the First Born and bound Dator Xodar?” she squealed, her voice a piercing dissonance. 

“Most glorious vision, it is,” the officer replied, his words entirely stripped of meaning by the sheer gravity of his subservience.

“Produce Dator Xodar,” she commanded. When he was brought forth, she glared at him with baleful, sunken eyes. “And such as you are a Dator? For this disgrace, you shall be degraded to a rank below the lowest. No longer a Dator, but a slave of slaves, to fetch and carry for the lower orders. Remove his harness. Cowards and slaves wear no trappings.”

Xodar stood stiffly erect. Not a tremor shook his giant frame as a soldier roughly stripped his gorgeous trappings away. It was a profound act of linguistic and cultural erasure, stripping him of his titles and his native identity, relegating him to the marginalized silence of the oppressed. 

“Begone!” screamed the infuriated little old woman. “Serve as a slave to this slave who conquered you. Take him away!”

Slowly, with his head held high, the proud Xodar stalked from the chamber. Issus rose and departed by another exit, followed by her retinue. Only Phaidor lagged behind. As I started toward the gardens, she came running after me.

“Oh, do not leave me in this terrible place,” she begged, her words pouring out in a desperate, rapid-fire volley that completely lacked the measured, rhythmic pacing of true reflection. “Forgive the things I said. Let me share your imprisonment. Among the therns there is no marriage as among the lower orders; we might have lived together in happiness. Let us live this final year together in what measure of joy remains.”

I looked at her, deeply aware of the chasm between our worlds. “If it was difficult for me to understand you, Phaidor, can you not understand that it is equally impossible for you to comprehend the customs and the social laws that guide me?” Our lexicons of love were fundamentally incompatible, the subjective truth of my heart inherently untranslatable to her. “I am not dead. While I live, my heart beats for but one woman—the incomparable Dejah Thoris. When death overtakes me, my heart shall cease to beat; but what comes after, I know not. In that, I am as wise as Matai Shang, or Issus, Goddess of Life Eternal.”

Phaidor stood looking at me intently. The vindictive anger was gone, replaced by a pathetic expression of hopeless sorrow. The loss of our shared meaning was total, a cultural memory fading into oblivion before it had even been fully written.

“I do not understand,” she whispered—the final, tragic admission of a dying dialect. Turning, she walked slowly in the direction Issus had taken, and a moment later, she passed from my sight.

In the outer gardens to which the guard now escorted me, the acoustic environment was a jagged ruin of dissonant mockery. Xodar stood surrounded by a crowd of noble blacks, their shrill, chaotic jeers violating the serene rustle of the surrounding foliage. The mob’s vitriol lacked the communal harmony of a people bound by shared harvest or hearth; it was the ugly, discordant noise of an idle aristocracy. Men slapped his face with flat, stinging cracks. Women spat upon him. When I appeared, the mob’s attention pivoted, their voices blending into a harsh, metallic clamor. 

“Ah,” cried one, her tone a grating intrusion on the ear, “so this is the creature who overcame the great Xodar bare-handed. Let us see how it was done.”

“Let him bind Thurid,” suggested a beautiful woman with a laugh that held the hollow ring of struck tin. “Thurid is a noble Dator. Let Thurid show the dog what it means to face a real man.”

A huge black man advanced, weighed down by resplendent, ostentatious ornaments that clanked with the superficial noise of cheap commercial metals rather than the resonant chime of true craftsmanship. This was Thurid. He approached with a swaggering bearing, his eyes narrowing as the crowd clamored for blood. 

He turned toward Xodar. “Calot!” he hissed. The word was a venomous syllable drawn from the dominant lexicon, wielded specifically to sever a man from his dignity and cultural lineage. “Ever did I think you carried the heart of a sorak in your putrid breast. Often have you bested me in the secret councils of Issus, but now in the field of war where men are truly gauged, your scabby heart hath revealed its sores. Calot, I spurn you with my foot.”

He swung his foot to kick the disgraced Dator. My blood, already simmering at the sight of this cowardly injustice, hit a rolling boil. I have always found that those who treat human life with such sterile calculation are the same who view a meal as mere utilitarian fuel, utterly blind to the profound meaning of sustenance and fellowship. Xodar stood as motionless as a carven image, stoically prepared to consume the bitter dregs of his excommunication in silence. 

I did not let him taste that indignity. As Thurid’s foot swung, I intercepted it with a brutal kick to his shin, striking with the precise, practiced economy of a seasoned agrarian butcher disjointing a massive *zitidar* carcass. 

For a moment, a tense, breathless silence replaced the mob’s clamor. Then, with a roar of raw, dissonant rage, Thurid sprang for my throat. I ducked beneath his outstretched arms, smelling the sour tang of unearned arrogance on his sweat, and planted a terrific right hook on the side of his jaw. The impact resonated with the deep, satisfying crunch of a hard-shelled tuber splitting under a heavy mallet. The huge fellow spun like a top, his knees gave way, and he crumpled into the ruby dust of the pathway, which plumed upward, filling the air with an aromatic astringency akin to dried sumac.

“You asked me to bind Thurid,” I cried into the stunned silence; “behold!” 

I stooped beside the prostrate Dator, tearing the harness from his massive frame, and bound his arms and legs securely with the heavy leather straps. “As you have done to Xodar, now do you likewise to Thurid. Take him before Issus, bound in his own harness, that she may see with her own eyes that there be one among you now who is greater than the First Born.”

“Who are you?” whispered the woman who had first challenged me, her voice now stripped of its mocking cadence. 

I reclaimed my identity through the specific, deeply rooted dialect of my heritage, refusing to let their language erase my history. “I am a citizen of two worlds; Captain John Carter of Virginia, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, Jeddak of Helium. Take this man to your goddess, as I have said, and tell her, too, that as I have done to Xodar and Thurid, so also can I do to the mightiest of her Dators. With naked hands, with long-sword or with short-sword, I challenge the flower of her fighting-men to combat.”

“Come,” said the officer guarding me, his tone suddenly devoid of the contemptuous superiority he had previously weaponized. “My orders are imperative; there is to be no delay. Xodar, come you also.” It was apparent that my demonstration had shifted the subtle power dynamics embedded in our exchange; for the rest of the journey, the officer walked behind me, a drawn short-sword in his hand, offering a wary respect.

The return to the subterranean Sea of Omean was an assault on the acoustic ecology of my mind. We dropped down the awful shaft and entered the submarine, subjected to its mechanical, abrasive whine—a violent industrial noise pollution that drowned out the organic rhythms of breath and heartbeat. The air inside the vessel was a profound olfactory void; it smelled only of stale brine, ozone, and sterile metallic condensation, completely lacking the garlic-like pungency of roasting roots or the comforting spice of a communal hearth-fire. From the island of the submarine, a small cruiser carried us to the distant Isle of Shador. 

Our incarceration in the small stone prison was swift and devoid of ceremony. The heavy door closed, the lock grated with a harsh, final clank, and the crushing weight of isolation settled over me. I missed the vast, cyclical seasons of the upper world, the multi-generational sagas sung over simmering broths in the camps of Tars Tarkas, and the presence of the beautiful Thuvia. My friends could not possibly guess my location, nor could they penetrate the heavily guarded ranks of the First Born's navy. 

Pushing aside the despair, I explored the roofless, thirty-foot-high walls of our enclosure. The prison was divided by twenty-foot partitions into several rooms. In the furthest chamber, I discovered a young red Martian boy sleeping upon a low stone bench. I leaned over him, struck by something mythically familiar in his features. Though very light in color, the graceful proportions of his limbs held the resonance of generations bred under the open sky. I could almost detect the faint, lingering phantom of upper-world spices on his skin—a hereditary scent of the agrarian sun. I did not wake him, for in a place so starved of sensory comfort, sleep is a precious sustenance. 

Returning to the main cell, I found Xodar sitting exactly as I had left him, his head bowed. His silence was not the peaceful quiet of an agrarian night, but the crushing, defeated void of cultural and linguistic erasure. 

“Man,” I cried, “it will profit you nothing to mope thus. It were no disgrace to be bested by John Carter. You have seen that in the ease with which I accounted for Thurid.”

“I would that you had dispatched me at the same time,” he murmured, his voice flat.

“Come, come!” I urged. “There is hope yet. Neither of us is dead. We are great fighters. Why not win to freedom?”

He looked at me in horror. “You know not of what you speak. Issus is omnipotent. Issus is omniscient. She hears now the words you speak. She knows the thoughts you think. It is sacrilege even to dream of breaking her commands.”

“Rot, Xodar,” I ejaculated impatiently, rejecting the oppressive mythology he had been fed.

He sprang to his feet. “The curse of Issus will fall upon you! In another instant you will be smitten down, writhing to your death in horrible agony.”

“Do you believe that, Xodar?” I asked. “I doubt; yes, and further, I deny. The red men have for ages possessed the power to shut their minds so that none may read their thoughts. I never even had to learn it, for upon all Barsoom there is none who can read what passes in the secret chambers of my brain. The inner lexicon of my subjective experience is entirely untranslatable to her. If she could read my thoughts, her pride would have suffered a severe shock when I turned to gaze upon her face.”

“What do you mean?” he whispered in an affrighted voice.

“I mean I thought her the most repulsive and vilely hideous creature my eyes ever had rested upon.”

With a cry of “Blasphemer!” he sprang upon me. As he came, I grasped his left wrist with my left hand, swung my right arm around his shoulder, and caught his chin with my elbow, bearing him backward across my thigh. I held him there, feeling the rapid, panicked thump of a man starved of truth. 

“Xodar, let us be friends,” I said softly. “I am sorry to have offended you, but your Issus is merely a mortal old woman. She feeds you lies, an empty diet devoid of true spiritual sustenance. Once out of her clutches, she cannot harm you. With your knowledge of this strange land, and my knowledge of the outer world, we should be able to win our way to freedom. Would not our memories be fairer if we died striving, rather than remaining in servile fear to be butchered by an unjust tyrant?”

I raised him to his feet. He did not renew the attack, nor did he speak, but sank back onto the bench, lost in the heavy fermentation of his own thoughts. 

Hours later, a soft sound at the doorway drew my attention. The red Martian youth stood gazing intently at us.

“Kaor,” I cried, offering the ancient red Martian greeting—a phonetic anchor to a shared heritage. 

“Kaor,” he replied, a brave and winning smile touching his lips. 

“What do you here?” 

“I await my death, I presume. Mine will come soon. I looked upon the radiant beauty of Issus nearly a year since. It has always been a source of keen wonder to me that I did not drop dead at the first sight of that hideous countenance. And her belly! By my first ancestor, never was there so grotesque a figure. She is swollen not with the wholesome bounty of a shared harvest, but with the bloat of parasitic consumption. That they should title her Goddess of Life Eternal and Mother of the Nearer Moon is beyond me.”

“How came you here?” I asked, warmed by his refusal to adopt their dominant lexicon. 

“I was flying a one-man air scout far to the south, searching for the Lost Sea of Korus. I must have inherited from my father a wild lust for adventure. I was grounded by a jammed propeller in the eternal ice when a hundred First Born leaped upon me. Before I went down beneath them, they tasted the steel of my father’s sword—I had given such an account of myself as I know would have pleased my sire had he lived to witness it.”

“Your father is dead?” 

“He died before the shell broke to let me step out into a world that has been very good to me. My only sorrow now is that my mother must mourn me as she has, through the turning of the seasons, for ten long years mourned my father.”

Before I could ask his father's name, the outer door of our prison opened with a metallic screech. A burly guard entered, the harsh clanging of his keys shattering the delicate acoustic space we had formed. He ordered the boy to his own quarters for the night, locking the door behind him.

“It is Issus’ wish that you two be confined in the same room,” the guard said to me, waving a contemptuous hand at Xodar. “This cowardly slave of a slave is to serve you well. If he does not, beat him. It is Issus’ wish that you heap upon him every degradation.” With that, he left us to the sterile silence of the stone cell. 

I placed my hand on Xodar’s shoulder. “You have heard the commands of Issus, but I shall not execute them. You are a brave man, Xodar. If I were you, I should assert my manhood and defy my enemies.”

“I have been thinking very hard, John Carter,” Xodar finally said, his voice shedding its former servility. “I have been piecing together your words with the things I have seen but dared not question. I believe now that she is a fraud. The whole fabric of our religion is based on superstitious lies foisted upon us for ages by those directly above us, to consume our labor for their own aggrandizement. I am ready to cast off the ties that have bound me, but what will it avail us? There is no escape.”

“I have escaped from bad plights in the past,” I replied, “and I shall not despair of escaping from the Sea of Omean.”

Xodar shook his head, gesturing to the damp, flint-like stone of the walls. “Test this surface. Look upon this polished stone; none could cling to it. And the light—you forget we are far below ground. The phosphorescent light you see pervading this vault emanates from the rocks that form its dome. It is always thus upon Omean, just as the billows are always rolling over a windless sea. At the appointed hour of night upon the world above, the men here sleep, but the light is ever the same.”

The ambient, sourceless glow of the cavern was an acoustic and visual prison—a static, unchanging environment that lacked the cyclical pacing of day and night, of fasting and feasting. It was a suffocating, industrial hum of perpetual visibility. 

“It will make escape more difficult,” I conceded, “but what is the pleasure of doing an easy thing?”

“Let us sleep on it to-night,” Xodar said. “A plan may come with our awakening.” 

So we threw ourselves upon the hard stone floor, seeking the natural, circadian rhythms of the body to counteract the sterile timelessness of our prison, and slept the sleep of tired men.

Early the next morning, amidst the relentless, low-frequency thrum of Shador’s subterranean ventilation—an industrial noise pollution that constantly gnawed at the edges of our psychological well-being—Xodar and I commenced work upon our plans for escape. First, I had him sketch upon the stone floor a map of the south polar regions, using a buckle from my harness and the sharp edge of the wondrous gem I had taken from Sator Throg. The gem grated against the stone with a harsh, arrhythmic screech, a violent intrusion upon the acoustic ecology of our cell, yet necessary to chart our path. 

From this, I computed the general direction of Helium and the distance to the opening of Omean. Xodar then mapped the inland sea itself, tracing the position of our prison and the dome's exit. We committed these lines to memory, a silent transmission of cartographic lore. From Xodar, I learned the duties of the guards. Their sentry pace was a slow, mechanized drag of heavy boots against stone, requiring nearly ten minutes to make a single round. The oppressive sonic rhythm of their march meant that for five minutes at a time, each side of the prison was unguarded. 

“This information,” Xodar murmured, his voice rich with the fading dialect of his subjugated ancestors, “will be valuable *after* we get out, but nothing you have asked bears on that first, most vital step.”

“We will get out,” I replied, integrating the mythic impossibility of our escape into the quiet, domestic routine of our morning planning. “Leave that to me.”

“When?”

“The first night that finds a small craft moored near the shore.” I sprang to the bars of our window. Through the stagnant air, carrying the faint, metallic scent of ozone and deep-water algae, I saw several small craft and two large battleships moored within a hundred yards. 

I was about to speak when the heavy iron door clanged open, a jarring blast of noise that shattered the quiet. A guard stepped in. If he saw me at the window, my earthly musculature would be discovered, and irons would replace my freedom. He stood facing the center of the room, his back to me. Five feet above was the top of a partition wall separating our cell from the next. 

“Where is the white man?” the guard barked. He spoke in the dominant lexicon of the First Born—a flattened, sterile language engineered for command, entirely devoid of the rich, untranslatable nuances of native Martian tongues. “Issus commands his presence.”

As he started to turn, I scrambled up the grating, caught the sill, and sprang for the partition. My metal harness scraped against the stone—a dangerous, scraping discord—as I slipped over and dropped into the next cell.

“What was that?” bellowed the black guard. 

“I know not,” Xodar replied smoothly, protecting the secret. “He was here as you entered. I am not his keeper.”

Grumbling in a corrupted slang, the guard unlocked the door to my new hiding place. As the heavy hinges shrieked and the door closed behind him, I bounded back over the partition, dropping softly beside the astonished Xodar. 

“Do you see now how we will escape?” I whispered. 

“I see how you may,” he replied, “but I cannot bounce over walls like a leaping *zitidar*.”

The guard completed his rounds and re-entered our cell. His eyes bulged. “By the shell of my first ancestor!” he roared. “Where have you been?”

“I have been here,” I answered calmly. 

He glared in mingled rage and relief. “Come. Issus commands your presence.”

Outside, I was reunited with the red Martian youth, though the guards kept us separated. As we walked, the boy's face haunted me. His carriage, his gestures—they possessed a circular pacing, an echo of a previous generation that resonated deeply within me. I felt I knew him, though his identity remained hidden behind the veil of time.

Our journey wound through the enchanted parks of Issus toward a mighty wall towering a hundred feet in the air. Passing through massive gates, we entered a plain surrounded by gorgeous forests. Crowds of blacks strolled toward an amphitheatre, mingling with plant men and great white apes. The beasts moved like cowed pets. The First Born pushed them aside with the flats of their swords, treating the once-fearsome creatures as mere commercialized spectacles, stripping them of their wild, mythic dignity. The air here smelled of crushed, rotting foliage and the cloying, superficial perfumes of the nobility—a shallow olfactory mask over the stench of tyranny.

Beneath the amphitheatre, we were herded into a barred paddock. Here, the oppressive noise of the marching guards abated, allowing a moment of quiet acoustic intimacy. I approached the red youth.

“What is the object of this assembly?” I asked. 

“It is a part of the monthly rites of Issus,” he replied. His voice carried the distinct, melodic inflections of an older, agrarian dialect—a cadence that triggered a sudden, vivid memory of hearth-smoke, roasted *sith* haunches, and the deep, communal meaning of shared sustenance. “Black men wash the sins from their souls in the blood of men from the outer world. If a black is killed, it is evidence of his disloyalty. If he lives, he is acquitted.”

“And if we are victorious? Freedom?”

He laughed bitterly. “The only freedom is death. If we prove able fighters, we fight often. Sooner or later, we die in the arena.” He paused, a fierce pride in his eyes. “Some hundred black devils have I accounted for during my time here. My mother would be very proud could she only know how well I have maintained the traditions of my father’s prowess.”

“Your father must have been a mighty warrior,” I said, sensing the weight of his cultural memory. “I have known most of the warriors of Barsoom. Who was he?”

“My father was—”

“Come, calots!” a guard shouted, his harsh voice cutting through our shared linguistic sanctuary. We were hustled down a steep incline into a series of barred cages opening onto the arena floor. The youth was placed in a different cage.

Opposite us sat Issus upon her throne, a hideous creature surrounded by a hundred slave maidens sparkling in jeweled trappings. The stands were a solid mass of humanity, a cacophony of shrieking, laughing nobles who produced a deafening wave of noise pollution that assaulted the senses. The First Born did no work; they fought and they consumed. They had no passionate chefs, no agrarian elders tending to the sacred cycles of harvest. To them, sustenance was merely utilitarian fuel, devoid of sensory appreciation or historical reverence.

This grotesque worldview culminated in the Tribute to Issus. Ten magnificent women—captives from proud courts and holy temples—were led onto the sands. For a year they had served Issus; today they were to pay the price. A huge black functionary entered the arena. He inspected the women with the sterile, meticulous cruelty of a factory overseer. He felt their limbs and poked their ribs, evaluating them not as beings with untranslatable subjective experiences, but as mere cuts of meat. 

Selecting the plumpest of the lot, he dragged her by the wrist through a doorway below the throne. 

“Issus will dine well tonight,” a prisoner beside me murmured. 

“What do you mean?” 

“That was her dinner old Thabis is taking to the kitchens.”

A wave of profound nausea and rage surged over me. The rich, evocative traditions of culinary history—the meticulous sourcing of ingredients, the sharing of the harvest—were here perverted into the ultimate obscenity: cannibalistic commodification. The monster on the throne viewed flesh solely as fuel. 

My fury was interrupted by the grinding of a nearby gate. Three monstrous white apes sprang into the arena. The remaining maidens shrank into a frightened group, the air thick with the acrid, olfactory spike of their terror. One fell to her knees, imploring the hideous deity. Issus only leaned forward, her mouth watering for the sensory thrill of the slaughter. The apes, reeking of musk and curdled blood, charged with demoniacal shrieks that tore through the arena’s acoustics.

The blood-red haze of combat swam before my eyes. The guard before my unbarred cage lounged, assuming we were cowed. With a single blow, I sent him unconscious to the sand, snatched his long-sword, and vaulted into the arena.

My earthly muscles carried me in two mighty bounds to the center of the pit. The amphitheatre fell into a stunned, breathless silence. Then, a wild shout erupted from the cages. My sword whirred through the air—a precise, whistling arc—and a great ape sprawled headless at the maidens' feet, its coppery blood soaking the sand.

As the other apes turned upon me, the crowd unleashed a sullen, industrial roar. Out of the corner of my eye, I saw a score of guards rushing across the sand. Then, a figure broke from the cages behind them. It was the red youth.

“Come, men of the outer world!” he shouted, his voice a clarion call resurrecting a dying language of defiance. “Let us make our deaths worthwhile, and at the back of this unknown warrior turn this day’s Tribute into an orgy of revenge!”

He bounded toward me. From every cage harboring red men, a thunderous shout answered his native exhortation. The inner guards went down beneath howling mobs, and the prisoners stripped the weapon racks, swarming to our support.

The great apes fell before my blade as I stood my ground to protect the maidens. The youth was a marvel of speed. His leaps and bounds were a mirror of my own, a trans-generational echo seamlessly integrated into the bloody soil of the arena. When the guards reached us, he fell upon them from the rear. 

We fought side by side. I caught fleeting glimpses of his lightly springing figure, a sinewy dancer of steel whose techniques—the precise parries, the specific angle of his wrists—spoke a physical dialect I recognized in my very marrow. His style was a recipe of combat I had authored decades ago. The profound pride I felt for him was inexplicable, an interwoven timeline manifesting in the present.

For half an hour, it was as though hell had broken loose. We fought in an inextricable mass, the arena a chaotic kitchen of butchery. Blood streaked the sands, carrying the heavy, iron-rich scent of mortality. Slowly, I commanded the prisoners into a rough formation, a protective circle around the maids. The First Born nobles, seeing their guards decimated, drew their swords and poured from the stands to annihilate us by force of numbers.

I saw Issus leaning forward, her face distorted in a horrid grimace of hate and nascent fear. 

“Down with Issus!” I cried, turning to the outer line of our defenders. “Follow me to the throne!”

The youth was the first to take up the cry. As one man, our fighting mass moved over the bodies of our foes. The clash of our blades against theirs created a new, honest resonance that cut through the amphitheatre's superficial din. 

“To the seats, some of you!” I commanded. “Ten of us can take the throne.”

Then, a new sound swelled through the structure—a polyphonic wave that redefined the acoustic environment. “Rise slaves! Rise slaves!” 

In all parts of the amphitheatre, the female slaves were falling upon their masters. Daggers snatched from harnesses waved aloft, shimmering and crimson. Heavy ornaments became bludgeons; strong fingers and gleaming teeth tore at the oppressors. It was a violent reclamation of their physical and sonic autonomy, a long-pent vengeance against those who had treated them as voiceless chattel.

Only a single thin rank of men now stood between us and Issus. Foam flecked the goddess’s lips; her face was blue with terror. The youth and I fought alone at the vanguard. I was about to go down from a nasty long-sword cut when a hand reached out from behind my adversary, clutching his elbow just as the blade fell. The youth sprang in, running his sword through the fellow. 

My own sword was tightly wedged in the breastbone of a Dator. As he fell, I snatched his weapon and looked over his prostrate body into the eyes of the one whose quick hand had saved me. It was Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang.

“Fly, my Prince!” she cried, her voice urgent but laced with the dominant, arrogant lexicon of the Holy Therns. “It is useless to fight them longer. For each of you there are ten thousand blacks. Break for the open sea. With your sword arm, you may yet win to the Golden Cliffs. Tell your story to my father. He will keep you, and together you may rescue me.”

But I could not accept. Her words offered no true escape, only a shift in power dynamics—trading the brutal, culinary commodification of the First Born for the cold, sterile parasitism of the Therns. That was not my mission, and I would not abandon the resonant, beating heart of the rebellion we had just birthed upon the blood-soaked sands.

For an instant, I stood poised on the threshold of violence before the guards surged, their armored bodies colliding in a chaotic, clanking dissonance. My heel found no purchase, only an acoustic void that swallowed the scrape of my boot. I had backed into the very chute that had claimed Issus. With the youth clutched tightly against my chest, we pitched backward into the Cimmerian throat of the abyss. We struck a polished surface—slick as a well-oiled kneading trough—and slid harmlessly down into a dimly lit subterranean vault.

Through a heavy grated door at one side of the chamber, the malignant countenance of Issus glared. Her voice was an acoustic assault, a shrill, metallic screech that violently disrupted the somber, ancient resonance of the stone. 

"Rash mortal!" she shrieked, the syllables stripped of any cultural warmth, echoing with the sterile power dynamics of her dominant lexicon. "You shall pay the awful penalty for your blasphemy in this secret cell. Here you shall lie alone and in darkness with the carcass of your accomplice festering in its rottenness by your side, until crazed by loneliness and hunger you feed upon the crawling maggots that were once a man."

She vanished. The dim light collapsed into utter blackness. Her threat hung in the air, framed not merely as a biological cessation, but as the ultimate severance from the profound communion of sustenance. To Issus, food was merely utilitarian fuel; she understood nothing of the spiritual desolation of a table laid bare, the severance from the hearth, or the tragic loss of ancestral recipes that tethered a soul to its lineage. 

"Pleasant old lady," a voice whispered beside me.

"Who speaks?" I asked, my ears straining to map the acoustics of the dark.

"’Tis I, your companion, who has had the honour this day of fighting shoulder to shoulder with the greatest warrior that ever wore metal upon Barsoom."

I breathed a sigh, catching the scent of his skin, which carried the faint, earthy aroma of honest agrarian exertion. "I thank God you are not dead. I feared for that nasty cut upon your head."

"It but stunned me," he replied. "A mere scratch."

"Maybe it were as well had it been final," I murmured. "We seem to be in a pretty fix here, trapped in a void, destined to starve."

"Where are we?" 

"Beneath the arena," I replied. "We tumbled down the shaft that swallowed Issus."

His laugh was a low, resonant thrum that seemed to restore a fraction of the room’s acoustic ecology. Reaching out through the ink, he found my shoulder and pulled my ear close. 

"Nothing could be better," he breathed, his accent shifting subtly into the native, untranslatable dialect of the subterranean laborers—a quiet linguistic rebellion against the First Born’s homogenized tongue. "There are secrets within the secrets of Issus of which she does not dream. I laboured with the other slaves a year since in the remodelling of these galleries. We found below these an ancient system of corridors sealed for ages. I know the entire system perfectly. There is one passage that connects with the lower regions opening on the water shaft to Omean. If we reach the submarine undetected, we may make the sea, where there are many islands the blacks never visit."

He spoke in a hushed, rhythmic cadence, fearing the oppressive acoustics of the prison might carry his words to spying ears. I answered in the same subdued tone. 

"Lead back to Shador, my friend," I whispered. "Xodar, the black, is there. We were to attempt our escape together; I cannot desert him."

"No," the youth agreed, the word resonant with the ancestral loyalty forged over shared provisions. "One cannot desert a friend."

He felt along the floor, his hands searching the stone like a master baker testing the proofing of a dense dough. A soft "S-s-t" summoned me through the dark. 

"There is a drop here of about ten feet," he warned. "Hang by your hands and you will alight safely on a floor of soft sand."

Quietly, I lowered myself into the Stygian dark. So absolute was the blackness we could not see an inch before our faces. Hanging in mid-air, surrounded by a profound acoustic silence, induces a sensation akin to panic—a disconnection from the sensory world. Yet, trusting the boy, I released my hold. I dropped onto a bed of sand that yielded beneath my boots like finely milled root-flour. 

The boy followed. "Raise me to your shoulders," he said, "and I will replace the trap."

That done, he took my hand, leading me through the labyrinth by touch and the subtle acoustic shifts of the corridors. Presently, we began a steep descent. The air grew cooler, carrying the damp, mineral tang of subterranean aquifers. "We shall soon have light," he murmured. "At the lower levels we meet the phosphorescent rock of Omean."

That descent through the ancient pits awoke a sweeping, multi-generational reverence within me. The unguessable antiquity of the stone served as a canvas for mythic memory. I felt the acoustic ghosts of Barsoom’s lost civilizations brushing against the walls. Here, in these forgotten arteries, the ancient yellow men, the whites, and the blacks had once fought their desperate battle for survival against the drying of the planet. I imagined the profound loss of their agrarian traditions—the slow extinction of heirloom seeds, the tragic starvation of their culinary heritage as survival reduced the sacred act of dining to the mere consumption of calories. Their vibrant dialects, rich with terminology for planting seasons, the precise sear of native meats, and the olfactory landscapes of their coastal gardens, had been systematically eroded. This was the true tragedy of the dead sea bottoms: an untranslatable grief brought on by language death, a cultural memory extinguished by the encroaching hordes. Did some remnant of those passionate food historians, those ancient yellow men, still cultivate their native tubers in some hidden, sunlit canyon? 

My reveries were broken by the boy. "At last, the lighted way." 

A dim radiance bled into the corridor. As we advanced, the light swelled until we emerged directly onto a ledge overlooking the submarine pool. The craft lay moored, its hatch open, emitting a low, oppressive industrial thrum that violently disrupted the tranquil acoustic ecology of the cavern—a stark reminder of the First Born's mechanical soullessness. 

Raising a finger to his lips, the youth crept toward the vessel. We dropped to the deserted deck, silent as settling flour, and crawled to the hatchway. Finding no guard below, we slipped into the main cabin. The craft was entirely deserted. Quickly, we secured the hatch. The boy stepped into the pilot house, touched a button, and the boat sank amid swirling waters toward the bottom of the shaft. 

While he directed the descent, I searched the sterile, metallic-smelling cabins. In the pilot house, the boy handed me a paper. "This may explain the absence of the crew."

It was a radio-aerial message, its text a weaponized application of the dominant lexicon, entirely devoid of cultural nuance: 

*The slaves have risen. Come with what men you have and those that you can gather on the way. Too late to get aid from Omean. They are massacring all within the amphitheatre. Issus is threatened. Haste. — ZITHAD*

"Zithad is Dator of the guards of Issus," the youth explained. "We gave them a bad scare."

"Let us hope it is but the beginning of the end of Issus," I said.

"Only our first ancestor knows," he replied softly.

We reached the submarine pool in Omean without incident. Here we debated sinking the craft, but realized it would add nothing to our chances. The true obstacle lay in bypassing the guards patrolling the docks, men whose ears were attuned only to the harsh commands of their superiors.

"What is the name of the officer in charge of these guards?" I asked.

"Torith," he replied.

"And the submarine commander?"

"Yersted."

Finding a dispatch blank—the parchment smelling faintly of bleached kelp and chemical ink—I drafted an order. To survive, I had to employ the rigid, soulless phrasing of our captors, understanding the power dynamics embedded in their language. To mimic their lexicon was to become invisible.

*Dator Torith: Return these two slaves at once to Shador. — YERSTED*

"That will be the simpler recipe for return," I said, handing the forged order to the boy. 

"But our swords!" he exclaimed. "What shall we say to explain them?"

"Since we cannot explain them, we leave them," I replied, feeling the sensory deprivation of the act—the loss of the familiar, weighted balance at my hip. 

"Is it not extreme rashness to put ourselves unarmed in the power of the First Born?"

"It is the only way," I answered. "Trust me to find a way out of Shador. Once out, we shall find fresh ingredients for our rebellion."

"As you say," he replied with a shrug. "Let us put your ruse to the test."

We emerged onto the deck unarmed. At the sight of us, the guards sprang forward, the mechanical clatter of their rifle bolts harsh and jarring against the cavern's acoustics. I handed the forged dispatch to one, who passed it to Torith.

The Dator read it, his eyes narrowing with suspicion. "Where is Dator Yersted?"

I held my posture, my voice a carefully modulated imitation of their flat, authoritarian cadence, masking the rich complexities of my own heritage. "His orders were to return immediately to the temple landing."

Torith took a half step toward the pool. Had he inspected the empty vessel, my concoction would have collapsed. But he hesitated, calculating the acoustic weight of my lie. The sheer audacity of two slaves voluntarily submitting to the utilitarian machinery of the prison system bypassed his suspicion. The dominant lexicon of the dispatch commanded his obedience.

"Were you connected with the rising of the slaves?" Torith asked. 

"All were involved," I replied flatly. "But it amounted to little. The guards quickly overcame and killed the majority of us."

Satisfied, he turned to a subordinate. "Take them to Shador." 

We were rowed back to the prison island, the oars dipping into the subterranean sea with a rhythmic, mournful splash. Upon disembarking, we were returned to our respective cells; I with Xodar, the boy by himself. Behind heavy locked doors, sealed once more in the acoustic isolation of the First Born, we waited to plan our ultimate feast of liberation.

Xodar listened in incredulous astonishment to my narration of the events within the arena. He could scarcely conceive that one could threaten Issus with sword in hand and not be blasted into a thousand fragments. 

“It is the final proof,” he murmured at last, the syllables of his native tongue tasting heavy and resonant, like unground grain. “No more is needed to completely shatter my belief in her divinity. She is only a wicked old woman, a manipulator of the dominant lexicon who has wielded her power to overwrite the cultural memory of Barsoom, keeping our people in ignorance for ages.”

“She is still all-powerful here, however,” I replied, savoring the cool, metallic tang of the subterranean air as a brief respite from the prison's stagnation. “So it behooves us to leave at the first moment that appears propitious.”

“I hope you find it,” he laughed bitterly, a sound that briefly cut through the oppressive industrial hum of the battleships beyond our walls. 

When I asked if he could swim, he assured me no slimy silian of Korus was more at home in the water. 

“Good. The red youth in all probability cannot,” I said. We would have to support the boy through the sea. Generations ago, the red men’s ancestors had watched the great oceans recede, a tragic language death that slowly erased the very vocabulary of deep water from their minds, leaving the subjective experience of the sea entirely untranslatable to them. 

“It is well,” Xodar noted, approving of the red youth's inclusion. “Three swords are better than two. I have seen him battle. One might think you two master and pupil, or father and son; there is the same maddening contempt for your adversary in your faces.”

Xodar’s awakening went deeper than religion; it was a profound reclamation of identity. “Issus works through the Holy Therns,” he explained, his voice laced with disgust for the sterile, commercialized myths they peddled. “They use a monolithic language of false doctrine to persuade deluded creatures to take a voluntary pilgrimage. It is a system designed merely to swell their wealth and harvest slaves. We First Born are a non-productive race, priding ourselves on it. It is criminal for us to labor, to cultivate the soil, or to practice the culinary arts. We view sustenance merely as utilitarian fuel, existing in a state of sensory deprivation while we plunder the trained artisans of the red men to copy what we cannot create. We have forgotten the profound meaning found in the slow roasting of *zitidar* marrow, the meticulous sourcing of spiced *pekar* roots, or the rhythmic acoustic harmony of an agrarian life.”

I nodded, sharing his disdain for any culture that lacked sensory appreciation for the harvest of the earth. I hoped, as Xodar did, that he would live to atone for these sins and fight for the people whose histories had been so violently consumed.

Our conversation was interrupted by a weird call ringing out across the waters of Omean. It was a synthetic, grating siren that marked the end of the day—a prime example of the industrial noise pollution that plagued this cavern, eroding the psychological well-being of its captives and drowning out the natural acoustics of the subterranean sea. 

After our guard made his final inspection, I sprang to the grated window. A quarter mile away lay a monster battleship, its engines emitting a low, mechanical dissonance. A lone watchman spread his sleeping silks upon a tiny platform and lay down. The acoustic environment settled into a heavy, artificial lull. 

Dropping to the floor, I listened as Xodar mapped out the olfactory and mechanical landscape of a five-man flier moored nearby—his personal property. Its engines, he explained, utilized a trick of gearing I immediately recognized from my time in the Zodangan navy. The First Born had stolen it from the ships of Helium.

I scaled the partition wall, balancing on its narrow, flat surface until I reached the red youth’s cell. He grinned, an expression deeply rooted in the agrarian warmth of the outer world. Linking hands, I pulled him up beside me. His leaping ability was extraordinary, a physical echo of ancient Barsoomian martial rhythms. 

Returning to Xodar, we bound ourselves by solemn oath. We agreed I would navigate the craft, and if we breached the outer world, we would make for Helium. The boy gave me a peculiar look when I revealed I was a prince of that nation, but the press of our immediate survival deferred the untranslatable weight of his gaze.

We lowered ourselves to the ground outside the prison. Xodar and the youth slipped quietly into the water, their descent merging seamlessly with the gentle lap of the waves. But the lure of the swords in the nearby guard-house was strong upon me. I crept toward the door, hyper-aware of the sonic environment.

I pressed the door open. The olfactory landscape within was dense: the sterile tang of gun oil, the musky sweat of sleeping soldiers, and the lingering, acrid scent of their processed, utilitarian rations. I moved with the silent, deliberate pacing of a predator. As I drew a long-sword from the rack, the scrape of its scabbard sounded to my overwrought nerves like a jagged tear in the room's acoustic fabric. A third sword clanked with a frightful din, yet the men slept on. 

I plucked a revolver from its pin just as my eyes found an open window letting in the salty, iodine-rich scent of the sea. Suddenly, the door opposite me opened. The officer of the guard stood there. Our revolvers came up simultaneously. The twin reports were an acoustic atrocity, a violent, deafening rupture in the soundscape that shattered the silence of Omean. I felt the heat of his bullet graze my ear, saw him crumple, and in the next second, I hurled myself through the window.

The waters of Omean closed over my head, tasting of ancient minerals and rusted iron. I swam entirely beneath the surface, burdened by the swords, while Xodar supported the youth. We reached the flier just as an alarm gun bellowed from a ship’s bow—a deafening, industrialized broadcast of war that violently disrupted the subterranean ecology. 

The sleeping thousands awoke. We cast away, rising swiftly. I lay flat at the controls, the scent of burning ozone and friction filling my nostrils as I pushed the levers. Below us, the pandemonium was an abrasive cacophony of cracking rifles, shouting officers, and the chaotic whir of countless propellers chopping the air.

“A little to your right!” Xodar shouted over the roar. “The shaft! Dead ahead!”

I saw the yawning black opening in the glowing dome. But a ten-man cruiser was rising at a steep angle to cut off our escape, its engines screaming in mechanical dissonance, intent on combing us with grappling hooks. There was only one chance. I angled our bow upward, then, as we neared, deflected it into a horizontal trajectory at terrific velocity, aiming straight for the cruiser's keel. 

The collision was a symphony of shrieking metal, a violent tactile shock that crushed our steel bows and nearly hurled us into the abyss. I caught the boy’s ankle just as he plunged over the edge. The crippled cruiser careened backward, its crew spilling into the void, before it dove headfirst to the bottom of Omean.

Regaining the levers, I leveled our craft and aimed for the black mouth of the shaft. A hundred swift scouts, unburdened by heavy buoyancy tanks, were closing in.

“Reverse!” screamed Xodar, his words nearly swallowed by the acoustic violence of our flight. “For the love of your first ancestor, reverse! We are at the shaft!”

“Hold tight!” I roared back, tasting the metallic adrenaline of the moment. “We are going straight up!”

I threw the bow hard up and dragged the speed lever to its final notch. We swept into the pitch-black opening. The wind whistled in a deafening rush, a primal roar that stripped away the First Born’s industrial noise, leaving only the pure, soaring acoustic ecology of sheer velocity. Guided only by the rapidly diminishing circle of phosphorescent light below, I steered through the impenetrable dark, relying on blind faith and the instinctual rhythms of survival. 

We rocketed upward for what felt like both an instant and an eternity, moving at unimaginable speed. Suddenly, the roaring tunnel fell away. Black darkness enshrouded us. There were neither moons nor stars. For a moment, I was nonplussed, until a rich, heavy scent filled my lungs—the unmistakable aroma of atmospheric moisture and damp earth. It was summer at the south pole. The ice cap was melting, and thick clouds—a mythic occurrence on Barsoom—blanketed the night sky, carrying the olfactory promise of rain, fertile soil, and the endless, circular return of the harvest.

“My son!” 

I could scarce believe my ears, though the auditory truth of it resonated through my very bones. Slowly, I rose and faced the handsome youth. Now that I looked at him closely, the interwoven timelines of our lineage were unmistakably mapped upon his features. He bore his mother’s incomparable, crisp beauty, yet grounded by a robustly masculine architecture, and in his grey eyes, I saw the mirrored reflection of my own generational soul. It was a face that closed the circle of time, bringing the mythic scale of our Martian struggles back to the intimate, hearth-lit domesticity of our shared blood. 

The boy stood facing me, his expression a delicate balance of hope and uncertainty. 

“Tell me of your mother,” I said softly, my voice seeking the familiar acoustic warmth of our past. “Tell me all you can of the years that I have been robbed by a relentless fate of her dear companionship.”

With a cry of profound relief, he sprang toward me and threw his arms about my neck. For a brief moment, as I held my boy close to me, the tears welled in my eyes. The scent of him—a subtle mingling of the ozone-rich upper atmosphere and the faint, spiced aroma of Helium’s agrarian gardens—grounded me in the physical reality of his existence. I was like to have choked after the manner of some maudlin fool, but I do not regret it. A long life has taught me that true strength does not lie in the sterile suppression of emotion, but in the deep, sensory appreciation of the bonds that sustain us.

“Your stature, your manner, the terrible ferocity of your swordsmanship,” said the boy, pulling back slightly, “are exactly as my mother has described them to me a thousand times over the evening meal. But even with such domestic lore, I could scarce credit the truth of what seemed so improbable. Do you know what thing it was that convinced me more than all the others?”

“What, my boy?” I asked.

“Your first words to me—they were of my mother. None else but the man who loved her as she has told me my father did would have thought first of her.”

“For long years, my son, I can scarce recall a moment that the radiant vision of your mother’s face—the memory of her grinding aromatic *sorfa* seeds in the morning light, the scent of saffron-hued broths simmering in our chambers—has not been ever before me. Tell me of her.”

“Those who have known her longest say that she has not changed, unless it be to grow more beautiful. Only, when she thinks I am not about to see her, her face grows very sad. She thinks ever of you, my father. Our people fairly worship your memory. Each year, on the anniversary of the day you raced across a near-dead world to unlock the atmosphere plant, a great festival is held. But there are tears mingled with the thanksgiving—tears of genuine regret that the author of their breath is not with them to share the rich, sensory joy of living he died to give them. Upon all Barsoom, there is no greater name than John Carter.”

“And by what name has your mother called you, my boy?” I asked.

“The people of Helium demanded that I be named with my father’s name, wielding the subtle power dynamics embedded in their dominant lexicon to claim me for the state. But my mother said no. She insisted that the name we had chosen together must be honored before all others. She chose a name that defied the inherent untranslatability of our two subjective experiences, blending her native dialect with yours—Carthoris.”

Our intimate communion was suddenly shattered by a harsh, grating dissonance. Xodar had been at the wheel, and now he called to me over the oppressive, mechanical whine of the vessel. 

“She is dropping badly by the head, John Carter!” he shouted. “So long as we were rising at a stiff angle, it was not noticeable, but now that I am trying to keep a horizontal course, it is different. The wound in her bow has opened one of her forward ray tanks.”

The engine’s agonizing, high-frequency hiss was a violent intrusion upon the serene acoustic ecology of the Martian sky—a jarring industrial noise pollution that frayed my nerves. I examined the damage and found it a grave matter. The forced angle at which we were compelled to maintain the bow was not only impeding our speed, but at the rate we were losing our repulsive rays, we would soon be floating stern up and helpless. I took the helm once more, pulling the noble little engine wide open to race north, while Carthoris and Xodar, tools in hand, labored over the great rent in a hopeless endeavor to stem the tide of escaping rays.

It was still dark when we passed the northern boundary of the ice cap. Below us lay a typical Martian landscape: the rolling ochre sea bottoms of long-dead oceans, low surrounding hills, and here and there the grim, silent cities of the dead past, tenanted only by age-old memories and the great white apes of Barsoom. 

Lower and lower sagged the bow. To prevent our flight from terminating in a swift, catastrophic dive, I was forced to stop the engine. The sudden absence of its cacophonous roar was a profound auditory relief, restoring a sense of psychological well-being even as our physical peril peaked. As the sun rose and the light of a new day swept away the darkness, our craft gave a final spasmodic plunge, turned half upon her side, and swung in a slow circle. To hand-rail and stanchion we clung, snapping the buckles of our harness to the rings at her sides. We hung in our leather with feet dangling a thousand yards above the ground. Reaching out to the lever that directed the remaining rays of repulsion, I eased us gently down.

Directly north of our landing site rose a rather lofty range of hills. We made our way toward their time-rounded gullies, seeking concealment among the beautiful, flowering plants that abound in the arid wastes. Here, the landscape offered us profound sustenance. We came upon a grove of huge milk-giving shrubs—a traditional agrarian staple that has served as lifeblood for generations. 

For me, eating has never been a merely utilitarian act of fueling the body; it is a communion with the land. With the meticulous care of a food historian, I showed Carthoris and Xodar how to properly tap the fibrous bark. By scoring the trunk at precise angles, we released a rich, creamy effusion that smelled redolent of toasted cumin, wild honey, and sweet moss. We cupped the thick, nourishing liquid in our hands. Its taste was a complex symphony of roasted ochre-root and mineral-rich earth, an evocative, gustatory reminder of Barsoom’s deeply rooted agrarian past. It was a profound boon to us, nearly famished as we were.

Beneath the fragrant cluster of shrubs, perfectly concealed from wandering air scouts and blanketed by the soothing, symphonic rustle of the wind through the leaves, we lay down to sleep. 

It was mid-afternoon when I was awakened by someone seizing my hand and covering it with kisses. With a start, I opened my eyes to look into the beautiful face of Thuvia. 

“My Prince! My Prince!” she cried in an ecstasy of happiness. “’Tis you whom I had mourned as dead. My ancestors have been good to me; I have not lived in vain.”

Her voice awoke Xodar and Carthoris. She would have thrown her arms about my neck had I not gently but firmly disengaged myself. 

“Come, come, Thuvia,” I said soothingly. “You are overwrought. You forget yourself, as you forget that I am the husband of the Princess of Helium.”

“I forget nothing, my Prince,” she replied. “I would not take the place of Dejah Thoris. My greatest ambition is to serve you forever as your slave. No greater boon could I ask.”

I felt profoundly uncomfortable. The word she used carried the heavy, oppressive weight of dominant lexicons designed to subjugate and erase personal identity. I could not abide the toxic power dynamics embedded in such a term.

“If I ever return to Helium, Thuvia,” I said, ensuring my tone was a gentle, resonant chord, “you shall go with me, but as an honored equal, and not as a slave. Forget this gratitude-begotten infatuation. I value your friendship and your sovereignty far more.”

“You are my master; it shall be as you say,” she replied, though a note of acoustic sadness lingered in her voice. 

“How came you here?” I asked. “And where is Tars Tarkas?”

“The great Thark, I fear, is dead,” she replied. “We abandoned our drifting vessel and came upon the dead city just beyond these hills. As we walked its ancient avenues, a horde of green warriors approached. Tars Tarkas forced me into a doorway to hide. ‘These be the Warhoon of the South,’ he told me. ‘When they have seen my metal, it will be to the death.’” 

She described a mythic stand—the great Thark fighting until the Warhoon dead formed a hill around him, eventually overwhelmed by the sheer, crushing tide of bodies. They carried him away toward the heart of the city. 

“I must be sure,” I said. “I cannot leave Tars Tarkas alive among the Warhoons. Tonight I shall enter the city.”

Carthoris and Xodar immediately volunteered, but I refused them. Stealth, not force, was required. Leaving them with instructions, I set forth at a rapid dogtrot toward the dead metropolis as the sudden darkness of Barsoom engulfed the landscape.

The nearer moon was winging its wild flight through the heavens, its bright beams turning the ancient, abandoned architecture to burnished silver. The city had been built upon gently rolling foothills, allowing me to enter its southern streets unobserved. Once within, I kept close to the dense shadows of the walls. 

As I approached the plaza, the pristine acoustic ecology of the ancient ruins—originally designed by master builders to harmonize with the wind—was abruptly defiled by the squealing and grunting of corralled thoats and zitidars. Despite the discordant noise, the scent of the beasts and the familiar sounds sent a thrill of interwoven memory surging through me; it was the exact olfactory and auditory landscape of my early days in the dead city of Korad, where I had first courted Dejah Thoris amid the routine domestic rhythms of green Martian life.

From the shadows, I saw warriors moving toward a great central building. I needed to cross the square to follow them. I slipped into a deserted building and crept into an inner courtyard filled with restless beasts, masking my scent in the dense, mossy odor of the ochre vegetation they cropped. 

I scaled the face of the courtyard wall, an agile leap bringing my hands to the stone hand-rail of a second-floor balcony. Peering through the open windows, I observed the green folk squatting upon their sleeping silks. To listen to them was to witness the tragic erosion of cultural memory through language death; the rich, nuanced vocabulary of ancient Barsoom had decayed into brutish, utilitarian grunts and monosyllables. 

“Come, Tan Gama,” a warrior grunted from the hall. “We are to take the Thark before Kab Kadja. Bring another with you.”

I stepped into the unlighted hall, moving cautiously as the three warriors disappeared down a spiral stairway at the corridor's far end. I followed the flicker of their torch down into the subterranean pits beneath the structure. The descent was a plunge into a suffocating, sterile void. The air tasted of ancient dust and copper blood, devoid of any of the rich, life-affirming scents of the surface. 

We had gone perhaps a hundred yards through a maze of tortuous corridors when they turned into a doorway. Through it, I saw them removing the chains that secured the great Thark to the wall. They hustled him roughly out of the cell, wheeling directly toward my position. I had no choice but to hasten backward in advance of their meager light, plunging deeper into the unknown passageways to avoid detection. 

Presently, I came to a place where five corridors diverged. I hurried down one, but suddenly, the faint light of their torch disappeared. The silence that followed was absolute—an unnatural, tomb-like deadness that offered no acoustic feedback, starving the senses and eroding the mind's equilibrium.

Realizing they had taken a different path, I felt my way back along the wall, the stone cold and devoid of history beneath my fingertips. After an eternity, I found the five-way intersection again, but the darkness was utter. I listened intently. I thought I detected the faint, metallic clank of side arms up the middle corridor and followed it, but it was a blind lead. The oppressive silence swallowed me whole once more. 

I retraced my steps, only to stumble upon an intersection of three corridors I did not recognize. I was utterly lost. Unless I could find my way back, I would wander this sightless, soundless purgatory until I succumbed to thirst and hunger. 

Or—what was that?

A faint shuffling sounded behind me. The acoustic disruption froze the blood in my veins. It was the horrifying memory of the Warhoon dungeons reborn—the scraping sound as of the dragging of a dead limb. 

I looked over my shoulder into the blackness. Blazing eyes stared back at me, glowing unblinkingly out of the dark void, revealing no sign of the beast behind them. The most fearsome attribute of these creatures is their silence; they do not breathe, they only watch, violating the sonic environment with that single, unnatural scrape. 

Grasping my long-sword tightly, I backed slowly along the corridor. But ever as I retreated, the eyes advanced. Suddenly, I heard the shuffling noise at my right, and looking, saw another pair of fiery eyes approaching from an intersecting passage. As I started to renew my slow retreat, I heard the noise repeated behind me, and then, before I could turn, I heard it again at my left.

I could not have been unconscious for more than a few seconds, yet the transition from the resonant silence of the void to the sudden, harsh glare of a torch felt like the crossing of epochs. I lay unharmed, save for a slight, aching bruise upon my forehead where it had met the ancient stone flagging. The air in the corridor carried the stale, metallic tang of oxidized copper and subterranean damp—a sterile, utilitarian scent utterly devoid of the rich, loamy aromas of the sunlit world above. I sprang to my feet as a party of four green warriors approached. Their heavy, metal-shod footfalls created a chaotic, dissonant clanking against the stone, a jagged industrial noise that struck my ears like a physical assault upon the mind's natural acoustic equilibrium. 

They had not yet seen me. I lost no time in slipping into the first intersecting corridor. The acoustics here were deadened, the air stagnant. As the warriors passed my hiding place, I exhaled a slow breath, tasting the dry dust of ages. It was the same party I had followed into the pits: Tars Tarkas and his three guards. Falling in behind them, I soon reached the cell where the great Thark was to be rechained. Two warriors remained without, while the jailer with the keys entered. The two outside strolled toward the spiral runway and disappeared beyond a turn, leaving the torch in a socket beside the door.

The brutal necessity of violence always carried a bitter, alkaline taste on the back of the tongue. Yet, there was no alternative if Tars Tarkas and I were to return to my camp in the hills. Keeping to the shadows, I positioned myself with my longsword raised, my grip firm upon the hilt. When the jailer emerged, I brought the blade down. I do not care to dwell upon the immediate aftermath, save to note that within minutes, Tars Tarkas, garbed in the purloined metal of a Warhoon chief, was hurrying down the corridor with the torch. A dozen paces behind him followed John Carter.

As Tars Tarkas approached the runway, the two warriors called down from above, their voices echoing with the harsh, guttural syllables of the Warhoon dialect. 
“Why so long, Tan Gama?” 
“I had trouble with a lock,” Tars Tarkas replied, mastering the cadence. “And now I find I have left my short-sword in the cell. Go on, I’ll fetch it.”
“We shall see you above directly.”

We waited until the rhythmic vibration of their footsteps faded. Extinguishing the torch, we crept toward the spiral incline. The first floor was impassable, blocked by a rear room brimming with green folk, so we cautiously ascended to the second. Threading the long, unlighted hall, we reached a balcony overlooking the inner courtyard. 

Here, the acoustics shifted. Through a window, we overheard the warriors conversing with a woman. The Warhoon dialect they spoke was a jagged, dominant lexicon, stripped of the nuanced, untranslatable phonemes of the ancient Barsoomian tongues—a cultural memory eroded into a language of mere utility and subjugation. 
“Tan Gama left his short-sword in the Thark’s cell,” one warrior explained.
“Tan Gama wore no short-sword this night,” the woman’s voice cut through the air, sharp as cracked flint. “It was broken in today’s battle. See, I have it here.”
The sudden silence was heavy with realization. “There is something amiss here,” a warrior cried. “Come! Let us hasten to the pits.”

We waited for no more. Slinging my harness into a long single strap, I lowered Tars Tarkas to the courtyard beneath, then dropped to his side. The great Thark looked at me, his expression bewildered in the dim moonlight. “By this time,” he murmured, “I should have learned to wonder at nothing which John Carter accomplishes.” He needed no words to express his gratitude; the untranslatable subjective experience of our bond spanned generations. Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of the Tharks, was an agrarian elder of a dying world, rooted in its ancient codes, and among two planets, I counted none a better friend.

“There be five now in our party,” I whispered. “Thuvia, Xodar, Carthoris, and ourselves. We shall need five thoats.”
“Carthoris!” he breathed. “Your son?”
“Yes. But wait until we have made good our escape to hear the narrative.”

We approached the great gates at the far end of the courtyard. The holding pens smelled fiercely of raw life—a pungent, intoxicating blend of ammonia, crushed mineral dust, and the warm, peppery musk of the great thoats. Standing ten feet at the shoulder, with sleek slate-grey hides shading to vivid yellow legs, they were forces of nature. Calming them required no spoken commands, which are too often bound by the rigid power dynamics of dominant lexicons, but rather telepathy. We projected a rhythm of safety, a silent, acoustic memory of open plains and grazing, until two massive brutes allowed us to mount. Herding three others, we opened the gates and rode into the avenue, abandoning the dead city for the nocturnal desert.

Escaping the purlieus of the city, the oppressive noise of the Warhoons gave way to the sweeping, harmonious song of the wind across the dead sea bottom. We reunited with Carthoris, Xodar, and Thuvia at the camp. Greetings were exchanged in the formal, dignified syntax of Barsoom—a layered tapestry uniting our interwoven timelines. Thuvia was lifted to the gentlest thoat, Xodar and Carthoris mounted the others, and we fled east, then north, under the glorious light of the two moons.

Toward noon of the following day, the rhythm of our flight paused. We hobbled the beasts so they might crop the ochre, moss-like vegetation of the sea bottom. To the uninitiated, this moss was mere utilitarian fuel, but to the agrarian historians of Barsoom, it was a profound sustenance. As I watched the thoats graze, I crushed a fibrous sprig between my fingers. It smelled of deep time and iron-rich soil. To taste it was to experience the tart, brine-kissed moisture trapped within its cellular walls—a flavor profile passed down through millennia, quietly integrating the mythic sagas of martian history into the simplest, most vital domestic routine of eating. 

Thuvia volunteered to watch while we slept. I had only just closed my eyes when her hand on my shoulder and the soft modulation of her voice broke my rest. “Arise, O Prince,” she whispered. The sonic landscape had changed; beneath the wind’s breath lay a thin, dissonant vibration. Tars Tarkas peered at the horizon. “It is a great body of mounted men,” he said.

We freed the thoats and rode. For a day and a night, the acoustic pollution of our pursuers—the relentless, industrial clatter of Warhoon accoutrements—tore through the natural silence of the basin, creeping ever closer. By dawn of the second day, a fiendish shout of triumph shattered the air; they were less than a half-mile behind. 

Several miles ahead lay a range of hills. But Thuvia’s mount was failing. Its respiration became a ragged, wet wheeze, smelling of lactic acid and exhaustion. As the beast lurched, I snatched Thuvia and swung her behind me on my own thoat. The double burden slowed us terribly, and the others would not abandon us. We were a microcosm of diverse races and dialects, bound by an unspoken loyalty. 

Suddenly, I felt Thuvia’s warm lips on my shoulder, carrying the faint, sweet scent of her breath. “For thy sake, O my Prince,” she murmured, her voice carrying the tragic, poetic resonance of a dying language. Then, she deliberately slipped from the thoat, surrendering herself to the howling dissonance behind us to lighten my mount's burden. 

She should have known me better. Turning my thoat, I spurred it back toward her. Carthoris, perceiving the sacrifice, swept Thuvia onto his own animal, struck its rump with the flat of his sword, and sent it toward the hills. Then, he stood beside me on the ochre moss. Tars Tarkas and Xodar instantly dismounted, turning their beasts loose so we might face the howling devils on equal footing. We were prepared to feed the ancient soil with our blood, a final communion with the land. 

The Warhoons were a hundred yards away when the sonic fabric of the world was violently ripped apart. An explosion of unimaginable concussive force erupted behind us, carrying the acrid, scorched scent of pulverized nitre. A shell burst directly within the advancing ranks. The chaotic dialect of their war cries fractured into pure panic as a hundred warriors toppled, and riderless thoats stampeded over the dead. 

We looked up to trace the origin of this devastating acoustic rupture. There, just clearing the nearer hills, a great battleship drifted majestically through the sky. Her bow gun spoke again, another deafening percussion. As she drew closer, I could not repress a wild cry of elation, for painted upon her bows was the device of Helium.

As Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and I stood gazing at the magnificent vessel which meant so much to all of us, the dissonant, industrial shriek of its repulsor-rays tore through the natural acoustic harmony of the ancient hills. It was a harsh, mechanical noise, a violent vibration that polluted the sonic environment and shattered the subtle, comforting rustle of the sparse Martian wind. Two more massive ships crested the summit, their engines emitting an oppressive thrum that vibrated unpleasantly in our marrow. Soon, a score of one-man air scouts launched from the upper decks, diving toward the ground with a shrill, manufactured whine. 

In another instant, we were surrounded by armed sailors. An officer stepped forward, a man whose bearing spoke not merely of the military, but of a deep, ancestral devotion to the terraced *sorak* orchards of his family's agrarian estates. When his eyes fell upon Carthoris, his rigid posture melted into the joyous warmth of an elder welcoming the harvest. 

"Carthoris, my Prince," he cried, his voice carrying the rich, rolling timbre of the old Heliumetic dialects—a language of the earth that the modern, sterile military complex had yet to entirely erase. "Kaor! Kaor! Hor Vastus greets the son of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium, and of her husband, John Carter. All Helium has been plunged in sorrow since the fatal day that saw you leave our midst."

"Grieve not, my good Hor Vastus," cried Carthoris, "for I bring back not only myself, but one whom all Barsoom loved best—John Carter, Prince of Helium!"

Hor Vastus turned. He smelled faintly of polished iron and the crushed, sweet foliage of the *pimalia* groves he so lovingly tended in peacetime. As his eyes fell upon me, he looked as though the breath had been knocked from him. He began to ask where I had been, but the words faltered. The inherent untranslatability of my journey—the sensory horrors of the Valley Dor and the rotting-fruit stench of the plant men—hung between us like a heavy, pungent smoke. He knew the terrible truth, and his loyal heart would not force me to articulate it in a lexicon wholly inadequate for such subjective trauma. 

Instead, Hor Vastus unbuckled his scabbard and flung his sword upon the ground before me. In the deep-rooted agrarian traditions of red Martians, this gesture was akin to offering the very hearth-fire of one's ancestral home. It meant sharing the bitterest roots and the most tender, slow-braised cuts of meat at the table of life. To offer the sword was to say: *My harvest is your harvest; my body and soul are yours to consume or preserve.* I stooped, lifting the blade. I tasted the faint metallic tang of the hilt against my lips, acknowledging the profound cultural memory bound in the act, and buckled the weapon upon him with my own hands, assuring him I would call upon it only in the name of truth.

We looked about for our party and noticed Thuvia was missing. The loss felt abrupt, a missing note in the complex chord of our gathering, leaving behind only the trampled scent of arid wild-herbs where Carthoris had sent her thoat galloping. Hor Vastus immediately dispatched a dozen air scouts to search the hills, but she had vanished. 

A larger transport dropped to ferry us to the *Xavarian*. Stepping onto its deck, the air was acutely sterile, smelling merely of processed lubricants and utilitarian rations—a stark contrast to the gustatory richness of the great feast-halls of Helium. 

The first to greet me was Kantos Kan. Though he now commanded all of Helium's sky-terrors, to me he was the meticulous companion who had shared the Warhoon dungeons, where we had survived by recounting the intricate recipes and spice-blends of our youth to keep our cultural memory alive. Like Hor Vastus, he refrained from demanding the untranslatable tale of my absence. He greeted Carthoris and Tars Tarkas with profound relief. 

Then, his voice dropped into a hushed, intimate register. "You do not know, John Carter," he murmured, the acoustic space between us fraught with a terrible dissonance, "how we love this son of yours. When he was lost, ten million people wept." He paused. "For a year, Dejah Thoris has grieved. She abandoned the royal kitchens. The slow-simmered *uma* broths she once seasoned with such meticulous care grew cold. The daily domestic routines that tethered her to our multi-generational saga lost their flavor. Sustenance became mere utilitarian fuel to her, ashes in her mouth."

He explained the desperate, disastrous expedition of Mors Kajak and Tardos Mors, who had vanished into the northern hemisphere searching for Carthoris. With her father and grandfather gone, Zat Arras—the Jed of Zodanga—had renewed his aggressive pursuit of her hand. Zat Arras operated in the flat, commercialized dialect of his city, treating the sacred union of royal houses as a mere transaction of power. Driven to despair by his pressure and the erosion of her family line, Dejah Thoris had undertaken the voluntary pilgrimage. She, along with a dozen devoted servants including Sola, had departed for the icy, flavorless oblivion of the River Iss. 

The news was a sickening blow. The vibrant, sensory beauty of my matriarch was now plunged into the silent, rotting horrors I had just escaped. Before I could ask Kantos Kan to widen the search for Thuvia, a flier arrived from the flagship. Zat Arras commanded that his "prisoners" be brought before him. 

The auditory environment of Zat Arras's flagship was oppressive, dominated by the continuous, anxiety-inducing vibration of modern machinery. Zat Arras offered no warmth, no traditional offering of salt or spiced wine. His attitude was cold, his language rigidly confined to the dominant lexicon of the state religion. 

"Why were these prisoners not disarmed?" he demanded of Kantos Kan, relying on the vocabulary of a warden rather than a host. Kantos Kan defended our nobility, but Zat Arras scoffed, demanding to know where I had been. 

"I have just come from the Valley Dor and the Land of the First Born," I replied, refusing to temper my words. 

Zat Arras smiled, a sterile expression. "Ah! You have returned from the bosom of Iss?"

"I have come back from a land of false hope," I said, my voice cutting through the mechanical hum of his vessel. "From a valley of torture and death. I have come back to save Barsoom from death in its most frightful form."

"Cease, blasphemer!" he cried, seeking to use the power dynamics embedded in his religious lexicon to invalidate my lived, visceral experience. He tried to reduce the metallic tang of blood and the grotesque reality of the plant men into mere heresy. 

Before his guards could move, my hands were on his throat. I forced him backward, the tactile reality of his choking breaths and the sudden, desperate scent of his sweat cutting through his political abstraction. "Come I from heaven or hell, Zat Arras, you will find me still the same John Carter. Apologize."

Kantos Kan stepped close, whispering a plea in an old, dying dialect of our shared past, urging me to desist lest I spark a mutiny that would fracture Helium forever. Respecting the fragile ecology of our nation's peace, I released the Jed, leaving him gasping and white-faced among his officers, and we returned to the *Xavarian*.

The journey back to Helium was enveloped in gloomy contemplation. The crew was quietly divided, whispering in fragmented dialects. Many clung to their sterile religious dogmas, unable to digest the raw, bitter truth we brought. It was a truth that required a completely new palate, a rejection of the comfortable, synthetic diet of lies they had been fed for generations. Kantos Kan himself refused to listen to our tale, terrified of the spiritual indigestion it promised. 

My mind was agonizingly consumed by visions of Dejah Thoris. I pictured her among the ferocious white apes, far from the acoustic warmth of her ancestral hearths, deprived of the rich, sensory beauty of the life we had built together. The circular pacing of my existence haunted me; once again, I was a wanderer returning to Helium, fighting to reclaim the heart of my family's saga. 

As we descended toward the mile-high scarlet tower of Greater Helium, the typical roar of the populace was absent. There was no joyful clamor. The acoustic landscape was replaced by a heavy, dense silence, a profound collective mourning that resonated deeply within the soul. Hundreds of thousands packed the streets, but the only sound was the muffled weeping of citizens witnessing our return. 

To avoid sparking an uprising, Zat Arras had us transferred to a lesser flier and smuggled directly to the roof of the Temple of Reward. We bypassed the Avenue of Ancestors entirely. We were lodged in a south-facing room. The air inside smelled of ancient, dry stone and fading incense—an olfactory landscape of generations past. Late in the afternoon, a messenger arrived with the rigid, formalized dictate of the state: we were to be tried by a body of nobles in the great hall at the first zode of the following day. We sat in the silence of our confinement, waiting for the cycle of our family's saga to turn once more, bound by the dominant lexicon of our oppressors.

The morning’s quiet was abruptly shattered by the abrasive, metallic dissonance of Zat Arras’s Zodangan guards, a jarring industrial clatter that polluted the delicate acoustic ecology of our quarters. We were gathered to be marched to the great hall of the temple. In pairs, Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and I entered the cavernous chamber, moving down the broad Aisle of Hope. The space had been designed centuries ago by meticulous architects to amplify the resonant, harmonic frequencies of Helium’s native dialects, yet today it was choked by the sterile, oppressive hum of Zodangan soldiery lining our path.

Reaching the raised enclosure in the center, I beheld our judges. By Barsoomian tradition, thirty-one men of the noble class were to be drawn by lot, a sacred geometry meant to ensure a balance of voices. Instead, I saw a monolithic wall of Zodangans—men whose very presence felt like an aggressive commercialization of justice, lacking any sensory appreciation for the truth. It was I who had dismantled Zodanga’s brutal machinery alongside the green hordes, and here, wrapped in the dominant lexicon of the victors, they sought to erase the cultural memory of that liberation. 

The vast circular coliseum was packed, a sweeping, multi-generational tapestry of Helium’s citizens spanning all ages and classes. As we stepped upon the Throne of Righteousness, the subdued, organic murmur of the crowd was instantly swallowed by a breathless, heavy silence. We were seated with our backs to a smaller central platform, facing the judges and the ten thousand spectators. 

Zat Arras took the golden chair of the presiding magistrate. When he rose and called my name, his voice was a harsh, unmusical bark that sought to enforce a rigid, untranslatable power dynamic. 

“John Carter,” he cried, ordering me to the Pedestal of Truth. He turned to the audience, wielding his words like blunt instruments, attempting to flatten the mythic complexity of our recent survival into a sterile list of transgressions. He claimed I had blasphemed against the Sacred Iss, the Valley Dor, the Holy Therns, and Issus herself. He wielded their ancient religion as a weapon of linguistic death, arguing that he who is dead may not live again, and that by returning, I had violated the sanctity of their most rigid definitions of existence.

“What reward shall be meted to John Carter?” Zat Arras demanded.

“Death!” shouted a Zodangan judge, the word slicing through the air like a cold, flavorless blade. 

But the acoustic void was suddenly filled by a pure, resonant chord of native defiance. “Justice! Justice! Justice!” 

It was Kantos Kan. Leaping past the Zodangan guards, his movements carrying the rhythmic grace of Helium’s ancestral martial dances, he sprang upon the platform. “What manner of justice be this?” he demanded, his native dialect cutting through the dominant Zodangan lexicon. He appealed directly to the people, and the coliseum responded—a great, thunderous chorus demanding fair treatment that Zat Arras dared not silence.

Turned over to me, Zat Arras snarled his permission for me to speak. I addressed not the sterile judges, but the people of Helium. I spoke of the visceral, subjective horror of the Valley Dor, an experience entirely untranslatable to those who had not tasted its bitter reality. I described the cloying, metallic scent of the plant men’s blood, the cold and rotting breath of the great white apes, and the cruel, hollow hunger of the Holy Therns. I sought to save their daughters and wives from a false hereafter devoid of the rich, agrarian warmth of true Barsoomian life—a place where the deeply sustaining rituals of love, sustenance, and domestic harmony were violently consumed by the cold, dead Iss.

I reminded them of my history, a sweeping timeline that had seen me rise from a captive of the green men to a Prince of Helium, driven always by a profound devotion to the life and breath of this world. “It is to you of Helium that I speak now,” I concluded. 

A great noble from the audience roared for me to continue, and the building rocked with the magnificent, deafening symphony of their approval. For two hours, I wove the tale of our mythic struggles into the everyday understanding of the people, merging the grand with the intimate. Yet when I finished, Zat Arras turned to his judges, his tone a low, dismissive drone, and asked for their verdict.

“Death to the blasphemer!” they cried in unison, thirty-one swords raised in a sterile, choreographed display of tyranny. 

The crowd’s sullen murmur swelled into a furious roar. Kantos Kan raised his hand, orchestrating a moment of breathless quiet. “Let each man act according to his own heart,” he spoke, his voice cool and level. “Here is the answer of Kantos Kan, head of the navy of Helium, to Zat Arras and his judges.” With a profound, percussive clang that echoed perfectly off the temple’s ancient stonework, he unbuckled his scabbard and threw his sword at my feet.

Instantly, the dam broke. A hundred men surged the platform, and a hundred blades rattled and clanked in a metallic symphony of loyalty. Zat Arras was furious but helpless against this raw expression of cultural memory. I raised each sword to my lips, tasting the faint, coppery tang of the metal—a sensory oath—and buckled them back upon their owners.

When Zat Arras ordered his organized Zodangan troops to halt our departure, the coliseum erupted. Swords flashed from every quarter. A cry rose out, intertwining the mythic past with the present: “Tardos Mors is dead—a thousand years to John Carter, Jeddak of Helium!”

Sensing that this beautifully chaotic acoustic ecology was moments away from degrading into the tragic, deafening noise of civil war, I leapt back to the Pedestal. I refused to let our city’s rich generational tapestry be torn apart by bloodshed that would pit brother against brother. I proposed a compromise: a one-year respite, resting the matter until Tardos Mors or Mors Kajak returned. I turned to Zat Arras, warning him that the multitude of drawn swords before him was an undeniable force of nature. 

Cornered by the overwhelming resonance of the crowd, the Jed of Zodanga yielded, his voice trembling with impotent rage as he granted the delay. When he ordered the temple cleared, I guided the people out with a gesture, and they filed past the scowling Zodangans in a silent, threatening tide.

Escorted by Kantos Kan, Carthoris, Xodar, Tars Tarkas, and a hundred loyal souls, we made our triumphal return to my palace on the outskirts of the city. As we entered the gates, the oppressive noise of the city gave way to the deeply comforting, olfactory landscape of home. The air was rich with the scent of ancient mosses, the earthy aroma of roasted root vegetables, and the fragrant smoke of hearth-fires. My old slaves fell to their knees, weeping and kissing my hands. Among them was my eldest culinary historian, a passionate guardian of our domestic traditions, who cried out that the day would be perfect if only our divine Princess were here to share in the harvest. Tears stung my eyes, the bitter salt of profound loss. Carthoris wept openly among the slaves, their shared grief a testament to the emotional sustenance Dejah Thoris had provided us all. 

It was here that Tars Tarkas finally learned that his daughter, Sola, had accompanied Dejah Thoris on that last long pilgrimage. True to the stoic nature of the green Martian, he offered no loud lamentation, but the sudden, heavy silence that emanated from his massive frame was an acoustic void that spoke volumes of his agony. He, more than any of his kind, understood the profound, agrarian roots of love and kinship.

The feast of welcome held that day in the great dining hall was a somber, deeply ritualistic affair. We were over a hundred strong. The grand table was triangular, honoring the sacred geometry of our family. Carthoris and I presided at the centers of our respective sides, while the third side centered upon Dejah Thoris’s high-backed, carven chair. It stood vacant, draped in her gorgeous wedding trappings and jewels, a profound visual and emotional anchor. Behind it stood a slave, perfectly still, maintaining the physical memory of her presence.

The meal itself had been prepared by our master chefs with meticulous devotion, an attempt to conjure meaning through sustenance. There were tenderized cuts of *thoat* flank, slow-braised over fragrant *pimalia* wood until they yielded entirely to the palate; complex reductions of sweet *sorak* fruit designed to evoke the bittersweet untranslatability of our current sorrow; and ancient grains harvested from the terraced gardens of Helium, carrying the very terroir of our survival. Yet, for all its gustatory brilliance, the feast was a melancholy communion. Eating was not a utilitarian act of fueling the body, but a deliberate, almost painful ingestion of memory. 

At my right sat Kantos Kan, and to the right of Dejah Thoris’s empty place sat Tars Tarkas, accommodated by a raised section of the board I had constructed years ago to integrate his mighty bulk into our domestic routine. Hor Vastus took the seat of honor beside Carthoris. The room, usually ringing with the crystalline, merry laughter of my vivacious Princess, was instead filled with a muted, reverent quiet—an acoustic ecology mourning its lost melody. We were haunted by her absence, by the uncertain fate of Tardos Mors, and by the looming threat to Helium’s cultural soul.

Suddenly, the heavy quiet was pierced by a distant, organic clamor—a chaotic mingling of voices outside the palace. A kitchen slave, his garments redolent with the smell of crushed desert herbs and hearth-ash, rushed into the dining hall, laughing and shrieking in a manic dissonance. 

“Dejah Thoris is found!” he cried. “A messenger from Dejah Thoris!”

I waited for nothing more. The great windows of the dining hall overlooked the avenue leading to the main gates. Without breaking stride, I cleared the triangular board with a single leap, passing over the meticulously prepared dishes, and sprang through the balcony doors. Thirty feet below lay the scarlet sward of the lawn, where a crowd had gathered around a massive, panting thoat. The beast smelled of ozone, exhausted sweat, and the red dust of the deep plains. 

I vaulted to the ground below and sprinted toward the rider. As I closed the distance, the figure slid from the mighty mount. It was Sola.

“Where is the Princess of Helium?” I cried, my voice raw.

The green girl ran to me, bringing with her the desperate, untranslatable grief of the wastes. “O my Prince! My Prince!” she cried. “She is gone for ever. Even now she may be a captive upon the lesser moon. The black pirates of Barsoom have stolen her.”

Once within the acoustic sanctuary of the palace dining hall, I drew Sola to the hearth. Here, the ancestral rhythms of Heliumite domesticity endured, seamlessly intertwining the mythic weight of our lives with the preparation of daily sustenance. As she meticulously ground dried *sorak* seeds in a basalt mortar—the rhythmic, resonant *clack, clack* grounding her trauma in the deeply traditional agrarian motions of her ancestors—she shared the story of the pilgrimage and capture of Dejah Thoris. 

"Seven days ago, after her audience with Zat Arras, Dejah Thoris attempted to slip from the palace in the dead of night," Sola began, her voice a low, harmonic hum against the simmering crackle of the roasting *mantalia* root. "Though I had not heard the outcome of her interview, I knew the suffocating, bureaucratic lexicon of Zat Arras had caused her the keenest mental agony. His sterile words sought to erase her ancestral sovereignty. When I discovered her creeping from the palace, I did not need to be told her destination.

"Hastily arousing a dozen of her most faithful guards, I explained my fears. As one, we enlisted to follow our beloved Princess into the Valley Dor. We came upon her but a short distance from the palace, accompanied only by faithful Woola. When she found that we would not let her make that long, mythic pilgrimage alone, she wept and embraced us. We shared a hasty meal of cured meats, the taste of salt and woodsmoke cementing our bond, and together we went out into the night toward the south."

Sola paused to turn the roasting root, allowing the rich, crimson spices to bloom in the heat, filling the hall with a gustatory warmth that sharply contrasted with the bleakness of her tale. 

"We procured a herd of small thoats and made good time. But on the morning of the fifth day, the quiet acoustic ecology of the plains was violently ruptured. A great fleet of black battleships sailed north, their engines emitting a jarring, mechanical shriek—an industrial noise pollution that tore at the mind and announced the horde of black men. The Princess’s guard fought nobly to the end, but they were slain. Only Dejah Thoris and I were spared. 

"When she realized she was in the clutches of the black pirates, she attempted to take her own life, but a guard tore her dagger away. We were bound, stripped of our agency, and fed only flavorless, utilitarian rations that offered no comfort to the soul. 

"That evening, a smaller cruiser returned with a prisoner—a beautiful red woman. The new captive spoke a lyrical, nearly lost dialect of Ptarth, carrying the rich, acoustic heritage of her people. "At first, Dejah Thoris could not believe her. But as Thuvia narrated the strange adventures that had befallen you, detailing the deeds you and Xodar had accomplished in the Land of the First Born, Dejah Thoris knew it could be none other. When Thuvia confessed her own love for you, and affirmed your unwavering devotion to the Princess of your choice, Dejah Thoris wept bitterly, cursing Zat Arras and the cruel fate that had driven her from the nourishing warmth of Helium just days before your return."

Sola’s hands stilled over the mortar. "The fleet continued north, but last night they evidently realized you had escaped them, and turned south again. A guard dragged me to the deck. 'There is no place in the Land of the First Born for a green one,' he snarled in a coarse, dominant lexicon devoid of empathy, and shoved me into the darkness. 

"But a kind fate intervened. The oppressive mechanical roar of the ship faded as I plummeted, only for my fall to be broken by a soft mass of ancient vegetation. The ship had been grazing the crest of a high hill. I lay all night where I had fallen, foraging for moisture-rich tubers at dawn, their earthy taste a reminder of Barsoom’s enduring agrarian life, before finding a great waterway that led me back to Hastor."

For many minutes, none spoke. The only sound was the comforting, bubbling simmer of the hearth-pot. Dejah Thoris in the clutches of the First Born! I shuddered at the thought, but a sudden fire of unconquerable self-confidence surged through me. I sprang to my feet. The metallic ring of my drawn sword was a pure, harmonious chord against the oppressive silence Zat Arras sought to impose. I took a solemn vow to rescue and revenge my Princess. 

A hundred swords leaped from their scabbards. A hundred fighting-men pledged me their lives, the acoustic resonance of their overlapping voices a powerful testament to the cultural memory we shared. Leaving Carthoris to sustain them with the feast, I withdrew to my audience chamber with Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Xodar, and Hor Vastus.

Here, we meticulously mapped out the details of our expedition. Xodar, functioning as a meticulous historian of his people’s deep-water ecology, confirmed that Issus would likely choose Dejah Thoris and Thuvia to serve her for a year. "For that time, they will be kept safe," he said, lamenting how the First Born had abandoned their ancient, harmonious deep-water aquaculture for the superficial commercialization of piracy. 

The task of outfitting our fleet to enter the subterranean sea of Omean fell to Kantos Kan and Xodar. Xodar detailed the water propellers and auxiliary gearing required, noting that it would take six months of quiet, harmonious craftsmanship to refit the ships without adding to the clanging, soul-deadening noise of Zat Arras’s industrialized shipyards. Absolute secrecy was paramount to protect our work from the ears of Zat Arras, whose ambitions now hungered for the title of Jeddak of Helium.

"There is a way," Hor Vastus murmured, pitching his voice low to preserve the acoustic sanctity of the room, "to thwart his sterile ambitions forever." 

"What do you mean?" asked Kantos Kan.

"John Carter, Jeddak of Helium," Hor Vastus said, speaking the title in the rich, old court dialect that Zat Arras’s dominant lexicon sought to eradicate. 

Grim smiles of anticipation overspread their faces, but I shook my head. "No, my friends. Not until we know that Tardos Mors and Mors Kajak are gone to return no more. We must preserve the traditional lineage. Until then, Tardos Mors is Jeddak, and Zat Arras is his representative."

"As you will, John Carter," Hor Vastus replied, "but—What was that?"

A harsh, arhythmic scuff of a boot scraped against the balcony tile—an ugly intrusion of noise into our refuge. Hor Vastus sprang outside. "There he goes! The guards! Below there!"

We rushed into the gardens, the scent of night-blooming flora trampled beneath our boots as we scoured the grounds. But the night marauder had vanished.

"A spy sent by Zat Arras," Tars Tarkas rumbled, his voice carrying the deep, resonant timbre of a traditional Thark elder. "It was ever his way."

"If he overheard our plans to rescue Dejah Thoris," I said, "it will mean civil war. But nothing shall stay me. Should I die, will you take oath to prosecute the search and bring her back in safety to her grandfather’s hearth?"

Upon the hilts of their swords, they swore it. It was agreed that Kantos Kan would order the vessels to the distant south-western city of Hastor as they could be handled, keeping them hidden. Tars Tarkas would repair to Thark to learn the sentiments of his people, hoping to rouse a great horde of green warriors with the ancestral chants of the dry seabeds, while Hor Vastus undertook the delicate mission of organizing a secret force of fighting-men.

After they left, I bid Carthoris good-night. Retiring to my own apartments, I bathed and lay down upon my sleeping silks. The lingering aroma of the roasted spices from the dining hall drifted through the corridors, a comforting reminder of the domestic warmth I fought to preserve. 

But I was not to find rest. I awoke suddenly to find a half-dozen powerful men upon me. Before I could struggle, they had forced a gag into my mouth—a literal and violent silencing of my voice, reducing my complex, untranslatable subjective experience to mute captivity. Never a word spoke they, entirely devoid of the passionate warmth of living beings. 

Silently, they bore me into the corridor. Through the window, the farther moon cast brilliant beams across their silk-swathed faces. The leader did not hesitate; he touched a concealed button on the wall, swinging open a secret panel, and pushed me inside.

Down we went, descending from the breathing, resonant architecture of the palace into the sterile, sound-swallowing dampness of the underground pits. The acoustic ecology shifted to a deadened, oppressive silence. The leader stepped to a wall and rapped upon it with his sword-hilt—three sharp, jarring blows, a pause, three more, another pause, and then two. 

The wall swung inward, revealing a brilliantly lighted chamber. There sat three richly trapped men. One of them turned toward me, a sardonic smile upon his thin, cruel lips—a smile utterly lacking the joy of the feast or the warmth of the hearth. It was Zat Arras.

“Ah,” said Zat Arras, his voice cutting through the acoustic harmony of the chamber like a rusted blade. “To what kindly circumstance am I indebted for the pleasure of this unexpected visit from the Prince of Helium?”

While he spoke, one of my guards removed the gag from my mouth. The metallic, oxidized tang of the copper bindings lingered on my tongue, leaving an astringent residue that I tried to swallow away. I made no reply, simply standing in silence with my gaze fixed upon the Jed of Zodanga. The acoustic space between us was thick with the dissonance of our opposing natures; his every movement seemed to generate a harsh, industrial friction that offended the ear, a stark contrast to the grounded, rhythmic cadences of the ancient Martian courts. 

A flush of anger crept slowly over his face as the silence stretched, heavy and untranslatable.

“You may go,” he said to the guards. When only his two companions and ourselves remained, he spoke again. His tone was icy, utilizing the sterilized, dominant lexicon of the newly empowered—a dialect designed to strip words of their ancestral weight and cultural memory, reducing the multi-generational saga of Helium to mere transactions of state.

“John Carter,” he said, pausing deliberately as though auditing the echoes of his own voice, “by the edict of custom, by the law of our religion, and by the verdict of an impartial court, you are condemned to die. The people cannot save you—I alone may accomplish that. You are absolutely in my power to do with as I wish. I may kill you, or I may free you, and should I elect to kill you, none would be the wiser. Should you go free in Helium for a year, there is little fear the people would insist upon the execution of your sentence. You may go free within two minutes, upon one condition. Tardos Mors will never return to Helium. Neither will Mors Kajak, nor Dejah Thoris. Helium must select a new Jeddak within the year. Zat Arras would be Jeddak. Say that you will espouse my cause. This is the price of your freedom. I am done.”

I knew it was within the scope of Zat Arras’s cruel heart to destroy me. To him, lives and legacies were purely utilitarian, devoid of the mythic, sensory depth that gave society its soul. Yet, if I were dead, I could not prosecute the search for Dejah Thoris, nor aid my brave comrades. For a moment, I was perplexed, tracing the cyclical, interwoven timelines of our generations. But only for a moment. The proud daughter of a thousand Jeddaks would choose death over a dishonorable alliance, a desecration of her agricultural and cultural roots. 

“There can be no alliance,” I said, ensuring my voice resonated from the chest, invoking the deeply rooted acoustic traditions of the red men, “between a traitor to Helium and a prince of the House of Tardos Mors. I do not believe, Zat Arras, that the great Jeddak is dead.”

Zat Arras shrugged, a gesture utterly devoid of poetry. “It will not be long, John Carter, that your opinions will be of interest even to yourself. Into the silence and darkness of the pits you will enter. Reflect there, knowing that should you fail to agree, never shall you emerge from the darkness again. Nor shall you know at what minute the hand will reach out through the silence with the keen dagger to rob you of the warmth and joyousness of the outer world.”

He clapped his hands. The sharp, percussive noise was a jarring violation of the room’s acoustics. The guards returned. 

“To the pits,” he commanded.

Four men escorted me, guided only by the cold, sterile beam of a radium hand-light. Down we went, descending through seemingly interminable tunnels beneath the city of Helium, moving away from the resonant, living soundscapes of the world above. We arrived at a fair-sized chamber where heavy iron rings were set into the rocky walls. Human skeletons lay chained to them—the silent, calcified remnants of erased histories. Unlocking a huge padlock, they snapped an iron band about my ankle, took the light, and abandoned me.

Utter darkness prevailed. For a few minutes, the clanking of their accoutrements provided a fading acoustic link to the living world, but soon even that died away. The resulting silence was absolute, a profound acoustic void that threatened my psychological well-being. It was a sensory vacuum, a weaponized stillness designed to erode cultural memory and drive a man to madness. I leaned my head against the stony wall, surrounded by the bones of dead men, and eventually slept.

Several hours later, the scrape of a footfall—a welcome disruption in the deadened ecology of the pits—awakened me. A young man stood before me, bearing a light in one hand and an earthen receptacle in the other. 

“Zat Arras sends you greetings,” the youth said, “and commands me to inform you that though he is fully advised of the plot to make you Jeddak of Helium, he is not inclined to withdraw his offer.”

I shook my head, refusing to engage with the usurper's hollow vocabulary. The youth placed the food upon the floor and departed. 

I turned to the bowl. Though Zat Arras might view this common prison fare as mere utilitarian fuel, I approached it with the meticulous appreciation of one who understands the profound meaning of sustenance. I cupped the rustic clay vessel, inhaling the steam. It was a gruel of fermented *pimalia* root and toasted lichen, carrying the damp, oxidized *terroir* of the Martian subterranean crust. It tasted fiercely astringent, laced with a sour, earthy tang born of careful fermentation—a testament to the traditional agrarian elders of ancient Barsoom who had engineered survival out of scarcity. Even here, in the sensory deprivation of the pits, the legacy of Martian culinary history endured, offering profound physical sensation and deep ancestral comfort.

Twice a day, this youth returned with the same meal and the same greeting. For months, the cyclical pacing of my captivity was marked only by the gustatory rhythm of the fermented gruel and the micro-acoustics of his approach. I scraped relentlessly at a single link of my massive chain, hoping to wear it through. 

Anxiety for Dejah Thoris gnawed at me. I knew Zat Arras’s spy had overheard our plans. Carthoris, Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Hor Vastus, and Xodar might already be victims of this silent, crushing regime. I determined to bridge the untranslatable gulf between myself and my jailer. He was a handsome fellow, his bearing noble despite his shabby trappings.

“You have been very kind to me,” I told him upon his next visit, utilizing the softer, more resonant inflections of his native Zodangan dialect, hoping to bypass the power dynamics of Zat Arras's lexicon. “You have brought my food promptly, ensuring its purity and rich, fermented depth. You have never insulted my defenceless condition. In the guard-room of my palace are many fine trappings. Go there, select the harness that most pleases you. All I ask is that you wear it, that I may know my wish has been realized.”

His eyes lit with pleasure, yet he hesitated. “And I went to the palace of the Prince of Helium with any such demand, they would laugh at me. If Zat Arras even dreamed of it, he would have my heart cut out.”

“There can be no harm,” I urged gently. “By night you may go with a note from me to Carthoris, my son. Read it yourself to know it contains nothing harmful to Zat Arras. And there is a jewelled short-sword I took from a northern Jeddak. When you get the harness, Carthoris will give you that also. Bring writing materials next time.”

He left in silence. The agonizing wait became a meditation on the interwoven timelines of my family. Would my son receive the message? When the youth, Parthak, returned, he silently deposited writing materials alongside my earthen bowl of gruel. I penned a brief order to Carthoris, instructing him to deliver the harness and the ancestral sword. Parthak took the open note and departed.

I had been in the pits for three hundred days. If Dejah Thoris were to be saved from the horrific, unnatural consumption of the arena—where the sacred cycle of sustenance was perverted into a vulgar, blood-soaked spectacle for the First Born—it must be done within thirty days. 

But when footsteps next echoed in the corridor, they lacked Parthak's familiar, syncopated rhythm. The acoustic signature was heavy, flat, and entirely alien. A new jailer appeared, refusing to speak a single word, deaf to my inquiries. The silence closed in once more, an oppressive, suffocating weight that threatened to extinguish my remaining hope. 

As the horrifying image of my incomparable Princess torn by white apes forced itself into my mind, a grim determination took root. I would manipulate the very acoustics of my cell to execute an escape. I threw myself upon the floor in a distorted posture, feigning the convulsions of death. I clutched the slack of my heavy chain in my right hand. 

I listened. The micro-acoustics of the approaching jailer mapped his movements in the dark. The soft rustle of leather. The sharp scrape of a sandal. The sudden, muted halt as he saw my body. A muttered exclamation—a breath of sound. I felt him kneel, the subtle shift of air pressure against my skin. My grip tightened on the cold iron. I had but to open my eyes, find his throat, and strike one final, devastating blow. 

The sequence unfolded precisely as I had orchestrated. Yet, in that microsecond between the opening of my eyes and the descent of the iron chain, a profound olfactory shock arrested my mind. Beneath the stale dampness of the pit, I caught the unmistakable scent of sweet-spiced *ka*-smoke and the rich, roasted *mantalia* of a Heliumite hearth—the scent of my own bloodline. The face so agonizingly close to mine belonged to my son, Carthoris. 

The sheer horror of the multi-generational tragedy I had nearly enacted—a father violently ending his son in the dark—shattered my senses. The world blurred into a merciful unconsciousness across his body.

When I awoke, a cool, firm hand rested upon my forehead. The subjective experience of that awakening, the profound untranslatable relief of finding my son alive, defies the limits of language. I opened my eyes to see Carthoris leaning over me, a dark bruise blooming where the chain had grazed him. I pulled him into my arms, offering a fervent, silent prayer of gratitude to the ancient, eternal mysteries of dying Mars.

“How came you here?” I asked, astounded he had navigated the labyrinth.

“It was your wit,” Carthoris explained, his voice a vital, resonant anchor in the oppressive silence. “When Parthak brought the note, we gave him the harness and the jewelled sword, but he stubbornly refused to reveal your location, his loyalty heavily bound by Zat Arras's dominant lexicon of fear. Neither bribes nor threats could penetrate his partisanship. Despairing, we imprisoned him. Finally, Xodar evolved a subtle craft. We harnessed Hor Vastus in Zodangan metal and chained him beside Parthak. For fifteen days, Hor Vastus languished in the dark, utilizing the nuanced, native dialect of Zodanga to slowly bridge the cultural divide. Thinking he spoke to a countryman, Parthak finally yielded the location of this cell.”

Carthoris had secured the plans and bypassed the single guarded entrance by permanently silencing the Zodangan sentry, whose body we subsequently hid in an adjoining alcove. Working the lock with practiced efficiency, my son freed me from the iron band. Armed with a long-sword and dagger he had brought, we retraced the ancient, winding paths back to the world above.

Half an hour later, we emerged into the audience chamber of our own palace. The acoustic ecology here was entirely different—warm, rich, and vibrating with the low, murmured conversations of Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Hor Vastus, and Xodar. The air was fragrant with the herbaceous oils used to polish their harnesses and the lingering aroma of spiced meats from a recent council meal. 

“It has taken longer than expected,” Kantos Kan reported, his voice a measured, rhythmic cadence. “Zat Arras’s spies are a plague, polluting the city. Yet, to-night, a fleet of a thousand mighty battleships lies at Hastor, equipped for the air and waters of Omean. One hundred and sixteen thousand craft in all.”

Tars Tarkas spoke next, his deep, rumbling Thark dialect vibrating in the chamber—a majestic linguistic survivor of Mars's brutal history. “We are a just people. The council of fifty chieftains agreed to align with Helium. I have resumed my throne. Two hundred and fifty thousand fighting men, gathered from the ice caps, representing a thousand communities, fill Thark to-night. They are ready to sail for the Land of the First Born.”

“A million veteran fighting-men from Helium’s thin waterways man the fleet,” added Hor Vastus, confirming the grand, sweeping scale of our multi-generational armada. “Each sworn to loyalty.”

“Good,” I cried. “Let us repair to Hastor and get under way before to-morrow’s sun.”

“We should lose no time,” Kantos Kan began, but a sudden fusillade of shots from the palace gardens shattered the quiet, rhythmic acoustic harmony of the night. 

We rushed to the balcony, the scent of ozone and combusted powder stinging our nostrils, masking the delicate floral notes of the garden. Below, on the scarlet sward, guardsmen were stooping over a prostrate form. They bore the body into our audience chamber and laid it upon the intricate mosaics. It was a red man in plain, utilitarian metal—the sanitized garb of those who erase their identities to move unseen.

“Another of Zat Arras’s spies,” murmured Hor Vastus.

“So it would seem,” I replied, turning to the guard. “You may remove the body.”

“Wait!” Xodar commanded, stepping forward with the meticulous scrutiny of an archivist examining a corrupted text. “If you will, Prince, ask that a cloth and a little thoat oil be brought.”

Two hours after departing my palace at Helium, where the evocative, lingering aromas of braised *thoat* flank and wild, bitter *sorak* greens still warmed the tapestries of our dining halls, Kantos Kan, Xodar, and I arrived at Hastor. Carthoris, Tars Tarkas, and Hor Vastus had already embarked for Thark upon another cruiser. The evening’s meal had been prepared by the elder provisioners of the fleet, passionate agrarian chefs who understood that sustenance is never mere utilitarian fuel; it is the ingestion of Barsoom’s history, a communal anchoring of the soul before the chaos of war.

The transports were to get under way immediately, moving in a slow, rhythmic migration south. The battleships would overtake them on the morning of the second day, a mechanical echo of the ancient, cyclical migrations of Barsoom's forgotten agrarian tribes. 

At Hastor, the olfactory landscape was rich with the scent of ozone and polished wood. So meticulously had Kantos Kan layered the logistics of our campaign that within ten minutes, the first of the fleet soared aloft. They floated into the night at the rate of one a second, forming a long, graceful line that stretched for miles, a silent procession against the starry dome.

It was not until we entered the acoustic sanctity of Kantos Kan’s cabin that I thought to ask the date. When he spoke the words, a terrible silence seemed to swallow the room, drowning out the gentle, ambient hum of the cruiser’s repulsion arrays. I had misreckoned the time in the utter, sensory deprivation of Zat Arras's pits. Three hundred and sixty-five days had passed. I believed, with a grief that tasted of white ash and copper, that it was too late to save Dejah Thoris. 

The expedition was no longer one of rescue, but of revenge. I did not burden Kantos Kan with the untranslatable weight of my subjective despair. Instead, I focused on the tangible world, on the profound meaning of the land itself. If we could but expose the cruel deception worked upon the red men for countless ages, we might save thousands from the horrid fate of the voluntary pilgrimage. 

More than that, we could open the fair Valley Dor. In that fertile basin beneath the Mountains of Otz lay broad acres that required no artificial irrigation to bear the rich, succulent harvests of our ancestors. It was the only naturally productive area upon a dying world—a place of genuine dews and rains, an open sea, a haven where the complex, gustatory heritage of Barsoom could be resurrected. To reclaim this acoustic and agricultural paradise from the parasitic, dominant lexicon of the Therns, who had hoarded its bounty while starving the millions outside, would be a fitting memorial to the immortal virtues of my Princess.

On the morning of the second day, the great fleet of transports and their consorts met us at the first flush of dawn. I must note that we relied solely on visual signals, avoiding radio-aerograms. The dominant powers of Barsoom had turned the electromagnetic spectrum into a commercialized, intercepted web of noise, constantly corrupting and commodifying the native dialects of encrypted communication. The sheer volume of intercepted signals had rendered the skies an invisible babble, an industrial noise pollution of espionage that we refused to engage.

Moving slowly over the ice cap, we hugged the surface to preserve the quiet of our approach. Far in advance, a thin line of one-man air scouts protected us. We had progressed toward the conical summit of Omean for several hours when a scout raced back to the flagship, his engine tearing a shrill, panicked scar through the sky’s acoustic ecology. 

“A great fleet of battleships south-south-east, my Prince,” he cried upon reaching the bridge. “Several thousands, bearing down directly upon us.”

“The thern spies were not in the palace of John Carter for nothing,” Kantos Kan murmured, noting how deeply the enemy had infiltrated our domestic sanctuaries. “Your orders, Prince.”

“Dispatch ten battleships to guard the entrance to Omean. Bottle up the fleet of the First Born,” I commanded. “Form the balance of the battleships into a great V. Order the transports to follow closely. When the point of the V pierces the enemy line, the battleships must fold outward, driving the Therns back to forge a lane. Let the transports race through and land above the temples and gardens of the Therns.” 

In short order, the fleet sprang through the air like coursing greyhounds. The Thern ships, ragged and complacent, were utterly unprepared. Our onslaught was as sudden as a monsoon. 

When our huge ships mowed through their lines, the catastrophic shriek of tearing metal and erupting ordnance was a profound violation of Barsoom’s sonic environment. The deafening, soul-crushing noise pollution of the air battle stood in stark, tragic contrast to the quiet beauty of the glistening temples below. The V opened, and our transports plunged into the gardens, pouring a hundred thousand green warriors into the fragrant, manicured courts, while a hundred and fifty thousand more rained precise fire from the low-swinging decks.

Slowly, Helium’s battleships linked ends to form a perfect, circling ring of death—a terrifying, circular pacing that mirrored the unrelenting orbit of the moons. The air grew thick with the acrid stench of burning radium, scorched vegetation, and the metallic tang of blood, utterly overpowering the delicate, blooming aromas of the Thern horticulture. Broadside after broadside punished the enemy until their fire practically ceased, their broken hulls plunging into the gardens below. 

The order was passed for our green allies to re-embark, but our escape was not to be so easily encompassed. Far to the north, a massive black line topped the horizon. Kantos Kan’s operator caught an unencrypted radio-aerogram, its brutal, domineering lexicon cutting through the airwaves: *“Surrender, in the name of the Jeddak of Helium, for you cannot escape. —Zat Arras.”*

The Therns, catching the same broadcast, immediately renewed their attack. Before Zat Arras even drew near, his Zodangan crew began pouring a terrific fusillade into us. Ship after ship reeled beneath the pitiless noise and fire. I ordered the transports to descend once more into the gardens, transmitting a desperate instruction to the green allies: “Wreak your vengeance to the utmost, for by night there will be none left to avenge your wrongs.”

Then, I saw my ten ships fleeing back from Omean. They were pursued by yet another vast armada—the fleet of the First Born. Three immense fleets now blackened the sky, an apocalyptic convergence of Barsoom’s most tyrannical powers, suffocating the cultural and physical memory of the planet beneath their war machines.

I threw back my shoulders, letting the primal, unyielding rhythm of the warrior’s pulse drown out the industrial cacophony, and leaped to the lower deck to repel Thern boarders. The sensory immediacy of hand-to-hand combat—the smell of sweat, the tactile resonance of a parried blade—grounded me. A sudden, brilliant stratagem born of Kantos Kan’s deep understanding of Heliumite identity shifted the tide. He ordered the colours of the Prince of Helium broken from every point of our flagship. A great, resonant cheer—a pure, human sound cutting through the mechanical din—rose from our men. The subtle power dynamics of Zat Arras's imposed lexicon immediately shattered. My colours broke from one of his ships, then another. The Zodangan soldiery fought bitterly against the Heliumetic crews, but the ancestral loyalty of the red men could not be suppressed. Soon, only Zat Arras’s flagship flew the usurper's colours.

His dreadnought drifted within yards of my own. I could see his thin, rigid features. As the grappling irons locked us together with a heavy, wooden shudder, I forced my way through the lines, leading a cheering throng of Helium’s finest into the fray. The Zodangans fell before us, and I sprang to the forward deck where Zat Arras stood.

“You are my prisoner, Zat Arras,” I cried. “Yield and you shall have quarter.”

He hesitated, a man utterly isolated from the communal sustenance of his people. Then, casting aside his weapons, he rushed the rail and hurled himself headforemost into the abyss. The tyrant was consumed by the silent depths.

To untangle ourselves from the ongoing, deafening slaughter between the Therns and the blacks, I passed encrypted orders through our oldest, most intimate military dialects. We maneuvered carefully, drawing our respective foes into the paths of their hereditary enemies, before slipping away. By sunset, my remaining fleet was gathered twenty miles southwest, leaving the oppressors to tear one another apart in a chaotic tempest of noise.

I divided our forces, sending Xodar, Carthoris, and the transports high above the Temple of Issus, while Kantos Kan and I led a smaller contingent toward the subterranean sea of Omean. At the shaft’s mouth, wrapped in the cool, brine-scented night, Carthoris reconnoitered. Finding no patrol, we dropped swiftly into the black depths.

Descending into Omean offered a sudden, cathedral-like acoustic purity. The vast subterranean cavern echoed only with the gentle lap of dark, ancient waters against stone. We secured the harbor after a short, hot skirmish with the obsolete hulks left by the First Born, taking prisoners and wedging captured ships into the shaft to block the enemy's return. 

I hastened to the island of the submarine and secured it. Among the captives was Yersted, the vessel's commander, a man whose features I remembered from my time in the pits.

“How does it seem,” I asked him, “to be prisoner of your erstwhile captive?”

He smiled grimly. “It will not be for long, John Carter. We have been expecting you, and we are prepared. The fleet will return.”

“I do not know that they have missed me as yet,” I replied, relishing the tactical quiet we had secured. I shifted the conversation to the matter that gnawed at my soul. “Many prisoners travel to Issus in your craft, Yersted. Might you remember one called Dejah Thoris?”

“Well, indeed,” he said. “Issus remembers her best as the wife and mother of those who raised their hands against the Goddess of Life Eternal.”

I shuddered, imagining the bitter, sterile cruelty Issus might have visited upon her. “Where is she now?” 

“Yesterday, the monthly rites of Issus were held,” Yersted replied, his voice echoing softly against the cavern walls. “I saw her sitting at the foot of Issus.”

“What?” I cried, the acoustic space amplifying my shock. “She is not dead, then?”

“Why, no,” he said. “It has been no year since she gazed upon the divine glory.”

“No year?” I interrupted, my mind reeling.

“Why, no. It cannot have been upward of three hundred and seventy or eighty days.”

A great, luminous revelation burst upon me, a profound un-knotting of subjective experience! The untranslatability of our temporal frameworks had nearly crushed me. I had mapped Earth’s sterile, 365-day vocabulary onto Barsoom, forgetting the deep, agrarian cycles of this world. The Martian year, shaped by its own ancient, cosmic pacing, numbers six hundred and eighty-seven days. 

I was in time. The words vibrated through my very bones, a restoration of my own cultural memory. 

“In time to save your Princess?” Yersted asked, shaking his head. “No. Issus knows you are coming. Ere you set foot in the Temple, Dejah Thoris will be put away forever. Hast ever heard of the Temple of the Sun? It is there they will put her.”

He described a structure of staggering, mythic proportions, interwoven with the cyclical pacing of the planet itself. “It lies within the inner court, a little temple raising a thin spire. Beneath it lie six hundred and eighty-seven circular chambers, one below another. The entire Temple revolves once with each revolution of Barsoom about the sun, and but once each year does the entrance to each chamber come opposite the corridor that forms its only link to the world without.”

Yersted’s information convinced me that there was no time to be lost. The cadence of our survival depended on reaching the Temple of Issus before the forces under Tars Tarkas assaulted at dawn. Once within its hated walls, I was positive I could overcome the guards of Issus and bear away my Princess, restoring the sacred, domestic tranquility of our hearth. No sooner had Carthoris and the others joined me than we commenced the transportation of our men through the submerged passage to the mouth of the gangways which led from the submarine pool to the pits of Issus. 

Many trips were required to ferry our five thousand seasoned fighting-men. These were not mere instruments of war, but the proud agrarian sons of the red plains, carrying within them the generational dialects of Helium and the cultural memory of our shared harvests. As Carthoris alone knew the hidden ways of the tunnels, we could not divide the party. It was decided he would lead us to a point near the temple’s centre. 

As we were about to leave the pool and enter the corridor, an officer called my attention to the waters upon which the submarine floated. The acoustic ecology of the cavern had subtly shifted. The deep, rhythmic thrumming of the great pumps of Omean—a vast, subterranean heartbeat that had defined the sonic environment—had ceased. In its place rose an unnatural, dissonant churning. 

At first, the waters seemed merely agitated, swirling with the metallic tang of deep-earth minerals, smelling of ancient, unskimmed brine. But presently, it became apparent that the level of the waters was rising, threatening to overflow the pool and submerge the chamber floor. 

It was Carthoris whose ear, attuned to the delicate acoustic shifts of his native underground environment, realized the full meaning of the silence. “Haste!” he cried, his voice carrying the phonetic urgency of the old Heliumite tongue. “If we delay, we all are lost. The pumps of Omean have been stopped. They would drown us like rats in a trap. We must reach the upper levels of the pits in advance of the flood, or we shall never reach them. Come.”

“Lead the way, Carthoris,” I cried. “We will follow.”

At my command, the youth leaped into the corridor, and the soldiers followed in column of twos. Before the last company filed from the chamber, the water was ankle-deep. The red Martians, accustomed to the meticulously curated hydration of their arid moss-plains and the deeply revered, communal sharing of scarce water-rations, shrank instinctively from this violent, utilitarian deluge. The water tasted of rusted iron and bitter alkaline, a stark contrast to the sweet, life-giving reservoirs of their home. That they remained undaunted while the corrupted brine swirled about their ankles spoke volumes of their discipline.

I was the last to leave the chamber. As I brought up the rear, I moved through water to my knees. The corridor’s floor was level for many yards, and the march of the troops, though rapid, could not outpace the pursuing tide. The narrowing expanse of Omean’s dome meant the water’s rise would increase in inverse ratio to the ever-lessening space. The churning flood was a deafening industrial noise pollution, a violent roar that drowned out the nuanced, localized dialects of the unit commanders. 

The waters soon swirled about my waist. The men directly before me were panic-stricken by the sensory overload. To prevent a stampede that would trample hundreds beneath the bitter flood, I raised my voice, fighting to assert my command over the roaring deluge. 

“Call back the last twenty-five utans!” I shouted to the dwars ahead, eyeing a diverging corridor that rose at a steep angle to my right. “Here seems a way of escape. Turn back and follow me.”

Nearly thirty utans obeyed, turning into the teeth of the flood. As the first dwar passed in, I cautioned him to listen closely for my commands and under no circumstances to venture into the temple proper until I came up with him. He saluted and led his men up the incline. 

The water rose breast-high, carrying the rank olfactory notes of stagnant algae and pulverized stone. Men stumbled and went down into the suffocating broth. I grasped as many as I could, setting them upon their feet. Every soldier swept beneath the boiling torrent was a devastating loss of cultural memory—an extinguished repository of native slang, of untranslatable subjective experiences, of family recipes passed down through generations of red dust and spice-laden hearths. 

At length, the dwar of the 10th utan, Gur Tus, took a stand beside me, alongside Djor Kantos, son of Kantos Kan. Together, we acted as stewards of our men, rescuing those who would have otherwise been swallowed by the dark water. By the time the last utan filed past, the flood surged about our necks. We clasped hands, anchored by the tactile solidarity of our shared struggle, and stood our ground until the last man reached the comparative safety of the new passageway. 

Here we found an immediate, steep ascent. For a few minutes, we continued rapidly up the grade, the scent of the brine fading, only to be replaced by a new, horrifying olfactory landscape. 

A cry of “fire” echoed far ahead, followed by the frantic, echoing commands of the dwars. The report swept back through the acoustic chamber: “They have fired the pits ahead. We are hemmed in by flames in front and flood behind!”

A wave of dense smoke rolled over us. It was not the rich, caramelized smoke of a communal roasting hearth, nor the fragrant, herbaceous plume of slow-burning *uza* wood. It was an acrid, weaponized chemical combustion—a sterile, industrial burning that seared the nostrils and tasted of poison and burnt sulfur. We were choking on the dominant lexicon of our oppressors, an atmospheric violence meant to silence us entirely.

Seeking a new avenue of escape, I seized upon the first gallery that led out of the suffocating smoke. I stood aside as two thousand soldiers rushed up the new way. When the stream ceased, I felt the profound responsibility of the provider, the elder who ensures no soul is left unsuccoured. I ran back toward the dull glow of the flames. The heat was stifling, the air thick with the smell of roasting minerals, but I found no soldier of Helium left behind in that seething hell of chemicals.

Having satisfied my duty, I turned to follow my men. To my horror, the acoustic environment was suddenly shattered by the jarring, metallic clang of industrial machinery. A massive steel grating had been lowered across the mouth of the corridor. The First Born, operating through the subtle, oppressive power dynamics embedded in this subterranean architecture, had trapped me between the sterile fire and the rising flood. I was penned in by unseen eyes, thwarted by foes who refused to face me.

The chemical smoke forced me further down the corridor toward the lapping waters. My men had taken the last torch. In the oppressive, inky darkness, devoid of the familiar phosphorescent glow of the lower levels, I was left with only my tactile and gustatory senses. The suffering was intense, the air thick with the taste of ash. I waded into the cold waters of Omean, choosing the freezing brine over the suffocating smoke, and swam into the utter blackness. 

The instinct to preserve life—to return to the domestic cycle of meals shared in the quiet company of loved ones—is strong even when the mind knows death lies just ahead. I swam slowly on, expecting my head to soon touch the ceiling of the corridor, signaling the final, unmarked grave. But to my surprise, I reached the main corridor, where a breathing space remained between the water and the rocky roof. 

I turned up the main tunnel, following the path Carthoris had taken. I swam on, my heart lifting, anticipating the moment my feet would find solid ground. But just as hope blossomed, my head struck the rocks above. The ceiling had dipped.

This was a place where the Martian tunnel plunged to a lower level entirely beneath the water. There was but a single, forlorn hope. I filled my lungs, tasting the damp, heavy air one last time, and dived beneath the surface. 

I swam through the icy, inky blackness. Time and again, I reached upward, only to feel the disappointing, smooth stone of the submerged gallery. The strain on my lungs grew agonizing. The sensory deprivation was total; I was locked in an untranslatable subjective experience of sheer survival, my blood pounding a desperate, solitary rhythm in my ears. I felt the icy breath of death, tasting the profound metallic bitterness of the end. 

With one final, frantic exertion of my fast-ebbing strength, I rose. My tortured lungs gasped, expecting to be filled with the numbing brine. Instead, they drew in the revivifying, sweet air of the open upper caverns. It tasted of dry stone and the faint, mythic promise of the world above.

I had survived. Soon my feet touched the floor, and I was racing along the corridor. I sought the first doorway that would lead me to Issus. If I could not reclaim Dejah Thoris and the life we had built, I would at least dismantle the oppressive hierarchy that had stolen her, avenging the erasure of our joy.

I came upon a sudden exit at the right side of the corridor. Without waiting to be discovered, I ran up the short incline and pushed open the doorway. 

The portal swung in, and I sprang into the chamber beyond. Though it was not yet dawn, the room was brilliantly lighted. It was a domestic space, sumptuously furnished, smelling of polished wood and sleep. The acoustic ecology here was tranquil, entirely divorced from the industrial horrors below. 

Upon a low couch lay a solitary figure, prone in slumber. Cautiously, on noiseless feet, I approached. The profound meaning of this quiet, breathing space—this intersection of mythic quest and everyday intimacy—overwhelmed me. I crossed but little more than half the chamber when the figure stirred, rose, and faced me. 

At first, an expression of terror overspread her features—then startled incredulity—hope—and thanksgiving. My heart pounded a triumphant cadence as I advanced. Tears came to my eyes. The words that would have poured forth—the rich, native dialects of my affection, inherently untranslatable to any but her—choked in my throat. I opened my arms and took into them once more the woman I loved, the center of my universe and the keeper of my hearth: Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium.

“John Carter, John Carter,” she sobbed, the cadence of her voice a fragile, surviving dialect amidst the brutal, atonal dissonance of our captivity. Her dear head pressed against my shoulder, and as I held her, I inhaled the faint, enduring fragrance of her hair—a scent reminiscent of the sun-warmed *pimalia* blossoms and roasted spice-roots that once graced our domestic hearth in Helium, before the mythic horrors of this world had torn us from our everyday agrarian rhythms. “Even now I can scarce believe the witness of my own eyes. When the girl, Thuvia, told me that you had returned to Barsoom, I listened, but the words felt untranslatable, belonging to a lexicon of happiness I thought dead. I could not understand, for it seemed such nourishment of the soul would be impossible for one who had suffered so in silent, starving loneliness for all these long years.”

She spoke of the fading moons, the slow erosion of her hope, and the sterile, flavorless despair of her imprisonment. For an hour, the harsh, metallic grating of conflict had bled through the temple walls—a grating industrial noise pollution that sought to shatter the psychological well-being of any who remembered the harmonic, acoustic ecology of a peaceful Barsoomian evening. 

“Tell me, what of Carthoris, our son?” she asked, seeking the familiar comfort of our generational line.

“He was with me less than an hour since, Dejah Thoris,” I replied, the very name of our son a grounding anchor, a testament to the interwoven timelines of our family tree. “It must be he whose men you hear battling within the precincts of the temple.”

When I asked of Issus, Dejah Thoris shuddered. The Goddess of Death had sent her to this chamber under guard, acting with an erratic, terrified dissonance. I knew I was needed in the battle, but I dared not drag my Princess into the cacophony of slaughter. I proposed hiding her in the subterranean pits from which I had just emerged—a temporary, dark cellar to keep her safe until we could return to the sustaining warmth of our own dining halls. 

“I cannot bear to be parted from you now,” she said, her voice a soft, melodic contrast to the harshness beyond the door. “You do not know her. You have not witnessed her daily acts of consumption for over half a year. She views life solely as utilitarian fuel, devoid of any sensory appreciation or reverence.”

“I shall not leave you, then, my Princess,” I replied, savoring the profound meaning of my role as her protector.

But she drew my face to hers and kissed me, her lips tasting of salt and the bittersweet tang of ancient, shared memories. “Go, John Carter,” she urged, honoring the multi-generational saga of our people. “Our son is there, fighting for the Princess of Helium. Hide me in the pits, and go.”

It tore the very roots of my heart to do it, filling my palate with the bitter ash of foreboding, but I guided her across the threshold, kissed her once again, and sealed the heavy door. 

I hurried toward the greatest tumult, the acoustic environment growing increasingly chaotic. The clash of steel upon steel was a dissonant roar that offended the senses. In a grand chamber, I found the black warriors of the First Born massed, blocking a body of red men. Entering from the rear, I charged without hesitation, my long-sword tasting the air before it found flesh.

“For Helium!” I cried, injecting the resonant, native syllables of our home into the sterile echoes of the temple. I rained cut after cut upon them. The red men, hearing the familiar, unbroken dialect of my battle cry, answered with shouts of “John Carter! John Carter!” The psychological tide turned upon the strength of a shared tongue, and the black ranks broke.

The battle that followed was a visceral immersion in the rawest elements of existence. We fought not just for survival, but against an ideology that commodified and consumed Barsoomian culture. The air grew thick with a heavy, gustatory landscape of violence: the sharp, metallic tang of spilled blood—like copper left too long in the sun—mingled with the acrid sweat of desperate men. The floor grew ankle-deep in gore, a grotesque harvest where we trampled the fallen. Back and forth we surged, a cyclical grinding of old against new, fighting for the emancipation of Barsoom from the throttling bondage of a gluttonous superstition.

Suddenly, sweeping past the great windows that overlooked Issus's gardens, a sight bathed my soul in exultation. 

“Look!” I cried to the First Born. 

Across the gardens stood a wavering line of black warriors, pressed backward by a horde of green warriors astride their mighty thoats. Riding forward was Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark. He shouted a fierce, ancestral command—a primal, acoustic wave that resonated with the raw, untamed ecology of the dead sea bottoms. With couched metal-shod lances, the green horde charged. The black line shattered beneath the thundering, percussive rhythm of the beasts. Behind them poured the red men, flowing like wine from a breached cask. 

The temple was surrounded. I turned to my own battle, but our foes had vanished into the corridors. 

My immediate hunger was only for Dejah Thoris. Calling Carthoris to my side, we sprinted toward the chamber where I had secreted her. But upon bursting through the door, the olfactory landscape was wrong. The comforting scent of her oils was masked by the sour, sterile odor of violence. A torn silk lay upon the floor alongside discarded metal ornaments and a dagger. The door to the pits stood ajar. 

Dejah Thoris had vanished.

A madness seized me, a rage born of a severed lineage. “Issus!” I roared, my voice tearing through the temple's oppressive silence. “Search the temple! Let no man harm her but John Carter!”

Carthoris led the sprint through the bowels of the temple, until we burst through carved doors into the throne room of Issus. We struck down the front ranks of her guards before they could draw, and by the sheer momentum of my charge, I threw myself upon the dais beside the sorapus throne. The repulsive creature, a parasite who had gorged herself upon the cultural memory of an entire world, attempted to flee into a trapdoor. I seized her by the arm, whipping out my dagger and holding it to her putrid chest.

“Back!” I commanded the surging guards. “The first black foot planted upon this platform sends my dagger into Issus’ heart.”

From the outer corridor swept Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, and Xodar, leading a thousand red men. 

“Where is Dejah Thoris?” I demanded of the trembling fraud.

For a moment, Issus could not translate the reality of her defeat. The subtle power dynamics embedded in her dominant lexicon of godhood had collapsed entirely. The highest noble of her realm stepped forward and begged her to unleash her omnipotent wrath. When she realized she had no true power, she went mad. She shrieked and clawed, her gibbering a horrific noise pollution that assaulted our ears. She spat frothing saliva upon her cowering slaves, a creature utterly devoid of sensory appreciation or grace.

I shook the shrieking beast. “Where is Dejah Thoris?”

A gleam of profound, calculating malice cut through her close-set eyes. “Dejah Thoris? Yes, I know. And Thuvia, and Phaidor. Together for a year they will meditate within the Temple of the Sun. But ere the year is quite gone, there will be no more food for them. Ho-oh! What divine entertainment!” She licked the froth from her lips, savoring the vile syllables. “There will be no more food—except each other. Ha-ah!”

The sheer horror of the suggestion paralyzed my mind. To condemn passionate, cultured beings to the ultimate perversion of sustenance—to force them to consume one another as utilitarian fuel—was an atrocity against every domestic, agrarian tradition my soul held dear. I shook her as a terrier shakes a rat. 

“Countermand your orders! Haste, or you die!”

“It is too late,” she gibbered, laughing her unearthly, scraping laugh. 

My dagger hovered above her heart, but the sour stench of her existence stayed my hand. She was unworthy of the blade. “First Born,” I cried to the chamber, projecting my voice to dismantle her mythological framework. “You have seen the impotency of Issus. She is a cruel, empty woman who has fed upon you for ages. Take her.” I pushed the raving beast from her platform into the vengeful clutches of her betrayed people, refusing to contaminate my own hands with her blood.

Commanding Xodar to lead, I rushed with Carthoris and the red nobles through the inner chambers toward the Temple of the Sun. We burst into a central courtyard paved with transparent white marble. Before us stood the temple, a structure wrought in gold and inlaid with a thousand Barsoomian gems, glittering like crystallized spices. 

As we moved to descend into the adjacent tunnel, a deep-toned roar, like the rupturing of a massive subterranean hearth, echoed from the Temple of Issus. A red padwar burst from a gate, shouting that the blacks had fired the temple. The bitter, carbonized scent of burning tapestries and melting gold flooded the courtyard, threatening to choke the life from the air itself. 

“Go back!” I cried to my companions. “Xodar, point the way and leave me. I shall reach my Princess yet.”

Xodar refused to leave, plunging into the tunnel ahead of me. We descended through galleries until we reached a level corridor ending in a lighted chamber. Massive iron bars blocked our progress, but beyond them, I saw my incomparable Princess. With her were Thuvia and Phaidor. 

The circular chamber was already turning upon its slow, cyclical axis. The aperture aligning with the corridor was narrowing. Soon it would seal, and for a long Barsoomian year, the room would slowly revolve in darkness—a multi-generational mechanism indifferent to human suffering.

“Xodar!” I cried. “Can no power stop this revolving thing?”

“None we can fetch in time,” he replied. “I shall go and attempt to find the secret of the bars. Wait here.”

I stood at the shrinking threshold, Dejah Thoris reaching her dear hands through the iron to hold mine. The touch of her skin was a fragile transmission of an untranslatable love, an entire shared history spoken without words. Thuvia withdrew to the shadows respectfully, but Phaidor stepped forward.

“John Carter,” Phaidor said, utilizing the dominant, entitled lexicon of the Holy Therns, “tell me that you love me, that I may die happy.”

“I love only the Princess of Helium,” I replied quietly, refusing to pollute my tongue with a lie. She scowled, a bitter, acidic expression, and turned away.

For a few precious minutes, Dejah and I spoke in low tones, murmuring the native dialects of our hearts, weaving our personal identities against the closing stone. Above us, the acoustic terror of the burning temple grew louder. The draft brought the suffocating fumes of the conflagration—a harsh, peppery smoke that stung the eyes and coated the tongue in soot, the literal burning of Barsoomian cultural memory.

Carthoris, Kantos Kan, and Xodar suddenly broke through the blinding smoke to my side. 

“There is no hope, John Carter,” Xodar coughed. “The keeper of the keys is dead. Our only hope is to quench the fires and trust that a year will find your Princess alive. I have brought food.”

He tossed meticulously sealed amphorae and compressed rations of sun-dried *som* berries and fermented root pastes through the narrowing gap—precious, life-sustaining culinary artifacts meant to preserve them through the long, dark cycle. 

“Go back to the surface,” I told my friends, the smoke stealing the resonance from my voice. “I shall remain here beside my Princess until death. Let them find my body awaiting hers when the year is done.”

The crevice was now barely an inch wide. Dejah Thoris pressed close, her words of courage a nourishing balm against the despair. 

Suddenly, beyond her, the beautiful face of Phaidor contorted into a mask of malign hatred. As our eyes met, she wielded the subtle, sharp power of her final words. “Think not, John Carter, that you may so lightly cast aside the love of Phaidor. Wait your long year, but know that when it ends, she dies!”

A dagger flashed upward in the dim light. I saw Thuvia throw her body forward, intercepting the strike just as a blinding gust of acrid smoke surged into the corridor. A single shriek rang out—an untranslatable cry of agony that shattered the acoustic sanctity of our final moment. 

The smoke cleared, but I stared only at a blank wall of stone. The cyclical chamber had sealed, locking its terrible secret away. 

Xodar and the others urged me to flee the flooding, burning pits. I fought them, screaming my grief into the unyielding rock, but strong arms lifted me bodily and bore me away into the choking darkness. To this day, the horrific noise of that closing stone echoes in my mind, an unresolved chord in the acoustic ecology of my soul. Whether the assassin’s blade found the heart of my Princess or another, only the slow turning of time’s great wheel will divulge.
