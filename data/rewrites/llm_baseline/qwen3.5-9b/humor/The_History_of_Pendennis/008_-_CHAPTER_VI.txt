For a while, Cicero and Euripides ceased to occupy Mr. Pen's mind entirely. His tutor, the honest Mr. Smirke, found himself with a remarkably easy schedule. However, the true victim of this mental shift was Rebecca. While Pen occasionally announced his intention to travel to Chatteris for fencing lessons—a trip undertaken openly with his mother whenever he secured three free hours—he frequently vanished into the city instead. He would dash straight to Prior's Lane, leaving his guardian in the lurch.

When Rebecca eventually grew lame, Pen was as frantic with distress as Richard III was when his horse was shot beneath him at Bosworth Field. He plunged deep into the medical texts of the stable-keeper at Chatteris, studying remedies for his own animals and hiring others for rent.

Once a week, usually under the guise of reading a Greek drama with Smirke, the young rogue timed his departure perfectly to catch the *Competitor* coach. He would linger in Chatteris for two hours before returning on the *Rival*, departing London at ten o'clock that night. On one occasion, his secret nearly slipped due to Mr. Smirke's simplicity. Mrs. Pendennis had asked if they had spent the previous night reading extensively. Smirke, about to confess that he hadn't seen Mr. Pen at all, was saved by a swift, grinding blow from Pen's boot heel directly onto the curate's toe beneath the table. The warning was immediate and effective; the curate remained silent.

Naturally, conversations regarding this "tender subject" occurred. It is excellent sport, for those not personally involved, to overhear two men in love discussing their affections. Somewhere, someone must serve as the confidant. Under solemn vows of secrecy, Pen revealed his state of mind. The curate, trembling slightly, expressed hope that Pen's object was "worthy"—not an "unlawful attachment." If it were illicit, the poor curate feared his duty would compel him to inform Mrs. Pendennis, leading to a quarrel that would sever his access to the one thing he loved most in the world.

"Unlawful? Unworthy!" Pen exclaimed, bouncing in his seat. "She is as pure as she is beautiful; I would give my heart to no other woman. I keep this secret within the family because—because—there are weighty reasons I cannot disclose. But any man who breathes a word against her purity insults both her honor and mine, and—darn me, I won't tolerate it!"

Smirke offered only a faint laugh. "Very well, Arthur, don't call me out. You know I cannot fight." Yet, this compromise placed the wretched curate firmly under his pupil's thumb, causing Greek studies and mathematics to suffer accordingly.

Had the reverend gentleman possessed greater discernment and glanced into the Poet's Corner of the *County Chronicle* arriving in the Wednesday mail, he might have noticed entries titled 'Mrs. Haller,' 'Passion and Genius,' and 'Lines to Miss Fotheringay, of the Theatre Royal.' These verses appeared weekly, written in a gloomy, thrilling, and passionate style. However, since the author no longer signed them "NEP" but rather "EROS," neither the tutor nor Helen—the good-natured soul who clipped her son's verses from the paper—realized that Nep was merely a flamboyant alias for Eros, the fervent admirer of the new actress.

Eventually, Mrs. Pendennis asked, "Who is the lady your rival is constantly singing about in the *Chronicle*? He writes somewhat like you, dear Pen, but yours is far superior. Have you seen Miss Fotheringay?"

Pen confirmed he had. That very night, he attended a performance of *The Stranger*, where she played Mrs. Haller. He mentioned her upcoming benefit and her role as Ophelia. "Suppose we went?" he suggested. "Shakespeare, you know, Mother. We can get horses from the Clavering Arms." Little Laura sprang up with delight; she longed for a play.

Pen invoked Shakespeare's name because the late Mr. Pendennis, true to his character, held an uncommon reverence for the Bard of Avon, claiming his works contained more poetry than all of Johnson's collected poets combined. Though Mr. Pendennis rarely read them himself, he urged Pen to study them, promising great pleasure in taking his son and wife to see the immortal poet's plays when the boy reached a suitable age.

Tears welled in the kind mother's eyes recalling these speeches. She kissed her son fondly and agreed to go. Laura leaped for joy. Was Pen happy? Was he ashamed? Holding his mother, he longed to confess everything but chose silence. He intended to observe her reaction; the play would be the test, and he would try his mother as Hamlet tested Ophelia.

Helen, in her cheerful mood, invited Mr. Smirke to join them. The clergyman, raised by a doting parent in Clapham who abhorred theatricals, had never seen a play. Yet, Shakespeare! To ride in Mrs. Pendennis's carriage and spend a whole night beside her! He could not resist the prospect of such pleasure. Making a feeble speech about temptation and gratitude, he accepted her generous offer. As he spoke, however, he cast a look at her that made her exceedingly uncomfortable. She had seen that gaze many times lately, one that pursued her relentlessly. In the widow's eyes, he was becoming positively odious daily.

We shall not dwell extensively on Pen's courtship of Miss Fotheringay, as the reader has already sampled much of her conversation, which requires little reporting. Pen sat with her hour after hour, pouring forth his entire honest, boyish soul. Every thought, hope, feeling, book, or fancy he possessed flowed toward her. He never tired of talking and longing. As ideas surged in his hot, eager brain, he clothed them in words and delivered them. Her role was not to converse, but to feign understanding—a difficult task given the volume of nonsense he blurted out—while maintaining an appearance of extreme handsomeness and sympathy.

While Pen delivered twenty-minute tirades, delighted in his own eloquence, the lovely Emily, unable to comprehend a tenth of his ramblings, utilized the time to attend to her own affairs. She mentally arranged how to dress the cold mutton, how to turn the black satin, or how to fashion a bonnet from her scarf à la Miss Thackthwaite. Pen spouted Byron and Moore; passion and poetry. Her job was simply to raise her eyes, fix them briefly on his face, and cry, "Oh, 'tis beautiful! Ah, how exquisite! Repeat those lines again." Off the boy would go, and she would return to her practical thoughts of turned gowns or hashed mutton.

Soon, Pen's passion was no longer a secret to Emily or her father. On his second visit, his admiration was obvious to both. As he departed, the old gentleman winked at his daughter over his glass of grog. "Faith, Milly darling, I think ye've hooked that chap."

"Pooh, 'tis only a boy, papa dear," Milly replied. "Sure he's but a child." Pen would have been thrilled by such a dismissal. He was galloping home wild with pleasure, shouting her name.

"Ye've hooked 'um any how," said the Captain. "Let me tell ye he's not a bad fish. I asked Tom at the George and Flint the grocer about his background—fine fortune, drives a chariot, owns Fairoaks Park, sole heir, property at twenty-one. Ye might go further and not fare so well, Miss Fotheringay."

"Them boys are mostly talk," Milly said seriously. "Recall young Poldoody in Dublin. I have a desk full of verses he wrote me during Trinity College, yet he went abroad, and his mother married him to an Englishwoman."

"Lord Poldoody was a young nobleman; things differ there. Besides, ye weren't in the position ye hold now, Milly dear. But ye mustn't encourage this young chap too much, for bedad, Jack Costigan won't have any trifling with his daughter."

"No more will his daughter, papa, you may be sure of that," Milly assured him. "A little sip more of the punch—sure, 'tis beautiful. Ye needn't fear the young chap. I think I'm old enough to take care of myself, Captain Costigan."

Thus, Pen visited day after day, rushing in and galloping away, growing increasingly wild with every encounter. Sometimes the Captain attended, but trusting his daughter implicitly, he often left the young couple alone, cocking his hat over his eye and strutting off on some errand when Pen entered. How delightful those interviews were! The Captain's drawing-room was a low-wainscoted space with a large window overlooking the Dean's garden. Here Pen sat and talked endlessly. Emily, working nearby, looked beautiful and calm. Sunlight streamed through the great windows, illuminating her superb features. Suddenly, the great bell would boom, causing Pen to pause, smile, and fall silent until the music faded, or the rooks cawed in the cathedral elms at sunset, or the organ and choristers drifted over the quiet air, gently hushing Pen's torrential speech.
