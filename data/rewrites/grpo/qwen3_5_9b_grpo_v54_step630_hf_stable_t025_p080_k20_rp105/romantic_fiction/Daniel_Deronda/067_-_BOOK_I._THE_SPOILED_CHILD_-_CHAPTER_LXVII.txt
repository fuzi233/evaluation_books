The divine spark within us twists our noblest acts from the reluctant clay of our own souls.

For Daniel Deronda, the return from the Abbey brought a bitter shock: the unwanted father had already taken root in the Brompton lodgings. Mirah, driven by a mixture of necessity and fear, had felt compelled to introduce her father to Deronda. She had carefully curated her narrative, weaving a tale of friendship with Ezra that began innocently, emphasizing the sympathy that bound them while meticulously omitting the rescue from drowning and the shelter provided by the Meyricks. She could not bear to let her father's gaze fully inspect the depth of her connection with Deronda. Consequently, Lapidoth, for reasons of his own, did not press for details regarding her flight or arrival in England. Yet, he was deeply intrigued by the existence of a benevolent friend who appeared to hold high station in the world.

It was Ezra's brother who first informed Deronda of this new, heavy addition to their lives. "I have grown calm in watching him now," Ezra concluded, his voice weary. "I try to believe that my sister's tenderness and the daily taste of peace may win him to stay away from temptation. I have forbidden her to give him money, convincing her that he would spend it on his own ruin."

Deronda arrived at the lodgings on the third day since Lapidoth's arrival. His new clothes were not yet ready, and wishing to make a favorable impression, he refused to appear in his old attire. Watching him from the window, Lapidoth was struck by Deronda's youthfulness—a detail Mirah had omitted, which Lapidoth had instinctively dismissed as irrelevant in a figure associated with the grave studies and somber demeanor of Ezra. Lapidoth quickly surmised that Deronda's true motive must be love for Mirah. To him, this was a blessing; a bond with Mirah promised more indulgence for her father than a tie to Ezra. Lapidoth hoped to recommend himself to Deronda, softening any hard prepossessions he might hold. He behaved with studied amiability, eager to domesticate himself within the household. He immersed himself in Mirah's music, feigned docility regarding smoking (which Mrs. Adam forbade in the parlor), and strolled the square with his German pipe, fueled by tobacco Mirah supplied. He was too sharp to remonstrate against Mirah's refusal to give him money, accepting her insistence on this solemn duty as a promise to her brother. He was content to wait.

On the next visit, Lapidoth, freshly dressed and pleased with his reflection, sat in the room with Ezra, who was teaching himself, as a severe penance, to tolerate his father's presence whenever imposed. Deronda felt cold and distant. The sight of this man, who had blighted the lives of his wife and children, created in him a repulsion that bordered on physical discomfort. Yet, Lapidoth did not falter. He asked permission to stay and listen to the reading of papers from the old chest, eventually proving useful by helping to decipher a difficult German manuscript. This led him to suggest transcribing the manuscript, offering his services to copy papers in Roman characters as well. Though Ezra's eyes were failing, Lapidoth's remained strong. Deronda accepted the offer, interpreting it as a sign of grace in Lapidoth's willingness to be employed usefully. He noted a gratified expression on Ezra's face, though Ezra soon interjected, "Let all the writing be done here; I cannot trust the papers out of my sight, lest there be an accident by fire or otherwise."

Poor Ezra felt as though he held a convict on leave under his guard. Unless he saw his father working, he could not believe he would labor in good faith. By this arrangement, Ezra fastened upon himself the crushing burden of his father's presence. It was made painful not only by his deepest, longest associations but also by Lapidoth's restless temperament, which grew more apparent as he became familiar with his situation and lost any awe he might have felt for his son. In truth, Ezra was placing a severe constraint on himself, confining his attention solely to win Deronda's favor. Like a man trapped in an uncomfortable garment, he sought relief at every opportunity: going out to smoke, pacing and talking, or slumping back in his chair in silence, yet incessantly communicating a dumb language of facial movements and gestures. If Mirah was present, he would revert to his old habit of gossiping with her about their former doings, companions, and the plots of plays he once adapted, believing he could command the vivacity of his earlier self. This was a mortal infliction for Ezra. When Mirah was home, she tried to alleviate his suffering by keeping her father in the parlor and watching over him. What duty is forged from a single difficult resolve? The difficulty lies in the daily, unflinching support of consequences that mar the blessed return of morning with the prospect of irritation to be suppressed or shame to be endured. Such burdens were borne by these, as by many other heroic children of an unworthy father, with the hope, at least to Mirah, that they would stretch onward through the solid part of life.

Meanwhile, Lapidoth's presence had erected a new, impalpable partition between Deronda and Mirah. Each dreaded the soiling inferences of the other's mind, each misinterpreting the increased reserve and diffidence of the partner they loved. But it was not long before light broke for Deronda.

As soon as he could, after returning from his brief visit to the Abbey, Deronda called at Hans Meyrick's rooms. He felt it a due of friendship that Hans should be acquainted with the reasons for his recent journey and the changes of intention it had wrought. Hans was not there; he was said to be in the country for a few days. Deronda left a note and waited a week, expecting a reply. Receiving none, and fearing some capricious mood in the incalculably susceptible Hans—whose planned sojourn at the Abbey had already been deferred—he made a second call. He was admitted to the painting room, where he found his friend in a light coat without a waistcoat, his long hair still wet from a bath, yet with a face looking worn and wizened—anything but country-like. Hans had taken up his palette and brushes and stood before his easel when Deronda entered, but the equipment and attitude seemed arranged on short notice.

As they shook hands, Deronda remarked, "You don't look much as if you had been in the country, old fellow. Is it Cambridge you have been to?"

"No," Hans replied curtly, throwing down his palette with the air of someone who had begun to feign by mistake. Pushing forward a chair for Deronda, he threw himself into another, leaning backward with his hands behind his head. "I've been to I-don't-know-where—No Man's Land—and a mortally unpleasant country it is."

"You don't mean to say you have been drinking, Hans," Deronda said, seating himself opposite in anxious survey.

"Nothing so good. I've been smoking opium. I always meant to do it sometime to try how much bliss could be got by it; and having found myself just now rather out of other bliss, I thought it judicious to seize the opportunity. But I pledge you my word I shall never tap a cask of that bliss again. It disagrees with my constitution."

"What has been the matter? You were in good spirits enough when you wrote to me."

"Oh, nothing in particular. The world began to look seedy—a sort of cabbage-garden with all the cabbages cut. A malady of genius, you may be sure," Hans said, creasing his face into a smile. "And, in fact, I was tired of being virtuous without reward, especially in this hot London weather."

"Nothing else? No real vexation?" Deronda pressed.

Hans shook his head.

"I came to tell you of my own affairs, but I can't do it with a good grace if you are to hide yours."

"Haven't an affair in the world," Hans said in a flighty way, "except a quarrel with a bric-à-brac man. Besides, as it is the first time in our lives that you ever spoke to me about your own affairs, you are only beginning to pay a pretty long debt."

Deronda felt convinced that Hans was behaving artificially, yet he trusted that the old frankness would return if he gave his own confidence.

"You laughed at the mystery of my journey to Italy, Hans," Deronda began. "It was for an object that touched my happiness at the very roots. I had never known anything about my parents, and I really went to Genoa to meet my mother. My father has been long dead—died when I was an infant. My mother was the daughter of an eminent Jew; my father was her cousin. Many things had caused me to think of this origin as almost a probability before I set out. I was so far prepared for the result that I was glad of it—glad to find myself a Jew."

"You must not expect me to look surprised, Deronda," Hans said, changing his attitude, laying one leg across the other and examining the heel of his slipper. "You knew it?"

"My mother told me. She went to the house the morning after you had been there—brother and sister both told her. You may imagine we can't rejoice as they do. But whatever you are glad of, I shall come to be glad of in the end—_when_ exactly the end may be I can't predict," Hans said, speaking in a low tone, which was as usual with him when out of humor with his lot, yet bent on making no fuss about it.

"I quite understand that you can't share my feeling," Deronda said. "But I could not let silence lie between us on what casts quite a new light over my future. I have taken up some of Mordecai's ideas, and I mean to try and carry them out, so far as one man's efforts can go. I dare say I shall by and by travel to the East and be away for some years."

Hans said nothing, but rose, seized his palette, and began working his brush on it, standing before his picture with his back to Deronda. Deronda also felt himself at a break in his path, embarrassed by Hans's embarrassment.

Presently Hans said, again speaking low and without turning, "Excuse the question, but does Mrs. Grandcourt know of all this?"

"No; and I must beg of you, Hans," Deronda said, rather angrily, "to cease joking on that subject. Any notions you have are wide of the truth—they are the very reverse of the truth."

"I am no more inclined to joke than I shall be at my own funeral," Hans replied. "But I am not at all sure that you are aware what are my notions on that subject."

"Perhaps not," Deronda said. "But let me say, once for all, that in relation to Mrs. Grandcourt, I never have had, and never shall have the position of a lover. If you have ever seriously put that interpretation on anything you have observed, you are supremely mistaken."

There was silence for a little while, and to each the silence was like an irritating air, exaggerating their discomfort.

"Perhaps I have been mistaken in another interpretation, also," Hans said presently.

"What is that?"

"That you had no wish to hold the position of a lover toward another woman, who is neither wife nor widow."

"I can't pretend not to understand you, Meyrick. It is painful that our wishes should clash. I hope you will tell me if you have any ground for supposing that you would succeed."

"That seems rather a superfluous inquiry on your part, Deronda," Hans said with some irritation.

"Why superfluous?"

"Because you are perfectly convinced on the subject—and probably have had the very best evidence to convince you."

"I will be more frank with you than you are with me," Deronda said, still heated by Hans's show of temper, yet sorry for him. "I have never had the slightest evidence that I should succeed myself. In fact, I have very little hope."

Hans looked round hastily at his friend, but immediately turned to his picture again.

"And in our present situation," Deronda said, hurt by the idea that Hans suspected him of insincerity, and giving an offended emphasis to his words, "I don't see how I can deliberately make known my feeling to her. If she could not return it, I should have embittered her best comfort; for neither she nor I can be parted from her brother, and we should have to meet continually. If I were to cause her that sort of pain by an unwilling betrayal of my feeling, I should be no better than a mischievous animal."

"I don't know that I have ever betrayed _my_ feeling to her," Hans said, as if vindicating himself.

"You mean that we are on a level, then; you have no reason to envy me."

"Oh, not the slightest," Hans said with bitter irony. "You have measured my conceit and know that it out-tops all your advantages."

"I am a nuisance to you, Meyrick. I am sorry, but I can't help it," Deronda said, rising. "After what passed between us before, I wished to have this explanation; and I don't see that any pretensions of mine have made a real difference to you. They are not likely to make any pleasant difference to myself under present circumstances. Now the father is there—did you know that the father is there?"

"Yes. If he were not a Jew I would permit myself to damn him—with faint praise, I mean," Hans said, but with no smile.

"She and I meet under greater constraint than ever. Things might go on in this way for two years without my getting any insight into her feeling toward me. That is the whole state of affairs, Hans. Neither you nor I have injured the other, that I can see. We must put up with this sort of rivalry in a hope that is likely enough to come to nothing. Our friendship can bear that strain, surely."
