"The broth is piping hot," Gerard announced, his voice cutting through the quiet of the tent.

"But how shall we manage to convey it to our lips?" the elder gentleman asked, his tone heavy with despair. "My joints are stiff as iron, and kneeling over such a blazing fire with mere stalks seems impossible." Saint John the Baptist, he muttered, "what dexterity this youth possesses!"

As the old man voiced his dilemma, Gerard swiftly solved it. With a practiced flick of his wrist, he loosened the knot at his chest, removed his cap, and slipped a smooth stone into each corner. Wrapping his handkerchief around the flask, he snatched it from the heat and nestled it securely between the stones. He then lifted the cap and pressed it gently against the elder's nostrils, a mischievous grin playing on his lips. Trembling slightly, the old scholar inserted the end of the wheat straw and drew deeply. Instantly, his pale, gaunt countenance seemed to ignite, flushing with a radiant warmth. As he drew another long, sustaining breath, he exclaimed:

"Hippocrates and Galen!" he cried. "'Tis a *soupe au vin*—the panacea of panaceas. Praise God for the nation that devised it, the lady who crafted it, and the lad who delivers it to the dying. Share a sip, my daughter, whilst I recount the lore and virtues of this sovereign mixture to our young host. This restorative, young sir, was unknown to antiquity; it appears nowhere in their medical treatises nor in those popular chronicles that preserve their remedies, whether surgical or medicinal. Hector, in the *Iliad*, if my memory serves me right..."

(Margaret whispered softly, "Alas, he has begun.")

"...was summoned by one of the maidens of the tale to partake of a draught of wine. Yet he declined, citing that he was about to enter battle and must not consume anything to dull his faculties. Now, if the *soupe au vin* had been known in Troy, it is evident that in refusing plain wine on those grounds, he would have added in hexameter, 'Yet a *soupe au vin*, fair lady, I shall taste, with grateful heart.' Not only would this have been common courtesy—a virtue lacking in no perfect commander—but failing to do so would have marked him as shallow and imprudent, unfit to lead a war. For men entering combat require sustenance and every form of support, as proven by the fact that foolish generals, sending hungry troops to clash with well-fed foes, have been vanquished by inferior forces throughout the ages. The Romans suffered a crushing defeat in northern Italy at the hands of Hannibal, the Carthaginian, solely due to this oversight. Now, this celestial elixir bestows strength upon the limbs and fervor upon the spirit in a single instant; had it fallen into Hector's cup at the critical moment, aided by Apollo, Venus, and the holy saints, it would most likely have secured a victory for the Greeks. Note how faint and weary I was but a moment ago; well, I inhale this heavenly cordial, and now behold me brave as Achilles and strong as an eagle."

"Oh, father, now?" Margaret gasped. "An eagle, alas!"

"Girl, I defy thee and all creation," he declared. "Ready, I say, like a foaming charger, to devour the distance between here and Rotterdam, and mighty enough to combat the ills of life, even poverty and old age, which latter philosophers term the supreme evil. Deny me not, unless the man's life has been squandered—which, by the way, it usually has. Now, for the moderns!"

"Father! Dear father!"

"Fear me not, child; I shall be brief, unreasonably and unseasonably brief. The *soupe au vin* is absent from modern science; but this is merely further proof, if proof were required, that for the last few centuries, physicians have been fools, relying on chicken broth and decoctions of gold, thereby attributing the highest qualities to flesh with the least juice and to a metal possessing fewer chemical properties than all others; charlatans! blockheads! murderers! Since, therefore, no light is to be gleaned from these sources, let us turn to the chroniclers. First, we find Duguesclin, a French knight, preparing to engage the English—who, at that time, held sway over half of France and struck with formidable vigor by sea and land. He drank, not one, but three *soupe au vin* in honor of the Blessed Trinity. Having done so, he charged the islanders; and, as might have been predicted, slew a multitude and drove the remainder into the sea. But he was but the first of a long lineage of holy and relentless warriors who, by this divine restorative, have been sustained, fortified, strengthened, and consoled."

"My dear father, pray join yourself to that venerable company ere the soup grows cold." Holding the cap imploringly in both hands, she urged him to drink again.

This saved them from the "modern instances" and afforded Gerard an opportunity to tell Margaret how proud his mother would be if her soup had benefited a man of learning.

"Ay! but," said Margaret, "it would displease her greatly to see her son give so freely and receive nothing himself. Why did you bring but two straws?"

"Fair mistress, I hoped you would permit me to press my lips to your straw, seeing there were but two."

Margaret smiled and blushed. "Never beg that thou mayest command," she replied. "The straw is not mine, 'tis thine: thou cut it in yonder field."

"I cut it, and thus it became mine; but afterward, thy lip touched it, and thus it became thine."

"Did it? Then I shall lend it thee. Here—it is thine again; thy lip has touched it."

"No, it belongs to us both now. Let us divide it."

"By all means; thou hast a knife."

"No, I will not cut it—that would be unlucky. I shall bite it. Thus I shall keep my half; thou wilt surely burn thine, once thou returnest home, I suspect."

"You know me not. I waste naught. It is odds I fashion a hairpin thereof, or something similar."

This answer, rather than provoking Gerard, dashed his spirits, leaving him silent. And now, having disposed of the bread and soup, the old scholar prepared to resume his journey. Then arose a minor difficulty: Gerard, the adept, could not retie his ribbon as Catherine had tied it. Margaret, after watching his futile efforts with a sly, affectionate gaze, offered assistance. For at her age, girls delight in alternating between coy tenderness and saucy gentleness, and she perceived she had flustered him, only to soothe him now. A fair head, crowned with a stately mass of auburn hair, glossy and luminous amidst the silver threads, bowed sweetly toward him. As it captivated his vision, two white, supple hands played delicately upon the obstinate ribbon, molding it with soft, airy touches. A heavenly thrill coursed through the innocent young man, and vague glimpses of a new realm of feeling and sentiment opened before him. And these exquisite sensations Margaret, unknowingly, prolonged: it is not natural for her sex to hasten matters pertaining to the sacred toilet. Nay, when the slender fingers had finally subjugated the ends of the knot, her mind remained uneasy until, by a maneuver peculiar to the female hand, she had made her palm convex and applied it with gentle pressure to the center of the knot—a sweet, little coaxing kiss of the palm, as much as to say, "Now be a good knot, and remain so." The palm-kiss was bestowed upon the ribbon, but the wearer's heart leaped to meet it.

"There, that is how it ought to be," said Margaret, drawing back to inspect her work with one last keen scrutiny. Then, seeking simple approval of her skill, she met a gaze of such ardent adoration that she lowered her eyes quickly, coloring all over. An indescribable tremor seized her, and she retreated with downcast lashes and tell-tale cheeks, taking her father's arm on the opposite side. Gerard, blushing at having startled her with his gaze, took the other arm; and so the two youths departed, heads bowed and conscious, supporting the elderly scholar in silence.

They entered Rotterdam via the Schiedamse Poort. As Gerard was unfamiliar with the town, Peter directed him toward the Hooch Straet, where the Stadthouse stood. Himself, he accompanied Margaret to his cousin, located in the Ooster-Waagen Straet; thus, almost upon crossing the threshold, their paths diverged. They exchanged a friendly farewell, and Gerard plunged into the great city. A profound sense of solitude descended upon him, yet the streets teemed with life. Then he lamented, too late, that, out of delicacy, he had not inquired of his former companions who they were and where they dwelt.

"Beshrew my shamefacedness!" he murmured. "But their words and breeding surpassed their station, and something whispered me they wished to remain unknown. I shall never see her again. Oh, weary world, I hate thee and thy ways. To think I must encounter beauty, goodness, and learning—three pearls of priceless value—and never behold them again!"
