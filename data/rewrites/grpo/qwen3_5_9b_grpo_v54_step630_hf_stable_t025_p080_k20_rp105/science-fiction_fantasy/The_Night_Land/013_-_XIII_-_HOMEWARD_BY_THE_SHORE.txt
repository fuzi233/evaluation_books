We emerged at last from that bleak, mournful valley that lies beyond the great mountains—the Upward Gorge—and halted between their towering feet, just past the gorge's dark mouth.

Naani looked in every direction, breathing rapidly, her eyes bright with wonder as she took in the new sights and the overwhelming freedom from the dread that had held her. She turned upward, peering into the gorge's darkness, spying the great mouth, and felt a fresh fear compel her to run further downward into the lighter realm of the Sea-Country. She paused again, looking back with awe and relief, then gazed out at the vast expanse of land and the great Sea. Overcome by amazement and gladness, she nearly laughed and cried simultaneously. She turned constantly, never ceasing to look, taking deep breaths of the wide air, for she had never in her life known such a broad place of light.

We both felt the need for hushed speech was gone, as it had been in the gloom of the gorge. She shouted, testing the echo like a child, her voice carrying pretty far before vanishing into the distance. Suddenly, an echo—or perhaps something stranger, an unnatural call from the gorge's gloom—returned from the dark mountains behind us. We spun around, unsure if it was merely an echo or a warning, and ran downward a bit more until we felt utterly free of the gorge and its lingering strangeness.

We searched for a flat rock and found a suitable spot still elevated above the land. Climbing up, we sat to eat and drink, sitting close and happy yet keeping a wise vigil against the Humpt Men or any other danger.

Naani questioned everything, staring over the country with shining wonder and joy at the sea. It stirred her so deeply that she was pained by vague, sudden memories—strange dreams mixing pleasure and pain. She broke into weeping, needing to be in my arms until she knew herself again, returning to her dear natural joy.

Often, Mine Own spoke of the clear wonder of the air, which seemed to her as it did to me, for I too had lived my life in a Dark Land. She suddenly broke from her rapture, speaking in vague words of an eternity ago, memories old and englamoured like moonlight. In a moment, she returned to that far future speech, her being close to mine, often in solemn silence. The greatness of the sea called to her with an olden voice, half-waking her; I stirred with her, though not as I had on my outward journey. My olden thoughts, my memory-dreams, came afresh upon my spirit.

Thus, we sat shaken by dreamings concerning the olden world lying upward in that dreadful night that roofed over the country. I am dumb, for I have no speech to convey the troublings and stirrings of our spirits in that moment.

Far off, beyond the mountains' feet where the shore lay to our left, a great mist and steam rose. This was the mist I had passed through on my outward way. Naani asked about it, and I told her we must soon pass through it. She wondered at the volcanoes burning in the sea and scattered across the wide country; their height and grandeur exalted her while giving her a strange humility. Presently, I took her into my arms to kiss her, for she was such a sweet maiden, lovely with interest and naturalness. She kissed me in turn, making questions between kisses, yearning to know the country more, but also, I think, because she was sweetly impudent in her joy.

She kissed me thrice passionately upon the mouth, then took my shoulders with her small hands, which seemed pretty against my broadness and metal armor. She strove to shake me to a speedier answering, full of dear naughtiness, needing to be kissed very hard.

I answered her, but in my own fashion—a word between each kiss. She quickly put her hand between our lips; I kissed the palm, small and pretty. When I had no thought, she opened her fingers quickly, kissing me through them, then shut them to stop me.

Afterward, I made her stand on the rock and set her hair free over her shoulders. I took off her boots, revealing her little bare feet. At first, she half-refused, but afterward stood dear and obedient, a little shy and prettier for her sweet blushings. When I had her to my liking, I stepped back and looked at her. She looked back, quaint and naughty, then turned grave, pretending to be a dummy figure. When she faced me again, sedate, she stretched out her pretty foot and put her pink toes suddenly upon my lips. I was so surprised I had no wit to act before she snatched them back. She then sprang into my arms, wanting to be hugged and loved greatly. I laughed tenderly, loving her utterly, and told her I wanted a pocket sufficient to carry her always anigh to my heart. She laughed mischievously, saying she would tickle and pinch me if I carried her that way. I had no answer but to shake her gently; she kissed me naughty on the mouth in the midst of my shaking. What shall a man do with such an one?

She wanted to be sedate and sit on the rock, making me turn so she could reach the pouch on my back. She took out the comb and combed her pretty hair. I sat and talked with her, jesting with a heart so light it hadn't been for a great while. Though I dreaded the Humpt Men and the monstrous animals of the Sea-Country, I had no abiding horror of what I had seen; there seemed a naturalness in all things, no loathing, no fear of an Evil Force.

When she bound her hair, I asked her to leave it on her shoulders because it was so pretty. She smiled, happy to please me.

We gathered our gear, I put the boots back on the Maid, and we began our journey downward into the Sea-Country. We went at a good pace, not bringing great weariness, for I intended to rest on this side of the boiling steam near the shore. We passed along the mountains' feet toward the steam for six good hours, still an hour off. By the nineteenth hour of the day's journeying, we were ready for slumber.

We found a tall, hard rock with a flat top twice my length every way. Safe on top, we ate, drank, and slept with the cloak under us, though the country was so warm we needed no covering.

We woke after seven good hours, sitting upward and looking newly at each other in the good light, as if seeing one another anew. Fresh joy was in each look. She came into my arms to kiss and be kissed; we both had our need, yet were hungrier for the having.

Mine Own made breakfast; the water fizzed strongly and surprisingly. We ate and drank, utter happy with each other, talking of this and that. The Maid looked about, marveling at the wonders and newness of the country, while I looked near, lest danger be anigh.

In a while, Mine Own drew my gaze to the mountains the Gorge came through. Truly, now that I looked in ease, I saw with her how they were monstrous, a wall going upward forever until lost in the dark night of the deathly Upper World, an eternity away. I had vague thoughts of this on the outward way, but now, with ease and the Maid to speak with, I perceived odd matters the more. I tell you this little thing so you may perceive the restfulness upon me compared to the Outward Going.

We talked and ate without great speed, but hurried somewhat in the end, conscious of leaning to slackness. We came down from the rock pretty speedily and went forward at a good pace.

Soon we heard the far hissing of steam and the noise of upward bursting waters boiling. The sound was strange, but I had heard it before, so it troubled me less than the Maid. I assured her, and she came nigh to me. Thus we entered the steam.

We went for more than three hours. I carried the Maid on my back, being first, so she would not walk into a boiling pool in the mazingness of the steam. I was guided by the shore of the sea to our left, though we could see neither sea nor otherwise except going so close we neared the water. The sea seemed to boil in parts, with hot pools everywhere; it was a constant puzzle whether we went by a great hot pool or the true sea. We went utter wary.

About us came strange burstings, shriekings, and whistlings of waters breaking upward from the deep world. Odd whiles the sounds were as of great monsters; the earth shook under us. Other-whiles there was a hush, only steam, and somewhere in the distance a low piping from a steam cranny, strange and lonesome.

Somewhere near the fourth hour, we came out of the thick steam. The pipings and roarings were to our rearward; the steam grew thin as mist, noises very far-seeming. Presently we were clear out into the air of that country.

Now the Maid perceived the trees, great forests to our right, while the shore went on our left. She was utter in wonder, needing to pluck branches, smell them, look at each leaf, all stirred. Never in that life had she seen such a matter as those great trees, yet she was stirred by vague memories that seemed no more than dreams. You to think but a moment and perceive how the thing was with her; you to have been likewise stirred if you were so strangely waked in a corner part of the heart.

When the sixth hour was full, we halted in a wise place, had our tablets and water. Afterward, the Maid bid me take her to a warm pool nearby, asking that I turn from her but remain anigh as her Protector.

So she washed, making herself happy with a sweet cleanness proper to her. Afterward, when done, she acted watch while I did mine, helping me in all matters she was able. Truly, I was happy indeed that she had delight to attend upon me and treat me mother-wise, yet with her maid-heart not all hid, as you have perceived.

Thus we went alway in these matters; oft I have not space to tell, and oft you shall remember if I am too full of other happenings.

Afterward we journeyed again, talking of this and that. I was watchful, telling the Maid to keep her eyes wary but not uneasy.

When we went thus for seven good hours, we came nigh opposed to the bright-burning fire-hill offward in the sea, which had made me a warm light when I slept in the tree, as you remember. Truly, as I shall mind you, we had been past seven hours coming to this place from where the steam was. Yet I had gone that space on the outward way at a greater speed; but I might not set so great a pace to the Maid, save odd whiles. I beg you to have this in your mind to understand why we are oft long upon this part of the journey compared to my outward going.

I had set off our hour for food, seeing we came nigh to the place where the tree was. I knew the Maid should like to eat and drink anigh to that place, where I had slept.

Surely I took her to the tree. When I told her, she begged me to indulge her and go upward to that branch where I slept, to eat our tablets there.

I was willing, entering her wishing, for there was no danger in the climb, and I went always below her to ensure her safeness. We came up to the great branch. She made how we should sit, I showed just where I had lain, and she looked very close, seeing that my weight had surely marked the hardness of the armor upon the bark. She then sat upon that branch alone, eating and drinking, looking outward at the light from the fire-hill, very hushed, thinking, and I not disturbing her with speech.

When she was done, she got from the branch, kissed the place where I had lain. Lo! in a moment a thought came into her; she drew her knife, cut out a piece of the bark, and put it into her breast as a keepsake, seeming somewise contented.

Truly, I told her about the great beast when we came down to the earth. She cried out, showing me the mark where the belly of the monstrous beast had brushed the earth as it ran, and the broken places of the foot-marks. By this she saw how great a beast it was, yet it was a little thing beside the Slug, being a thing of horn and hardness of skin, as you have perceived.

Truly, I mind how the Maid's observings brought very keen to me that but seventeen days had passed since I went onward from this place. This seemed very strange and scarce credible to me, for I had thought it, somewise, a great time. Truly, this was because it was so marked by stress of mind and great happenings; you to agree in this thing. But yet, also, we shall truly mind that those times I have called days did hold oft the hours of two days, and mayhap three, as you do remember.

Now we went onward. I made to carry the Maid, as ever, after she had walked twelve hours, though she walked thirteen this time. She said she would go on her own feet through the next six hours to ease me from the labor.

But I knew she would be all gone of her strength thus, in but a day or two. We made the better speed if I kept to my way, having her walk twelve hours of every journey and afterward come into my arms. Truly, she was bred less hardy than I, as you shall think from all I have told concerning the Peoples of the Lesser Pyramid; moreover, she was yet something weakened by the dreadful month of her lonesomeness and escapings before I came to succour her.

Truly, as I carried her, the Maid made remark of her wonderment concerning me, that I was so hard of body and set in the determination of my mind.
