The *Pall Mall Gazette* made a splash—big, loud, and rather messy—like a drunken gentleman tumbling into a fountain at a garden party. It was whispered that the paper had powerful political backing; great names were rumored to be lurking behind its columns like ghosts in a haunted library. Were these rumors true? We’re not at liberty to say—though we can confirm one thing: an article on foreign policy, widely believed to have been penned by a noble Lord whose face is as familiar to the Foreign Office as a bad cold in winter, was actually written by Captain Shandon, sitting in the parlour of the Bear and Staff public house near Whitehall Stairs. The printer’s boy had tracked him down like a bloodhound after a lost sausage roll, and there, beside his literary partner Mr. Bludyer (who was living in temporary exile from his own home), the noble Lord’s “masterpiece” was composed with a quill dipped in gin and a mind full of dubious facts.

And then there were the finance articles—those weighty, ponderous treatises that seemed to drip with wisdom and gravitas. Everyone assumed they came from the pen of a great statesman, possibly the very man who’d once said “I never read anything but the Times.” But no—it was George Warrington of the Upper Temple, writing between sips of weak tea and bouts of existential dread, who delivered those gems.

Was there a secret pact between the *Gazette* and some high-powered political cabal? Quite possibly. Percy Popjoy—son of Lord Falconet, a man so influential he could make a minister blush—was often seen creeping up the stairs to Warrington’s chambers like a thief stealing a sandwich. And certain tidbits appeared in the paper that only someone with insider access could know—like the fact that Lady Muffborough had left her gloves at the opera, or that Lord Steyne had secretly eaten a whole pie during a parliamentary debate.

Poetry also found its way into the *Gazette*, though it was poetry that looked like it had been written by a man trying to impress a woman while standing on one leg. These poems bore the initials “P. P.”—and yes, you guessed it: Percy Popjoy. His novel, which was less a book and more a series of fever dreams about love and politics, was praised in the *Gazette* with such over-the-top enthusiasm that even the author began to believe he was the next Shakespeare.

Mr. Pen, meanwhile, stayed clear of politics. He didn’t want to get tangled up in debates about tariffs or treaties—he preferred the quieter, more respectable battlefield of literature. And what a soldier he was! He arrived at the *Gazette*’s offices in Catherine Street, Strand, puffing like a steam engine, manuscripts clutched in his hand, eyes sparkling with the joy of seeing his name in print. It was like being crowned king of a tiny, imaginary kingdom where everyone read your words and nodded sagely.

At the heart of this literary circus was Mr. Jack Finucane, sub-editor and master of paste and scissors. With the focus of a hawk and the appetite of a bear, he combed through every newspaper in the land, hunting for news of aristocratic dinners, deaths, and scandals. If a duke died, Jack made sure the world knew. If a duchess hosted a dinner party with more than three courses, Jack would describe it in vivid detail—even if he’d never set foot in the place.

It was a sight to behold: Jack, in tattered trousers and shirt sleeves stained with ink and porter, eating a plate of meat from the cookshop while describing the splendours of a ball at Almack’s. He wrote as if he’d danced with the Queen herself, though his real life consisted of cheap beer and borrowed pens. Occasionally, he slipped up—once describing a lord’s carriage as “drawn by four magnificent mules,” when it was clearly horses. But the *Ballinafad Sentinel*, where he was chief correspondent, suffered more than the *Gazette*, which wisely kept him away from actual writing. Scissors and paste were his domain, not prose.

Pen, however, was another story. He took his reviews seriously. Armed with a fair amount of random reading and a sharp sense of fun, he produced articles that pleased both his editor and the public. He was proud—justifiably so—that he earned his keep with wit and honesty. And his work wasn’t just read in London: it reached Fairoaks, where the two ladies devoured each issue like it was a sacred text. It even made it to Clavering Park, where a young lady of refined tastes (and probably too many books) read it with admiration. Old Doctor Portman, who received the paper after memorizing all of Pen’s articles, declared that the lad had “spirit, taste, and fancy”—even if he didn’t write like a scholar, he certainly wrote like a gentleman.

Then came the moment of glory. Major Pendennis walked into the Regent Club, expecting a quiet cigar and a game of cards. Instead, he found Wenham, Lord Falconet, and other dignitaries gathered around a copy of the *Pall Mall Gazette*, laughing over an article that tore apart the latest travel memoir by the Countess of Muffborough.

The book, titled *Travels in Spain and Italy*, was a masterpiece of linguistic chaos. Written in both French and English (with equal confidence and equal incompetence), it was a treasure trove of errors. And the critic who pounced on it? None other than Pen himself. He danced around the subject like a jester at a royal court, mocking the countess with mock solemnity and impeccable manners. There wasn’t a single word that wasn’t polite—yet the poor lady was reduced to absurdity. Wenham grinned like a cat who’d just stolen cream. Lord Falconet laughed until he choked. Even Lord Steyne, whom Pen had never met, chuckled and said, “That’ll make the Muffborough writhe like a whale under a harpoon.”

And suddenly, the Major saw his nephew in a new light. “By gad,” he muttered, “the young rascal has some stuff in him!” He sat down immediately to write to the widow at Fairoaks, detailing how the great men of England praised Pen. Then he wrote to Pen himself, inviting him to eat a chop at the club and promising to take him to dinner at Gaunt House—because Lord Steyne liked people who could entertain him, whether by wit, folly, or sheer oddness.

Pen threw the letter across the table to Warrington. “Well?” he asked. But Warrington just smiled. Perhaps he was disappointed that Pen didn’t seem more thrilled. Or perhaps he was just saving his laughter for later.

Young critics are fearless creatures. They climb onto the judgment seat with the confidence of a lion tamer and the knowledge of a schoolboy. Had Macaulay’s *History* or Herschel’s *Astronomy* been handed to Pen at that time, he would have opened them, taken a thoughtful puff of cigar, and declared them “excellent works” with the air of a man who’d already read them ten times. With the help of the *Biographie Universelle* and the British Museum, he could summon up dates, names, and facts like a magician pulling rabbits from a hat—so much so that even his mother wondered where he’d learned all this. And when he re-read his old articles months later, he’d be stunned by how much he’d forgotten—and how little he’d really known.

Still, Pen had one ally: Warrington. Without him, Pen might have become unbearable. Warrington laughed at his pretensions, mocked his confidence, and kept him grounded. Shandon, too, enjoyed Pen’s bright flashes—though he preferred them to the heavier, more serious contributions of Warrington. But despite his occasional arrogance, Pen was honest. Too honest, in fact. Mr. Bungay, the publisher, grumbled constantly about his impartiality.

One day, the argument came to a head.

“Mr. Pendennis,” Shandon said, “what on earth have you been doing? Praising one of Bacon’s books?”

Pen blinked. “Praising it? Because it’s good?”

Shandon sighed. “Bungay nearly had a stroke when he saw it. We’re supposed to be attacking Bacon, not helping him!”

“But if the book’s good,” Pen said, “shouldn’t we say so?”

“And what do you suppose a publisher does with a critical journal?” Shandon asked, raising an eyebrow. “To benefit his rival?”

“To benefit himself, certainly,” Pen replied, “but also to tell the truth. Ruat coelum, let heaven fall—truth must be told.”

Shandon laughed. “And your prospectus? Was that mathematically accurate?”

“That’s not the point,” Pen said. “We agreed it was poetic license. The giant on the show-board is bigger than the real one inside the caravan. But we’re the fiddlers—we play our tunes. You’re the showman.”

“And leader of the van,” Shandon added.

“Well,” Pen said, “I’m glad my conscience lets me play.”

“But,” he continued, “we’re all party men in England. I’ll support our side with all my heart—after all, it’s my nest. And I’ll hit the enemy hard—but fairly. One can’t tell all the truth, I suppose—but one can tell nothing but the truth. And I’d rather starve than strike an unfair blow.”

Shandon shrugged. “When we need Bacon smashed, we’ll find another hammer.”

He didn’t say it aloud, but he thought it: *In a few years, you won’t be so squeamish.*

For Shandon, morality had long since gone out the window. He’d fought on every side, lied for every cause, and now felt no remorse. “You’ve a tender conscience, Mr. Pendennis,” he said. “It’s the luxury of beginners. I may have had one once—but it wore off with the rubbing of the world.”

“I don’t know,” Pen said quietly, “if some people’s hypocrisy isnches better than others’ cynicism.”

“It’s more profitable,” Shandon replied, biting his nails. “Wenham’s as dull a quack as ever lived—and look at the carriage he drives. Poor Mrs. Shandon will never ride in her own chariot. God help her.”

Pen left, thinking deeply. *Behold this man,* he mused, *brilliant, witty, learned—yet ruined by dishonesty.* And he vowed: *I will not sell my honour for a bottle. No, by heaven’s grace—I will speak only the truth.*

But fate had a cruel joke in store.

The very next issue of the *Gazette* featured an article that sent shockwaves through the office. Warrington read it aloud, roaring with laughter. Pen, working on his own review, froze.

It was a savage attack on the *Spring Annual*. And the person most viciously attacked? Pen himself.

His poems had appeared anonymously in the *Annual*. When he refused to review it, Shandon passed it to Mr. Bludyer—the man with the talent and the temper of a bull in a china shop. Bludyer, a genius of savage humour, tore the book apart with glee. He didn’t just criticize it—he destroyed it. Then, in a moment of literary madness, he sold the volume at a bookstall and bought a pint of brandy with the proceeds.

Pen stared at the page. He hadn’t expected this. Not from his own paper. Not from his own allies.

And yet, deep down, he couldn’t help but smile.

Because, after all, the joke was on him—and he was still alive to tell the tale.
