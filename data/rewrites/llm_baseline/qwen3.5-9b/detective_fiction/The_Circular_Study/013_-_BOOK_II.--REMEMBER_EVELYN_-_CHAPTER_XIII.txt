Had tragedies of such unpremeditated violence ever occurred before? Had characters ever revealed their deepest inconsistencies through such sudden impulses? History suggests yes. Yet, not a single soul present—whether haunted by the memory of Bartow's earlier pantomime or entirely unaware of it—failed to cast a shadow of doubt upon Mr. Adams's account of his brother's violent end. Every eye betrayed a mixture of distrust and disbelief as the tale was poured forth with such rapidity.

Thomas Adams, a young man possessed of the keenest sensitivities, perceived this collective skepticism instantly. His head bowed low. "I shall add nothing to and take nothing from what I have said," he declared, his voice dogged and flat. "Make of it what you will."

Inspector Gryce, leading the inquiry, glanced sharply at the young man. "We are sorry you have taken such a resolution," the detective remarked. "There remain many things to explain, Mr. Adams. For instance, if your brother indeed slew himself in this unforeseen manner, why did you leave the house so precipitately? Why did you offer no alarm, nor even proclaim your relationship to him?"

"You need not answer," the inspector interjected quickly. "No man is compelled to incriminate himself in this free and independent country."

A smile, the saddest imaginable, traced a path across the prisoner's pallid lips. Then, lifting his head with an air of utter desperation, Adams replied, "Incrimination is not what I fear now. By the looks upon your faces, I perceive I am already lost, for I possess no means of proving my story."

This admission, sounding like the cry of an innocent man crushed under weight, caused the interrogator to pause. "You forget," Gryce suggested gently, "that you had your wife with you. She can corroborate your words and serve as an invaluable witness in your favor."

"My wife!" Adams choked out, the word barely intelligible. "Must she be dragged into this? She is so sick, so weak. It would kill her, sir. She loves me—she..."

"Was she with you in Mr. Adams's study? Did she see him lift the dagger against his own breast?"

"No." With this denial, a flicker of new courage returned to the young man. "She had fainted several moments prior, while the altercation between my brother and myself reached its height. She witnessed neither the final act, nor—" He paused, gathering strength. "Gentlemen, I might as well speak the truth; I have nothing to gain by silence. She finds it just as difficult as you do to believe that Mr. Adams struck himself. I—I have tried with all my arts to impress the reality upon her, but oh, what hope remains when the wife of my bosom, an angel who loves me... oh, sirs, she can never testify for me. She is too conscientious, too true to her own convictions. To force her would mean losing her; she would die."

Mr. Gryce attempted to interrupt, but Adams would not be silenced. "Spare me, sirs! Spare my wife! Write me down guilty, accuse me of whatever you please, rather than drag that fragile creature into the light!"

The inspector cut off these rending appeals abruptly. "Have you read the newspapers for the past few days?"

"I? Yes, sir. How could I avoid it? Blood is blood; the man was my brother. I had left him dying. Naturally, I felt anxiety, naturally I saw my own peril, and naturally I read the reports."

"Then you know he was found with a large cross upon his breast—a cross that hung on the wall. How did it come to be torn down? Who placed it upon his bosom?"

"I, sir. I am not a Catholic, though Felix was. Seeing him dying without absolution or extreme unction, I thought of the holy cross and tore down the only one visible, placing it in his arms."

"A pious act. Did he recognize it?"

"I cannot say. I had my fainting wife to attend to. She consumed all my thoughts."

"I see. You carried her out, so absorbed in your care that you did not notice Mr. Adams's valet..."

"He is innocent, sir. Whatever others may whisper, he had nothing to do with this crime."

"You did not observe him, I say, standing in the doorway, watching you?"

At this, the inspector knew the truth: Bartow had not stood in the doorway but hovered at the loophole above. Yet, the opportunity to entrap the witness was too precious to forfeit. Adams fell into the trap, evidenced by the sudden beads of perspiration breaking out on his pale forehead. Still, he struggled to maintain his stance, shouting hoarsely, "That man is mad, and deaf-and-dumb besides! Or so the papers claim. Surely his testimony is worthless. You would not confront me with him?"

"We confront you with no one. We merely ask questions. You did not observe the valet, then?"

"No, sir."

"And you do not understand the mystery of the colored lights?"

"No, sir."

"Nor the plate of steel, nor the other contrivances with which your brother enlivened his solitude?"

"I do not follow you, sir."

Yet, a distinct shift had occurred in his tone.

"I see," the inspector observed, "that the complications which have delayed our collection of testimony have not entered into the crime as you describe it. That is possible. However, there remains a circumstance demanding explanation—a minor detail, yet one of profound importance, since your wife mentioned it to you the moment she regained consciousness. I refer to the six or more words written by your brother immediately preceding his death. The paper containing them has been recovered, and it is evident it was a factor in your quarrel, given her regret that it was left behind. Do you know where we found this paper?"

The eyes Thomas Adams raised at this query held no intelligence. The mere sight of the scrap of paper seemed to drain him of all faculties. He shook his head, reaching futilely toward the document the inspector held out. Then, he erupted in a loud cry.

"Enough! I cannot endure this longer, supported only by a wicked lie. I killed my brother for reasons as valid as any man has ever had for taking a life. But I shall not reveal them. I would sooner be tried for murder and hanged."

It was a total collapse, pitiful in its contrast to the man's herculean physique and noble, albeit contracted, features. If it was the end, it was a tragic one. Mr. Gryce, a deep furrow etched between his brows, slowly rose and stepped beside the young man, who appeared ready to fall. The inspector remained motionless, tapping a rhythmic tattoo with his fingers on the table, seemingly intent on continuing the beat.

Suddenly, another cry shattered the tension. "What is that?" Adams demanded, his eyes fixed on the door, his entire frame trembling violently.

"Nothing," the inspector began, but the door swung open.

From the grasp of a man attempting to restrain her, a figure darted forward—a woman white as a wraith, radiant with a sort of holy passion. It was Adams's young, invalid wife, whom he had left three hours prior at Belleville. So frail in form and exquisite in feature, she might have seemed an unearthly visitor were it not for the human anguish radiating from her gaze. Soon, this anguish found vent in a touching cry.

"What is he saying? Oh, I know exactly what he says. He claims he killed his brother, that he held the dagger that rid the world of a monster whose wickedness went unnoticed. But you must not heed him. Indeed, you must not. He is innocent. I, his wife, have traveled twenty miles from a bed of weakness and suffering to tell you so. He—"

A hand was laid gently, yet firmly, over her mouth. She looked up, meeting her husband's eyes filled with frantic appeal. In return, she gave him a glance that pierced the hearts of every man present. Laying her own hand over his, she drew it softly away.

"It is too late, Tom. I must speak. My father, my own frailty, or your peremptory commands could not keep me at Belleville when I learned you had been brought here. Shall I stop now, in the presence of these men who have heard your words and may believe them? No, that would be a cowardice unworthy of our love and the true lives we hope to lead together. Sirs!"

Every man in the room held his breath, straining to catch the faint, fading intonation of her next words.

"I know my husband to be innocent, because the hand that held the dagger was mine. I killed Felix Cadwalader!"

The horror of such a moment is never fully grasped until long afterward. Not a single person moved—not even her husband. Yet, a slow pallor spread across every cheek, testifying to the impact of such words from lips accustomed to smiles and reflecting the highest instincts. Only when someone shouted from the doorway, "Catch her! She is falling!" did anyone stir, releasing the pent-up breath that awe and astonishment had held captive.

The man in whose evident despair lay the true cause of the terror that had driven him to a false confession sprang forward. Renewed life flashed in every feature as he caught her in his arms.
