As Thomas Gryce observed Mr. Adams, now seated in the crowded train car, a peculiar sensation crept over him. He had initially followed the young man with a mixture of curiosity and professional duty, but now, he found himself drawn to the young stranger in a more personal way. It was as if the threads of their lives had become intertwined, and Thomas couldn't help but ponder the parallels between their stories. The lines on his face deepened, etched by years of experience and the weight of his own conscience.

As the train rumbled on, Thomas watched Adams' features relax, a calmness settling over his handsome countenance like a gentle rain. It was a stark contrast to the anguish he had witnessed mere hours before. Where did this young man find the strength to endure such trials? Was it the resilience born of desperation, or had he retreated into a world beyond the reach of pain? Thomas's eyes lingered on the brooding brow, the vacant stare, and wondered what secrets lay hidden behind those troubled eyes.

When they arrived at Brooklyn Station, Thomas reboarded the train, his prisoner seemingly oblivious to his presence. At Police Headquarters, the questioning began, and Thomas expected Adams to shrink away, as he had before. Instead, the young man seemed eager to unburden himself, to confess the weight that had been crushing him.

"My name is Cadwalader," Adams revealed, his voice steady despite the tremors beneath. "I'm not who you think I am. My father, a Scotsman by birth, settled in Pennsylvania, where I was born. He had a son, Felix, from his first marriage, who died in infancy. I, however, was the product of a later union, born in Harrisburg, but my memories are of distant lands, not this country. After my mother's passing, I was sent to the Old World, where I spent most of my childhood. Returning only this year, I found my father dying, and my brother, Felix, a wealthy man, with a fortune I'd been led to believe we'd share. But after our father's passing, Felix came to New York, leaving me adrift, with no prospects, no means to support myself."
