Generous with his newfound wealth and eager to play the gracious host, young Harry Warrington could do no less than invite his country acquaintances to an evening of merriment at the Assembly Rooms. Nearly every soul remaining at the Wells found their name upon his list, for Harry was determined to prove himself a man of fashion.

The entertainment was arranged in the style of the day: card-tables stood ready in one chamber for those who could not pass an evening without the rattle of dice or the shuffle of a deck; another room offered a lavish supper, complete with champagne and steaming bowls of negus; while the grand ballroom, lit by chandeliers, awaited the dancers.

The revelry, by modern standards, might seem stiff and formal. The minuet was called first, that stately dance of measured steps and bows, and two or three couples took their turns. Lady Maria Esmond, as an earl’s daughter, held precedence among the ladies (save for the lame Lady Augusta Crutchley), and so Harry led his cousin out for the opening dance. He acquitted himself admirably, his movements far more graceful than those of Mr. Wolfe, who partnered the blushing Miss Lowther. When the first minuet concluded, Harry turned at once to Miss Theo, whose cheeks warmed with pleasure as she accepted his hand. Her parents watched fondly, while Miss Humpleby of Liphook—who had expected to follow Lady Maria—fumed in silent indignation.

Once the minuets concluded, the company turned to country dances, the music provided by a modest ensemble—harp, fiddle, and flageolet—perched in the gallery. The tunes, now faded by time, might strike modern ears as plaintive, even mournful. Yet in their day, they set feet tapping and hearts fluttering. There is a melancholy in old airs, as if the ghosts of past merriment linger in their notes.

Among Harry’s guests was a duchess—none other than the celebrated Duchess of Queensberry, once the toast of London. Yet despite her rank and wit, she found herself eclipsed by another lady: the Countess of Yarmouth-Walmoden, the Hanoverian favorite of His Majesty. The countess had been particularly gracious to Harry that morning, promising him a game of cards that evening. The poor, half-blind Colonel Blinkinsop, mistaking her invitation for himself, bowed deeply—only for the lady to laugh and declare, *"Pooh! I mean the young Firshinian!"*

By nightfall, loyalty (or ambition) drew the crowd to the countess’s side. Lords and ladies, generals and clergymen, jostled for her attention. Lady Maria attended her with quiet deference, while Madame de Bernstein maintained an air of stately courtesy. Harry, for his part, bowed as politeness required—no more, no less.

Yet Miss Hester Lambert, his dance partner, took offense at even this civility. She scarcely spoke during their minuet, and when Harry escorted her to supper, her replies were curt. Passing the countess’s card-table, the lady called out, *"Does your pretty little partner like dancing?"*

*"I thank your ladyship, I do not like dancing—nor cards,"* retorted Hester with a frosty curtsey before marching away.

Harry, stung, followed in silence until his temper snapped. *"We are rough in Virginia, madam,"* he said, his voice low and dangerous. *"And we do not suffer disrespect gladly."*

For a moment, Hester faltered. She had never seen him so transformed—his eyes dark with anger, his bearing fierce. Then, with a flimsy excuse about the heat, she fled to her mother’s side.

Elsewhere, another clash brewed—this time between the Duchess of Queensberry and Madame de Bernstein. The duchess, sharp-tongued as ever, needled the Baroness over the company’s fawning loyalty to Lady Yarmouth.

*"Ah, what a Christian country we are!"* the duchess sneered. *"So quick to forgive—and quicker still to flatter!"*

The Baroness, though provoked, refused the bait. With a diplomat’s skill, she deflected the quarrel, reminding the duchess of their shared youth. The tension dissolved into a pair of mocking curtseys, and the battle was averted.

Later, as Harry recounted the evening to Chaplain Sampson, the clergyman chuckled. *"Egad, it was like two ships of the line on the verge of engagement! Yet your aunt sailed clear with all flags flying."*

*"Why should she not?"* Harry demanded, loyal to the last.

Sampson hesitated. *"My dear boy, no one escapes gossip. Even the best of us bear scars."*

Harry’s face darkened. *"If any man speaks ill of her in my hearing—"* His fist struck the table.

*"Peace, peace!"* laughed the chaplain. *"The world is full of slander. Even you have felt its sting."*

At that moment, Colonel Lambert arrived, and the conversation shifted. The colonel, ever kind, invited Harry to visit Oakhurst again—though with a curious haste, as if concealing something.

When Harry pressed to accompany him, Lambert demurred. *"Not now—we expect company."* (A lie, as he later confessed to himself.) *"But come when Tom returns! The girls will be glad to see you—even Hester, though she scolds."*

Harry, puzzled but touched, clasped his hand. *"You have all been good to me, sir."*

Yet after the colonel left, Harry’s mood darkened again. *"Confound women!"* he muttered, throwing himself into a chair.

Sampson, ever observant, hid a smile. *"Ah, which one troubles you? The little termagant, perhaps?"*

*"She treats me as if I were a fool!"* Harry burst out.

The chaplain wisely returned to his sermon. Some battles, he knew, a man must fight alone.

As for the missing pocket-book—the one with its mysterious notes and papers—Harry had no answers. It had vanished the night of their revelry, and neither Gumbo nor the toy-shop could account for it.

*"I can trust Gumbo with my life,"* Harry said bitterly. *"But not myself."*

Sampson, pen poised, quoted softly, *"‘Oh, that a man should put an enemy into his mouth to steal away his brains!’"*

Harry groaned. *"Aye, and his memory too."*

And so the evening’s gaiety faded into regret—for lost property, for sharp words, and for the fleeting company of those who had, for a time, made the Wells feel like home.
