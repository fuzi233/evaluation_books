Our party had grown weary of Tunbridge’s stagnant diversions. Madame de Bernstein, ever restless for livelier company, declared her intent to remove to Bath once autumn arrived, pausing only to visit a few country acquaintances along the way. Harry, with little enthusiasm, agreed to escort Lady Maria and the chaplain to Castlewood. Their route carried them once more past Oakhurst village, where Harry had once been so warmly received. Maria, however, could not resist needling him about the young ladies there—their obvious designs upon him, their mother’s transparent scheming—until Harry, irritated, declared they would bypass the house entirely. His mood remained sour that evening at the inn, impervious to Maria’s coquetry, her tears, even her sighs as she retired without the private conference she had hoped for.

Ten minutes later, a horse galloped from the yard.

When the chaplain, lingering over his pipe, was asked after Harry’s whereabouts, he offered vague replies—abed, or perhaps taking a moonlit stroll. The next morning, Harry resumed his place beside Maria’s carriage, his expression stormy. Had she confessed to stealing the keepsake? she wondered in dread.

For on their walk the previous afternoon—a steep ascent near Westerham—Maria, clinging to his arm and flushed with ardor, had whispered fervent vows. Yet the more she pressed, the colder he grew. The sunlight had cruelly etched the lines upon her face, and Harry, feeling the weight of her years—and the prospect of a lifetime bound to her—cursed his own rash pledge.

Her raptures wearied him. She quoted plays, called him her champion, swore eternal fidelity. "Ah, child! Do I not cherish your image, your hair, your very words here?" she murmured, pressing a hand to her bosom. Then came the jolt: the silken case—hadn’t Bernstein burned its contents? Maria still claimed to possess them!

At Castlewood, Harry’s temper did not improve. Only Will was present, returning from shooting, his face paling at the sight of his cousin. Supper passed stiffly until Harry, with ominous calm, inquired after his horse—the brown one won at cards.

Will feigned ignorance. "Your horse? What horse?"

"The one you lost to me the night you fled on it," Harry said.

Sampson, summoned as witness, confirmed the bet.

Will sneered. "Well, you got the other nag—sold it for thirty shillings!"

Harry smiled thinly. "A fine price for a broken-kneed hack."

The servants were dismissed; the door locked. Will, sensing danger, lunged with a knife. Harry, quicker, felled him with a blow that sent him crashing against the oak table.

"Bear witness, Chaplain—fair fight," Harry said, breathing hard.

Will revived with curses, his nose bloodied, pride shattered. The servants, loyal to Harry, showed no sympathy.

The next morning, Harry sent a mocking note, inviting Will to ride. The reply was predictably violent. Then came Maria’s letter—protestations of love, pleas against quarrels—which Harry skimmed with indifference.

His stay at Castlewood was over. A dignified letter to the lord explained his departure. Maria wept; Harry did not. Crossing the bridge, his spirits lifted. London beckoned—its clubs, its races, its thrill.

His funds, however, were dwindling. A draft on his patrimony would suffice, he decided blithely. Luck at play would replenish it. Thus resolved, he settled into Bond Street, ordering new finery, powdering his hair, and entering society with a Virginian’s swagger.

White’s Club, though not yet in season, admitted him on Lord March’s recommendation. He dined, he gamed, he spent. Far away, his mother prayed; at Oakhurst, a gentler heart still beat for him. But Harry, like the Prodigal, saw only the feast before him.

**A Letter from Mr. H. Walpole** *(abridged)*

*"A young Iroquois has descended upon London—a Virginian Esmond, no less! They say he’s bled Lord March dry. I encountered him at Vauxhall, gaping at fireworks like a child. Later, hearing his family slighted, he near drew his sword on me! A spirited savage, this..."*

And so Harry Warrington, coins slipping through his fingers, strode headlong into the whirl of London, blind to the reckoning ahead.
