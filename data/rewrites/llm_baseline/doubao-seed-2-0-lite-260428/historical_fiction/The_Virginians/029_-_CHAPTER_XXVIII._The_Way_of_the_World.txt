Three days after young Harry Warrington arrived at Tunbridge Wells, he was far and away the most talked-of, sought-after person in the whole giddy little watering place. No peer of the realm staying there drew half as much curious staring; even the Bishop of Salisbury himself was greeted with less deference than the 22-year-old colonial heir. Passersby on the high road turned on their heels to watch him walk past, country folk trundling into market gaped as if he was some sort of visiting royal, and matrons clustered by the assembly room doors fussed to draw him into their circles, slipping away to leave him alone with their simpering daughters the moment they could.

Every soul in the Wells knew down to the exact acre how much tobacco and pasture land he owned in Virginia, and to the last shilling his yearly rental income. At every tea table across the resort, people gossiped and tallied his recent winnings at the card tables, marveling at how intimately their neighbors’ affairs were known to strangers. The frenzy extended even to his servant: locals slipped Gumbo mugs of spiced ale and slices of cold venison pasty behind the tavern taproom, plying him for gossip about his master’s fortune. Mr. Gumbo grew round and sleek on the free treats, was elected head of the local Society of Valets, and spun wilder tall tales by the hour, claiming Harry owned whole rivers that flowed straight with rum and herds of wild deer bigger than any stag in all of England. No dinner, ball, or picnic was considered complete unless Mr. Warrington deigned to attend it.

The lad was half amused, half bewildered by this sudden tidal wave of popularity, and bore his new honors far better than most might have. Raised to think highly of himself as the heir to one of Virginia’s grandest estates, the constant admiration certainly stoked his natural self-satisfaction, but he never grew arrogant or bragged about his victories on the racecourse or at the tables. When local gentlemen challenged him to contests of skill, he acted by the odd, honor-bound code he’d brought from the colonies: it seemed to him that as the representative of Old Virginia, he could never back down from a fair contest against the best of the mother country, whether that contest was a horse race, a drinking bout, or a hand of cards. He had a strong head for liquor, a steady hand for games of chance, a perfect seat on horseback, and nerves of unflinching steel, and he won nearly every challenge thrown his way.

Aunt Madame de Bernstein, who gambled every night to make up for the losses she’d suffered the chapter prior, was absolutely delighted by her nephew’s triumphs and his sudden social cachet. He’d beaten Jack Morris at marksmanship, outridden Mr. Scamper in a three-mile match race, and within a few days of picking up a tennis racket for the very first time, he held his own against seasoned player Captain Batts. Even the famed professional gamesters Lords Chesterfield and March sang his praises, testifying to his cool head, good humor, and natural gentle breeding. Harry was no scholar, to be sure, but he wrote a clearer, more elegant hand than half the fops of London high society, and the unselfconscious naivete of his ignorance charmed his worldly old aunt. She had read widely in her day, could discourse brilliantly on Moliere and Mr. Fielding with literary guests, but she loved the glittering, messy, human world far more than any library, and would leave any half-finished novel mid-chapter the moment a card table opened up. She fussed over his wardrobe with fond delight, raiding her private stores of fine Mechlin lace for his ruffs and shirt cuffs, and gifting him a tiny sparkling diamond brooch to pin to his frilled shirt front. Before long, he was her undisputed favorite, outshining every other nephew and cousin in the family. Poor Lady Maria seemed almost glad of his success, pleased to see him outstrip her own unpleasant brothers, who no doubt shook in their boots with fear and envy every time they heard of Harry’s triumphs, and how fast he was winding his way into their wealthy aunt’s good graces.

By the end of his second week at Tunbridge Wells, Harry was a full-fledged celebrity. He knew every last member of the respectable upper class gathered there, and by no fault of his own, he soon found himself acquainted with the far less reputable set that orbited the town too. Was he really so wrong to take the world as he found it, to drink deep of the sparkling, brimming cup of pleasure the whole resort seemed to press into his hands? His old aunt, no rigid moralist and hardly a wholesome chaperone for a young man, only egged him on to enjoy himself. Had the French ballet dancer Cattarina sent him love letters, she would no doubt have told him to accept the invitations. Harry, though, still carried the quiet modesty of his colonial upbringing, the honest homespun decency of a place where large cities and their vices were almost unknown. He blushed like a girl at the daring, ribald jokes his new London friends cracked, and even Aunt Bernstein’s racier quips left him stammering so that she teased him constantly, calling him her little Joseph, her silly colonial simpleton.

But no one at the Wells gave him credit for that innocence. Harry had no idea he was not supposed to associate with the saucy Cattarina: he’d seen Lord March driving her around in his phaeton a dozen times, so he assumed she was a perfectly acceptable acquaintance. Barely anyone in the whole resort spoke a word of French, so he thought it kind to offer her his arm on the public promenade when the doctors ordered her to walk and drink the Tunbridge waters for her bad cough. When she paused at the toy shop window and fussed over a tiny enamel locket, his pockets full of recent winnings, he happily bought it for her. The next day it was a length of fine lace, which he also gave her. When she hinted next that she wanted a pair of expensive diamond drop earrings, he laughed off the request, shrewd enough not to waste his money on a trivial fancy.

No one gave him credit for that restraint, of course. Madame de Bernstein insisted on believing he was playing Don Juan, that he’d stolen Cattarina right out from under Lord March’s nose, and she teased poor Lady Maria about it constantly, just to twist the tender spinster’s heart and revel in her miserable, flustered silence. “Boys will be boys, my dear,” she’d cackle. “I won’t have Harry be the first milksop this family ever produced.” The bread Lady Maria ate at her aunt’s table often choked her, so bitter and indigestible did the old woman’s teasing make it.

Colonel Wolfe, meanwhile, was forever riding over from Westerham to court Lady Maria, so Harry hardly expected to see much of him, though he loved the officer’s quiet, thoughtful company far more than the ribald chatter of the gamesters and fops who surrounded him. Wolfe never babbled about cards, or horse pedigrees, or bragged about his hunting exploits, or gossiped about the latest scandal gripping high society. It was a loose, dissolute age: the open mistresses of the king were treated with deference by the greatest lords of the realm, and much of the nobility chased pleasure with a wild, reckless abandon that shocked quiet colonial sensibilities. Harry had fallen in with some of the wildest of that crowd, chaperoned by an old relative who lived right in the thick of their revels.

Harry did not notice at first how Wolfe began to avoid him, how their casual greetings grew colder and stiffer. He had no idea the stories spreading about him: that he was a drunkard, a habitual gamester, a blasphemer who mocked religion, a rake who kept a notorious ballet dancer in luxury. The tales drifted out of Tunbridge Wells all the way to honest Colonel Lambert’s household at Oakhurst, one bad story piling on top of another until the good man’s kind heart was heavy with grief, so heavy his whole family noticed his distress. He tried to hide it at first, but no anxious wife can be put off forever, and Lambert was forced to confess the awful gossip to his wife. Soon the whole household, Theo and Hester included, were crushed by the news. At first Hester flew into a white-hot rage, clenched her small fists, and swore she would not believe a single word of the wicked lies. But scandal almost always wears good, innocent people down, and before long they were all crying over the thought that the gentle, sweet-natured boy they had welcomed into their home was a hardened, profligate rake. Even the parson, when they took him into their confidence, preached a whole sermon the next Sunday denouncing the human weakness to believe the worst of one’s neighbors — but even he, and the whole kind-hearted household at Oakhurst, could not shake the conviction that Harry Warrington was lost.

Harry himself had no clue any of this was happening, until he walked off the tennis courts after a particularly good match and ran straight into Colonel Wolfe, who was just returning from a visit to Lady Maria. Harry held out his hand, and Wolfe took it, but his greeting was so icy that Harry could not miss it, especially when Captain Batts tipped his hat in salute and Wolfe barely grazed the brim of his own hat with his forefinger. The tennis captain walked away confused, leaving Harry to fall into step beside Wolfe. They walked on for a few minutes, Wolfe stiff, silent, and cold.

“I haven’t seen you these many days,” Harry said, breaking the silence.
“You’ve had other, far wilder companions to keep you occupied,” Wolfe said curtly.
“I’d rather be with you than any of them,” Harry protested.
“Then I ought to be far better company for you than some of the men you’ve been keeping,” Wolfe replied grimly. When Harry asked if he meant Captain Batts, Wolfe went on: “He was drummed out of the army for rascality years ago, and his reputation hasn’t improved a bit since. You could do far, far worse than stop calling him friend.”

Harry, bewildered, asked if he had somehow offended the colonel, if he had done anything to forfeit his regard. Wolfe spoke plainly: “I don’t like some of your friends. They are of rank and fashion far too high for a hardworking, poor soldier like me, and if you keep running with that crowd, all us steady, humdrum folk will have to drop your acquaintance. I am here courting an honorable lady, Mr. Warrington. I saw you yesterday walking openly arm in arm with that French ballet dancer, and you tipped your hat to me. I would rather, frankly, that you did not acknowledge me at all when you are in that sort of company.”

Harry’s face burned bright red. “Do you mean to say I must give up the honor of your acquaintance entirely?”
“I will ask you to, when you are in that woman’s company,” Wolfe snapped, using a sharp unprintable oath Shakespeare once put in Othello’s mouth.
“Great heavens, how dare you speak of any woman like that?” Harry cried. “That poor little thing is unprotected, barely anyone here speaks a word of her language! I speak a little French, so I offered her my arm. How was I to know her reputation was so dreadful?”

Wolfe stared at him, stunned. “You mean to say you do not know the whole town is telling the story that you won her from Lord March at the dice table? Every soul at the Wells has heard it. I heard it not an hour ago in the company of good Mr. Richardson the novelist, and the ladies there were calling you a colonial Lovelace, sent over to corrupt all the virtue in England.”

He laid out every single scandalous story circulating about Harry, every false accusation of drunkenness, gambling debauchery, and blasphemy. “I tell you all this,” Wolfe said, when he was done, “because I realized the moment you reacted to that last charge that you are innocent of nearly all the rest. I have done you a terrible wrong, Mr. Warrington, and I sincerely beg your pardon.” The two men shook hands, firm and sincere, all the hard feelings gone.

Harry easily refuted almost all the charges, though he owned that he enjoyed playing cards. It seemed to him no gentleman could turn down a fair challenge from his peers, especially when he was playing only with the money he had already won, and every last person in England but the Methodists gambled, even his own aunt. Wolfe did not argue with him at length, but he warned the lad plainly that an idle life spent racing horses, playing tennis all day and drinking and gambling all night, could never be a fulfilling one. “Every man is born to work,” he said. “You are a great landowner in Virginia: you ought to study the laws of your country, learn the trades and conditions of the people on your estates, prepare to serve as a magistrate for them. Honour is the aim of any gentleman’s life, and every man can serve his country one way or another. Idle bread is the most dangerous bread a man can eat. Cards and pleasure are fine for pastimes after work is done — not instead of work, all day long. Everyone calls you the Fortunate Youth, but I think you are Warrington the Unlucky, followed by nothing but idleness, flattery, and temptation on every side. May the Lord deliver you safely from all this so-called good fortune.”

Harry walked back to his aunt’s lodging with a heavy heart, telling himself he would not drink that night, would not sit down at the card table. But the jolly fellows at the communal supper passed the bottle round nonstop, and he could not be rude enough to refuse. Then Aunt Bernstein’s card table was missing a fourth player, and how could he turn down his own favorite aunt? He sat down as her partner, won hand after hand by some miracle of luck, and when the sun rose over the hills the next morning, he was still at the table, feasting on cold chicken and drinking sparkling champagne at dawn, the colonel’s warnings already slipping fast from his mind.
