For months, our existence at the fort was a weary rhythm of endurance. The Commandant and I shared a volatile bond, cycling through bitter quarrels and fragile reconciliations. Our evenings were filled with greasy card games, dismal duets featuring his asthmatic flute and my cracked guitar, and the harsh reality of our captives. Poor Fawn endured the blows and the vials of liquor dispensed by her master at whim, yet she remained a pillar of care, nursing her papoose, soothing her master's gout, and tending to prisoners shivering with ague. This stagnant life held steady until the early autumn of the preceding year, when a hunter arrived bearing news that electrified the tiny garrison.

General Montcalm had dispatched a significant reinforcement to secure French-held forts and expand their hold on enemy territory. Troops departing Quebec and Montreal traveled down the St. Lawrence and across the lakes in bateaux, laden with artillery and vast supplies. A commander of higher rank would soon replace Museau, posing a grave threat to my situation. He might exchange me, or worse, surrender me to the Indians in retaliation for the cruelties inflicted upon enemy officers. The men of the fort dreamed wildly of reinforcements, envisioning advances into Pennsylvania and New York, the seizure of Albany and Philadelphia, and the driving of the "Rosbifs" back to the sea. Yet, beneath this triumphant rhetoric, Museau found no genuine satisfaction in the prospect of a French conquest.

"Eh, Commandant!" I would say. "'Tis *fort bien*, but meanwhile, where is your farm in Normandy? Where is the pot of cider and the *trippes à la mode de Caen*?"

"Yes," he would reply, his voice dropping. "'Tis all well, my boy. But consider: when poor old Museau is replaced, where will you be? Few men possess the humanity I do. If a great garrison arrives, will my successors grant you the indulgences honest Museau provides? You will be penned like a pig awaiting slaughter. As surely as an officer falls into the hands of your frontier braves and suffers, so surely will you pay with your skin for his blood. They will give you to our red allies—the Brethren of La Biche yonder. Remember last year? What did they do to your countrymen captured at Braddock? Roasting was merely the least of their punishments, *ma foi*. Was it not, La Biche?"

Museau launched into a vivid, macabre catalog of tortures—eyes gouged, teeth and nails twisted, limbs severed. You, dear Miss Theo, would turn pale at the recollection. However, I spared you the full horror, for I promised mercy.

La Biche, however, remained unmoved by the tale of horrors. She had witnessed them herself. Born of the Senecas, whose villages lay near the Great Cataract between Ontario and Erie, her people swung between alliance with the English and hatred for them. In the endless cycles of tribal warfare, it was difficult to determine whether white or red skin proved the more savage.

"They may chop me into cutlets and broil me, Commandant," I said, meeting his gaze with feigned calm. "But again, you will never regain the farm in Normandy."

"Go fetch the whisky-bottle, La Biche," Museau commanded.

"And it is not too late, even now," I pressed. "I will reward the guide who returns me home. I promise, as a man of honor, ten thousand livres to whoever brings me a token—say, my watch and seal, inscribed with my grandfather's arms, hidden in a chest somewhere within this fort."

"*Ah, scélérat!* Howls the Commandant, with a hoarse laugh. 'Thou hast eyes, thou! All is fair prize in war.'"

"Think of a house in your village, a fine field with six cows, a orchard heavy with fruit."

"And Javotte at the door with her wheel, and a rascal of a child or two, cheeks as red as the apples! O my country! O my mother!" Museau whimpered. "Quick, La Biche, the whisky!"

That night, the Commandant sank into deep thought, as did La Biche, silent and melancholy. Sitting apart, nursing her child, I caught her eyes fixed upon me whenever I glanced her way. Her infant cried, and Museau, with his usual foulspeech, ordered it away to the lean-to where the unfortunate La Biche resided. Once she was gone, I spoke my mind freely, presenting reasons that his greed could not ignore.

"How do you know," he asked, "that this hunter will serve you?"

"That is my secret," I replied. "However, if you prefer, I may reveal it. When traders enter the settlements for barter, they often linger for days, seeking rest, drink, and camaraderie. Our new friend loved these things. He gambled with the men, traded furs for liquor, and delighted in the fort. I suspect he enjoys listening to my songs. For lack of better entertainment, I often sang and scraped my guitar, leading to many impromptu concerts, joined by the men in chorus or dance, until interrupted by the retreat drum."

Our guest, the hunter, attended one or two of these gatherings. Suspecting he understood English, I challenged the men: "Lads, I will sing you an English song." To the melody of "Over the hills and far away"—an air my grandfather once hummed in Marlborough's camp—I improvised doggerel verses: *"This long, long year, a prisoner drear; Ah, me! I'm tired of lingering here: I'll give a hundred guineas gay, To be over the hills and far away."*

"What is it?" asked the hunter. "I don't understand."

"'Tis a girl to her lover," I answered. Yet, I saw the twinkle in his eye; he understood.

The following day, when silence reigned, the trapper confirmed my suspicion. Passing me, he hummed the same tune, the burden of my verse, in perfect English.

"If you are ready," he said, "I am ready. I know your kin and the path to them. Speak to Fawn, and she will instruct you. What! You refuse to play?" He produced a deck of cards just as two soldiers approached. "*Milor est trop grand seigneur?* Bonjour, my lord!" He bowed mockingly, shrugged, and departed to seek other players.

I realized then that La Biche was to be my agent, and Museau's offer had been accepted. Fawn performed her role with faithfulness and dexterity. Having tended me through wounds and illness, assisted with my chamber tasks, and been permitted free movement to the river and fields, she was uniquely positioned to aid me. The trapper, having squandered most of his peltry earnings, gathered his flint, powder, and blankets, then departed. Three days later, Fawn signaled that the time for my trial of freedom had arrived.

Since my initial injury, I had been cared for by Florac in the officers' room. With the fort stripped of its senior officers save for an old lieutenant, my confinement varied: sometimes free, sometimes locked away on prisoners' rations, sometimes invited to share his mess by my tipsy jailer. This log house, identical to the others mounting four small-caliber guns (one guarding the bastion behind my cabin), overlooked the confluence of the Ohio and Monongahela rivers. Westward, past the guns, lay a small island where Duquesne stood, and beyond it, trees on the opposite shore.

"You see those trees?" La Biche asked me the day prior, speaking her broken French jargon. "He waits for you behind them."

During the day, the door to my quarters stood open, and La Biche moved freely. The eve before her return from the fields, carrying a pick and a basket of greens for soup, she sat on a bench outside. Leaning her pick against the doorframe, she watched me. Standing nearby, I was so stunned by the absurdity of the situation that I never considered using the tool she held. "Hide it," she whispered urgently. "You will need it soon." That afternoon, she pointed out the trees.

The next day, she appeared, feigning anger. "My lord! My lord! Why do you not attend the Commandant's dinner? He is ill! Entendez-vous?" Peering inside, she tossed a coil of rope at me.

"I am coming, La Biche," I said, hobbling after her on my crutch. As I entered the Commandant's quarters, she murmured, "*Pour ce soir*"—for tonight—and I knew the hour had struck.

Museau, oblivious to the scheme, grumbled about the cold soup and stared me down, talking of trifles while his servant watched. Later, smoking our pipes and playing piquet, with La Biche cowering in the corner, he noted my empty whisky bottle. "I must have another glass," he declared, rising to retrieve firewater from his locked inner room, deliberately keeping La Biche away from the temptation.

The moment his back was turned, La Biche rose. Before he returned, she knelt at my feet, kissing my hand, pressing it to her heart, and weeping openly on my knees.
