A Florentine Joke.

The morning sun rose warm and golden over the Piazza di San Giovanni, casting a spell of festive idleness upon the city. Yet, for Tito, the light offered no comfort. He had just returned from Bratti's shop in the narrow thoroughfare of the Ferravecchi, his pockets heavy with fifty florins but his spirit hollow. The Genoese stranger had departed with the onyx ring, leaving Tito with a bitter taste in his mouth. A fleeting thought crossed his mind: if Fortune had truly spared him the necessity of fleeing Florence, perhaps he should not have parted with the ring, for it was worn not merely as jewelry, but as a vessel for cherished memories and predilections. Still, the loss was a trivial matter compared to the weight of his current shame. The feverish excitement that had once driven him to look toward the future had cooled into a dull, regretful lassitude. Now, he envied the ignorance of those who had never risked ignominy by shrinking from the obligations his peers demanded.

Deeds, he realized with a sinking heart, were like children born to us; they lived and acted apart from our own will. While children might be strangled, deeds possessed an indestructible vitality, pressing hard upon the conscience whether we acknowledged them or not. This dreadful vitality was crushing Tito for the first time.

He turned away from the Mercato Vecchio, avoiding the nearest path lest he encounter Tessa. He was not in the mood to seek anything, only to await the first sign of his altered lot. The piazza itself was a tableau of life: stalls laden with fruit and flowers surrounded the octagonal Baptistery, while mules stood patiently in their nose-bags, their drivers perhaps already kneeling before the Virgin inside the sacred doors. On the broad marble steps of the Duomo, beggars and gossipers mingled. An old crone encouraged a round-capped baby to feel the warmth of the stone, while a suspicious dog sniffed nearby; shaggy-headed boys watched a pale cripple carving a face into a cherry-stone; and above them, men knotted ribbons in desultory laughter or gesticulated in earnest conversation.

Tito's destination, however, was the barber's shop of Nello. It was the busiest hour of the day, and under the pretty red and white awning, men preferred the fresh air to the narrow confines of the shop. To sit with a lathered chin thrown backward, having one's nose made a handle, was not a sublime posture, yet it was a fashion of Florentine respectability, and the men looked at each other with grave seriousness while undergoing the ritual. The barber's tongue was in its glory, the deft activity of the razor and the lather seeming set in motion by a common spring. Tito knew he could not escape the circle of conversation; he would have to smile, retort, and appear perfectly at ease. It was merely the ordeal of swallowing bread and cheese pills, he told himself. The man who let the anticipation of discovery choke him was simply weak of nerve.

Just as he prepared to enter, a hand laid heavily upon his shoulder. No amount of prior resolution could prevent the unpleasant shock of the sudden touch. Tito turned, his face betraying an inward start, only to find the owner of the hand breaking into a light laugh. It was a young man of Tito's own age, with keen features, a small, close-clipped head, and a close-shaven lip and chin that suggested a mind unburdened by non-essential matters. His eyes were bright with hope and friendliness, a brightness that would later fade into bitterness for many; at this moment, however, Niccolò Machiavelli was hailed as a man of promise destined to mend the fortunes of his ancient family.

"Why, Melema," Machiavelli said, "what evil dream did you have last night that you took my light grasp for that of an *sbirro* or something worse?"

"Ah, Messer Niccolò!" Tito recovered himself instantly. "It must have been an extra amount of dulness in my veins this morning that shuddered at the approach of your wit. But the fact is, I have had a bad night."

"That is unlucky, because you will be expected to shine without any obstructing fog today in the Rucellai Gardens. I take it for granted you are to be there."

"Messer Bernardo did me the honor to invite me," Tito replied, "but I shall be engaged elsewhere."

"Ah! I remember, you are in love," Machiavelli shrugged, "else you would never have such inconvenient engagements. Why, we are to eat a peacock and ortolans under the loggia among Bernardo Rucellai's rare trees; there are to be the choicest spirits in Florence and the choicest wines. Only, as Piero de' Medici is to be there, the choice spirits may happen to be swamped in the capping of impromptu verses. I hate that game; it is a device for the triumph of small wits, who are always inspired the most by the smallest occasions."

"What is that you are saying about Piero de' Medici and small wits, Messer Niccolò?" asked Nello, whose light figure dominated the Herculean frame of Niccolò Caparra.

The famous worker in iron, whom they had last seen with bared muscular arms and a leathern apron in the Mercato Vecchio, was now dressed in holiday attire. As he sat submissively while Nello skipped around him, lathering his chin, seizing his nose, and scraping him with magical quickness, he looked much as a lion might if it had donned linen and tunic to prepare for society.

"A private secretary will never rise in the world if he couples great and small in that way," Nello continued. "When great men are not allowed to marry their sons and daughters as they like, small men must not expect to marry their words as they like. Have you heard the news Domenico Cennini has been telling us?—that Pagolantonio Soderini has given Ser Piero da Bibbiena a box on the ear for setting on Piero de' Medici to interfere with the marriage between young Tommaso Soderini and Fiammetta Strozzi, and is to be sent ambassador to Venice as a punishment?"

"I don't know which I envy him most," Machiavelli mused, "the offense or the punishment. The offense will make him the most popular man in all Florence, and the punishment will take him among the only people in Italy who have known how to manage their own affairs."

"Yes, if Soderini stays long enough at Venice," Cennini added, "he may chance to learn the Venetian fashion, and bring it home with him. The Soderini have been fast friends of the Medici, but what has happened is likely to open Pagolantonio's eyes to the good of our old Florentine trick of choosing a new harness when the old one galls us; if we have not quite lost the trick in these last fifty years."

"Not we," said Niccolò Caparra, rejoicing in the free use of his lips again. "Eat eggs in Lent and the snow will melt. That's what I say to our people when they get noisy over their cups at San Gallo, and talk of raising a *romor* (insurrection): I say, never do you plan a *romor*; you may as well try to fill the Arno with buckets. When there's water enough, the Arno will be full, and that will not be till the torrent is ready."

"Caparra, that oracular speech of yours is due to my excellent shaving," Nello remarked. "You could never have made it with that dark rust on your chin. Ecco, Messer Domenico, I am ready for you now. By the way, my bel erudito," he continued as he saw Tito moving toward the door, "here has been old Maso seeking for you, but your nest was empty. He will come again presently. The old man looked mournful, and seemed in haste. I hope there is nothing wrong in the Via de' Bardi."

"Doubtless Messer Tito knows that Bardo's son is dead," said Cronaca, who had just come up.

Tito's heart gave a leap—had the death occurred before Romola saw him?

"No, I had not heard it," he said, with no more discomposure than the occasion warranted, turning and leaning against the doorpost as if he had abandoned his intention of leaving. "I knew that his sister had gone to see him. Did he die before she arrived?"

"No," said Cronaca. "I was in San Marco at the time, and saw her come out from the chapter-house with Fra Girolamo, who told us that the dying man's breath had been preserved as by a miracle, that he might make a disclosure to his sister."

Tito felt that his fate was decided. Again his mind rushed over all the circumstances of his departure from Florence, and he conceived a plan of retrieving his money from Cennini before the disclosure became public. If he once had his money, he need not stay long enduring scorching looks and biting words. He would wait now, go away with Cennini, and get the money from him at once. With this project in his mind, he stood motionless, hands in his belt, eyes fixed absently on the ground. Nello, glancing at him, felt sure that he was absorbed in anxiety about Romola. He thought Tito such a pretty image of self-forgetful sadness that he just perceptibly pointed his razor at him and gave a challenging look at Piero di Cosimo, whom he had never forgiven for refusing to see any prognostics of character in his favorite's handsome face. Piero, leaning against the other doorpost close to Tito, shrugged his shoulders; the frequent recurrence of such challenges from Nello had changed the painter's first declaration of neutrality into a positive inclination to believe ill of the much-praised Greek.

"So you have got your Fra Girolamo back again, Cronaca? I suppose we shall have him preaching again this next Advent," said Nello.

"And not before there is need," said Cronaca, gravely. "We have had the best testimony to his words since the last Quaresima; for even to the wicked, wickedness has become a plague; and the ripeness of vice is turning to rottenness in the nostrils even of the vicious. There has not been a change since the Quaresima, either in Rome or at Florence, but has put a new seal on the Frate's words—that the harvest of sin is ripe, and that God will reap it with a sword."

"I hope he has had a new vision, however," said Francesco Cei, sneeringly. "The old ones are somewhat stale. Can't your Frate get a poet to help out his imagination for him?"

"He has no lack of poets about him," said Cronaca, with quiet contempt, "but they are great poets and not little ones; so they are contented to be taught by him, and no more think the truth stale which God has given him to utter, than they think the light of the moon is stale. But perhaps certain high prelates and princes who dislike the Frate's denunciations might be pleased to hear that, though Giovanni Pico, and Poliziano, and Marsilio Ficino, and most other men of mark in Florence, reverence Fra Girolamo, Messer Francesco Cei despises him."

"Poliziano?" said Cei, with a scornful laugh. "Yes, doubtless he believes in your new Jonah; witness the fine orations he wrote for the envoys of Siena, to tell Alexander the Sixth that the world and the Church were never so well off as since he became Pope."

"Nay, Francesco," said Machiavelli, smiling, "a various scholar must have various opinions. And as for the Frate, whatever we may think of his saintliness, you judge his preaching too narrowly. The secret of oratory lies, not in saying new things, but in saying things with a certain power that moves the hearers—without which, as old Filelfo has said, your speaker deserves to be called, 'non oratorem, sed aratorem.' And, according to that test, Fra Girolamo is a great orator."

"That is true, Niccolò," said Cennini, speaking from the shaving-chair, "but part of the secret lies in the prophetic visions. Our people—no offense to you, Cronaca—will run after anything in the shape of a prophet, especially if he prophesies terrors and tribulations."

"Rather say, Cennini," answered Cronaca, "that the chief secret lies in the Frate's pure life and strong faith, which stamp him as a messenger of God."

"I admit it—I admit it," said Cennini, opening his palms as he rose from the chair. "His life is spotless: no man has impeached it."

"He is satisfied with the pleasant lust of arrogance," Cei burst out bitterly. "I can see it in that proud lip and satisfied eye of his. He hears the air filled with his own name—Fra Girolamo Savonarola, of Ferrara; the prophet, the saint, the mighty preacher, who frightens the very babies of Florence into laying down their wicked baubles."

"Come, come, Francesco, you are out of humor with waiting," said the conciliatory Nello. "Let me stop your mouth with a little lather. I must not have my friend Cronaca made angry: I have a regard for his chin; and his chin is in no respect altered since he became a Piagnone.
