*“Virtue, gentlemen—yea, virtue—is what makes the gentleman. It enriches the poor, ennobles the low-born, exalts the subject, beautifies the deformed, heals the sick, strengthens the weak, and turns misery into joy. In man’s nature, two sovereign gifts are bestowed: knowledge and reason. The one commands, the other obeys. These no fickle wheel of fortune can alter, no cunning of the world can sunder, no sickness diminish, nor age destroy.”*
—**LILLY’S *Euphues*, 1586**

It now falls to me to recount the founding of that most chivalrous fellowship—the Brotherhood of the Rose—which in time would earn renown not only in Devon but across distant lands: in Ireland, the Netherlands, the Spanish Main, and even the heart of South America. Should this tale strike some as fanciful, let them recall that the men who spoke thus of love and honor were also hardened soldiers, shrewd statesmen, and bold adventurers. The same hand that penned the *Arcadia* also drafted treaties; the poet of the *Faerie Queene* was also the strategist behind *The State of Ireland*. And if any sneer at *Euphues*, I ask only—have they read it? For beneath its occasional pedantry lies a noble spirit, a testament to the honor and piety of the Elizabethan age.

And so, to our tale—which those of sterner tastes may freely pass by.

Amyas could not sail the next day, nor the one after, for a stubborn southwesterly gale pinned the *Mary Grenville* fast in Appledore Pool. With the ship readied for departure at the first shift of wind, he turned homeward. When his mother rode for Clovelly to tend Frank’s wounds, he escorted her as far as Bideford, where he encountered a procession led by Will Cary. Clad in gleaming armor, sword at his side, Cary cut a gallant figure, trailed by servants bearing lances and longbows. Behind them, to Mrs. Leigh’s relief, came Frank himself—pale but insistent that his wounds were trifling, and that he would lodge at the Ship Tavern to bid his brother farewell.

No sooner had Frank settled in than he and Amyas devised a scheme. The next day, as the town swarmed with market-goers, Amyas waylaid each of Rose Salterne’s admirers with private invitations to a feast. One by one, they arrived—only to find themselves seated beside their rivals, stiff with mutual disdain. Yet Frank, reclining at the table’s head with Amyas at its foot, disarmed them with tales and jests until the wine worked its magic.

When the toasts began—first to the Queen, then to Devon’s mariners—Frank struck his masterstroke.

*“Gentlemen,”* he declared, *“let us drink to one whom beauty and virtue have made peerless—the Rose of Torridge!”*

A stunned silence followed. Each man flushed, then paled, each eyeing the other as if to say, *By what right do you dare name her?* But Frank pressed on, urging them to love not as rivals but as brothers, bound by shared devotion.

*“Shall we stain our hands with each other’s blood, only to prove ourselves unworthy of her? Let us instead contend in nobleness—in loyalty, in valor, in courtesy. Let our love lift us higher, not drag us into strife.”*

His words, at first met with skepticism, began to thaw even the proudest hearts. Coffin, who had risen to leave, paused as Frank implored him—not as a superior, but as a friend.

One by one, the young men yielded—first Cary, then Fortescue, then Coffin himself. Hands were clasped; old grudges dissolved. They swore a pact then and there: to set aside jealousy, to uphold one another, and to seek glory abroad, as befitted knights of the Rose.

And as the pact was sealed, a rustling betrayed an eavesdropper—Jack Brimblecombe, the schoolmaster’s son, discovered behind the arras. Plump, bright-eyed, and ever-curious, Jack was dragged forth to join the fellowship, completing the circle.

Thus was the Brotherhood of the Rose born—not in strife, but in shared honor, a bond as enduring as the virtues it celebrated.
