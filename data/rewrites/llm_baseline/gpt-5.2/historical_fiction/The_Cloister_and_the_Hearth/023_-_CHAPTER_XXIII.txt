Life and liberty are like the daily loaf: while they sit warm on the board, no one marvels; let the oven go cold, let hunger prowl the threshold, and each crust becomes treasure, each mouthful a prayer. So it was with Gerard and Margaret. A little while ago the wood had been all alarm—dogs baying, men’s coarse shouts cracking through the trunks like whips—yet now, sliding between the friendly trees, they drank in the deep, sheltered quiet as if it were broth after fever. The forest’s hush was not emptiness: it had its own homely music—the soft comb of wind through needles, the distant tap of a woodpecker like a cook’s cleaver on a block, the muted tread of their feet in leaf-mould. Against that sane, green acoustics, the remembered noise of pursuit rang all the fouler in their ears, and made the present silence taste sweeter.

But Gerard, glancing down as they went, saw dark stains on Margaret’s ankles, smeared where her shoes had brushed her hose.

“Martin! Martin!” he cried, voice breaking as if a cord had snapped. “Help! they have wounded her—the crossbow!”

Martin, trudging with the mule’s bridle looped in his fist, wheeled round. Margaret did too, and smiled quickly—too quickly.

“No, no,” she said, laying the smile on him like a cloth over a hurt. “I am not wounded, nor hurt at all.”

“But what is it then, in Heaven’s name?” Gerard’s hands hovered, helpless, as if he could cover her whole with them.

“Scold me not, then!” She blushed—red rising through her pallor like wine through water.

“Did I ever scold you?”

“No, dear Gerard. Well—Martin said it was blood those cruel dogs followed; so I thought, if I could but have a little blood on my shoon, the dogs would follow me instead, and let my Gerard wend free. So I scratched my arm with Martin’s knife—forgive me! Whose else could I take? Yours, Gerard? Ah, no. You forgive me?” She pleaded and caressed in the same breath, like one offering herself up and asking pardon for it.

“Let me see this scratch first,” said Gerard, and his throat tightened.

She showed her forearm.

“There,” he whispered, turning white. “A scratch? I call it a cut—a deep, terrible, cruel cut.” The fresh blood had the bright, honest smell of iron, sharp as a struck coin; it made him shudder more than any battlefield tale. “Oh, Margaret—”

“She might have done it with her bodkin,” growled Martin. “Milksop, that sickens at sight of a scratch and a little blood.”

Gerard heard him, but the words had no hold. “No,” he said hoarsely. “I could look on a sea of blood—but not on hers. Oh, Margaret! how could you be so cruel?”

Margaret’s smile softened, not offended—only full. “Foolish Gerard,” she murmured, and flung the guilty arm round his neck as if to bind him there. “As if I would not give all the blood in my heart for you, let alone a few drops from my arm.”

And under the memory of how near he had been to losing her—of how near she had been to losing him—she began to weep upon his neck, tears warm as fresh milk. He wept too, and the wood took their sobs and dulled them, the way moss takes a footfall.

“And I must part from her,” he sobbed, the words forced out like breath from a broken bellows. “We two that love so dear—one must be in Holland, one in Italy. Ah me! ah me! ah me!”

At that, Margaret wept afresh, but made no lament. Something in her—an instinct that was as watchful as any housewife’s ear for a pot boiling over—kept her from speaking what she thought. To pour out her whole heart now would be to burden his already weighted going. So she bore it in silence, and let only her tears speak.

They came suddenly out upon a beaten path. It felt, after the forest, like stepping from a chapel into a market. The very gravel underfoot had a harsher sound. Martin stopped and pointed with his chin, hanging his head as if he pointed to a grave.

“This is the bridle-road I spoke of,” said he. “And there away lies the hostelry.”

Gerard and Margaret looked at one another—one quick, frightened glance, like two children caught at a forbidden cupboard.

“Come a step with me, Martin,” whispered Gerard.

He drew the soldier behind a thicket, where the leaves could hide faces, if not sorrow. Gerard’s voice came brokenly, as if each word must be fetched up from deep.

“Good Martin, watch over her for me. She is my wife; yet I leave her. See—here is gold—it was for my journey; it is no use my asking her to take it—she would not; but you will for her, will you not? Oh, Heaven! and is this all I can do for her? Money? But poverty is a curse. You will not let her want for anything, dear Martin? The burgomaster’s silver is enough for me.”

Martin’s hard mouth worked once, as if he chewed a tough morsel. “Thou art a good lad, Gerard. Neither want nor harm shall come to her. I care more for her little finger than for all the world; and were she nought to me, even for thy sake would I be a father to her. Go with a stout heart, and God be with thee going and coming.”

He wrung Gerard’s hand and turned his head away, ashamed of the softness in him.

After a moment he moved as if to go back; but Gerard caught his sleeve.

“No, good Martin—prithee, stay here behind this thicket, and turn your head away from us, while I—oh, Martin! Martin!”

So Martin, who had seen wounds and deaths without blinking, spared himself this. He did not witness the poor young things kneel and renew before Heaven those vows which rough men had broken with brute noise. He did not see them cling together as if stitched by the same thread, and then try to part, and fail, and return, and cling again like drowning creatures who cannot trust the shore. But he heard Gerard sob, and sob, and Margaret’s low moan—the kind of sound that lives under speech, untranslatable into any tongue a man can boast of learning.

At last there was a hoarse cry, and the quick patter of feet on the hard road. Martin started up—and there was Gerard running wildly, both hands clasped above his head as if in prayer against the air itself. Margaret tottered after him, palms stretched out piteously as if asking to be held up; her cheek was ashy, her eyes fixed on nothing, like a lamp gone out while the wick still smokes.

Martin caught her in his arms and spoke comfort—plain, soldierly words—but her mind could not take them in. Only the sound of a voice reached her, and at that she moaned and clutched him, trembling from head to foot.

He got her on the mule, set his arm round her, and so, supporting the body that had been strong as a boy’s and was now slack and powerless, he led her slowly and sadly home. She shed no tear now, spoke no word. Grief had stopped her like a clog in a millstream.

At the edge of the wood he took her down and bade her go across to her father’s house. She went, obedient as one walking in sleep.

Martin went on to Rotterdam; Sevenbergen was too hot for him.

Gerard, severed from her he loved, moved like a man under enchantment. He hired a horse and a guide at the little hostelry and rode swiftly toward the German frontier; yet nothing entered him. Trees and houses and men slid by as if seen through oiled parchment. His companions spoke twice; he answered nothing. Only once, as if the words leapt out of him of their own cruelty, he cried, “Shall we never be out of this hateful country?”

After many hours they reached the brow of a steep hill. A small brook ran at the bottom, its sound thin but continual—the patient talking of water over stones.

“Halt!” cried the guide, and pointed across the valley. “Here is Germany.”

“Where?” Gerard asked, as if he doubted that any land could be real after what he had left.

“On t’other side of the bourn. No need to ride down the hill, I trow.”

Gerard dismounted without a word and drew the burgomaster’s purse from his girdle. As he opened it the guide, half sulky, said, “You will soon be out of this hateful country. Mayhap the one you are going to will like you no better; any way, though it be a church you have robbed, they cannot take you, once across that bourn.”

At another time Gerard would have rebuked him, or struck him. Now the words fell on him like smoke and drifted away. He paid the lad in silence and went down the hill alone.

The brook was silvery. It murmured over little pebbles that glittered as if varnished, each stone a small, clean thing, rinsed and made itself again. He sat and looked stupidly at them, at the way water made a continuous song without knowing the meaning of its own notes. The sound steadied him more than any counsel.

Then he drank. The water was cold and pure, tasting of earth and leaf-shadow. He laved his hot feet and hands; the chill bit, and the bite woke him, the way sharp vinegar wakes the palate.

He rose; then, taking a short run, leaped across into Germany.

Even as his foot struck the strange bank, he turned and looked back.

“Farewell, ungrateful country!” he cried. “But for her it would cost me nought to leave you for ever, and all my kith and kin, and—the mother that bore me, and—my playmates, and my little native town. Farewell, fatherland—welcome the wide world! omne solum forti patria.”

The brave Latin sat oddly with the rawness in his mouth; and with it still there, he suddenly drooped, arms and legs turned weak, and he sat down on the foreign soil and sobbed bitterly, like a child who cannot name what hurts him.

When he had sat a while bowed down, he rose, dashed the tears from his eyes like a man, and—lest one look back should undo him—cast not a single glance behind. He stepped out into the wide world.

His love and heavy sorrow left no room for vulgar misgiving. Compared with tearing himself from Margaret, it seemed a small thing to go on foot to Italy in that rude age. All nations met in a convent; and thanks to the monks’ teaching and his own thirst of knowledge, he could speak most tongues needful on that long road.

“I will soon be at Rome,” he said to himself. “The sooner the better now.”

After walking a good league he came to a place where four ways met—country roads, serpentine, puzzling even to neighbors who had trudged them all their lives between field and village. Gerard took out a little dial Peter had given him, set it in the autumn sun, and by this small, honest instrument—this portable scrap of ordered light—chose his way unhesitatingly for Rome, inexperienced as a young swallow flying south; but unlike the swallow, wandering south alone.
