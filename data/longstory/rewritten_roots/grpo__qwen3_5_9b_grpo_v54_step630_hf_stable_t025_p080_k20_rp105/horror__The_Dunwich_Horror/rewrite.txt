The wrong fork at the junction of the Aylesbury Pike, just past Dean's Corners, does not lead to a mistake; it leads to a trap. The traveler finds himself descending into a geography that defies the logic of the settled world. Here, the earth rises with a sickening steepness, and the stone walls, choked with thorny briars, press inward until the road feels like a throat being constricted. The forests are not merely dense; they are oppressive, their trees swollen to grotesque proportions, while the undergrowth—a chaotic luxuriance of wild weeds and brambles—suggests a nature that has forgotten how to be tamed. Yet, amidst this overgrown wildness, the cultivated fields are terrifyingly sparse, and the scattered houses stand as uniform monuments to a slow, agonizing decay. They are ancient, squalid, and dilapidated, wearing the same face of neglect that seems to haunt the very air.

One hesitates to seek aid from the figures spotted on crumbling doorsteps or lurking in the rock-strewn meadows. These natives are silent, furtive creatures, moving with a caution that suggests they are hiding from something far worse than the traveler. To approach them feels like an intrusion into a forbidden zone, a violation of a pact one should never break. As the road climbs, revealing the mountains looming above the deep woods, a primal unease settles in the gut. The summits are unnaturally rounded and symmetrical, lacking the rugged randomness of true geology, and often, the sky cuts a sharp silhouette against the tops, revealing the queer, circular crowns of tall stone pillars that pierce the heavens like the ribs of some buried leviathan.

Gorges of impossible depth slice through the landscape, bridged only by crude wooden structures that seem poised to collapse at the slightest tremor. When the road dips, it plunges into stretches of marshland that radiate a cold, instinctive dread, especially at twilight. Here, the unseen whippoorwills chatter with a malicious rhythm, and fireflies dance in abnormal profusion to the raucous, creeping pipes of bullfrogs whose calls sound less like nature and more like a warning. The Miskatonic River, winding close to the feet of these domed hills, looks less like water and more like a thin, shining serpent slithering through a nightmare.

As the hills draw nearer, their dark, precipitous sides loom like the walls of a tomb, offering no escape route. Beyond a covered bridge, a small village huddles between the stream and the vertical slope of Round Mountain. It is a cluster of rotting gambrel roofs, architectural relics from a time long past, standing in stark contrast to the modern world outside. Closer inspection reveals a grim truth: most houses are empty shells, falling into ruin, while the broken steeple of the church now shelters the only mercantile establishment in the hamlet—a slovenly, desperate outpost of commerce. One dreads the tenebrous tunnel of the bridge, yet there is no choice but to enter. Once across, the village street seems to exhale a faint, malign odor, the scent of massed mold and centuries of decay. It is a relief to flee, to follow the narrow road around the base of the hills and back to the Aylesbury Pike, only to learn later, with a chill that lingers for days, that one has passed through Dunwich.

Outsiders visit Dunwich as rarely as possible. Since the season of horror that ended in 1928, every signboard pointing toward the town has been torn down. By any ordinary aesthetic standard, the scenery is breathtakingly beautiful; yet, no artists or tourists dare tread here. Two centuries ago, when superstition held sway, people avoided the area due to whispers of witch-blood and strange forest presences. Today, in our sensible age, the avoidance is driven by a collective amnesia regarding the Dunwich Horror of 1928, a tragedy hushed up by those who claimed to care for the town's welfare. But perhaps the true reason lies deeper: the natives are repellently decadent, having walked a path of retrogression that has turned them into a distinct, degenerate race. Defined by the mental and physical stigmata of inbreeding, their intelligence is woefully low, and their history reeks of overt viciousness, half-hidden murders, incest, and acts of violence so perverse they defy naming.

Even the old gentry, the remnants of the armigerous families from Salem in 1692, cannot escape the tide of decay. While some branches of the Whateleys and Bishops still send their eldest sons to Harvard and Miskatonic University, those sons seldom return to the moldering gambrel roofs where their ancestors were born and died. No one can definitively explain what is wrong with Dunwich, though old legends persist. They speak of unhallowed rites and Indian conclaves where forbidden shadows were summoned from the great rounded hills, answered by wild orgiastic prayers and the loud crackings of the earth itself. In 1747, Reverend Abijah Hoadley preached a memorable sermon on the close presence of Satan, claiming to hear the cursed voices of Azazel, Buzrael, Beelzebub, and Belial rising from underground. He described a discourse of evil powers in the hill behind his house—a rattling, rolling, groaning, screeching, and hissing that no earthly thing could produce. Hoadley vanished shortly after delivering this sermon, but his text, printed in Springfield, remains.

Noises in the hills continue to puzzle geologists and physiographers to this day. Other traditions speak of foul odors near the hill-crowning stone circles and of rushing airy presences heard faintly from the bottom of the ravines. There is the Devil's Hop Yard, a bleak, blasted hillside where no vegetation can take root. The natives are mortally afraid of the numerous whippoorwills, which grow vocal on warm nights. They are believed to be psychopomps, lying in wait for the souls of the dying, timing their eerie cries to the sufferer's struggling breath. If they catch the fleeing soul, they flutter away in demoniac laughter; if they fail, they sink into a disappointed silence.

These tales, though dismissed by the rational mind as obsolete and ridiculous, stem from a reality that is undeniably old. Dunwich is older than any community within thirty miles. South of the village, one can still spy the cellar walls and chimney of the ancient Bishop house, built before 1700, while the ruins of the mill at the falls, constructed in 1806, represent the most modern architecture in sight. Industry never flourished here; the nineteenth-century factory movement proved short-lived. Oldest of all are the great rings of rough-hewn stone columns on the hilltops, generally attributed to the Indians rather than the settlers. Deposits of skulls and bones found within these circles and around the sizable table-like rock on Sentinel Hill sustain the popular belief that these were burial places of the Pocumtucks, even though many ethnologists, disregarding the absurd improbability of such a theory, persist in insisting the remains are Caucasian.

The air in Dunwich tasted of wet stone and ancient rot, a flavor that seemed to cling to the very soil of the township. Four miles from the village, nestled against a jagged hillside and isolated by a mile and a half of silence from any other soul, stood a farmhouse that was large only in its decay. It was here, at the break of dawn on Sunday, February 2nd, 1913, that Wilbur Whateley entered the world. The date itself felt wrong, a glitch in the calendar; it was Candlemas, though the villagers observed it under names that would make a priest shudder. The night before, the hills had groaned with unnatural noises, and the dogs of the countryside had barked in a persistent, hysterical chorus that refused to cease.

Born into this cacophony was the child of Lavinia Whateley. She was a creature of the margins, a thirty-five-year-old albino woman whose skin was the color of milk and whose eyes were sickly pink pools of nothingness. She lived with her father, an aged man whose mind had long since unraveled into a tapestry of frightful wizardry tales whispered in his youth. Lavinia had no husband, yet she made no move to deny the child she bore. Instead, she seemed strangely, almost feverishly proud of the infant who lay in her arms—a stark, dark contrast to her own pallid form. While the townsfolk speculated wildly about the boy's lineage, Lavinia muttered prophecies of terrible power and a future that would swallow the world.

She was a lone spirit, drawn to wander the hills amidst thunderstorms, seeking answers in the great, odorous books her father had inherited over two centuries. These tomes, bound in leather that had long since turned to dust and infested with wormholes, held the disjointed scraps of ancient lore Old Whateley had taught her. The farmhouse itself was a monument to fear, haunted by the reputation of black magic and the violent, unexplained death of Mrs. Whateley when Lavinia was merely twelve. Isolated by these strange influences, Lavinia indulged in wild, grandiose daydreams, her home a squalid ruin where standards of order had died long ago.

On the night of Wilbur's birth, a hideous scream tore through the air, echoing above the hill noises and the frantic barking of dogs. No doctor, no midwife attended the delivery. The neighbors remained ignorant of the child's existence until a week later, when Old Whateley drove his sleigh through the snow into Dunwich Village. He stopped at Osborn's general store, where a group of loungers gathered, and began to discourse incoherently. There was a palpable shift in the old man—a new layer of furtiveness clouding his already fractured brain, transforming him from a mere object of local fear into a subject of genuine dread. Yet, amidst his ramblings, a trace of pride emerged, mirroring his daughter's.

"I dun't keer what folks think," Old Whateley slurred, his voice carrying over the cold air. "Ef Lavinny's boy looked like his pa, he wouldn't look like nothin' ye expeck. Ye needn't think the only folks is the folks hereabouts. Lavinny's read some, an' has seed some things the most o' ye only tell abaout. I calc'late her man is as good a husban' as ye kin find this side of Aylesbury; an' ef ye knowed as much abaout the hills as I dew, ye wouldn't ast no better church weddin' nor her'n. Let me tell ye suthin'—some day yew folks'll hear a child o' Lavinny's a-callin' its father's name on the top o' Sentinel Hill!"

For the first month of his life, Wilbur was seen only by Old Zechariah Whateley of the undecayed line and Mamie Bishop, the common-law wife of Earl Sawyer. Mamie's visit was driven by frank curiosity, and her subsequent stories did justice to her observations. Zechariah, however, arrived with a purpose: to lead a pair of Alderney cows that Old Whateley had purchased from his son, Curtis. This marked the beginning of a strange era of cattle-buying for the Whateley family, a cycle that would not end until 1928, when the Dunwich horror finally came and went. Yet, the ramshackle barn never seemed overcrowded. At times, the curious would sneak up the steep hillside to count the herd grazing precariously above the farmhouse, but they could never find more than ten or twelve specimens. They were anemic, bloodless creatures, victims of a blight or distemper that seemed to spring from the unwholesome pasturage and the diseased fungi rotting the barn's timbers. Odd wounds, resembling surgical incisions, afflicted the visible cattle, and once or twice, callers swore they saw similar sores around the throats of the gray, unshaven old man and his slatternly, crinkly-haired daughter.

By the spring following his birth, Lavinia resumed her customary rambles in the hills, carrying the swarthy child in her misproportioned arms. Public interest waned once the town had seen the baby, and few bothered to comment on the swift, terrifying development he exhibited daily. Wilbur's growth was phenomenal; within three months, he possessed a size and muscular power rarely found in infants under a full year. His movements and vocalizations displayed a restraint and deliberateness highly peculiar for a baby. It was not surprising, then, that at seven months, he began to walk unassisted, stumbling slightly before a single month smoothed out his faltering steps.

It was somewhat after this time, on Hallowe'en, that a great blaze erupted at midnight atop Sentinel Hill, illuminating the old table-like stone standing amidst its tumulus of ancient bones. Talk spread quickly when Silas Bishop, of the undecayed Bishops, mentioned seeing the boy running sturdily up that hill ahead of his mother about an hour before the fire was spotted. Silas was rounding up a stray heifer but nearly forgot his mission when he fleetingly spied the two figures in the dim light of his lantern. They darted almost noiselessly through the underbrush, and the astonished watcher seemed to think they were entirely unclothed. Afterward, he could not be sure about the boy, who may have worn some kind of fringed belt and dark blue trunks. Yet, Wilbur was never subsequently seen alive and conscious without complete, tightly buttoned attire. The disarrangement or threatened disarrangement of his clothing always filled him with a profound anger and alarm, a stark contrast to his squalid mother and grandfather that would later suggest a far more valid reason for his obsession with dress.

The following January, gossip turned mildly interested in the fact that "Lavinny's black brat" had commenced to speak at the age of only eleven months. His speech was remarkable, differing from the ordinary accents of the region and displaying a freedom from infantile lisping that many children of three or four might envy. The boy was not talkative, yet when he spoke, he seemed to reflect an elusive element wholly unpossessed by Dunwich and its denizens. The strangeness did not reside in the words themselves, nor in the simple idioms he used, but seemed vaguely linked to his intonation or the internal organs that produced the sounds. His facial aspect was equally striking for its maturity; though he shared his mother's and grandfather's chinlessness, his firm, precociously shaped nose united with the expression on his large, dark, almost Latin eyes to give him an air of quasi-adulthood and well-nigh preternatural intelligence.

However, he was exceedingly ugly despite this appearance of brilliance. There was something almost goatish or animalistic about his thick lips, his large-pored, yellowish skin, his coarse crinkly hair, and his oddly elongated ears. He was soon disliked even more decidedly than his mother and grandsire, and all conjectures about him were spiced with references to the bygone magic of Old Whateley and how the hills once shook when he shrieked the dreadful name of Yog-Sothoth in the midst of a circle of stones with a great book open in his arms. Dogs abhorred the boy, and he was always obliged to take various defensive measures against their barking menace.

Old Whateley's hands, gnarled yet possessed of a terrifying vitality, moved with a frantic industry that defied his trembling frame. He purchased cattle, yet the herd remained stubbornly small; he felled timber, and he began the arduous task of mending the great, peaked-roofed house that clung to the rocky flank of the hill. While his mind occasionally slipped into the babbling fog of dementia, his carpentry was a study in cold, calculated precision. The restoration had begun the moment Wilbur drew breath, marked by the sudden, frantic ordering of a tool-shed, clad in fresh clapboards and secured with a heavy iron lock. Now, turning his gaze upward to the abandoned second story, the old man worked with the same ruthless thoroughness. His madness manifested not in the construction itself, but in the sealing of every window in the reclaimed attic, boarding them tight against the world. The townsfolk whispered that reclaiming such a space was folly, yet they watched in silence as Whateley fitted out a new nursery downstairs for his grandson, a room visible to all but forbidden to enter. Inside, tall shelves rose like the ribs of a crypt, slowly filling with the rotting, ancient tomes that had once been dumped in chaotic heaps throughout the house.

"I made some use of 'em," Whateley would rasp, his fingers stained with paste from the rusty stove as he glued a torn page of black-letter script back together. "But the boy is fit to make better use of 'em. He ought to have 'em as well sot as he kin, for they're goin' to be all of his larnin'."

By September of 1914, when Wilbur was but a year and seven months old, he was a creature of unsettling proportions. He stood as tall as a four-year-old, his movements fluid and his speech a torrent of incomprehensible intelligence. He roamed the fields with a wild freedom, shadowing his mother, yet at home, he sat hunched over the strange charts and pictures in his grandfather's library. Long, hushed afternoons passed as Old Whateley catechized him, drilling the boy with questions that seemed to dig deep into the earth. As the house restoration neared completion, the neighbors noticed a peculiar alteration: one window in the rear of the east gable, pressed tight against the hill, had been transformed into a solid plank door. Above it, a cleated wooden runway spiraled up from the ground, a silent invitation to the sealed darkness. Around this time, the tightly locked tool-house, abandoned since Wilbur's birth, fell silent once more. When Earl Sawyer ventured inside after a cattle sale, he recoiled from a stench so profound it felt alien to the senses—a foul odor reminiscent only of the ancient stone circles on the hills, a smell that suggested something utterly unsanctioned by nature. Yet, in Dunwich, such olfactory horrors were merely the baseline of existence.

The months that followed were quiet on the surface, save for a slow, grinding crescendo of noises emanating from the mysterious hill. By May Eve of 1915, tremors shook the very foundations of Aylesbury, and by Halloween, the earth beneath Sentinel Hill groaned with a rhythmic rumble, strangely synchronized with bursts of unnatural flame—what the locals dismissed as the Whateleys' witchcraft. Wilbur grew with uncanny speed, appearing as a ten-year-old before his fourth birthday. He read voraciously but spoke less, consumed by a settling taciturnity that chilled those who saw it. A dark, goatish malice began to bloom in his features. He muttered jargons unknown to any tongue and chanted in rhythms that induced a primal terror in listeners. Dogs, once loyal guardians, now fled from him with visceral hatred, forcing the boy to carry a pistol just to walk the countryside safely. His sporadic use of the weapon only deepened the community's dread.

Visitors to the house often found Lavinia alone on the ground floor, while eerie cries and heavy footsteps echoed from the boarded-up second story. She never explained the activities of her father and son upstairs, though her face would drain of color and her eyes widen in abject terror if a jovial fish-peddler attempted the locked stairway door. The peddler later claimed to Dunwich loungers that he heard a horse stamping on that upper floor. The townspeople, recalling the sealed door, the runway, and the cattle that vanished into the earth, shuddered with memories of Old Whateley's youth and the sacrifices made to heathen gods. Soon, the entire Whateley property became a zone of canine revulsion.

Then came 1917, and the Great War. As chairman of the local draft board, Squire Sawyer Whateley struggled to find a single young man fit enough for a development camp. Alarmed by the signs of regional decadence, the government dispatched officers and medical experts, conducting a survey that would become infamous across New England. It was the publicity surrounding this investigation that led reporters to the Whateley doorstep. The *Boston Globe* and the *Arkham Advertiser* printed flamboyant Sunday specials detailing Wilbur's precociousness, his grandfather's black magic, the shelves of forbidden books, the sealed second story, and the weirdness of the region. At four and a half years old, Wilbur looked like a fifteen-year-old, his lips and cheeks fuzzy with coarse dark down, his voice cracking into a harsh baritone. Earl Sawyer accompanied the press to the farm, drawing their attention to the foul trickle of odor descending from the sealed upper spaces—a scent identical to that found in the abandoned tool-shed and the stone circles. The Dunwich folk read these stories with grim amusement, mocking the obvious errors, yet they could not shake the question of why the Whateleys always paid for their cattle in gold coins of ancient, impossible date. They received the reporters with ill-concealed distaste, knowing that any violent resistance would only court further, more dangerous scrutiny.

For ten years, the Whateley name dissolved into the grim, indifferent fabric of Dunwich, a town hardened by the sight of their kin's peculiarities and accustomed to the orgiastic rites of May Eve and All-Hallows. Twice a year, fires blazed atop Sentinel Hill, triggering earth-shaking tremors that grew more violent with each passing season, while the isolated farmhouse remained a theater of portents. Neighbors claimed to hear whispers drifting from the sealed upper floors even when the entire family gathered below, and rumors swirled about the speed of cattle sacrifices. Proposals to alert the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals were quietly dropped; Dunwichers preferred to let the outside world remain ignorant of their local horrors.

By 1923, when Wilbur Whateley was merely ten years old yet possessed the bearded visage, deep voice, and imposing stature of a man, a second siege of construction engulfed the old house. This work occurred entirely within the sealed upper section. From scraps of discarded timber, villagers deduced that the boy and his grandfather had demolished every partition and stripped away the attic floor, carving a single, cavernous void between the ground level and the peaked roof. They had also razed the great central chimney, replacing the rusting range with a flimsy exterior tin stove-pipe.

In the spring following this transformation, Old Whateley noted a proliferation of whippoorwills emerging from Cold Spring Glen to chirp beneath his window at night. He regarded this phenomenon with profound foreboding, telling the loafers at Osborn's that his end was imminent.

"They whistle in perfect tune with my breathing now," he rasped, "and I reckon they are preparing to catch my soul. They know it is coming and calculate to take it. You will know, boys, once I am gone, if they succeeded or failed. If they did, they will keep singing and laughing until dawn. If they did not, they will settle down quietly. I expect these birds and the souls they hunt engage in rather brutal struggles sometimes."

On Lammas Night, 1924, Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury was urgently summoned by Wilbur, who had tethered his lone horse and ridden into the darkness to telephone from Osborn's. He found Old Whateley in a critical state, his heart failing and breath stertorous, signaling death was near. Beside the bed stood the shapeless albino daughter and the oddly bearded grandson. From the empty abyss above, a disturbing rhythmical surging emanated, like waves lapping against a shore. Yet, it was the chattering of night birds outside that most unsettled the doctor—a seemingly endless legion of whippoorwills crying a diabolical message timed precisely to the dying man's wheezing gasps. It felt uncanny, unnatural, an excess that mirrored the reluctant horror of the region itself.

Around one o'clock, Old Whateley regained consciousness, interrupting his labored breathing to choke out instructions for his grandson.

"Make more room, Willy, more space soon. You grow—and *that* grows faster. It will be ready to serve you shortly. Open the gates to Yog-Sothoth with the long chant found on page 751 of the complete edition, and *then* set fire to the prison. Fire from earth cannot burn it!"

He appeared utterly mad. After a pause, during which the flock of whippoorwills outside shifted their cries to match the altered tempo and distant hill noises began to rumble, he added a final warning.

"Feed it regularly, Willy, and mind the quantity; but do not let it grow too fast for the place. If it bursts the quarters or escapes before you open to Yog-Sothoth, it is all over and useless. Only those from beyond can make it multiply and work... Only them, the old ones who wish to return..."

Speech gave way to gasps, and Lavinia screamed as the whippoorwills seemed to react to the change. This cycle repeated for over an hour until the final throaty rattle ceased. Dr. Houghton covered the glazing gray eyes with shrunken lids as the bird tumult faded imperceptibly into silence. Lavinia sobbed, but Wilbur only chuckled, a low, heavy sound, as the hill noises rumbled faintly in the distance.

"They didn't get him," he muttered.

By this time, Wilbur was a scholar of terrifying, one-sided erudition, known by correspondence to librarians in distant lands guarding rare and forbidden texts. He was increasingly hated and feared in Dunwich due to vague suspicions surrounding youthful disappearances, yet he silenced inquiries through terror or by dispensing old-time gold for cattle purchases. Now, his aspect was tremendously mature; his height, having reached the normal adult limit, seemed poised to exceed it. In 1925, when a correspondent from Miskatonic University visited and left pale and bewildered, Wilbur stood fully six and three-quarters feet tall.

Throughout the years, Wilbur had treated his half-deformed albino mother with growing contempt, eventually forbidding her from joining him on the hills during May Eve and Hallowmass. By 1926, the poor creature confessed to Mamie Bishop that she feared him.

"There is more about him than I can tell you, Mamie," she whispered, "and nowadays there is more than I even know myself. I vow by God, I don't know what he wants or what he is trying to do."

That Halloween, the hill noises roared louder than ever, and fires burned on Sentinel Hill as custom dictated, but the villagers paid closer attention to the rhythmic screaming of vast flocks of unnaturally belated whippoorwills gathered near the unlit Whateley farmhouse. After midnight, their shrill notes erupted into a pandemonium of laughter that filled the countryside, quieting only at dawn. Then they vanished, hurrying southward, a full month overdue. No one could be certain what this meant until later. None of the countryfolk seemed to have died—but poor Lavinia Whateley, the twisted albino, was never seen again.

In the summer of 1927, Wilbur repaired two sheds in the farmyard and began moving his books and effects out to them. Shortly thereafter, Earl Sawyer reported to the loungers at Osborn's that more carpentry was underway in the Whateley farmhouse. Wilbur was sealing all doors and windows on the ground floor while removing partitions, just as he and his grandfather had done upstairs four years prior. He lived in one of the sheds, appearing unusually worried and tremulous. The townspeople generally suspected him of knowing something about his mother's disappearance, and very few ventured near his neighborhood. His height had increased to more than seven feet, showing no signs of stopping its relentless growth.

The winter that followed brought a calamity as bizarre as Wilbur's initial departure from Dunwich. Every correspondence sent to the Widener Library in Harvard, the Bibliothèque Nationale in Paris, the British Museum, the University of Buenos Aires, and the Miskatonic University in Arkham had returned empty-handed; the specific tome he craved remained unavailable for loan. Desperate, Wilbur traveled personally. He arrived in Arkham shabby, filthy, bearded, and speaking with a dialect that grated against the ears of the locals. Standing nearly eight feet tall and dragging a cheap, new valise from Osborn's general store, this dark, goatish gargoyle sought the dreaded volume locked within the college library—the hideous *Necronomicon* of the mad Arab Alhazred, in the Latin version printed in Spain during the Seventeenth Century. Having never witnessed a city before, he moved with singular purpose, ignoring the great white-fanged watchdog that barked with unnatural fury and tugged frantically at its chain as he passed toward the university grounds.

In his possession was the priceless yet imperfect English copy of Dr. Dee's translation, bequeathed by his grandfather. Upon gaining access to the Latin original, Wilbur immediately began collating the texts, searching for a specific passage located on the 751st page of his defective volume. He could not help but mention this discrepancy to the librarian, the erudite Henry Armitage—a man of degrees from Miskatonic, Princeton, and Johns Hopkins—who had once visited the farm and now politely interrogated him. Wilbur admitted he sought a formula or incantation containing the frightful name *Yog-Sothoth*. As he copied the chosen text, Dr. Armitage glanced involuntarily over his shoulder at the open pages, where the left-hand column of the Latin version displayed monstrous threats to the peace and sanity of the world.

*Nor is it to be thought,* the text seemed to whisper in Armitage's mind, *that man is either the oldest or the last of earth's masters... The Old Ones were, the Old Ones are, and the Old Ones shall be. Not in the spaces we know, but between them. They walk serene and primal, undimensioned and to us unseen. Yog-Sothoth knows the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the gate. Yog-Sothoth is the key and guardian of the gate. Past, present, future, all are one in Yog-Sothoth. He knows where the Old Ones broke through of old, and where They shall break through again... Great Cthulhu is Their cousin, yet can he spy Them only dimly. Iä Shub-Niggurath! As a foulness shall ye know Them. Their hand is at your throats, yet ye see Them not; and Their habitation is even one with your guarded threshold.*

Dr. Armitage, connecting these words with the brooding presences of Dunwich and the dim, hideous aura radiating from Wilbur Whateley—a figure stretching from a dubious birth to a cloud of probable matricide—felt a wave of terror as tangible as the cold dampness of a tomb. The bent, goatish giant before him appeared to be the spawn of another planet or dimension, linked to black gulfs of essence that stretched like titan fantasies beyond all spheres of force, matter, space, and time.

Suddenly, Wilbur raised his head and began speaking in a strange, resonant fashion that hinted at vocal organs unlike any found in mankind.

"Mr. Armitage," he said, "I calc'late I've got to take that book home. They's things in it I've got to try under sarten conditions that I can't git here, an' it 'ud be a mortal sin to let a red-tape rule hold me up. Let me take it along, sir, an' I'll swar they wun't nobody know the difference. I dun't need to tell ye I'll take good keer of it. It wa'n't me that put this Dee copy in the shape it is...."

He halted as he sensed the firm denial on the librarian's face, his own goatish features twisting into craftiness. Armitage, half-ready to suggest copying the necessary sections, suddenly recoiled at the potential consequences. To give such a being the key to such blasphemous outer spheres was too great a responsibility. Whateley perceived the refusal and attempted to answer lightly.

"Wal, all right, ef ye feel that way abaout it. Maybe Harvard wun't be so fussy as yew be." Without further comment, he rose and strode out of the building, stooping unnaturally at each doorway.

From the window, Armitage heard the savage yelping of the great watchdog and studied Whateley's gorilla-like lope across the visible campus. He recalled the wild tales and old Sunday stories from the *Advertiser*, the lore gathered from Dunwich rustics, and the certainty that unseen, non-earthly horrors rushed fetid through New England's glens. Now, he sensed the close presence of a terrible fragment of the intruding horror, glimpsing a hellish advance in the black dominion of the ancient nightmare. With a shudder of disgust, he locked away the *Necronomicon*, though the room still reeked with an unholy, unidentifiable stench. *"As a foulness shall ye know them,"* he quoted. Yes—the odor was identical to that which had sickened him at the Whateley farmhouse less than three years prior. He thought of Wilbur, goatish and ominous, and laughed mockingly at the village rumors of his parentage.

"Inbreeding?" Armitage muttered half-aloud. "Great God, what simpletons! Show them Arthur Machen's *Great God Pan* and they'll think it a common Dunwich scandal! But what thing—what cursed shapeless influence on or off this three-dimensioned earth—was Wilbur Whateley's father? Born on Candlemas, nine months after May Eve of 1912, when the talk about the queer earth noises reached clear to Arkham... What walked on the mountains that May Night? What Roodmas horror fastened itself on the world in half-human flesh and blood?"

Over the ensuing weeks, Dr. Armitage collected every scrap of data regarding Wilbur Whateley and the formless presences surrounding Dunwich. He contacted Dr. Houghton of Aylesbury, who had attended Old Whateley in his final illness, finding much to ponder in the grandfather's last words. A visit to Dunwich Village yielded little new information, but a close survey of the *Necronomicon* in the sections Wilbur had coveted supplied terrifying clues to the nature, methods, and desires of the strange evil threatening the planet. Conversations with students of archaic lore in Boston and letters to scholars elsewhere filled him with growing amazement, which slowly escalated from alarm to acute spiritual fear. As summer drew on, he felt dimly that something must be done about the lurking terrors of the upper Miskatonic valley and the monstrous being known to the human world as Wilbur Whateley.

The horror of Dunwich unfolded between Lammas and the autumnal equinox of 1928, a monstrous prologue witnessed by Dr. Armitage. He had already heard the tales of Wilbur Whateley's grotesque pilgrimage to Cambridge, where the young man had frantically attempted to borrow or copy the *Necronomicon* from the Widener Library. These desperate efforts had failed; Armitage had issued warnings of such piercing intensity to every librarian guarding the forbidden tome that Wilbur's quest remained fruitless. Yet, Wilbur had been shockingly nervous during his stay, oscillating between an anxious hunger for the book and a terrified desire to return home, as if he feared the very consequences of his prolonged absence.

In early August, the half-anticipated catastrophe arrived. During the small hours of the third day, Dr. Armitage was jolted awake by the wild, fierce cries of the savage watchdog patrolling the college campus. Deep and terrible, the snarling, half-mad growls and barks continued, rising in volume with hideously significant pauses. Then, a scream erupted from a throat entirely alien—such a shriek that haunted the dreams of Arkham forever, a sound capable of issuing only from a being born of the void rather than the earth.

Armitage hastily donned clothes and rushed across the street and lawn toward the college buildings, finding others already fleeing. He heard the shrill echoes of a burglar alarm still wailing from the library. An open window stood black and gaping against the moonlight. The intruder had indeed completed its entry; the barking and screaming, now fading into a mixed low growling and moaning, proceeded unmistakably from within. A primal instinct warned Armitage that what transpired inside was not for unfortified eyes to behold. With authoritative force, he brushed back the gathering crowd and unlocked the vestibule door. Among the fleeing figures, he recognized Professor Warren Rice and Dr. Francis Morgan, men to whom he had previously voiced his conjectures and misgivings. He motioned for them to accompany him inside.

By this time, the inward sounds had subsided, save for a watchful, droning whine from the dog. Yet, Armitage perceived with a sudden, chilling start that a loud chorus of whippoorwills among the shrubbery had commenced a damnably rhythmical piping, as if in unison with the last breath of a dying man. The building was thick with a frightful stench that Dr. Armitage knew too well. The three men rushed across the hall to the small genealogical reading-room, the source of the low whining. For a heartbeat, nobody dared to turn on the light. Then, summoning his courage, Armitage snapped the switch.

One of the three—uncertain which—shrieked aloud at what sprawled before them among disordered tables and overturned chairs. Professor Rice declared that he wholly lost consciousness for an instant, though he did not stumble or fall.

The creature lay half-bent on its side in a fetid pool of greenish-yellow ichor and tarry stickiness. It stood almost nine feet tall, and the dog had torn off all its clothing and much of its skin. It was not quite dead, but twitched silently and spasmodically while its chest heaved in monstrous unison with the mad piping of the expectant whippoorwills outside. Bits of shoe-leather and fragments of apparel were scattered about the room, and just inside the window lay an empty canvas sack where it had evidently been discarded. Near the central desk, a revolver had fallen, a dented but undischarged cartridge later explaining why it had not been fired. The creature itself crowded out all other images. It would be trite to say no human pen could describe it, but one may properly say it could not be vividly visualized by anyone whose ideas of form and contour are too closely bound to the common life-forms of this planet and its three known dimensions.

It was partly human, beyond a doubt, possessing very manlike hands and head, and the goatish, chinless face bore the distinct stamp of the Whateleys. But the torso and lower parts of the body were teratologically fabulous, rendering it impossible for such a form to walk on earth unchallenged or uneradicated under any guise of clothing. Above the waist, it was semi-anthropomorphic; though its chest, where the dog's rending paws still rested watchfully, displayed the leathery, reticulated hide of a crocodile or alligator. The back was piebald with yellow and black, dimly suggesting the squamous covering of certain snakes.

Below the waist, however, was the worst. Here, all human resemblance ceased, and sheer fantasy began. The skin was thickly covered with coarse black fur, and from the abdomen protruded a score of long greenish-gray tentacles with red sucking mouths, hanging limply. Their arrangement was odd, following the symmetries of some cosmic geometry unknown to Earth or the solar system. Set deep in a kind of pinkish, ciliated orbit on each hip was what seemed to be a rudimentary eye. In lieu of a tail, a kind of trunk or feeler depended downward, marked with purple annular rings and showing many evidences of being an undeveloped mouth or throat. The limbs, save for their black fur, roughly resembled the hind legs of prehistoric Earth's giant saurians, terminating in ridgy-veined pads that were neither hooves nor claws.

When the thing breathed, its tail and tentacles rhythmically changed color, as if driven by some circulatory cause normal to the non-human side of its ancestry. In the tentacles, this appeared as a deepening of the greenish tinge; in the tail, it manifested as a yellowish hue alternating with a sickly grayish-white in the spaces between the purple rings. There was no genuine blood, only the fetid greenish-yellow ichor that trickled along the painted floor beyond the radius of the stickiness, leaving a curious discoloration in its wake.

As the presence of the three men seemed to rouse the dying thing, it began to mumble without turning or raising its head. Dr. Armitage made no written record of its mouthings but asserted confidently that nothing in English was uttered. At first, the syllables defied all correlation with any speech of Earth, but toward the end, there came some disjointed fragments evidently taken from the *Necronomicon*, the monstrous blasphemy in quest of which the thing had perished. Those fragments, as Armitage recalled them, ran something like "*N'gai, n'gha'ghaa, bugg-shoggog, y'hah; Yog-Sothoth, Yog-Sothoth...*" They trailed off into nothingness as the whippoorwills shrieked in rhythmic crescendos of unholy anticipation.

Then came a halt in the gasping, and the dog raised his head in a long, lugubrious howl. A change came over the yellow, goatish face of the prostrate thing, and the great black eyes fell in appallingly. Outside the window, the shrilling of the whippoorwills had suddenly ceased, and above the murmurs of the gathering crowd came the sound of a panic-struck whirring and fluttering. Against the moon, vast clouds of feathery watchers rose and raced from sight, frantic at that which they had sought for prey.

All at once, the dog started up abruptly, gave a frightened bark, and leaped nervously out the window by which it had entered. A cry rose from the crowd, and Dr. Armitage shouted to the men outside that no one must be admitted until the police or medical examiner arrived. He was thankful that the windows were just too high to permit peering in, and he drew the dark curtains carefully down over each one. By this time, two policemen had arrived; Dr. Morgan, meeting them in the vestibule, urged them for their own sakes to postpone entrance to the stench-filled reading-room until the examiner came and the prostrate thing could be covered up.

Meanwhile, frightful changes were taking place on the floor. One need not describe the *kind* and *rate* of shrinkage and disintegration that occurred before the eyes of Dr. Armitage and Professor Rice; but it is permissible to say that, aside from the external appearance of face and hands, the truly human elements in Wilbur Whateley must have been very small. When the medical examiner arrived, there was only a sticky whitish mass on the painted boards, and the monstrous odor had nearly disappeared. Apparently, Whateley had had no skull or bony skeleton—at least, in any true or stable sense. He had taken somewhat after his unknown father.

The formalities were merely the prelude to the true Dunwich horror. Bewildered officials shuffled through the motions, carefully shielding the abnormal details from the press and the public, while men were dispatched to Dunwich and Aylesbury to inventory property and locate heirs of the late Wilbur Whateley. They found a countryside gripped by a feverish agitation, driven by the deepening rumblings beneath the domed hills and the suffocating stench rising from the great, empty shell of Whateley's boarded-up farmhouse. Earl Sawyer, the caretaker of the livestock, had succumbed to a paralyzing nervous breakdown. The officials, repelled by the noisome atmosphere, devised elaborate excuses to avoid entering the farmhouse, limiting their inspection of the living quarters and sheds to a single, hurried visit. Their ponderous report was filed at the Aylesbury courthouse, sparking a labyrinth of litigation concerning heirship among the countless, decaying Whateleys of the upper Miskatonic valley.

An almost interminable manuscript, scrawled in strange characters within a massive ledger, baffled those who discovered it on the old bureau. Judged to be a diary by its erratic spacing and shifting ink, it remained an unsolved puzzle. After a week of fruitless debate, it was sent to Miskatonic University alongside the deceased's collection of occult texts. Even the university's finest linguists soon conceded that unraveling its secrets would require more than mere scholarship. Not a trace of the ancient gold, with which Wilbur and Old Whateley had always settled their debts, had yet been found.

It was on the dark night of September ninth that the horror truly broke loose. The hill noises had been deafening throughout the evening, driving the dogs into frantic barks that echoed all night long. By early morning on the tenth, a peculiar, cloying stench hung heavy in the air. Around seven o'clock, Luther Brown, the hired hand at George Corey's farm, rushed frenziedly back from his morning trip to Ten-Acre Meadow. He stumbled into the kitchen almost convulsed with terror, his face pale and eyes wide. Outside, the herd of cows pawed the ground and lowed pitifully, having followed their terrified guide back to the safety of the yard. Between ragged gasps, Luther managed to stammer his tale to Mrs. Corey.

"Up there in the ravine beyond the glen, Mis' Corey—something has been there! It smells like thunder, and all the bushes and saplings have been pushed back as if a house had slid along them. But that isn't the worst. There are *prints* in the mud, Mis' Corey—great round depressions as large as barrel heads, sunk deep as if an elephant had walked there, only they are far larger than four feet could make. I looked at a couple before I ran, and I saw that every single one was covered with lines spreading outward from the center, like giant palm-leaf fans pounded twice or thrice as deep as any normal footprint. And the smell... it was awful, just like around Wizard Whateley's old house..."

He faltered, shivering anew with the primal fright that had sent him fleeing. Mrs. Corey, unable to coax further words from him, began calling the neighbors, thus igniting the overture of panic that would herald the coming terrors. When she reached Sally Sawyer, the housekeeper at Seth Bishop's farm, the role reversed; it became her turn to listen. Sally's son, Chauncey, who slept fitfully, had been up on the hill toward Whateley's and had dashed back in absolute terror after a single glance at the property and the pasture where Mr. Bishop's cows had grazed overnight.

"Yes, Mis' Corey," came Sally's trembling voice over the phone line. "Chauncey just came back a-post-haste, and he couldn't speak for being scared! He says Old Whateley's house is blown apart, with the timbers scattered around as if dynamite had been used inside. Only the bottom floor remains, covered with a kind of tar-like stuff that smells foul and drips down the edges onto the ground where the side timbers blew away. And there are awful marks in the yard—great round impressions bigger than a hogshead, all sticky with the same substance. Chauncey says they lead off into the meadows, where a great swath wider than a barn lies matted down, and stone walls have tumbled every which way wherever it goes.

"He says, Mis' Corey, how he sat to look for Seth's cows, terrified as he was, and found them in the upper pasture near Devil's Hop Yard in a terrible state. Half of them are gone entirely, and nearly half of the survivors are sucked almost dry of blood, with sores on them like nothing seen on Whateley's cattle since Lavinny's black brat was born. Seth has gone out now to check on them, though I vow he won't dare get very close to Wizard Whateley's place! Chauncey didn't look carefully to see where the big matted swath led after leaving the pasture, but he thinks it pointed toward the glen ravine leading to the village.

"I tell you, Mis' Corey, something is abroad that shouldn't be, and I, for one, think that black Wilbur Whateley, who came to the bad end he deserved, is at the bottom of this breeding. He wasn't all human himself, I always say to everyone; and I think he and Old Whateley must have raised something in that nailed-up house that isn't even so human as he was. There have always been unseen things around Dunwich—living things—that aren't human and aren't good for human folks.

"The ground was talking last night, and toward morning Chauncey heard the whippoorwills so loud in Cold Spring Glen he couldn't sleep. Then he thought he heard another faint sound over toward Wizard Whateley's—a kind of ripping or tearing of wood, like some big box or crate being opened far off. With all this, he didn't get a wink of sleep till sunup, and no sooner was he up this morning than he had to go over to Whateley's to see what was wrong. He saw enough, I tell you, Mis' Corey! This doesn't mean any good, and I think all the men-folks ought to get up a party and do something. I know something awful is about, and I feel my time is nigh, though only God knows exactly what it is.

"Did your Luther take account of where those big tracks led? No? Well, Mis' Corey, if they were on the glen road this side of the glen and haven't reached your house yet, I calculate they must go into the glen itself. They would do that. I always say Cold Spring Glen isn't a healthy nor decent place. The whippoorwills and fireflies there never acted like creatures of God, and there are those who say you can hear strange things rushing and talking in the air down there if you stand in the right place, between the rock falls and Bear's Den."

***

By noon, fully three-quarters of the men and boys of Dunwich were trooping over the roads and meadows between the new Whateley ruins and Cold Spring Glen. They examined in horror the vast, monstrous prints, the maimed Bishop cattle, the strange, noisome wreck of the farmhouse, and the bruised, matted vegetation of the fields. Whatever had burst loose upon the world had assuredly gone down into the great sinister ravine; all the trees on the banks were bent and broken, and a great avenue had been gouged into the precipice-hanging underbrush. It was as though a house, launched by an avalanche, had slid down through the tangled growths of the almost vertical slope. From below, no sound came, only a distant, undefinable fetor. It was not to be wondered at that the men preferred to stay on the edge and argue rather than descend to beard the unknown Cyclopean horror in its lair. Three dogs accompanying the party had barked furiously at first but seemed cowed and reluctant when near the glen. Someone telephoned the news to the *Aylesbury Transcript*, but the editor, accustomed to wild tales from Dunwich, did no more than concoct a humorous paragraph about it, an item soon afterward reproduced by the Associated Press.

That night, everyone went home, and every house and barn was barricaded as stoutly as possible. Needless to say, no cattle were allowed to remain in open pasturage. About two in the morning, a frightful stench and the savage barking of dogs awakened the household at Elmer Frye's, on the eastern edge of Cold Spring Glen. All agreed they could hear a sort of muffled swishing or lapping sound from somewhere outside. Mrs. Frye proposed telephoning the neighbors, and Elmer was about to agree when the noise of splintering wood burst upon their deliberations. It came, apparently, from the barn, quickly followed by a hideous screaming and stamping amongst the cattle. The dogs slavered and crouched close to the feet of the fear-numbed family. Frye lit a lantern through force of habit but knew it would be death to go out into that black farmyard. The children and women whimpered, kept from screaming by some obscure, vestigial instinct of defense which told them their lives depended on silence. At last, the noise of the cattle subsided to a pitiful moaning, and a great snapping, crashing, and crackling ensued. The Fryes, huddled together in the sitting-room, did not dare to move until the last echoes died away far down in Cold Spring Glen. Then, amidst the dismal moans from the stable and the demoniac piping of late whippoorwills in the glen, Selina Frye tottered to the telephone and spread what news she could of the second phase of the horror.

The next day, the entire countryside was in a panic. Cowed, uncommunicative groups came and went where the fiendish thing had occurred. Two titan swaths of destruction stretched from the glen to the Frye farmyard; monstrous prints covered the bare patches of ground, and one side of the old red barn had completely caved in. Of the cattle, only about a quarter could be found and identified. Some were in curious fragments, and all that survived had to be shot. Earl Sawyer suggested asking for help from Aylesbury or Arkham, but others maintained it would be of no use. Old Zebulon Whateley, of a branch that hovered halfway between soundness and decadence, made darkly wild suggestions about rites that ought to be practiced on the hilltops. He came of a line where tradition ran strong, and his memories of chantings in the great stone circles were not altogether connected with Wilbur and his grandfather.

Darkness fell upon a stricken countryside too passive to organize for real defense. In a few cases, closely related families banded together to watch in the gloom under one roof; but generally, there was only a repetition of the barricading of the night before, and a futile, ineffective gesture of loading muskets and setting pitchforks handy. Nothing occurred except some hill noises, and when day came, many hoped the new horror had gone as swiftly as it had arrived. There were even bold souls who proposed an offensive expedition down into the glen, though they did not venture to set an actual example to the still reluctant majority.

When night came again, the barricading was repeated, though there was less huddling of families. In the morning, both the Frye and Seth Bishop households reported excitement among the dogs and vague sounds and stenches from afar, while early explorers noted with horror a fresh set of monstrous tracks in the road skirting Sentinel Hill. As before, the sides of the road showed bruising indicative of the blasphemously stupendous bulk of the horror; the conformation of the tracks seemed to argue a passage in two directions, as if the moving mountain had come from Cold Spring Glen and returned along the same path. At the base of the hill, a thirty-foot swath of crushed shrubbery and saplings led steeply upward, and the seekers gasped when they saw that even the most perpendicular places did not deflect the inexorable trail. Whatever the horror was, it could scale a sheer stony cliff of almost complete verticality. As the investigators climbed around to the hill's summit by safer routes, they saw that the trail ended—or rather, reversed—there.

It was here that the Whateleys used to build their hellish fires and chant their rituals by the table-like stone on May Eve and Hallowmass. Now, that very stone formed the center of a vast space thrashed around by the mountainous horror, while upon its slightly concave surface lay a thick, fetid deposit of the same tarry stickiness observed on the floor of the ruined Whateley farmhouse. Men looked at one another and muttered. Then they looked down the hill. Apparently, the horror had descended by a route much the same as its ascent. To speculate was futile. Reason, logic, and normal ideas of motivation stood confounded. Only old Zebulon, who was not with the group, could have done justice to the situation or suggested a plausible explanation.

Thursday night began much like the others but ended less happily. The whippoorwills in the glen had screamed with such unusual persistence that many could not sleep. About three in the morning, all the party telephones rang tremulously. Those who took down their receivers heard a fright-mad voice shriek out, "Help, oh, my Gawd!..." and some thought a crashing sound followed the breaking off of the exclamation. There was nothing more. No one dared do anything, and no one knew till morning whence the call came. Then those who had heard it called everyone on the line and found that only the Fryes did not reply. The truth appeared an hour later when a hastily assembled group of armed men trudged out to the Frye place at the head of the glen. It was horrible, yet hardly a surprise. There were more swaths and monstrous prints, but there was no longer any house. It had caved in like an eggshell, and amongst the ruins nothing living or dead could be discovered—only a stench and a tarry stickiness. The Elmer Fryes had been erased from Dunwich.

The silence in the shelf-lined room of Arkham was not empty; it was thick with a spiritual dread that coiled unseen behind the locked door. Inside, the atmosphere churned with the black, slow unraveling of a horror that had already begun its work. Outside, the academic world buzzed with confusion over the curious manuscript delivered to Miskatonic University. Experts in ancient and modern tongues alike stared at the pages in bafflement. The script possessed a vague resemblance to the heavily shaded Arabic of Mesopotamia, yet it was utterly alien to every known authority. Linguists concluded it was an artificial alphabet, a cipher of such complexity that standard cryptographic methods yielded nothing, regardless of the languages assumed for the writer.

The ancient books seized from Wilbur Whateley's quarters offered no aid. While fascinating to philosophers and scientists, they contained no keys to the diary. One heavy tome, bound with an iron clasp, utilized a script unlike anything else, bearing a striking similarity to Sanskrit. Ultimately, the burden of decoding fell to Dr. Armitage. His peculiar obsession with the Whateley affair, combined with his vast knowledge of mystical formulas from antiquity and the Middle Ages, made him the only viable candidate.

Armitage suspected the symbols belonged to esoteric cults descending from the wizards of the Saracen world, but he deemed the origin secondary. If the text was a cipher in a modern language, the source of the glyphs mattered less than the message itself. Assuming the bulk of the writing was English, he plunged into the riddle. He knew, from his colleagues' repeated failures, that this was a deep and complex puzzle requiring no simple solution. Throughout late August, he fortified himself with the dense lore of cryptography, drowning night after night in the arcane texts of Trithemius, Porta, de Vigenère, Falconer, and Davys. He interwove his study of these authorities with relentless attacks on the manuscript.

Gradually, he realized he faced one of the most ingenious cryptograms ever devised: a system where separate lists of corresponding letters were arranged like a multiplication table, constructed with arbitrary keywords known only to the initiated. The older authorities proved more helpful than the modern ones, leading Armitage to conclude the code was of great antiquity, handed down through a lineage of mystical experimenters. For weeks, he teetered on the brink of understanding, only to be knocked back by unforeseen obstacles. But as September approached, the clouds began to dissipate. Certain letters emerged with definitive clarity, revealing that the text was indeed English.

On the evening of September second, the final major barrier crumbled. Dr. Armitage read the first continuous passage of Wilbur Whateley's annals. It was a diary, written in a style that betrayed a terrifying mixture of occult erudition and profound illiteracy. The first long entry, dated November 26, 1916, sent a chill through the scholar. It appeared to be written by a child of three and a half years, yet the vocabulary and concepts suggested a mind aged twelve or thirteen.

*"Today learned the Aklo for the Sabaoth,"* the entry read, *"which did not like, it being answerable from the hill and not from the air. That upstairs more ahead of me than I had thought it would be, and is not like to have much earth brain. Shot Elam Hutchins's collie Jack when he went to bite me, and Elam says he would kill me if he dast. I guess he won't. Grandfather kept me saying the Dho formula last night, and I think I saw the inner city at the 2 magnetic poles. I shall go to those poles when the earth is cleared off, if I can't break through with the Dho-Hna formula when I commit it. They from the air told me at Sabbat that it will be years before I can clear off the earth, and I guess Grandfather will be dead then, so I shall have to learn all the angles of the planes and all the formulas between the Yr and the Nhhngr. They from outside will help, but they can not take body without human blood. That upstairs looks it will have the right cast. I can see it a little when I make the Yoorish sign or blow the power of Ibn Ghazi at it, and it is near like them at May Eve on the Hill. The other face may wear off some. I wonder how I shall look when the earth is cleared and there are no earth beings on it. He that came with the Aklo Sabaoth said I may be transfigured, there being much of outside to work on."*

Morning found Dr. Armitage drenched in a cold sweat of terror, his mind trapped in a frenzy of wakeful concentration. He had not left the manuscript all night, sitting under the electric light, turning page after page with shaking hands as he deciphered the cryptic text. He had nervously informed his wife he would not be home, and when she brought breakfast, he could barely swallow a mouthful. All day he read on, halted maddeningly whenever a complex key needed reapplication. Lunch and dinner were brought to him, but he consumed only fractions of either. Toward the middle of the next night, he drowsed in his chair, only to wake instantly from nightmares as hideous as the truths he had uncovered.

By the morning of September fourth, Professor Rice and Dr. Morgan insisted on seeing him. They departed trembling, their faces ashen. That evening, Armitage slept fitfully. On Wednesday, he returned to the manuscript, taking copious notes from both current sections and previously deciphered passages. In the small hours, he dozed briefly in an easy-chair before dawn, only to return to the text before sunrise. Before noon, his physician, Dr. Hartwell, arrived and demanded he cease work. Armitage refused, insisting the reading was of vital importance, promising an explanation later.

That evening, as twilight fell, he finished his terrible perusal and collapsed, exhausted. His wife found him in a half-comatose state when she brought dinner, but he cried out sharply to keep her away from his notes. Weakly rising, he gathered the scribbled papers, sealing them in a great envelope which he shoved into his inside coat pocket. He managed to reach home, but his condition was critical; Dr. Hartwell was summoned immediately. As the doctor laid him in bed, Armitage could only mutter over and over, *"But what, in God's name, can we do?"*

Armitage slept, though partly delirious, the following day. He made no explanations to Hartwell but spoke in calmer moments of the imperative need to confer with Rice and Morgan. His wilder ravings were startling: frantic appeals to destroy a boarded-up farmhouse, and fantastic references to a plan by some terrible elder race to extirpate all human, animal, and vegetable life from the earth. He shouted that the world was in danger, claiming the Elder Things wished to strip the planet and drag it from the solar system into another plane of entity from which it had fallen eons ago. At other times, he screamed for the *Necronomicon* and the *Dæmonolatreia* of Remigius, hoping to find a formula to check the peril he conjured.

*"Stop them, stop them!"* he would roar. *"Those Whateleys meant to let them in, and the worst of all is left! Tell Rice and Morgan we must do something—it's a blind business, but I know how to make the powder... It hasn't been fed since the second of August, when Wilbur came here to his death, and at that rate..."*

Despite his seventy-three years, Armitage possessed a sound physique. He slept off his disorder that night without developing a fever. He woke late on Friday, clear-headed but sober, gnawed by a tremendous sense of responsibility. By Saturday afternoon, he felt strong enough to summon Rice and Morgan to the library. For the rest of that day and evening, the three men tortured their brains in wild speculation and desperate debate. Strange, terrible books were dragged from stacks and secure storage, diagrams and formulas copied with feverish haste. Skepticism vanished; all three had seen the body of Wilbur Whateley lying on the floor of that very building, and none could treat the diary as mere madness.

Opinions were divided on notifying the Massachusetts State Police, but the negative vote prevailed. There were things involved that simply could not be believed by those who had not seen a sample. Late at night, the conference disbanded without a definite plan, yet all day Sunday Armitage compared formulas and mixed chemicals from the college laboratory. The more he reflected on the hellish diary, the more he doubted the efficacy of any material agent in stamping out the entity Wilbur Whateley had left behind—the earth-threatening entity that, unknown to him, was about to burst forth and become the memorable Dunwich horror.

Monday mirrored Sunday with endless research and experiment. Further consultations of the monstrous diary brought about various changes of plan, and Armitage knew that even in the end, a large amount of uncertainty must remain. By Tuesday, he had mapped out a definite line of action and believed he could travel to Dunwich within a week. Then, on Wednesday, the great shock came. Tucked obscurely in a corner of the *Arkham Advertiser* was a facetious little item from the Associated Press reporting that the bootleg whisky of Dunwich had raised a record-breaking monster. Armitage, half-stunned, could only telephone for Rice and Morgan. Far into the night they discussed, and the next day became a whirlwind of preparation for all three. Armitage knew he would be meddling with terrible powers, yet saw that there was no other way to annul the deeper and more malign meddling others had done before him.

Friday morning, Armitage, Rice, and Morgan departed by automobile for Dunwich, arriving at the village around one in the afternoon. Though the day offered a deceptive brightness, a suffocating dread hung over the landscape, clinging to the strangely domed hills and the deep, shadowed ravines of the stricken region. Gaunt circles of stones occasionally pierced the skyline atop the mountains, silent sentinels of ancient folly. The air at Osborn's store was thick with hushed terror; within moments, the grim reality of the Elmer Frye household's annihilation became known to them.

Throughout the afternoon, the trio rode the perimeter of Dunwich, interrogating the locals and witnessing with rising horror the desolation of the Frye ruins. They saw the lingering tarry slickness on the ground, the blasphemous tracks gouged into the earth, the wounded cattle of Seth Bishop, and the vast swathes of vegetation torn apart as if by some unseen giant. To Armitage, the trail winding up and down Sentinel Hill possessed a cataclysmic significance; he stared long at the sinister, altar-like stone crowning the summit.

Eventually, learning that a party of State Police from Aylesbury had arrived earlier in response to the initial reports, the visitors resolved to locate them and exchange information. Yet, finding the officers proved more difficult than planning it. The police car stood empty near the Frye ruins, abandoned by its five occupants. The villagers, initially as bewildered as the outsiders, suddenly fell silent. Old Sam Hutchins turned pale, nudging Fred Farr and pointing a trembling finger toward the dank, yawning hollow of Cold Spring Glen.

"Gawd," he gasped, his voice cracking, "I told 'em not to go down into the glen. I never thought anyone'd do it with those tracks, that smell, and the whippoorwills screeching down there in the dark of noonday..."

A cold shudder rippled through the crowd, and every ear strained instinctively, listening to the unnatural silence. Armitage, now confronted with the full weight of the horror, trembled under the crushing responsibility of containment. Night approached, the hour when the mountainous blasphemy would lumber upon its eldritch course. *Negotium perambulans in tenebris...* The old librarian recited the formulas he had memorized, clutching the paper with the alternatives. He verified his electric flashlight, while Rice produced a metal insecticide sprayer and Morgan unslung his big-game rifle, ignoring warnings that no physical weapon could suffice.

Armitage, having read the diaries of the madman, knew precisely what manifestation to expect, yet he withheld any clues from the terrified populace, hoping to contain the secret until the creature could be neutralized. As shadows lengthened, the natives dispersed, retreating indoors despite the futility of locks against a force capable of bending timber and crushing stone. They shook their heads at the Arkham men's plan to guard the ruins, offering little hope that the watchers would survive the night.

That night, the hills groaned with subterranean rumbling, and the whippoorwills piped with threatening urgency. Occasionally, a wind sweeping from Cold Spring Glen carried a touch of ineffable fetor—the same stench the watchers had encountered fifteen and a half years prior above a dying thing. But the expected terror remained dormant. Whatever lurked in the glen bided its time, and Armitage deemed any nocturnal assault suicidal.

Morning broke wanly, the night sounds ceasing to leave behind a gray, bleak day punctuated by a drizzle. Heavy clouds piled up beyond the northwest hills, and the rain intensified. The men from Arkham debated under the shelter of a surviving Frye outbuilding: should they wait for the storm to pass, or take the aggressive path into the gloom? Thunder rumbled in the distance, and sheet lightning flashed, illuminating the accursed glen before a forked bolt descended nearby, plunging the sky into darkness.

Less than an hour later, a confused babel of voices erupted down the road. A dozen men, running, shouting, and whimpering hysterically, appeared in the gloom. The leader choked out a sobbing confession: "Oh, my Gawd, my Gawd! It's a-goin' agin, and this time by day! It's aout—it's aout an' a-movin' this very minute!"

Another man took up the tale, recounting how Zeb Whateley had heard the phone ring, only for Mrs. Corey to report that her hired boy, Luther, had seen trees bending at the mouth of the glen while driving cows after the lightning strike. Luther had smelled the same awful odor as the previous Monday and heard a swishing, lapping sound that defied the movement of the brush. Suddenly, the trees along the road had been pushed aside, accompanied by awful stomping and splashing in the mud. Luther had seen nothing but the bending foliage, yet the sound of wood cracking and splitting echoed from where Bishop's Brook crossed the road.

The narrative escalated as Sally, Seth Bishop's housekeeper, screamed into the phone. She described a mushy sound like an elephant puffing and trudging toward the house, followed by a fearful smell that triggered screams from her son, Chancey. The dogs went mad. Then, with a terrible yell, she reported the shed caving in—not by wind, but by some unseen force. The front yard picket fence crumpled without cause. Finally, heavy impacts battered the house repeatedly, invisible yet devastating, until a terrible crashing and a hullaballoo of screaming echoed over the line, worse than anything that had happened to the Fryes.

Silence followed, absolute and deafening. The crowd, convinced this was divine judgment for their iniquities, gathered around the Arkham men, unsure of what to do. Armitage saw the moment for decisive action had arrived.

"We must follow it, boys," he commanded, striving for a tone of reassurance. "I believe there is a chance to put it out of business. You know the Whateleys were wizards; this thing is of wizardry and must be met by the same means. I have read Wilbur Whateley's diary and the strange books he studied; I think I know the spell to make this thing fade. It is invisible—I knew it would be—but this sprayer contains a powder that might reveal it for a second. It is a frightful thing to have alive, but it is not as bad as what Wilbur would have unleashed had he lived longer. You will never know what the world has escaped. We must fight this single enemy; it cannot multiply, but it can do immense harm. We must not hesitate."

He directed them to the site of the latest wreck. Earl Sawyer, pointing through the diminishing rain, suggested a shortcut across the lower meadow, wading the brook and climbing through Carrier's mowing to reach the upper road near Seth's place. Armitage, Rice, and Morgan began the trek, followed slowly by the natives. As they scrambled up the twilight-covered, perpendicular wooded hill, twisting among fantastic ancient trees, courage and confidence mounted, tested severely by the oppressive atmosphere.

Emerging onto a muddy road just beyond the Seth Bishop place, they found the sun breaking through. Bent trees and hideously unmistakable tracks marked the path of the invisible destroyer. The scene was a grim repetition of the Frye incident; no bodies were found in the collapsed shells of the house and barn, only the stench and the tarry residue. Instinctively, the group turned from the ruins toward the line of horrible prints leading to the wrecked Whateley farmhouse and the altar-crowned slopes of Sentinel Hill.

Passing the site of Wilbur Whateley's former abode, the men shuddered visibly, hesitation mixing with their zeal. Tracking something as large as a house, yet invisible and possessing the vicious malevolence of a demon, was no joke. At the base of Sentinel Hill, the tracks left the road, leaving a fresh swath of bent and matted vegetation marking the monster's route to and from the summit.

Armitage produced a powerful pocket telescope and scanned the steep green side of the hill. He handed the instrument to Morgan, whose sight was keener. After a moment of intense gazing, Morgan cried out sharply, passing the glass to Earl Sawyer and indicating a specific spot on the slope. Sawyer, clumsy with optics, fumbled for a moment before Armitage helped him focus. When he finally saw, his cry was less restrained than Morgan's.

"Gawd almighty, the grass an' bushes is a-movin'! It's a-goin' up—slow-like—creepin' up ter the top this minute, heaven only knows what fer!"

Panic, the seed of which had been sown, began to spread among the seekers. It was one thing to chase a nameless entity; it was quite another to find it. Spells might work—or they might not. Voices began questioning Armitage about the nature of the thing, seeking answers that seemed to satisfy no one. Everyone felt they stood in close proximity to phases of nature and existence utterly forbidden, wholly outside the sane experience of mankind.

The three men from Arkham—Dr. Armitage, with his white beard and weary eyes; the stocky, iron-gray Professor Rice; and the lean, young Dr. Morgan—climbed the mountain alone. They had spent hours instructing the terrified locals on the proper use of the telescope, leaving the heavy glass with the trembling group below before ascending. As they toiled upward, watched closely by the crowd clutching the lens, the great swath of shrubbery high above trembled. Its hellish maker moved with a snail-like deliberateness, repassing the observers. Then, the pursuers gained ground.

Curtis Whateley, of the undecayed branch, held the telescope when the Arkham party abruptly diverted from the swath. He explained to the crowd that the men were racing toward a subordinate peak overlooking the entity's path. His prediction proved correct; shortly after the invisible blasphemy swept past, the party reached the minor elevation.

Wesley Corey, now holding the glass, suddenly cried out that Armitage was adjusting the sprayer Rice carried. Something was about to happen. The crowd stirred uneasily, remembering the sprayer was meant to grant the unseen horror a fleeting moment of visibility. Two or three men shut their eyes tight, but Curtis snatched the telescope back, straining his vision to the limit. From his vantage point above and behind the entity, Rice had a perfect angle to unleash the powder.

Those without the lens saw only an instant's flash of gray cloud, roughly the size of a large building, near the mountain's summit. Curtis, who had held the instrument, dropped it with a piercing shriek into the ankle-deep mud of the road. He reeled, collapsing where he stood, supported only by the frantic grip of two or three others. All he could manage was a half-inaudible moan:

"Oh, oh, great Gawd... *that*... *that*...."

Pandemonium erupted in questioning voices. Only Henry Wheeler thought to rescue the fallen telescope, wiping the mud from its lenses. Curtis was past coherence, his mind shattered.

"Bigger 'n a barn... all made o' squirmin' ropes... hull thing sort o' shaped like a hen's egg bigger'n anything, with dozens o' legs like hogsheads that haff shut up when they step... nothin' solid abaout it—all like jelly, an' made o' sep'rit wrigglin' ropes pushed clost together... great bulgin' eyes all over it... ten or twenty maouths or trunks a-stickin' aout all along the sides, big as stovepipes, an' all a-tossin' an' openin' an' shuttin'... all gray, with kinder blue or purple rings... *an' Gawd in Heaven—that haff face on top*!..."

That final image broke him completely. Before he could speak further, Curtis collapsed. Fred Farr and Will Hutchins carried him to the roadside, laying him on the damp grass. Trembling, Henry Wheeler turned the rescued telescope toward the mountain. Through the lenses, he discerned three tiny figures sprinting toward the summit as fast as the steep incline allowed. Nothing more.

Then, a strangely unseasonable noise rose from the deep valley behind, echoing even in the underbrush of Sentinel Hill. It was the piping of unnumbered whippoorwills, but within their shrill chorus lurked a note of tense, evil expectancy.

Earl Sawyer took the telescope and reported the three figures standing on the topmost ridge, virtually level with the altar-stone yet at a considerable distance. One figure seemed to raise its hands above its head at rhythmic intervals. As Sawyer described this, the crowd heard a faint, half-musical sound from the distance, as if a loud chant accompanied the gestures. The weird silhouette on that remote peak must have been a spectacle of infinite grotesqueness, yet no observer possessed the mood for aesthetic appreciation. "I guess he's sayin' the spell," Wheeler whispered, snatching the telescope back. The whippoorwills piped wildly, their rhythm singularly irregular and discordant with the visible ritual.

Suddenly, the sunshine seemed to lessen without the intervention of any cloud. A peculiar phenomenon marked by all, a rumbling sound brewed beneath the hills, mixed strangely with a concordant rumbling from the sky. Lightning flashed aloft, and the crowd looked in vain for the portents of a storm. The chanting of the men from Arkham became unmistakable; Wheeler saw through the glass that they were all raising their arms in rhythmic incantation. From a farmhouse far away came the frantic barking of dogs.

The quality of daylight shifted, and the crowd gazed about the horizon in wonder. A purplish darkness, born of nothing more than a spectral deepening of the sky's blue, pressed down upon the rumbling hills. Lightning flashed again, brighter than before, and the crowd fancied it revealed a certain mistiness around the altar-stone on the distant height. No one had been using the telescope at that instant. The whippoorwills continued their irregular pulsation, and the men of Dunwich braced themselves tensely against an imponderable menace that seemed to saturate the atmosphere.

Without warning came those deep, cracked, raucous vocal sounds that would never leave the memory of the stricken group. They did not come from any human throat, for the organs of man cannot yield such acoustic perversions. They seemed to emanate from the pit itself, had not their source been unmistakably the altar-stone on the peak. It was almost erroneous to call them *sounds*, for so much of their ghastly, infra-bass timbre spoke to dim seats of consciousness and terror far subtler than the ear; yet they were words, half-articulate and vague. They were loud—loud as the rumblings and thunder above which they echoed—yet they came from no visible being. Because imagination might suggest a conjectural source in the world of non-visible entities, the huddled crowd at the mountain's base huddled still closer, wincing as if expecting a blow.

"_Ygnaiih... ygnaiih... thflthkh'ngha... Yog-Sothoth...._" rang the hideous croaking out of space. "_Y'bthnk... h'ehye... n'grkdl'lh...._"

The speaking impulse seemed to falter here, as if some frightful psychic struggle were underway. Henry Wheeler strained his eye at the telescope but saw only the three grotesquely silhouetted human figures on the peak, moving their arms furiously in strange gestures as their incantation drew near its culmination. From what black wells of Acherontic fear, from what unplumbed gulfs of extra-cosmic consciousness or obscure, long-latent heredity, were those half-articulate thunder-croakings drawn? Presently, they gathered renewed force and coherence, growing in stark, utter, ultimate frenzy.

"_Eh-ya-ya-ya-yahaah... e'yaya-yayaaaa... ngh'aaaa... ngh'aaaa... h'yuh... h'yuh... HELP! HELP!... ff--ff--ff--FATHER! FATHER! YOG-SOTHOTH!..."_

But that was all. The pallid group in the road, still reeling at the *indisputably English* syllables that had poured thickly and thunderously down from the frantic vacancy beside that shocking altar-stone, were never to hear such words again. Instead, they jumped violently at the terrific report that seemed to rend the hills—a deafening, cataclysmic peal whose source, whether inner earth or sky, no hearer could ever place. A single lightning bolt shot from the purple zenith to the altar-stone, and a great tidal wave of viewless force and indescribable stench swept down from the hill to all the countryside. Trees, grass, and underbrush were whipped into a fury. The frightened crowd at the mountain's base, weakened by the lethal fetor that seemed about to asphyxiate them, were almost hurled off their feet. Dogs howled from the distance; green grass and foliage wilted to a curious, sickly yellow-gray, and over field and forest were scattered the bodies of dead whippoorwills.

The stench left quickly, but the vegetation never recovered. To this day, there is something queer and unholy about the growths on and around that fearsome hill. Curtis Whateley was only just regaining consciousness when the Arkham men came slowly down the mountain in beams of sunlight once more brilliant and untainted. They were grave and quiet, seeming shaken by memories and reflections even more terrible than those which had reduced the group of natives to a state of cowed quivering. In reply to a jumble of questions, they only shook their heads and reaffirmed one vital fact.

"The thing has gone for ever," Armitage said. "It has been split up into what it was originally made of, and can never exist again. It was an impossibility in a normal world. Only the least fraction was really matter in any sense we know. It was like its father—and most of it has gone back to him in some vague realm or dimension outside our material universe; some vague abyss out of which only the most accursed rites of human blasphemy could ever have called him for a moment on the hills."

There was a brief silence, and in that pause the scattered senses of poor Curtis Whateley began to knit back into a sort of continuity. He put his hands to his head with a moan. Memory seemed to pick itself up where it had left off, and the horror of the sight that had prostrated him burst upon him again.

"Oh, oh, my Gawd, that haff face... that haff face on top of it... that face with the red eyes an' crinkly albino hair, an' no chin, like the Whateleys.... It was a octopus, centipede, spider kind o' thing, but they was a haff-shaped man's face on top of it, an' it looked like Wizard Whateley's, only it was yards an' yards acrost...."

He paused, exhausted, as the whole group of natives stared in a bewilderment not quite crystallized into fresh terror. Only old Zebulon Whateley, who wandered through ancient memories but had been silent heretofore, spoke aloud.

"Fifteen year' gone," he rambled, "I heerd Ol' Whateley say as haow some day we'd hear a child o' Lavinny's a-callin' its father's name on the top o' Sentinel Hill...."

But Joe Osborn interrupted him to question the Arkham men anew.

"_What was it, anyhaow_, an' haowever did young Wizard Whateley call it aout o' the air it come from?"

Armitage chose his words carefully.

"It was—well, it was mostly a kind of force that doesn't belong in our part of space; a kind of force that acts and grows and shapes itself by other laws than those of our sort of Nature. We have no business calling in such things from outside, and only very wicked people and very wicked cults ever try to. There was some of it in Wilbur Whateley himself—enough to make a devil and a precocious monster of him, and to make his passing out a pretty terrible sight. I'm going to burn his accursed diary, and if you men are wise you'll dynamite that altar-stone up there, and pull down all the rings of standing stones on the other hills. Things like that brought down the beings those Whateleys were so fond of—the beings they were going to let in tangibly to wipe out the human race and drag the earth off to some nameless place for some nameless purpose.

"But as to this thing we've just sent back—the Whateleys raised it for a terrible part in the doings that were to come. It grew fast and big from the same reason that Wilbur grew fast and big—but it beat him because it had a greater share of the *outsideness* in it. You needn't ask how Wilbur called it out of the air. He didn't call it out. *It was his twin brother, but it looked more like the father than he did.*"
