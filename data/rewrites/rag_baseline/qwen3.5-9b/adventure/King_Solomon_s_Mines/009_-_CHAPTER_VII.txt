The air outside the cavern mouth bit with a biting chill that made our presence feel absurdly temporary, a fleeting intrusion against the eternal freeze.

"I am going back," Sir Henry declared, his voice tight with a sudden, pragmatic resolve.

"Why?" Good asked, his tone devoid of judgment, merely seeking clarity.

"Because it has struck me," Sir Henry replied, the realization settling heavy in his chest, "that what we saw... may be my brother."

It was a terrifying hypothesis, one that demanded immediate verification. We retreated into the gloom, our eyes, previously bleached and weakened by the glare of the snow, struggling initially to pierce the cave's oppressive darkness. Slowly, however, our vision adjusted to the semi-light, allowing us to advance toward the motionless figure.

Sir Henry dropped to his knees, peering closely into the face of the corpse. A long, shuddering sigh escaped him. "Thank God," he whispered, the tension draining from his shoulders. "It is *not* my brother."

I stepped forward, drawn by a morbid curiosity. The body belonged to a tall man of middle age, his features aquiline and severe, framed by grizzled hair and a long, black moustache. His skin was a ghastly, perfect yellow, stretched taut over the skeletal framework beneath. Most of his clothing had been stripped away, save for the ragged remnants of a woollen pair of hose, leaving the frame stark and naked. Around the neck of the frozen, rigid corpse hung a crucifix carved from yellow ivory.

"Who on earth can it be?" I breathed.

"Can't you guess?" Good countered, a spark of recognition igniting in his eyes.

I shook my head, confusion clouding my mind.

"Why, the old Dom, José da Silvestra, of course—who else?"

"Impossible!" I gasped, the thought absurd. "He died three hundred years ago."

"And what is there to prevent him from lasting for three thousand years in this atmosphere?" Good retorted, his voice rising with scientific fervor. "If only the temperature is sufficiently low, flesh and blood will keep fresh as New Zealand mutton forever, and Heaven knows it is cold enough here. The sun never penetrates this place; no animal enters to tear or destroy. Doubtless his slave, of whom he speaks in his writings, stripped him and abandoned him. He could not have buried him alone. Look!" He stooped, picking up a strangely shaped bone, the tip scraped to a razor-sharp point. "Here is the 'cleft bone' that Silvestra used to draw his maps."

We stared, momentarily paralyzed by astonishment, our own suffering forgotten in the face of this extraordinary, semi-miraculous tableau.

"Aye," Sir Henry added softly, pointing to a small, jagged wound on the Dom's left arm. "And this is where he got his ink from. Did ever man witness such a thing before?"

Doubt evaporated, replaced by a profound, chilling awe. There he sat, the dead traveler whose instructions, penned ten generations prior, had guided us to this precise coordinate. In my own hand lay the crude pen with which he had etched his legacy, and about his neck hung the crucifix his dying lips had kissed. My imagination raced, reconstructing the final moments of the drama: the traveler succumbing to cold and starvation, yet driven by an indomitable spirit to convey the great secret he had uncovered—the terrible solitude of his death, evidenced by the stillness before us. Even then, I seemed to detect a faint resemblance in his sharply marked features to my poor friend Silvestre, who had died twenty years earlier in my arms, though perhaps that was merely the fever of the moment playing tricks on me. Regardless, he remained there, a somber monument to the fate that claims those who dare penetrate the unknown, destined to sit, crowned with the dread majesty of death, for centuries yet unborn, waiting to startle the eyes of future wanderers who might invade his lonely domain. The sight overwhelmed us, nearly extinguishing the last dregs of our strength.

"Let us go," Sir Henry murmured, his voice low. "Stay, we will give him a companion." He lifted the emaciated body of the Hottentot, Ventvögel, and placed it beside the old Dom. With a sharp jerk, breaking the rotting string of the crucifix—which his numb fingers could not untie—he released the relic. I believe he still possesses it. I took the bone pen; it lies before me as I write, sometimes serving as my instrument for signing my name.

Leaving these two—the proud white man of a lost age and the poor Hottentot—to keep their eternal vigil amidst the endless snows, we crept out of the cave into the welcoming sunshine and resumed our journey, wondering in our hearts how many hours remained before we too became as they were.

After walking roughly half a mile, we reached the edge of the plateau. The mountain's summit did not rise from its exact center, despite appearances from the desert side. Below us, the landscape was obscured by billowing waves of morning fog. Gradually, however, the upper layers of mist thinned, revealing at the end of a long, snow-sloped descent a patch of green grass, some five hundred yards beneath us, through which a stream wound its way. And there, basking in the brilliant sun by the water's edge, stood and lay a group of ten to fifteen large antelopes, their species indiscernible at that distance.

The sight ignited an irrational, soaring joy within us. If only we could secure them, sustenance was abundant. Yet, the challenge loomed: the beasts were fully six hundred yards away, a formidable distance for a shot that could mean life or death.

We debated rapidly the feasibility of stalking the game, but quickly dismissed the plan. The wind was unfavorable, and furthermore, traversing the blinding white expanse would surely reveal us, regardless of our caution.

"Well, we must attempt a shot from here," Sir Henry decided. "Quatermain, which shall it be—the repeating rifles or the expresses?"

Again, a difficult choice presented itself. Our two Winchester repeaters, including the one Umbopa carried alongside Ventvögel's, were sighted to a thousand yards, whereas the expresses were calibrated only to three hundred and fifty. Beyond that range, shooting with them amounted to mere guessing. Conversely, if they did strike, the expanding express bullets offered a significantly higher probability of bringing the quarry down. It was a knotty problem, but I resolved to risk it. We would use the expresses.

"Each of you take the buck opposite to him," I instructed. "Aim precisely at the point of the shoulder, high up. Umbopa, give the command so we may all fire simultaneously."

A tense silence fell, each of us aligning his rifle with the utmost concentration, aware that life itself hung in the balance of the next trigger pull.

"Fire," Umbopa commanded in Zulu.

At almost the exact same instant, the three rifles roared, sending three plumes of smoke drifting before us as a hundred echoes shattered the silence of the snow. Moments later, the haze cleared to reveal—oh, joy!—a great buck lying on its back, kicking furiously in its death throes. We erupted in a yell of triumph; we were saved; starvation was averted. Though weak, we surged down the intervening slope of snow. Within ten minutes of the discharge, the animal's heart and liver lay before us.

Now, a new dilemma emerged: we possessed no fuel to cook the meat. We exchanged glances of dismay.

"Starving men should not be fanciful," Good stated pragmatically. "We must eat raw meat."

With no alternative, the gnawing hunger rendered the prospect less repulsive than it might otherwise have been. We buried the organs in a patch of snow to cool them briefly, then washed them in the ice-cold waters of the stream before devouring them greedily. It sounded horrific, yet honestly, I never tasted anything so exquisite as that raw meat. Within a quarter-hour, we were transformed men. Life and vigor returned to us; our feeble pulses strengthened, and blood coursed forcefully through our veins. Mindful of the dangers of overindulgence on empty stomachs, we exercised restraint, ceasing while we were still hungry.

"Thank Heaven!" Sir Henry exclaimed. "That brute has saved our lives. What is it, Quatermain?"

I rose to examine the antelope, uncertain of its identity. It was roughly the size of a donkey, sporting large, curved horns. I had never encountered such a creature; the species was entirely new to me. It was brown with faint red striping and bore a thick coat. I later learned that the natives of this wondrous land called these bucks "_inco_." They are exceedingly rare, inhabiting only high altitudes where no other game survives. The animal had been struck high in the shoulder, though we could not determine whose bullet felled it. I suspect Good, recalling his miraculous shot at the giraffe, privately attributed the kill to his own prowess, and we did not contradict him.

So engrossed in satisfying our hunger had we been that we had neglected to observe our surroundings. Now, having tasked Umbopa with cutting off the choicest portions of meat we could carry, we inspected the landscape. The mist had dissipated completely; it was eight o'clock, and the sun had consumed the vapor, granting us a panoramic view of the country ahead.
