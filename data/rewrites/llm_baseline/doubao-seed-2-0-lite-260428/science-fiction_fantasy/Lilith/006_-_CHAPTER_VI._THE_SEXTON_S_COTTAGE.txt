We had tramped for uncounted miles across rocky moorland where brittle dry heath and crumbly ash-gray moss crunched under every step, when I spied a tiny cottage huddled on the very edge of the far horizon. The sun had not yet set, but it was wrapped thick in a veil of murky gray cloud, leaking no warmth. The whole moor looked as if it had never once felt the touch of summer heat, and the wind bit through my coat like it blew straight off some hidden realm where it was midnight forever.
“Here we are at last!” croaked the raven, shaking feathers ruffled flat by the gale. “What an endless trudge! In half the time I could have flown all the way to Paradise to visit my cousin—you remember him, the one who never came back to Noah’s ark! Dear me, the cold’s so sharp it might as well be midwinter!”
“Winter?” I stared at him, bewildered. “It feels barely half a day since we left my front door back home!”
“That’s because we’re traveling faster than the rules of your world allow,” he cawed. “Back there you can’t yank up that heavy plumb line you call gravitation and let the whole world spin right under your boots! But look—there’s my wife’s house. She’s gracious enough to let me share it, and we call it the sexton’s cottage!”
“But where is your churchyard?” I frowned, scanning the empty moor, no headstones, no fence, no stone steeple in sight. “Where do you dig your graves?”
The raven stretched his long neck, held his beak perfectly horizontal, and slowly pivoted full circle across every point of the compass, not uttering a word. My eyes followed the slow sweep of his beak, and suddenly the truth hit me: there was no church, no carved stone markers, no fenced plots. *All of it* was a churchyard. Every stretch of wind-scoured heath, every dip and rise of the moor, was his cemetery. He was sexton of every acre his eyes fell on: lord of all the souls laid aside. I was standing in the burial ground of the whole universe, its borders nothing but the low, starless gray horizon. I had left spring, summer, autumn, and every last scrap of sunshine far behind me, and stepped straight into the waiting winter I had long dreaded. But I was wrong. The days of this strange realm stretched long enough to hold every season at once. Winter slept through the long dark, wrapped in a winding sheet of ice; Spring woke at dawn with a soft, childlike smile; Summer blazed at noon in all her lush, dazzling glory; old Autumn crept slow across the fading afternoons, and breathed his last the moment the ghostly, fog-choked night drew near.
As we drew within a few yards of the cottage door, the cloud-shrouded sun plunged down the steep western slope, and sank completely out of sight before we crossed the last stretch of heath. The very same instant, a cold that felt almost solid wrapped itself around my bones, and I stumbled over the threshold fighting free of its clutches, as if I had just dragged myself out of a frozen lake. The wind rose to a howl on the moor and hurled itself at the door, and I barely managed to slam it shut and bolt it behind me.
All at once it was dead still. A single beeswax candle guttered on a plain deal table in the middle of the room, and the first thing I spotted was what I thought was an upright coffin lid propped against the far wall. It swung open on silent hinges: it was a door, and a woman stepped through. She was clad head to toe in white, as bright and unmarked as fresh fallen snow, and her face was as pale as her dress—but it was not the cold white of snow. It hummed with quiet, steady warmth. Her features were so perfectly formed I could not find a flaw in them, but her eyes made me forget every other detail of her face. Every scrap of life in her whole body was gathered there, concentrated into glowing light. It might have been the hush of coming death that made her skin luminous, but her eyes held enough life for a thousand generations—large, dark, and growing deeper in shadow the longer I stared. A whole night sky was condensed in each pupil, every star pricking bright through the blackness, ringed by an iris of soft eternal twilight. No mortal eye could have been made so: those eyes must have come fresh from the palms of God’s own hands. Her still, perfect face was primeval, crafted at the dawn of time; those living eyes were being made new, every single second.
“Here’s Mr. Vane, wife!” said the raven.
“He is welcome,” she answered, in a low, rich, honeyed voice that sounded like buried harp strings thrumming. It held treasures of immortal sound I could not name. I stared at her, completely unable to speak.
“I knew you’d be glad to see him,” the raven added. She stayed still by the door she had stepped through, and did not move an inch closer.
“Will he sleep?” she asked.
“I fear not,” the raven replied. “He is not weary enough, not heavy laden with the weight of his deeds.”
“Then why did you bring him?”
“I am afraid it may have been a little precipitate,” he admitted.
“I do not quite understand you,” I cut in, a sharp uneasy foreboding pricking at my chest, even as I clung to a vague, faint hope of escape. “Surely a man must finish a full day’s work before he rests!” My heart fluttered as I stared at her pale white face. She held my gaze in silence. “Let me go home first,” I went on, “and come back once I have found something, made something, invented or discovered some work of my own!”
“He has not yet learned that the day begins with sleep,” the woman said, turning to her husband. “Tell him he must rest before he can do anything at all.”
“Men get so proud of the things they have already done,” the raven put in, “that they tuck themselves right up inside their own past and fall asleep, like a chicken crawling back into the eggshell it just hatched out of.”
His words pulled my gaze away from the woman, and the raven’s feathers vanished. Standing there was the librarian from my own house, that lean elderly man in his frayed rusty black coat, long jacket tails sweeping his calves. I had only ever seen his back before—now I saw his face for the first time. It was so thin I could trace the clean line of his skull under the pale skin, recalling all the graves his sexton work must have made him familiar with. But no face I had ever seen felt more alive, no gaze sharper or kinder than the one in his pale blue eyes, which held a faint soft haze as if he had wept a thousand tears.
“You knew I was not a raven,” he said, smiling.
“I knew you were Mr. Raven,” I laughed back, “but I truly thought you were a bird too!”
“What made you think that?”
“You looked exactly like a raven, and I saw you dig worms out of the dirt with your beak.”
“Then what did I do?”
“Toss them up in the air.”
“Then?”
“They sprouted wings and turned into butterflies, and flew away.”
“Did you ever see an ordinary raven do that? I told you I was a sexton!”
“Does a sexton toss worms in the air and turn them into butterflies?”
“Yes.”
“I never saw a sexton do that!”
“You saw *me* do it!” He grinned. “I’m still your house’s librarian, you know. I was never dismissed, and I never gave up the post. Now I’m librarian here too.”
“But you just told me you were sexton here!”
“So I am. It’s nearly the same profession. If you are not a true sexton, every book is just a dead body, and a whole library is nothing but a catacomb.”
“You have me all bewildered,” I said.
“That’s the whole point,” he said, and fell silent for a long moment. The woman stood as still as a marble statue, silent, by the door I had mistaken for a coffin lid.
“On some occasions,” the sexton said at last, “it’s far more convenient to let one’s bird-self take the front of things. Every human being, as you ought to know, has a beast-self, and a bird-self, and a silly fish-self, ay, and a slithering serpent-self too—something it takes a great deal of crushing to kill. In truth, you also have a tree-self and a crystal-self, and I can’t even count all the rest—all of them must learn to move in harmony. You can always tell what kind of a person someone is by the creature that slips to the front most often.”
He turned back to his wife. “We have traveled a very long way, wife. We are in want of something to eat and drink.”
“You know, husband,” she answered, “we can only give to the one who asks for it out loud.” She turned her radiant, star-filled eyes full on my face.
“Please give me something to eat, Mrs. Raven,” I said, “and something to drink, whatever you like, to quench my thirst.”
“Your thirst is not deep enough yet for the water that will truly quench it,” she replied, “but what I can give you, I will gladly.” She crossed to a cupboard set into the wall, brought out crusty warm bread and dark, rich wine, and set them on the table. We sat down to the perfect simple meal, and as I ate and drank, the bread and wine sank deeper than just satisfying my hunger. All my anxiety, all the sharp cold of the moor, vanished, and soft bright expectation took its place. All at once I was so sleepy I could barely keep my eyes open, and I felt for the first time how bone-deep tired I was from the journey.
“I have earned neither food nor sleep, Mrs. Raven,” I said, “but you gave me the food freely, and now I hope you will give me the sleep too, for I need it terribly.”
“Sleep is too fine a thing ever to be earned,” the sexton said. “It must be given, and accepted. It is a necessity. But it would be perilous to use this house as a halfway inn, stopping just for one night’s repose before heading on again.”
A scrawny, wild little black cat jumped onto his lap as he spoke. He patted it slow, the way you pat a fretful child to lull it to sleep, but the motion looked exactly like patting soft, warm turf down over a new grave, gentle, with a quiet lullaby hummed under his breath. “This is one of Mara’s kittens,” he said to his wife. “Give it a little something to eat, and put it outside. Mara will be looking for it.”
The woman took the cat from him gently, gave it a crumb of bread, and stepped out the door, closing it soft behind her.
“How then am I to make use of your hospitality?” I asked.
“By accepting it fully,” he answered.
“I do not understand.”
“In this house, no one wakes of their own accord.”
“Why?”
“Because no one anywhere ever wakes of their own accord. You can no more wake yourself up than you can make yourself from nothing.”
“Then perhaps you or Mrs. Raven would kindly wake me when it is time?” I said, a new vague foreboding coiling in my gut.
“We cannot.”
“How dare I then go to sleep?” I cried, afraid.
“If you would have the rest this house offers, you must stop troubling yourself about waking. You must go to sleep heartily, altogether, no half-wakeful fretting holding you back.”
My soul sank in my chest. The sexton sat looking me straight in the face, his eyes soft and gentle, seeming to say *Will you not trust me?* I held his gaze, and nodded. “I will.”
“Then come,” he said, rising to his feet. “I will show you your couch.”
As we stood up, the woman stepped back into the room. She picked up the guttering candle, turned toward the inner door I had first taken for a coffin lid, and led the way. I followed close behind her, the sexton stepping right after me, and we walked into the dim dark beyond.
