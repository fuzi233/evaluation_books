A profound, trembling awe seized me as I contemplated the weight of what lay ahead. Above, the sun burned with a golden intensity; below, the sea stretched in a vast, sapphire expanse. This same world nurtured life upward toward the light and sent creatures soaring into the air I had breathed since infancy. Yet, I perceived the splendor spread out before me as merely a fleeting stage act, destined at any instant to be raised like a drop-curtain to reveal wonders far surpassing this mundane reality.

Some days following my father's passing, I found myself seated in the library. My attention had wandered listlessly over a portrait hanging among the volumes—a likeness of a distant ancestor whose origins remained a mystery to me. Turning away, I retrieved a book from the shelf and opened it, seeking distraction.

As I glanced up, a figure emerged from the shadows—not approaching through the usual doorway, but materializing directly between me and the ancestral portrait. He was a thin, pale man clad in clothes of rusty black. His features were sharp and eager, dominated by a prominent nose that instantly recalled a specific ceramic jug my sisters had dubbed "Mr. Crow."

"Finding yourself in my vicinity, Mr. Vane, I have deemed it appropriate to pay a visit," he announced in a voice that was peculiar yet not unpleasant. "Your honored grandfather treated me, I may say without presumption, as a friend. I knew him from childhood as his father's librarian."

At that moment, I failed to register just how ancient the man truly appeared.

"May I inquire where you reside now, Mr. Crow?" I asked.

A smile curved his lips, amused and knowing.

"You nearly hit my name," he rejoined. "Which demonstrates a certain familial insight. You have encountered me before, though only once, and could not possibly have heard it!"

"In what place?" I pressed.

"In this very room. You were, however, quite a child at the time!"

Memory faltered. I could not be certain I recognized him, yet for a fleeting second, a ghost of familiarity stirred within me. I implored him to correct me regarding his identity.

"There exists a phenomenon wherein one remembers without recognizing the memory itself," he observed gently. "As for your name—which you have approximated sufficiently—it was formerly Raven."

The name resonated with me; I had heard it whispered in connection with marvels and legends.

"It is very kind of you to visit," I said, offering him a seat. "Pray, take a chair."

He settled immediately.

"You knew my father, then, I presume?"

"I knew him," he answered with a curiously enigmatic smile. "But he cared little for my acquaintance, nor did we ever meet. That gentleman, however," he added, gesturing toward the portrait, "Old Sir Up'ard, whom his kin so named—was in his day a friend of mine far more intimate than your grandfather ever became."

Slowly, I began to sense the strangeness of this encounter. Yet, truthfully, it was scarcely stranger that my visitor should recall Sir Up'ard than that he had served as my great-grandfather's librarian!

"I owe him a great debt," he continued. "Although I had read countless more books than he, it was through the specific direction of his studies that he revealed to me a certain relationship of modes. A truth I would never have discovered unaided, and likely never have learned from anyone else."

"Would you mind explaining all of that?" I urged.

"Not at all—at least as much as I am able. There are no such things as willful secrets," he replied, and launched into his tale.

"That closet housed his library—perhaps a hundred manuscripts, for printing had not yet been invented. One morning, I sat there cataloging them when he peered around the doorframe and simply said, 'Come.' I laid down my pen and followed him—across the great hall, down a steep, rough descent, and along an underground passage leading to a tower he had recently constructed. It consisted of a spiral staircase and a single room at the summit. The door possessed a tremendous lock, which he dismantled with the smallest key I had ever witnessed.

"As soon as I stepped across the threshold behind him, he began to diminish in my sight, shrinking smaller and smaller until my vision suddenly corrected itself. I realized he was moving rapidly away from me. Within a minute, he was nothing more than a mere speck in the distance, framed by the tops of blue mountains standing clear against a sky of paler blue. I recognized the landscape; I had traveled there and back many times, though I had never known this particular route existed.

'Many years later,' he mused, 'when the tower had long since vanished, I instructed one of his descendants in what Sir Up'ard had taught me. To this day, whenever I wish to return home via the shortest path, I utilize your house. Indeed, I feel compelled—even without your leave, for which I beg forgiveness—to have firmly established a right-of-way through it. Not from front to back, but from bottom to top!'

'Would you have me understand, Mr. Raven,' I interjected, 'that you traverse my house into another realm, indifferent to the separating space?'

'That I pass through it is an incontrovertible acknowledgment of space,' the old librarian returned.

'Do not quibble, Mr. Raven,' I countered. 'Please accept my question exactly as I intend it.'

'Therein resides a door within your house; a single step through it transports me into a world vastly different from this one.'

'A superior one?'

'Not entirely superior, but so profoundly distinct that most of its physical laws—and many of its mental ones—differ from those governing this world. As for moral laws, they must remain fundamentally identical everywhere.'

'You test the limits of my credulity!' I exclaimed.

'You likely take me for a lunatic?'

'You do not appear to be one.'

'A liar, then?'

'You afford me no grounds to suspect you of deceit.'

'Perhaps you simply do not believe me?'

'I shall walk through that door with you if you wish. I possess enough faith in you to risk the endeavor.'

'The blunder all my progeny commit!' he murmured. 'The sole exit is the entrance!'

A sudden chill ran through me; I began to suspect madness. He sat in silence for a moment, his head resting on his hand, his elbow propped on the table, his gaze fixed upon the books before him.

'A book,' he declared, raising his voice, 'is a door inward, and therefore also a door outward.' He closed his eyes, seemingly seeing something beyond the walls. 'I behold Old Sir Up'ard,' he continued, his voice swelling with emotion, 'and my heart overflows with love for him. What realm does he inhabit?'

'The world of your heart!' I responded. 'That is to say, the concept of him resides there.'

'There is, then, at least one world to which your hall-door does not open?'

'I concede that point; however, the entities inhabiting that world are not commodities to be acquired or held.'

'Reason further,' he prompted. 'Did anything ever truly become yours except by entering that world? The thought escapes you, alas, at present! I tell you, there exist more worlds, and more portals to them, than you could conceive in many years!'

He rose, left the library, traversed the hall, and ascended directly to the garret, navigating every turn with evident familiarity. I followed, scrutinizing his retreating back. His hair hung long, dark, straight, and glossy. His coat was wide, sweeping down to his heels, while his shoes seemed comically oversized for his frame.

Within the garret, light filtered through the edges of the massive roofing slabs, illuminating areas where the floorboards were absent, forcing us to step carefully from joist to joist. In the midst of one such gap, a partition rose, bearing a door. Through it, I followed Mr. Raven into a small, obscurant chamber whose ceiling contracted as it ascended, slanting sharply through the roof.

'That is the portal I mentioned,' he said, indicating an oblong mirror standing on the floor, leaning against the wall. I approached it, observing our figures dimly reflected in its dusty surface. An unease prickled my skin. It appeared old-fashioned and neglected, yet despite its ordinary appearance, the eagle perched atop the frame with outstretched wings seemed menacing.

'As a mirror,' the librarian noted, 'it has grown dingy with age; but that matters not. Its clarity depends solely upon the light.'

'Light!' I protested. 'There is no light here!'

He offered no reply but began tugging at a small chain mounted on the opposite wall. A creaking sound echoed, and the top of the chamber began to rotate slowly. He paused, checked his watch, and resumed pulling.

'We arrive almost to the precise moment!' he announced. 'It is upon the very stroke of noon!'

The ceiling groaned and revolved for a minute or so. Then he manipulated two additional chains, alternating between them, before returning to the first. Shortly thereafter, the chamber brightened considerably. A patch of sunlight fell upon a mirror on the wall opposite the one leaning against it, casting a beam across the dust.
