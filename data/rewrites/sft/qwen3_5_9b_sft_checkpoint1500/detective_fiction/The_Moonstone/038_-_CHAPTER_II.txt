"Betteredge!" I called out, gesturing toward the familiar volume resting on his knees. "Has *Robinson Crusoe* told you this evening that Franklin Blake might be expected?"

"The Lord Harry, Mr. Franklin!" cried the old man, his voice thick with emotion. "That is precisely what *Robinson Crusoe* has revealed!"

With my support, he struggled to his feet, standing for a fleeting moment caught between the book and me, utterly bewildered as to whether either of us had startled him more. Eventually, the verdict fell upon the book. Clutching it open in both hands, he stared at the pages with a gaze of unutterable anticipation, as if expecting Robinson Crusoe himself to step forth from the ink and grant us a private audience.

"There is the passage, Mr. Franklin!" he exclaimed once his tongue had returned to its normal function. "As I live by bread, sir, this is the very line I was reading the instant before you arrived! Page one hundred and fifty-six reads: 'I stood like one Thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an Apparition.' If that does not amount to saying, 'Expect the sudden arrival of Mr. Franklin Blake,' there is no logic left in the English language!" declared Betteredge, slamming the book shut and finally freeing one hand to grasp the one I extended.

I had anticipated, quite naturally, that he would overwhelm me with a barrage of inquiries. Yet, no such thing occurred. The hospitable impulse held absolute sway in the old servant's mind whenever a family member, however unexpected, appeared as a guest within the house.

"Step inside, Mr. Franklin," he said, bowing with his quaint, old-world grace and pushing aside the door behind him. "I will inquire as to your reasons for being here afterward—but first, I must ensure your comfort. Alas, terrible changes have occurred since you departed. The estate lies shuttered, and the staff have scattered. Do not trouble yourself with that! I will prepare your supper, and the gardener's wife will arrange your bed. Furthermore, if a bottle of our renowned Latour claret remains in the cellar, down your throat it shall go, Mr. Franklin! I bid you a hearty welcome, sir! A warm welcome!" shouted the poor old soul, battling valiantly against the desolation of the empty halls, greeting me with the genial courtesy of a bygone era.

It grieved me to disappoint him. However, this was now Rachel's house. Could I dine within its walls, or sleep beneath its roof, after the turmoil that unfolded in London? The simplest dictates of self-respect—forbade, properly speaking, to cross the threshold.

Taking Betteredge by the arm, I guided him out into the gardens. There was no alternative. I was compelled to speak the truth. Caught between his devotion to Rachel and his affection for me, he was deeply perplexed and distressed by the sudden shift in fortunes. When he finally voiced his opinion, it was delivered in his customary blunt fashion, seasoned with the most robust philosophy I had ever encountered—the philosophy of the Betteredge School.

"Miss Rachel possesses her flaws, I have never disputed it," he began. "And occasionally, she rides the high horse. Indeed, she has been attempting to ride over *you*, and you have endured it. Good heavens, Mr. Franklin, do you not know women by this point in life better than that? Recall what I told you concerning the late Mrs. Betteredge?"

I had listened to his tales of the late Mrs. Betteredge frequently, invariably presented as his irrefutable proof of the innate weakness and perversity inherent in the fairer sex. In this role, he produced her instance now.

"Very well, Mr. Franklin. Listen closely. Women of different natures ride the high horse in diverse manners. The late Mrs. Betteredge utilized her preferred steed whenever I dared deny her anything she had set her heart upon. Consequently, every time I returned home weary from labor, she would summon me up the kitchen stairs, declaring that, following my brutish treatment, she lacked the courage to cook my dinner. I tolerated this for some time—precisely as you endure it now from Miss Rachel. Finally, my patience frayed. I descended the stairs, lifted Mrs. Betteredge—affectionately, mark you—into my arms, and carried her, holus bolus, into the best parlor where she entertained her guests. I proclaimed, 'That is the proper place for you, my dear,' and retreated to the kitchen. I locked the door, removed my coat, rolled up my sleeves, and prepared my own meal. Upon finishing, I served it with the utmost ceremony and devoured it with gusto. Following that, I smoked my pipe, sipped a drop of grog, then cleared the table, scrubbed the crockery, polished the cutlery, stored everything away, and swept the hearth until it shone brighter and cleaner than possible. Only then did I unlock the door and admit Mrs. Betteredge. 'I have eaten, my dear,' I said, 'and I trust you will find the kitchen left in a state worthy of your fondest desires.' For the remainder of that woman's life, Mr. Franklin, I never again needed to cook my own dinner! The moral is plain: You have endured Miss Rachel in London; do not endure her in Yorkshire. Return to the house!"

His argument was utterly unassailable! I could only assure my faithful companion that even his formidable powers of persuasion were, in this specific instance, wasted upon me.

"It is a beautiful evening," I remarked. "I shall proceed to Frizinghall, spend the night at the hotel, and request that you join me for breakfast tomorrow. I have matters to discuss with you."

Betteredge shook his head solemnly.

"I am profoundly sorrowful regarding this," he stated. "I had hoped, Mr. Franklin, to hear that affairs were restored to smoothness and pleasantness between you and Miss Rachel. Should you insist upon your own way, sir," he continued after a brief pause, "there is no necessity to travel to Frizinghall tonight for lodging. Something more accessible exists. Hotherstone's Farm, barely two miles hence, offers accommodation. You could scarcely object to *that* on account of Miss Rachel," the old man added with a mischievous twinkle. "Mr. Franklin, Hotherstone resides on his own freehold."

The mention of the place sparked an immediate recollection. The farmhouse sat nestled in a sheltered inland valley, beside the prettiest stream in that stretch of Yorkshire. The farmer maintained a spare bedroom and parlor, which he customarily rented to artists, anglers, and tourists alike. As a dwelling during my sojourn in the neighborhood, I could not have desired a more congenial spot.

"Are the rooms available for rent?" I inquired.

"Mrs. Hotherstone herself, sir, requested my endorsement of the rooms yesterday."

"I shall accept them, Betteredge, with the utmost pleasure."

We retraced our steps to the yard where I had abandoned my traveling bag. After threading a cane through the handle and draping the bag over his shoulder, Betteredge seemed to revert to the confusion my abrupt arrival had caused when he found me seated in the beehive chair. He regarded the house with disbelief, then wheeled about to look at me with equal incredulity.

"I have inhabited this world for a considerable duration," said this finest and dearest of all old servants, "but such a spectacle, I never expected to witness. Behold the house, and behold Mr. Franklin Blake—and, Damme, if one of them is not turning his back upon the other to sleep in a lodging!"

Leading the way out, he wagged his head and growled ominously. "There exists but one further miracle capable of occurring," he muttered over his shoulder. "The next action you will undertake, Mr. Franklin, will be to repay me those seven-and-sixpence you borrowed when you were a lad."

This barb of sarcasm lifted his spirits considerably, restoring a better humor for himself and for me. We departed the house and passed through the lodge gates. Once clear of the grounds, the obligations of hospitality (within Betteredge's moral code) ceased, and the rights of curiosity commenced.

He fell back slightly to allow me to walk alongside him. "A splendid evening for a stroll, Mr. Franklin," he remarked, as though we had merely coincidentally met at that precise moment. "Pray, suppose you had ventured to the hotel at Frizinghall, sir?"

"Yes?"

"In that scenario, I should have had the honor of breaking bread with you tomorrow morning."

"Come to Hotherstone's Farm instead for breakfast."

"You owe me a debt of gratitude for your kindness, Mr. Franklin. But it was not precisely breakfast I sought to propose. You mentioned having something to impart to me? If it holds no secrets, sir," said Betteredge, suddenly abandoning the crooked path to take the direct route, "I am burning with curiosity to learn what has driven you down here in such haste."

"What drove me here previously?" I asked.

"The Moonstone, Mr. Franklin. But what drives you now, sir?"

"The Moonstone again, Betteredge."

The old man halted abruptly, staring at me in the grey twilight as if suspecting his own ears of betraying him.

"If that is a jest, sir," he said, "I fear I am growing somewhat dull in my advanced years. I do not comprehend it."
