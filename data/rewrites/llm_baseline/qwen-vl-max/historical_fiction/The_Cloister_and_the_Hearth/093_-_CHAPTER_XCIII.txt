One day, as Clement lay in his cave, consumed by sorrow and silence—no prayers, no songs, only a soul weighed down by despair—a sudden thought pierced through the darkness. If he had once given comfort to the poor and the weary, why should he not now offer himself the same solace? He would fill this prison of stone with the words of God, with sacred texts, with the gentle consolations of faith. Like Christian at the feet of Apollyon, he reached out—not for a sword, for he had none—but for his chisel, that humble tool of quiet labor, and began to carve as if his very soul depended on the strength of his arm.

They say Michaelangelo, in the centuries to come, did not model his statues in clay and hand them over to artisans to chisel. No—he seized the marble block, envisioned the form within, and struck it with mallet and steel until chips flew like sparks from a forge, and the shape burst forth. So too did Clement work: without plan or sketch, he drew upon memory, summoning holy phrases, then dashed into his task, carving them deep into the soft rock between moments of struggle and sleep.

He begged travelers for candle stubs and foul oil. “Anything will burn,” he said, “if only it gives light.” And so, at night, his cave glowed faintly, like a blacksmith’s hearth seen through cracks in the door and window. The villagers passing by crossed themselves, whispering of witchcraft. But inside, one sacred phrase after another emerged from the stone:
*O God, who in wondrous order appointest the ministries of angels…* (the full collect).

Beneath the great crucifix fastened to the wall, he carved these words from Augustine:
*O Christian soul, look upon the wounds of the suffering One, the blood of the dying One, the price paid for our redemption! Reflect on how great these things are, and weigh them in the balance of your mind—so that He may be fully fixed in your heart, who for you was wholly nailed to the cross. For when the sufferings of Christ are recalled, nothing on earth is too hard to endure with calmness.*

Soothed by the presence of these sacred words, and worn down by ceaseless labor and prayer, Clement one morning collapsed from exhaustion. A deep sleep overtook him, lasting many hours. When he awoke, quiet and still, he heard a tiny chirp. He opened his eyes—and there, perched on his breviary resting on a low stool, was a robin redbreast. The bird fluffed its feathers, smoothed them again, cocked its head with feigned wisdom, and preened itself with all the dignity of a courtier.

Clement held his breath. He half-closed his eyes, afraid to startle the fragile visitor. The robin hopped down to the floor, went through its little show of cunning, then with three stiff hops—like a ball of wool on wires—landed on the hermit’s bare foot. There it swelled and contracted, puffing its chest like a proud lord. But Clement closed his eyes completely, barely breathing, content only to feel the tiny claws upon his skin.

Then came a flutter of wings—the bird was back on the breviary. Clement decided to try feeding it. But how? He had bread nearby, yet dared not move. Slowly, slowly, he crept his hand toward the bread, froze, then slipped a crumb between thumb and forefinger. With a flick of the wrist—like a boy shooting marbles—he sent it flying, keeping his arm perfectly still.

The robin saw it fall, pretended to ponder, then remained still. Another crumb followed. Then another. Finally, it darted forward and snatched one, but seemed uncertain—then fled, carrying the prize to a higher perch: the hermit’s knee.

Thus the game continued, until a larger piece rolled along. This was a treasure. The robin pounced, lifted it high, and vanished into the night, the air humming with its swift flight.

“Bless you, sweet bird,” whispered Clement, “your wings are music, and you are a feathered ray that has come to brighten my darkened soul.”

And so, renewed, he returned to prayer and to his tools, his courage stirred. That day’s work read:
*Come, Creator Spirit, visit the minds You have made. Fill them with Your divine grace. Ignite the senses with light, strengthen our weak bodies with perpetual power.*

Days passed. The weather turned colder. But Clement’s heart grew warmer. Despair, like a cloud, had drifted in and now drifted out. It had come unbidden, and now left without warning. Soon, everything reversed: his cell shimmered with joy. His work pleased him; his prayers were rich with devotion; his psalms rang with praise.

Birds flocked to him, drawn by their crimson leader. They flew from snow and frost, from villages where men were Cain to each other, and found peace in the company of Abel—friends, true and fast.

One sharp, frosty night, as Clement sang praises on his psaltery, the hollow cave echoed with sacred song, as if it were Tubal’s shell or David’s own harp. Then he heard a clear, mournful whine—not discordant, but strangely melodic. It grew louder, less tuned. Peering through the cracks in his door, he saw a great red wolf sitting outside, nose raised, moaning softly into the night.

Clement smiled. “My sins are leaving,” he murmured, “and the creatures of God are accepting me, one by one.”

In a surge of joy, he broke into song:

And all the while, the wolf bayed in reply.

But greater still was the sense that he was drawing near to the celestial communion—the true mark of the hermit. He began to dream of saints and angels, visions so vivid they felt real. He saw figures clad in woven snow, eyes more beautiful than those of the antelopes he’d seen in Rome. Broad wings, colored like the rainbow, fanned him gently. Soft voices urged him onward.

Yet soon, his dreams took a strange turn. He wandered with Fra Colonna among ancient ruins. The friar was lame, leaning on a staff, which he waved over temples and palaces—Egyptian, Greek, Roman—and instantly they rose again, whole and grand. Musicians sang songs that might have outshone Apollo. Women, divine in form, danced in marble courts. Sculptors shaped beauty from Parian marble in minutes. Philosophers debated profound truths, while youth listened in reverence.

Yet beneath it all, a shadow lingered: *This is only a prologue. Something lies behind—something ominous, something hidden.*

One night, Colonna pointed. Cities vanished. They stood on a vast desert plain beneath a crimson sun sinking to rest. Palm trees stood tall; reed huts dotted the horizon. “These are the anchorites of the Theban desert,” Colonna said calmly, “followers not of Christ, nor His apostles, nor the great fathers—but of Greek disciples of Egyptian pupils of the Brahmins and Gymnosophists.”

Clement longed to rush forward, to embrace these holy men, to confess his troubles. But he was bound—his feet rooted to the ground. The sun sank. Darkness fell. Colonna’s figure blurred, yet his eyes blazed tenfold. From them came a voice:
“No, let them be. A pack of fools. I see how dismal it all is.”

Then, suddenly cheerful:
“But I hear one has a manuscript of Petronius, on papyrus. I go to buy it. Farewell—for ever, for ever, for ever.”

And it was pitch black. Then, behind him, a gentle glow—roseate, warm, radiant. It lifted his feet from the earth. He turned—and there stood Margaret Brandt, her face glowing with sunlight, her hair shimmering like a saint’s halo.

She blushed, smiled, and looked at him with ineffable tenderness.
“Gerard,” she whispered, “be whose thou wilt by day—but at night, be mine.”

At that moment, the shock of seeing her awakened him. He lay trembling, drenched in cold sweat. That vision—her radiant face, her voice—had become his nightly keynote. Awake, he could pray, worship, and master his thoughts. But asleep, she came—Margaret, or Satan in her likeness—an angel of light.

He knew it was a fiend. Not because it was ugly, but because angels cannot blush. And so he understood: the enemy had grown subtle.

He had read of such tricks—from Saint Anthony to Nicholas of the Rock—hermits beset by visions, sometimes of pleasure, sometimes of love, always of warmth and touch, yet always deceitful. He remembered the tale of Saint Macarius, visited by a lovely spirit who wept and wept, feigning sorrow, stealing his tears and half his virtue.

But alongside these warnings, he knew the hermits’ weapons: discipline. *Domandum est Corpus*—the body must be tamed. For twelve hundred years, this had been their creed. They crushed the heart through pain, mortified the flesh, silenced the passions.

So Clement resolved: the enemy had retreated only to return stronger. He had allowed him days of purity and joy—to lull him into carelessness. Now, the soft seductions began.

He tightened his rule. He shortened his sleep, lengthened his prayers, increased his fasting to abstinence. Once, he ate only at sunset. Now, he ate once every two days, drinking only water.

The visions grew clearer.

Then he turned to the hermits’ greatest remedy—the cold bath. He found the deepest part of the stream near his cell, fed by a holy well. He cleared stones, dug a hole where he could stand waist-deep in icy water. On the next diabolical attack, he leapt from his bed and plunged in.

The cold stole his breath. It froze his marrow.
“I shall die,” he gasped, “but better this than eternal fire.”

The next day, he was stiff, aching, bones like teeth gnawing at him. And even in sleep, he felt the pain—until the phantom appeared, smiled pityingly, and the ache vanished.

Now, he feared returning to the water, weakened by fasting. So instead, he scourged himself until blood ran. He lay down, smarting, exhausted. As the pain dulled to a throb, the past rushed back.

He sat with Margaret at the duke’s feast—music divine, purple fountains gushing. Youth and love filled the air. Then they stood at the altar, man and wife. No interruption. They walked hand in hand, sharing their nightmares.

He confessed: “I dreamed you were dead, and I was a monk. The dream was so real—so full of detail—that only my eyes prove it was not truth. Yet now, I know we are one.”

That key struck—every dream thereafter ran upon it. Awake, he was Clement, the hermit, risen from night’s visions. Asleep, he was Gerard Eliassoen, the happy husband, reunited with his beloved.

His constant fasts, combined with other austerities, had reduced him nearly to a skeleton. Yet flesh still clung—unsubdued, quivering with earthly desire. “Or why else would evil spirits hold such power?” he asked.

His certainty grew when one day he caught himself slipping into sleep, feeling *relief*—because now Margaret would come, and he would feel no pain. The unreal would become real, the real unreal—for an hour.

He rose with a cry of horror. Stripping to the skin, he climbed to the brambles above his cave and threw himself onto them, rolling and writhing in agony. He came back a mass of blood, moaning for hours—until exhaustion brought a deep, dreamless sleep.

He awoke in pain—but with joy in his soul. He had broken the spell.

Yes, it was broken. Day after day passed. Her image no longer haunted him. He sighed at his victory.

The birds grew bolder. They perched on his hand. Two let him gild their claws. With food scarce, he had more to give.

But peace did not last.

A woman’s voice came from outside. She told him his story in a few words, then asked: “Where can I find Gerard?”

He was stunned. Instinctively, he replied: “Pray for the soul of Gerard, son of Eli!”—meaning he was dead to the world.

He sat, bewildered. When she was gone, he decided to risk being seen. He peered after her—but she ran quickly, and the road was clear.

“Satan!” he muttered.

That night, the visions returned—more vivid than ever. He could count every auburn hair in Margaret’s head, see the pupils of her eyes.

Despair set in. “I must leave this land,” he said. “Here I am chained to memory. A breath from hell has infected this place—even these holy words lose their power.”

Unconsciously imitating St. Jerome, he took his warmest cloak and laid it out in the woods, beneath a tree. He lay down—bare, broken, like a sculpture of bone and leather that might once have served as Apollo.

Whether imagination ebbed, as modern minds suggest all things do—or whether this act severed some unseen bond—I know not. But his sleep was dreamless.

He awoke nearly frozen—but warm within, joyful.
“I shall yet be a true hermit, by God’s grace,” he declared.

The next day, someone left on his platform a new lambswool pelisse and cape—soft, warm, ample. He hesitated—its comfort was tempting. But then he smiled. It was the right skin. A sign from Heaven.

It warmed his bones after his brief rest.

Now, at one moment he saw victory ahead—if he could survive. At another, he warned himself: “It is only a pause. Be on guard, Clement.”

He was like a soldier deep in enemy territory.

One clear, frosty night, he returned to his cell. The stars shone brilliantly—sky seemed vaster, brighter, watching with a thousand eyes.
“Oh, wonderful,” he breathed, “that men should commit crimes by night, and others live only for this small world, ignoring that glorious one which reveals its wonders every night to any eye not blinded by habit. Thank God I am a hermit.”

He paused at his door. It was shut.
“Why, I thought I left it open,” he said. “The wind? There is no wind. What does this mean?”

He placed a hand on the rough stone. Through a crack, he saw the oil wick burning exactly where he had left it.
“What is happening to me?” he sighed. “I tremble at nothing. Either I closed it myself—or the fiend shut it behind me to disturb my peace. Back, Satan!”

He entered swiftly, lit a large taper. As he did, a soft sigh echoed in the cave. He started—dropped the candle. It went out. He bent, relit it, listening. When it burned steady, he shaded it with his hand and cast the light around.

In the farthest corner, the wall seemed broken. He stepped forward, heart pounding. The flame brightened—and he saw the outline of a woman.

Another step. Knees knocking.
