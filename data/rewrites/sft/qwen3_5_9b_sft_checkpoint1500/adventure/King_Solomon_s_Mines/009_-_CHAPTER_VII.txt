Outside the cavern, the biting chill of the snow forced us to halt. A profound, foolish silence seemed to hang over us, broken only by Sir Henry's resolve. "I am going back," he declared. "It has struck me," he explained to Good, "that what we witnessed within may well be my brother."

This notion, startling in its suddenness, compelled us to return to the gloom. Emerging from the brilliant daylight, our eyes, weakened by the glare, struggled initially against the cave's oppressive shadow. Gradually, however, vision adjusted to the semi-darkness, guiding us toward the figure slumped in the corner.

Sir Henry knelt, peering intently at the pale countenance. With a sigh that mingled awe and relief, he breathed, "Thank God... it is not my brother."

I approached cautiously to examine the remains more closely. The body belonged to a tall man of middle years, distinguished by aquiline features, grizzled hair, and a formidable black moustache. His skin was a terrifying, perfect yellow, stretched taut over a skeletal frame, devoid of all warmth. Almost every article of clothing had been stripped away, save for the ragged remnants of woollen hose clinging to his legs. Around his neck, frozen rigid, hung a crucifix carved from yellow ivory.

"Who on earth can this be?" I asked, my voice tight.

"Why, the old Dom, José da Silvestra!" Good exclaimed, his eyes gleaming with recognition. "Who else could it be?"

"Impossible!" I gasped. "He died three hundred years ago."

"And what prevents him from persisting for three millennia in this atmosphere?" Good countered, his reasoning sharp despite the freezing air. "If the temperature is sufficiently low, flesh and blood preserve as fresh as New Zealand mutton forever. And heaven knows, it is cold enough here. The sun never penetrates; no beast enters to tear or destroy. Doubtless his slave, mentioned in his writings, stripped him and departed. He could not have buried him alone. Look!" Stooping, Good picked up a peculiar, sharpened bone. "Here is the 'cleft bone' that Silvestra used to draw maps."

We stared, momentarily forgetting our own misery in the face of this extraordinary, semi-miraculous tableau.

"Ay," Sir Henry murmured, pointing to a small, healed wound on the Dom's left arm. "And this is where he obtained his ink." He paused, marveling. "Did ever man witness such a phenomenon?"

Doubt vanished completely, leaving me appalled. There sat the deceased traveler, whose directions, penned a decade of generations prior, had guided us to this desolate spot. In my hand now lay the crude pen with which he had scribbled his final testament, and about his neck rested the crucifix his dying lips had kissed. Gazing upon him, my imagination reconstructed the tragic finale: the traveler succumbing to cold and starvation, yet driven by a desperate will to convey the great secret he had uncovered—the terrible isolation of his demise. The evidence sat stark before us. I could almost trace a likeness in his sharply marked features to Silvestre, my poor friend who had died twenty years before in my arms, though perhaps that was merely fancy. Here he remained, a mournful monument to the fate awaiting those who dare probe the unknown, destined to sit there, crowned with the dread majesty of death, for centuries unborn, to startle any future wanderer who might stumble upon this lonely realm.

The thing overwhelmed us, already nearly perished from cold and hunger.

"Let us depart," Sir Henry whispered. "But first, we shall grant him a companion." Lifting the frozen body of the Hottentot, Ventvögel, he placed it beside the old Dom. Then, stooping, he broke the rotting string of the crucifix hanging from Silvestra's neck, for his fingers were too numb to untie it. I believe he retains it still. I took the bone pen, which remains before me as I write; sometimes, I use it to sign my name.

Leaving these two—the proud white man of a bygone age and the unfortunate Hottentot—to maintain their eternal vigil amidst the endless snows, we crept out of the cave into the welcoming sunshine and resumed our journey. We wondered in our hearts how many hours remained before we too should resemble them.

After traversing roughly half a mile, we reached the plateau's edge. Though the mountain's summit did not rise from its exact center, viewed from the desert, it had appeared so. Below us, obscured by rolling waves of morning fog, lay the hidden landscape. Soon, however, the upper layers of mist dissipated, revealing a patch of green grass, some five hundred yards down a long snow-slope, where a stream wound its way. Beside the stream, bathed in the radiant sun, stood and lay a group of ten to fifteen large antelopes.

The sight ignited an unreasoning joy within us. Food was plentiful, if only we could secure it. Yet, the challenge was daunting. The beasts were fully six hundred yards distant—a shot requiring precision beyond our current desperation. We debated stalking the game, but swiftly dismissed the plan. The wind was unfavorable, and traversing the blinding snowfield would surely reveal us.

"Well, we must attempt a shot from here," Sir Henry decided. "Quatermain, shall we employ the repeating rifles or the express revolvers?"

Again, a difficult choice presented itself. The Winchester repeaters, including the one Umbopa carried alongside Ventvögel's, were sighted to a thousand yards. Conversely, the express revolvers, limited to three hundred and fifty yards, rendered shots beyond that distance mere guesses. However, if they struck, the expanding bullets offered a far greater likelihood of bringing down the game. It was a knotty problem, but I resolved to risk the shorter-range weapons.

"Each of us takes the buck opposite to him," I instructed. "Aim well at the shoulder point, high up. Umbopa, give the command so we fire simultaneously."

A tense pause followed, each of us leveling his weapon with the utmost concentration, aware that life itself depended on the result.

"Fire!" Umbopa cried in Zulu.

At nearly the same instant, three rifles roared, sending clouds of smoke into the air and a hundred echoes reverberating over the silent snow. As the haze cleared, behold! A great buck lay on its back, kicking frantically in the throes of death. We erupted in a yell of triumph. We were saved; starvation was averted. Though weak, we rushed down the intervening slope. Within ten minutes, the heart and liver of the beast lay before us.

A new dilemma immediately arose: we possessed no fuel to cook the meat. We gazed at one another in dismay.

"Starving men should not indulge in fantasies," Good stated pragmatically. "We must eat raw."

With no alternative, and our gnawing hunger rendering the prospect less repulsive, we complied. We buried the organs in a patch of snow to chill them, then washed them in the icy stream water. Finally, we devoured them greedily. While the thought sounded horrific, I confess I had never tasted anything so exquisite as that raw meat. Within a quarter-hour, we were transformed men. Life and vigor returned to us; our feeble pulses strengthened, and blood coursed vigorously through our veins. Mindful of the perils of over-indulgence on starving stomachs, we restrained ourselves, eating until satisfied but no further.

"Thank Heaven!" Sir Henry exclaimed. "That brute has saved our lives. What species is it, Quatermain?"

I rose to inspect the quarry. It resembled a donkey in size, boasting large, curved horns. The species was entirely new to me: brown in color, marked with faint red stripes, and sporting a thick coat. Later, I learned the natives called these bucks "_inco_." Rare and confined to high altitudes where no other game survived, this animal had been struck high in the shoulder, though determining whose bullet felled it remained impossible. I suspected Good, recalling his marvelous shot at the giraffe, secretly claimed credit, and we did not contradict him.

So absorbed had we been in satisfying our hunger that we had neglected to observe our surroundings. Now, having tasked Umbopa with cutting strips of the finest meat for transport, we turned our attention to the landscape. The mist had lifted; it was eight o'clock, and the sun had evaporated the remaining vapor, unveiling a panoramic vista of breathtaking glory. I have seldom, if ever, beheld anything comparable.
