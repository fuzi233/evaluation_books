Breathing Tranquillity.

"And down the sunny beach she paces slowly,
With many doubtful pauses by the way;
Grief hath an influence so hushed and holy."

The silence of the room after the journey to Oxford was heavy, broken only by the soft rustle of fabric as Edith leaned over her husband. In the dim light, she pulled his tall head down, rising on her toes to whisper a plea that he not be shocked by what followed. Captain Lennox, however, remained a blank slate; if he had ever heard of Margaret's standing, the memory had long since evaporated. To him, a Fellow of a minor college leaving his post was a trivial matter, hardly worth the currency of two hundred and fifty pounds a year, especially since she was known to eschew wine. Edith descended from her tiptoes, her spirits deflated, feeling the delicate architecture of her romance crumble under the weight of mundane reality.

A week later, the atmosphere shifted. Edith pranced toward her husband, executing a low, theatrical curtsey.

"I am right, and you are wrong, most noble Captain," she declared. "Margaret has received a lawyer's letter. She is the residuary legatee. The legacies total about two thousand pounds, with the remainder valued at approximately forty thousand pounds in Milton property."

"Indeed!" Lennox replied, his interest piqued. "And how does she receive this sudden windfall?"

"Oh, it appears she suspected she was entitled to it all along; she simply lacked the imagination to grasp the magnitude. She looks remarkably pale and white, claiming fear of the fortune, but that is merely nonsense, destined to fade. I left my mother pouring congratulations down her throat and slipped away to inform you."

It became the universal consensus that Mr. Lennox was henceforth Margaret's legal guardian. Her ignorance of business forms was absolute; every transaction required his intervention. He selected her attorney, delivered papers for her signature, and derived a peculiar joy from instructing her in the arcane symbols of the law.

One day, Edith approached him with an arch smile. "Henry, do you know what I hope and expect these prolonged conversations with Margaret to conclude with?"

"No, I do not," he replied, flushing slightly. "And I pray you do not tell me."

"Oh, very well; then I need not instruct Sholto to cease inviting Mr. Montagu so frequently to our home."

"As you wish," he said, maintaining a forced composure. "Whatever you envision may or may not come to pass. However, before I commit myself, I must ensure my position is secure. Ask whoever you please. It may lack civility, Edith, but if you interfere, you will ruin it. She has been fiercely resistant to me for some time, only beginning to thaw from her Zenobian demeanor. She possesses the makings of a Cleopatra, were she but a shade more pagan."

"For my part," Edith countered with a touch of malice, "I am delighted she remains a Christian. There are so few of us left!"

That autumn brought no Spain for Margaret. Though she clung to the hope that a fortunate circumstance might summon Frederick to Paris, where a convoy awaited, she was instead confined to Cromer. Her aunt Shaw and the Lennoxes, bound for the coast, had long wished her to accompany them; consequently, their efforts to facilitate her separate desires were sluggish and perfunctory. Perhaps Cromer was, in a sense, the ideal refuge. She required physical strengthening and bracing alongside rest.

Among the hopes that had dissolved was the trust she had placed in Mr. Bell to reveal the simple facts of the family circumstances preceding the tragic accident that claimed Leonards' life. Regardless of how his opinion might have shifted from what Mr. Thornton once held, Margaret had desired his judgment to be rooted in a truthful understanding of her actions and motives. It would have been a source of peace, granting her rest on a point that now threatened to haunt her forever unless she could resolve to stop thinking upon it. Time had rendered explanation impossible save through the lens of Mr. Bell's death. She might submit to being misunderstood, as many did, yet her heart ached with a longing that, years hence, before his passing, he might finally comprehend how deeply she had been tempted. She wished not necessarily to hear the explanation, but to be certain he knew. This wish proved vain, like so many others. Having schooled herself into this conviction, she turned her entire heart and strength to the immediate future, resolving to strive and make the best of the days ahead.

She spent long hours seated upon the beach, staring intently at the waves as they churned perpetually against the pebbly shore, or gazing at the distant heaves sparkling against the sky. She heard the eternal psalm rising continuously, soothing her without her conscious awareness. Listlessly, she sat on the ground, hands clasped around her knees, while her aunt Shaw engaged in minor shopping expeditions and Edith and Captain Lennox rode far and wide across the shore and inland. Nurses sauntering with their charges would pass and repass her, whispering in wonder at what she could possibly find so captivating to gaze upon, day after day. At dinner, Margaret was so silent and absorbed that Edith deemed her moped, welcoming her husband's proposal with great satisfaction that Mr. Henry Lennox be invited to stay at Cromer for a week upon his return from Scotland in October.

Yet, these hours of solitude allowed Margaret to arrange events in their proper order regarding origin and significance, both for her past and her future. Those seaside moments were not wasted; anyone with the perception to read or the care to understand could see the transformation in the look of Margaret's face. Mr. Henry Lennox was profoundly struck by the change.

"The sea has done Miss Hale an immense deal of good, I should fancy," he remarked when she first entered the room after his arrival in their family circle. "She looks ten years younger than she did in Harley Street."

"That's the bonnet I procured for her!" Edith announced triumphantly. "I knew it would suit her the moment I saw it."

"I beg your pardon," Mr. Lennox replied in his characteristic tone, a mixture of half-contempt and indulgence. "But I believe I discern the difference between the charms of a dress and the charms of a woman. No mere bonnet could render Miss Hale's eyes so lustrous yet so soft, or her lips so ripe and red—and her face altogether so full of peace and light. She is like, and yet more," he lowered his voice, "like the Margaret Hale of Helstone."

From this moment, the clever and ambitious man devoted all his energies to winning Margaret. He loved her sweet beauty and perceived the latent sweep of her mind, which he believed could easily be guided to embrace all the objects of his heart. He viewed her fortune merely as a component of her complete and superb character and position, yet he was acutely aware of the social ascent it would immediately afford him, the poor barrister. Eventually, he would earn such success and honors as would enable him to repay her, with interest, that initial advance in wealth he owed to her. He had visited Milton on business concerning her property, returning from Scotland, and with the keen eye of a skilled lawyer, always ready to weigh contingencies, he had observed that additional value accrued yearly to the lands and tenements she owned in that prosperous, expanding town. He was pleased to find that the present relationship of client and legal adviser was gradually superseding the memory of that unlucky, mismanaged day at Helstone. This afforded him unusual opportunities for intimate intercourse with her, beyond those arising from the connection between the families.

Margaret was only too willing to listen as long as he spoke of Milton, though he had seen none of the people she particularly knew. It had been the tone of her aunt and cousin to speak of Milton with dislike and contempt; feelings Margaret was ashamed to recall having expressed and felt upon first moving there. Yet Mr. Lennox surpassed Margaret in his appreciation of the character of Milton and its inhabitants. Their energy, their power, their indomitable courage in struggle and combat; their lurid vividness of existence—captivated and arrested his attention. He was never tired of talking about them, failing to perceive how selfish and material were too many of the ends they proposed as results of their mighty, untiring endeavor, until Margaret, even amidst her gratification, possessed the candor to point this out as the tainting sin within so much that was noble and admirable. Still, when other subjects palled and she offered but short answers to many questions, Henry Lennox discovered that an inquiry into some Darkshire peculiarity of character restored the light to her eye and the glow to her cheek.

Upon returning to town, Margaret fulfilled one of her seaside resolves and took her life into her own hands. Before departing for Cromer, she had been as docile to her aunt's laws as if she were still the frightened little stranger who had cried herself to sleep that first night in the Harley Street nursery. But those solemn hours of thought had taught her that she must one day answer for her own life and what she had done with it. She strove to settle the most difficult problem for a woman: how much must be utterly merged in obedience to authority, and how much might be set apart for freedom in action. Mrs. Shaw was as good-tempered as could be; Edith had inherited this charming domestic quality; Margaret herself likely possessed the worst temper of the three, for her quick perceptions and over-lively imagination made her hasty, and her early isolation from sympathy had bred pride. Yet she possessed an indescribable childlike sweetness of heart that made her manners, even in her rarely wilful moods, irresistible. Now, chastened even by what the world termed her good fortune, she charmed her reluctant aunt into acquiescence with her will. Thus, Margaret secured the acknowledgment of her right to follow her own ideas of duty.

"Only don't be strong-minded," Edith pleaded. "Mamma wants you to have a footman of your own; and I'm sure you're very welcome, for they are great plagues. Only, to please me, darling, don't go and acquire a strong mind; it's the only thing I ask. Footman or no footman, don't be strong-minded."

"Don't be afraid, Edith. I shall faint on your hands at the servants' dinner-time, the very first opportunity; and then, with Sholto playing with the fire and the baby crying, you'll begin to wish for a strong-minded woman, equal to any emergency."

"And you won't grow too good to joke and be merry?"

"Not I. I shall be merrier than I have ever been, now I have my own way."

"And you won't go a figure, but let me buy your dresses for you?"

"Indeed, I intend to buy them for myself. You may come with me if you like; but no one can please me but myself."

"Oh! I was afraid you'd dress in brown and dust-color, merely to show the dirt you'll pick up in all those places. I'm glad you're going to keep one or two vanities, just by way of specimens of the old Adam."

"I am going to be just the same, Edith, if you and my aunt could but imagine so. Only, as I have neither husband nor child to give me natural duties, I must make myself some, in addition to ordering my gowns."

In the family conclave composed of Edith, her mother, and her husband, it was decided that perhaps all these plans would only secure her more firmly to Henry Lennox. They kept her away from other friends who might have been eligible sons or brothers. It was also agreed that she never seemed to take much pleasure in the society of anyone but Henry, outside their own family. Other admirers, attracted by her appearance or the reputation of her fortune, were swept away by her unconscious, smiling disdain into the paths frequented by other beauties less fastidious, or other heiresses with a larger amount of gold. Henry and she grew slowly into closer intimacy, yet neither he nor she were people to brook the slightest notice of their proceedings.
