In the hushed hours before dawn, when the city's rhythms slowed, George Warrington's fingers wandered across the keys of his harpsichord. The instrument, a gift from his mother, had been his constant companion during the long winter months spent in the French fort on the banks of Monongahela. In those isolated days, he had composed a tragedy, a work he titled 'Elegy for a Lost Melody.' Though never performed, it remained a testament to his youthful passion for the dramatic arts. 

As he played, the sounds of the city began to stir – the clang of pots and pans, the murmur of vendors calling out their wares, the distant rumble of horse hooves on cobblestone. George's thoughts turned to Melpomene, the muse of tragedy, whose name he had often invoked in his creative endeavors. His ambition, once as boundless as the ocean, had slowly ebbed with each passing year. Now, as he approached his thirtieth birthday, he found himself drawn to more modest pursuits – translating the odes of Horace, crafting verse in the manner of Waller or Prior. Yet, the memory of his lost tragedy lingered, a bittersweet reminder of his early aspirations.

With the first light of dawn creeping through the windows, George rose from his seat, his joints creaking in protest. He walked to the small library, where his books stood arranged on shelves, their leather bindings worn from years of use. Among them, he found a slim volume of Chaucer's works, the black-letter pages yellowed with age. As he opened the book, a fragment of his tragedy caught his eye – a phrase, a line, a moment of beauty that had eluded him in his earlier attempts to capture it. The memory of his failure was a familiar ache, one he had grown accustomed to over the years. Yet, as he read the words, he felt the sting of regret, the knowledge that his art, like the city outside, was a constantly evolving entity, always slipping away from his grasp.
