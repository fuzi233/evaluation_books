Here is the rewritten chapter of *The Cloister and the Hearth*, preserving its historical fiction tone while refining clarity and emotional depth:

---

Not many days after Margaret Van Eyck’s death, the will was read. To Margaret Brandt, her "dear daughter," she left her house, her sketches, and her paintings—treasures that would one day be worth a fortune. Among them was a diptych Margaret cherished above all others: *A Betrothal*, painted jointly by Jan and Margaret Van Eyck. The solemnity of the ceremony shone in the man’s grave face, the woman’s demure contentment.

Margaret sent word to Reicht Heynes to remain in the house until she could bear to enter it without aching for the voice and face now gone. "Guard the picture in the little cupboard," she instructed—the diptych, fragile as memory itself.

Then Luke Peterson returned, triumphant. Gerard was a monk. *Free at last!*

"Since he can’t wed you, I can," he declared.

Margaret’s eyes flashed. "I have seen him."

"But he’s a friar now."

"He was my husband and my child’s father long before he took vows. And I have seen him."

Luke scowled, baffled. "I’ve a cousin, a lawyer. I’ll ask him—are you wed or single?"

"Ask *me*, not a lawyer. Would you make me the town’s gossip?"

"It’s already gossip without my help."

"Then do not add to it, if you value my respect. Speak of this again, and never come near me."

Luke sighed. "To others, you’re a dove. To me, a tyrant."

"Because you woo me, Luke. Look around—plenty of lasses fairer than I, unspoiled by tears. Choose any. Speak to her, and I’ll speak to her mother. They’ll not refuse you."

Luke reddened. "I see your game. But if I can’t have your heart, I’ll not take your coin. I loved you poor—now you’re rich, you’d rather be a friar’s leman than an honest man’s wife? Then part we here—you to your sorrow, me to my bachelor’s grave."

"Farewell, Luke. And may God forgive you those words."

---

For days, Margaret dreaded and longed for Gerard’s return. *He stays away out of shame—as I would.* Yet surely the news of his son would draw him. But as time passed, suspicion crept in. *What if he stays away on purpose?* What if his brothers in the cloister had warned him never to see her again? The thought hollowed her heart.

Now anger stirred alongside grief. *Did he ever love me? To abandon his child without a word?* Even Luke, rough as he was, showed more care.

Then Giles burst in, roaring. "I’ve done it! Our Gerard is Vicar of Gouda!"

---

The dwarf’s rise at court had been swift. His wit, his voice, his size—all made him a favorite. The young Duchess and her ladies petted him; the court poet buttered his verses with flattery. But Giles’ true triumph came when he challenged the Duke’s giant to a trial of strength—and won.

"Climbed a pole like a squirrel," he crowed to Margaret. "Then wrestled the oaf blindfolded—made him bow to me! After that, none dared cross me."

Now he recounted his audience with the princess:

"I came to her gloomy. ‘What ails thee?’ she asked. ‘At heart,’ said I. ‘In love?’ she teased. The maids giggled. ‘Not so mad,’ I said. ‘I’ve seen women at court.’

"She bade me speak freely. So I did: ‘Princes never keep their word.’

"She stiffened—then melted when I reminded her of Gerard, the fair youth who’d brought a letter years ago. ‘Your brother?’ she gasped, eyeing me head to toe.

"I told her of the promised living, forgotten. ‘My mother said, *See thou to it.*’ She wept. ‘Then thy brother is Vicar of Gouda—so sure as I rule Burgundy.’"

Margaret seized Giles, kissing him. "Bless you! Now we shall see him again!"

---

Yet Gerard did not come. Giles had him cried in Rotterdam—still nothing. Margaret seethed. *He flees me because I live.*

Then a Tergovian brought word: Ghysbrecht Van Swieten had seen Gerard last.

She went to him. The old burgomaster, now feeble, greeted her warmly. "I saw him—not Gerard, but Friar Clement." He recounted their meeting: how Gerard had come to force the deed’s return, yet stayed to save his enemy’s soul.

"‘I forgive thee,’ he said—*he*, whom I wronged most!" Ghysbrecht wept. "A saint."

Margaret, moved, forgave him too. "Help me now. Gerard avoids me—he will lose his living."

Ghysbrecht summoned clerks, sending pleas to every town in Holland. "We *will* find the vicar."

---

But fresh sorrow came. Sybrandt, drunk, fell from a roof, his spine broken. He lay groaning, cursing his fate—and Gerard. "Lift the curse!" he begged Margaret.

She tended him, though he had been her foe. Catherine arrived, heartbroken. "I am punished for deceiving Eli."

Sybrandt’s pleas wore on her: "Find Gerard. He alone can heal me."

Margaret spent freely, hiring searchers—even Luke, who returned, thinner, pleading reconciliation.

"Be my brother," she said wearily. "Help me find my marriage lines."

"I’ll bring them—or him," Luke vowed.

But the months dragged. Gouda’s folk petitioned for a true vicar. Giles’ influence waned. Time was running out.

---

The chapter closes on Margaret’s quiet resolve: *I will not judge him until I hear his own voice.* But the question lingers—where is Gerard? And will he return before it is too late?
