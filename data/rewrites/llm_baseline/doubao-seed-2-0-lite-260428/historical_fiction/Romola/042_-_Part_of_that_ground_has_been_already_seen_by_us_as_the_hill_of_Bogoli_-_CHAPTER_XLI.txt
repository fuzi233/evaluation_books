The crisp Tuscan wind bit at the hill of Bogoli, the same stretch of high ground we have stood on before, and far below, the faint, hazy spires of Florence peeked over the wintry treeline. At last Fra Girolamo Savonarola spoke, his voice low and steady as he bade her rise. “Rise, my daughter. Your servant waits not far off with the mules. It is time we turn our steps back toward Florence.”

Romola pushed herself up from her knees. That long, silent stretch of kneeling had been a quiet, unspoken sacrament for her, sealing the new, tender state of surrendered yearning she had only just stumbled into. The single act of renouncing her desperate plan to flee her husband had bruised her will so thoroughly that she ached for guidance even over the smallest, trivial choices. She lifted the edge of her cowl and spotted Maso and the second Dominican monk, their backs turned a dozen paces off along the hill’s edge to give her privacy. But she said no word to call them, only looked back at Savonarola with a flicker of appeal, as if the order for Maso to turn home must come from him, not from her.

“I will go and call them,” he said, answering her unspoken plea. “And I will commend you to the Brother who travels with me. You seek to place yourself under guidance now, to learn that wisdom which until now has seemed no better than foolishness to you. One of its greatest gates is the sacrament of confession. You need a confessor, my daughter, and I wish to place you in the care of Fra Salvestro, one of the San Marco brethren, a man I trust deeply.”

“I would take no guidance but yours, father,” Romola said, her tone tight with quiet anxiety.

“My daughter, I do not act as a confessor. The vocation laid on me draws me away from offices that would force me into constant close contact with the laity, and distract me from the special duties I owe to all of Florence.”

“Then I shall not be able to speak to you in private? If I waver, if—” Romola cut herself off, her breath catching on a surge of sudden agitation. A sharp alarm pricked at her: what little new strength she had found in her choice to renounce flight would melt away if she lost the immediate, steadying pull of Savonarola’s personal influence.

“If your soul has need of a private word from my lips, you will send notice to me through Fra Salvestro, and I will meet you in the San Marco sacristy or the choir. I will never cease to watch over you. I will instruct my brother well in your case, that he may lead you into the path of labour for the suffering and the hungry, the work you are called to as a daughter of Florence in these hard, needy days. I long to see you stand among the feebler, more ignorant sisters of the city as the apple tree stands among the gnarled wild trees of the forest: your fairness, all your fine natural gifts, will be no more than a lamp through which Divine light may shine all the more purely. I will go now and call your servant.”

When Maso had been sent a little way ahead on the path, Fra Salvestro stepped forward, and Savonarola led Romola to meet him. She had already felt a quiet inward flinch at the thought of submitting to a total stranger as her spiritual guide, but to resist Savonarola’s advice would have been to cling to the very independence she had just sworn to renounce. Every fibre of her mind now leaned toward choosing the hard thing over the easy one. She bowed reverently before Fra Salvestro before she lifted her eyes to look at him full, and when she did, her reluctance curdled into a fluttering, unsteady doubt.

There are men whose very presence wraps you in unforced trust and reverence. There are others to whom you must bring your trust and reverence ready-made, with no help from them. Romola felt that difference flash through her the second Savonarola stepped aside and left her facing Fra Salvestro Maruffi. There was nothing openly repulsive about him: no hint of hypocrisy, no streak of coarse ill manners. His face was handsomer than Savonarola’s, his stance a little taller. He had long served as the trusted confessor for many of Florence’s most elite households, and carried years of experience as a spiritual director. But there was a perpetual, wavering vagueness to his expression, the look of a mind that could never focus fully on one single great emotion or unshakable belief—a flaw that was fatal to any chance of holding influence over a passionate, ardent nature like Romola’s. That vacuous look was not the stamp of insincerity, merely the mark of a shallow soul: the kind of soul that can genuinely strive to fulfill a high, sacred calling, genuinely arrange its features to utter the most sublime of prayers, yet find its muscles twitch and slack against its will, as plain prose forces its way onto the page instead of poetry for a man who has never known the divine frenzy of true conviction.

Fra Salvestro was prone to sudden, vivid visions, apparently sparked by a constitution given to somnambulism. Savonarola believed those visions were supernatural, while Fra Salvestro himself had once resisted that very interpretation, and even rebuked Savonarola for his public prophetic preaching. It was one more proof, if proof was needed, that the greatness of a man can never be measured by his willingness to reject the superstitions of his age. No one could have mistaken which of these two was the great man, and which was the small.

Romola’s shifting feeling toward Fra Salvestro measured that gap perfectly. The hot, angry rebellion she had once felt toward Savonarola was long gone, and she had all but forgotten her old, lingering dread that some narrow religious influence would drag her into the bitter, sour circle of fanatical monkish piety. Now that dread stole over her again, cold as a winter draft. It could not break the strong forward momentum her mind had built with her choice, but it was like grey cloud sliding over the face of sunrise, turning the whole road back toward Florence monotonous and dim. There is no sombre path more testing to the fire of a hard-won renunciation than the path we walk back, after having once resolved to never tread it again.

When they passed through Florence’s city gates, soft light snowflakes drifted down all around them. When they parted at the doors of San Marco, Romola walked hastily away across the familiar stone bridge, and turned in through the great carved doorway of her home on the Via de’ Bardi. Her dark footprints marked clear patterns in the thin new carpet of snow on the threshold, and her damp cowl hung heavy and cold around her face. She climbed the stairs to her chamber, stripped off the coarse grey serge of the pilgrim she had almost become, burned all the farewell letters she had written, put every one of her small precious trifles back in its usual place, unbound her hair, and slipped back into the plain black dress she always wore. Instead of being hours deep into a thrilling, life-altering voyage far away, she was settling back into the same old familiar chair by the window. Snow tapped against the panes, and she was entirely alone.

The dreariness settled thick around her, but her courage burned bright, as bright as a prospector’s who has just spotted the first glint of gold in the dirt. She was going to thread the rest of her life by a new, untested clue. She had poured every last ounce of her will into this act of renunciation. The empty little tabernacle that had once held her father’s lost manuscripts stayed locked, and she set Dino’s worn wooden crucifix on the shelf just outside it. No sound broke the outer monotony of her silent, empty home, until night pressed pale as a white ghost against the frosted windowpanes. It was Christmas Eve, 1494, and it would be the most memorable night of Romola’s whole life.
