The carriage wheels bit into the dust of the road, a rhythmic, grinding percussion that announced the arrival of His Excellency. He rode in splendor, the first personage of the North American colonies, flanked by a guard of dragoons and a loyal, shouting crowd of negroes whose voices rose and fell like a single, chaotic instrument. Inside the ponderous vehicle, the air was thick with the scent of leather and unwashed ambition. Captain Talmadge paced beside the coach, while Major Danvers sat opposite Benjamin Franklin, the former printer's boy.

Franklin was a small, sharp thing, his eyes moving rapidly over the landscape of the carriage interior. He possessed a knowledge of the colony and England that baffled the gentlemen around him—a variety of learning acquired not through the rigid channels of the university, but through the casual friction of life.

"Extraordinary," the General observed, tapping his hat brim against the ceiling. "How a person of such humble origin should acquire such politeness."

Franklin bowed, his movements economical. "Occasional good fortune, sir. I studied their honors' manners to adapt myself. My education... well, my father was in straitened circumstances. I gathered what I could. Nothing like what you have in England."

Braddock erupted in laughter, a sound that shook the carriage frame. "Education! By George, there are gentlemen of the army who cannot spell 'bull' with two b's. I hear the Duke of Marlborough was no special good penman. He thrashed the French soundly, though. No scholar, but effective."

"I am aware of both facts," Franklin replied, his tone flat, devoid of irony.

"Nor is my Duke a scholar," Braddock continued, leaning forward, his face a map of red velvet and arrogance. "You winked at that, Postmaster."

Franklin instantly withdrew the gesture, his eyes clearing to the innocence of a child. "He is no scholar, but he is a match for any French general that ever swallowed the English for fricassee de crapaud. He saved the crown for the best of kings."

From his powdered wig, a halo of starch escaped, framing a face that seemed to absorb the light rather than reflect it. "He is the soldier's best friend," Franklin said, his voice steady. "Enemy of the beggarly red-shanked Scotch rebels and the intriguing Romish Jesuits. Your Royal Highness is not a scholar, but one of the finest gentlemen in the world."

"He is indeed," Franklin added, referencing a review in Hyde Park. "Very fine on horseback."

"You shall drink his health today," Braddock commanded, the weight of command settling heavily on the small man. "Best of masters, best of sons."

"Epaulets are out of my way, sir," Franklin laughed softly. "I live in a Quaker City."

"Every man to his business," Braddock dismissed, swinging his legs. "We encourage you to read. We fight the enemy and govern the country. Look at these roads! How this confounded coach plunges!"

He gestured to the rear. Two negro boys in livery rode alongside, one on a good gelding.

"It is Mr. Washington," Danvers noted.

"I would like him for a corporal of the Horse Grenadiers," Braddock mused. "Good figure on a horse. Knows the country."

"Yes, indeed," Franklin agreed. "Monstrously genteel, considering his opportunities. I should have thought he had the polish of Europe."

"He does his best," Franklin said, watching the stout chief sway in his seat. Braddock was an exemplar of English elegance, swearing at every other word, ignorant of parade save for the merits of a bottle and the looks of a woman. Proud of his no-ancestry, brave as a bulldog, savage and lustful, yet gentle in soft moods. Utterly unread. Believing his country the first in the world and himself as good a gentleman as any in it.

"He was beat at Fort What-d'ye-call-um last year," Franklin remarked, testing the waters. "Down by the Thingamy river. What's the name on it, Talmadge?"

"The Lord knows, sir," Talmadge admitted. "And I dare say the Postmaster knows too, who is laughing at us both."

"Oh, Captain!"

"Caught in a regular trap. Only militia and Indians."

"Good day, Mr. Washington," Braddock called out as the young man approached, followed by natty negro grooms in velvet hunting-caps. "Pretty nag, sir. That was your first affair?"

"That at Fort Necessity? Yes, sir," Washington answered, saluting gravely. "I began ill, sir. Never having been in action until that unlucky day."

"You were all raw levies," Braddock said, dismissing the sentiment. "You should have seen our militia run from the Scotch. You should have had some troops with you."

"Your Excellency knows 'tis my passionate desire to see and serve with them," Washington replied.

"By George, we shall try and gratify you," Braddock promised, rolling the heavy carriage toward Castlewood. Washington asked leave to gallop ahead to announce the arrival.

The progress was glacial. Humble persons invited to meet the Commander-in-Chief found themselves unable to pass the great man, forming a procession in the dusty wake of his wheels. First came Mr. Dinwiddie, Lieutenant-Governor, attended by negro servants and Parson Broadbent, the jolly Williamsburg chaplain. They were joined by little Mr. Dempster, the schoolmaster, in his great Ramillies wig, and Mr. Laws, the judge, with Madam Laws on a pillion behind him, her negro man carrying a box containing her cap, bestriding a mule.

The procession looked ludicrous. Major Danvers and Mr. Franklin saw it and laughed outright, though quietly enough not to disturb the sleeping General inside the lead carriage. There was room for all at Castlewood. Meat, drink, and the best tobacco awaited.

Inside, the honest General required assistance with every dish. He held out his glass constantly, pronouncing Nathan's sangaree excellent. There was cider, ale, brandy, and Bordeaux wine, some brought by Colonel Esmond himself, fit for pontificis coenis, according to little Mr. Dempster.

Parson Broadbent returned the wink, drinking without caring for the Latin. He was a gambling, guzzling divine who had passed time in the Fleet Prison and Newmarket. Sent out to Virginia, he had fought matches of cocks with young George Esmond, hunted roe, and hauled in shad and salmon. It was said he was the midnight poacher on whom Mr. Washington set his dogs, caned by the river-side at Mount Vernon. Broadbent had scrambled to his boat in the dark, emerging two Sundays later with a black eye and a new collar.

All the birds of the Virginia air and fish of the sea lay on Madam Esmond's board. Gumbo was declared perfection—the young Mr. George's black servant had been discovered behind a door with his head in a bowl of the hotch-potch, grimly christened on the spot. Stewed terrapins were worthy of London aldermen, or any duke, or even emperor.

Negro-women possessed a genius for cookery. In Castlewood kitchens, adepts worked under the keen eye of the late and present Madam Esmond. Certain dishes, especially sweets and flan, she prepared herself with neatness and dexterity. Carving several principal pieces, she showed the prettiest round arms and small hands, performing the ancient rite of hospitality not so languid as theirs.

The old law of the table dictated that the mistress press her guests with decent eagerness, knowing culinary secrets and executing carving operations upon fowls, fish, and joints of meat. She cheered her guests to fresh efforts, whispering to Mr. Braddock, "I have kept for your Excellency the jowl of this salmon—I will take no denial!" To Mr. Franklin, she offered water despite the wholesome wine in the cellar. To Mr. Justice, she asked if he loved woodcock pie.

"Because I know who makes the pastry," said Mr. Laws, the judge, with a profound bow. "I wish, madam, we had such a happy knack of pastry at home as you have at Castlewood. I often say to my wife, 'My dear, I wish you had Madam Esmond's hand.'"

"It is a very pretty hand; I am sure others would like it too," said the Postmaster of Boston. Mr. Esmond looked but half-pleased.

"Such a hand for a light pie-crust," continued the Judge. "And my service to you, madam." He thought the widow could not but be propitiated. She said simply that she had lessons when at home in England, that certain dishes her mother taught her were liked by her father and sons. She was very glad if they pleased her company.

More remarks followed. More dishes. Ten times as much meat as was needful. Mr. Washington did not embark in the general conversation much, but he and Mr. Talmadge, Major Danvers, and the Postmaster were deep in talk about roads, rivers, conveyances, sumpter-horses, and artillery trains.
