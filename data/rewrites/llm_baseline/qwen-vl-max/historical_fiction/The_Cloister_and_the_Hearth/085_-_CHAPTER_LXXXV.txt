The sermon had already begun when Margaret stepped into the vast church of St. Laurens. It was a monument still in the making—no church rose in a single year. The side aisles were roofed, but the central nave and chancel remained open to the sky. Pillars stood tall and firm, some freshly whitewashed, arches bold and strong, yet only one window bore glass; the rest were jagged gaps in the stone walls, like wounds left by time. But on this radiant summer afternoon, those imperfections transformed the space into something sublime.

Sunlight poured through the broken openings, fractured into dazzling patterns that danced across the uneven surfaces. Beams sliced through the darkened side aisles like rivers of molten gold, illuminating the white columns with a glow that seemed almost divine. The central aisle shimmered with shifting squares of light and shadow, each patch cooler than any shade had ever been, each gleam like amber fire alive with heaven’s breath. Above, the blue vault of the sky stretched from west to east, so close it felt as if one could reach out and touch it. The women’s sunlit caps formed a sea of white beneath that azure dome, a striking contrast against the boundless sky.

Though the nave was packed, silence reigned. Every soul was held fast by the preacher’s voice—soft, steady, earnest—as he spoke words that carried both weight and grace. Margaret stood transfixed, her heart stirred by the beauty of the place and the solemnity of the moment. She slipped behind a pillar in the north aisle, where she could scarcely catch the words, yet found herself wrapped in a gentle devotional hush. A calmness spread through her like oil smoothing waves—a peace so deep it made her lean back, eyes half-closed, lost in reverie. To be here, in this sacred hush, felt right, even holy.

Then she saw a woman rise from a seat directly opposite, stepping aside to escape the harsh sunlight streaming through the lone glazed window. Without hesitation, Margaret moved swiftly and quietly around the aisle, claiming the spot. Now she stood near a pillar in the south aisle, less than fifty feet from the pulpit. At last, she could hear every word clearly. Her attention, however, was soon caught by something strange: the shadow of a man’s head and shoulders bobbing up and down in a most comical fashion. She fought to suppress a smile—until she turned and saw through the window the source: the sexton, Jorian Ketel, digging in the churchyard.

She watched him for a moment, then suddenly—without warning—the preacher’s voice struck her. Not just a tone, but a *sound*—a particular inflection—that pierced her soul. It resonated within her like a bell rung in an empty hall, shaking her from the inside out. Her hand flew to her chest, startled by the intensity of the sensation. Then, slowly, she turned her gaze toward the pulpit.

The preacher’s back was to her. All she saw was his tonsure, bare and round atop his skull. She sighed—a quiet, defeated sigh. That bald crown contradicted everything her heart had just heard. Yet she leaned forward slightly, eyes lowered, hoping the voice might carry that same note again. It did not come. But the voice itself began to grow upon her. As the preacher warmed, his tones swelled and softened, rising and falling with a rhythm that stirred faint echoes in her memory—echoes of laughter, love, and long-forgotten joy. She would not look up. To do so might shatter the melancholy sweetness filling her.

Then, mid-sentence, the preacher stopped. The silence that followed was sudden, profound. Margaret nearly sighed—had the sermon ended? No. She glanced around. The congregation remained still. But now, many faces turned toward her. She looked sharply behind her—nothing there. Then she noticed the others watching the pulpit. She followed their gazes.

There, in the pulpit, sat the friar—his face frozen in horror. His eyes, naturally large, now bulged unnaturally wide, staring straight at her from a pallid, bloodless face. He looked like a corpse woken from death. Margaret recoiled, cringing, certain something terrible lurked near her. But there was nothing—she was simply at the edge of the crowd.

At once, the church erupted. Figures rose from their seats, leaning forward. Hundreds of agitated faces darted between the friar and Margaret, then back again. The rustling of sunbonnets created a loud whispering sound. Women shrieked, men muttered. And Margaret, seeing all those eyes fixed on her, fled behind the pillar, trembling, casting one terrified glance at the preacher.

That glance—though fleeting—caught the expression on his face: a look carved in stone, etched with recognition. It passed through her like ice water. She gasped, sank onto a pile of wood chips left by the workmen, and buried her face in her hands.

The sermon resumed. She heard the sounds—words, cadence, passion—but they meant nothing. Her mind spun, refusing to settle. Only one thought took root: *He knew me.* That look—the look of recognition—was burning in her memory, sending shivers through her limbs.

The sermon ended at last, not with silence, but with a crescendo of fervent eloquence that lifted the crowd’s spirits, distracting them from the earlier pause, the ghastly stare, the unspoken dread.

Margaret slipped away with the others, joining the flow of people leaving the church. But at the door, she hesitated. Instead of going home, she turned and walked into the churchyard—toward Peter’s grave.

Though poor, she had given her father a slab and a headstone. She knelt beside it, kissed the cold stone, then draped her apron over her head so no one could see her hair. “Father,” she whispered, “you often said I was wading in deep waters. But now I think only God knows how deep they are. I’ll follow that friar to the ends of the earth—but I’ll keep my distance. I’ll make him tell me why he stared at me like a dead man roused from the grave, with no one behind me. Oh, father—you always praised me here. Speak for me there. For I am truly wading in deep waters.”

She sat upon the tombstone, facing the church door, her face hidden. There, in silence, she waited—for the holy man to return.
