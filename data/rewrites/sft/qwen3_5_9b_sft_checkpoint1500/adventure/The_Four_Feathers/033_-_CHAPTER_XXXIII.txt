The words that shattered Ethne's composure were spoken that evening. She retreated to her farmhouse, sinking into the parlor where she lit the fire against the chill of the summer air. Sitting before the bright coals, she possessed a stillness that betrayed the tension within her. The moment she had waited for with desperate longing—the half-hour when Harry Feversham would return—had arrived and passed. Now, alone in her secluded hill village, cut off from the world, she felt a loneliness more profound than any she had endured since Willoughby sailed away months ago. Every night since his arrival, she had lived for those fleeting minutes of reunion. They were gone. Yet, deep within her nature, she had anticipated this hollow ache. She knew that sending Harry away meant a terrible hour would follow, one where her heart would scream for him to stay. But she forced herself to remember what she had always known: this pain was temporary. Tomorrow, she would recall today's parting alongside the separation at Lennon House, realizing how insignificant this moment truly was. As the fire crackled, she wondered what Harry would do now that he had returned, constructing a vision of his glorious future in her mind. Beside her, Dermod's ancient collie nudged her hand, sensing her sorrow with a faithful instinct. Ethne rose, cradling the old dog's head in her hands and kissing him. He was dying soon, leaving her behind, and she feared the long, silent years that might stretch before she finally faced death itself.

A knock at the door interrupted her thoughts. A servant announced that Colonel Durrance was waiting. "Yes," Ethne replied, rising to greet him. She did not shy away from her role; she stepped out from her private chamber of grief the moment he was summoned. She conversed with her visitor as if nothing extraordinary had occurred an hour prior, discussing their impending marriage and the reconstruction of Lennon House. It was difficult, but she had grown accustomed to burdens. However, that night, Durrance made her path steeper. After the maid served tea, he requested that she play the *Musoline Overture* for him. "Not tonight," Ethne lied immediately, feigning fatigue. "I am rather tired." But almost instantly, she changed her mind. Ethne resolved that she would not avoid duty, however small, for the trivial details of daily life demanded the utmost vigilance. "I believe I can manage the overture," she said with a faint smile, retrieving her violin. She played the piece from start to finish. Durrance stood at the window, his back to her, listening intently. When the final note faded, he approached her side.

"I was rather a brute," he murmured quietly, "to ask you to play that overture tonight." "I wasn't anxious to play," she answered softly, setting the instrument down. "I know," Durrance replied. "But I was anxious to discover something, and I knew no better way." Ethne turned to him, her face pale with alarm. "What do you mean?" she asked, her voice trembling with suspense. "You are rarely caught off guard," Durrance continued calmly. "Except, perhaps, when you play. Once before, when you performed this overture, you were unguarded. I realized that if I could induce you to play it again tonight—the piece once strummed in a dim café at Wadi Halfa—I would catch you off balance." His words stole her breath. She rose slowly, staring at him with wide, terrified eyes. He couldn't know. It was impossible. He didn't know. Yet, Durrance pressed on gently. "Well? Did you take back your feather? The fourth one?" These were the incredible words. Spoken with a strange, gentle smile, they seemed to echo in a vacuum. It took Ethne a long time to comprehend their reality, doubting first if her overstrung nerves were deceiving her. But he repeated the question, and disbelief vanished. "Who told you of any fourth feather?" she whispered. "Trench," Durrance answered smoothly. "I met him at Dover. He only mentioned the fourth feather to me because I already knew of the first three. If I hadn't known of the three, I wouldn't have recognized him when he landed at Dover, nor would I have asked, 'Where is Harry Feversham?' Knowledge of the three feathers was sufficient." "How do you know?" Ethne cried, a feeling of despair washing over her, and stepping closer, he gently held her arm. "Since I know," he protested, "does it matter how I know? I have known for a long time, ever since Captain Willoughby brought the first feather to The Pool. I waited to reveal this knowledge until Harry Feversham returned, and he did return today." Ethne sank back into her chair, stunned. Her secret, so carefully guarded, had been exposed for a full year without her knowledge. Even amidst her confusion, she gathered her wits, speaking of inconsequential matters to buy time. "Were you in the church? Did you hear us on the steps? Did you meet him as he rode away?" "None of those guesses are correct," Durrance smiled. Ethne had struck the right chord to delay the inevitable decision. Since becoming blind, Durrance had cultivated a specific pride: the quickness of his perception. It delighted him to make discoveries no one expected a blind man to achieve, and to explain the logical steps leading to them. "None of your conjectures is right, Ethne," he said, practically inviting her to interrogate him. "Then how did you find out?" "I knew from Trench that Harry would arrive soon. Later, I passed the church and heard your collie bark at me, confirming you were inside. But a saddled horse was tied by the gate, meaning someone from outside the village was present. By getting you to play, I identified the rider." "Yes," Ethne murmured, barely hearing him. Then, regaining her composure, she spoke firmly. "You went away to Wiesbaden for a year, just after Captain Willoughby came. Was that why?" "We could not sustain the game of pretenses we were playing," Durrance explained gently. "You pretended to care nothing for Harry Feversham, not minding if he lived or died. I pretended not to realize that, above all else, you loved him. Eventually, one of us would have broken down. I dared not fail, nor did you. I could not let you, who said 'Two lives must not be spoilt because of me,' spend a year believing two lives had been ruined. Nor could you let me, who said 'Marriage between a blind man and a woman requires more than friendship,' remain ignorant that on one side, there was only friendship. We were too close to failure, so I departed." "You did not fail," Ethne said quietly. "It was only I who failed." She berated herself bitterly. She had sworn to shield this man from knowledge that would crown his misfortunes, yet she had failed. He had protected her from understanding her own failure, succeeding where she had stumbled. It was not merely humiliation that troubled her—that the man she thought she had deceived had deceived her—but the realization that her failure had robbed him of his final chance at happiness. That was the sting. "It was not your fault," he reassured her. "Twice, you slipped, but the proof was not revealed then. When you played the *Musoline Overture* previously, on the night Willoughby brought you news, I simply absorbed the happiness that inspired your playing. Do not blame yourself; instead, rejoice that I have discovered the truth." "Rejoice?" she exclaimed. "Yes, for my sake, rejoice." As she gazed at him in wonder, he continued, "Two lives should not be spoiled because of you. If you had your way, if I had not found out, not two but three lives would have been ruined because of your loyalty." "Three?" "Yours. Yes—yours, Feversham's, and mine. Keeping the pretense in Devonshire was hard enough. Admit it, Ethne! When I traveled to London to see my oculist, it was a relief; it gave you a pause, a respite to drop the act and be yourself. It could not have lasted long even in Devonshire. Imagine living under the same roof, seeing each other hourly, with no doctor's visits to distract us. The truth would eventually surface. Gradually, suspicion piling on suspicion, or suddenly, in one terrible flash. But it would have become undeniable. And then, Ethne? What then? You sought a compensation, wishing to make up for the loss of what I love—my career, the army, the exotic service. A cruel compensation would have been sitting before you, knowing you had married a cripple out of pity, thereby crippling yourself and forfeiting the happiness rightfully yours. Where as now—" "Whereas now?" "I remain your friend, which I would prefer to being your unloved husband," he said softly. Ethne offered no reply. The choice had been removed from her hands. "You sent Harry away this afternoon," Durrance noted. "You said goodbye to him twice." At the word "twice," Ethne lifted her head, but before she could speak, Durrance clarified: "Once in the church, again upon your violin."
