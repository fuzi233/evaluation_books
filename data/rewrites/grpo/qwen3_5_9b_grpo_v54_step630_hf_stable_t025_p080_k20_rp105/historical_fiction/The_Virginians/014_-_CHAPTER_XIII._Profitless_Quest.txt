With every mile Harry Warrington journeyed north toward Pennsylvania, the reports of the British defeat swelled in magnitude and grim detail. The two renowned regiments, veterans of Scotland and the Continent, had fled from an enemy that seemed almost invisible. Their celebrated discipline and valor had crumbled before a ragged band of savages and a handful of French infantry. The expedition's commander, a man of noted bravery, had ridden four times into the jaws of death; his horse shot beneath him, twice wounded, and finally mortally struck, dying three days after the battle. Harry's informants painted the scene with harrowing clarity: the river crossing, the long, exposed advance through the wilderness, the futile frontal assault, and the artillery's failure to clear the path. Then came the ambush—fire erupting from behind every bush and tree, a murderous fusillade that claimed at least half the force. Yet, Harry learned, not all perished. One aide-de-camp, a Virginian gentleman, lay feverish and exhausted at Dunbar's camp.

Which one? Harry hurried to the encampment. There, stretched in a tent, he found George Washington, not his brother. A sharper pain than the fever gripped Washington when he saw Harry, for he could offer no news of George. He dared not speak the full truth. For three days after the fight, his duty had bound him to the General's side. On the fatal ninth of July, he had seen George march forward with orders from the chief, never to return. After Braddock himself died, the aide had risked everything to retrace his steps to the field. The remaining corpses had been stripped and mutilated beyond recognition. Washington had buried one body, convinced it was George Warrington's. His own illness, perhaps exacerbated by the anguish of that desperate search, had nearly claimed him.

"Ah, George! If you had loved him, you would have found him dead or alive!" Harry cried, refusing to accept the news. He demanded to go to the ground himself. With money procured, he hired guides and forded the river at the army's crossing point. He traversed the dreadful field from end to end. The Indians were gone, but birds of prey fed on the mangled, festering carcasses. Save for his grandfather, who lay calm with a sweet smile, Harry had never seen the face of Death so plainly. The horror of the mutilation turned his stomach; the vacant woods and rotting bodies offered no comfort. He considered going unarmed, bearing a white flag, to the French fort where the victors had retreated, but his guides refused. "Keep your hair for your lady mother, my young gentleman," one warned. "'Tis enough that she loses one son in this campaign."

Upon returning to Dunbar's encampment, it was Harry's turn to succumb to the fever. Delirium seized him, and he lay in a tent and on a bed from which his friend had recently risen convalescent. For days, he drifted in and out of consciousness, unaware of his watchers. Poor Dempster, who had nursed him through previous illnesses, feared the widow would lose both her children. But the fever eventually broke. Harry rallied enough to mount his horse, escorted home by Mr. Washington and Dempster. They approached the gates of Castlewood with heavy hearts.

A servant had preceded them. First came Mrs. Mountain and her little daughter, Fanny, welcoming Harry with tears and embraces, yet offering Mr. Washington no more than a cold nod. The little girl's approach shattered the Colonel; she came up to him, hands clasped behind her back, asking, "Why have you not brought George back too?" Harry did not hear. The sobs and caresses of his nurse kept him from listening to Fanny's cruel question.

Dempster was received graciously by the ladies. "Whatever could be done, we know you would do, Mr. Dempster," Mrs. Mountain said, taking his hand. She instructed Fanny to curtsey to him and be grateful to their benefactors, then asked if the Colonel would take refreshment.

Mr. Washington, however, had ridden enough. "The time to feed my horse, and a glass of water for myself, and I will trouble Castlewood hospitality no further," he declared.

"Hush! Your mother won't see him, child," Mrs. Mountain whispered urgently.

"Not see George? Why, he is like a son of the house," Harry protested.

"She had best not see him. I don't meddle any more in family matters," Mrs. Mountain replied sharply. "But when the Colonel's servant rode in saying you were coming, Madam Esmond left this room, where she was reading Drelincourt, and said she felt she could not see Mr. Washington. Will you go to her?"

Harry took his friend's arm, excused himself to the Colonel, and left the parlour. He hastened across the corridor, averting his head from the door of his brother's room, which he dreaded to look upon. As he reached it, Madam Esmond emerged, folding him to her heart and leading him inside. A settee sat by the bed, and a book of psalms lay open on the coverlet. The room was exactly as George had left it.

"My poor child! How thin thou art grown—how haggard you look!" she exclaimed, her voice trembling. "Never mind. A mother's care will make thee well again. 'Twas nobly done to go and brave sickness and danger in search of your brother. Had others been as faithful, he might be here now. Never mind, my Harry; our hero will come back to us—I know he is not dead. One so good, so brave, so gentle, and so clever as he was, I know is not lost to us altogether." Perhaps Harry thought within himself that his mother had not always spoken of her eldest son with such reverence. "Dry up thy tears, my dear! He will come back to us, I know he will." When Harry pressed her for a reason, she claimed she had seen her father two nights running in a dream, who had told her her boy was a prisoner among the Indians.

Madam Esmond's grief had not prostrated her as Harry's had; instead, it had animated her. Her eyes were eager, her countenance angry and revengeful. Harry marveled at her condition. But when he besought her to welcome George Washington, who had accompanied him, the lady's excitement turned painful. "I should shudder at touching his hand," she declared. "Mr. Washington has taken my son from me; I cannot sleep under the same roof with him."

"He gave me his bed when I was ill, mother; and if our George is alive, how has George Washington a hand in his death?" Harry cried in bewilderment.

"If your brother returns, as return he will, it will not be through Mr. Washington's help," she insisted. "He neither defended George on the field, nor would he bring him out of it."

"But he tended me most kindly in my fever," Harry interjected. "He was yet ill when he gave up his bed to me, thinking only of his friend, when any other man would have thought only of himself."

"A friend! A pretty friend!" she sneered. "Of all his Excellency's aides-de-camp, my gentleman is the only one who comes back unwounded. The brave and noble fall, but he, to be sure, is unhurt. I confide my boy to him, the pride of my life, whom he will defend with his, forsooth! And he leaves my George in the forest, and brings me back himself! Oh, a pretty welcome I must give him!"

"No gentleman," Harry cried warmly, "was ever refused shelter under my grandfather's roof."

"Oh no—no gentleman!" the little widow exclaimed. "Let us go down, if you like, son, and pay our respects to this one. Will you please to give me your arm?" Taking an arm that could barely support her, she walked down the broad stairs into the apartment where the Colonel sat.

She made a ceremonious curtsey, extending a small hand that rested briefly in his. "I wish that our meeting had been happier, Colonel Washington," she said.

"You do not grieve more than I do that it is otherwise, madam," the Colonel replied.

"I might have wished that the meeting had been spared, that I might not have kept you from friends whom you are naturally anxious to see—that my boy's indisposition had not detained you. Home and his good nurse Mountain, and his mother and our good Doctor Dempster, will soon restore him. 'Twas scarce necessary, Colonel, that you, who have so many affairs on your hands, military and domestic, should turn doctor too."

"Harry was ill and weak, and I thought it was my duty to ride by him," the Colonel faltered.

"You yourself, sir, have gone through the fatigues and dangers of the campaign in the most wonderful manner," the widow said, curtseying again, her black eyes impenetrable.

"I wish to Heaven, madam, some one else had come back in my place!"

"Nay, sir, you have ties which must render your life more than ever valuable and dear to you, and duties to which, I know, you must be anxious to betake yourself. In our present deplorable state of doubt and distress, Castlewood can be a welcome place to no stranger, much less to you, and so I know, sir, you will be for leaving us ere long. And you will pardon me if the state of my own spirits obliges me for the most part to keep my chamber. But my friends here will bear you company as long as you favour us, whilst I nurse my poor Harry upstairs. Mountain, you will have the cedar-room on the ground-floor ready for Mr. Washington, and anything in the house is at his command. Farewell, sir. Will you be pleased to present my compliments to your mother, who will be thankful to have her son safe and sound out of the war—as also to my young friend Martha Custis, to whom and to whose children I wish every happiness. Come, my son!" With these words and another freezing curtsey, the pale little woman retreated, staring steadily at the Colonel, who stood dumb on the floor.

Though Madam Esmond's belief in her son's safety seemed absolute, the house of Castlewood remained sad and gloomy. She forbade mourning for herself and family, yet her heart was in black, whatever resolute face she wore before the world. To look for her son was to hope against hope. No authentic account of his death had arrived, and no one had seen him fall; hundreds more had been struck down that day with no eyes to behold their last pangs. A fortnight after the defeat, when Harry was absent on his quest, George's servant, Sady, reappeared wounded and maimed at Castlewood. He could give no coherent account of the battle, only of his flight from the center where he was with the baggage. He had no news of his master since the morning of the action. For many days, Sady lurked in the negro quarters, away from the sight of Madam Esmond, whose anger he dared not face. Her neighbors spoke of her as laboring under a delusion. So strong was it that there were times when Harry and the other members of the little Castlewood family were almost brought to share in it. It seemed nothing strange to her that her father, from another world, should promise her her son's life. In this world or the next, that family must be of consequence, she thought. Nothing had ever happened to her sons—no accident, no fever, no important illness—but she had a prevision of it. She could enumerate half a dozen instances, which her household was obliged to confirm, of how, when anything had happened to the boys at ever so great a distance, she had known of their mishap and its consequences. No, George was not dead; George was a prisoner among the Indians; George would come back and rule over Castlewood; as sure as sure as his Majesty would send a great force from home to recover the tarnished glory of the British arms and drive the French out of the Americas.

As for Mr. Washington, she would never behold him again with her own goodwill. He had promised to protect George with his life. Why was her son gone and the Colonel alive? How dared he to face her after that promise, and appear before a mother without her son? She trusted she knew her duty. She bore illwill to no one, but as an Esmond, she had a sense of honor, and Mr. Washington had forfeited hers in letting her son out of his sight. He had to obey superior orders? Psha! A promise was a promise. He had promised to guard George's life with his own, and where was her boy? And was not the Colonel sound and safe? Do not tell me that his coat and hat had shots through them! This was her answer to another humble plea in Mr. Washington's behalf. Could she not go into the study instantly and fire two shots with her papa's pistols through her paduasoy skirt—and should she be killed? She laughed at the notion of death resulting from any such operation, nor was her laugh very pleasant to hear. The satire of people who have little natural humor is seldom good sport for bystanders. I think dull men's facetiae are mostly cruel.

So, if Harry wanted to meet his friend, he had to do so in secret, at court-houses, taverns, or various places of resort; or in their little towns, where the provincial gentry assembled. No man of spirit, she vowed, could meet Mr. Washington after his base desertion of her family. She was exceedingly excited when she heard that the Colonel and her son had absolutely met. What a heart must Harry have to give his hand to one whom she considered little better than George's murderer! For shame to say so! For shame upon you, ungrateful boy, forgetting the dearest, noblest, most perfect of brothers, for that tall, gawky, fox-hunting Colonel, with his horrid oaths!
