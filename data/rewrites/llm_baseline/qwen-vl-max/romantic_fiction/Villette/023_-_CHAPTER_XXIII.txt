To wonder sadly? No—something new stirred within me, pushing sorrow aside for a time. Imagine a hidden valley, cradled deep in the forest’s quiet, cloaked in mist and shadow: its grass damp, its plants pale and dew-laden. Then, one day, a storm or an axe opens a break in the ancient oaks. The wind rushes in; sunlight spills down. That cold, dim hollow transforms into a cup of radiant light, filled with summer’s golden glow and the blue brilliance of a sky it had never known before.

A new belief took root in me—the belief that happiness was possible.

It had been three weeks since the incident in the garret, and now I held five letters from him—each sealed with the same clear wax, written in the same steady hand, brimming with warmth and comfort. At the time, they felt like life itself poured into words—like nectar drawn from a sacred spring. Years later, when I reread them, they seemed gentle, pleasant, shaped by someone content and at peace. In the last two, there were lines that flickered between playfulness and tenderness—“by _feeling_ touched, but not subdued.” Time, dear reader, had softened their edge, mellowed them into something mild and sweet. But when I first read them, fresh from his hand, they burned like wine from heaven—something only Hebe might serve, and even gods would savor.

Does the reader remember what I said earlier? Might they wonder how I replied to these letters—whether I answered with reason’s strict caution, or with the full surge of emotion?

Truthfully, I chose both. I served two masters. I bowed to Reason in one chamber, then turned my heart toward another altar. I wrote two replies—one for myself, one for Graham.

First, I cast Reason out. Feeling and I locked the door behind her, barred it tight, and sat down together. We spread paper, dipped our pen, and poured out our hearts without restraint. We wrote of love that clung close, of gratitude that ran deep—of a bond so strong it wanted to take on every pain in his life, to shield him from every storm, to carry away every lightning strike. Once, let me say this clearly—I scorn any whisper of “warmer feelings.” Women do not entertain such things when, from the very start, they’ve never doubted that to fall into them would be sheer folly. Love is never launched unless hope has already risen above its troubled waters. So when I wrote, I confessed a devotion that longed to absorb all sorrow, to protect him with fierce care. It was honest, raw, and real.

But just as I finished, the bolts gave way. Reason burst back in—vigorous, sharp, unyielding. She snatched the pages, read them, sneered, erased, tore them up, rewrote, folded, sealed, and sent off a short, firm letter of only one page. And she was right.

I did not live only on letters. I was visited, cared for. Every week, I was taken to La Terrasse. Dr. Bretton made sure I knew why: “To keep the nun away,” he said. “I mean to stop her from claiming you. I dislike her deeply—mostly because of that cold face-cloth and those grey eyes. As soon as I heard about them, I was repelled. I want to see who’s cleverer—her or me. I wish she’d come again while I’m here—but she never does.” In short, he saw me as a patient, and treated me with both medical skill and natural kindness—a course of warm, attentive care.

One evening in early December, I walked alone in the carré. It was six o’clock. The classroom doors were shut, but inside, students roared in playful chaos. The carré was dark, save for a red glow beneath the stove. Frost covered the glass doors and windows, and here and there, starlight pierced the white veil, glittering like scattered diamonds. It was a clear night, though moonless. That I dared walk alone in such darkness showed my nerves were healing. I thought of the nun, but no longer feared her—not even when the staircase rose behind me, leading upward through blackness to the haunted attic. Still, my heart fluttered when suddenly I heard breathing and rustling. Turning, I saw a deeper shadow in the steps—a shape moving downward. It paused near the classroom door, then glided past me. At that moment, the distant doorbell rang.

Sounds stir feelings. This figure was too low and round to be my gaunt nun—it was only Madame Beck on duty.

“Mademoiselle Lucy!” Rosine burst in, lamp in hand, from the corridor. “They’re waiting for you in the salon.”

Madame saw me, I saw Madame, Rosine saw both—yet no one recognized the other. I hurried to the salon. There I found whom I expected—Dr. Bretton—in evening dress.

“The carriage is at the door,” he said. “My mother sent it to take you to the theatre. She was going herself, but someone arrived, so she said, ‘Take Lucy instead.’ Will you go?”

“Now? I’m not dressed,” I said, looking down at my plain merino gown.

“You have half an hour. I meant to tell you sooner, but I decided only at five when I heard there’d be a true performance—by a great actress.”

He named her—and the name sent a shiver through me. Once, it could stir whole continents. Now, it rests in silence. She is gone—buried in night and memory. But then, her star blazed at its height, bright and blazing.

“I’ll go—I’ll be ready in ten minutes,” I vowed, and darted off, not once doubting what perhaps crosses your mind now: that going anywhere with Graham without Mrs. Bretton might be improper. I couldn’t have imagined such a thought—let alone voiced it—without feeling shame that would burn through me. Besides, my godmother, knowing her son and knowing me, would never have worried over such a thing. She’d as soon chaperone a brother and sister.

No grand attire was needed. My dull crape would do. I searched the great oak wardrobe in the dormitory, where forty dresses hung. But changes had been made—some garments moved to the garret, including my crape. I must fetch it. I took the key and climbed upstairs, fearless, almost careless. I unlocked the door and stepped in.

The reader may believe it or not—but as I entered, the garret was not entirely dark. From one corner, a solemn light glowed—like a star, yet broader. It lit the deep alcove and part of the tarnished scarlet curtain. Then, silently, it vanished—along with the curtain and alcove. That end of the room became pitch black. I didn’t investigate. I had no time, no will. I grabbed my dress—hanging near the door—and fled, locking the door with trembling hands, rushing down to the dormitory.

But I shook too much to dress myself. My fingers trembled. I called Rosine and offered her a bribe. She liked bribes, so she helped—plaiting my hair neatly, straightening my lace collar, tying my ribbon precisely. She worked like the tidy Phillis she could be when she wished. After handing me gloves and handkerchief, she took the candle and guided me downstairs. Then I realized—I’d forgotten my shawl. She ran back to get it, and I stood with Dr. John in the vestibule, waiting.

“What’s this, Lucy?” he asked, looking down at me closely. “You’re excited again. Ha! Is it the nun?”

I denied it sharply. I resented being suspected of another vision. He remained doubtful.

“She was here,” he insisted. “Her presence leaves a mark—on the eyes, on the expression. You can’t mistake it.”

“She wasn’t,” I repeated—because I truly believed she hadn’t been.

“The signs are there,” he said. “That pale look, that ‘raised’ gaze the Scots call it.”

I finally told him what I’d actually seen. Of course, he dismissed it as mere illusion—nervous disorder. I didn’t believe him, but I didn’t argue. Doctors are so certain, so fixed in their materialist views.

Rosine returned with the shawl. I was bundled into the carriage.

The theatre was packed—every seat filled. Royalty and nobility were there. Palaces and hotels emptied their guests into the hushed tiers. I felt deeply honored to be among them. I longed to see the woman whose fame had stirred such expectation. Would she live up to it? With intense curiosity, with stern focus, I waited.

She appeared at nine that December night—rising above the horizon. She could still shine with a pale, noble grandeur, but her star was nearing its end. Close up, she was a ruin—hollow, half-destroyed—an orb dying, half lava, half flame.

I’d heard her called “plain”—expected angularity, harshness, pallor. What I saw was the ghost of a queen—Vashti, once radiant as daylight, now faded like twilight, consumed like wax in fire.

At first, I thought she was merely a woman—though an extraordinary one—moving with grace and power before the crowd. Then I realized my mistake. Something neither human nor divine dwelled in her. In each eye sat a devil.

These forces drove her through the tragedy, sustaining her frail body. As the drama intensified, they shook her wildly—passions of the pit tearing her apart. They wrote HELL across her proud brow. They tuned her voice to torment. They twisted her regal face into a demonic mask. She stood as Hate, Murder, Madness incarnate.

It was astonishing—a revelation.

It was horrifying—immoral.

Swordsmen bleeding on sand, bulls goring horses—these were gentler sights than Vashti torn by seven devils. Devils screaming, rending their prison, refusing exorcism.

Suffering had struck her. She stood before the audience neither yielding nor enduring—nor even resenting it. She was locked in battle, rigid in defiance. She wore no costume, but draped in pale, sculptural folds. A background of crimson set her apart—white as alabaster, silver, death itself.

Where was the artist of Cleopatra? Let him come and study this. Let him seek the flesh, the muscle, the blood he adored. Let materialists come and look.

I said she does not resent grief. No—such weakness would lie. To her, pain becomes a thing to fight, to tear apart. Scarce a substance herself, she grapples with abstractions. Before calamity, she is a tigress—ripping her sorrows, shredding them in fury. Pain brings no wisdom—tears no harvest. Death, sickness—she sees them as rebels. Wicked, perhaps, but also strong. Her strength has mastered Beauty, subdued Grace, bound them at her side—captives fair and docile. Even in frenzy, her movements are imperial, regal. Her loose hair, flying in rage or revel, remains angelic, glowing under a halo. Fallen, rebellious, banished—she remembers the heavens she defied. Heaven’s light follows her exile, revealing the desolation beyond.

Place Cleopatra—or any soft creature—before her. Watch her slice through the mass like Saladin’s scimitar through a cushion. Let Rubens rise from the grave, bring his army of plump women—Moses’ rod could part the sea, drown them all in a wave.

Vashti was not good, I was told—and I saw she was not. Though a spirit, she came from Tophet. Yet if such unholy force can rise from below, might not equal holiness descend from above?

What did Dr. Graham think?

For long stretches, I forgot to watch him. The genius of the performance pulled me from my usual self—like a sunflower turning from the south to a fierce, red, comet-like light. I’d seen acting before—but never anything like this. Never anything that silenced desire, stunned hope, surpassed imagination. Instead of teasing what might be done, it revealed what *was*—a river swollen with winter thunder, carrying the soul like a leaf down its steel-edged current.

Miss Fanshawe, ever wise, said Dr. Bretton was serious, impassioned, too grave, too sensitive. I never saw him that way. His nature was not contemplative, nor sentimental. He was impressionable—as water is—but not easily shaped. The breeze moved him, the sun warmed him—fire could not brand him, metal could not carve him.

Dr. John could think—well. But he was more action than thought. He could feel—vividly—but not with passion. Bright, soft influences pleased him—like rose and silver, pearl and purple coloring summer clouds. But storms, wildness, danger, flame—he had no sympathy for them. When I looked at him again, I found amusement and understanding. He watched Vashti not with awe, nor reverence, nor fear—but with keen curiosity. Her agony didn’t move him. Her cry—worse than a shriek—barely stirred him. Her fury revolted him slightly, but not with horror. Cool Englishman! He watched the Pythian madness of the night as calmly as cliffs overlook the Channel tide.

Seeing his face, I longed to know his thoughts. Finally, I asked.

“How do you like Vashti?” I said.

“Hm-m-m,” he murmured—soft, but full of meaning. Then a strange smile played on his lips—critical, almost indifferent. Perhaps for minds like his, empathy was numb.

In a few crisp words, he gave his judgment—not as an artist, but as a man. It was a verdict of condemnation.

That night was already marked in my life—not with white, but with a deep-red cross. But I had not finished with it. More memories were to be inscribed in indelible ink.

Near midnight, as the tragedy reached its climax, the house held its breath. Even Graham bit his lip, furrowed his brow, sat motionless. The entire theatre was silent. All eyes focused on one point: the white figure slumped on a chair, trembling in her final struggle—fighting her worst enemy, the one she could not defeat. Only her gasps, her moans, her defiant breaths broke the silence. An overwhelming will convulsed a failing body, forcing it to resist death inch by inch, selling every drop of blood, fighting every faculty—determined to see, to hear, to breathe, to live, even after death whispered, “Thus far, and no farther.”

Then—behind the scenes—a rustle, a stir. Feet ran. Voices spoke. What was it? the house demanded.

A flame. A smell of smoke.

“Fire!” echoed through the gallery. “Fire!” screamed, repeated, roared. Panic erupted—rushing, crushing, blind, selfish, cruel chaos.

And Dr. John? Reader, I see him still—calm, courageous, serene.

“Lucy will stay seated,” he said, glancing down at me with the same quiet strength I’d seen beside his mother’s hearth. Yes, I would have stayed—even under falling rocks. But instinct kept me still. I wouldn’t move to burden him, defy his will, or distract him.

We were in the stalls. For moments, we were trapped in terrible pressure.

“How terrified the women are!” he said. “But if men weren’t nearly as afraid, order might hold. This is shameful. I see fifty selfish beasts. If I were near, I’d knock down every one. Some women are braver than some men. Look—Good God!”

As he spoke, a young girl clinging quietly to a gentleman before us was violently pushed from his arms and thrown underfoot. She vanished in seconds. Graham lunged forward. He and the older man—strong, though grey-haired—pushed back the crowd. Her head and long hair fell over his shoulder. She seemed unconscious.

“Trust her to me,” said Dr. John. “I’m a doctor.”

“If you have no lady with you, be it so,” the man replied. “Hold her—I’ll make a path. We must get her air.”

“I have a lady,” Graham said. “But she won suffice.”

He summoned me with his eyes. We were separated. Determined to reach him, I wormed through the living wall, crawling where I couldn’t climb or step.

“Hold onto me—don’t let go,” he said. I obeyed.

Our guide was strong and skilled. He cut through the throng like a wedge. With effort, he broke through the suffocating mass—and brought us to the freezing night.

“You’re English!” he said, turning to Dr. Bretton.

“English. And I speak to a countryman?” Graham replied.

“Right. Wait two minutes while I find my carriage.”

“Papa, I’m not hurt,” a girlish voice said. “Am I with papa?”

“You’re with a friend. Your father is near.”

“Tell him I’m not hurt—just my shoulder. Oh, my shoulder! They trod here.”

“Dislocation, perhaps,” muttered the doctor. “Let’s hope nothing worse. Lucy, help me.”

I assisted as he adjusted her position, wrapped her gently. She stifled a moan, lying quietly in his arms.

“She’s very light,” Graham said. “Like a child.” He leaned close. “Is she a child, Lucy? Did you notice her age?”

“I am not a child,” she said firmly. “I am seventeen.”

“Tell papa to come—I’m anxious.”

The carriage arrived. Her father took her from Graham. But during the transfer, she winced, moaned.

“My darling!” the father said tenderly. Then to Graham: “You said you’re a doctor?”

“I am—Dr. Bretton, of La Terrasse.”

“Good. Will you step into my carriage?”

“My own is here. I’ll find it and follow.”

“Then please come with us.” He named his address: “Hôtel Crécy, Rue Crécy.”

We followed. The carriage sped. I and Graham sat in silence. It felt like an adventure.

After some delay finding our own vehicle, we reached the hotel about ten minutes later. It was a foreign-style hotel—a collection of homes, not an inn—a tall, stately building with a vaulted arch leading into a square courtyard.

We alighted, climbed a wide public staircase, and stopped at Numéro 2 on the second floor. The first floor housed a “prince Russe,” Graham informed me. Ringing the bell at a massive door, we were admitted to elegant apartments. Announced by a servant in livery, we entered a drawing-room with a roaring English fire and foreign mirrors gleaming on the walls. Near the hearth stood a small group: a slight figure in a deep armchair, women attending, the iron-grey gentleman watching anxiously.
