“When captains bold, whom death could not daunt,
Marched to the siege of Gaunt’s proud town,
They mustered soldiers two and three—
But foremost in the fray was Mary Ambree.
When brave Sir John Major fell by her side,
Her true love, joy, and heart’s delight,
Slain by treachery most cruel and vile,
Then vowed she’d avenge him, fair Mary Ambree.”

—Old Ballad, A.D. 1584.

One last glance at the golden sea, the golden evenings, along the shore of New Granada, on the Spanish Main.

The bay of Santa Marta shimmered under the land breeze like a sheet of molten fire. The dense forests sparkled with countless fireflies. A lazy mist curled around the inner hills, glowing gold in the fading light; high above, nineteen thousand feet in the sky, the peak of Horqueta pierced the heavens, rose-red against the deep blue vault. As dusk deepened, the rosy glow faded into dull lead, but only for a moment. Stars blinked into view one by one, and Venus, like a second moon, bathed the eastern snows in gold and cast a long, rippling line of yellow light across the water. Everywhere was splendor, richness, beauty. Was it any wonder that the earth beneath such skies held treasures as dazzling as its surface? That every stream, every bank, glittered with emeralds and rubies, with grains of gold and silver threads?

Such thoughts drifted through the mind of the Bishop of Cartagena as he sat in the state cabin of the great galleon *The City of the True Cross*, gazing toward the shore. He was calm, almost holy in his stillness. His sturdy frame rested in an easy chair, his ankles propped on another, beside a table laden with oranges, limes, guavas, pineapples—the fruits of the Indies.

An Indian girl, adorned with scarves and gold chains, fanned away flies with a feathered fan. Nearby, in a pail of ice from Horqueta (a gift from a devout Spanish lady who had “spent” a few Indians to bring it down), stood several flasks of rich wine from Alicante. But the bishop was not selfish. He had invited Don Pedro, colonel of the shipboard soldiers; Don Alverez, intendant of the royal customs at Santa Marta; and Don Paul, captain of mariners aboard the galleon. Two friars sat at the lower end of the table, helping to ease the bishop’s sorrow over the recent sack and burning of his cathedral city—by none other than Sir Francis Drake.

“We have suffered greatly, senors,” the bishop sighed, quoting Scripture in the manner of the time—fluently, though often too freely, mixing sacred texts with grumbles about mosquitoes and Tita, his servant. “Great sufferers indeed. Yet there shall be a remnant—ah, a remnant like the shaking of the olive tree, or the gleanings after the vintage.” He paused, then added, “Ah! Gold? Yes, I trust Our Lady’s mercy is not closed, nor her arms shortened. Look, senors!” He pointed dramatically out the window. “It looks like gold! It smells of gold, if I may say so poetically. Even the waves sing of gold—gold, gold, gold!”

“It is a rare privilege,” said the intendant, “to receive comfort from both a churchman and a scholar.”

“And a poet,” added Don Pedro. “You’ve no idea what sweet sonnets—”

“Hush, Don Pedro—hush!” the bishop replied, smiling. “If I, a mateless bird, once taught lovers how to sing, what of that? I am a man of God, yes—but also a man. I feel, I sympathize, I understand. Who better knows the frailties of human nature than I, a fallen son of Adam? Tita!”

“Um?” the girl murmured, trembling slightly.

“Fill the intendant’s glass. Will more treasure come down, illustrious senor? May the poor of Mary hope for crumbs from her table?”

“Not a pezo, I fear,” replied the intendant. “The big white cow up there”—he gestured to Horqueta—“has been milked dry for this year.”

“Ah!” the bishop mused, gazing upward. “Only good for cooling wine, eh? And as safe now as Solomon’s birds.”

“Solomon’s birds? Explain your allusion, my lord.”

“Enlighten us, your excellency.”

“Ah, there’s a tale. You know the holy birds that wander the Prado in Seville, among the ladies’ feet—hooked beaks, cinnamon crests? Hoopoes—*Upupa*, as the classics call them. Once, it is said, they wore golden crowns. And what happened? They suffered for it. But one day, all the birds and beasts came to pay homage to King Solomon, and among them were these hoopoes. They made a humble request—can you guess what? That Solomon pray for them to wear any crown but those of gold. For listen, Tita—see the snare of riches—men hunted, shot, trapped, and killed them for those golden crowns. Life became unbearable. So Solomon prayed—and instead of gold, they received crowns of feathers. Since then, they live as cheerfully as crickets in an oven, and bear the name of his most Catholic majesty. Tita—fill the commandant’s glass. Fray Gerundio, what are you whispering?”

Fray Gerundio had simply muttered to his brother:
“O si sic omnia—if only all gold would turn to feathers!”

“Then, friend,” replied the other friar—a Dominican, darker and sterner—“corrupt human nature would find some new bauble within a week, for which to kill and die in vain.”

“What was that, Fray Gerundio?” the bishop asked again.

“I merely said it would be well if all mankind prayed as the hoopoes did.”

“World, sir? What do you know of the world? Convert your Indians, sir, and leave politics to your superiors. Excuse him, senors.” The bishop lowered his voice. “A good man, truly, but born of peasants. And… well—you understand. Too much fasting, too much watching, I fear.” He tapped his temple knowingly.

Fray Gerundio smiled quietly. He was one of those rare souls whose conscience had been stirred by the cruelty of his own people—like Las Casas, Gasca, and others whose names shine in history. He protested against injustice, even before kings. He called dark-skinned heathens brothers. He declared, in words astonishing for the age, that every human being bore the same flesh and blood as Christ. He was a martyr in spirit, bearing witness to truth, often mocked and scorned. Such was Fray Gerundio—a humble, awkward peasant’s son who could barely string sentences together, yet could move crowds of Indians and blacks to tears when preaching with a crucifix in hand. He endured the bishop’s insults without protest, sipped his wine in silence, and let the conversation flow.

“They say,” observed the colonel, “that only a small Plate fleet will sail to Spain this year.”

“What else?” said the intendant. “What can we send, in God’s name, since those cursed English Lutherans have stripped us bare?”

“And if we had anything,” said the sea captain, “what vessel would carry it? That devil incarnate, Drake—”

“Ah!” the bishop interrupted. “Spare my ears! Don Pedro, please refrain from mentioning that man—his name is Tartarean, unfit for polite lips. Draco—a dragon, a serpent—the very emblem of the Devil himself—ah! The guardian of the golden apples of the West, who seeks to devour our new Hercules, His Most Catholic Majesty. He deceived Eve with one of those same apples—a wicked name, senors—a Tartarean name—Tita!”

“Um!”

“Fill my glass.”

“Nay,” the colonel burst out, “this Englishman is of a different breed of serpent altogether.”

“Your reason, senor?”

“Because this one would have drowned Eve in the sea before letting her—or anyone else—taste anything that looked like gold.”

“Ah, ah—very good! But—we laugh, valiant senors, while the Church weeps. Alas for my sheep!”

“And alas for their fold! It will be four years before Cartagena can be rebuilt. And the blockhouse—when it will rise again, Heaven alone knows, while His Majesty drains the Indies to feed his English Armada. The town is naked now, as an Indian’s back.”

“Baptista Antonio, the surveyor, has sent a report to the king detailing our defenselessness. But reading a report and acting upon it are two very different things, bishop—as every statesman knows. May Heaven grant us orders by the next fleet to fortify, or we’ll be at the mercy of every English pirate!”

“Ah, that blockhouse!” sighed the bishop. “A villainous trick. One hundred and ten thousand ducats for the ransom of the town! After burning half of it—and making me dine with them—ah! Sitting between the serpent and his lieutenant-general—and drinking my health in my own private wine—wine from Xeres nine years old—offered, the shameless heretics, to take me to England, convert me to Lutheranism, find me a wife, make an honest man of me—ah! And then demand fresh ransom for the priory and the fort—perfidious!”

“Well,” said the colonel, “they had the law on their side, the cunning rascals. We forgot to mention anything but the town in the agreement. Who would have guessed such a trick?”

“So I told my dear friend the prior when he came to borrow a thousand crowns. It was Heaven’s will—unexpected as a thunderbolt, to be borne as such. Every man must carry his own burden. How could I lend him anything?”

“Your holiness’s money had already been taken by them,” said the intendant, who knew full well the opposite.

“Exactly—my entire savings! Desolate in my old age. Ah, senors, had we not received warning from my dear friend the Marquess of Santa Cruz—whom I remember daily in prayer—we might have gone down into the pit like the rest. I too might have saved something, had I thought of myself. But in caring for others, I forgot myself—alas!”

“Warning or not,” said the sea captain, “we had no right to be beaten by such a handful. And a shame it is, and will be for many days.”

“Do you mean to cast aspersions on the courage of His Catholic Majesty’s soldiers?” asked the colonel sharply.

“I? No. But we were soundly beaten—behind our barricades, too. That’s the plain truth.”

“Beaten, sir? Do you apply such a term to war’s fortunes? What more could our governor have done? We had poisoned caltrops in the roads, Indian archers guarding them, earth-filled butts blocking the way, culverins and arquebuses firing from every angle. What spirit could have told us they’d come along the beach, not the high road, like Christian men?”

“Ah!” said the bishop. “By diabolical intuition, I suspect. Satan must aid those who serve him. For my part, I believe these men were possessed by every fiend imaginable.”

“Well, your holiness,” said the colonel, “there may have been devilry. How else would men dare run into the mouths of our cannons, fire at our very faces, and tumble harmlessly over those massive earthworks?”

“Doubtless by the power of demons raging within them,” interposed the bishop.

“And then, with their blasphemous cries, leap upon us with sword and pike? I saw Lieutenant-General Carlisle strike down your excellency’s sister’s son’s nephew—the noble ensign—though he was fully armored. Was not art-magic here? And that furious Lutheran Captain Young—I saw him seize our general by the head after Don Alonzo had wounded him, throw him to the ground, and capture him. Was not art-magic here?”

“Well,” said the captain, “if you seek art-magic, what say you to their marching through the flank fire of our galleys—eleven guns, two hundred men shooting at them—like a swarm of mosquitoes? Some said my men fired too high. But that was the English rascals’ doing—they got down on the tide beach. Yet, senor commandant, though Satan may have taught them that trick, did he teach them to carry pikes a foot longer than ours?”

“Ah, well,” said the bishop, “we are sacked. San Domingo, I hear, fares worse. St. Augustine in Florida too. All that remains for a poor priest like me is to return to Spain, and beg His Majesty’s and the Universal Father’s mercy for relief—for the poor of Mary—perhaps—(who knows?)—to transfer me to a quieter post. I am old, senors, weary from many labors—Tita! Fill our glasses. I have saved something—as you may have, senors, from the general ruin. For the flock, when I am gone, Heaven’s mercies are infinite. New cities will rise from ashes, new mines yield treasures to the faithful, new Indians will come to the Cross, embrace the easy yoke and light burden of the Church, and—”

“And where shall I be then? Ah, where? I long to rest, to depart. Tita! Sling my hammock. Senors, forgive my age and weakness. Fray Gerundio, go to bed!”

The Dons rose to leave. The bishop continued muttering:

“Farewell! Life is short. Ah! We shall meet in heaven. And really—no more pearls?”

“Not a one. Nor gold either,” said the intendant.

“Ah, well. Better a dinner of herbs where love is, than—Tita!”

“My breviary—ah! Man’s gratitude is fleeting. Have you seen Senora Bovadilla?”

“No.”

“Ah! She promised—yet no matter. A trifle as a keepsake—a gold cross, an emerald ring—what not? I forget. And what need have I of worldly wealth? Ah! Tita! Bring me the casket.”

When his guests were gone, the old man began mumbling prayers from his breviary, fingers tracing jewels and gold with the dull, greedy eyes of avarice.

“Ah—it may buy the red hat yet! Omnia Romae venalia! Put it by, Tita, and do not gaze at it too much. Enter not into temptation. The love of money is the root of all evil. Heaven, in love for the Indian, has made him poor in this world, that he may be rich in faith. Ah—ugh—so!”

The old miser climbed into his hammock. Tita drew the mosquito net over him, wrapped another around her head, and lay coiled on the floor—seeming to sleep, but watchful. Soon, master and slave snored in harmony, beneath the hum of mosquitoes.

Long past midnight. The moon had set. The sentinels, having challenged until they believed their officers asleep, slipped away from the unhealthy glow of the stars to seek rest—and slept as soundly as the bishop himself.

Two long, silent boats glided from behind the isolated rocks of Morro Grande, five hundred yards astern of the galleon. Nearly invisible on the shimmering water—pure white—they left only faint phosphorescent trails along their sides.

The bishop stirred awake, shifting uneasily. The wine had worn off. His shoulders had slipped down, his heels up, and his head ached. He sat upright in his hammock, stared out at the bay, and called Tita.

“Put another pillow under my head, child! What is that? A fish?”

Tita looked. Not a fish, she thought—but she said nothing. To argue might wake him.

The bishop studied the shape, decided it must be a white whale or shark, crossed himself, prayed for a safe voyage—and snored again.

Then the cabin door opened softly. The intendant’s head appeared.

Tita sat up, then crawled silently across the floor, among chairs and tables, lit by the cabin lamp.

“Is he asleep?”

“Yes. But the casket is under his head.”

“Curse him! How shall we take it?”

“I brought him a fresh pillow half an hour ago. I hung his hammock crooked on purpose, so he’d need one. I tried to slip the box away—but the old ox clutched it in both hands the whole time.”

“What shall we do, in the name of all the devils? She sails tomorrow morning. If we don’t act now, all is lost.”

Tita showed her teeth, touched the dagger at the intendant’s side.

“I dare not!” he shuddered.

“I dare!” she hissed. “He whipped my mother because she refused to give me to him—to be taught in his schools—when she went to the mines. She went, and died in three months. I saw her go, chained. She never returned. Yes—I dare kill him! I will kill him! I will!”

The intendant felt relieved. He had no wish to murder the bishop himself—he was a good Catholic, afraid of damnation. But Tita was an Indian. Her soul was cheap. He answered, “But we’ll be discovered!”

“I’ll leap from the window with the casket and swim ashore. They’ll never suspect you. They’ll think I’m drowned.”

“The sharks may get you, Tita. Give me the casket.”

Tita smiled. “You wouldn’t want to lose that, would you? Though you care little for losing me. And yet you told me you loved me!”

“And I do love you, Tita! Light of my eyes! Life of my heart! I swear by all the saints—I love you. I’ll marry you. I swear it—I’ll swear on the crucifix, if you wish!”

“Swear, then, or I won’t give you the casket.” She held out her crucifix, eyes blazing with wild, tropical passion.

He swore—trembling, pale as death.

“Give me your dagger.”

“No—not mine. It might be found. I’d be suspected. What if my sheath were empty?”

“Your knife will do. His throat is soft enough.”

She crept like a shadow toward the hammock, while he stood frozen at the other end, turned away, unable to watch.

He waited—one minute—two—five? An hour? Cold sweat soaked his limbs. His temples throbbed. Was that a death bell? No—it was his own pulse. Impossible. Where could it come from?

Then—struggle. Ah! She was at it. A stifled cry. He had feared that most—heard the old man scream. Would there be much blood? He hoped not. Another struggle. Tita’s voice, muffled, called for help.

“I cannot help you. Mother of Mercy! I dare not help you!” he hissed. “She-devil! You started it—you finish it!”

A heavy arm seized him from behind. The bishop had broken free and grabbed him! Or was it his ghost? A demon dragging him to hell? In pure terror, he opened his mouth to scream—only for a handkerchief to be thrust in. Then he felt himself bound hand and foot, thrown onto the table by a giant figure.

The cabin filled with armed men. Two tied the bishop in his hammock. Two seized Tita. Others climbed into the stern gallery—wild figures, blades gleaming in the starlight.

“Now, Will,” whispered the giant who had captured him, “forward—shut the fore-hatches tight. Shout ‘Fire!’ with all your might. Girl—murderess—your life is in my hands. Tell me where the commander sleeps, and I’ll spare you.”

Tita looked up at the towering speaker and obeyed in silence.

The intendant heard him enter the colonel’s cabin—then a brief scuffle—silence.

But only briefly. The alarm had sounded. Chaos erupted across every deck. Amyas—none other—had reached the poop. Sentinels were gagged and bound. Every half-naked man who stumbled up the main hatchway—yelling “Fire!” “Wreck!” “Treason!”—was hurled into the scuppers and secured.

“Lower that boat!” Amyas shouted in Spanish to the first prisoners.

Unarmed and naked, they could only obey.

“Now—jump in. Hand them to the gangway as they come.”

Done. Each man was kicked to the scuppers and bundled overboard.

“She’s full. Cast loose—away! If you try to board again, we’ll sink you.”

“Fire! Fire!” cried Cary forward. “Up the main hatchway—save yourselves!”

The ruse succeeded perfectly. Within half an hour, every ship’s boat that could be lowered was filled with Spaniards in their shirts, scrambling ashore.

“A new kind of camisado,” quoth Cary. “The last Spanish one I saw was at Smerwick—but this is far more successful.”

“Raise the main and foresail, Will!” said Amyas. “Cut the cable. We’ll plume our quarry as we fly.”

“Spoken like a true falconer. Heaven grant this great woodcock carries a good trail inside!”

“I’ll warrant it does,” said Jack Brimblecombe. “She floats low.”

“Much like your build, Jack. By the way—where is the commander?”
