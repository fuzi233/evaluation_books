When Mr. Draper stormed off in high dudgeon after his morning’s sharp exchange with Harry Warrington, he half fancied he heard the young prisoner call his name from behind him—and indeed, Harry had half risen from his cot, a cry half on his lips to summon the lawyer back. But his pride was raw, and Draper’s temper was already roused; Harry bit back the words before they could escape, and the attorney marched on without a pause. The sting of having to humble himself to a man of the law, forced to yield for no better reason than empty pockets and desperate need of coin, galled Harry so deeply he sank back sullenly onto his straw-stuffed mattress, resolving he could postpone the conversation an hour longer.

He told himself he did not care a farthing for Maria Esmond, that was plain enough. He burned with shame at the memory of how he had been tricked into their engagement: a wily, world-worn woman who had played upon his boyish, Virginian ardour, taking unfair advantage of him just as her brother had cheated him at the card table. They were his own blood, his kinsfolk, and they should have spared him. Instead, they had hunted him like prey, using him for every selfish end of their own. He raged inwardly at their betrayal of hospitality, at how they had turned the young trusting cousin who had walked so confidingly through their gates into a dupe. His heart ached so sore he let his head sink back into his pillow, and hot, bitter tears scalded his cheeks. “If ever they had come to my plantation in Virginia,” he thought, “I would have given them a welcome far kinder than this!”

He was dragged out of this dark despondency by the sight of Gumbo’s grinning face peeking around his cell door, announcing that a lady had come to call on Master Harry. No sooner had the little black servant stepped aside than the lady in the hooded capuchin cloak stepped over the threshold. Harry sat bolt upright on his bed, pale and gaunt, his cheeks still streaked with tears. The lady gave a soft sob, and before Gumbo had even slipped out and shut the door behind him, she ran to his side, flung her arms about his neck with a wild, genuine tenderness that was almost maternal, sobbing against his pale cheek, covering it with kisses as her own tears fell thick and fast.

“Oh, my Harry! Did I ever, ever think to find you here, of all places?”

Harry started back, half scared at the sudden rush of affection, but she sank to her knees beside his bed, clasping his feverish hand, wrapping her arms around his knees. There was no pretense in that regard, no artifice: the sight of his squalid cell, his haggard face, filled her with a pity and love she had never dared show before.

“I—I thought none of you would come,” Harry groaned out.

For a long minute, her only reply was more tears, more soft kisses pressed to his burning knuckles, more tight, clinging clasps of his hand. “Oh, my dear, my dear! I cannot bear to think of you suffering so,” she sobbed. Hardened though her old heart might have seemed, it was no block of marble, her dreary life no total desert. Harry’s own mother could not have spoken more softly, or clung to him more fondly, than his kinswoman kneeling at his feet.

“Some of these debts are my fault, I fear,” she admitted, and the words were true. “You bought trinkets and jewels to give me pleasure, and I hate every last one of them now! I never thought I could hate a gift from you. I have brought all of them here, and more besides—see! And every last shilling I own in the world!” She emptied the contents of her pockets into his lap: brooches set with paste, gold rings, an enameled watch, and a heap of nearly twenty guineas that glinted in the dim cell light. The sight moved Harry so deeply he could barely breathe.

“Dearest, kindest cousin!” he sobbed, and no other words would come, though his stammering gratitude and brimming eyes spoke louder than any speech. He grew light and gay not long after, smiling as he tucked away the trinkets he had once given her, recounting how he had fallen into this trap by buying goods on credit he could not pay back, and how the lawyer Draper had just insulted him over that very business not an hour before. He would not take her money, he swore—he had more than enough to get by for the present—but those twenty guineas were worth as much to him as twenty thousand. He would never forget her kindness, no, he swore by all that was sacred, he never would. He would write to his mother at once and tell her how good she had been to him, how she had cheered him when he was on the very edge of breaking under the shame and misery of his imprisonment. Heaven bless her for it, he cried.

The pair talked on, cheerfully and affectionately, no need to track every soft word they exchanged. After Maria’s visit, the dark gray day seemed far brighter to Harry; the narrow cell did not feel half so oppressive. The world was not all cold and selfish, he told himself. There was a dear, loyal soul here who truly loved him. Even Castlewood, her brother, had not been as unkind as he first seemed: he had been distraught he could not help his cousin, every shilling he had won from Harry at cards lost to other gamesters the very next day. He swore he would do anything he could, that he would come to visit Harry soon, stuck as he was at court on duty, as much a prisoner as Harry himself. They talked on until the light faded completely from the small barred window, and Maria sighed, bade him farewell, and slipped out the door.

The latch had barely clicked shut behind her before it opened again to admit Mr. Draper. “Your most humble servant, sir,” the attorney drawled. His voice grated on Harry’s ears, his presence an open insult.

“I expected you hours ago, sir,” Harry said curtly.

“A lawyer’s time is never his own, sir,” Draper replied, still reeking of the bottle of port he had just finished at the Grecian Coffee House. “But never mind, I am at your service now. I presume all is settled, Mr. Warrington? Got your trunk packed? Why, you’re still in your bedgown! Call your black man to help you tidy yourself, and I’ll go settle all the debts downstairs. I’ve got a coach waiting at the door, and we’ll go dine with the old Baroness.”

“Dine with the Baroness de Bernstein, are you invited to that, pray?” Harry said.

“Not me, no such honour. I ate my dinner long since. It’s you that’s to dine with your aunt, I thought.”

“Mr. Draper, you presume far more than you know,” Harry said, drawing his brocade dressing gown tight around his broad shoulders, standing tall and fierce.

“Good God, sir, what do you mean?” Draper spluttered.

“I mean, sir, that I have thought it over. I gave my word to an honourable, faithful lady, and it does not become a man of honour to break it.”

“Confound you, sir!” the lawyer shrieked. “I tell you she lost the paper! There’s nothing binding you, nothing at all! Why, she’s old enough to be—”

“Enough, sir!” Harry roared, stamping his foot hard on the stone floor. “You seem to mistake me for some other pettifogging knave you’re used to dealing with. I’ll have you know, Mr. Draper, you do not speak to a man who will break his given word for a handful of coin.”

“Pettifogger, indeed!” Draper yelled, flying into a rage. “Man of honour, indeed! I’d have you know I’m as honourable as you are, sir! I haven’t gambled away my whole patrimony, and lived like a lord on two hundred a year! I haven’t bought watches on credit and pawned them—don’t you dare lay a hand on me, sir!” He scrambled back toward the cell door.

“That is the way out, sir,” Harry said. “You can’t escape through the window, it’s barred. So your answer to your client is No, then, is it?”

Draper screamed the word back at him, white with fury. Harry stepped forward, fists clenched. “If you utter one more word—” he began, but the cell door slammed shut before he could finish the threat, and Draper was gone, storming straight back to Madame de Bernstein. Even though he told her his version of the story to make himself look as blameless as possible, she unleashed a far sharper, angrier torrent of abuse on him than Harry had ever spoken.

“What? Shall she trust me, and I desert her?” Harry paced back and forth across his cell, the heavy brocade of his gown rustling as he moved. “Dear, faithful, generous woman! If I lie in this prison for years, I will never break my word to her.”

The Baroness, left alone after throwing her lawyer out, sank down at the dinner table she had hoped to share with Harry. His place was laid out, the silver polished, the glasses glinting in the candlelight. One dish after another was set before her by her silent major-domo, who watched as she picked at each, then pushed it away untouched. At last he dared to speak. “It is eight o’clock, my lady. You have not eaten a bite all day. It will do you no good to go hungry.”

She could not eat. She sent for her coffee instead, told Case the footman to bring it at once. The servants cleared the plates from the table, leaving the old woman sitting alone, staring at the empty chair across from her. A few minutes later, Case came back into the parlour, no coffee in his hands, his face white and as scared as a man who had seen a ghost.

“Mr. Warrington,” he gasped.

The Baroness cried out, half rising from her armchair, only to sink back again, trembling all over. “So you have come at last, sir, have you?” she said, her voice soft and shaking with fondness. “Bring back the—ah!” She shrieked at the top of her lungs. “Gracious God! Who are you?”

Her eyes bulged wide, her white skin looked ghastly under the layer of rouge on her cheeks, and she clung hard to the arms of her chair to keep from fainting. The gentleman who stepped toward her had the exact face and figure of Harry Warrington, and when he spoke his voice had the same rich, familiar tone, but it was not Harry. He bowed politely to the Baroness.

“You expected my brother, madam, I think?” he said. “I only landed in London this very hour. I went first to his lodgings, and met his servant Gumbo at your door, who was carrying a letter for you. I thought I would bring it to you myself before I went to find him.” He laid the sealed letter on the table before her.

“Are you—” the Baroness gasped, “are you my nephew that we all thought was dead?”

“Killed in the French wilderness, and very much alive,” he said. “I am George Warrington, madam. And I ask you, his own kinsfolk: what have you done with my brother?”

“Look, George!” the old woman babbled, bewildered. “I expected him here tonight! That chair was set for him! I have waited for him till I am faint! I do not like being alone. Stay, and sup with me!”

“Pardon me, madam. Please God, I will sup with my brother Harry tonight,” George said.

“Bring him back here! Bring him back on any terms! It is but five hundred pounds! Here is the money, sir, if you need it, all of it!” she cried, fumbling for her purse.

“I have no need of your funds, madam,” George said. “I have coin of my own, and there is no better use for it than to rescue my brother.”

“And you will bring him to me, sir? Promise you will bring him to me!”

George gave her a stately, formal bow by way of answer, then turned and quit the parlour, walking past the gawking, amazed servants. He called to Gumbo in a sharp, commanding tone to follow him. Had Master Harry received no letters from home? Gumbo stammered that Harry had not even opened all his post the past few days, that no letter telling him his brother had escaped the French settlements and sailed home to Virginia had reached him yet.

Quick, horses! Quick down the Strand, across Temple Bar! Here is the house of captivity, and the Deliverer has arrived at last, to break the prisoner free.
