Cheerfulness, it seems, depends as much on the spirit within as the world without—a truth evident in the high spirits of Messrs. Helstone and Moore as they rode from the mill-yard that drizzling night. Lantern light flickered over Moore’s face, revealing an unusual spark in his dark eyes, while Helstone’s stern features were uncharacteristically animated. Danger, it appeared, only sharpened their zeal.

The men knew well that any lurking frame-breaker would gladly shoot them from the shadows. Yet the knowledge seemed to invigorate them—both steel-nerved, both unshaken.

Now, some might protest that a clergyman ought not to relish such ventures, that his calling demands peace, not defiance. But let us be fair: Helstone was a man misplaced. Nature had forged him for a soldier; fate had made him a priest. He was hard, unyielding, fiercely loyal to his principles—flawed, yes, but not wicked. And if his profession sat awkwardly upon him, must he be damned for it?

That night, however, priest and manufacturer rode united—if not in harmony.

For though they shared a cause, they clashed as always. The war was their battleground. Helstone, a Tory of the old breed, burned with patriotic fire; Moore, pragmatic and embittered, scoffed at England’s chances against Bonaparte.

"Your Wellington is a wooden idol," Moore taunted. "Bonaparte’s marshals will sweep him into the sea."

Helstone’s grip tightened on his reins. "Wellington is England’s soul. He’ll crush those upstart Frenchmen when the hour comes."

"God defends the powerful, not the righteous."

"Did He not part the Red Sea for a band of slaves?" Helstone shot back. "Or do you forget how Pharaoh’s host drowned like stones?"

Moore smirked. "A pretty metaphor—but France is Israel now, and Napoleon her Moses. Europe is the rotting empire, and your Ferdinand a disgrace to his throne."

The argument might have spiraled further had a gig not rattled up beside them, its lamp cutting through the rain.

"Nah, maister—did th’ wagons get through?" called a familiar voice.

"Joe Scott?" Moore peered into the gloom.

"Aye, and in a right state," answered the driver—none other than Mr. Yorke, a man as changeable as the Yorkshire weather. "Found him trussed like a hen on the moor. What’s my reward?"

"Gratitude," Moore said dryly. "Since I’ve few better men to lose."

Yorke laughed, his dialect shifting fluidly between broad Yorkshire and crisp English as he recounted the rescue. Behind him, the rest of Scott’s party straggled into view.

"Eleven strong men tonight," Yorke declared. "Enough to hunt down every frame-breaker in the district—eh, Helstone? We’d make fine reading in the *Stilbro’ Courier*."

"Write it yourself," the rector grunted. "And urge them to hang the lot without delay."

Moore’s smile was cold. "Let them blunder on. They’ll hang themselves soon enough."

Yorke’s eyes gleamed. "Aye, but if one crosses your path—"

"I’ll snap him up."

With that, Yorke ushered them toward his home, its windows glowing warmly through the rain.

The hall was lined with paintings; the parlor, rich with Italian landscapes, spoke of a refined, traveled mind. Books, music, cameos—every detail whispered taste. Yet Yorke himself defied easy classification. One moment blunt as a Yorkshire farmer, the next polished as a gentleman, he was a man of contradictions.

He offered wine; Helstone refused.

"Worried Eastern customs bind us to friendship?" Yorke teased. "Drink or not—I’ll oppose you all the same."

Helstone bristled. "I’d expect nothing less."

Yorke turned to Moore, sprawled in an oak chair by the fire. "Up, lad! That seat’s mine by birthright."

Moore yielded with a lazy shrug. "Why so possessive?"

"My father sat there before me—same as Helstone’s notions, handed down and never questioned."

The banter continued, slipping into French as Moore’s temper flared.

"*Je me moque de vos paysans!*" he snapped.

Yorke’s retort was flawless. "*Et eux de toi, mon garçon.*"

Their exchange grew sharper, Moore’s bitterness rising until Yorke fell silent, watching him with keen, unreadable eyes.

And there—leaning back in his carved chair—Mr. Yorke became a puzzle worth unraveling.
