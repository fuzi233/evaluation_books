"My dear Romola," Tito began the following morning, two days since his impassioned appeal in the Piazza del Duomo, "grand company awaits me today. The Milanese Count returns, accompanied by the Seneschal de Beaucaire, the favorite confidant of the King of France. I suspect you hold little relish for the hollow rituals required by such glittering magnates, figures we shall scarcely behold again. Since they intend to inspect our antiquities and the library, perhaps you should suspend your labors and accompany me to visit Cousin Brigida?"

A subtle hope flickered within Romola's gaze at these words, and she nodded immediately. Yet, moments later, returning draped in her hood and mantle, she observed, "Ah, what a mighty exhalation Florence shall let loose when the gates swing wide and the final French footfall vanishes beyond them! Even you, my Tito, wear the shadow of fatigue beneath your patience. Confess it now. Indeed, your brow burns with fever."

Tito leaned forward over his desk, his quill scratching steadily across parchment. She reached out to rest her palm upon his forehead, intending a tender farewell gesture—a movement so habitual that he typically raised his chin, reclined slightly, and gazed upward at her with unguarded warmth. But now, though her hand hovered near his hair, he remained immovable, as if encased in a helmet of cold iron. Instead of lifting his gaze, he maintained his composure, speaking lightly as the ink flowed.

"The French depart from Florence with the swiftness of wasps abandoning a ripened pear the moment they have tasted its sweetness."

Sensing the void where his customary affection usually bloomed, Romola withdrew her hand and declared, "I am departing, Tito."

"Farewell, my sweet one. I remain to await the visitors. Take Maso with you."

Yet Tito did not lift his eyes from the page. Romola left the chamber without further word. In the quiet rhythm of matrimony, the faintest tremor can mark an epoch; for the first time, she acknowledged internally not merely that Tito had altered, but that his alteration extended specifically toward her. Was the fault hers? Perhaps, if not for the tangible evidence of the armor and the painting, which whispered of some external catastrophe shrouded in mystery.

As soon as Tito was certain she had crossed the threshold, he set down his pen and lifted his gaze, seeking solace in the solitude of his papers and fragments of marble. A sting of shame pricked him; he regretted his inability to summon his usual gentleness, to look upon her as before. If circumstances allowed, he would have surpassed his former kindness. Yet, he could not instantly conquer a visceral recoil, a shrinking born of those same qualities that drove him to cherish her. Looming before him lay a path fraught with grievance, requiring him to endure harsh confrontations to secure her pardon. Tito, by nature, abhorred defiance or insolence; every inclination bent toward preserving her tenderness. Though free from the paralyzing scruples of sentiment—having swiftly disproven their lack of practical utility—the absence of such scruples did not shield him from the agony of offense. Unscrupulousness dispels many troubles, yet it cannot cure toothache, nor heal wounded vanity, nor banish the crushing loneliness that plagues the world unless countered by robust health and a righteous, loving soul. At this moment, Tito felt acutely that no strategy could spare him the pain of the inevitable collision with Romola. No softening of manner could buffer the blow toward which he drifted, urged on by the terror of the approaching storm.

A secret conviction had gnawed at him for weeks: his stubborn adherence to Bardo's wishes regarding the library, under current duress, resembled a sentimental folly, robbing both himself and Romola of vital benefits. Had not the events of the past week forged a new urgency, coupled with a rare opening, this realization might have remained dormant. Only after witnessing Baldassarre regain his sanity, and only when he feared flight from Florence, did he resolve to exercise his legal right to sell the library before the chance of sale to the French and Milanese bidders slipped away. For if exile became necessary, he refused to depart as a beggar. Accustomed to a life of ease, he sought to carry with him the means to sustain it. Above all, he wished to carry Romola, and above all, he wished to carry no stain of infamy. Prosperity had whetted his appetite for the pleasures attendant upon elevated station; the notion of fleeing under disgrace, even laden with jewels, appeared to him not as temptation, but as a grotesque alternative. He could not render himself independent of those Florentines who treated him with mere courtesy, nor, certainly, of Romola. She was the spouse of his first love, and he still adored her; she was integral to his existence, a piece of furniture from which he shrank to part. He winced under her judgment, unsure how far her feelings had recoiled. While the absolute sway a husband holds over a wife often tempts betrayal that a lover dares not attempt, it proved insufficient to quell Tito's profound unease. This heavy stone, too burdensome for his will, prevented him from raising his head to meet her eyes. Her radiant countenance brought the prospect of struggle too sharply into focus. Yet, resistance was futile; if they were to leave Florence, funds were essential. Indeed, Tito could not construct a life satisfying to his mind without a considerable sum, and the obsession with arranging his existence to his satisfaction had been the root of all his transgressions. He would have faced any sacrifice, provided it lacked unpleasantness.

The distinguished guests arrived and departed; the negotiations concluded. That evening, however, no serious matters were broached. Tito displayed a buoyancy and chatterfulness unprecedented in recent memory, recounting tales and descriptions of the French visit to Romola. She perceived an effort in his liveliness, interpreting it as a penitent awareness of the wound he had inflicted that morning, and inwardly ached at the growing distance between them—the presence of an unspoken offense.

The following day, Tito remained absent until late hour. To Romola, it marked a significant day; Piero di Cosimo, spurred by the fear that his actions might have caused her sorrow in the past week, had dispatched her father's portrait. She propped it against the back of his ancient chair and gazed upon it for some time when the door opened behind her, revealing Bernardo del Nero.

"It is you, godfather!" Romola exclaimed, hastening toward him. "How I wish you had arrived sooner! Twilight is already descending."

"I stopped briefly to bring you tidings, knowing Tito had not yet returned," Bernardo replied. "The French King departs tomorrow; indeed, 'tis high time. Another clash occurred this morn between our citizens and his soldiers. Yet, there is now hope that the city regains order and trade resumes."

"That brings joy," Romola said. "But it seems precipitous. Tito seemed to believe yesterday the King would linger for some time."

"He has been thoroughly harried, hence the haste," Bernardo smiled. "His own generals quarreled fiercely, and finally, our Signoria unleashed the city's mastiff, Brother Girolamo. The Cristianissimo, startled by that thunderclap, issued orders to move. I fear discord may follow his departure, but at least all parties rejoice at ending Florence's suffocation under military occupation. The Friar barked with effect this time." He paused, then asked, "And what is this?" As Romola, clutching his arm, guided him before the portrait, he continued.

"Do you not require your spectacles, godfather?" Romola asked anxiously, wishing him to see exactly what she beheld.

"No, child, no," Bernardo answered, removing his cap and seating himself with dignified upright posture. "At this distance, perhaps my aged eyes surpass yours. Old eyes resemble old memories; they are sharpest for things distant in time."

"It is preferable to having no likeness at all," Romola offered apologetically, after a brief silence. "It resembles him less than the vision I hold in my mind, yet that image may fade with years." Resting her arm upon his shoulder, drawn strongly by their shared reverence for the dead, she spoke.

"I am not sure," Bernardo mused. "I almost perceive Bardo as he was in youth, more clearly than this canvas depicts him in age. Your father possessed a great fire in his eyes when young. What I could never fathom was how a spirit so fiery, seemingly more impatient than my own, could devote itself to books and dwell among shadows for a lifetime. Nevertheless, he poured his heart into it."

Bernardo shrugged slightly, but Romola heard a resonance in his voice that mirrored her own.

"And he was disappointed unto the end," she murmured involuntarily. Instantly fearing her words might sound like an accusation against Tito, she hurried on, "If we could but witness his longest, dearest wish realized precisely to his mind!"

"Then so we shall," Bernardo said kindly, rising and donning his cap. "Times are clouded now, yet fish are caught by waiting. Who knows? Should the wheel turn sufficiently, I may yet serve as Gonfaloniere before I breathe my last; and no creditor shall touch these treasures." He glanced around the room, then turned to her, patting her cheek. "And you need not fear my demise; my ghost claims naught. I have arranged for that in my will."
