Never once, when the Dashwood women first settled into their cozy Devonshire cottage, had they imagined their days would fill so quickly with social calls, outings, and near-constant invitations that left barely a spare hour for quiet reading or sketching. Yet that was exactly the life they soon found themselves leading.
The moment Marianne was fully recovered from her fever, all the amusements Sir John Middleton had spent weeks plotting came rushing to life. Small private dances began at Barton Park, and boating parties were arranged and set out as often as the drizzly, unpredictable October weather would allow. Willoughby was included in every single gathering of the sort. The easy, unstudied familiarity of these casual events only pulled him and the Dashwood sisters closer—most of all Marianne. He had endless chances to witness her wit, her passion for poetry and wild walks, the light that lit up her face at the sight of a sunset over the Devonshire hills, and every day he made his open, unashamed admiration clearer. Marianne, in turn, showed him every corner of her heart without reserve.
Elinor was not surprised by how fast the two fell for each other. She only wished they did not wear their hearts so completely on their sleeves, and once or twice she gently suggested Marianne show a little more discretion for the sake of local gossip. But Marianne hated all concealment when no shame could come of being honest. To hold back feelings that were kind, bright, and true seemed to her not just a waste of effort, but a surrender to stuffy, meaningless social rules that had no right to dictate how a person loved. Willoughby felt exactly the same, and their behavior at every turn made their shared beliefs impossible to miss.
When Willoughby was in the room, Marianne saw no one else. Every word he spoke was clever, every thing he did was perfect. If their evenings at the Park ended with card games, he would intentionally play badly, cheat the rest of the table out of points, just to make sure Marianne walked away with the best hand. If the night’s amusement was dancing, they were partners for half the set, and when they were forced to split apart for the required two country dances, they still hovered shoulder to shoulder on the edge of the floor, barely speaking a single word to anyone else. Their neighbors teased them relentlessly for it, but the ridicule did not embarrass them, did not even ruffle them. Mrs. Dashwood, for her part, shared all their warm, eager feelings, and had no urge at all to scold them for their open affection. To her, it was simply the natural, beautiful outcome of two young, ardent hearts finding each other.
This was the season of pure, uncomplicated happiness for Marianne. Her heart belonged entirely to Willoughby, and the fond attachment she had once carried for their old home at Norland, which she had thought nothing could soften, grew lighter and lighter as every walk, every shared poem, every quiet laugh with Willoughby made their little Devonshire cottage feel more like home than Norland ever had.
Elinor’s happiness was far less steady. Her heart was not at peace, and her enjoyment of all these parties was never untainted. None of her new acquaintances could make up for what she had left behind in Sussex, could make her think of Norland, and the quiet secret love she had left there, with any less regret. Neither Lady Middleton nor the garrulous Mrs. Jennings could give her the thoughtful, equal conversation she missed—though Mrs. Jennings never stopped talking, and had taken a great liking to Elinor from their very first meeting, she had already repeated the full story of her late husband’s final illness three or four times over. If Elinor’s memory was sharper, she would have known every last detail of Mr. Jennings’s last words to his wife before he drew his final breath. Lady Middleton was only more pleasant than her mother in that she spoke far less. Elinor quickly saw that her quiet reserve was not a mark of good sense, just a total lack of anything interesting to say. She treated her husband and her mother exactly the same way she treated the Dashwoods, so there was no hope of building any sort of warm friendship with her. She never had a new thing to say from one day to the next, her flat, unchanging good humor never shifted, and though she never protested her husband’s constant parties as long as everything was done in proper style and her two eldest children were close by, she never appeared to enjoy them any more than she would have enjoyed sitting silent at home. She added nothing to the group’s conversation, and people would often forget she was in the room entirely until she snapped to scold her two rambunctious boys for running off with the cake knives.
Of all their new acquaintances, only Colonel Brandon could give Elinor the feeling of having found a friend worthy of her respect, a companion who made the long evenings feel worth her time. Willoughby was out of the question as far as Elinor was concerned, of course: all his attention was reserved for Marianne, and a man far less charming than him would still have been beloved by the whole group, wrapped up as he was in his own happy love. The Colonel, by contrast, had no hope of Marianne’s regard, and found quiet, gentle comfort in talking to Elinor, even as he watched Marianne dote on another man. Elinor’s sympathy for him only grew when she began to suspect he already knew the sharp, lingering pain of a love that had not worked out.
Her suspicion was confirmed one evening at the Park, when they sat together by mutual consent on the sidelines while everyone else danced. His eyes were fixed on Marianne, spinning light as a feather in Willoughby’s arms, and after a long quiet minute, he spoke with a faint, sad smile.
“Your sister, I understand, does not approve of second attachments.”
“No,” Elinor replied. “All her opinions are thoroughly romantic.”
“Or rather, I think she believes such attachments are not even possible to exist at all.”
“I think she does,” Elinor agreed, half amused. “Though I cannot imagine how she holds that view without stopping to think of our own father, who married twice. In a few years, her opinions will settle into the sensible, reasonable views that come with living in the world, and then no one will have any trouble understanding her beliefs, not even herself.”
“That may well be true,” the Colonel said softly. “And yet there is something so very amiable in the untested prejudices of a young mind, that one can’t help but grieve to see them give way to the cold, common opinions most people carry.”
“I cannot agree with you there,” Elinor said. “There are real inconveniences to feelings as uncompromising as Marianne’s, that all the charm of youthful enthusiasm and inexperience can never make up for. Her whole system of beliefs tends to throw all common propriety aside, and I only hope that getting to know the world better will be the greatest good that ever comes to her.”
After a short pause, the Colonel spoke again, his voice low.
“Does your sister draw any line in her objections to second attachments? Or is it equally wrong for everyone? Do people who had their first love torn away from them, whether the fault was their beloved’s, or the cruelty of circumstance, owe it to themselves to never care for anyone ever again for the rest of their lives?”
“Upon my word, I don’t know the fine details of her rules,” Elinor said. “I only know I have never once heard her say that a second attachment of any kind could be pardonable.”
“That cannot stand,” the Colonel said, his voice tight with a emotion he did not usually show. “But a full change, a total uprooting of all a person’s most cherished feelings—no, no, do not wish for that for your sister. When those soft romantic idealisms of youth are forced to break, all too often they are replaced by opinions that are far too common, and far too dangerous. I speak from experience. I once knew a woman who had the exact same temperament, the exact same way of thinking as your sister. She judged the world the same way, but then a forced change, a whole string of terrible misfortunes—”
He cut himself off abruptly, his face going pale, like he had said far more than he ever intended to. That small, accidental slip was all Elinor needed to put the pieces together. The memory of that unknown woman, and the grief tied to her, was clearly still a tender, raw wound the Colonel had never shared with anyone. Elinor did not press him for details. But if Marianne had been sitting in her place, she would have had the whole tragic, sweeping story of a doomed, forbidden love plotted out in her imagination in minutes, every secret and every heartbreak filled in, and never once doubted it was all perfectly true.
