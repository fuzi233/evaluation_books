Near on four of the afternoon, when the day has begun to cool and even the road-dust lies down a little, Eli was in his shop with the shutters half-open to the street, weighing hose and measuring cloth as steadily as a man counts beads. The eldest and the youngest were abroad. Catherine and her little crippled maid had been so long strained upon Gerard’s absence that they had limped and hurried a little way down the road to peer for him, as if sight could conjure him nearer. So it fell out that Giles, whom the neighbours called Sampsonet in mockery and in awe, had the sitting-room to himself.

It was a room that told you, before a word was spoken, what sort of folk dwelt there. The very floor confessed a creed. Dutch tiles, blue and white and bright as a rinsed dish, lay underfoot—so glazed and so often washed that you could have set a trencher down and eaten without fear of taint. The house smelt not of stale rushes and old bones, but of lye-soap, clean water, and a faint ghost of yesterday’s broth lingering in the oak. From the shop came the mild rasp of shears, the soft thump of folded stockings, and the street’s harmless music: a cart-wheel grumbling in sand, a child’s call, a distant bell knitting the air together. The quiet was not emptiness but order; each sound had its place, and so did each object.

One large window looked to the road. Its stone cross-work, heavy and square, stood proud in the middle like a true cross to the eye, and had been stared at in prayer so often that the very light seemed to fall more devoutly through it. The panes were small, lozenge-shaped, and tied together with lead, each diamond holding a little slice of afternoon. The chairs were rude things, stout and plain, all but the great arm-chair of oak, carved at the crown and so high-backed that a man’s head, sitting, stopped two feet short of the top. On the wall hung the copper pail for holy water, narrowed at the waist like a woman’s body, with a little besom beside it to scatter blessing in a fine spray. A long narrow table of massive oak ran the length of the room—the sort of table that has received prayers, loaves, quarrels, and christening-cakes, and keeps them all in its grain.

And at the table’s rim, as if he were some grotesque carving come alive, clung Giles.

Nature had made him with lavish care—head and shoulders strong, the chest full and corded—then, as though interrupted by a call, had finished the rest in haste. He was all wedge: broad above, dwindled below, an inverted cone set upon legs too slight for such a furnace of arms. His very imbalance had become his craft. There was little weight to drag his limbs downward; his hands were like grapnels. He could shin up a pole as quick as a squirrel, and hang from a bough by one fist till others’ wrists burned at the thought. It pleased him—insanely, childishly—to make a playground of the household’s honesty: to grip the dinner-table with both hands and swing, and then, crowning joy, to seize the very rim with his teeth and let go with his hands, holding on by those great ivories as if he were a beast of the woods.

There he was now: teeth sunk in the oak, eyes bright and wild, claws of hands lifted as if to pounce—like some imp nailed to decent furniture.

Little Kate came in on her crutches and stopped short. The room’s cleanliness, the long table’s smooth edge, the order of things—these struck her before Giles’s bravado did. She had her mother’s heart for what fed and sheltered them, for the good oak that had borne their bowls and bread.

“Oh, Giles! how can you?” she cried, more fear than anger in her voice. “Mother is at hand. It dents the table.”

“Go and tell her, little tale-bearer,” snarled Giles, and his words were as sharp as a knife on a platter. “You are the one for making mischief.”

“Am I?” said Kate, steady as a cook with a hot pot. “That is news to me.”

“The biggest in Tergou,” growled Giles, and fastened on again with spiteful relish.

“Oh, indeed!” said Kate, and the dryness of it was new in her mouth, like vinegar tasted by a child. She had scarce flung that small dart before her eyes filled. She lowered herself as best she could, sat, and cried quietly, as if tears were another kind of washing.

Catherine came in almost upon the sound—her step quick, her hearing sharpened by care. The house’s hush broke a little: the crutch tips on tile, the mother’s skirt whispering, Giles’s breath under the table as he dropped and crouched there, glaring from the shadows.

“What is to do now?” said Catherine sharply, the way a woman speaks when her pot is in danger of boiling over and her child is missing besides. Her eyes, experienced as hands, went from Kate’s wet cheeks to Giles’s guilty posture and the way his mouth had been. Her palm itched.

“Nay, mother,” Kate said, wiping her face with the back of her hand. “It was but a foolish word Giles spoke. I had not noticed it at another time; but I was tired and in care for Gerard, you know.”

At that, a voice—faint, as if the road had taken it toll and left only breath—came from the door.

“Let no one be in care for me.”

Gerard stood there—pale, dusted with travel, the very lines of his face drawn thin by haste and disappointment and hope. He tottered in, and the room’s ordered sounds—shop-noises, street-bell, the small domestic hush—were drowned in a rising burst: Catherine’s cry, Kate’s sob turned to laughter, questions tumbling like apples into a basket. And Gerard, knocked up by beating Rotterdam like a covert for Margaret and then the long journey home, dropped exhausted into the nearest chair—the tall one, which made his tiredness look smaller by its height.

Yet youth is elastic. Give it a chair, a swallow of small beer, the sight of loving faces, and it springs back. In a handful of breaths he was no longer a ghost at the threshold but the bright centre of a ring.

“Now then,” said Eli’s wife, hands hovering as if to feel him safe and whole, “the prizes—tell us the prizes.”

Gerard’s eyes lit, and with the light came a new music in his speech, quick and eager as a spoon on a bowl.

“I was admitted,” he said, “to see the competitors’ works, all laid out in an enormous hall before the judges pronounced.”

His hands moved as if laying out pages, arranging colours, weighing gold.

“Oh, mother! oh, Kate! when I saw the goldsmiths’ work, I had liked to have fallen on the floor. I thought not all the goldsmiths on earth had so much gold, silver, jewels, and craft of design and facture. But in sooth, all the arts are divine.”

To please the women, and because he himself had been dazzled as any hungry man is by a rich board, he described the church treasures as if reciting a feast: reliquaries like small bright houses, feretories, calices with rims as clean as a well, crosiers curling like pastry hooks, crosses, pyxes, monstrances—each thing wrought so cunningly that you could almost taste the patient hours in the metal. Then the worldly splendours: goblets, hanaps, watches and clocks, chains and brooches, all shining, all exact. He spoke of them until their mouths did what mouths will do at the thought of plenty: they watered. For in every age, hunger and desire are cousins; the tongue knows.

“But, Kate,” he went on, and now his voice softened like butter melting, “when I came to the illuminated work from Ghent and Bruges, my heart sank. Mine was dirt by the side of it. For the first minute I could almost have cried; but I prayed for a better spirit, and presently I was able to enjoy them, and thank God for those lovely works, and for those skilful, patient craftsmen, whom I own my masters.”

He told how the coloured pages—greens like crushed herbs, reds like wine, blues deep as the canal at dusk—had been so beautiful he forgot his own black and white. “But next day,” he said, “when all the other prizes had been given, they came to the writing, and whose name think you was called first?”

“Yours,” said Kate simply, as if naming the next dish she knew must come.

The others laughed her down. Kate, who had learned to hear tones as much as words—who knew how joy sounds before it shows—only looked at Gerard.

“You may well laugh,” said Gerard, and his cheeks flushed as if heat had been brought near. “But for all that, Gerard Eliassoen of Tergou was the name the herald shouted.”

He described it so plainly it became present: the herald’s voice ringing in the high hall; Gerard standing stupid, shoved forward by hands he scarcely felt; the air swimming; himself on his knees on a cushion at the Duke’s feet, like a child at a great table. The Duke spoke; Gerard, fluttered like a caught bird, could not answer. And then—no glaive to cut off his dull head, as his fear had half-invented—but a gold medal laid into his hand.

“And there it is,” he said, and drew it out.

There was a yell and almost a scramble—human voices breaking into a small riot, the room suddenly full of edges and breath and reaching fingers.

“And then,” said Gerard, savoring the moment as one savors a good bite, “he gave me fifteen great bright golden angels. I had seen one before, but I never handled one. Here they are.”

He poured them out, and the gold rang softly as it struck apron and palm: a little rain of metal, a sound that changes a room. It made the tile-floor seem whiter, the oak table older, the air sharper. Even the street noises beyond the window seemed to lean in and listen.

“Oh, Gerard! oh, Gerard!”

He began to portion them with a tenderness that was like carving meat fairly, giving each his share not by greed but by story.

“There is one for you, our eldest; and one for you, Sybrandt, and for you, Little Mischief; and two for thee, Little Lily, because God hath afflicted thee; and one for myself, to buy colours and vellum; and nine for her that nursed us all, and risked the two crowns upon poor Gerard’s hand.”

The gold drew out what lay in each heart as fire brings out smell from spice. Cornelis and Sybrandt clutched their coins and stared—one glare of greed, another of envy toward Kate who had got two. Giles seized his and rolled it along the clean tiles, and went gambolling after it, his laughter a cracked little trumpet against the room’s quiet decency. Little Lily, with her crutches beside her, sat down as if her strength had been replaced by something gentler, and held out her arms to Gerard with a gesture so full of love it hushed even Giles for a blink. Catherine, at first benumbed by the shower of gold that had fallen on her apron like a sudden harvest, broke into tears.

“Leave kissing him, Kate,” she cried, half laughing, half sobbing, the old maternal jealousy rising like yeast. “He is my son, not yours. Ah, Gerard! my boy! I have not loved you as you deserved.”

Gerard slid from his chair and threw himself on his knees beside her as if the floor were a chapel. She flung her arms round him, weeping for joy and pride upon his neck; and the sound of her crying was not sorrowful but rich—like a pot boiling over because it is full.

“Good lad! good lad!” cried Eli from the doorway of the shop, drawn in by the commotion, his voice thickened with feeling he tried to keep stern. “I must go and tell the neighbours. Lend me the medal, Gerard; I’ll show it my good friend Peter Buyskens; he is ever regaling me with how his son Jorian won the tin mug a shooting at the butts.”

“Ay, do, my man,” Catherine said, wiping her cheeks and suddenly proud as if she had baked the bread that fed the whole street. “And show Peter Buyskens one of the angels. Tell him there are fourteen more where that came from. Mind you bring it me back!”

“Stay a minute, father,” said Gerard, and his joy deepened, for there was yet something beneath it like a sweeter seasoning. “There is better news behind.”

“Better! better than this?” Eli stared, as a man stares who thinks his table already groans with food and is told there is another course.

Then Gerard told them of his interview with the Countess, and the house rang with joy—joy that was not only loud but echoing, as if it bounced off the clean tiles and came back larger. To Catherine it sounded like the end of fear.

“Now God bless the good lady,” she cried, “and bless the dame Van Eyck! A benefice? our son! My cares are at an end. Eli, my good friend and master, now we two can die happy whenever our time comes. This dear boy will take our place, and none of these loved ones will want a home or a friend.”

From that hour the family’s speech changed, as a kitchen changes when a new spice is brought in: everything is flavored by it. Gerard was looked on as the stay of the house. He was a son apart—beloved, leaned upon, indulged. What had been shared before now began to be measured: what was “for Gerard,” what suited “his future.” Cornelis and Sybrandt, hearing that new tone, grew jealous; their faces tightened when his name was praised, and in their hearts they longed for the day he should go to his benefice—get rid of the favourite, and have his reverence’s purse open to them. With those views, Gerard himself unconsciously worked; for love, which had once made his blood leap, now throbbed dully like a bruise that heals but remembers.

He thought more highly of himself, as youths do when the world has clapped them on the back; and he resented with more spirit Margaret’s ingratitude and discourtesy. Yet the memory of her had power to chill him toward the rest of her sex; a wound, even when it closes, teaches the skin. For every reason—hurt pride, new ambition, the comfort of being needed—he wished to be ordained priest as soon as he could pass the intermediate orders.

He knew the Vulgate already better than most clergy about that country, and he studied the rubric and the dogmas with his friends the monks. Their Latin, their church-words, their solemn phrases came to him like a borrowed tongue—useful, authoritative, and yet, when he lay awake, not quite fitting the innermost parts of his thought the way his own dialect did. Certain feelings—shame, longing, the taste of his mother’s stew, the sound of Kate’s crutch on tile—would not translate cleanly into the Church’s dominant speech. But he pushed that unease down, as one pushes down a stubborn loaf, because the form must be made.

The first time the bishop came that way, Gerard applied to be admitted exorcist, the third step in holy orders. The bishop questioned him and ordained him at once. Gerard had to kneel; after a short prayer the bishop delivered to him a little manuscript full of exorcisms—compact as a spice-box, potent by reputation—and said, “Take this, Gerard, and have power to lay hands on the possessed, whether baptized or catechumens!”

Gerard took it reverently. The words rang in his ears on the walk home, mixing with the village’s familiar sounds—the lowing of cattle, the clack of a distant mallet, a lark falling through the air like a thread of song. The world was the same world of bread and tiles and neighbours’ gossip; yet now, by the Church’s utterance, he was invested with power to cast out demons. The thought sat oddly beside the homely: a holy book tucked under his arm, the smell of boiled onions from some doorway, his own hunger returning after excitement as hunger always returns.

As he came near his home, little Kate met him on her crutches, breathless with news. Her face shone, and the crutches clicked on the tiles within hearing, a small steady rhythm like a spoon against a bowl.

“Oh, Gerard!” she cried. “Who, think you, hath sent to our house seeking you?—the burgomaster himself.”

“Ghysbrecht Van Swieten!” Gerard stopped short. The name did not sound like joy; it sounded like iron laid down on wood. “What would he with me?”

“Nay, Gerard, I know not. But he seems urgent to see you. You are to go to his house on the instant.”

“Well,” said Gerard, and drew a breath as if bracing against wind, “he is the burgomaster: I will go; but it likes me not. Kate, I have seen him cast such a look on me as no friend casts. No matter; such looks forewarn the wise. To be sure, he knows.”

“Knows what, Gerard?”

“Nothing.”

“Nothing?”

“Kate,” he said, and though his voice was steady, something in it tightened like a cord, “I’ll go.”
