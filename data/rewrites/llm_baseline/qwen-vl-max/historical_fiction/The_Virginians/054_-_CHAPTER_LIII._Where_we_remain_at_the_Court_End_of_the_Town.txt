The previous evening, George Warrington had recounted his tale to Madame de Bernstein—though not in full, for the old lady dozed off repeatedly during his account. Only when he paused would she stir, murmuring, “Most interesting,” and urging him to go on. The young man fumbled through his words, stammering and blushing, clearly ill at ease. He spoke far less confidently than he had before his small, attentive audience in Hill Street—where Hetty’s wide-eyed wonder, Theo’s sympathetic gaze, his mother’s gentle smile, and his father’s amused expression had all served as encouragement enough. His father once joked that his wife’s tears were as powerful as Mr. Addison’s trunk-maker’s: if she were cast in a tragedy, she’d make it a success simply by weeping in the front row. That was why they’d chosen Lord Wrotham’s house as the stage for George’s first performance—to give him an audience that would truly appreciate him, not one like Madame Bernstein, whose indifference and weariness had dimmed his delivery.

“Very good and most interesting, I am sure, my dear sir,” said Madame Bernstein, raising three delicate fingers adorned with lace mittens to conceal a twitch of her lips. “And your mother must have been delighted to see you.”

George shrugged slightly and bowed low, catching his aunt’s sharp glance. “Have been delighted to see you,” she continued dryly, “and killed the fatted calf, and all that sort of thing. Though why I say *calf*, I don’t know, nephew George—for you were never the prodigal son. I may say *calf* to thee, my poor Harry! Thou hast been among the swine, surely. And evil companions have stripped you of your money and your coat.”

“He came to England, madam,” George replied, his voice rising slightly, “and his friends were yours.”

“Could not have come to worse advisers, nephew Warrington,” she snapped, lifting her chin. “I should have told my sister so earlier, had she condescended to write to me through him, as she has done through you.”

That night, as she prepared for a rout, she muttered to her maid: “This young gentleman’s mother is half sorry he’s alive again—I could see it in his face. She’s half sorry, and I am perfectly furious! Why didn’t he stay dead under that tree? And why did that young Florac carry him to the fort? I knew those Floracs in Paris, in the time of Monsieur le Régent. They were of the Ivry line—no great house before Henri IV. His ancestor was the king’s favourite. His ancestor—he! he!—his ancestress! Brett! *Entendez-vous*? Give me my card-purse. I don’t care for this young Monsieur George’s airs. Yet he bears a striking resemblance to his grandfather—the same look, sometimes the same tone. You’ve heard of Colonel Esmond when I was young? This boy has his eyes. I suppose I liked the Colonel because he loved me.”

Engaged in a card party—an amusement she never missed, whether weekday or Sabbath, so long as she could hold her trumps or sit upright—Madame Bernstein soon dismissed her two nephews after George finished speaking. To the elder, she offered a formal curtsey and a few fingers; but to Harry, she gave both hands and a warm pat on the cheek.

“My poor child,” she said, “now thou art disinherited, thou wilt see how differently the world treats thee. There is only one person in all London—a wicked, heartless old woman—who will treat thee as before.” She handed him a pocketbook. “Do not lose it at Ranelagh tonight. That suit does not become your brother nearly as well as it did you. You shall present your brother to everyone and walk up and down the room for at least two hours. Were I you, I would then go to the Chocolate-House and play as if nothing had changed. While you are there, your brother may return to me and eat a bit of chicken. My Lady Flint gives dreadful suppers, and I wish to discuss your mother’s letter with him. *Au revoir*, gentlemen!”

She departed for her toilette, her chairmen and torchbearers already waiting at the door.

The brothers went to Ranelagh, where only a few of Harry’s acquaintances happened to be present. They walked the rounds, met Mr. Tom Claypool and some countrymen, listened to music, and drank tea in a box. Harry acted as host, introducing George to the marvels of the place. George, in turn, was more excited than Harry had ever been on his own first visit—thrilled by the music, especially the full orchestra and a satisfying rendition of Handel. It was notable, too, that Harry showed respect for George’s passion for music at a time when playing the violin was considered effeminate and unmanly in England. Patriotic prints mocked such “frivolous accomplishments” as those of Squallini or Monsieurs. But today, no Briton boasts of ignorance. Conceit is rare. Dullness is unknown. Arrogance is extinct. Art, once an outsider, now holds near-equal status. If Mrs. Thrale were to marry a music master today, her friends would not flinch. If she had fortune and a good cook, people might even dine with her—despite the match—and treat Mr. Piozzi with civility.

After Ranelagh, following Madame Bernstein’s advice, George returned to her house while Harry went to the club, where gentlemen gathered nightly to dine and gamble. No one mentioned Harry’s recent absence. Mr. Ruff, his former landlord, greeted him with solemn courtesy, as though nothing had passed between them. Harry had already removed his belongings from Bond Street, and the brothers now lived elsewhere.

When supper ended and the men prepared to ascend to the gaming tables, Harry declared he would not play again. “I’ve burned my fingers already,” he said. “I can afford no more extravagance.”

“Why,” said Mr. Morris lightly, “you must have won more than you lost, Mr. Warrington, all things considered.”

“And of course I don’t know my own affairs as well as you do, Mr. Morris,” Harry replied sternly, remembering Morris’s coldness upon hearing of his arrest. “But I have another reason. A few months—or days ago—I was heir to a vast estate. I could afford to lose a little. Now, thank God, I am heir to nothing.”

He looked around, flushing slightly, at the group of gamblers lounging about the tables or near the fire.

“What do you mean, Mr. Warrington?” asked Lord March. “Have you lost Virginia too? Who has won it? I always fancied playing you myself for that stake.”

“And grow an improved breed of slaves in the colony,” added another.

“The rightful owner has won it,” Harry said. “You’ve heard me speak of my twin elder brother?”

“The one who died in Braddock’s affair two years ago? Yes. Good heavens, I hope he hasn’t come back to life!”

“He arrived in London two days ago. He was imprisoned in a French fort for eighteen months, escaped recently, and left our house in Virginia shortly after.”

“You haven’t had time to order mourning, I suppose?” asked Mr. Selwyn cheerfully. Simple Harry barely understood the joke until his brother explained it.

“Hang me, if I don’t believe the fellow is actually glad his brother’s come back!” exclaimed Lord March, as the conversation continued after Harry had left.

“These savages still practice simple virtues—affection, for instance,” yawned Selwyn. “They’re barely civilized in America yet.”

“They love their kin,” piped Mr. Walpole, “and scalp their enemies. It’s not Christian, but natural. Shouldn’t you like to witness a scalping-match, George? See a man skinned alive?”

“A man’s elder brother is his natural enemy,” said Mr. Selwyn calmly, arranging his counters.

“Torture is like broiled bones and pepper,” Horry continued. “You wouldn’t enjoy hanging afterward, George!”

“I’m hanged if there’s any man in England who’d want his elder brother alive,” declared Lord March.

“No more than his father, my lord!” cried Jack Morris.

“First time I’ve known you had one, Jack. Give me counters for five hundred.”

“I say,” Jack went on, “it’s all very fine about dead brothers coming back to life. But who’s to say it wasn’t a scheme between these two? Here comes a young fellow calling himself the Fortunate Youth, claiming to be a Virginian prince and heaven knows what, and getting into our society—”

A laugh broke out at Jack’s phrase “our society.”

“Who’s to know it wasn’t a cross?” Jack pressed. “The younger one comes first. He marries an heiress. Then, when he’s secured her, up rises the elder brother! When did this elder brother appear? Just when the younger’s plan was blown and all was lost! Who’ll tell me the older brother hasn’t been living in Seven Dials, eating tripe and cow-heel, until the younger was disposed of? Dammy, as gentlemen, I think we ought to take notice. This Mr. Warrington has taken a most outrageous liberty with the whole club.”

“Who put him up? Was it March?”

“Yes. But my lord thought he was putting up someone else. Didn’t you, March?”

“Hold your confounded tongue and mind your game!” the nobleman snapped.

Yet Jack Morris’s view found many allies. Many believed it indecorous for Harry to have ever accepted his brother’s death. The young man’s sudden appearance and behavior seemed suspicious. In short, there was growing unease about foreigners, adventurers, and such. One must be cautious.

Though Harry was free from prison, possessed Madame Bernstein’s generous gift, and had his brother restored—without once lamenting his return, as his friends at White’s imagined he would—though Maria had shown remarkable grace through his ordeal—yet when alone, Harry felt no joy. He smoked his pipe of Virginia tobacco with a troubled heart.

It was not the loss of his inheritance that distressed him. He knew his brother would share fairly, as he would have shared with him. But after all his struggles, doubts, and hopes, to find himself poor and bound irrevocably to his elderly cousin—yes, elderly indeed. When she visited that dreadful den in Cursitor Street and her tears washed away her rouge, she looked as old as his mother—her face wrinkled, yellowed. Thinking of her brought the same queasy feeling he’d felt when she fell ill in the coach en route to Tunbridge.

What would his mother say when he brought her home? What battles would ensue? He might flee to one of the plantations—far from home—and manage a few slaves, farm modestly, hunt often. But at Castlewood, or in any home he could build for her—what a life for poor Maria, who had grown up attending court, cards, balls, assemblies every night?

If only he could be overseer of the estates! He would be an honest factor, make up for his wasted years and reckless spending. Five thousand pounds—his entire fortune, accumulated over his minority—gone in six months! He was a beggar, except for George’s kindness. All he had left was an old wife—though a lovely one, dressed in velvet and silver lace, the poor lad in his best clothes. What a figure he had cut in Europe! What a fine end he had come to!

With all his grand friends at White’s and Newmarket, with all his extravagance—had he been happy a single day since arriving in Europe? Three days. Four. Yesterday evening, when he was with dear Mrs. Lambert and her kind daughters, and that brave Colonel. And the Colonel was right to rebuke him for his wasteful ways. He had been a brute to fly into a rage. God bless them all for their generosity!

These thoughts filled Harry’s pipe, and he smoked them quietly, waiting for his brother’s return from Madame Bernstein’s.
