The crowd had barely moved on when Romola stepped down from the church steps and hurried toward San Stefano. Cecco had followed the throng into the Piazza, leaving Baldassarre alone by the doorway, clutching his horn cup as he waited for her. There was a marked change in him—his earlier vacant stare, like that of a man half-awake from a dream, had vanished, replaced by an intensity so sharp it seemed to burn. As Romola approached and spoke, his eyes locked onto hers with sudden fury, a wildness that suggested a beast seeing its prey just beyond reach. She flinched at the look, but instantly thought: *He has seen Tito.* And in that moment, a flicker of hope stirred within her—perhaps this man was the one who had wronged her husband, not Tito. If she could confront them both now, perhaps she could find truth, and peace.

“If you will come with me,” she said gently, “I can give you shelter and food until you are strong again. Will you come?”

“Yes,” he replied, voice low and strained. “I shall be glad to get my strength.” He repeated the words as if rehearsing them, more to himself than to her.

She gestured for him to walk beside her, choosing the quieter path along the Arno toward Ponte Rubaconte. As they crossed the bridge, she ventured, “I think you are not from Florence.”

He turned sharply, silent, wary. The fog of confusion still clung to him, thickened by weakness, and suspicion guarded every word. But Romola’s gaze held a softness that finally broke through. After a pause, he answered cautiously: “No. I am no Florentine. I am a lonely man.”

She noticed his reluctance and dared not press further—fear that he might turn away. Yet as they walked, her thoughts raced. If this man were innocent, why the secrecy? Why the dread? Her fragile hope began to fade. Silence stretched between them until they reached the entrance to Via de’ Bardi. Then, suddenly, he stopped and looked at her as though struck by a memory.

“Ahh!” he said, before she could speak. “You are his wife.”

“Whose wife?” she asked.

He could not name him. The image of Tito filled his mind so completely that words failed. He only stared at her, eyes fixed, unblinking.

Romola opened the gate to her courtyard. Inside, straw covered the ground, where several sick lay resting. Children crawled among them, tiny and pale, nibbling straws, babbling softly. She stepped forward. “Come in,” she said, voice trembling. “I’ll find you a place to rest and bring you food.”

“No,” he said, stepping back. “I will not come in.”

But he did not move. He stood rooted, overwhelmed by sensations too chaotic to process. She reached into her purse and offered him several _grossi_. “Let me give you money. You can buy food. It will be easier soon.”

He studied the coins, then took them. “Yes,” he said quietly. “I will take them.”

She poured the coins into his hand. He gripped them tightly, as if they were already a weapon.

“Tell me,” she pleaded, “what shall you do?”

But he turned and walked away, heading back toward the bridge. He passed it without hesitation, continuing straight up Via del Fosso until he came upon Niccolò Caparra’s shop. The master was gone—marching with the armourers—but an apprentice remained. Weapons hung everywhere. Baldassarre’s eyes found what he sought: a small, sharp hunting knife. The boy sold it for three _grossi_, no questions asked.

Baldassarre slipped the blade into his tunic. It felt alive against his chest—a companion, a power. Even if it failed, it was better than nothing. It might shatter on armor, but would armor always be there? In prison, during those long months of waiting, pride may have grown careless. The knife had been bought with the traitor’s own money—that was justice.

Before taking the coin, he had known what he would do: buy a weapon. And food—yes, food too. To strengthen the body that carried vengeance. When he had eaten enough, he would think. First, how to hide—lest Tito have him seized again.

With that thought, he turned into narrow alleys, bought bread and meat, and sat beneath a loggia to eat. The bells rang louder, swelling with joy, shaking the air. To him, they were only part of a world that mocked him.

Romola watched until he disappeared around the corner into Piazza de’ Mozzi. Part of her felt relief—another part reproached herself for not pressing harder, for not knowing whether this man’s suffering was innocent, or whether she had failed him. What could she have done if the truth revealed something terrible about Tito? Could she bear it? Was she not allowed to wish ignorance, when silence was her only defense?

Yet the question stirred too many emotions. She pushed it aside. Now was a time for joy—the city was saved. She entered the courtyard, closed the door to block out the bells, and removed her black veil. Standing in the center, she told the sick and children that grain was coming, that Florence rejoiced.

They sat up, children scrambling to her side, pulling at her clothes, their small hands reaching for her face. She sat down, exhausted, letting them tug at her hair, peer into her basket. Around her, weak voices murmured: “Praised be the Holy Virgin!” “It was the procession!” “The Mother of God has taken pity on us!”

At last, she rose, too weary to smile. “I will return soon with your dinner,” she said, climbing the stone steps.

“Bless you, madonna! Bless you!” they called, in the same tone they’d used for the unseen Madonna moments before.

Romola cherished that sound. She did not naturally delight in caring for the sick or mending rags. Her upbringing had kept her apart from such duties. Without deeper purpose, these tasks would have felt burdensome. But now they were her anchor, the only place where clarity remained.

When doubt crept in—when the distance between her and Tito grew wider, when conversations with Fra Salvestro or followers of Savonarola left her feeling suffocated by narrowness, when old contempt for superstition surged—she returned here. Among the poor, her faith renewed itself. Helping others became proof that Savonarola had been right to call her back. Her place had never been empty. It had been filled with love and labor. Florence needed her. And the more her sorrow deepened, the greater her joy in remembering the countless hours she had eased another’s pain.

Her passion, once spent on family affection, had transformed into a fierce compassion for all humanity. She no longer believed her own life could be happy—nor did she seek happiness. Her purpose was simpler: to lessen sorrow.

Savonarola inspired this fire. Though she recoiled from his repetitive visions and allegories when others spoke them, his voice stirred her soul. His anger at corruption, his zeal for justice and reform, ignited her own spirit. His vision of Florence’s liberty tied to a broader renewal gave her a sense of belonging in history. Through daily contact with the suffering, this vision hardened into action—not sentiment, but resolve.

She rarely questioned doctrine. She avoided pondering his prophecies of divine judgment and rebirth. Instead, she trusted him. Not because of intellect, but because of emotion—because his nature moved her deeply. She listened patiently to his teachings, not for logic, but for the fire behind them. No soul is truly desolate as long as it holds trust in another. For Romola, Savonarola was that figure—a rope suspended across her path, steady and strong. Without it, even solid ground might not save her from falling.

And so, she continued—walking through sorrow, guided by compassion, sustained by faith.
