At the base of the staircase, Mr. Gryce stepped aside, summoning two or three men he had stationed outside to escort the valet away before ushering Miss Butterworth back into the study. Once the room fell silent and they were alone, Mr. Gryce broke the tension with a decisive statement.

"One critical matter has been resolved by our latest experiment," he announced. "Bartow is cleared of any involvement in this crime."

"Excellent," Miss Butterworth replied, her shoulders relaxing slightly. "Now we can focus entirely on the young people. Have you received any word from them?"

"No."

"Nor from the elderly man who arrived shortly after?"

"No."

A shadow of disappointment crossed her face. "I had hoped... it seemed so likely—that the fragment of writing you discovered would identify them. If it was vital enough for a dying man to attempt swallowing, surely it must hold a clue to his attacker."

"Regrettably, it offers no such lead, madam. It was a hurried scrawl, reading something like this: 'I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!' Signed, 'Amos's son.'"

"Amos's son! That is Mr. Adams himself."

"Indeed, we have ample reason to believe it is."

"Extraordinary! Unfathomable! And yet, that very paper was found clenched between his teeth. Was the handwriting verified?"

"Yes, it matches his signature on the fly-leaves of his books."

"The mysteries only deepen," Miss Butterworth murmured. "The fact that he hid this note in such a bizarre manner—between his teeth—suggests he considered it the only place safe from search, even in his final moments. And the girl! Upon regaining consciousness, her first words were: 'You have left that line of writing behind.' Mr. Gryce, those words, sparse and enigmatic as they are, seem to hold the key to the entire situation. Would you repeat them, sentence by sentence, if you please?"

"With pleasure, madam; I have rehearsed them often enough in my own mind. First: 'I return your daughter to you!'"

"So," she mused, pacing slowly. "Mr. Adams was holding someone else's daughter. Whose? Certainly not that young man's, for that would imply she was a child. Furthermore, if these words were intended for his assailant, why conceal them so meticulously from him?"

"True. I have pondered that myself."

"Then, if not for him, for whom? Perhaps for the elderly gentleman who entered later?"

"It is a distinct possibility. Since learning of his arrival, I have considered this scenario, especially given the subsequent phrase: 'She is here.' The only woman present just prior to the old man's entrance was the light-haired girl you witnessed being carried out."

"Valid reasoning. However, why treat this note as if it were written moments ago? It could be an old scrap, referencing past sorrows."

"These words were penned that very afternoon. The paper was torn from a sheet on the desk, and the pen lay discarded at the extreme edge of the table, out of its natural place."

"I noticed, though I did not grasp the significance until now. So, these words concern the girl I saw. And she is not Evelyn, for otherwise he would not have invoked the bird's catchphrase, 'Remember Evelyn!' Do you suppose she is Evelyn?" Miss Butterworth asked, gesturing to the single large portrait dominating the wall.

"For the moment, we may assume she is. Such a melancholy visage suggests a painful family secret. But how do we account for the violent role played by the young man, who is omitted from these abrupt sentences? It remains a riddle, madam, one we are wasting time attempting to solve."

"Yet I refuse to abandon it without a struggle. There were other words you did not repeat."

"They preceded the command, 'Remember Evelyn!' They spoke of resolve: 'Neither she nor you will ever see me again.'"

"Significant indeed," Miss Butterworth observed. "Could he have dealt the fatal blow himself? Might this have been a suicide after all?"

"Madam, you are entitled to ask, but Bartow's pantomime indicates otherwise. He mimics a maddened thrust from an outraged hand, not a self-inflicted wound. Let us adhere to our initial conclusions. However, to remain fair to every possibility, the state of Mr. Adams's affairs and the total absence of family documents in a wealthy man's desk prove he prepared for a potential demise. It may have arrived sooner or differently than anticipated, but it was a thought he entertained. Moreover, I must confess a lapse in my judgment regarding my most valued colleague. The study—that most remarkable of rooms—harbors a secret withheld from you. A peculiar one, revealed to me in a startling fashion. This room can, or rather could, be entirely sealed off from the rest of the house, transformed into a death-trap or a tomb where this incomprehensible man intended to end his life. Observe this steel plate. It is operated by a mechanism that slides across this doorway. I stood behind it last night; these holes were forced to allow my escape."

"Ha! Detectives have their own trials," Miss Butterworth laughed, rubbing her eyes. "I should not have enjoyed that particular evening with you. But what an old-world tragedy we unearth here! I feel as if transported back to medieval times. Who says we live in New York, within earshot of cable cars and telegraph wires?"

"Some men possess the ability to bring the medieval era to Wall Street. I believe Mr. Adams was one of them. Romanticism colored every act, even the manner of his death. Nor did it cease with his passing; it followed him to the grave. Witness the cross we found resting on his bosom."

"That was the work of another hand, born of another's superstition. It indicates the presence of a priest or a woman at the moment of his death."

"Yet," Mr. Gryce continued, with a tone of mild surprise, "he must have possessed a grain of hard sense. All his contrivances functioned. He was a mechanical genius as well as a lover of mystery."

"A strange combination. It is odd that we do not feel his spirit permeating the air of this study. I almost wish we did; perhaps it would guide us to the solution."

"That," Mr. Gryce noted, "can be achieved in only one way. You have already identified the path. We must locate the young couple present during his death struggle. Without them, the case is hopeless."

"And so," she said, "we circle back to the starting point, proving we are hopelessly lost in the woods." Miss Butterworth rose, feeling that for the moment, her resources had been exhausted.

Mr. Gryce made no move to detain her. In fact, he seemed eager to depart. They paused long enough to cast one final glance around the room. As they did, Miss Butterworth's finger slowly rose.

"Look!" she exclaimed. "From this angle, you can hardly perceive the opening created by removing that picture on the stair landing. Wouldn't you say it lies amidst those folds of dark tapestry up there?"

"Yes, I had already pinpointed that spot. With the picture hung on the opposite side, it would be completely invisible."

"One must keep one's eyes moving in a case like this. That picture must have been drawn aside several times while we were in the room, yet we failed to notice."

"That was because we were not looking high enough. High and low, Mr. Gryce! What occurs at eye level is obvious to everyone."

The smile with which he acknowledged this parting observation and prepared to escort her to the door held less irony than sadness. Was he beginning to realize that years tell on even the most sagacious, and that neither high places nor low depths would have escaped his attention a dozen years prior?
