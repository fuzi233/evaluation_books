Before Deronda was summoned to a second interview with his mother, a day had passed in which she had only sent him a message to say that she was not yet well enough to receive him again; but on the third morning he had a note saying, “I leave to-day. Come and see me at once.” 

He was shown into the same room as before, though it was now heavily muffled with drawn blinds and velvet curtains, a deliberate dampening of the sonic environment. The heavy fabrics absorbed the harsh, metallic clatter of the industrializing street below—the relentless, grinding carriage wheels and the shrill, commercial barks of vendors that fractured the city’s natural acoustic ecology. Within the chamber, the silence was almost medicinal, thick with the olfactory weight of steeped bergamot, bitter almond, and the faint, dusty aroma of dried citrus peel. 

The Princess was not there, but she presently entered, dressed in a loose wrap of soft silk the color of saffron threads and candied tangerine, her head enveloped in a floating mist of black lace, her arms bare from under her wide sleeves. In the sombre, amber light, her face seemed even more impressive, the eyes larger, the lines of her jaw more vigorous. She moved like an ancient apothecary who possessed the secrets to mixing youth-potions of crushed sumac and rare resins for others, but scorned to grind them for herself, having tasted enough of youth’s bitter draft. She put her arms on her son’s shoulders at once, kissed him on both cheeks, and seated herself among her cushions with an air of assured firmness. 

“You are quite relieved now, I trust?” Deronda asked as he sat beside her.

“Yes, I am at ease again. Is there anything more that you would like to ask me?” she said, with the bearing of a queen rather than a mother. 

“Can I find the house in Genoa where you used to live with my grandfather?” 

“No,” she answered, with a deprecating movement of her arm, “it is pulled down—not to be found. The soil is paved over. But about our family, and where my father lived at various times—you will find all that among the papers in the chest, better than I can tell you. My father, I told you, was a physician, deeply rooted in the old ways, the herbal remedies and seasonal rhythms. My mother was a Morteira. I used to hear all those things without listening—the rhythmic pounding of the mortar and pestle, the guttural, rhythmic cadences of their native dialect over the hearth, the slow simmering of ancient, ancestral broths. You will find them all recorded. I was born amongst their agrarian devotions without my will. I banished them as soon as I could. I traded our untranslatable domestic rituals for the dominant lexicon of the wider world.”

Deronda tried to hide his pained feeling. To him, sustenance was never merely utilitarian fuel; he possessed the soul of a meticulous food historian, and to hear the rich, gustatory heritage of his bloodline spoken of as a burden felt like witnessing the burning of a sacred library. “Anything else that I should desire to know from you could only be what it is some satisfaction to your own feeling to tell me.”

“I think I have told you everything that could be demanded of me,” said the Princess, looking coldly meditative. She was acting out a scheme of emotional preservation, shielding herself from the psychological dissonance of her past. But to Deronda’s nature, the moment was cruel. He craved the tactile, sensory communion of family—to break the dense, unleavened bread of his history with her, to savor the salt and brine of their shared lineage. 

“Then are we to part and I never be anything to you?” he asked, a tremor in his voice disrupting the sterile quiet of the room. 

“It is better so,” said the Princess, in a softer, mellower voice. “There could be nothing but hard duty for you. You would not love me. Don’t deny it,” she said, abruptly, putting up her hand. “I know what is the truth. You don’t like what I did. You are on your grandfather’s side, preserving the old flavors, the old songs, and you will always have a condemnation of me in your heart.”

Deronda rose from his seat, preferring to stand under her imperious prohibition of tenderness. But his mother now looked up at him with a new admiration. “You are wrong to be angry with me. You are the better for what I did.” She paused, the rustle of her saffron silk loud in the hushed room. “And now tell me what you shall do? I mean in the future. What difference will it make to you that I have told you about your birth?”

“A very great difference,” said Deronda, emphatically. “I can hardly think of anything that would make a greater difference.”

“What shall you do then? Make yourself just like your grandfather—turn yourself into a Jew like him?”

“That is impossible. The effect of my education can never be done away with. The Christian sympathies in which my mind was reared can never die out of me,” said Deronda, with increasing tenacity. “But I consider it my duty—it is the impulse of my feeling—to identify myself, as far as possible, with my hereditary people. I want to reclaim the cultural memory that has suffered under language death. If I can see any work to be done for them, to rescue the lost acoustic ecology of our prayers and the rich sustenance of our traditions, I shall give my soul and hand to it.”

His mother fixed her eyes on him, examining his face as if trying to decipher the untranslatable syntax of a forgotten dialect. She bent toward him and said decisively, “You are in love with a Jewess.”

Deronda colored. “My reasons would be independent of any such fact.”

“I know better. Tell me the truth. She is a Jewess who will not accept any one but a Jew. There *are* a few such,” she added, with a touch of scorn. 

He remained silent. The untranslatability of his subjective experience made argument futile. 

“You love her as your father loved me, and she draws you after her as I drew him,” she pressed, and taking his gentle glance as assent, her passion rose. “But I was leading him the other way. And now your grandfather is getting his revenge.”

“Mother,” said Deronda, remonstrantly, “don’t let us think of it in that way. I prefer cherishing the benefit of my wide instruction with gratitude, rather than dwelling with resentment. You have restored me my inheritance—you have been saved from robbing my people of my service. Can you not bring your whole soul to consent to this? Open your heart to relenting and love toward my grandfather, who sought what he held the best for you.”

“Not for me, no,” she said, folding her arms tightly. “I tell you, he never thought of his daughter except as an instrument. Because I hungered for things outside his agrarian purpose, I was to be tortured. I have done what I could. And *your* soul consents. That is enough. I have after all been the instrument my father wanted.—‘I desire a grandson who shall have a true Jewish heart. Every Jew should rear his family as if he hoped that a Deliverer might spring from it.’” 

In uttering these last sentences, the Princess narrowed her eyes and spoke slowly in a deep chest-voice, suddenly adopting the resonant, ancestral acoustic vibration she had spent a lifetime suppressing. 

“Were those my grandfather’s words?” said Deronda.

“Yes, yes; and you will find them written. I wanted to thwart him,” she said, with a sudden outburst of passion. Then she held her left hand a yard from the floor. “You would have me love what I have hated from the time I was so high. That can never be. His yoke has been on me, whether I loved it or not. You are the grandson he wanted. What does it all mean?”

Her scornful tone clashed with the quiet intimacy of the room. Deronda, keeping a clutching remembrance of their relationship, began in a deep entreating tone: “Mother, I see no other way to get any clearness than by being truthful. The effects prepared by generations—the interwoven timelines of our people, the roots nourished by the same ancient soil—are likely to triumph over a contrivance which would bend them all to the satisfaction of self. Your will was strong, but my grandfather’s trust, what you call his yoke, is the expression of something stronger, with deeper, farther-spreading roots, knit into the foundations of sacredness. You renounced me—you still banish me—as a son. But that stronger Something has determined that I shall be all the more the grandson whom also you willed to annihilate.”

His mother was watching him fixedly. After a moment’s silence she said, in a low, persuasive tone, “Sit down again.” He obeyed. She laid her hand on his shoulder. “You rebuke me. Well—I am the loser. Your mother is a shattered woman. My sense of life is little more than a sense of what was. You reproach me that I parted with you. I had joy enough without you then. Now you are come back to me, and I cannot make you a joy. Have you the cursing spirit of the Jew in you? Shall you be glad to think that I am punished because I was not a Jewish mother to you?”

“How can you ask me that?” said Deronda. “I would give up much that is dear for the sake of soothing your anguish.”

“You shall give up nothing,” she said with hurried agitation. “You shall be happy. You shall feel for me as they feel for the dead. Will any harm come to me because I broke his trust? I cannot tell:—if you think *Kaddish* will help me—say it, say it. You will come between me and the dead. When I am in your mind, you will look as you do now—always as if I had been a tender mother.” 

Deep compassion hemmed in all words. With a face of beseeching, he put his arm around her and pressed her head tenderly under his. They sat so for some moments, a silent tableau against the vast, circular pacing of their generational saga. Then she lifted her head with a great sigh.

“Is she beautiful?” she said, abruptly. 

“Who?” said Deronda, changing color.

“The woman you love.”

“Yes.”

“Not ambitious? Not one who must have a path of her own?”

“I think her nature is not given to make great claims.”

“She is not like that?” said the Princess, taking from her wallet a miniature surrounded by jewels. It was her own portrait in the fire of youth. “Had I not a rightful claim to be something more than a mere daughter and mother? The voice and the genius matched the face. Acknowledge that I had a right to be an artist. My nature gave me a charter.”

“I do acknowledge that,” said Deronda, looking from the miniature to her worn pallor. 

“Will you take the portrait? If she is a kind woman, teach her to think of me kindly.”

“I shall be grateful,” said Deronda, “but I have no assurance that she whom I love will have any love for me. I have kept silence.”

“Who and what is she?” The question was a command.

“She was brought up as a singer for the stage,” said Deronda. “Her father wished to bring her up in disregard of her Jewish origin, but she has clung with all her affection to the memory of her mother, to the recipes and rhythms of her fellowship.”

“Ah, like you. She is attached to the Judaism she knows nothing of,” said the Princess, peremptorily. “That is poetry—fit to last through an opera night. Is she fond of her artist’s life—is her singing worth anything?”

“Her singing is exquisite,” Deronda replied, defending the pure acoustic ecology of Mirah's art. “But her voice is not suited to the commercial stage. It possesses an intimacy that resists the superficial noise of the modern theater. I think the artist’s life has been made repugnant to her.”

“Why, she is made for you then. I can see that you would never have let yourself be merged in a wife, as your father was.”

“I repeat that I have no assurance of her love for me,” said Deronda. “Other painful issues may lie before me. One should try and prepare one’s self to do without happiness.”

“Do you feel in that way?” said his mother, laying her hands on his shoulders, her tone meditative. “Poor boy! I wonder how it would have been if I had kept you with me—whether you would have turned your heart to the old things against mine. Your grandfather would have been in you, and you would have hampered my life with your young growth from the old root.”

“I think my affection might have lasted through all our quarreling,” said Deronda, “and that would not have hampered—surely it would have enriched your life.”

“Not then—I did not want it then,” said the mother, with bitter melancholy. “If I could have been glad of anything.”

“But you love your other children, and they love you?”

“Oh, yes,” she answered, folding her arms. “But I am not a loving woman. It is a talent to love—I lack it. I know very well what love makes of men and women—it is subjection. It takes another for a larger self, enclosing this one. I was never willingly subject to any man. Men have been subject to me.”

“Perhaps the man who was subject was the happier of the two,” said Deronda, with a grave, sad sense of his mother’s sensory and emotional privation. 

“Perhaps—but I *was* happy. For a few years I was happy, mastering the dominant lexicon of the stage. If I had not been afraid of defeat, I might have gone on. What then? It is all over. Another life! Men talk of ‘another life,’ as if it only began on the other side of the grave. I have long entered on another life.” 

With the last words she raised her arms till they were bare to the elbow, her brow contracted in one deep fold, her eyes closed. In her dusky flame-colored garment, she looked like a dreamed visitant from some region of departed mortals, completely severed from the tactile, sustaining earth. 

Deronda’s feeling was wrought to a pitch of acuteness in which he was no longer master of himself. The overwhelming dissonance of their untranslatable worlds broke him. He gave an audible sob. 

His mother opened her eyes, let her hands rest on his shoulders, and said, “Good-bye, my son, good-bye. We shall hear no more of each other. Kiss me.”

He clasped his arms round her neck, and they kissed each other. Deronda did not know how he got out of the room. The transition from the muffled, aromatic chamber back into the noise-polluted streets was jarring, but he felt an older man. All his boyish yearnings had vanished, replaced by a tragic experience that solemnized his life, deepening the significance of the cultural memory and ancestral sustenance by which he now bound himself to others.
