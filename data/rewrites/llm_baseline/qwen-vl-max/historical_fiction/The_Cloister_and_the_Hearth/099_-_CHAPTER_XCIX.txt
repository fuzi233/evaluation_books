Jerome remained as stern and reserved in temperament as ever, yet his heart had softened toward Gerard. During the time he had spent in England, he had missed him more than he dared admit, and since then, the thought of his younger brother’s fate had haunted him. What Jerome heard in Gouda—especially that Gerard had stood firm against the Pope and the Minorites over what he called “the infernal heresy of the immaculate conception”—only elevated him in Jerome’s eyes. But when a young monk, weeping, revealed the true cause of Gerard’s illness, Jerome’s disdain flared anew.

“Dying for a woman?” he muttered, shaken by the idea. He resolved to prevent this disgrace. Twice each day he visited Clement in his cell, wielding all the authority and eloquence of his years in an effort to rouse him from his spiritual despair. He urged him not to abandon the Church, not to let worldly sorrow rob it of one of its most gifted and devout sons at such a pivotal moment.

Gerard listened with quiet reverence. He agreed with Jerome’s logic, admired his strength, confessed his own frailty—but still, he grew weaker, inching closer to the threshold of eternity.

One day, Jerome told him he had learned the full story—and done so with pride. “But now,” he said, “you undo it all. This is the triumph of earthly love. Better to have yielded, repented, and lived; than to resist while she lived, and surrender now, body and soul.”

“Dear Jerome,” replied Clement gently, his voice soft and free of reproach, “you do me injustice. There is no passion here—only deep affection, and I will not be ashamed of it, neither here nor in heaven. Think: is the dog who dies grieving at his master’s grave guilty of passion? Neither am I. Passion might have saved my life—but destroyed my soul. She was my guardian angel. She upheld me in duty, in charity. Her face gave me courage in the pulpit; her voice comforted me in times of ingratitude. She wove herself into every good thing I did. After leaning on her so long, I could not go on alone. And know this, Jerome—I am no rebel against God. It is His will that I depart. When they covered her coffin with earth, something within me broke—a bond too sacred to mend. I heard it, felt it. From that moment, no food tasted like anything. If I were blind, I could not tell bread from flesh by taste.”

“Holy saints!” murmured Jerome.

“And yet,” continued Gerard, “from that same hour, my sorrow lifted. I smile again—often. Why? Because I believe God, who holds life and death in His hands, gave me that moment—the great summons. A cord of life snapped within me. He is merciful. I would have lived in pain; but He said, ‘Enough has been done, enough suffered. Poor, weak, loving servant, your failings are forgiven. Your sorrows lead you to rest. Come to Me.’ I come, Lord. I come.”

Jerome groaned. “The Church has always known holy men of fragile hearts,” he said. “I would give ten years of my life to save yours. But I see it cannot be. Die in peace.”

And so, within days, Gerard lay dying—not in torment, but in serene joy, so radiant that even seasoned monks gathered to hear his final words.

That evening, he saw Giles and begged him to care for poor Jack, to ensure little Gerard’s trustees fulfilled their duties, to kiss his parents for him, and to send Denys to his friends in Burgundy. “Poor boy,” he whispered, “he’ll feel lost without his comrade.”

Then came a private meeting with Jerome. What passed between them was never fully known, but it must have been profound—so deeply moved was Jerome that he left the chamber with hands crossed over his chest, head bowed, and breath drawn in quiet sighs.

Two monks stayed through the night, watching vigil. All through the hours, Gerard spoke softly—prayers, praises, thanksgivings. Once, he wandered, seeing her walking in green meadows, surrounded by spirits in white robes, beckoning him forward. They smiled and gestured. The monks smiled too, touched by the vision.

And just before dawn, three slow, light taps sounded against the wall—one after another. Gerard heard them, and whispered, “I come, love, I6 I come.”

This much is certain: those words were spoken, and then he prepared to leave.

He summoned all the monks keeping watch. They came, hovering like gentle angels, whispering holy prayers. Some knelt with faces to the ground, others cradled his head with tender hands. When one began praising his life of self-denial and charity, Gerard interrupted.

“My dear brothers,” he said, “take note: he who dies here in such peace does not hold these modern notions of human merit. Oh, how terrible it would be if I did! No—like the Apostles, like the early Church fathers, I believe we are justified not by our wisdom, piety, or good works, but by faith.”(1)

Silence fell. The monks exchanged glances.

“Please sweep the floor,” he said, his voice clear and strong, as if restored by divine power.

They obeyed, awed and curious.

“Make me a great cross with wood ashes,” he added.

They formed a large cross upon the floor with ashes.

“Now lay me down upon it,” he said. “So shall I die.”

They lifted him gently from the bed and placed him upon the ash-cross.

“Shall we stretch out your arms, dear brother?” one asked.

“God forbid!” he replied. “Am I worthy of that?”

He lay silent, eyes raised upward in rapture.

Then, half to them, half to himself, he whispered, “Oh—how buoyant it feels. It lifts me floating in the sky, where my merits once dragged me down like lead.”

Daylight broke. His face was turned upward, hands clasped—just as Margaret’s had been. And near the hour she died, he spoke his last word.

“Jesu!”

With that, he fell asleep.

They dressed him for burial. Beneath his linen, they found a horsehair shirt.

“Ah!” cried the young monks. “Behold a saint!”

But beneath the hair cloth, they discovered a thick, long tress of auburn hair.

They recoiled. Horror seized them. Voices rose in protest and defense.

Just then, Jerome entered. Hearing the commotion, he turned to Basil, a zealous young monk shouting about scandal.

“Basil,” he said, “is the woman who owned this hair alive or dead?”

“How can I know, father?”

“Then for all you know, it may be the relic of a saint.”

“Perhaps,” Basil replied skeptically.

“You have broken our rule,” Jerome declared. “It says: 'Do not interpret any brother’s act with evil intent when it may be understood innocently.' Who are you to judge such a man? Go to your cell. Stay there a week in penance.”

He took the lock of hair and carried it away.

When the coffin was ready to close, Jerome cleared the room and laid the tress upon the dead man’s chest.

“There, Clement,” he whispered to the still face.

Then he fastened the coffin shut himself, performing a penance for the act.

The next day, Gerard was buried in the churchyard of Gouda. The monks followed in solemn procession from the convent. Jerome led the service, clearly acting on the deceased’s wishes.

The grave was deep. At its bottom rested a lead coffin. Gerard’s own, light as a feather from his wasting, was lowered beside it.

After the rites, Jerome addressed the crowd of mourners. As he spoke of the departed’s virtues, loud sobs and tears erupted—tears falling upon the coffin like rain.

The monks returned to the cloister. Jerome gathered them in the refectory.

“We have laid a saint in the earth today,” he said. “The convent will keep his trentals—but we shall feast, not fast. For our beloved brother is freed from the burden of flesh. His labors are ended. He has entered into joyful rest. I alone shall fast and do penance—for I confess, to my shame, I judged him unfairly, and only knew his worth when it was too late.

“And you, young monks—do not speculate whether that lock of hair was a token of love or a relic of sanctity. Instead, look to the heart he wore beneath it. Above all, study his life, his words, his deeds. Follow them if you can—for he was a holy man.”

Thus, after the fever of mortal life, these true lovers found peace.

The grave, kinder than the Church, united them forever.

And now, a man of another age and land, stirred by their story, has sought to build their tombstone, to rescue them from unjust oblivion.

He asks not your pity, but your sympathy—for their rare constancy, their pure devotion, and the cruel separation imposed by a vile heresy(2) within the Church itself.

No, do not mourn their early deaths. Their end was happy. Let their lives teach you: on earth, obstacles often stand between us and perfect joy. Let their passing strengthen your reason and your faith—preparing you for the inevitable.

For if you cannot bear the thought that these two died young, remember: had they lived a hundred years, they would still be dead. How then shall you endure when those you love most—gentle, true, faithful—slip from your arms into the grave, and fly from your home to heaven?

Such is the lot of all.

In every age, the Master of life and death—kinder, wiser than we—has taken the sweetest flowers of earth and transplanted them to heaven.

Let their story stir your heart, not your grief.

Beati sunt qui in Domino moriuntur.

(1) He quoted Clement of Rome: “Not by our own wisdom, nor by our piety, nor by our works done in holiness of heart, but by faith.” —Epistle to the Corinthians, i. 32.

(2) Celibacy of the clergy—an invention truly fiendish.
