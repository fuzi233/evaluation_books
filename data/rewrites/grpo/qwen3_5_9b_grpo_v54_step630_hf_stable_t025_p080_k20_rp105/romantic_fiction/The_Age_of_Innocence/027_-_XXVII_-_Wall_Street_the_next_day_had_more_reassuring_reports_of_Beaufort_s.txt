Wall Street the following day offered a glimmer of reassurance regarding Beaufort's precarious position. While the reports remained vague, they were undeniably hopeful. It was widely whispered that he had successfully leveraged powerful connections during an emergency, and indeed, that evening, when Mrs. Beaufort arrived at the Opera adorned with her familiar, radiant smile and a striking new emerald necklace, society collectively exhaled in relief.

Yet, New York remained a city of ironclad judgment regarding financial impropriety. Its unwritten law dictated that those who violated the sanctity of probity must pay the ultimate price, and everyone knew that neither Beaufort nor his wife would be spared this sacrifice. However, the prospect of offering them up was not merely painful; it was deeply inconvenient. Their sudden disappearance would leave a gaping void in their tightly knit social circle. Those too oblivious or indifferent to feel genuine horror at the impending moral catastrophe lamented in advance the loss of what was considered the finest ballroom in New York.

Archer had resolved to travel to Washington. He awaited only the filing date of the lawsuit he had discussed with May, hoping to align his visit with the legal proceedings. Yet, on the following Tuesday, Mr. Letterblair informed him that the case might be delayed for several weeks. Undeterred, Archer returned home that afternoon with the firm intention of departing the very next evening. He reasoned that May, unaware of his professional life and never having shown interest in it, would likely remain ignorant of any postponement, nor recall the names of the litigants if mentioned. More importantly, he could no longer delay seeing Madame Olenska; there were too many words he needed to speak to her.

On Wednesday morning, however, Mr. Letterblair met Archer at his office with a face etched in distress. Beaufort, it turned out, had failed to "tide over" the crisis. By circulating rumors of his recovery, he had temporarily soothed his depositors, causing a flood of payments until the previous evening, when alarming reports resurfaced. Consequently, a bank run had commenced, threatening to close the doors before sundown. The most damning accusations were already swirling about Beaufort's treacherous maneuvering, promising a failure that would rank among the most discreditable in Wall Street history.

The magnitude of the disaster left Mr. Letterblair pallid and incapacitated. "I have witnessed worse in my time," he confessed, "but nothing as catastrophic as this. Everyone we know will be affected, directly or indirectly. And what of Mrs. Beaufort? What *can* be done for her? I pity Mrs. Manson Mingott as much as anyone; at her age, the repercussions of this affair are unpredictable. She always believed in Beaufort—she made a friend of him! Then there is the entire Dallas connection; poor Mrs. Beaufort is related to every single one of us. Her only recourse would be to abandon her husband, yet how can one possibly tell her so? Her duty lies at his side, and fortunately, she has always been blind to his private failings."

A knock interrupted his lament. Turning sharply, he demanded, "What is it? I cannot be disturbed."

A clerk entered bearing a letter for Archer and withdrew. Recognizing his wife's handwriting, the young man opened the envelope and read: *"Won't you please come up town as early as you can? Granny suffered a slight stroke last night. In some inexplicable manner, she discovered this awful news about the bank before anyone else. Uncle Lovell is away hunting, and the weight of this disgrace has left poor Papa so nervous he has a fever and cannot leave his room. Mamma needs you desperately, and I sincerely hope you can depart immediately and go straight to Granny's."*

Archer passed the note to his senior partner. Moments later, he was squeezed into a crowded horse-car, transferring at Fourteenth Street to one of the swaying omnibuses of the Fifth Avenue line. It was past noon when this arduous vehicle deposited him at old Catherine's residence. The sitting-room window on the ground floor, usually occupied by her commanding presence, was instead filled by the frail figure of her daughter, Mrs. Welland, who offered a haggard welcome upon catching sight of Archer. At the door, May awaited him. The hall bore the unnatural stillness of a pristine home suddenly invaded by illness: wraps and furs lay in disarray on the chairs, a doctor's bag and coat rested on the table, surrounded by a growing pile of unheeded letters and cards.

May appeared pale yet managed a smile. Dr. Bencomb, who had visited for the second time, held a more optimistic view, and Mrs. Mingott's indomitable will to survive was already beginning to influence her family. May guided Archer into the old lady's sitting-room, where the sliding doors to the bedroom were drawn shut, draped behind heavy yellow damask portières. Here, Mrs. Welland whispered the horrific details of the catastrophe in terrified tones. It appeared that the evening before, something dreadful and mysterious had transpired. Around eight o'clock, shortly after Mrs. Mingott concluded her customary game of solitaire, the doorbell rang. A lady, so heavily veiled that the servants failed to recognize her immediately, requested an audience.

The butler, hearing a familiar voice, threw open the sitting-room door, announcing, "Mrs. Julius Beaufort," before closing it again on the two women. They must have remained together for an hour. When Mrs. Mingott finally rang her bell, Mrs. Beaufort had already slipped away unseen. The old lady, white, vast, and terrifying, sat alone in her great chair, signaling the butler to assist her to her room. At that moment, though visibly distressed, she seemed in complete command of her faculties. The mulatto maid settled her in bed, served her tea as usual, arranged the room, and departed. But at three in the morning, the bell rang again. Hastening in at this unusual summons—for old Catherine usually slept like a child—the two servants found their mistress propped against her pillows, wearing a crooked smile, with one small hand hanging limp from its massive arm.

The stroke proved to be relatively minor, as she was able to articulate her thoughts and convey her wishes. Soon after the doctor's initial visit, she began to regain control of her facial muscles. Yet the alarm had been profound, and equally intense was the outrage when it became clear from Mrs. Mingott's fragmented phrases that Regina Beaufort had come to implore her—incredible audacity—to support her husband, to see them through, not to "desert" them, as she termed it, but to compel the entire family to shield and condone their monstrous dishonor.

"I told her: 'Honor has always been honor, and honesty honesty, in the Manson Mingott house, and will be until I am carried out of it feet first,'" the old woman had stammered into her daughter's ear, her voice thick with partial paralysis. "And when she said, 'But my name, Auntie—my name is Regina Dallas,' I replied, 'It was Beaufort when he covered you with jewels, and it must remain Beaufort now that he has covered you with shame.'"

With tears and gasps of horror, Mrs. Welland recounted this, blanched and shattered by the unprecedented necessity of confronting the unpleasant and the discreditable. "If only I could keep it from your father-in-law," she wailed. "He always says, 'Augusta, for pity's sake, do not destroy my last illusions.' How am I to prevent him from knowing these horrors?"

"After all, Mamma, he hasn't *seen* them," her daughter suggested gently. Mrs. Welland sighed. "Ah, no; thank heaven he is safe in bed. Dr. Bencomb has promised to keep him there until poor Mamma recovers, and Regina has been spirited away somewhere."

Archer sat near the window, gazing blankly at the deserted street. It was evident he had been summoned less for practical aid than for the moral support of the stricken ladies. Mr. Lovell Mingott had been telegraphed for, and handwritten messages were being dispatched to family members across New York. Meanwhile, the only activity was the hushed discussion of Beaufort's disgrace and his wife's indefensible actions.

Mrs. Lovell Mingott, who had been writing notes in another room, reappeared and joined the conversation. In their era, the elder ladies agreed, the wife of a man who committed a business disgrace had only one option: to efface herself and disappear with him. "There was the case of poor Grandmamma Spicer," Mrs. Welland noted, referring to May's great-grandmother. "Of course," she hastened to add, "your great-grandfather's financial difficulties were private—gambling losses or signing notes for others—I never fully knew, because Mamma would never speak of it. But she was raised in the country because her mother had to flee New York after the disgrace, whatever it was. They lived up the Hudson alone, winter and summer, until Mamma was sixteen. It would never have occurred to Grandmamma Spicer to ask the family to 'countenance' her, as I understand Regina calls it; though a private disgrace pales in comparison to the scandal of ruining hundreds of innocent people."

"Yes, it would be more becoming for Regina to hide her own countenance than to speak of others'," Mrs. Lovell Mingott concurred. "I understand that the emerald necklace she wore at the Opera last Friday had been sent on approval from Ball and Black's in the afternoon. I wonder if they will ever retrieve it?"

Archer listened impassively to the relentless chorus. The concept of absolute financial probity as the cornerstone of a gentleman's code was too deeply ingrained in him for sentimental considerations to weaken it. An adventurer like Lemuel Struthers might accumulate millions through shady dealings, but unblemished honesty was the *noblesse oblige* of old financial New York. Nor did Mrs. Beaufort's fate greatly move Archer. He felt, perhaps, more sorrow for her than her indignant relatives; yet it seemed to him that the bond between husband and wife, even if breakable in prosperity, should be indissoluble in misfortune. As Mr. Letterblair had noted, a wife's place was at her husband's side in trouble, but society's place was not. Mrs. Beaufort's cool assumption that it was seemed almost to make her his accomplice. The mere notion of a woman appealing to her family to screen her husband's business dishonor was inadmissible, for it was the one thing the Family, as an institution, could not do.

The mulatto maid summoned Mrs. Lovell Mingott to the hall, and she returned moments later with a frowning brow. "She wants me to telegraph for Ellen Olenska. I had written to Ellen, of course, and to Medora; but now it seems that is insufficient. I am to telegraph to her immediately, instructing her to come alone."

The announcement hung in the air, met with silence. Mrs. Welland sighed resignedly, and May rose to gather scattered newspapers.

"I suppose it must be done," Mrs. Lovell Mingott continued, as if hoping to be contradicted. May turned back toward the center of the room.

"Of course it must be done," she said. "Granny knows what she wants, and we must fulfill all her wishes. Shall I write the telegram for you, Auntie? If it goes immediately, Ellen can probably catch tomorrow morning's train." She pronounced the syllables of the name with a peculiar clarity, as if tapping two silver bells.

"Well, it cannot go at once. Jasper and the pantry-boy are both out with notes and telegrams."

May turned to her husband with a smile. "But here is Newland, ready to do anything. Will you take the telegram, Newland? There will be just time before luncheon."

Archer rose with a murmured agreement. May seated herself at old Catherine's rosewood "Bonheur du Jour" and wrote the message in her large, immature hand. Once finished, she blotted it neatly and handed it to Archer.

"What a pity," she remarked, "that you and Ellen will cross paths on the way!" Turning to her mother and aunt, she added, "Newland is obliged to go to Washington regarding a patent lawsuit coming before the Supreme Court. I suppose Uncle Lovell will return by tomorrow night, and with Granny improving so much, it does not seem right to ask Newland to forego an important engagement for the firm—does it?"

She paused, awaiting an answer. Mrs. Welland hastily declared, "Oh, of course not, darling. Your Granny would be the last person to wish it." As Archer left the room with the telegram, he heard his mother-in-law mutter, presumably to Mrs. Lovell Mingott, "But why on earth she should make you telegraph for Ellen Olenska..." before May's clear voice rejoined, "Perhaps it is to urge her again that after all, her duty lies with her husband."

The outer door closed behind Archer, and he walked hurriedly away toward the telegraph office.
