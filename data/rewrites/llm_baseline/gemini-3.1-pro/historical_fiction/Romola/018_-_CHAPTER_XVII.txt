The loggia at the pinnacle of Bardo’s house rose high above the suffocating, mercantile clatter of Florence, forming a quadrangular sanctuary where the cyclical rhythms of the seasons felt profoundly intimate. On the street side, classical columns supported the roof, while the rear wall, pierced by arched openings, offered Romola a sheltered promenade. Down below lay a dense sprawl of irregular dwellings, from which the sharp, metallic shrieks of industrial coinage and the grating grind of commercial mills rose like a caustic vapor. But up here, the acoustic ecology was softened by the gentle, rhythmic rustle of the wind sweeping toward the hill of Bogoli, carrying with it the grounding, everyday scents of the agrarian city: the yeasty breath of sourdough fermenting in communal ovens, the sharp tang of wild fennel, and the rich, roasting aroma of garlic bulbs blistering over open fires. 

Near one of the arched openings, bathed in a sickening slant of afternoon sunlight, Tito awaited her. He inhaled the ambient scent of sun-baked terracotta and crushed thyme, seeking an anchor for his fraying nerves. He had never relied on Romola’s passion to withstand the bitter tannins of his secret; he knew her love grew from the same deep, untarnished root as her belief in him. Yet, as he imagined her approaching in her radiant, tactile beauty—made so devastatingly mortal by the soft hazel of her eyes—he found himself wishing she were woven of coarser, simpler cloth. If she belonged to a lesser lineage, perhaps she would just let him clasp her, to consume the sweetness of her mouth before they parted. Their courtship had thus far been starved of sensory feast; he had known only the briefest appetizers—a lingering glance, the fleeting pressure of a hand, a chaste kiss. Now, they were to be alone, but he envisioned her standing inexorably aloof, a marble statue beyond the reach of human hunger.

His heart executed a heavy, percussive throb as the heavy oak door groaned on its iron hinges. Romola was there. It happened with the suddenness of a lightning strike: he felt, before he saw, the luminous grief surrounding her. He registered the untranslatable resonance of her cry—a raw, native dialect stripped of the dominant, polished Florentine lexicon—when she wept, “Tito!” 

In the next breath, she was in his arms, her sobs vibrating against his chest, a pure, unadulterated sound of sorrow that broke the quiet harmony of the loggia. How she had yearned through the dark, slow-simmering watches of the night to see his bright face. The mythic intrusion of death; the bewildering doubt infused into her by a brother whose life had become a forgotten language to her; the haunting vision that she had not only heard in Dino’s low, gasping breaths but had tasted, bitter as gall, as if it were her own. All of it made her acutely aware that it was Tito who had first brought the warm, nourishing broth of hope to her lips, Tito who had first blunted the keen, cutting edge of her familial grief. She needed to pour it all out to him; he was the only vessel left. For hours she had restrained her grief before her father, but now, the long-fermented sob finally burst forth. Proud and meticulously controlled before the world, Romola was as unguarded as a child in her hunger for Tito’s warmth. 

“My Romola! My goddess!” Tito murmured, his voice a rich, resonant hum against her ear. He clasped her gently, burying his face in the thick, golden ripples of her neck, breathing in the scent of sweet almond oil and warm wheat. He was in an earthly paradise. Disgrace, shame, the looming famine of parting—the fear of them evaporated. This sudden gustatory rush of happiness was too potent to be soured by the knowledge that Romola was consuming a grand deception; rather, he savored the sweetness of her delusion. The concealment had been the necessary yeast for this perfect loaf. 

When the sobbing finally spent itself, Romola raised her head. They stood in a suspended silence, enveloped in the sweet, sensory wonder of young love—her long, pale hands resting on his dark-brown curls, his olive fingers bathed in the streaming gold of her hair. The cold, heavy stone of sadness in Romola’s chest made her linger in that quiet acoustic space, drinking in the soothing proximity. Tito refrained from claiming her lips, fearing any sudden movement might shatter the delicate flavor of the moment.

“Tito,” she breathed at last, her voice a fragile timber, “it has been altogether painful, but I must tell you everything. Your vitality will help me digest these impressions that reason alone cannot metabolize.”

“I know, Romola—I know he is dead,” said Tito. His lustrous, dark eyes betrayed nothing of the secret wishes that would have hastened that death years ago, had mere desires possessed such potency. Romola only saw her own pure reflections in his gaze, reading his silence as one reads a beloved recipe in a happy dream.

“He was so changed, Tito! It pierced me to recognize Dino beneath that starved asceticism. And so strangely hard: not a word for my father, nothing but a vision he insisted on translating to me. Yet it was so piteous—the struggling, rattling rhythm of his breath, the eyes straining toward the crucifix as if trying to consume it, yet unable to see it. That disrupted, dying sound... I shall never forget it. It feels as if its shadow will fall over every meal, every joy I ever know.” Her throat tightened, forcing her to pause. But the urge to disburden her mind pressed the rising bile of anguish down. When she spoke again, the narrative had shifted to an even stranger terrain. “The vision was about our marriage, Tito. And yet, he knew nothing of your existence.”

“What was it, my Romola? Sit down and tell me,” Tito said, guiding her to a wooden bench smelling of old cedar. A sudden, discordant spike of fear struck him—what if the vision was not a mere monkish hallucination, but a whisper of Baldassarre? He needed to change their physical alignment to weather the potential blow.

Romola recounted everything from her entry into the incense-heavy air of San Marco, preserving every syllable of her brother’s dying dialect, words that had seared themselves like hot iron into her memory. But as the vision concluded, she faltered. The visceral, mythic weight of it crowded her senses, and she stared out toward the distant hills, momentarily deaf to Tito’s presence. 

Tito’s internal acoustics leveled into a sigh of relief. The vague prophecy had passed over him like a harmless steam, leaving no residue of his father’s name. He waited, silent, letting the ambient sounds of the city return.

“I took it,” she whispered, as if he had read her thoughts. “I took the crucifix. It rests down below in my chamber.”

“And now, my Romola,” Tito entreated, his tone rich and coaxing, “you must cleanse your palate of these ghastly flavors. The vision was but the ordinary hallucination of a starved monk, bred of severe fasting and fanatical deprivation. It surely holds no sustenance for you.”

“No, Tito; no. But poor Dino believed it was a divine dialect. It is strange,” she mused, her voice blending with the wind, “this life of men consumed by fervid beliefs that sound like sheer noise to the rest of us. Dino was not a vulgar fanatic. And that Fra Girolamo—his very voice, its deep, resonant frequency, seemed to penetrate my bones, carrying a sonic truth that bypasses the intellect. A truth in a native tongue I no longer understand.”

“It resonated only because your own emotional strings were pulled so taut, my Romola. Your brother’s mind was infected by the same abstract theosophy that excitable, dreamy men have ingested for ages—the same sterile, flavorless ideas your father’s old rival, Marsilio Ficino, pores over in the New Platonists. Your brother merely possessed the tragic appetite to act upon what others relegate to parchment. As for Fra Girolamo, he is but a narrow-minded ascetic who has mastered the harsh linguistics of terror to manipulate the unlettered crowds. Any deep, commanding noise would have shaken you in that cavernous church. Once you return to the wholesome nourishment of our daily life, you will recognize these echoes for what they are.”

“Not about poor Dino,” Romola insisted softly. “I was angry with him; my heart closed its doors against his words while he lived. But now, I cannot stop trying to decipher his intent. Oh, Tito, the tragedy of a young life withering like unwatered vines. That starving look toward the crucifix as his lungs failed—last night, I stared at that cross by the lamplight, trying to extract some essence of the pity he saw in it.”

“Promise me you will reject a diet of such morbid thoughts. They are sustenance for sickly, cloistered nuns, not for my golden-tressed Aurora, who was born to scatter the twilight and feast upon the day.” Tito reached out, his fingertips tracing the warmth of her jaw. “Try not to dwell on the bitter herbs now; soon, we shall never be parted.”

His touch broke the spell of the chapter house, pulling her from the pale, scentless images of sorrow back into the vibrant, sun-drenched ecology of the loggia. In his rich, dark beauty, Tito seemed to gather all the abundant, sensory joys of the earth—the heavy, purple clusters of fermented grapes festooning the elms, the golden grain swelling beneath the vibrating heat of the sun, the percussive, joyous beating of feet upon the earth, and the light, acoustic thrill of stringed instruments singing in harmony. He was a sun-god, oblivious to the famine of the night. 

A profound, dizzying vertigo seized Romola. How could she reconcile the clashing mythologies of her world? On one side, the agonizing, ascetic starvation of her brother, straining after invisible nourishment; on the other, this radiant, gourmand vitality of Tito, bursting with the juices of life. Was there no translation between the two? Must humanity blindly worship at the altar of one, only to wail at the feet of the other? This hunger for meaning compressed the philosophical theorizing of generations into a single, aching pang within her chest. But the pang dissolved as the dawn of Tito’s loving, radiant gaze washed over her, replacing the grey ashes of grief with the flush of youth.

“Your mind lingers far from our feast, my Romola,” Tito murmured with soft reproach, the acoustic equivalent of a minor chord. “Our love seems a forgotten dialect to you.”

She looked into his beseeching eyes until the shadows melted from her own. “My joy!” she said, her voice finding its clear, resonant bell-tone once more.

“Do you care for me enough to banish the chill of the grave, or will you forever suspect me as the Great Tempter offering you the forbidden fruit?” he smiled, his teeth flashing white against his olive skin.

“How could I hunger for anything more than you? Everything I endured before—the dry, dusty lexicon of my father’s ancient library, the acoustic void of my loneliness—was merely the long, slow preparation to love you. You would laugh, Tito, if you knew the sterile, flavorless companion I once thought I would marry: some grey-haired scholar like Alamanno Rinuccini, who would agree with my father’s Aristotelian arguments and quietly consume his life among the scrolls. I read of banquets and passionate loves in the poets, but I never dreamed the aroma of such a life could permeate our old house in Florence. And then you arrived, bringing a completely new language to my father and proving to me that life could be a rich, satisfying feast.”

“My goddess! There is no woman whose vintage compares to yours,” Tito breathed, marveling at the seamless blend of majestic depth and earthly simplicity within her. “But, dearest,” he ventured delicately, “if you truly thirsted for our marriage, you would urge your father and Messer Bernardo to abandon these agonizing delays. Yet you seem content to let the fruit wither on the vine.”

“I do mind, Tito. I ache for it. But my godfather will undoubtedly advocate for a longer maturation now, to honor Dino’s passing. He, like a stubborn custodian of old traditions, never agreed with my father’s harsh disowning of Dino, and he insists a union must be aged properly—that we wait until you have been rooted in Florentine soil for at least a year. Do not judge Messer Bernardo too harshly. His lexicon is old and rigid, yes, but he possesses a profound agrarian nobility. He understands the power of preserving what has been, just as he honors my father’s obsessive desire to keep the library intact as a monument to our name, even though he finds the hoarding of unread books to be folly. To respect a dialect you do not speak—that is a rare and noble thing.”

“I bear no rancor toward Messer Bernardo for believing you too rare a delicacy to be consumed in haste,” Tito replied, and for once, his words carried no deception. “But your father—he knows his son is dead?”

“Yes. I told him. I could not suppress the words. I described the room, the scent of the herbs, the final, struggling breaths, but nothing of the vision. He immediately commanded me to strike the subject from our daily speech. He has remained trapped in a terrible silence all morning, pacing with restless, heavy steps that disrupt the entire house. It is as if he is trying to batter down the walls of his sightless prison, yet he lacks the vocabulary to mourn. Let us go to him now. I convinced him to rest, for he spent the night starving for sleep. Your voice, Tito, with its smooth, unbroken melody, always serves as a balm to him.”

“And not a single kiss to sustain me? I have been fasting,” Tito said in a tone of gentle, dependent reproach, a boyish charm that excused his presumption. 

A blush the color of ripe peaches spread over Romola’s cheeks and neck as she bent her head toward his. It seemed utterly impossible that the sensory explosion of their kiss could ever degrade into a mundane routine.

“Let us walk one circuit around the loggia,” Romola whispered as they parted, “before we descend into the shadows.”

“There is an unyielding, commercial grimness to Florence,” Tito remarked as they paused at the front parapet, gazing out over the terracotta roofs toward the Arno. The city’s sonic landscape rose up to them—the harsh, biting clatter of merchants and the shrill cries of street vendors. “Even its celebrations sound metallic, driven by the grind of commerce rather than the rhythm of the heart. I long to pull you away to Southern Italy. There, the acoustic world is not fractured by exhaustion, but suspended in a delicious, sun-drenched languor that these fiercely sharp Florentine minds can never comprehend. I want to see you reclining in a grove of ancient olives, the air thick with the scent of wild lemons and roasting lamb, subdued into the pure, tactile joy of existence. I would sit beside you, touching the lute, singing a soft, unconscious dialect that requires no translation—only the shared understanding of light, warmth, and the taste of salt on our skin. You have never been permitted to taste the happiness of the nymphs, my Romola.”

“No,” she murmured, her gaze drawn to the horizon. “But I have dreamt of that feast often since you arrived. I am deeply thirsty for that draught of joy, for a life brilliantly flavored, like you. But we must not dwell on it now, Tito. It feels as though, no matter how rich the banquet, there will always be pale, sad faces lingering among the flowers, their unfed eyes looking on in vain. Come. Let us go down.”
