Shortly after Colonel Altamont's stroke of luck at Epsom, he executed his plan for a continental tour. The chroniclers of high society, those who descend to London Bridge to bid farewell to the fashionable exiles, reported that aboard the *Soho* bound for Antwerp last Saturday sat "Sir Robert, Lady, and the Misses Hodge; Mr. Serjeant Kewsy, and Mrs. and Miss Kewsy; Colonel Altamont, Major Coddy, and others." The Colonel traveled in full state, befitting a gentleman of his station. He donned a rich traveling suit, consumed brandy-and-water with gusto throughout the voyage, remained perfectly sober while others suffered seasickness, and was accompanied by his personal attendant: the loyal Irish legionary who had served him and Captain Strong in their Shepherd's Inn chambers.

Before departing, the Chevalier shared a hearty dinner at Blackwall with his leaving friend, the Colonel, along with a couple of others. They toasted Altamont's health repeatedly at the Colonel's expense. "Strong, old boy," the Chevalier's reliable companion declared, "if you find yourself needing a bit of cash, now is your lucky hour. I'm your man. You've been a good chap to me, and a twenty-pound note makes no difference to me." But Strong demurred. He didn't need money; he was flush, quite flush—"though perhaps not flush enough to repay your last loan, Altamont, but certainly able to carry on for a while." With a greeting that wasn't entirely cordial, they parted ways.

Had the possession of money truly made Altamont more honest and amiable, or merely seemed so to Strong? Perhaps the Colonel was genuinely better, wealth having polished his character. Or perhaps it was simply the radiance of riches that Strong admired. Inside himself, Strong reasoned, "This poor devil, this unfortunate outcast, a returned convict, is ten times the man my friend Sir Francis Clavering, Bart. possesses. He has pluck and a code of honor. He sticks to friends and faces enemies. The other lacks the courage to do either. What casts a shadow over this poor soul? He was merely reckless and signed his father-in-law's name. Many men have done worse and walk tall today. Clavering does not. In fact, he never held his head up in his prime."

Strong perhaps regretted the lie he'd told the open-handed Colonel—that he wasn't in want of funds. It was a lie born of honesty; the Chevalier simply couldn't bring himself to borrow again from his outlawed friend. Besides, he could manage. Clavering had promised money—though promises from Clavering were rarely kept—but the Chevalier was optimistic, trusting in various chances to intercept stray remittances meant for his patron, a task in which Mr. Strong primarily engaged.

Though he had complained about sharing rooms with Altamont at Shepherd's Inn, the Chevalier found the solitude drearier without his partner. His social nature craved company, and he had developed extravagant, luxurious habits thanks to a servant who ran errands, arranged his toiletries, and cooked his meals. Now, seeing the portly, handsome gentleman paint his own boots and fry his own mutton chops was a rather grand and touching sight.

It was previously noted that the Chevalier had a wife, a Spanish lady from Vittoria, who had returned to her family after a few months of marriage, having broken the Captain's skull with a dish. He began to wonder if he shouldn't return to see his Juanita. Since the Colonel's departure, the Chevalier grew melancholy—or, in his own vivid phrasing, "down on his luck." Such moments of depression and bad fortune happen constantly in the lives of heroes: Marius at Minturnae, Charles Edward in the Highlands, Napoleon before Elba. What great man hasn't faced adversity?

No supplies came from Clavering anytime soon. The twenty-five pounds, or "pony," the exemplary Baronet had received from Mr. Altamont, had vanished from Clavering's possession as quickly as many previous ponies. He had recently joined a select group of sportsmen who evaded the police to land in Essex, where they staged a boxing match featuring Billy Bluck versus Dick the Cabman, backed by the Baronet. For thirteen rounds, Dick dominated until an unlucky blow to the windpipe claimed his life.

"It's always my luck, Strong," Sir Francis lamented. "The odds were three to one on the cabman, and I felt as secure of thirty pounds as if it were in my pocket. Dammit, I owe my man Lightfoot fourteen pounds now—he lent and paid for me—and he duns me, that confounded impudent blackguard! I wish to heaven I knew how to draft a bill or squeeze a little something out of my lady! I'll give you half, Ned, upon my soul and honor. I'll give you half if you can get anyone to advance us a fifty."

"Ned" responded sternly that, as a gentleman, he had given his word of honor not to participate in any further financial dealings involving her husband (who had also given his word). The Chevalier pledged to keep his vow, promising to polish his own boots for the rest of his life rather than break his promise. Furthermore, he vowed to warn Lady Clavering that Sir Francis intended to betray his faith toward her the moment he detected such intent.

Armed with this intelligence, Sir Francis Clavering, per usual, erupted in tears and curses. He spoke of death as his only recourse, imploring his dear Strong, his best friend, his dearest Ned, not to abandon him. Yet, as he descended the stairs of Shepherd's Inn, he swore and blasphemed against Ned as the most infernal villain, traitor, blackguard, and coward under the sun, wishing Ned were buried in a worse place, provided the rascal lived long enough for Frank Clavering to exact his revenge.

Inside Strong's chambers, the Baronet encountered a gentleman whose visits to Shepherd's Inn had become increasingly frequent: Mr. Samuel Huxter, of Clavering. This young man, who had once poached walnuts in Clavering Park in his youth and watched the Baronet parade through town with four horses and powdered footmen, held immense respect for his Member and delighted greatly in making his acquaintance. He introduced himself with flushed cheeks and trembling hands as a "Clavering man"—son of Mr. Huxter of the marketplace, whose father had tended Sir Francis's gamekeeper, Coxwood, when his gun burst and severed three fingers. Proud to meet Sir Francis, he delivered this introduction with much affectation. Sir Francis received it affably. Honest Huxter then praised Sir Francis to the lads at Bartholomew's and told Fanny in the lodge that, ultimately, nothing beat a thoroughbred, a proper old English gentleman of the old school.

Fanny retorted that she thought Sir Francis was an "ojous" creature—she didn't know why—but she couldn't abide him. She was sure he was wicked, low, and mean; she knew it. When Sam countered that Sir Francis was very affable and had kindly borrowed half a sovereign from her, Fanny burst into laughter, tugged at Sam's long hair (which lacked impeccable cleanliness), patted his chin, and called him a "stoopid, stoopid, old foolish stoopid." She added that Sir Francis was always borrowing money from everyone, noting that Mary had actually refused him twice and made him wait three months to retrieve seven shillings he'd borrowed from her.

"Don't say 'er' but 'her,' 'borrer' but 'borrow,' 'actially' but 'actually,' Fanny," Mr. Huxter replied—not correcting her logic, but her grammar.

"Well then, 'her,' and 'borrow,' and 'hactually'—there then, you stoopid!" the other exclaimed. The scholar made such a charming face that the grammar master was instantly appeased and would gladly have taught her a hundred more lessons on the spot for the price of one.

Mrs. Bolton happened to be present, and presumably, by this time, Fanny and Dr. Sam were on exceedingly familiar and confidential terms. Time had brought Fanny certain consolations and soothed certain regrets that are deucedly bitter when they arise, yet no more painful than tooth-pulling or any other temporary pang; they are eternal, nonetheless.

Consider, sitting surrounded by respect and affection, honored and flattered in your old age; your foibles gently indulged, your slightest words kindly cherished, your garrulous tales received with dutiful forbearance and hypocritical smiles; the women of your house constant in their flatteries; the young men hushed and attentive when you speak; the servants awestruck; the tenants capping hats, ready to serve as mounts when your honor takes a drive—it has often struck you, O thoughtful Dives! that this respect and glory are largely transferred, with your fee simple, to your successor. Servants will bow, tenants will shout, for your son as for you. The butler will fetch him the wine (improved by aging) currently in your cellar. When your night comes, and the light of your life fades, as surely as the morning rises after you and without you, the sun of prosperity and flattery shines on your heir.
