Generous with his newly acquired fortune, hospitable and warm-hearted to all, our young Virginian, now fully embraced in the role of a gentleman of fashion, could not help but host an evening’s entertainment for his country friends at the Assembly Rooms. True to the customs of the day, he invited nearly every remaining guest from the Wells, ensuring no one was left out of the festivities.

Card tables were arranged in one room for those who found amusement in gaming—a pastime as common then across Europe as breathing. In another chamber, supper was laid out with generous quantities of champagne and bowls of negus, its rich, spiced warmth meant to comfort after a long day. The grand assembly hall itself was reserved for dancing, where Harry Warrington’s guests indulged in the social rituals of their ancestors—courteous, deliberate, and steeped in tradition.

Yet I cannot pretend the night was especially lively. First came the minuets, performed slowly and with solemn grace by a few couples. The most distinguished ladies took precedence: the spinsters of highest rank stepped forward first, and among them stood my Lady Maria Esmond, daughter of an earl and the most noble woman present—save for Lady Augusta Crutchley, who sat quietly aside due to her lameness. It was only natural that Mr. Warrington should open the dance with his cousin, and he did so with such elegance and poise that the entire room applauded silently. He danced more gracefully than Mr. Wolfe, who partnered Miss Lowther, and moved with a quiet confidence that drew admiration.

Having completed the first minuet, Harry courteously asked Miss Theo to join him for the next. She blushed, delighted, and glided through the steps with joy, winning smiles from her parents and sharp envy from Miss Humpleby, daughter of Sir John Humpleby of Liphook, who had hoped to follow Lady Maria herself.

After the minuets came country dances, played upon a harp, fiddle, and flageolet perched in a small balcony above the dancers. Their music, though feeble and somewhat melancholy, echoed through the room like a sigh from the past. One need only pick up an old manuscript and play these tunes today to understand why they seem so sorrowful. And yet, people once danced, laughed, courted, and flirted beneath their sad strains. There is a faint bitterness in almost every air—an *amari aliquid*, a trace of sorrow. Perhaps it is because they are relics now, cast into the limbo of memory, their echoes calling softly from beyond time. Or perhaps they were once bright and joyful—just as future generations may one day listen to our modern music and marvel: “Good heavens! Was this truly what amused our ancestors?”

Among the guests was none other than the Duchess of Queensberry—Colonel Lambert’s and Mr. Prior’s famed heroine. Though she turned her back on a countess present, laughed loudly, and pointed at her with her fan, the rest of the company bowed low and cringed before the countess, scarcely acknowledging the duchess’s presence or her jokes. This countess was none other than the Countess of Yarmouth-Walmoden, the lady whom His Majesty George the Second, King of Great Britain, France, and Ireland, Defender of the Faith, held in high favour.

She had met Harry earlier that morning in the walks and had been kind to him, even promising a game of cards that very evening. Poor Colonel Blinkinsop, mistaking the invitation for himself, bowed deeply. “Pooh! Pooh!” exclaimed the Countess, laughing. “I don’t mean you—I mean the young Virginian!”

And so, everyone congratulated Harry on his good fortune.

That evening, the whole world seemed to gather around my Lady Yarmouth—not out of mere politeness, but in deference to her royal status. My Lord Bamborough eagerly sought to partner her at quadrille. My Lady Blanche Pendragon, a model of virtue; Sir Lancelot Quintain, a paragon of knightly honour; Mr. Dean of Ealing, a celebrated preacher; and countless noblemen, generals, matrons, and spinsters—all vied for her attention, hoping for a smile, a nod, or just a chance to stand near her card table.

Lady Maria waited upon her with humble respect. Madame de Bernstein treated her with dignified courtesy. But Harry’s bow was no lower than propriety required—and yet, Miss Hester took offense.

During their dance, she scarcely spoke to him. When he led her to the supper-room for refreshments, she remained cold and distant. As they passed the Countess’s card table, the older woman called out cheerfully: “Mr. Warrington, does your little bird enjoy dancing?”

“I thank your ladyship,” replied Miss Hester sharply, tossing her head, “but I do not like dancing, nor cards.” Then, with a curtsey stiff as a cheese, she marched away.

Harry was deeply offended. Sarcasm from youth toward age wounded him. Flippant remarks directed at himself stung. Courteous in his own simple way, he expected the same in return.

Hetty knew exactly what she was doing. She saw the flush of anger rise on his face, caught the momentary fire in his eyes—but chose to wear her innocent smile. As they approached the sideboard, she casually remarked: “What a horrid, vulgar old woman she is—don’t you think?”

“Which woman?” he asked.

“That German woman—my Lady Yarmouth—everyone is bowing to.”

“She has been kind to me,” Harry said, grimly.

“And you’ve bowed to her too! You look as if your negus isn’t pleasant,” she continued lightly.

“It’s not very good,” he admitted, swallowing.

“And the custard is terrible! I swear it’s made with bad eggs!” she declared.

“I wish, Hester,” he said, voice low, “that the evening and the company had pleased you better.”

“It’s unfortunate,” she retorted, tossing her curls, “but I suppose you couldn’t help it.”

Harry groaned inwardly—perhaps aloud. He clenched his fists and teeth. The little tormentor pressed on: “You seem upset. Shall we go to your mother?”

“Yes,” he snapped, glaring. “Let us go to your mother.” And, turning sharply, he muttered under his breath: “Curse you—why are you always in the way?”—to a waiter who happened to be standing nearby.

“La! Is that how you speak in Virginia?” asked Miss Pertness.

“We can be rough there sometimes,” Harry replied slowly, his voice quivering, “and we can’t help being disturbed.” He looked down at her, eyes blazing.

Hetty saw nothing clearly after that—only a blur until she reached her mother.

Never had she seen Harry look so noble, so striking. “You look pale, child!” her mother fretted, as all mothers do when anxious. “It’s the heat—or the cold. Thank you, Mr. Warrington.” She offered a faint curtsey, while Harry returned a deep, formal bow and walked away, lost in thought.

At first, he barely registered what was happening—so great was his anger. But soon, another quarrel erupted—this time between his aunt and the Duchess of Queensberry.

As the royal favourite passed the duchess, the latter gave her a fierce stare—her eyes, once brilliant enough to “set the world on fire,” now dimmed by age. She turned to her neighbour with a forced laugh, then launched a steady stream of giggles and sneers at the Hanoverian lady.

The Countess continued her game, unaware—or unwilling to notice—the insults aimed at her. A feud had long existed between the two noblewomen.

“How you all bow down to the idol!” the Duchess declared. “Don’t tell me—you’re just as bad as the rest, my good Madame Bernstein!”

“Oh, what a Christian country this is!” the Baroness replied. “And how your dear first husband, the Bishop, would have loved to see such a sight!”

“Forgive me, Your Grace,” said Madame de Bernstein, “if I fail to understand.”

“We are both growing old,” the Duchess said, “or perhaps we simply choose not to understand. That’s how women are, my dear young Iroquois.”

“You mentioned it was a Christian country,” the Baroness reminded her. “But I fail to grasp the point.”

“Indeed,” the Duchess replied, “there is very little point! I meant only that we are such good Christians—we forgive so easily. Don’t you remember reading, when you were young, or your husband the Bishop preaching from the pulpit, about the Jewish woman caught in sin? The Pharisees wanted to stone her. But today? We don’t stone her—we adore her! Any man in this room would crawl on his knees if she bid him. Yes, Madame Walmoden, you may raise your painted face and frown with your painted brows at me. I know I’m talking about you—and I intend to keep talking. I say any man here would kneel round the room if you commanded it!”

“I think,” said Harry firmly, “I know two or three who wouldn’t.”

“Quick, let me hug them to my heart!” the Duchess cried. “Which ones? Bring them to me, my dear Iroquois! Let us play a game of four—of honest men and women. If we can find two more partners, Mr. Warrington!”

“We are three,” said the Baroness, forcing a laugh. “Let us play a dummy.”

“Where is the third?” the Duchess asked, scanning the room.

“Madam!” the Baroness replied, rising sharply. “I leave your Grace to boast of your honesty—which I have no doubt is unblemished. But I beg you not to question mine before my family and children!”

“See how she fires up at a word!” the Duchess chuckled. “I am sure, my dear creature, you are as honest as most of the company.”

“Which may not be good enough for Her Grace the Duchess of Queensberry and Dover,” Harry interjected. “She might have stayed away, but it is the best my nephew could offer—and the best he has given you. You look astonished, Harry, my dear—and well you may. He is not used to our ways, madam.”

“Madam,” the Duchess retorted, tapping her fan, “he has found an aunt who can teach him our ways—and much more! She will teach him to welcome all guests, young or old, rich or poor. That is the Virginian way, isn’t it, Harry? She will tell him that when Catherine Hyde is angry with his old aunt, they were friends as girls—and ought not to quarrel now that they are old women. And she will not be wrong, will she, Duchess?”

With that, one dowager made a perfect curtsey to the other—and the battle, poised to begin, dissolved into silence.

“Egad,” Chaplain Sampson exclaimed the next morning, as Harry recounted the evening’s events, “it was like Byng and Galissoniere!”

“No force on earth could have stopped them from going to war,” Harry said.

“Seventy-fours at least—both of them!” laughed the chaplain.

“But the Baroness avoided battle,” Harry added, “and sailed out of danger with unmatched skill.”

“Why should she fear?” Sampson asked. “I’ve heard you say your aunt is as witty as any woman alive—no dowager’s tongue could match hers.”

“Hem!” Harry murmured. “Perhaps she had good reason to stay peaceful.”

Sampson understood perfectly. He knew the Baroness’s reputation was tarnished, her name linked to whispers too dark to repeat. Any insult thrown at Madame Walmoden could just as easily reflect upon her.

“Sir,” Harry said suddenly, “you don’t mean to suggest there’s anything against my aunt’s character?”

The chaplain looked at him with such astonishment that Harry realized there must be some hidden history.

“Good heavens!” he groaned. “Are there two in the family…?”

“Which two?” the chaplain asked.

But Harry stopped, flushing crimson. He remembered—soon we shall learn—how he’d come by knowledge of the other family secret. He bit his lip and fell silent.

“Bygones are always unpleasant,” said the chaplain gently. “Best we say nothing. No one lives long in this wicked world without some scandal clinging to them. I fear our excellent Baroness has not escaped fate’s judgment. We cannot avoid calumny, my dear young friend. You’ve already tasted it in your short time here. But we can keep our consciences clear—that is what matters.”

He raised his eyes heavenward, trying to appear as pure as the ceiling above.

“Has there been something serious about my Aunt Bernstein?” Harry pressed.

“O sancta simplicitas!” the chaplain muttered. “Stories older than you or I. Tales told about everyone—*de me, de te*—with varying degrees of truth.”

“Confound the villain!” Harry exclaimed. “If anyone dares speak ill of that dear old lady, I’ll pull his nose—sure as my name is Harry Esmond.”

“The world,” said the chaplain, “is full of lies and gossip.”

“And you’re just beginning to learn it,” the clergyman added, smiling beatifically.

“Who hasn’t been attacked?” he continued. “My lord, you, me—everybody. We must endure and forgive as best we can.”

“You may,” Harry said. “It’s your cloth. But I won’t! If anyone speaks a word against her, I’ll give them a good shove!”

And with that, he slammed his fist down on the table.

“Hello, Colonel Lambert!” he called, as the officer entered. “You find us late again, sir. Me and the reverend kept it up with the young fellows after the ladies left. I hope the ladies are well?”

“Perfectly,” the Colonel replied, grinning. “Whose nose were you pulling when I came in, Mr. Warrington?”

“Isn’t it shameful?” Harry said. “The parson told me there are villains here who attack my aunt’s character!”

“You don’t mean to say so!” the Colonel exclaimed.

“I tell Mr. Harry that everyone is slandered,” said the chaplain, adopting a clerical tone—though he winked at the Colonel, meaning: *Keep him ignorant.*

The Colonel took the hint.

“Yes,” he said, “slander’s jaws are always moving. Take that story about the dancing girl—everyone believed it about you, Harry.”

“What—everyone?” Harry asked.

“No,” the Colonel corrected. “One didn’t—Hetty didn’t. You should have heard her defend you the other day when someone brought up another tale—about a game of cards on Sunday morning, when you and a friend might have done better.”

The chaplain nodded. “Faith, I confess it. It was *mea culpa*, *mea minima culpa*—only a rehearsal of an old piquet game we’d discussed.”

“And did Miss Hester stand up for me?” Harry asked.

“She did,” the Colonel said. “But why that look?”

“She scolded me last night,” Harry admitted. “Like—like nothing I’ve ever heard. She mocked everyone—young and old—until I had to tell her that in Virginia, young folks don’t talk to elders like that. And we had a sort of quarrel. I’m glad you told me she defended me.”

He shook the Colonel’s hand, emotion glowing in his eyes.

“You won’t come to much harm if your worst enemy is Hester,” the Colonel said gravely, watching Harry’s flushed face. *Is he fond of her? How fond?* he wondered. *He knows nothing. Hetty’s been playing her tricks.* Still, he admired the boy—honest, passionate, quick to feel.

Then the chaplain asked: “And the young lady of the minuet? She danced beautifully—everyone admired her.”

“I trust Miss—Miss—”

“Miss Theodosia is fine,” the Colonel said. “Ready to dance with you right now.”

“Or wait,” he added with a twinkle, “Chaplain—do you only dance on Sundays?”

Turning back to Harry, he said: “You handled the great lady very well, Mr. Flatterer. My Lady Yarmouth has been praising you at the Pump Room. She says she has a little boy in Hanover who looks just like you—and you’re a charming young man.”

“If she were queen,” the chaplain mused, “they’d treat her no better.”

“Call her a vice-queen, parson,” the Colonel joked.

“She pocketed forty of my guineas at quadrille,” Harry laughed.

“She’ll play you again on the same terms,” the Colonel said drily. “The Countess loves games—and wins from most.”

“Why not bet her five thousand on a bishopric?” Harry suggested. “I’ve heard of a clergyman who made such a bet, lost it, paid it, and got the bishopric.”

“Ah,” the chaplain said, “who will lend me the five thousand? Will you, sir?”

“No,” the Colonel replied firmly. “I won’t give her five thousand to become Commander-in-Chief or Pope. I won’t throw stones at her—but I won’t bow either. Not like the rabble. No offence—I don’t mean you. Or you, Harry. You were right to be civil—and lose your money with good humour.”

Then, with sudden seriousness, he said: “Harry, I’ve come to bid you farewell. Our pleasures are over—my money’s gone. We must return to Oakhurst. Will you visit us again?”

“Now, sir, now!” Harry said eagerly. “I’ll ride back with you!”

“No—now is not the time,” the Colonel said quickly. “We haven’t room—we’re expecting friends.”

[“The Lord forgive me for the lie!” he muttered.]

“But—when Tom’s home—yes, when Tom’s home,” the Colonel continued. “That’ll be fun. And I want you to know, sir, my wife and I love you sincerely. So do the girls—even if they scold you. And if you ever get into trouble—such things happen, Mr. Chaplain—you may count on me. Mind that, sir!”

He turned to leave—but Harry followed him downstairs, insisting on saying goodbye to the ladies.

Instead of going straight to the Colonel’s lodgings, the two men walked toward the common. From Harry’s window, the chaplain saw them in earnest conversation. At first, the Colonel smiled roguishly. Then, as the talk progressed, he threw up his hands in surprise and agitation.

“The boy is telling him,” the chaplain thought.

When Harry returned an hour later, he found the chaplain deep in sermon-writing. His face was grave, his mood somber. He threw down his hat, sank into a chair, and muttered something like a curse.

“The young ladies are leaving—and your heart is affected?” the chaplain asked, looking up.

“Heart?” Harry scoffed. “Which one is the conqueror? I thought the youngest’s eyes followed you about at the ball.”

“Confound the little termagant!” Harry burst out. “What does she mean by treating me like a fool?”

“And no man is,” the chaplain said wisely, “with a woman.”

“Ain’t they, Chaplain?” Harry growled, letting loose more curses.

“By the way,” the chaplain asked, “have you heard anything of your lost property?”

“No!” Harry snapped, using a word too strong for print.

“I suspect,” the chaplain said, “there was more money in that book than you care to admit. I wish I could find it.”

“There were notes,” Harry said gloomily, “and papers I regret losing. What in the devil has happened to it? I had it when we dined together.”

“I saw you put it in your pocket,” the chaplain recalled. “I saw you take it out and pay for a gold thimble and workbox at the toy shop—for one of your young ladies. Have you asked there?”

“Of course,” Harry said, sinking deeper into melancholy.

“Gumbo put you to bed—at least, I think he did. I was so upset myself, I barely remember. Can you trust those black fellows?”

“I can trust him with my head,” Harry said bitterly. “With my head? I can’t trust myself with it.”

“Oh, that a man should put an enemy into his mouth to steal away his brains!”

“You may call it an enemy, Chaplain,” Harry said. “Hang it—I’ve a mind to swear off drink forever. A man says anything when he’s drunk.”

The chaplain laughed. “You, sir,” he said, “are close enough.”

In truth, for the past few days, no amount of wine could make Harry speak freely when the chaplain touched on the subject of his loss.

“And so the little country girls are gone, or going?” the chaplain asked.

“They were sweet, fresh creatures,” he said. “But I think the mother was the finest of the three. I declare, a woman at thirty-five is at her prime. What do you say, sir?”

Harry glanced sideways at him.

“Confound all women,” he muttered. “I say!”

For which sentiment, every decent person will surely rebuke him.
