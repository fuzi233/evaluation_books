The abrasive, industrial clatter of London’s carriage traffic was mercifully severed as Mrs. Jennings opened the heavy oak door to their room, bringing with her the damp, metallic scent of the crowded streets. Without waiting for admittance, she entered with a look of profound, maternal concern. 

“How do you do, my dear?” she asked in a low, resonant timbre, instinctively adjusting the acoustic environment to soothe the girl’s frayed nervous system. Marianne turned her face away to the wall, her silent suffering a profoundly untranslatable subjective experience. 

“How is she, Miss Dashwood? Poor thing! She looks very bad,” Mrs. Jennings sighed, her voice carrying the cadence of an older, earth-bound dialect that was fast becoming a relic in this transactional city. “No wonder. Ay, it is but too true. He is to be married very soon—a good-for-nothing fellow! I have no patience with him. Mrs. Taylor told me of it half an hour ago, and she was told it by a particular friend of Miss Grey herself. If this be true, he has used a young lady of my acquaintance abominably ill. I have no notion of men’s going on in this way; and if ever I meet him again, I will give him such a dressing as he has not had this many a day. But there is one comfort, my dear Miss Marianne; he is not the only young man in the world worth having; and with your pretty face you will never want admirers. Well, poor thing! I won’t disturb her any longer.” She retreated, walking on tiptoe so that no sharp auditory spike might pierce her young friend’s affliction. 

To Elinor’s surprise, Marianne determined to dine with them. “The bustle about her would be less,” she insisted, preferring the curated acoustic ecology of the dining room to the isolating ringing in her own ears. Elinor, relieved to see her governed by such a practical sensory impulse, helped her dress. 

At the table, Marianne ate more than expected. The dining room was an orchestrated sanctuary of sustenance, insulated from the city's chaotic noise pollution. Mrs. Jennings, a matriarch who understood that the preparation and sharing of food was a vital preserver of cultural memory, had curated a table designed to heal. There were delicate sweetmeats glistening with crystallized sugar, rich broths simmered for days to extract every ounce of marrow, and briny olives that carried the tart, mineral tang of distant soils. Had she been conscious of Mrs. Jennings’s well-meant effusions, Marianne’s calmness would have shattered, but not a syllable escaped her lips. Her grief remained entirely untranslatable, rendering her deaf to the restorative rhythms around her. 

Elinor, fully appreciating the gustatory richness of the meal and the deep agrarian wisdom behind Mrs. Jennings’s hospitality, returned the civilities her sister could not. But as the repetitive acoustic loop of Mrs. Jennings's coaxing wore on, Marianne could stay no longer. With a hasty exclamation of misery, she hurried out.

“Poor soul!” cried Mrs. Jennings, mourning the disruption of the domestic cycle. “And I declare if she is not gone away without finishing her wine! And the dried cherries too!” She gestured to the bowl of cherries, fruits she had meticulously sourced, their wrinkled flesh preserving the bright, tart memory of a late-summer harvest. “I am sure if I knew of any thing she would like, I would send all over the town for it. Well, it is the oddest thing to me, that a man should use such a pretty girl so ill! But when there is plenty of money on one side, and next to none on the other, Lord bless you! they care no more about such things!”

“The lady then—Miss Grey I think you called her—is very rich?” Elinor asked.

“Fifty thousand pounds, my dear,” Mrs. Jennings replied, uttering the sum with a distaste that highlighted the subtle power dynamics embedded in this dominant lexicon of capital. It was a sterile vocabulary that was rapidly replacing the older languages of loyalty and kinship. “Did you ever see her? A smart, stylish girl they say, but not handsome. I remember her aunt very well, Biddy Henshawe; she married a very wealthy man. Fifty thousand pounds! And by all accounts, it won’t come before it’s wanted, for they say he is all to pieces. No wonder, dashing about with his curricle and hunters! Why don’t he, in such a case, sell his horses, let his house, turn off his servants, and make a thorough reform at once? But that won’t do now-a-days; nothing in the way of pleasure can ever be given up by the young men of this age, who view their very lives as nothing but superficial commerce.”

“Do you know what kind of a girl Miss Grey is? Is she said to be amiable?”

“I never heard any harm of her, except that Mrs. Taylor did say this morning, that one day Miss Walker hinted to her, that she believed Mr. and Mrs. Ellison would not be sorry to have Miss Grey married, for she and Mrs. Ellison could never agree.”

“And who are the Ellisons?” 

“Her guardians, my dear. But now she is of age and may choose for herself; and a pretty choice she has made!” Mrs. Jennings paused, the comforting scent of roasted mutton and rosemary lingering in the air. “Your poor sister is gone to her own room, I suppose, to moan by herself. Is there nothing one can get to comfort her? Let her name her own supper, and go to bed. Poor soul! I am sure if I had had a notion of it, I would not have joked her about it for all my money. But then you know, how should I guess such a thing? I made sure of its being nothing but a common love letter. Lord! how concerned Sir John and my daughters will be when they hear it!”

“It would be unnecessary, I am sure, for you to caution Mrs. Palmer and Sir John against ever naming Mr. Willoughby before my sister,” Elinor said softly. “The less that may ever be said to myself on the subject, the more my feelings will be spared.”

“Oh! Lord! yes. I think the less that is said about such things, the better. And what good does talking ever do you know?”

“In this affair it can only do harm. I must do this justice to Mr. Willoughby—he has broken no positive engagement with my sister.”

“Law, my dear! Don’t pretend to defend him. No positive engagement indeed! After taking her all over Allenham House, and fixing on the very rooms they were to live in hereafter!”

After a short silence, the rhythmic crackle of the hearth fire filling the space between them, Mrs. Jennings burst forth again, her agrarian roots shining through. “Well, my dear, ’tis a true saying about an ill-wind, for it will be all the better for Colonel Brandon. Two thousand a year without debt or drawback. Delaford is a nice place, I can tell you; exactly what I call a nice old-fashioned place, full of comforts and conveniences. It is quite shut in with great garden walls that absorb the sun and radiate it back, covered with the best espaliered fruit-trees in the country. And such a mulberry tree in one corner! Lord! how Charlotte and I did stuff the only time we were there, the dark juice staining our hands. Then, there is a dove-cote where the birds offer a lovely, calming acoustic rhythm, some delightful stew-ponds teeming with fat, sweet-fleshed carp, and a very pretty canal. A butcher hard by in the village, so you know exactly the pasture from which your meat is sourced. To my fancy, a thousand times prettier than Barton Park, where they are forced to send three miles for their meat over noisy, rutted roads. Well, I shall spirit up the Colonel as soon as I can. One shoulder of mutton, you know, drives another down. If we can but put Willoughby out of her head!”

Elinor retreated to her sister’s room. She found Marianne leaning in silent misery over the small remains of a fire, the sharp, smoky olfactory notes of charred wood clinging to the air. After gentle persuasion, Elinor finally saw her lay her aching head on the pillow. 

In the drawing-room, Elinor was soon joined by Mrs. Jennings, who carried a wine-glass full of dark, viscous liquid. It caught the firelight, glowing like liquid amber. 

“My dear,” said she, “I have just recollected that I have some of the finest old Constantia wine in the house that ever was tasted. It is a true heirloom of the vine. My poor husband! How fond he was of it! Whenever he had a touch of his old colicky gout, he said it did him more good than anything else in the world. Do take it to your sister.”

“Dear Ma’am,” replied Elinor, smiling at the mythic, multi-generational reverence bestowed upon the bottle, “how good you are! But I have just left Marianne in bed. If you will give me leave, I will drink the wine myself.” 

As Elinor swallowed the chief of it, she closed her eyes, allowing the gustatory landscape to unfold. The wine was extraordinarily complex, bursting with notes of caramelized apricot, bruised nectarine, and a honeyed warmth that spoke of the sun-baked, maritime terroir of the Cape. It was liquid history, its healing, sensory power serving as a profound antidote to a disappointed heart. 

Colonel Brandon came in while the party were at tea, the room richly perfumed by steeping Assam leaves and hot porcelain. By his manner of looking round the room for Marianne, Elinor perceived he was already aware of the news. Mrs. Jennings whispered for Elinor to tell him, before returning to the card tables. 

He drew his chair close, the soft rustle of his coat a gentle contrast to the harsh realities he carried. “Marianne is not well,” Elinor told him. “We have persuaded her to go to bed.”

“Perhaps, then,” he hesitatingly replied, “what I heard this morning may be—there may be more truth in it than I could believe possible at first.”

“Yes, we do know it all. This very morning first unfolded it to us. Mr. Willoughby is unfathomable! Where did you hear it?”

“In a stationer’s shop in Pall Mall, where I had business,” he said, his voice tinged with a deep aversion to the environment he was describing. “Two ladies were waiting for their carriage. The auditory assault of the street was terrible, but even over the grinding iron wheels and industrial clatter, one lady was giving the other an account of the intended match in a voice so shrill, so little attempting concealment, that it was impossible for me not to hear. It was the absolute destruction of privacy, broadcast in the dominant lexicon of transaction. The name of John Willoughby was frequently repeated, followed by a positive assertion that everything was now finally settled respecting his marriage with Miss Grey. As soon as the ceremony was over, they were to go to Combe Magna. The communicative lady I learnt, on inquiry, was a Mrs. Ellison.”

“Miss Grey’s guardian,” Elinor confirmed. “But have you likewise heard that Miss Grey has fifty thousand pounds? In that, if in anything, we may find an explanation.”

“It may be so; but Willoughby is capable—at least I think—” he stopped, his voice dropping as he sought a language adequate for genuine emotion. “And your sister—how did she—”

“Her sufferings have been very severe. Till yesterday, she never doubted his regard. He has been very deceitful! and, in some points, there seems a hardness of heart about him.”

“Ah!” said Colonel Brandon, “there is, indeed!” 

He made no further answer. As the tea-things were removed with a gentle clatter, Mrs. Jennings watched them, expecting to see a sudden, lively gaiety bloom in the Colonel’s demeanor now that his rival was gone. Instead, she saw him remain the whole evening remarkably serious and thoughtful, his quiet presence a respectful tribute to a sorrow that no words could ever truly translate.
