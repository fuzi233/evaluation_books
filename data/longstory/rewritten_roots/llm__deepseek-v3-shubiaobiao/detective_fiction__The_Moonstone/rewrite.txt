The truth of the Moonstone affair could only be pieced together through the eyes of those who lived it. What follows is not a single tale, but a mosaic of voices—each adding their fragment to the whole.

First, the Fourth Narrative, where secrets long buried begin to surface.

Then, the Fifth, where motives are laid bare.

The Sixth unfolds in four acts—a drama of suspicion, betrayal, and revelation.

The Seventh Narrative strikes at the heart of the mystery.

And finally, the Eighth, where justice—or something like it—finds its shape.

An Epilogue in three parts closes the circle, though some shadows linger.

Here, then, are the testimonies. Listen closely. The truth is in the telling.

To my kin in England, I pen these words from distant India. My purpose is plain: to justify my refusal to extend the hand of friendship to my cousin, John Herncastle. My silence on this matter has bred misunderstanding among those whose esteem I value. I ask only that they withhold judgment until they have heard my account. On my honor, every word I write is truth.

The rift between Herncastle and myself began in the shadow of a great battle—the storming of Seringapatam under General Baird on May 4th, 1799. To grasp the full measure of our quarrel, you must first know of the whispers that haunted our camp in those fevered days before the assault: tales of gold and jewels heaped within the Sultan’s palace, and one story stranger than all the rest.

Men spoke of a diamond unlike any other—a cursed yellow gem sacred to the moon-god of Hindoo legend. For centuries, it had been guarded by three Brahmin priests, passed down through generations since the days of Mahmoud of Ghizni’s pillage. They said the stone carried a prophecy: disaster would follow any hand that stole it, and all who inherited it thereafter.

By the time of our campaign, the diamond had found its way into the hilt of Tippoo Sultan’s dagger, locked away in his armory. Yet rumor insisted the three Brahmins still watched in disguise, biding their time. Most of us laughed at such superstitions—but not Herncastle. On the eve of battle, he swore with that reckless bravado of his that if Seringapatam fell, we’d see the gem gleaming on his finger. We mocked him for it. By dawn, we’d forgotten his boast.

We forgot too soon.

The assault was chaos. I lost sight of Herncastle in the fray, and it was only at dusk, when the city was ours and Tippoo lay dead, that our paths crossed again. We’d been tasked with curbing the looting, but the treasury was a riot of drunken soldiers. Amid the chaos, a cry cut through the night—a sound that still chills me. I followed it to an armory, where two slain Indians barred the door. Inside, a third man knelt, bleeding, before a figure with a torch and a bloodied dagger.

The torchlight caught the pommel of that dagger as the man turned—and I saw Herncastle’s face. The dying Indian pointed to the blade and gasped: *"The Moonstone will have its vengeance yet on you and yours!"* Then he fell.

Herncastle’s eyes burned with something worse than battle-madness. He drove me out, barring the door. By morning, when Baird threatened hanging for looters, my cousin met me with a curt greeting. I demanded the truth of what I’d seen. He shrugged. *"The Indian died of his wounds. As for his ravings—who can say?"*

I turned my back on him then. I turn it still.

Let my family weigh this account as they will. I’ve no proof to condemn Herncastle beyond my own certainty—no witness to the killings, only a dying man’s curse. Yet I write this with a soldier’s superstition: evil deeds cast long shadows. If Herncastle keeps that diamond, it will haunt him. If he parts with it, worse will follow.

Mark my words.

There’s a passage in *Robinson Crusoe*—page one hundred and twenty-nine, if you care to look—that reads: *“Now I saw, though too late, the Folly of beginning a Work before we count the Cost, and before we judge rightly of our own Strength to go through with it.”*

I’d just stumbled upon those very words yesterday when, this morning—May 21st, 1850—Mr. Franklin Blake, my lady’s nephew, paid me a visit. He wasted no time in getting to the point.

“Betteredge,” he said, “I’ve been to the lawyer’s on family business, and the matter of that cursed Indian Diamond came up—the one that vanished from my aunt’s house in Yorkshire two years ago. Mr. Bruff agrees with me: the whole affair ought to be set down in writing. For the sake of truth, and before time muddles the facts.”

Not yet grasping where I fit into this, but always keen to side with the lawyers (peacekeeping instincts, you understand), I nodded along.

Mr. Franklin continued. “Innocent people have already been shadowed by suspicion in this business,” he said. “And if we don’t put the truth to paper now, their reputations may suffer later for it. This story needs telling—properly. And Bruff and I think we’ve hit upon the right method.”

All very well for them, but I still didn’t see my role in it.

“Here’s the plan,” Mr. Franklin explained. “We’ll each write our own part of the Moonstone’s tale—only what we’ve witnessed ourselves. We begin with how the Diamond fell into my uncle Herncastle’s hands in India fifty years ago. That’s already documented in an old family paper. Next, we must account for how it came to be in my aunt’s house… and how it disappeared within a matter of hours. And that, Betteredge, is where you come in. No one knows the household’s affairs during that time better than you. So you’ll take up the pen and start the story.”

Ah. Now I understood.

If you’re wondering how I responded, I’ll confess: I did precisely what you might’ve done. I protested—modestly, of course—that I wasn’t equal to the task. Privately, though, I fancied myself more than capable, given half a chance.

Mr. Franklin, sharp as ever, saw right through me. He dismissed my false humility and insisted I give it a try.

That was two hours ago. The moment he left, I sat at my desk, ready to begin. And there I’ve remained since, utterly stalled—staring at the page, much like Crusoe staring at his own folly. Prophetic, wasn’t it? That I’d open to that very passage just before rashly accepting this undertaking? If that’s not fate nudging a man, I don’t know what is.

Now, don’t mistake me for some credulous fool. I’ve read my share of books. I’ve a sound mind, a strong memory, and legs that still carry me briskly at seventy. And I’ll say this plainly: there’s no book like *Robinson Crusoe*, nor will there ever be. It’s been my companion through every trial life has thrown at me. Low spirits? *Crusoe*. Need counsel? *Crusoe*. Wife vexing me? A drop too much drink? *Crusoe*, every time. I’ve worn out six copies in my service. My lady gave me a seventh on her last birthday—bound in blue, with an illustration, no less. I celebrated with a tad too much brandy, and who set me right again? *Robinson Crusoe*.

But this isn’t getting the Diamond’s story told, is it? I’m meandering like a drunkard lost in a fog. Let’s begin anew—fresh paper, fresh ink, and my respects to you.

My lady’s household was where the trouble began—or rather, where it ended, though none of us knew it then. The Diamond’s disappearance would never have touched us if it hadn’t been for my lady’s daughter, and my lady’s daughter wouldn’t have existed at all if not for my lady herself. So, if we’re to make sense of this business, we must begin with her.

You’ve likely heard of the three Miss Herncastles—Adelaide, Caroline, and Julia—the finest trio of sisters to grace London society. Julia, the youngest, was the best of them, though I may be partial, having served in their father’s house as a boy. The old lord was a man of endless words and short temper, but we needn’t dwell on him. My place was with the sisters, and there I stayed until Miss Julia married Sir John Verinder.

Sir John was a good man, if a bit in need of steering—a task my lady took on with relish. Under her care, he prospered, grew stout, and lived content until his last breath. When my lady became a widow, she kept me in her service, and I followed her to this house, where I took up the duties of bailiff. I’d been a farmer’s son, so the work suited me.

Years passed. My lady, ever perceptive, noted my advancing age and one winter’s evening presented me with a woolen waistcoat—her own handiwork—before proposing I retire from outdoor labor and take up the steadier role of house-steward. I resisted, of course, but she had a way of getting her way. After some deliberation (aided by my trusty *Robinson Crusoe*), I relented.

But I digress. The heart of the matter is the Diamond, and I’ve strayed far from it. My daughter Penelope, peering over my shoulder, reminds me sharply of this fact. So let me set myself right: the Diamond came to us through Miss Rachel, my lady’s daughter, and with it came the first whispers of misfortune. But that, as they say, is another chapter.

The problem of how to begin this tale properly vexed me until I sought the counsel of my daughter Penelope. Her suggestion was simple: start at the beginning, the day we received word that Mr. Franklin Blake was returning to the house. Dates, she argued, have a way of sharpening memory—and she, ever dutiful with her diary, could supply them. When I proposed (rather cleverly, I thought) that she might narrate the tale herself from those very pages, she flushed crimson and declared her journal private, punctuating her refusal with a sharp, *"Fiddlesticks!"* I, in turn, murmured, *"Sweethearts."*

And so, guided by Penelope’s method, I begin with the morning of Wednesday, the twenty-fourth of May, 1848, when my lady summoned me to her sitting room.

*"Gabriel,"* she said, *"prepare yourself for a surprise. Franklin Blake has returned from abroad. He stays with his father in London now, but tomorrow he joins us—remaining through Rachel’s birthday next month."*

Had I held a hat at that moment, sheer propriety alone would have kept me from hurling it to the ceiling in delight. I had not seen Mr. Franklin since his boyhood, and in my recollection, he was the finest lad ever to spin a top or shatter a windowpane. Miss Rachel, present for this exchange, countered with her own memory: *"I recall him as the most insufferable tyrant who ever tormented a doll, and the cruelest taskmaster to harness a weary little girl."* She concluded with theatrical fervor, *"Even now, I burn with indignation and ache with fatigue at the mere thought of Franklin Blake!"*

You may wonder why Mr. Franklin spent his youth abroad. The answer lies in his father’s misfortunes. The elder Mr. Blake, a man of vast wealth and litigious appetite, spent years—and a fortune—battling in court to claim a dukedom he believed his by right. After his wife and two children perished, the courts finally dismissed his claim, leaving him embittered. He resolved that England, having failed him, would not educate his son. *"How can I trust my native institutions,"* he declared, *"when they have treated me thus?"* And so, young Franklin was dispatched to Germany, then France, then Italy—returning a polymath of sorts, dabbling in writing, painting, and music, though I suspected his talents, like his childhood debts to me (a ball of string, a four-bladed knife, and seven shillings sixpence still unpaid), were borrowed. His inheritance, seven hundred a year, vanished swiftly, for money slipped through Mr. Franklin’s fingers like water. Yet his charm made him welcome everywhere; his address, as he jested, was simply *"Post Office, Europe—to be left till called for."* Twice he resolved to visit us, and twice, a lady (of unmentionable character) detained him. The third attempt, as you know, succeeded.

On Thursday, the twenty-fifth, we would see what manner of man our boy had become. The day dawned splendid, and my lady and Miss Rachel, not expecting Mr. Franklin until dinner, departed for a luncheon with neighbors. I inspected his prepared quarters, then—as butler and steward (the latter role my own insistence, for I would tolerate no other hand on Sir John’s cellar key)—I fetched a bottle of our finest Latour claret, setting it to breathe in the sun. Deciding the same warmth might benefit my aged bones, I carried my beehive chair to the courtyard—only to halt at an odd, rhythmic tapping from the terrace.

Rounding the corner, I discovered three Indians in white linen, small drums at their waists, accompanied by a fair-haired English boy bearing a bag. The leader, a man of striking courtesy, introduced them as traveling performers seeking an audience with the lady of the house. Now, I am no miser with amusement, nor do I judge a man by his complexion—but let a stranger of refined manners linger near my pantry, and I think instantly of the plate basket. I informed him my lady was absent and saw them off the premises.

I had scarcely settled into my chair when Penelope descended upon me in a fury, insisting the Indians be apprehended at once. Why? Because they knew of Mr. Franklin’s arrival and meant him mischief.

At this, I sat straighter. *"Explain."*

The Indian produced a bottle, pouring a black liquid (ink-like) into the boy’s palm. Touching his head, the man commanded, *"Look."* The boy stiffened, gaze fixed on his hand.

Penelope, aflame with conviction, demanded I see the gravity of this. Particularly the third question: *"Has the English gentleman got It about him?"*

*"What is 'It'?"* she pressed.

I teased that we might ask Mr. Franklin—though I meant it lightly. She did not.

*"Ask him,"* she retorted. *"See if he laughs."*

Later, I did ask. And to my astonishment, Mr. Franklin did not laugh at all.

For he believed *"It"* meant the Moonstone.

I regret to delay you with the musings of an old man drowsing in his chair. A sunlit garden and a dozing servant are hardly the stuff of thrilling narrative, but truth demands its due—and so, with patience, you must bear with me until Mr. Franklin Blake makes his entrance later in the day.

No sooner had my daughter Penelope left me than the clatter of dishes from the servants’ hall announced dinner. As I take my meals alone, I had no business there beyond wishing them all a hearty appetite before settling back into my repose. But just as I stretched my legs, another interruption came barreling toward me—not Penelope this time, but Nancy, the kitchen-maid, her face stormy. Now, as steward of the household, I do not permit sullen looks to pass unchallenged.

"What’s this?" I said, blocking her path. "Turning your nose up at a good meal? Out with it, Nancy."

She tried to sidestep me without reply, so I took firm hold of her ear—a gesture of approval, mind you, for she’s a sturdy lass, and it’s my custom to show favor where it’s due.

"Rosanna’s late again," Nancy snapped. "And I’m sent to fetch her. Always me doing the hard work! Let me by, Mr. Betteredge!"

Rosanna, our second housemaid, was a girl I had a soft spot for (for reasons you’ll soon learn). Seeing Nancy’s temper, I judged it wiser to fetch Rosanna myself and spare her a tongue-lashing.

"Where is she?" I asked.

"Where else? The sands!" Nancy tossed her head. "Had another of her fits this morning and begged for air. I’ve no patience with her nonsense."

"Then keep your patience and your dinner both," I said. "I’ll bring her in."

Nancy—never one to refuse a meal—brightened at once. A pretty girl when she smiles, so I gave her chin a friendly tap (a harmless habit, nothing more) and took up my stick for the walk to the shore.

But before we proceed, you must hear two tales—that of the sands, and that of Rosanna—for both are knotted tight around the mystery of the Diamond. Try as I might to march straight through my account, life insists on tangling the thread. So let us be brief, and soon enough, the heart of the puzzle will be upon us.

Rosanna was the sole newcomer among our staff. Some months prior, my lady had visited a London reformatory, one of those charitable institutions meant to steer wayward women clear of prison’s relapse. The matron, noting my lady’s interest, pointed out Rosanna Spearman and unspooled a tale too wretched to repeat here—misery without purpose serves no one. The short of it? Rosanna had been a thief, though not the kind who plunders thousands behind a desk. The law had seized her, prison had held her, and the reformatory had tried to mend her.

Yet the matron swore—past sins notwithstanding—that the girl was one in a thousand, needing only a chance to prove her worth. My lady, ever a Christian soul, declared then and there: "Rosanna Spearman shall have that chance in my service."

A week later, she joined us. None knew her history save Miss Rachel and myself. My lady, in her wisdom, had consulted me, and I—having adopted the late Sir John’s policy of agreeable concurrence—endorsed the plan heartily. No girl could have asked fairer: decent wages, fair treatment, and now and then a quiet word of encouragement from my lady herself.

To her credit, Rosanna earned it. Frail though she was, prone to those fainting spells, she worked diligently and without complaint. Yet she made no friends among the other maids—save Penelope, who was kind but never close. I cannot say what offense they took. She was plain as a pewter plate, with one shoulder higher than the other. Perhaps it was her silence, her preference for solitary reading over gossip. She never quarreled, never bristled—only held herself apart, civil but firm.

And there was something else. A trace of refinement in her voice or bearing, a shadow of the lady she might have been. The others seized on it at once, unjustly declaring she put on airs.

Now, about those sands. Our house stands high on the Yorkshire coast, ringed by pleasant walks—save one. A quarter-mile tramp through a grim fir plantation spits you out onto the ugliest stretch of shore for miles: a narrow bay bracketed by two jagged spits of rock, the North and South, between which lurks the Shivering Sand—a quicksand that trembles like a living thing when the tide turns. No boats dare near it; no children play there. Even the birds avoid it.

Yet Rosanna, who might have walked anywhere, chose this bleak spot whenever she had leave. She’d sit alone, stitching or reading, as if drawn by some grim enchantment.

So it was there I sought her. No sign in the plantation, but on the beach, she stood in her grey cloak (always worn to mask her crooked shoulder), gazing at the sand and sea. At my approach, she started and turned away—a habit I never tolerate in my staff. I steered her round and saw tears on her face.

My pocket yielded one of the fine handkerchiefs my lady had given me. "Sit," I said, easing onto the slope. "Dry your eyes, then tell me what troubles you."

(Note, young readers: sitting on sand is harder at my age than you’d think.)

Rosanna dabbed her eyes with a shabby bit of cambric but obeyed. She looked wretched, yet composed. Now, the quickest way to comfort a woman is to take her on your knee—but Rosanna was no Nancy, and the moment called for restraint.

"My past haunts me, Mr. Betteredge," she said quietly.

"Then forget it," I urged. "It’s done with."

She plucked at my coat—a grease-stained garment I’m too idle to keep tidy. The day before, she’d cleaned a spot with some new concoction. The stain was gone, but the cloth bore a faint mark where it had been.

"The stain’s gone," she said, "but the place remains."

A sharp observation, and one that silenced me. There was a sadness in her brown eyes, a respect for my untroubled life as something forever beyond her, that weighed on my heart. Failing words, I did the next best thing.

"Help me up," I said. "You’re late for dinner, and I’ve come to fetch you."

"You, sir?"

"They sent Nancy, but I thought you’d take a scolding gentler from me."

Instead of assisting, she squeezed my hand—a small, grateful pressure. She fought back fresh tears and won.

"You’re kind," she murmured. "I’ve no appetite today. Let me stay awhile."

"Why come here, of all places?"

"Something pulls me," she said, tracing shapes in the sand. "I try to stay away, but I can’t. Sometimes"—her voice dropped—"I think my grave waits for me here."

"Roast mutton waits for you inside," I said sternly. "This is what comes of brooding on an empty stomach!"

But she gripped my shoulder, holding me beside her.

"This place has a spell on me," she whispered. "I dream of it. I think of it at my needlework. I’m grateful, truly—I strive to earn your kindness, my lady’s trust. But sometimes… this quiet life feels too pure for what I am. The other maids’ honesty is a reproach. Don’t scold me, sir. I do my work. Only tell my lady I’m not discontent—I’m not. It’s just… my mind frets at times."

Suddenly she pointed to the quicksand. "Look! It’s terrible, wonderful! Always changing, always new."

The tide was turning. The sand shuddered, its surface dimpling like a living skin.

"Do you know what I see?" She clutched my arm. "Hundreds beneath it—suffocating, struggling, sinking deeper. Throw a stone, Mr. Betteredge! Watch the sand swallow it!"

Unhealthy fancy, this. An empty stomach feeding dark thoughts. I was set to rebuke her when a voice from the dunes cut me short.

"Betteredge! Where are you?"

Rosanna sprang up, staring. Before I could rise, her face transformed—flushed, radiant, breathless.

"Who is it?" I asked.

"Oh," she whispered, more to herself than me, "*who* is it?"

I turned. Emerging from the hills was a young gentleman in fawn-colored elegance, gloves and hat to match, a rose in his buttonhole, and a smile that might charm even the Shivering Sand. Before I could stand, he dropped beside me, threw an arm about my neck (foreign manners!), and near crushed the life from me.

"Dear old Betteredge!" cried Mr. Franklin Blake. "I owe you seven-and-six. Now, do you know me?"

Lord save us! Four hours early, here he was!

Before I could speak, his gaze flicked to Rosanna. She flushed deeper, then fled without word or curtsey—most unlike her.

"Odd girl," Mr. Franklin mused. "What surprises her so about me?"

"Perhaps," I jested, "it’s the foreign polish, sir."

I record this exchange as comfort to duller minds: even the cleverest among us miss what’s plain before them. Neither Mr. Franklin, for all his Continental wit, nor I, with my years and sense, grasped Rosanna’s turmoil then. She vanished from our thoughts as her grey cloak disappeared among the dunes.

And why should we have thought of her? you may ask. Read on, and perhaps you’ll grieve for Rosanna Spearman as I did—when at last I understood.

The moment we were left alone on the Shivering Sand, I made another attempt to rise. Mr. Franklin stopped me with a hand on my shoulder.

“This cursed place has one advantage,” he said. “Privacy. Stay where you are, Betteredge. I’ve something to discuss.”

I studied him as he spoke, searching for the boy I remembered in the man before me. The years had reshaped him entirely. Gone were the rosy cheeks, the trim jacket—replaced by a pale complexion and a thick brown beard that unsettled me. His manner was quick, engaging, but lacked the careless charm of his youth. Worse still, he’d fallen short of the height he’d once promised. Neat, slight, well-built—yet undeniably ordinary in stature. Only his eyes remained unchanged: bright, direct, unmistakably *him*.

“Welcome home, Mr. Franklin,” I said. “Earlier than expected, sir.”

“There’s a reason for that,” he replied. “I believe I’ve been followed in London these past few days. I took the morning train to lose a certain dark stranger.”

The words struck like a spark. The three jugglers—Penelope’s suspicions—flashed through my mind.

“Who’s watching you, sir? And why?”

Instead of answering, he said, “Tell me about those Indians who came to the house today. It’s possible my pursuer and your jugglers are part of the same puzzle.”

“How do you know of them?” I asked—a breach of manners, but shock loosens the tongue.

“Penelope told me,” he said, amused. “She’s grown into a fine young woman. Small ear, small foot—did her mother share those *inestimable* advantages?”

“The late Mrs. Betteredge had many faults,” I said dryly. “Not least her habit of flitting from topic to topic like a fly.”

“She’d have suited me,” Mr. Franklin laughed. “I’ve never settled on anything either. Now, the jugglers—what did they do?”

Reluctantly, I relayed Penelope’s account. His amusement faded as I spoke. By the end, his brow was knotted, fingers twisting his beard. He repeated the jugglers’ questions with eerie precision:

Then, to my astonishment, he drew a sealed packet from his pocket.

“I suspect ‘It’ means *this*,” he said. “And *this*, Betteredge, is my uncle Herncastle’s cursed Diamond.”

“God alive!” I burst out. “How did *you* come by that villain’s gem?”

“The Colonel left it to Rachel as a birthday gift,” he said. “My father, as executor, entrusted me to deliver it.”

Had the sea before us turned to stone, I couldn’t have been more stunned.

“The Diamond—to Miss Rachel? And your father agreed to handle the Colonel’s affairs? I’d have wagered my life he’d sooner touch a viper!”

Mr. Franklin’s gaze sharpened. “You called him ‘the wicked Colonel.’ Why?”

So I told him.

The late Lord Herncastle had five children: two sons, three daughters. My mistress, Lady Verinder, was the youngest and finest. The eldest son inherited title and estate. The second, John—the Honourable John—took a military path.

And here, I must tread carefully. A servant doesn’t blacken his master’s family lightly. But the truth is this: John Herncastle was a devil in human skin.

After being dismissed from the Guards (reasons best left unsaid), he fled to India, seeking laxer discipline. Brave? Undoubtedly—a mix of bulldog and gamecock, with a streak of savagery. He fought at Seringapatam, hopped regiments, and finally returned to England as Lieutenant-Colonel Herncastle—sunstruck, disgraced, and shunned.

The whispers clung to him like rot. The worst? The Moonstone. He’d acquired it through means even *he* dared not name. Never sold it. Never showed it. Some said he feared military retribution; others, assassination. Both held grains of truth. His life had been threatened twice in India—both times, the Diamond lurked in the shadows.

England offered no refuge. Clubs barred him. Women refused him. Family crossed streets to avoid him. Most men would’ve groveled for redemption. Not the Colonel. He kept the Diamond, defying death abroad and scorn at home. A man who laughed at hell itself.

We heard sporadic rumors: opium, obscure books, chemical experiments, debauchery in London’s filthiest corners. A recluse, rotten to the core.

I saw him once—two years before his death. Miss Rachel’s twenty-first birthday. A ball was underway when a footman summoned me. There stood the Colonel: gaunt, ragged, eyes burning with undiminished malice.

“Tell my sister,” he said, “I’ve come to wish my niece a happy birthday.”

I delivered the message. My lady’s reply was ice: *“Miss Verinder is engaged. I decline to see him.”*

I softened the blow, but the Colonel only laughed—a sound like poison dripping into glass.

“Thank you, Betteredge. I’ll remember my niece’s birthday.”

He left. The next year, he was dead. A clergyman’s letter claimed he’d repented, forgiven his sister, died edified. I don’t believe it. The devil collected his own.

Mr. Franklin listened like a man deciphering a cipher. When I finished, his face was taut.

“You’ve set the stage,” he said. “Now let me clarify my suspicions.” He counted off on his fingers:

“One: Was the Diamond hunted in India?

“Two: Have its hunters followed it to England?

“Three: Did the Colonel *know* this—and deliberately plant a trap for his sister through Rachel?”

The implications coiled around me like smoke. Our peaceful home, targeted over a jewel? In *England*? In this age of reason? Preposterous—yet his logic was sound.

My stomach churned. Mr. Franklin noticed.

“What’s wrong?” he snapped.

I didn’t answer. But I’ll confess to you: I craved the comfort of my pipe and *Robinson Crusoe*. Some storms require steadying.

I held my tongue, though my own thoughts on the matter were far from settled, and urged Mr. Franklin to continue. With a careless wave of his hand, he obliged.

Our young gentleman began by recounting a visit he had made—before arriving at our house—to the family solicitor in Hampstead. A casual remark over dinner had revealed that Mr. Franklin had been entrusted with a birthday gift for Miss Rachel. One word led to another, and soon the lawyer disclosed the true nature of the present, as well as the peculiar history of the late Colonel Herncastle’s connection with Mr. Blake, Sr. The tale was so extraordinary that I feared my own plain speech might fail to do it justice. Instead, I resolved to relay Mr. Franklin’s account as faithfully as possible, in his own words.

*"You recall, Betteredge,"* he said, *"the time my father was entangled in that wretched dispute over the Dukedom? Well, it was then that my uncle Herncastle returned from India. My father learned that the Colonel possessed certain papers that might prove useful in his legal battle. Under the pretense of welcoming him home, he paid a visit. The Colonel, however, was not so easily deceived. ‘You want something,’ he said, ‘or you would never risk your reputation by calling on* me.’ *My father, seeing no alternative, admitted he sought the papers. The Colonel requested a day to consider his reply. When it came, it took the form of a most peculiar letter—one which our lawyer later showed me."*

Mr. Franklin then detailed the contents of this letter. The Colonel proposed an exchange of favors. By the fortunes of war (his own phrase), he had come into possession of one of the largest diamonds in the world—and he was convinced that neither he nor the stone was safe so long as they remained together. He sought a guardian for the jewel, one who need not take any active role beyond securing it in a bank vault or similar stronghold. This guardian—or his appointed agent—would, on fixed dates each year, receive a letter from the Colonel confirming he still lived. Should a letter fail to arrive, it would signal his murder. In that event alone, sealed instructions accompanying the diamond were to be opened and followed without question. If my father agreed to this arrangement, the Colonel’s papers would be his in return.

*"And what did your father do?"* I asked.

*"Applied common sense,"* Mr. Franklin replied. *"He declared the whole affair absurd. The Colonel, he reasoned, had stumbled upon some worthless crystal during his travels and mistaken it for a diamond. As for the alleged danger—this was the nineteenth century! If the Colonel feared for his life, the police were the proper recourse. The man had long been known to indulge in opium; if accepting his delusions was the price of obtaining the papers, my father was willing to humor him—especially since it cost him no effort. The diamond and its instructions were deposited in a bank, and the Colonel’s annual letters were received by our solicitor, Mr. Bruff. No rational man, my father maintained, would have acted otherwise. We believe only what aligns with our own narrow experience, Betteredge—and even then, only if it appears in the newspapers."*

It was clear Mr. Franklin did not share his father’s dismissive view.

*"What is your own opinion, sir?"* I ventured.

*"Let us finish the Colonel’s story first,"* he said, chiding me for my unsystematic English mind. *"My father obtained the papers and never saw his brother-in-law again. Year after year, the letters arrived, each identical: ‘Sir,—This is to certify that I am still a living man. Let the Diamond be. John Herncastle.’ This continued without variation until six months ago, when the wording changed: ‘Sir,—They tell me I am dying. Come to me, and help me to make my will.’ Mr. Bruff went at once and found the Colonel in a secluded villa, surrounded by his animals but no human company save a daily maid and his physician. His will was brief: provisions for his pets, the endowment of a chemistry professorship, and the bequest of the Moonstone to his niece—on the condition that my father act as executor. Reluctantly, my father agreed, persuaded partly by assurances that the duty would be simple, and partly by Mr. Bruff’s suggestion that the stone might, after all, hold some value."*

*"Did the Colonel explain why he left it to Miss Rachel?"* I asked.

*"He did better—he set it down in the will itself,"* said Mr. Franklin, producing a copy. *"The diamond was left as a token of forgiveness for the injury her mother had done to his reputation—specifically, for the insult of having his own sister’s door shut against him by her orders."*

The will further stipulated that if either Lady Verinder or Miss Rachel were dead, the diamond should be sent to Amsterdam, cut into smaller stones, and sold to fund the chemistry professorship.

Handing back the paper, I was at a loss. My long-held belief in the Colonel’s wickedness had been shaken—though not wholly overturned.

*"Now,"* said Mr. Franklin, *"having read his own words, what do you conclude? In bringing the Moonstone here, am I the blind instrument of his vengeance, or the proof of his repentance?"*

*"It is a hard thing, sir,"* I said, *"to accuse a dying man of harboring malice and deceit. God alone knows the truth."*

Mr. Franklin fell into an uncharacteristic solemnity, turning the matter over in his mind.

*"There are two ways to consider this,"* he mused. *"The Objective and the Subjective. Which shall we choose?"*

I confess, I did not follow this foreign philosophy. Mr. Franklin, however, proceeded to dissect the Colonel’s motives with alarming precision. Had he left the diamond to spite his sister through her child? Or to offer a posthumous olive branch, prettily wrapped as a gift? Both interpretations, he declared, were equally plausible.

Having exhausted his intellect on the question, Mr. Franklin—now abruptly idle—threw himself onto the sand and demanded to know what we ought to do next.

*"Isn’t that your concern, sir?"* I asked, baffled by his sudden helplessness.

*"I wish neither to alarm my aunt needlessly nor leave her unwarned,"* he said. *"If you were in my place, Betteredge, what would you do?"*

*"Wait,"* I said.

And so we resolved to do just that. The diamond would be given to Miss Rachel on her birthday, as stipulated—but until then, we would watch, wait, and let events guide us.

As for the stone itself? That remained a question for another day.

The events of the day had left me thoroughly unsettled, and what I needed most was a moment of quiet to collect my thoughts. But my daughter Penelope—bless her, though as persistent as her late mother ever was—cornered me at once, demanding to know what had transpired between Mr. Franklin Blake and me. Under the circumstances, the only course was to smother her curiosity before it took root. So I told her we had discussed foreign politics at such length that we both succumbed to the warmth of the sun and dozed off. A man learns, in time, that such answers are the best defense against inconvenient questions from the women in his life—and that their natural sweetness ensures reconciliation soon enough.

The afternoon waned, and my lady and Miss Rachel returned. Their astonishment at hearing of Mr. Franklin’s abrupt arrival and equally sudden departure was to be expected. So, too, was their barrage of questions. My earlier excuse of foreign politics and sun-induced slumber would not serve me twice. With invention exhausted, I declared that Mr. Franklin’s early arrival was merely one of his peculiar whims—and when pressed about his hasty exit, I insisted it was yet another. A neat evasion, if I do say so myself.

No sooner had I weathered the ladies’ interrogation than Penelope reappeared—first to mend our disagreement with a daughter’s tenderness, then to pry further. This time, her concern was Rosanna Spearman, our second housemaid, who had returned from the Shivering Sand in a most peculiar state. According to Penelope, the girl had been a whirlwind of contradictions: one moment unnaturally merry, the next inexplicably melancholy. She had fired off a volley of questions about Mr. Franklin, then snapped at Penelope for implying she might take an interest in him. She had been caught smiling to herself, scribbling his name inside her workbox—only to dissolve into tears moments later, staring at her misshapen shoulder in the mirror.

Had she and Mr. Franklin met before? Impossible. Had either heard of the other? Equally so. I could vouch for the genuine shock on his face when she stared at him; Penelope could swear to the girl’s relentless curiosity about him. We might have debated the matter endlessly had Penelope not abruptly silenced me with the most outrageous suggestion I’d ever heard.

"Father," she said, deadly serious, "there’s only one explanation. Rosanna has fallen in love with Mr. Franklin Blake at first sight."

Now, young ladies of beauty and breeding falling headlong into love is a tale as old as time—but a housemaid, plain and deformed, struck by such a passion for a gentleman she had only just laid eyes on? Preposterous! I laughed until my sides ached. Yet Penelope’s reaction gave me pause.

"I never knew you to be cruel before, father," she said softly—and left.

Her words chilled me. Worse still, they left me uneasy. But enough of that.

Evening fell, and the dressing bell rang before Mr. Franklin returned from Frizinghall. Eager for news, I carried his hot water up myself, only to be met with disappointment. He had encountered no Indians, neither going nor returning. The Moonstone now rested safely in the bank—discreetly described as "a valuable of great price"—with the receipt secure in his pocket. A dull conclusion to a day that had begun with such intrigue.

Of the reunion between Mr. Franklin and his aunt and cousin, I can say little, for I was not present. Penelope reported that Miss Rachel had never taken such care with her hair, nor looked so radiant, as when she descended to meet him. The footman confessed that maintaining his composure while serving Mr. Franklin had been a trial of his professional restraint.

Later, music floated through the open windows—duets, with Mr. Franklin’s voice lifting high, Miss Rachel’s higher still, and my lady’s piano guiding them both as though over hill and dale. By the time I brought Mr. Franklin his brandy in the smoking-room, the Diamond seemed the furthest thing from his mind.

"She’s the most charming girl I’ve seen since returning to England," was all I could wrest from him.

At midnight, as I made my rounds with Samuel to secure the house, the night lay still and heavy under a full moon. The only sound was the distant murmur of the sea. The terrace lay in shadow, but moonlight bathed the gravel walk beside it. It was there I saw a figure’s shadow flit past—then heard light footsteps retreating in haste. By the time I reached the corner, the intruders had vanished into the shrubbery.

Too old to give chase, I roused Samuel, and together we armed ourselves and searched the grounds. Finding nothing, we turned back—but not before I spotted a small, glinting object on the gravel: a bottle of thick, sweet-smelling liquid, black as ink.

I said nothing to Samuel. But I remembered Penelope’s tale of the jugglers and their ink trick—and knew then that the Indians had been here, lurking in the dark, hunting the Diamond.

A brief pause is necessary here.

Consulting my own memory—and with Penelope’s journal to assist me—I find little worth recording in the days between Mr. Franklin Blake’s arrival and Miss Rachel’s birthday. With your permission, then, I shall skip ahead, noting only the essential dates before returning to a full account when the matter of the Moonstone takes center stage in our affairs.

Now, let us resume with the peculiar discovery of the sweet-smelling ink bottle on the gravel walk.

The next morning (the twenty-sixth), I showed Mr. Franklin this curious object and relayed the events as I’ve already described them to you. His opinion was clear: the Indians had not only been lurking about in pursuit of the Diamond but were also foolish enough to believe in their own superstitions—the markings on the boy’s head, the ink in his palm, all meant to grant him visions beyond ordinary sight. Mr. Franklin explained that such practices existed even in England, though without the ink—a French-named trick, something to do with *clairvoyance*.

*"They assumed we still had the Diamond here,"* he said. *"That boy was their guide, meant to locate it if they got inside."*

*"Will they return, sir?"* I asked.

*"That depends,"* he replied, *"on whether the boy’s sight is genuine. If he can peer through the iron vault of Frizinghall Bank and spot the Diamond there, we’ll see no more of them. If not, we’ll catch them in the shrubbery before long."*

I expected another attempt, but none came. Whether they learned of Mr. Franklin’s visit to the bank, whether the boy truly *saw* the Diamond (which I refuse to believe), or whether it was mere coincidence, the fact remained—not a shadow of an Indian crossed our grounds again until Miss Rachel’s birthday. They lingered in town, plying their trade, while Mr. Franklin and I waited, careful not to alert them by showing suspicion. And so ends my report on the Indians for now.

On the twenty-ninth, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin devised an amusement to pass the time—one worth noting, for it bears on events yet to unfold.

Idleness is a perilous thing for gentlefolk. Left with nothing to occupy their hands or minds, they often drift into absurd or even cruel pastimes under the guise of *intellectual pursuit*. I’ve seen ladies and gentlemen alike collect insects, impale them on pins, or dissect flowers with morbid curiosity, all while calling it *natural history*. At least Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel confined their mischief to inanimate objects—though they did ruin a perfectly good door.

Mr. Franklin, ever the dabbler, had taken up *decorative painting* and invented a new medium—a *vehicle*, as he called it—whose odor could make a dog sneeze. Miss Rachel, eager to try, was soon draped in an apron, daubing grotesques and flourishes onto her sitting-room door under his guidance. Slow, messy work—yet they were absorbed in it for hours, like children smearing mud.

By June, whispers began below stairs. Seeing how well Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel suited one another, speculation arose: would there be a wedding before summer’s end? Some said yes. Others (myself included) doubted the groom would be Mr. Franklin Blake.

His affection was plain enough, but Miss Rachel’s feelings were harder to discern. Allow me to paint her for you.

She was small, dark, and exquisitely proportioned—every movement graceful, her figure unadorned by artifice. Black hair, blacker eyes, a mouth that Mr. Franklin declared *a morsel for the gods*, and a complexion warm as sunlight but without its inconveniences. She carried herself with a spirited confidence, her voice clear, her smile beginning in her eyes before it reached her lips.

Yet for all her charm, she had a will of her own. Unlike most girls her age, she formed her own opinions and defied convention when it displeased her. In trifles, this independence was harmless; in greater matters, it led her astray. She confided in no one, asked no advice, and kept her own counsel—her best friend and worst enemy, as my lady often said.

But mark this: for all her secrecy, she was never false. She would sooner take punishment than lie. A creature of contradictions, like all women worth knowing.

On the twelfth, an invitation was sent to London—to the man I suspected held Miss Rachel’s heart: Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

A philanthropist of renowned charm, he stood tall and golden-haired, a fixture at ladies’ charitable committees where his speeches could draw both tears and coin. Next to him, Mr. Franklin—for all his cleverness—stood at a disadvantage.

Godfrey’s reply arrived on the fourteenth: he would come, though his *duties to the Ladies’ Charities* demanded an early return. Enclosed were verses for his cousin’s *natal day*, which Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin laughed over at dinner.

*"Wait,"* I told Penelope. *"The man himself will follow."*

On the sixteenth, a foreign-accented stranger called upon Mr. Franklin. The matter, whatever it was, displeased Miss Rachel, for sharp words were exchanged that evening. Work on the door ceased the next day—then resumed as abruptly. Had some old folly of Mr. Franklin’s resurfaced? I could only guess.

Meanwhile, Rosanna Spearman, our second housemaid, grew increasingly strange—wan, restless, and fixated on Mr. Franklin in a manner that troubled me. When the doctor pronounced it nerves, I foolishly advised keeping her on. A decision I would soon regret.

But that, as they say, is a matter for another day.

The morning of June twenty-first—Miss Rachel’s birthday—dawned gray and uncertain, but by midday the skies cleared, as if in deference to the occasion. Below stairs, we marked the day as tradition dictated: with humble offerings presented to the young mistress, accompanied by my annual speech. Like the Queen’s address to Parliament, my words followed a time-honored script, met each year with the same eager anticipation, and the same mild disappointment when the expected novelty failed to materialize. Still, they endured it with good humor—proof, I mused, that whether governing a nation or a household, consistency breeds contentment.

Breakfast concluded, Mr. Franklin drew me aside to discuss the Moonstone. The time had come to retrieve it from the bank at Frizinghall and place it in Miss Rachel’s hands. Yet Mr. Franklin was uncharacteristically indecisive, torn between conflicting impulses. Had his courtship of his cousin met another rebuff? Or were sleepless nights exacerbating the contradictions in his nature? Whatever the cause, he vacillated like a weathervane in a gale.

For my part, I held firm to the facts. No incident justified alarming Lady Verinder, and the terms of the Colonel’s will left no choice but to surrender the Diamond. After much debate, Mr. Franklin conceded. He would ride to Frizinghall after luncheon, returning with the jewel—and likely in the company of Mr. Godfrey and the two Miss Ablewhites.

With that settled, he rejoined Miss Rachel. The pair devoted the entire morning and half the afternoon to decorating her sitting-room door, aided by my Penelope, who dutifully mixed paints under their direction. Lady Verinder, her handkerchief pressed to her nose (Mr. Franklin’s varnish proving particularly potent), flitted in and out, attempting in vain to lure them away. Not until three o’clock did they relinquish their aprons, liberating my poor daughter—who had suffered most from the fumes—and scrubbing the evidence of their artistry from their hands.

The result, I confess, was striking: a riot of gilded griffins, cupids, and floral wreaths, so exuberantly rendered that one’s head ached for hours afterward from the mere memory of their whirlwind antics. As for Penelope’s subsequent retreat to the back-kitchen, let no one accuse me of disparaging Art. If such sacrifices were required, so be it—though I might wish the victim had been someone else’s child.

Mr. Franklin snatched a hasty meal and rode for Frizinghall, ostensibly to escort his cousins, though our private understanding was otherwise. With the evening’s festivities looming, I had duties enough to occupy me—inspecting the wine, marshaling the servants—before stealing a moment’s respite in my pantry, aided by a restorative pinch of snuff and a familiar volume of *Robinson Crusoe*.

I was roused by the clatter of hooves. Mr. Franklin had returned, accompanied by the Ablewhite party. Mr. Godfrey, I noted, seemed out of sorts—his greeting cordial but subdued, his manner clouded. His sisters, however, more than compensated. Stout, golden-haired, and brimming with vigor, they descended upon the house like a pair of exuberant Valkyries, their laughter ringing through the halls.

Under cover of their clamor, I managed a private word with Mr. Franklin.

“The Diamond is safe?”

A nod, his hand brushing his coat’s breast pocket.

“And the Indians?”

“Not a trace.”

With that, he sought Lady Verinder. Moments later, a chorus of shrieks erupted from the drawing-room. Recognizing the Miss Ablewhites’ signature exclamations, I entered under pretext of consulting her ladyship on dinner arrangements—and beheld a curious scene.

There stood Miss Rachel, the Colonel’s cursed Diamond glinting in her palm, her face alight with fascination. Flanking her, the two sisters knelt in rapture, gasping at each new play of light. Across the room, Mr. Godfrey applauded like an enraptured child, murmuring, “Exquisite!” Near the bookcase, Mr. Franklin tugged at his beard, his gaze fixed on Lady Verinder, who stood at the window, the Colonel’s will clutched in her hand, her back resolutely turned.

At my inquiry, she faced me, her expression stormy. “Attend me in half an hour,” she said curtly, then swept out. Clearly, the Diamond’s legacy troubled her as deeply as it had troubled Mr. Franklin and me at the Shivering Sand. Was it proof of her brother’s vindictiveness—or her own misplaced severity?

Before I could follow, Miss Rachel detained me. “Look, Gabriel!” she exclaimed, tilting the Diamond into a shaft of sunlight.

Heaven help me—what a stone! Nearly the size of a plover’s egg, it held depths like liquid gold, swallowing the gaze into its luminous heart. In darkness, it burned with an eerie lunar glow. No wonder the Miss Ablewhites shrieked; no wonder I myself echoed their “O!” Only Mr. Godfrey remained unmoved, murmuring something about carbon, as if that explained anything at all.

Dinner preparations reclaimed me until, half an hour later, I presented myself to Lady Verinder. Our interview echoed my earlier conference with Mr. Franklin—though I omitted mention of the jugglers, seeing no cause for alarm. Her ladyship’s verdict was plain: the Colonel’s motives were black indeed, and she meant to wrest the Diamond from her daughter’s possession at the first opportunity.

On my return, I crossed paths with Mr. Franklin, who inquired after Miss Rachel and Mr. Godfrey with mounting suspicion. A sharp tug at his beard, a decisive library door slam—his feelings were clear.

I had scarcely donned my waistcoat when Penelope accosted me, her brush attacking my scalp with unwarranted zeal. “News, father!” she whispered. “Miss Rachel has refused him!”

“Him?”

“That philanthropic hypocrite!” she declared, tightening my cravat with vengeful vigor. “I saw them in the rose-garden—arm in arm when they went out, cold as January when they returned. ‘Let us remain cousins,’ she said, and left him grinding his heel into the gravel like a thwarted schoolboy.” She delivered a final, emphatic stroke of the brush. “Mr. Franklin’s the man, mark my words!”

I had no time to reprimand her impropriety. The first carriages had arrived. Adjusting my lobster-hued face in the mirror, I strode to my post just in time to announce Mr. and Mrs. Ablewhite—though I doubt their names stir your interest any more than they did mine.

The dinner party at Lady Verinder’s should have been a triumph—twenty-four guests, fine wine, and the radiant Miss Rachel presiding as queen of the evening. Yet from the moment the company assembled, an uneasy tension hummed beneath the surface. Perhaps it was the Moonstone itself, glinting coldly from Rachel’s dress like an omen.

Two guests in particular could not take their eyes from it.

To Rachel’s left sat Dr. Candy, our Frizinghall physician—a man whose tongue outpaced his tact. With a wink and a chuckle, he declared the Diamond ought to be sacrificed to science. *"Let me reduce it to smoke, Miss Verinder! A puff of carbon, and your anxieties vanish!"* Lady Verinder, tight-lipped, looked as though she wished he were serious.

To Rachel’s right was the far more unsettling figure of Mr. Murthwaite, the famed Indian traveler. Silent as a shadow, he studied the gem with an intensity that bordered on reverence. When at last he spoke, his voice was cool, detached: *"If you ever set foot in India, Miss Verinder, leave this behind. Some treasures are not merely stolen—they are sacred."* The Bouncers gasped in delight; my lady stiffened.

The dinner itself was a disaster. Conversations sputtered and died. Dr. Candy, oblivious, cheerfully recommended skeleton-viewing to a widow who—unbeknownst to him—had been mourning her husband for a decade. The resulting eruption left the company in stunned silence.

Even Mr. Franklin, usually the life of any gathering, fumbled terribly. His foreign wit misfired—first scandalizing the vicar’s aunt with French philosophy, then enraging our local squire by dismissing his prized bulls as *"products of insufficient introspection."* By the time he provoked Dr. Candy into a full-blown quarrel over medical quackery, the party was beyond saving.

Then came the drumbeat on the terrace.

The Indians had returned.

Before I could intervene, the guests spilled outside, eager for spectacle. The jugglers bowed low—too low, their eyes darting past the crowd to fix on Rachel. On the *Moonstone.* Only Mr. Murthwaite noticed their tension. A few hissed words in their own tongue, and the color drained from their leader’s face. The performance ended abruptly.

Later, among the shadowed trees, Murthwaite delivered his verdict: *"Those men are no jugglers. They are Brahmins—high-caste priests who have defiled themselves by crossing oceans and donning disguises. For them, this is no theft. It is a holy war."*

Franklin blanched. *"You think they’d kill for it?"*

Murthwaite lit a cheroot. *"As casually as you’d knock out a pipe’s ashes."*

The solution was brutal: *"Destroy the Diamond. Cut it into six stones. End its legend."*

As thunder growled in the distance, Murthwaite’s dry voice cut through the gloom: *"The Indians will want umbrellas tonight."*

I retired to my room, my hands unsteady. Opening *Robinson Crusoe* at random, I found a grim comfort:

The danger, however, was all too real.

The last guest had departed, and the house settled into uneasy silence. I returned to the inner hall, where Samuel lingered by the brandy and soda. Lady Verinder and Miss Rachel emerged from the drawing-room, trailed by Mr. Godfrey and Mr. Franklin. The former helped himself to a drink; the latter sat, drained from the evening’s exertions.

Lady Verinder’s gaze fixed on the Diamond, its eerie glow catching the folds of Rachel’s gown.

“Rachel,” she said, “where do you intend to keep that tonight?”

Rachel, giddy with the night’s excitement, toyed with the question. First, she claimed she hadn’t decided. Then, with a laugh, she suggested her dressing-table—until the thought of its spectral shimmer in the dark unnerved her. Finally, she declared she would place it in her Indian cabinet, “where one wonder might admire another.”

Her mother cut through the nonsense. “The cabinet has no lock.”

Rachel’s eyes widened in mock alarm. “Are we expecting thieves?”

Lady Verinder ignored the jest. “Let me keep it for you tonight.”

Rachel recoiled as if asked to surrender a childhood treasure. Seeing reason would not prevail, her mother sighed. “Come to me in the morning,” she said, withdrawing with a frown.

Rachel bid the gentlemen goodnight. To Mr. Godfrey—lingering by a painting—she offered a polite handshake. To Mr. Franklin, slumped in weary silence, she hesitated. Then, by the looking-glass, I caught the reflection of her slipping a locket from her bodice—his gift—flashing it at him with a smile that spoke volumes before she vanished upstairs.

Mr. Franklin, noticing me, dismissed his earlier fears of the Indians. “Perhaps I overreacted. Still, unleash the dogs tonight.”

I obliged, though the animals, bewildered by their midnight freedom, bounded like pups. The rain drenched them into submission soon enough.

Inside, Samuel and I secured the house. I checked every lock myself. Yet sleep eluded me, my mind restless as the wind in the trees.

At dawn, Penelope’s shriek shattered the calm.

“Father! The Diamond—it’s gone!”

Upstairs, Rachel stood pale as her nightgown, the cabinet’s drawer gaping empty.

“You saw me place it there,” Penelope insisted.

Rachel’s voice was hollow. “It’s gone.”

The household erupted. Lady Verinder, stricken, debated summoning the police. Mr. Franklin, ever practical, urged haste. “The Indians—we must act before they flee.”

But his theory crumbled by midday. The jugglers had alibis—seen in Frizinghall at the hour of the theft. The magistrate, obliging but skeptical, held them on a trivial charge while the investigation floundered.

Superintendent Seegrave arrived, brusque and confident. He swept through the house, declaring the thief must be among us. The servants quailed; Rachel refused to speak to him.

The trail was cold. The Moonstone had vanished—not into the hands of foreigners, but perhaps someone far closer.

And Rachel’s silence? That, more than the theft, unnerved me.

What did she know? Why wouldn’t she say?

The mystery had only begun.

The night passed without incident, but by Friday morning, two developments had emerged.

First, the baker’s man insisted he had seen Rosanna Spearman the previous afternoon, veiled and walking toward Frizinghall across the moor. A curious claim, given Rosanna’s unmistakable stoop—yet the girl had been confined to her room all Thursday, ill. A case of mistaken identity, then? Or something more?

Second, word arrived via the postman that Mr. Candy, our jovial physician, had fallen gravely ill. His boast about a doctor’s "waterproof skin" had proven tragically false; the rain-soaked ride on Rachel’s birthday had left him feverish and delirious. Franklin, ever attentive to Rachel’s nerves, voiced concern—if the Moonstone’s fate remained unresolved, she might soon need expert medical care.

Then came the telegram. Mr. Blake had secured assistance from London: one Sergeant Cuff, a detective of considerable repute, would arrive by the morning train. Franklin’s reaction was immediate—a mix of relief and intrigue. "If half the stories about him are true," he said, "there’s no man in England better suited to untangle this mystery."

Anticipation thickened as the hour of Cuff’s arrival approached. Superintendent Seegrave, eager to preempt criticism, sequestered himself with pen and paper to draft his report. I would have gladly fetched the Sergeant myself, but the carriage was reserved for Godfrey, who lingered only to hear Cuff’s assessment before departing for a charitable engagement in town.

At the gate, I awaited the famed detective. The fly from the station delivered a gaunt, elderly man—all angles and austerity, clad in unrelenting black. His face was sharp as a blade, his skin sallow, his eyes a piercing grey that seemed to see beyond your words. He moved softly, spoke mournfully, and carried himself like a man more accustomed to funerals than felons.

"Lady Verinder’s?" he asked.

I confirmed it and led him toward the house, offering my name and position to establish my role in the affair. He ignored the matter entirely, remarking instead on the brisk sea air. I wondered, silently, how this wraith of a man had earned his formidable reputation.

We found my lady in the conservatory. En route, Cuff’s demeanor shifted abruptly when he spotted the rose garden. To my astonishment, the dour detective transformed into an enthusiast, discoursing on soil, exposure, and the virtues of grass paths over gravel. "A man’s tastes," he mused, "are often the opposite of his trade. Show me two things more opposed than a rose and a thief."

My lady, uncharacteristically flustered by his presence, recovered when Cuff cut to business: Had another investigator been consulted? Upon learning of Seegrave’s involvement, he requested an immediate interview.

The two men withdrew for what felt like an age. When they emerged, Seegrave was agitated; Cuff, languidly bored. "The Sergeant wishes to see Miss Verinder’s sitting room," Seegrave announced with forced pomp.

Upstairs, Cuff examined Rachel’s boudoir with methodical precision. His attention fixed on the smeared paint beneath the door lock—a detail Seegrave had noted earlier.

"How did this happen?" Cuff asked.

I explained the servants’ crowding, their skirts likely causing the mark. Seegrave interjected, proud of having ordered them out.

"Did you note which skirt left the smear?" Cuff pressed.

Neither of us could say.

Cuff’s expression turned withering. "In my line of work, Mr. Seegrave, there is no such thing as a trifle." He turned to Franklin. "You supervised this painting. When was it finished?"

"Wednesday afternoon," Franklin confirmed. "The vehicle dries in twelve hours."

Cuff did the arithmetic. "By Thursday morning, the paint was dry. Yet you, Superintendent, assumed wet smears from petticoats eight hours later." A devastating blow to Seegrave’s credibility.

Dismissing the superintendent as a lost cause, Cuff turned his focus to Franklin. "You may have handed us the key to this puzzle," he said.

At that moment, Rachel entered—ignoring protocol, her gaze sharp as flint. "Did you say *he* gave you the clue?" she demanded, pointing at Franklin.

Her tone was venomous. I scarcely recognized her.

Cuff studied her, unflinching. "Possibly, miss."

She fired questions back, evading his own about the paint smear. Then, with startling vitriol: "Do your duty yourself—don’t let Franklin Blake help you!"

The outburst was so unlike her I felt a chill. Even Cuff, imperturbable as stone, seemed to file it away for later consideration.

After she retreated, Franklin murmured, "You see now why I’ve been uneasy."

Cuff merely remarked on the natural distress over a lost jewel—but his eyes were calculating.

Methodically, he narrowed the timeline: The smear had been made between midnight and three that morning. Penelope, summoned, confirmed the paint was untouched when she left at twelve. Her new dress—a gift from Rachel—was scrutinized and cleared.

Cuff’s final deduction: Someone had entered that room in the dead of night.

With Seegrave dismissed (not without a parting jab about "mountains from molehills"), Cuff turned to the window, alone with his thoughts.

The game was afoot.

I entered my lady’s sitting room to find her visibly unsettled. The mere mention of Sergeant Cuff’s request to speak with her drew a sharp reaction—her lips tightened, her fingers twined restlessly in her lap.

*"Must* I see him?" she asked. "Can you not handle this in my stead, Gabriel?"

Her reluctance puzzled me, and I must have shown it plainly, for she sighed and continued with uncharacteristic hesitation.

"My nerves are not what they should be," she confessed. "There is something about that London policeman—something ominous. I cannot shake the feeling that his presence here will bring nothing but trouble. Foolish, I know, but there it is."

I scarcely knew how to respond. The more I spoke with Sergeant Cuff, the more I found him a shrewd and reasonable man. Yet my lady, ordinarily so composed, seemed almost superstitious in her aversion.

After a moment, she steadied herself, that iron resolve I had long admired reasserting itself. "Very well. If it must be done, it must. But I will not face him alone. Bring him in, Gabriel, and remain with us."

It was the first time I had seen her so affected since her youth. I returned to the boudoir, where Mr. Franklin excused himself to join Mr. Godfrey in the garden—the latter’s departure now imminent. Meanwhile, Sergeant Cuff and I proceeded to my lady’s chamber.

I could not miss the way her face paled slightly at his entrance. Yet she held herself with dignity, inquiring if he objected to my presence.

"Not at all," the Sergeant replied smoothly. "Mr. Betteredge’s insights have already proven valuable. I welcome his attendance."

With that, we took our seats, and the Sergeant began without preamble.

"I have formed certain conclusions regarding the Moonstone’s disappearance," he said, "though I will reserve those for the present. My immediate concern is the smear upon Miss Verinder’s sitting-room door—paint, as I’ve noted, transferred from a garment. That garment must be found before we proceed further."

My lady’s gaze flicked to me. "Does this mean you believe the thief will be uncovered?"

"I do not yet say *thief*, my lady," Cuff corrected. "Only that the Diamond is missing. The stained fabric may lead us to it."

"And how do you propose to find it?" she asked. "My servants have already endured one indignity at the hands of your predecessor. I will not subject them to another."

(Here, I confess, my admiration for her swelled. A mistress who defends her staff so fiercely is rare indeed.)

Sergeant Cuff inclined his head. "Precisely the difficulty I wished to address. Superintendent Seegrave’s heavy-handedness has made the servants wary. If they suspect they are singled out, they may resist cooperation. Yet their wardrobes *must* be examined—not for the Diamond, but for the marked clothing."

A standoff, then. My lady said as much, though in finer words.

"I have a solution," Cuff offered. "Let me explain the situation openly—to all the household. If they understand the search is universal, extending even to your own wardrobe, my lady, they will see it as fair. No one singled out; no cause for resentment."

I frowned. "The women will still take it as accusation."

"Not if they are told *everyone* who slept here that night is subject to inspection," Cuff countered. "A formality, yes—but one that assures them of impartiality."

My lady considered this, then nodded. "You are certain it is necessary?"

"It is the swiftest path to the truth."

She moved to ring for her maid. "You shall address them with the keys to my own wardrobe in hand."

But Cuff hesitated. "One precaution first. We must ensure *all* in the house consent—not only the servants."

My lady looked puzzled. "Miss Verinder is the only other lady present. As for gentlemen, my nephews, Mr. Blake and Mr. Ablewhite, will hardly refuse."

At that moment, as if summoned, Mr. Godfrey knocked to bid farewell—Mr. Franklin accompanying him. When the situation was explained, Mr. Godfrey promptly ordered his luggage brought back inside and handed his key to the Sergeant without protest.

"A mere formality," Cuff acknowledged with a courteous nod. "But the example set by gentlemen eases the way with the staff."

With Mr. Godfrey’s departure deferred, only Miss Rachel’s compliance remained. My lady excused herself to secure her daughter’s keys.

We waited. And waited.

At last, a note arrived—not with keys, but with a few penciled lines from my lady. Miss Rachel had refused outright. Pressed for reasons, she had wept, then declared, *"I won’t, because I won’t. Force me if you must, but I yield to nothing else."*

Dismayed, I relayed this to the Sergeant. His reaction was infuriatingly calm.

"Ah," he said. Just that—*Ah.* As if he had expected it.

"Does this end the search?" I demanded.

"It does. We cannot proceed partially. Mr. Ablewhite’s luggage may go, and return the washing-book to the girl who brought it." He set the book aside, idly trimming his nails with sudden focus.

"You seem untroubled," I observed.

"I am."

"Why would Miss Rachel hinder you? It is in her interest to aid this inquiry!"

"Patience, Mr. Betteredge. All in good time."

Had I been less devoted to Rachel, or sharper of wit, I might have grasped his meaning then. But I did not.

"What now?" I asked.

Sergeant Cuff closed his penknife. "Let us walk in the garden," he said. "I should like to see the roses."

The quickest path from my lady’s sitting-room to the garden led through the shrubbery—a place well known to you by now. I might add, for clarity’s sake, that this was Mr. Franklin’s preferred stroll. Whenever he vanished into the grounds, more often than not, we’d find him there.

Now, I’ll admit I’m a stubborn old man. The tighter Sergeant Cuff clamped his thoughts shut, the harder I strained to pry them open. As we turned onto the shrubbery path, I tried another angle.

“Given the state of things,” I said, “if I were in your position, I’d be at a loss.”

“If you were in my position,” the Sergeant replied, “you’d have settled on a theory—and any lingering doubts would’ve vanished. Never mind what that theory is just yet, Mr. Betteredge. I didn’t bring you out here to badger me. I brought you for answers. You could’ve given them indoors, but walls have ears, and in my profession, fresh air breeds clearer thinking.”

How does one outmaneuver such a man? I surrendered—waiting to see where he’d steer the conversation next.

“We’ll leave aside your young lady’s reasons,” he continued, “but her refusal to cooperate complicates matters. Since she won’t assist, we must tackle the mystery of the stained door—and thus the Diamond—another way. I’ve decided to interrogate the servants, to sift through their minds rather than their wardrobes. But first, a question or two. You’re observant—did you notice anything odd among the staff after the Diamond went missing? Any quarrels? Unusual moods? Sudden tempers or illnesses?”

Rosanna’s collapse at yesterday’s dinner sprang to mind—but before I could speak, Sergeant Cuff’s gaze snapped toward the shrubbery. He muttered, *“Hullo!”*

“What is it?” I asked.

“A twinge of rheumatism,” he announced loudly, as if for an unseen listener. “The weather’s due to change.”

We rounded the house, stepped onto the terrace, and descended into the garden. There, in the open, the Sergeant halted.

“About Rosanna Spearman,” he said. “Given her looks, it’s unlikely she’s taken a lover. But for her sake, I must ask—has she, like the others, found herself a sweetheart?”

The question staggered me. Why ask such a thing now? I gaped at him.

“I saw Rosanna hiding in the shrubbery as we passed,” the Sergeant explained.

“When you said *‘Hullo’*?”

“Precisely. If there’s a sweetheart, the hiding’s harmless. If not—given this house’s current troubles—it’s damning, and I’ll have to act accordingly.”

God help me, what was I to say? I knew the shrubbery was Mr. Franklin’s haunt. I knew Penelope had often caught Rosanna lurking there, convinced the girl was scheming to catch his eye. If my daughter was right, Rosanna might’ve been lying in wait for him when the Sergeant spotted her.

Caught between betraying the girl or letting her face Cuff’s suspicions, pity won out. I confessed Rosanna’s hopeless infatuation with Mr. Franklin.

The Sergeant’s lips twitched—his version of a laugh.

“Might as well say she’s mad for being plain and a servant,” he remarked. “Falling for a gentleman like Mr. Blake strikes me as the least of her follies. Still, I’m glad it’s settled. Secrets weigh heavy, and I’ll keep this one. Ugly women suffer enough in this world—may they find justice in the next.” He gestured at the garden. “Fine roses. Pity to pluck them, just as it pains you when discord brews below stairs. Speaking of—did you notice anything amiss among the servants after the Diamond vanished?”

His sudden shift put me on guard. I had no desire to aid his prying.

“Nothing,” I lied, “save that we all panicked—myself included.”

“Is that so?” His piercing eyes fixed on me. “Mr. Betteredge, would you humor me with a handshake? I’ve taken a peculiar liking to you.”

(Why he’d choose that moment—when I’d just deceived him—to express such regard, I’ll never fathom. I confess, I felt a flicker of pride at outwitting the famed Cuff!)

We returned to the house. The Sergeant requested a private room and ordered the indoor servants sent in one by one, by rank. I showed him to my quarters, then assembled the staff in the hall. Rosanna stood among them, calm as ever—too calm, perhaps. Had she overheard us earlier?

One by one, they faced the Sergeant:

- The cook emerged first. *“Sergeant Cuff’s a gentleman, though gloomy.”*
- My lady’s maid followed, lingering. *“If he doubts a respectable woman, he might keep it to himself!”*
- Penelope was brief. *“Poor man—crossed in love, no doubt.”*
- The first housemaid stayed long. *“I won’t be questioned by some low policeman!”*
- Rosanna stayed longest. She left pale, silent.
- The footman scoffed. *“His bootblack should be ashamed.”*
- The kitchen-maid pitied him. *“He’s got a heart—no jests at a girl’s expense.”*

When it was over, I found the Sergeant at the window, whistling *“The Last Rose of Summer.”*

“Any discoveries?” I asked.

“If Rosanna asks to go out,” he said, “let her—but inform me first.”

My interference had only sharpened his suspicions.

“You don’t truly think Rosanna took the Diamond?” I ventured.

His lips curled. *“Best I don’t say, Mr. Betteredge. You’ve a habit of losing your head.”*

A knock interrupted us—Rosanna had requested leave to walk, pleading a headache. At the Sergeant’s nod, I consented.

“Where’s the servants’ exit?” he asked. I showed him.

“Lock your door,” he instructed. “If anyone asks, I’m meditating.” With that infuriating half-smile, he vanished.

Left alone, curiosity gnawed at me. Clearly, something in the servants’ testimonies had deepened his distrust of Rosanna. The maid and housemaid—her chief tormentors—had been questioned longest. I joined them in the hall, inviting myself to tea. (A woman’s tongue, after all, loosens with a cuppa.)

Within half an hour, I’d pieced it together. The two had spied on Rosanna the day before: her locked door, the silence within, the midnight light, the crackling fire at dawn—in *June*, no less. They’d shared this with Sergeant Cuff, who’d met their eagerness with cold skepticism. Hence their grievances.

Knowing Cuff’s methods—and having seen him stalk Rosanna earlier—I grasped his game. He’d downplayed their tales to avoid alerting her.

I wandered out, uneasy, my heart heavy for the girl. Near the shrubbery, I met Mr. Franklin, freshly returned from the station. His talk with my lady had left him grim.

“Betteredge,” he said bitterly, “do you recall the morning I brought the Moonstone? I wish we’d sunk it in the quicksand.”

After a silence, he asked after Sergeant Cuff. I relayed all—including the maids’ claims about Rosanna.

Mr. Franklin saw the implication at once.

“Didn’t you say a tradesman claimed to see Rosanna in Frizinghall yesterday,” he pressed, “when we thought her ill?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Then she lied. The ‘illness’ was a ruse. That paint-stained dress is hers; the fire was to burn it. She stole the Diamond. I’ll inform my aunt at once.”

Sergeant Cuff’s voice cut in. We turned to face him.

“Why not?” Mr. Franklin demanded, heat rising.

“Because her ladyship will tell Miss Verinder.”

“And what if she does?”

The Sergeant’s gaze hardened. *“Is it wise, sir, to ask me that—now of all times?”*

A charged silence followed. Mr. Franklin stepped closer.

“You tread on delicate ground, Mr. Cuff.”

“I’ve treaded on delicate ground before.”

“Am I to understand you forbid me to speak to my aunt?”

“Understand this: if you tell Lady Verinder—or anyone—what’s happened before I permit it, I quit the case.”

Defeated, Mr. Franklin turned away, storming off.

Left trembling, I grasped two things: Miss Rachel was tangled in this somehow, and those two men understood each other perfectly—without a word of explanation.

Sergeant Cuff fixed me with a look. *“You’ve played detective on your own, Mr. Betteredge. In future, you’ll work with me.”* He steered me toward the path he’d come by.

I shook free. Thief or not, I pitied Rosanna.

“What do you want?” I demanded.

“Local knowledge,” he said. “Is there a path from here to the sea?” He pointed toward the Shivering Sand.

“Yes.”

“Show me.”

And so, beneath the fading summer sky, we set off—side by side—toward the quicksand.

Sergeant Cuff fell into silence again, his sharp mind turning over the puzzle before us, until we reached the edge of the fir plantation leading down to the quicksand. There, as if arriving at some private conclusion, he roused himself and addressed me with renewed focus.

“Mr. Betteredge,” he began, “since you’ve been good enough to assist me—and since you may prove useful before the night is through—I see no reason to dance around the truth any longer. You refuse to speak against Rosanna Spearman out of loyalty and pity. Admirable sentiments, but entirely wasted in this case. Rosanna is in no danger—none at all, even if I prove her involvement in the Diamond’s disappearance as clearly as I see your nose before me.”

“You mean my lady won’t press charges?” I asked.

“I mean she *can’t*,” the Sergeant corrected. “Rosanna is merely a pawn in another’s game, and she’ll be shielded for that person’s sake.”

His tone brooked no argument. Yet something in me still resisted. “Can’t you name this other person?” I pressed.

“Can’t *you*, Mr. Betteredge?”

“No.”

Sergeant Cuff stopped dead, studying me with something between pity and fascination.

“I’ve always had a soft spot for human weakness,” he mused. “At this moment, Mr. Betteredge, I feel particularly tender toward *you*. And you, in turn, feel tender toward Rosanna, don’t you? Tell me—has she had new linen lately?”

The question caught me off guard. Seeing no harm in honesty, I admitted that Rosanna had arrived with scant wardrobe and that my lady, pleased with her conduct, had gifted her fresh linens not two weeks prior.

“A wretched world indeed,” sighed the Sergeant. “Life’s a target, Mr. Betteredge, and misfortune never misses. Without that new linen, we’d have found a paint-stained nightgown among her things and had our proof. You follow me, don’t you? You’ve seen what the maids discovered outside her door. You know what she did after falling ‘ill’ yesterday. Must I spell it out? It’s plain as daylight. When Seegrave showed the smear on the door, Rosanna—suspecting her own garments—slipped away to replace the stained item. She crafted a new one in secret, lit a fire (not to burn evidence, mind you—too risky with prying eyes nearby), and now, mark my words, she’s disposing of the original somewhere along that desolate shore ahead of us. I tracked her to Cobb’s Hole tonight, to a particular cottage. She left with something concealed beneath her cloak—and mark this, Mr. Betteredge—a cloak hides many sins. She headed north along the coast. Tell me, do you consider this shoreline picturesque?”

“Yes,” I replied curtly.

“Tastes differ,” he said dryly. “From a detective’s view, it’s a miserable place to tail someone—no cover at all. I had to choose between confronting her or letting her play out her hand. For reasons I’ll keep to myself, I chose the latter. Now, sand is an excellent witness—it remembers every step. If we don’t intercept Rosanna, the sand may yet tell her secrets. Shall I take the lead?”

A feverish curiosity had taken hold of me. I nodded, and the Sergeant moved ahead, scanning the ground with hawk-like precision. I followed, my pulse quickening.

The fading light cast long shadows over the Shivering Sand, a place that had always unnerved me. The tide was turning, the quicksand’s surface rippling faintly—the only movement in that dead, silent expanse.

The Sergeant stiffened as the quivering sand caught his eye. After a moment’s study, he turned back.

“Treacherous ground,” he muttered. “No sign of Rosanna—yet.”

He led me farther down, where only our own footprints marked the shore.

“Where lies Cobb’s Hole from here?” he asked.

“Due south,” I answered.

“Then she walked *toward* this spot,” he mused. “Can we reach the village by the beach now?”

I confirmed we could.

“Then let’s move briskly,” he said. “I want to find where she left the shore before dark.”

We hadn’t gone far when the Sergeant dropped suddenly to his knees.

“Your marine landscape has its uses,” he remarked. “Here—a woman’s footsteps. Rosanna’s, unless proven otherwise. Notice how they’re deliberately muddled. She knows sand’s betraying nature as well as I do. But haste made her careless. See here—steps toward the water’s edge, then returning. Heel marks near the rocks. She waded out, circled back, then resumed her path further down. Why? Because she carried something she meant to hide—not destroy, or why such pains to conceal her route? Let’s see what the cottage reveals.”

My enthusiasm waned. “What good am I there?”

“Modesty becomes you, Mr. Betteredge,” he said, almost amused. “But your presence loosens tongues. Alone, they’d clam up. With you, conversation flows.”

Reluctantly, I agreed.

The cottage belonged to the Yollands—decent folk, parents to Limping Lucy, Rosanna’s friend. If she had visited them, surely it was innocent? Perhaps the Sergeant’s suspicions were misplaced.

Mrs. Yolland welcomed us, starry-eyed at hosting London’s famed detective. The Sergeant, ever indirect, steered talk from fish prices to Rosanna’s hardships, painting himself as her champion. Within minutes, Mrs. Yolland was pouring him gin, singing Rosanna’s praises.

Then came the revelation: Rosanna meant to leave us. No warning to my lady or me—a breach of propriety. And she’d bought traveling supplies, paid for them herself, though Mrs. Yolland fretted over taking the girl’s savings.

“She wrote a long letter upstairs,” Mrs. Yolland added. “Wouldn’t say to whom. But she’s got a friend somewhere—mark my words.”

I bristled. “She’d have told *me* first.”

Mrs. Yolland laughed. “She bought chains and a tin case from me tonight—for her ‘cuffs and collars,’ she said. Odd choices, but there’s no fathoming Rosanna.”

The Sergeant’s eyes gleamed. He examined the items—cheap, yet telling. My heart sank. Without meaning to, I’d led him straight to damning clues.

When we finally left, the Sergeant was pensive. “Mrs. Yolland has given me much to ponder,” he admitted.

I said nothing. The night’s discoveries weighed heavily, and I couldn’t shake the feeling that I’d unwittingly betrayed Rosanna—and myself.

The lamplight in my lady’s sitting room was dim, the shade drawn low over her face. She did not lift her eyes when we entered, her gaze fixed stubbornly on the open book before her.

“Officer,” she said, her voice cool, “does your investigation require advance notice if someone in this house intends to depart?”

Sergeant Cuff inclined his head. “It would be most useful, my lady.”

“Then you should know that Miss Verinder has arranged to leave for her aunt’s house in Frizinghall tomorrow morning.”

The Sergeant’s eyes flicked to me. My pulse hammered—whether from dread or anger, I could not say. I opened my mouth, then closed it, my courage failing.

“Might I ask when Miss Verinder informed you of this?” the Sergeant pressed.

“An hour ago,” my lady replied.

Again, the Sergeant’s gaze found mine. My heart pounded as if I were a young man again, though age should have steadied me.

“I’ve no right to hinder Miss Verinder’s movements,” said Cuff, “but I ask that her departure be delayed until afternoon. I must go to Frizinghall myself tomorrow and will return by two o’clock. If she remains until then, I wish to speak with her—unexpectedly—before she goes.”

At my lady’s command, I instructed the coachman to delay the carriage until two.

“Is there more?” she asked, her fingers tightening on the edge of her book.

“Only this: should Miss Verinder question the change, do not mention me as the cause.”

My mistress’s head lifted sharply, as if she meant to speak, but she checked herself. With a dismissive wave, she returned to her reading.

Once in the hall, Cuff exhaled. “A remarkable woman. Had she less restraint, the truth you seek would have been plain tonight.”

Then, like a bolt, understanding struck me. I seized the Sergeant by his collar, slamming him against the wall.

“Damn you!” I snarled. “You’ve known something foul about Miss Rachel all along!”

Cuff didn’t struggle. He merely met my fury with that weary, unshaken stare.

“Ah,” he said. “You’ve caught up at last.”

My grip slackened. Shame flooded me. For fifty years I had served this family. Miss Rachel had been the child who clambered onto my knees, who tugged my whiskers with laughing hands. Faults she might have, but to me, she was still the bright-eyed girl I’d loved as my own.

I muttered an apology, my voice thick.

Cuff waved it off. “In my profession, thin skin is a luxury. If it eases you, grip my collar again—though you’ve no talent for it.” His mouth twitched, as if at some private jest.

I led him to my sitting room and shut the door.

“Out with it, Sergeant. What do you *know*?”

“Not suspect—*know*,” he said. “Miss Verinder has had the Moonstone from the start. She confided in Rosanna Spearman, counting on our suspicion falling on the maid instead. There’s your case, neat as a pin.”

My hands clenched. “Your proof?”

“You’ll have it tomorrow. If Miss Verinder refuses to delay her trip—and she will—I’ll lay the matter before your mistress. Be present. Hear it yourself.” He rose. “Enough for tonight. Supper awaits, and grace must be said.”

I had no appetite. I saw him served, then retreated to the terrace, the night air heavy with coming storm. The sky churned; the dogs whined restlessly. My lady’s message recalled the Indians, soon to be released. Cuff, ever sharp, seized on it—he would seek the traveler mentioned in Seegrave’s report.

Penelope found me, her face drawn. Miss Rachel had raged at the delay, declaring the house intolerable with a policeman under its roof.

“Nothing’s right, Father,” Penelope whispered. “It’s as if doom hangs over us.”

Before I could answer, Rosanna rushed past, her hand pressed to her heart. When I called after her, she twisted away like a creature wounded.

Franklin, emerging, confessed he might have spurred her distress. He’d been at billiards when she approached, trembling, as if on the verge of confession.

“I handled it poorly,” he admitted. “Tell her I meant no cruelty.”

But Rosanna had locked herself away. The night deepened. I found Cuff later, asleep across three chairs near Miss Rachel’s corridor—a sentinel waiting for secrets to unfold.

“The Diamond’s to blame,” he said when I roused him. “Not me.”

I stared into the dark. “I wish to God it had never come here.”

The Sergeant sighed, shifting on his hard bed. “So do I.”

The night passed without incident, and—to my relief—Sergeant Cuff’s watch yielded no sign of any secret meeting between Miss Rachel and Rosanna.

I had expected the Sergeant to depart for Frizinghall at first light, but he lingered, as though some unfinished business held him back. Leaving him to his own devices, I wandered into the grounds, where I encountered Mr. Franklin pacing his favored path along the shrubbery. No sooner had we exchanged greetings than the Sergeant himself appeared, stepping forward with an air of purpose.

Mr. Franklin received him coldly. "Have you something to say to me?"

"I do, sir," replied Cuff, unfazed. "Regarding my investigation. You perceived its true direction yesterday. Naturally, given your position, you are disturbed. And naturally, you resent me for exposing what you would prefer remain hidden."

"What do you want?" Franklin snapped.

"Only to remind you that I have yet to be proven wrong. And to suggest that, as a citizen, you might assist the law with any special knowledge you possess."

"I possess none," said Franklin.

The Sergeant pressed on, undeterred. "You could spare me a fruitless errand if you chose to be frank."

"I have nothing to say."

"A servant spoke to you privately last night."

Again, Franklin cut him short. "I have nothing to say."

I recalled then the rustling in the passage the evening before—the flicker of skirts vanishing into shadow. No doubt Cuff had caught enough before my interruption to suspect Rosanna had unburdened herself to Franklin.

At that very moment, Rosanna herself appeared at the path’s end, trailed by my daughter Penelope, who seemed to be urging her back toward the house. Seeing Franklin was not alone, Rosanna halted, uncertain. Cuff, ever the tactician, pretended not to notice them.

Before Franklin or I could react, the Sergeant raised his voice deliberately. "You needn’t fear for the girl, sir. If you have any regard for Rosanna Spearman, I advise you to confide in me."

Franklin, playing the same game, answered loudly, "I have no interest whatsoever in Rosanna Spearman."

I glanced toward the girls. Rosanna stiffened as if struck, then turned abruptly, allowing Penelope to lead her away. The breakfast bell rang, and even Cuff had to concede defeat.

"I’m for Frizinghall," he told me quietly. "I’ll return by two." With that, he departed, leaving us in uneasy respite.

Once alone, Franklin turned to me. "You must make amends with Rosanna. That was a trap, Betteredge. Had he flustered me or provoked her, one of us might have given something away. I saw no choice but to answer as I did—it silenced her and showed Cuff I saw through him."

I kept my thoughts to myself. The Sergeant had done worse than listen—he had weaponized Rosanna’s infatuation, baiting Franklin where she could hear.

"Listening is the least of it, sir," I said. "At this rate, we’ll all be too busy eavesdropping on one another to speak at all."

Franklin sighed. "I can’t help Cuff trap Rosanna—yet I see no way out of this unless the Diamond is traced to her. I’m caught between two horrors."

I understood too well.

With Cuff gone, the household settled into uneasy quiet. Miss Rachel remained barricaded in her room. Franklin, after a tense breakfast, took to the moors to clear his head. The weather had turned, wind lashing the coast, though the rain held off—a day for vigorous walking, if one had the strength for it.

Later, Penelope sought me out, troubled. "You must speak to Rosanna, Father. She’s not herself."

I pressed for reasons—a habit of mine, as men ought to steer women toward reason.

"Mr. Franklin’s words crushed her," Penelope said. "She moved like a ghost afterward."

I found Rosanna sweeping the corridor, her face pale, her eyes dull as tarnished silver.

"Cheer up, lass," I urged. "Mr. Franklin meant no harm."

She replied mechanically, thanking me without meeting my gaze.

When I pressed further, she said, "I’ll confess—but not to my lady. To Mr. Franklin."

"Not today," I cautioned. "He’s gone walking."

She only nodded, resuming her work like an automaton.

Penelope and I withdrew, uneasy. "This wants a doctor," I muttered.

But Mr. Candy was ill, and his assistant, Ezra Jennings, was a stranger none of us trusted. I debated informing my lady, yet hesitated to burden her further.

Time slipped away. At a quarter to two, a familiar voice called from the drive.

Sergeant Cuff had returned.

The air was thick with tension as I descended the front steps, only to collide with Sergeant Cuff. Pride warred within me—after our recent clashes, I had no wish to betray curiosity about his investigations. Yet curiosity won, as it so often does. My dignity abandoned me entirely.

“Any news from Frizinghall?” I demanded.

The Sergeant’s expression remained unreadable. “I’ve seen the Indians,” he replied. “And I’ve uncovered Rosanna’s purchase in town last Thursday.” His voice lowered. “The Indians will be released next Wednesday. Make no mistake, Mr. Betteredge—they came for the Moonstone. Their plans were disrupted by the events of Wednesday night, but if *we* fail to recover the jewel, rest assured *they* will not.”

A chill crawled down my spine. At that moment, Mr. Franklin returned from his walk. Unlike me, he mastered his curiosity, passing us in silence. I, however, had already sacrificed my pride—I might as well reap the benefits.

“Enough of the Indians,” I pressed. “What of Rosanna?”

Cuff’s frown deepened. “The mystery thickens. She visited Maltby’s linen drapery in Frizinghall—bought nothing but long cloth, enough for a nightgown, and of a very particular quality.”

“Whose nightgown?” I interrupted.

“Her own, naturally.” His tone was patient, as if explaining to a child. “Between midnight and three on Thursday, she must have slipped into Miss Verinder’s room to conceal the Diamond. Returning, her nightgown brushed the wet paint. She couldn’t remove the stain, nor destroy the garment without raising suspicion. Hence, the replacement.”

I bristled. “What proof is there it was hers?”

“The simplicity of the fabric,” he countered. “A lady’s nightgown would require lace, frills—far more effort than a servant could manage overnight. No, Mr. Betteredge, the question isn’t *what* she did, but *why* she hid the stained gown instead of burning it. The answer lies at the Shivering Sand.”

“And how do you propose to find it?”

A ghost of a smile flickered across his face. “That, I keep to myself.”

(For the curious, I’ll reveal what he withheld: armed with a search warrant, Cuff suspected Rosanna carried some record of the hiding place. A seasoned officer, he knew such precautions were common among thieves.)

“Enough speculation,” he said briskly. “Where is Joyce?”

As if summoned by the hour, the clock struck two, and the carriage rolled into view—bound for Frizinghall, bearing Miss Rachel to her aunt’s.

“One matter at a time,” Cuff said, halting me. “Miss Verinder first.”

The rain still loomed, so the closed carriage had been prepared. With a gesture, Cuff beckoned Samuel from his perch.

“A friend of mine waits among the trees,” he murmured. “He’ll join you in the rumble. Keep silent, and shut your eyes—unless you wish trouble.”

The implication struck like a blow: Rachel was to be watched. A spy in her own carriage! I could have bitten off my tongue for engaging with Cuff at all.

Lady Verinder emerged first, a silent sentinel on the steps, arms folded beneath her cloak. Then Rachel descended—a vision in yellow, her dark beauty accentuated by the tight-fitting jacket, straw hat, and veil. But her eyes burned with a feverish intensity, her lips pale and unsmiling.

She kissed her mother hastily. “Try to forgive me,” she whispered, then yanked down her veil with such force it tore. In a flash, she was in the carriage, as if fleeing a storm.

Cuff moved swiftly, blocking her path. “A word, miss,” he said, hand on the door. “Your departure hinders the Diamond’s recovery. The choice is yours.”

Rachel’s answer was a cold command: “Drive on, James!”

The door slammed. Mr. Franklin lunged forward. “Goodbye, Rachel,” he called, offering his hand.

“Drive on!” she cried again, ignoring him entirely. Franklin recoiled, stunned. The coachman hesitated, looking to Lady Verinder, who stood rigid. With a fraught gesture, she signaled the carriage onward, then retreated inside.

Franklin found his voice. “Aunt, you were right,” he shouted after her. “Thank you—let me go.”

Lady Verinder turned, her face a tempest of emotions. “See me before you leave,” she managed, her voice breaking, then vanished upstairs.

Franklin faced me, eyes glistening. “Get me to the train, Betteredge. Quickly.”

Rachel had undone him completely. Need I say more of his devotion?

Left alone with Cuff, I glared as he hummed *The Last Rose of Summer*, watching the carriage disappear down the drive.

“There’s a time for whistling,” I snapped, “and this isn’t it.”

The carriage reappeared briefly through the trees—Samuel in the rumble, and beside him, another figure.

“All right,” Cuff murmured. He turned, all business. “Now, Mr. Betteredge, we proceed without mercy. Starting with Rosanna. Where is Joyce?”

Joyce, when found, was trembling. He’d lost Rosanna nearly an hour prior, too cautious in his surveillance.

Cuff dismissed him with icy calm. “Your talents are wasted here, Mr. Joyce. Good morning.”

Rosanna’s disappearance left me reeling. Cuff, however, was unmoved.

“She won’t escape,” he assured me. “As long as Miss Verinder is watched, Rosanna will lead us to her. They’ll meet in Frizinghall now, not here. The investigation shifts—sooner than expected.” He paused. “Gather the servants again.”

Despite myself, the old detective fever surged. I gripped his arm. “What now?”

Cuff stared into the distance, as if addressing the heavens. “If this man understood roses,” he mused, “he’d be the most perfect creature alive.”

Then, briskly: “Rosanna has two choices—Frizinghall directly, or the Shivering Sand first. We must learn who last saw her.”

The kitchenmaid Nancy provided the clue: Rosanna had handed a letter to the butcher’s man, insisting it be posted in Frizinghall despite the detour.

“The hiding place is in that letter,” Cuff concluded. “I must see its address. If it’s as I suspect, Mrs. Yolland will have another visitor come Monday.”

As we prepared the pony-chaise, a fresh revelation awaited us in the stable yard—but that, as they say, is another chapter.

The word of Rosanna’s disappearance had spread like wildfire among the outdoor staff. They, too, had been searching, and soon enough, they cornered a wiry little garden hand known as Duffy—a boy who’d seen Rosanna not half an hour before, sprinting through the fir plantation toward the shore.

Sergeant Cuff’s eyes sharpened. “Does this boy know the coast?”

“Born and raised on it,” I answered.

“Duffy,” said the Sergeant, “fancy earning a shilling? Then come with me. Mr. Betteredge, keep the pony-chaise ready.”

And with that, he was off toward the Shivering Sand at a pace my old legs couldn’t hope to match. Duffy, wild with excitement, let out a whoop and dashed after him.

What followed was a blur. A restless, gnawing dread took hold of me. I moved about the house like a man half-asleep, doing things I scarcely remember now. It might have been minutes or hours before Duffy came tearing back, clutching a scrap of paper torn from the Sergeant’s pocket-book.

I sent the nearest maid to fetch it, then hurried after the boy myself—determined to see this mystery through before surrendering the boot. Some stubborn instinct to shield the girl, even now, flared up in me.

The storm rolled in as I neared the shore, rain slashing sideways, the sea roaring against the sandbank. I spotted Duffy crouched under the dunes, then, further on, the lone figure of Sergeant Cuff standing like a black sentinel against the yellow waste of the beach.

He waved me toward him. “This way! Down here!”

By the time I reached him, I was gasping, my heart hammering as if it meant to break free. His face stopped me cold—there was something in his eyes, something hollow and horrified. Without a word, he snatched the boot from me and pressed it into a fresh footprint in the sand.

A perfect fit.

The print led straight toward the jagged rocks of the South Spit, not yet washed away by the rain. Cuff followed the trail, testing each step with that dreadful patience of his, but no matter where he looked, the marks only went *toward* the sea. Never back.

At last, he straightened. His gaze locked onto mine, then drifted to the churning water, the tide swallowing the quicksand inch by inch. I didn’t need words to understand. A numb terror seized me, and my knees gave way.

“She returned to the hiding place,” Cuff muttered, more to himself than to me. “Something happened on those rocks.”

But I knew better. The way she’d looked at me earlier—empty, resigned—rose like a specter in my mind. This was no accident.

I tried to speak, to force the words out: *She went there to die.* But my throat closed around them. All I could see was Rosanna as she’d been that first morning, whispering how the Shivering Sand called to her, how she wondered if her grave waited there. The horror of it struck deep, twisting like a knife—my own daughter was her age. The thought was unbearable.

Cuff pulled me to my feet and turned me away from the water just as the others came running—the outdoor men, the fisherman Yolland—all shouting, asking if she’d been found.

The Sergeant showed them the footprints, his voice grim. “An accident. A fatal one.”

Then he turned to Yolland. “Could a boat have taken her from those rocks in this weather?”

Yolland shook his head, eyeing the monstrous waves. “No boat ever made could reach her there.”

Cuff studied the vanishing prints. “Then she didn’t leave by land.” He glanced at Yolland again. “Nor by sea.”

A pause. Then, calculating: “She was seen running here half an hour before I arrived. Call it an hour ago. How high was the tide then, on the south side?”

“Not enough to drown a kitten,” Yolland said.

Cuff’s gaze shifted north, toward the quicksand. “And here?”

“Less. Just awash.”

The Sergeant turned to me, but I found my voice before he could speak.

“No accident,” I said. “She came here to end her life.”

A ripple went through the men. Cuff stiffened. “How do you know?”

But before I could answer, Yolland stepped forward, his voice low. “There’s a ledge of rock four feet out from the Spit—waist-deep at most. If she’d slipped, she’d have struck it. She’d have been found.” He met my eyes. “She waded into the Deeps. On purpose.”

The Sergeant fell silent. So did we all.

As we trudged back toward the house, the under-groom came running, his face drawn. He pressed a note into my hand. “From Penelope, sir. Found in Rosanna’s room.”

Her last words.

*You’ve forgiven me often, Mr. Betteredge. When you see the Shivering Sand again, forgive me once more. I’ve found my grave where it waited for me. I lived and died grateful for your kindness.*

That was all. But it was enough.

The tears came then—great, heaving sobs I couldn’t control. Cuff moved toward me, perhaps to offer comfort, but I recoiled.

“It was the dread of you that drove her to it,” I choked out.

He shook his head. “You’re wrong. But we’ll speak of it later.”

With the groom’s help, I followed the others back through the lashing rain, toward whatever fresh nightmare awaited us at the house.

The news had raced ahead of us. By the time we reached the house, the servants were in turmoil. As we passed Lady Verinder’s door, it flew open with a violent force. My mistress stormed out, her composure shattered, her face a mask of horror. Behind her, Mr. Franklin followed, his attempts to calm her proving futile.

"You are responsible for this!" she cried, her voice trembling with fury as she leveled an accusing finger at Sergeant Cuff. "Gabriel! Pay this man his wages and rid me of his presence!"

The Sergeant alone remained unshaken. He met her outburst with quiet authority. "My lady, I am no more to blame for this tragedy than you are," he said, his tone respectful but firm. "If, in half an hour, you still wish me gone, I will accept my dismissal—but not your money."

His words struck home. My mistress hesitated, then allowed Mr. Franklin to guide her back inside. As the door closed, the Sergeant’s sharp eyes swept over the gathered servants, lingering on Penelope, the only one among them in tears.

"When your father has changed into dry clothes," he said to her, "join us in his room."

Before the half-hour had passed, I had dressed and lent the Sergeant what spare clothing I could offer. Penelope entered, her eyes still damp, and sat beside me. I held her close, struck by the depth of her loyalty. She buried her face against my shoulder, and for a moment, we sat in silence—both of us, I think, mourning the poor lost soul who had met such a wretched end.

The Sergeant stood by the window, his back to us, lost in thought. I thanked him for his consideration, knowing full well that folk of our station seldom had the luxury of indulging in grief. Life demanded we swallow our sorrow and carry on.

When the questioning began, Penelope confirmed what I had suspected: Rosanna’s despair had been born of love—love for Mr. Franklin. Yet she had kept silent, for Rosanna’s sake and his.

"I’ll not have him burdened with guilt," I added. "If Rosanna died for love of him, let him leave this house without that knowledge weighing on his conscience."

The Sergeant murmured agreement, then fell silent, as if weighing Penelope’s words against some private theory.

Soon after, my lady’s bell summoned me. In the hall, I crossed paths with Mr. Franklin, who informed me that Lady Verinder was ready to receive the Sergeant—with me in attendance, as before. But first, he wished to speak to Cuff himself.

As we walked, Mr. Franklin paused, his gaze fixed on the railway timetable. "You mean to leave us, then, sir?" I asked. "Surely Miss Rachel will come around, given time?"

"She will," he replied, his voice hollow. "When she learns I am gone—and that she need never see me again."

It was not anger that drove him, but resignation. He had seen, at last, how even the mention of his name inflamed Miss Rachel’s temper. The realization had wounded him deeply. Now, resolved to spare her further distress, he would depart.

What he had to say to the Sergeant was brief. He conveyed my lady’s regret for her earlier outburst and asked whether Cuff would now accept his fee and leave the matter of the Diamond unresolved.

The Sergeant’s refusal was firm. "My duty is not yet done," he said. "When I have laid out the facts plainly for Lady Verinder, and advised her on how to proceed, then—and only then—will I consider my obligation fulfilled. The choice to continue will be hers."

There was no arguing with such integrity. As I prepared to escort him to my lady, he asked if Mr. Franklin wished to join us.

"Not unless Lady Verinder insists," Mr. Franklin replied. Then, in a low voice to me, he added, "I know what he means to say about Rachel. I love her too much to hear it and remain calm. Leave me be."

I left him there, leaning heavily against the window, his face hidden in his hands. Penelope hovered nearby, her heart aching for him. Had I been in his place, I might have called her in—for there is no solace like a woman’s sympathy when another has wounded you.

But I had my duty. With the Sergeant at my side, I went to face my lady.

The last time we had stood before her, she had scarcely looked up from her book. Now, her gaze was steady, her bearing unshaken. The Verinder spirit burned in her eyes. Whatever the Sergeant had to say, she would meet it head-on—a worthy match for any man, even one as formidable as Sergeant Cuff.

The moment we were seated, Lady Verinder broke the silence. Her voice was steady, but I could see the effort it cost her.

"Sergeant Cuff," she began, "I spoke to you earlier in a manner I now regret. I make no excuses. If I wronged you, I sincerely apologize."

The Sergeant, ever the professional, accepted her words with a respectful nod. He was not a man easily moved, but even he could not deny the dignity in her tone.

"Permission to justify myself, my lady," he replied. "My conduct in this investigation has been guided by one principle—not to alarm Rosanna Spearman prematurely. Mr. Betteredge can attest to that."

I confirmed it. Yet the Sergeant, relentless, pressed further. He was steering toward a confrontation, and none of us could avoid it.

"I’ve heard theories about Rosanna’s death," he said, "but my own belief remains unchanged. The Diamond drove her to despair. I don’t know the nature of that despair, but I suspect someone in this house does."

A silence fell, thick and suffocating. The storm outside rattled the windows, as if nature itself braced for what came next.

"Speak plainly," Lady Verinder commanded.

"The person I refer to," said Cuff, "is your daughter."

Her ladyship’s hand, resting near her cheque-book, trembled slightly. It pained me to see it—this woman who had always been strength itself.

"I had hoped," she said, her voice low but unwavering, "to settle this matter without Rachel’s name being spoken between us. But you leave me no choice. Proceed."

Cuff consulted his watch. "Ordinarily, I’d prefer to submit my findings in writing. But time is against us. What I must say will be painful—for you to hear, and for me to speak."

Lady Verinder lifted her chin. "Then let me make it easier. You suspect my daughter of hiding the Diamond for her own purposes. Is that correct?"

"Unquestionably."

"Then know this," she said, her gaze unflinching. "As her mother, I tell you she is *incapable* of such deceit. You have known her days; I have known her a lifetime. State your suspicions—you will not offend me. But you are wrong."

She turned to me, offering her hand. I kissed it, wordless.

"Continue, Sergeant," she said.

And so he did, with methodical cruelty.

"For twenty years," he said, "I’ve dealt with the secrets of families. Young women of rank, burdened by debts they dare not confess—it is not uncommon. Milliners, jewelers… or other, darker obligations." He paused. "Now consider Miss Verinder’s behavior. She refused to speak to Superintendent Seegrave. She resented Mr. Blake’s efforts. She barred the examination of her wardrobe. These are not the actions of an innocent woman. They are the actions of one hiding something."

My lady’s expression did not change. "You are misled by circumstance."

Cuff pressed on, relentless. He spoke of Rosanna—her history, her possible role as an accomplice. He painted a picture so damning that even I, loyal as I was, felt a chill.

When he finished, my mistress was pale but composed. "Your proposal?"

"Two paths," said Cuff. "First, surveillance—watch Miss Verinder’s movements, her correspondence. Place a discreet agent in this house. Trace the Diamond if she tries to sell it."

Lady Verinder’s eyes flashed. "Unacceptable."

"Then the alternative," said Cuff smoothly. "A shock. Tell her of Rosanna’s death—abruptly, without warning. If guilt or remorse stirs her, she may confess."

To my astonishment, my lady agreed. "I will do it myself."

And with that, she rose, summoned her carriage, and departed for Frizinghall—leaving Sergeant Cuff, for once in his career, utterly speechless.

With my mistress gone, I turned my attention to Sergeant Cuff. I found him in a quiet corner of the hall, flipping through his memorandum book, his lips curled in quiet irritation.

"Making notes on the case?" I asked.

"No," he replied shortly. "Seeing what my next job is."

"Oh," I said. "So you think it's over, then?"

"I think," said Sergeant Cuff, "that Lady Verinder is one of the sharpest women in England. And I think a rose is far more interesting than any diamond. Where’s the gardener, Mr. Betteredge?"

That was all I could get out of him on the matter of the Moonstone. His interest in the case had evaporated, replaced by a single-minded determination to track down the gardener. Within the hour, I heard them arguing loudly in the conservatory—another dispute over the dog-rose.

Meanwhile, I needed to learn whether Mr. Franklin still intended to leave on the afternoon train. After hearing of the failed conference with my lady, he chose to delay his departure, waiting instead for news from Frizinghall. This reasonable decision, which would have caused no trouble for most men, only agitated Mr. Franklin further. Left with nothing to occupy him, his restless nature took over, and he ricocheted between his various foreign affectations like a man possessed.

Now Italian, now German, now French, he drifted from room to room, with no topic but Miss Rachel’s cruelty and no audience but me. I found him under the map of Modern Italy in the library, utterly absorbed in his own grievances.

"I have aspirations, Betteredge," he lamented. "But what use are they now? There are depths of goodness in me, if only Rachel had drawn them out!"

He painted such a tragic portrait of his own neglected virtues that I was at a loss for comfort—until I remembered the universal remedy: *Robinson Crusoe*. I fetched my copy, but when I returned, the library was empty. Only the map of Modern Italy stared back at me.

I checked the drawing-room—nothing but his discarded handkerchief. The dining-room revealed only Samuel, standing with a biscuit and sherry, perplexed by Mr. Franklin’s abrupt disappearance after furiously ringing for refreshments.

At last, I found him in the morning-room, tracing idle shapes on the fogged windowpane.

"Your sherry is waiting, sir," I said.

He didn’t hear me. He was lost in thought.

"How do *you* explain Rachel’s behavior, Betteredge?" he demanded.

Unprepared, I offered *Robinson Crusoe* as a possible source of wisdom. He snapped it shut and launched into a tirade of German-inflected philosophy.

"Why not examine it logically?" he cried. "First, the Objective view: the loss of the Moonstone distressed her. Then, the Subjective: her nerves make her act unlike herself. Finally, the Objective-Subjective synthesis: Rachel is not Rachel at present, but Somebody Else entirely! Do I resent Somebody Else’s cruelty? Of course not. So, logically, I am perfectly content. Now—where’s that sherry?"

By this point, I wasn’t sure whose thoughts were whose. I fetched the sherry, retreated to my room, and smoked the most soothing pipe of my life.

But Mr. Franklin wasn’t done with me. Drifting into the hall, he caught the scent of my tobacco and was reminded that he had foolishly given up smoking for Rachel’s sake. In an instant, he burst in with his cigar-case, waxing poetic in his French manner.

"Betteredge, a light! Can a man smoke as long as I have and not realize that the secret to women lies in his cigar-case? Listen closely. A bad cigar? You toss it aside and try another. A woman breaks your heart? The solution is obvious!"

I shook my head. Clever, perhaps, but experience had taught me otherwise.

"The law, Mr. Franklin," I said, "insists you finish the cigar you’ve chosen."

He laughed, and we were merry—until his next mood surfaced.

So passed our time—until the pony-chaise returned earlier than expected. My lady had decided to stay with her sister. The groom brought two letters: one for Mr. Franklin, one for me.

Mine contained a cheque, confirming Sergeant Cuff’s dismissal. I summoned him at once. He arrived, still muttering about the gardener’s obstinacy.

"You’ve heard from her ladyship," he said wearily. "Am I involved?"

"Judge for yourself," I said, and read the letter aloud.

Lady Verinder detailed Rachel’s sworn declaration: no private words with Rosanna, no encounter the night the Diamond vanished, no communication since. She had confronted Rachel with Rosanna’s suicide, but Rachel remained silent, saying only: *"The day will come when you’ll know why I don’t fear suspicion—and why I keep silent even to you."*

The letter concluded with praise for the Sergeant’s skill but firm conviction that he had been misled.

I handed him the cheque. "Do you believe that part?" I asked sharply.

He studied it. "Her ladyship is generous. I’ll remember this amount when the time comes."

"What do you mean?"

"Scandals like this don’t stay buried," he said. "We’ll hear more of the Moonstone before long."

His tone made it clear: he believed Rachel had lied to her mother.

"Sergeant Cuff," I said hotly, "that’s an insult to my lady and Miss Rachel!"

"Take it as a warning, Mr. Betteredge."

I turned to the window to compose myself—just in time to see the gardener, Mr. Begbie, waiting to resume their quarrel.

"My compliments to the Sergeant," Begbie called. "If he’s walking to the station, I’ll join him!"

"Not convinced yet?" Cuff retorted.

"Not a bit!"

"Then I’ll walk!"

"Then I’ll meet you at the gate!"

My anger dissolved. Cuff seized the moment.

"Why not accept my view as her ladyship does? Say the circumstances misled me."

Pride made me resist, but curiosity won. I prodded him further.

"Very well. Back your opinion. How will we hear of the Moonstone again?"

He gripped my hand, shaking it warmly. "Mr. Betteredge, I’d serve in any household with you! But since you ask—three things will happen."

"Go on."

"First, you’ll hear from the Yollands when Rosanna’s letter reaches Cobb’s Hole on Monday."

The words struck like ice water. Rachel’s innocence left Rosanna’s actions unexplained—a fact I’d overlooked until now.

"Second, the Indians will return. If Rachel stays here, you’ll see them nearby. If she goes to London, they’ll follow."

I dismissed this—the jugglers no longer concerned me.

"And third?"

"Third, you’ll hear from the moneylender—Septimus Luker of Lambeth." He wrote the address in my pocket-book.

With that, he shook my hand again. "I’ve grown fond of you, Mr. Betteredge. If we don’t meet again, visit me in retirement. My garden will have grass walks—and perhaps a white moss rose."

"Ye’ll never grow it without budding it on the dog-rose first!" Begbie shouted through the window.

Cuff laughed, dashed outside, and the two marched off, still debating roses.

I confess—despite myself, I liked the man. Even as I hated him. Contradictions, indeed.

But now, at last, I am done with this day’s events. Soon, I’ll recount the strange occurrences of the following week—then gladly pass the narrative to another. If you’re half as weary of reading as I am of writing, we’ll both rejoice when the time comes.

The pony-chaise stood ready, as I had arranged, in case Mr. Franklin held to his resolve of departing that evening. The sight of his luggage descending the stairs, followed by the man himself, told me plainly that for once, he meant to keep his word.

“So you’re truly set on going, sir?” I ventured as we met in the hall. “Mightn’t you wait another day? Give Miss Rachel one more chance.”

The polished ease that usually cloaked Mr. Franklin had worn thin. Instead of answering, he pressed into my hand the letter her ladyship had sent him. Much of it echoed what I’d already read in my own—but one passage stood out, sharp as a knife.

*You will wonder,* my lady wrote, *at my allowing Rachel to keep me in the dark. A diamond worth twenty thousand pounds vanishes, and my own daughter treats its loss as no mystery—yet claims some unbreakable silence binds her, imposed by persons unknown, for reasons beyond my grasp. Should I tolerate such defiance? Under any other circumstances, no. But Rachel is in a state of nervous distress so profound it would wound you to see her. I dare not press her further. I have even dismissed the police officer; his presence torments her. This is not a riddle for outsiders to solve.

My course is clear. I shall take Rachel to London—for change, and for the counsel of physicians. Join us there, Franklin. You, like me, must exercise patience. Your efforts to recover the Moonstone, though well-intended, have only deepened her distress. She blames you for threatening secrets she refuses to name. I cannot defend such unreason—only plead for time.*

I returned the letter, my heart heavy for him. He loved Miss Rachel; that was plain enough. “You know what they say, sir,” I offered weakly. “When things are at their worst, they mend.”

Mr. Franklin gave no sign he took comfort from the proverb.

“When I first arrived with that cursed diamond,” he said, voice low, “this house was the happiest in England. Now look at it. Broken. Poisoned with suspicion. Do you remember our talk by the Shivering Sand, Betteredge? My uncle Herncastle’s revenge has worked through means he never dreamed of.”

With that, he clasped my hand and strode to the chaise.

I followed, wretched at the sight of him leaving so. Penelope—her spirits shaken by all that had passed—came weeping to bid him farewell. Mr. Franklin kissed her cheek. I raised a hand in silent blessing. Other maids peered around corners, drawn by the magnetism he had for women. At the last moment, I caught the chaise’s side.

“Where shall we write to you, sir?”

He barely seemed to hear, his gaze roaming over the house and grounds as though imprinting them on his soul. Then, abruptly, he pulled his hat low.

“Where am I going?” He laughed, a sound without joy. “To the devil, Betteredge.”

The pony startled as if the word scorched its ears. “God keep you, sir, wherever you go!” I called as the chaise rolled away. A gentleman to the last, for all his faults. The house felt hollow without him.

The evening stretched long and bleak. I clung to my pipe and *Robinson Crusoe* for solace. The maids (Penelope excepted) filled the hours with talk of Rosanna’s suicide, convinced she had stolen the Moonstone and killed herself in terror of discovery. My daughter held firm to her own theory—though it, like Miss Rachel’s claims of innocence, faltered at the mystery of Rosanna’s trip to Frizinghall and the nightgown. Arguing was useless; Penelope’s mind, like mine, was a fortress against reason.

Sunday brought the carriage back from Mr. Ablewhite’s, empty. A message informed me my lady would take Miss Rachel to London the next day. The maids were to follow with the necessary wardrobe. Most of the staff would go; I was to remain, overseeing the estate.

The stillness deepened my unease about Mr. Franklin. By Sunday’s post, I wrote to his father’s valet, Jeffco, begging news of his plans.

Monday jolted me alert. The first of Sergeant Cuff’s prophecies came true: I heard from the Yollands.

After seeing Penelope and my lady’s maid off with the luggage, I was wandering the grounds when a voice hailed me. I turned to face Limping Lucy, the fisherman’s daughter. Lean, fierce-eyed, and leaning on her crutch, she fixed me with a glare.

“Where’s Franklin Blake?” she demanded.

“You’ll speak of him as *Mr.* Blake, if you please,” I reproved.

She lurched closer, her face alight with fury. “*Murderer* Blake would fit better.”

Years of marriage had taught me how to handle a woman’s temper. I smiled. “Pooh.”

It worked. She stamped her crutch, near shaking with rage. “He killed Rosanna Spearman!”

Nearby workers glanced over, recognized Lucy, and wisely looked away.

“Why do you say that?” I asked gently.

Her wrath dissolved into grief. “She loved him,” Lucy whispered, head bowed. “A life of misery, yet her heart stayed sweet. We were to go to London—live as sisters. Then *he* came. ‘I can’t live without him,’ she told me. ‘And he never even looks at me.’” Lucy’s voice broke. “I had savings, plans… Then her letter came. She was done with life. Where is he?” She lifted her face, tears blazing. “When the poor rise against the rich, I pray they start with *him*.”

I steered her back. “What do you want with Mr. Franklin?”

“To give him a letter.”

“From Rosanna?”

“Yes.”

Here, at last, was a thread to follow. “He’s in London,” I said.

She studied me, saw the truth, and turned without a word.

“Wait! I’ll send it to him.”

Lucy glanced back. “It goes from my hand to his—no other way.”

“Shall I tell him you hate him?”

“Tell him the truth.”

She limped away, leaving me fevered with questions. Later, I learned no more from her parents. My last hope was a letter to Mr. Franklin.

Tuesday’s post brought two letters. Penelope wrote that my lady and Miss Rachel were settled in London, with rounds of amusements planned to lift Rachel’s spirits. Mr. Godfrey had called, still smitten, and been received kindly. My mistress, however, seemed downcast, consulting her lawyer at length.

The second letter, from Jeffco, struck like lightning. Mr. Franklin had left England. Arriving at his father’s house, he’d been dismissed—the elder Blake was immersed in parliamentary business. “Grieved for Rachel,” said he, “but speak to me at the session’s end.” Mr. Franklin’s response was terse: “Jeffco, when does the first train leave?” By dawn, he was gone—destination unknown.

Lucy’s letter would never reach him now. Whether it held a confession or mere despair, we’d never know. Rosanna’s secret sank with her into the Quicksand.

The week rolled on. Thursday brought more gossip from Penelope: balls, operas, and Mr. Godfrey’s unshaken devotion. My lady remained troubled.

Then, on Saturday, a London paper arrived—directed in Sergeant Cuff’s hand. A marked report detailed Indians harassing a Mr. Luker, a dealer in gems. The same Indians, perhaps, who had haunted us? The Sergeant’s third prophecy had come to pass.

So ends this chapter of the Moonstone’s shadow—with mysteries unresolved, and fate still weaving its net.

I owe my dear parents (both now departed) the lifelong habit of order and discipline instilled in me from childhood.

In those simpler days, I was taught to keep my hair neat at all hours, and to fold each article of clothing with care, placing them in precise order upon the same chair at the foot of my bed before retiring. Each evening concluded with an entry in my diary, followed by the recitation of the “Evening Hymn” before sleep claimed me—a ritual as dependable as the sunrise.

Time, alas, has eroded such comforts. The hymn has been replaced by restless thoughts, and the sweet slumber of youth by troubled nights. Yet two habits endure: the folding of my garments, a tender link to happier days, and the keeping of my diary—a practice that has proven unexpectedly useful. It has even secured me the patronage of Mr. Franklin Blake, a wealthy relation by marriage, who now calls upon my records for his own purposes.

Exiled by circumstance to a quiet town in Brittany, I live frugally among a circle of English friends, spared the excesses of popery by the presence of a Protestant clergyman and an affordable market. Here, isolated and overlooked, I received a letter from Mr. Blake—not out of familial kindness, but because he requires my account of certain events at Aunt Verinder’s London home. He offers payment, as though coin could soothe the wounds of revisiting such painful memories. Yet humility prevails; I accept his terms.

Without my diary, I could not have complied. But its meticulous entries ensure accuracy. Let it be known: I shall recount the truth, unvarnished by deference. Mr. Blake may edit as he pleases, but my pen will not falter.

---

On the 3rd of July, 1848, I happened upon Aunt Verinder’s residence in Montagu Square. Noting signs of occupancy, I knocked—only to be met by a insolent servant, one of the housemaids, whose brazen demeanor demanded correction. I offered her a tract (“A Word With You On Your Cap-Ribbons”), which she dismissed with shocking impertinence. Undeterred, I left it in the letterbox and another by the railings—small seeds of righteousness sown in stony ground.

I was invited to luncheon the following day. Aunt Verinder greeted me warmly, though tension lingered beneath her courtesy. Rachel, her daughter, was another matter—vulgar in her exuberance, her laughter too loud, her manners unrefined. My aunt’s anxious glances betrayed her concern.

After the meal, Rachel retired, declaring she would await news of Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, whose recent misadventure in Northumberland Street had gripped her interest. Left alone, my aunt confided the dreadful tale of the Moonstone’s disappearance—a story too widely known to bear repeating here.

---

The events in question began on the 30th of June. Mr. Godfrey, ever the philanthropist, had been lured to a house under false pretenses, assaulted, and robbed—though nothing of value was taken. The same fate befell Mr. Luker, a dealer in antiquities, who lost only a receipt for a certain valuable. The perpetrators—foreigners, by all accounts—vanished without a trace.

The police concluded it a botched robbery, the thieves misinformed. Yet the precision of their methods suggested darker designs. Mr. Godfrey, ever resilient, bore the ordeal with characteristic grace.

As for Rachel, her fascination with the affair troubled me deeply. But that is a matter for later reflection. For now, let it suffice that the shadow of the Moonstone looms over us all—and I, dutiful chronicler, must trace its reach.

Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite entered with the precision that marked all his actions—neither too swiftly to startle nor too tardily to leave us waiting. A model of Christian virtue, he moved through life with an effortless grace that spoke of perfect harmony.

“Inform Miss Verinder that Mr. Ablewhite has arrived,” my aunt instructed the servant.

We greeted him warmly, inquiring after his health, still shaken from his ordeal the week prior. With characteristic tact, he answered us simultaneously—my aunt received his words, while I was blessed with his radiant smile.

“What have I done to deserve such kindness?” he said, his voice rich with warmth. “A mere case of mistaken identity—blindfolded, throttled, and deposited upon a rather unforgiving floor. It might have been far worse! I could have been robbed. Murdered. Yet I emerge with nothing lost but a little Nervous Force—hardly a matter for the courts.” He sighed. “I would have preferred to keep the affair private, but Mr. Luker’s public outcry dragged me into the spotlight. Now the newspapers feast upon it, and I, like the gentle reader, grow weary of the tale.”

His gaze softened further. “And dear Rachel—does she still delight in London’s diversions? I must beg your forgiveness, Miss Clack, for my neglect of our charitable endeavors. But I fully intend to attend the Mothers’-Small-Clothes meeting next week. How fares the Committee? Are we sufficiently stocked in Trousers?”

His smile could have disarmed the sternest critic. Indeed, we were nearly *overwhelmed* by Trousers, but before I could say so, the door burst open, and in swept Miss Verinder—hair disheveled, cheeks flushed, her manner more befitting a boy in a tavern than a lady of refinement.

“Godfrey!” she exclaimed, seizing his hands. “You should have brought Mr. Luker. The two of you are the most intriguing men in London at present—though I know Miss Clack despairs of my morbid curiosity. No matter. Tell me everything the newspapers omitted about Northumberland Street.”

Even a man of Mr. Godfrey’s virtue is not immune to the temptations of our fallen nature. I watched, dismayed, as he clasped Rachel’s hand and rested it against his waistcoat—an unseemly encouragement to her forwardness.

“The papers have said all there is to say, and more elegantly than I could,” he demurred.

“He finds the subject tedious,” my aunt remarked.

Rachel’s eyes flashed. “Why?”

“My dear,” I interjected, “true courage is ever modest.”

She ignored me entirely. “You’re too accustomed to the flattery of women, Godfrey. It’s made you dishonest. Sit. I have questions, and I will have answers.”

With shocking impropriety, she dragged him to the window, where the light fell stark upon his face. My pen trembles to recount such behavior, but truth compels me.

“Have the police made progress?” she demanded.

“None.”

“And the men who attacked you—they’re the same who targeted Mr. Luker?”

“Almost certainly.”

“And no trace of them?”

“None.”

“Some believe they’re the Indians who came to our house in Yorkshire.”

“Some do.”

“Do *you*?”

“Rachel, I was blindfolded. I know nothing.”

Still, she pressed—until at last, she struck upon the heart of the matter.

“These rumors linking Mr. Luker’s gem to the Moonstone—what do they say of *you*?”

Mr. Godfrey paled. “Leave it be.”

“Tell me!”

“Scandal claims,” he said at last, his voice strained, “that the Moonstone was pawned to Mr. Luker… and that I am the man who did it.”

Rachel recoiled as if struck. “This is *my* fault!” she cried, wild-eyed. “I will not let an innocent man be ruined for my silence!”

She raged, she wept, she demanded a written declaration of his innocence—which he, ever indulgent, provided. She signed it feverishly.

“Show it to the world,” she said. “I misjudged you, Godfrey. You are better than I thought.”

Yet when the knock came—the summons to the flower-show—she turned brittle once more, donning gaiety like a mask. “Don’t pity me, Miss Clack,” she hissed as she left. “See how happy I am?”

The moment she was gone, Mr. Godfrey burned the declaration with a match. “Let her believe I accepted her sacrifice,” he said gently. “But I will not have her honor stained for my sake.”

I was moved beyond words.

Yet the afternoon’s trials were not over. My aunt, her face ashen, confided in me at last—her health was failing. She had a Will to sign.

And I was to bear witness.

I could not bring myself to distress Lady Verinder by revealing that I had already guessed the painful truth before she spoke. I waited in respectful silence, resolved to offer comfort when the moment permitted. Whatever duty lay before me—no matter how difficult—I was prepared to fulfill it.

“I have been gravely ill, Drusilla,” my aunt began, “and, strange to say, I did not realize it myself.”

My thoughts flew at once to the multitudes of souls who wandered lost in spiritual sickness, unaware of their peril. I feared my dear aunt might be among them.

“Yes, dear,” I murmured sorrowfully. “Yes.”

“I brought Rachel to London, as you know, for medical consultation,” she continued. “I thought it prudent to seek the opinion of two physicians.”

Two physicians! And yet (with Rachel’s soul in such peril!) not a single man of God among them!

“Yes, dear?” I pressed gently.

“One of the doctors was unknown to me,” she explained. “The other had been a dear friend of my late husband’s and had always shown me kindness for his sake. After examining Rachel, he asked to speak with me privately. I expected instructions for her care—but instead, he took my hand gravely and said, ‘Lady Verinder, I have observed you with both professional and personal concern. I fear you are in far greater need of medical attention than your daughter.’”

He questioned her closely, and though she had at first dismissed his concerns, she soon saw his distress deepen. The next day, accompanied by another physician, he returned when Rachel was absent. Their verdict, delivered with the utmost gentleness, was irrevocable: precious time had been lost. An insidious heart disease had weakened her over years without warning, and now her condition was beyond remedy. She might live months—or hours.

“Of course, my dear,” she confessed, “I have known despair since hearing this. But I am resigned now, and I labor to set my affairs in order. My greatest fear is that Rachel should learn the truth. If she did, she would blame herself for my decline—believing it caused by the Diamond’s loss—when the physicians agree my illness began long before. You will keep my secret, Drusilla? I see true sorrow for me in your face.”

Sorrow? Sympathy? Oh, how little she understood the divine fire that burned within me! As she spoke, my heart swelled with sacred gratitude. Here was a soul teetering on eternity’s brink, utterly unprepared—and Providence had guided her to *me*! The harvest was ripe, and laborers were plentiful. With holy fervor, I embraced her.

“Oh, the boundless concern I feel for you!” I cried. “Oh, the good I shall do you before we part!”

I offered her the choice of three devoted clergymen, each a beacon of mercy in her neighborhood, each eager to minister at my word. Alas, she recoiled, pleading weakness. I yielded—temporarily. Experience had taught me that books must prepare the soil. My library was well stocked for such emergencies.

“You will read, dear, won’t you?” I coaxed. “My own precious books, marked where you must pause and ask, ‘Does this apply to me?’”

Even this gentle plea seemed to startle her. “I will try, Drusilla,” she said faintly.

Time was short. The clock warned me to hasten home, select a dozen volumes, and return before the lawyer arrived for the will’s signing. Normally frugal, I indulged in a cab for my aunt’s sake. The driver, when paid, cursed—so I gifted him a tract. He fled as if I’d brandished a pistol. No matter; a second tract flew through his window as he sped away.

At the house, the footman informed me the doctor had just left, and Mr. Bruff waited in the library. The solicitor arched a brow at my carpet-bag.

“Here to stay, Miss Clack?”

I would not dignify his skepticism with the truth. “My aunt has asked me to witness her will.”

“Ah. Well, you’ll do. You’ve no stake in it.”

No stake! What blessed relief. Had she left me even a pittance, my motives might be questioned. Now, none could doubt my pure intent.

Mr. Bruff, restless under my silence, soon broached the scandal surrounding Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

“A vile falsehood!” I declared.

He smirked. “Appearances damn him. He was present when the Diamond vanished—and first to London after.”

I let him prattle on, relishing the moment before striking with Rachel’s testimony of Godfrey’s innocence. The lawyer staggered as if struck.

“If Rachel vouches for him,” he conceded, “I believe it.”

Yet his mind still writhed. “Then who *did* take the Moonstone?” he muttered.

Seizing my chance, I whispered, “Mr. Franklin Blake was also present that night.”

Mr. Bruff’s smile turned icy. “You overreach, Miss Clack. Why would Blake steal it? His debts are stalled, and Rachel would have married him.”

“The human heart is inscrutable,” I murmured.

He scoffed. “Then why did he lead the search? No, the case is a deadlock. Rachel is innocent, Ablewhite is innocent, Blake is innocent—yet the Diamond is in London, in Luker’s hands!”

Before I could mention Sergeant Cuff’s unshaken suspicions, we were summoned to Lady Verinder.

Thus ends my account—a necessary revelation of suspicion’s path, though it costs me the humiliation of admitting my own petty triumph over Mr. Bruff. Yet in that confession, I reclaim the higher ground. The balance is restored. Onward, dear friends.

The signing of the Will was concluded with unsettling haste. Samuel, the footman, was summoned as a witness, and before I could gather my thoughts, the pen was thrust into my aunt’s trembling hand. The solemnity of the moment demanded reflection, but Mr. Bruff’s impatient demeanor stifled any words I might have offered. In mere minutes, it was done—Samuel dismissed, the document sealed, and my aunt left paler than before.

Mr. Bruff studied me with the weary skepticism of a man accustomed to dismissing zealots. I, however, was immovable. My satchel of devotional tracts weighed heavy in my lap, a silent testament to my resolve. Recognizing futility, he muttered something unbecoming and withdrew, leaving me alone with my aunt.

She reclined upon the sofa, her voice faint with exhaustion. “You mustn’t think yourself slighted, Drusilla. Your legacy shall be given with my own hand.”

Here was my opportunity! With practiced ease, I retrieved the first of my treasured volumes—*The Serpent at Home*—a work of singular moral clarity. Its chapters, such as *Satan in the Hairbrush* and *Satan Behind the Looking-Glass*, laid bare the devil’s cunning in domestic trivialities.

“Dear aunt,” I urged, “this book will provide all the comfort you require.” I pressed it into her hands, opened to a particularly stirring passage: *Satan Among the Sofa Cushions*.

She glanced at it, then away, her discomfort plain. “Drusilla, I fear I must wait until I am stronger. The doctor insists—”

Ah, the doctor! That agent of secular oppression! How often had his kind interposed between a soul and salvation under the guise of *rest* and *quiet*? Yet I would not be deterred.

“Of course,” I conceded with false meekness. “But perhaps tomorrow, when your mind is clearer?” I slipped the book discreetly between the sofa cushions, where her fingers might later brush against its divine wisdom.

A sudden inspiration struck me as I noted the flowers lining her window—her one worldly indulgence. “Might I take a bloom?” I inquired, drifting toward them. Instead, I left another volume nestled among the geraniums.

Thus began my campaign. In the library, I replaced frivolous novels with uplifting tracts. In the breakfast room, I tucked a pamphlet beside the canary’s seed. The drawing-room piano received two more beneath its sheet music, and a third hid within her embroidery frame. Behind a curtain in the back parlor, I wedged another into her fan.

Emboldened, I ascended to her bedchamber. There, upon her nightstand, I placed a volume beside the matches—should she wake in darkness, she would find illumination of a higher kind. Another nestled near her chocolate drops. Only one book remained in my satchel, and only one room unexplored: the bath. A dressing gown hung nearby, its pocket gaping. With reverent hands, I deposited my final offering.

My mission complete, I slipped away, my heart light as a child’s. That night, I slept the untroubled sleep of the righteous.

The next morning, Samuel arrived at my lodgings with a parcel—my aunt’s reply. His manner was skittish, his face flushed. “Her ladyship’s regards,” he stammered, depositing the bundle as though it burned him.

Inside lay all twelve of my books, returned. A note accompanied them—my aunt dared not defy her physician.

Undaunted, I devised a new stratagem: if books were forbidden, letters would serve. Selecting the most potent passages, I transcribed them into personal missives. Some I posted; others I would deliver by hand.

By afternoon, I stood once more at her doorstep. Samuel informed me she rested in the drawing room, still unwell. The house was quiet—Rachel and her frivolous companions, including the lamentably wayward Mr. Ablewhite, were likely at some concert.

I set to work. In the library, I left one letter prominently upon the mantel. Another I dropped conspicuously in the breakfast room. As I moved toward the stairs, a knock echoed through the hall—soft, yet deliberate.

Samuel’s voice carried up to me. “Upstairs, if you please, sir.”

Footsteps followed. A man’s. The doctor? No—the cadence was too restless. A murmur reached me, a voice I almost recognized. Then, clear as a struck bell:

I knew that voice. It belonged to Godfrey Ablewhite.

My fingers slipped from the curtain, but not—oh, not—from any trivial embarrassment. No, my thoughts burned with a fiercer flame: the ruin of a man I had once admired. Mr. Godfrey, absent from the concert, stood now in this room, his voice thick with resolve. *He would do it today.* The words coiled in my ears like a serpent. What fresh degradation awaited him? Would he forsake his faith? Abandon the Mothers’-Small-Clothes, the committees, the adoring audiences who hung upon his every syllable? The horror of it seized me—I nearly flung aside the drapes to plead with him in the name of every charitable society in London—when another voice cut through the stillness.

Rachel Verinder’s. Harsh, unadorned, devoid of womanly grace.

“Why are you here, Godfrey? The library was empty.”

A soft laugh. “Miss Clack occupies the library.”

“Clack?” A rustle of skirts as she settled onto the ottoman. “Then you’re right. We stay here.”

A moment ago, I had been fevered with indecision. Now, ice filled my veins. To reveal myself was impossible. Retreat? Only into the fireplace. Martyrdom, then. With grim resolve, I adjusted the curtain just so—enough to see, to hear—and braced myself like the earliest Christians facing the lions.

“Not there,” Rachel commanded. “Sit opposite me. I prefer it.”

He obeyed, folding his too-tall frame into a low chair that did him no favors. I had never seen his legs look so absurd.

“Well?” she pressed. “What did you tell them?”

“Precisely what you told me. That your mother was unwell, and you hesitated to leave her.”

“And they believed it?”

“Entirely. They sent their love, their hopes for her swift recovery.”

“*You* don’t think it’s serious, do you?”

“Not at all. A matter of days.”

“I thought the same. Still, it was kind of you to make my excuses to near-strangers. But why miss the concert? You needn’t have suffered on my account.”

“Rachel!” His hands clasped, his face alight with that same Exeter Hall fervor—only now it turned my stomach. “If you knew how much happier I am here, with you.”

“Spare me the flattery, Godfrey.”

“Flattery? Never. Hopeless love speaks only truth.” He leaned closer, took her hand. A pause. Even she, it seemed, was not immune to his practiced tremolo.

Then—his knees struck the carpet. Both of them. Arms encircled her. Two words, delivered with the force of a revivalist’s climax:

“Noble creature!”

She did not pull away.

I, meanwhile, wrestled with propriety. Ought I to shut my eyes? Cover my ears? In the end, I did neither, clinging to the curtain as if it alone could steady me.

“A woman who speaks truth,” he murmured, “who sacrifices pride for honesty—what greater treasure exists? Rachel, let me heal you. Marry me.”

Her reply, at last, was rational: “You’re mad.”

“I speak for your future. Must you wither for a man who does not know your heart? Forget him. Let *me* offer you respect, a home, devotion.”

She wavered. Oh, the poor, misguided girl!

“Don’t tempt me,” she whispered.

“One question: Do you object to me?”

“I? I’ve always liked you. Now I admire you.”

“Many wives admire without loving. Yet their marriages thrive. Yours could too.”

A breath. Then—

“Take me.”

Two words, and she was his.

What followed, I shall not detail. Some scenes defy narration. Suffice it to say, I was one merciful instant too late in shutting my eyes.

They parted only when a footman’s panicked cry shattered the moment: “Miss Rachel! My lady has fainted—we cannot rouse her!”

Chaos. Godfrey bolted for the doctor. Rachel cradled her mother’s lifeless head. And I? I saw the truth in Lady Verinder’s still face before the doctor confirmed it.

Later, I noted with bitter amusement that she had died without opening a single one of my letters. The legacy, too, went unmentioned. A fitting end to the day’s betrayals.

May these extracts (Miss Clack earnestly prays) resound as a clarion call to her esteemed kinsman, Mr. Franklin Blake."*

The events following Lady Verinder’s death left little room for sentiment. A month had passed before I saw Rachel again—this time under the same roof, where an unexpected turn in her engagement to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite demanded recording. With this final disclosure, my account of these painful affairs will be complete.

Lady Verinder was laid to rest in the quiet churchyard on her estate. Though invited, I could not bring myself to attend—not only due to the shock of her passing but also because the rector of Frizinghall, a man I had once seen playing cards at her whist table, was to officiate. Some lines, even in grief, cannot be crossed.

Rachel, now under the guardianship of Mr. Ablewhite the elder, proved difficult to settle. The London house was tainted by her mother’s death, Yorkshire by the scandal of the Moonstone, and Frizinghall was deemed too lively for mourning. Brighton, then, was chosen—a furnished house, where Rachel, her invalid cousin, and Aunt Ablewhite would reside, with Mr. Godfrey shuttling between them and London.

I was summoned to assist in hiring servants—a task I undertook with solemn diligence. My aunt, a woman of remarkable passivity, greeted me with her usual helplessness. “Drusilla, dear, I want some servants. You are so clever—please get them for me.” Resistance was futile.

In the adjoining room, Rachel stood by the window, frail in her mourning. To my astonishment, she approached me with an apology. “I have spoken foolishly to you before,” she said. “I hope you can forgive me.” Her reasoning—that she now sought comfort among her mother’s friends—was misguided, but it revealed an opening. If sentiment had stirred her conscience, conviction might yet take root.

Testing the waters, I probed delicately about her engagement. She evaded the subject, but her manner had softened from the defiance of our last meeting. Here was fertile ground for guidance.

Brighton was secured. I prepared meticulously—selecting servants of sound character, securing church sittings, and placing edifying literature in Rachel’s path. Doctrine would surround her at every turn.

Yet when the party arrived, Mr. Godfrey was absent, replaced by the lawyer Mr. Bruff. His presence unsettled me. That evening, after Rachel retired early with a headache, I sensed his influence. The next day, she walked out with him—returning pensive, almost transformed. Bruff departed with a meaningful glance, leaving Rachel locked in her room, refusing dinner.

The following morning, she confessed over tea: “I shall never marry Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.” The words struck like a thunderclap. Before I could press further, she dismissed me.

At breakfast, she was silent, restless. She played scandalous music—worldly distractions I allowed for the moment. When I returned from visiting friends, I found Godfrey himself in the dining room, unnervingly composed.

“Rachel has ended our engagement,” he said, as calmly as if discussing the weather. “She believes it best for us both.”

I stared. “What does it mean?”

He smiled—that same effortless charm. “Let me explain.”

And with that, the trap was set—though whether for Rachel, for Godfrey, or for myself, I could not yet say.

Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite sat across from me, his noble features shadowed by an expression of bewildered resignation.

*"I have lost a beautiful woman, a distinguished position, and a considerable fortune,"* he began, *"and yet I submit to it without protest. Can you explain such behavior, my dear Miss Clack? I confess—I cannot."*

*"No motive?"* I echoed, intrigued despite myself.

*"Consider the innocence of a child,"* he continued, spreading his hands in a gesture of helplessness. *"When a child acts, it does so without reason—like the grass growing or the birds singing. I am much the same. Why did I propose to Miss Verinder? Why have I neglected my charitable endeavors? Why have I strayed from the righteous path of the Mothers’ Small-Clothes? I cannot say. Ask a child why it has misbehaved, and it will only stare in mute confusion. So too with me, Miss Clack. I confess it freely—to you alone."*

Here was a puzzle indeed. As one deeply versed in matters of the soul, I felt the stirrings of divine insight. It is not uncommon for those of great virtue to be humbled—perhaps as a reminder of their mortal frailty. Was this not such a moment? The revulsion Mr. Godfrey now felt for his ill-considered engagement, his eagerness to return to his charitable work—these were signs of a spirit redeemed.

I laid a gentle hand upon his. *"Dear friend, your distress is but the awakening of your better nature. You recoil from this union because your soul knows it to be wrong. Rejoice! The path of righteousness lies open before you once more."*

His relief was palpable. He clasped my hands fervently, his eyes alight with gratitude. *"You have lifted a darkness from me!"* he declared.

For a fleeting moment, I felt the warmth of spiritual communion—so intense that I nearly succumbed to a swoon. But fate, in the form of a clattering luncheon service, intervened.

Mr. Godfrey sprang to his feet. *"Time has slipped away! I must catch the next train."*

When I pressed him for haste, his face darkened. *"My father is coming,"* he admitted. *"He will not take this broken engagement lightly. I must intercept him before he arrives."*

With that, he departed, leaving me to reflect on the fickleness of worldly opinion. Let slanderers say what they will—that Mr. Godfrey had selfish reasons for releasing Rachel, or that he sought my favor for mercenary ends. I knew the truth.

---

At luncheon, I studied Rachel closely. Had her newfound freedom eased her spirit? No—if anything, she seemed more agitated, as if tormented by thoughts she dared not voice. *Who was the man who held her heart?* I wondered. Conversion would loosen her tongue in time.

That evening, she resisted all attempts at moral guidance, turning instead to the piano with defiant energy. Undaunted, I waited, the works of Miss Jane Ann Stamper at the ready.

---

The next day, as anticipated, Mr. Ablewhite arrived—followed, to my dismay, by Mr. Bruff. The lawyer’s presence was an ill omen.

*"Rachel,"* Mr. Ablewhite began with forced amiability, *"I have heard disturbing news. Might we speak privately?"*

*"Whatever you have to say, may be said here,"* Rachel replied, cool and unyielding.

His genial mask slipped. *"Am I to understand that you have jilted my son?"*

The pink flush creeping up his bald crown betrayed his rising fury. *"You insult my family!"* he snarled. *"Just as your kin once scorned me for marrying beneath them. Now my son is not good enough for you? I see the Herncastle pride in you, girl!"*

Rachel’s composure was glacial. *"If that is your belief, I shall not waste words correcting it."*

At this, Mr. Ablewhite erupted. *"Then let us be clear! If my son is unfit to be your husband, I am unfit to be your guardian. This house is in my name. You may remove yourself from it—at your earliest convenience."*

With that, he stormed out, leaving silence in his wake.

Aunt Ablewhite, roused from her usual torpor, managed to cross the room in a flurry of agitation.

Thus ended Mr. Ablewhite’s revenge—a petty strike at Rachel’s pride, and a test of her resolve. The battle lines were drawn. The storm, I sensed, was only beginning.

When Miss Clack set down her pen, it falls to me, Mr. Bruff, to take up the tale. My part in these events is not without significance, for there are shadows I alone can illuminate—secrets which, until now, have remained hidden.

First, consider the matter of Miss Rachel Verinder’s broken engagement. The truth of it lies in my hands. And as for Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite’s sudden withdrawal of his suit? That, too, is a riddle I have unraveled.

Second, fate—whether kind or cruel, I cannot say—entangled me in the affair of the Moonstone. I stood face-to-face in my office with an Oriental gentleman of imposing demeanor, none other than the chief of those three Indians who have haunted this business. The following day, I crossed paths with Mr. Murthwaite, the renowned traveler, and our discourse upon the Diamond cast a long shadow over all that followed. These are my credentials.

But let us begin where the story does: at the deathbed of my late friend and client, Sir John Verinder.

Sir John, for all his virtues, possessed a most human reluctance to confront his own mortality—at least, while in good health. The making of his will was a duty he deferred until illness finally compelled him. Lady Verinder and I pressed upon him the necessity; he conceded the point, yet took no action until his final hours. Then, at last, I was summoned to his bedside.

I found him drowsing, but he roused himself when I entered.

"Ah, Mr. Bruff," said he. "Let us be brief. Then I may sleep again."

Pen, ink, and paper in hand, I waited.

"I leave everything to my wife," he declared. "That is all." And with that, he turned away as if to dismiss the matter entirely.

I was forced to disturb him.

"To be clear," I asked, "you bequeath the entirety of your estate—land, investments, all of it—unconditionally to Lady Verinder?"

"Yes," said Sir John, impatient. "Must you make it so tedious? Everything to my wife. There is my Will."

Had this been any other client, I might have urged reconsideration. But Lady Verinder was no ordinary woman. She was not only trustworthy—as all good wives ought to be—but possessed the rare competence to manage such a trust. The will was drawn, executed, and Sir John returned to his rest.

In widowhood, Lady Verinder proved herself worthy of his faith. She consulted me at once regarding her own will, displaying such sound judgment that my role was merely to frame her wishes in legal terms. Her daughter’s future was secured with wisdom and affection.

Years passed before that will was disturbed. Then, in the summer of '48, came grim news: Lady Verinder was under a death sentence. She wished to review her will once more.

The provisions for Rachel remained untouched, but minor legacies required adjustment. To avoid confusion, I drafted a second will—identical in substance to the first, save for the appointment of a guardian under my advisement. Miss Clack, ever officious, witnessed its execution.

Three weeks later, an anomaly arose. My proctor informed me, with some intrigue, that Lady Verinder’s will had already been examined at Doctors’ Commons—by none other than Mr. Smalley, of Skipp and Smalley.

This was peculiar indeed. The will was uncontestable. Who, then, had an interest in scrutinizing it?

A firm such as Skipp and Smalley held little power over me. My clerk was Smalley’s brother, and their firm had long subsisted on the crumbs of my practice. A pointed inquiry soon revealed the truth: the client behind this curiosity was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

That name told me all I needed to know.

Now, let me lay bare the contents of Lady Verinder’s will as plainly as I knew them myself. Rachel’s inheritance was a life interest only—no more. The principal remained beyond her reach, or that of any husband. She would have homes, an income, but no power to raise funds against the estate.

This discovery left me in a quandary. Rachel, whom I held in the highest esteem, was on the brink of marriage to a man I had always suspected of mercenary motives. Now, the truth was clear: Godfrey sought her for her money. But if that money was locked away, would he still want her?

I resolved to see Rachel at once.

At Brighton, I found her listless, resigned.

"You may speak of my engagement," she said, when I broached the subject, "if you have nothing better to discuss."

"Tell me, Miss Rachel," I ventured, "is your heart truly in this match?"

"I marry out of despair," she replied. "A stagnant happiness is all I hope for."

Such words hinted at deeper sorrows, but I pressed on.

"And Mr. Ablewhite? Is his heart as indifferent as yours?"

"He claims it is not. I suppose I must believe him."

The cruelty of my task struck me then. How could I tell her that the man she meant to marry cared only for her fortune? Yet tell her I must.

When I did, she walked in silence, her grip on my arm tightening, her face pale. At last, she spoke.

"Contradict any rumors of my marriage," she said. "It will not proceed."

"But have you considered the difficulty of withdrawing?" I asked. "Is there no one you might consult?"

"No one," she said.

The loneliness in her voice moved me deeply. I advised her to confront Godfrey with the truth, to force his hand. But Rachel, proud even in humiliation, refused.

"I believed in him," she said. "To call him a deceiver to his face would be to degrade myself. I will not do it."

Her resolve was unshakable. She would end the engagement, but without accusation.

Before I could receive word from her, old Mr. Ablewhite came to me in a fury, demanding explanation. His son, he said, had been dismissed—and had accepted it without protest.

The reason was plain: Godfrey needed money, and quickly. Rachel’s inheritance could not serve his purpose. And so, he let her go.

As for Mr. Ablewhite senior, his temper foretold ill for Rachel. But that, as they say, is another chapter.

The next matter I must relate concerns the Moonstone—or, more precisely, the continued machinations of those who sought to steal it. Though the details I possess are few, they are of considerable significance, for they cast a shadow over events yet to unfold.

Some days after Miss Verinder’s departure, my clerk interrupted my work with a visitor’s card. The name upon it was foreign—now lost to memory—but beneath it, in clear English, was written:

The audacity of a man like Luker daring to recommend anyone to *me* was so staggering that, for a moment, I could only stare in silence. My clerk, noting my bewilderment, offered his own observations on the stranger below.

"An unusual sort of gentleman, sir. So dark he might be taken for an Indian."

The mention of an Indian, coupled with Luker’s name, immediately stirred suspicion. Could this be connected to the Moonstone? Setting aside professional caution, I instructed my clerk to admit him at once.

Let it be understood—no man in England has been more entangled in the Diamond’s history than I. I knew Herncastle’s fear of assassination, received his letters confirming his survival, drafted his will bequeathing the cursed stone, persuaded his executor to act, and even overcame Franklin Blake’s reluctance to deliver it to Lady Verinder. If any man had a right to curiosity about the Moonstone, it was I.

The moment my visitor entered, I knew him. Not by name, but by bearing. Though dressed impeccably as an Englishman, his complexion, his fluid grace, and the quiet precision of his movements betrayed his true origin. One of the three Indians—likely their leader.

He offered a courteous apology for intruding, his English flawless, then produced a small parcel wrapped in cloth of gold. Unfolding it, he revealed a second layer of silk before placing upon my desk an exquisite casket—ebony inlaid with jewels.

"I seek a loan," he said, "and leave this as surety of repayment."

I indicated his card. "You come at Mr. Luker’s suggestion?"

A bow confirmed it.

"And why does Luker not lend the money himself?"

"Mr. Luker informed me he had none to spare."

"So he sent you to me?"

The Indian merely touched the card. "It is written there."

Succinct. Efficient. Had I possessed the Moonstone, I’ve no doubt this man would have slit my throat without hesitation. Yet for all that, he was the model of an ideal client. He valued my time—a courtesy no Englishman had ever shown me.

I declined his request. Strangers and unusual collateral fell outside my professional practice.

To my surprise, he did not press further. No protest, no persuasion. Only another bow before carefully rewrapping his casket. Then, as he turned to leave, he paused.

"Might I ask one final question?"

One question! Most men would have plagued me with fifty.

"Had you lent me the money," he said, "what period would custom allow for repayment?"

"One year is the usual term."

His final bow was the deepest of all. Then, like a shadow, he was gone—swift, silent, unnervingly feline.

Alone again, I turned the encounter over in my mind. His control had been absolute—face, voice, manner all impenetrable. Yet one crack had appeared. Only when I spoke of the repayment period had his gaze locked onto mine. That question, then, had been his true purpose. The casket, the loan—mere formalities.

Before I could unravel the meaning, a letter arrived—from Luker himself. Groveling, he begged an audience, promising explanations. Against my better judgment, I agreed.

The next day, Luker proved as wretched as expected—a sniveling, roundabout contrast to the Indian’s lethal poise. His tale, stripped of whining, amounted to this:

The Indian had come to him first. Though disguised, Luker recognized him instantly as the chief of the three who had once loitered near his home, forcing him to seek a magistrate’s protection. Terror convinced Luker he faced one of the men who had once blindfolded, gagged, and robbed him.

The Indian, playing the stranger, made the same request—a loan, with the casket as surety. Luker, desperate to be rid of him, claimed poverty. When asked whom he might approach instead, Luker, in his panic, named the first solicitor that came to mind: me.

"I was sweating like a drowned rat, sir," he moaned. "Hardly knew what I was saying!"

I dismissed him, but not before one final question: Had the Indian said anything notable upon leaving?

Indeed he had. The same question he had put to me—*how long before repayment?*

What it meant, I could not yet fathom. Luker’s account offered no clarity, and my own thoughts circled uselessly. That evening, as I prepared for a dinner engagement, I little suspected that the path to my dressing-room would also lead me toward revelation.

The most striking figure at the dinner party was, without question, Mr. Murthwaite. Having returned to England after years of perilous travels, he had become something of a celebrity—a man who had stared danger in the face and lived to recount his exploits. Now, he announced his intention to venture once more into uncharted territories, a decision that reignited public fascination. There was something thrilling in the idea that this might be the last time society would see him alive. After all, what were the odds of a man tempting fate twice and surviving?

When the gentlemen retired to the dining-room, I found myself seated beside the illustrious traveler. The conversation, as was customary among Englishmen once freed from the moderating presence of ladies, soon turned to politics—a subject I have always found tedious. Glancing at Mr. Murthwaite, I noticed he seemed equally disinterested, though he concealed it with admirable politeness. It occurred to me then that a different topic might rouse him: the Moonstone.

"If I recall correctly, Mr. Murthwaite," I began, "you were acquainted with the late Lady Verinder and took some interest in the curious affair of the Moonstone?"

The effect was immediate. He turned to me with sudden attention, asking who I was. I explained my professional connection to the Herncastle family and my involvement in the matter of the Colonel’s Diamond. At once, Mr. Murthwaite shifted in his chair, dismissing the political debate around us, and fixed his gaze on me.

"Have you heard anything recently of the Indians?" he asked.

"I have reason to believe," I replied, "that one of them visited my office just yesterday."

This visibly startled him—no small feat. I recounted the events concerning Mr. Luker and myself in detail.

"The Indian was clearly probing for something when he asked about repayment terms," I added. "Why such interest in when a debt might be settled?"

"You truly don’t see his motive, Mr. Bruff?"

"I confess my ignorance."

Mr. Murthwaite studied me with something like amusement. "Let me ask you this: where does the conspiracy to reclaim the Moonstone now stand?"

"I couldn’t say. The Indian plot remains a mystery to me."

"Only because you’ve never examined it closely. Shall we trace it step by step? It may prove vital to Miss Verinder’s interests that you understand this fully. Would you prefer to deduce the Indian’s motive yourself, or shall I spare you the effort?"

I chose the former.

"Good," he said. "First, consider the ages of the three Indians. They appear middle-aged—not yet forty, I’d say. Now, think back to Colonel Herncastle’s return to England. These men must be successors to the original Brahmins who pursued him here. But they’ve done more than inherit the quest—they’ve assumed an entire network established by their predecessors. A modest one, by our standards: access to funds, connections with certain disreputable Englishmen, and sympathizers among their countrymen in London. Not formidable, but worth noting."

He leaned forward. "Now, when did the Indians first get their chance at the Diamond?"

"Upon the Colonel’s death," I said. "They would have known of it, and the Will named Miss Verinder as heir."

"Precisely. They’d obtain a copy, learn the Diamond would be transferred to her, and gather intelligence on the family. The question was whether to strike during its removal from the bank or wait until it reached Yorkshire. The latter was safer—hence their appearance in Frizinghall, disguised as jugglers, biding their time."

I recalled how Franklin Blake had outmaneuvered them, arriving early and securing the Diamond in the bank before they could act. Yet why, then, had they not attempted to take it from Lady Verinder’s house in the intervening weeks?

"Because no Indian takes unnecessary risks," Murthwaite explained. "The Will specified Miss Verinder would possess the Diamond outright on her birthday. Why confront Blake, who had already thwarted them, when they could wait for a young woman who would wear it openly? Their patience was rewarded when they saw it on her person that very night."

He went on to describe how the Diamond’s disappearance that evening had shattered their plans, and their subsequent arrest had left them powerless.

"Now," he said, "when did their second chance arise?"

He produced a letter from his pocketbook—a cryptic message in Hindustani, urging the Indians to London upon their release. They had obeyed at once.

"And what followed?" he asked.

"They harassed Mr. Luker," I said.

"Indeed. His testimony revealed a dismissed foreign worker, likely their accomplice. The Indians had traced the Moonstone to Luker—but his swift actions thwarted them again. He sent the Diamond to his banker before they could strike."

I suddenly understood the Indian’s visit to my office. "They assume it’s pledged and want to know the earliest redemption date—when it can be moved from the bank!"

"Exactly. A year from the pledging date—likely late June ’forty-nine—marks their next opportunity. I advise you to be in London then."

"You expect violence?"

"I’d rather face Central Asian fanatics," he said dryly, "than carry the Moonstone past that bank’s doors. The Indians have lost twice. They won’t accept a third defeat."

With that, our discussion ended. I noted the date: *June, ’forty-nine. Watch for the Indians.*

And now, the narrative passes to another hand.

The spring of 1849 found me adrift in the East, my travel plans altered unexpectedly. No longer stopping in a certain city as originally intended, I dispatched a servant to retrieve my letters and funds from the English consul there, with instructions to rejoin me at a designated time and place. But fate intervened—a delay, through no fault of his own, left my camp waiting at the desert’s edge for a week.

When he finally appeared at my tent, the weight in his voice was unmistakable. *“I’m afraid I bring bad news, sir.”* His finger tapped a letter edged in black, addressed in the precise hand of Mr. Bruff. Suspense is a torment I’ve never endured well. That mourning-bordered envelope was the first I opened.

My father was dead. His fortune—now mine—came shackled with duty, and Bruff’s message was clear: *Return to England at once.* By dawn, I was on my way home.

Betteredge’s account of my departure, though earnest, paints me with too broad a brush. He took my cousin’s jests about my “continental eccentricities” as gospel, weaving a fiction of German sternness and French flair that never existed outside his own imagination. But on one point, he was painfully accurate: Rachel’s rejection had cut deep. I left England resolved to forget her—or to let time and distance dull the ache.

And they did, in their way. The sharpness faded, replaced by the distractions of new places, new faces. Yet the moment my course turned homeward, the old wound throbbed anew. The closer I drew to England, the more her ghost tightened its grip.

Upon landing, Rachel was my first question to Bruff. He relayed the events in my absence—most of them, at least. One detail he withheld: the true reason she and Godfrey Ablewhite had broken their engagement. I didn’t press. It was relief enough to know she had reconsidered marrying him.

But where was she now? Under the care of Mrs. Merridew, a widow and sister of the late Sir John Verinder, residing in Portland Place. Within the hour, I stood on her doorstep.

The servant’s blank stare told me everything. *“Miss Verinder is not at home.”* Not once, but twice. No message. No explanation. Only the cold certainty: Rachel refused to see me.

Mrs. Merridew received me with polite regret but no answers. A letter to Rachel met with a single, icy line: *“Miss Verinder declines any correspondence with Mr. Franklin Blake.”*

Insult sharpened my resolve. Bruff, when I confronted him, could shed no light. Had slander touched her ears? Had she spoken of me at all? Nothing. Then I showed him Lady Verinder’s old letter—the damning lines accusing me of “unpardoned offence” in the Diamond affair.

“Is that hatred still alive?” I demanded.

Bruff’s hesitation was answer enough.

I rang for my bags. *“I’m going to Yorkshire. Tonight.”*

“For what purpose?”

“To finish what I started. If Rachel’s silence is rooted in that cursed jewel, I’ll unearth the truth myself—link by missing link. And where better to begin than with Betteredge?”

By sunset, I stood once more on the terrace of the old house. The gardener pointed me to the backyard, where I found him: the old man in his chair, pipe in mouth, *Robinson Crusoe* in lap, the dogs sprawled at his feet.

A shadow fell across the gate. The dogs growled. He looked up, hand shading his eyes against the fading light.

Mine were too blurred with memory to speak at once.

The moment I saw Betteredge seated in his familiar beehive chair, the well-worn copy of *Robinson Crusoe* resting on his knee, I couldn’t resist.

“Betteredge,” I said, nodding toward the book, “has *Crusoe* predicted my arrival tonight?”

“By the Lord Harry, Mr. Franklin!” he cried, nearly toppling from his chair in surprise. “That’s precisely what he’s done!”

With my help, he steadied himself, his gaze darting between me and the book as if deciding which was the greater marvel. The verdict, it seemed, favored *Crusoe*. Clutching the volume with reverence, he stared at it as though expecting the castaway himself to stride from the pages.

“Here’s the very passage, sir!” he declared, jabbing a finger at the text. “Page one hundred and fifty-six: *‘I stood like one Thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an Apparition.’* If that’s not a prophecy of your sudden appearance, then words have no meaning!” He snapped the book shut and seized my hand, his grip as firm as ever.

I had braced myself for an interrogation, but Betteredge’s instincts as a servant—and a host—prevailed. The sight of a family member, no matter how unexpectedly arrived, demanded hospitality before curiosity.

“Come inside, Mr. Franklin,” he said, swinging the door open with an old-fashioned bow. “We’ll discuss your business in good time. First, let’s see you fed and settled. The place isn’t what it was—shut up, servants gone—but I’ll cook for you myself, and the gardener’s wife can make up a bed. And if there’s a bottle of the Latour left in the cellar, it’s yours!” His voice wavered only slightly as he fought the melancholy of the empty house, determined to welcome me as he would have in brighter days.

I hated to disappoint him. But this was Rachel’s house now, and after what had passed between us in London, I couldn’t—in decency—cross its threshold. Taking his arm, I guided him into the garden. There was no choice but honesty.

His loyalty torn between Rachel and me, he listened with deepening distress. When he finally spoke, it was with the blunt wisdom I’d come to expect.

“Miss Rachel has her faults,” he said, “and pride is one of them. She’s tried to ride roughshod over you, and you’ve borne it. But Lord, sir, haven’t you learned by now? Take my late wife—”

He launched into the familiar tale of marital strife, concluding triumphantly: “After I cooked my own dinner *once*, she never dared deny me again. Moral? Don’t let Miss Rachel bully you here as she did in London. Come back to the house!”

His logic was impeccable—and useless. “It’s no good, Betteredge,” I said. “But join me tomorrow for breakfast. I’ve something to discuss.”

He sighed. “I’d hoped you and Miss Rachel might have mended things by now. But if you’re set on leaving, at least spare yourself the trip to Frizinghall. Hotherstone’s Farm is but two miles off—and on freehold land,” he added slyly.

I remembered the place at once: a quiet farmhouse nestled in the valley, its spare rooms often let to visitors. “Are they available?”

“Mrs. Hotherstone asked me to recommend them just yesterday.”

“Then I’ll take them gladly.”

As we retrieved my bag, Betteredge shook his head in bewilderment. “I’ve lived a fair span, Mr. Franklin, but I never thought to see the day when you’d turn your back on the house.” He chuckled darkly. “Next, you’ll be repaying that seven-and-sixpence you borrowed as a boy!”

The jest restored his spirits, and as we walked, his curiosity got the better of him. “Fine evening for a stroll,” he remarked, as if we’d met by chance. “What brings you down so suddenly, sir?”

“The same as before,” I said. “The Moonstone.”

He halted, peering at me in the dusk. “If that’s a joke, I’m too old to laugh.”

“No joke. I mean to find out who took it.”

“Leave it be, Mr. Franklin! That cursed stone’s been nothing but trouble. If Sergeant Cuff couldn’t solve it—”

“My mind’s made up. Speaking of Cuff, where is he now?”

“Retired to Dorking, growing roses. He’s even bred a white moss rose without grafting—so he writes. But he won’t help you.”

“Then I’ll manage without him. And with your help.”

Something in my tone nettled him. “You might do worse,” he said sharply.

There was more he wasn’t saying. “What do you know, Betteredge?”

He feigned ignorance, but I pressed him, appealing to his love for Rachel and for me. At last, he relented.

“Rosanna Spearman left a letter for you,” he confessed. “Sealed, and in the keeping of Limping Lucy at Cobb’s Hole.”

“Lucy Yolland? The fisherman’s daughter?”

“The same. She refused to give it to anyone but you.”

“Then let’s fetch it tonight.”

“Too late now. Cobb’s Hole keeps early hours. But tomorrow, at first light—”

Below us, a lantern glowed at Hotherstone’s Farm.

“Tomorrow, then,” I agreed.

And with that, we descended the path toward the waiting light.

My memory of that night at Hotherstone’s Farm is hazy at best. I recall a boisterous welcome, a supper fit for an Eastern feast, and a spotless bedroom marred only by that abominable relic of our ancestors—a featherbed. The night was restless, marked by the constant strike of matches and the flicker of a single candle. Dawn brought relief, and with it, the promise of escape from that suffocating mattress.

Betteredge and I had agreed I would fetch him early on our way to Cobb’s Hole—early, in my eagerness, meaning *as soon as humanly possible*. I snatched a crust of bread from the kitchen and set off, half-expecting to find the old steward still abed. To my surprise, he was already up, stick in hand, vibrating with the same restless energy that gripped me.

“How are you this morning, Betteredge?”

“Poorly, sir. Very poorly.”

“What’s the trouble?”

“A new affliction of my own invention. Don’t be alarmed, but you’ll catch it before noon.”

“The devil I will!”

“Feel any heat in your stomach? A nasty pounding in your skull? No? It’ll seize you at Cobb’s Hole, Mr. Franklin. I call it *detective-fever*—first caught it in Sergeant Cuff’s company.”

“Ah! And the cure, I suppose, is opening Rosanna Spearman’s letter? Then let’s be off.”

The fisherman’s wife was already at work in her kitchen when we arrived. Upon my introduction by Betteredge, Mrs. Yolland performed what I later learned was a ritual reserved for distinguished strangers—placing a bottle of Dutch gin and two clean pipes on the table before asking, “What news from London, sir?”

Before I could muster a reply, a figure emerged from the shadows—a pale, wild-eyed girl with striking beauty and a fierce intensity. She limped forward on a crutch, her gaze locked onto me with a mix of fascination and revulsion.

“Mr. Betteredge,” she said, never looking away, “say his name again.”

“This gentleman,” Betteredge emphasized, “is Mr. Franklin Blake.”

The girl turned sharply and vanished. Mrs. Yolland may have apologized; Betteredge may have translated. I scarcely noticed. My attention was fixed on the rhythmic *thump-thump* of her crutch—up the stairs, across the floor above, then down again—until she reappeared at the door, letter in hand, beckoning me outside.

Leaving hasty excuses behind, I followed her down the beach, past the boats, out of sight and earshot of the village. There, she wheeled on me.

“Stand still,” she commanded. “I want to look at you.”

Her expression was unmistakable: *loathing*. Not the polite aversion of society, but raw, unfiltered disgust.

“I believe you have a letter for me,” I ventured. “Is that it?”

“Say it again.”

I obeyed.

“No,” she muttered, still dissecting me with her eyes. “I can’t see what she saw in his face. I can’t guess what she heard in his voice.” Abruptly, she looked away, resting her head on her crutch. “Oh, my poor dear,” she whispered, the first softness I’d heard from her. “What could you see in this man?”

Her head snapped up again. “Can you eat and drink?”

“Yes.”

“Can you sleep?”

“Yes.”

“When you see a servant girl, do you feel *nothing*?”

“Should I?”

With a furious motion, she shoved the letter at me. “Take it! I never saw you before. Pray God I never see you again.” And with that, she was gone.

Madness, I concluded. Nothing else explained it. Shaking off the encounter, I turned to Rosanna’s letter. The envelope bore my name, underlined with strict instructions: *To be given into his own hands—and no one else’s.*

Inside, a note and a slip of paper. The note read:

> *Sir—If you wish to understand my conduct toward you at Lady Verinder’s, follow the enclosed directions exactly—and alone.*
> *Your servant,*
> *Rosanna Spearman.*

The memorandum was precise:

> *Go to the Shivering Sand at low tide. Walk the South Spit until the beacon and Coast-guard flagstaff align. Lay a stick along this line, its end at the rocks’ edge above the quicksand. Feel along the stick’s length in the seaweed for a chain. Follow the chain over the edge—and pull.*

I had just finished reading when Betteredge’s voice broke in. The detective-fever had him in its grip.

“I can’t bear it, Mr. Franklin! What does it say?”

I handed him the papers. The memorandum electrified him.

“The *Sergeant* knew!” he cried. “He said she’d left directions! And here they are—the secret that baffled even Cuff!” He checked his watch. “The tide turns in an hour. We’ve time to reach the Sand by the coast path.”

“Then let’s go.”

As we walked, I pressed Betteredge to recount Rosanna’s movements during Cuff’s investigation. The pieces fell into place: her feigned illness, her locked-door vigils, her purchase of a tin case and chains from Mrs. Yolland. Cuff had been certain she’d hidden *something* at the quicksand—but what?

Now, standing on the South Spit, I aligned the beacon and flagstaff, laid my stick as instructed, and waited. The tide turned. The Sand shuddered—an eerie, living tremor. I tossed aside my cigar and returned to the rocks.

Groping along the stick, I found the chain buried in seaweed. Following it toward the quicksand, I hit a snarl of growth. Impossible to clear. Marking the spot, I waded in, probing beneath the rocks. My fingers closed on metal.

A tug, and the chain came free—rusted, but intact. At its end, a japanned tin case. The lid resisted, but I wrested it open. Inside, linen wrapped around a crumpled letter. I pocketed the letter and unrolled the fabric.

A nightgown.

Flipping it over, I froze.

A smear of paint—from Rachel’s boudoir door.

Sergeant Cuff’s voice echoed in my mind:

*Find the paint-stained garment. Learn whose it is. Demand an alibi for those midnight hours. If they fail—you’ve your thief.*

A shout broke my trance. Betteredge, impatient, was trudging toward me.

The nightgown’s mark?

The sun, the bay, Betteredge’s approaching figure—all blurred.

The evidence was irrefutable.

*I* was the thief.

The shock had left me numb, my thoughts scattered like leaves in a storm. When Betteredge found me on the beach, I scarcely knew my own actions—laughter, they tell me, a hollow sound, as I thrust the stained nightgown into his hands and bade him decipher the riddle himself.

Of our conversation by the shore, I remember nothing. The next clear fragment of time finds us among the firs, walking back to the house. Betteredge, ever practical, assured me that a glass of grog would steel us both for what lay ahead.

And so, against my earlier resolve, I crossed the threshold of Rachel’s home once more. The dim coolness of Betteredge’s sitting room was a mercy. The grog—a novelty to me at such an hour—burned its way down, not dulling my senses as it might have under gentler circumstances, but sharpening them, bracing me as the old man had promised.

I confess, my behavior must seem inexplicable. Confronted with an accusation so monstrous, did I retreat into solitude? Did I dissect the impossible truth before me? Did I rush to London to summon experts, to set investigations in motion? No. I sat in a servant’s quarters, drinking spirits before noon, seeking solace in the familiar face of an old retainer. Let those who have never faltered in dignity condemn me. In that moment, Betteredge’s presence was the only anchor I had.

He tossed the nightgown onto the table between us, pointing at it as though it were a living thing. "One thing’s certain," he declared. "*He’s* a liar, to start with."

But his certainty was not mine. "I know no more of the Diamond’s theft than you," I said. "Yet there lies the evidence—the paint, the name on the fabric."

Betteredge pushed the glass back into my hand. "Facts?" He scoffed. "Another sip, Mr. Franklin, and you’ll see past such frailties. This reeks of foul play—and we’ll unearth it. Was there nothing else in that tin case?"

The question jolted me. The letter. I drew it from my pocket, unfolded it—pages densely written. My eyes flicked to the signature. *Rosanna Spearman.*

A memory flared. "She came from a reformatory," I said slowly. "She was a thief?"

Betteredge’s grip tightened on my arm. "Read the letter," he urged. "For the girl’s sake, read it."

And so I did aloud:

The page slipped from my fingers. "What in God’s name does this mean?"

Betteredge would not meet my eyes. "Did Lucy say nothing of Rosanna this morning?"

"Not a word."

"Then let her speak for herself," he said grimly. "And drink, Mr. Franklin. For your own sake, drink."

I read on.

Rosanna’s voice, even in ink, was raw with desperation. She wrote of her past—the reformatory, the theft, the mother abandoned by a gentleman father. She spoke of Lady Verinder’s kindness, of Betteredge’s decency, and then—of me.

*"You were like a prince from a tale. I wrote our names in my work-box, tied with a lover’s knot. I kissed your pillow, folded your clothes with hands that trembled. You never saw me. Not once."*

Her hatred for Rachel seethed between the lines—the roses Rachel gave me, replaced in secret by Rosanna’s own. Her bitterness coiled tighter as she recounted the discovery of the paint-stained nightgown, her first suspicion, her warped hope that my guilt might bind me to her.

The letter laid bare her scheme: the duplicate nightgown sewn in frantic haste, the paint scraped from my dressing-gown, the damning fabric hidden away. And then, the fatal twist—Superintendent Seegrave’s accusation against Penelope, which had sent Rosanna’s thoughts spiraling.

Her final, delirious gamble: confronting me in the library, testing me with veiled words about the Diamond, desperate for any scrap of acknowledgment.

There would be no next time.

I thrust the letter at Betteredge, my hands unsteady. "Finish it yourself. I cannot."

He took it without a word. The silence between us was thick with the weight of Rosanna’s sorrow—a life bent by loneliness, twisted by longing, and ended in the Shivering Sand.

Betteredge, having disclosed the name of Mr. Candy’s assistant, seemed to consider the matter trivial enough to dismiss. He returned to Rosanna Spearman’s letter, while I remained by the window, waiting.

Gradually, the peculiar impression left by Ezra Jennings—odd, given the turmoil of my own mind—faded. My thoughts returned, grim and relentless, to the impossible position I found myself in. Once more, I forced myself to confront it. My course was decided: return to London at once, lay the case before Mr. Bruff, and, above all, secure an audience with Rachel, no matter the cost.

The train would not depart for another hour. There was still the slim chance Betteredge might uncover something useful in the remainder of Rosanna’s letter. So I waited.

Her words concluded thus:

*"You need not resent my fleeting triumph, Mr. Franklin, knowing I held your fate in my hands. But fear soon overtook me. Sergeant Cuff’s suspicions meant every inch of the house would be searched. Where could I hide the nightgown where even he would not find it? And how, without wasting a moment? The solution, when it came, might make you laugh. I put it on. After all, you had worn it—and I took a small, bitter pleasure in that."*

The next development proved her haste justified. Cuff demanded the washing-book.

*"I brought it to him in the sitting room. We had crossed paths before—I knew he might recognize me, and I dreaded what that might mean. Better to face it at once. But he treated me as a stranger, polite and detached. A bad sign. I was certain he was biding his time. Then came word of your return from the railway. I went to the shrubbery, hoping for one last chance to speak. You never came. Worse, Betteredge and Cuff passed by—and the Sergeant saw me."*

She had no choice but to retreat.

*"As I moved to leave, you appeared—walking straight toward me, then turning sharply away as if I carried contagion. (A misunderstanding, sir. You never noticed her. Your change of path was mere coincidence—a thought of your aunt diverting you indoors.)*

*"Back in the laundry, alone, my thoughts spiraled. The Shivering Sand whispered to me again. Which would be harder to endure: your indifference, or the quick end the quicksand promised?"*

She confessed her inability to explain her own actions—why she hadn’t stopped me, why she hadn’t spoken plainly.

*"I had the power to compel you, had I chosen. I even knew a man in London who would have given you money for the Diamond, no questions asked. But fear silenced me—fear of your anger, your contempt. Behind your back, I loved you desperately. To your face, I was a coward."*

Penelope interrupted then, bringing grim news: Rachel, furious at the police presence, meant to leave for her aunt’s. And where Rachel went, Franklin would not stay.

*"A flicker of hope—if you and Rachel were truly at odds. But Penelope dashed it: ‘He’s too fond of her to quarrel.’ Then came the summons. Cuff was questioning the staff."*

Rosanna’s turn came after the upper housemaids.

*"His questions were cunning. He knew of the nightgown, but not the truth of it. He suspected me—yet not as the prime culprit. Someone else, he thought, had directed me. Who? I couldn’t guess. But one thing was clear: as long as the nightgown remained hidden, you were safe."*

Desperation drove her next move. She could no longer risk wearing the garment.

*"Destroy it? I couldn’t. It was my only proof—my only leverage if you ever doubted me. So I resolved to hide it in the one place I knew best: the Shivering Sand."*

Under pretext of fresh air, she went to the Yollands’ cottage to write this letter and remove the nightgown unseen.

*"When this is done, I will make one last attempt to speak to you. If I fail—if you leave, if you spurn me again—then I have nothing left. Forgive me, sir, if it ends that way. But if you ever learn what I’ve done for you, spare me a kind word. Perhaps my ghost will hear."*

She sealed the letter, resigned yet clinging to frail hope.

Betteredge finished reading in heavy silence. At my question, he shook his head.

I tucked it away, remembering the two occasions I had unwittingly turned Rosanna aside—once at the billiard table, once in the shrubbery. Each time, my attempts to shield her had only driven her deeper into despair.

Now, the past was laid bare. The tragedy of Rosanna Spearman was done. It remained only to look forward—to the living, and the slow, arduous path from shadow into light.

The walk to the railway station was a quiet one, with Gabriel Betteredge at my side—uncharacteristically silent. The weight of Rosanna Spearman’s letter and the stained nightgown in my bag pressed on me, soon to be laid before Mr. Bruff’s scrutiny. Only once we were beyond the lodge gates did I break the silence.

"I have two questions for you, Betteredge," I said. "And I suspect they’ll catch you off guard."

He exhaled sharply. "If they’ll shake that wretched girl’s letter from my thoughts, Mr. Franklin, ask away. Surprise me, for heaven’s sake."

"First—on the night of Rachel’s birthday, was I drunk?"

Betteredge halted mid-step, gaping. "*You?* The man who sips wine at dinner and shuns spirits after? Not likely!"

"But it was a celebration. Might I have strayed from habit?"

He rubbed his chin. "Well, you were pale as death that evening. We coaxed you into a brandy-and-water—half a glass of fine Cognac drowned in a tumbler of water. A child wouldn’t have tipsied on it, let alone you."

His memory was reliable. Intoxication was impossible.

"Second question," I pressed. "In my boyhood, before I went abroad—did you ever find me walking in my sleep?"

The old man’s eyes narrowed. "Ah! You’re grasping for excuses—how the paint got on your nightgown without your knowing. But sleepwalking? Never in your life, sir."

Again, his certainty was sound. I’d never been solitary; hundreds would have noticed such a habit. Yet stubbornly, I clung to the only explanations that spared my dignity: drunkenness or somnambulism.

Betteredge saw my hesitation and dismantled both theories with brutal logic.

"Assume you *were* drunk or sleepwalking when you took the Diamond. That covers the birthday night. But how does it explain what followed? Transporting it to London? Pledging it to Luker? Were you drunk when I saw you off in the chaise? Did you sleepwalk to Luker’s office?" He shook his head. "You’re too shaken to think clearly. Lay the matter before Bruff."

At the station, I hastily scribbled my London address for him, promising updates. As I turned to leave, I spotted Ezra Jennings—the peculiar assistant from Frizinghall—lingering by the newsstand. Our eyes met; he tipped his hat. I returned the gesture and boarded the train, oddly fixated on encountering that piebald-haired man twice in one day.

***

London’s twilight hours ruled out finding Bruff at his office. I went straight to Hampstead, where I roused the dozing lawyer from his wine and pug-dog companionship.

His methodical mind went to work at once: lights, strong tea, and orders not to be disturbed. He examined the nightgown, then read Rosanna’s letter. When he finally spoke, his words were knives.

"Franklin, this is grave. Rachel’s behavior makes sense now. She believes you stole the Diamond."

I’d skirted that vile conclusion, but it was undeniable. My resolve to confront Rachel solidified.

"We must break her silence," Bruff declared. "Either persuasion or force—but she *must* disclose why she suspects you. The case likely crumbles if she does."

"Comforting," I muttered.

"From a legal view, the evidence falters. The nightgown is yours, the paint matches Rachel’s door—but where’s proof *you* wore it that night?"

The flaw was glaring.

Bruff continued, dissecting Rosanna’s letter. "She was a liar by her own admission. What if she manipulated Rachel with the nightgown? Jealous, scheming—she’d have exploited any rift between you and Rachel."

"I wondered the same," I admitted.

"But sentiment blinded you. Now—suppose you *did* wear the nightgown? Proving innocence would still be treacherous. We must learn if Rachel’s suspicion rests *solely* on that garment."

I bristled. "Since when does Rachel Brand me a thief on *any* evidence?"

Bruff’s gaze sharpened. "Search your past. Did anything—however slight—shake her faith in your character?"

A memory struck like lightning: the Parisian debt collector, his accusations ringing through the house, Rachel’s horrified judgment. *Heartless. Dishonorable.*

Bruff read my face. "Ah. That incident would linger. A predisposing bias against you."

He paced, deliberating. "The key is making Rachel speak freely. Suggestions?"

"I’ll confront her myself."

Bruff froze. "*You?*" After a fraught silence, he conceded. "Rashness may serve. You’ve one advantage: some stubborn affection in Rachel, buried but alive. Exploit it. But how to arrange the meeting?"

"Your home. An invitation under neutral pretenses."

"Cold-blooded," he muttered. Yet he agreed. Rachel would be invited for lunch in two days’ time. I’d enter through the garden gate, find her alone in the music room.

"Gratitude doesn’t cover it," I said.

"Save your thanks. Blame me later if this goes awry."

***

The next day was torment. Though innocent, I skulked indoors, ashamed to face acquaintances. Innocence, I realized, often wears guilt’s mask.

Bruff arrived the following morning, shamefaced, bearing a key. "Rachel comes today. My wife and daughters play unwitting hostesses. Jesuitical, but necessary."

"The key?"

"To my garden gate. Be there at three. Enter through the conservatory, cross the drawing-room, and you’ll find her alone at the piano."

As Hampstead’s clock chimed three, I turned the key in the wall. The garden was still, the conservatory empty. Through the drawing-room door, piano notes drifted—a melancholy strain Rachel often played idly in years past.

I steadied myself. The past and present collided. Then, steeling my nerve, I opened the door.

The moment I stepped into the room, Rachel rose from the piano.

The door clicked shut behind me. We stood frozen, the length of the room between us, the air thick with unspoken accusations. She had moved to rise, but now she was still again, as if the act of seeing me had drained her of all other motion. Her eyes fixed on me—wide, searching, full of something I couldn’t name.

Had I startled her? I took a step forward, my voice low. *"Rachel."*

The sound of her name broke whatever spell held her. Color rushed back into her cheeks, her breath quickened. She moved toward me—not with purpose, but as if drawn against her will. Step by step, she closed the distance between us, her gaze never leaving mine. The closer she came, the more I forgot why I was there—forgot the suspicions, the disgrace, everything but the woman before me.

Then she was within reach. I saw her tremble, saw the hesitation flicker in her eyes. I couldn’t stop myself. I pulled her into my arms, my lips finding hers in a desperate, reckless kiss.

For a heartbeat, I thought she kissed me back.

Then—horror. A sharp cry tore from her throat, and she shoved me away with a strength I hadn’t known she possessed. Her eyes burned with fury, her mouth twisted in contempt. She looked at me as if I were a stranger. Worse—a criminal.

*"You coward!"* she spat. *"You miserable, heartless coward!"*

The words struck like a blow. Of all the reproaches she could have chosen, this was the cruelest.

*"There was a time,"* I said, my voice tight, *"when you would have told me I’d offended you in a worthier way than that. I beg your pardon."*

Something in my tone made her flinch. Her gaze flickered away, then back, reluctant. When she spoke again, her voice was quieter, but no less bitter.

*"Perhaps I deserve some excuse. After what you’ve done, was it manly to come here like this? To test my weakness for you? To surprise me into letting you touch me?"* She gave a sharp, mirthless laugh. *"But that’s a woman’s view. I should have known better than to expect honor from you."*

The apology was worse than the insult. It cut deeper.

*"If my honor weren’t in your hands,"* I said, *"I’d walk out that door and never return. You speak of what I’ve done. Tell me—what have I done?"*

Her laugh this time was raw, disbelieving. *"You dare ask me that?"*

*"I’ve kept your disgrace a secret!"* she shot back. *"I’ve suffered for it! And now you stand there and ask me what you’ve done? Was there ever a shred of gratitude in you? You were a gentleman once. You were dear to my mother. You were—"* Her voice broke. She turned away, collapsing into a chair, her face buried in her hands.

I waited, struggling for control. Her contempt stung, but so did the sight of her distress. Pride warred with something deeper, something that refused to let me walk away.

*"If you won’t speak first,"* I said, *"then I must. I came here with something to say. Will you hear me?"*

She didn’t move.

So I told her everything—the discovery at the Shivering Sand, Rosanna’s suicide, the nightgown, the suspicions that had led me here. She didn’t interrupt. She didn’t even look at me.

When I finished, the silence was suffocating.

Then, calmly, I asked the question that would decide everything.

She shot to her feet, her eyes blazing. *"Are you mad?"*

*"What game are you playing?"* she demanded. *"Do you think I don’t see through you? You’ve come here to buy my silence, haven’t you? Your father’s death made you rich. Is that it? You think you can compensate me for the Diamond now?"* Her voice turned venomous. *"Or are you ashamed of what you did and trying to hide behind lies about Rosanna?"*

I couldn’t hold back any longer.

*"You’ve wronged me!"* I snapped. *"You suspect me of stealing the Diamond. I have a right to know why!"*

Her laugh was sharp, brittle. *"Suspect you?"* She stepped closer, her voice dropping to a whisper. *"I saw you take it. With my own eyes."*

The words hit like a bullet.

I stood frozen, my mind reeling. *She saw me.* How? When? None of it made sense—yet her certainty was absolute.

She drew back, watching me crumble. *"I spared you then,"* she said. *"I would have spared you now if you hadn’t forced this out of me."* She turned toward the door, then hesitated. *"Why did you come here? Why humiliate us both?"*

I moved without thinking, catching her wrist. *"Rachel,"* I said hoarsely, *"you once loved me."*

She shuddered but didn’t pull away. Her hand trembled in mine.

*"Let go,"* she whispered.

But I didn’t. And in that moment, despite everything, I still had power over her.

Slowly, I led her back to the chair. *"Listen to me,"* I said. *"You saw me take the Diamond. Before God, I swear I didn’t know I’d taken it until this moment. Do you believe me?"*

She didn’t answer. Her head bowed, her fingers tightening around mine even as she whispered again, *"Let go."*

I didn’t. Instead, I pressed on, desperate for the truth.

*"Tell me everything,"* I urged. *"From the moment we said goodnight to the moment you saw me take the Diamond."*

She lifted her head, tears streaking her cheeks. *"Why go back to it?"*

*"Because we’ve both been deceived,"* I said. *"There’s something here we’re missing. Help me see it."*

And so, haltingly, she did.

She told me of that night—how she hadn’t slept, how she’d risen to fetch a book, how she’d seen a light under the sitting-room door. She thought it was her mother at first. Then she saw *me.*

*"You were in your nightclothes,"* she said, her voice hollow. *"You had a candle. You walked to the cabinet, opened the drawer, and took the Diamond. I saw it in your hand."*

Every word was a knife.

I released her hand and stood, pacing like a caged animal. There had to be an explanation. *Had to.* But what?

She watched me, her expression hardening again. *"Well?"* she said. *"You made me hope. What do you say now?"*

I had nothing.

And then, like a fool, I accused her. *"If you’d spoken sooner—"*

She exploded.

*"You think I should have called you a thief to your face?"* she shouted. *"After loving you? After defending you? You vile, wretched man!"*

I reached for my hat. It was over.

But she slammed the door before I could leave.

*"No,"* she said, her voice shaking. *"You’ll hear me out. Then you can crawl away like the liar you are."*

And so I stayed.

What choice did I have?

The hour was late when Mr. Bruff appeared unexpectedly at my door. The man was altered—his usual brisk assurance replaced by a weary silence. He took my hand without a word.

“You’ve come from Hampstead?” I ventured.

“From Hampstead, yes,” he replied, his voice low. “I’ve just left Rachel at Portland Place. You must understand, Mr. Franklin, that I would rather have left you in ignorance forever than see her pay this price for the truth.”

“You’ve spoken with her?”

“I brought her back myself. She was in no state to travel alone.” His gaze sharpened. “I don’t blame you—you acted under my roof, with my consent. But I will not permit a repetition of this disaster. She is young, resilient. Time and quiet will mend what’s been broken—provided you do not interfere. Will you swear to make no further attempt to see her without my approval?”

The answer came without hesitation. “After what she has endured—after what I have endured—you have my word.”

“Your promise?”

“My promise.”

The tension in his frame eased. He set aside his hat and drew nearer.

“Good. That’s settled. Now, to the future—*your* future.” He leaned forward. “The situation, as it stands, is this: Rachel has told you the truth, plainly and completely. And while we know there must be some monstrous error at work, we cannot fault her for believing the evidence of her own eyes—corroborated, as it was, by circumstances that damn you utterly.”

I cut in. “I do not blame Rachel. I only wish she had spoken more plainly when she had the chance.”

Mr. Bruff gave a dry laugh. “You might as well wish her to be another woman entirely. Even then, I doubt any lady of delicacy—one who had hoped to marry you—could bring herself to accuse you to your face. It is not in Rachel’s nature. In another matter, unrelated to yours but not dissimilar in principle, she acted from the same motive. Besides”—his voice hardened—“she told me herself tonight that had she confronted you then, she would have disbelieved your denial just as she disbelieves it now. What answer can you make to that? None. So let us be practical, Mr. Franklin. The past is a tangle we cannot unravel. Our only path lies forward.”

“You forget,” I said, “that for me, this is entirely a matter of the past.”

“Is it?” His eyes gleamed. “Answer me: is the Moonstone the root of all this misery?”

“Of course.”

“Then consider. What do we believe happened to the Moonstone in London?”

“It was pledged to Mr. Luker.”

“We know *you* did not pledge it. Do we know who did?”

“No.”

“Where is the Moonstone now?”

“In the vaults of Luker’s bankers.”

“Precisely. Now mark this: we are in June. By month’s end, a full year will have passed since the gem was pledged. There is a chance—a slim one, but a chance—that the thief may redeem it when the term expires. If so, Luker must retrieve it personally. My proposal is simple: we watch the bank at the critical hour and learn who claims it.”

I frowned. “It’s daring. Ingenious. But—”

“But you object to the delay.”

“A fortnight is an eternity in my position, Mr. Bruff. I cannot endure this limbo without acting.”

He exhaled sharply. “Then act. Have you a plan?”

“Sergeant Cuff.”

“Retired. Useless.”

“I know where to find him.”

A pause. Then, grudgingly: “Try. The case has twisted into something even Cuff might find intriguing. But hear this—the key lies in discovering who pawned the Diamond. Let me know what the Sergeant says.” He rose, collecting his hat. “And if you’ve made no progress by month’s end, I’ll set my watch at the bank—unless you’ve relieved me of the necessity.”

With a thin smile, he was gone.

---

At first light, I set out for Dorking—Betteredge’s directions in hand. The Sergeant’s cottage lay beyond the town, a quiet refuge buried in roses. The gate was locked. Peering through the trellis, I saw blossoms crowding every inch of the garden, framing the windows, spilling over the path. Here, the great detective had retreated into horticultural oblivion.

The woman who answered my ring dashed my hopes at once. “He’s gone to Ireland, sir. Left yesterday.”

“On business?”

She smiled. “Roses are his business now. Some gardener’s made a discovery—off he went to investigate.”

No certainty of return. I left a message—*Moonstone. Urgent.*—and turned back to London, restless as a caged beast.

That night, logic gave way to fevered theories, each more absurd than the last. I woke tangled in philosophical doubts, questioning the very existence of the Diamond—until chance intervened. Rummaging for a handkerchief, I found Betteredge’s forgotten letter.

The act of replying—mundane as it was—cleared my mind. I turned my focus to the birthday dinner, probing memory for any overlooked detail. The guest list was essential. Betteredge would have it. I resolved to return to Yorkshire at once.

With hours to kill before the next train, I sought those few London guests: Murthwaite (gone abroad), Miss Clack (fled to France), and Godfrey Ablewhite—whose recent misfortunes I learned at his club. A broken engagement, a sudden legacy of £5,000, and a hasty departure for the Continent. Another dead end.

Defeated, I boarded the train to Frizinghall. Tomorrow, Betteredge would join me—my last hope. That evening, I prepared to question the remaining guests, starting with Mr. Candy.

The doctor was a shock. The vivacious little man I remembered was a specter—eyes dim, hair white, frame shrunken. Only his gaudy clothes remained unchanged.

“Mr. Blake!” he cried, seizing my hand. “Command my services, sir! Anything!”

I launched into my rehearsed explanation—the Diamond, the need to reconstruct that fateful evening. But his gaze drifted, fingers plucking at nothing. My words meant nothing to him.

A change of tack: “Betteredge mentioned you wished to see me.”

At that, he brightened. “Ah! Yes! Yes!”

But the spark faded as quickly as it came. Whatever message he’d intended was lost in the ruin of his mind.

I left, heavy-hearted. Three witnesses beyond reach, and a fourth useless. All now depended on Betteredge—and the ghosts of Lady Verinder’s circle.

The morning sun streamed through the open door as I stepped into the hall of Mr. Candy’s house, his pretty housemaid holding it ajar for me. The light fell sharply upon the face of Ezra Jennings as I turned to look at him, and I understood at once why Betteredge had spoken of his appearance with such misgiving.

There was something unsettling about the man—his sun-darkened skin, the hollows beneath his cheekbones, the strange duality of his features that made him seem both youthful and prematurely aged. His eyes, deep-set and contemplative, held an unsettling gleam, and his hair, streaked with incongruous shades, only added to his unsettling presence. And yet, despite it all, I felt an inexplicable pull toward him, a sympathy I could not rationalize. Common sense urged me to answer his question about Mr. Candy’s decline and be on my way, but curiosity rooted me to the spot.

“Are you walking my way, Mr. Jennings?” I asked, noting the hat in his hand. “I’m bound for my aunt’s—Mrs. Ablewhite.”

He replied that he had a patient to attend to but would accompany me part of the distance.

As we left, I observed the housemaid’s reaction to his murmured instructions about his return—her lips tightened, her gaze darting anywhere but his face. The dismissal in her manner was unmistakable. Betteredge had claimed Jennings was disliked everywhere, and here was proof of it. *What a life he must lead,* I thought as we stepped into the street.

Having broached the subject of Mr. Candy’s illness, Jennings now fell silent, leaving it to me to press further. I obliged.

“The change in him is severe,” I remarked. “Was his condition graver than I realized?”

“It’s a wonder he survived at all,” Jennings replied.

“And his memory—is it always so fragmented? He seemed to be struggling to tell me something—”

“—About events before his fever?” Jennings finished for me.

“Yes.”

“His recollection of that time is shattered,” he said. “It would almost be a mercy if he forgot entirely. As it is, he grasps at fragments—plans half-formed, words unspoken—but cannot piece them together. He knows his own deficiency, and it torments him.” His voice softened. “Perhaps we would all be happier if we could forget entirely.”

“Some memories,” I countered, “are worth preserving.”

“For most men, I hope so. But not all.” He studied me. “Do you believe the memory he sought had significance for *you*?”

The question struck at the heart of my own thoughts. My interest in Jennings had been cautious, but his manner—his quiet, unforced dignity—persuaded me he was a man to be trusted.

“I believe it may,” I admitted. “Is there any way to help him recover it?”

Jennings’s dreamy eyes sharpened with sudden focus. “His memory is beyond repair. I’ve tried.”

My disappointment must have shown.

“That isn’t to say the recollection itself is lost,” he added. “It may be possible to trace it without relying on Mr. Candy.”

“How?”

“By reconstructing his delirium.” He paused. “May I explain in detail?”

At my eager assent, he continued. During Candy’s fever, Jennings had recorded his ravings in shorthand, later transcribing them with gaps where the words broke apart. He had then pieced them together like a puzzle, inferring the missing links until the fragments formed coherent thought.

“You see,” he said, “the mind may still reason even when the tongue falters.”

“Did he speak of *me*?” I cut in.

Jennings met my gaze. “He did. Over one long night, his thoughts circled some matter between you and him. I have the fragments—and the connections I’ve drawn. The question is whether this aligns with the memory he sought today.”

“We must look at once!” I exclaimed.

“Impossible.” His tone was firm. “I will not share a man’s fevered words lightly. If you wish to know more, you must first tell me why it matters.”

I hesitated. To confess my suspicion—that I was accused of theft—was unbearable. I dodged with vague explanations, but Jennings listened without satisfaction.

“I’m sorry,” he said at last. “Nothing in his ravings concerned the Moonstone.”

We reached a fork in the road. Jennings took the path toward the village, bowing farewell. But as he walked away, conviction seized me—he held the key I needed.

I called him back.

When he turned, I forced the words out. “I haven’t been honest. My interest isn’t in the diamond. It’s personal—shamefully so.”

A shadow crossed his face. “Then I must be honest in turn,” he said, his voice low and taut. “Before you confide in me, you should know how I came to be in Mr. Candy’s employ. It’s not a long tale, but it may change your mind about trusting me.”

The intensity in his eyes silenced me. I nodded, and we walked on.

After a distance, he paused by a break in the stone wall bordering the moor.

“May we rest?” he asked. “I am not as strong as I appear, and some truths weigh heavily.”

The weight of those two hours of waiting pressed upon me in a way I cannot easily describe. My body refused stillness; my mind shrank from human company. Until I heard Ezra Jennings’ revelation, I could neither rest nor speak to another soul.

I abandoned my intended visit to Mrs. Ablewhite—even avoided Gabriel Betteredge. Returning to Frizinghall, I left a note for him, claiming urgent business would keep me away until afternoon. I urged him to dine without me and seek his usual diversions among friends. Then I strode out into the lonely moorland, wandering until my watch told me it was time to face Jennings.

I found him waiting in a grim little room adjoining the surgery—a place that spoke of suffering. Diagrams of disease lined the walls; a skull crowned a shelf of medical texts. The bare deal table, ink-stained and utilitarian, the threadbare rug, the sink with its ghastly surgical implications—all conspired to unsettle the spirit. Outside, bees hummed among flowers, and a distant, tuneless piano jangled intermittently. These ordinary sounds felt like trespassers in a space reserved for pain.

Jennings offered no apology for the setting. "This is the only place we won’t be disturbed," he said, gesturing to papers on the table. "My notes are ready. Bring your chair closer—we’ll need to consult them together."

I obeyed. The notes consisted of two folio sheets—one sparsely covered in fragments, the other densely filled in red and black ink. Frustrated, I pushed the second aside.

"Mercy, Jennings," I said. "Tell me what to expect before I drown in this."

He studied me with that quiet, sorrowful smile. "Very well. You’ve told me you’ve never knowingly taken opium."

"Never knowingly," I agreed.

"Then let me reconstruct the past. Last year, you suffered sleepless nights—until the night of the birthday, when you slept deeply. Correct?"

"Entirely."

"Did you ever determine the cause of your insomnia?"

"Betteredge guessed it was due to my quitting tobacco."

"Ah. A sound guess. Sudden cessation can unsettle the nerves. Now—do you recall quarreling with Mr. Candy about medicine?"

The memory surfaced reluctantly. At the birthday dinner, I had rashly disparaged medical practice, provoking Candy’s irritation. Lady Verinder had intervened, and we reconciled, but the dispute had slipped my mind entirely.

Jennings pressed further. "Were you anxious about the Diamond last year?"

"Profoundly. I knew conspirators sought it—I was warned to protect Miss Verinder."

"Was its safety discussed before you retired that night?"

"Yes. Lady Verinder and Rachel spoke of it—in my presence."

Jennings handed me his notes. "Read these now, and you’ll uncover two staggering truths. First: you took the Diamond in an opium-induced trance. Second: Mr. Candy, without your knowledge, dosed you to disprove your own arguments against medicine."

I sat stunned.

"Don’t condemn him too harshly," Jennings said gently. "He meant no harm. Had illness not struck him, he would have confessed the trick the next morning. The truth might have been uncovered within a day."

Anger simmered beneath my shock. "I may forgive, but I won’t forget."

"Every physician deceives patients at times. Opium is distrusted in England—even by the educated. But let’s focus on the present. My notes are useless as proof—too esoteric, too reliant on my word. To clear your name, we must recreate the past."

"How?"

"A bold experiment. You must unknowingly ‘steal’ the Diamond again—before witnesses whose testimony cannot be doubted."

I stared.

"It can be done," he insisted. "But I’ll need your full cooperation. First—you’ve resumed smoking. Give it up again, abruptly, as before."

Understanding dawned. "From this moment," I agreed.

"If sleeplessness follows, as it did last June, we’ll have our first success. Next, we must revive the circumstances of that night—the household’s atmosphere, your preoccupation with the Diamond. Then, a second dose of laudanum may reproduce the trance."

He opened a medical text, citing authorities to justify the plan. A passage from Dr. Carpenter suggested that past sensory impressions could resurface unconsciously. Another case described a drunkard recalling lost items only when intoxicated again.

"The science supports me," Jennings said. "But success isn’t guaranteed. If we cannot sufficiently approximate the conditions, the experiment fails. If we succeed, you may prove your innocence beyond doubt."

One question still nagged me. "Opium stupefies—yet I moved, opened drawers, returned to my room. How?"

Jennings handed me *Confessions of an English Opium-Eater*. "De Quincey wandered markets or attended operas under its influence. Opium doesn’t always induce passivity."

"And its effect on me?"

"That," said Jennings, "is what we must discover."

A few words from me will suffice to conclude the account left by Ezra Jennings in his journal.

As for myself, I awoke on the morning of the twenty-sixth with no memory of my actions under opium’s influence—from the moment the drug first seized me to the instant I opened my eyes in Rachel’s sitting room.

What followed requires little elaboration. Rachel and I reached an understanding before a single word of explanation passed between us. How this reconciliation occurred so swiftly, neither of us cares to justify. To those who have loved passionately, the scene that unfolded after Ezra Jennings closed the door needs no further illumination.

I will only add that we narrowly avoided discovery by Mrs. Merridew, thanks to Rachel’s quick thinking. Hearing the rustle of the old lady’s dress in the corridor, Rachel intercepted her. “What is the matter?” Mrs. Merridew demanded. “The explosion!” Rachel replied. With remarkable composure, she guided Mrs. Merridew into the garden, sparing her the supposed shock. Upon returning, the lady met me in the hall, marveling at modern science. “Explosions are far milder than in my youth, Mr. Blake! I scarcely heard Mr. Jennings’s from the garden—and not a trace of odor remains! Your medical friend must accept my compliments. He has managed it beautifully.”

Thus, after triumphing over Betteredge and Mr. Bruff, Ezra Jennings conquered even Mrs. Merridew. It seems liberal sentiment thrives in unexpected places.

At breakfast, Mr. Bruff made no secret of his desire for me to accompany him to London on the morning train. Rachel, intrigued by the bank’s surveillance and its possible outcome, resolved to join us—provided Mrs. Merridew consented. The old lady, still mollified by the “considerate” explosion, obliged without protest. Betteredge, entrusted with refurbishing the house, was too absorbed in his duties to succumb to detective fever.

Our sole regret was parting so abruptly with Ezra Jennings. He refused all persuasion to accompany us. I could only promise correspondence; Rachel insisted he visit her in Yorkshire. Though we expected to reunite within months, the sight of our dearest friend standing alone on the platform as the train departed weighed heavily upon us.

In London, Mr. Bruff was met at the station by an odd little boy clad in threadbare black, his most striking feature being his protuberant eyes—so loose and rolling, one marveled they remained in their sockets. After a brief exchange, Mr. Bruff excused us from escorting the ladies to Portland Place. I barely had time to assure Rachel I would return with a full account before the lawyer hurried me into a cab. The boy, whom Mr. Bruff affectionately called “Gooseberry,” perched beside the driver as we set off for Lombard Street.

“News from the bank?” I asked.

“News of Luker,” Mr. Bruff replied. “An hour ago, he left Lambeth in a cab with two plainclothes officers. If his fear of the Indians prompted this, the implication is clear: he means to withdraw the Diamond.”

“And we are to witness the result?”

“Or learn of it, if the affair is already concluded. Did you notice my boy?”

“His eyes were difficult to overlook.”

Mr. Bruff chuckled. “At the office, they call him ‘Gooseberry.’ I employ him for errands—and wish my clerks were half as reliable. Despite those eyes, he’s one of the sharpest lads in London.”

At twenty minutes to five, we halted before the bank. Gooseberry gazed longingly as Mr. Bruff opened the cab door.

“Coming in?” the lawyer asked kindly. The boy eagerly complied. “He’s quick as lightning,” Mr. Bruff whispered. “Two words suffice where twenty would be wasted on another.”

Inside, the outer office bustled with patrons before closing time. Two men approached Mr. Bruff immediately.

“Well?” the lawyer inquired.

“He passed through half an hour ago, sir, and entered the inner office.”

“Has he not emerged?”

“No, sir.”

Mr. Bruff turned to me. “We wait.”

I scanned the crowd for the Indians but found no trace. The sole dark-complexioned man present was a tall sailor in a pilot coat and round hat—too large to be one of them in disguise.

“They must have a spy nearby,” murmured Mr. Bruff, eyeing the sailor. “Perhaps him.”

Before he could elaborate, Gooseberry tugged his coat-tail. “Hush!” Mr. Bruff warned. “Here comes Luker.”

The moneylender emerged, flanked by his guardians. “Watch closely,” Mr. Bruff whispered. “If he transfers the Diamond, it will be here.”

Luker moved deliberately through the crowd. As he passed a stout, gray-suited man, I saw his hand flicker. The man started slightly, then stared after him. Luker proceeded to the door, his escort close behind, followed by one of Mr. Bruff’s men—and vanished from view.

I glanced meaningfully at the gray-suited man. “Yes,” Mr. Bruff whispered. “I saw it too.” He turned to locate his second man—nowhere in sight. Even Gooseberry had disappeared.

“What the devil?” Mr. Bruff muttered. “Deserted when most needed.”

The gray-suited man completed his transaction and turned to leave.

“What now?” Mr. Bruff asked. “We can’t trail him without debasing ourselves.”

“I can,” I declared. “I wouldn’t lose him for ten thousand pounds.”

“Then I shan’t lose you for twice that,” Mr. Bruff sighed. “A fine occupation for a man of my standing,” he grumbled as we followed the stranger out. “Not a word of this, Blake. Ruin awaits if it’s known.”

The man boarded a westward omnibus; we followed. Mr. Bruff, to my astonishment, flushed as he took his seat—latent youth resurfacing.

In Oxford Street, the man alighted and entered a chemist’s shop.

“My chemist!” Mr. Bruff exclaimed. “We’ve blundered.”

A brief exchange with the proprietor confirmed it. The lawyer returned, crestfallen.

“At least we may credit ourselves with one thing,” he said, leading me away.

“What?”

“We are the two worst amateur detectives ever to attempt the craft. That man has served the chemist thirty years. He was merely depositing funds and knows nothing of the Moonstone.”

I asked our next move.

“Back to my office,” Mr. Bruff said. “Gooseberry and my man must have followed someone else. Let us hope they kept their eyes open.”

At Gray’s Inn Square, the second man awaited us.

“Report,” Mr. Bruff demanded.

“A mistake, sir. I’d have sworn Luker passed something to an elderly gentleman in a light coat. Turns out he’s a respectable ironmonger of Eastcheap.”

“Where’s Gooseberry?”

The man stared blankly. “Haven’t seen him since the bank.”

Mr. Bruff dismissed him. “Either the boy’s fled, or he’s on his own hunt,” he mused. “Dine here, and we’ll see if he returns. I’ve good wine, and the coffee-house can furnish a chop.”

We dined in his chambers. Before clearing the cloth, a visitor was announced—not Gooseberry, but the man tasked with surveilling Luker. His report held no intrigue: Luker had returned home, dismissed his guards, and bolted his doors. The streets showed no sign of the Indians.

“Do you think he took the Diamond home?” I asked.

“Not likely,” Mr. Bruff said. “He’d have kept his guards if so.”

After another futile half-hour wait, Mr. Bruff departed for Hampstead, and I returned to Rachel. I left word for Gooseberry at my lodgings—but, true to form, I arrived late, finding only his note: he had grown sleepy and would return in the morning.

At nine the next day, I awaited him. Instead, Sergeant Cuff entered—as lean and dreary as ever, though now dressed as a country gentleman.

“I thought to call before writing to Yorkshire,” he said.

His sharp eyes still held their knowing gleam. He declined breakfast (shocked at my late hours) and came straight to the point.

“I misjudged the case last year,” he admitted. “But I’ve a sealed letter for you. Open it only when the truth is known—then compare my suspect with the guilty party.”

I pocketed the letter and asked his thoughts on our bank measures.

“Well-intended,” he said, “but another person required watching—the one named in that letter.”

Before he could elaborate, Gooseberry arrived. The boy, awed by the legendary Sergeant, recounted trailing a black-bearded sailor after seeing Luker pass him something.

“Why not tell Mr. Bruff?” Cuff pressed.

“No time, sir. The sailor left too quickly.”

Cuff praised the boy’s sharpness. Gooseberry beamed.

The sailor had hailed a cab; Gooseberry clung to its rear.

At that moment, Mr. Bruff’s chief clerk arrived with ill tidings: the lawyer was bedridden with gout. I sent assurances of Cuff’s involvement and the boy’s testimony.

Returning, I found Cuff ringing for a cab.

“The boy’s onto the right man,” he declared. “Time’s been lost—we must act now.”

Minutes later, we rode eastward, Gooseberry guiding the driver.

“That lad,” Cuff remarked, “will go far in my former profession. Now, let me relay what he told me in your absence…”

On the twenty-seventh of June, I was dispatched by Sergeant Cuff to track three Indian suspects linked to a murder. They had been spotted boarding a Rotterdam-bound steamer at Tower Wharf. I followed on a separate vessel departing the next morning.

Upon reaching Rotterdam, the ship’s captain confirmed the Indians had boarded—but only as far as Gravesend. There, they feigned distress upon realizing their "mistake," claiming they had intended to sail to Calais. They forfeited their passage and were set ashore. A clever ruse, no doubt, to evade pursuit.

I traced them back to London, then to Plymouth, where they had already sailed for Bombay aboard the *Bewley Castle*. Cuff alerted the Bombay authorities to intercept the ship upon arrival. My role ended there. The trail went cold.

I commanded the *Bewley Castle* when three Hindus boarded at Plymouth. Their conduct was unremarkable until we were becalmed off India’s coast. Discipline slackened; passengers took to the boats for leisure.

On the third night, the smallest boat vanished—and so did the Hindus. They must have rowed ashore under cover of darkness. By morning, pursuit was futile. Only later did I learn why they fled. The authorities blamed me for lax oversight. Regrettable, but nothing more was heard of them.

Do you recall our conversation in London about the Moonstone? Years later, in the remote province of Kattiawar, I witnessed its final chapter.

Posing as a pilgrim, I joined thousands converging on Somnauth, where a lunar ceremony was to be held. Under moonlight, three figures emerged on a rocky platform—the same Indians I’d seen in England. Stripped of caste, they were condemned to eternal pilgrimage, never to meet again.

Then the shrine’s curtain parted. There, upon the moon god’s forehead, blazed the Diamond—once hidden in an Englishwoman’s gown, now returned to its sacred origins. How it came back, I cannot say. But this much is certain: the Moonstone has slipped beyond European grasp forever.
