"Seven long hours in all men's sight this fight endured, until our men so feeble grew that they could fight no more. And then upon dead horses full savorly they fed, and drank the puddle water, for they could no better get. When they had fed so freely, they kneeled on the ground and gave God thanks devoutly for the favor they had found; then beating up their colors, the fight they did renew, and turning to the Spaniards, a thousand more they slew."

When the sun leaped up the next morning, casting its tropic light suddenly into the tropic day, Amyas paced the deck. His hair was disheveled, his clothes torn, his eyes red with rage and weeping, and his heart full of a pain difficult to describe. Picture it, you who have ever lost a brother; and you, who have not, thank God that you know nothing of such agony. Filled with impossible projects, he strode and staggered up and down as the ship thrashed close-hauled through the rolling seas. He would go back and burn the villa. He would take Guayra and demand the life of every man in it in return for his brother's.

"We can do it, lads!" he shouted. "If Drake took Nombre de Dios, we can take La Guayra." Every voice shouted, "Yes."

"We will have it, Amyas, and have Frank too, yet," cried Cary. But Amyas shook his head. He knew, and knew not why he knew, that all the ports in New Spain would never restore to him that one beloved face.

"Yes, he shall be well avenged. And look there! There is the first crop of our vengeance." He pointed toward the shore, where, between them and the now distant peaks of the Silla, three sails appeared, not five miles to windward.

"There are the Spanish bloodhounds on our heels—the same ships we saw yesterday off Guayra. Back, lads, and welcome them, if they were a dozen."

A murmur of applause rose from all around. If any young heart sank at the prospect of fighting three ships at once, it was awed into silence by the cheer rising from the older men and by Salvation Yeo's stentorian voice.

"If there were a dozen, the Lord is with us, who has said, 'One of you shall chase a thousand.' Clear away, lads, and see the glory of the Lord this day."

"Amen!" cried Cary, and the ship was kept still closer to the wind.

Amyas had revived at the sight of battle. He no longer felt his wounds or his great sorrow; even Frank's last angelic look grew dimmer as he bustled about the deck. Ere a quarter-hour had passed, his voice cried firmly and cheerfully as of old:

"Now, my masters, let us serve God, and then to breakfast, and after that clear for action."

Jack Brimblecombe read the daily prayers and the prayers before a fight at sea. His honest voice trembled as, in the *Prayer for all Conditions of Men*, he added, despite Amyas's despair, "and especially for our dear brother Mr. Francis Leigh, perhaps captive among the idolaters." So they rose.

"Now, then," said Amyas, "to breakfast. A Frenchman fights best fasting, a Dutchman drunk, an Englishman full, and a Spaniard when the devil is in him—and that's always."

"And good beef and the good cause are a match for the devil," said Cary. "Come down, captain; you must eat too."

Amyas shook his head, took the tiller from the steersman, and bade him go below and fill himself. Will Cary went down and returned in five minutes with a plate of bread and beef and a great jack of ale. He coaxed them down Amyas's throat as a nurse does with a child, then scuttled below again with tears hopping down his face.

Amyas stood still, steering. His face had grown seven years older in the last night. A terrible, set calm rested upon him. Woe to the man who came across him that day!

"There are three of them, you see, my masters," he said as the crew came on deck again. "A big ship forward and two galleys astern of her. The big ship may keep; she is a race ship. If we can but recover the wind of her, we will see whether our height is not a match for her length. We must give her the slip and take the galleys first."

"I thank the Lord," said Yeo, "who has given so wise a heart to so young a general—a very David and Daniel, saving his presence, lads. And if any dare not follow him, let him be as the men of Meroz and of Succoth. Amen! Silas Staveley, smite me—that boy over the head, the young monkey; why is he not down at the powder-room door?"

Yeo went about his gunnery as one who knew how to do it, possessing the most terrible mind to execute it thoroughly and the most terrible faith that it was God's work.

So all fell to. Though there was comparatively little to be done, as the ship had been kept in fighting order all night, there was "clearing of decks, lacing of nettings, making of bulwarks, fitting of waist-cloths, arming of tops, tallowing of pikes, slinging of yards, doubling of sheets and tacks"—enough to satisfy even the pedantic soul of Richard Hawkins himself. Amyas took charge of the poop, Cary of the forecastle, Yeo, as gunner, of the main-deck, while Drew, as master, settled himself in the waist. All was ready, and more than ready, before the great ship was within two miles of them.

Now, while the mastiffs of England and the bloodhounds of Spain neared each other over the rolling surges, thirsting for one another's blood, let us pause to consider the causes that enabled the English to face and conquer armaments immensely superior in size and number. It is a boast that in the whole Spanish War but one queen's ship, the *Revenge*, and one private man-of-war, Sir Richard Hawkins's *Dainty*, ever struck their colors to the enemy.

What enabled Sir Richard Grenville's *Revenge*, in his last fearful fight off the Azores, to endure for twelve hours the attack of eight Spanish squadrons, two of which (three times her own burden) sank at her side? After all her masts were gone and she had been boarded three times without success, she defied to the last the whole fleet of fifty-four sail lying around her, waiting for her to sink "like dogs around the dying forest king."

What enabled young Richard Hawkins's *Dainty*, though half her guns were useless through the carelessness or treachery of the gunner, to maintain for three days a running fight with two Spaniards of equal size, double the weight of metal, and ten times the number of men?

What enabled Sir George Cary's illustrious ship, the *Content*, to fight single-handed from seven in the morning till eleven at night against four great squadrons and two galleys, though her heaviest gun was but one nine-pounder and for many hours she had but thirteen men fit for service?

What enabled, in the very year of which I write, those two "valiant Turkey Merchantmen of London, the *Merchant Royal* and the *Tobie*," with their three small consorts, to cripple off Pantellaria the whole fleet of Spanish galleys sent to intercept them, and return triumphant through the Straits of Gibraltar?

Lastly, what in the fight of 1588 enabled the English fleet to capture, destroy, and scatter that Great Armada, with the loss (but not the capture) of one pinnace and one gentleman of note?

There were more causes than one. The first seemed to lie in the build of the English ships; the second in their superior gunnery and weight of metal; the third (without which the first would have been useless) in the hearts of the English men.

The English ship was much shorter than the Spanish. This, combined with the rig of those days, gave them an ease of maneuvering that utterly confused their Spanish foes. "The English ships in the fight of 1588," says Camden, "charged the enemy with marvelous agility, and having discharged their broadsides, flew forth presently into the deep and leveled their shot directly, without missing, at those great ships of the Spaniards, which were altogether heavy and unwieldy." Moreover, the Spanish fashion in the West Indies, though not in the ships of the Great Armada, was to build their men-of-war flush-decked, or "race" (*razes*), leaving those on deck exposed and open. The English fashion was to heighten the ship as much as possible at stem and stern, both by the sweep of her lines and by stockades ("close fights and cage-works") on the poop and forecastle, giving the men shelter. This was further increased by strong bulkheads ("cobridgeheads") across the main-deck below, dividing the ship into separate forts fitted with swivels ("bases, fowlers, and murderers") and loopholed for musketry and arrows.
