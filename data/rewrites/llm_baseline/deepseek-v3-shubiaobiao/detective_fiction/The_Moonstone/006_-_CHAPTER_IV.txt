I regret to delay you with the musings of an old man drowsing in his chair. A sunlit garden and a dozing servant are hardly the stuff of thrilling narrative, but truth demands its due—and so, with patience, you must bear with me until Mr. Franklin Blake makes his entrance later in the day.

No sooner had my daughter Penelope left me than the clatter of dishes from the servants’ hall announced dinner. As I take my meals alone, I had no business there beyond wishing them all a hearty appetite before settling back into my repose. But just as I stretched my legs, another interruption came barreling toward me—not Penelope this time, but Nancy, the kitchen-maid, her face stormy. Now, as steward of the household, I do not permit sullen looks to pass unchallenged.

"What’s this?" I said, blocking her path. "Turning your nose up at a good meal? Out with it, Nancy."

She tried to sidestep me without reply, so I took firm hold of her ear—a gesture of approval, mind you, for she’s a sturdy lass, and it’s my custom to show favor where it’s due.

"Rosanna’s late again," Nancy snapped. "And I’m sent to fetch her. Always me doing the hard work! Let me by, Mr. Betteredge!"

Rosanna, our second housemaid, was a girl I had a soft spot for (for reasons you’ll soon learn). Seeing Nancy’s temper, I judged it wiser to fetch Rosanna myself and spare her a tongue-lashing.

"Where is she?" I asked.

"Where else? The sands!" Nancy tossed her head. "Had another of her fits this morning and begged for air. I’ve no patience with her nonsense."

"Then keep your patience and your dinner both," I said. "I’ll bring her in."

Nancy—never one to refuse a meal—brightened at once. A pretty girl when she smiles, so I gave her chin a friendly tap (a harmless habit, nothing more) and took up my stick for the walk to the shore.

But before we proceed, you must hear two tales—that of the sands, and that of Rosanna—for both are knotted tight around the mystery of the Diamond. Try as I might to march straight through my account, life insists on tangling the thread. So let us be brief, and soon enough, the heart of the puzzle will be upon us.

Rosanna was the sole newcomer among our staff. Some months prior, my lady had visited a London reformatory, one of those charitable institutions meant to steer wayward women clear of prison’s relapse. The matron, noting my lady’s interest, pointed out Rosanna Spearman and unspooled a tale too wretched to repeat here—misery without purpose serves no one. The short of it? Rosanna had been a thief, though not the kind who plunders thousands behind a desk. The law had seized her, prison had held her, and the reformatory had tried to mend her.

Yet the matron swore—past sins notwithstanding—that the girl was one in a thousand, needing only a chance to prove her worth. My lady, ever a Christian soul, declared then and there: "Rosanna Spearman shall have that chance in my service."

A week later, she joined us. None knew her history save Miss Rachel and myself. My lady, in her wisdom, had consulted me, and I—having adopted the late Sir John’s policy of agreeable concurrence—endorsed the plan heartily. No girl could have asked fairer: decent wages, fair treatment, and now and then a quiet word of encouragement from my lady herself.

To her credit, Rosanna earned it. Frail though she was, prone to those fainting spells, she worked diligently and without complaint. Yet she made no friends among the other maids—save Penelope, who was kind but never close. I cannot say what offense they took. She was plain as a pewter plate, with one shoulder higher than the other. Perhaps it was her silence, her preference for solitary reading over gossip. She never quarreled, never bristled—only held herself apart, civil but firm.

And there was something else. A trace of refinement in her voice or bearing, a shadow of the lady she might have been. The others seized on it at once, unjustly declaring she put on airs.

Now, about those sands. Our house stands high on the Yorkshire coast, ringed by pleasant walks—save one. A quarter-mile tramp through a grim fir plantation spits you out onto the ugliest stretch of shore for miles: a narrow bay bracketed by two jagged spits of rock, the North and South, between which lurks the Shivering Sand—a quicksand that trembles like a living thing when the tide turns. No boats dare near it; no children play there. Even the birds avoid it.

Yet Rosanna, who might have walked anywhere, chose this bleak spot whenever she had leave. She’d sit alone, stitching or reading, as if drawn by some grim enchantment.

So it was there I sought her. No sign in the plantation, but on the beach, she stood in her grey cloak (always worn to mask her crooked shoulder), gazing at the sand and sea. At my approach, she started and turned away—a habit I never tolerate in my staff. I steered her round and saw tears on her face.

My pocket yielded one of the fine handkerchiefs my lady had given me. "Sit," I said, easing onto the slope. "Dry your eyes, then tell me what troubles you."

(Note, young readers: sitting on sand is harder at my age than you’d think.)

Rosanna dabbed her eyes with a shabby bit of cambric but obeyed. She looked wretched, yet composed. Now, the quickest way to comfort a woman is to take her on your knee—but Rosanna was no Nancy, and the moment called for restraint.

"My past haunts me, Mr. Betteredge," she said quietly.

"Then forget it," I urged. "It’s done with."

She plucked at my coat—a grease-stained garment I’m too idle to keep tidy. The day before, she’d cleaned a spot with some new concoction. The stain was gone, but the cloth bore a faint mark where it had been.

"The stain’s gone," she said, "but the place remains."

A sharp observation, and one that silenced me. There was a sadness in her brown eyes, a respect for my untroubled life as something forever beyond her, that weighed on my heart. Failing words, I did the next best thing.

"Help me up," I said. "You’re late for dinner, and I’ve come to fetch you."

"You, sir?"

"They sent Nancy, but I thought you’d take a scolding gentler from me."

Instead of assisting, she squeezed my hand—a small, grateful pressure. She fought back fresh tears and won.

"You’re kind," she murmured. "I’ve no appetite today. Let me stay awhile."

"Why come here, of all places?"

"Something pulls me," she said, tracing shapes in the sand. "I try to stay away, but I can’t. Sometimes"—her voice dropped—"I think my grave waits for me here."

"Roast mutton waits for you inside," I said sternly. "This is what comes of brooding on an empty stomach!"

But she gripped my shoulder, holding me beside her.

"This place has a spell on me," she whispered. "I dream of it. I think of it at my needlework. I’m grateful, truly—I strive to earn your kindness, my lady’s trust. But sometimes… this quiet life feels too pure for what I am. The other maids’ honesty is a reproach. Don’t scold me, sir. I do my work. Only tell my lady I’m not discontent—I’m not. It’s just… my mind frets at times."

Suddenly she pointed to the quicksand. "Look! It’s terrible, wonderful! Always changing, always new."

The tide was turning. The sand shuddered, its surface dimpling like a living skin.

"Do you know what I see?" She clutched my arm. "Hundreds beneath it—suffocating, struggling, sinking deeper. Throw a stone, Mr. Betteredge! Watch the sand swallow it!"

Unhealthy fancy, this. An empty stomach feeding dark thoughts. I was set to rebuke her when a voice from the dunes cut me short.

"Betteredge! Where are you?"

Rosanna sprang up, staring. Before I could rise, her face transformed—flushed, radiant, breathless.

"Who is it?" I asked.

"Oh," she whispered, more to herself than me, "*who* is it?"

I turned. Emerging from the hills was a young gentleman in fawn-colored elegance, gloves and hat to match, a rose in his buttonhole, and a smile that might charm even the Shivering Sand. Before I could stand, he dropped beside me, threw an arm about my neck (foreign manners!), and near crushed the life from me.

"Dear old Betteredge!" cried Mr. Franklin Blake. "I owe you seven-and-six. Now, do you know me?"

Lord save us! Four hours early, here he was!

Before I could speak, his gaze flicked to Rosanna. She flushed deeper, then fled without word or curtsey—most unlike her.

"Odd girl," Mr. Franklin mused. "What surprises her so about me?"

"Perhaps," I jested, "it’s the foreign polish, sir."

I record this exchange as comfort to duller minds: even the cleverest among us miss what’s plain before them. Neither Mr. Franklin, for all his Continental wit, nor I, with my years and sense, grasped Rosanna’s turmoil then. She vanished from our thoughts as her grey cloak disappeared among the dunes.

And why should we have thought of her? you may ask. Read on, and perhaps you’ll grieve for Rosanna Spearman as I did—when at last I understood.
