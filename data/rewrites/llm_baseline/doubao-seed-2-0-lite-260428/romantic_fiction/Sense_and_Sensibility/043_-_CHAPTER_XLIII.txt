Marianne dragged herself out of bed at her usual early hour the next morning, her cheeks flushed and her hands ice-cold, and insisted to every concerned inquiry that she was perfectly well. She even forced herself to pick up her usual embroidery hoop and the volume of poetry she’d been halfway through, determined to prove her words true—but the day that followed did little to back her claims. She sat shivering by the parlour fire for hours, eyes glazing over on lines she could not parse, then dragged herself to the sofa to lie languid and listless, too worn out even to sigh. When she finally stumbled off to bed long before her usual time, clearly growing sicker by the minute, Colonel Brandon could only stare in bewilderment at Elinor’s apparent calm. She had tended Marianne all day against her sister’s grumpy protests, forced her to take the cooling tonics the apothecary had recommended, and clung, just as Marianne did, to the hope that a full night of sleep would set everything right.

That hope shattered entirely when Marianne passed a feverish, restless night, tossing and turning and murmuring incoherently. When she tried to drag herself out of bed at dawn, she had to admit she had not the strength to sit up, and crawled back under the covers of her own accord. Elinor did not hesitate to take Mrs. Jennings’ advice and send for Mr. Harris, the local apothecary the Palmers swore by.

When he arrived, he examined Marianne carefully, and told Elinor he was confident she would recover in a few days—but his offhand mention that her fever trended putrid, and that the disorder was contagious, sent Mrs. Palmer into immediate hysterics for the sake of her infant son. Mrs. Jennings, who had suspected Marianne’s illness was far more serious than Elinor would admit from the very start, confirmed her daughter’s fears at once, and insisted Charlotte leave the house with the baby that very instant. Mr. Palmer teased his wife for being foolish at first, but her anxious pestering wore him down, and within an hour of Mr. Harris’ arrival, Charlotte, her boy, and his nurse were on the road to the home of a Palmer relation a few miles outside Bath. Mr. Palmer promised he would join them in a day or two, at her desperate pleading, and even tried to persuade his own mother to come with him—but Mrs. Jennings would not budge. With a warmth that made Elinor’s throat ache with gratitude, she declared she would not leave Cleveland for as long as Marianne was unwell, determined to step in and care for the girl as if she were the Dashwood sisters’ own mother, far away in Barton. She proved a tireless, helpful nurse, happy to split every late shift with Elinor, and her decades of experience tending to sick household members pulled Elinor through more than one exhausting hour.

Marianne, meanwhile, was too weak and ill to feel anything but dull, universal ache. She had clung to the hope that she would be well enough to leave for Barton at the end of the week, the trip where she and Elinor were meant to surprise their mother at the cottage door the next morning—and that lost prospect made every twinge of her fever feel sharper. She lamented the delay in every quiet, ragged sentence she spoke, even as Elinor assured her their journey home would only be pushed back a few short days, a belief Elinor truly held at that point.

For two days, Marianne’s state barely shifted. She was no worse, but she was no better. Mr. Palmer finally left to join his wife, persuaded at last by Colonel Brandon to keep his promise to her—and the Colonel, who had been on the verge of leaving too to give the two sisters privacy, was stopped in his tracks by Mrs. Jennings’ quick wit. She told him outright that she needed him to stay: every evening, when Elinor was upstairs with Marianne, she would have no one to play piquet with, and she would be bored out of her mind. He did not pretend to demur for long. He had no wish to leave Marianne, ill and unknowing of how deeply he cared for her, and Mr. Palmer was relieved to leave the house knowing there was a steady, level-headed man on the grounds to help Elinor in an emergency.

Marianne never learned that the owners of Cleveland had all but abandoned their home seven days after their arrival for her sake. She never asked after Mrs. Palmer, and her complete indifference to the subject saved Elinor from having to explain it all.

Mr. Harris still insisted Marianne would be well soon, but everyone else’s hope frayed thin fast. Mrs. Jennings declared early on that she was sure Marianne would not recover, and Colonel Brandon, who spent most of his days wandering the grounds alone with his thoughts, could not fight the dark, sinking fear that he would never see Marianne well, never get the chance to tell her how he felt.

For a bright moment on the third day, that fear lifted. Mr. Harris arrived that morning and declared her markedly improved: her pulse was stronger, every symptom trending in the right direction. Elinor’s spirits soared, and she fired off a letter to her mother playing down the severity of the illness, certain Marianne would be well enough to travel home in a matter of days.

But the day did not end as brightly as it began. As dusk fell, Marianne’s fever spiked again. She grew restless, heavy, and flushed far worse than she had been all week. Elinor told herself it was just the exhaustion of sitting up while her bed was remade, gave her the prescribed cordial, and watched her sink into what she thought would be a restorative sleep. She refused to leave her side, determined to watch over her through the whole rest. Mrs. Jennings went to bed unusually early, the house maid who had been helping nurse wandered off to the housekeeper’s room for a snack, and Elinor was entirely alone with her sister.

Marianne’s rest grew more and more disturbed. She tossed from side to side, letting out soft, incoherent little moans, and Elinor was just about to wake her from the clearly painful slumber when a random creak from the hallway made Marianne jolt upright, eyes wild and glassy with fever.

“Is mama coming?” she shrieked.

“Not yet,” Elinor said, tucking the blankets around her and hiding how her own hands were shaking. “But she will be soon, I promise. Barton is a long way away, remember?”

“She must not go through London,” Marianne babbled, thrashing. “I will never see her again if she goes through London.”

Panic coiled tight in Elinor’s chest. She pressed her fingers to Marianne’s pulse, and it was fluttering far too fast, far too faint. She knew instantly she had to send for Mr. Harris at once, and send a rider to Barton to fetch their mother. The first person she thought to turn to for help was Colonel Brandon. She rang for the maid to sit with Marianne, then ran down to the drawing room, where she knew he would still be sitting, long after everyone else had gone to bed.

There was no time for politeness. She laid out all her fears, all her plans, and to her overwhelming relief, he did not waste a single minute. Before she could even finish suggesting he find a reliable man to ride to Barton, he offered to go himself. No persuasion was needed. Elinor thanked him through burning, grateful tears, and while he sent his own servant racing to wake the stables for fresh post horses and send a messenger for Mr. Harris, she scribbled a short, urgent note to her mother.

There could not have been a better person to fetch her. His steady judgement would guide her frantic mother the whole ride back, his quiet kindness would soothe her when she got the terrible news, and Elinor could not imagine a safer, more caring companion for her on that desperate journey. He did not waste a single second. The horses were harnessed even faster than he had expected, and he pressed Elinor’s hand one solemn, wordless time before climbing into the carriage, and disappearing down the drive into the dark night.

It was almost midnight when Elinor went back up to Marianne’s room to wait for the apothecary. The night stretched on for what felt like an eternity: Marianne raved in delirium, calling her mother’s name over and over, and Elinor was torn apart by the thought that she had wasted days playing down Marianne’s illness, that they had waited too long, that her mother would arrive only to find her youngest daughter gone. Mr. Harris did not show up until after five in the morning. Though he was shocked by the sudden turn for the worse, he insisted the danger was not yet fatal, adjusted her treatment, and left Elinor just enough fragile hope to keep her standing.

When Mrs. Jennings woke up and learned what had happened, her old, grim fears came flooding back, and she could not even pretend to offer Elinor hope that Marianne would pull through. She grieved openly for the kind, bright young girl she had grown so fond of over the past three months, and Elinor’s heart ached all the more for it.

Mr. Harris returned that afternoon, but his new medicines had done little to break the fever. Marianne was quieter now, but not lucid, trapped in a heavy, unresponsive stupor. Elinor begged to call in another doctor, but he insisted there was no need, that the next new treatment he planned would work. It was almost noon when Elinor, holding her sister’s wrist, thought she felt a faint, steady thrum in Marianne’s pulse that had not been there an hour before. She waited, watching, checking again and again, too scared to say a word to Mrs. Jennings for fear she would be mistaken. Soon enough, the signs of improvement multiplied: Marianne’s breath slowed and evened out, the flush faded from her cheeks, and she opened her eyes to look directly at Elinor, clear and lucid, though still terribly weak.

By the time Mr. Harris returned at four o’clock, he could do nothing but congratulate her, and declare Marianne entirely out of danger.

Elinor did not laugh, or cheer, or make any loud show of joy. Her relief was quiet, heavy, brimming over with gratitude. She sat by Marianne’s bed the rest of the afternoon, tending to her every small need, watching her sleep peacefully, counting the minutes until her mother and Colonel Brandon would arrive. The night was cold and stormy: wind howled around the eaves, rain lashed against the windows, but Elinor barely noticed. She knew the two travelers would be pushing their horses as hard as they could, and every second dragged by too slow.

At eight o’clock, she heard the distant clatter of hooves on the drive. She ran to the window, and there was the carriage, its lamps flaring bright in the rain, pulled by four fresh, panting horses—they had changed teams again and again, rushing the whole way from Barton, her mother no doubt beside herself with fear.

Elinor did not wait. She left the maid sitting by Marianne, and hurried down the stairs, her legs shaking, desperate to tell her mother that Marianne was safe, that everything would be alright. She burst through the drawing room door, breathless, ready to fling herself into her mother’s arms—

And saw only Willoughby, standing by the fire.
