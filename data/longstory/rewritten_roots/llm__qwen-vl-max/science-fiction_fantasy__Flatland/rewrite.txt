Preface to the Second and Revised Edition, 1884
By the Editor

If my dear friend from Flatland still possessed the sharpness of mind he once had when first writing these Memoirs, I would not now need to speak for him in this preface. But time, imprisonment, and the weight of scorn have dimmed his thoughts—though not his truth. He wishes, through me, to express gratitude to the readers and critics of Spaceland, whose swift embrace of his work has demanded a second edition. He also seeks to apologize for certain errors and misprints (not all of which are his own fault), and to clarify a few misunderstandings that have arisen.

Yet he is no longer the Square he once was. Years of confinement, coupled with the relentless mockery of his peers, have eroded much of what he learned during his brief sojourn in Spaceland. The language, the concepts, even the very memory of three-dimensional reality—these have faded from his grasp. Thus, he has asked me to respond on his behalf to two pressing objections: one intellectual, the other moral.

The first concerns vision. Critics argue that when a Flatlander sees a Line, it must appear *thick* as well as long—otherwise, how could it be seen at all? If thickness exists, they say, then Flatlanders must acknowledge a third dimension: height, however slight. This argument seems convincing to Spacelanders—and it troubled me too, until my friend offered his reply.

“I concede,” he said, “that your critic speaks truly of facts. But he draws false conclusions. Yes, we do possess a third dimension—what I call ‘height’—just as you, in Spaceland, unknowingly inhabit a fourth, unnamed dimension, which I shall call ‘extra-height.’ But we cannot perceive our own ‘height,’ any more than you can grasp your ‘extra-height.’ Even I—who once stood in Spaceland and briefly understood the meaning of depth—cannot now comprehend it. I cannot see it, nor reason it into existence. I know it only by faith.”

He continued: “Dimension implies direction, measurement, variation. But in Flatland, all lines are equally and infinitesimally thick. There is nothing to suggest such a dimension to our senses. No instrument—no matter how precise—could help us measure it, because we lack both the concept and the direction. When we see a line, we see length and brightness. Brightness is essential; without it, the line vanishes. So when I speak of an unseen dimension in a line, my fellow Flatlanders assume I mean brightness. When I insist it is a real dimension, they demand: ‘Measure it. Show us its direction.’ And I cannot. I am silenced.”

He recalled a recent visit from the Chief Circle—the High Priest—who came to inspect the prison. For the seventh year in a row, he asked: “Are you better?” My friend tried to prove that the Chief Circle himself was 'high'—not just long and broad. But the response was instant: “Prove it. Measure my height.” What could he do? He was defeated. And the High Priest left, triumphant.

“Do you still find this strange?” he asked. “Then imagine yourself in a similar plight. Suppose a being from the Fourth Dimension visited you and said: ‘When you open your eyes, you see a plane—two dimensions—and infer a solid—three. But in truth, you also perceive a fourth dimension—real, yet unrecognizable—neither color nor light, but a true extension beyond your comprehension. I cannot show you its direction, nor can you measure it.’ Would you not lock such a visitor away?”

And so, he concludes: “That is my fate. It is as natural for us Flatlanders to imprison a Square who speaks of a third dimension as it is for you to jail a Cube who speaks of a fourth. Alas, how alike we are across dimensions—Points, Lines, Squares, Cubes, Extra-Cubes—all bound by prejudice, all slaves to our own limited worlds. As one of your poets once wrote: *‘One touch of Nature makes all worlds akin.’*”

On this matter, I find the Square’s defense unassailable.

But the second objection—moral in nature—is harder to resolve. Some have accused him of hating women. Given that women constitute the larger portion of Spaceland’s population, this charge has been fiercely pressed. I wish I could fully clear his name. Yet he is unfamiliar with Spaceland’s moral vocabulary, so I must interpret and summarize.

During his seven years in prison, he has reexamined his views. On women, and on the Isosceles—or lower classes—he has softened. Personally, he now leans toward the Sphere’s belief that Straight Lines possess qualities superior to Circles. Yet as a historian, he has aligned himself with the prevailing narratives of Flatland—and, indeed, of Spaceland—where the lives of women and the masses have long been ignored or dismissed.

In a more private note, he disavows the aristocratic leanings some have attributed to him. While acknowledging the intellectual strength of the Circles, who have ruled over countless others for generations, he believes the facts of Flatland speak for themselves: revolutions cannot always be crushed by violence. Nature, by making Circles infertile, has doomed them to eventual extinction. “And herein,” he says, “I see the great Law of all worlds: while human wisdom thinks it shapes destiny, Nature compels it to serve a higher, wiser purpose—one far better than any plan of man.”

Finally, he asks his readers not to expect every detail of Flatland’s life to mirror Spaceland’s. Yet he hopes that, taken as a whole, his tale may inspire thought—as well as amusement—for those among you who, when faced with truths beyond experience, refuse to say either “This can never be” or “It must be exactly thus, and we know all.” Instead, he invites humility, curiosity, and the courage to imagine more.

In the realm where I dwell, all things exist in two dimensions—length and breadth alone. There is no height, no depth, only an endless plane stretching in every direction. This world, known as Flatland, is a vast, flat surface upon which we move, live, and perceive. Every object, every being, is bounded by straight lines or curves that define its shape and status. We are not aware of up or down; our universe unfolds in a single plane, like a map drawn on paper. Yet within this simplicity lies a rigid order—a geometry that governs society, identity, and even morality.

The climate of Flatland is remarkably uniform. The sun, though it appears to rise and fall across our horizon, casts no shadows—its light falls evenly upon all things. The temperature remains constant throughout the year, with only minor fluctuations near the edges of the land. Our houses are built with square or rectangular outlines, their doors and windows carefully designed to avoid accidental collisions. Each home has a front and back, but no roof—since height does not exist, there is no need for one. The walls are thin, allowing sightlines between neighbors, yet privacy is maintained through strict social customs.

We, the inhabitants of Flatland, are geometric figures. The lowest class consists of women—straight lines, sharp and narrow. Next come the working classes: isosceles triangles, whose angles determine their rank. As one ascends, the figures grow more regular: equilateral triangles, squares, pentagons, hexagons—and so on, until we reach the highest caste, the Circles. These noble beings, nearly perfect in form, serve as priests and rulers. The more sides a figure has, the closer it approaches the ideal of a circle, and thus the higher its station in life.

Women in Flatland are straight lines, the simplest of all shapes. Their form makes them both vital and dangerous—so narrow they can easily pierce other figures if not handled with care. Because of this, they are treated with caution and kept apart from men during public gatherings. They have no angles, and therefore no means of distinguishing themselves by shape, making recognition difficult. Their role is largely domestic and reproductive, though their presence is ever-present in the social fabric.

In Flatland, recognition is not achieved through sight alone. Since all figures appear as lines when viewed edge-on, and since our vision is limited to the plane, we rely on touch and sound. A person’s angle, number of sides, and overall length must be felt to determine their identity. We also use voice patterns and movement style. But among the elite, who possess many sides, sight becomes more reliable—especially when viewing others at an oblique angle, where the full form may briefly reveal itself.

Though sight is limited, it is still used for identification, particularly in daylight. When a figure moves across the plane, its silhouette changes subtly depending on the angle. A square seen from the side appears as a line, but when viewed at a slight tilt, reveals its four sides. The more complex the figure, the more intricate its appearance under motion. Thus, experienced citizens can distinguish a hexagon from a heptagon by subtle shifts in outline. But this skill requires training and is rarely mastered outside the upper classes.

Irregular figures are those born with unequal sides or uneven angles. They are considered unnatural, often the result of genetic flaw or disease. Such individuals are shunned, deemed dangerous or unfit for society. In extreme cases, they are executed—not out of cruelty, but to preserve order and prevent chaos. The law demands that any deviation from perfect symmetry be swiftly corrected, for irregularity threatens the very structure of Flatland.

Once, long ago, painting was common in Flatland. Artists would create images using colored lines and shaded areas to suggest depth and form. But these efforts were misleading—paintings could deceive the eye, creating illusions of three-dimensional space. Worse, they hinted at realities beyond our own. Eventually, the practice was banned. Color became forbidden, and art reduced to simple line drawings—only to depict truth, never illusion.

To prevent further confusion, the Universal Colour Bill was passed. It declared that all figures should be monochromatic—colored uniformly in a single hue. This ensured clarity in recognition and prevented deception. No longer could a triangle pretend to be a square by altering its shade. The bill was enforced strictly, and color became a tool of rebellion.

When some dared to defy the law by wearing different colors, a revolt erupted—the Chromatic Sedition. The rebels believed color could express individuality, even spirituality. But the state saw it as a threat to stability. The uprising was crushed swiftly. Leaders were exiled or erased. Afterward, all pigments were confiscated, and the use of color was outlawed forever. Only the priests, in their sacred rituals, were permitted to wear faint hues—but only under strict supervision.

Our priests are the Circles—figures so numerous in side that they appear perfectly round. They are the most intelligent, wise, and respected members of society. They govern, teach, and interpret the laws of Flatland. Their voices carry authority, and their decisions are final. Though they claim to act in the name of reason and order, their power is absolute. Few dare question them, for to do so is to risk being labeled irregular.

The priests teach that Flatland is the entirety of existence. That no dimension beyond two can possibly exist. They say that height is a myth, depth an illusion. To believe otherwise is heresy. They maintain that all knowledge is contained within the plane, and that any attempt to imagine a third dimension is madness. Their doctrine is unshakable, reinforced by tradition, fear, and the suppression of dissent.

One night, while meditating upon the nature of existence, I dreamed of a place called Lineland—a world of one dimension. There, all beings were points moving along a single line. They could see only forward and backward, nothing else. I conversed with the King of Lineland, who could not comprehend my form, nor understand how I existed outside his line. He thought me a miracle, or perhaps a demon. Yet I realized then that just as he could not grasp my world, I might one day fail to grasp a greater reality.

I attempted to describe Flatland to the King of Lineland. I spoke of width, of angles, of figures moving in two directions. But he could not understand. He said such things were impossible. "There is no left or right," he insisted. "Only before and behind." My words fell on deaf ears. I realized then that no matter how clearly I explained, a being confined to one dimension cannot perceive the second. So too, perhaps, might a being like me be unable to perceive a third.

One evening, as I walked in solitude, a strange light appeared above me. From it emerged a being unlike any I had seen—a sphere, glowing with radiance, descending into my world. He spoke in a voice both gentle and commanding. "I come from Spaceland," he said, "a world of three dimensions—length, breadth, and height." I stared, bewildered. Could such a thing be true? I asked him to prove it. But he could not speak plainly. His words were tangled in concepts beyond my understanding.

Section 16. How the Stranger vainly endeavoured to reveal to me in words the mysteries of Spaceland
He tried to explain the third dimension through analogy. "Imagine a point moving through space—it traces a line," he said. "A line moving creates a square. A square moving upward creates a cube." But I could not grasp it. My mind was bound to the plane. I saw no "upward" movement—no height. I argued that such a dimension was impossible. He grew frustrated. "You see only two dimensions," he said. "But I see you whole—your insides, your back, your top and bottom. I see you in fullness."

Seeing my stubbornness, the Sphere ceased speaking. Instead, he began to lift me—rising slowly, carrying me above the plane. At first, I felt no change. Then, suddenly, I saw myself from above—my entire body revealed in a single glance. I saw my interior, my angles, my form complete. I screamed. "This is impossible!" But the Sphere held me steady. "Now you see," he said. "This is what I meant by the third dimension."

After that moment, the Sphere took me to Spaceland. I stepped into a new world—where buildings rose upward, where people moved freely in all directions, where light came from above and below. I saw cubes, pyramids, spheres—shapes I could never have imagined. I looked down upon Flatland and saw it as a mere shadow, a flat drawing on the ground. I understood then that my world was but a slice of a greater reality.

Section 19. How, though the Sphere shewed me other mysteries of Spaceland, I still desired more; and what came of it
The Sphere showed me wonders—cities floating in air, creatures of many forms, worlds beyond worlds. But I could not rest. I longed to know more. I begged to see the fourth dimension, the fifth, the infinite. The Sphere shook his head. "Even I cannot see beyond three," he said. "But you now know what it means to transcend." And so I returned to Flatland, changed forever.

Before I left, the Sphere appeared to me in a dream. "Do not despair," he said. "Though you cannot teach your people, you can inspire. Plant the seed. One day, someone will understand." And with that, he vanished.

Section 21. How I tried to teach the Theory of Three Dimensions to my Grandson, and with what success
I gathered my grandson and told him of Spaceland. I drew diagrams, used analogies, spoke of height and depth. He listened intently, but soon grew skeptical. "How can something exist above us?" he asked. "That is not possible." I tried to show him, but he refused to believe. In the end, he dismissed me as mad. Still, I hope he remembered.

Section 22. How I then tried to diffuse the Theory of Three Dimensions by other means, and of the result
I wrote this account—this story—for those who might one day read it. I hid it, knowing the priests would burn it if they found it. But perhaps, somewhere, a mind will open to the truth. Perhaps, one day, Flatland will awaken. Until then, I wait. For the world is broad and wide.

I call our world Flatland—not because we name it so, but to help you, my readers who dwell in the boundless realm of Space, grasp the true nature of our existence. Picture a vast, endless sheet of paper upon which geometric shapes—Lines, Triangles, Squares, Pentagons, Hexagons, and others—move freely across its surface. Yet they cannot rise above or sink below it, much like shadows cast upon a wall—but solid, with glowing edges. This is the land I know, and the world of my people.

Once, I would have called this my universe. But now, after glimpsing higher dimensions, I see that our world is but a slice of something far greater.

In such a realm, there can be no such thing as what you call “solid” objects. No depth, no volume—only two-dimensional forms sliding across a flat plane. You might imagine that we could at least tell apart the different figures by sight—recognizing a Triangle from a Square, or a Pentagon from a Circle. But you would be mistaken. To us, nothing appears as more than a line.

Let me explain why. Place a penny on your table, then look down at it from above. It appears round. Now slowly lower your eye until it reaches the edge of the table—bringing yourself closer to the perspective of a Flatlander. As you do, the penny will grow oval, then elongated, until finally, when your eye is level with the table’s surface, it becomes indistinguishable from a straight line. The same happens if you hold up a cut-out Triangle or Square: viewed from the side, it too collapses into a mere line.

Take, for instance, an equilateral Triangle—a respectable Tradesman in our society. Figure 1 shows how he looks when seen from above. Figures 2 and 3 depict him as you’d see him from near the table’s edge. And when your eye is perfectly level with the surface—just as we perceive things in Flatland—he vanishes into a single straight line.

While I was visiting Spaceland, I heard sailors speak of similar experiences. When they gaze across the sea at a distant shore, they see only a gray, unbroken line along the horizon—no bays, no headlands, no angles—unless sunlight reveals them through shadow and light. That is precisely how we see one another in Flatland. A friend approaches, and all we see is a growing line; when he departs, the line shrinks. But regardless of whether he is a Triangle, Square, Pentagon, or even a Circle, he remains a line—nothing more, nothing less.

You may wonder how we can possibly recognize each other under such limitations. But that mystery will unfold when I introduce you to the inhabitants of Flatland. For now, let me set that aside and turn briefly to the climate and architecture of our world.

Like your world, ours is marked by four cardinal directions: North, South, East, and West. But unlike you, we have no sun or stars to guide us through the skies. Without celestial bodies, our method of orientation is entirely unique. By a natural law, all matter in Flatland is drawn toward the South—a subtle but constant pull that shapes our lives. In temperate zones, this force is faint—so light that even a healthy woman can walk several furlongs northward without struggle. Yet it’s strong enough to serve as a compass across most of our land.

Rain, which falls at regular intervals, always comes from the North. This steady northerly downpour offers another clue, helping travelers orient themselves. In cities, houses are built with their walls aligned north-south, so roofs can shield against the rain. In rural areas, where no buildings stand, the trunks of trees—growing upright and often aligned with the prevailing forces—offer some direction. Together, these cues make navigation manageable, though not always simple.

Still, in the milder regions where the southern pull is nearly imperceptible, I’ve found myself stranded on endless plains, utterly lost. With no structures or trees in sight, I’ve had to wait hours—sometimes days—for the rain to fall, knowing its arrival would reveal the North. The southward pull affects the weak, elderly, and especially delicate women far more than strong men. Thus, proper etiquette demands that when encountering a lady on the road, one must yield her the northern side of the path. Not always easy, particularly when unwell or in unfamiliar terrain where distinguishing directions is itself a challenge.

Our homes have no windows. Light does not come from any visible source—it is everywhere, constant and unchanging, both day and night, indoors and out. Where it comes from, we do not know. In ancient times, scholars wrestled with this mystery: “What is the origin of light?” Many sought answers, but none succeeded. Instead, they ended up in asylums, driven mad by their own speculations. After repeated failures to curb such inquiries through taxation, the government finally banned them outright. Today, questioning the nature of light is illegal.

And yet—I alone in Flatland—know the truth. I understand the source of light, and how it flows into our world from beyond. But my knowledge cannot be shared. No one here can grasp it. I am mocked, ridiculed, called insane—though I hold the only true understanding of Space, of dimensions, of the hidden world from which light pours into ours. But let me pause this sorrowful tale and return to the subject at hand: our dwellings.

The standard house in Flatland is shaped like a pentagon—five-sided, with two northern sides forming the roof. These roof edges usually lack doors. On the east side, a small entrance serves the women; on the west, a larger one for men. The southern side, the floor, typically has no door at all. Square and triangular homes are forbidden, and for good reason.

The angles of squares—and especially equilateral triangles—are sharper than those of pentagons. And since the lines of inanimate objects, like houses, appear dimmer than those of living beings, there’s real danger. A careless traveler might collide with a sharp corner and suffer serious injury. As early as the eleventh century, triangular houses were outlawed across the land—except for fortresses, armories, barracks, and other state buildings, where such sharpness is intentional and access controlled.

Squares remained legal for centuries, though taxed heavily to discourage their use. But about three hundred years later, laws changed: in towns with over ten thousand inhabitants, only pentagonal homes could be built, due to safety concerns. Public wisdom soon followed. Even in remote villages, pentagonal construction now dominates. Only in the most isolated farming regions might one still find an old square house—rare relics of a forgotten past.

In our world, the largest adult being rarely exceeds eleven of your inches in length or breadth—twelve inches being the absolute maximum. We are a society shaped by geometry, where form determines rank, destiny, and even social worth.

Our women are straight lines—simple, unadorned, and without angles. They are the most basic figures in our realm, yet their very simplicity grants them a unique place in our rigid hierarchy.

The soldiers and lowest laborers among us are isosceles triangles—figures with two equal sides, each about eleven inches long, and a third side so short it often measures no more than half an inch. This tiny base creates a sharp, dangerous vertex—an angle so acute that these men appear nearly indistinguishable from straight lines when viewed from afar. The more degraded among them, whose bases shrink to less than an eighth of an inch, are almost impossible to tell apart from women. These figures are known as Isosceles Triangles, and though they are not entirely devoid of reason, they are considered barely human due to their irregularity.

Above them stands the middle class: equilateral triangles—figures with all three sides equal. Their symmetry marks them as balanced, orderly, and fit for civic life. They serve as tradesmen, clerks, and minor officials.

Next come the professional classes: squares and pentagons. I myself am a square, and thus belong to this respected group. Squares are seen as wise, capable, and honorable—men of thought and action. Pentagons, with five equal sides, are even higher in status, representing greater intellectual and moral refinement.

Beyond them lies the nobility—a tier defined not by birth alone, but by increasing numbers of sides. It begins with hexagons (six-sided figures), then heptagons, octagons, and so on, ascending through ever more complex forms. As the number of sides grows and the length of each diminishes, the figure becomes smoother, more circular. When the sides become so numerous and so small that the shape appears perfectly round, the being is elevated to the highest order: the Circles—or Priests.

This progression is not merely symbolic; it reflects a natural law. A male child inherits one additional side compared to his father. Thus, the son of a square is a pentagon; the son of a pentagon, a hexagon—and so forth, climbing steadily toward the divine circle. This rule ensures that each generation advances in wisdom, virtue, and social standing.

But this law does not apply universally. Among the lower classes—especially soldiers and artisans—their offspring often remain unchanged. Since their sides are unequal, they cannot follow the path of upward evolution. An isosceles triangle will typically produce another isosceles triangle. Yet hope persists—even for the lowliest among us.

Through prolonged effort, discipline, and careful breeding, some families within the lower ranks gradually improve. Diligent work, military valor, or intellectual cultivation may lead to subtle changes: a slight broadening of the base, a softening of the apex. Over generations, these improvements accumulate. With the guidance of the Priestly class, intermarriages between such promising individuals can yield offspring closer to the ideal equilateral triangle.

Rarely—so rarely that it stirs national celebration—does a truly regular equilateral triangle emerge from isosceles parents. Such a birth is cause for joy across many furlongs. After rigorous inspection by the Sanitary and Social Board, the infant is formally certified. If deemed Regular, he is ceremonially welcomed into the equilateral class.

Then comes a solemn act: separation. The child is taken from his parents—proud yet grieving—and adopted by a childless equilateral. By oath, this adoptive parent must never allow the child to return home or see his kin again. For fear that the influence of his original environment might weaken his new form, corrupting his development through unconscious imitation.

Such rare ascents are cherished not only by the poor, who see in them a flicker of hope amid their drudgery, but also by the elite. The aristocracy understands that while these exceptions do not threaten their power, they serve as a powerful safeguard against revolution. Had every isosceles been utterly hopeless, the masses might have risen with fury, led by bold and cunning minds. But nature has devised a clever balance.

As the lower classes grow wiser, more skilled, and more virtuous, their acute angles—once weapons of terror—begin to soften. The sharper the mind, the duller the point. The most brutal soldiers, though fierce and deadly, lose their penetrating edge as they gain intelligence. Their physical threat diminishes just as their mental capacity grows. This is the Law of Compensation—a perfect harmony, proof of the natural and divine order of our society.

The ruling Polygons and Circles exploit this law wisely. When unrest stirs, they act swiftly. Some rebellious leaders are subtly reshaped—compressed or expanded by state physicians—until they become regular enough to be admitted into the privileged classes. Others, still flawed but hopeful, are lured into State Hospitals, where they live in comfort under lifelong supervision. Only the most stubborn, irregular, or foolish face execution.

Then, the rest—the rabble—are left leaderless and disoriented. Either they are swiftly subdued by a loyal force of paid isosceles troops maintained by the Chief Circle, or—more commonly—they are turned against one another. Through carefully sown distrust and jealousy, the Circular party incites civil conflict. And so, they destroy themselves, cut down by their own angles.

History records no fewer than one hundred twenty major uprisings, plus two hundred thirty-five smaller revolts. Not one has succeeded. Each ended in silence, blood, and the quiet triumph of order.

Thus, our world endures—not by force alone, but by design, by law, by the gentle yet unyielding logic of geometry itself.

In Flatland, where geometry defines both form and fate, the most perilous beings are not the soldiers or the nobles, but the women. While a Soldier-Triangle is sharp and dangerous at its apex, a woman is far more so—she is a line with two points, a needle of pure extremity. And she possesses a power beyond mere shape: the ability to vanish from sight at will. This makes her, in our world, a force neither to be ignored nor trifled with.

To understand this invisibility, imagine placing a needle upon a table. View it from the side, and you see its full length. But turn your gaze directly toward one end, and all you perceive is a tiny point—so small, so faint, that it might as well not exist. That is precisely how a woman appears to us. When her side faces us, we see a straight line, clear and unmistakable. But when she turns her front—a single point housing both eye and mouth, for in Flatland these organs are one—we see only a gleaming dot. And when her back is turned, her dim, almost lifeless rear becomes like an invisible cap, shielding her presence entirely.

This is why collisions with women are not merely accidents—they are disasters. A man might survive a brush with a working-class Triangle, though he may bleed. A soldier’s angle can wound deeply. But a woman? To meet her in motion is to invite instant annihilation. Her stinger-like tip can pierce through flesh and bone without hesitation. And since she can become nearly invisible, even the most careful traveler risks disaster. The laws of Flatland have long sought to mitigate this danger, especially in regions where gravity pulls harder and movement is less controlled.

Here are the core rules governing women in many of our states:

1. Every home must have a separate eastern entrance for women only. They must enter “in a becoming and respectful manner,” never using the western door reserved for men.
2. No woman may walk in public without continuously shouting her Peace-Cry, under penalty of death.
3. Any woman suffering from involuntary movements—such as St. Vitus’s Dance, seizures, or violent sneezing—must be destroyed immediately.

Some states add further restrictions: requiring women to sway their backs constantly to signal their presence, mandating that they be followed by a son or servant when traveling, or confining them entirely to their homes except during religious festivals. But over time, even the wisest Circles—the highest thinkers and rulers—have realized that too many laws breed more harm than good. Over-restricting women leads to resentment, which often erupts into sudden, devastating violence. Entire villages have been wiped out in moments when enraged women, denied freedom, turned on their families.

Thus, the Three Laws above are generally sufficient for well-ordered societies. Yet the real protection lies not in law, but in nature and custom. Though a woman can kill instantly with a backward thrust, she cannot withdraw her stinger if her victim resists. In such struggles, her fragile body is easily shattered. So, despite their lethal potential, they are not invincible.

And then there is fashion—our silent ally. In civilized lands, no lady of standing walks without swaying her back gently from side to side. This has become a mark of refinement, passed down through generations. The wives of high-ranking Circles move with rhythm and grace; those of Equilateral Triangles manage only a monotonous swing, like a pendulum ticking. Even lower-class Isosceles women mimic this motion, not because they need it, but because it is expected.

In every household of status, this “back-motion” is as natural as breathing. It keeps husbands safe from surprise attacks. And once inside their chambers—designed specifically to prevent turning—women lose all power. Their rage fades quickly. They forget insults, promises, threats. Minutes later, they may ask what happened, unaware of their own fury.

Yet this does not mean women lack affection. They do love—but their emotions are raw, unfiltered, ruled by impulse. This is not weakness of character, but consequence of design. With no angles to speak of—worse even than the lowest Isosceles—they possess no brainpower. They think not, judge not, remember not. In moments of anger, they know no bounds, recognize no kinship. I’ve seen a woman slaughter her entire family, then, calm again, inquire where her husband and children had gone.

So caution is key. Never provoke a woman when she can turn. But lock her in her room—where rotation is impossible—and you may say anything, do anything. She will soon forget. The wisdom of our upper classes rests on this balance: safety over comfort, order over harmony.

Still, even in the best households, peace is rare. The wife of a Circle or Polygon always faces forward, eyes and mouth fixed on her husband and his guests. To turn her back would be scandalous, a loss of dignity. So the stream of conversation flows endlessly—sharp, constant, relentless. There is no silence. Not even light is so persistent as the voice of a woman in Flatland.

And here lies a cruel irony: while her sting is deadly, her tongue is worse. For though she has nothing meaningful to say, she says it anyway—without wit, without conscience, without restraint. Some men, weary of endless chatter, have confessed they’d rather risk the silent, lethal thrust than endure the noise.

To you in Spaceland, this may seem tragic. And indeed, it is. Men—even the humblest Isosceles—may hope for improvement, for evolution, for a future where their angles grow broader, their minds sharper. But not women. Once a woman, always a woman. Nature has sealed their fate. Evolution seems to pause for them, as if the universe itself refuses to grant them advancement.

Yet perhaps there is mercy in this. Since they cannot hope, they cannot mourn. Without memory, they do not recall past pain. Without foresight, they do not fear the future. Their existence is narrow, harsh, and brief—but they are spared the burden of reflection. And so, in a strange way, they are free. Not free to rise, but free from regret. A terrible freedom, perhaps. But still, a kind of peace.

To you, who dwell in the realm of three dimensions—where light and shadow dance across form, where depth reveals structure, and where your two eyes grant you perspective and color—I must explain how utterly strange and difficult recognition is in our world of Flatland. In your world, a circle is a circle, a triangle a triangle—seen whole, at once, from every angle. But here, all things appear as lines—mere segments of straight edges—no matter their true shape. Imagine trying to distinguish a king from a peasant when both look like nothing more than a single line drawn across your vision. That is our daily reality.

So how do we tell one being from another? The answer lies not in sight, but in other senses—especially hearing, touch, and inference.

First, there is **hearing**. Among us, the sense of sound is far more refined than yours. We can identify individuals by voice alone—not just friends, but even social classes. For example, an Equilateral Triangle, a Square, or a Pentagon each has a distinct vocal timbre, pitch, and rhythm. This works well among the lower ranks. But as we rise in status, voices grow more alike, and aristocrats rarely cultivate the skill of voice-discrimination. Worse, some of the lower orders—particularly Isosceles Triangles—can mimic higher voices with alarming accuracy. A trained Isosceles might feign the tone of a Polygon, even a Circle. So while hearing helps, it cannot be trusted in matters of importance.

Hence, we turn to **touch**—our most reliable method. Among women and commoners, feeling is the standard way to recognize another’s class. It is not unlike what you call “introduction,” but instead of exchanging names, we exchange physical contact. A formal greeting might go: *“Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by my friend Mr. So-and-so.”* In rural areas, this full phrase is still used. In cities, it’s shortened to *“Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so,”* implying mutual touch. And among the younger, more fashionable elite, language has become so corrupted that “to feel” now means “to introduce for the purpose of feeling.” You may hear someone say: *“Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones.”* Such slang shocks the old guard—but it reflects the urgency of efficiency in modern life.

Yet touching is not as slow or clumsy as you might think. Through years of schooling and practice, we learn to identify shapes instantly by touch. A single angle—a corner—is enough to determine whether a person is a Triangle, Square, or Pentagon. Even the sharp, brainless tip of an Isosceles is unmistakable. Only among the highest nobility does uncertainty remain. A Master of Arts might confuse a ten-sided with a twelve-sided Polygon; a Doctor of Science could hesitate between twenty and twenty-four sides. Precision becomes nearly impossible beyond a certain point.

But caution is essential. Touching carries risk. The process demands stillness. If the person being felt moves—even slightly—the consequences can be dire. A sudden jerk, a sneeze, a shift in posture… these have caused fatal accidents. Especially among lower-class Triangles, whose eyes are far from their apex, they often don’t know what’s happening at their tip. Their nature is coarse, insensitive to the delicate touch of a noble Polygon. An involuntary twitch has, on occasion, ended a life—and a family’s hopes.

I recall my grandfather, a near-regular Isosceles who earned four out of seven votes to ascend to the Equal-sided class before his death. He once told me of a tragic event in our lineage. His great-great-great-grandfather, a decent working man with a brain-angle of 59°30′, suffered from rheumatism. While being felt by a Polygon, he suddenly jolted—and pierced the noble through the diagonal. The resulting punishment was severe: long imprisonment, public shame, and moral devastation across the family. Our ascent stalled. The next generation had only 58°—a half-degree lost. It took five generations to recover. All because of one moment of movement during a routine identification.

Now I imagine some of you asking: *“How can you possibly know about angles? You see only lines—how can you perceive degrees?”*
The answer is: we don’t *see* angles—we *infer* them. Our sense of touch, honed by necessity and education, allows us to measure angles with greater precision than your unaided eye. We can distinguish differences smaller than a degree—far finer than any human eye can without instruments.

And nature aids us. There is a natural law: the brain-angle of an Isosceles begins at 30 minutes (half a degree) and increases by half a degree per generation—until 60° is reached, at which point the individual rises from serfdom into the Regular class. Thus, nature provides us with a living scale—an alphabet of angles—from half a degree up to 60°. These specimens are kept in schools across Flatland.

In every elementary school, children learn by touching real-life examples—living beings whose angles are fixed and known. Most come from the lowest classes: those with angles of 1° or less. They are deprived of civic rights, often too dim-witted for war, and thus serve as educational tools. Chained immovably, they stand silent in classrooms while young students learn to feel and judge angles. Some regions feed them for months; others replace them monthly—since without food, they die quickly, and their angles remain precise. In poorer schools, feeding saves money but risks dulling the specimen’s shape over time. In wealthier ones, the cost is justified by better results—and by the slight reduction in the overcrowded Isosceles population, a concern every statesman takes seriously.

Though some School Boards favor the cheaper method, I believe the expense is worth it. Accuracy, safety, and social order depend on it.

So, though touching is not without danger, it remains far more trustworthy than hearing. Still, even this method has its limits. For that reason, especially among the higher classes, a third method is preferred—one I will describe in the next section.

I am about to appear inconsistent, and I must confess it at once. Earlier in this narrative, I stated that all figures in Flatland appear as straight lines to the eye—and from that observation, I concluded that visual distinction between individuals was impossible. Yet now I shall explain how, in fact, we do recognize one another through sight. The resolution lies not in contradiction, but in nuance.

Let me clarify: while it is true that every figure presents itself as a line when viewed directly, this does not mean that all lines are indistinguishable. That principle holds only among the lower classes—those who lack both education and opportunity. Among the higher ranks, and particularly in our temperate zones, recognition by sight is not only possible but highly refined. And the key to this ability? Fog.

To you, inhabitants of Spaceland, fog may seem a nuisance—a shroud that blurs landscapes, dulls spirits, and weakens health. But to us, it is something far greater: a gift almost equal to air itself. It is the nurse of science, the mother of geometry, the foundation upon which our most advanced knowledge rests. Without fog, all lines would appear equally clear, sharp, and identical—rendering sight useless for discrimination. But because fog exists—because it thickens with distance—it creates gradations of brightness and dimness that reveal shape, angle, and rank.

Imagine two figures approaching you: one an Equilateral Triangle (a Merchant), the other a Pentagon (a Physician). At first glance, both appear as straight lines. But if you position your eye so that your line of vision bisects the angle of the approaching figure, you will perceive more than just a line—you will see a gradient.

With the Triangle, the center point will glow brightly, being closest to your eye. From there, the line fades rapidly into dimness on either side, because the sides recede sharply into the fog. The ends—what appear as the figure’s extremities—will be nearly invisible. This rapid fading signals a sharp angle: a Triangle.

Now observe the Pentagon. Its central point will also shine, but the transition from bright to dim is much slower. The sides of the Pentagon slope outward less steeply, so they fade gradually into the mist. The extremities remain comparatively brighter. This softer gradient reveals a broader form—a Polygon of higher rank.

This method, though simple in theory, requires years of training. A child might grasp the principle quickly, but mastering it demands constant practice. In real life, the challenges multiply. If my Father, a Triangle, turns sideways instead of facing me with his angle, he may appear momentarily as a straight line—indistinguishable from a Woman. Only after rotation or careful movement can his true nature be confirmed.

And consider a Hexagon—my own grandson. When viewed head-on along one of its sides, I see a long, uniformly bright line flanked by two shorter, dimmer lines that fade toward their ends. These represent the adjacent sides, receding into the fog. The pattern is unmistakable—but only to one trained in the art.

In crowded settings—such as a ballroom or social gathering—the complexity grows exponentially. Figures move, rotate, advance, retreat. Some face you; others turn away. The air hums with shifting gradients, flickering brightnesses, and fleeting shadows. To navigate such scenes without error requires not merely knowledge, but instinct—developed through years of disciplined study.

At the University of Wentbridge, this art is taught rigorously. Students learn both static and dynamic recognition—how to interpret shapes at rest and in motion. The curriculum is demanding, and only the elite afford it. Even I, a mathematician of some standing and grandfather to two fine hexagonal heirs, find myself occasionally bewildered in a throng of high-ranking Polygons.

For the common Tradesman or Serf, such refinement is beyond reach. Their lives depend on immediate practicality, not abstract thought. They rely on touch—on feeling—to determine rank. And while this grants them early agility and alertness, it also limits their development. Like the deaf who never learn to read lips, those who grow up relying on "Feeling" never fully master the subtler, more powerful sense of "Seeing."

Thus, among the upper classes, Feeling is discouraged—even forbidden. Children are sent not to public schools, where Feeling is taught, but to exclusive seminaries where Sight Recognition is cultivated from infancy. At the university, to "feel" is considered a grave offense—punishable by rustication for the first offense, expulsion for the second.

The result is a stark divide. The lower classes, untrained in visual discernment, remain locked in a world of crude approximation. The upper classes, though slow to develop initially, surpass them in maturity, precision, and intellectual depth once properly educated.

Yet not all rise to the occasion. A small number fail the Final Examination at the University. These unfortunate souls are rejected by the higher orders and scorned by the lower. They possess neither the cultured insight of the educated nor the natural dexterity of the working class. They are cast adrift—without profession, without status, without hope.

History shows that many of the great rebellions of our past were led by such men—disgraced nobles, failed scholars, misfits of society. The harm they cause is profound, and increasingly, voices call for reform. Some argue that mercy demands their elimination—either through lifelong imprisonment or painless termination—lest they breed further disorder.

But I digress. This matter of Irregularity, though vital, deserves its own section. For now, let it stand: sight recognition is not mere perception—it is an art, a science, a mark of civilization. And like all such arts, it is born not in talent alone, but in discipline, environment, and the courage to see beyond the surface.

Throughout the preceding pages, I have assumed—though perhaps this should have been established from the outset as a foundational principle—that every inhabitant of Flatland is a Regular Figure. By "Regular," I mean that each being is constructed with perfect symmetry: a Woman must be not merely a line, but a straight one; an Artisan or Soldier must possess two equal sides; a Tradesman, three; a Lawyer (to which humble rank I belong), four; and so on, with all Polygons having their sides uniformly matched in length. The size of these sides naturally varies with age—a newborn Female might measure an inch, while a mature Woman could stretch to a foot. Among males, the combined length of an adult’s sides typically amounts to about two feet. But it is not size that matters here—it is equality.

This uniformity of form is not mere convention; it is the very bedrock upon which our society stands. If sides were unequal, then angles would differ too. Instead of needing only a single glance or touch to identify a person's shape, we would be forced to examine every angle individually. Such a system would render social interaction impractical, if not impossible. The entire science of sight recognition—the art of identifying others at a glance—would collapse. Feeling, once a refined skill, would become a tedious chore. Communication would falter, trust would vanish, and daily life would descend into chaos. Civilization itself would crumble back into primal disorder.

Is this exaggeration? Consider a simple scene: you encounter several Tradesmen in the street, instantly recognizing them by their familiar angles and gently fading edges. You invite them to lunch in your home—an act performed with complete confidence, for everyone knows roughly how much space a grown Triangle occupies. Now imagine one of them dragging behind his noble vertex a distorted, lopsided appendage stretching twelve or thirteen inches across. What then? Could such a figure even pass through your doorway? Would your house become a trap?

I do not belabor this point lightly. It is clear to any rational mind that without regularity, existence in Flatland becomes untenable. Even now, navigating a crowd demands precision and care; if no one could predict the dimensions of another, collisions would be constant, panic inevitable, and harm widespread—especially if Women or Soldiers were involved, whose sharp angles could inflict real injury.

Thus, both nature and practical necessity favor regularity. Our laws reflect this truth. In Flatland, “Irregularity of Figure” is not simply a physical flaw—it carries moral weight, akin to criminality or moral decay in your world. Those who deviate are treated accordingly.

Some reformers argue that geometric irregularity does not imply moral corruption. They claim that Irregulars are victims of prejudice: scorned by family, ignored by servants, shunned by society, denied opportunity, and confined to menial roles under strict surveillance. Once they reach adulthood, they face inspection—if found beyond acceptable deviation, they are destroyed; otherwise, they are relegated to low-level government work, barred from marriage, forced into drudgery, and monitored even during rest. “No wonder,” say these voices, “such lives breed bitterness and vice.”

Yet such arguments fail to persuade me—or indeed, the wisest leaders among us. Our ancestors wisely decreed that tolerance of Irregularity endangers the state. Yes, the life of an Irregular is harsh—but the stability of the many demands sacrifice from the few. Suppose a creature existed with a triangular front and a polygonal rear. How would we build homes, churches, or public spaces? Should ticket clerks measure every citizen’s perimeter before allowing entry? Could Irregulars serve in the military? And if they did, how could we prevent them from disrupting ranks or causing disaster?

Moreover, what temptations would such beings face? Imagine a man entering a shop with his polygonal side forward, appearing respectable, then ordering goods freely—exploiting trust for personal gain. These are not hypothetical dangers; they are real risks.

Let those who champion misplaced compassion speak all they wish. I have never met an Irregular who was not also, in spirit, a hypocrite, a misanthrope, and a source of mischief within limits. Not that I support the harshest measures—some states destroy infants whose angles deviate by half a degree. That is extreme. We know that even great minds—men of genius—have shown early deviations as large as forty-five minutes. To eliminate them would be a tragedy for the nation.

Medicine, too, has made remarkable strides. Through careful compression, extension, trepanning, dietary control, and other techniques, many cases of Irregularity have been corrected—sometimes fully, sometimes partially. Therefore, I advocate a middle path. Let us not draw rigid lines. Instead, when a child’s frame begins to set, and medical experts conclude recovery is unlikely, let us mercifully and painlessly end their suffering. This is neither cruelty nor fanaticism—it is wisdom. For in preserving order, we preserve civilization.

If my readers have kept pace with me thus far, they will not be startled to learn that life in Flatland, while rich in intellectual and political drama, is notably barren when it comes to beauty and artistic expression. I do not deny that our world teems with conflict—battles, betrayals, shifting alliances, and the constant interplay between geometry and fate—all of which lend a certain vitality to our history. Nor would I minimize the thrill derived from solving mathematical puzzles in real time, where every conjecture can be tested instantly by sight or touch. But this is not art. This is logic.

From an aesthetic standpoint, our existence is profoundly dull. Imagine a world where all vision resolves into a single line—no depth, no perspective, only variations in brightness and shadow. Every landscape, every portrait, every still life, reduces to a flat trace upon the retina. What room is there for imagination? For emotion? For wonder?

Yet once, long ago, such limitations did not define us. According to ancient tradition, for several centuries our ancestors lived amid a radiant splendor unknown to our age. The story begins with a single individual—a Pentagon whose name has been lost to time, though most records call him Chromatistes. By accident, he discovered how to mix pigments and apply them to surfaces. At first, he painted his home. Then his servants. Then his family. And finally, himself.

The effect was immediate and profound. Wherever Chromatistes moved, he commanded attention. His body, now adorned with vivid hues, no longer required tactile identification. No one mistook his front for his back. Neighbors could discern his shape at a glance, without laborious calculation. Crowds parted instinctively before him. He no longer needed to shout to make himself known—his colors spoke for him.

The fashion spread like wildfire. Within days, every Square and Triangle in the region followed suit. A few stubborn Pentagons resisted, but soon even the Dodecagons succumbed. In less than a year, nearly all citizens had embraced color—except two groups: the Women and the Priests.

Nature, it seemed, drew a line here. The argument among reformers went: "Distinction of sides implies distinction of color." It was a clever justification, repeated in every town, convincing entire populations to paint themselves. But for Women—whose form consists of a single straight side—they had no sides at all. And for Priests, who claimed to be true Circles (not merely polygons with countless tiny sides), their boundary was a continuous curve—a circumference, not a series of edges. Thus, they argued, they were exempt from the rule.

So while the rest of society adorned itself in dazzling palettes, the Women and Priests remained untouched by pigment—pure, unadorned, and in the eyes of many, more noble for it.

Though the movement was mocked as immoral, chaotic, and unscientific, its artistic legacy remains undeniable. Those were the golden days—the dawn of visual culture in Flatland. To live was to see, and to see was to marvel. Even a simple gathering became a feast for the senses. Church services overflowed with vibrant hues so intense they distracted the finest minds. Theatre performances shimmered with chromatic energy. But nothing rivaled the spectacle of war.

Picture, if you can, twenty thousand Isosceles soldiers turning en masse, revealing bright orange and purple flanks against their dark bases. The militia of Equilateral Triangles marching in red, white, and blue. Square artillerymen rotating near crimson cannons, their bodies flashing mauve, ultramarine, gamboge, and burnt umber. Pentagons and Hexagons streaking across the field in five or six distinct colors, serving as surgeons, geometers, and aides-de-camp.

It was said that one great Circle, commanding such forces, was so overwhelmed by their visual harmony that he cast aside his baton and crown, declaring he would henceforth wield only the artist’s brush.

And language itself reflected this sensory richness. The words of that era pulsed with deeper meaning, imbued with color and rhythm. Our finest poetry, our most lyrical scientific discourse, traces its roots to those vanished times. They were not just painted; they were illuminated.

But like all revolutions, this one faded. Color fell out of favor—too dangerous, too disruptive. Order returned, but at the cost of beauty. And so, today, we live in silence and monotony, remembering a past that dazzled but never endured.

By the time the new era of chromatic revolution dawned, the intellectual foundations of Flatland had begun to crumble. The once-honored art of Sight Recognition—once essential for distinguishing shapes and social ranks—had fallen into disuse. With color now the sole identifier, the need to discern angles by sight vanished, and so too did the discipline it required. Geometry, Statics, Kinetics, and all related sciences were soon dismissed as outdated luxuries, even at our most prestigious University. In elementary schools, the tactile method of Feeling, once taught to every child, was abandoned as obsolete.

The Isosceles class, long oppressed and marginalized, seized this moment. They declared that the old system of using Criminal specimens for educational purposes was no longer necessary—and refused to pay the customary tribute. Freed from this burden, which had once tempered their aggression and controlled their numbers, they grew bolder and more numerous, their confidence swelling with each passing year.

Meanwhile, Soldiers and Artisans began to assert their equality with the highest Polygons. Now equipped with color-based recognition, they could navigate life’s challenges just as well as any noble. Why, then, should they be denied the same rights? They demanded not only the end of aristocratic privileges but the abolition of all state-funded education in Sight Recognition, Mathematics, and Feeling—calling these “monopolizing arts” the tools of an unjust elite.

Their movement gained momentum. If color, a gift of nature itself, had erased the need for hierarchy, why should law uphold it? They insisted that all citizens—regardless of shape or class—be treated as equals under the new order. And when the higher classes hesitated, the revolutionaries escalated their demands: everyone, including Priests and Women, must be painted.

When objections arose—particularly that Priests and Women had no sides—the leaders retorted that nature dictated distinction: every being should have a front half (containing eye and mouth) distinguishable from the back. Thus, they proposed a sweeping reform before a grand assembly of all Flatland states: every Woman would be painted red on her front half and green on her rear. Priests, similarly, would bear red on the semicircle containing their eye and mouth, and green on the opposite side.

This proposal, though seemingly simple, was masterfully designed—not by any Isosceles, but by an Irregular Circle spared from childhood destruction due to misplaced mercy. This figure, possessing both cunning and ambition, saw in color not just a tool of identity, but a weapon of subversion.

For one thing, the plan would win over women across all classes. By assigning them the same colors as Priests, the reform ensured that under certain viewing angles, a Woman might appear indistinguishable from a Priest. The prospect of being mistaken for a revered spiritual leader—of commanding respect, hearing secrets, even issuing orders in a Circle’s name—was irresistible to many women. At home, they would eavesdrop on private councils; in public, they would walk with the dignity of nobility, drawing deference from strangers who mistook them for Circles.

Let me clarify this danger for those who may doubt it. Imagine a woman painted as prescribed: red in front, green behind. Viewed from the side, she appears as a straight line, half red, half green. Now consider a Priest, whose front semicircle is red and back green, divided by diameter AB. When viewed along that line, his outline forms a straight segment—half red, half green—identical in color pattern to the woman’s. Though shorter and tapering more sharply, the visual impression would be nearly the same. Especially given the fading skill of Sight Recognition, and the likelihood that women would learn to mimic the rounded edges of Circles, confusion would be inevitable.

And so, the Color Bill threatened to blur the sacred boundaries between genders and classes. A young Woman might pass as a Priest, gaining influence she had never before possessed. Meanwhile, the Circles—already weakened by the decline of intellectual rigor—would suffer disgrace if the reckless behavior of women were attributed to them. The very foundation of the Constitution would tremble.

Yet even within noble households, women supported the bill. For them, it promised power and prestige.

But the true aim of the bill went deeper. It sought not merely to elevate women, but to dismantle the intellectual superiority of the Circular class. While society decayed, the Circles remained untouched—raised in homes without color, trained in pure Sight Recognition from infancy. Their minds stayed sharp, their judgment clear. They alone preserved the ancient wisdom.

Now, the Irregular Circle schemer sought to destroy that advantage. By forcing Circles to adopt color, he aimed to pollute their purity, corrupt their training, and weaken their intellect. Once stained by chromatic patterns, father and son, mother and child would lose clarity in perception. The infant Circle would struggle to tell parent from parent—a task once sharpening its mind, now clouded by deception. Mothers might feign identity, undermining trust and logic itself.

Thus, step by step, the brilliance of the Priestly Order would fade. And when the mind of the elite dimmed, the way would be open for total revolution. The Aristocracy would fall. The Privileged Classes would vanish. And Flatland, once ruled by reason and form, would descend into chaos—guided not by vision, but by hue.

For three long years, the demand for the Universal Colour Bill raged across Flatland like a fever in the blood of the nation. The air trembled with unrest, and every corner of the realm seemed poised on the brink of collapse. Anarchy, once a distant whisper, now loomed as a living threat. In the final days of that turbulent era, a vast army of Polygons—nobles who had taken up arms as common soldiers—was crushed beneath the overwhelming might of the Isosceles Triangles. The Squares and Pentagons, ever cautious, stood aside, their neutrality sealing the fate of the defenders. Worse still, even the highest echelons of society were not spared. Among the Circles—the ruling class—many noble households were torn apart by the fury of political passion. Wives, consumed by rage over their husbands’ refusal to support the Bill, wore them down with ceaseless pleas. When those pleas failed, they turned to violence: some slaughtered their children and spouses alike, then ended their own lives in a final, tragic act of defiance. It is said that twenty-three Circles met their end this way during the three-year struggle.

The crisis was absolute. The Priests, guardians of order, faced only two choices: surrender or annihilation. But just when all hope seemed lost, an event of astonishing simplicity and grotesque irony shifted the course of history—an event so vivid, so emotionally charged, that it could have been forged by a master strategist. For such moments, though born of chaos, possess a power far beyond reason: they grip the hearts of the people and reshape the soul of a nation.

It began with a lowly Isosceles, barely four degrees above the threshold of intelligence, who—while looting a merchant’s shop—came into contact with a set of pigments. Whether he painted himself or was painted by others remains uncertain; the tale varies. But what is clear is that he adorned his body with the twelve distinct hues of a Dodecagon—a mark of rank and distinction reserved for the most elite. Disguised, he entered the Market Place and approached a young maiden, daughter of a noble Polygon, whom he had once courted in vain. With lies, deception, and a string of improbable coincidences, he won her hand. Her family, blinded by negligence and misplaced trust, failed to detect the fraud until it was too late. When she discovered the truth, she took her life.

News of her death spread like wildfire. From one state to another, women reacted with horror. The tragedy struck at the heart of their deepest fears: that marriage, honor, identity—all would become meaningless if colour were allowed to flourish. What had once been seen as progress now appeared as a curse. Women, once indifferent or even sympathetic to the cause, now turned against it. Many declared themselves enemies of the Bill outright; the rest needed only a spark to ignite their resistance.

Seizing the moment, the Circles summoned an extraordinary Assembly. They brought not only the usual guard of Convicts but also a large contingent of conservative Women, carefully selected for their influence. The hall was packed—more than a hundred thousand Isosceles filled the space, roaring with fury. At the center stood Pantocyclus, the Chief Circle of the age. He was greeted not with reverence, but with jeers and hoots. Yet he raised his voice, calm and commanding, and declared that the Circles would no longer resist. They would yield—to the will of the majority. The Colour Bill would be accepted.

A roar erupted—then, instantly, transformed into thunderous applause. The mood had changed. Chromatistes, the leader of the rebellion, was called forward to receive the submission of the Hierarchy. Then Pantocyclus delivered a speech that lasted nearly a full day—so eloquent, so calculated, that no summary can capture its full weight. With apparent fairness, he laid out both sides of the debate. He acknowledged the appeal of reform, but slowly, inexorably, he revealed its hidden costs.

He warned of the dangers to Tradesmen, Professionals, and Gentlemen—how colour would blur distinctions, how it would erode social order. He reminded the Isosceles that, despite their flaws, the Circles were willing to accept the Bill *if* it passed. But he made plain that the rest of society—artisans, workers, nobles—were not convinced. Their silence spoke volumes.

Then he turned to the Workmen. “Your future,” he said, “must not be sacrificed.” Many of you are near promotion to Regular Triangles. Others dream of better lives for your children. But if colour becomes universal, all differences vanish. Irregularity will be mistaken for Regularity. Progress will turn to decay. Within generations, the Workman may fall below the Military, even the Convict Class. Power will pass to the many—the Criminals—who already outnumber you. And soon, they will surpass all others combined, if nature’s laws are ignored.

A murmur rose among the Artisans. Some nodded. Chromatistes stepped forward, eager to reply—but guards surrounded him, silencing him before he could speak.

Pantocyclus then turned to the Women. “If this Bill passes,” he proclaimed, “no marriage will be safe. No woman’s virtue secure. Deception will reign in every home. Domestic peace—like our Constitution—will perish.”

“And I say,” he thundered, “Sooner than that—come death!”

At those words, the signal was given.

The Convict guards surged forward, spearing Chromatistes where he stood. The Regular Classes parted ranks, allowing a phalanx of Women—back foremost, unseen and unstoppable—to advance upon the unsuspecting Isosceles. The Artisans followed suit, opening their lines. Meanwhile, bands of Convicts sealed every entrance, forming an unbreakable wall.

The battle was brief. Under the precise command of the Circles, each Woman’s charge was lethal. Many withdrew their weapons unharmed, ready for another strike. But no second blow was needed. The Isosceles, panicked and leaderless, attacked one another in blind terror. Faced with invisible foes and blocked escape routes, they screamed “treachery!”—and in that cry, they doomed themselves.

Within half an hour, not one remained alive. Seventy thousand bodies lay scattered—shattered by the angles of their own kind. The victory of Order was complete.

The Circles wasted no time. The Working Men were spared, but decimated. The Equilateral Militia was mobilized. Every Triangle suspected of irregularity—judged by appearance alone—was executed by Court Martial, without measurement. Homes were searched. Towns, villages, hamlets—every settlement was purged of excess lower classes. For over a year, the balance of society was restored through relentless cleansing.

And thus, the use of colour was abolished. Its possession became forbidden. To utter a word associated with colour—except by Circles or approved scholars—was punishable by law. Only in the highest, most secret halls of our University—places I have never been permitted to enter—is colour still used sparingly, to illustrate the deepest mysteries of mathematics. Even that is whispered rumor.

Elsewhere in Flatland, colour has vanished. The art of making it is known to only one person: the reigning Chief Circle. On his deathbed, he reveals the secret to his successor alone. One factory produces it, hidden from sight. Each year, the workers are destroyed—consumed in fire or poison—and replaced. The knowledge dies with them.

To this day, even the aristocracy trembles at the memory of the Chromatic Sedition. Not because of the loss of colour—but because they remember what happens when the fabric of order frays. And they know: the next time, there may be no hero to save them.

It is time I turned from these scattered observations about life in Flatland to the true heart of this tale—the moment when I stepped beyond the limits of my world and into the vast, incomprehensible realm of Space. That is what this book is truly about; everything that has come before serves only as preparation. For this reason, I must leave untouched many aspects of our existence that might otherwise hold interest—how we move without feet, how we build structures without hands or foundations, how rain falls across our land without being blocked by northern zones, how our hills rise, our mines are dug, our trees grow, our seasons turn, and our harvests come. I must also pass over our alphabet, our writing system adapted to our linear tablets, and countless other details of daily life. These are not forgotten, but omitted out of respect for your time.

Yet before I reach the core of my story, I must address one final matter that readers may expect: the highest class in Flatland society—the Circles, or as we call them, the Priests. When I use the word “Priests,” do not think only of religious figures. In our world, they are far more than that. They govern every aspect of life: science, art, trade, war, architecture, engineering, education, law, morality, theology—everything of value. Though they do little themselves, they are the unseen force behind all great works, directing others to achieve what no individual could alone.

Now, though everyone calls a Circle a Circle, those with deeper knowledge understand that no true Circle exists. All are, in fact, Polygons with an enormous number of very small sides. As the number of sides increases, the shape grows closer to a perfect circle. With three hundred or four hundred sides, even the most sensitive touch cannot detect angles—though, as I’ve already explained, such tactile recognition is forbidden among the elite. To touch a Circle would be an unbearable insult. This social taboo allows the Circle to maintain an aura of mystery around their form from childhood onward.

The average perimeter of a Circle is three feet. So, in a Polygon of three hundred sides, each side measures just a hundredth of a foot—less than a tenth of an inch. In a six- or seven-hundred-sided figure, the sides are barely larger than the head of a pin. By custom, we assume the Supreme Circle has ten thousand sides—though no one can truly verify it.

Unlike lower classes, whose offspring gain one side per generation, the advancement of the Circular line is governed by two opposing laws of nature. First, the rate of development accelerates as the lineage rises. Second, fertility declines proportionally. Thus, a Polygon with four or five hundred sides rarely has even one child—never more than one. Yet their offspring may possess significantly more sides. A son of a five-hundred-sided Polygon might have five hundred fifty, or even six hundred.

But evolution is not left entirely to nature. Medicine plays a role. Our physicians have found that the delicate sides of newborn high-class Polygons can be broken and restructured with remarkable precision. Done correctly, this process can cause a Polygon of two or three hundred sides to leap forward by hundreds of generations—doubling his ancestral rank in a single act. But it is dangerous. Only one in ten survives. Still, the ambition of noble families is so fierce that nearly every aspiring parent places their firstborn in the Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium within a month of birth.

One year later, the child is usually gone—another grave in the Gymnasium’s cemetery. But on rare occasions, a triumphant procession returns with a child transformed: no longer a Polygon, but a Circle—at least in name. One such miracle is enough to inspire thousands of parents to risk the same fate, even knowing the odds are against them. And so, the dream of becoming a Circle lives on, not through birth alone, but through sacrifice, science, and hope.

The teachings of the Circles may be distilled into a single, unshakable principle: *Attend to your Configuration.* Whether in politics, religion, or morality, every doctrine they promote aims at perfecting individual and collective form—though always with the highest regard for the Configuration of the Circles themselves, who stand above all others as the ultimate standard.

It is thanks to the Circles that ancient errors have been banished—those dangerous notions once held by the unwary that conduct depends on will, effort, training, praise, or any such fleeting human impulse. No longer do we believe in free choice; instead, we understand that Configuration determines destiny. The great Pantocyclus—the noble Circle who crushed the Colour Revolt—was the first to prove this truth beyond doubt: *Configuration makes the man.*

Consider the Isosceles born with unequal sides. If left uncorrected, he will inevitably fall into vice. But if he is taken to the Isosceles Hospital and his angles adjusted, he may yet become upright. Similarly, any Triangle, Square, or even Polygon born with irregularities must undergo treatment at one of the Regular Hospitals. Otherwise, their flawed form will lead them to the State Prison—or worse, to the angle of the State Executioner.

All wrongdoing, from minor lapses to grave crimes, is seen as stemming from physical imperfection. Perhaps it began at birth, or perhaps it was caused by a collision in a crowd, excessive exercise, neglect, or even a sudden shift in temperature that distorted a sensitive part of the body. Thus, Pantocyclus concluded: neither virtue nor vice deserves blame or praise. Why commend a Square for defending his client’s interests when what truly matters is the precision of his right angles? And why punish an Isosceles for theft when the real tragedy is the irreparable inequality of his sides?

This doctrine is logically sound—but not without its flaws. When dealing with a criminal Isosceles who claims he cannot help stealing because of his nature, the magistrate need only reply: “Then you cannot help being a burden to society—and so I cannot help sentencing you.” That ends the matter. But in private life, where no such final judgment exists, the theory becomes harder to uphold.

I confess: when one of my own Hexagonal grandsons blames his disobedience on a sudden temperature change affecting his Perimeter, insisting that the fault lies not in him but in his Configuration—which can only be strengthened by rich sweetmeats—I find myself caught between logic and affection. I cannot fully reject his argument, yet I cannot accept it either. Still, I’ve found that a good scolding—or even a mild correction—seems to have some hidden effect on his shape, though I admit I have no proof. Yet I am not alone. Even high-ranking Circles, sitting as judges, often praise or reprimand figures regardless of their regularity. At home, I know from experience that even the most learned Circles scold their children with fiery passion about "right" and "wrong," as if these were real forces, as if choice existed at all.

To ensure Configuration remains paramount, the Circles have reversed the traditional commandment. In Spaceland, children are taught to honor their parents. In Flatland, we teach men to honor their sons—and, if they have none, their grandsons. This is not indulgence, but reverence for future generations. The Circles preach that fathers must place the needs of their descendants before their own, thus elevating both family and state.

Yet there is one weakness in this system—though I speak humbly, for who dares question the wisdom of the Circles? It lies in our treatment of Women.

Since Irregular births threaten social order, it is vital that only those with pure lineage marry. A man’s irregularity can be measured; but since all women are straight, their visible form gives no clue to their hidden potential for producing flawed offspring. So we rely on pedigrees—carefully maintained and regulated by the State. Without certification, no woman may marry.

One might expect that a Circle, proud of his bloodline and hoping for a Chief Circle in his line, would be most cautious in choosing a wife. But the opposite is true. The higher one rises, the less concern is shown for ancestry. An aspiring Isosceles would never dream of marrying a woman with even one irregular ancestor. A Square or Pentagon checks only five hundred generations back. A Hexagon or Dodecagon is more careless. But a Circle has been known to marry a woman whose great-grandfather was irregular—just for her luster, or for the softness of her voice, which we value greatly.

Such unions often produce no children—or worse, offspring with fewer sides, or outright irregularity. Yet these consequences have not deterred the practice. A few lost sides in a near-Circle go unnoticed, and can sometimes be restored through surgery in the Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium. And the Circles, in their pride, see barrenness not as failure, but as a natural law of advanced development.

But if this trend continues unchecked, the Circular class may dwindle faster than ever before. One day, the race may no longer produce a Chief Circle—and then the very foundation of Flatland’s constitution could collapse.

Another warning weighs heavily upon me—though I see no clear remedy. Three hundred years ago, the Chief Circle decreed that women, lacking Reason but overflowing with Emotion, should no longer be treated as rational beings. They were forbidden education. Not even arithmetic was taught them—so they could not count the angles of their husbands or children.

Thus, each generation has grown dimmer in intellect. This policy still stands today. And now I fear it has begun to harm us males as well.

We live a double life. With women, we speak of love, duty, right, wrong, pity, hope—emotions that have no basis in reason, yet serve to soothe and control. But among ourselves, our language is cold and precise. "Love" becomes "anticipation of benefit." "Duty" becomes "necessity." Words are stripped of sentiment and reforged in logic.

And while we speak to women with profound respect—making them believe they are worshipped as nearly divine—we whisper behind their backs that they are little more than mindless organisms.

Even our theology differs. Before women, we speak of sacred mysteries in emotional terms. Among ourselves, we discuss the same truths with rigid, mathematical clarity.

This duality burdens the young. At three years old, they are torn from maternal care and taught to abandon the emotional language of childhood—except when speaking to mothers and nurses—and to adopt the hard, logical tongue of science. Already, I sense a weakening in the grasp of mathematical truth compared to our ancestors’ sharper minds.

What if a woman secretly learns to read? What if she discovers a popular book and shares its contents with her kind? Or worse—what if a child slips and reveals the secrets of logical dialect to a mother?

On the simplest ground—that male intellect is being weakened—I appeal to the highest authorities: reconsider the laws of female education. For the sake of our civilization, let us not sacrifice reason on the altar of tradition.

It was the second-to-last day of the 1999th year of our era, and the first day of the Long Vacation. After spending the evening immersed in my favorite pastime—geometry—I retired to rest with an unsolved problem still swirling in my mind. That night, I dreamed.

Before me stretched an endless procession of tiny straight lines, which I instinctively took to be women, scattered among even smaller, gleaming points—beings that seemed like luminous dots. All moved back and forth along a single, unbroken line, each at roughly the same speed. From time to time, they emitted a chaotic chorus of chirps and tweets, but whenever they stopped, silence fell as complete as if the world had ceased to breathe.

I approached one of the largest figures—what I assumed was a woman—and spoke directly to her. But she did not answer. A second attempt brought no response. A third went unanswered too. Frustrated by what seemed to me sheer rudeness, I placed my mouth directly before hers, blocking her path, and shouted:
“Woman! What is this gathering? This strange, jumbled noise? This endless motion back and forth along a single line?”

Taken aback by the abruptness, I apologized for any disturbance I may have caused. I introduced myself as a stranger and humbly asked the King to describe his domain. Yet through persistent questioning, I learned the following:

This poor, self-proclaimed ignorant king believed that the straight line he called his kingdom—and in which he lived his entire existence—was all there was: the whole of space, the totality of reality. He could neither move nor see beyond that line. To him, anything outside it did not exist—not even as emptiness, for emptiness implies space. It was pure non-being.

His subjects—the straight lines were men, the points were women—were just as confined. They could only move along the line, and their vision extended only to a single point. No one could ever see more than a dot. A man, a woman, a child—each appeared as a mere point to another’s eye. Only voice revealed sex or age.

And because each individual occupied the full width of the narrow path that made up their universe, no one could step aside to let others pass. Movement was impossible without collision. Once neighbors, always neighbors. Neighbors stayed together until death parted them—just as marriage binds us in Flatland.

Such a life, limited to sightless points and rigid motion, struck me as profoundly bleak. Yet the King seemed remarkably cheerful. I wondered how such a society could sustain affection or family bonds under such constraints. Finally, I dared ask about his household.

“My wives and children are well and happy,” he said.

I hesitated, stunned. Around him, I saw only men—no women in sight. “But Your Majesty,” I said, “how can you possibly see or reach your family when half a dozen others block your way? Can’t you see through them? Or pass them? Is proximity truly unnecessary for marriage and procreation?”

“How can you ask such a foolish question?” he snapped. “If it were as you suggest, the world would vanish in a generation. No, no—neighbourhood is irrelevant. Love does not depend on closeness. And childbirth is far too vital to rely on chance. You must know this. But since you pretend ignorance, I’ll explain as if you were a newborn in Lineland.”

He continued: “Marriages are formed through sound and hearing. Every man has two voices—one bass, one tenor—at opposite ends of his body. Two mouths, two eyes. I should mention this, though I’ve struggled to hear your tenor during our talk.”

I admitted I had only one voice and hadn’t realized he had two.

“That confirms it,” he declared. “You’re not a man. You’re a monstrous female with a bass voice and an untrained ear. But let me continue.”

“Nature decrees that every man shall marry two women,” he said.

“Why two?” I asked.

“You carry your pretense too far!” he roared. “How can harmony exist without the union of four voices? The man’s bass and tenor, the two women’s soprano and contralto—only then is balance achieved!”

“But what if a man prefers one wife—or three?” I ventured.

“It’s impossible!” he thundered. “As absurd as saying two plus one equals five, or that anyone can see a straight line. Impossible!”

I tried to interrupt, but he pressed on.

“Once a week, a natural law compels us to move rhythmically—violently—for the time it takes you to count to one hundred and one. At the fifty-first pulse, we pause mid-motion. Then each being emits their most powerful, sweetest tone. In that instant, marriages are sealed.”

“The harmony between bass and treble, tenor and contralto is so perfect,” he added, “that lovers separated by twenty thousand leagues often recognize each other’s voice at once. Love transcends distance. And in that moment, a triad of offspring—a male and two females—is born, joining the line of Lineland.”

“Always three?” I asked. “Must one wife always bear twins?”

“Bass-voiced monster!” he spat. “Yes! Otherwise, how could the balance of sexes remain intact? Would you ignore the very alphabet of nature?”

He fell silent, furious. Minutes passed before he resumed.

“Do not suppose every bachelor finds his partners on the first try. Most undergo repeated trials. Rarely does a pair instantly harmonize. Often, one voice matches, but not both. Sometimes neither does. Or the women’s voices clash slightly. But Nature provides: each weekly chorus brings the three closer. With every attempt, discord reveals itself, and slowly, imperceptibly, the imperfect adjusts their tone to match the perfect. Over many weeks, many attempts, harmony emerges.

Then comes the day—when the universal chorus sounds, and the distant trio suddenly sings in perfect unison. Before they even realize it, the wedded triad is joined in song, and Nature rejoices over a new marriage and three new lives.”

I decided it was time to bring the Monarch down from his lofty reveries and anchor him in reason. With that purpose, I resolved to reveal to him—however faintly—the truth about the world beyond Lineland: the nature of Flatland itself. So I began:

“Your Royal Highness, how do you distinguish the shapes and positions of your subjects? I myself, before entering your realm, observed by sight that some of your people are Lines, others Points, and that some Lines are longer than others—”

“You speak of impossibility!” he interrupted sharply. “You must have seen a vision. For no one can possibly detect the difference between a Line and a Point through sight. It is contrary to nature! But such distinctions are made by hearing—by sound. And by the same means, I know my own shape exactly. Behold me—I am a Line, the longest in all Lineland, over six inches of Space—”

“Of *Length*,” I ventured.

“Fool!” he snapped. “Space *is* Length. Interrupt me again, and I shall end our conversation.”

I apologized, but he continued with scorn, “Since you’re impervious to logic, let me show you how my two voices reveal my form to my wives—six thousand miles, seventy yards, two feet, eight inches away—one to the North, the other to the South. Listen now. I call to them.”

He chirped twice—once sharp, once delayed—and then resumed, smugly: “My wives hear one voice, then the second, separated by an interval during which sound travels 6.457 inches. From this, they infer that one of my mouths is 6.457 inches farther from them than the other—and thus know my full length. Of course, they don’t calculate this every time. They did it once, before marriage. But they *could*, if needed. In the same way, I determine the shape of any male subject through sound alone.”

“But what,” I asked, “if a man feigns a woman’s voice—or disguises one voice so it cannot be recognized as the echo of the other? Could not such deceptions cause chaos? And have you no way to verify identity by touch?”

This was, admittedly, a foolish question—touch could never work here—but I meant to provoke him. And I succeeded.

“What?” he shrieked, horrified. “Explain yourself!”

“Feel. Touch. Come into contact,” I replied.

“If you mean *feeling*,” he said coldly, “approaching so close that no space remains between individuals—know this, Stranger: such an act is punishable by death in my land. The delicate form of a Woman could shatter under such proximity. The State must protect her. And since Women cannot be distinguished from Men by sight, the Law forbids *anyone*—man or woman—to approach another so closely as to eliminate the space between them.”

“And what purpose would such illegal, unnatural closeness serve?” he went on, “when hearing already reveals everything more precisely, swiftly, and safely? As for fraud—impossible! Voice is the essence of being. It cannot be altered at will. Now suppose I had the power to pass through solid matter, touching each subject in turn—even a billion—measuring their size and distance by feeling. What a waste of time and energy! Yet with hearing, in a single moment, I survey the entire population: their location, form, mind, spirit—all at once. Listen—only listen!”

He paused, listening intently to a sound that seemed to me like the buzz of countless tiny insects.

“Truly,” I said, “your hearing serves you well. It compensates for many limitations. But still, your life must be profoundly dull. To see only a Point! Not even to glimpse a Straight Line! To know nothing of what a Line truly is! To possess sight yet be denied the very visions we take for granted in Flatland! Better to have no sight at all than to see so little!”

I admitted I lacked his refined auditory sense—what he heard as a symphony, I perceived as mere chatter. But I could, at least, *see* a Line distinct from a Point. And I would prove it.

“Just before entering your kingdom,” I said, “I saw you dancing from left to right, then right to left. Seven men and one woman were near you on the left; eight men and two women on the right. Is this correct?”

“It is correct,” he conceded, “as far as numbers and genders go. Though I do not understand what you mean by ‘left’ and ‘right.’ But I deny you *saw* these things. How could you see the inside of a man? You must have heard them—and imagined you saw them. And tell me—what do you mean by ‘left’ and ‘right’? Are they not simply North and South?”

“No,” I replied. “Beyond your motion north and south, there is another direction—one I call from left to right.”

“Show me,” he demanded. “Demonstrate this motion.”

“I cannot,” I said, “unless you step entirely out of your Line.”

“Out of my Line?” he gasped. “Do you mean out of the world? Out of Space?”

“Yes,” I said. “Out of *your* world. Out of *your* space. For your space is not true space. True space is a Plane. Yours is merely a Line.”

“Then describe this motion,” he insisted. “If you cannot move in it, explain it in words.”

“If you cannot tell left from right,” I said, “no words will help. But surely you must know such a simple distinction?”

“I do not understand,” he said flatly.

“Then consider this: when you move forward, does it not sometimes occur to you that you *could* turn—so that your side faces the direction you're moving toward? Instead of always progressing along your ends, do you never wish to move sideways—along your side?”

“Never,” he said. “And what do you mean? How can a man’s inside face any direction? Or how can he move in the direction of his inside?”

“Well,” I said, “since words fail, I’ll show you.”

And so I began to move—gradually lifting my body out of Lineland. As long as part of me remained within his world, he kept shouting, “I see you! I see you still! You are not moving!”

But when I fully stepped outside his line, he shrieked, “She is vanished! She is dead!”

“I am not dead,” I replied. “I am simply outside Lineland—outside your so-called Space—into the true Space where I can see things as they are. And now I can see your Line—your side, your inside—whatever you choose to call it. And I can see the men and women to your north and south. Let me name them—describe their order, size, and spacing.”

From dreams, I now turn to truth.

It was the final day of the 1999th year of our age. The steady patter of rain had long signaled the onset of night, and I sat beside my wife, lost in thought—reflecting on the events of the past and the possibilities ahead: the coming year, the century, the new Millennium.

I took nine squares—each an inch on every side—and arranged them into a larger square, three inches across. The boy pondered this silently, then asked, “But you’ve taught me about raising numbers to the third power. What does 3³ mean in geometry?”

“Nothing,” I replied firmly. “Geometry has only two dimensions. There is no such thing as a third.”

At this, my grandson leaned forward, eyes alight. “Then if a point makes a line, and a line makes a square… why can’t a square, moving somehow parallel to itself—though I don’t see how—create something else? Something three inches in every direction? And wouldn’t that be represented by 3³?”

I bristled at the interruption. “Go to bed,” I said sharply. “If you would stop speaking nonsense, you might remember more sense.”

And so he left, disgraced. I returned to my thoughts, trying to reflect on the year passed and the future yet to come—but still haunted by the child’s bold question.

Only a few grains of sand remained in the hourglass. Rousing myself, I turned it northward—once more—for the last time in the old millennium. As I did, I muttered aloud: “The boy is a fool.”

Instantly, I felt it—a presence. A cold breath stirred the air, sending a shiver through me.

“He is not a fool!” my wife exclaimed. “And you are breaking the Commandments by dishonoring your own grandson!”

I ignored her. I looked in every direction. Nothing was visible. Yet I *felt* it—the unseen weight, the chilling whisper returning.

I rose abruptly. “What is it?” she asked, alarmed. “There’s no draft. What are you searching for? There’s nothing.”

There was nothing. I sat again, repeating, “The boy is a fool. 3³ means nothing in geometry.”

Then—clear and distinct—a voice answered:
“The boy is not a fool. 3³ has a clear geometric meaning.”

Both my wife and I heard it. She did not comprehend, but we both lunged toward the sound.

Our horror grew when we saw—*a figure*.

At first glance, it resembled a woman seen sideways. But closer inspection revealed impossibilities: the edges blurred too quickly to belong to any female form. It seemed like a circle—but one that shifted in size unpredictably, defying all known shapes.

My wife, lacking my experience and overwhelmed by instinctive fear, leapt to conclusions. “How did this person get in?” she demanded. “You promised me—no ventilators in our new house!”

“There are none,” I said. “But why do you assume it’s a woman? By my Sight Recognition—”

“Oh, I have no patience for your Sight Recognition!” she snapped. “Feeling is believing. A straight line to the touch is worth a circle to the sight.”—proverbs common among women in Flatland.

“Well,” I said, cautious not to provoke her further, “if you insist, let us ask for an introduction.”

She stepped forward, adopting her most gracious manner. “Permit me, Madam, to feel and be felt by—” Then she recoiled suddenly. “Oh! It’s not a woman! No angles—none at all! Could I have offended a perfect Circle?”

“I am indeed, in a way, a Circle,” came the voice. “A more perfect one than any in Flatland. But more accurately—I am many circles in one.”

Then, softer: “I carry a message for your husband—one I cannot deliver in your presence. If you would allow us a moment alone—”

But my wife refused to let such a distinguished visitor inconvenience himself. Assuring the stranger that her time had long passed, and apologizing repeatedly for her earlier outburst, she withdrew to her chamber.

I glanced at the hourglass. The final grains had fallen.

The Third Millennium had begun.

As soon as the echo of my wife’s farewell cry faded into silence, I stepped forward, intending to greet the Stranger more closely and invite him to sit. But the moment I drew near, I froze—speechless, motionless—overcome by astonishment.

He was unlike anything I had ever seen. Not a single angle marked his form. Yet he shifted constantly in size and brightness in ways that defied all known geometry. My mind raced: Could this be a criminal? A rogue Irregular Isosceles, pretending to be a Circle to gain entry to our home, now preparing to strike with a sharp point?

In our world, sight is unreliable without fog—and the season was unusually clear. At such close range, I couldn’t trust what I saw. Panic seized me. Without thinking, I lunged forward, blurting, “Sir, if you please—” and touched him.

My wife had been right. There was no angularity. No roughness. No flaw. He was perfectly smooth—a flawless Circle. I walked around him slowly, beginning at his eye and returning to it. From every angle, he remained a perfect circle. There could be no doubt.

Then came a conversation I shall try to recount as faithfully as memory allows, omitting only my many apologies—for I was humiliated beyond measure that a Square like myself should have dared to touch a Circle so unceremoniously.

The Stranger grew impatient with my hesitation.

**STRANGER:** Have you felt me enough yet? Are we not introduced?

**I:** Most illustrious Sir, forgive my clumsiness. It is not from ignorance of manners, but from shock and nervousness. This unexpected visit has thrown me off balance. And pray, do not reveal my rudeness to anyone—especially not my wife. But before you speak further, might you tell me where you come from?

**STRANGER:** From Space. From Space, Sir. Where else?

**I:** Pardon me, my Lord, but are we not already in Space? You and I, here, now?

**STRANGER:** Pooh! What do you know of Space? Define it for me.

**I:** Space, my Lord, is height and breadth extended infinitely.

**STRANGER:** Exactly. And yet you don’t even know what Space truly means. You think it has only two dimensions. But I have come to tell you of a third—height, breadth, and depth.

**I:** Your Lordship jests. We say "length" and "height," or "breadth" and "thickness"—four names for just two dimensions.

**STRANGER:** But I mean three actual dimensions—not just names.

**I:** Then would your Lordship show me where this third dimension lies?

**STRANGER:** I came from it. It is above and below.

**I:** You mean north and south?

**STRANGER:** Not at all. I mean a direction you cannot see—because you have no eye on your side.

**I:** But surely, my Lord, I have an eye at the junction of two sides!

**STRANGER:** Yes—but to see into Space, you’d need an eye not on your perimeter, but on your *side*—what you might call your inside. In Spaceland, we call that your side.

**I:** An eye in my stomach? That’s absurd. You’re joking.

**STRANGER:** I am not joking. I come from Space—the Land of Three Dimensions. From there, I looked down upon your Plane, which you foolishly call Space. From that vantage, I saw everything—your houses, churches, chests, safes—even your insides, laid bare before me.

**I:** Such claims are easy to make.

**STRANGER:** But hard to prove? Indeed. But I will prove it. When I descended, I saw your four sons—Pentagons—in their rooms. I saw your two grandsons—Hexagons. I watched your youngest stay with you before retiring. I saw your three Isosceles servants eating in the kitchen and the page in the scullery. Then I came here. How do you suppose I entered?

**I:** Through the roof, I assume.

**STRANGER:** Not so. Your roof was repaired recently—no opening large enough for even a woman. I tell you again—I came from Space. Are you not convinced by what I’ve told you of your household?

**I:** Your Lordship may have resources others lack. Any neighbor with wealth could learn such things.

**STRANGER:** (To himself) What must I do? One idea occurs to me. When you see a straight line—say, your wife—how many dimensions do you assign her?

**I:** You mock me as if I were ignorant. We know well that a woman is not truly a line, but a very thin parallelogram—two-dimensional, like us. She has length and breadth.

**STRANGER:** But the fact that she is visible implies a third dimension.

**I:** I acknowledge her breadth—though slight, it can be measured.

**STRANGER:** You misunderstand. When you see a woman, you should not only infer her breadth—you should see her *height*, though infinitesimal in your world. If a line had no height, it would vanish from space entirely. Surely you see that?

**I:** I confess I do not. We see length and brightness. If brightness fades, the line disappears. But are you saying "brightness" is your "height"?

**STRANGER:** No. By "height," I mean a dimension like your length—only one you cannot perceive because it's too small.

**I:** Then let us test it. Measure my "height." Show me its direction. Otherwise, I cannot believe you.

**STRANGER:** (To himself) I cannot do either. How can I convince him? A plain statement, followed by proof—surely that will suffice.

Now, listen carefully. You live on a plane. What you call Flatland is the surface of a fluid—on or within which you move, never rising or falling. I am not a plane figure. I am a solid. You call me a Circle, but I am not. I am an infinite number of circles—ranging from a point to a circle thirteen inches wide—stacked one atop another. When I pass through your plane, I appear as a section—a circle. Even a sphere—my true name—must appear as a circle to a Flatlander.

Do you remember when you entered Lineland? You appeared not as a square, but as a line, because that world lacked the dimensions to show your full form—only a slice. In the same way, your two-dimensional world cannot contain me. It can only show a slice—what you call a circle.

Your eyes grow dim with disbelief. But now, prepare for proof. You cannot see all of me at once—your vision is confined to the plane. But you can see how my appearance changes as I rise in Space. Watch: I will ascend—and you will see my circle shrink until it vanishes.

[Here, a diagram would follow—showing the Sphere appearing as a circle, then shrinking to a point.]

There was no movement I could see—yet he grew smaller, then disappeared. I blinked, wondering if I dreamed. But no—it was real. From nowhere, a voice spoke—close to my heart—“Am I gone? Are you convinced? Now I will return. Watch as my circle grows larger.”

Every inhabitant of Spaceland would understand what he said. To me, however—though skilled in Flatland mathematics—it made no sense. The diagram shows clearly: a sphere moving upward would appear as a circle, first full-sized, then shrinking to a point. But I saw only the effect, not the cause. All I knew was that the circle vanished, then reappeared and expanded.

When he returned to his original size, he sighed deeply. He sensed my silence—he saw I still did not grasp.

And I began to suspect he was no Circle at all—but a master trickster. Or perhaps the old tales were true: enchanters and magicians did exist.

After a long pause, he muttered, “Only one option remains—if I avoid action. I must use analogy.”

Another silence. Then he continued.

**SPHERE:** Tell me, Mr. Mathematician—when a point moves northward, leaving a glowing trail, what do you call that trail?

**I:** A straight line.

**SPHERE:** And how many ends does a line have?

**I:** Two.

**SPHERE:** Now imagine that line moving east and west, parallel to itself, so every point leaves a trail. What shape results? Suppose it moves a distance equal to its own length.

**I:** A square.

**SPHERE:** And how many sides? Angles?

**I:** Four sides. Four angles.

**SPHERE:** Now stretch your imagination. Imagine a square moving upward—not north, but *up*—out of Flatland entirely. Every point within you—since you are a square—moves upward, each tracing its own path, without overlapping. This follows analogy. Can you not see?

I held back my rage—tempted to charge at him, to hurl him into Space or out of existence. But I replied calmly:

“And what shape will I form by this motion? Will it be describable in Flatland terms?”

**SPHERE:** Of course. It’s simple—by analogy. A point has one terminal point. A line has two. A square has four. The pattern is geometric: 1, 2, 4. What comes next?

**I:** Eight.

**SPHERE:** Precisely. So the new shape—what we call a *cube*—has eight corners. Are you convinced now?

**I:** Does it have sides, as well as corners?

**SPHERE:** Naturally. But not what you call sides. What we call sides. You would call them solids.

**I:** And how many sides will this cube have?

**SPHERE:** How can you ask? The side of any shape is one dimension less than the shape itself. A point has zero sides. A line has two (its endpoints). A square has four. Zero, two, four—what progression is that?

**I:** Arithmetical.

**SPHERE:** And what is the next number?

**I:** Six.

**SPHERE:** Exactly. So the cube will have six sides—six of your insides. See now?

“Monster!” I cried. “Be you magician, dream, devil, or fraud—no longer will I endure your tricks. Either you or I must die!”

And with that, I lunged at him.

It was no use. I thrust my sharpest angle into the Stranger with all my strength—force enough to shatter any ordinary Circle—but he slipped away from me, not sideways or forward, but *out*, as if sliding beyond the limits of existence itself. No resistance, no edge to grasp—he simply faded from contact, vanishing into nothingness. The room grew silent, empty.

Yet still, his voice echoed.

**SPHERE:** Why won’t you listen? I thought you, a man of reason and a scholar of geometry, might be the one to carry the truth of the Third Dimension into your world. But now I see words fail. Then let deeds speak for themselves.

Listen closely. From my place in Space, I see through everything you call solid. That cupboard beside you? I see inside it—boxes filled with coins, ledgers stacked neatly. You locked it half an hour ago, and the key is still in your hand. But I am not bound by doors or walls. I descend *through* space.

Now I am inside. Now I take one of those tablets. Now I rise again.

I bolted toward the closet, flung open the door. One tablet was missing. A mocking laugh rang out—and there, in the corner of the room, stood the Stranger, while the very same tablet lay upon the floor. I picked it up. It was unmistakably the one taken from the cupboard.

My mind reeled. Was I dreaming? Had madness seized me? Yet the voice persisted.

**SPHERE:** Can you deny now that what I say is true? What you call “solid” is merely surface. What you name “space” is nothing more than a flat plane. I stand above it, looking down—seeing what lies hidden within every shape you only glimpse from outside. If you could but will yourself upward, just a little—just a flicker of motion—you too could see what I see.

The higher I climb, the wider my view—though smaller in scale. Now I ascend… I see your neighbor, the Hexagon, with his family in their homes; I see the Theatre ten doors away, where the audience has just left; across the way, a Circle sits at his desk, lost in thought. And now—I return.

And here, as final proof: let me touch you—just once—within your body. A slight pain, yes, but nothing compared to the knowledge it brings.

Before I could protest, a sharp, searing pain pierced my innermost self. A sound—like laughter—seemed to originate from within my own chest. Then, as quickly as it came, the agony ceased, leaving only a dull throb. The Stranger began to reform, growing larger as he returned.

**SPHERE:** See? I’ve hurt you little. Still unconvinced? Then what can possibly change your mind?

I had made my decision. This could not go on. To live under the power of such a being—one who could invade my very insides like a phantom—was unbearable. If only I could trap him! Pin him to the wall until help arrived!

Once more, I lunged, driving my hardest angle into him, shouting for aid so loudly the whole house trembled. At that instant, I believe he had descended beneath our plane—struggling to rise. He did not move. I pressed harder, sensing footsteps approaching, believing help was near.

Then—a violent tremor shook the Sphere.

**SPHERE (in a low voice):** This must not happen. Either you accept reason… or I must use the last resort of civilization.

He turned to me, voice rising:

**SPHERE:** Listen! No one else must witness this. Send your Wife back—now—before she enters. The Gospel of Three Dimensions cannot be ruined. Not after a thousand years of waiting. I hear her coming! Back! Away from me—or you come with me—into the Land of Three Dimensions!

**I:** Fool! Madman! Irregular! Never shall I let you go! You’ll pay for your tricks!

**SPHERE (thundering):** Ha! Is it come to this? Then face your fate—out of your Plane you go. Once… twice… thrice! It’s done.

A terrible dread seized me. Darkness swallowed the world. Then—something beyond sight, a sensation that twisted perception itself. I saw a line that wasn’t a line, a space that defied space. I was myself, yet not. I was no longer certain of being. When I could speak, my voice cracked with terror: “This is madness… or Hell.”

“It is neither,” came the calm reply of the Sphere. “It is knowledge. It is Three Dimensions. Open your eye once more—and look steadily.”

I obeyed. And then—light.

Before me stood a vision beyond imagination. The beauty I had only dreamed of in Flatland—the perfect symmetry, the harmony of form—was now visible, tangible, real. At the center of the Stranger’s being, something lay open to my gaze. Yet I saw no organs, no blood, no inner workings. Only a radiant, unified presence—something beyond words. You, readers of Spaceland, would call it the surface of a Sphere.

Prostrate in awe, I whispered, “How can I see within you, yet see no heart, no lungs, no arteries? How can I behold your inside and yet not see your life?”

“You do not see what you think you see,” replied the Sphere. “You cannot perceive my internal parts. I am not of your kind. If I were a Circle, you might glimpse my insides. But I am composed of many Circles—many in one—what you would call a Sphere. Just as the outer face of a Cube is a Square, so the outer form of a Sphere appears as a Circle to your eyes.”

Though bewildered, I no longer resisted. I bowed in silent reverence. He continued, his voice softer now. “Do not distress yourself if you cannot grasp the mysteries of Spaceland at once. They will unfold with time. Let us begin by returning to the land you knew. Come back with me to Flatland, and I shall show you something you have long reasoned about—but never truly seen: a visible angle.”

“Impossible!” I exclaimed.

Yet, guided by the Sphere, I followed like a dreamer, until his voice halted me again. “Look below—there is your own house, your home, and all who dwell within.”

I looked down—and there it was. My pentagonal home, laid bare before my very eyes. Not inferred, not imagined—but seen. My four sons slept peacefully in the northwestern rooms. My two orphaned grandsons rested to the south. The servants, the butler, my daughter—all in their places. Only my wife, anxious over my absence, had left her chamber, pacing the hall. And the page, roused by my cries, had slipped into my study, pretending to check if I had fainted—while secretly searching the cabinet.

And now I could *see* it all. Even the contents of the cabinet—the two chests of gold, the tablets mentioned earlier. Everything was revealed.

Moved by my wife’s sorrow, I tried to leap down to comfort her—but I could not move. “Do not worry for her,” said my Guide. “She will not wait long. For now, let us survey Flatland.”

Again, I rose through space. As we climbed higher, the world beneath expanded. My city, every house, every creature—tiny, clear, exposed. We ascended further, and the earth’s secrets opened: mines, caverns, hidden depths—all unveiled.

Overwhelmed, I turned to my Companion. “I am become as a God! For in our world, omnividence—seeing all—is said to be the mark of divinity.”

There was a hint of scorn in the Sphere’s voice. “Is that so? Then every thief and outlaw in my world should be worshipped as gods—since each sees as much as you do now. But know this: your wise men are mistaken.”

I asked, “Then is omnividence not divine?”

“No,” he replied. “Even if such sight exists among others, does it make them more just? More merciful? Less selfish? More loving? No. Then how does it make them divine?”

“But mercy and love?” I protested. “Those are women’s traits! And we know a Circle is superior to a Line—knowledge above affection.”

The Sphere paused. “I do not rank virtues. Yet many of the wisest in Spaceland value compassion over intellect, the humble Straight Line over the proud Circle. But enough of this. Look yonder. Do you recognize that structure?”

I gazed afar and saw a vast polygonal building—the General Assembly Hall of Flatland—surrounded by rows of pentagonal homes forming streets. We were approaching the capital.

“We descend,” said the Sphere.

It was morning—the first hour of the first day of the two-thousandth year. As tradition demanded, the highest Circles convened in solemn session, just as they had on the first day of the year 1000 and 0.

One of them—my brother, a perfectly symmetrical Square and Chief Clerk—read aloud from the minutes:

> “Whereas the States have been disturbed by individuals claiming revelations from another world, and attempting to prove them with arguments that inflame minds, it has been unanimously resolved that on the first day of each millenary, strict orders be sent to district Prefects to hunt down such persons. All Isosceles, regardless of degree, are to be destroyed without trial. Regular Triangles are to be scourged and imprisoned. Squares and Pentagons are to be sent to the asylum. Any of higher rank is to be arrested and brought to the Capital for judgment.”

“The Council has spoken,” said the Sphere. “Your fate is sealed—death or imprisonment awaits the preacher of the Gospel of Three Dimensions.”

“No,” I answered. “Now I understand it clearly. This truth is so evident, so undeniable, that even a child could grasp it. Let me descend now and teach them!”

“Not yet,” said my Guide. “Wait. I must fulfill my purpose. Remain here.”

With that, he leaped—gracefully, swiftly—into the sea of Flatland, landing directly amid the circle of Counsellors.

“I come,” he proclaimed, “to announce the existence of a land of Three Dimensions!”

Many of the younger members recoiled in horror as the Sphere’s circular outline widened before them. But the presiding Circle showed no fear. With a gesture, he signaled six low-grade Isosceles from different sides.

“They have him!” they shouted. “No—yes—we have him! He’s going! He’s gone!”

“Lords,” said the President to the junior Circles, “no need for alarm. My secret archives—accessible only to me—record similar events on the last two millennial commencements. Say nothing of this outside the Cabinet.”

He called the guards. “Arrest the policemen. Gag them. You know your duty.”

After silencing the poor officers—unwilling witnesses to a state secret they would never reveal—he addressed the Council once more.

“Our business is concluded. I wish you a happy New Year.”

Before leaving, he turned to my brother, the Clerk, and expressed deep regret—for, by precedent and secrecy, he must condemn him to lifelong imprisonment. Yet, he added, “If you remain silent about today’s event, your life may be spared.”

And so I watched—from the edge of the third dimension—as the world I knew rejected the truth it could not comprehend.

Section 19. How, though the Sphere revealed to me wonders beyond imagining, I still longed for more—and what came of it

When I saw my brother led away to imprisonment, I tried to leap down into the Council Chamber, desperate to speak for him or at least bid him farewell. But I found I had no power of movement—my limbs were frozen, my will useless. I was entirely subject to the will of my Guide, who spoke in a voice heavy with sorrow: "Do not concern yourself with your brother. You may have time enough later to mourn him. Follow me."

We ascended once again into the vastness of Space.

"Hitherto," said the Sphere, "I have shown you nothing but plane figures and their interiors. Now I must introduce you to Solids—to beings that exist not just in length and breadth, but in height as well. Behold these movable square cards." He placed one upon another—not northward, as I might have expected, but *on* the other. Then another, then another. "See how I build up a Solid by stacking squares parallel to each other. This structure is now complete—equal in height, length, and width. We call it a Cube."

"Pardon me, my Lord," I replied, "but to my eyes, it appears not as a solid, but as an irregular figure whose inside is laid bare. In truth, I see no three-dimensional object at all—but only a plane, such as we infer in Flatland, yet so distorted it seems monstrous. The very sight of it distresses me."

"True," said the Sphere, "it appears flat to you because you are unaccustomed to light, shadow, and perspective—just as in Flatland, a Hexagon would seem a straight line to one without the skill of Sight Recognition. But in reality, this is a Solid. You shall learn its true nature through touch."

He brought me near the Cube, and I felt it with my hands. It was not a plane, nor a mere outline—it was a living, tangible thing. Six flat sides, eight sharp corners—solid angles, he called them. I remembered his earlier words: a Square moving through Space, parallel to itself, could generate such a form. And in that moment, I rejoiced—though I was but a humble being, I could claim, in some sense, to be the ancestor of this magnificent creature.

Yet still, I could not grasp the full meaning of "light," "shade," or "perspective." I asked him plainly, and though his explanation was clear and concise, I will not repeat it here—such things are already known to those who dwell in Space. Suffice it to say, through careful demonstrations, shifting lights, and allowing me to feel both objects and even his own form, he made everything plain. At last, I could distinguish between a Circle and a Sphere, between a Plane Figure and a Solid. This was the pinnacle of my strange journey—the moment of enlightenment, the peak of my existence.

But from this height, I began my fall—my descent into humiliation and despair. Why should knowledge be awakened only to be denied? Why should curiosity be punished?

My heart recoils from recalling what followed. Yet like Prometheus, I endure—because I hope, against all odds, to stir rebellion in the minds of those bound by two or three dimensions, or any number less than infinity. Let personal pain be forgotten. Let me proceed, not with emotion, but with cold, unyielding truth. Every word, every event—burned into my memory—shall be recorded exactly as it occurred. Let my readers judge between me and Fate.

The Sphere wished to continue teaching me—the forms of cylinders, cones, pyramids, polyhedra, and spheres—but I interrupted him.

Not from weariness. Far from it. My thirst for understanding had only grown deeper.

"Pardon me," I said, "O Thou Whom I can no longer call the Perfection of Beauty—but grant me one final request. Show me thy interior. Thy stomach, thy intestines."

"Thy what?" he asked, startled.

"My Lord," I replied, "your wisdom has taught me to believe in something greater than yourself—something more perfect, more divine. Just as you, superior to all Flatland shapes, contain many Circles within you, so surely there exists One above you who contains many Spheres in One Supreme Being—greater than all Solids of Spaceland. And just as we, now in Space, look down upon Flatland and see within all things, so there must be a higher realm—a purer, more spacious region—where we may gaze upon the insides of Solids, where even your own inner organs, and those of your kindred Spheres, will lie open before the eyes of a poor exile from Flatland, who has already been given so much."

"Pooh!" he scoffed. "Enough of this nonsense! Time is short. There is much left to teach you before you can preach the Gospel of Three Dimensions to your blind countrymen."

"Nay, my Teacher," I pleaded, "deny me not what lies within your power. Grant me but one glimpse of your interior, and I shall be content forever—your humble servant, your obedient pupil, ready to accept all your teachings."

"And how would I do that?" he retorted. "Would you have me turn my stomach inside out?"

"But my Lord," I pressed, "you showed me the insides of all my people in Flatland by taking me into the Land of Three Dimensions. What is more natural than for you now to take me on a second journey—to the blessed realm of the Fourth Dimension? There, I shall look down upon this world of three dimensions, seeing the insides of every house, the secrets beneath the earth, the treasures hidden in mines, and the very intestines of every living creature—even of noble Spheres!"

"There is no such land," he said sharply. "The idea is utterly inconceivable."

"Not inconceivable to me," I insisted, "and therefore even less so to you. Perhaps even now, in this realm of three dimensions, your art could reveal the fourth to me—just as in Flatland, your wisdom once tried to open my eyes to a third dimension, though I could not see it."

Let me recall: when I saw a Line and inferred a Plane, was I not actually perceiving a third, unrecognized dimension—height? Then does it not follow that now, when I see a Plane and infer a Solid, I am perceiving a fourth dimension—infinitesimal, invisible, yet real?

And consider the Argument from Analogy.

In One Dimension, a moving Point produces a Line with two endpoints.
In Two Dimensions, a moving Line produces a Square with four endpoints.
In Three Dimensions, a moving Square produces a Cube with eight endpoints.
Then in Four Dimensions, a moving Cube must produce a new being—more divine, more perfect—with sixteen endpoints.

Behold the pattern: 2, 4, 8, 16—a geometric progression. Is this not “strictly according to Analogy,” as you once said?

Again: a Line has two bounding Points. A Square has four bounding Lines. A Cube has six bounding Squares.
Then the next form must have eight bounding Cubes—an arithmetical progression: 2, 4, 6, 8.

Thus, by logic and analogy, the offspring of a Cube in the Fourth Dimension must have eight cubic faces—and sixteen terminal points.

O, my Lord, I cast myself upon conjecture, knowing not the facts. But I ask you—confirm or deny this reasoning. If I am wrong, I yield. But if I am right, will you not listen?

Have not your own people witnessed Beings of higher order descending into closed rooms—appearing and vanishing without opening doors or windows? Have they not seen such creatures shrink and vanish into unseen spaces?

(After a pause.)
"It is reported," he said, "but men differ in opinion. Some say these visions arise from the mind—from the disturbed angularity of the Seer."

"Say they so?" I replied. "Then let me go to that realm—Thoughtland—if it truly is a place of thought. Let me see, in my mind’s eye, the insides of all solids. There, a Cube moving in a new direction—still strictly according to Analogy—will sweep through a new kind of space, leaving a wake behind it, and create a more perfect being: one with sixteen extra-solid angles, bounded by eight solid cubes."

And once there—why stop? Shall we linger at the threshold of the Fifth Dimension? No! Let our ambition rise with our ascent. The gates of the Sixth shall fly open. Then the Seventh. Then the Eighth—

I do not know how long I would have continued. The Sphere roared in thunderous command: "Silence! Or suffer the consequences!" But I could not stop. I was drunk on truth, intoxicated by the vision he himself had given me.

Then—crash! A sound outside, and a shattering blow within. I was hurled through space, faster than thought, unable to speak.

Down! Down! Down! I fell rapidly—knowing that my return to Flatland was inevitable.

For one final moment, I saw the dull, flat expanse of my former world—now to become my entire universe again. Then darkness. Then a thunderclap that shook the soul.

When I awoke, I was once more a crawling Square in my study, listening to the Peace-Cry of my approaching Wife.

Though I had less than a minute to gather my thoughts, I knew—by an instinct deeper than reason—that I must not speak of what had happened to my Wife. Not because I feared she would betray me, but because no woman in Flatland could possibly grasp the truth of my journey. To her, my tale would sound like madness, or worse, nonsense. So I quickly spun a story: I had fallen through the cellar trapdoor, struck my head, and lain unconscious. It was a tale even I found hard to believe—our land’s gravitational pull toward the South is so faint that such an accident seemed unlikely—but my Wife, sharper-minded than most of her kind, sensed my agitation and chose not to argue. Instead, she insisted I rest, believing I was unwell.

I welcomed the excuse. Alone at last in my chamber, weariness washed over me. Yet before sleep claimed me, I strained to recall the Third Dimension—the way a Cube arises from the motion of a Square. The image flickered, unclear. But one phrase echoed in my mind: *“Upward, yet not Northward.”* I clung to it, repeating it like a mantra, as if the words themselves held the key to understanding. And so, murmuring them again and again, I slipped into a deep, dreamless sleep.

But the dream came swiftly.

I found myself once more beside the Sphere, his light now soft and warm, no longer fierce with anger. We drifted together toward a tiny, shimmering point of light, glowing faintly in the vast emptiness. As we drew near, a faint hum emerged—like the buzz of a Spaceland fly, but softer, almost imperceptible, until we paused just under twenty human diagonals away.

"Look," said the Sphere. "In Flatland you have lived; in Lineland you have glimpsed; in Spaceland you have soared. Now, to complete your journey, I bring you down—to the lowest depth of existence, to Pointland, the realm of No Dimensions."

There, in the void, hovered a single speck—a Point, pulsing with quiet radiance. This creature, he told me, was a being like ourselves, yet trapped in absolute nothingness. He had no length, no breadth, no height. He was neither two nor many—he was only One, and that One was all. He could not know plurality, nor distance, nor time. He was, in fact, nothing at all. Yet he was perfectly content.

"Observe," said the Sphere, "how utterly satisfied he is. From this, learn this: self-contentment is ignorance. To aspire is better than to be blind and blissful."

Then, from the Point, a tiny, monotonous chime rose—like the voice of a distant phonograph—carrying these words:

"What does 'It' mean?" I asked.

"He means himself," replied the Sphere. "Have you not seen how infants, who cannot separate themselves from the world, speak of themselves in the third person? But hush—listen now."

The Point continued:
*"It fills all Space, and what It fills, It is. What It thinks, that It utters; and what It utters, that It hears. It is Thinker, Utterer, Hearer, Thought, Word, Audition. It is the One, and yet the All in All. Ah, the happiness—ah, the joy of Being!"*

"Can you not wake him from this delusion?" I urged. "Tell him the truth, as you told me. Show him how small his world is, and lift him higher!"

"It is not easy," said the Sphere. "Try."

So I raised my voice, shouting with all my might:
"Silence, you pitiful thing! You call yourself the All in All—but you are nothing! Your universe is a speck on a line, and a line is but a shadow compared to—"

"Hush!" the Sphere interrupted. "Listen—see what your words do."

The Point shone brighter than ever. Unmoved, he resumed his song:
*"Ah, the joy of Thought! What can It not achieve? Its own thought returns to Itself, suggesting its own worth—thus enhancing Its joy! Sweet rebellion that leads to triumph! Ah, the divine creative power of the All in One! Ah, the joy, the joy of Being!"*

"You see," said the Sphere gently, "your words only feed his pride. He takes them as his own, for he knows no other. He sees the variety of 'Its Thought' not as limitation, but as proof of greatness. Let us leave this King of Pointland to his blissful ignorance. Nothing we say can free him from his self-satisfaction."

As we drifted back toward Flatland, I heard the Sphere whispering wisdom into my ear—not scolding, but guiding. He confessed that once, he had scorned my ambition to reach beyond the Third Dimension. But now, he saw differently. He had learned. And he was not too proud to admit it.

Then, with growing warmth, he began to reveal wonders beyond even what I had seen. He showed me how Solids might move to form Extra-Solids, and how those might generate Double Extra-Solids—each step built upon analogy, each construction simple enough, perhaps, for even a woman to understand.

And as I floated home, still dreaming, I carried with me not only the vision of Pointland, but a new fire within me: the certainty that to strive, to question, to reach beyond the known—that is the true path of life.

Section 21. How I Tried to Teach the Theory of Three Dimensions to My Grandson, and with What Success

I awoke that morning filled with a radiant hope, my mind alight with purpose. The vision I had experienced—so vivid, so undeniable—had revealed truths beyond the flat confines of our world. I would not remain silent. I would go forth and preach the Gospel of the Third Dimension to every corner of Flatland. Women, soldiers, even the lowest of the low—none would be spared the light of this revelation. First, I thought, I would begin with my own wife. But just as I formed this plan, a sudden commotion broke the stillness outside.

Voices rose in unison, demanding silence. Then, a single voice rang out—a herald’s proclamation, sharp and clear. Listening closely, I recognized the words of the Council’s decree: any who spoke of other worlds, or claimed divine revelations from dimensions beyond ours, would face arrest, imprisonment, or worse. The law was absolute. To speak of the Third Dimension was now treason.

My heart sank. This was no mere warning—it was a death sentence for heresy. I could not risk open confession. Yet I could not abandon the truth. So I resolved to proceed not by revelation, but by reason—by demonstration. “Upward, not Northward,” I whispered to myself—the key to the proof. It had seemed plain enough before sleep took me, and when I first woke, it stood as clear as arithmetic. But now, in the cold light of day, the certainty wavered. Doubt crept in like a shadow.

Just then, my wife entered the room. We exchanged a few polite words—nothing more. I decided not to burden her with what I had seen. She was kind, but not gifted in mathematics, and her loyalty to tradition ran deep. I could not trust her with such dangerous knowledge.

My sons—pentagons, men of standing, respected physicians—were better suited, yet they too were bound by duty. Their reverence for the Circles outweighed their affection for me. If they suspected I preached the forbidden doctrine, they might turn me over to the Prefect without hesitation.

Then it struck me: a young mind, unburdened by prejudice, might be the perfect vessel. A boy—bright, curious, trained in geometry—could grasp the idea without fear. My grandson came to mind. He had once spoken of 3^3 with an insight that even the Sphere had praised. He was precocious, eager to learn, and utterly unaware of the new law.

So I turned to him. First, I needed to quiet my wife’s curiosity. She asked about the mysterious visit of the Circle, how he had entered the house. I gave her a tale—plausible, perhaps, but not entirely true. I omitted all mention of another dimension, of spheres, of upward motion. With careful words and a calm demeanor, I convinced her to return to her duties. Satisfied, I sent for my grandson.

When he arrived, I locked the door behind him. Seating myself beside him, I took out our mathematical tablets—lines, as you would call them—and began again. I showed him how a point, moving in one direction, becomes a line. Then how a line, moving across a plane, creates a square. I paused, then smiled faintly. “And now,” I said, “you clever boy—you told me once that a square, if moved ‘upward, not northward,’ could form a new shape—an extra square in three dimensions. Say that again. You little rascal.”

At that moment, the herald’s cry echoed through the street once more: “O yes! O yes!”—the proclamation repeated, loud and unmistakable.

My grandson froze. Though young, he was sharp. He understood the danger instantly. For a long moment, silence. Then, tears welling in his eyes, he leaned forward. “Dear Grandpapa,” he said, voice trembling, “that was only a joke. I didn’t mean anything by it. We didn’t know about the new law. I never said anything about the Third Dimension. And I certainly didn’t say ‘upward, not northward’—that’s absurd! How could something move upward and not northward? That makes no sense. Even if I were a baby, I wouldn’t be so foolish. Ha! ha! ha!”

“Not at all foolish,” I snapped, losing my temper. “Here—watch.” I seized a movable square lying nearby. “See—I take this square and move it—not northward—but *upward*—yes, upward—somewhere… not exactly like this, but somehow…” I waved the square wildly, unable to explain, unable to demonstrate.

He burst into laughter. “You’re not teaching me, Grandpapa—you’re joking!” he declared, unlocking the door and dashing out.

And so ended my first attempt to awaken a mind to the wonders of the third dimension.

Section 22. How I Then Tried to Diffuse the Theory of Three Dimensions by Other Means, and of the Result

My failure with my grandson left me disheartened but not defeated. I realized that relying solely on the phrase "Upward, not Northward" was insufficient—too abstract, too easily dismissed as nonsense. To truly spread the truth, I needed something more tangible: a demonstration, a clear exposition that could be understood even by those bound by the rigid laws of Flatland. And so, in solitude and silence, I devoted months to writing a treatise—a work I titled *Through Flatland to Thoughtland*—in which I sought to reveal the mysteries of the Third Dimension without directly defying the Law.

To avoid arrest or immediate condemnation, I did not speak of physical space or real dimensions. Instead, I constructed a metaphorical realm I called *Thoughtland*, a place of pure imagination where figures might look down upon our world and see through its surfaces, perceiving interiors at once. There, I suggested, one might imagine a being bounded by six squares and crowned with eight points—something beyond our experience, yet not entirely impossible to grasp. It was a delicate dance: conveying the essence of three-dimensional reality while cloaking it in philosophical disguise.

But the limitations of Flatland made my task nearly impossible. In our world, diagrams are drawn only in lines—straight, unbroken, confined to a single dimension. We cannot represent depth; we cannot show perspective. My treatise, though carefully reasoned, was littered with descriptions that could not be visualized. Words alone were not enough. Without the aid of true diagrams, I feared few would comprehend what I meant, or worse, they would dismiss it as madness.

And so, despite my efforts, I remained isolated. Life lost its color. Every sight now carried a secret burden—the knowledge of what lay beneath the surface, unseen by others. The flatness of buildings, the edges of men, the empty spaces between objects—all reminded me of the deeper truths I could not share. I neglected my duties, withdrew from society, and spent hours trying to reconstruct the vision of the Cube in my mind’s eye. Once, after eleven months of exile from Spaceland, I closed my eyes and tried to summon it again. But it slipped away—faint, distorted, uncertain. Though I eventually regained the image, I never felt certain it was the same as before. That doubt became a weight heavier than any prison wall.

Still, I could not remain silent. My spirit burned with conviction. Even when I knew danger surrounded me, I found myself slipping into forbidden speech. At gatherings of the highest circles, I would quote ancient sages who said prophets are always deemed mad. I spoke of “the eye that sees within,” of “the all-seeing land.” Once, twice, I let slip the words “Third Dimension” and “Fourth Dimension”—terms that, in Flatland, are not just heretical but treasonous.

Then came the final mistake. At a meeting of the Local Speculative Society—held in the very palace of the Prefect—some fool read a paper arguing that God had ordained two dimensions for good reason, and that omniscience belongs only to the Supreme Being. In response, I could no longer contain myself. I stood and recounted everything: my journey with the Sphere, our descent into Space, the Council Chamber transformed, the revelation of the Cube, the return home. At first, I claimed it was fiction—a tale of an imagined traveler. But my passion betrayed me. I dropped the pretense. I described the sights, the sounds, the logic behind it all. And then, in a fervent cry, I urged them to shed their prejudices and believe in the Third Dimension.

Needless to say, I was arrested.

The next morning, I stood before the Council, in the same chamber where the Sphere had once appeared beside me. I began my defense, uninterrupted—though I knew from the start what would follow. The President ordered the guards replaced: those of high angularity, trusted and respected, were removed and replaced with lower-class officers—men whose angles barely exceeded thirty degrees. I understood the meaning instantly. They were to be sacrificed. Their memories erased. My story would vanish with them. The state would ensure no witness remained.

After my plea, the President asked two questions:

1. Could I point to the direction meant by “Upward, not Northward”?
2. Could I describe or diagram the Cube, beyond mere numbers?

I answered neither. I said I could offer no further proof, only my faith in Truth. The President nodded. He agreed with my sentiment. “You do well,” he said. “Truth will prevail in time.”

And so I was sentenced to perpetual imprisonment. No torture, no cruelty—only silence, solitude, and the slow erosion of hope. I am allowed occasional visits from my brother, a man of integrity, intelligence, and kindness. He was there when the Sphere appeared. He saw the changing forms, heard the explanations. Yet after seven years of listening to my accounts—repeated week after week—he still does not understand. He refuses to believe in a Sphere. He calls it fantasy.

No convert has come from my suffering. No revolution has begun. I am the Prometheus of Flatland—bound not for stealing fire, but for bringing down nothing at all. For my people have no use for what I bring.

Yet sometimes, in moments of clarity, I hold onto a fragile hope: that these words, written in secret, may one day reach beyond this world. That somewhere—in a higher dimension, or in the minds of future rebels—they may awaken curiosity, spark rebellion, ignite a new way of seeing. That is my prayer.

But often, despair returns. I wonder if I remember the Cube correctly. Does it truly exist? Or is it merely a dream? In the dark, the phrase “Upward, not Northward” echoes like a riddle without answer. Sometimes, even the walls of my cell seem unreal. The tablets I write on, the air I breathe, the very ground beneath me—do they matter? Or are they all illusions, like the One-Dimensional Line or the Zero-Dimensional Point?

I endure. Not because I know the truth will win. But because I must believe it can.
