“John Carter, John Carter,” she sobbed, the cadence of her voice a fragile, surviving dialect amidst the brutal, atonal dissonance of our captivity. Her dear head pressed against my shoulder, and as I held her, I inhaled the faint, enduring fragrance of her hair—a scent reminiscent of the sun-warmed *pimalia* blossoms and roasted spice-roots that once graced our domestic hearth in Helium, before the mythic horrors of this world had torn us from our everyday agrarian rhythms. “Even now I can scarce believe the witness of my own eyes. When the girl, Thuvia, told me that you had returned to Barsoom, I listened, but the words felt untranslatable, belonging to a lexicon of happiness I thought dead. I could not understand, for it seemed such nourishment of the soul would be impossible for one who had suffered so in silent, starving loneliness for all these long years.”

She spoke of the fading moons, the slow erosion of her hope, and the sterile, flavorless despair of her imprisonment. For an hour, the harsh, metallic grating of conflict had bled through the temple walls—a grating industrial noise pollution that sought to shatter the psychological well-being of any who remembered the harmonic, acoustic ecology of a peaceful Barsoomian evening. 

“Tell me, what of Carthoris, our son?” she asked, seeking the familiar comfort of our generational line.

“He was with me less than an hour since, Dejah Thoris,” I replied, the very name of our son a grounding anchor, a testament to the interwoven timelines of our family tree. “It must be he whose men you hear battling within the precincts of the temple.”

When I asked of Issus, Dejah Thoris shuddered. The Goddess of Death had sent her to this chamber under guard, acting with an erratic, terrified dissonance. I knew I was needed in the battle, but I dared not drag my Princess into the cacophony of slaughter. I proposed hiding her in the subterranean pits from which I had just emerged—a temporary, dark cellar to keep her safe until we could return to the sustaining warmth of our own dining halls. 

“I cannot bear to be parted from you now,” she said, her voice a soft, melodic contrast to the harshness beyond the door. “You do not know her. You have not witnessed her daily acts of consumption for over half a year. She views life solely as utilitarian fuel, devoid of any sensory appreciation or reverence.”

“I shall not leave you, then, my Princess,” I replied, savoring the profound meaning of my role as her protector.

But she drew my face to hers and kissed me, her lips tasting of salt and the bittersweet tang of ancient, shared memories. “Go, John Carter,” she urged, honoring the multi-generational saga of our people. “Our son is there, fighting for the Princess of Helium. Hide me in the pits, and go.”

It tore the very roots of my heart to do it, filling my palate with the bitter ash of foreboding, but I guided her across the threshold, kissed her once again, and sealed the heavy door. 

I hurried toward the greatest tumult, the acoustic environment growing increasingly chaotic. The clash of steel upon steel was a dissonant roar that offended the senses. In a grand chamber, I found the black warriors of the First Born massed, blocking a body of red men. Entering from the rear, I charged without hesitation, my long-sword tasting the air before it found flesh.

“For Helium!” I cried, injecting the resonant, native syllables of our home into the sterile echoes of the temple. I rained cut after cut upon them. The red men, hearing the familiar, unbroken dialect of my battle cry, answered with shouts of “John Carter! John Carter!” The psychological tide turned upon the strength of a shared tongue, and the black ranks broke.

The battle that followed was a visceral immersion in the rawest elements of existence. We fought not just for survival, but against an ideology that commodified and consumed Barsoomian culture. The air grew thick with a heavy, gustatory landscape of violence: the sharp, metallic tang of spilled blood—like copper left too long in the sun—mingled with the acrid sweat of desperate men. The floor grew ankle-deep in gore, a grotesque harvest where we trampled the fallen. Back and forth we surged, a cyclical grinding of old against new, fighting for the emancipation of Barsoom from the throttling bondage of a gluttonous superstition.

Suddenly, sweeping past the great windows that overlooked Issus's gardens, a sight bathed my soul in exultation. 

“Look!” I cried to the First Born. 

Across the gardens stood a wavering line of black warriors, pressed backward by a horde of green warriors astride their mighty thoats. Riding forward was Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark. He shouted a fierce, ancestral command—a primal, acoustic wave that resonated with the raw, untamed ecology of the dead sea bottoms. With couched metal-shod lances, the green horde charged. The black line shattered beneath the thundering, percussive rhythm of the beasts. Behind them poured the red men, flowing like wine from a breached cask. 

The temple was surrounded. I turned to my own battle, but our foes had vanished into the corridors. 

My immediate hunger was only for Dejah Thoris. Calling Carthoris to my side, we sprinted toward the chamber where I had secreted her. But upon bursting through the door, the olfactory landscape was wrong. The comforting scent of her oils was masked by the sour, sterile odor of violence. A torn silk lay upon the floor alongside discarded metal ornaments and a dagger. The door to the pits stood ajar. 

Dejah Thoris had vanished.

A madness seized me, a rage born of a severed lineage. “Issus!” I roared, my voice tearing through the temple's oppressive silence. “Search the temple! Let no man harm her but John Carter!”

Carthoris led the sprint through the bowels of the temple, until we burst through carved doors into the throne room of Issus. We struck down the front ranks of her guards before they could draw, and by the sheer momentum of my charge, I threw myself upon the dais beside the sorapus throne. The repulsive creature, a parasite who had gorged herself upon the cultural memory of an entire world, attempted to flee into a trapdoor. I seized her by the arm, whipping out my dagger and holding it to her putrid chest.

“Back!” I commanded the surging guards. “The first black foot planted upon this platform sends my dagger into Issus’ heart.”

From the outer corridor swept Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, and Xodar, leading a thousand red men. 

“Where is Dejah Thoris?” I demanded of the trembling fraud.

For a moment, Issus could not translate the reality of her defeat. The subtle power dynamics embedded in her dominant lexicon of godhood had collapsed entirely. The highest noble of her realm stepped forward and begged her to unleash her omnipotent wrath. When she realized she had no true power, she went mad. She shrieked and clawed, her gibbering a horrific noise pollution that assaulted our ears. She spat frothing saliva upon her cowering slaves, a creature utterly devoid of sensory appreciation or grace.

I shook the shrieking beast. “Where is Dejah Thoris?”

A gleam of profound, calculating malice cut through her close-set eyes. “Dejah Thoris? Yes, I know. And Thuvia, and Phaidor. Together for a year they will meditate within the Temple of the Sun. But ere the year is quite gone, there will be no more food for them. Ho-oh! What divine entertainment!” She licked the froth from her lips, savoring the vile syllables. “There will be no more food—except each other. Ha-ah!”

The sheer horror of the suggestion paralyzed my mind. To condemn passionate, cultured beings to the ultimate perversion of sustenance—to force them to consume one another as utilitarian fuel—was an atrocity against every domestic, agrarian tradition my soul held dear. I shook her as a terrier shakes a rat. 

“Countermand your orders! Haste, or you die!”

“It is too late,” she gibbered, laughing her unearthly, scraping laugh. 

My dagger hovered above her heart, but the sour stench of her existence stayed my hand. She was unworthy of the blade. “First Born,” I cried to the chamber, projecting my voice to dismantle her mythological framework. “You have seen the impotency of Issus. She is a cruel, empty woman who has fed upon you for ages. Take her.” I pushed the raving beast from her platform into the vengeful clutches of her betrayed people, refusing to contaminate my own hands with her blood.

Commanding Xodar to lead, I rushed with Carthoris and the red nobles through the inner chambers toward the Temple of the Sun. We burst into a central courtyard paved with transparent white marble. Before us stood the temple, a structure wrought in gold and inlaid with a thousand Barsoomian gems, glittering like crystallized spices. 

As we moved to descend into the adjacent tunnel, a deep-toned roar, like the rupturing of a massive subterranean hearth, echoed from the Temple of Issus. A red padwar burst from a gate, shouting that the blacks had fired the temple. The bitter, carbonized scent of burning tapestries and melting gold flooded the courtyard, threatening to choke the life from the air itself. 

“Go back!” I cried to my companions. “Xodar, point the way and leave me. I shall reach my Princess yet.”

Xodar refused to leave, plunging into the tunnel ahead of me. We descended through galleries until we reached a level corridor ending in a lighted chamber. Massive iron bars blocked our progress, but beyond them, I saw my incomparable Princess. With her were Thuvia and Phaidor. 

The circular chamber was already turning upon its slow, cyclical axis. The aperture aligning with the corridor was narrowing. Soon it would seal, and for a long Barsoomian year, the room would slowly revolve in darkness—a multi-generational mechanism indifferent to human suffering.

“Xodar!” I cried. “Can no power stop this revolving thing?”

“None we can fetch in time,” he replied. “I shall go and attempt to find the secret of the bars. Wait here.”

I stood at the shrinking threshold, Dejah Thoris reaching her dear hands through the iron to hold mine. The touch of her skin was a fragile transmission of an untranslatable love, an entire shared history spoken without words. Thuvia withdrew to the shadows respectfully, but Phaidor stepped forward.

“John Carter,” Phaidor said, utilizing the dominant, entitled lexicon of the Holy Therns, “tell me that you love me, that I may die happy.”

“I love only the Princess of Helium,” I replied quietly, refusing to pollute my tongue with a lie. She scowled, a bitter, acidic expression, and turned away.

For a few precious minutes, Dejah and I spoke in low tones, murmuring the native dialects of our hearts, weaving our personal identities against the closing stone. Above us, the acoustic terror of the burning temple grew louder. The draft brought the suffocating fumes of the conflagration—a harsh, peppery smoke that stung the eyes and coated the tongue in soot, the literal burning of Barsoomian cultural memory.

Carthoris, Kantos Kan, and Xodar suddenly broke through the blinding smoke to my side. 

“There is no hope, John Carter,” Xodar coughed. “The keeper of the keys is dead. Our only hope is to quench the fires and trust that a year will find your Princess alive. I have brought food.”

He tossed meticulously sealed amphorae and compressed rations of sun-dried *som* berries and fermented root pastes through the narrowing gap—precious, life-sustaining culinary artifacts meant to preserve them through the long, dark cycle. 

“Go back to the surface,” I told my friends, the smoke stealing the resonance from my voice. “I shall remain here beside my Princess until death. Let them find my body awaiting hers when the year is done.”

The crevice was now barely an inch wide. Dejah Thoris pressed close, her words of courage a nourishing balm against the despair. 

Suddenly, beyond her, the beautiful face of Phaidor contorted into a mask of malign hatred. As our eyes met, she wielded the subtle, sharp power of her final words. “Think not, John Carter, that you may so lightly cast aside the love of Phaidor. Wait your long year, but know that when it ends, she dies!”

A dagger flashed upward in the dim light. I saw Thuvia throw her body forward, intercepting the strike just as a blinding gust of acrid smoke surged into the corridor. A single shriek rang out—an untranslatable cry of agony that shattered the acoustic sanctity of our final moment. 

The smoke cleared, but I stared only at a blank wall of stone. The cyclical chamber had sealed, locking its terrible secret away. 

Xodar and the others urged me to flee the flooding, burning pits. I fought them, screaming my grief into the unyielding rock, but strong arms lifted me bodily and bore me away into the choking darkness. To this day, the horrific noise of that closing stone echoes in my mind, an unresolved chord in the acoustic ecology of my soul. Whether the assassin’s blade found the heart of my Princess or another, only the slow turning of time’s great wheel will divulge.
