The autumnal mist swirled through the streets of Milton-Northern, shrouding the brick facades of the textile mills in a damp, grey veil. Inside the Thornton household, the atmosphere was no less opaque. Mr. Thornton's thoughts were consumed by the memory of Margaret Hale's words, their cadence echoing in his mind like the mournful refrain of a Scottish ballad. He couldn't shake the image of her standing beside another man, her eyes aglow with a warmth he had once believed reserved for him alone.

As he paced the parlour, his footsteps a steady beat against the cold stone floor, he grappled with the bitter taste of jealousy. It wasn't just the falsehood that rankled; it was the implication that she had been willing to deceive him, that she had been willing to hide her true feelings behind a mask of courtesy and obligation. The thought gnawed at him, a constant reminder of his own vulnerability.

His mother, seated in her favourite armchair, watched him with a mixture of concern and annoyance. "John, dear, can you sit still for a moment?" she asked, her voice a gentle entreaty. "I need to speak with you about something."

Mr. Thornton stopped his pacing and sank onto a nearby chair, his movements stiff and unyielding. "What is it, Mother?"

"I've spoken with Betsy," she began, her voice measured. "She's told me she must leave our employ. Her lover's passing has left her spirits shattered, and she feels she can no longer dedicate herself to her work."

Mr. Thornton's response was curt, a reflection of his own turmoil. "Very well. We can find another cook, I suppose."

His mother's expression was a mixture of disappointment and frustration. "That's all you can say, John? It's not just about the cooking, you know. Betsy has been with us for years, and she knows the inner workings of this household better than anyone. And besides, she's been a good friend to Margaret."

The name hung in the air, a challenge to Mr. Thornton's wounded pride. He felt the familiar sting of resentment, the conviction that Margaret's heart belonged to another. As he sat there, his eyes fixed on some distant point beyond the parlour window, he couldn't help but wonder: what would it take for her to choose him, truly choose him, above all others?
