After a fortnight of careful nursing, Mrs. Palmer had recovered sufficiently for her mother to resume her usual routine, no longer needing to dedicate every moment to her care. With only occasional visits to check on her daughter’s well-being, Mrs. Jennings returned to her own home and habits—her cheerful presence once again filling Berkeley Street with warmth and lively conversation. The Dashwood sisters welcomed the return of familiar rhythms, finding comfort in the quiet stability of their daily lives.

It was on the third or fourth morning after this renewed sense of normalcy that Mrs. Jennings burst into the drawing-room, her face flushed with urgency and excitement. Elinor, seated alone by the window, looked up as her friend approached with the air of one bearing a secret too immense to contain.

“Lord! my dear Miss Dashwood,” she exclaimed, “have you heard the news?”

“No, ma’am,” Elinor replied calmly. “What is it?”

“Something so astonishing!” Mrs. Jennings leaned forward, lowering her voice as if afraid even the walls might betray the tale. “When I arrived at Mr. Palmer’s, Charlotte was in such a state about the baby—crying, fretting, covered in little red spots. She was convinced it was seriously ill. But I took one look and said, ‘Lord! my dear, it’s nothing but the red gum.’ Nurse agreed. Still, Charlotte wouldn’t be placated, so Mr. Donavan was summoned—and luckily, he’d just come from Harley Street, so he came right over. As soon as he saw the child, he confirmed what we’d said: just the red gum. Charlotte relaxed immediately.”

Mrs. Jennings paused, gathering her breath before delivering the real news.

“But then—just as he was leaving—I suddenly thought to ask him if there was any other news. And do you know? He smirked, glanced around, and gave me this grave look. Then, in a whisper, he said, ‘For fear any unpleasant report reaches the young ladies under your care regarding their sister’s indisposition, I think it best to say that I believe there is no great cause for alarm; I hope Mrs. Dashwood will do very well.’”

Elinor’s heart skipped a beat. “Fanny ill?”

“That’s exactly what I said!” Mrs. Jennings clapped her hands. “‘Lord!’ I cried, ‘is Mrs. Dashwood unwell?’ And then—it all poured out. It seems that Edward Ferrars—the very young man I used to tease you about (though now, thank goodness, I’m glad nothing ever came of it)—has been secretly engaged to my cousin Lucy Steele for over a year!”

She leaned back, eyes wide. “Can you imagine it? Not a soul knew—except poor Nancy, who couldn’t keep her mouth shut. They were both so careful, afraid of Mrs. Ferrars, and neither your brother nor sister had the faintest idea until today. And then—poor Nancy, always meaning well but not much of a thinker—she blurted it all out. She thought, ‘They adore Lucy; they’ll have no objections,’ and off she went to tell Fanny, who was sitting quietly at her carpet-work, completely unaware of what was coming. Just five minutes earlier, she’d been telling your brother she hoped to match Edward with some nobleman’s daughter—can you believe it?”

Elinor felt her breath catch. She could only imagine the devastation.

“She fell into violent hysterics,” Mrs. Jennings continued, “screams so loud they reached your brother’s ears as he sat downstairs writing to his steward. He rushed upstairs, and chaos broke loose. By then, Lucy had already arrived, blissfully ignorant of the storm brewing. Poor thing! I feel so sorry for her. Your sister scolded like a fury, drove Lucy into a fainting fit, and Nancy fell to her knees, sobbing. Your brother paced the room, helpless. Mrs. Dashwood declared they must leave immediately, and he had to beg on his knees just to let them stay long enough to pack. Then *she* collapsed again—hysterics, again—and he sent for Mr. Donavan. When he arrived, the house was in ruins. The carriage waited at the door, and they were stepping inside when he came. Lucy, he says, could barely walk. Nancy was nearly as bad.”

Mrs. Jennings shook her head. “I can’t stand your sister. I truly hope this marriage goes through despite her. Think of poor Edward—how shattered he’ll be when he hears! To have his love treated so cruelly! They say he’s deeply fond of her—no wonder. I wouldn’t be surprised if he flies into a rage. Mr. Donavan agrees. And the best part? He’s gone straight back to Harley Street, ready to attend Mrs. Ferrars when she’s told. She was summoned as soon as the cousins left. Your sister was certain *she* would fall apart too. Well, maybe she will—but I don’t care. I’ve no patience for people making such a fuss over money and status. Why shouldn’t Edward marry Lucy? Mrs. Ferrars can afford to provide for her son. Lucy may have next to nothing, but she knows how to make the most of what she has. If Mrs. Ferrars gives him five hundred a year, Lucy will live as well as anyone with eight. They could settle in a cottage like yours—or slightly larger—with two maids and two men. And I might even help them find a housemaid—Betty’s sister is looking for work, and she’d suit perfectly.”

Mrs. Jennings paused, catching her breath. Elinor, having collected herself, responded with measured calm, offering thoughtful observations without betraying her inner turmoil. She was relieved—not to be suspected of lingering feelings for Edward, nor to have Marianne present to witness her composure. For the first time in months, she could speak freely, with dignity and fairness.

She found herself torn between pity for Edward and disdain for Lucy, though she forced herself to acknowledge the latter’s merit. Yet she could not bring herself to admire her. Her thoughts turned instead to the inevitable outcome—Edward and Lucy united, against all odds.

But she knew Marianne would not hear such news lightly. It was her duty to prepare her sister, to reveal the truth gently, to shield her from further pain. She would have to break the news, knowing it would shatter Marianne’s hopes and stir deep resentment.

The task was painful. She was about to remove what she believed was Marianne’s greatest comfort—her belief in Edward’s devotion—and replace it with a harsh reality that would likely destroy her regard for him forever. Worse still, she feared Marianne would see parallels between their situations, reliving her own heartbreak all over again.

Yet Elinor resolved to act with honesty and grace. She did not wish to dwell on her own sorrow, nor to appear weak. Instead, she spoke plainly, simply, her voice steady despite the emotion beneath. She described the engagement, its length, and the circumstances—without melodrama, without despair.

Marianne listened in silence at first, then burst into tears. Her grief was immediate and overwhelming. Elinor, ever the anchor, offered reassurance—calm words, firm assurances of Edward’s character, defending him against blame except for imprudence. But Marianne refused to accept it.

“Edward is just like Willoughby!” she cried. “And you—*you* loved him truly. How could you bear it?”

Elinor met her gaze steadily. “I did love him. But I loved others more than myself. I kept silent because I owed it to Lucy, to our family, to you—to spare you all from suffering I could not ease.”

Marianne stared, stunned. “Four months? You’ve known for four months? While I was sick, while I accused you of being happy—*you* carried this burden?”

“I could not tell you,” Elinor said softly. “It would have destroyed you. And I could not betray my promise to Lucy. I had to pretend indifference, while my heart broke. I endured her triumph, her repeated joy in my misfortune. I knew Edward was lost to me—yet I found no fault in him. Nothing proved him unworthy. Nothing showed he cared less for me. I suffered the consequences of love without its rewards. And all this, while I was already grieving for you.”

Marianne fell silent, her anger melting into shame. “Oh, Elinor,” she whispered, “I have been cruel. You’ve been my only comfort, and I’ve repaid you with bitterness. I cannot forgive myself.”

Tears streamed down her cheeks as she embraced her sister. In that moment, Elinor felt a rare peace. She asked Marianne to promise never to speak ill of Lucy, to meet her with kindness, and even to greet Edward with the same warmth as before—if fate brought them together.

Marianne agreed, her voice trembling but sincere.

The next morning, their brother arrived, his face grave. He sat down heavily and began, “You’ve heard, I suppose, about the terrible discovery made yesterday?”

They nodded, unable to speak.

“Your sister has suffered terribly,” he continued. “And Mrs. Ferrars—oh, it’s been a scene of unbearable distress. But I hope we’ll weather it. Poor Fanny—she was in hysterics all day. But Donavan says there’s no serious danger. Her constitution is strong, and her spirit unshakable. She says she’ll never trust anyone again—after such betrayal, such ingratitude! She had invited these girls out of kindness, thinking them harmless, pleasant companions. We had wanted to invite you and Marianne, but she insisted on hosting them. And now—this reward! ‘I wish,’ she said, ‘with all my heart, we had asked your sisters instead.’”

He paused, expecting gratitude, which came swiftly.

“And Mrs. Ferrars?” Elinor asked.

“Her reaction was worse,” John said. “When Fanny told her, she was devastated. She’d been planning a grand future for Edward—had imagined him marrying Miss Morton, with a fortune of thirty thousand pounds. She never dreamed he’d been secretly engaged to someone else. ‘There,’ she said, ‘I thought I was safe.’ She was in agony. We discussed what to do, and she decided to send for Edward.”

He hesitated. “He came. But I regret to say… nothing worked. All of Mrs. Ferrars’s arguments—about duty, about wealth, about his future—fell on deaf ears. She offered the Norfolk estate, clear of taxes, worth a thousand a year. Then twelve hundred. She promised him his two thousand pounds, but warned him she would never see him again. She vowed to block his career if he chose this path. Yet Edward stood firm. He refused to break his word.”

Marianne gasped. “Gracious God—could he really be so stubborn?”

“He was,” John said. “He said little, but everything he said was resolute. Nothing would make him give up his engagement.”

Mrs. Jennings, unable to hold back, interrupted. “Then he’s acted like a man of honour! I beg your pardon, Mr. Dashwood, but if he hadn’t, I’d have called him a coward. Lucy is my cousin, and she’s one of the kindest girls alive—deserves a good husband.”

John blinked, surprised, but replied with polite restraint. “I wouldn’t speak ill of any relation of yours, madam. Miss Steele is undoubtedly a fine young woman. But in this case, the match is impossible. A secret engagement with a man under her uncle’s care—especially one whose mother is so wealthy—seems rather extraordinary. Still, I don’t mean to criticize anyone you care for. We all wish her happiness. Mrs. Ferrars has acted with dignity and generosity. Edward has chosen his path, and I fear it will be a difficult one.”

Marianne sighed, her eyes distant.

“Well, sir,” Mrs. Jennings asked, “how did it end?”

“With a complete rupture,” John said. “Edward is disowned. He left yesterday. Where he is now, or whether he’s still in town, I don’t know. We cannot inquire.”

“Poor young man,” Mrs. Jennings murmured. “What will become of him?”

“What indeed?” John echoed. “It’s heartbreaking. Born to such prosperity—now reduced to living on the interest of two thousand pounds. And consider—within three months, he could have had two thousand five hundred a year. I can’t imagine a more miserable fate. We all feel for him. But there’s nothing we can do.”

“I’d welcome him at my house,” Mrs. Jennings declared. “Bed and board—anyone would be welcome. He shouldn’t be scraping by in lodgings.”

Elinor smiled faintly, touched by the gesture—even if delivered with Mrs. Jennings’s usual bluntness.

“If he’d only done what his friends wished,” John said, “he’d be in his rightful place, wanting for nothing. But now—there’s nothing anyone can do. And there’s one more blow—hearing that Mrs. Ferrars intends to settle the estate on Robert immediately, rather than wait for Edward’s conditions. I left her this morning with her lawyer, finalizing the details.”

Mrs. Jennings scoffed. “That’s her revenge. Everyone has their way. But I wouldn’t punish one son just because another made me angry.”

Marianne rose, pacing slowly. “How galling,” John continued, “for a man to see his younger brother inherit what should have been his own. Poor Edward. I truly feel for him.”
