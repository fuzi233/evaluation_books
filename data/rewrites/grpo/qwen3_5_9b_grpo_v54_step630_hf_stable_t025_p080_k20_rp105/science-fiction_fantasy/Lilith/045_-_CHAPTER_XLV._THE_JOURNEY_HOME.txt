The darkness had lifted, replaced by a twilight so profound it felt like a second dawn. We walked through this dimness, inhaling the very breath of the spring, as a wondrous transformation settled over the world—or perhaps, more marvelously, within us. There was no sunlight to illuminate the path, yet every heather bush, every shrub, every blade of grass stood in perfect, radiant clarity. They glowed either from within, like the fire Moses witnessed in the desert, or reflected a light emanating from our own eyes. Shadows were nonexistent; instead, all things exchanged a gentle luminescence. Each living thing revealed its inner essence, its guiding thought, through its shape and color. My bare feet seemed to embrace the plants beneath them. The boundary between the microcosm and the macrocosm had dissolved; my life and the world's life were one. To perceive anything was to instantly know its existence and my own, to realize that we are all what we are because Another exists as He is. Senses long dormant awoke in me—sensations too vast for words, too pure for imagination. My consciousness expanded endlessly, a peaceful ocean upon which the swell of living joy raised new waves, yet remaining eternally the same joy in countless forms. Life was a cosmic holiday.

I knew now that life and truth were identical; that pure life is bliss itself, and where being lacks bliss, it is merely life-in-death. Every gust of the dark wind became a sigh of gratitude. At last, I existed fully, untouchable by harm. My darling walked beside me, and together we journeyed home to the Father.

What we possessed before the first sun rose upon our freedom was immense; what must the eternal day bring?

We arrived at the hollow where the earth's monsters once wallowed. As I had dreamed, it was now a lovely lake. Gazing into its pellucid depths, I saw a whirlpool had scourged away the soil where the abortions had burrowed, leaving the entire horrid brood visible at the bottom. A dim greenish light permeated the crystal water, illuminating every hideous form. Coiled in spires, folded in layers, or extended long and large, they weltered in motionless heaps—shapes more fantastic in their ghastly dismay than any fevered poet could conjure. Even the diver in the Maelstrom would find them incomparable in horror: tentacular convolutions, tumid bulges, glaring orbs of sepia deformity. Every head resembled a wicked flower bursting from an abominable stalk, perfecting its evil significance.

None moved as we passed, yet they were not dead. As long as men and women of unwholesome minds exist, this lake will remain populated with loathsomeness.

Then came the herald of the sun, the auroral wind, softly trumpeting his arrival. The master-minister of the human tabernacle was at hand. Heaving a huge, ripple-fretted wave of crimson and gold before his prow, he rushed aloft, as if newly launched from the Maker's hand into the upper sea. He paused, looking down upon the world—a white-raving storm of molten metals, yet nothing more than a coal from the altar of the Father's endless sacrifice to His children. See how every little flower straightened its stalk, lifted its neck, and stood expectant with outstretched head. Something greater than the sun, greater than light, was coming. None the less surely coming that the road was long. What mattered today, tomorrow, or ten thousand years to Life itself, to Love itself? He was coming, and the necks of all humanity were stretched to witness it. Every morning they would outstretch themselves; every evening they would droop in waiting—until He came. Was this but an air-drawn vision? Would He indeed find them watching thus when He arrived?

It was a glorious resurrection morning, the night spent in preparation for it.

The children gambolled ahead, followed by the beasts. Fluttering butterflies and darting dragonflies hovered and shot hither and thither around our heads, a cloud of colors and flashes. Sometimes they descended like a snowstorm of rainbow flakes; at other times, they rose into the humid air like a rolling vapor of embodied odors. It was a summer day more ideal than any man who had not died had ever known. I walked on the new earth under the new heaven, finding them the same as before, except now they opened their minds to me, and I saw into theirs. The soul of everything I met came forth to greet me and make friends, telling me we came from the same source and meant the same. "We are going to Him," they seemed to say, "with whom we always were, and whom we always meant." They were lightnings taking shape as they flashed from Him to His. The dark rocks drank the showering rays like sponges; the great world soaked up the light and sent out the living. Two joy-fires burned in Lona and me. Earth breathed heavenward her sweet-savoured smoke; we breathed homeward our longing desires. Our very consciousness was an act of thanksgiving.

We reached the channels, once so dry and weary, now running, flashing, and foaming with living water that shouted in its gladness. Far as the eye could see, it was a rushing, roaring, dashing river made vocal by its rocks.

We did not cross it but walked in glory and joy up its right bank until we reached the great cataract at the foot of the sandy desert, where the river roared and swirled, dropping sheer to divide into two branches. There we climbed the height—and found no desert. Through grassy plains, between grassy banks, flowed the deep, wide, silent river, full to the brim. For the first time, the glory of God was revealed to the Little Ones in the limpid flow of the water. Instinctively, they plunged and swam, and the beasts followed them.

The desert rejoiced and blossomed like a rose. Wide forests had sprung up, their undergrowth filled with flowering shrubs and songbirds. Every thicket gave birth to a rivulet, and every rivulet sang its water-song.

The place of the buried hand gave no sign. Beyond and still beyond, the river came in full volume from afar. Up and up we went, now along the grassy margin, now through forests of gracious trees. The grass grew sweeter, its flowers more lovely and varied; the trees grew larger, and the wind fuller of messages.

At length, we arrived at a forest whose trees were greater, grander, and more beautiful than any we had seen. Their living pillars upheaved a thick, embowed roof, between whose leaves and blossoms hardly a sunbeam filtered. Into the rafters of this aerial vault, the children climbed, scrambling and leaping in a land of bloom, shouting to the unseen elephants below and hearing them trumpet their replies. Lona understood these conversations while I guessed at them blunderingly. The Little Ones chased the squirrels, who frolicked and drew them on, always eventually allowing themselves to be caught and petted. Often, a bird, lovely in plumage and form, would light upon one of them, sing a song of what was coming, and fly away. Not one monkey of any sort could they see.
