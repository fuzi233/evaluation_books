The beginning of any acquaintance—whether with people or things—is the moment when our ignorance takes on a shape, however faint.

Gwendolen had long anticipated Mr. Grandcourt’s introduction; yet when Lord Brackenshaw stepped aside to make way for him, and she found herself face to face with the real man, a sudden tremor passed through her. Her cheeks flushed, and the flush deepened as she became aware of it—an unwelcome warmth that seemed to betray her. The surprise came not from his presence, but from how utterly he defied her expectations. Grandcourt was slightly taller than she, their eyes meeting on equal ground. There was no smile on his face, no trace of self-consciousness in his posture. When he lifted his hat, his broad forehead revealed a wide expanse of baldness bordered by a thin fringe of reddish-blond hair—but his hand, raised in greeting, was perfectly formed. His features, clean-shaven from brow to chin, were strikingly regular, with only slight deviations from symmetry. Even his narrow whiskers followed a straight line. His expression carried no hint of grimace or nervous tension—indeed, it was almost unnervingly still. He looked neither animated nor bored, merely… present. Unlike the stiff Englishman who stands rigid after a bow, radiating suppressed energy, Grandcourt seemed drained of motion. His complexion was pale, like an actress stripped of makeup—faded, translucent. His long, narrow gray eyes held no emotion, only a quiet indifference.

To describe a person fully at first glance is impossible. We glimpse the outline, not the whole. We recognize the alphabet, but not yet the language. Gwendolen saw only this much: *He is not ridiculous.* That was enough for now.

Lord Brackenshaw vanished, and conversation began—though it felt more like an exchange of glances. Grandcourt fixed his gaze on her steadily, unchanging, while she glanced back only occasionally, her observations softened by a touch of coquetry. After each of her responses, silence fell—longer or shorter—before he spoke again.

“I used to think archery was a bore,” he said, voice smooth but broken by a slow drawl, as if speaking through a chest cold.

“Are you converted today?” Gwendolen asked.

(Pause. She imagined all manner of thoughts he might be forming about her.)

“Yes,” he replied. “Since I saw you shoot. In such matters, one usually sees people missing—and simpering.”

“I suppose you’re a fine shot with a rifle?”

(Pause. Gwendolen quickly sketched him in her mind for some invisible listener.)

“I’ve stopped shooting.”

“Oh,” she said, “then you’re formidable. People who do something once and then abandon it make others feel trivial—as though we’re wearing outdated fashions. I hope you haven’t given up all your follies, because I indulge in many.”

(Pause. She mentally reviewed her own words, wondering if they sounded too bold.)

“What do you call follies?”

“Well,” she answered, “in general, anything agreeable is considered a folly. But you haven’t given up hunting, I hear.”

(Pause. She recalled what she’d heard of Grandcourt’s standing and decided—he was the most aristocratic-looking man she’d ever seen.)

“One must do something.”

“And do you care for the turf? Or has that too been abandoned?”

(Pause. She mused: perhaps a man so cool and reserved would make a better husband than others—less likely to interfere with her choices.)

“I run a horse now and then. But I don’t get involved in it the way some men do. Do you enjoy horses?”

“Yes, indeed. Nothing makes me feel more alive than galloping across open land. I forget everything. I just feel strong and free.”

(Pause. She wondered whether he approved of her honesty, but resolved not to hide her nature.)

“Do you like danger?”

“I don’t know,” she admitted. “When I’m riding, I don’t think of danger. It feels as though, even if I broke my bones, I wouldn’t feel it. I’d charge at anything that stood in my path.”

(Pause. In her mind, she rode through an entire hunting season with two favorite steeds.)

“You might enjoy tiger-hunting or pig-sticking. I tried both for a few seasons in the East. Everything here pales in comparison.”

“So *you* enjoy danger?”

(Pause. She reflected: perhaps those who seem coldest are actually the most daring. And she felt a flicker of pride in her own insight.)

“One must have something,” he said. “But one grows accustomed to it.”

“I begin to think I’m lucky,” she said. “Everything is new to me. That’s the only thing I can’t get enough of. I’m not used to anything—except boredom. And I’d like to leave that behind, just as you left off shooting.”

(Pause. A thought crossed her mind: perhaps such a man would be dull company. But then she reminded herself—most people were dull. Husbands weren’t companions, really. And anyway, she wasn’t going to accept Grandcourt.)

“Why are you bored?”

“This place is dreadful. There’s nothing to do. That’s why I took up archery.”

(Pause. She realized that an unmarried woman with no freedom and no resources would inevitably grow dull, no matter how high her station.)

“You’ve made yourself queen of it,” he said. “I imagine you’ll win first prize.”

“I don’t know. I have strong rivals. Didn’t you notice how well Miss Arrowpoint shot?”

(Pause. She thought: men have chosen others—not the woman they admired most. She recalled several such stories from novels.)

“Miss Arrowpoint. No—that is, yes.”

“Shall we go see the scores now? Everyone’s moving toward the other end. Shall we join them? I think my uncle is looking at me. He may want me.”

She turned the moment into action—more for herself than for anyone else. The tête-à-tête hadn’t been unpleasant, but it had stirred something unfamiliar in her: a lingering flush, a sense of being unsettled. She didn’t want Grandcourt to assume she valued him highly, or that she was waiting for his attention. Others might speculate about him as a match, but she wouldn’t let him believe she was at his mercy.

Later, Mr. Gascoigne told her, “You just missed the gold arrow, Gwendolen. Miss Juliet Fenn scored eight points higher.”

“I’m glad to hear it,” she replied lightly. “I’d have felt too selfish—taking the best of everything.”

It was impossible to envy Juliet Fenn. She was ordinary in every way except for her archery—and her plainness, which echoed her father’s: underhung jaw, receding brow, like a fish of greater intelligence. (One might wonder: if men truly cared about women’s appearance, should they not examine themselves honestly in the mirror? For a man’s own ugliness could easily outweigh a woman’s beauty, even if she were prettier than he.)

Now the crowd stirred, voices mingling in lively exchanges. Gwendolen watched—curious now about Grandcourt’s interactions with others. He was being introduced to a middle-aged man with a dark, full face and thick hands—Klesmer, whom she recognized vaguely. Klesmer seemed at ease with both men, speaking animatedly—gesturing widely, stretching fingers out, pointing downward, folding arms, tossing his mane. Grandcourt listened impassively, eyes narrowed, left hand in his waistcoat pocket, right finger brushing his thin whisker.

*Which style does Miss Arrowpoint prefer?* Gwendolen wondered, a mocking twist playing on her lips. But she refused to dwell on it, instead turning her full attention to those around her. She was determined not to care whether Grandcourt approached her again.

Yet he did. At the moment he offered to escort Mrs. Davilow to her carriage, he paused and said, “Shall we meet again in the ballroom?”

She nodded. “Yes.” His reply came with the same slow drawl, the same calm gravity.

“You were wrong this time, Gwendolen,” Mrs. Davilow said during their short ride back to the castle.

“In what, mamma?”

“About Mr. Grandcourt’s looks and manners. You can’t find anything ridiculous in him.”

“I suppose I could if I tried,” Gwendolen retorted, a little sharply. “But I don’t want to.”

Her mother wisely said no more.

At dinner, the ladies and gentlemen dined separately—a tradition meant to offer relief and ease. The gentlemen traded tales of the ladies’ supposed taste for venison, especially the fat, which they claimed proved the alarming speed at which corruption might spread among women, unchecked by society. Every year, Lord Brackenshaw—himself a connoisseur—would quote Byron: “A woman should never be seen eating,” adding, confidentially, “The fact is…” as if confessing a long-held belief.

In the ladies’ dining room, Gwendolen was not universally liked. No close friendships formed between her and the other girls. Conversations flowed around her rather than to her. Perhaps it was because she showed little interest in them—when left alone, she felt the emptiness of unoccupied seats. Mrs. Vulcany once remarked that Miss Harleth was too fond of the gentlemen. But Gwendolen wasn’t fond of them—she craved their admiration. And women rarely gave her that.

The exception was Miss Arrowpoint, who often positioned herself near Gwendolen, speaking quietly and kindly.

“She knows,” Gwendolen thought, “as I do, that our friends will fight over a husband. And she refuses to enter the fray.”

“I think Miss Arrowpoint has the best manners I’ve ever seen,” Mrs. Davilow said later, in the dressing room, where Gwendolen, Anna, and Mrs. Gascoigne were preparing for the ball.

“I wish I were like her,” Gwendolen said.

“Why? Are you becoming discontented with yourself, Gwen?”

“No. But I’m discontented with things. She seems content.”

“I’m sure you ought to be satisfied today. You enjoyed the archery, didn’t you?”

“Oh, that’s over,” Gwendolen sighed, stretching her arms upward. “And I don’t know what comes next.”

She was bare-armed now—dancing in her archery dress, jacket discarded. Her white cashmere gown, trimmed with pale green, framed her figure beautifully. A thin gold chain circled her neck, a star pinned to her breast. Her soft hair, piled high in a regal crown, outlined her brow clearly. Sir Joshua Reynolds would have loved to paint her—though he’d have had an easier task than history, needing only to capture one perfect moment, not the shifting truth of a life.

“The dancing comes next,” Mrs. Davilow said. “You’ll enjoy it.”

“I’ll only dance in the quadrille,” Gwendolen replied. “I told Mr. Clintock so. I won’t waltz or polka with anyone.”

“Why suddenly say that?”

“I can’t bear having ugly people so close.”

“Who do you mean?”

“Oh, plenty.”

“Mr. Clintock, for instance, isn’t ugly.” Mrs. Davilow dared not mention Grandcourt.

“Well, I hate woolen cloth touching me.”

“Fancy!” Mrs. Davilow whispered to her sister, who had joined them. “Gwendolen says she won’t waltz or polka.”

“She’s rather whimsical,” Mrs. Gascoigne observed gravely. “It would be more proper for her to behave like other young ladies on such an occasion—especially since she’s had excellent dancing lessons.”

“Why should I dance if I don’t like it, aunt? It’s not in the catechism.”

“My dear!” Mrs. Gascoigne said sharply. Anna looked alarmed. But no one pressed further.

Something had shifted in Gwendolen since the archery field. Yet she looked radiant under the chandeliers of the ballroom, where soft light and floral scents calmed the air. She was clearly the center of attention—every eligible man eager to partner her. Each she accepted was left in mild despair that she refused to waltz or polka.

“Are you under a vow, Miss Harleth?”
“Why are you so cruel to us all?”
“You waltzed with me in February.”
“And you who dance so perfectly!”

These remarks stung with irony. The women who danced assumed she was simply being difficult. But her uncle, overhearing her refusal, defended her: “Gwendolen usually has good reasons.” He admired her distinction—and wished her to remain distinct. The archery ball was meant to be subdued, respectful of all ranks. Dancing, in his view, was too wild, too frivolous.

Among the disappointed suitors, Grandcourt was silent. After dancing a quadrille with Miss Arrowpoint, he appeared content to stay with her. Gwendolen watched him often, but he never approached her. Mr. Gascoigne spoke with him occasionally—but Mr. Gascogne was everywhere.

Still, Gwendolen noticed something: whenever she danced, Grandcourt subtly repositioned himself so he could see her. If he didn’t admire her—so much the worse for him.

This movement became more obvious late in the evening, when she danced with Klesmer. As they walked, Klesmer said, “Mr. Grandcourt is a man of taste. He likes to watch you dance.”

“Perhaps he likes to look at what goes against his taste,” she laughed lightly. “Maybe he’s tired of admiration and enjoys disgust for variety.”

“Those words are unsuitable for your lips,” Klesmer said sternly, frowning. He waved his hand as if dismissing the discord.

“Are you as critical of words as of music?”

“Certainly. I demand your words match your face and form—always part of noble music.”

“That’s both a compliment and a correction. Thank you. But do you know—I’d like to correct *you*, and ask you to understand a joke?”

“One may understand jokes without liking them,” Klesmer replied. “I’ve received opera books full of them. I understood them—so I disliked them. Comic people challenge me: ‘You don’t see the wit, sir?’ ‘No, sir—but I see what you meant.’ Then I’m labeled ‘without esprit.’ But truly,” he added, dropping into reflection, “I am very sensitive to wit and humor.”

“I’m glad you told me that,” Gwendolen said, mischief in her tone. But Klesmer was already lost in thought. She kept her amusement to herself.

“Who’s that near the card-room door?” she asked, spotting the same stranger who’d spoken with Klesmer earlier.

“A friend of mine? No. An amateur I’ve met in town—Lush, Mr. Lush. Too fond of Meyerbeer and Scribe—too fond of the mechanical-dramatic.”

“Thanks. Was his face and form worthy of noble music in speech?”

Klesmer smiled—delightfully, unexpectedly. They were friendly until she asked to be returned to her mother.

Three minutes later, her assumptions were shattered. Turning to speak to her mother, she saw Grandcourt beside her.

“May I ask if you’re tired of dancing, Miss Harleth?” he began, his gaze steady, expression unchanged.

“Not at all.”

“Would you do me the honor—the next quadrille—or another?”

“I should have been happy,” she said, glancing at her card, “but I’m engaged to Mr. Clintock for the next—and in fact, I’m booked for every quadrille. I have none left to give.”

She wasn’t sorry to punish his delay—but she also wanted to dance with him. She smiled warmly as she spoke, and he remained still, staring down at her, no smile in return.

“I’m unfortunate to be too late,” he said after a pause.

“It seemed you didn’t care for dancing,” she said. “I thought it was another thing you’d left off.”

“Yes,” he replied. “But I haven’t begun dancing with you. You make dancing new—as you made archery.”

“Is novelty always pleasant?”

“No. Not always.”

“Then I don’t know whether to feel flattered or not. Once you’ve danced with me, there’ll be no more novelty.”

“On the contrary,” he said. “There would probably be more.”

“That’s deep. I don’t understand.”

“It’s difficult to make Miss Harleth understand her power?” Grandcourt turned to Mrs. Davilow, who smiled gently at her daughter.

“I think she’s rarely seen as slow to grasp.”

“Mamma,” Gwendolen said, teasingly, “I’m adorably stupid. I need everything explained—when the meaning is pleasing.”

“If you’re stupid,” Grandcourt replied, after a pause, “then stupidity is adorable.” He spoke without inflection, but clearly, he knew exactly what to say.

“I begin to think my cavalier has forgotten me,” Gwendolen said after a while, seeing the quadrille forming.

“He deserves to be abandoned,” Grandcourt said.

“I think he’s quite pardonable,” she replied.

“There must have been a misunderstanding,” Mrs. Davilow said. “Mr. Clintock was too concerned about the engagement to forget it.”

But Lady Brackenshaw arrived. “Miss Harleth,” she said, “Mr. Clintock sent me to express his deep regret that he had to leave without dancing with you again. An urgent message came from his father, the archdeacon. Something important. He was to depart immediately. He was _au désespoir_.”

“Oh,” Gwendolen said, “he was kind to remember the engagement. I’m sorry he had to go.”

She was polite, even joyful—on such a favorable occasion.

“Then I may profit from Mr. Clintock’s misfortune?” Grandcourt asked. “May I hope you’ll allow me to take his place?”

“I shall be very happy to dance the next quadrille with you.”
