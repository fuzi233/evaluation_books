In the spring of eighteen hundred and forty-nine I was wandering in the East, living as travellers do—by altered plans, by improvised camps, by the daily arithmetic of water-skins and baggage, and by that thin domestic comfort which a well-ordered tent can still contrive in a wilderness. My route had shifted since the last letters I had caused to be sent to me; and the change obliged me to despatch one of my servants to the English consul in a city which my revised scheme no longer touched. He was to fetch my correspondence and remittances, and to rejoin me at a named place and time.

An accident, for which the man was in no way answerable, detained him on his errand. For a week we waited on the desert’s edge—camped where the sand began, and the last stubborn scrub clung to its stony ground. The days had a hard, bright silence to them; the nights filled, not with music, but with the uneasy little sounds of a temporary household: canvas snapping, a kettle’s lid ticking as it cooled, the dry scrape of sand against tent-pegs, and—beyond all—an immense hush that made each human movement feel like an intrusion. Even the men spoke lower than usual, as if the land demanded it. I remember how the clink of a spoon on a metal cup travelled; how a servant’s cough seemed to stain the air; how the nearest camel’s slow chewing sounded like machinery in a cathedral.

At the end of that week the missing man appeared at the tent-door with my money and my letters.

“I am afraid I bring you bad news, sir,” he said; and, among the packets, he pointed to one with a mourning border, addressed in Mr. Bruff’s hand.

There is nothing, in calamities of this sort, so unendurable as suspense. I broke the seal of the black-edged letter first.

It told me my father was dead, and that I was heir to his great fortune. The wealth came with its weight of obligation. Mr. Bruff entreated me to return at once. By daybreak the next morning I was on my way home.

If my old friend Betteredge has painted me, at the time of my departure from England, a shade too fantastically—if, in his honest quaintness, he has credited one of my cousin’s satirical allusions with serious truth, and persuaded himself he actually saw French, German, and Italian “sides” of me that existed nowhere but in his own imagination—I can forgive the over-colouring. In one point he did not exceed the truth. I had been wounded to the heart by Rachel’s treatment of me, and I had left England in the rawest keenness of the bitterest disappointment of my life.

I went abroad resolved to forget her, if change and absence could serve me. Those who deny that they help do not read human nature with any accuracy. They force the mind to turn outward; they compel a man, by sheer multiplicity of new objects, new faces, new voices, to look away from the sore place in himself. I never forgot Rachel. But the remembrance, in time, lost its sharpest venom. Little by little, distance interposed; novelty laid its cool hand on my fever; other landscapes, other habits, other tongues made a screen between her and me.

And yet, with the first act of turning homeward, the screen began to thin. The nearer I drew to England—the nearer to the streets where English speech ran quick and careless in the mouth, and the old familiar noises returned (wheels on paving-stones, the iron cough of an engine, the shout of porters, the very advertising cries that cheapened everything they touched)—the more irresistibly her influence recovered its hold. When I left, her name was the last I would have suffered to pass my lips. When I returned, it was the first I asked after when Mr. Bruff and I met again.

I was told, of course, of all that had happened in my absence: in other words, of all that has been related since Betteredge’s narrative began—one circumstance only excepted. Mr. Bruff did not then feel himself at liberty to tell me the private motives which had influenced Rachel and Godfrey Ablewhite, on either side, to recall their promise of marriage. I spared him embarrassing questions. It was relief enough, after the jealous misery of hearing that she had ever contemplated being Godfrey’s wife, to know that she had thought better of it, and had released herself from the engagement.

Having heard the past, my questions moved—naturally, inevitably—to the present. Under whose care had Rachel been placed after leaving Mr. Bruff’s house? Where was she living now?

She was living with Mrs. Merridew, a widowed sister of the late Sir John Verinder, whom Lady Verinder’s executors had requested to act as guardian, and who had accepted the charge. They were reported to be getting on admirably, and to be established for the season in Mrs. Merridew’s house in Portland Place.

Half an hour after receiving this information I was on my way to Portland Place—without having had the courage to confess it to Mr. Bruff.

The servant who opened the door was not sure whether Miss Verinder was at home. I sent him up with my card as the shortest way of settling the question. He returned with an impenetrable face and informed me that Miss Verinder was out.

I might have suspected other people of denying themselves to me by contrivance. It was impossible to suspect Rachel. I left word that I would call again at six that evening.

At six o’clock I was told, for the second time, that Miss Verinder was not at home.

Had any message been left for me? No.

Had Miss Verinder received my card? The servant begged my pardon—Miss Verinder had received it.

The inference lay too plain to be escaped. Rachel declined to see me.

On my side, I declined to accept such treatment without at least seeking the reason. I sent up my name to Mrs. Merridew, and requested the favour of a personal interview at whatever hour she pleased to name. Mrs. Merridew made no difficulty; she received me at once.

I was shown into a comfortable little sitting-room and found a comfortable little elderly lady. The room had the air of a household that valued quiet order—the kind of place where the domestic sounds are softened on purpose: a thick carpet that drinks footsteps, curtains that blunt the street-noise, a coal-fire tended to glow rather than blaze. Even the tea-things, set ready on a table, seemed arranged with a kind of mild discipline. Outside, Portland Place sent up its constant hum—carriage-wheels and harness, door-knockers, the commerce of voices—but within there was a careful hush, as if the house were a shell shut against the coarser world.

Mrs. Merridew was very good. She expressed regret and surprise—entirely on my account. But she declared, with polite patience which nothing could tire, that she was not in a position to offer me any explanation; and that she could not press Rachel on a matter which appeared to relate only to private feeling.

Over and over again, in different forms of the same gentleness, she said it. Over and over again, in different forms of the same hopelessness, I heard it. And that was all I gained.

My last chance was to write to Rachel.

The next day my servant took my letter, with strict orders to wait for an answer. The answer came back—literally one sentence:

“Miss Verinder begs to decline entering into any correspondence with Mr. Franklin Blake.”

Fond as I was of her, I felt the insult of it. The few words had the chill of a door shut in the face—language used as a weapon because it is so neat, so unmistakable, so final. There are affronts that shout; this one whispered, and yet cut deeper for its restraint.

Mr. Bruff came in to speak to me on business before I had recovered my self-command. I dismissed the business at once and laid the whole case before him. He proved as incapable of enlightening me as Mrs. Merridew.

Had any slander been spoken of me in Rachel’s hearing? Mr. Bruff knew of none.

Had she referred to me in any way while she stayed under his roof? Never.

Had she, during all my long absence, asked so much as whether I was living or dead? No such question had ever passed her lips.

I took from my pocket-book the letter Lady Verinder had written to me at Frizinghall on the day I left Yorkshire. I pointed out two sentences to Mr. Bruff:

“The valuable assistance which you rendered to the inquiry after the lost jewel is still an unpardoned offence, in the present dreadful state of Rachel’s mind. Moving blindfold in this matter, you have added to the burden of anxiety which she has had to bear, by innocently threatening her secret with discovery through your exertions.”

“Is it possible,” I asked, “that the feeling towards me described there is as bitter as ever against me now?”

Mr. Bruff looked honestly distressed.

“If you insist on an answer,” he said, “I own I can place no other interpretation on her conduct than that.”

I rang the bell and ordered my servant to pack my portmanteau and send out for a railway guide.

Mr. Bruff stared at me in astonishment. “May I ask what you are going to do?”

“I am going to Yorkshire,” I answered, “by the next train.”

“For what purpose?”

“Mr. Bruff, the assistance I innocently rendered to the inquiry after the Diamond was an unpardoned offence in Rachel’s mind nearly a year since; and it remains an unpardoned offence still. I won’t accept that position. I am determined to find out the secret of her silence towards her mother, and her enmity towards me. If time, pains, and money can do it, I will lay my hand on the thief who took the Moonstone.”

The worthy old gentleman attempted to remonstrate—to call me back to reason—to do his duty by me in short. I was deaf to everything. No earthly consideration could have shaken the resolution in me then.

“I shall take up the inquiry again,” I went on, “at the point where I dropped it; I shall follow it onward, step by step, till I come to the present time. There are missing links in the evidence as I left it which Gabriel Betteredge can supply; and to Gabriel Betteredge I go.”

Towards sunset that evening I stood once more on the well-remembered terrace and looked at the peaceful old country house. It lay as it had lain in my memory, quiet in the long light, with the sense of settled habit clinging to it like the smell of stored linen and old wood. The very air had a different voice here. Instead of London’s ceaseless clangour there were rural layers of sound—the small click of insects, a distant cart on a softer road, the wind worrying the leaves, a bird calling as if it had always called and would always call. The place seemed to speak in its own dialect of stillness: not the dead silence of the desert, but the living hush of land that has been listened to for generations.

The gardener was the first person I met in the empty grounds. He told me Betteredge had left him an hour since, sunning himself in the customary corner of the back yard.

I knew the corner well, and I said I would go to him myself.

I walked round by the familiar paths and passages and looked in at the open gate of the yard. There he was—the dear old friend of days that were never to come again—there he was in the old corner, on the old beehive chair, with his pipe in his mouth and his Robinson Crusoe on his lap, and the two dogs—his constant table-companions, as it were—dozing on either side of him. The yard held its homely smells: earth warmed all day by the sun; straw; a faint tarry breath from the woodwork; and, underneath, that indefinable savour of a place where people have lived and worked long enough for their very habits to become part of the air.

As I stood where I was, the last slanting rays threw my shadow before me. Either the dogs saw it, or their quick scent warned them; they started up with a growl.

Betteredge started too, quieted them with a word, shaded his failing eyes with his hand, and looked inquiringly at the figure in the gateway.

My own eyes filled. I was obliged to wait a moment before I could trust myself to speak to him.
