With a voice that balanced firmness and caution, Elinor ventured into the conversation once more. "I fear I would be unworthy of the trust you have placed in me," she began, "if I harbored no wish to see it renewed, or no further curiosity regarding its subject. Pray, do not think me rude for pressing this matter again."

"Oh, thank you!" Lucy exclaimed, her warmth immediate and genuine. "You have melted the ice instantly. My heart was so heavy, fearing that what I shared last Monday had somehow offended you."

"Offend you?" Elinor smiled, her expression radiating absolute sincerity. "Could you possibly imagine that my intention was anything but kindness? Surely, the motive behind your confidence must be honorable."

"And yet," Lucy countered, her gaze sharpening slightly, "I confess I felt a chill in your manner—a coldness that made me uneasy. I was convinced you were angry with me, and I have been tormenting myself over this 'liberty' ever since. But how relieved I am to find it was merely my own imagination! If you knew how much comfort it brings me to speak to you about what occupies every thought of mine, surely your compassion would forgive everything else."

"It truly was a great relief for you to confide in me," Elinor assured her gently. "And you need never regret it. Your position is undeniably difficult; you require the strength of mutual affection to endure it. I understand, however, that Mr. Ferrars relies heavily on his mother."

"He possesses only two thousand pounds of his own," Lucy noted softly. "It would be madness to marry on such a sum, though I would willingly sacrifice every other hope without a sigh. I am accustomed to little, and could struggle against poverty for him. Yet, I love him too deeply to become the instrument that deprives him of whatever his mother might bestow if he wed to satisfy her wishes. We must wait. Perhaps for many years. With most men, this would be terrifying, but Edward's love and constancy are gifts I know will never be taken from me."

"That conviction must be everything to you," Elinor observed carefully, ensuring her face betrayed no suspicion. "And he supports himself with equal faith in yours. Had the strength of your reciprocal attachment failed—as it often does under the strain of a four-year engagement without union—your situation would indeed be pitiable."

Lucy lifted her eyes, but Elinor remained vigilant, guarding her features against any hint of doubt. "Edward's love for me," Lucy declared, "has been tested thoroughly by our prolonged separation since first engaging, and it has passed the test with flying colors. I can say with safety that he has never given me cause for alarm."

Elinor found herself torn between smiling and sighing at the certainty of these words.

Lucy continued, "I possess a naturally jealous temperament. Given our disparate stations in life, his immersion in the world compared to my seclusion, and our constant separations, I was prone to suspicion. Any slight change in his behavior upon our meetings, any unexplained low spirits, or excessive talk of another lady would have revealed the truth instantly. I am not claiming to be exceptionally observant in general matters, but in such a case, I am certain I could not be deceived."

*All this,* Elinor thought internally, *is very neat, yet it imposes upon neither of us.*

"After a brief silence, she asked, 'But what are your plans? Is your view limited to waiting for Mrs. Ferrars's death—an outcome so melancholy and shocking? Has her son resolved to submit to this, enduring the tedious years of suspense rather than risking her temporary displeasure by revealing the truth?'

"'If only we could be certain it would be for a while!' Lucy sighed. 'Mrs. Ferrars is a formidable, proud woman. In her first fit of anger, she would likely secure all of Norland for her youngest son, Robert. The mere thought of that frightens me into abandoning any inclination for hasty measures.'

'And for your own sake as well, or you are pushing your disinterestedness beyond reason,' Elinor pressed.

Lucy glanced at Elinor, fell silent, and the room grew quiet until Miss Steele interrupted. 'Do you know Mr. Robert Ferrars?' she asked abruptly.

'Not at all; I have never seen him,' Lucy admitted. 'But I fancy he is very unlike his brother—silly and a great coxcomb.'

'A great coxcomb!' Miss Steele repeated, laughing as Marianne paused in her music. 'Oh, they must be talking of their favorite beaux, I daresay.'

'No, sister,' Lucy protested indignantly. 'Our favorite beaux are _not_ great coxcombs.'

'I can answer for it that Miss Dashwood's is not,' Mrs. Jennings chimed in, her laughter hearty. 'He is one of the modestest, prettiest-behaved young men I have ever known. As for Lucy, she is such a sly little creature; there is no finding out who _she_ likes.'

'Oh,' Miss Steele cried, casting significant glances around the circle, 'I dare say Lucy's beau is quite as modest and well-behaved as Miss Dashwood's.'

Elinor blushed despite her efforts to remain composed. Lucy bit her lip and shot an angry look at her sister. A mutual silence descended upon them, broken only when Lucy lowered her voice, masking her intent behind Marianne's powerful concerto.

'I will honestly tell you of a scheme recently conceived to bring matters to a head. Indeed, I am bound to reveal this secret, for you are a party concerned. You have seen enough of Edward to know he prefers the church to any other profession. My plan is for him to take holy orders as soon as possible. Then, through your influence—which I am sure you would kindly exert out of friendship for him, and perhaps some regard for me—your brother might be persuaded to grant him the Norland living. I understand it is a very good appointment, and the current incumbent is unlikely to survive long. This would suffice for us to marry upon, trusting to time and chance for the remainder.'

'I should always be happy,' Elinor replied, 'to demonstrate any mark of esteem for Mr. Ferrars. However, do you not perceive that my interest in such an occasion would be utterly superfluous? He is the brother of Mrs. John Dashwood—that fact alone serves as ample recommendation to her husband.'

'But Mrs. John Dashwood would scarcely approve of Edward entering the clergy,' Lucy interjected.

'Then I rather suspect my influence would accomplish very little,' Elinor mused.

Silence reigned for many minutes. Finally, Lucy exhaled a deep sigh. 'I believe it would be the wisest course to terminate this business immediately by dissolving the engagement. We are beset with difficulties on every side. Though it would render us miserable for a time, we might be happier in the end. Will you not give me your advice, Miss Dashwood?'

'No,' Elinor answered, a smile concealing her agitated feelings. 'On such a subject, I certainly cannot. You know very well that my opinion would carry no weight with you unless it aligned with your wishes.'

'Deeply do I wrong you,' Lucy responded with grave solemnity. 'I know no one whose judgment I hold in higher esteem than yours. I truly believe that if you were to say, "By all means dissolve your engagement with Edward Ferrars; it will prove more conducive to your joint happiness," I would resolve to act immediately.'

Elinor flushed with shame at the insincerity of Edward's intended bride. She replied, 'Such a compliment would effectively deter me from offering any opinion on the subject had I formed one. It elevates my influence far too high; the power to divide two souls so tenderly attached is beyond the reach of an indifferent observer.'

'It is precisely because you are an indifferent person,' Lucy said, her tone tinged with pique, emphasizing the word, 'that your judgment might justly hold such sway over me. Were you suspected to be biased by your own feelings, your opinion would be worthless.'

Elinor deemed it wiser to offer no reply, lest they provoke an escalation of ease and unreservedness unsuited to their situation. She was nearly resolved never to mention the topic again. Consequently, a profound silence followed, lasting many minutes, until Lucy broke it anew.

'Shall you be in town this winter, Miss Dashwood?' she asked, her usual composure intact.

'Certainly not.'

'I am sorry,' returned the other, her eyes brightening at the news. 'It would have brought me such pleasure to meet you there! But I suppose you will go regardless. After all, your brother and sister will surely invite you.'

'It will not be within my power to accept their invitation, should they extend one.'

'How unfortunate!' Lucy lamented. 'I had fully counted on meeting you there. Anne and I are departing late in January to visit relatives who have desired our company for years! But I go solely for the sake of seeing Edward. He will be there in February; otherwise, London holds no charm for me. I lack the spirits for it.'

Soon, the conclusion of the first rubber summoned Elinor to the card table, ending the confidential discourse of the two ladies. Both submitted to the interruption without reluctance, for nothing had been said to diminish their previous animosity. Elinor seated herself at the game with a melancholy conviction: that Edward possessed no affection for the woman destined to be his wife
