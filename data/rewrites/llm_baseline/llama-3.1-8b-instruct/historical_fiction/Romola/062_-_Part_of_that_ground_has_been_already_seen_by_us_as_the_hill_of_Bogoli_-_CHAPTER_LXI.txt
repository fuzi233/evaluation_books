As the warm sunlight danced across the tranquil Mediterranean, Romola stood at the edge of the sea, her gaze drawn to the serene waves lapping against the shore just above the small fishing village of Viareggio. Eight days had passed since her departure from Florence, and this time, no compelling voice had beckoned her back. Once again, she wore the simple grey habit of a nun, though now, her heart felt heavy with a sense of disconnection. In her emotional turmoil, she no longer cared whether she wore the veil or her secular attire; she had grown indifferent to the label that defined her. A new wave of disillusionment had washed over her, a sense of hopelessness that made her question the very notion of duty. What was it that drove people to act with conviction, to find meaning in their lives? Was it not the love they shared with others, the deep connections that gave their actions purpose?

The ties that bound her to those she loved had frayed, and in their place, she felt a void that could not be filled. Her marriage, once the foundation of her existence, had become a hollow shell. She no longer saw the sacred bond that united her and her husband, the promise they had made to each other. Hadn't she proven that the ideals they had pledged themselves to were unattainable? The urge to break free from the constraints of her life had resurfaced, but freedom would come at a cost. For a woman who felt that her most significant relationship had been a mistake, there was no compensation to be found. She had lost her sense of purpose.

Now, the person who had been her guiding light, Savonarola, was gone from her life. The vision of a higher purpose, something that could elevate the mundane aspects of life and make them sacred, had vanished for her. All efforts seemed futile, like trying to grasp at tangled threads. Fellowship, whether for support or solidarity, felt hollow and exclusive. She had lost her admiration for the friar, and with it, her ability to overlook his flaws. In her disillusionment, she saw the imperfections in his teachings with clarity, and they seemed to mock her.

In her bitterness, she believed that Savonarola's crusade for the renewal of the Church and the world was nothing more than a means to an end – a way to strengthen his own position in Florence.
