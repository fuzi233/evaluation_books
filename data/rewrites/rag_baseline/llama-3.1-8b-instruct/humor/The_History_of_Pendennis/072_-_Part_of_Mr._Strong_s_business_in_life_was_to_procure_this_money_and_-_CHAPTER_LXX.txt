As Pendennis settled into the chambers in Lamb Court, he couldn't help but notice the stark contrast between the dreary yet comforting atmosphere of the building and the vibrant city outside. His domestic announced the arrival of Warrington, and he hastened to join his friend in the adjacent room. The air was thick with the scent of pipe smoke and the soft rustle of papers as George sat engrossed in his newspapers and reviews. The two friends exchanged the characteristic rough cordiality of young Englishmen, a warmth beneath their reserved exteriors. Warrington smiled, extracted his pipe from his mouth, and greeted Pen with a familiar phrase, "Well, young one!" Pen reciprocated with a hearty "How are you, old boy?" The exchange was a testament to the peculiar nature of British friendship, where camaraderie was tempered by an unspoken stoicism.

As they conversed, Pendennis inquired about Warrington's travels, and he revealed a brief visit to Switzerland, where he had finally secured a satisfactory supply of tobacco in Strasbourg. Though his experiences and emotions ran deep, Warrington's reserve prevented him from openly expressing himself, instead cloaking his thoughts behind a veil of tobacco smoke. However, it was evident that his journey had left an indelible mark, and his writings would eventually bear witness to the profound impact of the natural wonders he had encountered.

Warrington's account of his travels was delivered in a subdued tone, as if the very recollection of the places he had visited—Switzerland, North Italy, and the Tyrol—had left him somewhat dispirited. He had traversed Europe via Vienna, Dresden, and the Rhine, and his descriptions were laced with an air of reluctance, as if the grandeur of these destinations had only served to heighten his melancholy. As he concluded his narrative, Pendennis took the opportunity to share his own news, confessing to a recent bout of boredom while canvassing, and his plans to visit friends in the vicinity of Tunbridge Wells.

The conversation turned to politics, with Warrington inquiring about Pendennis's prospects for securing a parliamentary seat. Pendennis replied that, pending the next election, he expected to assume Clavering's position, paving the way for a new era in British politics.
