Shirley must have enjoyed every moment of her previous evening with Sir Philip Nunnely, for she drifted down to breakfast the next morning glowing in the kind of unguarded, unselfconscious joy that graced her only on her very best days.
"Who’ll come for a walk with me?" she called cheerfully once the plates were cleared. "Isabella? Gertrude? Care to join?"
Such an invitation from Miss Keeldar to her two cousins was so rare that the girls hesitated, half-convinced they’d misheard. When their mother nodded her approval, they scrambled for their bonnets and cloaks, and the three set off together.
The trio had never been particularly suited to one another’s company. Shirley counted almost no ladies as friends, her only genuine, unreserved affection given over entirely to Mrs. Pryor and Caroline Helstone. She was always polite, kind, even attentive to her cousins, but usually she had little to say to them. That sunlit autumn morning, though, she wove even the most mundane small talk into something bright and captivating, her sparkling wit skittering across every casual phrase until the two quiet girls found themselves laughing, hanging on her every word, wondering how they’d never noticed how much fun she could be.
No obvious cause fed that joy. The day itself was dim, a pale, fading late autumn afternoon, the paths through the dark wood damp underfoot, the air heavy, the sky streaked with heavy overcast. It didn’t matter. Shirley carried all the blue, sun-drenched light of Italy in her chest, all its warm, unruly laughter glinting out of her grey English eyes.
On their way back to Fieldhead, she fell a few paces behind the cousins to pass a handful of quick, practical instructions to her foreman, John. Twenty minutes slipped by before she made her way up the drive again. By the time the bell for luncheon rang, she’d wandered lingering in the lane by the gate, and when the maid called her in to eat, she excused herself entirely and went straight up to her room.
"Isn’t Shirley coming down for luncheon?" Isabella asked, confused. "She said she was ravenous an hour ago."
When an hour passed and she still hadn’t left her chamber, Gertrude went to check on her. She found Shirley perched on the floor at the foot of her bed, head resting on one hand, pale, thoughtful, almost sad.
"You’re not ill, are you?" Gertrude fussed.
"Just a little queasy," Shirley replied.
The change in her from the bright, laughing girl who’d led them on the walk two hours earlier was staggering. This shift didn’t pass like a wisp of summer cloud. She joined them for dinner, chatted as usual, stayed with them the whole evening, laughed off all questions about her health as nothing more than a passing faintness, a silly momentary sensation not worth dwelling on. But everyone in the house could feel it: something fundamental had shifted in Shirley.
That strange, soft shadow lingered on her face, in her movements, even in the tone of her voice, for days, then weeks, then a full fortnight. A strange quietude settled over her, not deep enough to let anyone press her on it too often, but impossible to miss, heavy as a cloud no wind could ever blow away. It soon became clear that mentioning the change would annoy her. She’d shrink away from gentle concern at first, and if people persisted, her old sharp hauteur would flare up to push them back.
"Are you ill?" they’d ask, and she’d snap back sharp and firm: "I am not."
"Is something weighing on your mind? Did something happen to upset you?"
She’d scoff, mock the very idea. "What do you even mean by ‘spirits’? I don’t have a stash of black or white or blue spirits floating around my head waiting to get upset."
"You must be different for a reason, you’re not yourself at all."
"I’m allowed to change if I please. I know I look plainer. If I want to let myself grow less pretty, why on earth should anyone else lose sleep over it?"
"What’s the cause, Shirley? Tell us what’s wrong."
And then she’d order them sharply to leave her alone.
She’d force herself to be bright and gay around everyone else, and seethe at herself when she couldn’t pull it off perfectly, muttering sharp, angry little insults at herself when she was alone. *Fool! Coward!* she’d hiss. *Poltroon! If you’re going to tremble, tremble where no one can see you. Don’t quail where any eye can catch you.*
"How dare you," she’d berate her own reflection, "show your weakness, betray your silly, silly anxieties? Shake them off. Rise above them. If you can’t do that, at the very least hide them."
And hide them she did. She threw herself back into being resolutely lively in company, and when the effort wore her too thin, she fled out of the house instead of moping locked in her bedroom. She’d mount her mare Zoë and ride out for hours at a time, half-day long treks over the moor. Her uncle disapproved, but he never dared argue with her. Facing Shirley’s anger had never been pleasant, even when she was well and bright, but now that her face was growing thin, her large eyes hollowed out, the dark flash of her temper was as unsettling as it was achingly sad.
To every casual stranger who commented on how gaunt she looked, she gave the same flat reply: "I’m perfectly well. I haven’t a single thing wrong with me."
And she had to be tough as nails to weather the exposure she put herself through. Rain or shine, gale or calm, she rode daily across Stilbro’ Moor, her tartan terrier Tartar loping at her side with his endless, wolf-like gallop.
Twice, three times, gossips’ prying eyes spotted her veering away from her usual route up Rushedge ridge, riding straight for the little town below. It didn’t take long for busybodies to track her destination: she dismounted every time at the office of Mr. Pearson Hall, a solicitor whose family had handled all of the Keeldar estate’s affairs for generations. Rumours flew. Some said she’d sunk money into bad speculations for Hollow’s Mill and lost a fortune, forcing her to mortgage Fieldhead. Others whispered she was secretly engaged, drawing up marriage settlements.

Louis Moore and young Henry Sympson were alone in the schoolroom. The tutor waited for his pupil to finish preparing his lesson, but the boy sat hunched over his book, not turning a single page.
"Henry, hurry up," Moore said gently. "The afternoon’s getting away from us."
"Is it, sir?"
"Certainly. Are you nearly done with your translation?"
"No."
"Not even nearly?"
"I haven’t construed a single line."
Moore looked up. There was a tight, strange tremor in the boy’s voice.
"The work isn’t hard, Henry. If you’re stuck, show me. We’ll work through it together."
"Mr. Moore, I can’t work at all."
"Are you feeling unwell, my boy?"
"I’m no more sick in body than usual, but my heart’s so full I can barely breathe."
"Shut the book. Come over here to the fireside."
Henry limped across the room. Louis pulled a chair out for him, and the boy’s lips were already quivering, his eyes brimming with tears. He set his crutch on the floor, dropped his head in his hands, and sobbed.
"This grief isn’t from physical pain, you say? Tell me what’s weighing on you."
"Sir, it’s the worst grief I’ve ever had. I wish I could make it go away. I can barely stand it."
"Maybe if we talk it through together, we can lighten it. What’s the cause? Who does it concern?"
"It’s Shirley, sir. It’s all about Shirley."
"You think she’s changed, don’t you?"
"Everyone who knows her notices. You do too, Mr. Moore."
"Not seriously," Moore said, even as his chest tightened. "I’ve not seen anything that a few good weeks of rest couldn’t fix. She swears herself she’s perfectly well, after all."
"That’s the trouble!" Henry cried. "As long as she kept saying she was fine, I believed her. I’d be sad when I was away from her, but the second I stepped up to her I’d feel light again. But now…"
"Go on, Harry. What happened? The two of you were talking in the garden for two hours this morning, I saw you. If she told you she was sick and made you swear to secrecy, don’t keep that promise. For her sake, tell me everything. Speak, my boy."
"Her say she’s ill! I’m convinced, sir, that if she was lying on her deathbed she’d still smile and say nothing at all ails her."
"Then what have you found out? What new thing did she tell you?"
"She made her will. Just a few days ago."
Louis froze, and the two of them sat silent for long minutes.
"She told you that?" Moore asked at last.
"She told me like it was the most ordinary, unthreatening thing in the world, though I felt every word like a punch to the chest. She said only her solicitor Mr. Hall, Mr. Helstone, Mr. Yorke, and I know about it, and she wanted to explain every part of it to me, specially."
"Go on, Harry."
"‘Because,’ she said, looking down at me with her beautiful eyes—oh Mr. Moore, they’re the most beautiful eyes in the whole world! I love her, she’s my star! Heaven can’t take her! She’s made for this world, she’s not some distant angel, she’s a real woman who belongs here with us. Even if an archangel with wings as wide as the sky came down to claim her for heaven, I’d fight him off, boy and cripple as I am."
"Henry Sympson, go on when I tell you to."
"‘Because,’ she said, ‘if I died without a will before you came of age, all my property would pass straight to you, and I won’t let that happen, even though your father would be over the moon about it. But you’ll inherit your father’s whole estate one day, it’s larger than Fieldhead, you’ll never want for anything. Your sisters, though, will get nothing from their father, so I left them a sum of money each, even though I don’t love the two of them half as much as I love a single lock of your fair hair.’ She called me her darling, and let me kiss her hand, Mr. Moore. She said she left Caroline Helstone a nice little legacy too, that this old manor house, all its books and furniture, would pass straight to me, because she didn’t want to take the old family home away from her own blood line. And all the rest of her property, twelve thousand pounds after all the other legacies are paid? She didn’t leave it to me, because I’m already rich enough. She left it to a good man, she said, who would use it better than any other person alive: a man gentle and brave, strong and merciful, who might not shout about his piety to the whole parish, but who lives a pure, unshowy kind of faith that cares for widows and orphans, who never lets the world turn him hard. Then she asked me ‘Do you approve of what I’ve done, Harry?’ I couldn’t answer. I was crying too hard to speak."
Louis let the boy catch his breath for a minute before he spoke again. "What else did she say?"
"When I told her I thought every part of her will was perfect, she said I was a generous boy, that she was proud of me. ‘And now,’ she said, ‘if anything ever happens to me, you’ll know what to say when people whisper lies about Shirley, saying I wronged you, that I never loved you. You’ll know I did love you, Harry—no sister could have loved you better than that, my own treasure.’ Mr. Moore, just thinking about her voice, her face that morning, my heart feels like it’s going to burst out of my chest. She might go to heaven before me, if God calls her home, and that’s His right—but every day of the rest of my life, and I know my life won’t be long, I’ll walk straight down the path her feet already trod. I always thought I’d be buried in the Keeldar family vault before her. If it turns out the other way, promise me you’ll lay my coffin right by her side."
Moore spoke slow, calm, steady, a deliberate contrast to the boy’s wild, aching devotion.
"You’re both being silly, you’re scaring yourselves for no reason. When young people get caught in the grip of a shadowy terror, they convince themselves the sun will never shine again. You think this one little shadow will last forever. What else did she say? Anything else?"
"We settled a few little family things between us."
"I’d very much like to hear what they were."
"But you’re smiling, sir. I couldn’t even crack a smile seeing Shirley look like that."
"Harry, I’m not excitable, I’m not prone to poetic fancies, and I’m far older than you. I see things as they really are, which you don’t yet. Tell me those little family points."
"Only, sir, she asked me if I considered myself more a Keeldar or a Sympson, and I told her I was Keeldar all the way down to my bones. She was so happy to hear it, she said I was the only other Keeldar left in all of England besides her. Then we agreed on a few things."
"Go on."
"If I live long enough to inherit my father’s estate and her house, I’ll change my last name to Keeldar, and make Fieldhead my permanent home. I’ll be Henry Shirley Keeldar. Her name, her home, they’re ancient, the Sympsons and Sympson Grove are nothing new, nothing compared to that."
"Neither of you are going anywhere near heaven any time soon. I’ve got very high hopes for both of you, you pair of half-fledged eaglets. Now, tell me what you make of all this. Say it out plain."
"That Shirley’s convinced she’s going to die."
"She never mentioned her health once to you?"
"Not once. But look at her, sir! She’s wasting away. Her hands are thin, her cheeks are sunken."
"Has she ever complained to your mother or your sisters?"
"Never. She laughs them off when they ask if she’s unwell. She’s such a strange, wonderful creature—soft, girlish, nothing like a loud, domineering Amazon, and yet she’s too proud to take help or sympathy from anyone."
"Do you know where she is right now, Henry? Is she in the house, or riding out?"
"Surely she’s not out, sir. It’s pouring rain."
"It is pouring, but that’s never stopped her before. Lately, no weather, no matter how wild, has kept her from riding."
"Remember last Wednesday? The storm was so bad she wouldn’t even let Zoë be saddled, the wind was that brutal. But the gale she thought was too rough for her mare she walked straight out into on foot. She tramped all the way to Nunnely and back. I asked her if she wasn’t scared of catching a terrible cold. ‘Not I,’ she laughed. ‘Catching a good fever and drifting off like any normal Christian would be far too lucky for me. I don’t know, Harry, but the best thing that could happen to me right now would be to fall ill and slip away.’ She’s reckless, sir."
"Reckless doesn’t even cover it. Go find her. If you can ask her to come here for a minute without drawing anyone else’s attention, do it."
"Yes sir."
He grabbed his crutch and started for the door.
"Harry!"
He turned back.
"Don’t make the message sound formal, like I’m summoning her for a meeting. Say it like you used to, the same way you’d call her to the schoolroom for a silly French reading lesson. She’ll be far more likely to agree."
"I understand, sir."
"And Harry—"
"Yes?"
"I’ll call you if I need you. You’re excused from the rest of your lessons today."
Henry left. Louis stood up from his desk the second the door clicked shut.
"I can act all cool and superior with Henry," he muttered to himself, pacing the room. "I can act like his silly little fears are nothing, like the pair of them are just overexcited children. Let’s see if I can keep that act up with her. I’ve come so close to slipping before, nearly letting my composure shatter, nearly letting her see that I’m not her calm, stoic tutor at all, that I’m just a man so wrapped up in her I can barely think straight. I can’t afford to be a fool. Sir Philip Nunnely can blush every time he meets her eye, he can tremble when their hands brush, no one would think less of him for it. But I’m poor. If I let myself show how soft I am for her, everyone would think I’m just a grasping fortune hunter, after her money. I’ve sat right next to her a hundred times and never shaken, never broken composure, not once. I’ve never even touched her hand, I’ve never put myself to that test. I’m no servant of hers, I’m no serf—but I have to guard my self-respect, not give up an inch of it. Those little comments she used to make about cold people who turn flesh to marble? They made my chest feel light, for no reason I’d ever dare admit. I don’t let myself dwell on what she means, or stare too long at her face, because if I did I’d stop believing in plain sense and start believing in romance. Sometimes a silly, secret joy runs right through my veins. I don’t encourage it. I refuse to even remember it. I’ll keep my head, I swear it."
He paused, listening.
"Is she coming? Or is she going to refuse me?" he wondered. "Will she laugh it off, or turn me away haughtily? She’s half wild child, half queen, anything could happen."
