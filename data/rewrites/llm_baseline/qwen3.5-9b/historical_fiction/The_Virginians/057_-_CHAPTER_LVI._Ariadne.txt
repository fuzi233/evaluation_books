Lord Castlewood possessed a residence in Kensington Square sufficiently grand to house his entire noble household, conveniently situated for attendance upon the Palace whenever His Majesty resided there. While Her Ladyship hosted her evening gatherings and card-parties within its walls, Kensington remained a considerable distance from London in those days—a century ago, to be precise. George Selwyn confessed that he personally dreaded the journey, fearing robbery by footpads draped in crape or by ladies powdered in rouge at the quadrille table; the specifics of such perils remain unknown to us.

About noon, the day following Harry's dramatic return to White's Club, the virtuous kinfolk gathered for breakfast, even Mr. Will, who was scheduled to begin his service later that afternoon. The ladies arrived first for their chocolate, during which Mr. Will offered his customary courtly attentions. Finally, the Master of the House entered, languid and clad in his bedgown and nightcap, having yet to don his wig for the day. This attire itself constituted news, information Mr. Will had procured the previous evening from the Star and Garter, having heard it whispered among men who had frequented White's and witnessed it at Ranelagh.

"Pray, what news? What have you seen?" queried the head of the house, picking up his *Daily Advertiser*.

"Ask Maria!" declared Lady Fanny. The Master of the House turned to his elder sister, whose countenance wore an expression of portentous sadness, her pallor matching that of a fresh tablecloth.

"'Tis merely one of Will's usual elegant and polite fabrications," dismissed Maria.

"No," swore Will, punctuating his denial with several oaths. "It was no invention of his. Tom Claypool of Norfolk saw them both at Ranelagh; and Jack Morris emerged from White's, having heard the tale directly from Harry Warrington's own lips. Curse him, I am glad of it!" roared Will, slamming his fist against the table. "What do you think of your Fortunate Youth, your Virginian, whom your lordship held in such high esteem, turning out to be a mere second son?"

"The elder brother not dead?" asked my lord.

"Not dead any more than you are. Never was. In my belief, it was a chimera born of the two."

"Mr. Warrington is incapable of such duplicity!" cried Maria.

"I never encouraged the fellow; I trust you will grant me justice on that point," assured the lady of the house. "Nor did Fanny: neither did we, indeed!"

"Not we, indeed!" echoed Lady Fanny.

"The fellow is nothing but a beggar, and I dare say he hasn't even paid for the clothes on his back," continued Will. "I am glad of it, for, hang him, I hate him!"

"You do not regard him with favorable eyes; especially since he blackened yours, Will!" grinned my lord. "So the poor fellow has found his brother, and consequently lost his estate!" Turning toward his sister Maria, who, despite appearing the picture of woe, must have struck the humorist beside her as somewhat ludicrous, my lord gazed at her for a moment before bursting into a shrill laugh. The effect was immediate: the poor lady's face flushed crimson, and soon tears streamed down her cheeks.

"It's a shame! It's a shame!" she sobbed, burying her face in her handkerchief. Maria's stepmother and sister exchanged glances. "We never quite comprehend your lordship's sense of humor," the stepmother remarked gravely.

"I perceive no reason whatsoever why you should," replied my lord, coolly. "Maria, my dear, pray excuse me if I have said—or done—anything to hurt your feelings."

"Done anything! You pillaged the poor lad in his prosperity and now mock him in his ruin!" Maria exclaimed, rising from the table and glaring around at her assembled family.

"Excuse me, my dear sister, I was not laughing at him," said my lord, gently.

"Oh, never mind at what or whom else, my lord! You have stripped him of everything he had to lose. All London points to you as the man who feasts upon his own flesh and blood. And now that you hold his entire fortune, you make merry over his misfortune!" With a rustle of silk, she departed the room, casting looks of defiant challenge at every member of the party.

"Tell us what has occurred, or what you have heard, Will, and my sister's grief shall not disturb us further." Will proceeded, at great length and with immense exultation regarding Harry's defeat, to recount the story now buzzing through London: the sudden apparition of George Warrington. Lord Castlewood professed sorrow for Harry; he was a good, brave lad, and his kinsman liked him as much as worldly folk typically like one another. Certainly, he played Harry at cards and exploited the market against him, but why not? The peach others would surely pluck, he might as well devour. *Eh!* If that were the sole reproach my conscience bore, I need not be overly uneasy, my lord reflected.

"Where does Mr. Warrington reside?"

Will expressed readiness to descend into a state of reprobation if he knew or cared.

"He shall be invited here and treated with every respect," decreed my lord.

"Including piquet, I suppose!" growled Will.

"Or shall we take him to the stables and sell him one of your bargains of horseflesh, Will?" asked Lord Castlewood. "You would have won of Harry Warrington swiftly enough if you could; but you cheat so clumsily at your game that you were paid with a cudgel. I desire, once more, that every courtesy be extended to our cousin Warrington."

"And that you are not to be disturbed when you sit down to play, of course, my lord!" cried Lady Castlewood.

"Madam, I demand fair play—for Mr. Warrington, for myself, and for every member of this amiable family," retorted Lord Castlewood, fiercely.

"Heaven help the poor gentleman if your lordship intends to be kind to him," said the stepmother, curtsying deeply; and one wonders how far this familial dispute might have escalated had not, at that very moment, a phaeton drawn up to the house, carrying the two young Virginians.

It was the carriage the prodigal had purchased in his days of prosperity. He still drove it; George sat beside him, their negro servants trailing behind. Harry had been meekly handing the whip and reins to his brother, ceding the entirety of the property to him. *"What business has a poor devil like me with horses and carriages, Georgy?"* Harry had humbly asked. *"Beyond the coat on my back and the purse my aunt gave me, I have nothing in the world. You take the driving-seat, brother; it will ease my mind if you do."* George laughed, claiming ignorance of the route, while Harry knew the way; regarding the carriage, he claimed only half, as he had already divided his brother's wardrobe. *"But a bargain is a bargain; if I share thy coats, thou must divide my breeches' pocket, Harry; that is but fair dealing!"* Again and again, Harry swore there was no such brother on earth. How he rattled his horses over the road! How pleased and proud he was to drive such a companion! They arrived at Kensington in high spirits, and Gumbo's thunderous knock upon Lord Castlewood's door was worthy of the finest footman in St. James's.

Only Lady Castlewood and her daughter, Lady Fanny, were present in the room where the young gentlemen were ushered. Will had no particular desire to face Harry; my lord was undressed; Maria had her reasons for absence, at least until her eyes had dried. As we drive up to friends' houses today in our coaches-and-six, when John carries our noble names aloft, when finally we enter the drawing-room with our best hats and Sunday smiles foremost, does it ever occur that we interrupt a family quarrel? Do we arrive simpering and smiling, stepping over the delusive ashes of a still-burning domestic fire? Do we, in the interval between the hall-door and the drawing-room, witness the Mrs., Mr., and Misses Jones group themselves into a family tableau—the girl artlessly arranging flowers, that one reclining over an illuminated devotional work, mamma on the sofa with the butcher's and grocer's bills tucked beneath a cushion, holding an elegant volume, and extending a pretty little foot advantageously; while honest Jones, far from muttering curses against Brown, extends a kindly hand, displays a pleased visage, and exclaims, *"What, Brown my boy, delighted to see you! Hope you've come to lunch!"* I ask, does it ever happen that we become victims of domestic artifices, spectators of domestic comedies staged solely for our amusement?
