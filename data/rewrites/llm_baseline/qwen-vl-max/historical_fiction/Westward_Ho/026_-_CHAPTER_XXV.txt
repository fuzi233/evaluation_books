“God will relent, and quit thee all thy debt,
Who ever more approves, and more accepts
Him who imploring mercy sues for life,
Than who self-rigorous chooses death as due,
Which argues over-just, and self-displeased
For self-offence, more than for God offended.”

—Samson Agonistes.

A fortnight had passed in grueling labor, though no harder than they had endured many times before. Farewell forever to the green expanse of the eastern plains, they had crossed the Cordillera range, casting a long look back at Santa Fe—perched high on its mountain plateau, surrounded by lush gardens. But it was too large, too fortified, for any raid they could hope to carry off. Yet their journey had not been wasted. Their Indian boy had learned that a gold convoy was making its way from Santa Fe toward the Magdalena River. Now, they waited beside the rutted track that served as a road, camped beneath an oak forest so thick and leafy it might have fooled them into thinking they were back in England—had it not been for the tree ferns below, or the deep gorges yawning at their feet. From the cool breezes above, they could glimpse far down through swirling steam the vibrant, tangled world of the tropical jungle.

They had made camp among the ferns, just above a stretch of path winding along a steep hillside, with a sheer cliff plunging hundreds of feet below. Once, perhaps, this had been a proper road—when Cundinamarca was a cultivated kingdom. Now only crumbling steps remained, slipping from their foundations into a muddy ditch. Spanish misrule had left nothing but ruins: aqueducts collapsed, bridges gone, post-houses vanished, llama flocks scattered, gardens choked. Only architecture survived—grand cathedrals rising atop ancient temples of the Sun, built in honor of a gentler pantheon. Or so it seemed—yet if such a pantheon demanded human sacrifice, as the Spaniards did, then it was no milder than the old gods of the Incas.

Now the tropics had reclaimed what man had destroyed. Amyas and his men were utterly alone—within miles of a major Spanish settlement, yet as isolated as if they were lost in the heart of the Orinoco or Amazon.

All attempts to find sulphur and nitre had failed. They were forced to rely once more—much to Yeo’s chagrin—on swords and arrows. So be it. Drake had taken Nombre de Dios and seized a gold train with no better arms. If they could do the same, why not?

They had blocked the path above by felling a massive tree across it. Now they sat among wildflowers, chewing coca instead of eating, and pondering a strange sound that had followed them each night since leaving the Meta River—a deep, mysterious roar. Not a jaguar, nor monkey, nor anything familiar. Why should it follow them? But they were in a land of wonders; and the gold train mattered far more than any noise.

Then came a sharp crack—and a cry.

Not a branch snapping. Not a woodpecker tapping. Not a parrot screeching. Not a monkey howling.

“That was a whip,” said Yeo. “And a woman’s wail. They’re close, lads!”

“A woman?” asked Amyas. “Do they chain women in their gangs?”

“Why not? The brutes!” replied Yeo. “There they are, sir. Did you see their basnets glitter?”

“Men,” whispered Amyas. “I trust you all not to shoot until I give the word. Then one arrow each. Draw your swords. Charge! Pass the word.”

Up they came, slowly, and every heart beat loud.

First came about twenty soldiers—half on foot, half carried in chairs slung on the backs of single Indians. Those marching had handed their heaviest armor and arquebuses to slave attendants, prodded forward by the pikes of the soldiers behind.

“The men are mad to let their weapons out of their hands,” muttered one.

“Oh, sir,” said Yeo, “an Indian will pray to an arquebus not to fire on him. He knows their artillery is safe enough.”

“Look at the proud villains,” whispered another. “Making beasts of men like that.”

Amyas counted: ten muskets, ten pikes. “Will can handle them up above.”

Then came an officer, seated in a chair, moving slowly up the slope. As he passed, he drew his cigar from his lips every few seconds, muttering prayers—or curses—to the Virgin, saints, devils, wooden idols, and even his own body parts. Such piety earned the Spanish of the sixteenth century a reputation both as devout idolaters and shameless swearers.

“The blasphemous dog!” said Yeo, fingers twitching on his bowstring. But Amyas touched his arm just as a second procession emerged—and all forgot the officer.

It was a sight of horror—yet common enough in those remote lands where royal edicts against cruelty were ignored. The Dominican friars had pleaded for mercy; Pedro de la Gasca had enforced laws in Peru. But these were futile efforts against horrors unmatched in Christendom—save perhaps in the conquests of Sennacherib or Genghis Khan. On the frontiers, where enslaved Africans replaced Indians who died under the burden, and tribes accused of cannibalism were hunted down for their souls and bodies, such scenes still occurred. And, according to Humboldt, even in the late eighteenth century, Jesuit missions—though praised—were ruled by military tyranny, preaching the Gospel with fire and sword, much as the first conquistadors had done.

A line of Indians, Negroes, and Zambos marched barefoot, emaciated, scarred by whips and chains, linked wrist to wrist. Each bore a basket suspended by a strap across the forehead, heavy with burden. Yeo’s scorn was justified: there were not only men and youths, but women—slender girls, mothers with children running at their heels. A low murmur rose among the Englishmen, born of righteous anger. In those days, when Raleigh spoke of humanity and freedom, when English hearts still knew manhood and liberty, such injustice stirred outrage. It was a time before the seventeenth century turned them cold with slavery’s legacy.

But the first forty bore something that silenced even that sorrow. Each basket held a square package of tightly bound hide. Amyas recognized it instantly.

“What’s in them, captain?”

“Gold.” At that word, eyes lit with greed. A rustle ran through the ambush. Amyas hushed them.

“Be men. Be men. Or we’ll ruin everything.”

The last twenty carried larger baskets, lighter loads—manioc, maize bread, food for the convoy. Behind them came twenty more soldiers, then the officer again, smirking in his chair, twirling his mustache, unaware that English arrows burned to pierce his ribs. The trap was set. Only when and how?

Amyas hesitated. To draw bow on men so unsuspecting—even while committing cruelty—was hard. Yet it was cruelty indeed. Three drivers armed with whips walked the line, striking every stumble, every lag, with the manati-hide whip that cracked like gunfire against naked skin. The victims endured silently.

Then the moment came.

Near the end of the chain was an old man, gray-haired, followed by a slender girl of eighteen. Amyas’s heart ached for them. Just as they passed, the lead guard rounded the bend above.

“Halt, senors! A tree blocks the path!”

“A tree?” roared the officer, cursing Heaven, Hell, Saint James, and every saint in between. The line halted above, driven forward below. The old man stumbled, fell face-down.

The officer leapt from his chair, rushed up. He found the old man prostrate.

“Sin peccado concebida! Grandfather of Beelzebub—what place is this to worship your demons?” He jabbed the man with his sword.

The old man tried to rise—but the weight crushed him. He fell again, motionless.

The driver lashed him twice with the whip. No response.

“Gastado, Senor Capitan,” he shrugged. “Worn out. He’s failing for three months.”

“What does the intendant think sending me broken cattle like this? Forward! Clear the tree, senors. I’ll clear the chain. Hold it up, Pedrilo!”

The driver lifted the chain from the old man’s wrist. The officer stepped back, brandishing a Toledo blade—so fine it made Amyas forget the commandments.

He raised the sword—once, twice—then struck.

Not the chain.

The wrist.

A shriek. A flash of crimson. The chain broke. The prisoner was severed.

One moment more, and Amyas’s arrow would have pierced the murderer’s throat. But vengeance came from elsewhere.

Like a tiger-cat, the girl sprang—clutched the officer—leapt with him from the ledge into the abyss.

A shout. A rush. All leaned over the edge. She hung by her chained wrist. The officer was gone. A silence fell. Then—crash!—the sound of a body breaking through tree limbs far below.

“Haul her up! Cut her to pieces! Burn the witch!” screamed the driver, pulling the chain. Others leapt from their chairs, gathered at the brink.

Now—now was the time.

Amyas loosed an arrow—ten yards away—it pierced the driver’s chest. Then, roaring like a lion, he charged into the stunned Spaniards.

His first thought: the girl. With strength, he pulled her to safety. The Spaniards recoiled—thinking him a mountain spirit. His cry revealed the truth. “English! Lutheran dogs!” they shouted—but too late.

The Devon men followed. Arrows flew—five dead, a dozen wounded. Salvation Yeo, white hair streaming, leapt down with twenty swords. Death began.

The Spaniards fought fiercely—but no time to load arquebuses. No room to use pikes. The English held the wall. That meant life in their hands.

Five minutes. No Spaniard stood. None lived below.

Two armored men escaped—behind the rest—ran down the hill.

“After them! Michael Evans, Simon Heard—catch them if they run a league.”

The two Clovelly men, swift as deer, raced after. Ten minutes later, they returned—mission complete.

Amyas and his men hurried forward—shouts and gunfire signaled a fight ahead.

Their arrival ended it. Most Spaniards fell. A few scrambled down the cliffs.

“Let none escape! Slay them as Israel slew Amalek!” cried Yeo. Before they reached shelter, arrows found each—bodies rolled down the rocks.

“Now—free the Indians!”

They found tools on a corpse. Chains were cut.

“We are your friends,” said Amyas. “We ask only that you help us carry this gold to the Magdalena. Then you are free.”

Some young ones knelt, kissed his feet, calling him child of the Sun. Most sat silent, indifferent. The iron had entered their souls. They seemed beyond hope, joy, understanding.

But the young girl—last in line—when freed, ran to her father’s body. Without a word, she lifted it, laid it on her knees, kissed his lips, stroked his cheeks, murmured sounds like a dove’s coo—only she understood. Then, suddenly—she rose, holding him.

She leapt into the abyss.

They watched her dark form, wrapped around the corpse, turning over, over—then a crash in the trees, a scream from birds. The green canopy hid her.

“Brave lass!” shouted a sailor.

“The Lord forgive her,” said Yeo. “But, sir, we must take their arms.”

“And their clothes,” added Amyas. “If we’re to pass unchallenged down the Magdalena. Listen, my men. By God’s grace, we’ve won enough gold to last our lives—without losing a man. We may win more—if we stay wise, and He allows. But remember Mr. Oxenham and his crew. Let not this gift become our ruin—by greed, faithlessness, or haste.”

“You’ll find no such thing in us!” several called. “We trust you, sir.”

“Thank God,” said Amyas. “Now—no shame in making the Indians carry the gold. Save the women. God forbid we burden them. But we must pass through Spanish settlements—past Saint Martha itself. So we need their clothes and weapons. How many lie here?”

“Thirteen here. Ten above,” said Cary.

“Twenty missing. Who’ll go down the cliff and bring up the spoils?”

“I—I—I,” said a dozen. Always ready—because they knew Amyas would join them.

“Very well. Follow me. Sir John, take the Indian lad. Comfort these poor souls. Tell them they’re free.”

“Who comes up the road?”

All turned.

And there—wonder of wonders—came Ayacanora herself, blow-gun in hand, bow on back, feathered garments worn from weeks in the woods.

Silence. Then, seeing Amyas, she cried, ran, and fell at his feet, panting.

“I found you!” she gasped. “You ran from me—but you couldn’t escape me!” She circled him like a faithful dog, then sat on the bank, sobbing wildly.

“God help us,” said Amyas, clutching his hair. “What am I to do with her—besides all these poor heathens?”

No time. He scrambled over the cliff. The girl watched from a rock.

Half an hour later, weapons, clothes, armor were hauled up. Corpses thrown over the edge. They moved on—toward the Magdalena. Yeo snorted like a warhorse, thrilled at handling powder again.

“We can face the world now, sir! Why not try Santa Fe?”

But Amyas shook his head. Enough was enough. They pressed onward. The slaves followed—no thanks, but quiet obedience. Some noticed the Englishmen’s calabashes of coca. That woke them. They begged—begged abjectly—for a taste. For coca gave strength, eased breath, and drove away the worst enemy of all—the pain of thought.

As they turned the mountain corner, they paused—one last look.

Vultures streamed from the sky, drawn by the feast. In hours, no trace would remain—only bones amid flowers.

Now Amyas asked Ayacanora why she was there.

He wished she weren’t. But now she was—how could any heart be hard enough to ignore her?

He spoke gently, afraid of hurting her.

She told her tale—passionately. Every night, she had made loud noises, hoping they’d hear, turn back. Now he remembered—the roaring.

“Noises? What made them?”

Ayacanora lifted a finger, then slowly drew from under her cloak an object that made Amyas struggle to keep his composure.

“Look!” she whispered, as if afraid the thing might hear. “I have it—the holy trumpet!”

There it was—the bone of contention. A glazed earthen tube, two feet long, painted with animals and patterns—clearly a relic of a vanished civilization.

Brimblecombe clapped his hands. “Brave maid! You’ve cheated Satan this time!” Yeo insisted the “idolatrous relic” be thrown over the cliff.

“Let be,” said Amyas. “What is this, Ayacanora? And why did you follow us?”

She told her story. The trumpet had tormented her for years—the one thing greater than her, forbidden because she was a woman. She vowed to prove a woman could equal a man. The Piache refused to show it. She feared the tribe’s wrath. But after the English left, the Piache celebrated—leading to a quarrel. She burned his hut, he fled, vowing to take the trumpet to another tribe.

So she took coca, her weapons, her feathers, and chased him—found him just as he revealed the trumpet. Afraid, she almost fled—but remembering the whites laughed at such things, she attacked and stole it.

“I hope you didn’t kill him?”

“I beat him—just a little. I thought you wouldn’t let me kill him.”

Amyas smiled—amused by her belief in his authority.

Then she added: “And I dared not return to the Indians. So I came after you.”

“And is that your only reason?” asked Amyas, stupidly.

He struck a hidden chord. She stiffened, blushing crimson.

“You never lie. Do you think I would?”

Then she fell back—silent, steady—following them, speaking to no one, determined to go wherever they went.
