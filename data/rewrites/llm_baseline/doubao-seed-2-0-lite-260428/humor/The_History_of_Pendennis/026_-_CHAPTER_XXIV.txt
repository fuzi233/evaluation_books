Every house has a skeleton tucked somewhere in its closets, and it’s a great comfort for miserable folks to learn that even their richest, sunniest-seeming neighbours have secret annoyances that make their lives a living drag. You’d never guess that Blanche Amory – the sweet-voiced, poetry-spouting, soft-smiling little “muse” everyone cooed over – was the exact skeleton of Clavering House, the bane of every single person who lived under its roof. It’s just like that tiny little pebble in your shoe that makes a 10-mile hike absolute torture: one stupid little obstacle, no bigger than a thimble, can ruin every single minute of your day. Who on earth would have guessed that a dainty, grinning little fairy like Blanche could turn an entire household against each other?

“Oi, Strong,” Sir Francis Clavering slurred one evening, leaning over the billiards table post-dinner, both men puffing on cigars – the world’s most reliable secret-spilling devices – “I swear to God, I wish your wife would drop dead already.”
“Mate, so do I,” Strong replied, sinking a cannon shot with a grin. “But she won’t. She’s gonna outlive all of us, mark my words. Why d’you want her six feet under, Frank?”
“Because then you could marry Missy,” Sir Francis drawled. “She’s not bad looking, and she’s got ten thousand quid coming to her – that’s a pretty penny for a sad old bloke like you.” He took a long drag and scowled. “And Strong, I swear I hate her more every single day. I can’t stand the little madam. Not one bit.”
“Not for twice that money, I’d marry her,” Strong cackled. “I’ve never met such a tiny demon in all my days.”
“I’d bloody poison her if I thought I could get away with it,” the Baronet said darkly. “No joke.”
“What’s she done this time, then?” his friend asked.
“Nothing new, just the same old rubbish. That girl’s got a superpower for making everyone around her miserable, it’s honestly impressive. Last night she made the governess run off crying straight from the dinner table. Then I passed little Frank’s bedroom, heard the poor kid wailing in the dark, turns out his sister spent half an hour scaring the life out of him with made-up ghost stories about the house. At lunch she gave my wife a right turn, too – and my lady’s a bit daft, but she’s a good sort, I won’t hear a word against her.”
“What’d Missy do to her?” Strong asked.
“Got out one of those silly keepsake books, pointed at a random portrait and said it looked exactly like her precious dead dad,” Sir Francis said, rolling his eyes. “Then she demanded to know where his grave was. Ugh, her dad! Every time Blanche so much as mentions the late Amory, my wife bursts straight into tears, and the little cow does it on purpose just to wind her up. I lost my rag this afternoon, yelled that I was her dad, that’s enough of that rubbish – and then she had a go at me.”
“What’d she say?” Strong snickered.
“Said I wasn’t her dad, that I was too thick to even understand her, that her real dad must’ve been a genius with fine feelings, unlike some bloke who only married her mum for her money.”
“Well, you did marry her for her money, didn’t you?” Strong pointed out.
“Doesn’t make it any less crap to hear it out loud, does it?” Sir Francis huffed. “I’m no literary genius, but I’m not the total idiot she makes me out to be. She always finds a way to make me look like a right prat, you know? Sways the whole house around her with her stupid sentimental airs. I wish she was dead, Ned.”
“Five minutes ago you were wishing my wife was dead,” Strong said, still perfectly good-humoured, and the Baronet, ever the model of unfiltered candour, replied: “Fair. If someone’s ruining my life, I wish they’d just pop off. I wish Blanche was at the bottom of a well, honestly.”

So it’s pretty clear from that very honest chat that our accomplished little friend Blanche had a few… character quirks that made her extremely unpopular with everyone who had to live with her. She fancied herself a misunderstood genius, full of exquisite deep feelings and brilliant literary talent, stuck surrounded by dull relatives who couldn’t possibly appreciate her. Her stepdad’s reading material extended no further than *Bell’s Life* and the Racing Calendar, and her wife Lady Clavering still wrote like a 13 year old schoolgirl who’d never heard of grammar. Since Blanche was completely convinced she was intellectually leagues above everyone she lived with, she made a point of reminding them of their inferiority every five minutes, framing herself as a poor martyred soul and making absolutely sure no one forgot how much she was suffering. If a poetess can’t wail about how unfair her life is, what’s even the point of owning a lyre? Blanche only played hers at the most miserable, weepy tunes, belting out elegies for her dead hopes and dirges for her nipped-in-the-bud teenage romances like she was the main character in the saddest French novel ever written.

Her actual hardships, for the record, were basically nonexistent. All her griefs lived inside her own head – she was perpetually miserable and dissatisfied, so of course she cried all the time. Her “Mes Larmes” bit was a party trick she could pull out on demand, and the more she practiced crying, the easier it got. Sentimentality is just like the dropsy Horace wrote about: the more you indulge it, the worse it gets.

Blanche was gushing dramatic nonsense from a shockingly young age. Lamartine was her favourite poet as soon as she could read, and by 16 she’d devoured every single novel Balzac and George Sand ever wrote. While her real-life family were a bunch of boring oafs, she insisted she had hundreds of soulmates in the “spirit world” – the moody heroines of all those French novels, the high-souled convict Trenmor, the fiery poet Stenio, the random princes from all those romance paperbacks. She’d been in love with two separate fictional princes while she was still in pinafores, and had strong opinions on divorce and women’s rights long before she was old enough to go to a party. She played at romance with these imaginary characters the same way other little girls played at being mums with their dolls – one week her favourite tragic hero was Rodolph, the next week he was tossed under the bed and replaced with a different sad poet.

Novelists, you see, know all the secrets of the female heart even the ladies themselves don’t pick up on, so we can confirm that 11 year old Betsy (her real, original name, don’t tell anyone) fell madly in love with a little Savoyard organ grinder in Paris and was fully convinced he was a kidnapped prince. At 12, she had a huge crush on her old, ugly drawing master, because obviously no amount of old or ugly can stand up to a great tragic romance. At 13, at her Parisian boarding school right next to an all-boys academy, she was pen-pals with two separate teen boys at the same time.

Oh right, that name! She was christened plain old Betsy, but rebranded herself “Blanche” all on her own when she got old enough to decide Betsy was far too common for a poetic genius. Sir Francis’s favourite secret weapon to keep her in line was the threat to yell “BETSY” at the top of his lungs in the middle of a fancy dinner party, a move that would make her turn bright red and back down immediately.

We were talking about dolls earlier, right? Well Blanche treated her real-life human friends exactly the same way: collecting locks of their hair to keep in a little fancy box, declaring they were soulmates for life, then dropping them the second they disappointed her. She once ran into her old boarding school “beloved sister” Leocadie in a Paris grocery shop, working the till for her grocer dad, and was so horrified by the disillusionment she wrote three poems about it. She considered every single one of these tiny inconveniences the well-earned penalties of being a great genius.

While she was off writing sad verses about her tortured soul, she made her mum so nervous she couldn’t even speak without messing up her words – if Lady Clavering accidentally said “sparrowgrass” instead of asparagus, Blanche would calmly correct her in front of guests until the poor woman was stuttering so bad she’d mix up half the other words in the sentence. The only person completely immune to her snark was Captain Strong, who just laughed at everything she said.

Over in the little town of Clavering, no one was more desperate to snoop on the fancy new family at the Park than Madame Fribsby, the local milliner. The very first Sunday the Claverings showed up at church, she ignored Doctor Portman’s entire 40 minute sermon (one of his proudest, oldest ones, too) to catalogue every single piece of clothing the ladies were wearing, from their bonnets down to their little booties. A few days later she’d wangled her way into a chat with Lady Clavering’s head lady’s maid at the Park’s housekeeper’s room, passed over her fancy bilingual business cards boasting of direct Paris fashion shipments and dressmaking for the entire local aristocracy, and was immediately hired.

Head maid Mrs. Bonner was soon a regular at Madame Fribsby’s little shop, spending every free evening there gorging on green tea, hot Sally Lunn cakes, scandal, and cheap novel readings. Blanche’s own lady’s maid Pincott never got to join the fun, though – her “muse” mistress worked her harder than any urchin in a factory. Blanche was forever getting random new urges to completely remake her dresses at 10pm, and since Pincott had trained as a dressmaker in Paris before coming to work for her, she was ripping seams, stitching new frills, and ironing crimps from 5am to 1am every single day. She couldn’t quit, either – her dad was a disabled cripple back home in England who couldn’t work, and she was the only one sending money home to feed her family.

Blanche, ever the gracious “generous” soul, never let Pincott forget it. “I should sack you, you know,” she’d say softly, while Pincott was doing her hair. “Your eyes are all red and puffy, you’re always snivelling about needing a doctor, you’re absolutely useless – but I’m keeping you on for the sake of your poor starving parents. You should be grateful.” Other days she’d sigh and say “Your sad little pale face is giving me a migraine, Pincott. I think I’ll make you wear rouge tomorrow so you don’t look so miserable.” Then she’d keep Pincott standing over her for an hour combing her hair while she read a George Sand novel, and at 1am she’d pat her hand and say “Alright, you can kiss me goodnight. Make sure the pink dress is completely remade by 7am tomorrow, okay?” before rolling over and going to sleep. She truly thought she was a benevolent, kind mistress, too. For sheer quiet soft-voiced cruelty, no screaming slave driver with a whip could ever top a delicate, weepy poetic genius like Blanche.

While Pincott was slaving away to the point she never got a single night off, another member of the Clavering household was a permanent guest at Madame Fribsby’s: the head chef, Monsieur Alcide Mirobolant.

The rustic locals of Clavering had no idea what to make of this very fancy Frenchman. On his days off, he’d wander the streets dressed head to toe in his favourite “Parisian gentleman” fit: a light green frock coat, crimson velvet waistcoat with blue glass buttons, gigantic bright plaid trousers, orange satin necktie, shiny leather-tipped boots, gold embroidered cap, and a fancy gilded cane. He was fully convinced he looked like the most stylish man alive.

He’d stroll down the main street grinning and winking at every woman he passed, peeking over garden railings at ladies through their windows, thinking his smouldering looks would make them drop dead on the spot. Unfortunately for him, Mrs Pybus’s maid Betsy shrieked and hid behind a laurel bush when he winked at her, the Miss Baker family stared at him like he was a madman, and a whole crowd of ragged urchins abandoned their dirt pies to follow him down the road.

At first he thought they were all gathering to admire him, and he strutted even prouder. But then the factory kids got off work, joined the crowd, and started hooting “FRENCHY! FROG EATER!” at him, yelling to get a lock of his long curly ringlets for a souvenir. It finally dawned on him he was being laughed at, not admired.

That’s when Madame Fribsby spotted him, poor red-faced and flustered with a whole jeering mob at his heels. She ran straight across the road, hailed him in her very terrible but very enthusiastic French, and dragged him inside her shop. Then she planted herself on the doorstep, yelled at the whole crowd that they were a bunch of cowards picking on a poor foreign man who couldn’t even speak their language, and everyone scattered, a little sheepish – Madame Fribsby was well-liked in town, and no one wanted to mess with her.

Mirobolant was so grateful to hear his native language spoken, he immediately declared her his guardian angel, the only nice, refined English lady he’d ever met. The next day he sent a basket over to her shop, full of pineapple flavoured cream, a lobster mayonnaise he’d made himself, and a box of Provence preserved fruits, alongside a gallant little note saying her kindness had made a blooming garden in the desert of his sad life. A fast friendship bloomed immediately.

The only fly in the ointment? Mirobolant kept calling her “my respectable Fribsbi! My virtuous Fribsbi!” and insisting he thought of her as his mother. Madame Fribsby, staring up at the old portrait of her long lost Carabineer love hanging over her mantelpiece, sighed deeply – she’d been half hoping this dashing young Frenchman was going to sweep her off her feet, and now she’d been demoted to mum. She told him, a little sadly, that she still felt as romantic as an 18 year old girl. Mirobolant very politely steered the conversation elsewhere, and told her he was actually very deeply in love with someone else entirely.

He didn’t waste any time launching into the full, dramatic backstory of his great grand passion.
