Major Pendennis, that seasoned old social warhorse who’d spent half his life honing a schmooze so smooth it could slip past a duchess’s chaperone without a second glance, ambled back to the George Inn at a very leisurely pace, fully prepared to toddle up to his room, blow out his candle, and put off dealing with his idiot lovesick nephew for at least another eight hours. Fate, however, had other (far more entertaining) plans.

His valet Morgan, the man who knew every secret in three counties before the gossips had even finished their first breakfast tea, was loitering by the front door like he’d been waiting to pounce. He intercepted his master mid-trudge for the stairs, bobbing his head with that infuriatingly smug air of someone holding top-tier blackmail-adjacent gossip. “Beg pardon, sir—you might want to pop into the coffee-room first. There’s a young gent in there I reckon you’d be over the moon to chat to.”

“Arthur? Did the little fool drag himself back to town at last?” the Major squawked, immediately tensing up like he was about to have to bail the boy out of some new mess.

“Not him, sir—Mr. Harry Foker, that’s. Lady Agnes Foker’s son. He ate such a massive dinner he conked out on the bench right after, only just woke up and rang for his coffee. Thought you two might hit it off,” Morgan said, yanking the coffee-room door open before the Major could protest.

The room was all but empty, save for Foker himself, rubbing bleary eyes like he’d just woken from a three-day bender, his boots propped up on the table next to a graveyard of empty decanters and half-eaten dessert leftovers. He’d meant to head to the theatre, see a play, maybe make eyes at the chorus girls, but a second helping of plum pudding and a couple bottles of claret had knocked him flat before he could even find his hat.

The Major was just mentally drafting his most charming opening line when Foker beat him to it, holding up the evening paper with the easy friendliness of a bloke who’d never met a stranger in his life. “Wanna borrow the Globe, sir?”

“Obliged to you, my boy,” the Major beamed, bowing so deep his old military coat flapped. “If my eyes don’t deceive me, you’re Harry Foker, Lady Agnes Foker’s son! I’ve had the pleasure of dining at your grandfather’s table many a time—you’ve got that classic Rosherville face, no mistaking it.”

Foker nearly blurted out that he’d pegged the Major for a traveling haberdashery salesman, but bit the comment back at the last second. “Oh, right! Pleasure to meet you, sir—who might I be addressing, exactly?”

“Arthur Pendennis’s uncle and guardian, that’s who. The boy’s talked about you so often I feel like we’re old friends already. Mind if I park my soda water at your table?”

“Sir, you do me proud!” Foker said, shifting all his half-eaten cake scraps to make room. “Arthur’s the best sort of bloke alive, if you ask me. Way cleverer than I am— I was always the dunce at school, could barely get through my Latin verses without crying. But I know a good chap when I see one, y’know?”

“Flattery and modesty both, that’s what that is!” the Major chortled, settling in. “Arthur’s told me all about what a sharp fellow you are, actually.”

“Pfft, not with books,” Foker said, wagging his head. “Couldn’t stick ’em for nuts. Arthur? He’d write half the whole school’s verses for ’em for a shilling and a glass of beer. But—look, sir, you’re his guardian, right? Hope you don’t mind me saying, but he’s a total flat when it comes to women. Proper daft.”

The Major’s ears pricked right up, like a hound that’s just spotted a fox. “A flat? How do you mean?”

Foker winked at him so boldly he’d have winked straight at the Duke of Wellington himself, no remorse required—two bottles of wine had left him with zero filters and zero fear of anyone’s rank. “Oh, c’mon, you know. He’s gaga over that actress, Miss Fotheringay. Real name’s Costigan, her dad’s that old rip they call the General, who lives on whisky and water and IOUs. We went to the theatre together a while back, and he fell for her so hard he face-planted right into her lap, practically. Proposed to her, even! Can you believe that?”

The Major nearly spilled his soda water all over his waistcoat. “He did what?”

“Proposed! We were all ribbing Sir Derby Oaks about it at mess yesterday, the other bloke who’s hanging around her, and he got so mad he almost challenged Pen to a duel. D’you know Oaks? You walked right past us smoking at the inn door the other night, before you went in to dinner. His old dad was a doctor I remember, back before he got made a baronet.”

“Wait a second—Oaks is another admirer?” the Major leaned forward, suddenly giddy.

“Mate, they’ve got a literal schedule! Sir Derby gets Mondays, Wednesdays, Fridays. Pen gets Tuesdays, Thursdays, Saturdays. Miss Fotheringay’s not daft, she’s playing ’em off against each other like a fiddle. Two strings to her bow, and all that.”

“Seems like you’re pretty wide awake to the whole game yourself, eh, Harry?” the Major snickered.

“Aw, I’m not clever or nothing, but I know what o’clock it is, yeah,” Foker shrugged, unphased. “Can I help you out with this whole mess? Seems obvious your lot ain’t exactly chuffed that your nephew’s about to shack up with an Irish theatre family that spends more on whisky than rent.”

“Couldn’t have put it better myself,” the Major nodded, even though half of Foker’s silly slang went right over his gray old head. So he started grilling Foker for every last scrap of tea about the Costigans, and Foker spilled it all, fair and square: the old man was a total scamp but chaperoned his daughter like a hawk, no funny business allowed, they were both holding out for a rich husband. And the kicker? All those gushy love letters Pen had been shoving in his breast pocket like they were holy scripture? They weren’t written by Miss Fotheringay at all. They were penned by her former bestie, Miss Rouncy, who was twice as sharp and way better at spelling, ever since the two girls had a screaming fight over a pair of ostrich feathers.

At that the Major’s eyes nearly popped out of his head. He wheedled Foker so hard that by the end of the night, Foker had slipped him a sample of Miss Rouncy’s handwriting right into his pocketbook. Score one for the Major.

Then came the bombshell that made the old half-pay major feel like he’d won the lottery. “Wait a second,” Foker said offhand, “the General’s dead certain Pen’s got two thousand a year coming to him when he turns twenty-one, y’know? That’s why he’s not kicked him out yet.”

“Two thousand a year?” the Major shrieked so loud the whole coffee-room echoed. “His mother’s only got five hundred a year total! She’ll probably live to be eighty, and Arthur hasn’t got a single penny to his name beyond whatever pocket money she tosses him! He’s not rich at all! They’ve been barking up the wrong money tree entirely! You’ve just made me the happiest man alive, Harry!”

They shook hands so hard their knuckles ached, made plans to chat more at breakfast before Foker drove off in his drag the next morning. Meanwhile, ten miles away, Pen was probably curled up in bed kissing one of those fake love letters like it was the crown jewels, none the wiser.

Bright and early the next day, Foker handed over that little note from Miss Rouncy, just like he promised, and trundled off in his drag. The Major, grinning like he’d robbed a bank, popped over to see Doctor Portman at the Deanery, spilling every last detail of his new discoveries over breakfast. They sat by the window staring across the lawn straight at Captain Costigan’s lodgings, the very same window where they’d spotted Pen mooning around a few weeks prior. The Doctor was so mad at the landlady Mrs. Creed for hiding Sir Derby’s visits that he threatened to excommunicate her from the cathedral entirely. But the Major was already raring to go.

“Right,” he laughed, finishing his coffee and standing up. “Off I go to fight the dragon.”

“God speed, Major!” the Doctor cackled back. “I’ll hear your confession when you get back, if you come back alive.”

The Major trundled round to Mrs. Creed’s front door, climbed the stairs to Costigan’s rooms, and heard a whole racket of stamping feet and shouts of “Ha, ha!” coming from behind the door. The tiny kid leading him up the stairs piped up, “That’s Sir Derby Oaks having his fencing lesson! He comes Mondays, Wednesdays, Fridays, exactly on the dot.”

The Major knocked, and a tall gentleman popped the door open, foil in one hand, fencing mask dangling from the other. The Major bowed as polite as could be. “Captain Costigan? Major Pendennis here, Arthur Pendennis’s uncle.”

Costigan snapped his foil up into a perfect military salute, his face lighting up like he’d just spotted a long-lost drinking buddy. “Major! The honer’s moine! Deloighted to make yer acquaintance, sure and begorrah!”
