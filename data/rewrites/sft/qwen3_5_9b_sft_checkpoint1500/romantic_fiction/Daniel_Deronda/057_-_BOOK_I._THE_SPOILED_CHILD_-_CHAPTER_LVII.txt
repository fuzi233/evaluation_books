"The unripe grape, the ripe, and the dried. All things change, not into nothing, but into that which is not yet." So wrote Marcus Aurelius, a man who understood the weight of time. For deeds are the pulse of existence; whether righteous or flawed, they beat onward long after the actor has ceased, echoing until the world falls silent.

It was late afternoon when she summoned him again. The light within the room was soft, muted by drawn blinds and tempered by the gentle breeze drifting through open windows. It was nearly the hour of her arrival the previous evening, brought in from the sea, yet here she sat, gazing fixedly at the water. Resting her cheek against her hand, she appeared less shattered than before, though a profound melancholy clouded her features. As Daniel Deronda approached, that sadness shifted into an anxious timidity. She did not offer her hand. "How long ago it is!" she whispered, then, "Will you sit near me again a little while?"

He moved to her side, just as he had before. Seeing her turn to him with an expression that hinted at a desire to speak, he waited patiently. Yet she looked back toward the window, silent, turning again with the same look, yet finding no words. Some fear held her tongue. To soothe her hesitation, Daniel averted his gaze. Soon, however, he heard her voice, imploring and thin.

"You will not say that anyone else should know?"

"Most decidedly not," Daniel replied firmly. "No action needs to be taken because of this. No injury exists that could be corrected by revelation. No retribution lies within the power of any mortal to dispense fairly."

For a long moment, she remained perfectly still, as if holding her breath. Then, softly, "But if I had not had that murderous impulse—that single moment—if I had cast the rope instantly... perhaps it would have prevented death?"

"No, I do not think so," Daniel answered slowly. "If he possessed the ability to swim, he must have succumbed to cramp. With your swiftest, most desperate effort, it seems impossible that you could have altered the outcome. That fleeting, dark will of yours likely could not have changed the course of events. Its impact remains confined to the motives within your own breast. Inside us, evil intent is mighty; sooner or later, it spills outward. Perhaps it breeds the vices that lead to wicked acts, but equally, it may forge the self-loathing that drives us toward better striving."

"I am saved from stealing from others," Gwendolen murmured, her voice trembling. "There are others who will take everything—their due share. I knew this sometime before I left town. Did you not suspect me of such dark desires regarding those matters?" She spoke with great hesitation.

"I had not considered that angle," Daniel admitted, "my mind was consumed by the immediate tragedy."

"Perhaps you do not quite grasp the beginning of it all," Gwendolen said slowly, as if battling a heavy reluctance. "There was another whom he ought to have married. I knew it, and I promised her I would stand aside. I left town—that was when you first saw me. But then we fell into poverty all at once, and misery overtook me. I was tempted. I told myself, 'I shall do as I please and set everything right.' I convinced myself. And everything changed. It became terrible. Then came hatred, and wicked thoughts. That was how it unraveled. I told you I feared myself. And I did what you advised—I tried to let that fear guard me. I imagined what would happen if I acted, feeling the dread of the morning approaching, wishing forever for night, yet even in the darkness, I saw something, saw death. If you truly knew how wretched I was, you might understand—but now, it has been useless. I care for nothing but shielding the rest from knowledge, especially poor Mamma, who has never known happiness."

Silence stretched between them before she spoke, her voice choked with a repressed sob. "You cannot bear to look at me anymore. You think I am too wicked. You do not believe I can become better—worth anything, worthy enough. I shall always be too wicked..." Her voice broke, leaving her helpless.

Daniel's heart pierced. He turned his eyes to her humble, beseeching face. "I believe you may become worthier than you have ever been," he said gently. "Worthy to lead a life that brings blessing. No evil condemns us to hopeless doom unless we love it, desire to continue it, and refuse to escape it. You *have* made efforts, Gwendolen. You will continue to make them."

"But you were the start of them," Gwendolen insisted, leaning forward with clasped hands, her gaze locked on his. Her face bore the piteous marks of a life experience compressed into twenty-four hours—a terrifying new reality lying just beyond the act that fulfilled a criminal desire. "I will endure any penance. I will live any life you command. But you must not abandon me. You must stay close. Had you been near me—if I could have poured my soul into your ears, I would have been different. Will you not abandon me?"

"It could never be my impulse to leave you," Daniel said promptly. His voice, like his eyes, carried an unintentional warmth that made his ready sympathy feel deeply personal, almost exclusive. In that moment, however, he was not entirely free from a premonition of a self-imprisonment. His fierce compassion for this stricken creature could not stop the rush of images regarding future difficulties. Though he met her pleading eyes, he did so with the painful awareness that her ear might hear a promise that time would eventually prove unfulfilled. He was offering an indefinite vow to an indefinite hope. Immediate and distant anxieties crowded his mind, shaping his next words.

"After a brief silence," he said, "I expect Sir Hugo Mallinger to arrive by tomorrow night at the latest. I harbor hope that Mrs. Davilow will follow him soon. Her presence will be your greatest comfort; it will give you a motive to spare her further pain."

"Yes, yes—I will try," Gwendolen agreed eagerly. "And you will not depart?"

"Not until Sir Hugo arrives."

"But shall we all travel to England?"

"As soon as possible," Daniel replied, avoiding specifics.

Gwendolen turned her gaze back to the window, an expression suggesting a gradual awakening to new thoughts. The twilight deepened perceptibly, yet Daniel observed a stirring in her eyes and hands, a return of perception in someone dazed by shock.

"You will always be with Sir Hugo now!" she said, looking at him. "You will always live at the Abbey—or perhaps at Diplow?"

"I am quite uncertain where I shall reside," Daniel said, blushing slightly.

Sensing his shift in color, Gwendolen realized she had spoken too hastily and fell silent. After a pause, she began again, eyes averted. "It is impossible to foresee how my life will unfold. I think now it would be better for me to be poor and compelled to labor."

"New prompts will emerge as the days pass," Daniel reassured her. "Amidst your friends again, you will discern fresh duties. Make it your current task to regain your strength and composure—to become as calm and composed as you were, before—" He hesitated.

"Before my mother returns," Gwendolen finished. "Ah! I must change. I have not examined myself. Would you recognize me," she asked, turning to face him, tears gathering, "if you met me now? Would you know me for the girl you saw at Leubronn?"

"Yes, I would recognize you," Daniel said, his tone mournful. "The external change is slight. I would have seen immediately that it was you, and that you had endured some great sorrow."

"Do not wish now that you had never seen me," Gwendolen implored, "do not wish that."

"I would despise myself for wishing it," Daniel confessed. "How could I possibly know what I wished? We must find our duties in what comes to us, not in imaginary alternatives. If I indulged in such foolish longing, I would not wish that I had never met you, but rather that I had been able to save you from this."

"You have saved me from worse," Gwendolen sobbed. "I would have been far worse had it not been for you. If you had not been good, I would have become more wicked than I am."

"It will be best for me to leave now," Daniel said, his spirit weary from the relentless strain of the scene. "Remember what we discussed regarding your task—to recover your health and peace before other friends arrive."

As he rose, she extended her hand to him in submission. But when he departed, she sank to her knees, overcome by hysterical weeping. The distance between them was vast. She was a soul exiled, beholding a possible life from which she had, through sin, severed herself.

She was discovered thus, crushed upon the floor. Such grief seemed natural for a gentle lady whose husband had perished in her sight.
