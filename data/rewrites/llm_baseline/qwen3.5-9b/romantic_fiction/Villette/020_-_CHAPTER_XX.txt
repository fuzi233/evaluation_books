One crisp morning, Mrs. Bretton breezed into my room with purposeful efficiency. "Open your drawers," she commanded, "and let me inspect your wardrobe." I complied silently, pulling aside the fabrics. After a brief perusal, she declared, "That will suffice. However, you require something new."

Before departing, she summoned a dressmaker. Measurements were taken with brisk precision. "I intend," she stated firmly, "to dictate my own taste and have my own way regarding this minor detail."

Two days later, a vibrant pink gown arrived.

"That is not for me," I protested hastily, feeling a sudden aversion to dressing like a high-ranking Chinese noblewoman.

"We shall determine its suitability shortly," my godmother retorted, her resolve unyielding. "Mark my words: you will wear it tonight."

I convinced myself I never would; that no earthly force could compel me into such attire. Pink was alien to my identity; I had yet to prove otherwise. Yet, my godmother decreed that I accompany her and Mr. Graham to a grand concert that very evening. Held in the magnificent hall of the city's premier musical society, the event featured the Conservatoire's most advanced students. It concluded with a charity lottery for the poor, capped by the attendance of the King, Queen, and Prince of Labassecour. Mr. Graham, having sent the tickets, emphasized that proper costume was a requisite respect for royalty and urged punctuality for the seven o'clock start.

Around six, I was escorted upstairs. Against my resistance, I found myself passively led by another's will—unconsulted, unpersuaded, and quietly overruled. Soon, the pink dress was on, softened by a mantle of black lace. I was deemed *en grande tenue* and asked to admire myself in the mirror. With palpable trepidation, I glanced at my reflection, then quickly turned away. When the clock struck seven, Dr. Bretton arrived, and we descended together. My godmother wore a somber brown velvet gown; walking in her shadow, I secretly coveted those folds of grave, dark majesty. Mr. Graham awaited us in the drawing-room doorway.

"I truly hope he doesn't think I've dressed merely to attract attention," I worried internally.

"Here, Lucy, some flowers," he offered, presenting a delicate bouquet. He paid no further heed to my outfit than a kind smile and a satisfied nod, which instantly alleviated my shame and fear of ridicule. Upon closer inspection, the dress was surprisingly simple, devoid of excessive frills; it was merely the light fabric and bright hue that unsettled me. Since Mr. Graham found nothing absurd in it, my own judgment soon reconciled itself to the garment.

Those accustomed to nightly public entertainments struggle to grasp the fresh excitement felt by novices attending an opera or concert for the first time. Though I harbored vague expectations of pleasure, I greatly enjoyed the journey. The cozy warmth of the carriage on a cold, clear night, the joy of traveling with such cheerful companions, and the sight of stars flickering through the trees as we rolled down the avenue created a delightful atmosphere. Emerging onto the open road, passing through the city gates with their glowing lights and stationed guards, subjected to a mock inspection that amused us all—these novel experiences possessed a uniquely exhilarating charm. Much of this stemmed from the enveloping aura of friendship; Dr. John and his mother were in their finest spirits, debating animatedly throughout the ride, treating me with frank kindness akin to family.

Our route took us through Villette's finest streets, brilliantly illuminated and teeming with life far more vibrantly than at midday. The shops sparkled with brilliance, and the river of existence flowed gladly and abundantly along the broad pavements. As I observed, memories of Rue Fossette surfaced—the walled garden, the schoolhouse, and the vast, dark classrooms where I usually wandered alone, gazing at stars through high, blind windows while listening to the monotone recitation of religious lectures in the refectory. Soon, I would return to that solitude; this shadow of the future crossed the radiant present with sobering timeliness.

Soon, we merged into a stream of carriages heading in one direction, and the facade of a great, illuminated building blazed ahead. Having never entered a venue of public entertainment before, my knowledge of what awaited inside remained imperfect. We alighted beneath a bustling portico, lost momentarily in the crowd, until I found myself ascending a majestic staircase. Wide, soft, and carpeted in crimson, it led to heavy doors draped in matching red cloth.

I barely registered the magic by which these doors swung open. Dr. John managed the mechanism; they rolled back to reveal a hall of immense grandeur. Its sweeping circular walls and domed ceiling appeared to be crafted entirely of dead gold, accented by cornicing, fluting, and garlands of bright gold or snow-white alabaster, or a mingling of both in wreaths of gilded leaves and lilies. Wherever drapery hung or carpets lay, the dominant color was deep crimson. Suspended from the dome was a dazzling chandelier—a mass of rock crystal sparkling with facets, streaming with droplets, ablaze with starlight, and gorgeously tinged with dewy gemstones or shattered rainbow fragments. To me, it seemed the work of Eastern genii; I almost expected to see the dark, cloudy hand of the Slave of the Lamp hovering in the perfumed air of the cupola, guarding its wondrous treasure.

We moved forward, unaware of our destination, until we suddenly encountered a party approaching from the opposite direction. For a fleeting moment, I perceived them clearly: a handsome middle-aged lady in dark velvet, a gentleman who might be her son possessing the finest face and figure I had ever seen, and a third figure in a pink dress and black lace mantle. I noted them all, including the stranger in pink, believing them to be unknowns and forming an impartial impression. But the illusion vanished almost instantly. Realizing I faced a great mirror filling the space between two pillars, the truth revealed itself: the party was our own. For the first, and perhaps only, time in my life, I experienced the strange gift of seeing myself as others see me. No need to elaborate on the aftermath; it brought a jarring discord and a pang of regret. It was not flattering, yet ultimately, I should be grateful, for it could have been far worse.

Finally, we took seats offering a commanding view of the vast, dazzling, yet warm and cheerful hall. It was already filled with a splendid assembly. While the women were not uniformly stunning, their dresses were flawless. Foreigners, even those ungainly in private, seem to possess an innate art of appearing graceful in public. Regardless of how blunt or boisterous their daily movements may be, they maintain a reserved elegance—a specific carriage of the head, arms, and mouth—reserved strictly for gala occasions, deployed with the *grande toilette* and the accompanying jewelry.

Among the guests were several fine figures, models of a peculiar beauty rarely seen in England: a solid, firm-set, sculptural style. These forms lacked sharp angles; a marble caryatid was almost as flexible, and a Phidian goddess possessed a perfection of stillness and stateliness. Their features resembled those painted by Dutch masters for their Madonnas: low-country classic traits, regular yet round, straight but stolid. Their depth expressed a calm so profound and passionless that only a polar snowfield could serve as a true type. Such women required no ornament; their smoothly braided hair provided sufficient contrast to their cheeks and brows. Their dresses needed not be ornate, for their rounded arms and perfect necks demanded neither bracelets nor chains.

Once, I had the honor of knowing one such beauty intimately. The inert force of the deep, settled love she bore herself was remarkable, surpassable only by her proud inability to care for any other living soul. Her cool veins carried no flow; placid lymph nearly obstructed her arteries.

A Juno of this description sat prominently before us, a focal point for all eyes, fully aware of her status yet immune to the magnetic pull of gazes. Cold, rounded, blonde, and beautiful as a white column topped with gilding, she stood beside us. Observing that Dr. John's attention was heavily drawn toward her, I pleaded in a whisper, "For heaven's sake, shield his heart. Do not fall in love with that lady, for you might die at her feet, and she would never love you in return."

"Very well," he replied. "How do you know that witnessing her grand insensibility might not be the strongest stimulus to my homage? The sting of desperation is, I believe, a wonderful irritant to my emotions." Shrugging his shoulders, he added, "But you know nothing of these things. I'll address my mother instead. Mamma, I'm in a dangerous way."

"As if that interests me!" Mrs. Bretton dismissed.

"Alas! the cruelty of my lot!" her son lamented. "No man ever had a more unsentimental mother. She never seems to consider that such a calamity as a daughter-in-law could befall her."

"If I don't prevent it, it isn't for lack of holding that same calamity over your head. You've threatened me with it for the last decade. 'Mamma, I am going to be married soon!' was your cry before you'd even finished taking off your jackets."
