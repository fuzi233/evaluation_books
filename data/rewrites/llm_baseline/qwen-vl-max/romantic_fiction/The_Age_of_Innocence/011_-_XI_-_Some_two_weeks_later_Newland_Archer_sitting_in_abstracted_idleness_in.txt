XI. Some two weeks later, Newland Archer sat in quiet contemplation within the private corner of the office of Letterblair, Lamson and Low—attorneys at law—when he was summoned by the firm’s senior partner. Old Mr. Letterblair, a man whose name had long stood for legal wisdom among three generations of New York's most distinguished families, sat behind his massive mahogany desk, looking troubled. As he smoothed his neatly trimmed white whiskers and ran a hand through his tousled gray hair above his prominent brows, Archer couldn’t help but think how much he resembled a family physician exasperated by a patient whose illness defied diagnosis.

“My dear sir,” Letterblair began—always addressing Archer with formal respect—“I have asked you to come in to discuss a matter of some delicacy—one I prefer not yet to share with Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood.” The men mentioned were the other senior partners, though in truth, all the names listed on the firm’s letterhead belonged to deceased predecessors. Letterblair himself was, in professional terms, the grandson of the original founder. He leaned back, frowning slightly. “For family reasons—” he paused, then smiled faintly—“the Mingott family.”

Archer looked up sharply.

“The Countess Olenska,” said Letterblair, “wishes to sue her husband for divorce. Mrs. Manson Mingott called me yesterday. Certain documents have been entrusted to me.”

He paused, tapping his fingers lightly on the desk. “Given your impending marriage into the family, I thought it wise to consult you first—before any further action is taken.”

A sudden warmth surged through Archer’s temples. He had seen the Countess only once since their last meeting—briefly, at the opera, seated in the Mingott box. In that time, she had receded from his thoughts, replaced by May Welland’s radiant presence, which had slowly restored the world to its familiar order. He had dismissed talk of a divorce as idle gossip, something whispered by Janey Mingott and no more than that. Divorce, after all, was nearly as repugnant to him as it was to his mother. And now, here was Letterblair—clearly acting under pressure from old Catherine Mingott—trying to draw him into the affair. Why must it be *him*? There were plenty of Mingott men who could handle such matters. He wasn’t even officially part of the family yet.

Still, he waited quietly for Letterblair to continue.

The older man unlocked a drawer and pulled out a sealed packet. “If you would kindly review these papers—”

Archer frowned. “I beg your pardon, sir—but precisely because of this prospective connection, I should prefer you consult either Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood.”

Letterblair blinked, surprised. It was rare for a junior partner to decline such an opportunity. He inclined his head. “I respect your scruples, sir—but in this case, I believe true discretion demands otherwise. This request does not come from me, but from Mrs. Manson Mingott herself—and her son. I’ve spoken with Lovell Mingott, and also with Mr. Welland. They both named you.”

Archer felt a flicker of irritation. For the past fortnight, he’d drifted along, allowing May’s charm and grace to gently erase the lingering tension stirred by the Mingotts. But now, this summons from the matriarch awakened a sense of obligation—of being expected to serve the family’s interests before his own. “Her uncles should deal with this,” he said firmly.

“They have,” Letterblair replied. “The family has discussed it. They oppose the idea. But the Countess is resolute. She insists on a legal opinion.”

Archer remained silent. He hadn’t opened the packet yet.

“Does she intend to remarry?” he asked finally.

“I believe it’s been suggested—but she denies it.”

“Then—”

“Would you do me the favor, Mr. Archer, of reviewing these papers first? Afterward, we can discuss them thoroughly. Then I’ll give you my view.”

Archer left reluctantly, clutching the documents like a burden. Since their last encounter, he had unconsciously allowed life to move forward without him—letting go of the emotional weight the Countess had placed upon his heart. That solitary evening by the firelight had brought them close, but the arrival of the Duke of St. Austrey and Mrs. Struthers had shattered the moment, and her warm greeting of them had restored social equilibrium. Two days later, he had watched her re-enter society with ease and dignity, and he had told himself, with a touch of bitterness, that a woman who could so skillfully charm powerful men did not need the private comfort—or public defense—of a young man like him.

That realization had cleared his mind. He could not imagine May Welland ever seeking counsel from strangers in moments of crisis. She seemed purer, stronger, more admirable than ever. He had even agreed to her wish for a longer engagement, moved by her simple reply when he pressed for haste: “You know, when it comes down to it, your parents have always let you have your way since you were a little girl.” And she had answered, with a clarity that touched him deeply: “Yes—and that’s what makes it so hard to refuse the very last thing they’ll ever ask of me as a little girl.”

That was the voice of old New York—the kind of sentiment he longed to hear from his future wife. To breathe that air was to feel clean, clear, untouched by shadows.

But the papers he now read offered no such clarity. They were mostly correspondence between Count Olenski’s lawyers and a French firm handling the Countess’s financial affairs. There was also a brief letter from the Count to his wife. When Archer finished reading it, he stood abruptly, stuffed the documents back into their envelope, and returned to Letterblair’s office.

“Here are the letters, sir,” he said, voice strained. “If you wish, I’ll see Madame Olenska myself.”

“Thank you, Mr. Archer,” Letterblair replied, relieved. “Come to dinner tonight, if you’re free. We’ll discuss it afterward. If you wish, you may call on our client tomorrow.”

Archer walked home that afternoon. The winter evening was crisp and bright, the moon rising innocent and pale over the rooftops. He wanted nothing more than to fill his lungs with the pure air, to remain silent until he could speak freely with Letterblair after dinner. He knew his decision was inevitable—he would meet the Countess himself. Not because he wished to interfere, but because he could not bear the thought of her secrets being exposed to others.

A wave of compassion washed over him, erasing his earlier indifference. She stood before him now as a vulnerable figure, wounded and defiant, fighting against forces too great for her to control. He remembered her words about Mrs. Welland’s plea to spare her from anything “unpleasant”—and suddenly understood the cost of such purity. Was their society merely a group of Pharisees, pretending innocence while hiding behind rigid rules?

He wondered, painfully, whether he could reconcile his natural revulsion at moral failure with his deep sympathy for human weakness. For the first time, he saw how shallow his own principles had always been. He was known as a man unafraid of risk—his secret romance with Mrs. Thorley Rushworth had earned him a reputation for daring. But now he realized: Mrs. Rushworth was “that kind of woman”—frivolous, vain, drawn more to secrecy than to love. The affair had been, in truth, just another episode common among young men of his class—a momentary lapse that ended without consequence, leaving them with clean consciences and a firm belief in the sacred divide between respectable women and those meant only to be enjoyed and pitied.

This illusion was carefully nurtured by mothers, aunts, and grandmothers—women like Mrs. Archer—who believed that when such things happened, the man was foolish, but the woman was always guilty. Any woman who loved recklessly was seen as scheming, dangerous, capable of ensnaring a good man. The solution? Marry early, marry well, and trust your wife to keep you safe.

But Archer began to suspect that in the complex, ancient societies of Europe, love was not so easily categorized. Among the idle, wealthy, and isolated, such dilemmas might arise naturally. Perhaps there were cases where a woman—sensitive, refined, alone—was pushed into a bond that conventional morality condemned, not from malice, but from desperation.

At home, he wrote a note to the Countess, asking when she might receive him. A messenger returned with word: she would be at Skuytercliff, visiting the van der Luydens, but would be alone that evening after dinner. The note was written on a half-sheet, untidy, undated—yet her handwriting was strong, confident. He smiled at the image of her staying at Skuytercliff, that vast, silent estate. But then he thought: nowhere else would she feel the coldness of minds so determined to ignore the “unpleasant.”

He arrived at Letterblair’s house at seven sharp, eager to excuse himself soon after dinner. He already had his own opinion formed from the papers—but he didn’t want to debate it with his senior partner. Letterblair was a widower, and they dined alone in a dim, shabby room adorned with yellowing prints of “The Death of Chatham” and “The Coronation of Napoleon.” On the sideboard stood a decanter of Haut Brion and another of old Lanning port—a gift from a client, Tom Lanning, who had sold off his cellar before dying mysteriously in San Francisco. The incident had been less scandalous than the sale itself.

They ate slowly—velvety oyster soup, shad and cucumbers, broiled turkey with corn fritters, canvas-back duck with currant jelly, and celery mayonnaise. Letterblair, who lunched on tea and a sandwich, dined with solemn deliberation, urging Archer to do the same. Finally, after dessert, cigars were lit, and Letterblair, leaning back in his chair and pushing the port glass toward the fire, said:

“The whole family opposes the divorce. And rightly so.”

Archer felt himself shift instantly to the opposite side. “But why, sir? If there ever was a case—”

“Well—what’s the use? _She’s_ here. _He’s_ there. The Atlantic is between them. She won suffice beyond what he’s already returned. Their marriage contracts protect that. As things stand over there, Olenski’s behaved generously—he might have left her penniless.”

Archer knew this. He said nothing.

“But,” Letterblair continued, “I understand she doesn’t care about the money. So, as the family says—why not let well enough alone?”

Archer had entered the house fully agreeing with Letterblair’s stance. But now, hearing it voiced by this comfortable, self-satisfied old man, it sounded hollow—like the sterile logic of a society devoted solely to shielding itself from discomfort.

“I think that’s for her to decide,” he said quietly.

“H’m. Have you considered the consequences if she chooses divorce?”

“You mean the threat in his letter? What weight would that carry? It’s nothing more than the rant of an angry man.”

“Yes—but if he actually defends the suit, it might stir some unpleasant talk.”

“Unpleasant—!” Archer burst out.

Letterblair raised an eyebrow, studying him. The young man sensed the futility of trying to explain what stirred in him. He bowed instead, letting the elder man continue.

“Divorce is always unpleasant,” Letterblair repeated.

“You agree with me?” he asked, after a pause.

“Naturally,” Archer said.

“Then I may count on you? The Mingotts may count on you—to use your influence against the idea?”

Archer hesitated. “I can’t promise until I’ve spoken with the Countess.”

Mr. Letterblair looked alarmed. “Mr. Archer, I don’t understand. Do you wish to marry into a family with a scandalous divorce hanging over it?”

“I don’t think that changes the matter,” Archer replied.

Letterblair set down his glass, fixing him with a wary gaze. Archer realized he might lose his mandate—and for some reason, he didn’t want that. Now that the task had been thrust upon him, he refused to abandon it. He needed to reassure the unimaginative old man who served as the Mingotts’ legal conscience.

“You may be certain, sir,” he said, “that I will not commit myself until I report to you. I simply meant—I’d rather not form an opinion until I’ve heard her side.”

Letterblair nodded, satisfied. Such caution was the hallmark of the best New York tradition. Archer glanced at his watch, excused himself with an engagement, and took his leave.
