“And after all I’ve done for her,” said Mr. Reginald Cracknell, with the damp nobility of a man who has been wronged by Woman and fortified by his own private stock, “after all I’ve done for her she throws me down.”

Sally said nothing, partly because the Flower Garden orchestra was at that moment playing with the cheerful brutality of a steam-hammer, and partly because she was occupied saving her toes from Mr. Cracknell’s. The man danced as if the floor were a hostile nation and he meant to conquer it foot by foot. They lurched past the table where Miss Mabel Hobson—newly installed as one of the place’s “hostesses”—sat in old gold and black, distant as a statue and twice as critical. Mr. Cracknell, catching her eye, emitted a gulp like a siphon being turned off too suddenly.

“If I told you,” he moaned into Sally’s ear, “what—was that your ankle? Sorry! Don’t know what I’m doing to-night—if I told you what I’ve spent on that woman, you wouldn’t believe it. And then she throws me down. All because I said I didn’t like her in that hat. Won’t speak to me a week. Won’t answer when I call up on the ’phone. And I was right too. Rotten hat. Didn’t suit her a bit. But that,” said Mr. Cracknell, gloomily, “is a woman all over!”

Sally made a small sound as his foot came down on hers with the deliberation of a court sentence. He mistook it for a protest against his doctrine.

“I don’t mean you’re like that,” he said quickly, being gallant in the manner of a man who has just committed assault and wishes to pass as the injured party. “You’re different. I could see that directly I saw you. You’ve got a sympathetic nature. That’s why I’m telling you. You’re a sensible and broad-minded girl—sorry!—and can understand. I’ve done everything for that woman. I got her this job as hostess here—you wouldn’t believe what they pay her. I starred her in a show once. Did you see those pearls she was wearing? I gave her those. And she won’t speak to me. Just because I didn’t like her hat. I wish you could have seen that hat. You would agree with me, I know, because you’re sensible and broad-minded and understand hats. I don’t know what to do. I come here every night.”

Sally was aware of this. She had seen him often enough—like a recurring symptom.

“I come here every night and dance past her table, but she won’t look at me. What,” asked Mr. Cracknell, with tears collecting in his pale eyes as if they had been ordered in advance, “would you do about it?”

“I don’t know,” said Sally, and meant it.

“Nor do I. I thought you wouldn’t, because you’re a sensible and broad-minded— I mean, nor do I. I’m having one last try to-night, if you can keep a secret. You won’t tell anyone, will you?” He clutched at the idea with desperate hope. “But I know you won’t because you’re sensible— I’m giving her a little present. Having it brought here to-night. Little present. That ought to soften her, don’t you think?”

“A big one would do it better,” said Sally.

Mr. Cracknell, registering this as a form of blasphemy, kicked her on the shin in an absent-minded way.

“I never thought of that. Perhaps you’re right. But it’s too late now. Still, it might. Or wouldn’t it? Which do you think?”

“Yes,” said Sally, because “no” would have required energy.

“I thought as much,” said Mr. Cracknell, relieved.

The orchestra stopped with a thump and bang, leaving him clapping feebly in the middle of the floor as if trying to restart it by hand. Sally slipped back to her table. Mr. Cracknell, after one of those uncertain glances men give the universe when it has mislaid them, zigzagged off in search of his own seat.

Conversation surged up again—voices, laughter, the crackle of plates, the rattle of cutlery, the little metallic percussion of a city feeding itself while it pretended to be merry. The Flower Garden air was hot and close, full of perfume and cigar-smoke and the exhausted scent of too many human beings pressed together for too long. Sally put her hands over her eyes. Her headache sat behind them like a tenant who had paid rent for a month and intended to stay.

A month had passed since she had come back to Mr. Abrahams. It had been a dull month, heavy as wet wool, each day sliding into the next until life seemed something she watched rather than lived. In a queer nightmare way she felt cut off from her own sort. She hadn’t seen anyone from the old boarding-house days. Fillmore, presumably uneasy of conscience, had not shown his face. Ginger was far away, working out whatever destiny Long Island had for him.

She lowered her hands and looked about her. The room was crowded as always. The Flower Garden was one of those Broadway establishments that had floated up on the dancing craze and stayed afloat, partly because, as its proprietor boasted, it was a nice place and run nice. It advertised itself as “a supper-club for after-theatre dining and dancing,” “large and spacious,” “sumptuously appointed,” with “incomparable dance-floor, enchanting music, cuisine, and service de luxe.” Even from a distance you could feel Isadore Abrahams thinking well of it.

Once, Sally had thought well of it too. In her first spell here it had been diverting—bright, busy, a show. But then she had not had headaches; and worse than headaches she had not had this dead, listless depression that made each night’s work feel like lifting a weight that never grew lighter.

“Miss Nicholas.”

The orchestra, which at the Flower Garden was never silent long enough for anyone to hear themselves regret, had started again. Lee Schoenstein, the master of ceremonies, stood smiling with the air of a man offering a gift, and presented her next partner. Sally rose as if a spring had been wound inside her and released.

“This is the first time I been in this place,” said the man as they began to bump through the crowd. He was large and clumsy—of course—and to-night Sally had the sensation that the whole world had been built for people with broad shoulders and no regard for the laws of motion. “It’s a swell place. I come from up-state myself. We got nothing like this where I come from.”

He cleared a space in front of him by using Sally as a battering-ram. By comparison with this, Mr. Cracknell’s dancing now seemed almost a refined old waltz at a duchess’s ball. This man was, beyond argument, the worst dancer in America.

“Give me li’l old New York,” said the man, knocking gently into a couple that had been minding its own business. “It’s good enough for me. I been to some swell shows since I got to town. You seen this year’s Follies?”

“No.”

“You go,” he urged, earnest as a prophet. “You go! Take it from me, it’s a swell show. You seen Myrtle Takes a Turkish Bath?”

“I don’t go to many theatres.”

“You go! It’s a scream. I been to a show every night since I got here. Every night regular. Swell shows all of ’em, except this last one. I cert’nly picked a lemon to-night all right. I was taking a chance, y’see, because it was an opening. Thought it would be something to say, when I got home, that I’d been to a New York opening. Set me back two-seventy-five, including tax, and I wish I’d got it in my kick right now. The Wild Rose, they called it,” he said with savage irony, as if he had discovered a conspiracy. “‘The Wild Rose!’ It sure made me wild all right. Two dollars seventy-five tossed away, just like that.”

The title stirred something in Sally’s mind, and then the shock came. Gerald’s new play. For weeks after she had returned to New York, she had been haunted by the fear of meeting him in the hall—his door opening as hers did, his face lifting and recognizing her. Then she had seen a paragraph in the morning paper: Gerald Foster was out on the road with a new play. And The Wild Rose—yes, that was it.

“Is that Gerald Foster’s play?” she asked quickly.

“I don’t know who wrote it,” said the man, “but he’s one lucky guy to get away alive. There’s fellows breaking stones on the Ossining Road done a lot less to deserve a sentence. Wild Rose! I’ll tell the world it made me go good and wild. Why, before the second act was over, the people was beating it for the exits, and if it hadn’t been for someone shouting ‘Women and children first,’ there’d have been a panic.”

Sally found herself back at her table without remembering the route, as if her body had done the necessary travelling while her mind stood still somewhere on the words Gerald Foster.

“Miss Nicholas.”

She started to rise again—and then stopped. This voice was not Lee Schoenstein’s official voice, coming through gold teeth with professional sweetness. The man had sat down beside her and was speaking her name in precise, clipped accents that her memory recognized before her eyes did. The blur cleared.

Bruce Carmyle.

“I called at your place,” Mr. Carmyle was saying, “and the hall porter told me you were here, so I ventured to follow you. I hope you do not mind. May I smoke?”

He struck a match and lit a cigarette with an air of self-command. His fingers trembled, but he acted as though this were a mere vibration in the atmosphere and not an earthquake in himself. Bruce Carmyle’s ideal was the strong man who rises above his emotions. He meant to rise above them now, even if he had to climb on his own head to do it. He glanced sideways at Sally and thought that never, not even in the garden at Monk’s Crofton on that morning that had changed everything, had he seen her look prettier. Her face was flushed and her eyes bright—bright with fatigue, with pain, with too much noise. The stout wraith of Uncle Donald, which had been puffing disapproval somewhere in the background of Mr. Carmyle’s consciousness, faded for the moment into mist.

There was a pause, filled by the Flower Garden’s soundscape: brassy music, laughter that cut short, the hiss of seltzer, the thick shuffle of feet. Mr. Carmyle puffed vigorously, as if smoking could supply missing conversation.

“When did you land?” asked Sally, because silence felt like standing in the path of a train.

“I landed to-night,” said Bruce Carmyle, turning and facing her squarely.

“To-night!”

“We docked at ten.”

He turned away again, having delivered the fact like a challenge and leaving it to do its work.

Sally sat still. She understood the meaning: he had come straight from the ship to find her. It should have stirred her into excitement or alarm. It hardly did. Fighting—thinking—seemed like things done by other people. She felt inert, like a swimmer who has battled too long and is ready to let the water take her. The heat pressed on her. The noise worried her nerves like hands.

“Shall we dance this?” he asked.

The orchestra had begun again—one of those creamy, sensuous melodies Broadway takes up, plays to death, and throws away. Sally had heard it a hundred times until it had become stale; yet tonight it seemed part of the room’s physical weight, like air you had to push through.

“If you like.”

Efficiency was Bruce Carmyle’s religion. He did nothing unless he could do it well. Dancing, he had long ago decided, was part of a gentleman’s education, and he had taken lessons as conscientiously as he would have taken Latin. Sally, bracing herself for the usual Flower Garden experience—bumping, wrenching, the slow grinding war of bodies trying to obey different rhythms—found herself in the arms of a master. He steered her through the crowd as if he had drawn a map of it beforehand. His control was oddly soothing. Her nerves, pulled taut for weeks, slackened. A miraculous peace came over her, as though the entire room had lowered its voice by a degree. With eyes half closed she yielded to the rhythm; and the tune, mysteriously, was no longer cheap and overfamiliar. For those minutes it might have been something new, something belonging to her.

And in that moment her whole attitude toward Bruce Carmyle shifted.

She had never examined her feelings about him in detail, but she had known one thing since the beginning: he had been physically distasteful to her. Handsome, yes, in a dark and rather sinister way; but she had shrunk from him. Now, carried by the dance, that repugnance slipped away as if a barrier between them had simply fallen. He no longer seemed alien. He seemed—dangerously—like relief.

“Sally!”

His arm tightened. She saw his face. His dark eyes blazed into hers, and she stumbled with a sudden helplessness. The half-dream shattered. This dance had not postponed anything. It had only made her defences drowsy. In a hot whisper, swept and broken by the flood of music that turned raucous again, he repeated what he had said under the trees at Monk’s Crofton, in the mild English spring. Dizzily she resented the unfairness of being attacked at such a moment, when her mind was too tired to parry; but resentment itself required energy, and she had little to spare.

The music stopped abruptly. Clapping demanded more, and the orchestra began again. Sally moved away to her table. Bruce Carmyle followed like a shadow.

He had said his say. Now he waited. Sally stared before her, trying to marshal thoughts that slid away like playing-cards on a tilted tray. She was tired—tired until even fear felt heavy. Her eyes burned. She tried to face it squarely: was anything worth struggling for? And the only answer she could find was the ache of exhaustion.

The music stopped again. This time, though people clapped, the orchestra did not respond. The dance-floor emptied. Shoes ceased their soft scraping. The Flower Garden became as quiet as it ever allowed itself to be. Even the babble dimmed, as if the room, like a person, had stopped talking for a moment to listen inwardly.

Sally closed her eyes; and from somewhere high near the roof came the song of a bird.

Isadore Abrahams, a man of his word, had advertised a Flower Garden, and he had attempted to supply something resembling one. Paper roses looped the walls. Real tulips stood in tubs by the pillars. And from the roof hung cages with birds. One of these, stirred by the lull in the tumult, had begun to sing.

Often Sally had pitied those birds, condemned to spend their lives performing pastoral charm above Broadway uproar. More than once she had pleaded with Abrahams to release them, and had been refused. But now it didn’t occur to her that the bird’s song might be a prayer in its own language—like the prayers she had made in her thoughts—to be taken out of this place. To her, at this moment, the song sounded cheerful. It soothed her. It seemed to lay a cool hand on her forehead. And in her mind there rose a vision of Monk’s Crofton—green lawns, shaded paths, the clean smell of earth and leaf, the quiet that let a person hear themselves breathe. It beckoned like an oasis to a traveller in a hot, loud desert.

She felt Bruce Carmyle’s hand close on hers. She looked down and shivered slightly. She had always disliked his hands. Strong, bony, with black hair on the back of them—hands that seemed made for gripping. One of her earliest feelings about him had been that she would hate those hands to touch her. Yet she did not draw away. Again the vision of that cool garden flickered—a haven where she could rest.

He leaned toward her, whispering. The room seemed hotter than ever, noisier than ever, fuller than ever. The bird sang again, and now Sally understood what it said. Take me out of this.

Did anything matter except that? What did it matter how one was taken, or where, or by whom, so long as one was taken?

Monk’s Crofton looked cool and green and peaceful…

“Very well,” said Sally.

Bruce Carmyle, now an accepted suitor, found himself oddly dissatisfied. It was not that Sally had accepted without warmth—warmth could come later, he told himself; warmth was a detail. What troubled him was that his correct mind suddenly perceived he had chosen an unsuitable time and place for what ought to have been a properly conducted scene. He belonged to the orthodox school of proposals: moonlight, solitude, and a decorous hush. The Flower Garden, for all its nice-ness and the nice way it was run, jarred on him like a wrong note.

Music had begun again, but it was not the music a lover requires if he wishes to be eloquent. It was brassy, clashy—a ribald one-step that seemed to mock sentiment. Couples dipped and swayed and collided as far as he could see. Behind him two waiters had halted to argue, as waiters love to do, with rapid, emphatic gestures.

To continue the scene at the proper emotional level was impossible. Bruce Carmyle began his engagement by falling back on small-talk.

“Deuce of a lot of noise,” he said, querulously.

“Yes,” said Sally.

“Is it always like this?”

“Oh, yes.”

“Infernal racket!”

“Yes.”

The romantic side of Bruce Carmyle could have wept. In his private imaginings of himself as a triumphant wooer, the moments after the whispered yes had always been his best. He saw himself bending proudly, murmuring memorable phrases into a bowed head. But how could any man murmur memorably in a pandemonium? His tenderness, failing to find atmosphere, turned sharply into irritability.

“Do you often come here?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“What for?”

“To dance.”

He chafed. The scene had suddenly reminded him of his first ball at twenty, when he had sat behind a potted palm, melting in a too-high collar and attempting conversation with a formidable nymph in pink. It was one of the few times he had been at a complete disadvantage. To have that memory resurrected now, at what ought to have been the supreme moment of his life, annoyed him. Almost angrily he tried to jerk the conversation into romance.

“Darling,” he murmured—by shifting his chair two feet to the right and bending sideways, he discovered he could murmur—“you have made me so—”

“Batti, batti! I presto ravioli hollandaise!” cried one of the waiters behind him.

“La donna e mobile spaghetti Napoli Tetrazzini,” came back the second waiter, spirited and fluent.

“—you have made me so—”

“Infanta Isabella lope de Vegas mulligatawny Toronto,” said the first, weakening but returning bravely.

“—so happy—”

“Funiculi funicula Vincente y Blasco Ibanez vermicelli sul campo della gloria risotto!” concluded the second, clinching the matter with what sounded like a technical knock-out.

Bruce Carmyle abandoned literature and lit a moody cigarette. He was oppressed by the feeling—known to many—that it was all wrong.

The music stopped. The two leading citizens of Little Italy vanished, presumably to begin a vendetta elsewhere. Comparative calm returned. But Mr. Carmyle’s emotions, like sweet bells jangled, were out of tune. He could not recover the first fine careless rapture. Small-talk remained.

“What has become of your party?” he asked.

“My party?”

“The people you are with.”

“I’m not with anybody.”

“You came here by yourself?” Bruce Carmyle exclaimed, aghast. In his correctly ordered world girls did not go alone to restaurants. The wraith of Uncle Donald returned, large and stern, puffing disapproval through an invisible walrus moustache.

“I am employed here,” said Sally.

Bruce Carmyle started as if someone had touched him with an icicle.

“Employed here?”

“As a dancer, you know. I—”

She broke off. Her attention had been caught by something across the room: a red-headed young man of sturdy build had appeared beside the chair where Mr. Cracknell sat in huddled gloom. The newcomer carried a basket, and from inside it rose a sudden sharp yapping that cut through conversation like a knife through soft bread.

Mr. Cracknell roused, took the basket, lifted the lid. The yapping increased as if the creature inside had opinions and intended them to be heard. Mr. Cracknell got up, basket in his arms, and with the uncertain steps of a man walking toward either reconciliation or execution, crossed the floor to Miss Mabel Hobson’s table. The next moment that haughty lady—centre of an admiring, curious crowd—was hugging to her bosom a protesting Pekingese puppy, while Mr. Cracknell, seizing his chance like a general who sees the enemy’s flank, deposited himself in a chair at her side. The course of true love, lubricated by small dogs, ran smooth again.

The red-headed young man, having delivered his cargo of sentiment, looked across the room and gazed fixedly at Sally.

“As a dancer!” repeated Mr. Carmyle. Of all the people watching the little drama of puppy and pearls and hat-hatred resolving into peace, he alone had missed it entirely. His thoughts were elsewhere, where Uncle Donald stood pointing an accusing finger.

A dancer. A professional dancer at a Broadway restaurant.

Hideous doubts began creeping into Bruce Carmyle’s mind, cold and insinuating. What did he truly know about this girl to whom he had just offered the priceless boon of his society for life? How did he know what she was—he could not find the exact adjective, but he knew what he meant. Was she—worthy?

All his life he had maintained a prim shrinking from the part of the feminine world connected with the light-life of great cities. He had seen acquaintances in London marry girls from the Gaiety Chorus, and though he had no objection to the Gaiety Chorus in its proper place—on the other side of the footlights—he had always regarded the young men afterward as social outcasts. The fine, dashing frenzy that had brought him all the way from South Audley Street to win Sally began to ebb like a tide turning.

Sally, hearing him, looked at him. There was a candid honesty in her gaze that, for a moment, sent the creeping doubts scuttling back into the darkness. He had not made a fool of himself, he protested inwardly to Uncle Donald. Who, he demanded, could look at Sally and think for an instant she was not perfect and lovable?

A warm revulsion swept over him, like a returning tide.
