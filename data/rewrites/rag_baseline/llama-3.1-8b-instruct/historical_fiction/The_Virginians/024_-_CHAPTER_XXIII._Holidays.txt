The air was heavy with anticipation in the Lambert household. Mrs. Lambert's idealized vision of their young guest, Mr. Warrington, had begun to fray at the edges. As she and her daughter, Theo, sat in the parlor, they couldn't help but feel a sense of disillusionment creeping in. The romantic notions they had woven around him now seemed shallow and trite. Mrs. Lambert's attempt to console Theo was a mixture of tender words and gentle gestures, a soothing balm for the wounded ego of her daughter. "Never mind, my dear," she whispered, her lips brushing against Theo's cheek, "our expectations were unrealistic, and we mustn't let them cloud our judgment."

Theo's sister, Hetty, on the other hand, was irrepressible. She delighted in teasing her family members, often at the expense of their guest. Her remarks about Prussia and Persia were laced with sarcasm, and she wouldn't hesitate to bring up the Persian Tales from her mother's bookshelf, leaving them on the table as a provocative reminder of their guest's supposed nobility. When her mother and sister grew frustrated, Hetty would feign innocence, feigning tears and coaxing them back into a state of affectionate truce.

Simple, unassuming Mr. Warrington remained oblivious to the undercurrents of humor and tension surrounding him. He was still finding his footing, uncertain about his place within this new family dynamic. His interactions with the Lamberts were a gentle dance, with him trying to gauge the social norms and conventions that governed their relationships. A query about the Shaw or the Sophy, the rulers of Prussia and Persia, left him bewildered, unsure of the relevance or context. The complexities of their conversations, the nuances of their emotions, were lost on him, much like a stone dropped into a tranquil pool, creating ripples beneath the surface, unseen and unperceived by the observer above.
