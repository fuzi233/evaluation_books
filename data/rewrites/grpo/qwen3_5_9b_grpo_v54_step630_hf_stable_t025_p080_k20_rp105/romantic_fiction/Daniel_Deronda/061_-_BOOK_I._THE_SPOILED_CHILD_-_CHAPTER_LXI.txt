The news of Grandcourt's death sent a tremor through two distinct houses that day. In the white house at Pennicote, the shock was contained by Rex Gascoigne's stoicism. But in the modest home of the Meyricks, the same tidings stirred a violent agitation that Mirah struggled desperately to suppress.

Hans Meyrick had a ritual: he delivered the *Times* to his mother, a woman who devoured news from high politics to the latest marriage lists, treating the latter as a way to finish novels without the labor of reading them. Usually, Hans arrived just as Mirah finished teaching her sister Mab, claiming he came to hear her sing. But on this particular Wednesday, he burst into the parlor with a crackle that shattered the air. Mab was mid-verse on *Lascia ch'io pianga*, her imitation of her teacher faltering as the paper tore open. Mirah, playing the accompaniment, jumped, startled by the thunderous noise.

"O-o-o, Hans!" Mab cried, "Why do you bring a more horrible noise than my singing?"

"What on earth is the wonderful news?" Mrs. Meyrick asked, her eyes darting to her daughter. "Italy? The Austrians giving up Venice?"

"Nothing about Italy, but something *from* Italy," Hans replied, his tone carrying a peculiar weight that made his mother uneasy. He held the paper aloft, his expression suggesting a private triumph. "Nothing bad for anyone we care about. Rather uncommonly lucky, I think. I never knew anybody die conveniently before. Considering what a dear gazelle I am, I'm constantly wondering to find myself alive."

"Oh me, Hans!" Mab snapped, impatient. "If you must talk of yourself, let it be behind your own back. What *is* it?"

"Duke Alfonso is drowned, and the Duchess is alive," Hans announced, pointing a finger at a paragraph. "But the real story is this: Deronda was at Genoa in the same hotel. He saw her brought in by the fishermen who saved her. They say she jumped in after her husband—a less judicious action than I'd expect of the Duchess. However, Deronda was there to take care of her. Lucky fellow."

Mirah sank back onto the music stool, her eyelids lowering, her hands clasping tightly in her lap. Mrs. Meyrick handed the paper to Mab. "Poor thing! She must have been fond of her husband to jump in after him."

"It was an inadvertence—a little absence of mind," Hans said, creasing his face roguishly and slouching into a chair near Mirah. "Who can be fond of a jealous baritone with freezing glances, always singing asides? That was the husband's role, depend upon it. Nothing can be neater than his getting drowned. The Duchess is at liberty now to marry a man with a fine head of hair and glances that will melt instead of freezing her. And I shall be invited to the wedding."

Mirah sprang from her seat, her eyes fixing on Hans with an angry gleam. Her voice, deeply shaken, rang with indignation. "Mr. Hans, you ought not to speak in that way. Mr. Deronda would not like you to speak so. Why will you say he is lucky? Why use words of that sort about life and death when what is life to one is death to another? How do you know it would be lucky if he loved Mrs. Grandcourt? It might be a great evil to him. She would take him away from my brother—I know she would. Mr. Deronda would not call that lucky to pierce my brother's heart."

The room fell silent, struck by the sudden transformation. Mirah's face, usually rich of tint, was pale with a look of anger that might have suited Ithuriel. Hans sat transfixed, blushing as if he were a girl, his composure shattering.

"I am a fool and a brute," he stammered, withdrawing every word. "I'll go and hang myself like Judas—if it's allowable to mention him." Even in sorrow, his improvisations retained a drollery, but the damage was done.

Mirah's anger did not abate. She had spoken as creatures in intense pain bite through their own flesh to make the agony bearable. She said no more, seating herself at the piano and pressing the sheet music as if preparing to play, though her hands trembled.

Mab broke the tension. "Mirah is quite right to scold you, Hans. You are always taking Mr. Deronda's name in vain. And it is horrible, joking in that way about his marrying Mrs. Grandcourt. Men's minds must be very black, I think," she concluded with scorn.

"Quite true, my dear," Hans murmured, rising to walk toward the back window, avoiding his mother's gaze.

"We had better go on, Mab; you have not given your full time to the lesson," Mirah said, her tone higher than usual. "Will you sing this again, or shall I sing it to you?"

"Oh, please sing it to me," Mab rejoiced, eager to ignore the storm that had passed.

Mirah sang *Lascia ch'io pianga*, pouring forth its melodious sobs with new fullness and energy. Hans paused, leaning against the mantelpiece, keeping his eyes carefully averted from his mother. When the final chord faded, Mirah rose. "I must go home now. Ezra expects me."

She offered her hand silently to Mrs. Meyrick, hesitating, unable to look at her, instead of the usual kiss. The little mother drew Mirah's face down, soothingly whispering, "God bless you, my dear." Mirah felt a sharp pang of compunction, sensing she had committed an offense against her mother by rebuking Hans, mixing her suffering with a sense of proud ingratitude. Her friend had divined this guilt.

Hans seized his wide-awake, ready to open the door. "Now, Hans," Mab said, her sisterly tenderness cunningly disguised, "you are not going to walk home with Mirah. I am sure she would rather not. You are so dreadfully disagreeable to-day."

"I shall go to take care of her, if she does not forbid me," Hans said, opening the door.

Mirah said nothing. As he opened the outer door and closed it behind him, he walked by her side unforbidden. She lacked the courage to speak again, conscious that her words had been unbecomingly severe, yet finding only severer words behind them in her heart. A crowd of thoughts thrust forward, interpreting the consciousness that remained unaltered within her.

Hans's mind was equally busy. Mirah's anger had awakened a new perception in him, accompanied by the unpleasant realization that he was a dolt for not seeing it sooner. He wondered if Mirah's heart was entirely preoccupied with Deronda in a character other than that of her brother's benefactor. The supposition brought anxieties that, to his credit, were not wholly selfish. Hans was convinced of a serious attachment between Deronda and Mrs. Grandcourt. He had pieced together fragments of observation, completed by what his sisters heard from Anna Gascoigne, convincing him not only of the Duchess's passion but also of Deronda's concealed love, masked by austere self-repression. Some men, holding such a conviction, would avoid allusions that roused such susceptibility; but Hans's talk naturally fluttered toward mischief, a form of experiment on live animals consisting in irritating his friends playfully. His experiments had satisfied him that what he thought likely was true.

On the other hand, any susceptibility Deronda showed regarding attentions to Mirah, Hans dismissed as accounted for by her dependent position. He credited his friend with unselfish anxiety for those he could rescue. Deronda's insistence that Mirah would never marry one who was not a Jew seemed to exclude himself, since Hans shared the ordinary opinion that Deronda was the son of Sir Hugo Mallinger.

Thus, Hans felt clear about Deronda's affections. But now, the events striking him as conducive to a union between Deronda and Mrs. Grandcourt had called forth a flash of revelation from Mirah—a betrayal of her passionate feeling that made him melancholy for her, as well as for himself. Yet, on the whole, he felt less melancholy than if he had imagined Deronda's hopes fixed solely on her. It was common, he reasoned, for a man to see the beloved object unhappy because his rival loves another, with more fortitude and milder jealousy than if he saw her entirely happy in his rival. At least, it was so with the mercurial Hans, who fluctuated between contradictory states: wounded because Mirah was wounded, and almost obliged to Deronda for loving somebody else. Impossible to give Mirah a direct sign of how he understood her anger, he longed for his speechless companionship to be eloquent in a tender, penitent sympathy, an admissible form of wooing a bruised heart.

Thus, the two walked side by side in a companionship that seemed an agitated communication, like two chords whose quick vibrations lie outside our hearing. When they reached Mirah's home, and Hans said "Good-bye," putting out his hand with an appealing look of penitence, she met the look with melancholy gentleness. "Will you not come in and see my brother?"

Hans could not but interpret this as a sign of pardon. He lacked the understanding of what Mirah's nature had been wrought into by her early experience to divine how the strength of her late excitement made it pass quickly into the resolute acceptance of pain. When he said, "If you will let me," and they went in together, half his grief vanished, replaced by a little romance of how his devotion might make him indispensable to Mirah in proportion as Deronda gave his elsewhere. This was fair, since his friend was provided for according to his own heart; and on the question of Judaism, Hans felt fortified: who ever heard in tale or history that a woman's love went in the track of her race and religion? Moslem and Jewish damsels were always attracted toward Christians, and now, if Mirah's heart had gone forth too precipitately toward Deronda, here was another case in point. Hans was wont to make merry with his own arguments, calling himself a Giaour and antithesis the sole clue to events, but he believed a little in what he laughed at. Thus, his bird-like hope, constructed on the lightest principles, soared again in spite of heavy circumstances.

They found Mordecai looking singularly happy, holding a closed letter, his eyes glowing with a quiet triumph that, in his emaciated face, gave the idea of a conquest over assailing death. After the greeting between him and Hans, Mirah put her arm round her brother's neck and looked down at the letter without the courage to ask about it, though she felt sure it was the cause of his joy.

"A letter from Daniel Deronda," Mordecai said, answering her look. "Brief—only saying that he hopes soon to return. Unexpected claims have detained him. The promise of seeing him again is like the bow in the cloud to me," he continued, looking at Hans. "And to you it must be a gladness. For who has two friends like him?"

While Hans answered, Mirah slipped away to her own room. She did not go to indulge in an outburst of passion. If angels, once supposed to watch the toilet of women, had entered the little chamber with her and let her shut the door behind them, they would only have seen her take off her hat, sit down, and press her hands against her temples as if she had suddenly reflected that her head ached. Then she would rise to dash cold water on her eyes, brow, and hair until her backward curls were full of crystal beads, drying her brow and looking out like a freshly-opened flower from among the dewy tresses of the woodland. Then, giving deep sighs of relief, she would put on her little slippers, sit still for a couple of minutes—an eternity to her, so full of things to come—and rise with an air of recollection to make tea.

Something of the old life had returned. She had been used to remember she must learn her part, go to rehearsal, act and sing in the evening, and hide her feelings from her father. The more painful her life grew, the more she had been used to hide. The force of her nature had long found its chief action in resolute endurance, and today the violence of feeling that caused the first jet of anger had quickly transformed itself into a steady facing of trouble, the well-known companion of her young years. But while she moved about and spoke as usual, a close observer might discern a difference between this apparent calm, the effect of restraining energy, and the sweet genuine calm of the months when she first felt a return of her infantine happiness.

Those who have been indulged by fortune and have always thought of calamity as what happens to others feel a blind, incredulous rage at the reversal of their lot, half believing their wild cries will alter the course of the storm. Mirah felt no such surprise when familiar Sorrow came back from brief absence and sat down with her according to the old use and wont. This habit of expecting trouble rather than joy hindered her from having any persistent belief in opposition to the probabilities which were not merely suggested by Hans, but were supported by her own private knowledge and long-growing presentiment. An attachment between Deronda and Mrs. Grandcourt, ending in their future marriage, had the aspect of a certainty for her feeling. There had been no fault in him; facts had ordered themselves so that there was a tie between him and this woman who belonged to another world than hers and Ezra's—nay, who seemed another sort of being than Deronda, something foreign that would be a disturbance in his life instead of blending with it. Well, well—but if it could have been deferred so as to make no difference while Ezra was there!
