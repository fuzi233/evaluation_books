I left London with the drums beating in my heart and the flags waving in my brain. Somewhat more than a year later, on a foggy, wet December evening, I sneaked back to it—defeated, disgraced. Ah, defeat is a small thing, easily corrected; disgrace is heavier. I returned to the city not as a conqueror, but as a fugitive seeking a hiding place. Lost in the crowd, I hoped to waste my days unnoticed until I could summon the resolution to end this dead life.

Once, I had been ambitious. I dwelled again amid the bitterness of those following months, writing now in the past tense. I had been eager to make a name, to secure a position. But to claim no higher aim would be an injustice to my blood—to the memory of the great-souled gentleman whose life was an ode to honor, and to the woman of simple faith who taught me only one prayer: "God help me to be good!" I had wished to be a great man, yes, but it was to have been a *great good* man. The world was to admire me, but respect me too. I was to be the knight without fear, and rarer still, without reproach—Galahad, not Lancelot. Instead, I had learned to be a feeble, backboneless fighter, conquered by the first serious assault of evil. I became a creature of mean fears, a slave to every crack of the devil's whip, feeding like a swine.

Urban Vane, I discovered, was a common swindler. He had stolen his play from the desk of a well-known dramatist he met in Deleglise's kitchen. When the original author fell ill, Vane visited constantly. Partly recovered, the man fled to Italy. Had he died there, as many feared, the theft might never have surfaced. News reached us in a small northern town that he had regained his health and was returning to England. It was then that Vane, with calm indifference, smoked his cigar over a bottle of wine I had poured for him, and told me the bald truth, adorned with touches of wit. Had the recital come sooner, I might have acted differently. But six months of companionship with Urban Vane—if it hadn't, by grace of God, destroyed the roots of whatever flower of manhood might have been planted in me—had most certainly withered its leaves.

The man was clever. That he wasn't clever enough to realize from the start what he learned later—that honesty is the best policy, especially for men with brains—remains a mystery to me. Where once he made hundreds through shady dealings, he now, I suppose, makes thousands in the broad daylight of legitimate enterprise. Chicanery in the blood seems to require working out. Urban Vanes can be found in every calling. They begin as scamps; years later, to one's astonishment, they become ornaments to their professions. Wild oats vary in quality depending on the soil from which they are preserved. We sow them in our various ways.

At first, I stormed. Vane sat with an amused smile, listening.

"Your language, my dear Kelver," he replied, my vocabulary already exhausted, "might wound me were I able to accept you as an authority on this vexed question of morals. With the rest of the world, you preach one thing and practice another. I've noticed it often. It's perhaps sad, but the preaching ceases to interest me. You profess to be indignant that I used another man's ideas. Yet it happens daily. You yourself were quite ready to take credit not due to you. For months, we traveled with this play: *'Drama, in Five Acts, by Mr. Horace Moncrieff.'* Not more than two hundred lines are yours—excellent lines, I admit—but they do not constitute the play."

This aspect had not occurred to me. "But you asked me to put my name to it," I stammered. "You said you didn't want your own to appear—for private reasons. You made a point of it."

He waved away the smoke from his cigar. "The man you pose as would never have put his name to work that wasn't his. You never hesitated; on the contrary, you jumped at the chance of such an easy opening to your career as a playwright. My need, as you imagined it, was your opportunity."

"But you said it was from the French," I argued. "You merely translated it; I adapted it. I don't defend the custom, but it is the custom: the man who adapts a play calls himself the author. They all do it."

"I know," he answered. "It has always amused me. Our sick friend himself, whom we are both delighted to welcome back to life, has done it more than once and made a very fair profit. Indeed, from internal evidence, I am strongly of opinion that this present play is a case in point. Well, chickens come home to roost: I adapted from him. What is the difference?"

"Simply this," he continued, pouring himself another glass of wine, "that whereas, owing to the anomalous state of copyright laws, stealing from a foreign author is legal and commendable, stealing from a living English author carries a certain prejudice."

"And the consequences, I am afraid, you will find somewhat unpleasant," I suggested.

He laughed—it was not a frivolity to which he was prone. "You mean, my dear Kelver, that *you* will."

"Don't look so dumbfounded," he went on. "You cannot be so stupid as you pretend to be. The original manuscript at the Lord Chamberlain's Office is in your handwriting. You knew our friend as well as I did, and visited him. Why, the whole tour has been under your management. You arranged everything—most excellently; I was quite surprised."

My anger came later. For the moment, the sudden light blinded me to everything but fear.

"But you told me," I cried, "it was only a matter of form, that you wanted to keep your name out because—"

He looked at me with genuine astonishment. My words began to appear humorous even to myself. I found it difficult to believe I had been the fool I now saw myself to be.

"I am sorry," he said. "I am really sorry. I took you for a man of the world. I thought you merely did not wish to know anything."

Still, to my shame, fear dominated my heart. "You aren't going to put it all on me?" I pleaded.

He had risen. He laid his hand upon my shoulder. Instead of flinging it off, I was glad of its kindly pressure. He was the only man to whom I could look for help.

"Don't take it so seriously," he said. "He will merely think the manuscript has been lost. As likely as not, he won't remember whether he wrote it or merely thought of writing it. No one in the company will say anything; it isn't their business. We must set to work. I altered it considerably before you saw it and changed all the character names. We will retain the third act; it is the only thing of real value. The situation isn't original; you have as much right to dish it up as he did. In a fortnight, we will have the whole thing so different that if he saw it himself, he would only imagine we had gotten hold of the idea and forestalled him."

During the next few weeks, there were moments when I listened to the voice of my good angel, seeing clearly that even from the lowest viewpoint, he was giving sound advice. I would go to the man and tell him frankly the whole truth.

But Vane never left my elbow. Suspecting, I suppose, he gave me clearly to understand that if I did so, I must expect no mercy. My story, denounced by him as an outrageous lie, would be regarded as the funk-inspired subterfuge of a young rogue. At best, I would handicap myself with suspicion lasting throughout my career. On the other hand, what harm had we done? Presented in twenty or so small towns where it would soon be forgotten, the play was something like most plays. Our friend would produce his version and reap a rich harvest; ours would disappear. If discussion arose, the advertisement would benefit him. Soon, we would replace it with a new piece. A young man of my genius could surely write something better than this hotch-potch; I simply lacked experience. As for one's own conscience, was not the world's honesty a mere question of convention? Had he been a young man, and had we diddled him out of his play for a ten-pound note, we should have been applauded as sharp men of business. The one commandment of the world was: *Don't get found out.* The whole trouble, left alone, would sink and fade. Later, we would tell it as a good joke—and be laughed with.

So I fell from my own esteem. Vane helping me—and he had brains—I set feverishly to work. I am glad to remember that every line I wrote was born in misery. I tried to persuade Vane to let me make a new play altogether, offering it to him for nothing. He expressed gratitude, but his frequently declared belief in my dramatic talent failed to induce acceptance.

"Later on, my dear Kelver," was his reply. "For the present, this is doing very well. Going on as we are, we shall soon improve it out of all recognition while losing nothing essential. All your ideas are excellent."
