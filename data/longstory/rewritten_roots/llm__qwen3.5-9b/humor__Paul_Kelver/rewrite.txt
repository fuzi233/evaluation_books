At the intersection of a long, straight, brick-lined street deep in London's East End—one of those soul-crushing boulevards defined by two drab walls stretching into a weary vanishing point, looking less like architecture and more like petrified math problems proving that life is a solved equation—stands a house that possesses a gravitational pull I cannot resist. Often, when I am least expecting it, I wake up mid-stride, tearing through noisy, crowded arteries where gas lamps bathe grim, patient faces in sickly yellow light; darting through shadowed alleys where monstrous silhouettes dance against drawn curtains; skittering past smelly culverts teeming with children and doorways spewing chaos; weaving past reeking corners and wastelands, until finally, driven by a memory I didn't choose, I arrive at my destination. Halting beside the broken iron railings, I find my peace.

The house is larger than its neighbors, built when this street was merely a country lane bordering the marshes. It stands out like a sore thumb amidst the surrounding uniform ugliness. Two sides of it are fenced off by what used to be a garden, now just a dusty plot of stones where laundry hangs in eternal suspension—a fact that baffles anyone who thinks about hygiene. Around the door, the crumbling stucco reveals the skeleton of a porch, exposing its naked, dishonest structure.

Sometimes, during the day, I wander this way. Slatternly women gossip behind the area gates, and the silence is shattered by the hoarse, wailing chant of "Coals! Any coals! Three and sixpence a sack!" sung with such chronic despair that no one ever buys. Yet, the street doesn't recognize me. My old friend at the corner, embarrassed by its shabbiness under the unforgiving sun, turns its face away, refusing to acknowledge my passing.

Only when Night, the sole merciful entity in existence regarding ugliness, pulls her veil over the squalor does the house transform. Like a devoted grandmother seeking out a lost grandchild, it opens its arms wide. Inside, the teeming life quiets down; the flickering sign of the "King of Prussia" opposite goes dark. Then, the house begins to talk. It asks questions, reminds me of things I've buried. Into the silent street drift familiar footsteps; through the creaking gate pass the faces I once knew, oblivious to my presence. We converse like old cronies flipping through a tattered photo album, discussing the faded portraits in outdated fashions, speaking in hushed tones of those now dead or scattered, punctuated by sighs, smiles, and endless "Ah me!"s.

Then, a bent, weary man approaches with quick, impatient strides, pausing every fifty yards to lean heavily on his high Malacca cane.

"It is a handsome face, isn't it?" I ask, staring at the shadow-framed visage.

"Aye, handsome enough," the Old House replies. "And handsomer still it must have been before you and I met, before petty worry carved those fretful lines into it."

"I never could figure out," the House muses, "who you took after. They were a lovely pair, your parents. Though, Lord! What a couple of children!"

"Children?" I exclaim in shock. My father must have been over thirty-five when the House was built, and my mother's face is so close to mine in the dark that I see the grey hairs mixing with her bonny brown ones.

"Children!" the House repeats, irritated, as if offended by my skepticism—a common flaw in the elderly. "The most helpless pair of infants I ever laid eyes on. Who, pray tell, would conceive the idea of fixing their fortunes by becoming a solicitor at thirty-eight? Or, having decided on that, pick the outskirts of Poplar as a prime location for a signboard?"

"It was considered a rising neighborhood," I counter, feeling a twinge of defensiveness. No son likes hearing his family's wisdom critiqued, even if he secretly agrees.

"All sorts and conditions of men connected to the sea were supposed to move here, near the new docks," the House scoffs. "And if they did, they would have argued and bickered endlessly, providing excellent business for a slick solicitor conveniently located nearby."

"Nonsense," the House snaps. "The mere smell of the place would drive a sane man away. And"—the grim brick facade twists into a goblin-like grin—"he, of all people, calling himself 'the clever solicitor,' advising shady clients on how to escape trouble the fastest! Can you imagine? He starved himself for two years, living on five shillings a week—that was before you arrived in London, when he was alone. Even your mother didn't know until years later, ensuring no one went bankrupt trusting his reputation. Do you really think the crew of chandlers, brokers, dock hustlers, and freight wreckers would have found him useful, even if they settled here?"

I have no answer. The House doesn't wait, continuing its monologue.

"And your mother! Would anyone but a fool take that soft-spoken temptress into her bed without seeing right through her act? Would anyone but a naive child, unfit to walk alone in the world, have thought to endure such madness? Children! Poor babies, both of them."

"Tell me," I insist—for at moments like these, my common sense fails to convince me the House is merely clay. Its walls are full of voices, its floors thick with footsteps; surely it has learned to live, like a violin that eventually finds its own song. "Tell me, I was just a child to whom life spoke a strange language. Was there any truth to the story?"

"Truth!" the House snaps. "Just enough truth to plant a lie upon. And heaven knows, little soil is needed for that weed. I saw what I saw, and I know what I know. Your mother had a good man, and your father a true wife, but it was the old tale: a man's path is not a woman's path, and vice versa, so doubt lives forever between them."

"But they ended up together," I remind it.

"Aye, in the end," the House agrees. "That is when you begin to understand, you humans, when you reach the finish line."

Suddenly, the grave face of a poorly washed angel peeks shyly through the railings, then darts back into the shadows as I turn my head.

"What happened to her?" I ask.

"She? Oh, she's fine," the House replies. "She lives close by. You must have walked past her shop. If you'd looked, you'd have seen her. She weighs fourteen stone, give or take, and has nine children. She'd be delighted to meet you."

"Thanks," I say, offering a laugh that isn't quite a laugh. "I don't think I'll stop by."

Yet, I still hear the *pit-pat* of tiny feet fading down the long street. Faces crowd around me. A large, red, booming face smiles kindly, stirring an old, odd mix of instinctive affection checked by moral hesitation. I turn away to see a massive, clean-shaven countenance featuring the ugliest mouth and the most beautiful eyes I've ever seen in a man.

"Was he as terrible as they claimed?" I ask my ancient companion.

"Shouldn't wonder," the House answers. "I never knew a worse... nor a better."

The wind sweeps them away, revealing a diminutive old woman hobbling along with a stick. Three cork-screw curls bounce on either side of her head with every step. As she draws near, making terrifying grimaces, I hear her whisper, as if sharing a thrilling secret, "I'd like to skin 'em. All of 'em. Alive!"

It sounds diabolical, yet I only laugh. The little lady performs a final, dreamlike facial contortion before limping out of sight. Then, as if selecting a contrast, a fair, laughing face appears. I saw it in life just hours ago—or rather, the pathetic smear of paint Evil has slapped over it, hiding the sweetness beneath. Gazing at it, wishing it belonged to someone immortal, unchanging, another face drifts from the shadows: the wicked mouth paired with tender eyes. Once again, I stand paralyzed between the two I loved so dearly—the one who taught me my first steps in manhood, the one who showed me the beauty and mystery of woman.

Again, the cry rises from my heart: "Whose fault was it—yours or hers?"

Again, I hear his mocking laughter answering, "Whose fault? God made us."

Thinking of her and the love I bore her, pure as a young pilgrim's devotion to a saint, hatred wells up in my blood toward him. But when I look into his eyes and see the pain residing there, my pity swallows my misery, and I can only echo his words: "God made us."

Merry faces and sorrowful, fair faces and foul ones ride upon the wind, circling a single center: a little lad with golden curls more suited to a girl than a boy, possessing shy, awkward manners, a silent tongue, and a serious, old-fashioned expression. Turning from him to my brick friend, I ask, "Would he know me, could he see me, do you think?"

"How should he?" the House replies. "You're so different from what he'd expect. Could you recognize your own ghost, I wonder?"

Fate had a peculiar sense of humor regarding my birth certificate. Ideally, I should have been born in June—the universally acknowledged champion of luck among months. Thoughtful parents everywhere select June for their progeny precisely because of this celestial endorsement. Instead, I arrived in May, the statistical loser of the calendar, a fact I have personally verified through a lifetime of minor calamities. As for *why* the universe chose the unluckiest month for the luckiest possible outcome, I leave that theological knot to experts in astrology and bad timing.

My earliest memory belongs to a bony woman named Mrs. Fursey, whose nursing duties seemed to include the art of pessimistic prognostication. Upon my arrival—two hours after the news broke that my father's mines had flooded—she immediately diagnosed my misfortune as a symptom of "natural impatience." She predicted this trait would lead to greater trouble, a prophecy that history has since stamped with the seal of accuracy. It was also from her that I learned the mechanics of my entry into the world: I had arrived just as my mother attempted to ring the bell, only to collide with a cheval-glass and shatter it. To a superstitious Welsh soul, breaking glass is not merely an accident; it is a declaration of doom. Thus, my first seven years were officially branded as destined for disaster.

"And I must confess," sighed Mrs. Fursey, tapping her knobby finger against her chin, "there appears to be some truth in the old adage after all."

"Then ain't I a lucky little boy?" I countered. Up until this point, Mrs. Fursey had been my chief propagandist for good fortune. I was allowed to sleep at six while other children were kept awake until nine—a privilege I envied in my quieter moments, though I secretly resented them in my riotous ones. Furthermore, whenever I caught a chill, I was granted the exclusive right to consume "linseed gruel for lunch and brimstone-and-treacle for supper." The latter concoction was a culinary masterpiece designed to deceive the innocent palate; the treacle was merely a cruel garnish hiding the dominant flavor of sulfur. Other children, unluckily, were left alone to suffer their illnesses. Finally, there was the trump card: I possessed Mrs. Fursey as my nurse.

The good lady realized her error instantly and scrambled to correct the record.

"Oh, you!" she exclaimed, smoothing my hair. "You are lucky enough! I was thinking of your poor mother."

"Isn't Mamma lucky?"

"Well, she hasn't been too lucky since you came along."

"Wasn't it lucky for her to have me?"

"I can't say it was, considering the timing."

"Didn't she want me?"

Mrs. Fursey was one of those well-intentioned souls who believed the only logical stance for a child was perpetual self-abasement.

"Well, I daresay she could have managed without you," she admitted gently.

I can still see the scene vividly. I sat on a low chair before the nursery fire, knees drawn up, while Mrs. Fursey's needle clicked with the rhythmic monotony of a ticking clock. Suddenly, the concept of life struck my young soul like a thunderclap. Without moving, I asked the question that would haunt me forever:

"Then why did she take me in?"

The clicking needle stopped dead.

"Took you in! What on earth is the child babbling about? Who took you in?"

"Why, Mamma. If she didn't want me, why did she bring me home?"

Even as I posed this logically irrefutable query with a heart full of dignified resentment, I felt a surge of relief. I imagined the alternative: being rejected at the bedroom window. I pictured the stork, perplexed and annoyed, mirroring the expression Tom Pinfold wore when Anna sniffed the fish dangling from his tail. Would the stork have retreated, scratching his long, compass-like leg and muttering to himself? And how exactly had the stork transported me? I had watched blackbirds carry worms; the worms always looked terrified. Had I wriggled and squirmed inside the bird? If so, where would he have dropped me? Probably at Mrs. Fursey's cottage, the nearest neighbor. But surely she wouldn't accept me. Next door lived Mr. Chumdley, the cobbler, a lame man who spent his days hammering boots in a damp cave with filthy hands, looking like a poor-spirited ogre. I certainly wouldn't want to be his son. Perhaps no one would want me. I grew somber, envisioning myself as the village's ultimate reject. Left stranded on the stork's hands, what would become of me?

"Nurse," I ventured, "where did I actually come from?"

"The stork brought you," she replied automatically.

"Yes, I know. But where did the stork get me from?"

Mrs. Fursey paused, weighing the risk of inflaming my ego. Deciding the danger was worth the potential enlightenment, she finally answered:

"Oh, from Heaven."

"But I thought Heaven was a place you go *to*, not a place you come *from*," I corrected. (I distinctly recall using the word "comed" here; my irregular verbs were a source of great anxiety for my mother.)

Mrs. Fursey ignored my grammar with dignified silence. Her mother had instructed her not to worry about correcting my speech, fearing that conflicting opinions would confuse a child.

"You came from Heaven," she repeated firmly. "And you will go to Heaven—if you behave yourself."

"Do all little boys and girls come from Heaven?"

"So they say." Her tone suggested this was merely a popular rumor she wasn't personally vouching for.

"And did *you* come from Heaven, Mrs. Fursey?"

Her response was emphatic.

"Of course I did. Where else do you think I came from?"

Instantly, Heaven lost its lofty status in my eyes. Previously, I had wondered why everyone I knew was heading there. Now, knowing that Mrs. Fursey originated from the same celestial factory, much of its mystique evaporated.

But the revelation brought another sorrow. I stopped trying to comfort myself with the idea that my fate was universal. Childish egoism dictated that I focus solely on my personal tragedy.

"Didn't they want me in Heaven, then?" I asked. "Weren't they fond of me up there?"

The misery in my voice pierced Mrs. Fursey's defenses. She softened her tone.

"Oh, they liked you well enough, I suppose. I like you, but I admit I prefer getting rid of you sometimes."

There was no ambiguity in that last statement. Even then, I suspected my six o'clock bedtime was often pushed back to half-past five.

The answer offered no solace. It became clear: I was unwanted in Heaven and unwanted on Earth. God was eager to discard me; my mother could have done without me. Nobody wanted me. So, why was I here?

Then, like the sudden slam of a door in a dark room, a new sensation bloomed in my childish mind. Somewhere, Something must need me, or I wouldn't exist. Something I belonged to, and which belonged to me. Something integral to my identity. This feeling would recur throughout my childhood, though I couldn't articulate it until years later, when a Portuguese Jew explained my thought process to me. All I knew at the time was that in that moment, I understood for the first time that I lived, that I was *me*.

The darkness returned, and I was once again a puzzled boy by the fire.

A new thought—or rather, a recollection of an old one—struck me.

"Nurse, why haven't we got a husband?"

Mrs. Fursey dropped her needle and stared.

"What maggot has gotten into the child's head now? Who doesn't have a husband?"

"Why, Mamma."

"Don't talk nonsense, Master Paul. You know your mother has a husband."

"No, she doesn't."

"And don't contradict. Your mother's husband is your father, living in London."

Mrs. Fursey's reaction was unnecessarily heated.

"Wicked child! Where are your commandments? Your father is in London working tirelessly to earn money to keep you in idleness, and you sit there complaining, 'What's the good of him!' I'd be ashamed to raise such an ungrateful little brat."

I hadn't meant to be ungrateful. I was simply repeating a conversation I had overheard the previous day between my mother and my aunt.

Let's be honest about my folks immediately: they weren't normal. They'd been split up for years, and while they certainly weren't teenagers—by the time I was old enough to notice, they looked like ancient fossils, my dad nearing forty and my mom well past thirty—they acted less like middle-aged humans and more like a boy and a girl who'd forgotten how to stop playing. If anyone had asked me back then what marriage was, I'd have described a husband as a man who couldn't breathe unless his wife was standing right next to him, calling from his office door every twenty minutes, "Maggie, are you busy? I need to discuss a merger!" And a wife? A woman whose eyes sparkled whenever she looked at her man, thrilled wherever he was and panicked wherever he wasn't. Obviously, that's not how it works for everyone, but that's the image I sold.

Living with married couples also comes with specific hazards. First, keep your feet tucked under the table; otherwise, someone will mistake them for yours and stomp on them. Second, never walk into a room silently. In Stageland, we call this "noise without." It's terrifying to burst in, door still in hand, only to hear people suddenly launch into the air, followed by frantic scuttling, to find them frozen in opposite corners, pretending to read or sew. Fortunately, in my house, these precautions were unnecessary.

I didn't actually control my parents' behavior much. By the time I walked in, they'd already sighed, "Oh, it's only Spud," and thrown a sofa cushion at me. But my aunt? They treated her with genuine terror. She never spoke, yet she moved through the house sounding like a chainsaw. My dad once complained that the amount of barking she produced in a single day would have cured a ward of dying tuberculosis patients. To stay invisible, she'd sneak backward into rooms, slamming doors shut before turning around, and sing loudly down dark hallways. No woman could be more polite, yet my parents hated her. The more sympathy she offered, the angrier they got.

One day, my mom came out of the kitchen while my aunt dropped teaspoons at five-second intervals outside the drawing-room door. "Fanny," my mom snapped, "I think you hate seeing Luke and me happy. Stop acting so ridiculous."

"Hate it?" my aunt replied, surprisingly calm. "Why wouldn't I? Why shouldn't a pair of turtle-doves coo when their combined age is barely seventy?"

My dad, however, turned vindictive. I remember him stopping mid-stride by the canal bank. "Your aunt," he said, gesturing vaguely toward the sunset as if she were personally responsible for her own existence, "is the most cantankerous creature alive. I wish to God she had her own money and a cottage in the countryside!" Then, realizing how cruel that sounded, he quickly added, "She *can* be nice, don't tell Mom what I said."

Our maid, Susan, watched this drama with tearful fascination. She was a stout, sleepy woman whose heart was supposedly in the right place, even if her brain wasn't. Whenever she saw my parents together, she'd collapse and sob, reminiscing about her own glorious past marriages. Her stories were a confusing mix of contradictions. On Monday, she cried over Brussels sprouts because she hadn't visited "his" grave at Manor Park. On Tuesday, she talked about choking him by the scruff of his neck.

"But Susan," I'd ask, "he's dead."

"So was I, Master Paul," she'd say. "That was his trick."

"Who says he ain't?" she'd shout, switching moods faster than a lightning strike. "If anyone says he ain't, I'll gouge their eye out!"

Eventually, Susan left. One Saturday night, my mom ran upstairs pale-faced. "Luke, get the doctor! Susan is on the kitchen floor, gasping and can't speak!"

Dad sprinted for Dr. Washburn, returning five minutes later panting. The doctor was a massive, black-bearded giant who seemed to shake the entire house as he descended the stairs. He ignored my mom, bent over the unconscious Susan, and then looked up at us. Suddenly, he erupted into a roar of laughter so loud and wild I'd never heard anything like it.

Next, he grabbed a bucket of water and dumped it over Susan. She jolted awake.

"Feeling better?" he asked, holding the bucket. "Need another dose?"

Before Susan could form a sentence, he shoved us all outside and slammed the door. From above, we heard her furious screaming drowning out his savage guffaws. When she paused for breath, he'd cheer, "Bravo! Keep going, beauty! I love hearing you bark!"

"What a monster," my mom whispered.

"He's fascinating," Dad corrected.

"I don't want to know him," my mom insisted. But it's hard to ignore someone who makes the air feel different.

When the doctor finally paid, he refused payment. "A glass of beer and a chop," he announced, "which I'll cook for myself in an hour. Since you're without a servant, I'll cook for you too. I'm an expert at grilling." He laid a huge hand on my mom's shoulder, forcing her to sit, and declared, "She belongs on the doorstep. Give her a month's wages and a hot fire, and I'll be back to supervise."

That night changed everything. The doctor arrived on time, and his laughter echoed through the house until even my aunt joined in. Feeling sick with a stomach ache that felt like swallowing St. Paul's Cathedral, I crept downstairs seeking comfort. Instead, I found my mom glowing with happiness, shielding her flushed face from the fire. My dad looked taller, more dignified. Aunt Fan was witty. Everyone laughed at my silly questions.

Dr. Washburn, or as the locals called him, Dr. Fighting Hal, possessed a magnetic energy that pulled the best out of everyone. Clever people became geniuses; dullards discovered hidden talents. Talking to him was like listening to a symphony where the walls dissolved, revealing a brave, laughing world where fate was just the Master of Ceremonies.

Sometimes he was a brute, wrinkling his nose like a dog before biting. Other times, his deep blue eyes softened, and he looked like an angel in a loose coat. I've wondered if knowing him was good or bad for me, but it's like asking a tree if the wind shaped it or broke it.

After Susan left, the house fell silent. We pretended to wait for a better servant, but months passed. My dad would angrily ask why "Jane" or "Mary" hadn't shown up, while my mom hid behind a thick veil to buy groceries herself, terrified neighbors would gossip about our lack of staff. We were poor and ashamed of it, hiding our empty larders behind bottles of port and sherry on the sideboard. We wore our best clothes to church despite the cold, trying to maintain dignity.

It wasn't snobbery; it was desperate self-respect. Dr. Washburn would have shared his bread and cheese with royalty, but my parents served weak tea so guests could drink wine. We ate one egg, splitting it between us, arguing over the yolk. Yet, amidst the struggle, there was joy. We cleaned the doorstep at night, pretending to be mysterious workers. We played cricket in our tiny garden with rules invented to avoid breaking windows. We rowed up the River Lea.

Life was light, filled with laughter and hope. Until one Sunday dinner.

"We could save the stew," my mom said, "and make an omelette."

My dad dropped his spoon. "An omelette?"

"Yes! I want to try again."

He marched to the back kitchen and returned with a heavy wood chopper.

"What are you doing with that?" my mom asked.

"Dividing the omelette," he said flatly.

I realized then that no one likes jokes aimed at themselves, least of all women.

Over time, a bond formed between my dad and me. He was the youngest adult I'd ever met. Though his hair was greying and lines etched his face, his eyes and laugh remained those of a boy. We played our own version of cricket, dodging windows and retrieving balls from gangs of urchins. We waded through mud in the marshes to swim.

Mostly, though, we just lived. We cleaned knives, ran errands, and laughed at the absurdity of school lessons. Even my parents took it lightly. There was always morning light and hope. Like a prisoner seeing sunlight through brambles, we forgot our scars.

"One day," my mom dreamt aloud, "I'll organize the servants' schedules properly."

Moments later, the kitchen door creaked open. My mother's hand appeared through the crack, beckoning me with mysterious urgency.

"Walk on your toes," she whispered, demonstrating as she led me upstairs. The stairs, seemingly offended by our deceit, protested loudly with every step, groaning and creaking far worse than usual. I had never worn this ensemble on a weekday morning. All chatter irrelevant to the urgent task of dressing quickly was swept aside by my mother. Once fully attired, down to the brand-new Blucher shoes (the fashion of the day), she took my hand. Together, we crept down the stairs as we had ascended them: silent, stealthy, and alert.

At the street door, my mother opened it.

"Shan't I want my cap?" I whispered anxiously.

My mother merely shook her head and slammed the door shut behind us. Only then did the true purpose of this pantomime click into place. Was it comedy or tragedy? I was becoming increasingly familiar with such dramatic performances. With my mother's hand resting reassuringly on my shoulder, we entered my father's study.

There was a reason why this room served double duty. Perhaps because evenings were spent in the drawing-room—which remained perpetually staged as a showroom for unexpected guests, complete with Cowper's poems face-down on the wobbly table and half-finished crochet work draped elegantly over an armchair—or perhaps simply because my father was prone to switching games instantly from cricket in the garden to ninepins in the hallway, retreating to the study whenever a knock sounded at the front door. Either way, as a child, I never viewed my father's profession seriously. To me, he was merely playing dress-up amidst towering books and bundles of documents labeled impressively but containing mostly blank paper. Japanned tin boxes bore grand titles like "Sutton Hampden, Esq.," which functioned essentially as my mother's sewing box. "Drayton Estates" produced nothing but apples, a fruit my father adored. As for "Mortgages," I didn't discover until adulthood that they had nothing to do with the manuscripts of poetry lying around, some corrected, others finished.

With a twist of his thick lips, he rolled the foul-smelling cigar from the corner of his mouth to the other side. "You have a daughter?" my mother asked. As a mother, a child instantly replaced the role usually filled by respectful servants or competent help; she sought a bond of sympathy with strangers.

"I won't tell you about her. But I'll bring her to visit you occasionally, ma'am, if you don't mind," Mr. Hasluck replied. "She doesn't often meet genteel folk, and it would do her good."

My mother glanced hopefully at my father, but he intercepted her gaze and answered for himself.

"Don't worry, ma'am, she isn't anything like me," he assured her cheerfully. "Nobody believes she's my daughter except me and the old woman. She's a proper little lady. A freak of nature, I call it."

"We shall be delighted," my mother explained.

"You will be once you see her," Mr. Hasluck confirmed, sounding entirely satisfied.

He pressed half a crown into my palm, dismissing my parents' modesty with the casual confidence of a man used to getting his way.

"No squandering it on the heathen," he warned as I turned to leave. "Spend that on a Christian tradesman."

It was the first money I distinctly remembered receiving. Visions of new Sunday gloves, a personal Latin grammar, and other luxuries had previously clouded my thoughts regarding stray coins. But this time, I was left to choose.

The pressure was immense. I tossed sleeplessly in bed, wandered bewildered through the streets, and received no guidance.

The East India Dock Road today is a chaotic artery of London, choked with traffic where the jingle of tram bells and the rattling of omnibuses mix constantly with the relentless drumming of thousands of footsteps on wet pavement. But in the era of my story, it was a silent, hollow corridor. Looking at them side-by-side, one might be tempted to believe that nature had finally reverted to its original blueprint, molding her directly from one of his ribs. We lived poorly again, but there was no longer that pathetic straining to maintain appearances, no paralyzing fear of the neighbors' opinions. The petty anxieties regarding things not worth a single thought, the base desires and fears that usually disfigure us, simply fell away. Even the self-imposed misery of the shy young man, cursed by nature to live in isolation, passes. Winter evenings came, and I retreated to work; it is the only season where one can drown personal sorrows in the flicker of a fire. My "apartment" felt surprisingly inviting with its handful of embers and two lit candles. I purchased paper, pens, and ink. What else does a writer need? He is merely a vessel waiting to see if the spirits within will speak through him or silence him forever.

Before me lay London—a city of a million characters, half grave and half gay, teeming with ten thousand romances, mysteries, pathos, and humor. It stretched out, begging only for intelligent observation and a reasonably truthful report. Yet, the absurd notion that I could craft a story from the reality I actually knew never occurred to me. Instead, my tales were populated by cottage maidens and rustic yeomen. My scenes unfolded in windmills, amidst mountains, or within moated granges. I suspect this phase of foolishness is common to nearly all youthful fictionists.

A trail of gentle melancholy draped over these creations. Sentiment was the rage then, and as a novice, I scrupulously followed fashion. Generally speaking, to be a heroine in my books was a death sentence. No matter how naturally her hair curled—curly locks supposedly signaling vitality—or how vigorously she danced, laughed, or leaped in Chapter One, the poor girl was destined for an untimely grave. Occasionally, however, it was the gentleman who fell victim to my lethal literary atmosphere. Of no great consequence, since he would invariably conclude his final soliloquy that "it was better so." Snowdrops and primroses offered no consolation; his grave, marked by a rough cross, usually sat in an unfrequented patch of the African veldt or amid burning sands. Descriptions of such final scenery required a trip to the British Museum reading-room.

Dismal little fledglings! Again and again I drove them from the nest, only to watch them flutter back, soiled, crumpled, and physically damaged. Yet, one person had admired them, wept over them—myself.

I tried every method. Sometimes I dispatched them with a curt, take-it-or-leave-it note. Other times, I attached pathetic pleas for consideration. Occasionally, I assigned a value of five guineas, requesting a crossed cheque; at other times, I sought to tickle editorial greed by offering my first contribution gratis—"my sample packet," as it were, hoping one trial was sufficient. Now I wrote sarcastically, enclosing a brutally penned rejection note with the stamped return envelope. Or I wrote frankly, explaining my status as a beginner and begging to be told my faults, if any existed.

None found a resting place. They would vanish for a month, a week, or a couple of days, then return. I never lost a single one. I wished I had; it would have varied the monotony.

I despised the poor little slave-girl who burst joyfully into the room, holding them out between her apron-hidden thumb and forefinger. Her chronic sniff I interpreted as contempt. Understanding that the would-be happy warrior must shine in camp as well as on the battlefield, I sought to prepare myself for the social side of life. Smoking and drinking were the twin sins I found most difficult to acquire. I am not claiming mental excellence here, but confessing a bodily infirmity. The spirit was always willing, but my flesh was weak. Fired by emulation, I had occasionally attempted a cigarette at school. The results were distinctly unsatisfactory. After two or three tries, I abandoned the endeavor, excusing my faint-heartedness with a sanctimonious air by declaring smoking bad for growing boys. I attempted to delude myself by adopting a fine pose of disapproval in the presence of contemporaries with stronger stomachs, secretly knowing myself a young hypocrite disguising physical cowardice in the robes of moral courage—a self-deception to which human nature is prone.

Similarly, I had occasionally tasted the red wine that stood year-in, year-out on our sideboard. But now, a gentleman at large, my education could no longer be delayed. Training—and severe training—is an absolute necessity for the artist. Fashion had shifted slightly, but a quarter-century prior, a genius who did not smoke and drink (and drink more than was good for him) would have been dismissed as an impostor without further evidence. Toys! What wise child despises them? Are art, fiction, and musical glasses not merely preparing us for the time, however distant, when we shall finally grow up?

My anger came later. For the moment, the sudden light blinded me to everything but fear.

"But you told me," I cried, "it was only a matter of form, that you wanted to keep your name out because—"

He looked at me with genuine astonishment. My words began to appear humorous even to myself. I found it difficult to believe I had been the fool I now saw myself to be.

"I am sorry," he said. "I am really sorry. I took you for a man of the world. I thought you merely did not wish to know anything."

Still, to my shame, fear dominated my heart. "You aren't going to put it all on me?" I pleaded.

He had risen. He laid his hand upon my shoulder. Instead of flinging it off, I was glad of its kindly pressure. He was the only man to whom I could look for help.

"Don't take it so seriously," he said. "He will merely think the manuscript has been lost. As likely as not, he won't remember whether he wrote it or merely thought of writing it. No one in the company will say anything; it isn't their business. We must set to work. I altered it considerably before you saw it and changed all the character names. We will retain the third act; it is the only thing of real value. The situation isn't original; you have as much right to dish it up as he did. In a fortnight, we will have the whole thing so different that if he saw it himself, he would only imagine we had gotten hold of the idea and forestalled him."

During the next few weeks, there were moments when I listened to the voice of my good angel, seeing clearly that even from the lowest viewpoint, he was giving sound advice. I would go to the man and tell him frankly the whole truth.

But Vane never left my elbow. Suspecting, I suppose, he gave me clearly to understand that if I did so, I must expect no mercy. My story, denounced by him as an outrageous lie, would be regarded as the funk-inspired subterfuge of a young rogue. At best, I would handicap myself with suspicion lasting throughout my career. On the other hand, what harm had we done? Presented in twenty or so small towns where it would soon be forgotten, the play was something like most plays. Our friend would produce his version and reap a rich harvest; ours would disappear. If discussion arose, the advertisement would benefit him. Soon, we would replace it with a new piece. A young man of my genius could surely write something better than this hotch-potch; I simply lacked experience. As for one's own conscience, was not the world's honesty a mere question of convention? Had he been a young man, and had we diddled him out of his play for a ten-pound note, we should have been applauded as sharp men of business. The one commandment of the world was: *Don't get found out.* The whole trouble, left alone, would sink and fade. Later, we would tell it as a good joke—and be laughed with.

So I fell from my own esteem. Vane helping me—and he had brains—I set feverishly to work. I am glad to remember that every line I wrote was born in misery. I tried to persuade Vane to let me make a new play altogether, offering it to him for nothing. He expressed gratitude, but his frequently declared belief in my dramatic talent failed to induce acceptance.

"Later on, my dear Kelver," was his reply. "For the present, this is doing very well. Going on as we are, we shall soon improve it out of all recognition while losing nothing essential. All your ideas are excellent."

At first, I sent every manuscript to Norah. She'd copy it out, charge me for the copying, deduct the fee, and send me the rest. My early earnings were tiny, but Norah was a brilliant agent, and soon the money started flowing. Dan, my publisher, got quite cross with her. He wrote letters full of pained surprise at her "greed." He claimed the stuff was fine, just not *remarkable*. Naturally, since she was his friend, he wanted to help. But business is business. In fairness to his shareholders, he couldn't and wouldn't pay above market rate.

Miss Deleglise, answering sharply in the third person, agreed entirely with Mr. Brian. Mr. Brian realized he'd found his match. He grumbled, yes, but he paid. Whether he suspected who "Jack Homer" actually was, he never admitted it. Suddenly, "Jack Homer" became a hot commodity. Sitting at my wobbly table, laughing as I wrote, the grim walls of my room seemed to dissolve, opening up views of a wider world.

Still, it was slow going. Humor isn't an industrious maid. She refuses to be rushed, works only when she feels like it—which rarely happens—and throws a tantrum if you bother her. Then she vanishes, heaven knows where, only to return when she decides to. An unreliable young soul. Because of this, I had to supplement my writing with journalism. I lacked Dan's magnificent confidence. Fate never favors the nervous. If I'd burst into an editor's office, he'd either flee or become a short-tempered man who'd kick me out immediately. Besides, I was dressed in one coat; even Robert Macaire would have been too shy to barge in.

So, I joined the ranks of the penny-a-liners—literally, three-halfpence-a-liners. Along with half a dozen other ragged outsiders—young guys trying to climb the ladder and older men who had slid down—I attended inquests and police court sessions. We chased fire engines, cheered at street accidents, and longed for murders. We lived like vultures, scavenging off the misfortunes of others. We earned occasional half-crowns by selling scandals and full crowns by keeping secrets.

"I think, gentlemen," our spokesman would whisper hoarsely when we returned to the table, "I believe the deceased's brother-in-law wants this kept out of the papers if possible."

We would follow that specific case with intense focus, taking remarkably complete notes. Then the spokesman would rise, drift away casually, and return five minutes later, wiping his mouth.

"Not a very interesting case, gentlemen, I don't think. How about five shillings apiece?" Sometimes, feeling the dignity of our profession, we'd ask for ten.

Here, my sense of humor gave me an unfair edge over my rivals. Twelve respectable men had been summoned to determine how a Lascar sailor died. It was obvious: a plumber working on the roof of a small two-story house slipped and fell on him. The plumber escaped with bruises; the sailor was dead. Some blame lay with the plumber. His mate, a good witness, told us the whole story.

"I was fixing a gas pipe on the first floor," the man said. "The prisoner was on the roof."

"We won't call him 'the prisoner,'" the coroner interrupted gently. "Refer to him as the 'last witness.'"

"The last witness," the man corrected himself. "He shouted down the chimney to ask if I was ready."

"'Ready and waiting,' I said."

"'Right,' he said. 'I'm coming in through the window.'"

"'Wait a minute,' I said. 'I'll go down and move the ladder for you.'"

"'It's all right,' he said. 'I can reach it.'"

"'No, you can't,' I said. 'It's on the other side of the chimney.'"

"'I think I'm okay,' he said. 'But come down. The guy I fell on looks pretty sick.'"

The others titled their report "Sad Accident"—truthful but dull. I changed mine to "Plumber in a Hurry—Fatal Result." I mentioned the sailor as little as possible, focusing instead on warning plumbers against the coroner's just criticism of rushing jobs. I pointed out the dangers of hurrying without losing a moment's concentration.

This built a useful reputation. The sub-editor of an evening paper even came out in his shirt sleeves to shake my hand.

"That's exactly what we need," he told me. "A light touch, a bit of humor."

I snatched fun from fires (hoping the insurance premiums weren't overdue), found oddities in street fights, and extracted joy from the most painful disasters. I prospered.

Although I lived close to the street, I strictly avoided the old house in Poplar. At this stage, I was struggling with a split personality. Two distinct individuals were fighting inside me. My goal was to nurture the new Paul—the sensible, practical, ambitious Paul whose future looked bright—and banish the old, childish Paul with those sad, far-seeing eyes. Sometimes, out of the cracked mirror, his wistful face would plead with me. But I shook my head sternly. I knew his tricks. If I let him lead the way, he'd guide me through the streets he knew so well, past the broken railings (where he'd leave my body standing), along the weedy path, through the battered door, up the creaking stairs to the dismal little room where we once dreamed foolish dreams together.

"Come," he would whisper. "It's so close. Let's quietly push aside the dresser, softly lift the broken window sash, prop it open with the Latin dictionary, lean our elbows on the sill, and listen to the tired voices of the city calling us from the dark."

But I was too cautious to be caught. "Later," I'd reply. "When I've made my mark, when I'm strong enough to resist your coaxing. Then I'll go with you, if you're still around, my sentimental little friend. We'll dream those old impractical dreams again—and laugh at them."

So he would fade away, replaced by a nodding, business-like, wide-awake young man.

However, I did give in to one sentimental temptation. By now, my position was secure; there was no need to hide anymore. I decided to move westward. I hadn't planned to fly so high, but walking through Guildford Street one day, a creeper-covered corner house that my father had once considered renting flashed into my mind. A card hung in the fanlight. I knocked and asked. A bed-sitting room on the third floor was available. The moment the chatty landlady opened the door, I recognized it instantly.

"This shall be your room, Paul," my father's voice seemed to say. So clearly did it sound behind me that I turned, momentarily forgetting it was just a memory. "You'll be quiet here, and we can block out the bed and washstand with a screen."

So my father had his way. It was a lovely, sunny little room overlooking the hospital gardens. Following his advice, I blocked out the furniture with a screen. Sometimes, late at night, it amused me to imagine my father turning the door handle.

"How are you getting on—all right?"

"Famously."

"But shouldn't one aim high?" I had asked.

My father had fidgeted in his chair. "It's hard to say. It's all so... so incomprehensible. You aim high and miss everything—or so it seems. Maybe it's better to aim low and hit the target. One evening, Hodgson approached me. "I want you to write me a comic opera," he announced, holding an open letter. "The public seems tired of these endless translations from the French. I want something English, something new and original."

"The English is easy enough," I replied cautiously. "But I wouldn't clamor for anything 'new and original' if I were you."

"Why not?" he asked, looking up from his letter.

"You might get it," I answered. "And then you'd be disappointed."

He laughed. "Well, you know what I mean—something we can list on the program as 'new and original.' What do you say? It will be a big break for you, and I'm willing to risk it. People are beginning to talk about you."

I had written a few farces and short comedies that had been successful, but this proposal carried a heavy, serious responsibility. A young man might be excused for hesitating; it could make him, or it could ruin him. Above all other arts, a comic opera demands experience.

"I explained my fears," I noted later. "I didn't mention that in my desk lay a four-act drama throbbing with humanity and life—a work I had hoped, though that hope faded daily, would take the theatrical public by storm and establish me as a serious playwright."

"It's very simple," Hodgson urged. "Just provide Atherton with plenty of comic business—you can certainly manage that. Give Gleeson something pretty in waltz time, and give Duncan a part where she changes her frock every quarter-hour, and the job is done."

"I'll tell you what," Hodgson continued. "I'll take the whole cast down to Richmond on Sunday. We'll hire a coach and leave the theater at half-past ten. It'll be a chance for you to study them. You can talk to them and figure out exactly what they can do. Atherton has ideas in his head; he'll explain them to you. Next week, we'll draft a contract and get to work."

It was too good an opportunity to ignore, even though I knew that if it succeeded, I would be pinned down forever to the role of the jester. But, as the saying goes, it pays well to write comic operas.

"People sometimes ask me," said the low comedian, brushing dust from his knees, "why I do this sort of thing off-stage. It amuses me."

"I was coming up to London from Birmingham recently," he continued. "At Willesden, when the ticket collector opened the door, I sprang out and ran down the platform. "It must be a troublesome way to amuse yourself," I suggested.

"Exactly what my wife says," he replied. "She can never understand the desire that comes over us all, I suppose, at times, to play the fool. As a rule, when she is with me, I don't do it."

"She's not here today?" I asked, glancing around.
