The morning light filtered through the windowpanes, illuminating a stack of correspondence where Hans Meyrick's latest missive lay waiting. Written in the family's distinctive, elegant script across four quarto pages, the letter began with a flourish of intellectual posturing that immediately signaled the sender's state of mind.

"My dear Deronda," it commenced, "in return for your sketch of Italian movements... 'time will show.' As to the present causes of past effects, it is now seen that the late swindling telegrams account for the last year's cattle plague..."

Deronda traced the lines with a weary eye. Hans was weaving a tapestry of cynical analogies, predicting a murrain among the commercial class and foreseeing the ready sale of rejected paintings as proof of his prophetic vision. He spoke of finding advantage in Deronda's absence, describing himself as "shining like Hesperus when Hyperion has departed," sitting with their Hebrew prophet, Mordecai, and claiming credit for the sage's wisdom. To Hans, the logic was circular and self-congratulatory: if whatever is best is Jewish, and B happens to be best, then B must be Jewish. He treated Mordecai not as a complex soul, but as a philosophical prop, a "better model than Rembrandt had for his Rabbi," capable of absorbing Hans's correct opinions while conveniently ignoring the vast remainder of the universe.

The letter drifted further into the realm of Hans's peculiar romantic delusions. He confessed that he could not help making declarations, lamenting that Mirah's distractions prevented him from throwing himself at her feet. He described his own hope as an "immortal, delicious maiden" forever courted, contrasting it sharply with Certainty, whom he visualized as an "ugly Janus-faced deity." With characteristic theatricality, Hans concluded that truth would prevail and that the Jewess he preferred would eventually prefer him, dismissing any counter-evidence as mere "blockhead" generalities that failed to discern the particular case.

Yet, amidst this flow of vanity, there was a thread of genuine observation regarding the household dynamics. Hans noted the arrival of Jacob Cohen, a young Israelite who treated Mordecai with a familiar ease that bordered on condescension, viewing the older man as a "second-hand Christian commodity." But when Mirah entered the room, the dynamic shifted instantly; Jacob transformed into a child, his demeanor softening until he resembled a dog trained by women, bringing sugar-plums to share with Mordecai. Hans marveled at this duality, wondering why the Jewish race hadn't already bought up the weaker generations of Europe like slave property, noting that there was one Jewess he wouldn't mind being enslaved to.

As the days passed, the tone of the letter grew lighter, filled with news of Rex Gascoigne and his sister, cousins to the Vandyke Duchess, and invitations to various social gatherings. Hans proposed painting Sir Hugo's daughters in the Gainsborough style, boasting of his ability to capture likeness while admitting his limitations in higher artistic realms. He ended with a cryptic reference to the Klesmers' departure and a final, desperate plea for Deronda to visit Topping Abbey, framing his own unrequited feelings as a noble, persistent dance among orchard blossoms.

Deronda sat alone with the letter, the paper silent in his hands. Some months prior, this correspondence would have irritated him, its romanticizing of Mirah carrying an unpleasant edge devoid of compassion for his friend's inevitable heartbreak. But the landscape of his own life had shifted irrevocably since March. The revelation of his birth had opened a new horizon, casting fresh light on the past and altering his perception of the present. Where he once might have seen Hans's hopes as mischievous unreasonableness, he now viewed them as the frantic, extravagant flight of a restless spirit.

"It is too restless, too readily excitable by novelty," Deronda murmured to the empty room, his voice low. "Too ready to turn itself into imaginative material, and wear its grief as a fantastic costume."

He looked down at the page where Hans claimed to understand Mirah's silence. Deronda realized that Hans was playing at love, treating the entire affair as a comedy, completely blind to the objections that stood between him and the woman he adored. Hans imagined the world to fit his own inclinations, projecting his own capacity for suffering onto others while failing to recognize that Deronda shared the same burden.

"Poor old Hans!" Deronda sighed, folding the letter carefully. "If we were under a fiery hail together, he would howl like a Greek, and if I did not howl too, it would never occur to him that I was as badly off as he."

He recognized the tenderness in Hans's intentions, the active imagination that sought to fill the voids of other people's lives with his own narrative. Yet, beneath the surface of this affectionate folly, Deronda sensed a deeper tragedy. Hans's inability to see the walls around him mirrored the very blindness that plagued the world at large. As Deronda prepared to set aside the letter, he felt a surge of pity—not for the foolishness of the hope, but for the isolation of the man who wore his despair as a costume, unaware that the storm he feared was already breaking within him.
