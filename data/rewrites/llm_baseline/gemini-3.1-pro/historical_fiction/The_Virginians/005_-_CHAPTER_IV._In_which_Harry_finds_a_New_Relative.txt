The heavy oak panels of the wainscoted parlour absorbed the superficial chatter of the Castlewood family, creating a sanctuary of quiet acoustic resonance. In this room, where meals had been taken in ordinary for at least two centuries, the air still seemed to hold the faint, ghostly olfactory memory of roasted pheasants, clove-studded oranges, and the rich, yeasty breath of proving bread. Here, Madam Bernstein sat with her knitting needles, declining her hostess's relentless offers of cards or a dish of ordinary tea. Instead, she found profound sustenance in a steeped infusion of dried bergamot and crushed elderberry, and infinite amusement in the native, unvarnished cadence of young Harry Warrington’s voice. 

Whenever the other Castlewood cousins intruded, their sharp, commercialized tones disrupting the room’s delicate sonic ecology, the old lady redoubled her caresses to the boy. “Hush, my dears!” she would command, gesturing to the door. “I cannot hear our cousin speak.” And the interlopers would retreat, striving to look pleased, leaving the acoustic space solely to Harry and the rhythmic click of her needles. 

Harry’s narrative wove a circular path, folding the deep, mythic past of his Virginia home into the domestic routines of the present. He spoke of his mother, Madam Esmond Warrington, whom the locals had dubbed the little Princess Pocahontas. She was an agrarian elder of the most meticulous order, a woman who found the divine not merely in her scrupulous morning devotions, but in the precise curation of her estate’s harvests. Her dominions at Castlewood in Westmoreland County were a sensory tapestry of her own making: the curing of broad-leaf tobacco over hickory fires, the autumnal slaughter and the meticulous salting of winter hams, the creation of rich, fragrant broths fed to both her dutiful sons and the poor whites who took her medicinal doses with implicit gratitude. 

Yet, for all her sensory appreciation of the land, Madam Esmond demanded absolute acoustic and linguistic dominance. The subtle power dynamics of her household were governed entirely by her lexicon. If people submitted to her vocabulary of authority, she was their greatest benefactor. If they resisted, she fought them until their subjective experiences were entirely erased by her own. Her father, the late Colonel Esmond, used to watch her freaks of despotism with quiet amusement, his silent gaze the only force capable of subduing her. But with the Colonel gone, there was no one left to temper her resonant, resolute voice, which led the public acknowledgment of sins every Sunday with absolute, unyielding clarity. 

She purged the estate of any vernacular that did not suit her. When Mr. Dempster, the lad’s tutor and her late father’s secretary, dared to introduce books she felt insinuated a dangerous, untranslatable philosophy, she drove him out. To her, bookworms threatened the agrarian rhythm of her world. The poor Dominie was banished to a clearing to teach and practice medicine among the sparse inhabitants, though Master George, loyal to his old tutor’s teachings, vowed never to forsake him. Harry, who favored the visceral, muddy thrill of fishing and sporting over the dry rustle of parchment, had never formed such an intimacy with the man.

Madam Esmond’s insistence on imposing her own subjective truth eventually clashed with the rigid, sterile language of English law. Upon her husband's demise and the death of an aunt, an inheritance of six thousand pounds fell to the heir, George. Madam Esmond, as a trustee, could not—or would not—translate the legal distinction between proprietor and trustee into her own emotional dialect. To her, the sustenance of her family was paramount. She envisioned the money purchasing Mr. Boulter’s neighboring estate, a tract of rich, loamy soil and skilled hands that would yield a thousand pounds a year, providing an eternal, bountiful harvest for her younger, portionless son, Harry. 

When the London lawyer, Mr. Draper, refused to release the funds, clinging to the cold, utilitarian logic of commerce, Madam Esmond was furious. Her young neighbor, Mr. Washington of Mount Vernon, alongside George himself, attempted to explain the legal bounds of the trust. But to the mother, their logic was an abrasive noise. Feelings were her reasons. To think her firstborn would side with an insolent London pettifogger over the future sustenance of his own brother! The unyielding walls of her maternal logic were blotted with tears, her court of justice forever ruled by passion.

In retaliation, the widow imposed a sudden, severe famine upon the estate’s sensory and domestic life, systematically dismantling the cultural memory her father had begun to build. The fine architectural improvements at Castlewood were halted. The Dutch bricks imported from New York, the carved cornice-work, the sashes and fragrant cedar upholstery from home—all lay abandoned. She commanded her agent to cease the shipment of fine wines, silencing the rich clink of Madeira glasses at the evening table. Meals were reduced to utilitarian fuel, stripped of their former gustatory glory, all to save a portion for her beloved Harry.

The profound psychological toll of this deprivation peaked during the Sunday carriage rides to church. Madam Esmond deeply regretted the expense of the fine English carriage, and the iron-rimmed wheels grinding against the Virginia dirt created a harsh, industrial clatter that mirrored the discord within. 

“Harry, Harry!” she groaned over the noise, looking at her sons. “I wish I had put by the money for thee, my poor portionless child—three hundred and eighty guineas of ready money to Messieurs Hatchett!”

“You will give me plenty while you live, and George will give me plenty when you die,” Harry said, trying to inject a bright, harmonious chord into the heavy air. 

“Not unless he changes in spirit, my dear,” the lady replied, her voice a grim strike against her elder boy. “Not unless Heaven softens his heart and teaches him charity, for which I pray day and night; as Mountain knows; do you not, Mountain?”

Mrs. Mountain, the manager and companion who shared their agrarian labors, sat in the fourth seat. She refused to adopt Madam Esmond’s lexicon of martyrdom. “Humph! I know you are always disturbing yourself and crying out about this legacy, and I don't see that there is any need.”

“Oh no! no need!” cried the widow, the aggressive rustle of her silks filling the confined space. “Of course I have no need to be disturbed, because my eldest born is a disobedient son and an unkind brother—because he has an estate, and my poor Harry, bless him, but a mess of pottage.”

At the mention of this utilitarian, flavorless sustenance, George looked despairingly at his mother. The untranslatability of his deep, subjective love for his brother against the harshness of her accusations overwhelmed him. His eyes welled with tears. “I wish you would bless me, too, O my mother!” he cried, bursting into a passionate fit of weeping.

Harry instantly threw his arms around his brother’s neck, his own sensory truth—the warmth of his brother’s shoulder, the smell of his woolen coat—overriding the mother’s bitter words. He kissed George a score of times. “Never mind, George. I know whether you are a good brother or not. Don't mind what she says. She don't mean it.”

“I do mean it, child,” cried the mother. “Would to Heaven—”

“HOLD YOUR TONGUE, I SAY!” Harry roared. The violent volume of his voice shattered the oppressive acoustic space of the carriage. “It's a shame to speak so to him, ma'am.”

“And so it is, Harry,” Mrs. Mountain agreed, shaking the boy's hand. “You never said a truer word in your life.”

Madam Esmond turned her fierce gaze upon her companion. “Mrs. Mountain, do you dare to set my children against me? From this very day, madam—”

“Turn me and my child into the street? Do,” Mrs. Mountain retorted, immune to the widow's power dynamics. “That will be a fine revenge because the English lawyer won't give you the boy's money. Find another companion who will tell you black is white, and flatter you: it is not my way, madam. When shall I go? I shan't be long a-packing.”

Before the rupture could finalize, the heavy, institutional tolling of the church bells cut through the morning air. The dominant, rhythmic sound enforced an immediate cessation of hostilities. “Hush! the bells are ringing for church, Mountain,” the widow said, retreating into her pious routine. “Let us try, if you please, and compose ourselves.” During the sermon, George kept his head bowed, while Harry sat close, his arm firmly around his brother’s neck, a silent, tactile rebellion against their mother's divisive rhetoric.

Harry recounted all of this in his own youthful fashion, his narrative punctuated by the sensory details of his distant home. Madam Bernstein listened, entranced, her knitting resting in her lap. 

“Are you my cousin, too?” the honest boy finally asked, looking around the wainscoted parlour. “You seem kinder than my other cousins.”

Above Madam Bernstein’s great chair hung a Kneller portrait, a brilliant rendering of a young lady in the flowing robes of Queen Anne’s time. She possessed a quantity of auburn hair parted off a fair forehead, cascading over pearly shoulders, her hand resting softly on a velvet cushion. 

“That picture is by Sir Godfrey, who thought himself the greatest painter in the world,” the old lady said, her voice dropping into a register of melancholic reflection. “But he was not so good as Lely, who painted your grandmother—my Lady Castlewood, Colonel Esmond's wife; nor he so good as Sir Anthony Van Dyck, who painted your great-grandfather yonder. Some of us are painted blacker than we are. Did you recognise your grandmother in that picture? She had the loveliest fair hair and shape of any woman of her time.”

“I fancied I knew the portrait from instinct, perhaps, and a certain likeness to my mother,” Harry mused.

“Did Mrs. Warrington—I beg her pardon, I think she calls herself Madam or my Lady Esmond now—did she never tell you of another daughter her mother had in England, before she married your grandfather?”

“She never spoke of one.”

“Nor your grandfather?”

“Never,” Harry replied. The absolute erasure of a life from their family’s spoken history felt unnatural to him. “But in his picture-books, which he constantly made for us children, he used to draw a head very like that above your ladyship. That, and Viscount Francis, and King James III., he drew a score of times, I am sure.”

“And the picture over me reminds you of no one, Harry?”

“No, indeed.”

A heavy silence filled the room, the acoustic weight of a cultural memory deliberately starved to death. “Ah! Here is a sermon!” the lady said, a bitter sigh escaping her lips. “Harry, that was my face once—yes, it was—and then I was called Beatrix Esmond. And your mother is my half-sister, child, and she has never even mentioned my name!”
