Messer Bernardo del Nero remained as steadfast in his counsel as Romola had anticipated. His advice—that the marriage be postponed until Easter—held firm, and Bardo, wholly guided by his wise and pragmatic friend, saw no reason to dissent. Yet, even Bernardo, though immune to the personal charm Tito exerted over others, could not entirely ignore the seductive logic of success that invariably sways worldly men. Tito was ascending rapidly through the highest circles. He had secured the favor of the young Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici, who had even hinted at inviting him into his scholarly entourage for an upcoming journey to Rome. This brilliant Greek, possessed of a tongue that spoke freely yet never quarreled, became a sought-after presence at lavish suppers along the Via Larga and at Florentine games where he did not claim to compete, yet admired the unparalleled skill of Piero de' Medici with effortless grace.

By an unbroken chain of cause and effect, Tito's reputation as a delightful companion in "magnificent" society cast a lustrous glow upon his learning and talents; indeed, he was sufficiently accomplished that praise of him required no exaggeration. Messer Bernardo harbored old prejudices and attachments, which now began to outweigh the newer, weaker bias against the supple young Greek stranger. To the aged Florentine, it was impossible to disregard the endorsement of standing well with the city's most distinguished families. Since Tito had become fully integrated into that elite circle whose opinions defined social worth, it seemed irrational to deny that there were no longer any obstacles to accepting such a son-in-law for Bardo and such a husband for Romola.

Undeniably, Tito's arrival marked the dawn of a new era for both father and daughter, surpassing even the initial promises. The blind old scholar, whose proud integrity refused to participate in the deceitful flattery and equally excessive condemnation that wove the fabric of learned discourse, had fallen into neglect among his fellow citizens. When mentioned, the common refrain was that while his blindness and the loss of his son were tragic, he was tedious in defending the value of his own labors. His discontent appeared inconsistent in a man who had previously ignored religious rites and rejected offers of patronage unless they included ordination—a prerequisite for securing support. However, since Tito's arrival, the monotonous shadow surrounding Bardo's name had lifted. The old man ceased his complaints, and the fair daughter was no longer confined in dowerless pride, waiting for a *parentado*.

The winning manners and rising favor of the handsome Greek, destined to fulfill the dual roles of son and husband, transformed the situation into something thoroughly friendly. Visitors frequently enlivened the quiet library, drawn by an indefinable urge to renew contact whenever an old acquaintance became the topic of fresh conversation. Young men, invited once by Tito, readily returned when they met him on his way to the Via de' Bardi, resting their hands on his shoulder to fall into easy chat. It was a pleasure to behold Romola's beauty; seated with a certain grandeur, speaking with gravity, smiling with modesty, and radiating an aura of queenliness—as described by the old Firenzuola—she seemed to unfold like a strong white lily under this genial breath of admiration. To her, this new bright life intertwined with Tito's love was indistinguishable from joy itself.

Tito had also strengthened Bardo's hope that, before his death, he might secure the long-desired protection for his library. He hoped it would not be absorbed into another collection or transferred to a monastery under a new name, but would remain forever the Bardi Library, open to the Florentines. While the old habit of trusting the Medici persisted as long as their influence dominated the state, Tito, having gained the ear of Cardinal Giovanni, could perhaps achieve what Bernardo could not: demonstrating to a learned audience the unique value of Bardi's collection. Tito spoke sanguinely of this outcome, eager to comfort the old man, aware that Romola rewarded these gentle words with an adoration that no direct compliment to herself could elicit.

This matter of the library became the subject of numerous discussions between Bernardo and Tito after Christmas passed and the wedding approached, always conducted away from Bardo's hearing. Bardo clung to a vague belief, which they dared not disturb, that his property outside the library was sufficient to cover all demands. He refused, except during moments of angry despair, to acknowledge that the will disinheriting Dino left Romola heir only to debts, or that he needed patronage beyond ensuring a separate location for his books in exchange for a deed gifting them to the Republic.

"My opinion," Bernardo told Romola beneath the loggia, "is that since you are to marry and Messer Tito possesses a competent income, we should begin winding up affairs. We must ascertain exactly the sum required to shield the library from creditors, rather than allowing debts to accumulate further. Your father requires nothing but his daily shred of mutton and macaroni; I believe Messer Tito may undertake to provide this for the remainder of your father's life, effectively replacing the *morgen-cap*."

"Tito has always known that my life is bound up with my father's," Romola replied. "He is kinder to my father than I am; he delights in making him happy."

"Ah, he is not made of the same clay as other men, is he?" Bernardo smiled. "Your father tried to shut out women's folly by cramming you with Greek and Latin, yet you proved just as ready to believe in the first pair of bright eyes and soft words as if you knew nothing but Paternosters, like other Christian daughters."

"Now, godfather," Romola said, shaking her head playfully, "as if only bright eyes and soft words made me love Tito! You know better. You know I love my father and you because you are both good, and I love Tito because he is so good. I see it, I feel it, in everything he says and does. And if he is handsome, too, why should I not love him the better for that? Beauty seems to me part of the finished language by which goodness speaks. You must have been a very handsome youth yourself, godfather"—she looked up with a happy, loving smile at the stately old man—"about as tall as Tito, with very fine eyes. Only you looked a little sterner and prouder, and..."

"And Romola likes to keep all the pride to herself?" Bernardo teased, not impervious to this charming coaxing.

"However, it is fortunate that in one regard Tito's demands are more modest than those of any Florentine husband of suitable rank we might have found for you; he asks for no dowry."

Thus, the arrangement was settled between Messer Bernardo, Romola, and Tito. Bardo assented with a wave of his hand when Bernardo informed him that it was time to sell property and clear debts, accustomed as he was to view finances as a dense thicket his imagination never penetrated. Meanwhile, Tito set about earning Bernardo's respect by inquiring with his quick wit into Florentine monetary matters—the secrets of the *Monti*, the valuation of real estate, and the profits of banking.

"You will soon forget that Tito is not a Florentine, godfather," Romola observed. "Watch how he learns everything about Florence."

"It seems to me he is one of the *demoni*, children of no particular country," Bernardo replied, smiling. "His mind is too nimble to be weighed down by all the burdens we men carry in our hearts."

Romola smiled back, filled with happy confidence.
