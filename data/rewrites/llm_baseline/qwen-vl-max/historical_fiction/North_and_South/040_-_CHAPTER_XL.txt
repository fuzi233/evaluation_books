“I have no wrong, where I can claim no right,
Naught ta’en me fro, where I have nothing had,
Yet of my woe I cannot so be quite;
Namely, since that another may be glad
With that, that thus in sorrow makes me sad.”

Margaret had not expected much joy from Mr. Bell’s visit—only comfort for her father. But when the old scholar arrived, she found herself instantly at ease, as if they had known each other all their lives. He declared she had no merit in being what she was—such a girl after his own heart—because it was simply inherited, a natural gift to walk into his affections and take them by right. She smiled and praised him in return for his youthfulness under the solemn cap and gown of Oxford.

“Fresh and young in warmth and kindness,” she said. “But I must confess, I think your opinions are the oldest and most stubborn I’ve met in a long while.”

“Hear this daughter of yours, Hale!” Mr. Bell laughed. “Her time in Milton has entirely corrupted her. She’s a democrat, a red republican, a member of the Peace Society, a socialist—”

“Papa!” Margaret interrupted gently. “It’s only because I believe in progress—especially in commerce. Mr. Bell would have trade stay still, exchanging acorns for wild-beast skins.”

“No, no,” he replied with a grin. “I’d dig the ground and grow potatoes. I’d shear the beast’s hide and spin it into broadcloth. But don’t exaggerate, missy. Still, I’m tired of this constant rush—everyone trampling over everyone else in their mad race to get rich.”

“It’s not everyone who can sit comfortably in college rooms and watch their wealth grow without lifting a finger,” Mr. Hale observed. “No doubt many men here would give anything if their property increased as steadily as yours has, without effort.”

“I don’t believe they would,” Mr. Bell answered. “They like the struggle. The bustle is what excites them. As for sitting still, learning from the past, or shaping the future through honest work done with foresight—why, pooh! I don’t believe there’s a man in Milton who knows how to sit still. And that, truly, is an art.”

“Then perhaps Milton folk think Oxford men don’t know how to move,” Mr. Hale suggested. “It might be good if they mixed more.”

“It might benefit the Miltoners,” Mr. Bell countered. “Many things could help them that would be very disagreeable to others.”

“Are you not a Milton man yourself?” Margaret asked. “I should have thought you’d be proud of your town.”

“I confess,” he said, “I see little to be proud of. But come to Oxford, Margaret—I’ll show you a place worth glorying in.”

“Well,” Mr. Hale said, “Mr. Thornton is coming to tea tonight. He’s as proud of Milton as you are of Oxford. You two should try to make each other a bit more open-minded.”

“I don’t want to be more liberal-minded, thank you,” Mr. Bell replied.

“Is Mr. Thornton coming to tea, Papa?” Margaret whispered.

“Either to tea or shortly after. He didn’t say exactly. He told us not to wait.”

Mr. Thornton had resolved not to ask his mother whether she had spoken to Margaret about her conduct. He knew well enough that if such a conversation had taken place, her account of it would only wound him—distorting the truth through her own lens. He dreaded hearing her name even mentioned. Though he blamed her, though he resented her, though he rejected her—he still loved her deeply, against his will. He dreamt of her: dancing toward him with arms outstretched, light and carefree, a vision that both repelled and drew him. Yet this image, stripped of her true character—like a body possessed by an evil spirit—had become fixed in his mind. When he woke, he could barely distinguish the real Margaret from the phantom. The loathing he felt for the latter clung to the former, disfiguring her in his thoughts. Still, he was too proud to admit his weakness by avoiding her. He would neither seek nor flee her presence. To prove his self-control, he delayed every task that afternoon, forcing himself into unnatural slowness. It was past eight before he reached the Hales’ house. Then came business with Mr. Bell in the study—talk that lingered far beyond necessity, as Mr. Bell sat by the fire, weary and talkative, while Mr. Thornton grew restless, chafing at the delay. He found Mr. Bell dull, and Mr. Bell, in turn, considered him brusque and cold, lacking both wit and grace.

At last, a faint sound from above signaled the need to move upstairs. They found Margaret seated, a letter open before her, engaged in animated discussion with her father. At their entrance, she quickly closed it—but Mr. Thornton caught fragments of her father’s words.

“A letter from Henry Lennox. It makes Margaret very hopeful.”

Mr. Bell nodded. Margaret flushed crimson when Mr. Thornton looked at her. He nearly rose and fled the room then and there, never to return.

“We were thinking,” Mr. Hale said, “that you and Mr. Thornton had taken Margaret’s advice and were trying to convert each other—you were so long down there.”

“And you thought we’d end up like the Kilkenny cats, with nothing left but our opinions. Whose opinion do you suppose would survive longest?”

Mr. Thornton had no idea what they meant—and refused to ask. Mr. Hale explained.

“This morning, we accused Mr. Bell of a kind of Oxonian medievalism—his prejudice against his own town. Margaret suggested he might benefit from associating with Milton manufacturers.”

“I beg your pardon,” Mr. Bell corrected. “Margaret thought it would do the Milton people good to mix more with Oxford men. Wasn’t that it, Margaret?”

“I believe I thought it would help both sides to know each other better,” she said. “I didn’t realize it was my idea any more than Papa’s.”

“So you see, Mr. Thornton,” Mr. Hale continued, “we should have been improving each other downstairs instead of talking about vanished families of Smiths and Harrisons. Still, I’m willing to do my part now. I wonder when you Milton men intend to live. Your lives seem spent gathering materials for life.”

“By living,” Mr. Thornton said sharply, “you mean enjoyment?”

“Yes,” Mr. Hale replied. “Enjoyment—I won’t specify what, for I trust we both consider mere pleasure poor enjoyment.”

“I’d rather have the nature of the enjoyment defined.”

“Well,” Mr. Hale said, “enjoyment or leisure—the enjoyment of power and influence that money brings. You’re all striving for money. What do you want it for?”

Mr. Thornton remained silent. Then: “I really don’t know. But money isn’t what I strive for.”

“What, then?”

“It’s a personal matter. I’d have to lay myself bare to such a catechist—and I’m not sure I’m ready.”

“No,” Mr. Hale said. “Let’s not be personal. Neither of you is representative. Each is too individual.”

“I’m not sure whether that’s a compliment,” Mr. Bell said. “I’d like to stand for Oxford—its beauty, its learning, its proud history. What do you say, Margaret? Should I be flattered?”

“I don’t know Oxford,” she replied. “But there’s a difference between being a city’s representative and the representative man of its people.”

“Very true,” Mr. Bell said. “Now I recall—you were against me this morning, quite Miltonian and manufacturing in your tastes.”

Margaret saw the sudden look of surprise on Mr. Thornton’s face and winced—fearing the wrong interpretation of Mr. Bell’s words. Mr. Bell went on:

“Oh, I wish I could show you our High Street—our Radcliffe Square. I leave out the colleges, just as I ask Mr. Thornton to omit his factories when speaking of Milton’s charm. I have a right to criticize my birthplace. After all, I am a Milton man.”

Mr. Thornton was more annoyed than he ought to have been. He was not in the mood for jest. At another time, he might have enjoyed Mr. Bell’s half-irritated critique of a town so alien to his ways. But now, he felt provoked—defending something never meant to be attacked.

“I don’t set Milton up as a model.”

“Not in architecture?” Mr. Bell asked, slyly.

“No. We’ve been too busy to care for outward appearances.”

“Don’t say *mere* outward appearances,” Mr. Hale said gently. “They shape us all—from childhood onward, every day of our lives.”

“Wait a moment,” Mr. Thornton said. “We’re of a different race from the Greeks, to whom beauty was everything. Mr. Bell might speak of leisure and serene enjoyment—much of which entered through the senses. I don’t despise them, nor would I imitate them. But I belong to Teutonic blood—though it’s rare in this part of England. We keep much of their language, more of their spirit. We don’t see life as a time for enjoyment, but for action and effort. Our strength and beauty come from overcoming resistance, from conquering greater difficulties. We are Teutonic here in Darkshire in another way—we resist distant laws. We want to govern ourselves, not be governed by flawed legislation. We value self-rule and oppose centralization.”

“In short,” Mr. Bell said, “you’d like the Heptarchy back. Well, at least I retract my earlier claim—that Milton people don’t revere the past. You worship Thor.”

“If we don’t honor the past as you do in Oxford,” Mr. Thornton said, “it’s because we seek something more useful to the present. It’s fine when studying the past leads to prophecy. But for those navigating new circumstances, it would be finer if experience guided us directly—how to act in matters closest to us, full of challenges. How we meet and overcome these—not merely push them aside—shapes our future. Yet people speak easily of Utopia, but rarely of tomorrow’s duty. And when that duty is done by others, they’re quick to cry, ‘Fie, for shame!’”

“All this time,” Mr. Bell said, “I don’t see what you’re talking about. Would you Milton men send your daily troubles to Oxford? You haven’t tried us yet.”

Mr. Thornton laughed outright. “I believe I was referring to recent troubles—our strikes, painful and costly as they’ve been. And yet this latest strike, though it hurts me now, was respectable.”

“A respectable strike!” Mr. Bell exclaimed. “That sounds like deep devotion to Thor.”

Margaret sensed—rather than saw—that Mr. Thornton was wounded by the repeated mockery of what he took seriously. She tried to shift the topic—something trivial, unimportant to one, deeply personal to the other. She forced herself to speak.

“Edith says the printed calicoes in Corfu are better and cheaper than in London.”

“Does she?” Mr. Hale said. “I suspect that’s one of Edith’s exaggerations. Are you sure, Margaret?”

“I’m sure she said so, Papa.”

“Then I accept it as fact,” Mr. Bell declared. “Margaret, I trust your truthfulness so completely, I extend it to your cousin. I don’t believe a cousin of yours could exaggerate.”

“Is Miss Hale so remarkable for honesty?” Mr. Thornton asked, bitterly. The moment the words left his mouth, he regretted them. What was he? Why did he wound her with such cruelty? How ill he was tonight—tormented by delay, irritated by names, angry at the ease with which Mr. Bell joked, mocking a man whose heart was already strained. And yet—what had he said to her?

She did not rise and leave, as she once might have. She sat still, her eyes flashing briefly with hurt—like a child suddenly rebuffed—then softening into quiet sadness. Her gaze fell, and she bent over her sewing, silent. But he could not stop watching her. He saw a shiver pass through her, as if chilled by an unexpected wind. He felt like a mother abruptly called away while rocking her child—before the slow, trusting smile had returned. He gave curt answers, restless, irritable, unable to tell jest from earnest. All he wanted was a glance, a word from her—so he might kneel in penitence. But she offered neither. Her fingers moved swiftly, steadily, as if sewing were the sole purpose of her life.

He thought she couldn’t care for him—or else the fire of his desire would have made her lift her eyes, even for a second, to read his remorse. He would have struck her before leaving—just to earn the right to confess the pain gnawing at his soul. Fortunately, the long walk outside cooled his temper. He returned to resolve: henceforth, he would see her as little as possible—for her face, her voice (like gentle winds of melody) held such power to unbalance him. Yes, he had known love—a fierce, consuming flame. But through it, he would fight his way to calm maturity, richer and deeper for having known such passion.

When he left abruptly, Margaret rose and began folding her work. The seams seemed heavier than usual. Her features softened into lines of weariness. She looked as if she had endured a long, exhausting day.

As they prepared for bed, Mr. Bell muttered, “Never saw a man so spoiled by success. Can’t bear a word, a joke—everything touches his pride. Once, he was noble and simple, like daylight. You couldn’t offend him—no vanity.”

“He’s not vain now,” Margaret said, turning from the table, her voice calm. “Tonight, he wasn’t himself. Something must have troubled him before he came.”

Mr. Bell gave her a sharp look over his spectacles. She met it quietly. After she left, he turned to Mr. Hale.

“Hale—have you ever noticed that Thornton and your daughter share what the French call *tendresse*?”

“Never!” Mr. Hale stammered, startled. “No, I’m certain you’re mistaken. If there’s anything, it’s all on Mr. Thornton’s side. Poor fellow! I hope he doesn’t think of her—because I’m sure she wouldnches him.”

“Well,” Mr. Bell said, “I’m a bachelor, never touched love affairs. So my opinion may be worthless. But I’d say there are very pretty signs in her!”

“Then I’m sure you’re wrong,” Mr. Hale insisted. “He may care for her—though she’s been rude at times. But she? Margaret would never think of him. Such a thing has never crossed her mind.”

“Entering her heart would be enough. I only hinted. I dare say I’m wrong. And whether I am or not, I’m very sleepy. Having disturbed your night with my idle fancies, I’ll retire with peace of mind.”

But Mr. Hale vowed not to let such nonsense disturb him. So he lay awake, determined not to think about it.

The next day, Mr. Bell took his leave, telling Margaret to remember him as one who had a right to help and protect her in any trouble. To Mr. Hale, he said:

“That Margaret of yours has gone deep into my heart. Take care of her—she’s a precious creature, far too good for Milton—really fit only for Oxford. The town, I mean—not the men. I can’t match her yet. When I can, I’ll bring my young man to stand beside your young woman—like the genii in the Arabian Nights bringing Prince Caralmazan to match Princess Badoura.”

“I beg you not to,” Mr. Hale said. “Remember the misfortunes that followed. And besides—I can’t spare Margaret.”

“No,” Mr. Bell said. “On second thought, let’s have her nurse us ten years from now, when we’re two grumpy invalids. Seriously, Hale—I wish you’d leave Milton. It’s unsuitable for you, though I recommended it. If you would, I’d forget my doubts and take a college living. You and Margaret could come live at the parsonage—you as a sort of lay curate, handling the unwashed; she as our housekeeper—the village Lady Bountiful by day, reading us to sleep at night. I’d be happy. What do you think?”

“Never,” Mr. Hale said firmly. “My great change is made. My price of suffering paid. Here I stay, and here I’ll die, lost in the crowd.”

“I won’t give up my plan,” Mr. Bell said. “But I won’t press it now. Where’s the Pearl? Come, Margaret—give me a farewell kiss. Remember, my dear, where you may find a true friend, as far as his ability allows. You are my child, Margaret. Remember that. God bless you.”

And so they slipped back into their quiet routine. No invalid to worry over; even the Higginses, once so vivid, seemed distant. The Boucher children, orphaned and motherless, claimed Margaret’s attention. She visited Mary Higgins often, who cared for them. The two families lived together—older children in humble schools, younger ones tended by a kind neighbor whose sense had impressed Margaret at Boucher’s death. She was paid for her help. Nicholas, in managing the children’s affairs, showed surprising judgment and method—unlike his earlier erratic ways. He worked steadily, so Margaret rarely saw him. When she did, he avoided any mention of the children’s father—whom he had taken in fully, though with difficulty. He spoke little of Mr. Thornton.

“To tell the truth,” he said once, “he bamboozles me. He’s two men. One I knew before—strict master, all authority. The other—no trace of master’s flesh. How these two are joined in one body, I can’t figure. But I won’t be beaten by it. He comes here often—that’s how I know the man, not the master. And I reckon he’s as surprised by me as I am by him. He sits and stares, like some strange beast caught in the wild. But I’m not daunted. It would take a lot to daunt me in my own house, as he sees. And I tell him my mind—things I think he’d have benefited from hearing when younger.”

“And does he answer?” Mr. Hale asked.

“Well,” Nicholas said, “the advantage isn’t all his. I’ve improved him a bit. Sometimes he says rough things—hard to hear at first, but with a queer truth when you chew on them. He’ll be coming tonight about the children’s schooling. He’s not satisfied with it and wants to examine them.”

“What are they”—Mr. Hale began, but Margaret touched his arm and showed him her watch.

“It’s nearly seven,” she said. “Evenings are growing longer. Come, Papa.” She didn’t breathe freely until they were some distance from the house. Then, calming, she wished she hadn’t hurried—because now they saw Mr. Thornton so seldom. He might have come to see Higgins, and for old friendship’s sake, she would have liked to see him tonight.

Yes, he came rarely—even for lessons. Mr. Hale was disappointed in his pupil’s fading interest in Greek literature, once so passionate. Now, hurried notes from Mr. Thornton often arrived at the last moment: “Too engaged to come tonight.” Though others filled his time, none matched his first scholar in Mr. Hale’s heart. He grew melancholy, pondering why this bond had weakened.

One evening, he startled Margaret, working quietly, by suddenly asking:

“Margaret—have you ever had reason to think Mr. Thornton cared for you?”

He blushed slightly as the words left him—Mr. Bell’s suggestion returning. But he had already spoken.

Margaret didn’t answer at once. By the droop of her head, he guessed her reply.

“Yes,” she whispered. “I believe—oh, Papa, I should have told you.” She dropped her work and buried her face in her hands.

“No, dear,” he said softly. “Don’t think I’m prying. I’m sure you’d have told me if you could return his feelings. Did he speak to you?”

No answer at first. Then, slowly, a small, reluctant “Yes.”

“And you refused him?”

She nodded, her silence heavy with grief.
