Gerard felt a fervor for this promised land nearly matching Denys's own. The soldier was a relentless evangelist for Burgundy; at every minor inconvenience, he would chant its virtues, contrasting them sharply with their current surroundings. Above all, he exploited Gerard's fastidious nature by promising the pristine linens of that polished nation. "Ask no more!" Denys would declare, shaking his head in disgust. "To think I haven't slept in a bare bed since leaving home! When I gaze upon their linen, I wish nothing more than to close my eyes and nose forever." His patriotism bordered on the absurd; he had once walked twenty leagues in shoes that had long since disintegrated rather than purchase provisions from a German merchant, whom he deemed a churlish obstacle to civilization, citing the man's incivility and poor fare as reasons enough to endure the journey.

As the sun began to dip below the horizon, they found themselves equidistant between a small town and a monastery, the latter situated slightly off the main road. Denys favored the inn; Gerard preferred the convent. Denys relented, however, on one condition: once within Burgundian borders, they were to lodge exclusively at inns. Gerard agreed all too readily, for his chart of monasteries concluded precisely at this point. Turning off the road toward the cloister, Gerard soon voiced his bewilderment regarding Denys's sudden aversion to places that had previously offered them free board and lodging.

The soldier hesitated, hemming and hawing, before finally letting his grievances spill forth. It emerged that this was not a fleeting dislike but an ancient, abiding horror, suppressed until now only through sheer effort and politeness. "I suspected they had poisoned your drink," Denys confessed, "so I held back. But since this is our final stop, why not clear my conscience? Consider me a fish out of water in all those great dungeons. You rise immediately with some old friar, so you never witness my purgatory."

"Forgive me," Gerard said, feeling guilty. "I have been selfish."

"Ay, ay, I forgive thee, little one; 'tis not thy fault," Denys replied dismissively. "Art thou not the first fool ever priest-ridden and monk-hitted? Yet, I shall not forgive *them* my misery." With a grim determination, he launched into his indictment, recounting tales from a century prior to Henry VIII.'s commissioners.

"These gloomy piles are all constructed identically," Denys raged. "Inns vary, but here everything is monotony. A great gate, a little gate, countless steps leading to a gloomy cloister. There is the dormitory, then the vast, freezing refectory where one must sit silent, or at least inaudible, if one wishes to keep one's thoughts private. 'And then,' I say, 'no one is truly human here; everyone is a slave. Slaves to what? To a peevish, tinkling bell that knows no sleep.' If it were a trumpet blowing constant alarms, it might not freeze a man's heart quite so thoroughly. *Tinkle, tinkle, tinkle*, and yet you must sit down to eat with perhaps no appetite whatsoever. Before your food has settled in your stomach, *tinkle, tinkle!* and you must rush to church with perhaps no desire for devotion. I am no hog at prayers, I assure you. *Tinkle, tinkle,* and now you must retire to bed with your eyes wide open. By the time you manage to shut them, some restless spirit of darkness has seized the bell-rope, and *tinkle, tinkle!* compels you to recite a prayer in the dark, knowing none or caring less. If they could hear the sort of prayers I mutter while they shatter my rest with their *tinkling*! Well, you drift off again, gaining perhaps an hour's sleep: lo, it is *tinkle, tinkle* once more for Matins."

"And the only clapper you seem to cherish is a woman's," Gerard interjected, speaking with half-contempt.

"Because there is some music in her voice, even when she scolds," Denys retorted stoutly.

"And then to be constantly corrected. If I merely touch my finger in the salt-cellars, instantly I hear, 'Have you no knife with which to handle the salt?' And if I dare wipe my blade on the cloth to save time, the rebuke comes: 'Wipe thy knife dirty on the bread, and clean upon the cloth!' Oh, small souls! These petty, pedantic trivialities fall upon good fellowship like tiny icicles melting down from thatched eaves."

"I hold cleanliness to be no mere pedantry," Gerard countered firmly. "Thou shouldst learn better manners once and for all."

"Nay; 'tis they who lack manners. They stop a fellow's mouth at every word."

"At every other word, you mean; every obscene or blasphemous one."

"Exaggerator, go to! At the very last of these dungeons, I found poor travelers sitting chilled and mute around a single friar, looking like rogues awaiting execution. To cheer them, I cried out, 'Courage, tout le monde, le diable—'"

"Connu! What happened?" Gerard interrupted.

"Marry, this. 'Blaspheme not!' quoth the executioner. 'Plait-il?' I replied. Didn't he wheel and wryte at me in a sort of Alsatian French, turning all the P's into B's? I had much ado not to laugh in his face."

"Being thyself unable to speak ten words of his tongue without error," Gerard noted dryly.

"Well, all the world ought to speak French. What avail so many jargons except to erect frontiers between men's hearts?"

"But what did he say?"

"What signifies it what a fool says?"

"Oh, not all the words of a fool are folly, or I should not listen to you."

"Well, then, he declared, 'Those who begin by making free with the Devil's name end by doing it with all the names in Heaven.' 'Father,' I said, 'I am a soldier, and this is but my consigne, my watchword.' 'Oh, then it is just a custom?' he asked. Not seeing through the old fox, and hoping to clear myself, I answered, 'Ay, it was.' 'Then that is ten times worse,' he replied. ''Twill bring him about your ears one of these days. He still comes where he hears his name often called.' Observe! No gratitude for the tidings which neither his missals nor his breviary had ever revealed to him. Then, deigning further instruction, he informed me that soldiers commonly commit the crimes for which all other men are broken on the wheel—a savoir murder, rape, and pillage."

"And is't not true?" Gerard asked cautiously.

"True or not, it was ill manners," Denys replied guardedly.

"And so does this courteous host of mine add, 'Being the foes of mankind, why make enemies of good spirits into the bargain by still shouting the names of evil ones?' and a host of similar nonsense."

"Well, but, Denys, whether you heed his advice or scorn it, why blame a man for raising his voice to save your soul?"

"How can his voice save my soul when he keeps twisting his P's into B's?"

Gerard was staggered. Before he could recover from this thunderbolt of Gallicism, Denys veered triumphantly onto another tangent, stigmatizing all monks as hypocrites. "Do but look at them," he sneered. "How they creep about, unable to eye you like honest men."

"Nay," Gerard said eagerly, defending his order. "That modest, downcast gaze is part of their discipline; it is *custodia oculorum*."

"Cussed toads eating *hoc hac horum*? No such thing! Just so looks a cutpurse. Cannot meet a true man's eye. Doff the cowl, monk, and behold a thief; don the cowl upon the thief, and lo, a monk. Tell me not they will ever be able to look God Almighty in the face when they cannot even look a true man in the face down here. Ah, here it is, black as ink! Into the well we go, comrade."

Just then, the sound approached. "Misericorde, there goes the tinkle already," Denys announced. "'Tis the best of tinkles though; 'tis for dinner: stay, listen! I thought so: the wolf in my stomach cried 'Amen!'"

He confirmed this last statement with two oaths and marched forward like a victorious gamecock into the convent, believing that by Gerard's silence he had won the argument, unaware of the profound disgust he had actually instilled in his companion.
