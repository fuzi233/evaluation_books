Silence enveloped the small, dimly lit study, the only sound the faint scratch of a quill on parchment. I sat hunched over the desk, my eyes scanning the lines of my latest letter to my mother. The words seemed to mock me, a jumbled mess of hurt and anger, a desperate attempt to justify my actions. I had written of betrayal and heartache, of a love that had been torn apart by her disapproval. The ink still wet, I sealed the letter and handed it to the servant, who departed with a somber expression.

Days passed, and the silence grew thicker, a palpable thing that filled every corner of the house. I avoided my mother, unable to bear the weight of her disappointment. It was as if the very air itself had been drained of sound, leaving only an oppressive stillness. I wandered the empty halls, searching for a distraction from my turmoil, but every door led only to more silence.

It was then that I received her response, a letter written in elegant script, its words a gentle rebuke. She spoke not of my transgressions, but of her own actions, of the choices she had made as a mother. She referred the matter to a higher authority, one that would judge us both. I felt a pang of guilt, a sense of being called to account for my own behavior. The letter ended with a quote from Homer, a reminder that even in our darkest moments, we call upon the gods to intervene.

I read the words again and again, searching for a glimmer of understanding. But the more I read, the more I felt lost, adrift in a sea of uncertainty. My thoughts turned to the past, to the countless times we had appealed to the heavens, to the justice of our causes. Had we been right, or wrong? And what of the little woman, Mrs. Esmond, who had stood firm against my desires, appealing to the same authority that my mother now invoked?

The days passed, and the silence deepened. I began to feel like a stranger in my own home, a man without a place to belong. The world outside seemed to move on, unaware of my inner turmoil. I was left with only my thoughts, and the haunting memory of my mother's words: "Might I, as a son, be equally able to answer for myself, and to show, when the Great Judge demanded the question of me, whether I had done my own duty, and honoured my father and mother?"
