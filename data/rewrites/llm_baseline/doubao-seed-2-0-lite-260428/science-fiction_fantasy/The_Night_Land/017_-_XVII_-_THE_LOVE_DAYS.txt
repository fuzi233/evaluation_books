When I finally drifted back into full consciousness, I knew I was ascending in the great lift, laid out upon the very bed I had once been certain I would never need again—never return to, never wake in, after vanishing forever into the silent, sunless vast of the Country of Silence. Vague, strange echoes rose up through miles of stone from the world’s deepest abysses: the low, rolling thunder of the Underground Organs, sounding like distant music that hummed even beyond the border of death. A deep chant followed, like the voices of uncounted multitudes singing far beyond the edge of the world’s farthest mountains, now dipping low as a wind soughing through the black Deep, then rising clear, bright, the ancient, holy melody of the Song of Honour. I understood, half in a dream, that the millions dwelling in that sunken realm were pouring out rejoicing over the impossible, unhoped-for joy that had come to pass.

All of it was faint, half-shrouded from my senses. My eyes would not lift to open, my whole self bobbing like a buoy on unsteady, unreal waters. Only the scents were solid: sweet, heady blooms drifting up from the vast flower-fields that lined the lift’s ascent path, as the car climbed mile after mile toward the upper levels of the Pyramid. I must have stirred, for the Master Doctor spoke to me, low and gentle, bidding me rest: the Maid was whole and well, all harm had passed.

I slipped then into a soft haze, drifting through long stretches where I half-lived, half-slept, untroubled by the quiet question of whether I was still among the living. For days after that I lay still, no thought for anything beyond the slow thud of my own heartbeat, and the Master Doctor leaned over me again and again, peering keenly into my slack face. At last, after long, uncounted hours, someone else bent over me: the dear, beloved face of Mine Own, her eyes spilling quiet, wordless love straight into my soul, her manner soft and still.

I fumbled weakly with my hands, and she took them in hers, pressing warmth and life into me through the palms. She did not speak, only held me, and slow, soft contentment bloomed in my chest until I drifted off into a deep, natural, healing sleep.

When the day came that I was allowed to rise, my attendants carried me to one of the Pyramid’s quiet, walled gardens, and left me there alone for a time. A moment later, someone stepped around a clump of flowering bushes, pausing to glance at me with half-shyness—though the love blazing in her eyes made that shyness small, trivial. It was Mine Own Maid, Naani, dressed in soft, frilled finery I had never seen on her before, more dainty and luminous than I had ever dared imagine. I struggled to stand to go to her, and she ran fast to stop my foolish exertion, sinking to sit beside me, tucking my head against her breast and pressing her lips to mine, half tender mother and half blushing maid in the same breath. She bade me lie still, and we sat wrapped in utter, wordless happiness, until my attendants returned with the Master Doctor, whose face softened with quiet satisfaction at the sight of us.

After that day, I saw Mine Own Maid every day, and my strength returned with impossible speed—for love was my truest medicine. Soon I was permitted to venture out into the vast fields, though we traveled only by private, hidden paths, for the Multitudes of the Pyramid would have crowded after us constantly, and we craved perfect quiet. The Master Monstruwacan and the Master Doctor gave their approval, and an officer of the marriage rites joined us in a small, private ceremony, far simpler than the grand public wedding we would hold later: that great public rite, where the Millions formed an eight-mile high Guard of Honour from the very peak of the Mighty Pyramid all the way to its lowest foundations, was a celebration the people insisted on giving, for they would not be denied the chance to honour what we had done.

But those grand days came later. For now, we were alone, husband and wife, my strength steadily building, and Naani’s own health fully restored at last. These were the Love Days, the most perfect, radiant stretch of existence possible, when love is true and unshakable. We wandered the vast fields as we pleased, treading the hidden Love Paths that ran near the small scattered villages that dotted the countryside, each field so large it might have passed for a whole country of old. We hid our names from strangers, lest curiosity and well-meaning kindness draw crowds around us, for we needed nothing but each other, uninterrupted. We chose our resting places in spots thick with the rarest, most beautiful blooms, carried small stores of food with us, and stopped to eat at the village inns dotted across the lands when our supplies ran low. Mine Own kept her promise to me a hundred times over, preparing huge, hearty feasts for me to devour, teasing me for being a glutton as I ate, then kissing me before I could ever get a word in to defend myself. She was every last thing my heart and my spirit had ever ached for: a companion wrapped in love, who had led my spirit straight into a state of perfect, unending joy.

Once we ventured down into the Country of Silence for a short visit, though we did not stay long at first, for the weight of all we had endured there rose over me fast. Later, we would wander those quiet sunken lands often, wrapped in shared memory, quiet reverence, and the love that holds all pain and all joy together. As we left that dark realm that first day, I told Naani that while she had been trapped in the death-like stasis the evil force of the ancient House had inflicted on her, I had lived with a terrible, lingering grief that I might never wake to catch her sneaking kisses to me while I lay asleep. She blushed so soft and lovely, for she had never guessed I had been half-aware of that sweet secret naughtiness, and her face fell as she remembered the agony of those days, when she had grieved for me thinking I was truly dead, before the great living vapour of the Earth-Force had pulled her free of the Silence. She curled into my side, full of tender understanding, and told me what the doctors had explained: she had been as if stunned, her spirit frozen, every thread of her life suspended, until the great Earth-Current’s life force had stirred her spirit awake, set her blood flowing warm again in her veins. The doctors had pored over the oldest surviving records of their craft recently, and found one faint, half-forgotten account of a similar miracle eons in the past—but nothing at all like ours, not in the whole span of the millions of years the Night Land had stood.

As we wandered and rested through the fields, I told Naani old stories of my adventures, and learned that she had grieved almost as deeply as I while I lay near death, refusing to leave my bedside until the Master Doctor had ordered her rest, for he had feared my spirit would slip away forever if she did not lend me her warmth and her will to live by my side. I can scarce put to words the depth of my love, when I thought of how brave and steady she had been, holding my hands all those days, when she could barely stand on her own feet from grief and exhaustion.

There is only one last thing left to tell, that happened some while after we held our grand public marriage ceremony. One day, my wife, my own Naani, led me with sweet, gentle cunning to the great Hall of Honour. When we stepped inside, I saw hundreds of the people of the Pyramid lined the great walls, standing in total, soft silence, as if they waited for something, and did not wish to disturb us. She led me forward to the centre of the hall, and I understood at last why she had hidden her plan from me: there, in the sacred Place of Honour reserved only for the Great Dead, stood a statue of a man clad in broken, dented armour, carrying a limp maid in his arms, striding through the dark. I was struck dumb. No one living had ever been granted this honour, not in all the long ages of the Night Land—and I was only a young man, no great hero, made great only by the fact that I had loved with every thread of my heart and soul, so that death had become a small, trivial thing to me next to that love.

Mine Own stood beside me, weeping quiet tears of joy and proud love for her man, and the whole great hall hummed with soft, dear sympathy. The people there let us leave in quiet, no cheering, no crowding, that gentle respect that is the sweetest mark of true honour. I walked back out into the light with my wife pressed close to my side, and I understood a simple truth: honour is no more than empty ash, if you do not have love to give it meaning. But to have true love is to have everything. For that perfect, true love gives birth to honour, and to faithfulness, and the three together build the House of Joy, where no shadow of the dark Night Land outside can ever reach.
