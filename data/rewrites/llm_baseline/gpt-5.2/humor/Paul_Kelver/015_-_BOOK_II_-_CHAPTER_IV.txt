“Don’t be nervous,” said the O’Kelly, with that air of brisk tenderness which, in men of genius, often masquerades as impatience. “And don’t try to do too much. You’ve a very fair voice, but it’s not powerful. Keep cool and open your mouth.”

It was eleven o’clock in the morning, and the air outside the stage-door smelt of wet stone, stale paint, and that peculiar theatrical draught which seems to whistle from one century into the next. We stood at the mouth of a narrow court that funneled sound as neatly as a kitchen funnel funnels broth: every cart-wheel in the street became a drum, every distant hammer a cymbal. The O’Kelly, who heard the world as other men taste it, winced once at an iron-shod hoof striking the cobbles.

“For a fortnight past,” as I say now, “the O’Kelly had been coaching me,” and if you think that means singing in comfort, you don’t know the domestic arrangements of a man married to virtue with a conscience.

Mrs. O’Kelly—thin, acid, and morally trimmed to a point—held a conscientious objection to all music not of a sacred character. To win her husband from at least one of his sinful tendencies, the piano had been sold off like contraband, and its place filled by an American organ of exceptionally lugubrious tone. It did not so much play hymns as pronounce sentence on them. Its notes did not ring; they exhaled—like boiled cabbage left too long, only without the consolation of nourishment.

With this instrument we had had to make shift. The O’Kelly—veritable musical genius—coaxed from it an accompaniment to “Sally in Our Alley” that was less misleading than might reasonably have been expected from an object apparently designed to accompany funerals of people one hadn’t liked. Still, it did nothing to lighten our labours. My rendering of the famous ballad acquired, in consequence, a dolefulness not intended by the composer; the sort of dolefulness you get when you salt your custard by accident, and must go on eating it because there is no more milk in the house and your aunt is watching.

“Lighter, lighter. Not so soulful,” the O’Kelly would plead, while the organ groaned beneath his hands like a beast remembering its sins.

Once we were nearly caught. Mrs. O’Kelly returned from a district visitors’ committee meeting earlier than was expected. Hastily I was hidden in a small conservatory adjoining the first-floor landing; crouching behind flower-pots, breathing geranium and damp earth, I listened—heart thumping like a kitchen pestle—to the severe cross-examination of the O’Kelly.

“William, do not prevaricate. It was not a hymn.”

“Me dear, so much depends upon the time. Let me give ye an example of what I mean.”

“William, pray in my presence not to play tricks with sacred melodies. If you have no respect for religion, please remember that I have. Besides, why should you be playing hymns in any time at ten o’clock in the morning? It is not like you, William, and I do not credit your explanation. And you were singing. I distinctly heard the word ‘Sally’ as I opened the door.”

“Salvation, me dear,” corrected the O’Kelly, with the innocent boldness of a man seasoning a lie with confidence.

“Your enunciation, William, is not usually so much at fault.”

“A little hoarseness, me dear.”

“Your voice did not sound hoarse. Perhaps it will be better if we do not pursue the subject further.”

With this the O’Kelly appeared to agree—wisely, as a cook agrees with a hostess who insists that boiling improves everything.

“A lady a little difficult to get on with when ye’re feeling well and strong,” he would explain afterward; “but if ye happen to be ill, one of the kindest, most devoted of women. When I was down with typhoid three years ago, a tenderer nurse no man could have had. I shall never forget it. And so she would be again to-morrow, if there was anything serious the matter with me.”

I murmured the well-known quotation.

“Mrs. O’Kelly to a T,” concurred the O’Kelly. “I sometimes wonder if Lady Scott may not have been the same sort of woman.”

“The unfortunate part of it is,” he continued, “that I’m such a healthy beggar; it don’t give her a chance. If I were only a chronic invalid, now, there’s nothing that woman would not do to make me happy. As it is—” He struck a chord; the American organ responded as though a tomb had sighed. We resumed.

But to return to that morning at the stage-door.

“Meet me at the Cheshire Cheese at one o’clock,” said the O’Kelly, shaking hands as though sealing a bargain for a fine side of beef. “If ye don’t get on here, we’ll try something else; but I’ve spoken to Hodgson, and I think ye will. Good luck to ye!”

He went his way, and I mine. In a glass box just behind the door a curved-nose, round-eyed little man demanded my business. He looked like an angry bird in a cage—a starling with grievances.

I showed him my letter.

“Up the passage, across the stage, along the corridor, first floor, second door on the right,” he snapped, all in one breath, as if afraid a pause might let in humanity. The window shut with a click like the lid of a biscuit tin.

I proceeded up the passage. It surprised me to discover I was not in the least excited at the thought of my first introduction to “behind the scenes.” Perhaps the reason was that my mind, like a cook’s at a banquet, was occupied not with glory but with the dreadful practicalities: Would my voice rise when called? Would my mouth go dry? Would my stomach, that treacherous assistant, decide to make its own remarks at the wrong time?

I recall my father asking a young soldier home from the Crimea what sensations he had at the commencement of his first charge.

“Well,” replied the young fellow, “I was worrying all the time, remembering I had rushed out leaving the beer tap running in the canteen, and I could not forget it.”

So far as the stage I found my way in safety. Pausing for a moment and glancing round, my impression was not so much disillusionment as the realization of my worst forebodings. In that instant, all glamour fell from me as skin peels from a scalded tomato. The theatre—tawdry decorations, childish make-belief littered about—showed itself a painted thing of shreds and patches: the grown child’s doll’s-house. One could smell the paste behind the grandeur; hear, beneath the applause imagined, the honest squeak of boards and the clatter of unseen props. The sonic world here was all wrong: too many hard surfaces, too many sharp echoes. Even silence sounded manufactured, like canned soup.

The Drama may improve us, elevate us, interest and teach us. I am sure it does; long may it flourish. But so likewise does the dressing and undressing of dolls, the opening of the front of the house, the tenderly putting of them away to bed in rooms they completely fill, train our little dears to the duties and joys of motherhood. Toys! what wise child despises them? Art, fiction, the musical glasses—are they not preparing us for the time, however distant, when we shall at last be grown up?

In a maze of ways beyond the stage I lost myself, but at length, guided by voices—those penetrating, domesticated stage voices that seem to sit on your ear like a parrot—I came to a large room furnished barely with many chairs and worn settees. Some twenty to thirty ladies and gentlemen were already seated.

They were of varying ages, sizes and appearance, but all alike in having about them that impossible-to-define but impossible-to-mistake suggestion of theatricality. The men were chiefly remarkable for having no hair on their faces, but a good deal upon their heads; the ladies, one and all, were blessed with remarkably pink and white complexions and exceptionally bright eyes, as though they had been basted frequently with youth. The conversation, carried on in subdued but penetrating voices, was chiefly of “him” and “her.” Everybody appeared to be on affectionate terms with everybody else: “My dear,” “My love,” “Old girl,” “Old chappie,” Christian names—when any name at all was needed. I hesitated with the door in my hand, fearing I had stumbled upon a family gathering; but as nobody seemed disconcerted, I ventured to take a vacant seat next to an extremely small and boyish-looking gentleman and asked him if this was the room where I, an applicant for a place in the chorus of the forthcoming comic opera, ought to be waiting.

He had large, fishy eyes, with which he looked me up and down. For such a length of time he remained thus regarding me in silence that a massive gentleman sitting near, who had overheard, took it upon himself to reply in the affirmative, adding that from what he knew of Butterworth we should all of us be waiting here a damned sight longer than any gentleman should keep other ladies and gentlemen waiting for no reason at all.

“I think it exceedingly bad form,” observed the fishy-eyed gentleman in deep contralto tones, “for any gentleman to take it upon himself to reply to a remark addressed to quite another gentleman.”

“I beg your pardon,” retorted the large gentleman. “I thought you were asleep.”

“I think it very ill manners,” remarked the small gentleman, slow and impressive, “for any gentleman to tell another gentleman, who happens to be wide awake, that he thought he was asleep.”

“Sir,” returned the massive gentleman—umbrellas, like gravies, improve some men’s dignity—assuming a Johnsonian attitude with the aid of his, “I decline to alter my manners to suit your taste.”

“If you are satisfied with them,” replied the small gentleman, “I cannot help it. But I think you are making a mistake.”

A bright little woman at the other end asked, “Does anybody know what the opera is about?”

“Does anybody ever know what a comic opera is about?” replied another lady, whose face suggested experience of many plots and few explanations.

“I once asked the author,” observed a weary-looking gentleman in a corner. “His reply was: ‘Well, if you had asked me at the beginning of the rehearsals I might have been able to tell you, but damned if I could now!’”

“It wouldn’t surprise me,” said a good-looking gentleman in a velvet coat, “if there occurred somewhere a drinking chorus for male voices.”

“Possibly, if we are good,” added a thin lady with golden hair, “the heroine will confide to us her love troubles, which will interest us and excite us.”

The door at the far end opened and a name was called. An elderly lady rose and went out.

“Poor old Gertie!” said the thin lady sympathetically. “I’m told she really had a voice once.”

“When poor young Bond first came to London,” began the massive gentleman on my left, “I remember his telling me he applied to Lord Barrymore’s ‘tiger’—Alexander Lee, I mean, of course—then running the Strand Theatre, for a place in the chorus. Lee heard him sing two lines, and then jumped up. ‘Thanks, that’ll do; good morning,’ says Lee. Bond knew he’d got a good voice, so he asked Lee what was wrong. ‘What’s wrong?’ shouts Lee. ‘Do you think I hire a chorus to show up my principals?’”

“Having regard to the company present,” commented the fishy-eyed gentleman, “I consider that anecdote distinctly lacking in tact.”

The feeling of the room seemed to be with the fishy-eyed young man. The massive gentleman snorted as though he had swallowed pepper.

For the next half hour the door continued to open and close, devouring, ogre-fashion, some dainty human morsel each time—now chorus gentleman, now chorus lady. Conversation among our thinning ranks became fitful; an anxiety grew that made even the stage voices pause, as if listening to their own hearts.

At length a voice called, “Mr. Horace Moncrieff.”

In common with the rest, I glanced round languidly to see what sort of man Mr. Horace Moncrieff might be. The door was pushed open further; the voice’s owner—pale-faced, drooping moustache—put his head in and repeated impatiently the invitation to the apparently coy Moncrieff.

It suddenly occurred to me I was Mr. Horace Moncrieff.

“So glad you’ve found yourself,” said the pale-faced young man as I joined him. “Please don’t lose yourself again; we’re rather pressed for time.”

He led me across a deserted refreshment bar—one of the saddest of sights. A bar without people is like a table without bread: it tells you, not that hunger has ceased, but that something has gone wrong with the world.

We entered a room beyond. A melancholy-looking gentleman sat at the piano. Beside him stood a tall, handsome man reading rapidly from a bundle of letters. A big, burly, bored-looking gentleman was making desperate efforts to be amused at the staccato conversation of a sharp-faced, restless-eyed gentleman who never looked at the person to whom he spoke, but always at something else, as if the present were beneath his attention.

“Moncrieff?” enquired the tall, handsome man—Mr. Hodgson, the manager, as I later learned—without raising his eyes.

The pale-faced gentleman responded for me.

“Fire away,” said Mr. Hodgson.

“What is it?” asked the melancholy pianist, wearily, as though I had offered him not a song but a bill.

“‘Sally in Our Alley,’” I replied.

“What are you?” interrupted Mr. Hodgson. He still had not looked at me.

“A tenor,” I said. “Not a full tenor,” I added, remembering the O’Kelly’s instructions, as a cook remembers to warn you the dish is “not a full curry.”

“Utterly impossible to fill a tenor,” remarked the restless-eyed gentleman, looking at me but speaking to the burly man. “Ever tried?”

Everybody laughed except the melancholy pianist. Mr. Hodgson contributed a laugh without looking up from his letters. Throughout, the restless-eyed gentleman continued his humorous observations, and everybody produced the same short, sharp, mechanical laugh—like knives being sharpened in unison—except always the accompanist. The restless-eyed gentleman, I discovered, was the leading low comedian.

“Go on,” said the melancholy pianist, and began.

After the first verse Mr. Hodgson remarked, “Tell me when he’s going to begin.”

“He has a fair voice,” said my accompanist. “He’s evidently nervous.”

“There is a prejudice throughout theatrical audiences,” observed Mr. Hodgson, “in favour of a voice they can hear. That is all I am trying to impress upon him.”

So I sang the second verse in what I imagined to be the voice of a trumpet. It came out to my own ears as something closer to a kitchen funnel being tested for leaks. The burly gentleman—translator of the French libretto, though he preferred “author of the English version”—admitted he had distinctly detected a sound. The comedian suggested an announcement from the stage requesting strict silence during my part of the performance.

The sickness of fear stole over me. My voice, disappointed at the effect it had produced, retired sulkily into my boots and refused to emerge, like a child hiding under the table at Sunday dinner.

“Your voice is all right—very good,” whispered the musical conductor kindly. “They want to hear the best you can do, that’s all.”

At this my voice ran up my legs and out of my mouth, in a hurry, as if it had been promised something hot and savoury at the top.

“Thirty shillings a week, half salary for rehearsals. If that’s all right, Mr. Catchpole will give you your agreement. If not, very much obliged. Good morning,” said Mr. Hodgson, still absorbed in his correspondence.

With the pale-faced young man I retired to a desk in the corner. A few seconds sufficed for the completion of business. Leaving, I sought to catch the eye of my melancholy friend at the piano, but he appeared too sunk in dejection to notice. The restless-eyed comedian, looking at the author of the English version and addressing me as Boanerges, wished me good morning; everybody laughed; and, informed of the way out by Mr. Catchpole, I left.

The first “call” was for the following Monday at two o’clock.

I found the theatre then full of life and bustle—noise not as nature makes it (wind in trees, water over stones), but as industry makes it: feet thudding, voices cutting, a bell ringing with the insistence of a factory. The principals, just finished with their own rehearsal, talked together in a group. We of the chorus filled the centre of the stage. I noticed the lady called Gertie, and the thin lady with the golden hair. The massive gentleman and the fishy-eyed young man were again in close proximity; so long as I knew them they always were, possessed apparently of a sympathetic antipathy. The fishy-eyed young man was explaining the age at which he thought decayed chorus singers ought, in justice to themselves and the public, to retire; the massive gentleman replied with the age and size at which he thought parcels of boys ought to be learning manners across their mother’s knee.

Mr. Hodgson stood close to the footlights reading letters exactly as I had left him four days ago. My melancholy friend, the musical director, armed now with a violin and supported by about a dozen other musicians, occupied the orchestra. The adapter and the stage manager—a Frenchman whom it was good policy to mistake for a born Englishman—sat deep in confabulation at a small table under a temporary gas jet.

A quarter of an hour passed; then the stage manager, becoming suddenly in a hurry, rang a small bell furiously.

“Clear, please; all clear!” shouted a small boy with an important air suggestive of a fox terrier; and, following the others, I retreated to the wings.

The comedian and the leading lady—whom I knew well from the front, but whom I should never have recognised—severed themselves from their companions and joined Mr. Hodgson by the footlights. As a preliminary we were sorted out, according to our sizes, into loving couples.

“Ah,” said the stage manager, casting an admiring gaze upon the fishy-eyed young man—height perhaps a little over five feet two—“I have the very girl for you—a beauty!” Darting into the group of ladies, he returned with quite the biggest specimen, a lady of magnificent proportions; with the air of the virtuous uncle in melodrama he bestowed her upon the fishy-eyed young man. To the massive gentleman was given a sharp-faced little lady who at a distance appeared quite girlish. I found myself mated to the thin lady with the golden hair.

Complete at last, we took our places in the approved semi-circle, and the attenuated orchestra struck up the opening chorus.

My music—sent me by post—I had gone over with the O’Kelly, and about the notes I felt confident; but for the rest I was ill at ease, as a man is ill at ease who has been told to carve without being told which end of the bird is which.

“I am afraid,” said the thin lady, “I must ask you to put your arm round my waist. It’s very shocking, I know, but you see our salary depends upon it. Do you think you could manage it?”

I glanced into her face. A whimsical expression of fun replied to me and drove away my shyness. I carried out her instructions to the best of my ability, and discovered that there are embraces performed not out of passion but out of payroll—an odd kind of choreography, like kneading dough because the recipe says so.

The indefatigable stage manager ran in and out among us while we sang, driving this couple back a foot or so, that other forward, herding this group closer, making space through another—suggesting a sheep-dog at work, only with more profanity and less wool.

“Very good, very good indeed,” commented Mr. Hodgson at the conclusion. “We will go over it once more, and this time in tune.”

“And we will make love,” added the stage manager, “not like marionettes, but like ladies and gentlemen all alive.” Seizing the lady nearest him, he explained by object lesson how the real peasant invariably behaves under the grand passion: standing gracefully with hands clasped upon heart, head inclined at an angle of forty-five, countenance eloquent with tender adoration.

“If he expects,” remarked the massive gentleman _sotto voce_ to an experienced-looking young lady, “a performance of Romeo thrown in, I, for one, shall want an extra ten shillings a week.”

Casting the lady aside and seizing upon a gentleman, the stage manager then showed us how a village maiden should receive affectionate advances: one shoulder a trifle higher than the other, body from the waist upward gently waggling, roguish expression in the left eye.

“Ah, he’s a bit new to it,” replied the experienced young lady. “He’ll get over all that.”

Again we started.

Whether others attempted to follow directions I cannot say; my attention was centred upon the fishy-eyed young man, who did—implicitly. Soon it became apparent the whole chorus was watching him to the utter neglect of our own business. Mr. Hodgson even looked up from his letters. The orchestra played out of time. The author of the English version and the leading lady exchanged glances. Three people only did not appear to be enjoying themselves: the chief comedian, the stage manager, and the fishy-eyed young gentleman himself, who pursued his labours methodically and conscientiously, making love as though it were a duty on a list to be ticked off before tea.

There was a whispered confabulation between the leading low comedian, Mr. Hodgson and the stage manager. As a result the music ceased and the fishy-eyed young gentleman was requested to explain what he was doing.

“Only making love,” replied the fishy-eyed young gentleman.

“You were playing the fool, sir,” retorted the comedian severely.

“That is a very unkind remark,” replied the fishy-eyed young gentleman, evidently hurt, “to make to a gentleman who is doing his best.”

Mr. Hodgson behind his letters was laughing. “Poor fellow,” he murmured; “I suppose he can’t help it. Go on.”

“We are not producing a pantomime, you know,” urged our comedian.

“I want to give him a chance, poor devil,” explained Mr. Hodgson in a lower voice. “Only support of a widowed mother.”

The comedian appeared inclined to argue; but at this point Mr. Hodgson’s correspondence became absorbing again, and the fate of art was defeated by the post.

For the chorus the second act was a busy one. We opened as soldiers and vivandières, every warrior thus possessing his own private travelling bar—like a picnic that had taken a wrong turning into patriotism. The stage manager explained by example how a soldier behaves, first under stress of patriotic emotion and secondly under stress of cheap cognac, the difference being subtle: patriotism displaying itself by slaps upon the chest, cheap cognac by slaps upon the forehead.

A little later we were conspirators; the stage manager, with the help of a tablecloth, showed us how to conspire, which is to say how to hunch and whisper as though seasoning a stew with treason. Next we were a mob led by the sentimental baritone; the stage manager ruffled his hair and expounded how a mob led by a sentimental baritone would naturally behave. The act wound up with a fight. The stage manager, minus his coat, demonstrated how to fight and die, the dying being painful and dusty, necessitating much rolling about on the stage until one felt like a cutlet breaded in sawdust.

The fishy-eyed young gentleman throughout the whole of it was again the centre of attraction. Whether he solemnly slapped his chest and sang about glory, or solemnly patted his head and sang about grapes, was immaterial. He was the soldier for us. What the plot was about did not matter so long as he was in it. Who led the mob one did not care; one’s desire was to see him lead. How others fought and died was no moment; to see him slaughtered was sufficient. Whether his unconsciousness was assumed or natural I cannot say; in either case it was admirable. An earnest young man, over-anxious, if anything, to do his duty by his employers—that was the extent of the charge that could be brought against him.

Our chief comedian frowned and fumed; our stage manager despaired. Mr. Hodgson and the author of the English version, on the contrary, appeared kindly disposed. In addition to the widowed mother, Mr. Hodgson invented for him five younger brothers and sisters utterly destitute but for his earnings. To deprive so exemplary a son and brother of livelihood was not in Mr. Hodgson’s heart. Our chief comedian dissociated himself from all uncharitable feelings—would subscribe towards the young man’s subsistence out of his own pocket—his only concern being the success of the opera. The author of the English version was convinced the young man would not accept charity; had known him for years—was a most sensitive creature.

The rehearsal proceeded.

In the last act it became necessary for me to kiss the thin lady.

“I am very sorry,” said the thin lady, “but duty is duty. It has to be done.”

Again I followed directions.

The thin lady was good enough to congratulate me on my performance.
