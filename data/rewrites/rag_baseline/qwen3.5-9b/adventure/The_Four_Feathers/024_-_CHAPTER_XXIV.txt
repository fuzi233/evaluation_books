Calder regarded the landscape with a mixture of pity and disdain as the steamer cut its way down the Nile from Wadi Halfa to Assouan, marking the final stretch of his three-month furlough. To his eyes, the land was a testament to a profound, callous indifference—a country inhabited by a race equally devoid of compassion. Leaning against the iron railing of the upper deck, he gazed downward at the barge lashed tightly to the steamer's side. Upon the lower deck, amidst the throng of native passengers, rested an *angareb*—a rough bedstead of woven mats supported by four legs. Stretched upon it lay a motionless figure, entirely concealed beneath a black veil.

The burden had been hoisted aboard at Korosko just hours prior by two Arabs, who now sat in the stern of the barge, their laughter and chatter rising in sharp contrast to the silent suffering of their charge. They treated the form upon the *angareb* with the casual disregard one might afford a broken piece of driftwood; it might have been a corpse, male or female, that lay so still, yet neither man showed the slightest flicker of concern. Calder shifted his gaze, scanning the horizon where glaring sands and barren rocks rose in hard, geometric shapes reminiscent of ancient pyramids. The only sign of vitality along the riverbanks was a narrow, meager strip of green clinging desperately to the water's edge—the sole response of the Sudanese earth to the relentless heat of spring and summer. It was a harsh land, populated by a cruel people.

His eyes returned to the *angareb*. The black veil hid everything, outlining only the rigid geometry of a nose, the hollows of eye sockets, and the set line of a mouth. It was impossible to discern gender; whether the lips bore a moustache or the chin a beard remained a mystery wrapped in shadow. As the slanting sunlight advanced, creeping inexorably closer to the sleeping form, the natives nearby instinctively retreated into the shade of the upper deck, yet no one moved the *angareb*. The two men in the stern continued their frivolous conversation, utterly oblivious to the fate of their passenger. Calder watched as the golden light burned pitilessly over the black fabric, moving from the feet upward until it seared the face beneath. Still, the living creature did not stir. The veil did not flutter above parted lips; the legs remained stiff and extended; the arms lay pressed tight against the sides.

Driven by a compulsion he could not suppress, Calder shouted to the men in the stern. "Move the *angareb* into the shadow!" he commanded, his voice cutting through the humid air. "And be quick!"

The Arabs rose with reluctant grunts and shuffled the bedstead. "Is it a man or a woman?" Calder demanded.

"A man," one replied casually. "We are taking him to the hospital at Assouan, but we fear he will not survive. He fell from a palm tree three weeks ago."

"And you give him nothing to eat or drink?"

"He is too ill," the Arab dismissed.

It was a familiar tragedy, a logical conclusion born of a fatalistic worldview where life and death were merely scripts waiting to be enacted. The man lying quietly beneath the black covering had likely begun with nothing more severe than a bruise, a grievance easily cured by simple remedies within a week. Instead, he had been abandoned to the mercy of the sun and swarms of flies, unwashed, unfed, and parched. The initial bruise had festered into a sore, the sore had gangrened, and by the time the Egyptian Mudir of Korosko discovered the accident, all hope was lost. The man was dispatched downriver, a walking condemnation.

Yet, despite the familiarity of the tale, Calder could not shake the fascination the scene held for him. The absolute immobility of the sufferer on the native bedstead mesmerized him. When a strong wind sprang up near sunset, blowing against the current, Calder felt a strange, genuine comfort knowing the sick man would at least find some respite from the scorching heat. Later that evening, as his neighbor at the dinner table spoke with a distinct German accent, an impulse seized Calder.

"You are not a doctor by any chance?" he asked abruptly.

"Not a doctor," the man replied smoothly, "but a student of medicine at Bonn. I came from Cairo to witness the Second Cataract, but was denied passage beyond Wadi Halfa."

Calder interrupted him immediately. "Then I will trespass upon your holiday and claim your professional assistance."

"For yourself? With pleasure, though I should never have guessed you were ill," the student smiled, adjusting his eyeglasses.

"Nor am I," Calder corrected. "It is an Arab for whom I seek your help."

"The man on the bedstead?"

"Yes, if you will be so good. I must warn you—he was injured three weeks ago, and I know these people. No one has tended to him since the fall. The sight will not be pretty. This is not a hospitable country for neglected wounds."

"All experience is good," the German shrugged, and the two men rose from the table to join him on the upper deck. The wind had freshened during dinner, raising choppy waves that caused the steamer and its barge to toss violently, sending sheets of water crashing over the decks.

"He was below there," the student noted, leaning over the rail to peer into the gloom of the lower deck.

It was night, thick and impenetrable. Above the lower deck, only a single lamp suspended from the center of the upper deck glimmered, casting uncertain, dancing shadows over a small circle of visibility. Beyond that circle, darkness reigned absolute, save for the bow where the breaking water flung a white spray like driven snow. It sounded against the deck like the lash of a whip.

"He has been moved," the German observed.

"No doubt," Calder agreed. "There is no one in the bows."

Bending his head, Calder stared into the abyss for a moment, saying nothing. "I believe the *angareb* is there," he said finally.

"I believe it is."

Following the German, Calder hurried down the stairs to the lower deck and stepped to the side. Now he could be certain. The *angareb* stood in a wash of water precisely where it had been moved earlier that day. Upon it, the figure beneath the black covering lay as motionless as ever, an expressionless mask of lifelessness, though the cold spray broke continuously against its face.

"I thought it would be so," Calder murmured.

He retrieved a lantern and, accompanied by the German student, climbed across the bulwarks onto the swaying barge. Summoning the two Arabs, he ordered, "Move the *angareb* from the bows." Once they complied, he added, "Now remove that covering. I wish my friend, who is a doctor, to examine the wound."

The two men hesitated, then one, with an air of insolent defiance, objected. "There are doctors in Assouan, whither we are taking him."

Calder raised the lantern high and pulled the veil away from the wounded man's face himself. "Now, if you please," he said to his companion.

The German student began his examination of the wounded thigh while Calder held the lantern aloft. As predicted, it was a grim spectacle; the wound was alive, crawling with infection. The student quickly covered it up again.

"I can do nothing," he declared. "Perhaps in a hospital, with baths and dressings... relief might be given at all events. But more? I do not know. Here, I could not even begin to treat him. Do these two men understand English?"

"No," Calder answered.

"Then let me tell you something. He did not sustain this injury by falling from a palm tree. That is a lie. The wound was inflicted by the blade of a spear, or some similar weapon."

"Are you sure?"

"Yes."

Calder bent suddenly toward the Arab sitting on the *angareb*. Though the man never moved, he was conscious. Calder had been watching him intently and noticed that his eyes tracked the movement of Calder's lips.

"Do you understand English?" Calder asked.

The Arab could not answer verbally, but a look of dawning comprehension flashed across his face. "Where do you come from?" Calder pressed.

The lips attempted to move, but no whisper escaped. Yet his eyes spoke, communicating a desperate eagerness to respond. Calder knelt close beside the man's head, bringing the lantern beam down low. He enunciated the names of towns slowly.

"Dongola?" No reaction in the Arab's eyes.

"Metemneh? Berber? Omdurman?"

At the mention of the last name, the Arab's eyes closed.

"You were wounded there?" Calder asked, his tone sharpening.

"No," the Arab indicated.

"Where then? At Berber?"

"Yes," the eyes confirmed.

"You were imprisoned at Omdurman and escaped?"

"No," the eyes replied.

"Yet you were wounded."

Calder sank back onto his heels, his mind racing. Excitement surged through him. Bending forward, he lowered his voice. "Were you helping someone to escape? Yes. Who?"

"El Kaimakam Trench?"

"No."
