In the lives of families, seasons do not wait their turn. They overlap as they do in an old orchard where last year’s apples still sweeten the straw while new blossom opens and the pruning hook flashes in the same morning light. In one household, a loaf is set to rise while a candle is set to burn low; in the same hour a hand sprinkles seed and another measures out wine. So it was now: Daniel’s new joy and Ezra’s nearing end moved together, braided like two scents in one room—the warmth of bread and the clean, sharp tang of crushed herbs.

Among love’s gifts there is hardly one more tender than the wish to keep watch over the beloved’s happiness, to put gentle order where hardship had ruled, to lay a cloth over old hunger and make it a table. Daniel’s love for Mirah had always carried that guarding sweetness. Even her earliest steps had been among thorns; the first time he had seen her face it had been, to him, the very likeness of a girl who had tasted despair as if it were bitter rind. Now she shone with that quiet change which is not loud happiness but a settled, daily comfort—like a pale flower tipped with dark, made lovelier by the sun because it is no longer beaten by wind.

He watched the new grace in her movements as if it were a music he had been allowed to keep close to his ear: the hush of her dress, the soft click of a needle, the low breath she drew when she leaned over Ezra’s chair to smooth his pillow, the way her hands had learned peace. It was enough of personal joy, he thought, simply to have brought her to this—saved from pain, not by spectacle, but by the steady custom of tenderness.

Mirah knew nothing of Hans’s struggle, nothing of Gwendolen’s sharp inward pang. Once Daniel had made it plain that the love he had hidden was for Mirah, she could easily believe that Gwendolen’s eager solicitude had been only gratitude and reliance—the sort of clinging to goodness which Mirah herself had known when she was saved from drowning. Daniel’s few distant words about Mrs. Grandcourt confirmed that harmless interpretation; he never spoke of it otherwise. Mirah, whose heart had been schooled by want into reverence for kindness, was ready to think that Daniel had been an angel of rescue to many besides herself. The wonder, always, was that she—she—should have the continual blessedness of being at his side.

So when the bridal veil went round Mirah, it covered no tremulous doubt, only the awe that comes with receiving a gift too great to be worn idly. The canopy above them—velvet, deep as a summer plum—sheltered no fairer bride and bridegroom. Truthful lips touched the marriage-wine; the blessing fell with a weight of promise because their pledge had integrity in it, not mere fervor.

They were married, naturally, according to the Jewish rite. And since no religion has yet decreed that feasting must be a narrow performance for rank alone, it will offend few to hear that among the guests at Daniel’s small wedding meal was the whole Cohen family—save the baby, who stayed at home conducting her teething with intelligent perseverance. How could Ezra have borne to shut out those companions of his adversity from rejoicing with him? Mrs. Meyrick understood that so entirely that she had reconciled herself, with a brave domestic cheerfulness, to meeting the Jewish pawnbroker, and she came with her three daughters. Each of them carried, as women do, the private satisfaction of a romance in which their own hands had had their share: there was no denying it—each, mother and girls, had given the best she had in feeling and in act to a creature who needed both.

If Hans could have been there, it would have been better still; but Mab had already pronounced, with her quick little tyranny of common sense, that men must suffer for being so inconvenient. Suppose she, Kate, and Amy had all fallen in love with Mr. Deronda?—being women, they would not have been so ridiculous. Their prejudice, conquered, earned its reward in a speech from Mr. Cohen which had the rare merit among speeches of not quite following the usual pattern. There was more plain human savor in it than ornament—like a broth honestly made from bones and root vegetables, not dressed up to imitate something foreign.

The meal itself, though humble, had its own abundance of feeling, and feeling gives flavor where costliness cannot. There were the familiar domestic sounds—plates set down, a cork eased from a bottle with a soft sigh, the small clink of cutlery, the murmur of voices that never rose to the harsh edge of display. Outside, the street had its ordinary racket; yet within, it seemed held at bay by a little island of attentive quiet. Mirah listened as if the very air mattered, as if a room’s peace were a kind of mercy. Modern noise has a way of making even joy thin and restless; but here, for once, sound belonged to the people who cared for one another: the laughter that did not bruise, the praise that did not clamor, the silence that was not empty.

Jacob ate beyond his years, as if he intended to keep pace with the occasion, and punctuated his father’s words with several small whinnying laughs—not irreverently, but out of lively pride that his family was distinguishing itself. Adelaide Rebekah, in a new Sabbath frock, held a grave air of responsibility all through, as if she were personally accountable for the dignity of the day.

Ezra’s brilliant eyes, deep in their large sockets, rested on the scene with the cherishing benignancy of a spirit already lifting itself into an aloofness that nullified only selfish requirement and left sympathy alive. His gaze traveled over faces and returned again and again to Daniel with a fresh gleam of trusting affection, as if the look itself were a bequest.

The wedding feast was modest; the gifts, by contrast, had their splendor. As soon as the betrothal was known, friends began those graceful contrivances by which affection tries to take visible shape. Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger had taken pains to provide a complete equipment for Eastern travel—things chosen with that mixture of worldly knowledge and kind intent which makes generosity practical: what would endure heat, what would pack, what would not fail at a seam. With it came a precious locket, and inside it an inscription that struck like warm light on the heart: “To the bride of our dear Daniel Deronda all blessings. H. and L. M.”

The Klesmers sent a watch, perfect in workmanship, with its own pretty inscription—time measured, not as a threat, but as something meant to be kept faithfully.

Yet something more precious than gold or gem came to Daniel from the neighborhood of Diplow on the morning of his marriage. It was only paper and ink, but it carried a life in it, and the small strokes of a dominant language strained to hold what was almost unholdable: a private experience no vocabulary can fully translate from one soul to another. The letter said—

Do not think of me sorrowfully on your wedding-day. I have remembered your words—that I may live to be one of the best of women, who make others glad that they were born. I do not yet see how that can be, but you know better than I. If it ever comes true, it will be because you helped me. I only thought of myself, and I made you grieve. It hurts me now to think of your grief. You must not grieve any more for me. It is better—it shall be better with me because I have known you.

GWENDOLEN GRANDCOURT.

Daniel read it with a heaviness that was not regret for his own fate, but a sober knowledge of how power works between people—how one person’s voice, by accident or by care, can become another’s law; how a name, a phrase, a promise in the dominant tongue can bind like iron, while the unsaid—what cannot be phrased—bleeds inward. He did not show the letter to Mirah at once. He folded it, feeling the paper’s slight resistance under his fingers, and carried it as one carries a hot coal: carefully, without display.

The preparations for departure began at once. All three—Daniel, Mirah, and Ezra—were to go eastward; and Daniel could not deny Ezra’s wish that they should set out on the voyage forthwith, so that he might go with them, instead of detaining them to watch over him. Daniel had no belief that Ezra’s life would last through the voyage. There were signs—subtle to the untrained eye, unmistakable to loving attention: the way breath shortened as if the air had thickened, the pallor that no warm drink could mend, the peculiar stillness that comes when the body begins to withdraw from ordinary appetite. Yet Ezra had said, with the calm insistence of a man who has long been living on something other than food, “Never mind where I die, so that I am with you.”

But he did not set out with them.

One morning, early, when the house was not yet fully awake and the outside world had begun its daily grind—wheels over stone, distant hammering, the shrill insistence of commerce—Ezra said to Daniel, “Do not quit me to-day. I shall die before it is ended.”

He chose to be dressed and sit up in his easy chair as usual. Daniel and Mirah took their places on either side of him. For some hours he was unusually silent, not even making the effort to speak. He looked at them now and then with eyes full of a restful meaning, as if to assure them that although this remnant of breathing-time was difficult, he felt an ocean of peace beneath him.

The room held its small household sounds—the faint settling of coals, a curtain’s soft stir, Mirah’s careful movement when she adjusted a shawl—each one distinct, not drowned. It seemed as if Ezra’s soul, already loosening, was exquisitely aware of what it meant to be sheltered from needless noise. The great outside roar of modern life—its iron, its haste, its indifferent din—could not follow him into this intimate circle. Here, the soundscape was made by love: the quiet that allowed breath to be heard, the living pulse of another’s presence.

It was not until late afternoon, when the light began to fall and the colors of the room deepened as if the day were steeping like tea, that Ezra took one hand of each in his. He looked at Daniel and said, slowly, with the effort of one passing a treasure from mouth to mouth,

“Death is coming to me as the divine kiss which is both parting and reunion—which takes me from your bodily eyes and gives me full presence in your soul. Where thou goest, Daniel, I shall go. Is it not begun? Have I not breathed my soul into you? We shall live together.”

He paused. Daniel waited, believing there might be another word meant for him alone, something like a last instruction, a last tender demand. But Ezra, pressing their hands, raised himself slowly and with effort, and uttered in Hebrew the confession of the divine Unity—words that for generations have been on the lips of the dying Israelite, carrying a cultural memory that refuses to be swallowed by the surrounding tongues, a faith that endures not by ornament but by repetition from mouth to mouth, breath to breath.

Then he sank back gently into his chair and did not speak again.

Still, it was some hours before he ceased to breathe, with Daniel’s and Mirah’s arms around him—holding him as one holds what cannot be kept, giving the body the last warmth it can receive while the spirit goes where language cannot follow. There was nothing here for tears as for a catastrophe, nothing to wail over as if this were failure. It was a death noble in its quietness, and in the love that made the room itself—light, air, sound—fit to receive it.
