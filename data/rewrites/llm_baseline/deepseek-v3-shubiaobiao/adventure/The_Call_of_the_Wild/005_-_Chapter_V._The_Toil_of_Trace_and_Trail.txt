The Salt Water Mail team, with Buck at its lead, stumbled into Skaguay after thirty brutal days on the trail. They were hollow-eyed specters of their former selves—bones barely held together by exhaustion. Buck’s powerful frame had withered from one hundred forty pounds to one hundred fifteen. The others fared worse: Pike, the sly shirker, now limped in earnest; Sol-leks dragged his injured leg; Dub’s shoulder-blade screamed with every step.

Their paws were raw, their muscles liquid lead. This wasn’t the weariness of a single hard day’s labor—this was the slow, grinding depletion of months. Twenty-five hundred miles in less than five months, with only five days’ rest in the last eighteen hundred. They were spent, down to the marrow.

"Keep movin’, you poor beat-up devils," the driver coaxed as they swayed down Skaguay’s muddy thoroughfare. "Almost there. Then—long rest. A *real* one."

But rest never came.

Three days later, two greenhorns bought the team for pocket change. Hal, a brash boy with a revolver strapped to his hip like a toy, and Charles, a watery-eyed man whose mustache seemed the only firm thing about him, fumbled through the transaction. Their camp was a disaster: half-pitched tent, filthy dishes, chaos. And then there was Mercedes—Charles’s wife, Hal’s sister—a woman who fluttered like a trapped bird, offering useless advice while the men botched packing the sled.

Neighboring trappers watched, smirking.

"That load’s top-heavy as a drunk on stilts," one remarked. "Ditch the tent."

Mercedes gasped. "*No tent?* How *dare* you suggest—"

Charles and Hal ignored them, lashing the wobbling monstrosity together. When the dogs strained, the sled didn’t budge. Hal raised his whip—

"*Don’t!*" Mercedes wrenched it from him. "They’re *trying*!"

A bystander spat. "Runners are frozen. Break ’em loose or you’ll kill your team."

Hal heaved against the sled. It lurched forward—then capsized on the first turn, scattering supplies across Skaguay. The dogs, furious and starved, bolted. Buck led the charge, the sled bouncing behind like a dying beast.

---

The trio repacked, drowning in advice: *Half the load. Double the dogs.* They scoffed. Instead, they bought six mangy Outsiders—pointers, a Newfoundland, mongrels—useless creatures who cowered under Buck’s glare. Fourteen dogs now, a ludicrous parade. Hal beamed. Mercedes nodded sagely. None understood: one sled couldn’t carry enough food for fourteen mouths.

The journey was a slow-motion collapse.

They bungled every step—slack ropes, misplaced loads, camps pitched in darkness. The dogs starved as Hal overfed then underfed, his arithmetic crumbling. The Outsiders died first, their stomachs rejecting frozen horse-hide traded for Hal’s revolver. Dub was shot. Billee’s skull met the axe. Koona vanished into the snow.

Misery stripped the humans bare. Mercedes, who’d never lifted a finger in her life, now rode the sled, her 120 pounds crushing the team. When they dragged her off, she went limp, forcing them to carry her. Arguments raged—over firewood, over ancestors, over art. The trail’s cruelty had peeled them down to raw, snapping nerves.

---

By the end, only five dogs remained: Joe, hollow-eyed; Pike, limping; Sol-leks, blind and loyal; Teek, beaten for being fresher; and Buck, a ghost of himself, moving by instinct alone.

Spring bloomed around them—willows greening, ice cracking on the Yukon—but they were corpses in motion. The world sang with life; they staggered toward death.

And still, the whip fell.
