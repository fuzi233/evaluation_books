It seemed strange that Arthur Pendennis, usually so open and frank with his friend Warrington, withheld the details of his recent escapades at the villa near Tunbridge Wells. He freely discussed the rediscovery of his old tutor, Smirke, his new wife, the peculiarities of his Anglo-Norman church, and his journey from Clapham to Rome. Yet, whenever the subject turned to Blanche, his responses became evasive. He dismissed her as merely "a good-natured clever little thing," suggesting that with proper guidance she might not turn out such a disastrous wife after all. He declared his romantic days over, content with his current state, and generally avoided committing himself.

Meanwhile, however, the truth was arriving at Lamb Court in the form of elegant satin envelopes, penned in neat script and sealed with a cryptic cipher. If Warrington had possessed the curiosity to inspect his friend's mail—or if the code had been decipherable—he would have discovered that Arthur was corresponding with a young lady whose initials were B.A. In return, Mr. Pen replied with his customary gallantry, mixing town gossip, witty remarks, and even original verse in response to the rhyming efforts of Blanche's muse, 'Mes Larmes.' After all, since Blanche rhymes with words like "branch" and "launch," a man of Pen's ingenuity would surely exploit such poetic advantages. Indeed, it is highly probable that the love poems which later achieved such fame in *The Roseleaves*—that charming annual edited by Lady Violet Lebas and illustrated by the renowned artist Pinkney—were originally composed during this period as private missives to Blanche before ever appearing in print.

"Don't let your head spin on poetry," advised the elder Pendennis, watching his nephew scratch out one of these innocent verses at the club while awaiting dinner. "Letter-writing is fine if Mother permits it, especially with old country friends, but beware, Pen. Don't tie yourself down. Who knows what mischief might ensue? My advice is to keep your letters safe. I've never written a letter in my life that committed me, and *demmy*, sir, I have considerable experience with women." The Major, growing increasingly garrulous as he aged, regaled his nephew with harrowing tales of societal disasters caused by a lack of caution. He recounted how young Spoony, after sending overly ardent verses to the Widow Naylor, was forced by her brother, Colonel Flint, into a marriage with a woman old enough to be his mother. Similarly, he detailed how Hopwood of the Blues, having secured Louisa Salter, was exposed by letters she had written to him, leading to his withdrawal and subsequent union with Miss Stickney of Lyme Regis. The Major, lacking books but rich in worldly observation, backed his wisdom with countless modern examples drawn from the great book of the world.

Pen laughed at the anecdotes, blushing slightly at his uncle's warnings, and promised to be cautious. He likely blushed because he already was; instinctively, or perhaps driven by honesty, he had refrained in his letters to Miss Blanche from any declarations that could compromise him. "Remember the lesson I learned in Lady Mirabel's regarding Miss Fotheringay's affair?" Arthur said with mock humility. "I shall not be caught twice, Uncle." Old Pendennis congratulated himself and his nephew on this newfound prudence, pleased to see Arthur maturing into a man of the world.

Had Warrington been consulted, his verdict would undoubtedly have differed. He would have argued that the boy's foolish letters were superior to the man's slick compliments and slippery gallantries. To truly win the woman one loves, only a knave or a coward hides behind subterfuge and secures a retreat. Yet, Pen knew his friend was guilty of such tactics and wisely chose not to discuss the matter with him.

Just as fate began to weigh heavily upon the remaining partner of the firm at Shepherd's Inn, Colonel Altamont returned from his foreign tour, and Sir Francis Clavering retired to the countryside. Captain Strong, however, faced sudden misfortune. Having refused a generous loan from Altamont earlier—a refusal born of excessive delicacy and conscience—Strong now regretted his scruples bitterly. Why deny a thirsty man a pitcher of water simply because it was slightly soiled? His conscience gnawed at him as he realized that Altamont's cash would have been safer in his pocket than in the hands of gambling den owners in Baden or Ems. Rumors spread quickly among tradesmen and bill-discounters that Strong and the Baronet had fallen out, rendering Strong's credit worthless. Once trusted for his jolly demeanor, Strong was now besieged. Tailors, bootmakers, and pastry cooks descended upon his chambers, followed closely by the silent, dangerous threat of young clerks carrying writs demanding his appearance before the Queen.

Cornered within his fortress, Strong shut the doors against the creditors. While the foes cursed outside, the Chevalier peered from behind a curtain covering his letterbox, enjoying the sight of furious debtors retreating in defeat. Though they could not guard his gates twenty-four hours a day, the siege was relentless. Fortunately, Strong had allies. Friends like Huxter and Miss Fanny Bolton maintained a supply line via private signals. When hostile visitors prowled, Fanny's younger sisters would emit a distinctive whoop from the courtyard. Upon hearing this signal, Fanny and Huxter would approach, singing the same note, prompting Strong's loyal garrison to emerge, provisions and ale in tow, allowing the beleaguered captain to pass a comfortable night. Strong, a brave soldier who had never lost heart in peril, thrived on this excitement.

Beyond his allies, Strong had secured a vital asset: a retreat. Years ago, he had discovered a leaden water-pipe connecting his kitchen window to the room of his neighbors, Messrs. Costigan and Bows. Laughingly demonstrating this shortcut to Altamont, they had agreed to keep the secret from Costigan, whose own debts were numerous. Now, in times of trouble, Strong utilized this passage. One afternoon, bursting into Bows and Costigan's apartment with his characteristic jolliness, he explained that the enemy was blocking his stairs and that he had escaped via the pipe. Thus, while the sheriff's officers waited in the hallway of No. 3, Strong descended the steps of No. 4, dined at the Albion, attended the theater, and returned home at midnight, baffling Mrs. Bolton and Fanny, who could not imagine how he had bypassed the sentries.

For weeks, Strong endured this siege with admirable spirit, though the pain and privation tested even a veteran's resolve. What truly riled him was the cold indifference of Clavering, to whom he wrote repeatedly yet received not a single acknowledgment or remittance. A five-pound note, Strong noted, would have been a fortune at that time.

However, better days were soon in store. Amidst his despair, a welcome aid arrived. "If it hadn't been for this good fellow here," Strong exclaimed, referring to Altamont, "Pendennis, I think it would have been all up with Ned Strong. I was in my fifth week of captivity, unable to risk crossing the pipe daily, and my spirit was broken. I considered ending it all when—who should descend from heaven but Altamont!"

"Heaven isn't exactly the location, Ned," Altamont corrected cheerfully. "I came from Baden-Baden, where I'd had a remarkably lucky month." True to his word, Altamont settled Marks's bill and paid off the other creditors. "I'll buy a magnum of claret for this company," the Colonel announced, blushing slightly. "And may every good fellow like Strong find another good fellow to stand by him at a pinch. That's my sentiment, Mr. Pendennis, though I don't care for your name."

"No! And why?" asked Arthur.

Strong pressed the Colonel's foot under the table. Excited, Altamont refilled his glass, nodded to Pen, drained his wine, and declared, "He was a gentleman, and that was sufficient; they were all gentlemen."

This gathering of "gentlemen" occurred at Richmond, where Pendennis had gone to dine. Finding the Chevalier and his friend hilariously intoxicated in the coffee room, Strong launched into a vivid account of his siege, the signals of Fanny, Costigan's groans, and his dramatic rescue by Altamont.

"As for me, it's nothing," Altamont boasted. "When a ship is paid off, a chap spends his money. The fellows at the black and red at Baden-Baden did it. I won a good sum there and intend to win more. I'm taking someone with me—I've got a system. I'll make fortunes, yours included. But seriously, I'm going to pursue that little Fanny. Did you know she lent two pounds to Ned Strong? Let's drink her health."

"With all my heart," Arthur pledged cordially.

Altamont then unveiled his "infallible" gambling system, claiming it originated from a man at Baden who had lost only due to insufficient capital. He planned to establish a bank, investing everything he owned, and intended to take Strong along as his aide-de-camp, trusting his temperament more than his own or that of his partners, Bloundell-Bloundell and an Italian named "Stand-in." As he emptied his bottle, the Colonel outlined his grandiose plans to Pen, who listened with interest to the confessions of his daring and lawless humor.
