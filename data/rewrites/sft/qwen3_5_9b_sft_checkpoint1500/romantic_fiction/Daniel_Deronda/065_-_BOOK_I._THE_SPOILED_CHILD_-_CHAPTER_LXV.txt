"O, welcome, pure-eyed Faith, white-handed Hope, Thou hovering angel, girt with golden wings!"

Deronda did not answer Gwendolen's call without a flutter of unease within him. It was not pride that stirred him, but a sharp, piercing sympathy. He feared deeply that another's heart might demand more of him than he could possibly give. To deny the truth of her love, he realized with a sudden, heavy clarity, was no longer an intellectual exercise; it was a visceral awareness. Gwendolen's soul clung to his with a desperate, consuming need. One does not debate the anger or the disdain that vibrates through a stranger's voice; one simply feels it, undeniable and absolute. Deronda felt a young woman's fate hanging over him, poised on the edge of a chasm of despair. Anyone who knew him well would not marvel at his silent admission: if this tragedy had unfolded but a year prior, he would scarcely have paused to question whether he loved her. The driving force within him would have been immediate—to shield her from sorrow, to build a sanctuary for her life forever, to complete the rescue he had begun with the necklace.

But now, the chains of love and duty bound him differently. That primal impulse remained, yes, but it was tempered by a compassionate ache, a trembling at the thought of meeting her eyes and hearing her pleas again and again. Precisely because he held firm to the resolve that kept him apart, his pity for her lonely lot grew sharper, more stinging.

He waited for her in the back drawing-room—a space of white and crimson where they had once sat amidst music, where she had whispered that her future rested on his never leaving her, and her plea had seemed to dissolve into a song of *Per pietà non darmi addio*. Yet, that melody had belonged to Mirah's voice.

Deronda paced this familiar room, feeling a profound metamorphosis in his own life. The objects around him—from Lady Mallinger's gentle portrait to the lion heads on the chimney-piece—seemed to belong to a past existence, ghosts of a time he was merely visiting through memory rather than living in reality. The impressions he had recently endured were so deep, the conditions of his life so transformed, that the house, once a home, now felt alien. He stood with his hat in hand, awaiting the entrance of a young creature whose life was undergoing a tragic shift toward an uncertain end, a shift in which he felt his actions still dangerously entangled.

Gwendolen entered, altered. Not only by her mourning dress, but by a quiet, settled calm that he had not seen on her face since Genoa. Her contentment lay in his presence alone. There was no smile when they met, no warmth in their clasping hands; both were heavy with remembrance, burdened by anxious foreboding. "It was good of you to come," she said immediately, sitting down in the nearest chair. "Let us sit." He took the seat opposite her.

"I asked you to come because I need you to tell me what I ought to do," she began at once. "Do not hesitate to tell me what you think is right, even if it sounds harsh. I have decided to do it. Once, I was terrified of poverty; I could not bear the thought of serving others, and that is why I married. I have borne far worse things now. I believe I could endure poverty if you think it necessary. Do you know about my husband's will?"

"Yes, Sir Hugo informed me," Deronda replied, anticipating her question.

"Should I accept anything he left me?" Gwendolen asked, her voice tightening with nervous urgency. "I want to tell you what I've been thinking. Perhaps you don't realize how much I truly considered my mother when I married. I was selfish, yes, but I loved her, and I felt deeply about her poverty. The greatest comfort I found, when I was miserable, was knowing she was better off because of my marriage. Now, the hardest thing for me would be to see her in poverty again. I've been thinking... if I take enough to support her, and nothing for myself, it wouldn't be wrong. She was precious to me, and he took me from her, and he meant..."

Gwendolen broke off. She had prepared for this meeting by focusing solely on this question of right toward her mother, but the inquiry dragged up thoughts and reasons she could not articulate. Perilous memories swarmed between her words, agitating her speech until it trembled. She looked down helplessly at her hands, now bare of every ring save her wedding band.

"Do not harm yourself by speaking of that," Deronda said softly. "There is no need; the case is straightforward. I doubt I could judge wrongly. You seek my counsel because I am the only one to whom you have confessed the most painful parts of your experience, and I understand your scruples." He paused, allowing her to gather herself. The silence felt thick with the tenderness in his voice, giving her the courage to lift her eyes and look at him as he continued. "You are conscious of something you feel to be a sin against someone who is dead. You believe you have forfeited all rights as a wife. You shrink from accepting what belongs to his estate. You desire to punish yourself, to scourge the self that yielded to temptation rather than following your higher will. I have known this struggle myself. Do I understand you?"

"Yes—I want to be good. Not like what I have been," Gwendolen replied. "I will try to bear whatever you think I must. I have tried to show you the worst of myself. What ought I to do?"

"If no one else were involved," Deronda said, "I would not dare urge you against your remorseful impulses. However, I am guided now by your feelings regarding Mrs. Davilow, which seem to me entirely just. I cannot believe your husband's obligations to you are voided by your actions. He voluntarily stepped into your life and shaped its course in the most significant ways. Setting that aside, it was his duty, given his position, to provide for your mother, and he surely understood that if this will took effect, she would share the provision made for you."

"She has received eight hundred a year," Gwendolen noted. "What I hoped to do was take that amount and leave the rest." Having argued internally for so long that this was a permissible path, her mind could not instantly shift gears.

"I do not think it is your duty to set such limits," Deronda countered gently. "You would create a painful mystery for Mrs. Davilow; an income from which you exclude yourself would inevitably bitter her. Furthermore, your own path would become unnecessarily difficult. At Genoa, we agreed that the burden on your conscience is one no one should be forced to acknowledge. The future goodness of your life will be best served by sparing everyone else the pain of knowing this. In my judgment, you should simply follow the terms of your husband's will, letting your remorse influence only how you choose to live with your financial freedom."

As he spoke the final sentence, Deronda instinctively reached for the hat resting on the floor beside him. Gwendolen, acutely sensitive to his slightest movements, felt her heart leap as if it possessed a consciousness of its own, warning him not to depart. Simultaneously, she rose from her chair, forgetting to consider that his motion signaled his intent to leave. Deronda, of course, rose and stepped forward slightly.

"I will do what you tell me," Gwendonen said quickly. "But what else shall I do?" Those were the only simple words possible to her, and even they were too heavy for a spirit where her proud secrecy had crumbled. As the childish sentences fell from her lips, they struck her like a mirror of her own helplessness, and she could not stifle the sob that brought large tears to her eyes. Deronda, too, felt a crushing pain, though imminent consequences weighed heavily on his conscience. When she brushed away her tears, he asked in a gentle, questioning tone, "You will probably be traveling with Mrs. Davilow into the country soon."

"Yes, in a week or ten days." Gwendolen hesitated, turning her eyes vaguely toward the window as if gazing at some imaginary prospect. "I want to be kind to them all—they can be happier than I am. Is that the best I can do?"

"I think so. It is a duty beyond doubt," Deronda affirmed. He paused, feeling a weight of anxiety settle over his words. "Other duties will arise from this. Viewing your life as a debt may seem the dreariest perspective from afar, but it is not truly so. What makes life bleak is the lack of motive. Once you begin acting with this penitent, loving purpose you hold in your mind, you will find unexpected satisfactions. New needs will constantly emerge to carry you forward, day by day. Your life will grow like a plant."

Gwendolen turned her eyes to him with the look of one thirsting for unseen water. Deronda felt the gaze as if she were stretching her arms toward him from a deserted shore.
