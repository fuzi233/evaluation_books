Were the heaviness of a corrupted conscience measured by the spoilage of a harvest, human history would read as a ledger of blighted orchards and soured wines, where the contrivers of greedy wars and the marauders of the money-market stand alongside the petty cut-purse in a troop of self-lacerating penitents. Wickedness, undoubtedly, distributes its own hollow rewards; but he who wins in this devil’s game must needs be baser, more brutal than the deep, organic rhythms of this planet will allow. The multitude born of woman carries a form of conscience—a fear that is the shadow of justice, a pity that is the shadow of love—which preserves the moral palate from the utter deadness of serene cruelty, a state difficult to maintain in the complex, breathing terroir of our composite flesh. 

On the twenty-ninth of December, Deronda knew that the Grandcourts had arrived at the Abbey, though he had caught no glimpse of them before retiring to dress for dinner. Outside, a splendid fall of snow had blanketed the ancient grounds, muffling the sharp, discordant noises of the modern world and returning the estate to a pristine, acoustic purity. The Mallinger girls had spent the afternoon immersed in the rare, tactile pleasure of snow-building, demanding the architectural guidance of their “cousin.” After this bracing outdoor exertion, breathing air as crisp as snapped celery, Deronda had passed the hours playing billiards, the satisfying, geometric clack of ivory spheres masking any conscious dwell upon his impending reunion with Gwendolen. 

Yet, when he retreated to his room before the half-hour bell, the prospect of her presence began to steep in his thoughts like a complex infusion. He speculated on the influence her marriage to Grandcourt would yield, anticipating discernible shifts in her manner since their encounter at Diplow, much like the maturation—or curdling—of a volatile ferment since he first observed her at Leubronn. 

“I fancy there are some natures one could see growing or degenerating every day, if one watched them,” he mused, splashing his face with water that held the faint, metallic memory of the estate’s artesian wells. “She is a creature who keeps strong traces of anything that has once impressed her. That affair of the necklace, the realization that her gambling was spiritually bankrupt, evidently bit into her like a sharp acid. But such impressibility may drive one to desperation as easily as to refinement. And Grandcourt—good heavens! Who can believe that man, whose palate for life is entirely deadened, could draw forth tender affections? One might be tempted to horsewhip him merely to provoke some raw, unadulterated flavor of passion into his face. I fear she married him to escape poverty, hanging her blind, starving expectations on that remnant of a human being.”

Deronda’s notion of Grandcourt as a “remnant” stemmed from a profound sensory aversion; the man interacted with the world as a purely utilitarian construct, chewing through life without tasting it, having long ago exhausted any healthy, agrarian connection to the earth or its inhabitants. For Gwendolen—young, headlong, eager for a feast of flattery—how quickly might expectancy turn to the bitter, ashen taste of the irremediable! 

Downstairs, a varied party had assembled to meet the new couple, bringing their own distinct sociological dialects to the drawing-room. The old aristocracy was represented by Lord and Lady Pentreath; the old gentry by the Worcestershire Fitzadams; while politics and the public good—specifically the rich, loamy heritage of the cider interest—were embodied by Mr. Fenn, member for West Orchards, and his daughters. Lady Mallinger’s brother, Mr. Raymond, and his wife added familial warmth, while the bachelor element was supplied by Mr. Sinker, an eminent counsel, and Mr. Vandernoodt, an acquaintance from Leubronn. 

The scene possessed a mythic, circular gravity. Full-length portraits of ancestors gazed from deep cedar paneling, the wood exuding a resinous, olfactory history of centuries past. The ceiling glowed with heraldic colors, illuminated by the primal red fire of burning oak-boughs and pale wax-lights. The acoustic ecology of the room was masterful: the deep-piled carpets and the high English breeding subdued all voices into a harmonious, murmuring brook, saving the space from the grating noise pollution of lesser assemblies. Lady Mallinger, carrying a tiny white dog, moved with matronly grace, while Deronda found himself momentarily trapped in the conversational orbit of Mr. Vandernoodt.

Vandernoodt was an industrious gleaner of superficial details, a man who spoke the dominant, commercialized lexicon of society gossip. His voice lacked any resonant depth, clattering like cheap tin as he discussed the awaited bridegroom. 

“Men who have seen a good deal of life don’t always end by choosing their wives so well,” Vandernoodt buzzed, his words flattening the complex human experience into trite anecdote. “He has gone rather deep into pleasures, lazy as he is. But you know all about him.”

“No, really,” Deronda replied, his tone an indifferent shield against this auditory intrusion. “I know little more of him than that he is Sir Hugo’s nephew.”

The heavy oak door opened, sparing Deronda further chatter. Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt entered, projecting an undeniable, chilling distinction. Grandcourt’s impassibility of face was as perfectly cut and devoid of organic warmth as ever; he demanded the finest outward equipment, wife included. But it was Gwendolen who commanded the room's atmosphere. She wore white silk and a cascade of diamonds that flashed at her neck, ears, and hair. To Deronda, her beauty possessed a new, imposing hardness, striking him with the force of an original, albeit startling, work of art. Yet, as he watched her receive greetings with a proud, cold quietude and a superficial smile, he detected a dissonant frequency. The same demonic force that had gripped her at the Leubronn roulette table seemed to vibrate beneath her silk—a profound, untranslatable subjective suffering trapped within the rigid syntax of her new social elevation.

At dinner, Deronda sat nearly opposite her in the Abbey’s grand dining room, originally the monastic refectory. The air was thick with the scent of roasted meats, the earthiness of ancient root vegetables, and the complex reductions of solitary kitchen artistry—a culinary heritage stretching back to the Benedictines. Yet, Gwendolen seemed entirely detached from the sensory profoundness of the sustenance before her, viewing the meal merely as a stage for survival. 

Sir Hugo, serving as a steward of both the estate’s history and its lively conversation, directed his booming, jovial voice toward Gwendolen. “Deronda, you will like to hear what Mrs. Grandcourt tells me about your favorite Klesmer.”

Gwendolen’s eyelids, previously lowered in defensive stillness, fluttered. Deronda caught a quivering reluctance as she met his unembarrassed bow with a smile that failed to reach the eyes—a mere mechanical gesture of the lip. 

“The Arrowpoints have condoned the marriage,” Sir Hugo continued, carving into a beautifully aged roast, “and he is spending Christmas with his bride at Quetcham.”

“I suppose he will be glad of it for the sake of his wife,” Deronda noted, appreciating Klesmer’s devotion to genuine artistic and emotional resonance. 

“It’s a sort of troubadour story,” rumbled Lady Pentreath in her deep, aged voice. “I’m glad to find a little romance left among us.”

“It shows the Arrowpoints’ good sense after the fuss in the paper,” said Sir Hugo. “Disowning your child because of a *mésalliance* is like disowning your own eye.”

“As to *mésalliance*, there’s no blood on any side,” Lady Pentreath countered, dismantling the dominant lexicon of class with historical precision. “Old Admiral Arrowpoint was a doctor’s son. We all know how the mother’s money came.”

“If there were any *mésalliance*,” Deronda interjected softly, defending the sanctity of Klesmer’s pure, acoustic genius against societal commodification, “I should say it was on Klesmer’s side.”

“Ah, you think it is a case of the immortal marrying the mortal,” Sir Hugo said, looking at Gwendolen. “What is your opinion?”

“I have no doubt Herr Klesmer thinks himself immortal,” Gwendolen replied, her composure artificially restored. “But I dare say his wife will burn as much incense before him as he requires.”

“Don’t you approve of a wife burning incense before her husband?” Sir Hugo joked.

“Oh, yes,” she said with a sudden, brittle gayety. “If it were only to make others believe in him. When Herr Klesmer admires his own genius, it will take off some of the absurdity if his wife says Amen.”

Her sudden seriousness carried a bitterness that Deronda recognized as the agonizing untranslatability of her own reality. She harbored a secret resentment against Klesmer, adopting the harsh, cynical dialect of the world to mask a profound internal starvation. 

Seeking to lighten the atmosphere, Sir Hugo swept his hand toward the vaulted ceiling. “Is not this a beautiful room? It was part of the refectory of the Abbey. There used to be rows of Benedictines sitting exactly where we are, breaking their daily bread in silence. Suppose we were suddenly to see the lights burning low, and the ghosts of the old monks rising behind our chairs!”

“Please don’t!” Gwendolen said with a playful shudder. “It is very nice to come after ancestors and monks, but they should keep underground. I suppose the old generations must be angry with us because we have altered things so much.”

“Oh, the ghosts must be of all political parties,” Sir Hugo laughed. “But you and Grandcourt ought to see it all. We will ask Deronda to go round with us. He is more learned about the estate's history than I am.”

As Deronda carefully served himself an *entrée* of braised partridge swimming in a dark, thyme-scented jus, he remained impassive. But Gwendolen, painfully aware that this magnificent, ancestral home might have been Deronda’s had history been written in another hand, felt a rush of awkwardness. 

With her usual readiness of resource against betrayal, she said, “You don’t know how much I am afraid of Mr. Deronda.”

“How’s that? Because you think him too learned?” asked Sir Hugo.

“No. It is ever since I first saw him at Leubronn. When he came to look on at the roulette-table, I began to lose. He cast an evil eye on my play. And now whatever I do before him, I am afraid he will cast an evil eye upon it.”

Sir Hugo chuckled, a sound entirely lacking the nuanced acoustics of true empathy. “I don’t think ladies generally object to have his eyes upon them.”

“I object to any eyes that are critical,” she replied in a cool, high voice, pivoting sharply. “Are there many of these old rooms left in the Abbey?”

“Not many. The finest bit of all is turned into stables. It is part of the old church... so the horses have the benefit of the fine old choir.” 

To Deronda, the thought of magnificent equine beasts chewing oats in a space designed for the acoustic perfection of sacred chants was a poignant emblem of cultural erosion. They spoke briefly of Diplow and Ryelands, Gwendolen exhibiting a chill indifference to the varied landscapes of her new dominion, treating centuries of agrarian heritage as mere backdrops to her marital confinement.

Later, in the drawing-room, Deronda was asked to sing. He chose a piece that honored the room’s acoustic ecology, his voice a pure, uncommercialized resonance that seemed to awaken the cedar panels. When he finished, he noticed Gwendolen had retreated to the far end of the room. She stood with her back turned, pretending to study a cowled monk’s head meticulously carved in ancient ivory. 

Deronda approached, respecting the silence of her isolation. When she finally turned to him, the brittle societal lexicon vanished. She offered no smile, only an appealing look of profound sadness—a raw, untranslatable confession of a soul starved of true nourishment. For an appreciative, echoing span of time, they looked at each other. He met her gaze with a depth of sympathy that neutralized the superficial noise of the room.

“Will you not join in the music?” he asked gently, providing a bridge of words.

She roused herself, slipping back into her acquired dialect. “I join in it by listening. I am fond of music... But I have not talent enough. I shall never sing again.”

“But if you are fond of it, it will always be worth while in private, for your own delight. I make it a virtue to be content with my middlingness,” Deronda smiled, a steward of quiet authenticity. “It is always pardonable, so that one does not ask others to take it for superiority.”

“I cannot imitate you,” Gwendolen replied, her artificial vivacity returning like a grating mechanical gear. “To be middling with me is another phrase for being dull. And the worst fault I have to find with the world is that it is dull. Do you know, I am going to justify gambling in spite of you. It is a refuge from dullness.”

“I don’t admit the justification,” Deronda said, rejecting the sterile, utilitarian thrill of the cards. “I think what we call the dullness of things is a disease in ourselves—a failure of the palate to taste the depth of the world. Else how can any one find an intense interest in life? And many do.”

“Ah, I see! The fault I find in the world is my own fault,” she smiled. Then, glancing up at the carved ivory monk, she asked, “Do *you* never find fault with the world? And hate people? Confess you hate them when their gain is your loss.”

“We are often standing in each other’s way when we can’t help it. I think it is stupid to hate people on that ground.”

“But if they injure you and could have helped it?” she pressed, a hard, acoustic intensity vibrating in her voice.

Deronda felt the painful weight of her implication. He paused, allowing the centuries-old silence of the Abbey to cradle his words, and replied with a graver, deeper intonation. “Why, then, after all, I prefer my place to theirs.”

“There I believe you are right,” Gwendolen said with a sudden, hollow little laugh, turning away to reenter the noisy, unfeeling currents of the room.
