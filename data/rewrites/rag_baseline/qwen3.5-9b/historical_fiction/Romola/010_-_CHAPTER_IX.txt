"Five hundred florins," the printer declared, tapping the heavy stack of coins on the table. "A fortune, *giovane mio*. Take my word for it: if you seek wisdom, deposit these florins with me today. Let them grow in a vault rather than slip through your fingers on the banquet tables of our Florentine youth. Scholars, especially those who scent their books with vanity like Pietro Crinito, often squander with one hand until they beg with the other. I bring the capital; you choose the path. Put them to usury, or save them for a *morgen-cap*—a dowry for a wife you may yet wed. The choice is yours, but the temptation is the test of every man's art."

Cennini locked the door, leaving Tito alone with the silence and the glint of gold. The smile died instantly from his lips. He stood rigid, thumbs hooked in his belt, staring down at the florins as if they were not currency but a verdict.

"A man's ransom!" The phrase echoed in the hollow of his mind. Who had spoken it? Five hundred florins was the price of a life, yet it was also the cost of a conscience.

If, under this relentless midday sun, a man of advanced years—stricken, yet possessing a heart of fire and a mind of high thought—was currently toiling as a slave in the heat, hewing wood and hauling water, buffeted by overseers for his sluggishness... if that man were Baldassarre Calvo, the father who had once pulled a starving boy from the gutter and raised him with a tenderness that bordered on divinity...

Then the math changed. If Baldassarre were alive, trapped somewhere in the Archipelago, waiting for the manuscripts and gems that Tito carried within him, then this pile of gold was not wealth; it was a ticket to redemption. Tito could raise funds, sail to Venice, track the survivor, and break the chains.

But the certainty required to justify such a leap was missing. The galley carrying the cargo had been intercepted by a Turkish vessel en route to Delos. That was the report from the companion ship that had escaped. Resistance had occurred; blood had likely been spilled. A man had been seen plunging into the sea. Was he the sole survivor? Or had the storm claimed them all?

Tito had faced shipwreck himself, clinging to wreckage while death hovered inches away. He knew the ocean's indifference. Rumors of pirate settlements on Delos were unreliable whispers, useful only to frighten the superstitious. If he abandoned Florence now, burning his bridges to the city's elite, selling his reputation, and rushing to Venice only to find the Archipelago empty or his father already dead... he would be left with nothing but debt and shame. He would be a wanderer again, poorer than when he started, having gambled his future on a ghost.

The vision of failure was sharper, more tangible, than the faint possibility of reunion. To turn his back on the soft airs of Florentine promise, on the adoration of Bartolommeo Scala, on the intellectual ascent awaiting him as a professor of Greek... it felt like a betrayal of his own potential. Yet, the alternative was a lifetime of wandering, searching for a man who might not exist.

"Do I not owe something to myself?" Tito whispered, the words scraping against his throat. He shifted his weight, the first movement he'd made since the door clicked shut. "Before I abandon everything to a desperate gamble, I require a reasonable hope. Do I possess it? No. I believe he is dead. Cennini is right; I will entrust these florins to him tomorrow."

By the next morning, the decision hardened into steel. In choosing to bury the truth, Tito had inadvertently chosen his fate. He had painted himself into a corner where the only logical exit was to pretend the past never happened. He had made it impossible for himself to accept anything less than the conclusion that Baldassarre was lost. The temptation to lie, to protect his own comfort and status, became stronger than the memory of the father who had once called him his own. The facts of his conduct remained sealed, forever concealed, as the weight of the gold settled firmly onto his shoulders.
