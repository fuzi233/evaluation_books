Ethne stood rigid before the drawing-room window of the Hill Street house, her breath held in a suspended tension. Beside her, Mrs. Adair sat before her tea-table, both women acting as sentinels, ears straining against the afternoon air for a specific signal. The sash was thrown wide, a deliberate invitation for sound to breach the sanctuary of the room. It was half-past five; June had returned with its golden fervor, turning London into a glittering tapestry of pleasure and verdant shade. Across the way, windows bloomed with floral gaiety, and below, carriages glided smoothly toward the Park. Then, a sudden jingle of bells cut through the hum, swelling into a crescendo. Mrs. Adair lifted her head sharply.

"That's a cab," she whispered.

"Yes."

Ethne leaned forward, peering down. "But it isn't stopping," she murmured, as the metallic clatter faded into the distance.

Mrs. Adair glanced at the clock, her expression shifting from anticipation to a dawning unease. "Colonel Durrance is late," she said, turning a curious gaze toward Ethne. To her, Ethne's affirmative had carried a weight of dread rather than eagerness; her posture at the window suggested apprehension. Yet, as the carriage passed without halting, Mrs. Adair sensed a flicker of relief in Ethne's stance. "Why didn't you go to the station to meet him?" she asked slowly.

The reply came instantly.

"He might have assumed I came because I viewed him as helpless, and I cannot allow him to think that. He has his servant with him." Ethne watched the street again, pausing briefly as if to gather courage before speaking. "You recall the telegram I showed you?"

"From Lieutenant Calder, stating that Colonel Durrance had gone blind?"

"Yes. I require a promise from you: never to mention it. I do not want him to know I ever received it."

Mrs. Adair was perplexed, a sensation she detested. She had seen the telegram, but she had not been privy to Ethne's subsequent letter to Durrance, a pledge made the moment the news arrived. When Ethne had first displayed the telegram, she had simply declared, "I am engaged to him." Mrs. Adair had accepted this as a settled fact, allowing herself to remain in that comfortable delusion.

"You will promise?" Ethne pressed.

"Certainly, my dear, if you wish," Mrs. Adair replied, her shoulders lifting in an ungracious shrug. "But there must be a reason. I fail to comprehend why you demand such secrecy."

"Two lives must not be ruined because of me."

Mrs. Adair suspected Ethne feared the blind man she was betrothed to, and she was right. It had been exactly one year since Ethne had bid Durrance attempt to forget her in this very room, on a sunlit afternoon just like this one. Every letter since had proven futile; he had not forgotten. Even the last missive, three weeks prior—a childish scrawl from Wadi Halfa with uneven, straggling letters—where he detailed his calamity and renounced his suit, confirmed it more surely than any hopeful correspondence before it. As she waited, Ethne realized with crushing clarity that the burden of forgetting now rested entirely on her shoulders. If she could not erase the memory, she must ensure that no careless word betrayed her own lingering attachment.

"No," she said firmly. "Two lives shall not be spoiled because of me," and she turned to her friend.

"Are you certain, Ethne," Mrs. Adair asked, "that this path of yours—the path of marriage—will not ultimately destroy both of you? Do you not fear that you will eventually come to see Colonel Durrance as a hindrance, a drag? Might he not feel the same?"

"No," Ethne answered with decisive conviction. "I shall not feel it, and he must not."

In Mrs. Adair's mind, the two lives at stake were those of Durrance and Harry Feversham. She was mistaken; the true stakes were Durrance and Ethne herself. Ethne did not correct her, nor did she dispute the point. Instead, she was secretly relieved that her friend held this erroneous belief, allowing Mrs. Adair to maintain her comforting illusion.

Ethne resumed her vigil at the window, mapping out her future with military precision, anticipating every potential ambush. The task was arduous, and fear began to coil within her chest as the minutes ticked by, lest she falter. Yet, the goal justified the peril. Durrance had lost everything that gave life meaning the moment his sight failed—everything, save one thing. "What should I do if I were crippled?" he had asked Harry Feversham that morning on the river bank. "A clever man might endure it. But what if I were condemned to sit in a chair for the rest of my days?" Ethne had not heard those words, yet she understood the man perfectly without them. By birth, he was heir to vast estates and a legacy of exploration; he had lost his heritage. The long journeys, the exotic faces of foreign lands, the crackle of the campfire, the red spark against the black desert silence, the sleep beneath bright stars, the cool night winds, and the work of governance—all were gone. Only one thing remained: herself, and only as long as he could believe she desired him.

Just as she solidified her resolve, the cab arrived at the door, unnoticed until the servant's bell rang.

"He has come!" she gasped, starting.

Durrance was not naturally inquisitive; he accepted friends as they were, refusing to dissect them under a microscope. Ethne reasoned that quieting his suspicions would be easy, especially now. There was no cause for alarm. Yet, despite her logic, she braced herself for the encounter rather than rushing to welcome him.

Mrs. Adair slipped away, leaving Ethne alone as Durrance entered. She did not move immediately, holding her pose, expecting the change in him to strike her with force. Instead, she was surprised by the absence of the changes she feared. His face was weathered, his hair streaked with grey, but there was no air of helplessness or uncertainty—the very trait she dreaded most for his sake. He walked into the room with the confidence of sight; his memory seemed to chart the exact position of every piece of furniture. At most, he reached out a hand once or twice where he expected a chair.

Ethne retreated silently to the window, unsure of how to greet him, but he smiled and approached her directly.

"Ethne," he said.

"It isn't true, then," she exclaimed, the words forced out by the ease of his movement. "You have recovered."

"It is quite true, and I have not recovered," he answered calmly. "But you shifted at the window, and so I knew you were there."

"How did you know? I made no noise."

"No, but the window was open. The street noise suddenly intensified, so I knew someone had moved aside. I guessed it was you."

Their exchange lacked the customary formality of an engaged couple meeting after a separation, yet it successfully bypassed embarrassment. Ethne avoided any perfunctory expressions of regret, sensing there was no need, and Durrance showed no desire to hear them. They shook hands, and Ethne stepped back into the room.

"I shall prepare some tea," she offered. "Then we can speak."

"Yes, we must have a talk, mustn't we?" Durrance replied seriously. He soon shed this gravity, chatting with good humor about his journey home. He even found amusement in recounting his initial days of blindness, and Ethne's anxieties began to dissolve. They had nearly vanished when a shift in his demeanor brought them crashing back.

"I wrote to you from Wadi Halfa," he said. "Do you suppose you could read the letter?"

"Quite well," Ethne replied.

"I had a friend of mine hold the paper and tell me when I was writing on it versus the blotting-pad," he continued, laughing lightly. "Calder—of the Sappers—but you do not know him."

He dropped the name quickly, and it struck Ethne like a blow; he had set a trap. The stillness of his face suggested he was listening with intense scrutiny for a reaction, a startled exclamation that would reveal her knowledge of Calder. Did he suspect? Did he know of the telegram? Did he guess her letter was born of pity? She searched his face, finding nothing but alertness. In the past, a year ago, the expression of his eyes would have answered her definitively, even if he held his tongue.

"I could read the letter without difficulty," she answered softly. "It was the letter you would have written. But I had written to you before, and of course your news could make no difference. I retract no word of what I wrote."

Durrance sat with his hands on his knees, leaning forward slightly. Ethne was lost again. She could not discern from his manner or face whether he accepted or questioned her response. She realized painfully that a year ago, with his sight, she would have known the truth instantly.

"Yes, I know you. You would take nothing back," he said finally. "But there is my point of view."

Ethne looked at him, apprehension tightening her chest.

"Yes?" she replied, striving for an air of unconcern. "Will you tell me?"

Durrance agreed, beginning to speak in the deliberate tone of a man who had weighed his thoughts, memorized his argument, and decided on the most lucid order of words.

"I know what blindness means to all men—a growing, narrowing egotism unless one guards oneself perpetually. And will one guard oneself perpetually? Blindness means that to all men," he repeated emphatically. "But it must mean more to me, who am deprived of every occupation. If I were a writer, I could dictate. If I were a businessman, I could manage my affairs. But I am a soldier, and not a clever one. Jealousy, a continual and irritable curiosity—there is no Paul Pry like a blind man—a querulous claim upon your attention—these are my special dangers." Ethne laughed gently, contradicting his gloom.

"Well, perhaps one may hold them off," he acknowledged. "But they must be considered. I have considered them. I am not speaking without thought. I have pondered and puzzled over this whole matter night after night since receiving your letter, wondering what I should do. You know how gladly, with what gratitude, I would have answered, 'Yes, let the marriage proceed,' if I dared. If I dared! But I think—don't you?—that great trouble often clears one's wits. I used to lie awake in Cairo thinking; the trivialities gradually fell away, and a few straight, simple truths stood out vividly. One feels compelled to cling to them with all one's might, because nothing else remains."

"Yes, I understand that," Ethne replied in a low voice. She had undergone precisely such an experience herself. It might have been she, not Durrance, speaking. She looked up at him, and for the first time, she began to understand that they shared much in common. She repeated to herself with renewed firmness, "Two lives shall not be spoiled because of me."

"Well?" she asked.

"Well, here is one of those straight truths. Marriage between a man crippled like myself, whose life is effectively ended, and a woman like you, active and young, whose life is in full bloom, would be quite wrong unless each brings to it far more than friendship. It would be quite wrong if it implied a sacrifice for you."

"It implies no sacrifice," she answered firmly.

Durrance nodded. The answer satisfied him, and Ethne felt it was the intonation he valued more than the words. His concentrated attitude confirmed this. She began to wonder if quieting his suspicions would be easier now that he was blind, or if the very condition made it more difficult.

"Then do you bring more than friendship?" he asked suddenly. "You will be very honest, I know. Tell me."

Ethne was in a quandary. She knew she must answer, immediately and without ambiguity, and she must answer honestly.

"There is nothing," she replied, as firmly as before, "nothing in the world which I wish for so earnestly as that you and I should marry."

It was an honest wish, spoken honestly. She knew nothing of the conversation between Harry Feversham and Lieutenant Sutch in the Criterion Grill Room; she knew nothing of Harry's plans; she had not heard of the Gordon letters recovered from the ruins on the Nile. Harry Feversham had, as far as she knew and intended, vanished completely from her life. Therefore, her wish was pure. But it was not an exact answer to Durrance's question, and she hoped he would listen to the tone rather than the words. He seemed content with it.

"Thank you, Ethne," he said, taking her hand and shaking it. His face smiled at her. He asked no further questions. There was no doubt, she thought; his suspicions were quieted; he was entirely content. Then Mrs. Adair entered with discretion.

She possessed the tact to greet Durrance as a man suffering no disadvantage, speaking as though she had seen him only the week prior.

"I suppose Ethne has told you of our plan," she said, taking her tea from Ethne's hand.

"No, not yet," Ethne answered.

"What plan?" Durrance asked.

"It is all arranged," Mrs. Adair said. "You will wish to return home to Guessens in Devonshire. I am your neighbor—only a couple of fields separate us. So Ethne will stay with me during the interval before your marriage."

"That is very kind of you, Mrs. Adair," Durrance exclaimed; "for, of course, there will be an interval."

"A short one, no doubt," Mrs. Adair replied.

"Well, it is this way. If there is a chance I may recover my sight, it would be better to seize it immediately. Time counts for much in these cases."
