The winter silence wrapped Romola in its hushed embrace as she lay resting, her thoughts drifting between the weight of the past and the uncertain shadows of the future. Everything she had known now appeared altered, as if the wax of her soul had been remolded by a fresh seal. Before, she had felt herself adrift—unmoored, purposeless—whispering to the darkness, *"I am tired of life. I want to die."* But from the moment she had answered that desperate cry in the night, something within her had shifted. She no longer reasoned, as she once had in Florence, that life held value because she could ease suffering. She simply *acted*, driven by an instinct so fierce that it left no room for hesitation.

Here, in this valley, she had rediscovered a truth stripped bare of complication. In Florence, her life had been knotted with the burdens of marriage, civic duty, and faith—each bond fraying until the shock of betrayal left her numb, severed from the world. But now she understood: *"It was cowardice to crave death. If all else is uncertain, suffering is not. If the glory of sacrifice is illusion, the pain is real. And while I have hands to lift the fallen, I will."*

Yet the past did not release her so easily. With her work in the valley complete, her thoughts surged back toward Florence—toward the life she had fled. In this rare stillness, severed from all familiar things, she saw herself with unflinching clarity. Regret, that quiet torment of the compassionate, stirred within her. Had she been too proud? Too quick to judge others while failing her own ideals? She questioned the righteousness of her flight. Had it been mere selfishness? Fra Girolamo had once drawn her back with words that cut deeper than her own justifications. *How could she claim to feel the pain of strangers yet turn from those closest to her?*

But memory answered sharply. The truth of her marriage to Tito rose like a specter—its hollow union, the lies she had been forced to cloak in duty. No, flight had been her only escape. And yet, even now, the ghost of what had been gnawed at her. *Could any bond truly break once it had woven itself into the blood?* Savonarola's words haunted her: *"You cannot cease to be a wife."* The thought of Tito—wretched, desperate—pricked at her conscience. What if he needed her?

Florence called to her, not as an idea, but as hunger calls the body. Her heart could not wander in abstractions; it clung to the known, the lived. The doubt returned: *Had she been wrong to leave?* If Florence was flawed, was she so pure that she could scorn it? If she had been stronger, would her faith have shattered so easily? Savonarola's voice echoed—his fire, his flawed conviction, the life he had rekindled in her. No failure could erase that debt.

Restlessness burned in her veins. Was it the returning strength of spring, or the need to stand once more within sight of Florence? She did not dare think of entering the city, but the longing to be near, to *know*, grew unbearable.

On a clear March morning, the valley gathered to bid her farewell. Jacopo brought a mule; the Padre would guide her to the nearest town. Her hands were clasped, kissed, her skirts clutched by those who wept. *"Why leave us now, when the earth grows generous? Stay!"*

*"My people may need me,"* she answered softly.

Benedetto wailed as she mounted, and so the villagers insisted he ride with her, if only to the slope's edge. Step by step, they followed, unwilling to let her go. Even as distance swallowed her, Romola turned, again and again, to see them still standing, arms raised, until the road bent and the valley vanished behind her.
