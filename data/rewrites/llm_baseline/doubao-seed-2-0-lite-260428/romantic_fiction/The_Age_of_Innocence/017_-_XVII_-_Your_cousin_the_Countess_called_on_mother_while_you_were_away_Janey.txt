“Your cousin the Countess called on mother while you were away,” Janey Archer chirped, as Newland hung up his fur-lined overcoat in the front hall, his coat still holding the faint, sun-warmed scent of orange blossoms from his trip down to St. Augustine. The three of them were dining alone that evening, the gas lamps gilding the edges of the dining room’s mahogany sideboard, and Newland glanced up in surprise to catch his mother’s gaze demurely fixed on her plate, as if she were determined not to let him think a visitor could have caught her unawares in her quiet, cloistered routine. Newland smiled to himself, guessing she was a little irked that he could be surprised Ellen Olenska would think to stop by.
“She had on that black velvet polonaise all the fashion plates are gushing over, covered in jet buttons that glinted like crushed starlight, and the tiniest little green monkey muff I ever saw,” Janey rattled on, her eyes bright with awe. “I never saw her look half so stylish. She showed up all alone, early on Sunday afternoon, thank goodness we’d lit the drawing room fire. She pulled out one of those brand new engraved card cases you see the girls from New Paris carrying, and said she’d come calling to thank us for how good you’d been to her.”
Newland laughed, stirring his wine. “Madame Olenska always talks that way about anyone who shows her the smallest kindness. She’s thrilled to finally be surrounded by people she’s related to, people who don’t look at her like she’s a ghost from a bad story.”
“Indeed she did,” Mrs. Archer said, dabbing her lips with her linen napkin. “I will say she seemed genuinely grateful to be home. Though I can’t help noticing she lays herself out to charm even a fusty old widow like me.”
“Mother doesn’t think she’s as simple as May,” Janey interjected, her eyes crinkling as she peered up at Newland from across the table.
“It’s just my old-fashioned taste,” Mrs. Archer said softly. “Dear May will always be my idea of what a young lady ought to be.”
Newland’s chest tightened a little. “Ah. They aren’t alike, not in the slightest.”
A day or two later, when he’d dropped off the stack of little gifts and handwritten messages May had sent for old Mrs. Manson Mingott, he headed over to the matriarch’s house above Fortieth Street—the one all the old conservative clans still gossiped about, as if she’d built it on the moon rather than just a few blocks north of the rest of them. The old woman greeted him with unusual warmth, her puff-ball soft hand patting his knee when he admitted he’d snuck off from the office without leave just to rush down to Florida and see May.
“Ah, so you kicked over all the traces, did you?” she cackled, her adipose chins wobbling like wobbly little jellies. “I bet Augusta and the Wellands pulled those long, glum faces of theirs, acting like the world was ending? But little May—she got it, didn’t she? She knew why you ran.”
“I hoped she did,” Newland said, his shoulders slumping a little. “But she still wouldn’t say yes to what I went down there to ask.”
“Wouldn’t she? And what was that?”
“I wanted her to agree we’d get married in April. What’s the point of wasting another whole year waiting?”
Old Catherine screwed up her face into a mock-prudish grimace, her eyes twinkling like mischievous marbles. “‘Ask Mamma,’ I’ll wager she said. The same old Mingott nonsense! They’re all born stuck in a rut, and you couldn’t dig them out of it with a pickaxe. When I built this house, everyone acted like I’d run off to California! No one had ever lived above Fortieth Street, they squawked, like the whole rest of Manhattan was just wilderness for the Indians. None of them want to be different, they’re scared of it like it’s smallpox. Thank my stars I’m just a vulgar Spicer, none of that stuffy Mingott nonsense in my bones. Only my little Ellen got that wild, fire-in-the-blood streak from me.”
She paused, still grinning at him, and tossed out a line as casual as if she was commenting on the weather: “Tell me, why in the world didn’t you marry my little Ellen, all those years back?”
Newland’s throat went dry, and he forced a laugh. “For one thing, she wasn’t around to ask.”
“No, that’s true. More’s the pity. Now it’s too late. Her life’s already all tangled up,” she said, cold and matter-of-fact, the way only very old people can talk about young people’s ruined hopes. Newland’s heart turned cold in his chest, and he spoke quickly.
“Can’t I convince you to use your influence on the Wellands? I wasn’t made for these endless long engagements.”
Old Catherine beamed, her face crinkling. “I can see that. You’ve got a quick, hungry eye. I bet when you were a little boy you always shoved to be first in line for the cake.” She threw back her head and laughed, and just then the drawing room portières rustled open.
Ellen Olenska stepped in, her cloak dusted with a fine powder of snow, her cheeks bright pink from the cold. She smiled, and her whole face lit up, and she held her hand out gaily to Newland before leaning down to kiss her grandmother’s cheek.
“Perfect timing, Ellen!” old Catherine cackled. “I was just telling him, ‘Why didn’t you marry my little Ellen?’”
Ellen looked over at Newland, that soft, knowing smile still playing on her lips. “And what did he say to that?”
“Oh, darling, I’ll leave you to pry that out of him!” her grandmother laughed. “He ran all the way down to Florida to beg his sweetheart to marry him sooner.”
“I know,” Ellen said, still looking right at him. “I stopped by your mother’s house to find out where you’d gone. I sent you a note that you never answered. I was afraid you’d fallen ill on your trip.”
Newland stammered out that he’d left in such a hurry he’d meant to write her from St. Augustine and it had slipped his mind entirely.
“And once you got down there with your pretty girl you forgot all about little old me, didn’t you?” she teased, bright and giddy, like she was deliberately putting on a show of easy indifference so he wouldn’t see how much his silence had stung. It made his chest ache. He wanted to thank her for calling on his mother, but with old Catherine’s sharp, amused eyes on them he couldn’t get the words out, too tongue-tied to be honest.
“Look at him! Rushing off to elope almost, to get that silly girl to agree to an early wedding!” old Catherine cawed. “That’s the kind of lover my own father was, handsome Bob Spicer, ran off with my mother before anyone could stop him, and got tired of her before I was even weaned! And they only had to wait eight months for me! But you’re no Spicer, young man, lucky for you and lucky for May. Only my poor Ellen got that wild, wicked Spicer blood. The rest of you are all perfect little model Mingotts.”
Newland noticed Ellen had sat down at her grandmother’s side, her eyes soft and thoughtful, no trace of that bright giddiness left. “Granny,” she said gently, “surely the two of us together can badger the Wellands into giving him what he wants.”
Newland stood up to go, and when his hand brushed hers at the door, he could feel she was waiting for him to mention the unanswered note, to say he’d cared enough to notice her missing.
“When can I see you?” he asked, low, as she walked him to the drawing room door.
“Whenever you like, but soon. I’m moving out of my little house next week.”
A sharp, soft pang went through him at the memory of all those quiet, lamplit hours he’d spent there, the low-ceilinged drawing room stuffed full of her little trinkets, every minute of them thick with a feeling he couldn’t name.
“Tomorrow evening?”
She nodded. “Tomorrow, yes. But come early. I’m going out later.”
Tomorrow was Sunday, of course, and the only place anyone in their set went out on a Sunday evening was Mrs. Lemuel Struthers’s house. Newland felt a flicker of annoyance—not because he thought Ellen ought not go, not because he cared about what stuffy old van der Luydens would say, but because he knew full well Julius Beaufort would be there. He was sure she’d known that too, before she agreed to go.
“Tomorrow evening,” he repeated, and resolved he would show up late. Late enough that he could either convince her not to go at all, or show up after she’d already left, which would be the simpler, less painful choice.
But by half past eight he was at her door, ringing the bell under the wisteria vine, restless and thrumming with a tension he couldn’t shake, half an hour earlier than he’d meant to be. He expected to find the drawing room empty at first, then stopped short when he saw a whole stack of hats and overcoats in the hall, laid out by Nastasia. The coats were the strangest he’d ever seen under a respectable New York roof: one shaggy yellow ready-made ulster, the other an ancient, rusty cape cloak that looked like it had seen a hundred years of hard use, reeking of sawdust and cigar smoke, with a ragged grey scarf and a half-clerical felt hat laid on top of it.
Nastasia just shrugged fatalistically and pulled open the drawing room door for him.
There was no sign of Ellen. Instead, a tall, lean lady with faded, streaky white hair, draped in layers of fringed, plaid silk that looked like it had been sewn together from scraps, was standing by the fire, flanked by Ned Winsett and a giant, shaggy grey-haired man who moved his big paws like he was handing out blessings to a crowd. They were all staring at an enormous bouquet of crimson roses tied with purple pansies, set on the sofa where Ellen usually sat.
“Think of what these must cost in the middle of winter!” the lady sighed in a staccato little breath, as Newland walked in. Then she turned, held out her silk-mittened hand, and beamed at him. “Dear Mr. Archer—almost my cousin Newland! I am the Marchioness Manson. Ellen has taken me in for a few days while I pass through New York, fresh from wintering with the old Castile nobility in Cuba. Such delightful, spiritual people.” She gestured to the giant man. “This is Dr. Agathon Carver, founder of the famous Valley of Love Community upstate.”
Dr. Carver nodded his big lion-like head, and boomed a greeting. Ned Winsett winked at Newland from the corner.
They chatted for a few minutes, Winsett slipping out the door with a dry quip about Ellen’s house being the only oasis on their street, before Dr. Carver lumbered off to give a lecture at the Blenkers’. The Marchioness waved Newland to a seat, her little ivory fan fluttering in her gloved hand.
“I know everything you’ve done for my Ellen,” she said, leaning in, her voice dropping low like a secret. “That wise advice you gave her, that firm stand you took—you have no idea what that did. Because at that exact same moment, I was being reached out to… from the other side of the Atlantic.” She pressed the fan to her lips, glancing over her shoulder like someone might be listening at the door. “By the Count himself. My poor, foolish Stanislas Olenski. He asks only to take her back. On her own terms.”
Newland shot to his feet, cold all over. “Good God.”
“He sends his letter, his plea, through me,” the Marchioness went on, tapping her thin chest. “Think of what she’s throwing away, Mr. Archer. Acres of those crimson roses under glass in his terraced gardens in Nice. Historic pearls, the Sobieski emeralds, sables, priceless art, all the brilliant society she was adored in. And a remorseful husband begging her to come home.”
Newland stared at her, numb, realizing the woman standing in front of him was no silly old eccentric—she was a messenger straight from the hell Ellen had run so far to escape.
“Does she know? Has she seen the letter?” he stammered.
“Not yet. I wanted to see you first. I knew if I had your support, your influence over her… we could convince her it’s the right choice.”
“That she ought to go back?” Newland said, his voice sharp and violent. “I would rather see her dead first.”
The Marchioness didn’t even flinch. She tilted her head, and they both heard the soft sound of Ellen’s footsteps on the stairs coming down. She pointed a thin, purple-stained finger at the crimson roses on the sofa, her eyes glinting.
“Ellen’s here now. After all, Mr. Archer—marriage is marriage. And my niece is still a wife.”
