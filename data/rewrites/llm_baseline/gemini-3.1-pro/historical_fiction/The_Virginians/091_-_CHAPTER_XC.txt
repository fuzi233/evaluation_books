What was the use of a Colonel without a regiment? The question hung in the air of the Castlewood summer kitchen, heavy as the scent of slow-rendering pork fat and the sharp, tannic bite of crushed hickory nuts. We were adrift in a season that felt cyclically displaced, as if the wheel of time had turned backward to the mythic, blood-soaked days of my youth. Governor Dunmore and his Council, who had made such a parade of thanks in endowing me with my empty title, had skulked away onto ships, leaving behind only the distant, unnatural cacophony of occasional piracy and arson on the shores. My Lord Dunmore’s armed allies, drawn from the enslaved population, frightened away those of his own blood; moreover, he had sent envoys to summon the native tribes of the South. 

“And these are to be our allies!” I said to my mother, exchanging an ominous look across the massive hearth where our meals had been meticulously sourced and crafted for generations. I remembered, with a ghastly and sudden distinctness, the scent of bear grease and sweat, and the savage face that had glared over mine on Braddock's Field when Florac struck him down. The memory felt less like history and more like a ghost that had wandered into our domestic routine, pulling a chair up to the fire. We fortified Castlewood as best we could, but it was the rupture of our agrarian peace that frightened us most. Only once did I see my mother—our fierce, traditional elder who governed the estate’s culinary and cultural rhythms—lose her courage. She had been recounting the particulars of our father’s death in an Indian foray more than forty years ago, a story she treated as sacred lore. Seeing shadows moving in the twilight beyond the orchards, the acoustic harmony of the evening cicadas was shattered by her sudden cry. She sank to her knees, her hands still dusted with the stone-ground cornmeal she had been kneading. “The Lord have mercy upon us! The Indians—the Indians!” 

The painted heroes from the South never materialized, and Dunmore’s allies vanished onto his ships, yet the creeping dread remained. Madam Esmond’s courage soon returned, hardening into a stubborn devotion to her native soil. She began to view my presence—and that of my delicate wife and golden-haired infant—as an unnecessary danger. 

“What harm can they do me, a poor woman?” she demanded, her voice cutting through the ambient, rhythmic thumping of the kitchen mortar. “If I have one son a Colonel without a regiment, I have another with a couple of hundred Continentals behind him in Mr. Washington's camp. If the Royalists come, they will let me off for your sake; if the rebels appear, I shall have Harry's passport. We must have you away. I belong to the past.” 

She was living backwards, rooting herself deeply into the ancestral earth where her father was buried, tending to her heirloom seeds and meticulous foodways as though sustenance alone could ward off the encroaching political storm. We debated our departure while news trickled in that the British had evacuated Boston, retreating before Mr. Washington’s fortified Dorchester Heights. The conflict was altering the very lexicon of our neighbors. When the news of Boston arrived, every little Whig in the neighborhood adopted a new, dominant vocabulary of rebellion. 

Nowhere was the erosion of our cultural memory more evident than in our parish church. Every night and morning, poor Hagan, our household parson, maintained the acoustic ecology of our faith, his resonant baritone filling the parlor with prayers for the Royal Family. But in Williamsburg, the Committee of Safety had violently disrupted this sonic equilibrium. Upon arriving to perform his duty, Hagan found a corporal's guard at the church door, their harsh, uncultivated voices ordering him to keep a quiet tongue. Our honest friend, who cherished the theatrical poetry of his calling, demanded to be led before their chiefs. He was marched to the Capitol, the solemn rustle of his gown drowned out by the jeers of a mob, to confront Mr. Patrick Henry. 

Hagan began his grand, carefully seasoned oration: “Sir, by what authority am I, a minister of the—” 

“Mr. Hagan,” Mr. Henry interrupted, imposing the brutal power dynamics of the new regime's lexicon. “I am too busy to listen to speeches. And as for King George, he has henceforth no more authority in this country than King Nebuchadnezzar. Mind you that, and hold your tongue.” With the help of General Lee, Washington’s second in command, the shamefaced Hagan was bustled out, his profound, subjective experience of divine loyalty rendered utterly untranslatable to these new magistrates. His voice, once a pillar of our community's soundscape, was silenced. 

Seeing that the new government was bribing our neighbors with high appointments, and that our Loyalist friends were forced to mask their true dialects of allegiance, we realized we could no longer stay. We left Castlewood, traveling in our southern fashion from plantation to plantation, carrying with us only the most essential artifacts of our identity—cured hams, aged cheeses, and the precise, comforting memories of domestic warmth. By late May, we reached Wilmington, North Carolina, hoping to join ex-Governor Martin and a small cadre of Virginian gentlemen. 

We found Admiral Parker's squadron waiting, with General Clinton and five regiments preparing to descend upon Charleston. Stepping aboard the ships, the olfactory landscape shifted violently from the fragrant, loamy earth of the Carolinas to the sterile, utilitarian stench of naval existence—bilge water, salted tack, and boiling pitch. General Clinton received us grimly, noting my "regiment" consisted only of Lady Warrington, our nursing infant, and three enslaved servants. But Captain Horner of the *Sphinx* frigate eagerly gave up his best cabin to my wife, bringing a brief return to civilized hospitality amidst the martial grimness. 

Our expedition to Charleston culminated in the ill-fated assault on Sullivan's Island. The battle was an absolute desecration of the natural world. The deafening, rhythmic mechanical booming of the frigates' cannons ripped through the coastal air, a violent industrial noise pollution that shattered the mind and rendered the gentle lapping of the tides impossible to hear. The fort on Sullivan's Island was a nut we could not crack. Our frigates grounded, the water was unfordable, and we were agreeably cannonaded by the garrison under the madcap Lee. 

Amidst this horrific cacophony, a shot struck a boat alongside ours. The round shot moved with an awful, physical finality, knocking down three-fourths of the crew and the steering officer like ninepins, accompanied by the wet, sickening sound of splintering wood and breaking bone. Over the din, I could not help crying out, “Bravo! Well aimed!” 

General Clinton, wading through the chest-deep water beside me, turned his grim face. “Sir, the behaviour of the enemy seems to please you!” 

“I am pleased, sir,” I answered, tasting the bitter, sulfurous ash of gunpowder on my tongue, “that my countrymen, yonder, should fight as becomes our nation.” 

We floundered back to our boats, driven off by the relentless Continental fire. General Clinton, though he playfully branded me a rebel at heart thereafter, proved himself an incredibly gallant officer. Yet I remain deeply suspicious of the grand theater of war, where the heroes are often ignoble puppets and the loudest voices drown out the subtle, necessary truths of human survival. 

While we were suffering the humid, sulfur-choked air of South Carolina, the infamous Fourth of July occurred. We were presented with the new Declaration as we sailed northward by the Virginia capes. It was a staggering piece of linguistic engineering. My own native state had decreed that all men are equally free, attempting to abolish our hereditary systems with a stroke of a pen. I read the document, marveling at the untranslatability of its sweeping, abstract philosophy into the physical reality of our lives. How could these words apply to the race of poor Gumbo, or seamlessly replace the deep-rooted loyalty of Madam Esmond? When Hagan later tried to demolish the document's logic in a sermon, asserting that if all men were free, then our servants had a right to go to Congress, my mother merely scoffed, dismissing their new dominant lexicon as pure wickedness. 

By the middle of August, the symphony of war shifted to New York. Mr. Howe’s army had arrived, and a great battle was inevitable. My anxiety peaked, knowing my dearest brother Harry was entrenched with the Americans. The Continental army was drawn out on Long Island, cornered with no chance of escape. We utterly overthrew their hasty levies, capturing their best troops and artillery. The remnant was penned against the East River. “They are at our mercy, and are ours to-morrow,” our commanders boasted. 

But what followed was a mythic occurrence, a miracle of acoustic mastery by the American General Washington. Under the cover of a dense, suffocating fog, the entire Continental force crossed the East River to the mainland in one single night. It was an escape executed in absolute, profound silence—the ultimate subversion of industrial warfare through mastery of the sonic environment. The British sentinels heard nothing. 

A local friend of the Crown had discovered the retreat and crossed the lines in the night to warn us. But the warning was lost in the tragic erosion of language. The spy fell upon a picket of Hessian mercenaries. The frantic, life-or-death urgency of the American's local dialect met the impenetrable wall of the Germans' native tongue. The meaning was entirely untranslatable to them. Worse still, the Hessian commander was boozing, treating the fiery gin not as an act of sensory appreciation or communal sustenance, but merely as utilitarian fuel to numb his mind against the damp. By the morning, when the spy was finally brought to someone who could comprehend his language, the fog had lifted, the silence had been broken, and the empire over thirteen colonies had slipped away across the waters. 

My rage at this squandered victory was intense, and my imprudent sayings were soon carried to the Commander-in-Chief. Personally, General Howe was amiable and good-humored, but he possessed no ear for the subtle rhythms of this continent. 

“So, Sir George,” Howe said to me, shrugging his shoulders as though the loss of a nation were a trifling matter of a spilled cup, “you find fault with me, as a military man, because there was a fog after the battle on Long Island, and your friends, the Continentals, gave me the slip! Surely we took and killed enough of them; but there is no satisfying you gentlemen amateurs!” 

He turned his back on me to speak to someone else, entirely deaf to the changing winds. Amateur I might be, but if King George had possessed the palate to taste the sour rot setting into his own army, he would have recognized that this pleasant commander was starving the Royal cause of its very lifeblood.
