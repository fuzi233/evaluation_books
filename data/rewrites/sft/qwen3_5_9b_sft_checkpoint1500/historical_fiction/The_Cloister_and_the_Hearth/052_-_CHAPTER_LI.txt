Margaret descended the stairs a transformed figure. The storm of bitterness and wild emotion that had churned within her moments before had settled into a profound, quiet calm. Though her eyes held traces of unshed tears, they were dry now, clear, and steady.

"Denys," she whispered, her voice soft yet firm, "I have received my orders. I am to read my lover's letter to his family."

"You will not do that?" Denys asked, disbelief coloring his tone.

"I shall," she replied without hesitation.

Denys studied her face, searching for a crack in her resolve. "I see some word in that letter has softened your heart toward them."

"Not a single jot, Denys," she corrected gently, a sad smile touching her lips. "Even if I loathed them as one hates poison, I could not disobey the command of my love. It is so sweet to obey, Denys. And infinitely sweeter to obey a soul who stands afar, unable to force my hand, trusting entirely in the fidelity of my love. Ah, Gerard, my darling, were you standing beside me, close enough to touch, I might well have slighted your wishes, repelled by the grudge you bear against them. But distance makes the bond unbreakable. If you had ordered me to wade into a raging sea and read your sweet letter to the sharks, I would have done so. Therefore, Denys, go to his mother. Tell her I have the letter. If she and her kin wish to hear it, I am their servant. Let them set the hour, and I will sit them where they please, and welcome them with every courtesy I possess."

Denys departed with this hopeful tidings. He found the household gathered around the dinner table. Announcing the arrival of a long letter from Gerard, he watched the joy ripple through the room. Yet, amidst the celebration, he added, "Her heart has been softened; she intends to read it to you personally. You may choose whatever time suits you."

"What notion is this?" Catherine asked, her brow furrowing. "That no one else can read it save her?" "Let her send it here, and we shall read it together."

"Nay, Mother," interjected little Kate, her voice small but earnest. "Perhaps she cannot bear to part with it. She loves him so dearly."

"Do you think she fears we shall steal it?"

Cornelis, ever the pragmatist, suggested she sought merely to wedge herself into the family circle through this token of affection. Denys shot a look of utter scorn at the suggestion. "A foul heart speaks there," he declared. "La camarade hates you all with the venom of a serpent. Do not mistake me, Dame; I defend her not—she is full of spleen, and Gerard's mere mention of them stirs her wrath. Yet, despite her hatred, she offers to read his letter with her own lips, and her voice sounds like honey—it is a fair offer."

"'Tis true," the father, Eli, admitted gravely. "It merits a civil response. Hold your tongues, you women, and let me answer. Tell her that I, her husband's father, setting aside all past grievances, thank her for this grace. And if she desires double thanks, let her send my son's letter via your faithful hands. I shall read it to his flesh and blood, and I shall return to her with such surety and faithfulness as Eli a Dierich, a William a Luke, a free burgher of Tergou, bound by the honor of my ancestors. A man of his word."

"Ay, and a man who keeps his word better than most," Catherine cried, tears glistening. "He is the only one I have ever known."

"Hold thy peace, wife!" Eli commanded sternly.

"Art a man of sense, Eli? Art a dirk? Art a chose?" Denys shouted, laughing. "The she-comrade will be delighted to obey Gerard, yet she dares not face you, whom she hates as wormwood, unless you are present. Blessed be, the world has changed. She is wholly submissive today. 'Obedience is honey,' she quoth. And truly, it is a sweetmeat she cannot resist, tasting so little of it. With her fair face and mellow tongue, coupled with her fitful flights that terrify us soldiers to death when we thwart her, and her alternating chidings and pettings that turn us, two honest swashbucklers, into crawling slaves, she has achieved wonders. I am the ironing ruffian; the other knave washes. What next? Why, whenever the brat enters the world, I shall rock the cradle, and the other scoundrel shall wash the tucker and bib. So, I shall fetch the letter instantly. Will you let me stay and hear it read?"

"Else our hearts were black as coal," Catherine sighed.

Denys hastened forth. He returned, however, crestfallen. "She will not let it leave her hand—not to me, nor to you, nor to any living soul."

"I knew she would not," Cornelis observed quietly.

"Hush!" Eli interrupted. "Let Denys speak his tale."

"Nay," Denys protested, recounting the scene. "'Be ruled by me,' I said. 'Not I,' she retorted. 'But consider the honey of Obedience!' 'You are a fool,' she answered. 'Obedience to Gerard is sweet, but obedience to any other body?'"

"At length, she seemed to yield slightly," Denys continued, holding up a piece of paper. "And here, Mademoiselle, is a written note for you."

"For me?" Little Kate blushed, reaching for it.

"Give it here!" Eli commanded, scanning the handwriting. He read aloud, his voice dropping to a whisper. "Hearken! 'And, sweetheart, if these lines should travel safe to thee, make thou trial of my people's hearts withal. Maybe they are somewhat turned towards me, being far away. If 'tis so they will show it to thee, since now to me they may not. Read, then, this letter! But I do strictly forbid thee to let it from thy hand; and if they still hold aloof from thee, why, then say nought, but let them think me dead. Obey me in this; for, if thou dost disrespect my judgment and my will in this, thou lovest me not.'"

A heavy silence fell upon the room as Gerard's words, copied by Margaret's hand, circulated among the listeners.

"Well," Catherine finally spoke, breaking the stillness. "That is another matter. Methinks 'tis for her to come to us, not for us to journey to her."

"Alas, Mother! What odds does that make?" little Kate exclaimed, distressed.

"Much," Eli replied, his expression grim. "Tell her we are too numerous to visit her, and bid her come hither, the sooner the better."

When Denys departed to deliver the message, Eli confessed to himself that it was a bitter pill to swallow. "When that girl crosses my threshold, all the mischief and misery she has wrought here will seem to tumble into a single heap. Yet, what could I do, wife? We must hear the news of Gerard. I saw it in thine eyes, and felt it in my own heart. She is backed by our undutiful, yet still beloved son, and thus she stands stronger than we. She drives us to the grindstone, that sly, cruel jade. But never heed. We will listen to the letter; and then, let her depart unblessed, just as she arrived unwelcome."

"Make your mind easy," Catherine assured him, though a faint tone of regret lingered in her voice. "She will not come at all."

Shortly thereafter, Richart arrived from Amsterdam. Grave and dignified in his burgher's robe, gold chain, ruff, and furred cap, he was received not merely with affection but with deep respect. For he had risen a step higher than his parents, and in medieval society, such ascents were marked as visibly as staircases.

Admitted to the family council, Richart displayed plainly, though without overt discourtesy, that his pride had been deeply wounded by his parents' willingness to treat with Margaret Brandt. "I see the temptation," he remarked. "But which of us hath not, at times, wished one thing and done another?" These words cast a chilling frost over the elderly couple.

"So little," little Kate ventured timidly, "vex not thyself, dear Richart. Mother says she will not come."

"All the better, sweetheart," Richart replied coldly. "I fear me, if she do, I shall hie me back to Amsterdam."

Just then, Denys popped his head through the doorway. "She will be here at three on the great dial."

They all stared at one another in a silence that stretched like a taut wire, waiting for the inevitable collision.
