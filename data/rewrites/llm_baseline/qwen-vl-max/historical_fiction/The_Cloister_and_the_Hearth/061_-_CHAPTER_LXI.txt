The Princess Claelia decided she wished to have a full-length portrait painted. Gerard suggested his friend Pietro Vanucci, but she refused. “We’ll see how well the Gerardo does before I give him any special favour,” she said, with a hint of challenge.

Gerard, knowing he was skilled in drawing but less confident in colouring, proposed that she pose as a Roman statue. He showed her how closely he could imitate marble on paper. She agreed at first—but hesitated when he explained that she would need to wear the ancient garments: tunic, toga, and sandals.

“Why,” she said bluntly, “I’d just as soon appear in my nightgown.”

“Oh, signorina,” Gerard replied, “you’ve never truly seen the dignity of classical dress—how free, noble, and graceful it is. And it suits you perfectly. Heaven has given you Roman features, a shapely arm, and hands worthy of antiquity. You are born for this!”

“You can flatter like the rest, then, Gerardo?” she teased. “Give me time to think. Come back on Saturday, and I’ll tell you yes or no.”

In the days that followed, the princess’s chamber became a scene of quiet preparation. The tunic and toga were carefully sewn, tried on, and adjusted—each fold examined to see if it enhanced her beauty enough to justify its anachronism.

As Gerard hurried toward their meeting, something arrested him in his tracks—a shop window. His trained eye caught sight of an open book: Lactantius. “At least it’s well written,” he thought.

But then he saw: it wasn’t handwritten. It was printed.

He groaned inwardly. “It’s over. My enemy stands at the door. The printing press has reached Rome.”

He stepped inside, pretending calmness, and asked how long printing had been in the city. The shopkeeper chuckled. “No press here, messer. That Lactantius? It came from the top of the Apennines.”

“Did the press fall from the moon?”

“No, messer,” laughed the man. “It rose from Germany. See the title page!”

Gerard took the book eagerly. The imprint read:

“Would you buy it, messer?” the seller asked. “Look at the letters—so even, so fine. Few scribes today can match them. And half the price!”

“I would gladly take it,” Gerard said sadly, “but my heart won’t let me. Know that I am a calligrapher—and these disciples of Fust chase me across the world, stealing my bread. Yet I wish them no ill.” He turned and left quickly.

“Dear Margaret,” he murmured to himself, “we must act now. We must gather our harvest while the sun shines. One month more, and a flood of type will pour down from those mountains—destroying us all who write by hand.”

He ran to the princess’s residence.

He was ushered into a room unfamiliar to him—small but opulent. A fountain played gently in the centre, and the floor was covered with panther skins, so thick no footstep could be heard. It was an antechamber to her private boudoir, one side open only to a rich tapestry curtain.

Left alone, Gerard began to grow uneasy—had he been led to the wrong place?

Then, a light step approached behind the curtain. It parted, and there stood a figure that might have inspired worship among the ancients. Not quite Venus, not quite Minerva—but a fusion of both. Nobler than Venus, softer than Jupiter’s daughter. Her attire was entirely ancient—tunic, toga, sandals—no trace of modern fashion. And her beauty—ah, that transcended time.

Gerard leapt up, every artist’s instinct aflame.

“Oh!” he breathed, staring.

That very gaze deepened her charm. A blush warmed her cheeks; her eyes sparkled; her lips curved into a smile of quiet delight.

After a moment, she spoke. “Well, Gerardo, you see I’ve made myself an antique monster for you.”

“A monster?” he replied. “I doubt Fra Colonna would kneel and worship you in such garb.”

“Nay, I care not to be adored by an old man. I’d rather be loved by a young one—by one of my own choosing.”

He pulled out his pencils, arranged his canvas, covered with sturdy paper, and began to draw. So absorbed was he that he paid no heed to his model. At last, after nearly an hour of stillness, she spoke faintly: “Gerardo, I cannot stand like this any longer—even for you.”

“Sit down and rest, Signora.”

“Thank you,” she whispered, sinking into a chair, pale and sighing.

Gerard felt alarmed. He saw he had been careless. He fetched water from the fountain and moved to splash it on her face—but she raised a delicate hand. “No. Hold it to my brow with your hand. Please, don’t throw it.”

Tentatively, he touched her forehead with his wet palm.

“Ah!” she sighed. “That revives me. Again.”

He did so again. She thanked him and rang a small bell on the table. He obeyed. A maid arrived and was sent to fetch Floretta with a large fan.

Floretta returned swiftly with the fan.

No sooner had she entered than the princess’s nostrils flared—like a bloodhorse sensing danger. “Wretch!” she snapped, rising with sudden strength. She seized Floretta’s hair with one hand and struck her sharply three times across the ears.

Floretta screamed and wept—but received no mercy.

The bare arm beneath the toga moved with startling power—like a piston—delivering heavy slaps to Floretta’s shoulders. The final blow drove her sobbing through the curtain, where her cries echoed for some time.

“Saints of heaven!” Gerard cried. “What has she done?”

“She knows well,” the princess said coldly. “It’s not the first time. The foul beast! I’ll teach her to come near me smelling of musk-cat.”

“But, signora,” Gerard protested, “it seems a small fault.”

“A small fault? No—it’s a vile one.” Then, with sudden humility, she added, “Are you angry with me for striking her, Gerar-do?”

“Signora,” he replied gently, “it ill becomes me to correct you. But surely one meant to rule others should first rule herself.”

“That is true,” she said softly. “How wise you are, for your youth.” She called another maid and handed her a purse. “Take this to Floretta. Tell her ‘the Gerardo’ pleaded for her—and so I must forgive her. There, Gerardo.”

He blushed deeply at the compliment—but, unsure how to respond, asked if he should resume painting.

“Not yet,” she said. “Beating that hussy has refreshed me. Sit awhile. Talk to me. I know you can talk—as well as you draw.”

“That would be easy,” he said.

“Then do it, Gerardo.”

He was taken aback. “But, signora, I don’t know what to say. This is sudden.”

“Say what you truly feel. Say you wish you were anywhere but here.”

“No, signora,” he answered. “But I do wish one thing.”

“And what is that?” she asked, gentle now.

“I wish I could have drawn you as you struck that girl. You were terrible—yet beautiful. Oh, what a subject for a Pythoness!”

“Ah,” she murmured, “he thinks only of art. Why fuss over my beauty, Gerardo? You are far fairer than I. More like Apollo than I am Venus. And you have lovely eyes and hair—but you don’t know what to do with them.”

“I do,” he said. “To draw you, signora.”

“Yes,” she smiled. “You see my features—but not what any Roman gallant would have seen in your place long ago. Yet surely you’ve noticed how much you mean to me, Gerardo?”

“I see your highness is always kind to me—a poor stranger.”

“No, Gerardo,” she said quietly. “I’ve often been cold, even rude. You’re too simple to see why. I feared my own heart. I feared becoming your slave. I who have always made others slaves. Ah, Gerardo, I am unhappy. Ever since you came, I’ve lived for your visits. The day you come, I am bright. The other days—listless, wishing they were past. You are unlike the Roman nobles. You make me hate them. You are ten times braver in my eyes. Wise, scholarly, never flattering or lying. I despise men who lie. Gerar-do, teach me your magic—teach me to make you as happy beside me as I am beside you.”

Her voice sank to a whisper, trembling with suppressed emotion. Her white hand slid down his arm, resting lightly on his wrist—like a bird seeking shelter, ready to flee at a word.

Gerard, innocent and devoted to Margaret and his art, had not foreseen this moment—though it had crept upon him gradually. He blushed, his heart pounding. He admired her regal beauty, but Margaret remained in his soul. Yet this was real—passionate, tender, overwhelming. How to refuse without hurting her?

He trembled.

She saw and encouraged him. “Poor Gerardo,” she murmured. “Don’t fear. No harm shall come to you under my wing. Will you not speak to me, Gerar-do mio?”

“Signora,” he stammered.

His gaze dropped—falling on her delicate hand curled around his elbow, like a vine. And suddenly, he remembered how that same arm had struck Floretta.

He flushed again.

“Alas,” she sighed. “I frighten you. Am I so terrible? Is it the Roman robe? I’ll take it off—dress as you first saw me. Remember? To write a letter to that barren knight Ercole d’Orsini. Shall I tell you? It was seeing you—your manners, your words—that made me hate him instantly. I liked him well enough before—or thought I did. Now—how many times have you been here since then? Eighteen, Gerardo. And each time dearer. When you don’t come, it’s night, not day, to Claelia. Alas—I speak for both. Cruel boy, am I not worth a word? Do you have a princess at your feet every day? Speak to me, Gerar-do.”

“Signora,” he faltered, “what can I say that wouldn’t be better left unsaid? Oh, cursed day I ever came here.”

“Ah!” she said. “Do not say that. It was the brightest day of my life—and perhaps yours too. I’ll make you admit it soon, ungrateful one.”

“Your highness,” he began, voice low and pleading.

“Call me Claelia, Gerar-do.”

“Signora,” he continued, “I am too young, too inexperienced to know how to speak to you—without seeming blind or ungrateful. But this I know: I would be both unworthy and ungrateful, and your worst enemy, if I took advantage of this madness. Surely some evil spirit has allowed you to suffer this. For it is unnatural that a princess of grace should lower herself to love a commoner.”

She slowly withdrew her hand from his wrist—yet as it passed, it lingered a moment, as if reluctant to leave.

“You fear my kinsmen’s daggers,” she said, half sad, half scornful.

“No more than I fear your women’s bodkins,” he replied proudly. “But I fear God, the saints, and my own conscience.”

“The truth, Gerardo—the truth! Hypocrisy sits poorly on you. Princesses aren’t scorned for loving God—but for loving another woman. Tell me whom you love. If she is worthy, I’ll forgive you.”

“No woman in Italy,” he swore. “Upon my soul.”

“Ah,” she said. “Then there is one elsewhere. Where?”

“In Holland. My native land.”

“Ah! Marie de Bourgoyne is fair, they say. But she is only a child.”

“Princess, the woman I love is not noble. She is like me. Nor is she as fair as you. Yet she is fair—and bound to my heart forever by virtue, by shared dangers, by griefs borne together. Forgive me—but I would not betray my Margaret for all the noblewomen in Italy.”

The princess rose abruptly—paler now, fiercer than before. Her eyes burned with fury.

“This to my face, wretched man?” she shrieked. “Was I born to be insulted as well as scorned? Beware! We nobles brook no rivals. Choose: the love of a Cesarini—or her hatred. After all I have done for you, it must be love or hate—until death. Choose now!”

He looked up at her—awe-struck. She stood tall in her toga, offering him a choice like a goddess of old. He, a mere mortal.

He sighed deeply—but said nothing.

Perhaps his silence stirred pity. Or perhaps passion ebbed, and sorrow flowed. She sank into a seat, tears rolling down her cheeks.

“Alas,” Gerard whispered. “Do not weep, sweet lady. Your tears accuse me—and I may weep too. Be yourself, my kind patron. You’ll see how much better a friend I am than I seemed.”

“I see it now,” she said. “Friend is the only word that can pass between us. I was mad. Any other man would have used my folly. You must teach me to be your friend—and nothing more.”

Gerard rejoiced. He quoted Cicero on the nobility of friendship—how it surpasses love in depth and lastingness. To prove his sincerity, he even told her of Denys and their bond.

She listened, eyes half-closed, studying him—not just his words, but his soul.

At last, she said calmly: “Leave me now, Gerardo. Come tomorrow as usual. You will find your lesson well spent.”

She held out her hand. He kissed it—and left, pondering deeply.

The next day, she received him with cold distance. She stood before him like a statue, dismissed him after a brief sitting. He felt the chill—but told himself, “She is wise.” Indeed, she was, in her way.

The following day, she sat surrounded by young nobles, all flattering her extravagantly. They treated Gerard like a dog who could perform one trick. The cavaliers mocked his work with ignorant arrogance—making his cheeks burn.

She watched him silently, half-lidded eyes noting every flicker of anger.

When they left, she shut the doors against them all.

The next day, she was alone—cold and silent. After standing for some time, she said, “You treated my company with less respect than was fitting.”

“Did I, signora?”

“Did you? You glowered at their comments—honours they paid your work.”

“I said nothing,” he replied.

“Oh, your looks speak plainly. Your face was crimson.”

“I was irritated—seeing such ignorance and malice together.”

“Now you insult me, their hostess.”

“Forgive me, signora. I meant no disrespect. I have no greater patron or friend in Rome—except one.”

“How humble you are suddenly. In truth, Ser Gerardo, you are a master of pretence. You can insult or grovel at will.”

“Grovel? To whom?”

“To me—for one. To one you once offended for a base-born girl like yourself—yet whose favour you claim.”

Gerard rose, hand to his heart. “These are harsh words. Have I truly deserved them?”

“What are words to an adventurer like you? Only cold steel terrifies you?”

“I am no duelist—but I’ve met steel with steel. And I’d rather face your kinsmen’s swords than your cruel tongue, lady. Why do you treat me so?”

“Gerar-do,” she said, “for no good reason—only because I am capricious, sharp-tongued, and curst. Because everyone admires me—except you.”

“I admire you, signora. Your friends may flatter more—but none see half your beauty. Their babble yesterday proved it. None admire you more truly, or wish you better, than I—the poor artist who cannot be your lover—but hoped to be your friend. Yet I see that cannot be—between one so high, and one so low.”

“Ay,” she said eagerly, “but it shall, Gerardo. I will not be so harsh. Tell me—where does your Margaret live? I’ll send her a gift. On that, we shall be friends.”

“She is the daughter of a physician named Peter. They live in Sevenbergen. Ah me—will I ever see it again?”

“Good. Now go.”

She dismissed him abruptly.

Poor Gerard. He had entered deep waters with this Italian princess—crafty and fiery. He resolved to finish her portrait and never return. Indeed, he regretted having begun such a task.

But his resolve wavered when the next day brought kindness.

After posing for some time, she said she was tired—and wanted his help in another way: would he teach her to draw?

He sat beside her, guiding her hand through simple lines. She learned quickly.

“I had a teacher before you, Gerar-do,” she said. “And one as handsome as you.”

She opened a drawer and pulled out several drawings—crude but full of spirit. All were of Gerard—some surprisingly accurate.

“There,” she said. “Now you see who taught me.”

“Not I, signora.”

“Who teaches us women to do all things? Love, Gerar-do. Love made me draw—because you draw. Love prints your image in my heart. My fingers touch the pen—and love supplies the lack of skill. And lo—your face lies on the paper.”

Gerard stared. “Oh, signora—you promised friendship only.”

She laughed. “How naive you are. Who believes a woman promising nonsense? Friendship? Foolish boy—no temple is built on red ashes. Between you and me, it must be love or hate.”

“Which you choose, signora,” he said firmly. “But I will neither love nor hate you. With your permission, I will leave.”

He rose.

She rose too—pale as death. “Before you go—know your fate. Outside that door wait armed men. One word from me—and they will kill you.”

“But you will not speak that word.”

“That word I will speak. And I’ll spread it abroad: that you were slain for daring to offer me brutal love. I’ll send a clever messenger to Sevenbergen—one who knows his tale. Your Margaret will hear you dead—and believe you faithless. Go to your grave like a dog. For a man you are not.”

Gerard turned white. “God have mercy on us both.”

“No,” she said. “Have mercy on her—and on yourself. She’ll never know what you do in Rome—unless I’m forced to tell her. Yield, Gerar-do mio. What does it cost to say you love me? Just feign it—handsomely. You are young. Don’t die for the sake of denying a lady the shadow of a heart. Who will weep for you? Men will laugh at your tombstone—ah!”

She screamed—because Gerard threw himself at her feet and poured out his story in one impassioned torrent: his love for Margaret, her sacrifice, his imprisonment, the bloodhounds, exile, the perils across Europe, the beasts, the attacks, the shipwreck—all for her.

The princess trembled, trying to pull away—but he clung to her robe, holding her hand, crying into it, pleading with her to consider the suffering of true lovers torn apart, and the remorse that would follow such a betrayal.

In such moments, no one resisted Gerard.

She finally broke. “You conquer me,” she sobbed. “You—or my better angel. Leave Rome!”

“I will. I will.”

“If you speak a word of my folly, it will be your last.”

“Never,” he said. “You are my benefactress again. Would I slander you?”

“Go. I’ll send you the means. I know myself—if you cross my path again, I’ll kill you. Addio. My heart is broken.”

She rang the bell. “Floretta,” she called, voice choked. “Take him safely out—through my chamber, by the side postern.”

He turned at the door. She leaned on a chair, head bowed, weeping.

He thought only of her kindness—and ran back to kiss her robe.

She did not move.

Once outside, he dashed home, thanking Heaven for his escape.

“Landlady,” he said, “someone wants to fight me. What should I do?”

“Strike first—when you have the advantage! Get behind him, then draw.”

“Alas, I lack your courage. To be serious—it’s a noble.”

“Oh, holy saints—that changes everything. Change your lodging. Stay hidden. And alter your clothes.”

She took him to her niece, who offered rooms farther away. He settled in.

With no portrait to paint, he turned to the deed of Floris Brandt—finally reading it. He understood at once: the loan had been repaid repeatedly through rents. Ghysbrecht was illegally keeping Peter Brandt from his land.

“Fool!” he exclaimed. “Why didn’t I read this before?”

He hired a horse and rode to the nearest port. A ship sailed for Amsterdam in four days.

He booked passage and paid extra to secure it.

“The land is full of cutthroats,” he said. “But the sea is fair weather. Our Dutch skippers don’t sink like these clumsy Italians.”

When he returned home, his landlady sat waiting—eyes sparkling.

“News,” she said. “You’re safe. But beware—your name is already spoken in certain circles. They say you were seen leaving the palace with a secret. And that you fled Rome.”
