Durrance found his shadow-caster waiting in the corridor when he emerged from the fields to the sanctuary of "Guessens." "Extinguish the lamps and retire," Durrance commanded, his voice carrying the weight of a command issued to a ghost. He passed through the hall and entered his study—a title that mocked the reality of the chamber, for it had always served as a trophy room rather than a place of ink and paper.

He sank into his chair, then rose to pace the darkness, a solitary figure moving through a museum of his own making. Scattered across the floor lay the spoils of his youth: cups and goblets, each a map of a specific victory. He knew them by the topography of their rims and the scar of their handles. Touching them was an act of communion; he ran his fingers over the cool metal, wondering if, in his absence, the dust had claimed their shine. He lifted a slender goblet, remembering the grey skies of Colchester and the muddy churn of the ploughed fields between the hedges where he had won it in a regimental race. He moved to the pewter pen-holder on his desk, a relic from his freshman year at Oxford, acquired in the chaotic fervor of college fours. On the mantelpiece gleamed the silver hoof of a beloved steed, a testament to a life lived in motion.

These objects constituted his diary, a tactile record of days long extinguished. He traced the contours of his history until his hand met the smooth, cold lines of his firearms. He removed them from their racks. To Durrance, they were what Ethne's violin had once been to her: extensions of his soul, speaking a language audible only to him. He settled with a Remington across his lap, reliving a scorching afternoon in the hills west of Berenice. He saw the lion crossing the stony expanse, felt the tension in the trigger, and remembered killing it at three hundred yards just as the sun dipped below the horizon. Another weapon whispered of the first ibex taken in the Khor Baraka, of antelope stalked in the northern mountains near Suakin. There was a small Greener, used on bitter winter nights aboard a boat in the Salcombe estuary, with which he had bagged his first mallard. He lifted it, sliding his left hand beneath the barrel until the stock nestled perfectly into the hollow of his shoulder.

But as he manipulated the locks, acutely aware that his sight could no longer guide his aim, the sum total of his deprivation crashed upon him with undeniable force. The silence of the room pressed in, heavier than the weight of the guns. With a sudden, violent impulse, he decided to deny his blindness. He chose to pretend it was merely a minor inconvenience, a triviality that could be ignored. This denial surged through him like a fever, lifting him from the dim, starless ceiling of his present reality and casting him winged toward the East.

Suddenly, the air in the room thickened with the phantom scent of the Orient—the dust of ancient roads, the incense of mosques, the heat of a relentless sun. He smelled the chaos of its streets and saw the azure dome of its sky. The illusion was so potent it physically pulled him from his chair. He paced frantically, dreaming himself to Port Said, marching in the endless line of steamers descending the canal. He heard the rhythmic chant of Arab coalers, so loud he could visualize them as naked figures climbing the gangplanks in the red glow of braziers, an infinite chain of movement. He traveled past the crimson headlands of Sinai, into the chilling waters of the Gulf of Suez. He zigzagged down the Red Sea as the Great Bear hung low in the southern sky and the Southern Cross blazed above the rail. He touched at Tor and Yambo; he watched the towering white houses of Yeddah rise from the sea, admiring the weathered, dark wood of their carved windows. He wandered through the twilight of its roofed bazaars, feeling the strange comfort of a homesick traveler returning home. From Yeddah, he crossed the narrowing reefs into the land-locked harbor of Suakin.

Westward from Suakin stretched the desert, a landscape that had once defined him and now sought to consume him. He imagined the soft padding of camel feet in the sand, the massive rock cones rising abruptly like waves in a glass ocean, forever receding despite the marcher's progress. He saw the fleeting brilliance of sunset colors painting the west, the rustle of wind turning the twilight into a pale green against the deepest blue of the east, and the planets swooping down from the void to touch the earth.

In this trance, the heir to lost worlds forgot his blindness, parched with a thirst that burned like a fever. Until, abruptly, the sounds of the real world pierced the veil: the chatter of blackbirds and the piping of swallows in the garden. Dawn was breaking, painting the world outside his window in white light. He woke from the dream at the homely sound. No further journeys awaited him; affliction had built a cage and forged a chain around his leg. He felt his way along the balustrade to his bed and fell asleep as the sun climbed the sky.

At Dongola, on the great bend of the Nile south of Wadi Halfa, the sun was already a hammer striking the earth, and the people were awake. Sport was being prepared under the sparse palm trees before the residence of Emir Wad El Nejoumi. A white prisoner, captured a week prior near the wells of El Agia on the great Arbain road by a band of Arabs, had been brought in during the night and now waited for judgment. News of the spectacle spread through the town faster than fire; crowds of men, women, and children flocked to witness the rare event.

Before the palms, an open space led to the Emir's gate; behind them, a slope of sand descended flat and bare to the river. Harry Feversham stood beneath the trees, guarded by four Ansar soldiers. His clothes had been stripped away; he wore only a tattered, ragged jibbeh and a twisted cloth on his head to shield him from the searing sun. His bare shoulders and arms were scorched and blistered. His ankles were shackled, his wrists bound with coarse palm fiber, and an iron collar locked tight about his neck, tethered by a chain held by one of the soldiers.

He stood and smiled at the jeering crowd, appearing strangely content, like a madman who has accepted his role. This was the persona he had adopted. If he could maintain it, baffling his captors into uncertainty—as to whether he was truly insane or an agent promising aid to the discontented tribes of Kordofan—there was a slim chance they would hesitate to execute him personally, preferring instead to send him onward to Omdurman. But it was grueling work. Inside the house, the Emir and his counselors debated his fate; on the riverbank, visible to his eyes, a high gallows stood out stark and sinister against the yellow sand.

Feversham was relieved by the chain and the fetters. They anchored him, preventing any slip into panic by reminding him of his inevitable end. These hours of waiting, as the sun climbed higher and no one emerged from the gateway, were the most torturous he had yet endured. Throughout his fortnight in Berber, a flickering hope of escape had sustained him, but when that lantern had shone upon him in the ruined acres, action had demanded speed, leaving no room for fear. Now, there was time—and far too much of it. He had time to anticipate, to foresee the future. His heart sank until he felt faint, echoing the distant days when he had heard hounds scuffling in a covert while he sat trembling on his horse.

He glanced nervously toward the gallows, imagining vultures perching on his shoulders, circling his eyes. Yet the man had changed during his years of trial. The fear of physical pain was secondary, as was the terror of walking unmanly to the scaffold. A greater dread consumed him: if he died now, at Dongola, Ethne would never retrieve the fourth feather, and his profound hope for the "aftermath" would remain unfulfilled.

Grateful for the collar and the chains that constrained him, he gathered his wits. Standing alone, without a companion to share his misery, he laughed, scraped, and grimaced at his tormentors. An old hag danced and gesticulated before him, singing a monotonous song. Her movements were pantomimic, threatening abhorrent mutilations; her words described in crude detail the agony of his immediate death and the eternal torture awaiting him in hell. Feversham understood and shuddered inwardly, yet he merely mimicked her gestures, nodding and bowing as if she sang of paradise.

Others took their war-trumpets, pressing the bells against his ears and blowing with all their might. "Do you hear, Kaffir?" cried a child, dancing with delight. "Do you hear our ombeyehs? Blow louder! Blow louder!" The prisoner only clapped his hands, shouting that the music was excellent.

He stood before the captive, swinging the spear backward and forward to loosen his muscles, like a bowler preparing to deliver a ball.
