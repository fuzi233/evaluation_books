Life has a habit of conspiring against my best-laid plans, doesn't it? I had resolved to tackle a subject so utterly novel, so delightfully obscure, that it would leave the literary world gasping in awe. "I shall write a piece on something entirely new," I declared to myself with the confidence of a genius; "something no human soul has ever conceived; something that will be mine alone." For days, I paced my room, my brain churning like a wet sponge, searching for this golden nugget of originality. Nothing. Zip. Nada.

Then came Mrs. Cutting, our charwoman, yesterday. I won't mention her name in print; she'd likely faint if she saw this book. She reads nothing but the Bible and *Lloyd's Weekly News*, considering all other literature a sin and a waste of time.

"Lor', sir," she observed, eyeing my furrowed brow, "you do look worried."

"I am, Mrs. Cutting," I explained, adopting a tone of profound seriousness. "I am attempting to conceive a subject so startling, so revolutionary, that it will shock the world into a new era of thought—a topic on which no previous mortal has ever spoken a word."

She let out a guffaw that shook the tea-tray in her hands. "A funny gentleman, you are, sir."

My luck, it seems, is to be misunderstood. My earnestness is mistaken for eccentricity, and my jokes land flat on their faces. Last week, I concocted a masterpiece of wit. I had been discussing Shakespeare's stance on the Reformation at a dinner party, and I dropped a line that I was certain would bring the house down. "Ah," I said, "that reminds me; a terribly funny thing happened recently in Whitechapel."

"Oh," they murmured, "what was that?"

"Oh, it was hilarious," I replied, suppressing a giggle. "It'll make you roar!" And I launched into my tale.

Silence. Absolute, suffocating silence. Finally, someone asked, "And that was the joke?"

I insisted it was, and they politely accepted my word. Except for one elderly gentleman at the far end of the table, who demanded to know exactly what the punchline was—what was said to whom—and we ended up arguing for twenty minutes.

Some people are just the opposite. I knew a chap once whose laugh was so reflexive that if you wanted to discuss serious matters, you had to preface your speech with a warning that it wouldn't be amusing. Otherwise, he'd burst into hysterics over the most mundane statements. I've seen him stop mid-stride on the road, slap his knee, and erupt into laughter simply because someone asked him the time. You couldn't say anything truly funny to that man without risking his life; a good joke would have killed him instantly.

Back to Mrs. Cutting. I vigorously denied being frivolous and pressed her for a genuine idea. After a long pause, during which she stared into the middle distance with a tea-tray balanced precariously, she suggested "samplers." She claimed they were all the rage when she was a girl.

I waved that away. Samplers? Boring. Please, think harder. She pondered for another eternity, and then, with a shrug, she suggested the weather. She noted it had been particularly trying lately.

And there I am, stuck. Ever since that idiotic suggestion, I haven't been able to think of anything else.

It is, admittedly, wretched weather. At least, it is right now, and if it doesn't improve by the time you read this, it soon will.

To us city dwellers, the weather is like the government: perpetually in the wrong. In summer, it's stifling; in winter, it's lethal; in spring and autumn, it's indecisive and annoying. If it's fine, we complain the crops are suffering; if it rains, we beg for sunshine. If December passes without snow, we act as though we've been cheated out of a purchased commodity, demanding to know where our "good old-fashioned winters" have gone. And when snow finally arrives, our language becomes a disgrace to civilization. We will never be satisfied until everyone controls their own personal climate.

If that's impossible, we'd prefer to live without weather altogether.

Yet, I suspect this dissatisfaction is unique to us urbanites. Out in the countryside, Nature is delightful in every mood. Who can resist the beauty of snow falling in silent softness, blanketing the fields in white like a fairy wedding dress? Or the thrill of walking on frozen ground that rings beneath your boots, the sharp air making your blood sing, while sheepdogs bark and children laugh like Alpine bells across the hills? And skating! Gliding across the swaying ice, making whirring music. And spring! Nature at eighteen, fresh and green, like young lives peeking shyly into the world. Fruit trees blossoming like village maidens in Sunday frocks, hiding cottages in clouds of fragrant splendor, while cuckoos call through the woods. Summer, with its deep greens and drowsy hums, where raindrops whisper secrets to the leaves. And autumn! Oh, the sad beauty of it—the golden glow, the blood-red sunsets, the ghostly mists, the harvest festivals.

Even the rain, sleet, and hail seem like useful servants when they are doing their jobs in the country. Even the East Wind is just a boisterous friend between the hedgerows.

But in the city? Where painted stucco blisters under the smoky sun, and sooty rain turns the streets into slurry, and snow piles up in dirty heaps, Nature offers no charm. Weather in towns is like a skylark trapped in a counting-house—out of place and in the way. Cities should be sealed tight, heated by pipes, and lit by electricity. The weather is a country lass who looks terrible in town. We liked flirting with her in the hayfields, but she's not so fascinating when we meet her in Pall Mall. There's too much of her. Her frank laugh jars against the artificiality of city life; her ways are exhausting.

Lately, she's been favoring us with relentless rain for three weeks. As Mr. Mantalini put it, I am a "demned damp, moist, unpleasant body."

Our neighbor pops into his back garden occasionally to claim the weather is doing the country a world of good. He doesn't understand it, but since he started a cucumber frame last summer, he considers himself an agriculturist and tries to impress the terrace with this absurd notion. I only hope he's right this time, because the weather is doing me considerable damage. It's ruining my clothes and my temper. I have plenty of temper to spare, but seeing my dear old hats and trousers sink prematurely under the cold blasts is a wound to the quick.

Take my new spring suit. It was beautiful. Now it hangs there, a bespattered mess I can't bear to look at.

That was Jim's fault. I should never have worn it that night if he hadn't interfered. I was trying it on when he burst in, threw up his arms, and yelled, "Got 'em again!"

"Does it fit all right behind?" I asked.

"Spiffin, old man," he replied. Then he asked if I was going out.

"No," I said.

He overruled me. "A man with a suit like that has no right to stay indoors," he declared. "Every citizen owes a duty to the public. Come out and give the girls a treat."

Jim speaks in slang I don't know where he gets it. Certainly not from me.

"Do you think it will really please them?" I asked.

"It'll be like a day in the country for them," he said.

That decided me. It was a lovely evening, so I went.

When I got home, I undressed, rubbed myself down with whisky, soaked my feet in hot water, applied a mustard plaster to my chest, ate gruel, drank brandy-and-water, tallow my nose, and crawled into bed.

These vigorous measures, aided by a strong constitution, saved my life. But as for the suit? Well, it's no longer a suit; it's a splashboard.

I did fancy that suit. But that's just how it is. I never get attached to anything unless something dreadful happens to it. When I was a boy, I had a tame rat I loved dearly. One day it fell into a large dish of gooseberry fool cooling in the kitchen, and nobody knew what happened to it until the second helping arrived.

I hate wet weather in town. Actually, it's less the wet and more the mud. I seem to have an irresistible attraction to it. I only have to step onto a muddy street to be half-smothered by it. An old lady once said I was struck by lightning because of my attractiveness; I suppose that explains it. Others can walk for hours on dirty days without a speck, but if I cross the road, I return a disgrace. If there were even one dab of mud in all of London, I'm convinced I'd carry it off from every competitor.

I wish I could return the affection, but I fear I never will. I have a horror of the "London particular." I feel miserable and muggy all day, and it's a relief to strip off and get into bed, escaping the filth. Everything goes wrong in wet weather. There seem to be more people, dogs, prams, cabs, and carts, and they all get in your way more. Everyone is disagreeable—except me, apparently. And I always find myself carrying more things when it rains. You have a bag, three parcels, a newspaper, and then the rain starts, and you can't open your umbrella.

Which reminds me of another phase I can't stand: April weather (so-called because it always arrives in May). Poets think it's lovely. Since it changes its mind every five minutes, they compare it to a woman, claiming it's charming. I don't appreciate it. Such lightning changes might be agreeable in a girl, but in the weather, the disadvantages are glaring. A woman's tears don't soak you; rain does. Her coldness doesn't cause asthma and rheumatism; the east wind does. I can handle a consistently bad day, but these "ha'porth-of-all-sorts" days drive me mad. It aggravates me to see a bright blue sky above me while I walk through, soaking wet. There's something exasperating about the sun smiling after a drenching shower, as if to say, "Lord love you, you don't mean to say you're wet? Well, I'm surprised. Why, it was only my fun."

You don't get time to open or shut your umbrella in an English April, especially if you have an "automaton"—the umbrella, not the month.

I bought one once in April and had a nightmare. I wanted an umbrella that kept the rain off and didn't get left behind in a train.

"Try an 'automaton,'" the shopman suggested.

"What's an 'automaton'?" I asked.

"Oh, it's beautiful," he enthused. "It opens and shuts itself."

I bought one and found he was right. It opened and shut itself. I had no control whatsoever. When it rained—which it did every alternate five minutes—that season—I tried to make the machine open, but it wouldn't budge. I'd stand there shaking it and swearing while the rain poured down. Then, the moment the rain stopped, the absurd thing would jerk upward and refuse to close. I'd have to walk under a bright blue sky with an umbrella over my head, wishing it would rain again just so I wouldn't look insane.

When it did close, it did so unexpectedly, knocking my hat off.

I don't know why, but losing your hat makes a man look supremely ridiculous. The feeling of helpless misery that shoots down your back when you realize your head is bare is one of the bitterest ills of life. Then there's the wild chase, accompanied by an excited small dog thinking it's a game, during which you inevitably upset three or four innocent children (and their mothers), butt a fat old gentleman onto a pram, and carom off a ladies' seminary into the arms of a wet sweep.

After that, the idiotic hilarity of the crowd and the disreputable appearance of the recovered hat seem like minor details.

All in all, with March winds, April showers, and the total absence of May flowers, spring is a failure in cities. It's fine in the country, as I've said, but in towns with populations over ten thousand, it should be abolished. In the world's grim workshops, it's like children—out of place. Neither shows to advantage amid the dust and din. It's sad to see little dirt-grimed brats trying to play in noisy courts and muddy streets. They aren't children. Children are bright-eyed, chubby, and shy. These are dingy, screeching elves, their faces seared and withered, their laughter cracked and hoarse.

The spring of life and the spring of the year were meant to be cradled in nature's green lap. For us in the town, spring brings only cold winds and drizzle. We must seek it among leafless woods, brambly lanes, heathy moors, and great still hills if we want to feel its joyous breath. There is a glorious freshness there. The scurrying clouds, the open bleakness, the rushing wind, and the clear air thrill one with vague energies and hopes. Life, like the landscape, seems bigger, wider, freer—a rainbow road to unknown ends. Through the silvery rents in the sky, we glimpse the great hope and grandeur surrounding this little throbbing world, and a breath of its scent is wafted to us on the wings of the wild March wind.

Strange thoughts stir in our hearts that we don't understand. Voices call us to great efforts, to mighty works. But we don't comprehend their meaning yet, and the hidden echoes within us that would reply are struggling, inarticulate and dumb.
