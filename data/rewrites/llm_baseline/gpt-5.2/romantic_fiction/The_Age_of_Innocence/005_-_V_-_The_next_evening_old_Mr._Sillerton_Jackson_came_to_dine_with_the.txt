The next evening old Mr. Sillerton Jackson came to dine with the Archers.

Mrs. Archer, who had been a widow so long that the fact had the quiet air of a family custom, shrank from society even while she hungered to know its flavour; she liked information as other women liked preserves—sealed, labelled, and brought out at the right hour to be tasted in small spoonfuls. For this purpose she kept, among her few indulgences, an old friend who had turned gossip into a science. Mr. Sillerton Jackson applied to his acquaintances’ affairs the patience of a collector, the classification of a naturalist; and his sister, Miss Sophy Jackson, who lived with him and was entertained everywhere by those who could not secure the brother, brought home the lesser scraps—crusts and crumbs of hearsay—that helped complete his dish.

So, whenever anything happened that Mrs. Archer wished to have served up plainly to her and to Janey, she asked Mr. Jackson to dine. And because she honoured few people with her invitations, and because she and Janey made such an attentive audience—leaning forward, eyes bright, as if a name might carry a scent—Mr. Jackson usually came himself instead of sending Miss Sophy.

If he could have chosen all the conditions, he would have preferred the nights when Newland was out—not because the young man was uncongenial (at their club they went on capitally), but because Mr. Jackson felt, from Newland, a certain inconvenient habit of weighing evidence, of asking how a tale was cooked, while the ladies accepted it as it was set before them. Perfection, besides, would have required that Mrs. Archer’s dinners be a little better.

But New York, as far back as memory could reach, had been divided into two fundamental tribes. There were the Mingotts and the Mansons and their clan, who cared about eating, clothes, and money—who knew the season of a bird by the look of its breast, the age of a wine by the sound its cork made in leaving the bottle. And there were the Archer-Newland-van der Luyden people, who were devoted to travel, horticulture, and “the best fiction,” and looked down—gently, serenely—on the grosser forms of pleasure, as if appetite were something one ought to keep decorously in the pantry.

You could not have everything. If you dined with the Lovell Mingotts you got canvas-back and terrapin and vintage wines that spoke, on the tongue, of distant cellars and sea-voyages. At Adeline Archer’s you could talk about Alpine scenery and The Marble Faun; and luckily the Archer Madeira had indeed gone round the Cape and come home with a briny depth, as if it remembered the creak of rigging and the long breath of ocean.

Therefore when Mrs. Archer’s summons came, Mr. Jackson, true eclectic, would say to his sister: “I’ve been a little gouty since my last dinner at the Lovell Mingotts’—it will do me good to diet at Adeline’s.”

Mrs. Archer lived with her son and daughter in West Twenty-eighth Street. An upper floor was given over to Newland, and the two women squeezed themselves into narrower quarters below, as if the house were a loaf and the best slice had properly gone to the man. They passed their days in an unclouded harmony of tastes and interests: they cultivated ferns in Wardian cases, whose damp glass held the smell of dark soil and sprouting life; they made macramé lace and wool embroidery on linen, their fingers busy while their minds wandered through imagined landscapes; they collected American revolutionary glazed ware—plates that held, in their crazed surfaces, the faint echo of old kitchens; they subscribed to Good Words; they read Ouida for the Italian atmosphere, preferring the peasant novels because the scenery was ample and the sentiments pleasanter. They liked novels about society too, where motives and habits were more comprehensible; they spoke severely of Dickens, who “had never drawn a gentleman,” and thought Thackeray less at home in the great world than Bulwer—though Bulwer was beginning to be considered old-fashioned.

They were great lovers of scenery. It was what they chiefly sought abroad; architecture and painting they left to men, and especially to those learned persons who read Ruskin.

Mrs. Archer had been born a Newland; she and Janey, alike as sisters, were “true Newlands”—tall, pale, slightly round-shouldered, with long noses, sweet smiles, and a drooping distinction like that in certain faded Reynolds portraits. Their resemblance would have been complete had not an elderly embonpoint stretched Mrs. Archer’s black brocade, while Janey’s brown and purple poplins hung, year by year, more slackly on her unclaimed frame.

Mentally the likeness was less perfect than their identical turns of phrase suggested. The long habit of living together in mutually dependent intimacy had given them the same vocabulary, the same habit of beginning sentences with “Mother thinks” or “Janey thinks,” according as one or the other wished to float her own opinion as if it had been brewed in a common pot. Yet Mrs. Archer’s serene unimaginativeness rested easily in the accepted and familiar; Janey was subject to little starts and aberrations of fancy, romantic bubbles rising from springs she kept carefully covered.

They adored each other and revered their son and brother. Newland loved them with a tenderness made compunctious and uncritical by the sense of their exaggerated admiration, and by his secret satisfaction in it. After all, he thought it good for a man to have his authority respected in his own house, even if his sense of humour sometimes made him smile at the solemnity of his own decrees.

This evening he was sure Mr. Jackson would rather have had him dine out. But Newland had his own reasons for staying.

Of course old Jackson wanted to talk about Ellen Olenska. Of course Mrs. Archer and Janey wanted to hear what he could tell. All three would be a little embarrassed by Newland’s presence now that his prospective relation to the Mingott clan was known; and he waited, with amused curiosity, to see how they would work around the difficulty—how they would set the dish down without naming it.

They began obliquely, with Mrs. Lemuel Struthers.

“It’s a pity the Beauforts asked her,” Mrs. Archer said gently, as if she were regretting a spoiled custard. “But then Regina always does what he tells her; and Beaufort—”

“Certain nuances escape Beaufort,” Mr. Jackson observed, cautiously inspecting the broiled shad. He had the patient, faintly despairing look of a connoisseur confronted with earnest ignorance. For the thousandth time he wondered why Mrs. Archer’s cook always burnt the roe to a cinder—turning what should have been creamy, saline richness into a bitter charcoal that smelled faintly of over-hot iron. Newland, who had long shared his wonder, could always detect it in the older man’s expression of melancholy disapproval.

“Oh, necessarily; Beaufort is a vulgar man,” Mrs. Archer said. “My grandfather Newland always used to say to my mother: ‘Whatever you do, don’t let that fellow Beaufort be introduced to the girls.’ But at least he’s had the advantage of associating with gentlemen; in England too, they say. It’s all very mysterious—” She glanced at Janey and paused. She and Janey knew every fold of the Beaufort mystery, but in public Mrs. Archer continued to behave as though certain subjects, like certain foods, were not for the unmarried.

“But this Mrs. Struthers,” she resumed; “what did you say she was, Sillerton?”

“Out of a mine,” Mr. Jackson answered, “or rather out of the saloon at the head of the pit. Then with Living Wax-Works, touring New England. After the police broke that up, they say she lived—” He in his turn glanced at Janey, whose eyes began to bulge with avid curiosity from under her prominent lids. There were still gaps in Mrs. Struthers’s past where Janey’s imagination could wander like a child into a pantry.

“Then,” Mr. Jackson continued—and Newland saw him, even while speaking, note with inward irritation that the cucumbers had been sliced with a steel knife, giving them that faint metallic tang which no amount of vinegar can disguise—“then Lemuel Struthers came along. They say his advertiser used the girl’s head for the shoe-polish posters; her hair’s intensely black, you know—the Egyptian style. Anyhow, he—eventually—married her.”

There were volumes of innuendo in the spacing of that “eventually,” each syllable weighed as carefully as spice.

“Oh, well—at the pass we’ve come to nowadays, it doesn’t matter,” Mrs. Archer returned, with an indifference too deliberate not to betray that her mind was elsewhere.

The ladies were not interested in Mrs. Struthers. Ellen Olenska was too new, too exciting a flavour. Indeed, Mrs. Struthers had been introduced only that Mrs. Archer might soon say, with the least possible appearance of eagerness: “And Newland’s new cousin—Countess Olenska? Was she at the ball too?”

There was a faint touch of sarcasm in her reference to her son, and he knew it and had expected it. Even Mrs. Archer, who was seldom unduly pleased by human events, had been altogether glad of Newland’s engagement—especially after “that silly business with Mrs. Rushworth,” as she had once remarked to Janey, alluding to what had seemed to Newland, at the time, a tragedy that would scar him for life.

There was no better match in New York than May Welland, whichever way one looked at it. Such a marriage was only what Newland was entitled to; but young men were foolish and incalculable, and some women ensnaring and unscrupulous. To see one’s only son safe past the Siren Isle and in the haven of blameless domesticity seemed almost miraculous.

Newland knew his mother felt all this; he knew also that she had been perturbed by the premature announcement of the engagement, or rather by its cause. And because on the whole he was a tender and indulgent master, he had stayed at home tonight.

“It’s not that I don’t approve of the Mingotts’ esprit de corps,” Mrs. Archer had grumbled to Janey in private, “but why Newland’s engagement should be mixed up with that Olenska woman’s comings and goings I don’t see.” She had behaved beautifully—beautiful behaviour was her element—during the call on Mrs. Welland; but Newland knew (and May likely guessed) that all through the visit his mother and sister had been nervously on watch for Madame Olenska’s possible intrusion. And when they left the Wellands she had permitted herself: “I’m thankful that Augusta Welland received us alone.”

The inward disturbance moved Newland the more because he, too, felt the Mingotts had gone a little too far. Yet it was against every rule of their code that mother and son should ever touch what lay uppermost in their thoughts. So he had merely answered: “There’s always a phase of family parties to be gone through when one gets engaged, and the sooner it’s over the better.”

His mother had pursed her lips under the lace veil that hung from her grey velvet bonnet trimmed with frosted grapes.

Now he sensed her lawful revenge: she would draw Mr. Jackson out on Countess Olenska before him; and having publicly done his duty as a future member of the Mingott clan, he had no objection to hearing the lady discussed in private—except that the subject was already beginning to weary him with its predictable vibrations.

Mr. Jackson helped himself to a slice of the tepid filet the mournful butler offered with a sceptical look, and rejected the mushroom sauce after a scarcely perceptible sniff. He looked baffled and hungry, as though he would have to finish his meal on scandal.

He leaned back, glanced up at the candlelit faces of Archers, Newlands and van der Luydens in their dark frames on the dark walls—ancestral mouths set in permanent discretion, as if every portrait were listening and disapproving.

“Ah, how your grandfather Archer loved a good dinner, my dear Newland!” he remarked, his eyes resting on the plump, full-chested young man in a stock and blue coat, with a white-columned country-house behind him. “Well—well—well… I wonder what he would have said to all these foreign marriages!”

Mrs. Archer ignored the allusion to ancestral appetite. Mr. Jackson, with deliberate satisfaction in prolonging what his audience had come for, continued: “No, she was not at the ball.”

“Ah—” Mrs. Archer murmured, in a tone meaning: She had that decency.

“Perhaps the Beauforts don’t know her,” Janey suggested, with an artless malice that was almost a spice.

Mr. Jackson gave a faint sip, as though tasting invisible Madeira, letting its imagined warmth travel down his throat. “Mrs. Beaufort may not—but Beaufort certainly does, for she was seen walking up Fifth Avenue this afternoon with him by the whole of New York.”

“Mercy—” moaned Mrs. Archer, as if the sound alone of Fifth Avenue had become louder, harsher, more exposed with that image: carriages rattling, voices rising, the city’s public ear pressed to their private acts.

“I wonder if she wears a round hat or a bonnet in the afternoon,” Janey speculated. “At the Opera I know she had on dark blue velvet, perfectly plain and flat—like a night-gown.”

“Janey!” her mother warned; and Janey blushed, then tried to look audacious.

“It was, at any rate, in better taste not to go to the ball,” Mrs. Archer went on.

A spirit of perversity stirred Newland. “I don’t think it was a question of taste with her. May said she meant to go, and then decided the dress in question wasn’t smart enough.”

Mrs. Archer smiled, satisfied to have her inference confirmed. “Poor Ellen,” she said simply; then, compassionately: “We must always bear in mind what an eccentric bringing-up Medora Manson gave her. What can you expect of a girl who was allowed to wear black satin at her coming-out ball?”

“Ah—don’t I remember her in it!” Mr. Jackson said, and added “Poor girl!” in the tone of one who had enjoyed the omen even as he recognized it.

“It’s odd,” Janey ventured, “that she should have kept such an ugly name as Ellen. I should have changed it to Elaine.” She glanced about the table to see what effect this small poetic rebellion might have.

Newland laughed. “Why Elaine?”

“I don’t know; it sounds more—more Polish,” Janey said, blushing at her own daring.

“It sounds more conspicuous,” Mrs. Archer replied distantly; “and that can hardly be what she wishes.”

“Why not?” Newland broke in, suddenly argumentative, as though a word—Ellen, Elaine—were not a trifle but a kind of chain. “Why shouldn’t she be conspicuous if she chooses? Why should she slink about as if it were she who had disgraced herself? She’s ‘poor Ellen’ certainly, because she had the bad luck to make a wretched marriage; but I don’t see that that’s a reason for hiding her head as if she were the culprit.”

“That, I suppose,” Mr. Jackson said speculatively, “is the line the Mingotts mean to take.”

Newland reddened. “I didn’t have to wait for their cue, if that’s what you mean, sir. Madame Olenska has had an unhappy life: that doesn’t make her an outcast.”

“There are rumours,” Mr. Jackson began, glancing at Janey.

“Oh, I know: the secretary,” Newland cut in. “Nonsense, mother; Janey’s grown-up. They say, don’t they, that the secretary helped her to get away from her brute of a husband, who kept her practically a prisoner? Well—what if he did? I hope there isn’t a man among us who wouldn’t have done the same in such a case.”

Mr. Jackson glanced over his shoulder to the sad butler and said, with an air of concession that was almost humorous: “Perhaps… that sauce… just a little, after all—” He helped himself, as if resigning to what was offered rather than desiring it, and then returned calmly to his point. “I’m told she’s looking for a house. She means to live here.”

“I hear she means to get a divorce,” Janey announced boldly, her voice a little too clear in the restrained room, as if a window had been opened on a street of louder sounds.

“I hope she will!” Newland exclaimed.

The word fell like a bombshell into the pure, tranquil atmosphere of the Archer dining-room. It seemed to ring, to have an echo of its own, as though even the heavy curtains could not fully muffle it. Mrs. Archer’s delicate eyebrows rose in the particular curve that signified: The butler—

And Newland, suddenly aware of the indecency of discussing such matters in public, branched off hastily into an account of his visit to old Mrs. Mingott, as if changing the subject were a napkin thrown over a stain.

After dinner, according to immemorial custom, Mrs. Archer and Janey trailed their long silk draperies up to the drawing-room. There, while the gentlemen smoked below stairs, they sat beside a Carcel lamp with an engraved globe, facing each other across a rosewood work-table with a green silk bag under it, and stitched at two ends of a tapestry band of field-flowers meant to adorn an “occasional” chair in the drawing-room of young Mrs. Newland Archer. Their needles made small, steady sounds; the lamp made a faint, confidential hiss; and in that soft domestic acoustics their hands translated colour into pattern—petals and stems that would outlast the day’s talk even if no one could ever name what, exactly, had been felt while making them.

Downstairs, in the Gothic library, Newland settled Mr. Jackson in an armchair near the fire and handed him a cigar. Mr. Jackson sank back with satisfaction, lit it with perfect confidence—Newland bought them—and stretched his thin old ankles toward the coals.

“You say the secretary merely helped her to get away, my dear fellow?” he asked. “Well, he was still helping her a year later, then; for somebody met ’em living at Lausanne together.”

Newland reddened. “Living together? Well, why not? Who had the right to make her life over if she hadn’t? I’m sick of the hypocrisy that would bury alive a woman of her age if her husband prefers to live with harlots.” He stopped, turned away angrily to light his cigar, as if the flame could cauterize his impatience. “Women ought to be free—as free as we are,” he declared, making a discovery whose consequences he was too irritated to measure.

Mr. Sillerton Jackson edged his ankles nearer the coals and emitted a sardonic whistle—thin, sharp, a little like the winter wind finding a crack in a window-frame.

“Well,” he said at length, “apparently Count Olenski takes your view; for I never heard of his having lifted a finger to get his wife back.”
