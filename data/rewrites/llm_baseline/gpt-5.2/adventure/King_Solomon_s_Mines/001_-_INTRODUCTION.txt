FOUND PREPARER’S NOTE

This was typed from a 1907 edition published by Cassell and Company, Limited.

DEDICATION

This faithful but unpretending record of a remarkable adventure is hereby respectfully dedicated by the narrator, ALLAN QUATERMAIN, to all the big and little boys who read it.

AUTHOR’S NOTE

The author takes this quiet moment—as one might step aside from a bright, clattering dining-room into a cool pantry where the scents of pepper, dried herbs, and stored fruit hang in the dark—to thank his readers for the generous welcome they have given to the successive editions of this tale over the last twelve years. He hopes that in its present shape it may pass into still more hands, and that, in years to come, it will keep its savour for those who remain young enough at heart to hunger for a story of treasure, war, and wild adventure.

Ditchingham, 11 March, 1898.

POST SCRIPTUM

Now, in 1907, as this edition is set forth again, I can add only this: it pleases me to know the romance has continued to satisfy so many, not as a gaudy sweet sold at a fair, but as a plain, sustaining dish whose flavour deepens when it is warmed and served a second time—yes, even when it reaches the table of another household altogether.

The curious thing is that imagination has been verified by fact. The King Solomon’s Mines that I once dreamed of have indeed been found, and they are giving up their gold once more, and, by the latest reports, their diamonds also. The Kukuanas—or, more truly, the Matabele—have been subdued by the white man’s bullets; the loud, harsh sound of that mastery has travelled farther than any song, and where it passes, older words and older ways grow faint, like a dialect spoken lower and lower until even the children cannot catch it. Yet still, despite the din of modern doings and the coarse advertising of wonder, there seem to be many who find pleasure in these simple pages.

May they continue to do so—even to the third and fourth generation, or perhaps longer still—would, I am sure, have been the hope of our old and departed friend, Allan Quatermain, who knew better than most that what a man has lived cannot be perfectly translated into what he can tell, and that some part of the taste and sound of it must always remain his alone.

H. Rider Haggard.

Ditchingham, 1907.
