There is a peculiar magic in the memory of university days, a golden haze that clings to every man, no matter how brief or unglorious his academic tenure. It is the era when the boy's leading strings are severed, and he rushes into the novel, dizzying freedom of adulthood. He knows nothing yet of cares, ill health, roguery, poverty, or the crushing weight of tomorrow's disappointments. The play is fresh, unacted, and vibrant. While the after-drink of life eventually turns stale and bitter, that first sparkling draught of pleasure remains pure and brilliant in the mind. Even the most seasoned epicure, restricted now to a poached egg and a glass of water, finds joy in watching others feast; and surely, no amount of age or cynicism should make a man so glum that he cannot smile at the sight of happy youth.

I recall returning recently from a visit to the ancient universities of Oxbridge, where my friend Mr. Arthur Pendennis once spent a significant portion of his life. On the railway, I sat beside a young student of Saint Boniface who had somehow secured an exeat for a day's lark in London. This honest lad never ceased rattling on from the moment we departed until the train screeched to a halt—a performance so relentless it nearly drove me to distraction, though I confess I was never tired of his jokes and cheery laughter. Upon arriving at the terminus, he demanded a hansom cab immediately, eager to plunge into the pleasures awaiting him. Away he went, whirling through the streets with joy lighting up his face. As for me, burdened only by a small carpet-bag, I climbed onto the outside of the omnibus and sat contentedly sandwiched between a Jewish pedlar smoking foul cigars and a gentleman's servant guarding a poodle-dog. We drifted leisurely toward the city, neither of us in a rush to join the smoking Babylon of town, nor thinking of club dinners or casino dances. Yet, a few years later, my young friend of the railway would likely feel no less eager than I do now.

Railroads were nonexistent when Arthur Pendennis attended the famous University of Oxbridge. Instead, he traveled in a well-appointed coach, crammed with dons, gownsmen, prospective freshmen, and their guardians. Inside sat a fat old gentleman from the City in grey stockings, accompanied by his pale-faced son, who was terrified to learn the coach was being driven by young Mr. Foker of Saint Boniface. Foker was the friend of all men, including coachmen, and could drive with the skill of Tom Hicks himself. Perched on the roof, Pen examined the coach, the passengers, and the countryside with boundless delight. His heart leaped as the university came into view, the magnificent prospect of venerable towers, pinnacles, tall elms, and the shining river spreading before him.

Before setting out, Pen had spent a few days with his uncle at the Major's lodgings in Bury Street. The Major felt the lad's wardrobe needed renewal, and Arthur was only too happy to accept any plan involving new coats and waistcoats. The self-denying uncle spared no sacrifice for the youth. London felt awfully lonely; the Pall Mall pavement was deserted, the red jackets had fled the city, and the club windows were empty. The Major escorted his nephew into a couple of these ghostly mansions and wrote Arthur's name on the candidate list of one. Pen's pleasure at this gesture of his guardian was excessive. He read his name and titles in the parchment volume—"Arthur Pendennis, Esquire, of Fairoaks Lodge, —shire and Saint Boniface College, Oxbridge; proposed by Major Pendennis, and seconded by Viscount Colchicum"—with a thrill of intense gratification. "You will come in for ballot in about three years, by which time you will have taken your degree," the guardian noted. Pen longed for those three years to vanish, surveying the stucco halls, vast libraries, and drawing-rooms as if they were already his private property. The Major laughed slyly at the pompous airs of the simple young fellow as he strutted out.

Later, the Major and Foker drove down to the Grey Friars in the latter's cab to renew acquaintance with old comrades. Boys crowded around the cab, admiring the chestnut horse and the gravity of Stoopid, the tiger. As the bell for afternoon school rang, the awful Doctor passed into the school with his grammar book. Foker slunk away uneasily at his presence, but Pen went up blushing, shaking the dignitary's hand, laughing as he recalled how that well-remembered Latin Grammar had boxed his ears many a time. Generous, good-natured, and perfectly conceited, Pen was thoroughly satisfied with himself.

Their next stop was the parental brew-house. Foker's Entire was an enormous pile of buildings near the Grey Friars, its name gilded on innumerable signs. The venerable junior partner honored the young lord of the vats and his friend, serving them silver flagons of brown-stout so potent that one might think the very horse Mr. Harry Foker drove was affected by the potency. Stoopid rushed home at a pace that endangered pie-stalls and women on crossings, causing the cab steps to collide with posts and the horse to swing fearfully on his board.

The Major was delighted to see Pen with his young acquaintance, listening intently to Mr. Foker's artless stories. He treated the two boys to a fine dinner at a Covent Garden Coffee-house before proceeding to the play. But his greatest happiness came when Mr. and Lady Agnes Foker, visiting London, invited the Major and Arthur Pendennis to dinner in Grosvenor Street. "Having obtained the entree into Lady Agnes Foker's house," the Major told Pen with affectionate solemnity, "it behoves you to keep it. Never neglect to call in Grosvenor Street. Read up carefully in Debrett on the alliances of the Earls of Rosherville, and make some trifling, historical, complimentary allusions. You, with your poetic fancy, can do this well." He added that Mr. Foker always insisted on serving family porter after dinner, which one must never refuse, even though the Major himself confessed that all beer disagreed with him. True to his word, the heroic martyr sacrificed himself on the day of the dinner, drinking the porter while old Mr. Foker made his time-honored joke about Foker's Entire. We should all have loved to see the Major's grin at the worthy old gentleman's jest.

Lady Agnes, wrapped up in her son Harry, was the fondest of mothers and one of the most good-natured, if not the wisest, of women. She received her son's friend with great cordiality, astonishing Pen with accounts of the severe studies her darling boy pursued, fearing they might injure his health. Foker the elder burst into a horse-laugh at these speeches, and the heir winked knowingly at his friend. Lady Agnes then recounted her son's history from the earliest time—his miraculous escapes from drowning, his suffering from measles and hooping-cough, and the tyrannies of the horrid school where Mr. Foker had sent him. She never forgave that disagreeable Doctor. After an hour of incessant prattling about her son, she voted the two Messieurs Pendennis most agreeable men. When pheasants arrived with the second course—which the Major knew perfectly well came from Logwood—she hoped they would both visit there at Christmas or during Harry's vacations.

"God bless you, my dear boy," Pendennis said to Arthur as they lit their candles in Bury Street to go to bed. "You made that little allusion to Agincourt, where one of the Roshervilles distinguished himself, very neatly, although Lady Agnes did not quite understand it. It was exceedingly well for a beginner—though you oughtn't to blush so, by the way. Remember through life that with a good entree, it is just as easy to have good society as bad. It costs a man, when properly introduced, no more trouble to keep a footing in the best houses in London than to dine with a lawyer in Bedford Square. Be particular in the acquaintances you make. The premier pas in life is the most important of all. Did you write to your mother today? No? Well, do so before you go, and ask Mr. Foker for a frank. They like it. Good night. God bless you."

Pen wrote a droll account of his doings in London, the play, the visit to the old Friars, the brewery, and the party at Mr. Foker's, to his dearest mother, who was saying her prayers at home in the lonely house at Fairoaks, her heart full of unutterable love and tenderness for the boy. She and Laura read those letters many times, brooding over them as women do. It was the first step in life Pen was making—Ah! what a dangerous journey it is, and how the bravest may stumble and the strongest fail. Brother wayfarer! May you have a kind arm to support yours on the path, and a friendly hand to succour those who fall beside you. May truth guide, mercy forgive at the end, and love accompany always. Without that lamp, how blind the traveler would be, and how black and cheerless the journey!

So the coach drove up to the ancient and comfortable inn, the Trencher, in Main Street, Oxbridge. For the first time, Pen remarked with delight and eagerness the gownsmen going about, the chapel bells clinking from morning-tide to even-song, and the towers and pinnacles rising calm and stately over the gables of the homely busy city. Previous communications had taken place between Dr. Portman on Pen's part and Mr. Buck, Tutor of Boniface, on whose side Pen was entered. Once the Major had arranged his personal appearance to make a satisfactory impression on the tutor, they walked down Main Street, passed the great gate and belfry-tower of Saint George's College, and arrived at Saint Boniface. Again, Pen's heart began to beat as they entered the wicket of the venerable ivy-mantled gate. It was surmounted by an ancient dome almost covered with creepers and adorned with the effigy of the Saint from whom the House takes its name, along with many coats-of-arms of its royal and noble benefactors.

The porter pointed out a queer old tower at the corner of the quadrangle where Mr. Buck's rooms were approached. Walking across the square, the main features were instantly and forever stamped in Pen's mind: the pretty fountain playing in the center of the fair grass plats; the tall chapel windows and buttresses rising to the right; the hall with its tapering lantern and oriel window; the lodge from which the Master issued with rustling silks; and the lines of surrounding rooms broken by carved chimneys, grey turrets, and quaint gables. Pen drank in these sights with an eagerness belonging to first impressions, while the Major surveyed them with the calmness of a gentleman who does not care for the picturesque and whose eyes have been somewhat dimmed by the constant glare of the Pall Mall pavement.

Saint George's is the great College of the University, with its four vast quadrangles, beautiful hall, gardens, and the Georgians, who wear gowns of a peculiar cut and give themselves airs of superiority over all other young men. Little Saint Boniface is but a petty hermitage compared to the huge consecrated pile alongside it. Yet, considering its size, it has always kept an excellent name. Its tone is very good; the best families of certain counties have sent up their young men there for generations. The college livings are remarkably good, the fellowships easy, and the Boniface men have had more than their fair share of university honors. Their boat was third upon the river, their chapel-choir not inferior to Saint George's itself, and the Boniface ale the best in Oxbridge. In the comfortable old wainscoted College-Hall, round about Roubilliac's statue of Saint Boniface (who stands in an attitude of seraphic benediction over the uncommonly good cheer of the fellows' table), hang portraits of many eminent Bonifacians. There is the learned Doctor Griddle, who suffered in Henry VIII.'s time, and Archbishop Bush who roasted him; Lord Chief Justice Hicks; the Duke of St. David's, K.G., Chancellor of the University and Member of this College; Sprott the Poet, of whose fame the college is justly proud; Doctor Blogg, the late master and friend of Doctor Johnson, who visited him at Saint Boniface; and other lawyers, scholars, and divines whose portraitures look from the walls or whose coats-of-arms shine in emerald, ruby, gold, and azure in the tall windows of the refectory. The venerable cook of the college is one of the best artists in Oxbridge (his son took the highest honors in the other University of Camford), and the wine in the fellows' room has long been famed for its excellence and abundance.

Into this certainly not the least snugly sheltered arbour amongst the groves of Academe, Pen now found his way, leaning on his uncle's arm. They speedily reached Mr. Buck's rooms and were conducted into the apartment of that courteous gentleman. He had received previous information from Dr. Portman regarding Pen, whose family, fortune, and personal merits the honest Doctor had described with no small enthusiasm. Indeed, Portman had described Arthur to the tutor as "a young gentleman of some fortune and landed estate, of one of the most ancient families in the kingdom, and possessing such a character and genius as were sure, under the proper guidance, to make him a credit to the college and the university." Under such recommendations, the tutor was most cordial to the young freshman and his guardian, inviting the latter to dine in hall to see his nephew wear his gown and eat his dinner for the first time.
