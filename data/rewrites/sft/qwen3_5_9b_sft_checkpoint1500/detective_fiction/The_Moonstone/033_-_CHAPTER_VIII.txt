"Behold!" Mr. Godfrey declared, his voice trembling with a strange mixture of relief and bewilderment, "I have squandered a beautiful girl, forfeited a respectable station in society, and surrendered a comfortable income. And yet, I have endured this catastrophe without a single murmur or struggle. Pray, what motive could possibly drive one to such extraordinary conduct? My dearest friend, there is no motive."

"No motive?" I echoed, leaning forward with the eager curiosity of a scholar confronting a fresh puzzle.

"Calm yourself, Miss Clack," he urged, his eyes pleading. "Allow me to draw upon your vast experience regarding children. A child follows a path, and you marvel at it, seeking the 'why.' Yet the little angel is utterly incapable of articulating its reason. To ask a child why it behaves thus is like asking the grass why it grows or the bird why it sings. In this matter, I am precisely like the child, like the grass, like the bird. I am at a loss to explain why I proposed to Miss Verinder. I cannot fathom why I have abandoned my duties to the Ladies. I cannot comprehend why I have deserted the Mothers' Small-Clothes. When you ask a child, 'Why have you been naughty?' the innocent soul merely sticks a finger in its mouth and remains silent. That is my exact condition, Miss Clack! I could not confide this to any other soul, yet I feel compelled to unburden myself to *you*!"

My composure returned, bolstered by the thrill of a genuine intellectual challenge. Mental problems fascinated me, and I prided myself on possessing the requisite acumen to solve them.

"Friends, exert your faculties and assist me," he beseeched. "Explain to me: at what juncture do matrimonial proceedings begin to resemble a waking nightmare? Why does it suddenly dawn upon me that true felicity lies in serving my dear Ladies, in performing my modest rounds of charity, and in uttering my few earnest words when summoned by my Chairman? What need do I have of a position? I possess one! What craving do I have for an income? Am I not content to purchase my bread, cheese, and lodgings, along with two coats annually? What desire do I harbor for Miss Verinder? She has explicitly stated, though I whisper this as a confidence between friends, that she adores another man and views our union solely as a strategy to displace him from her thoughts. How terrible is such a bond! Alas, what a dreadful alliance!"

He sighed, his magnificent head drooping onto his chest as he succumbed to despair over his own internal contradictions.

I was profoundly moved. To me, acting as a spiritual physician, the diagnosis was clear. It is not rare to witness those of elevated ability humbled to the level of the most ordinary folk around them. Presumably, within the divine economy, this serves as a reminder that greatness is mortal and that the power granting it can likewise strip it away. Thus, I perceived in Mr. Godfrey's actions a salutary humiliation, stripping away his vanity. Equally evident was the resurgence of his nobler nature, manifested in his horror at the prospect of marrying Rachel and his ardent longing to return to his charitable works.

"I share your sentiments," I assured him in simple, sisterly tones. "Welcome back, dear friend."

His joy was radiant. As I likened him to a wanderer emerging from darkness into light, he embraced the metaphor wholeheartedly. When I promised a warm reception from the Mothers' Small-Clothes, his gratitude overflowed. He pressed my hands alternately to his lips, overwhelmed by the exquisite triumph of regaining my esteem. So absorbed was I in the spiritual ecstasy of restoring him to the fold that I nearly swooned into his embrace, had not a harsh, metallic clatter from outside the door interrupted the moment. A footman entered to set the table for luncheon.

Mr. Godfrey sprang upright, glancing nervously at the mantel clock.

"How swiftly time elapses!" he cried. "I shall scarce catch the train."

I dared to ask why he hastened so urgently back to town. His response alluded to lingering familial complications and impending discord.

"My father has written," he explained. "Business compels him to depart Frizinghall for London today, and he intends to join me here, either tonight or tomorrow. I must inform him of what transpired between Rachel and me. His heart is fixed on our marriage; I fear significant difficulty in convincing him to dissolve the engagement. I must intercept him, for the sake of all concerned, before he arrives. Let us meet again, my dearest friend!"

With that, he departed in a flurry. Simultaneously, I ascended the stairs to compose myself in my chamber prior to joining Aunt Ablewhite and Rachel at the luncheon table.

Regarding Mr. Godfrey, I am acutely aware that the profane world attributes his decision to release Rachel to selfish motives, seizing the first opportunity afforded. Furthermore, rumors suggest his eagerness to restore his standing in my eyes stems from a mercenary desire to appease a venerable committee-woman of the Mothers' Small-Clothes—a woman rich in worldly goods and intimately connected to me. I mention these slanders solely to affirm that they held zero sway over my judgment. In strict adherence to my principles, I have chronicled every fluctuation in my perception of our Christian hero in my diary. By divine justice, once restored to my favor, he never lost that place again. Though I write with tears, driven to speak further, I am constrained by reality. Within a month, financial exigencies in the market, which eroded even my meager income, forced me into exile abroad. All I retain is a cherished memory of Mr. Godfrey, undiminished by the world's vicious slander.

Let me wipe away these tears and resume my narrative.

Descending for luncheon, I was naturally anxious to gauge Rachel's reaction to her emancipation from the marriage contract.

To my observation, albeit lacking expertise in such nuances, her newfound liberty seemed to reignite thoughts of the man she truly loved. She appeared furious with herself, struggling against a revulsion of feelings she secretly despised. Who was this man? Suspicions formed in my mind, though speculating idly served no purpose. Once I had fully converted her, as was inevitable, she would reveal all her secrets, including the identity of the suitor and the truth surrounding the Moonstone. Even without my higher spiritual objectives, the mere prospect of lifting the burden of her guilt would have sufficed as motivation for my efforts.

In the afternoon, Aunt Ablewhite exercised in an invalid chair, accompanied by Rachel. "I wish I could drag this chair!" Rachel exclaimed recklessly. "I wish I could exhaust myself until I collapsed."

Her mood persisted into the evening. While perusing a publication of mine, the *Life, Letters, and Labours of Miss Jane Ann Stamper*, forty-fourth edition, I found passages strikingly pertinent to Rachel's plight. Upon suggesting I read them aloud, she retreated to the piano. One must imagine how little acquaintance she possessed with serious souls if she assumed my patience could be worn down by music! I retained the volume, waiting with unshakeable faith for the unfolding of events.

Old Mr. Ablewhite failed to appear that night. However, I was cognizant of the immense value his worldly greed placed upon his son's union with Miss Verinder. I harbored a firm conviction that, despite Mr. Godfrey's efforts, the patriarch would arrive the following day. With his intervention, the storm I anticipated would inevitably break, leading to the salutary collapse of Rachel's resistance. I was not unaware that Old Mr. Ablewhite enjoyed a reputation for amiability, particularly among his inferiors. In my observation, however, this benevolence lasted only so long as he maintained his own way.

As predicted, the next day Aunt Ablewhite was nearly astounded by her husband's sudden arrival. Almost immediately, he was followed, to my own surprise, by an unexpected complication: Mr. Bruff.

I never felt the presence of a solicitor so unwelcome as I did at that precise moment. He appeared ready to initiate any obstructive procedure, seemingly capable of maintaining peace with Rachel for either party.

"This is a delightful surprise, sir," said Mr. Ablewhite, addressing Mr. Bruff with his characteristic, deceptive cordiality. "Upon departing your office yesterday, I did not anticipate the honor of seeing you at Brighton today."

"I have been reflecting upon our conversation," Mr. Bruff replied smoothly. "It occurred to me that I might prove of service on this occasion. I arrived just in time to catch the train, having missed the opportunity to locate your carriage."

Offering this explanation, he seated himself beside Rachel. I withdrew modestly to a corner, clutching *Miss Jane Ann Stamper* in case of emergency. Aunt Ablewhite sat by the window, fanning herself placidly as usual. Old Mr. Ablewhite stood in the center of the room, his bald head flushed a deep pink, and addressed his niece with feigned affection.

"Rachel, my dear," he intoned, "I have received extraordinary news concerning Godfrey. I am here to inquire into the matter. Since you occupy a sitting-room in this house, will you condescend to guide me to it?"

Rachel remained motionless. Whether she intended to precipitate a crisis or acted upon a private signal from Mr. Bruff, I could not discern. She refused to escort Old Mr. Ablewhite to her chambers.
