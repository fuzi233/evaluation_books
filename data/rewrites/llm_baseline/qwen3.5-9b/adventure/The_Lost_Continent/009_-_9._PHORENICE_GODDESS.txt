The tunnel ahead, though its mouth had been cunningly concealed by human ingenuity, was one of the ancient arteries through which the fiery blood of our Mother, the Earth, once flowed. Centuries had passed since it carried rivers of molten rock, yet the air within remained warm and thick with sulfur. There was no reason to linger in such a place.

I struck a match against a lantern resting in a prepared niche and hurried onward, coughing violently. I wished desperately for the herbal remedies that soothe a throat prone to catarrh. Alas, the entire packet of medicines—the only spoils I had managed to salvage from the Vice-Royalty of Yucatan—had been lost in the naval engagement with Dason's fleet. Since landing in Atlantis, there had been scant time to indulge in the refinements of medicine.

The network of subterranean veins branched wildly. Had any of the other Six Priests stumbled into these depths by accident, they would have perished hopelessly in the labyrinthine twists or fallen into the boiling pits leading to the Earth's core. Fortunately, I carried the chart of the true course etched firmly in my memory, a relic from my initiation twenty years prior. Back then, as an appointed Viceroy, I was elevated to the highest rank within our Clan, entrusted with its deepest secrets and tools.

The journey was arduous. The floor was monstrously uneven, the air foul, and I knew daylight was fading before the signs indicated I had passed beneath the city walls and entered the sacred precincts. Here, the oath of the Seven Priests hindered my progress: under no circumstance, however desperate, was egress permitted from this passage until a mortal eye confirmed safe arrival.

Branch after branch I explored, finding loiterers guarding every exit. I had hoped to emerge via the path leading inside the Royal Pyramid, but the area hummed with activity like a beehive; stealth was impossible. The same held true for the five subsequent outlets I checked. The city was gripped by a strange, feverish excitement.

Finally, I arrived at the temple of a minor deity. Standing behind the statue, I observed the interior. It was empty—not merely vacant, but abandoned long ago, evidenced by the thick layer of dust coating the floors and the seats of the worshippers. Satisfied, I moved the necessary objects, slipped out, and sealed the entrance behind me.

A broom lay neglected on a pew. Using it, I quickly erased all traces of my footprints and approached the main temple door. It was shut tight. Even as a Priest, I lacked the secret to open it. Only the attending clergy possessed the knowledge to manipulate the mechanism controlling the massive stone slab blocking the doorway. If everyone had fled to witness whatever spectacle was stirring the populace, I was trapped.

There was no sign of life within the temple grounds. Decay and disuse littered every corner. Yet, by ancient law, at least one priest should have remained on watch in the residential quarters located atop the temple structure. Down the dark passages leading to these roofs, I ventured. It would have surprised me little to find them deserted as well. I knew the old order had crumbled, realizing only now just how ruthlessly it had been swept away and replaced by the new regime. Still, amidst an age of general apostasy, faithful souls must remain.

Reaching the door of the dwelling, I called out. Soon, it opened.

The figure peering dully through the gloom was Ro. He had kept his vows, and I greeted him with respect. We had studied together in the Sacred College, though he was always known as "the dullard." He possessed little aptitude for the cult of the Gods, less for the arts of ruling, and almost none for warfare. Appointed to a humble post in this obscure temple, he rose to become its Second Priest and one of two custodians solely because all his colleagues had deserted him.

It was bitter to witness a fool remaining steadfast while the clever abandoned their beliefs. Ro performed a profound bow. His beard was curled in the current fashion, albeit poorly. His clothes were ill-fitting and unbrushed; he was, by nature, a slovenly man.

"The temple door is shut," Ro said, his voice flat. "Only I hold the secret of its opening. My lord enters by the secret way, as one of the Seven. I am merely your servant."

"Then I request a small favor," I replied. "Tell me, what stirs the city?"

"That impious Phorenice has declared herself Goddess!" Ro exclaimed, his agitation breaking through his usual lethargy. "She claims she will light the sacrifice with her own divine fire. She intends to do it. She does everything. I pray the flames consume her when she summons them. This new Empress is the bane of our Clan, Deucalion, in these latter days. The people neglect us; they bring no offerings. And now, with these rebels battering our walls, I might have starved had I not saved a small store of grain. Tell you what, Deucalion... the cult of the True Gods is nearly drained from this land."

"My brother," I said softly, "it seems the Priests of our Clan have grown limp in their service, allowing these things to happen."

"I suppose we did our best," Ro muttered. "We acted as taught. But if the people refuse to listen to your exhortations and neglect to adore the Gods, what grip do you have on their religion? Yet, I tell you, Deucalion, the High Gods test our own faith severely. Come, enter my dwelling. Look there, on my bed."

I saw the shape of a man, swathed untidily in reddened bandages.

"This is all that remains of the poor priest who was my immediate superior," Ro whispered. "It was his turn yesterday to celebrate the weekly sacrifice to our Lord the Sun using the Circle of Great Stones. *Faugh!* Deucalion, you should have seen how he was mangled when they dragged him back to me."

"Did the people rise against him? Has it come to violence?"

"The people remained passive," Ro said bitterly, addressing the few who attended. "What few had the interest to show up. But our Lord the Sun deemed it fit to try His minister harshly. The wood was piled; the sacrifice placed upon it according to the rites. The procession formed around the altar, and the drums and trumpets sounded, signaling that the smoke of their prayers would soon ascend to Those Who Sit in the Great Places Above.

"But then, cutting through the ceremonial noise, came the rushing sound of wings. From the sky descended one of those great, featherless, man-eating birds—a size rarely seen before. An arrow to the eye or a long shafted spear would have ended it best."

"Oh, all men learn that as children," Ro scoffed. "But these priests were unarmed, per the rubric, which demands they entrust themselves wholly to the guardianship of the High Gods during sacrifice. The great bird swooped down, settled on the pyre, and attacked the offering with beak and talon. My poor superior, still strong in his faith, shouted loudly for our Lord the Sun to lend him strength. He sprang onto the altar armed only with his teeth and bare arms. Perhaps he expected a miracle—he hasn't spoken since, poor soul—to explain himself. Instead, he met only blows from leathery wings and raking talons that nearly disemboweled him. The bird brushed him aside as easily as we swat a fly. He lay bleeding on the pavement beside the altar while the sacrifice was torn and eaten before the eyes of all. When the bird was full, it flew away to the mountains."

"And the people offered no aid?"

"They cried out that it was a portent!" Ro continued, his voice rising. "They claimed our Lord the Sun was no longer a God if He could not protect His own sacrifice. Others screamed there was no God left. But a few began shouting that Phorenice stood for all the Gods now in Atlantis. That cry was picked up until the stones of the great circle rang with it. Some proclaimed it out of conviction; many because the cry was new and pleasing; but I am certain not a few joined simply because it was dangerous to oppose such an outburst. The Empress can be cruel to those who fail to give her adoration."

"The Empress is Empress," I stated formally. "Her name commands respect. It is not for us to question her actions."

"I am a priest," Ro insisted, defending his duty. "I speak as taught and defend the Faith as commanded. Whether a Faith remains, I begin to doubt. But regardless, it yields a poor livelihood now. No offerings have reached this temple in five months. Without my stored jars of corn, I would surely starve. And I fear the affair of that sacrifice will not ignite new enthusiasm among our cold votaries."

"When did it occur?"

"Twenty hours ago. Today, Phorenice conducts the sacrifice herself. That is the cause of the commotion. The city is in thrall, preparing for her pageant."

"Then I must ask you to open the temple doors and grant me passage. I must witness this event firsthand."

"It is not for me to advise one of the Seven," Ro hesitated.

"Not for you," I agreed.

"But they say the Empress is displeased by your absence," he murmured. "I would not wish harm to come your way, Deucalion."
