The Shipwrecked Stranger.

The Loggia de' Cerchi lay nestled in the labyrinthine heart of ancient Florence, hidden behind the Badia in a network of narrow lanes that few strangers ventured to thread. They sought it only when driven by a desperate need to find a specific, austere door marked with an inscription: *Qui Nacque Il Divino Poeta*. To Dante's ear, these same alleys had once echoed with the clamor of bloody feuds between warring factions; but by the fifteenth century, they resounded merely with the raucous, unbecoming squabbles and broad jests of wool-carders working in the districts of San Martino and Garbo.

Beneath this loggia, upon the early morning of the ninth of April, 1492, two men gazed at one another. One stood slightly stooped, peering down with the scrutinizing curiosity of a tradesman assessing his wares; the other lay sprawled upon the cobblestones, gazing upward with the wide-eyed surprise of a dreamer abruptly awakened.

The standing figure spoke first. He was a gray-haired man of broad shoulders, physically "moulded with the fist and polished with the pickaxe" in the rough Tuscan idiom. Yet, a gravity etched deeply into the lines of his brow and mouth served to counterbalance any contempt one might derive from the rugged, unrefined nature of his appearance. At his feet rested a large sack of leather, filled to capacity, and before him hung a peddler's basket adorned with trinkets for women—thread, pins, and shards of glass, likely acquired in trade for these smaller goods.

"Young man," he said, gesturing to a ring on the finger of the reclining figure, "once your beard grows thick enough, you'll learn better than to nap in street corners with a gem like that on your forefinger. By the holy Evangelists! If anyone else had stood over you two minutes past, you'd be in trouble—but Bratti Ferravecchi is no thief. The cat cannot devour her mouse if she does not catch it alive, and Bratti cannot enjoy profit without tasting the sweetness of a bargain. Indeed, on St. John's Day, three years hence, the Saint sent a dead body my way—a blind beggar, his cap lined with coins. My stomach revolted at the unearned money until I realized St. John was owed his tithe for what I spent annually at the festival. So I buried the body, paid for a mass, and found it a fair trade. But how does a youth with the countenance of Messer San Michele sleep on a stone bed with the wind for a curtain?"

The deep, guttural tones of the speaker were barely intelligible to the newly awakened listener, yet he grasped the gesture regarding the ring. With a half-automatic obedience to the warning, he removed it and shoved it inside his doublet, rising simultaneously to stretch his limbs.

"Your tunic and hose clash badly with that jewel, young man," said Bratti, speaking deliberately. "One might say the Saints sent *you* a corpse; but if you took the jewels, I trust you interred him—and you can certainly afford a couple of masses for him, along with the rest."

A chill, akin to pain, seemed to shoot through the listener's frame, halting his careless stretching. For a fleeting moment, he turned upon Bratti with a sharp frown, but quickly masked it with an air of indifference. He plucked a red Levantine cap, hanging like a great pouch over his left ear, pushed back his long, dark-brown curls, glanced at his attire, and smiled.

"You speak truth, friend: my garments are as weather-stained as an old sail, and they are not old sails, only like an old sail, they have been sprinkled with the sea as well as the rain. The fact is, I am a stranger in Florence. When I arrived footsore last night, I preferred casting myself into a corner of this hospitable porch to hunting further for a hostel, which might prove to be a nest of bloodsuckers of every variety."

"A stranger, truly," said Bratti, "for your words melt from your throat so smoothly that neither a Christian nor a Florentine can distinguish a hook from a hanger. Are you not from Genoa? More likely Venice, judging by the cut of your clothes?"

"At this present juncture," said the stranger, smiling, "where I come from matters less than where I may go next for a morsel of breakfast. Your city casts a grim aspect upon me right here: can you direct me to a livelier quarter where I might procure a meal and lodging?"

"That I can," said Bratti, "and it is your good fortune, young man, that I have walked in from Rovezzano this morning and detoured to Mercato Vecchio to offer an Ave at the Badia. That, I say, is your luck. But it remains to be seen what is my profit. Nothing for nothing, young man. If I guide you to Mercato Vecchio, you shall swear by your patron saint to let me bid for that stained suit of yours when you acquire better gear—as doubtless you will."

"Agreed, by San Niccolò," said the other, laughing. "But now let us depart to this said Mercato, for I feel the urgent lack of a better lining for this doublet which you covet."

"Coveting? Nay," said Bratti, hoisting his bag onto his back and stepping forward. However, he paused mid-step, bursting into loud, harsh tones that grated like a cartwheel on stones: "*Chi abbaratta*—*baratta*—*b'ratta*—*chi abbaratta cenci e vetri*—*b'ratta ferri vecchi*?" ("Who wants to exchange rags, broken glass, or old iron?")

"It is worth but little," he remarked presently, returning to his conversational register. "Hose and all, your clothes fetch a low sum. Nevertheless, if you wish to equip yourself with a lute surpassing any new instrument, or a sword worn by a Ridolfi, or a paternoster of the latest fashion, I can offer you a grand bargain by allowing credit for your clothes. For, simple as I stand here, I possess the best-furnished shop in the Ferravecchi district, situated close to the Mercato. Blessed be the Virgin! It is not a pumpkin I carry on my shoulders. Yet I do not remain caged in my shop all day; I have a wife and a raven to tend the stock at home. *Chi abbaratta*... And now, young man, whence do you come, and what is your business in Florence?"

"I thought you delighted in nothing without a bargain," observed the stranger, somewhat scornfully. "You have offered me nothing yet in exchange for that information."

"Well, well; a Florentine does not mind paying a fair price for news, even if it fills the stomach less than winning a pair of hose. If I take you to the fairest maiden in the Mercato for a cup of milk—that shall constitute a fair bargain."

"Nay; I can locate her myself if she truly resides in the Mercato, for beautiful heads often peer from doors and windows. No, no. Moreover, a sharp trader like yourself ought to know that he who bids for nuts and news may discover them hollow."

"Ah! young man," said Bratti, casting a sideways glance of admiring surprise, "you were not born on a Sunday—the salt shops were open when you entered the world. Are you not a Hebrew? Hark from Spain or Naples? Let me inform you that the Friars Minor are attempting to heat Florence as hot as Spain against those dogs of hell who seek to monopolize the profits of usury, leaving none for Christians. And when you stroll the Calimala with a scrap of yellow cloth in your cap, it will mar your beauty far more than a sword-cut across that smooth olive cheek of yours.—*Abbaratta, baratta*, *chi abbaratta*? I tell you, young man, gray cloth stands against yellow cloth; and there is as much gray cloth in Florence as would clothe and hood the entire Duomo, whereas there is not so much yellow cloth as would make hose for Saint Christopher—blessed be his name, and send me a glimpse of him today!—*Abbaratta, baratta, b'ratta*—"

"All that is delightful information you are dispensing gratis," said the stranger, "but it unfortunately concerns me not. I am no Hebrew."

"See, now!" exclaimed Bratti, triumphant. "I have secured a good bargain with mere words. I have compelled you to reveal something, young man, though you are as slippery as a lamprey. St. John be praised! A blind Florentine matches two one-eyed men. But here we arrive at the Mercato."

They had now emerged from the narrow thoroughfares into a vast square, known to the older chroniclers of Florence as Mercato Vecchio, or the Old Market. Although this piazza had served as a provision market from time immemorial and, according to fond imagination, might have been the precise spot where the Fiesolan ancestors of the Florentines descended from their lofty fastnesses to trade with the valley's rustic populace, it had never been shunned by Florentine wealth. In the early decades of the fifteenth century, which now drew to a close, the Medici and other powerful families of the *popolani grassi*, or commercial nobility, maintained residences there.
