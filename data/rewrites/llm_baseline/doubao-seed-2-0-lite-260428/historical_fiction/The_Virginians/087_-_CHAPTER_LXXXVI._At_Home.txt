An appeal from our mother such as that we had received would have softened hearts far sterner than ours, and we at once began spinning plans for a swift return to Virginia, even arranging to book every last cabin berth aboard the *Young Rachel* for the crossing. But fate intervened: our youngest child fell ill, and no one could dream of risking a long ocean voyage for a feverish little one, nor could the mother bear to part from her, so the *Young Rachel* sailed that year without us. The next brought a new obstacle: my first sharp, humbling attack of the gout, which kept me hobbling by the fireside for weeks, though it later did much to clear my wits and stir my lagging spirits. Then came a far sadder, heavier reason to delay, in the grim news that reached us from Jamaica.

Some two years after we had settled ourselves at our English Manor, dear General Lambert returned home from his colonial governorship. He came a little richer in worldly coin than he had set out, but bearing a loss no fortune on earth could repair; indeed, no sooner had he stepped ashore than he declared he never meant to return to the West Indies. My Theo’s beloved mother – the tenderest, truest friend (save one) I have ever known – had died out there of the island fever. Her last spoken regret was that she would not live to see our household thriving, and hold our little ones on her knee.

“She sees us still, though we cannot see her,” her husband said, quiet and steady, pressing Theo’s hand. “And she thanks you, George, for being good to her girls.”
We all thought he would not linger long before joining her. His love for his wife had been the whole business and joy of his life; to be parted from her felt to him like no life at all. It was pitiable to watch the good man sitting with us of an evening. My wife, in the tilt of her head, the lilt of her laugh, a hundred tiny unthinking gestures, would constantly bring his lost wife back to his heart, sharp and sudden as a knife twist. All the comfort we could offer him we gave freely, but little Hetty, under Heaven, soon became his chief stay and consolation. The General told us she had turned down no fewer than three advantageous matches while in Jamaica; and no sooner had they landed back in England than the heir to Lord Wrotham had laid his coronet and fortune at her feet. But Hetty only smiled and said she would rather stay with her father. As long as he did not tire of her company, she wanted no husband.

“Nonsense,” we told them, when the Wrotham match was first proposed. “Let the General spend six months here at our Manor, and the other six with you at Oakhurst. You will have all the time together you could wish.”
But Hetty declared her father could never bear to live at Oakhurst now, with no beloved wife there to fill the empty spaces, and she would never marry a man for his title or his gold, not she. The General, when we spoke of it later, said gravely that Hetty’s refusal to marry likely stemmed from some early heartbreak she never spoke of, and we, respecting her silence, never pressed her on the matter.

By this time, my brother Jack Lambert held a small college living near Winchester, and of course had a wife to grace his parsonage. We bore no ill will to the woman, but never pretended to more than lukewarm liking for her, though we made her welcome when she came to stay. Her theory for Hetty’s determined celibacy was far more foolish than my own. This Mrs. Jack was a chatterbox, proud as anything of speaking her “unvarnished mind” – which was, in fact, only a habit of saying cruel things to her friends and calling it honesty.

“My dear Sir George,” she would prattle at any who would listen, “I have told our dear Theo over and over that I would never suffer a pretty sister living under my roof, making tea for my husband Jack while I was upstairs with the children, all neat and trim in her best gown while I am fussing over pies and puddings in the kitchen. Oh, I trust Jack! To be sure I do! I should like to see him so much as glance at another woman! But that does not mean I will leave them alone together when my back is turned – no, that I promise you! And so I warned Theo, before Hetty ever came to stay.”

“Am I to understand, madam,” the General said to her, very grave, “that you have done my Lady Warrington the favour of warning her against my own daughter, Miss Hester?”
“To be sure I have, sir!” she cried. “Duty is duty, and a woman is a woman, and a man is a man, as I know very well. Do not tell me otherwise! Every man alive is flesh and blood, even with all his parson’s sanctified airs!”
“Ah, and you yourself have a married sister, I think?” the General went on. “It was at her house that your husband Jack first had the honour of making your acquaintance, was it not?”
“Of course I do! Everyone knows that, and I have been a second mother to her children, that I have!”
“Then am I to gather from your words that your own attractions proved a powerful temptation to your sister’s husband?”
Mrs. Jack went scarlet as a berry, voluble and indignant. “Law, General! I never said any such thing! How can you go and put words in my mouth like that?”
“Do you not see, my good woman, that it is you yourself who have insinuated as much – not only about yourself, but about my two own daughters, and Theo as well?”
“Never, never, never, as I am a Christian!” she shrieked. “It is most cruel of you to say so, sir! All I said was that a sister is best kept out of the house, and that with Theo lying in soon, I was doing my duty to warn her!”
“Have you happened to notice, madam,” the General said, still quiet, “if my poor Hetty has stolen any of our silver spoons? I came down to breakfast this morning, and she was all alone at the table, with no less than twenty pieces of silver laid out before her.”
“Law, sir! Who ever said a word about spoons? I never accused the dear girl of anything! May I drop dead on this very hearthrug if I did! I am not used to being spoken to in such a way! Jack and I both remarked on it, and I was only doing my duty!” With that, she flounced out of the room, tears streaming down her face.

The General turned to Theo, who had come in to join them at the tea table, looking a little pale and delicate as her time drew near. “Has that woman really had the impudence to say all this to you, my child?”
“She has told me every single day since she arrived,” Theo said, with a dry little laugh. “She comes bursting into my dressing room to tell me, and stands in my nursery wringing her hands and crying, ‘Ah, I would never have a sister prowling about my nursery, that I wouldn’t!’ Oh, what a blessing it is to have such amiable, well-bred relatives, is it not?”
“Thank Heaven your poor mother was spared that woman,” the General groaned.
“Our mother would have taught her better, papa,” Theo said, leaning up to kiss his cheek, and I saw that both of their eyes were glistening, half in amusement, half in sorrow.

It must be owned that loving one’s relatives is not always the easiest of tasks, and living beside one’s neighbours is far from a constant amusement. The next day, I could see from Jack Lambert’s sullen demeanour that his wife had given him her own lopsided version of the argument. He was sulky, but not dignified; angry, but not so angry that he lost his appetite, and he preached us a sermon that Sunday so perfectly dull and stupid I nearly fell asleep in the pew. Little Miles, all in his small mourning sables, sat perched on his grandfather’s knee, his tiny hand curled tight around the old man’s palm.

We proposed that the General and Hetty should stay and keep house for us during our Virginia trip. The housekeeper would be entirely under Hetty’s direction, and the General should hold all the butler’s keys, for Gumbo – who had married our Molly some years prior, and filled a neat cottage on our park grounds with a whole brood of little tawny Gumbos – had agreed to sail with us, though not, I thought, with any great show of good grace. So we left our English Manor in the care of the two people we trusted most in all the world, travelled to London, and then on to Bristol, where our obsequious local agent bowed and scraped before us, vowing he would offer up constant prayers for our safe voyage, and declaring he had never seen two children as beautiful as our Miles and our new baby daughter, little Theo, who came aboard with us for the crossing.

We had a perfectly uneventful sail, and the feeling that washed over me as we stepped ashore at Richmond was stranger than any I had ever known. A coach stood waiting for us, surrounded by a whole host of grinning negro servants in our livery, and nearby a gentleman on horseback, also attended by our own people, who sprang from his saddle and came rushing over to embrace us. It was my dearest Hal! We were both half beside ourselves with joy to see him. He rode beside our coach all the way to our mother’s door, and there she stood, waiting on the steps to welcome us, and Theo knelt down on the path to beg her blessing.

Harry got back into the coach with us for a short way, but said he would not spoil the moment by coming in with us at once. “She sees me often enough,” he said, “and we are on pretty good terms, but Fanny and she are best kept apart, I can promise you there is no love lost between them. Come and meet me at the tavern when you are free, George. Tomorrow I will bring Fanny over to pay her respects to your wife. I only chanced to ride into town yesterday, heard the ship had been signalled, and waited for you to land. I sent a negro boy home ahead to tell Fanny the news.” With that, he jumped down from the moving carriage and left us to meet our mother alone.

Since I had last parted from her, I had dined with kings and queens, and paid our formal respects to King George and Queen Charlotte at St. James’s, but I had never seen a more stately, graceful person than the woman who welcomed us now. She raised Theo from her knee, embraced her, and led us into the house. It was a plain, wood-built Virginian manor, with a wide gallery running all around it, but if it had been a royal palace with an empress on the throne, we could not have been received with more courtesy and warmth. Old Nathan was still there, as major-domo, and a score of familiar black faces all grinning to welcome us. Some I remembered as small children were now grown tall, strapping young lads and lasses; some I had left with jet black hair now had snowy white pates; and new little ones born long after my departure peered round doorways with great round eyes and bare little feet, piping out their names to us. “I’m little Sip, Master George!” “I’m Dinah, Sir George!” “I’m Master Miles’s own boy!” squeaked a tiny urchin in a brand new livery, his boots polished only by the dirt of the earth. Before the day was out, every single soul on the plantation had found some excuse to pass before us, bowing and grinning and making us welcome. I do not know how many feasts were laid before us that first day. That evening, Theo received every gentleman and lady of the little town, and carried it off with such perfect grace and good humour that everyone sang her praises afterwards. I shook hands with a dozen old acquaintances – old enemies, I almost said, but I had come into my fortune now, and was no longer the naughty prodigal son returning home. A whole drove of fatted calves was slaughtered in our honour. Poor Hal was there at the entertainment, but he looked gloomy and crestfallen. His mother spoke to him, but it was as a queen might speak to a rebellious prince who has not yet earned full pardon.

Later that night, we slipped away from the crowd and went up to the rooms that had been set aside for me, ready to talk freely at last, but before long our mother appeared at the door, taper in hand, her face soft and pale. Did I want for anything? Was everything to my liking? She had peeped in at the children, she said, and they were sleeping like little cherubs. She cooed over them, stroked their curls, delighted in Miles’s bossy little airs and baby Theo’s dimpled smiles. “Supper is just coming to the table, Sir George. And if you prefer our home cookery to the tavern, Henry, you may stay and join us.” The difference in the warmth of her tone to each of us was so plain even a blind man could see it. Hal hung his head, and followed her down to the parlour. The local clergyman asked a blessing over the meal, speaking with great eloquence of our safe passage across the stormy Atlantic, and of the love and forgiveness that would await all of us in our Heavenly Father’s house when the storms of mortal life were done.

This new clergyman was nothing like the lazy, drunken parsons I remembered from my boyhood, and I praised him to my mother, but Madam Esmond shook her head. She feared his principles were very dangerous, she said, and feared a great many other leading men in the colony shared those dangerous opinions. Had I not seen the petition signed by the burgesses and merchants at Williamsburg the year before – the Lees, the Randolphs, the Washingtons, the Bassets, and oh, my dear, it pained her to say it, even our own name, your brother Harry’s, and that very Mr. Belman who had said the blessing over supper the night before?

If there had been petty quarrels in colonial society when I left home, they were nothing to the raging feuds I found on my return. We in England had passed the Stamp Act, then been forced to repeal it. Then we tried a new set of duties on glass, paper, and other goods, and were forced to repeal almost all of that too – save for a small duty on tea. From Boston to Charleston, every shipment of tea was seized and poured into the harbour. Even my mother, loyalist as she was, gave up drinking her favourite tea rather than pay the tax, and my poor Theo would have had to forgo hers too, if we had not brought a private cask of it over on the ship, for which we had already paid four times the duty at home. For my own part, I would never have hesitated to pay the lawful tax: the home government must have some source of revenue, or all its claims to authority are nothing but empty air. People say the colonies were tyrannised over, but I say the home government was tyrannised and tried beyond endurance. It is no more than a matter of history now; we fought the quarrel, and we lost, and the matter is as settled as the Norman Conquest of Britain. But from honest conviction, I have always sided with the British side of the dispute. In my own short, unhappy experience of warfare in my youth, every man in the army, every loyal subject, knew full well that Braddock’s expedition had failed, and disaster had fallen on all of us, because of the selfishness, the rapacity, and the utter remissness of the very colonists we had come to defend against the French. The colonists wanted every favour done for them, and would do nothing in return. They haggled for exorbitant prices with the soldiers who had crossed the Atlantic to protect them; they broke contracts, gave out supplies in niggardly dribbles, and dragged their feet with endless delays until the hour for action was long past, and the disaster came that need never have happened but for their ill will. When the news came of the conquest of Canada, there were shouts of joy across every colony, and ovations for the great British statesman who had planned and won that victory. Monsieur de Vaudreuil was right when he said that the fall of Canada was the very signal for the North American colonies to throw off their allegiance to Great Britain. Lord Chatham, who had done more than any man to win Canada, then turned around and did more than any man in England to encourage the colonies to rebel. Scores of thousands of waverers must have taken heart to resist, because the great Chatham cheered on their mutiny. It was as if a general stood before a rioting regiment and cried “God save the King! Men, you are perfectly right to mutiny!” No wonder one town put up a statue to him, another hung his portrait, and a third hanged the King’s ministers in effigy. The wiseacres of Williamsburg even subscribed to buy a grand portrait of Chatham, painted as a Roman orator speaking in the Forum, his hand pointing directly at the window of Whitehall Palace where Charles I had been beheaded! A very neat little allegory, that, and a charming compliment to a British statesman! Though I hear the portrait was copied from a bust, and Chatham himself never even knew the controversial detail was there.

My home was England, not America or Virginia, and I took – I take still – the English side of that old dispute. My loyalties were always to the old country, where I was now a squire and a citizen of long standing. But I know full well that if my lot had been to plant tobacco and live on the banks of the James River, my opinions would no doubt have been very different. When I visited Harry at his new plantation house, I found him and his wife Fanny as staunch American patriots as we were loyal British. We had our share of words over the dispute, as everyone did in those troubled times, but our arguments were never bitter. Even my new sister-in-law, for all her efforts to stir up strife, could not make us quarrel, though I have no doubt she lectured Harry by the hour behind closed curtains. But we trusted each other far too deeply for Harry’s wifely duty ever to turn him against his own brother. He loved me from the old days when my word was law to him, and he still swore that he and every other Virginian gentleman of his party had no ill will to the Crown. The war had not yet broken out, and men of opposing views still treated each other with courtesy. At public dinners and festivals, everyone ostentatiously drank the health of the King.
