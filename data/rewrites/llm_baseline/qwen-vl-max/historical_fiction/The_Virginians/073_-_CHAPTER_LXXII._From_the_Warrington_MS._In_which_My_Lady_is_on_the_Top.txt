CHAPTER LXXII. (From the Warrington MS.) In which My Lady is on the Top of the Ladder

Looking across the fire, towards her accustomed chair—my dear companion for the better part of my life—I often find myself asking (as middle-aged gentlemen may, with the indulgence to repeat their jokes and questions) whether any two young people were ever more reckless and ill-advised than we were when we married, in the year of the old King’s death? My son, who has taken some astonishing leaps during fox-hunts, tells me he looks back upon those daring crossings of rivers and chasms with a kind of horror, marveling at his own boldness; yet there is no more spirited rider in either county than Miles. He loves his sport so thoroughly that nothing else matters. He has broken his collarbone, endured a hundred falls—great cause of anxiety to his mother—but so too have I, once thrown over my old mare’s head as she slipped on a stone while we both drifted along a park road at four miles an hour, lost in thought—perhaps composing a line or rehearsing a speech for Quarter Sessions.

Miles’s reckless joy in hunting has been the delight of his life, just as my marriage has been the blessing of mine. I never reflect upon it without gratitude. Yet I do not hold up our union as a model. I would not advise young folk to marry on a mere twopence a year—though such advice might draw stern glances at vestry meetings. Still, my wife is known far and wide as a matchmaker of fierce determination. When Hodge and Susan appear before me in my justice room, whispering about allowances, we encourage them to spend their half-crown at home, add a little from our own purse, and summon the vicar.

When I pose a question to my dear oracle, I already know the answer. That is, no doubt, why I so frequently consult her. A certain look upon my face, and my Diana instantly mirrors it. Suppose I say, “My dear, did you not think the moon looked like cream cheese tonight?” She replies, “Well, papa, it certainly did—there’s no one like you for whimsical comparisons.” Or if I remark, “My love, Mr. Pitt’s speech was impressive, though I fear he does not reach the heights of his father,” she responds, “No one could match Lord Chatham.” Then one of the girls will exclaim, “But I’ve heard Papa call Lord Chatham a charlatan!” To which mamma says, “How much she resembles her Aunt Hetty!”

As for Miles, Latin or Greek—Tros Tyriusve—is all one to him. He reads only the sporting columns in the Norwich paper. So long as the scent is good, he cares not a whit for the state of the nation. I suspect he has never read my poems, let alone my tragedies—though I mentioned Pocahontas to him recently, and he thought she was a river in Virginia! And how can he grasp my Latin verses when I know he cannot translate Corderius? This notebook lies openly on the small table beside my fireside, with my spectacles resting on its cover—anyone who takes the trouble to lift them may read. But Miles has never done so.

I have tucked into the loose pages caricatures of him—jokes at his expense—and yet he neither sees nor minds them. Once, instead of my neat China-ink sketch of Miles asleep after dinner—a drawing so lifelike that even Bunbury claimed it was his own—I found a crude portrait of myself tumbling over Sultana’s head, labeled *The Squire on Horseback, or Fish out of Water*. The fellow roared with laughter, and the girls tittered when I came upon the page. My wife confessed she had never felt such fright as when I opened the book. But I can bear a joke at my own expense—I have heard many, though (strange to say, given my years among the wits of the age) I have never encountered a truly clever one in my life.

Never mind, Miles—though thou art no wit, I love thee none the less. There has never been a quarrel between wits in a family. Though thou hast no great beauty, thy mother thinks thee as handsome as Apollo—or His Royal Highness, the Prince of Wales, born in the same year as thee. Indeed, she insists Coates’s portrait of the Prince bears a striking resemblance to her eldest son, and keeps the print in her dressing-room to this day.

[Note, in a female hand: “My son is not a spendthrift nor a breaker of hearts, as some gentlemen are. But that he resembled H.R.H. as infants is most certain—the Duchess of Aneaster herself remarked it in St. James’s Park, where Gumbo and my poor Molly used to take him for an airing. Th. W.”]

In that very year, with such different destinies, my Lord Esmond, son of Lord Castlewood, also entered the world. At that time, my Lord C. and I had already grown cool toward one another, and heaven knows, my honest Miles’s godmother brought no golden pap-boats to his christening! Matters have improved since—laus Deo, indeed! For I suspect neither Miles nor his father would ever have managed well by their own devices.

Castlewood House now presents quite a different appearance from the venerable structure I knew in my youth—once scarred by time, war, and weather, its façade etched with the marks of centuries. I cherish the memory of it as it stood when young Mr. George Warrington descended upon it at the owner’s invitation, to attend the marriage of his lordship to Miss Lydia Van den Bosch—“an American lady of noble Dutch descent,” as the county paper proclaimed her.

Then the towers stood as they had under the Colonel’s gaze—Warrington’s grandfather, whom Madam Esmond fondly called the Marquis. The woods, though thinned somewhat, remained largely unchanged; perhaps the very same rooks cawed above them that the Colonel had seen sixty years prior. His portrait hung in the hall—might have been his, had he chosen wealth and title over love and loyalty. As I walked those ancient corridors, dew-damp terraces, and shadowed avenues, I felt for a moment as if I were one of Walpole’s cavaliers—ruff, rapier, buff-coat, gorget—and feared at every turn that an Old Pretender or Jesuit spy might emerge from behind a tree or carved cupboard.

Strange, melancholy, and delightful fancies filled my mind. I imagined intrigues, serenades, sieges—Oliver’s Roundheads battering the walls, or Hal’s Beefeaters charging across the plain. I was then courting a certain young lady—madam, your eyes needed no spectacles then, and the brow above them bore no wrinkle or silver hair—and I wrote reams of romantic descriptions under my Lord Castlewood’s frank, letters she never tired of reading. Now, when I am away in London or elsewhere, I send her only three lines—so as not to weary your aged eyes, my dear.

Mr. Warrington deemed himself justified in ordering a fine new suit for the wedding, and with Mons. Gumbo in attendance, arrived at Castlewood several days before the ceremony. I must mention that it had been necessary to send my faithful Sady back to Virginia aboard a ship. A severe inflammation of the throat and lungs—fatal in many cases during Wolfe’s expedition—had nearly killed my boy. Doctors advised that his native air was the best cure. We parted in tears, and Gumbo shed as many when his master departed for Quebec. But Gumbo had ties here and no taste for military life, so he stayed in my service.

We found Castlewood teeming with guests—friends, relatives, and visitors. Lady Fanny was present, albeit reluctantly, as a sulky bridesmaid. Some local maidens attended the young Countess. The Baroness Beatrix, widow of a bishop, brought a holy brother-in-law from London to officiate the marriage between Eugene, Earl of Castlewood, and Lydia Van den Bosch, spinster. For weeks before and after the nuptials, the old Hampshire house wore a festive air unfamiliar to it for years.

Country families flocked to pay their respects to the newlyweds. The bride’s fortune became the talk of the land—no doubt growing in the telling. Scandalous tales circulating in town, spread by her disappointed suitors, took time to reach Hampshire. When they did, the locals chose civility over curiosity. Had she rejected one man and mocked another? It mattered little—they cared only for her money, and she was no more mercenary than they.

To the Hampshire folk, it was a blessing to see Castlewood House reopened—beer in the cellars, horses in the stables, spits turning before kitchen fires. The new Countess assumed her place with dignity. All acknowledged her rare talents and sharp intellect. Was it not stated in the marriage announcement that she brought her noble husband seventy thousand pounds? On a *beaucoup d’esprit* with seventy thousand pounds. The people said that was but a fraction of her wealth. When the grandfather passed, they believed many plums would fall from that old tree.

That quiet, keen-eyed old man soon set about restoring Castlewood’s finances, long neglected through poverty and carelessness. His life’s work became improving the estate—raising rents where possible, cutting expenses. He watched every yard of worsted lace on the footmen’s coats, every pound of beef served at dinner. A vigilant eye noted each flagon of beer drawn from the buttery, ensuring no waste in the larder. Servants were fewer, but paid regularly. Liveries were cleaner, and tailors no longer pursued payment. Gardeners and grooms grumbled, though wages were no longer overdue. But horses grew fat on less corn, and fruit and vegetables abounded—such was the precision of my lady’s grandfather, watching from his solitary chamber in the turret.

These changes, though summarized here in a few lines, took months and years at Castlewood. Through thrift, order, and wise spending—though my lord sometimes lamented he had no funds—the estate flourished. No one denied it was a prosperous, economical household—not even the dowager lady and her two children, who now rarely crossed Castlewood’s threshold. My lady regarded them as enemies—what gentleman, after all, would welcome a stepmother-in-law?

The reigning Countess gave the dowager battle and routed her swiftly. Though educated in the colonies and unversed in polite society, Countess Lydia possessed a mastery of language and a strength of will that all who opposed her had to acknowledge. The dowager and Lady Fanny proved no match for the young American. They fled to their jointure house in Kensington—and no wonder their absence was not mourned by my lord, who seldom regretted anyone whose back was turned.

Could cousin Warrington, whose hand his lordship clasped so warmly on arrival and departure—whose companion he was so gay and open with—expect that his lordship would grieve at his passing, or any misfortune befalling him? Cousin Warrington knew better. Always skeptical, Mr. W. took grim pleasure in observing his neighbors’ quirks, and could admire this one, despite his lack of courage or heart.

Courage? Heart? What do these mean to us in the world? A man may possess private virtues as he may hold half a million in government stock. What society demands is liveliness, charm, a decent appearance, and the ability to keep up one’s end.

Colonel Esmond Warrington’s grandfather—whose history and dwelling-place fascinated Mr. W.—might once have owned Castlewood and its titles. The gentleman often studied the Colonel’s portrait, still hanging in the saloon, a copy of which Mr. Warrington cherished from Virginia.

“He must have been a little touched here,” my lord said, tapping his own tall, placid forehead.

There are actions—simple, common to some—that others cannot comprehend, dismissing them as lies or madness. “I do you the justice to think, cousin,” Mr. Warrington said, “that you would never surrender any advantage for a friend.” “Eh! I am selfish,” replied my lord, with a French shrug and a pinch from his snuffbox. “But am I more selfish than the rest?”

Once, while walking in the fields, my lord wore a bright scarlet coat. A cow charged toward him, and the usually languid nobleman sprang over a stile with the agility of a schoolboy. He did not hide his fear, nor his natural timidity.

“I dare say you respect me no more than I respect myself, George,” he would say, candidly, and launch into a sardonic discourse on human frailty—why Heaven had not made us all brave, tall, handsome, and rich?

As for Mr. Warrington, who likely enjoyed being the center of attention, he could not help admiring this kinsman—so witty, graceful, polished, high-born—yet utterly his inferior. Like the creature in Sterne’s famous tale, “Do not beat me,” his lordship seemed to say, “but if you wish, you may.” No man, save a bully and coward himself, treats such a spiritless soul harshly.
