The initial leg of their journey was cast in such a shadow of gloom that it felt interminable and dreary. Yet, as they neared their destination, the anticipation of inhabiting a new land began to lift their spirits. Upon entering Barton Valley, the landscape offered a balm to their sorrow; it was a verdant, fertile expanse, lush with trees and rich grazing lands. After winding through the valley for over a mile, they finally arrived at their home.

Barton Cottage possessed a modest charm. Its entire frontage consisted of a small, grassy courtyard, accessed by a tidy wicket gate. Though the structure itself was compact and cozy, it lacked the rustic character of a true cottage. It was too orderly—the roof was tiled rather than thatched, the window shutters remained unpainted, and the walls were bare of climbing honeysuckle. A narrow corridor cut straight through the center of the house, leading directly to the garden behind. Flanking the entrance were two sitting rooms, each roughly sixteen feet square, with the offices and staircase situated beyond. The remaining space comprised four bedrooms and two attic rooms. Built relatively recently and kept in excellent condition, the house stood in stark contrast to the grandeur of Norland. It was undeniably smaller and poorer—but as they stepped inside, the tears summoned by painful memories quickly evaporated. The cheerful greetings of the servants warmed their hearts, and driven by mutual affection, both sisters resolved to project an air of happiness.

It was early September, and the weather was splendid. Seeing their new surroundings bathed in sunshine instantly fostered a favorable impression, a psychological boost that proved invaluable in helping them accept their fate. The location was picturesque: high hills rose immediately behind the cottage, flanked on either side by open downs and cultivated woodlands. The village of Barton sprawled across one of these slopes, offering a delightful vista from the cottage's windows. The view ahead was even more expansive, commanding the entire valley and stretching toward distant horizons. While the surrounding hills enclosed the valley in that direction, the land branched out again further downstream, cutting between two particularly steep peaks.

Mrs. Dashwood was largely pleased with the house's size and furnishings. Although her accustomed lifestyle meant many refinements were necessary, she found the act of improvement deeply satisfying. Moreover, she possessed sufficient liquid assets to furnish the apartments with greater elegance should the need arise. "As for the house itself," she remarked, "it is certainly too small for our family, but we shall manage comfortably for now, especially since it is far too late in the year for major renovations. Perhaps come spring, if my finances allow—and I daresay they will—we can consider expansion. These parlors are both too cramped for the gatherings of friends I hope to host frequently. I am thinking of merging the central passage with one of the rooms, perhaps incorporating part of the other, leaving the remainder as a proper entrance. Add a new drawing-room, a bedroom, and an attic above, and we shall have a very snug little cottage. I would also like to beautify the stairs, though one cannot expect perfection everywhere. Still, widening them should prove straightforward. Come spring, I shall assess exactly where I stand financially, and we can plan our enhancements accordingly."

For the interim, until these alterations could be funded from the meager savings of a five-hundred-pound-a-year income—a sum accumulated by a woman who had never previously known thrift—they wisely chose contentment. Each sister busied herself organizing her personal affairs, arranging books and cherished belongings to transform the empty spaces into a home. Marianne carefully unpacked and positioned her pianoforte, while Elinor pinned her sketches to the walls of their sitting room.

These domestic pursuits were abruptly interrupted shortly after breakfast the following morning by the arrival of their landlord. He came to welcome them to Barton and to pledge every convenience available from his own estate to supplement any deficiencies in theirs. Sir John Middleton was a handsome man of about forty. Though he had once visited Stanhill, the intervening years had faded from the memory of his younger cousins. His countenance radiated good humor, and his mannerisms matched the warm tone of his correspondence. Their presence clearly delighted him; their comfort appeared to be a genuine concern. He spoke passionately of his desire for close social ties with his family and urged them so warmly to dine daily at Barton Park until they were fully settled that, despite his persistence bordering on impertinence, they could hardly take offense. His kindness extended beyond words. Within an hour of his departure, a large basket brimming with garden produce and fruit arrived from the park, followed later that same day by a gift of game. Furthermore, he insisted on personally delivering and collecting their mail, refusing to relinquish the pleasure of sending them his daily newspaper.

Lady Middleton conveyed a courteous message via Sir John, indicating her intent to pay her respects as soon as she knew her visit would cause no inconvenience. Responding with equal politeness, an invitation was issued, and the next day she was formally introduced to the sisters. Naturally, the Dashwoods were eager to meet the woman whose influence on their comfort in Barton would be paramount. Her elegant appearance exceeded their expectations. Lady Middleton was barely twenty-seven; her face was beautiful, her figure tall and imposing, and her bearing graceful. Her manners possessed all the refinement her husband lacked. However, they might have benefited from a touch of his frankness and warmth. Indeed, her visit eventually tempered their initial awe, revealing that while impeccably bred, she was reserved, cool, and unable to contribute anything beyond the most mundane inquiries or observations.

Conversation was unnecessary, for Sir John was endlessly talkative. Lady Middleton had wisely brought along their eldest son, a charming little boy of about six, ensuring that whenever the ladies faced a lull, there was always a topic: his name, his age, his beauty, and endless questions answered by his mother while he clung to her skirts, holding his head low. This shyness surprised his mother greatly, who noted that he could make quite a racket at home. On formal visits, a child is essential to facilitate conversation. In this instance, ten minutes were spent debating whether the boy resembled his father or mother, and in what specific ways, for opinions naturally varied, and everyone marveled at the differing assessments. Soon, the Dashwoods would have ample opportunity to discuss the rest of the Middleton children, as Sir John refused to leave the house without securing their promise to dine at the park the following day.
