“As angels in some brighter dreams
Call to the soul when man doth sleep,
So some strange thoughts transcend our wonted themes,
And into glory peep.”

Mrs. Hale found herself curiously captivated by the idea of the Thornton dinner party. It stirred her imagination like a child anticipating a grand feast, eager for every detail to be laid bare before the moment arrived. The quiet, confined life of an invalid often brings forth such childlike wonder—both she and children alike live within narrow walls, believing their world to be the only one that matters. Mrs. Hale had once known vanity as a young woman; perhaps she had felt its sting keenly when she became the wife of a poor clergyman. Those feelings had been buried beneath years of modesty, but they were not gone. Now, she delighted in picturing Margaret dressed for such an occasion, musing over what gown she might wear, with a nervous excitement that amused her daughter.

“Then you’re certain you’ll wear your white silk?” Mrs. Hale asked, her voice trembling slightly. “Are you sure it still fits? It’s nearly a year since Edith was married!”

“Oh, yes, Mama,” Margaret replied cheerfully. “Mrs. Murray made it, so it should fit perfectly. Maybe it’s a hair shorter or longer-waisted, depending on whether I’ve gained or lost weight. But I don’t believe I’ve changed at all.”

“Perhaps Dixon should see it,” suggested Mrs. Hale. “It might have yellowed from sitting unused.”

“If you wish,” Margaret said gently. “But if it comes to the worst, I have a lovely pink gauze—Aunt Shaw gave it to me just a few months before Edith’s wedding. That couldn’t have faded.”

“No,” Mrs. Hale murmured, “but it may have lost its color.”

“Well,” Margaret laughed, “then there’s always the green silk. I feel as though I’m drowning in choices.”

“I only wish I knew what would be best,” Mrs. Hale confessed, wringing her hands.

Margaret’s tone softened instantly. “Shall I go try them on one by one, Mama? Then you could decide which you prefer?”

“Yes,” Mrs. Hale said hesitantly. “That might be best.”

So Margaret left the room, already half tempted to playfully exaggerate her transformation—making her white silk billow like a cloud, or stepping backward like a queen before her mother. But seeing how seriously her mother took the matter, she quickly composed herself. She couldn’t understand why the world—her world—should care so much about a dress. Yet that very afternoon, when she mentioned her invitation to Bessy Higgins—prompted by Mrs. Thornton’s promise to inquire about a servant—Bessy reacted with sudden intensity.

“Dear!” she exclaimed. “You’re going to dine at Mr. Thornton’s at Marlborough Mills?”

“Yes, Bessy. Why are you so surprised?”

“Oh, I dunno,” Bessy replied, her eyes wide. “But they entertain all the finest folk in Milton.”

“And you don’t think we’re among the finest here, then, Bessy?”

A flush crept across Bessy’s cheeks. “Well,” she admitted, “they reckon money counts for a lot around here. And I suppose you haven’t got much.”

“No,” Margaret agreed quietly. “That’s true. But we’re educated people. We’ve lived among educated people. Is it so extraordinary that someone like Mr. Thornton—once a draper’s assistant—would come to my father to learn, and now invite us to his home? I don’t mean to fault him. Few men who began where he did could have risen so far.”

“But can you return the favor?” Bessy pressed. “Can you host a dinner in your little house? His place is three times the size.”

“I think we could manage,” Margaret said steadily. “Maybe not in such a large room or with so many guests. But we’ve never thought about it in terms of competition.”

“I never imagined you’d be dining with the Thorntons,” Bessy repeated, astonished. “Why, even the mayor dines there—the Members of Parliament, everyone important.”

“I believe I could hold my head high beside the mayor of Milton,” Margaret said with quiet confidence.

“But those ladies—they dress so finely!” Bessy added, glancing nervously at Margaret’s simple print dress, which her Milton eyes valued at sevenpence a yard.

Margaret smiled, her face lighting up. “Thank you, Bessy, for caring so much about how I’ll look among all those elegant people. But I have several fine dresses—just last week, I’d have said they were far too grand for anything I’d ever need here. But since I’m going to Mr. Thornton’s, and might meet the mayor, I’ll certainly wear my very best.”

“What will you wear?” Bessy asked, relieved.

“White silk,” Margaret answered. “The same gown I wore to my cousin’s wedding a year ago.”

“That’ll do!” Bessy declared, sinking back into her chair. “I wouldn’t want you looked down upon.”

“Oh, I’ll be fine enough,” Margaret teased. “If that keeps me from being overlooked in Milton.”

“I wish I could see you dressed,” Bessy sighed. “I don’t suppose you’re what folks call pretty—you’re not red and white enough for that. But do you know, I dreamed of you long before I ever saw you?”

“Nonsense, Bessy!”

“Ay, but I did,” she insisted. “Your very face—looking out with those clear, steady eyes from the darkness, your hair blown back from your brow, spreading like rays around your forehead, just as it is now—smooth and straight—and you always came to give me strength, drawing it from your deep, comforting gaze. You were clothed in shining garments—just like the one you’re going to wear. So, you see—it was you!”

“No, Bessy,” Margaret said softly. “It was only a dream.”

“And why shouldn’t I dream in my affliction, like others have?” Bessy retorted. “Didn’t many in the Bible? And see visions too! Even my father believes in dreams. I tell you again—I saw you clearly, coming swiftly toward me, your hair flying back from the motion, just like it grows, standing slightly away—exactly like this—and wearing that white shining dress. Let me come and see you in it. I want to touch you, as if you were truly the one from my dream.”

“My dear Bessy,” Margaret said gently, “it’s just your fancy.”

“Fancy or no,” Bessy insisted, “you’ve come, just as I knew you would when I saw your movement in my dream. And when you’re near me, I feel easier, comforted—just as a fire warms you on a cold day. You said it was the twenty-first. Please God, I’ll come to see you.”

“Oh, Bessy,” Margaret whispered, “you may come, of course—but don’t speak like this. It makes me sorrowful.”

“Then I’ll keep it to myself,” Bessy said, biting her lip. “Even if it kills me. But it’s true, all the same.”

Margaret remained silent. After a long pause, she said, “Let’s talk about it another time, if you believe it. But not now. Tell me—has your father returned?”

“Ay,” Bessy replied heavily, her earlier brightness gone. “He and many others—Hamper’s men, and more besides. The women are as fierce as the men this time. Food is high—and they must feed their children, I suppose. If the Thorntons sent them meals—same money spent on potatoes and flour could quiet many a crying baby, and calm a mother’s heart for a while!”

“Don’t say such things,” Margaret pleaded. “You make me feel guilty for going to this dinner.”

“No,” Bessy said firmly. “Some are predestined to sumptuous feasts and fine linen—perhaps you are one. Others toil all their lives, and even dogs are less pitiful today than Lazarus was. But if you ask me to cool your tongue with the tip of my finger, I’ll cross the great gulf to reach you—for the sake of what you’ve done for me.”

“Bessy,” Margaret said, alarmed, “you’re feverish. I can feel it in your hand, and in your words. There won’t be enough division in that terrible day to separate us by wealth—some were beggars, some rich. We’ll not be judged by that, but by our faithfulness to Christ.”

Margaret rose and fetched water. Dipping her handkerchief, she placed the cool cloth on Bessy’s forehead and began warming her icy feet. Bessy closed her eyes, allowing herself to be soothed. At last, she murmured,

“You’d have been driven mad, just like me, if you’d had one after another coming in, asking for Father, staying to tell me their stories. Some spoke of deadly hatred—what they said about the masters made my blood run cold. But most—especially the women—kept crying, tears streaming down their faces, unheeded, about the price of meat, and how their children couldn’t sleep at night from hunger.”

“And do they think the strike will help?” Margaret asked.

“They say so,” Bessy replied. “They claim trade has been good for years, and the masters have made huge profits. How much, Father doesn’t know—but the Union does. And naturally, they want their share now that food is rising. The Union says they’d fail in duty if they didn’t demand their portion. But the masters have the upper hand somehow—and I fear they’ll keep it forever. It’s like Armageddon—fighting and grinning, until even as they fight, they’re dragged down into the pit.”

At that moment, Nicholas Higgins entered. He caught the last of her words.

“Ay!” he declared. “I’ll fight on too—and I’ll win this time. It won’t take long for them to give in. They’ve got orders under contract—soon they’ll realize it’s better to give us five percent than lose the profit, not to mention the fine for breaking the contract. Hah! My masters—I know who’ll win.”

Margaret sensed from his manner that he had been drinking—not from what he said, but from the fervor of his speech. Bessy’s evident anxiety confirmed it. She turned to Margaret.

“The twenty-first—that’s Thursday next. I may come to see you dressed for Thornton’s, I reckon. What time is your dinner?”

Before Margaret could reply, Higgins burst in:

“Thornton’s! Are you going to dine at Thornton’s? Tell him to raise a toast to the success of his orders. By the twenty-first, I’ll bet he’ll be scratching his head wondering how to finish them on time. Tell him seven hundred of us will march into Marlborough Mills the morning after he gives the five percent—and we’ll help him through his contract in no time. You’ll have them all there. My master, Hamper—he’s old-fashioned. Never speaks ill, never swears. I’d think he’d die if he spoke civil to me. But his bark’s worse than his bite. You can tell him one of his men said so, if you like. Eh! You’ll have a fine gathering of mill owners at Thornton’s! I’d love to speak to them after dinner, when they’re tired and can’t run away. I’d tell them my mind. I’d speak up against the cruel way they treat us!”

“Good-bye,” Margaret said quickly. “Good-bye, Bessy. I’ll expect you on the twenty-first, if you’re well.”

Dr. Donaldson’s treatments had brought significant relief to Mrs. Hale at first. Both she and Margaret began to hope he might have been mistaken—that recovery might yet be possible. Mr. Hale, though he’d never fully grasped the severity of their fears, now showed visible relief, proving how deeply the ordeal had shaken him. Only Dixon remained grim, whispering warnings to Margaret. But she refused to listen—she chose to hope.

This small light inside their home was needed, for outside, even to their untrained eyes, the air was thick with unrest. Mr. Hale had friends among the working men, and they shared their tales of hardship and endurance with him. They would never have spoken so openly to someone in power—but he was a stranger, confused by the system, and each wanted him to judge their suffering. He brought these grievances to Mr. Thornton, hoping the factory owner could explain their causes. Thornton always responded with economic logic: trade had its cycles—prosperity waxed and waned. In decline, some masters and workers inevitably fell, crushed by circumstance. He spoke as if this were inevitable, unavoidable—neither employer nor worker had a right to complain. The master, unable to continue, would feel failure, wounded pride, trampled by rivals, humbled into begging for work. The worker, passed by in progress, would long to rest—but could not, haunted by the cries of children left behind. He envied the bird that fed its young with its own blood.

Margaret recoiled from this cold reasoning—as if commerce were everything, humanity nothing. Yet Thornton had shown kindness—coming that evening to offer her, privately, every comfort his wealth and foresight had accumulated, knowing from Dr. Donaldson that Mrs. Hale might need them. His presence, after such words—his knowledge of the fate she tried to deny—set her teeth on edge. How could he, who understood her secret pain, speak so harshly of human suffering? His pitying eyes, his grave voice—how reconcile them with his rigid doctrines? The contradiction tore at her soul, especially with Bessy’s tales fresh in her mind.

Nicholas Higgins, however, spoke differently. Appointed a committee-man, he claimed to know secrets the public did not. On the eve of Mrs. Thornton’s dinner, Margaret found him arguing fiercely with Boucher, a neighbor whose name she recognized—sometimes a source of compassion, sometimes rage. When she entered, Higgins was clearly agitated. Boucher stood by the mantel, hands gripping it, staring wildly into the fire, his despair both touching and infuriating. Bessy rocked violently, a sign of distress Margaret now recognized. Her sister Mary tied on her bonnet clumsily, blubbering as she fled, desperate to escape.

Margaret paused at the door, then silently slipped to a seat near Bessy. Higgins nodded curtly. Mary rushed out, sobbing. Boucher paid no attention.

“It’s no use, Higgins,” he said. “She can’t live long like this. She’s sinking—not from lack of food, but from seeing the children starve. Five shillings a week suits you—two mouths, one earning her own. But it’s crushing us. And I swear—if she dies before we get the five percent, I’ll throw the money back in the master’s face and curse the whole cruel world. I’ll hate you, and the entire Union. I’ll chase you through heaven with my hatred—if you’re leading me astray. You said on Wednesday last week—now it’s Tuesday of the second week—that within two weeks the masters would come begging us to return, at our wages. Time’s nearly up—and our little Jack lies in bed, too weak to cry, just sobbing now and then from hunger. Our little Jack, I tell you! She’s never looked up since birth, and loves him as if he were her life—for I know he’s cost me that price. Our little Jack, who woke me each morning with his sweet lips pressing my rough face, seeking a smooth spot to kiss—now he lies starving.” His sobs choked him. Higgins looked up, eyes full of tears, toward Margaret.

“Hold up, man,” he said. “Your little Jack won’t starve. I’ve got money—we’ll buy him milk and a four-pounder right now. What’s mine is yours. Just don’t lose heart.” He fumbled in a tea-pot for coins. “I swear we’ll win. One more week, and you’ll see the masters begging us to return. The Union—I’ll see to it you have enough for the children and your wife. Don’t turn faint-hearted, and go to the tyrants for work.”

Boucher turned slowly—his face pale, gaunt, streaked with tears, utterly hopeless. Its stillness forced Margaret to weep.

“You know well,” he said, “that a worse tyrant than any master says, ‘Starve to death, let them all starve, before you return to the Union.’ You know it, Nicholas, even though you’re one of them. You may be kind individually—but banded together, you have no more mercy than a wolf ravenous with hunger.”

Higgins gripped the door handle, then turned sharply.

“So help me God!” he cried. “If I’m not doing right, if I’m wrong when I think I’m right, it’s their sin who left me in ignorance. I’ve thought till my brain ached, John. I say again—there’s no help but faith in the Union. They’ll win. See if they don’t!”

Neither Margaret nor Bessy had spoken. Their silence was filled only by the breathless sighs they exchanged.

“I never thought to hear Father call on God again,” Bessy whispered. “But you heard him say, ‘So help me God!’”

“Yes,” Margaret said softly. “Let me bring you what money I can spare—let me bring food for that man’s children. Don’t let them know it comes from anyone but your father. It will be little.”

Bessy lay back, ignoring her words. She didn’t cry—only trembled.

“My heart’s drained dry of tears,” she said. “Boucher’s told me of his fears lately. He’s weak, I know—but he’s a man. I’ve been angry with him and his wife before, but now I see—people aren’t wise, yet God lets them live. Gives them someone to love, someone who loves them back, as good as Solomon. And when sorrow comes to them, it hurts as much as it did Solomon. I can’t understand. Perhaps it’s better that Boucher has the Union to care for him. But I’d like to see the men who built the Union—face them one by one with Boucher. I reckon, if they heard him, they’d tell him—go back, get what you can, even if it’s less than ordered.”

Margaret sat in silence. How could she leave this scene and return to comfort, forgetting that voice—so full of agony, saying more than words could bear? She took out her purse. Not much was hers, but she placed it in Bessy’s hands without speaking.

“Thank you,” Bessy said. “Many get no more, and are not as bad off. But Father won’t let them starve now he knows. Boucher’s been pulled down by his children—his wife sick, everything pawned this past year. Don’t think we’d let them starve, even if we’re hard-pressed. If neighbors don’t care for neighbors, who will? Besides, Father’s certain the masters must give in soon. But thank you—thank you for yourself, as much as for Boucher. It warms my heart.”

Bessy seemed quieter today, but terribly weary. As she finished, she looked so faint that Margaret grew alarmed.
