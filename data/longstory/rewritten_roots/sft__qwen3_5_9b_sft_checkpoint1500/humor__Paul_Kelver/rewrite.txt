IN WHICH THE AUTHOR SEES IT AS HIS DUTY TO PASS THE BURDEN OF THIS TALE ONTO SOMEONE ELSE.

Down a long, straight avenue of red-brick buildings in the very bowels of the East End—those kind of streets that feel like giant, bored skeletons stretching into infinity, lined with windows so perfectly identical they look like rows of teeth on a fossilized jawbone—stands a house that refuses to stay hidden. It pulls at me, constantly, like a magnet made of bad memories. Often, when I am lost in the noise of the city, rushing down roads lit by flaring gas lamps where the faces of the workers look like heavy, leaden coins stamped with patience, I wake up suddenly, heart pounding, already sprinting through the fog. I cut through empty alleys where shadows stretch long and thin against closed shutters, past narrow lanes so smelly they make you hold your breath, where gutters overflow with water and children splash, and every open door spews out a fresh riot of sound and smell. I weave past reeking corners and cross wastelands until, finally, driven by a memory that feels older than my own, I stop right next to broken iron railings and breathe.

The house is bigger than its neighbors, a relic from when this street was just a muddy country lane skirting the marshes. It stands out like a sore thumb against a background of uniform ugliness. Two sides of it are surrounded by what used to be a garden, but now it's just a dusty, rocky scar where laundry hangs forever, dripping wet, looking absurdly bright in the gloom. Near the front door, bits of a porch remain, exposed raw plaster that looks like a skeleton stripped of its flesh, showing off the "insincerity" of the stucco work beneath.

Sometimes, during the day, I wander this way. Slatternly women chatter around the area gates, breaking the silence with the hoarse, wailing cry of "Coals! Any coals! Three and sixpence a sack!" shouted in a voice that sounds permanently depressed because nobody ever buys anything. But usually, the street doesn't recognize me. My old friend at the corner, embarrassed by how shabby it looks under the unfeeling sunlight, turns its face away and pretends I'm not passing by.

Only when Night, the only thing merciful enough to cover up the dirt, drops her veil over the squalor, does the house transform. Like a kindly old nurse summoned in later life, it opens its arms wide to welcome me. The frantic life inside settles down, the flickering light from the "King of Prussia" across the street goes out, and the brickwork begins to talk. It asks me about the past, reminding me of things I've tried hard to forget. Then the familiar footsteps start: *creak, step, creak*, moving in and out of the gate without noticing me. Familiar faces emerge, and we sit down to chat, like two old cronies flipping through a tattered photo album. We discuss the portraits in forgotten fashions, speaking in hushed tones of those who are now dead or scattered, exchanging smiles, sighs, and endless "Ah, me!"s and "Dear, dear!"s.

A bent, weary man approaches us, moving with quick, impatient steps that stop every fifty yards or so as he leans heavily on a tall Malacca cane. "It is a handsome face, isn't it?" I ask, staring at it, framed in shadow.

"Aye, handsome enough," the old House replies. "And it must have been even handsomer before you and I met, before the worry lines started carving themselves into it like wrinkles on an angry old aunt."

"I never could figure it out," the House muses, "who you took after. They were a lovely pair, your father and your mother, though Lord help us, what a terrible couple of children!"

"Children!" I exclaim, surprised. My father must have been well past thirty-five before the House could have known him, and my mother's face is so close to mine in the dark that I can see the many grey hairs mixing with her nice brown hair.

"Children!" the House repeats, sounding irritated, as if I've offended an elder, a common trait of the aged. "The most helpless pair of babies I ever laid eyes on. Who, pray tell, would have dreamed of trying to fix their fortune by becoming a lawyer at thirty-eight? Or, if they did dream it, who would pick the outskirts of Poplar as a smart place to put up a signboard?"

"It was supposed to be a booming neighborhood," I counter, feeling a bit defensive. No son likes hearing his family history criticized, even if deep down he agrees. "Everyone thought sailors and dock workers would move in to be near the new piers. If they did, it makes perfect sense that they would fight and argue, which is exactly what a clever lawyer needs to survive."

"Nonsense," the House snaps. "Just the smell of the place would have kept a decent man running away. And"—the grim brick face twists into a goblin-like grin—"you, of all people, calling yourself 'the clever lawyer,' advising shady characters on how to escape trouble the fast way, can you imagine it? He starved himself for two years, living on five shillings a week—that was before you arrived in London, when he was all alone. Even your mother didn't know until years later, just so no one else would lose money trusting his reputation. Do you really think the crew of merchants, brokers, and dock bullies would have found him useful, even if they moved in?"

I have no answer. The House doesn't wait for one; it keeps talking.

"And your mother!" The House continues. "Would anyone but a baby take that smooth-talking flirt into her bed without seeing right through her act? Would anyone but the silliest child, the sort that shouldn't be allowed outside alone, try to deal with her in such madness? Babies! Poor little fools, both of them."

"Tell me," I say—for at moments like this, all my common sense fails to convince me the old House is just mud and bricks. Its walls are full of voices, its floors thick with footsteps; surely it has learned to live, like a violin that plays so long it develops its own soul. "Tell me, I was just a child when life spoke to me in a foreign tongue. Was there any truth in their story?"

"Truth!" the House barks. "Just enough truth to plant a lie upon, and God knows how little soil you need for that weed. I saw what I saw, and I know what I know. Your mother had a good man, and your father a true wife, but it was the old tale: a man's path is not a woman's path, and a woman's path is not a man's path, so doubt lives between them forever."

"But they ended up together," I remind him.

"Aye, in the end," the House agrees. "That's when you understand, you humans, when you reach the finish line."

A grave-looking angel, clearly not washed since yesterday, peeks shyly at me through the railings, then, as I turn my head, darts back and vanishes.

"What happened to her?" I ask.

"She? Oh, she's doing fine," the House replies. "She lives just nearby. You probably walked past her shop. You might have seen her if you'd looked. She weighs fourteen stone, give or take, and has nine children living. She'd be delighted to see you."

"Thank you," I say, with a laugh that isn't quite a laugh. "I don't think I'll drop by." Yet I still hear the soft *pit-pat* of her tiny feet fading down the long street.

Faces crowd around me. A huge, reddish face smiles kindly, stirring up an old, strange mix of instinctive affection held back by moral judgment. I turn away and spot a massive, clean-shaven countenance featuring the ugliest mouth and the most beautiful eyes I've ever seen on a human being.

"Was he as bad, do you think, as everyone says?" I ask my ancient friend.

"Wouldn't surprise me," the House answers. "I never knew a worse one—and I never knew a better one."

The wind sweeps it away, revealing a tiny old lady hobbling along with a stick. Three corkscrew curls bob on either side of her head with every step. As she gets closer, making terrifying faces, I hear her whisper, as if sharing a secret treasure, "I'd like to skin 'em. I'd like to skin 'em all. I'd like to skin 'em all alive!"

It sounds like a demon's wish, yet I only laugh, and the little old lady, pulling off a facial expression that defies all imagination, limps beyond my sight.

Then, as if picking a contrast, a fair, laughing face appears. I saw it only a few hours ago—or rather, the poor painting that Evil has slapped over it, hiding the sweetness underneath. As I stare at it, wishing it belonged to someone who doesn't age, another face drifts back from the shadows: the one with the wicked mouth and the gentle eyes. So I stand there again, paralyzed, trapped between the two I loved so dearly, the one who taught me how to walk, the one who showed me the beauty and mystery of women. PAUL, ARRIVED IN A STRANGE LAND, LEARNS MANY THINGS, AND GOES TO MEET THE MAN IN GREY.

Fate had clearly picked a joke on me right at the start. Proper logic dictated I should have been born in June—the universal month of good luck. Instead, I popped into existence in May, the season of absolute misfortune. As I would later prove, this timing wasn't accidental. My first nurse, a formidable woman with knees like knitting needles, immediately blamed my arrival on my "natural impatience." She warned that this trait would lead to greater trouble, a prophecy that stuck like glue, confirmed by every disaster that followed.

According to Mrs. Fursey, I had landed like a bombshell, arriving two hours after news of my father's flooded mines had hit the house. Misfortunes, she taught me, never travel alone. To complete the tragic tableau, my poor mother, trying frantically to ring the bell, had smashed the cheval-glass. Superstition ran thick in her Welsh blood; she was convinced that by breaking the mirror, she had doomed my first seven years to disaster.

"And I must confess," sighed the knobbly Mrs. Fursey, wiping away a tear that probably smelled of soap and vinegar, "it does look as though there must be some truth in the saying, after all."

"Then ain't I a lucky little boy?" I asked. Up to this point, Mrs. Fursey had been selling me a bill of goods regarding my exceptional fortune. I could retire to bed at six, leaving other children starving for sleep until eight or nine. Other boys, she claimed, had no beds at all—a tragedy I secretly envied in my riotous moments. Furthermore, at the first sniff of a cold, it was my divine privilege to consume a diet of linseed gruel and brimstone-and-treacle soup. While other children suffered illness alone, I was fortified with medicinal goo designed specifically to deceive the innocent palate. If further proof were needed of my status as Providence's special protegé, consider this: I possessed Mrs. Fursey as a nurse. Suggesting I wasn't the luckiest kid alive was a bold new strategy for her.

Perceiving her error, the good dame corrected herself with alacrity.

"Oh, you!" she declared, smoothing her apron. "You are lucky enough. I was thinking of your poor mother."

"Isn't Mamma lucky?"

"Well, she hasn't been too lucky *since* you came."

"Wasn't it lucky, her having me?"

"I can't say it was, at that particular time."

"Didn't she want me?"

Mrs. Fursey was one of those well-meaning souls who believed the only sensible posture for a child was perpetual apology for existing.

"Well, I daresay she could have done without you," was the answer.

I could still see the scene vividly. I sat on a low chair before the nursery fire, knees crossed, while Mrs. Fursey's needle scratched a rhythmic, monotonous melody against her thimble. Suddenly, without moving, I struck the first blow of the war of life.

"Then why did she take me in?" I asked.

The needle stopped instantly. A sharp, clicking silence filled the room.

"Took you in! What's the child talking about? Who's took you in?"

"Why, Mamma. If she didn't want me, why did she take me in?"

Even as I posed this logically unanswerable question with a heart full of dignified resentment, a secret relief washed over me. I pictured the alternative: being refused at the bedroom window. I imagined the stork, perplexed and annoyed, looking exactly as Tom Pinfold did when Anna sniffed the fish he held by the tail. Would the stork have muttered to himself, scratching his long, compass-like leg? Had I wriggled nervously inside him like a worm carried by a blackbird? Where would he have dropped me? Probably Mrs. Fursey's cottage, nearest to the action. But surely she wouldn't take me. Next door lived Mr. Chumdley, the cobbler, a poor-spirited ogre who hammered boots in a damp cave with dirty hands. I would have hated being his boy. Perhaps nobody would want me. I grew pensive, imagining myself as the village's ultimate reject, stranded on the stork's hands.

"Nurse," I pressed, "where did I come from?"

"The stork brought you," she replied, her thread picking up speed again.

"Yes, I know. But where did the stork get me from?"

Mrs. Fursey paused, likely calculating whether the truth might inflate my ego too dangerously. Finally, she gambled.

"Oh, from Heaven."

"But I thought Heaven was a place where you *went* to," I countered. "Not where you *comed* from." I remembered using "comed" and "goned" frequently, a source of great anxiety for my mother, who feared divergent opinions confused the young mind.

Mrs. Fursey ignored my grammar in dignified silence.

"You came from Heaven," she repeated, "and you'll go to Heaven—if you're good."

"Do all little boys and girls come from Heaven?"

"So they say." Her tone suggested this was merely a popular fallacy she personally disclaimed responsibility for.

"And did you come from Heaven, Mrs. Fursey?"

"Of course I did. Where do you think I came from?"

Instantly, Heaven lost its exalted pedestal in my eyes. Previously, everyone I knew was going there. Now, linked to the origin of Mrs. Fursey, much of its charm evaporated.

But this revelation brought a fresh grief. Refusing to console myself with the idea that my fate was shared by all children, I seized upon my specific case with childish egoism.

"Didn't they want me in Heaven then, either?" I asked. "Weren't they fond of me up there?"

The misery in my voice pierced her bosom. She answered more sympathetically than usual.

"Oh, they liked you well enough, I daresay. I like you, but I like to get rid of you sometimes."

There was no doubt about the last statement. At six o'clock bedtime, I often suspected it was actually half-past five. Her answer offered no comfort. It became crystal clear: I was unwanted in Heaven, unwanted on Earth. God was glad to discard me. My mother could have done without me. Nobody wanted me. So why was I here?

Then, like the sudden slam of a dark door, a feeling struck my childish brain. Somewhere, Something must need me, or I couldn't exist. Something I belonged to, belonging to me, a part of me as I was of It. This sensation would recur throughout my childhood, though I couldn't articulate it until much later, when a Portuguese Jew explained my thought. All I knew then was that in that moment, I realized I lived, that I was "I".

The next instant, darkness returned, and I was once again a puzzled little boy by the nursery fire, questioning a village dame about life.

A new thought—or rather, the recollection of an old one—struck me.

"Nurse, why haven't we got a husband?"

Mrs. Fursey ceased her sewing and stared.

"What maggot has the child got into its head now?" she observed. "Who hasn't got a husband?"

"Why, Mamma."

"Don't talk nonsense, Master Paul; you know your Mamma has got a husband."

"No, she ain't."

"And don't contradict. Your Mamma's husband is your Papa, who lives in London."

"What's the good of *him*!"

Mrs. Fursey's reply seemed unnecessarily vehement.

"You wicked child, you! Where's your commandments? Your Father is in London working hard to earn money to keep you in idleness, and you sit there and say 'What's the good of him!' I'd be ashamed to be such an ungrateful little brat."

I hadn't meant to be ungrateful. My words were a direct echo of a conversation I'd overheard the previous day between my mother and my Aunt.

"There she goes, moping again," my Aunt had said. "Drat me if ever I saw such a thing to mope as a woman."

My Aunt was entitled to preach. She grumbled all day, cheerfully. My Mother stood with her hands clasped behind her back, gazing through the high French window into the garden beyond. It must have been spring; white and yellow crocuses decked the grass.

"I want a husband," my Mother had answered in a tone so ludicrously childish that I had looked up from my fairy story, expecting her to transform into a little girl. "I hate not having a husband."

"Help us and save us," my Aunt had retorted. "How many more does a girl want? She's got one."

"What's the good of him all that way off," my Mother had pouted. "I want him here where I can get at him."

I had often heard of this Father, living far away in London, to whom we owed all life's blessings. Yet, my childish attempts to reconcile information with reflection led me to assign him an entirely spiritual existence. I agreed with my Mother that such a figure, however reverent, was no substitute for the flesh-and-blood father possessed by luckier folk—the big, strong, masculine type who could carry a fellow pig-a-back or take a chap sailing.

My parents' situation was, to put it mildly, irregular. They had been estranged for several years, yet despite their advanced ages—my father approaching forty, my mother somewhere past thirty—they possessed the vital energy of adolescents trapped in geriatric bodies. To the uninitiated eye, particularly that of an inquisitive maid or a lonely bachelor, they presented a distorted vision of matrimony. I would have described a husband as a creature incapable of finding peace unless his spouse hovered nearby, someone who would burst from his office door twenty times a day to inquire, "Maggie, are you busy? I need to discuss a matter of business," or "Maggie, are you alone? I'm descending immediately." Conversely, I would have defined a wife as a woman whose gaze was perpetually illuminated with affection when fixed upon her partner, someone delighted by his presence and distressed by his absence. However, in cases like theirs, such definitions were woefully inadequate.

Furthermore, living with any married couple required specific tactical maneuvers. One must raise one's feet high above the table surface lest they be mistaken for others and subsequently crushed. Furthermore, entering a room without prior auditory signaling—a practice known in Stageland as "noise without"—proved highly inadvisable. The nervous intruder, clutching the door handle, would be met with the sudden, spring-like eruption of occupants into the air, followed by a bizarre scuttling of limbs, only to discover the residents seated rigidly in opposite corners, deeply engrossed in books or embroidery. Yet, regarding certain households, such precautions were superfluous.

I, unfortunately, exerted little control over my parents in these matters. My interruptions were quickly dismissed with the weary sigh of, "Oh, it's only Spud," usually accompanied by the removal of a sofa cushion. My aunt, however, commanded a different kind of reverence. Though she rarely spoke, her efforts to spare their feelings bordered on the excessive. She traversed the house solely to the rhythm of a sustained, piercing cough. As my father once noted, albeit ungraciously, the volume of barking generated by my aunt in a single day would have earned accolades from a hospital ward full of dying consumptives; for a robust and healthy lady, the expenditure of nervous force must have been astronomical. Moreover, ensuring that her eyes had witnessed nothing unintended, she invariably entered rooms backward, slamming doors loudly and laboriously before turning around. Through dark passages, she ambulated while singing. Few women could have displayed greater consideration, yet—such is the nature of humanity—neither my father nor my mother extended gratitude to her, as far as I could discern.

Indeed, paradoxically, the more sympathy she showed, the more irritable they became.

"I believe, Fanny, you hate seeing Luke and me happy together," my mother declared one day, emerging from the kitchen to intercept my aunt, who was preparing to invade the drawing-room by dropping teaspoons at precise five-second intervals outside the door. "Do not make such a spectacle of yourself." Her tone was genuinely unkind.

"Hate it!" my aunt replied. "Why should I? Why not allow a pair of turtle-doves to coo, when their combined age barely exceeds seventy?" The gentle candor of her responses often took me aback.

As for my father, he turned positively vindictive. I recall the occasion vividly; it was the first, though certainly not the last, time I witnessed him lose his temper. Whatever sparked the subject, my father halted abruptly. We were strolling along the canal bank.

"Your aunt"—my father likely did not intend it, but his tone and manner invariably conveyed to me that he regarded me as personally responsible for her existence. This burden weighed heavily on me. "Your aunt is the most cantankerous, the most—" he broke off, shaking a fist toward the setting sun. "I wish to God," he cried, "your aunt had a comfortable little income of her own, with a freehold cottage in the countryside, by God I do!" But the next moment, shame washing over him: "Not but what sometimes, of course, she can be very nice, you know," he added hastily; "do not tell your mother what I said just now."

Another observer of this domestic farce was Susan, our maid-of-all-work, the first of a long and varied lineage extending until the advent of Amy, to whom heaven sent blessings. Susan was a stout, elderly female prone to sudden, inexplicable fits of sleepiness, allegedly caused by "trouble." Yet her heart, she proudly boasted, was always in the right place. She could never witness my parents sitting near one another without collapsing into tears, the sight of conjugal bliss serving as a painful reminder of the past glories of her own failed marriage.

Though an earnest seeker, I could never fully grasp the intricacies of Susan's marital history. Whether her answers were deliberately evasive or the result of a naturally incoherent mind, I could not say; they tended toward confusion. On Mondays, I observed Susan weeping regretfully into Brussels sprouts because she had been prevented from watering "his" grave at Manor Park due to other duties. By Tuesdays, I listened, blood chilling, to her recitals of intentions to seize "him" by the scruff of his neck if she ever regained the luxury of touching him.

"But, I thought, Susan, he was dead," I ventured naturally.

"So did I, Master Paul," she rejoined. "That was his artfulness."

"Then he isn't buried in Manor Park Cemetery?"

"Not yet; but he'll wish he was, the half-baked monkey, when I get hold of him."

"Then he wasn't a good man?"

"Who?"

"Your husband."

"Who says he ain't a good man?" It was Susan's ability to leap from tension to tension that most bewildered me. "If anybody says he ain't, I'll gouge their eye out!"

I hastened to assure Susan that my remark was an inquiry, not an assertion.

"He brings me a bottle of gin—for my headaches—every time he comes home," Susan continued, citing evidence, "every blessed time."

At such junctures, I would retreat to the safer atmospheres of German grammar or mixed fractions.

We suffered greatly from Susan in various ways. Regarding the admirable position of her heart, we all felt it our duty to overlook mere failings of the flesh—all but my aunt, who never feigned sentimentality.

"She's a lazy hussy," my aunt declared one morning while rinsing her mouth; "a gulping, snorting, lazy hussy, that's what she is." There was some justification for my aunt's indignation. It was eleven o'clock, and Susan was still asleep off an attack of what she termed "new-ralgy."

"She has seen a good deal of trouble," said my mother, who was drying dishes.

"And if she were my cook and housemaid," replied my aunt, "she would see more, the slut!"

"She's not a good servant in many respects," my mother admitted, "but I think she's good-hearted."

"Oh, drat her heart," was my aunt's retort. "The proper place for that heart is on the doorstep. And that's where I'd put it, along with her and her box, if I had my way."

Susan's departure did occur shortly thereafter. It happened one Saturday night. My mother ascended the stairs looking ghastly.

"Luke," she said, "please run for the doctor."

"What's the matter?" asked my father.

"Susan," gasped my mother, "she's lying on the kitchen floor breathing in the strangest fashion and quite unable to speak."

"I'll go for Washburn," said my father; "if I am quick, I shall catch him at the dispensary."

Five minutes later, my father returned panting, trailed by the doctor. This was a large, black-bearded man who possessed the uncanny knack of appearing larger than he actually was. He descended the kitchen stairs two at a time, shaking the entire edifice. He brushed my mother aside and bent over the unconscious Susan, who lay on her back with her mouth agape. "Feeling better?" asked the doctor, still holding the pail; "have another dose?"

Susan began to gather herself, intending to express her feelings; but before she could utter the first word, he had pushed the three of us outside and slammed the door behind us.

Whether from the fact that so often of an evening—our drawing-room being reserved always as a show-room in case of chance visitors; Cowper's poems, open face-downwards on the wobbly loo table; the half-finished crochet work, suggestive of elegant leisure, thrown carelessly over the arm of the smaller easy-chair—this office would become our sitting-room, its books and papers, as things of no account, being huddled out of sight; or whether from the readiness with which my father would come out of it at all times to play at something else—at cricket in the back garden on dry days or ninepins in the passage on wet, charging back into it again whenever a knock sounded at the front door—I cannot say. But I know that as a child it never occurred to me to regard my father's profession as a serious affair. To me he was merely playing there, surrounded by big books and bundles of documents, labelled profusely but consisting only of blank papers; by japanned tin boxes, lettered imposingly, but for the most part empty. "Sutton Hampden, Esq.," I remember was practically my mother's work-box. The "Drayton Estates" yielded apparently nothing but apples, a fruit of which my father was fond; while "Mortgages" it was not until later in life I discovered had no connection with poems in manuscript, some in course of correction, others completed.

Now, as the door opened, he rose and came towards us. His hair stood up from his head, for it was a habit of his to rumple it as he talked; and this added to his evident efforts to compose his face into an expression of businesslike gravity, added emphasis, if such were needed, to the suggestion of the over-long schoolboy making believe.

"This is the youngster," said my father, taking me from my mother, and passing me on. "Tall for his age, isn't he?"

With a twist of his thick lips, he rolled the evil-smelling cigar he was smoking from the left corner of his mouth to the right; and held out a fat and not too clean hand, which, as it closed round mine, brought to my mind the picture of the walrus in my natural history book; with the other he flapped me kindly on the head.

"Like 'is mother, wonderfully like 'is mother, ain't 'e?" he observed, still holding my hand. "And that," he added with a wink of one of his small eyes towards my father, "is about the 'ighest compliment I can pay 'im, eh?"

His eyes were remarkably small, but marvellously bright and piercing; so much so that when he turned them again upon me I tried to think quickly of something nice about him, feeling sure that he could see right into me.

"And where are you thinkin' of sendin' 'im?" he continued; "Eton or 'Arrow?"

That afternoon, she took me to a hall in the neighborhood where we saw a magic lantern and a conjurer. "It was a silly dream," she explained with a curious, broken laugh. "I must have been thinking of the conjurer. I dreamt a wicked Magician spirited you away from me. I could not find you and was all alone in the world."

She put her arms around me, tight as almost to hurt, and we stayed thus until sleep claimed me again.

Towards the close of the holidays, my mother and I visited Mrs. Teidelmann at her great stone-built house in Clapton. A note had announced terrible headaches and impending departure from England. Inside, the house was a cavern of treasures, a chaotic heap of pictures, statues, and curiosities collected by the late Mr. Teidelmann, contrasting sharply with the shrivelled, uninteresting corpse in the coffin.

Mrs. Teidelmann lay asleep on a sofa, dressed in a loose lace robe revealing thin, snow-white arms. As we entered, the room fell silent, save for the muffled ticking of a great French clock. After a few restless movements, she began to murmur, whispering my father's name clearly in the quiet: "Ah no, we must not. Luke, my darling."

My mother rose swiftly, speaking in matter-of-fact tones. "Go, Paul," she said, pushing me gently out and closing the door.

Half an hour later, she returned, walking as one in a dream, eyes seeing nothing. At the gate, she snapped back to life. "You must say nothing, Paul," she commanded. "Delirious people use strange words. Never breathe a word."

I promised, and from that day, her manner changed entirely. Not another angry word escaped her lips, no angry flash lit her eyes. Mrs. Teidelmann remained away for three months, during which time my father managed her affairs, though my mother also wrote long letters in the twilight, a secret vigil near the window. When she returned, they were instantly reconciled, appearing as the closest of friends. Peace returned to the house; my father became tender again, and my mother thoughtful and soft-spoken. Yet, despite this restoration, every day my mother grew feebler, losing the little ways that had once been hers.

Summer came, with its panting heat and stifling nights, and my mother moved about the house ever more listlessly. Dr. Hal suggested the air was the problem, and eventually, we packed for a holiday to the village where old Jacob's ruined tower stood on the headland.

The eighteen months that followed—though the finale arrived quicker than our wildest calculations anticipated—are, I am convinced, the golden era of my parents' existence. If "happy" feels too cliché for a description of perfection, let us call it "sublime." To them, it seemed as though Divine Providence had knocked gently at the door of mortality, whispering, "Not yet. Hold onto each other a trifle longer. Just a little while." In those final, luminous days, they discarded every artificial burden. The world's noise, the neighbors' prying eyes, the petty anxieties of maintaining a facade—all vanished. They stripped themselves bare, caring for nothing but the warmth of their mutual embrace, learning to understand one another with a tenderness that bordered on the miraculous. Though their circumstances remained humble, the poverty they endured was spiritual rather than material; they wore their rags with dignity because they felt no shame, no terror of judgment. A profound charity bloomed within them, wider than their needs, overflowing toward the universe. Looking back, I suspect we err when we try to banish the thought of Death entirely. Perhaps He is not a grim reaper but a friend who whispers, "Is it worth it? To worry so much about yourselves? Be kind."

From them radiated a grace that transformed everyone around. Even Aunt Fan, in a reversal of her lifelong policy, decided to attempt amiability. One afternoon, shortly after returning from Devonshire, she announced this new regime with dramatic flair.

"I'm a beast of an old woman!" she declared.

"Don't say that, Fan!" my mother pleaded.

"They'd be fools if they didn't!" Aunt Fan interrupted. "Do you really mean it? Of course not."

"Nonsense," my mother laughed. "You like seeing people squirm."

"I *am* disagreeable," Aunt Fan insisted, though there was a glint of mischief in her eye. "I can be agreeable if I choose, and I'm the queen of agreeableness."

"So why not choose?" my mother asked.

"Tried it once," Aunt Fan mused, falling into a reverie. "Fell flat. Nothing could fall flatter."

"It didn't attract attention," my mother smiled. "But one shouldn't be agreeable just to stand out."

"It wasn't about standing out," Aunt Fan reasoned. "It gave no satisfaction. A disagreeable person shines brightest when they are disagreeable."

"My dear sister, I struggle to agree with you there."

"Let 'em have it!" Aunt Fan whispered fiercely. "That's how you teach 'em sense."

Strangely, my aunt was right, and my mother wrong. My father, the first to notice, asked one evening if "poor old Fan" was unwell.

"Her manner is... weird," he observed wistfully. "I almost wish she wouldn't try. We were used to it, and she was amusing."

James Gadley, a former office boy, remained blissfully ignorant of the firm's decline. On subsequent visits, armed with knitting needles, Aunt Fan maintained a safe distance, and Gadley, realizing his tactics failed, retreated with a startled exclamation.

Yet, despite the physical comedy, there was a genuine intellectual spark. Gadley once proclaimed, "Miss Davies, you should have been a solicitor's wife!" Convinced that flattery was the quickest path to agreement, she set about flattering everyone with a sincerity that often sounded like thinly veiled sarcasm. When my father told a trifling joke, she would erupt in exaggerated delight. When my mother displayed a new bonnet, Aunt Fan would simulate shock, declaring it the attire of a saucy fourteen-year-old girl.

For a brief, chaotic interlude, she also targeted Amy, our new servant, descending into the kitchen to be deliberately disagreeable. Amy, however, was a philosopher who accepted the world by calculating that there were only twenty-four hours in a day. She reconciled herself to Aunt Fan's rudeness with perpetual cheerfulness. "If I wasn't listening to her, something worse might be happening," Amy would tell my mother. "Everything's all the same when it's over."

Amy herself was a whirlwind of romantic potential. A stop-gap arrangement, she had arrived claiming to marry in a few weeks. A month later, she revealed she was reconsidering. "Better change your mind before it's too late," she advised my mother, hinting at a new suitor. Indeed, Amy was a creature of infinite variety, engaging a dozen "poor dears"—butchers, bakers, soldiers—and treating them all with equal devotion. "You've got something else to do," she decreed. And so, we found ourselves in the capable, chaotic hands of Amy, left to praise the Lord for His goodness.

Meanwhile, Barbara appeared frequently, bringing sunshine that made troubles seem distant. Old Hasluck, though unable to visit himself, sent fruit and flowers through her. My father, growing lighter in spirit as the end approached, seemed to age backward. In the beginning, I was far from a favorite with my schoolmates, and trouble first dwelt upon me. My ostracism was painfully plain, and, true to my nature, I brooded over it in silence.

All things pass, even the self-imposed misery of the shy young man condemned to solitude by nature's decree. Winter evenings came, and I turned to work; in the glow of a handful of fire and two flickering candles, my "apartment" became a haven. I procured paper, pens, and ink. What more does a writer require? He is merely a vessel waiting to be filled by spirits who may speak or remain silent.

Before me lay London, teeming with a million characters—grave and gay—and ten thousand romances, mysteries, and humors. It stretched out, begging only for intelligent observation. Yet, I could never conceive of weaving a story from the things I truly knew. My tales were populated by cottage maids and bucolic yeomen; my scenes unfolded in windmills, amidst mountains, or beside moated granges. I suspect this specific brand of folly is common to most youthful fictionists.

A trail of gentle melancholy draped over these creations. Sentiment was then the reigning fashion, and as a beginner, I scrupulously adhered to trends. Generally speaking, to be a heroine in my stories was a death sentence. Regardless of how naturally her hair might curl—or how curly hair, I was convinced, signified vitality—whatever signs of robust health she displayed in Chapter One ("dancing eyes," "colour that came and went"), she was tolerably certain to meet an untimely grave. Snowdrops and early primroses (botanical knowledge gleaned from *Our Garden Favourites, Illustrated*) bloomed there like flowers in a greenhouse. Near the coast, sea breezes would whimsically select that particular churchyard as their playground. Years later, a white-haired man would lead little children there to retell the tale, a dismal entertainment indeed.

Occasionally, the gentleman fell victim to my literature's deadly atmosphere. It mattered little, for in his final soliloquy, he would usually conclude, "It was better so." Snowdrops and primroses offered no consolation, and his grave, marked by a rude cross, typically sat in an unfrequented corner of the African veldt or amidst burning sands. Describing such final scenery required a trip to the British Museum reading-room.

Dismal little fledglings! Again and again I drove them from the nest, only to watch them flutter back, soiled, crumpled, and physically damaged. Yet, one person had admired them, cried over them—myself.

I exhausted every method. Sometimes I dispatched them with curt, take-it-or-leave-it business notes. Other times, I attached pathetic pleas for consideration. I declared prices of five guineas, requesting crossed cheques, or I tickled editorial greed by offering my first contribution free—a sample packet, so to speak, with one trial supposedly sufficient. At other junctures, I wrote sarcastically, enclosing a brutally penned rejection note. Or I wrote frankly, explaining I was a novice and asking to be told my faults, if any.

None found a resting place for their feet. A month, a week, a couple of days, they would vanish, only to return. I never lost a single one. I wished I had; it would have varied the monotony.

Their triumph would come. Rewriting the first and last sheets, I would send them forth again with fresh hope.

The spirit had always been willing, but my flesh was weak. Fired by emulation, I had occasionally essayed a cigarette at school. Indeed, from internal evidence, I am strongly of opinion that this present play is a case in point. Well, chickens come home to roost: I adapt from him. What is the difference?"

"Simply this," he continued, pouring himself another glass of wine, "that whereas, owing to the anomalous state of the copyright laws, stealing from a foreign author is legal and commendable, against stealing from a living English author there is a certain prejudice."

"And the consequences, I am afraid, you will find somewhat unpleasant," I suggested, trying to inject a note of warning.

He laughed, a sound devoid of frivolity. "You mean, my dear Kelver, that *you* will."

"Don't look so dumbfounded," he went on. "You cannot be so stupid as you are pretending to be. The original manuscript at the Lord Chamberlain's office is in your handwriting. You knew our friend as well as I did, and you visited him. Why, the whole tour has been under your management. You have arranged everything—most excellently; I have been quite surprised."

My anger did not arrive until later. For the moment, a sudden, blinding light of fear obscured everything else.

"But you told me," I cried, the words tumbling out, "it was only a matter of form, that you wanted to keep your name out of it because—"

He was looking at me with an expression of genuine astonishment. My words began to appear humorous even to myself. I found it difficult to believe I had been the fool I was now seeing myself to have been.

"I am sorry," he said, "I am really sorry. I took you for a man of the world. I thought you merely did not wish to know anything."

Still, to my shame, fear was the thing uppermost in my heart. "You are not going to put it all on to me?" I pleaded.

He had risen. He laid his hand upon my shoulder. Instead of flinging it off, I was glad of its kindly pressure. He was the only man to whom I could look for help.

"Don't take it so seriously," he said. "He will merely think the manuscript has been lost. As likely as not, he will be unable to remember whether he wrote it or merely thought of writing it. No one in the company will say anything: it isn't their business. We must set to work. I had altered it a good deal before you saw it, and changed all the names of the characters. We will retain the third act: it is the only thing of real value in the play. The situation is not original; you have as much right to dish it up as he had. In a fortnight we will have the whole thing so different that if he saw it himself he would only imagine we had got hold of the idea and had forestalled him."

There were moments during the next few weeks when I listened to the voice of my good angel, when I saw clearly that even from the lowest point of view he was giving me sound advice. I would go to the man, tell him frankly the whole truth.

But Vane never left my elbow. Suspecting, I suppose, he gave me clearly to understand that if I did so, I must expect no mercy from him. My story, denounced by him as an outrageous lie, would be regarded as the funk-inspired subterfuge of a young rogue. At the best, I should handicap myself with suspicion that would last me throughout my career. On the other hand, what harm had we done? Presented in some twenty or so small towns, where it would soon be forgotten, a play something like. Most plays were something like. Our friend would produce his version and reap a rich harvest; ours would disappear. If by any unlikely chance discussion should arise, the advertisement would be to his advantage. So soon as possible, we would replace it by a new piece altogether. A young man of my genius could surely write something better than hotch-potch such as this; experience was all that I had lacked. As regards one's own conscience, was not the world's honesty a mere question of convention? It took me three years to win that handshake. For the first six months I remained in Deptford. There was excellent material to be found there for humorous articles, essays, stories; likewise for stories tragic and pathetic. But I owed a hundred and fifty pounds—a little over two hundred it reached to, I found, when I came to add up the actual figures. So I paid strict attention to business, left the tears to be garnered by others—better fitted maybe for the task; kept to my own patch, reaped and took to market only the laughter.

At the beginning I sent each manuscript to Norah; she had it copied out, debited me with the cost received payment, and sent me the balance. At first my earnings were small; but Norah was an excellent agent; rapidly they increased. Dan grew quite cross with her, wrote in pained surprise at her greed. The "matter" was fair, but in no way remarkable. Any friend of hers, of course, he was anxious to assist; but business was business. In justice to his proprietors, he could not and would not pay more than the market value. Miss Deleglise, replying curtly in the third person, found herself in perfect accord with Mr. Brian as to business being business. If Mr. Brian could not afford to pay her price for material so excellent, other editors with whom Miss Deleglise was equally well acquainted could and would. Answer by return would greatly oblige, pending which the manuscript then in her hands she retained. Mr. Brian, understanding he had found his match, grumbled but paid. Whether he had any suspicion who "Jack Homer" might be, he never confessed; but he would have played the game, pulled his end of the rope, in either case. Nor was he allowed to decide the question for himself. Competition was introduced into the argument. Of purpose a certain proportion of my work my agent sent elsewhere. "Jack Homer" grew to be a commodity in demand. For, seated at my rickety table, I laughed as I wrote, the fourth wall of the dismal room fading before my eyes revealing vistas beyond.

Still, it was slow work. Humour is not an industrious maid; declines to be bustled, will work only when she feels inclined—does not often feel inclined; gives herself a good many unnecessary airs; if worried, packs up and goes off, Heaven knows where! comes back when she thinks she will: a somewhat unreliable young person. To my literary labours I found it necessary to add journalism. I lacked Dan's magnificent assurance. Fate never befriends the nervous. Had I burst into the editorial sanctum, the editor most surely would have been out; if in, would have been a man of short ways, would have seen to it that I went out quickly. But the idea was not to be thought of; Robert Macaire himself in my one coat would have been diffident, apologetic. I joined the ranks of the penny-a-liners—to be literally exact, three halfpence a liners. In company with half a dozen other shabby outsiders—some of them young men like myself seeking to climb; others, older men who had sunk—I attended inquests, police courts; flew after fire engines; rejoiced in street accidents; yearned for murders. Somewhat vulture-like we lived precariously upon the misfortunes of others. We made occasional half crowns by providing the public with scandal, occasional crowns by keeping our information to ourselves.

"I think, gentlemen," would explain our spokesman in a hoarse whisper, on returning to the table, "I think the corpse's brother-in-law is anxious that the affair, if possible, should be kept out of the papers."

The closeness and attention with which we would follow that particular case, the fulness and completeness of our notes, would be quite remarkable. Our spokesman would rise, drift carelessly away, to return five minutes later, wiping his mouth.

"Not a very interesting case, gentlemen, I don't think. Shall we say five shillings apiece?" Sometimes a sense of the dignity of our calling would induce us to stand out for ten.

And here also my sense of humour came to my aid; gave me perhaps an undue advantage over my competitors. Twelve good men and true had been asked to say how a Lascar sailor had met his death. It was perfectly clear how he had met his death. A plumber, working on the roof of a small two-storeyed house, had slipped and fallen on him. The plumber had escaped with a few bruises; the unfortunate sailor had been picked up dead. Some blame attached to the plumber. His mate, an excellent witness, told us the whole story.

"I was fixing a gas-pipe on the first floor," said the man. "The prisoner was on the roof."

"We won't call him 'the prisoner,'" interrupted the coroner, "at least, not yet. Refer to him, if you please, as the 'last witness.'"

"The last witness," corrected himself the man. "He shouts down the chimney to know if I was ready for him."

"'Ready and waiting,' I says.

"'Right,' he says; 'I'm coming in through the window.'

"'Wait a bit,' I says; 'I'll go down and move the ladder for you.

"'It's all right,' he says; 'I can reach it.'

"'No, you can't,' I says. 'It's the other side of the chimney.'

"'I can get round,' he says.

"Well, before I knew what had happened, I hears him go, smack! I rushes to the window and looks out: I see him on the pavement, sitting up like.

"'Hullo, Jim,' I says. 'Have you hurt yourself?'

"'I think I'm all right,' he says, 'as far as I can tell. But I wish you'd come down. This bloke I've fallen on looks a bit sick.'"

The others headed their flimsy "Sad Accident," a title truthful but not alluring. I altered mine to "Plumber in a Hurry--Fatal Result." Saying as little as possible about the unfortunate sailor, I called the attention of plumbers generally to the coroner's very just remarks upon the folly of undue haste; pointed out to them, as a body, the trouble that would arise if somehow they could not cure themselves of this tendency to rush through their work without a moment's loss of time.

It established for me a useful reputation. The sub-editor of one evening paper condescended so far as to come out in his shirt-sleeves and shake hands with me.

"That's the sort of thing we want," he told me; "a light touch, a bit of humour."

I snatched fun from fires (I sincerely trust the insurance premiums were not overdue); culled quaintness from street rows; extracted merriment from catastrophes the most painful, and prospered.

Though often within a stone's throw of the street, I unremittingly avoided the old house at Poplar. I was suffering inconvenience at this period by reason of finding myself two distinct individuals, contending with each other. My object was to encourage the new Paul—the sensible, practical, pushful Paul, whose career began to look promising; to drive away from interfering with me his strangely unlike twin—the old childish Paul of the sad, far-seeing eyes. Sometimes out of the cracked looking-glass his wistful, yearning face would plead to me; but I would sternly shake my head. I knew well his cunning. Had I let him have his way, he would have led me through the maze of streets he knew so well, past the broken railings (outside which he would have left my body standing), along the weedy pathway, through the cracked and dented door, up the creaking staircase to the dismal little chamber where we once—he and I together—had sat dreaming foolish dreams.

"Come," he would whisper; "it is so near. Let us push aside the chest of drawers very quietly, softly raise the broken sash, prop it open with the Latin dictionary, lean our elbows on the sill, listen to the voices of the weary city, voices calling to us from the darkness."

But I was too wary to be caught. "Later on," I would reply to him; "when I have made my way, when I am stronger to withstand your wheedling. Then I will go with you, if you are still in existence, my sentimental little friend. We will dream again the old impractical, foolish dreams—and laugh at them."

So he would fade away, and in his place would nod to me approvingly a businesslike-looking, wide-awake young fellow.

But to one sentimental temptation I succumbed. My position was by now assured; there was no longer any reason for my hiding myself. I determined to move westward. I had not intended to soar so high, but passing through Guildford Street one day, the creeper-covered corner house that my father had once thought of taking recalled itself to me. A card was in the fanlight. I knocked and made enquiries. A bed-sitting-room upon the third floor was vacant. I remembered it well the moment the loquacious landlady opened its door.

"This shall be your room, Paul," said my father. So clearly his voice sounded behind me that I turned, forgetting for the moment it was but a memory. "You will be quiet here, and we can shut out the bed and washstand with a screen."

So my father had his way. It was a pleasant, sunny little room, overlooking the gardens of the hospital.

I ascended the slope of my ambition with the slowness and steadiness of a man carrying a heavy burden, deliberately discarding every romantic delusion in favor of cold, hard business. Inside me, however, resided "Little Paul"—the boy with the sad eyes—who constantly tried to seduce me from this grim duty. Yet, my determination was ironclad; I refused to rest until I had bought back my honesty. Driven by a hunger that grew daily, almost physical in its intensity—to feel again the grip of Norah's strong white hand in mine—I pushed forward. Heaven alone could guess what the seducer might have wrought of me otherwise: perhaps a politician, a minor poet, or a hamstrung editor, paralyzed by convictions and rendered useless to the world. Occasionally, when humor granted me a week-long holiday, I would scribble stories purely for pleasure. They made no waves. Success in purposeful labor, I learned, grows like moss—slowly. And by the time I cleared my debts, I had finally carved out a reputation.

"Madness!" cried my practical friends. "You'd be tossing away a guaranteed fortune for a dubious competence. Stick to what you know; starting over now?"

"You'd find it nearly impossible," they warned, dispensing words seasoned with false wisdom. "Once a humorist, always a humorist. It's as futile as asking a comic actor to play Hamlet. Even if he delivers the finest performance ever seen, the audience will simply laugh—or flee."

Seeking solace in the Club, "Goggles" and I retired to a quiet corner to pour our souls into one another. He regaled me with his vision of Romeo—a concept I suspected was brilliant, though I couldn't visualize it. I listened with feigned enthusiasm, paying the price for him listening to my grandiose theories on monumental literature and its potential to aid mankind.

"Perhaps in a future existence," Goggles mused one evening as the clock tolled seven, "I shall be a great tragedian, and you a famous poet. Meanwhile, as your friend Brian says, we are sinning against our mercies. After all, to live is the most vital thing."

I escorted him to the cloakroom, assisting him with his coat.

"Take my advice," he tapped my chest, fixing his fish-like, humorous eyes on me. If I hadn't known his intent was serious, I would have burst out laughing at the comedy of his expression. "Marry some dear little woman. One truly doesn't understand life until the babies arrive to explain it."

Back at my fireside chair, the images of wife, children, and home swam before me. Was this not the true vocation of a living man, not a dreamer? I saw them surrounding me, lending dignity and responsibility to my days. The fair, sweet companion, the helpers, the little faces molded in our image, their questioning voices offering the keys to life's riddles. Compared to these phantoms of morning mist, what were my other ambitions? Fading illusions in the sunlight.

Then Hodgson arrived. "I need you to write a comic opera," he announced, holding an open letter. "The public is weary of these endless French translations. I want something English, something fresh and original."

"The English is easy enough," I replied cautiously. "But I wouldn't clamor for 'original' if I were you."

"Why not?" he asked, looking up.

"You might get it," I answered. "And then you'd be disappointed."

He chuckled. "What I mean is, something we can call 'new and original' on the program. You've written a few successful farces. This will be a big break. I'm willing to risk it. People are starting to talk about you."

A serious responsibility hung heavy in the air. A young man hesitates; it can make or mar. Comic opera, above all arts, demands experience. I hid the truth: in my desk lay a four-act drama throbbing with humanity, a serious play I had hoped to use to storm the theater and establish myself as a legitimate dramatist.

"It's simple," Hodgson urged. "Just give Atherton plenty of comic business—you can handle that. Give Gleeson something pretty in waltz time, and Duncan a part where she changes costumes every fifteen minutes. Done."

"I'll tell you what," Hodgson continued. "I'll take the whole troupe down to Richmond on Sunday. Coach, leaves the theater at half-past ten. It'll be a chance for you to study them. Talk to them, learn what they can do. Next week, we draft a contract."

It was too good an opportunity to refuse, even if success would pin me forever to the role of the jester. Remunerative, comic opera certainly was.

Outside on the Strand, a crowd had gathered.

"Nothing wrong, is there?" the leading lady asked anxiously, alighting late from her cab. "Is it a fire?"

"Merely assembled to see you," Hodgson replied, not lifting his eyes from his letters.

"Oh, good gracious!" she cried, "Let's move quickly!"

"Box seat, my dear," Hodgson countered.

Accepting our aid, she climbed the ladder with charming hesitation. Our low comedian caused a delay, twice pretending to lose his footing and sliding back into the arms of the stoic door-keeper. The crowd, mostly small boys enjoying the spectacle, laughed and applauded. Encouraged, the comedian made a third attempt, crawling onto the roof and sitting atop the tenor, who smiled mechanically.

As the buses rolled by, our low comedian bowed profoundly to the first dozen, then collapsed back onto the tenor or me. Only we two seemed to appreciate the absurdity; the crowd largely ignored him after Charing Cross. Settling down, he brushed dust from his knees.

"People ask why I do this off-stage," he said. "It amuses me."

"At Willesden," he continued, "I sprang from the carriage and ran down the platform. Fifty people left their seats to chase me. I explained I'd traveled second class with a first-class ticket. People think I do it for attention. I do it for pleasure."

"She says it's troublesome," he noted, "but she can't understand why anyone wants to play the fool. As a rule, when she's with me, I don't."

"She's not here?" I asked.

"Suffers from headaches," he replied. "She hardly goes anywhere."

I felt genuine pity.

Throughout the drive to Richmond, his irrepressible urge to entertain surfaced repeatedly. In Kensington, he balanced a horn on his nose; at Kew, he stopped the coach to offer a boarding school for sixpence; at Richmond Hill, he attempted to hire a deaf gentleman in a Bath-chair to race us up for a shilling. The party roared with laughter, save for Hodgson, buried in correspondence, and an elegant young lady fresh from the Divorce Court, whose reputation was valued at a hundred pounds a week.

At the hotel, before lunch, we strolled into the garden. Our low comedian spotted an elderly gentleman drinking vermouth and froze in astonishment. Making a bee-line, he seized the stranger's hand.

"Aunt Martha?" he gasped, his face twisting into terror. "How is she?"

"Don't tell me she's dead!"

The gentleman, bewildered, shook his head. "I don't know you."

"Not know me? You've forgotten... Aren't you Steggles?"

"No."

"My mistake!" The comedian tossed down the stranger's drink and sprinted away. The elderly gentleman, taken aback, was consoled by an explanation that the intruder was Charlie Atherton—the *real* Charlie Atherton.

"Oh, is it?" the gentleman growled. "Tell him when I want his tomfoolery, I'll come to the theater and pay full price."

"What a disagreeable man," we muttered, trailing the comedian back inside.

Lunch proceeded in high spirits until lunch ended. Then, the comedian's demeanor shifted. Slipping his arm through mine, he led me along unfrequented paths.

"Now, about this new opera," he said urgently. "No stale business. Something new."

"I suggest that might be difficult," I ventured.

"Not at all," he insisted. "I'm a young fellow in love with a girl. Her father—an apoplectic old idiot—must be comic. 'Damme, sir! By gad!' All that sort of thing."

"He thinks I'm an ass," he continued, explaining his internal monologue. "I look like a fool, but I'm not."

"And the second act?" I pressed.

"I disguise myself! Organ grinder, broken English, then a policeman, then a swell. Give me opportunities to be funny—that's the key. We don't want old tricks."

"But some pathos?" I asked gently.

"Yes! A scene showing I have depth. Send them out crying, 'Charlie made me laugh, but I never knew he could make me cry!' Does that ring true?"

I nodded.

Later, the leading lady intercepted us, requesting privacy.

"I want to talk about my part," she pleaded, her tone authoritative yet soft. "Can't you give me something original? Please."

"Tired of the simple village maiden?" I guessed.

"I want a coquettish part! I can flirt!" she assured me, arching an eyebrow. "Try me."
