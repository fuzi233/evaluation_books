Buck did not read the newspapers, or he would have known that a storm was brewing—not just for himself, but for every strong, thick-furred dog from Puget Sound to San Diego. Men, searching in the frozen dark of the Arctic, had uncovered gold. News spread fast, and steamships and railroads began ferrying thousands into the Northland. These men needed dogs—big, powerful dogs with muscles built for labor and coats thick enough to survive the cold.

But Buck lived far from such troubles. He lived at Judge Miller’s estate in the sun-drenched Santa Clara Valley, where life moved slowly and gently beneath blue skies. The house stood back from the road, half hidden by trees, with wide verandas and shaded walkways winding through lawns and poplar groves. Behind it sprawled stables, vine-covered cottages, orchards, pastures, and berry patches. A cement tank held cool water for the Judge’s boys, and over all this land, Buck ruled.

He was no ordinary dog. Born here, raised here, he had lived four years as a prince among animals. There were other dogs—terriers who yapped from windows, pugs and hairless Mexican dogs who stayed indoors—but they were mere shadows in his world. Buck was neither kennel dog nor house pet. He swam in the tank, hunted with the Judge’s sons, walked beside the daughters on twilight strolls, and slept before the library fire on winter nights. He carried the grandsons on his back, rolled them in grass, guarded their steps down to the stable fountain, and even beyond. Among the terriers, he moved like a king; Toots and Ysabel, the odd little pets, barely registered in his awareness. He was lord of the land—over every creature, human included.

His father, Elmo, had been a great St. Bernard, the Judge’s constant companion. Buck, though smaller—just 140 pounds—carried himself with equal dignity. His mother, Shep, a Scotch shepherd, had given him strength and endurance. But more than size or breed, it was his life that shaped him: good food, open spaces, hunting, swimming. He had never become soft. His muscles were hard, his spirit proud. He knew no fear—only loyalty, pride, and a quiet confidence born of being respected.

And so, in the fall of 1897, when the Klondike gold rush pulled men from around the world into the frozen north, Buck remained blissfully unaware. He didn’t know that Manuel, one of the gardeners’ helpers, had a secret weakness: gambling. He played Chinese lottery, believing in systems that always failed. And now, with money tight and debts growing, Manuel saw a chance.

The Judge was away at a meeting. The boys were busy starting an athletic club. That night, no one noticed when Manuel led Buck through the orchard—not for a walk, but for something darker. No one saw them arrive at College Park station, except one man. They spoke briefly. Money changed hands.

“You might wrap up the goods before you deliver ’em,” the stranger said gruffly.

Manuel tied a thick rope around Buck’s neck under his collar. “Twist it, and you’ll choke him plentee,” he warned.

Buck accepted it calmly. It was unusual, yes, but he trusted men—he believed they knew better than he did. But when the stranger took the ends of the rope, Buck growled. Not out of fear, but defiance. He thought his growl was command.

It wasn’t.

The rope tightened. Breath left his body. In rage, he lunged—only to be grabbed by the throat, thrown over, and pinned. The rope bit deeper. He fought wildly, tongue lolling, chest heaving. Never had he been treated so cruelly. Never had he felt such fury.

Then darkness.

When he came to, he was moving—jolted along in a train car. The shriek of a whistle told him where he was. He remembered riding with the Judge. But this was different. This was stolen time. He opened his eyes—and saw only betrayal.

The man rushed for his throat. Buck snapped—teeth clamped down on the hand. The man screamed, but Buck didn’t let go until unconsciousness swallowed him again.

“Yep, has fits,” the man muttered, hiding his bleeding hand from the baggageman. “I’m takin’ ’m up for the boss to ’Frisco. A dog doctor there thinks he can cure him.”

That night, the man sat in a dingy shed behind a saloon on the San Francisco docks. “All I get is fifty,” he grumbled. “And I wouldn’t do it over for a thousand.”

His hand was wrapped in bloodstained cloth. His pants ripped from knee to ankle.

“How much did the other guy get?” the saloon-keeper asked.

“A hundred. Wouldn’t take a sou less.”

“That makes a hundred and fifty. And he’s worth it—or I’m a fool.”

The kidnapper unwrapped his hand. “If I don’t get hydrophoby…”

“It’ll be because you were born to hang,” laughed the saloon-keeper. “Here, lend me a hand before you pull your freight.”

Buck woke again—dazed, throat burning, tongue raw. He tried to rise, but was knocked down repeatedly. Finally, they removed his heavy brass collar. Then the rope. Then he was shoved into a cage-like crate.

There he lay, trapped and furious. What was happening? Why were these strangers holding him? He didn’t know, but a deep dread settled over him. Each time the door rattled, he hoped for the Judge, or the boys. But only the saloon-keeper appeared—ugly face, flickering candlelight. Each time, the joy in Buck’s heart turned to snarling hatred.

They left him alone. But in the morning, four rough-looking men lifted the crate. They laughed at him, poked sticks through the bars. He bit them—until he realized that was what they wanted. Then he lay still, sullen, as they carried him into a wagon.

From there, he passed through many hands: clerks, wagons, ferries, trains. For two days and nights, he rode in an express car, bound for Seattle. He refused food. He refused water. The messengers teased him—growling, barking, crowing, mocking. It was childish. But to Buck, it was an insult. His pride burned hotter than hunger.

Thirst gnawed at him. His throat swelled. His eyes turned red. He became a monster—feral, wild, unrecognizable. Even the Judge wouldn’t have known him. But the express men breathed easier when they dropped him off in Seattle.

Four men carried the crate into a small yard walled high. A stout man in a red sweater stepped forward, signed the book, and smiled grimly. He brought a hatchet and a club.

“You ain’t going to take him out now?” the driver asked.

“Sure,” the man replied, driving the hatchet into the wood.

The four men scattered to the wall, watching.

Buck raged inside—tearing at the splintering wood, snapping at every blow from outside. When the opening was wide enough, the man dropped the hatchet and gripped the club.

“Now, you red-eyed devil,” he said.

Buck launched himself—140 pounds of fury, teeth bared, eyes blazing. Midair, the club struck. He crashed to the ground, jaw snapping shut painfully. He rose again. Again, the club broke his charge. Again and again, he charged. Again and again, he was crushed.

After one brutal blow, he staggered, blood flowing from nose and mouth. Still, he roared—a lion’s roar—and charged once more.

This time, the man caught him by the jaw, twisted downward, and swung him in a full circle before slamming him onto the ground.

For the last time, Buck rushed.

The man delivered the final blow—sharp, clean, decisive.

Buck crumpled. Unconscious.

“He’s no slouch at dog-breakin’,” one man shouted.

“Druther break cayuses any day,” the driver replied, climbing into his wagon.

Buck regained consciousness slowly. Weak. Wounded. But awake.

He watched the man in the red sweater.

“‘Answers to the name of Buck,’” the man murmured, reading from a letter. “Well, Buck, my boy. We’ve had our little fight. Best let it go at that. You’ve learned your place. I know mine. Be a good dog, and all will go well. Be bad, and I’ll beat the stuffing out of you. Understand?”

As he spoke, he patted the dog’s head—the same head he’d beaten mercilessly.

Buck bristled—but didn’t move.

Later, when the man offered water, Buck drank greedily. Then raw meat, chunk by chunk, from the man’s hand.

He was beaten. He knew it.

But not broken.

He had seen the truth: a man with a club was law. The club was a revelation. It marked the beginning of a new world—one ruled by force, instinct, and survival.

From then on, Buck watched as other dogs arrived—some calm, some raging. All were subdued by the man in the red sweater. Some fawned. Some begged. One refused. That dog was killed.

Buck learned quickly: obedience was expected. Respect was earned. But submission? Not required.

Then came the stranger—a wiry man who spat broken English and strange words.

“Sacredam!” he exclaimed, seeing Buck. “Dat one dam bully dog! How moch?”

“Three hundred,” the man replied. “And a present at that.”

Perrault grinned. The price was fair. The dog was exceptional.

Money changed hands.

Curly, a gentle Newfoundland, and Buck were led away.

That was the last Buck saw of the red sweater. From the deck of the *Narwhal*, he looked back at Seattle—the warm Southland fading into memory.

Below, Perrault handed them over to François, a black-faced giant with sharp eyes and a calm voice. They were French-Canadians—different from any men Buck had known. Not kind, but fair. Not soft, but wise.

In the ’tween-decks, Buck met two others. One was a white dog from Spitzbergen—friendly, but deceitful. He stole from Buck’s food. When Buck lunged, François cracked his whip—swift, precise—striking the thief first. Buck recovered the bone. He respected that.

The other dog—Dave—was silent, morose, indifferent. He ate, slept, yawned. When the ship rocked violently across Queen Charlotte Sound, Buck and Curly panicked. Dave merely glanced at them, yawned, and went back to sleep.

Day after day, the ship hummed with the engine’s pulse. The air grew colder. The sky grayed.

One morning, the engine stopped. A hush fell. Then excitement.

François leashed them and brought them on deck.

Buck stepped onto the cold surface—and sank into something white and mushy. He snorted, leapt back.

More fell from the sky.

He shook himself. More landed on him.

He sniffed. Then licked it.

It burned. Then vanished.

He tried again. Same result.

The others laughed.

Buck felt ashamed—though he didn’t know why.

It was snow.

And it was the beginning of everything.
