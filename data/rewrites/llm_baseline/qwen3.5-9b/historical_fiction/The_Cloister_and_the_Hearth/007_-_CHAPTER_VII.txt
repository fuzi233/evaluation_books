On a morning of unusual brilliance, the streets of Tergou shimmered under a canopy of velvet clouds. Uncommon splendor filled the air; feathers danced in the breeze, and the gleaming hooves of horses clattered rhythmically along the cobblestones. Windows and balconies were lined with faces agape in wonder. The French Ambassador rode through the city en route to hunt in the nearby forest. Accompanying him was a retinue including several servants lent by the Duke of Burgundy to render him honor and attend to his pleasures. Leading the procession was the Duke's jester, a figure of grave and sedate majesty whose dignity made his more noble companions appear light and frivolous by comparison. Yet, whenever the atmosphere grew too thick with reverence, the jester would dismount in a manner so ignoble and comical that even the dignified Ambassador could not suppress a laugh. He would climb back onto his steed by grabbing the tail, a spectacle designed purely to provoke mirth, playing his role with theatrical flair.

Trailing behind the main group was a spectacle that drew even greater attention: the Duke's leopard. Mounted on a Flemish horse of colossal size and power, a huntsman carried a long box strapped securely to his waist. Perched atop this box, chained firmly to the rider, sat a brilliant leopard, crouching low. The crowd marveled at her glossy coat and distinctive spots, pressing close until one or two reached out to touch her or pull her tail. Instantly, the huntsman's voice boomed in terror, "Beware! In Antwerp, a man dared throw a handful of dust at her, and the Duke reduced him to mere dust!"

"Thank heavens!" someone cried.

"I speak the truth," the huntsman insisted. "The good Duke imprisoned him in a subterranean cell, where rats stripped the flesh from his bones within a single night. He served him right for molesting the beast."

A murmur of fear rippled through the Tergovians, who recoiled from the idea of teasing the sovereign's leopard. However, their spirits were soon restored by the arrival of the Duke's giant bringing up the rear. Standing seven feet four inches tall, this Hungarian giant possessed a treble, flute-like voice of negligible volume—a flaw he was entirely unaware of due to his vanity. As he approached, he spotted Giles perched atop a balcony. Stopping abruptly, the giant began to mock him.

"Hallo! Brother!" squeaked the giant in a high-pitched whine. "I nearly passed without seeing thee!"

"You are plain enough to see," bellowed Giles in a deep bass roar.

"Come on my shoulder, brother!" squealed the giant, extending a massive, mutton-fist-sized arm to assist him down.

"If I do, I'll cuff your ears," roared the dwarf.

The giant, sensing the homunculus's irritability and emboldened by the crowd's laughter, continued his jest. He failed to realize that the spectators laughed not at his wit, but at the absurd incongruity of the two voices—the gigantic body emitting a feeble whistle, and the tiny form unleashing a thunderous boom. It was a mountain delivering a squeak and a molehill belching thunder. The bizarre duet ended as unexpectedly as it began. Overcome with loss of patience and self-command, fueled by a rage born of his fearless nature, Giles leaped onto the giant's neck. Seizing the giant's hair with one hand and punching his head with the other, he attacked with ferocity.

The giant's initial impulse was to laugh, but the sudden weight and speed of the blows quickly extinguished that amusement.

"He! he! Ah! ha! Hallo! Oh! Oh! Holy saints! Here! Help! Or I must throttle this imp! I can't! I'll split your skull against the—"

He made a wild dash backward toward the balcony. Giles, realizing his peril, grabbed the railing with both hands and swung over just as the giant's head collided with the stone with a stunning crack. The crowd erupted in roaring laughter and exultation at the little champion's agility. Indignant, the giant seized two of the laughers, smashed them together like dumbbells, shook them, and laid them flat. Catherine shrieked, throwing her apron over Giles, while the giant strode wrathfully away to join the rest of the party.

Though no one present foresaw the future consequences, the immediate result was favorable. The citizens of Tergou became proud of Giles, listening with newfound kindness to his pleas for parchment. He conducted a steady trade with his brother Gerard in this commodity, begging for sheets gratis while Gerard supplied the copper coins.

Later that afternoon, Catherine and her daughter Kate chatted animatedly about their favorite topic: Gerard, his goodness, his impending benefice, and the brightened prospects of the entire family. Fortune had smiled upon them exactly as they desired. Beyond the advantage of a benefice that the Countess of Charolais would not refuse, there lay the feminine joy of having a priest, a holy man, within their own household.

"He will marry Cornelis and Sybrandt," Catherine declared. "For they can wed—good housewives—as they will. Gerard will care for you and Giles when we are gone."

"Yes, mother, and we can confess to him instead of a stranger," Kate added.

"Aye, girl! And he can administer the sacred oil to your father and me, and close our eyes when our time comes."

"Oh, mother! Not for many, many years, I pray Heaven. Please, do not speak of that; it always saddens me. I hope to go before you, dear mother. No; let us be cheerful today. I am free of pain, quite free of all pain; it seems so strange. I feel so bright and happy that..."

Kate paused, her eyes lighting up. "Mother, can you keep a secret?"

"Nobody better, child. You know I can."

"Then I will show you something so beautiful. You've never seen its like, I trow. Only Gerard must never know; surely he intends to surprise us with it. He hides it so well, and sometimes he carries it away entirely."

Taking her crutches, Kate moved slowly away, leaving her mother in a state of heightened curiosity. Soon she returned, holding something wrapped in cloth. She uncovered it to reveal a lovely painting of the Virgin Mary, adorned with all her insignias, wearing her tiara over a cascade of golden hair flowing loosely over her shoulders. Catherine was immediately struck with awe.

"It is herself," she cried. "It is the Queen of Heaven. I have never seen one like her before."

"And her eyes, mother! Lifted to the sky, as if they belong there, not to a mortal creature. And her beautiful hair of burning gold."

"To think I have a son who can make the saints live again upon a piece of wood!"

"The reason is, he is a young saint himself, mother. He is too good for this world; he is here to portray the blessed, and then depart to be with them forever."

Before they could finish admiring the masterpiece, a strange voice echoed from the doorway. Driven by instinct, the women hastily concealed the picture, though there was no real cause for alarm. Moments later, a man entered, casting furtive glances around. It was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the burgomaster, who had not visited the house in ten years.

Caught completely off guard, the two women simply stared at him and at each other, murmuring, "The burgomaster!" in a tone so laden with meaning that Ghysbrecht felt compelled to respond.

"Yes! I admit the last time I came here was not on a friendly errand. Men love their own interests—Elias's and mine were contrary. Well, let this visit atone for the last. Today I come on your business, and none of mine."

Catherine and Kate exchanged a swift glance of contemptuous disbelief. They knew the man far better than he imagined.

"It is about your son, Gerard," Ghysbrecht stated.

"Ay! Ay! You want him to work for the town for nothing. He told us."

"I come on no such errand. It is to inform you that he has fallen into bad hands."

"Now, Heaven and the saints forbid! Man, do not torture a mother! Speak out, and quickly: speak before you have time to coin falsehoods; we know you."

Ghysbrecht paled at this affront, spite mixing with his other motives.

"Thus it is, then," he ground out, speaking rapidly. "Your son Gerard is more likely to become a father of a family than a priest: he is forever with Margaret, Peter Brandt's red-haired girl, and loves her like a cow her calf."

Both mother and daughter burst into laughter. Ghysbrecht stared at them, bewildered.

"What! You knew it?"

"Carry this tale to those who do not know my son, Gerard. Women are naught to him."
