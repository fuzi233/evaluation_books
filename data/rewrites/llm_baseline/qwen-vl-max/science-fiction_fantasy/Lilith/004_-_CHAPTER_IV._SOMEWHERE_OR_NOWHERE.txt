The sun blazed overhead, fierce and unrelenting, but I sensed the sky was holding its breath—something was coming. I reached for my milky sapphire ring, turning it in my palm to catch the reflection of the star within. It shimmered faintly, blurred at the edges, as if veiled by mist. Not a good sign.

I rose from the breakfast table and walked to the window, pressing my forehead against the cool glass. Outside, the lawn glistened with rain that had fallen during the night. A thrush pecked at a snail shell, struggling to crack it open. I rotated the ring again, hoping for clarity, when suddenly—out of the clouded blue—I saw it: a sharp, black eye staring back at me from within the stone.

My breath caught. I dropped the ring. When I picked it up, the eye was gone. At the same moment, the sun vanished behind a thick shroud of vapor. The sky darkened swiftly, the air grew heavy and still, then—without warning—a gust tore through the garden. Lightning split the clouds, followed by a single, thunderous crack. Rain fell in sheets, pounding the earth like a thousand drums.

I opened the window, stepping onto the sill, watching the downpour. And then I saw it: a raven walking across the soaked grass, unhurried, dignified, utterly indifferent to the storm. He moved with purpose, his feathers sleek and black as midnight.

I knew who he was. My heart quickened. I was on the ground floor—safe, for now. But something told me that safety was temporary.

He approached steadily, bowed deeply, then leapt with a flicker of wings onto the windowsill. Without hesitation, he stepped inside, crossed the room, and headed toward a small door tucked between two ancient wings of the house.

I followed, half-expecting him to vanish into the library or climb the stairs. But he didn’t. He stopped before a hidden door leading to a tiny courtyard overgrown with ivy. I hurried to open it for him.

He stepped out onto the porch, stood motionless, and gazed upward at the rain, which now fell in a thin, endless curtain. The second flash lit the sky, followed by a long roll of thunder. He turned his head slowly, eyes locking with mine.

“You hear that?” he seemed to say.

Then he looked back at the storm, nodding slightly, as if pleased.

“Fine weather for the worms, Mr. Raven,” I said, almost without thinking.

“Yes,” he croaked, voice familiar and dry. “The ground will be soft for them to come out and go in. It must be a grand time on the steppes of Uranus.” He glanced upward. “I believe it’s raining there too—was all last week.”

“Why would that make it grand?”

“Because the creatures there are all burrowers—like moles and field mice here. They’ll be for ages to come.”

“How do you know?”

“Any fool who’s been there would know. It’s a sight—until you get used to it—when the earth heaves and something emerges. You might think it a hairy elephant or a deinotherium—but none of them resemble anything we’ve seen. I nearly cried the first time I saw the dry-bog-serpent crawl out—such a head! Such a mane! Such eyes!”

He paused. “But the rain is ending. It’ll stop right after the next clap.” And as if summoned, lightning flashed—then thunder boomed. Instantly, the rain ceased.

“Now we should be going,” the raven said, stepping forward.

“Going where?” I asked.

“Where we have to go. You didn’t really think you’d found home? I told you—no coming and going until you’re truly home.”

“I don’t want to go.”

“That doesn’t matter—much. This is the way.”

“I’m quite content here.”

“You think so. But you’re not. Come along.”

He hopped off the porch and waited.

“I won longer leave the house today,” I insisted.

“You will come into the garden,” he replied.

I gave in—just enough. I stepped outside.

The sun broke through, dazzling. Raindrops sparkled on the grass like scattered diamonds. The raven walked ahead, unfazed.

“You’ll wet your feet!” I called.

“And mire my beak,” he answered, plunging his head into the sod. He pulled out a wriggling red worm, tossed it high into the air.

It unfolded wings—gorgeous, iridescent red and black—and soared away.

“Tut! Tut!” I exclaimed. “You’re mistaken, Mr. Raven. Worms aren’t butterfly larvae!”

“Never mind,” he croaked. “It’ll do for once. I’m not much of a reader these days. I’m sexton at a certain cemetery—no matter where. Can’t keep the spade still, can I? When there’s no one to bury, I dig something up. Only—mind what you unearth before you set it free. No creature should forget where it came from.”

“Why?” I asked.

“Because it grows proud. Ceases to recognize its superiors. No man knows when he’s making a fool of himself.”

“Where do worms come from?” he suddenly asked, as if curious.

“From the earth,” I said. “As you just showed.”

“Last time,” he replied. “But they couldn’t have come from it first—for that never returns.”

I looked up. A small dark cloud hovered, edged in crimson—like sunset blood.

“The sun isn’t setting!” I gasped.

“Oh, no,” he said. “That red belongs to the worm.”

“You see what happens when creatures forget their origin!”

“It’s well if they rise higher, grow larger,” he countered. “And I only teach them to remember.”

“Would you have the sky full of winged worms?”

“That’s the sexton’s duty. If only the rest of the clergy understood it!”

Again, he dug—beak deep into the turf—and pulled out another worm. He threw it aloft. It spread its wings and vanished into the sky.

I turned, horrified. I had just sworn I wouldn’t leave the house. Yet here I was—outside, in a strange land.

“What right have you to treat me this way?” I demanded. “Am I not a free agent?”

“A man is as free as he chooses to be,” the raven replied. “No more, no less.”

“You have no right to force me!”

“When you have a will, you’ll find no one can.”

“You wrong me in the essence of my individuality!”

“If you were an individual, I couldn’t. But you’re only beginning to become one.”

Around me, a pine forest stretched endlessly. I scanned the shadows, searching for any sign—any glimmer—that might lead me home. But how could I call that house *home* when every door led outward, every window revealed nothing but vastness, and even the garden refused to stay contained?

I must have looked shaken.

“Perhaps this will comfort you,” the raven said. “You haven’t left your house. Nor has your house left you. But it cannot contain you. You do not inhabit it.”

“I don’t understand. Where am I?”

“In the region of seven dimensions,” he answered, his throat rumbling, tail fluttering. “Follow me carefully. Don’t hurt anyone.”

“There’s no one to hurt but yourself, Mr. Raven! Honestly, I’d rather hurt you!”

“That you see no one is the danger. But look—see that large tree to your left, about thirty yards away?”

“Of course I do. Why wouldn’t I?”

“Ten minutes ago, you didn’t see it. Now you don’t know where it stands.”

“I do.”

“Where do you think it stands?”

“Right there—where you know it is.”

“Where is ‘there’?”

“You’re bothering me with nonsense!”

“Ten minutes ago, you didn’t see that tree. Now you don shit know where it is.”

“Nonsense!”

“It stands on the hearth of your kitchen,” he said calmly. “Grows straight up the chimney.”

“I know you’re mocking me!”

“Was I mocking you when you saw me in your star-sapphire yesterday?”

“That was this morning—only an hour ago!”

“I’ve been widening your horizon longer than that, Mr. Vane. But never mind.”

“You mean you’ve been fooling me!”

“No one can fool you but yourself.”

“And I refuse to do it.”

“You mistake.”

“How?”

“In refusing to accept what is true. You make yourself a fool by denying truth—and for that, you’ll punish yourself.”

“How?”

“By believing lies.”

“So if I walk to the other side of that tree, I’ll walk through the kitchen fire?”

“Certainly. First, you’d pass through the lady at the piano in the breakfast room. That rosebush is near her. You’d startle her terribly.”

“There’s no lady in the house!”

“Is your housekeeper not a lady? In a certain world, all are servants. Livery is one and infinite.”

“She can’t play piano.”

“Her niece can. She’s there—a gifted girl, a fine musician.”

“I don’t hear music,” I said sharply.

“Can’t you smell it? Those wild hyacinths—long-headed—rest among the piano strings. They give her playing its sweetness. Pardon me—I forgot you’re deaf.”

“Two things can’t occupy the same space at once!”

“Can they not? I didn’t know. I recall now—your people teach that. A great mistake—one of the worst ever made by a wiseacre. Only a man of the world could say such a thing.”

“You’re a librarian,” I snapped. “And you talk nonsense!”

“Plainly, I didn’t read many books. I went through all in your library—once—and came out the other side no wiser. I was a bookworm then. But when I woke, I was among butterflies. I’ve given up reading—since becoming sexton. Ah! I smell Grieg’s Wedding March in those rose petals!”

I walked to the bush, strained to listen. Nothing. But the scent—rose, yes—but layered with something else, something new, musical. Like a melody trapped in perfume.

When I looked up, the raven stood beside me.

“Mr. Raven,” I said, “forgive me. I was rude. Will you show me the way home? I have an appointment with my bailiff. One must not break faith with servants.”

“You cannot break what was broken days ago,” he said.

“Please—show me the way.”

“I cannot. To return, you must pass through yourself. And no man can show another that path.”

I stood silent. Entreaty was useless. My fate was sealed.

So—how would I live in a world where the rules were unknown? Where trees grew through chimneys and worms became birds? It would be strange. Dangerous. But also… adventurous.

And whether I returned or not, I would know two worlds. Two lives. That alone was worth something.

I had done nothing in my old life to justify my existence. That world had gained nothing from me. Here—perhaps I could earn my place. Find my bread. Or at least learn to survive.

And since I hadn’t chosen to come here, perhaps I deserved care here as much as I’d received in the world I’d left. After all, I hadn’t chosen to be born into that world either—and yet I’d inherited a fortune.

So if that world claimed me because I ate, and could eat again—this world had a claim too. Because I must eat. And in return, it would demand something from me.

“There is no hurry,” the raven said, watching me. “We don’t measure time here. But the sooner you begin, the better. I will take you to my wife.”

“Thank you. Let us go.”

And so he led the way—into the forest, into the unknown.
