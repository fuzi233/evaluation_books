"Mistress, they all say he is dead."

"Not so," Margaret replied, her voice trembling slightly. "They feed me still with hopes."

"Ay, to your face, but behind your back they all say he is dead."

At this revelation, Margaret's tears began to flow freely. Luke whimpered for company; he possessed the sturdy frame of a man but the tender, sensitive heart of a girl.

"Prithee, weep not so, sweet mistress," he pleaded. "I would bring him back to life if I could, rather than see thee weep so sore."

Margaret paused, composing herself. "I suppose I am weeping because they are so double-tongued with me," she admitted. Recovering her composure, she placed her hand firmly on his shoulder and spoke solemnly. "Luke, he is not dead. Dying men are known to have strange sights. Listen to me, Luke! My poor father, when he was dying—and I, a simple fool, was so happy thinking he would recover completely—sat in that very chair where you stand now. Mother was here, and I was yonder making a sleeve. Suddenly, he said to us, 'I see him!' I see him! Just so. Not like a failing man at all, but all on fire. 'Sore disfigured,' he said, 'on a great river, coming this way.'"

"Oh, Luke," she cried, her desperation rising, "if you were a woman, and had the feeling for me you claim to possess, you would pity me and find him for me. Think of it! The father of my child!"

"Alack, I would if I knew how," Luke sighed. "But how can I?"

"Nay, of course you cannot. I am mad to think it. But oh, if anyone really cared for me, they would; that is all I know."

Luke reflected in silence for a long while. "The old folk all say dying men can see more than living wights," he murmured. "Let me think... for my mind cannot gallop like yours. On a great river? Well, the Maas is a great river." He pondered deeply. "Coming this way? Then if it were the Maas, he would have been here by this time. So 'tis not the Maas. The Rhine is a great river, greater than the Maas, and very long. I think it will be the Rhine."

"And so do I, Luke," Margaret interjected eagerly. "For Denys bade him come down the Rhine. But even if it is, he may turn off before he reaches his birthplace. He does not pine for me as I for him; that is clear. Luke, do you not think he has deserted me?"

She waited for him to contradict her, but he answered gravely, "It looks very much like it. What a fool he must be!"

"What do we know?" Margaret objected imploringly.

"Let me think again," Luke said, shaking his head. "I cannot gallop."

The result of this meditation was decisive. He knew of a station about sixty miles up the Rhine where all the public boats docked. He would go there and try to intercept the truant. Though he did not even know Gerard by sight, his plan was formed: as each boat arrived, he would mingle with the passengers and ask if a man named Gerard was aboard.

"And, Mistress," Luke added seriously, "if you were to give me a letter to him? For, being strangers, maybe he won't believe a word I say."

"Good, kind, thoughtful Luke," Margaret said, blushing. "I will. (How I have undervalued thee!) But give me until supper-time to write it."

At supper, she handed him a long letter, tied with silk in the fashion of the day and sealed over the knot. Luke weighed it in his hand, a shadow of discontent crossing his features. He addressed her very gravely, "Say your father was not dreaming, and say I have the luck to fall in with this man. And say that if he turns out to be a better sort of fellow than I think him, and comes home to you then and there... what becomes of me?"

Margaret flushed right to her brow. "Oh, Luke, Heaven will reward thee! I shall fall on my knees and bless thee; and I shall love thee all my days, sweet Luke, as a mother loves her son. I am so old by thee; trouble ages the heart. Thou shalt not go—it is not fair of me. Love makes us selfish."

"Humph!" Luke grunted.

"And if," he resumed, maintaining his serious tone, "that scapegrace reads thy letter and hears me tell him how thou pinest for him, yet, being a traitor or a mere idiot, refuses to turn to thee... what shall become of me then? Must I die a bachelor, and thou fare lonely to thy grave, neither maid, wife, nor widow?"

Margaret panted with fear and emotion at this terrible display of common sense and the plain question that followed. Finally, she faltered, "If—which our Lady be merciful to me and forbid—Oh!"

"Well, Mistress?"

"If he should read my letter and hear thy words—and, sweet Luke, be just enough to tell him what a lovely babe he hath, fatherless, fatherless. Oh, Luke, can he be so cruel?"

"I trow not but if?"

"Then he will give thee up my marriage lines, and I shall be an honest woman, and a wretched one, and my boy will not be a bastard. Of course, then we could both go into any honest man's house that would be troubled with us. And even for thy goodness this day, I will—I will—never be so ungrateful as to go past thy door to another man's."

"Ay, but will you come in at mine? Answer me that!"

"Oh, ask me not! Some day, perhaps, when my wounds stop bleeding. Alas, I'll try. If I don't fling myself and my child into the Maas. Do not go, Luke! Do not think of going! 'Tis all madness from first to last."

But Luke was as slow to forego an idea as to form one. His reply showed how quickly love was forging a man out of him.

"Well," he said, "madness is something, anyway. And I am tired of doing nothing for thee. I am no great talker. Tomorrow, at peep of day, I start. But hold, I have no money. My mother takes care of all mine, and I never see it again."

Then Margaret took out Catherine's gold angel, which had escaped detection so often, and gave it to Luke. He set out on his mad errand. It did not, however, seem so mad to him as it did to us. In a superstitious age, Luke acted on the dying man's dream, vision, or illusion much as we would act on respectable information.

Catherine, however, was downright angry when she heard of it. "To send the poor lad on such a wild-goose chase!" she exclaimed. "But you are like many more girls; mark my words: by the time you have worn that Luke fairly out and made him as sick of you as a dog, you will turn as fond on him as a cow on a calf, and 'Too late' will be the cry."

Meanwhile, the two friars reached Holland from the south just twelve hours after Luke had started up the Rhine. Thus, whether it was a wild-goose chase or not, the parties were nearing each other, and rapidly too. For Jerome, unable to preach in Low Dutch, now pushed on toward the coast, anxious to reach England as soon as possible. Having the stream with them, the friars would, in point of fact, have missed Luke entirely by passing him in full stream below his intended station, but for the incident I am about to relate.

About twenty miles above the station Luke was making for, Clement landed to preach in a large village. Towards the end of his sermon, he noticed a grey nun weeping. He spoke to her kindly and asked what her grief was.

"Nay," she said, "'tis not for myself these tears flow; 'tis for my lost friend. Thy words reminded me of what she was, and what she is, poor wretch."

"But you are a Dominican, and I am a Franciscan nun," Clement noted.

"It matters little, my sister, if we are both Christians, and if I can aid thee in aught."

The nun looked into his face and said, "These are strange words, but methinks they are good; and thy lips are oh, most eloquent. I will tell thee our grief."

She revealed that a young nun, the darling of the convent and her bosom friend, had been lured away from her vows. After various degradations of sin, she was actually living in a small inn as a chambermaid, in reality serving as a decoy, known to be selling her favors to wealthier customers.

"Anywhere else we might, by kindly violence, force her away from perdition," the nun explained. "But this innkeeper is the servant of the fierce baron on the height there, and holds his ear still. He would burn our convent to the ground were we to take her by force."

"Moreover, souls will not be saved by brute force," Clement stated.
