The glass tank in Regent's Park held a suspended silence, a curated ecosystem where finned and feathered creatures traded their freedom for bread and observation. Yet, amidst the swirling currents and the deliberate grace of the goldfish, my attention drifted, as it often does when the mind wanders from the immediate to the distant. I watched a flatfish, a flounder, execute a series of impossible arcs through the water. Its movements were fluid, a study in hydrodynamic elegance, displaying a white chest and a glistening black back with a rhythm that suggested music rather than biology. It was a creature of profound beauty, until gravity reclaimed it.

Remove the flounder from the water, and the illusion shatters instantly. The beauty vanishes, replaced by a grotesque flapping of fins against the gravel, a desperate gasping that signals the rapid decay of life. Out of its element, the creature becomes ridiculous, a caricature of itself. This is the condition of Harry Warrington for years. He had been adrift, floundering in a sea that offered him no buoyancy. But the moment he stepped onto solid ground—the moment the epaulet touched his shoulder—he transformed. The water receded, and the true form emerged. He possessed an innate aptitude for the military machine, mastering its details with a hunger that bordered on obsession. To witness him marching, dining in the mess, or standing rigid on the parade ground would have been a spectacle of vitality. Since I lack the descriptive power of my friend Lorrequer to paint the full canvas of his campaign life, the reader must supply the colors: imagine Harry in his scarlet coat and yellow facings, radiating a joy derived solely from the discipline of war, eager to learn and perform every duty of his new trade.

While Harry found his purpose in the chaos of battle, George discovered his in the quiet architecture of sentiment. He viewed his brother's ascent with a pride that was almost painful, a recognition of the superior qualities he admired in Harry. In a letter to his mother in Virginia, George recounted the praises Harry had earned, withholding the specifics because he knew Harry's modesty would never allow him to boast. George detailed how Harry had secured his first commission, and then he turned the page of his own life. He spoke of the Lamerts, the only true friends the brothers had found in England, contrasting their generosity with the cold indifference of their biological relatives. George confessed a deep attachment to the elder Miss Lambert, a feeling he believed constituted the axis upon which his future happiness turned. He had not yet made formal proposals, but the sentiment was clear, the reception favorable. He painted Miss Lambert as a paragon of virtue, a model daughter whose approval he trusted implicitly. He assured his mother that her son's destiny hung in the balance, dependent entirely on winning her hand.

Why withhold this letter? Some might ask, seeking the raw data of youthful infatuation. We refuse. It is a cruelty to dissect the incoherent vows of a young man, to catalogue the sighs exchanged at twilight, to listen with clinical detachment to the beating of a heart that knows only devotion. Love is a sacred geometry; to map it precisely is to destroy its shape. We prefer to salute the signal whenever it appears—a glance between lovers, a mother's watchful eye, the simple presence of an old couple holding hands. To expose these moments to the public gaze is sacrilege. Let the reader trust our word: the letters exchanged between George and Miss Theo were modest, pure, and steeped in a tenderness that demands privacy.

George's pursuit was a gentle dance compared to Harry's martial fervor. He penned sonnets to the curve of a brow, paced the shadows beneath her window watching a single lamp glow, and cultivated pretexts to send notes that demanded no answer yet received one anyway. He plucked verses from favorite poets and flowers from Covent Garden markets, arranging them for her adornment. He attended services at St. James's, his mind wandering so completely he heard none of the sermon, consumed instead by the memory of her smile. He was affectionate to her entire circle: the siblings at school, the brother at college, the witty Miss Hetty, and even his mother, who, according to Martin Lambert, had fallen in love with him anew. For if fathers grow stern at the prospect of a son-in-law, mothers often become sentimental, reliving their own youth through the eyes of their children's affections.

Meanwhile, Gumbo and Sady kept a constant circuit between Southampton Row and Dean Street, orchestrating a calendar of diversions. Summer brought endless invitations to Ranelagh, Hampstead, Vauxhall, and Marylebone Gardens. George desired a specific tragedy transcribed in Miss Theo's exquisite Italian hand, and the exchange of manuscripts became a ritual of gratitude and admiration. These packets, marked neatly in Warrington's script, contained whispers meant for ears alone. To publish them would be to violate a vow. We assure the reader that the correspondence reflects the highest standards of respect and purity, a testament to a love that flourished in the dark.

George had decided he had found the woman of women. She may not possess the striking features of cousins Flora, Coelia, or Ardelia, nor the intellectual brilliance of a dozen rivals. Her face simply pleased him more than any other; her voice was the most agreeable sound in the world. In her presence, his own intellect sharpened, his words gained eloquence. He felt generous, noble, and witty, elevated by her company. Conversely, he believed Miss Theo endowed him with every virtue imaginable: wit, wisdom, beauty, valor, and goodness. It was a mutual projection, a perfect mirror reflecting the idealized version of oneself.

When George's letter arrived at Castlewood, the atmosphere around Madame Esmond's fireside shifted. The news of his engagement was received with relief; marriage was seen as a stabilizing force, a shield against the dangers of the world. Madame Esmond accepted the union with a well-bred Christian woman without hesitation.

"They will certainly be trying to capture him," observed Mountain, his tone suggesting a calculation of assets. "They assume his wealth matches his estate. He hasn't mentioned whether the young lady possesses funds. I suspect she does not."

"People would try to catch Mr. Esmond Warrington for their daughters here," Madame Esmond replied, her gaze sharp as she looked at her dependent. "Even if they are no richer than Miss Lambert."

"Perhaps you mean me?" Mountain interjected, feigning offense. "My Fanny is poor, as you say, and it is kind of you to remind me!"

"I stated that people would attempt to secure him. If the cap fits you, so be it," Madame Esmond retorted, invoking a phrase from her father.

"You imply I am scheming to keep George for Fanny? On my word, I thank you! After years of cohabitation, you hold a surprisingly low opinion of us!"

"My dear Mountain, I know you better than to believe you would consider your daughter a suitable match for a gentleman of Mr. Esmond's rank," Madame said, maintaining her dignity.

"Fanny Parker was as good as Molly Benson at school, and Mr. Mountain's daughter is as good as Mr. Lambert's!" Mrs. Mountain cried out, her ambition surfacing despite her protests.

"Then you intended to marry her to my son! I shall inform Mr. Esmond Warrington of your disappointment," the mistress of Castlewood declared. We may infer that Mrs. Mountain harbored ambitions for her daughter, hence her anger at the prospect of George's marriage elsewhere.

Madame Esmond's response to her son acknowledged the fraternal bond George displayed. She noted it was fitting that he compensate Harry, whose mistaken belief regarding his inheritance had led to unnecessary expenditures. Regarding the cost of buying Harry's promotion, she agreed to share the burden, grateful that her son's gallantry had already secured his entry into the army. She contrasted this sacrifice with other extravagances she refused to condone, specifically the ransom paid to the French captain's family—a sum George's mother, sharing her son's stubborn spirit, had steadfastly refused to pay. She labeled his persistence as pride, though her biographer, writing from a century later, cannot claim to understand the nuances of such obstinacy. People, it seems, remain remarkably similar across generations, driven by the desire to impose their will upon the world.

After settling his own ransom, clearing his brother's debts, and funding half of Harry's promotion, George realized a significant portion of his private patrimony would vanish. Nevertheless, he made the sacrifice with a clear conscience. His mother's letters constantly reminded him of his grandfather's legacy and the necessity of maintaining family dignity. She commissioned purchases in England, sent trifling sums, yet spoke incessantly of the exalted rank of the Esmonds. She urged him to frequent the courts his ancestors had visited, to inhabit the highest circles of society, and to conduct himself in a manner worthy of his name. George, confident that his mother's resources would eventually replenish his coffers, obediently adopted this expensive lifestyle. The Esmond papers from this era record bills for genteel entertainments, tailoring accounts for court suits, liveries for his negro servants and chairmen, and receipts from horse dealers. Thus, the elder of our Virginians, like his brother, learned to spend lavishly, believing that the performance of status was as vital as the substance of it.
