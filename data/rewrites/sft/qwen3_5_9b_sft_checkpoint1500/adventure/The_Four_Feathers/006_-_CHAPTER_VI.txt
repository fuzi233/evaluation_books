The night of August the thirtieth. A month had slipped by since the glittering ball at Lennon House, yet the uneventful countryside of Donegal buzzed with a feverish, stimulative rumor: Harry Feversham's disappearance. In the climbing streets and around dinner tables, the townsfolk and gentry gossiped to their hearts' content. The prevailing theory was that Harry had been spotted on the morning after the dance, at five minutes to six—or so Mrs. Brien O'Brien claimed, ten minutes past—still dressed in his formal attire, his face the ghastly pallor of a suicide, hurrying along the causeway beside Lennon Bridge. A drag-net, it was suggested, was the only solution to the mystery. Mr. Dennis Rafferty, residing on the road to Rathmullen, went so far as to refuse salmon, pleading that he was not a cannibal, a sentiment that gained wide vogue. Their speculations on the cause of his vanishing drifted further from the truth with every passing hour. For there were only two who knew the reality, and those two carried on the business of living with steady, unflappable composure, their heads held a trifle higher than usual. Ethne might have softened slightly, Dermod grown a touch more irritable, but these were the sole changes. Gossip held the entire field to itself.

Yet, in London, Harry Feversham was present, a fact Lieutenant Sutch confirmed on the night of the thirtieth. All day, the city had trembled under rumors of a colossal battle at Kassassin, east of Ismailia. Messengers raced ceaselessly through the streets, shouting conflicting tidings of victory and disaster. There had been a moonlit charge by General Drury-Lowe's Cavalry Brigade, rolling up Arabi's left flank and capturing his guns. Rumors swirled of an English general slain, the York and Lancaster Regiment cut to pieces. London lay uneasy. At eleven o'clock at night, a vast, silent crowd gathered beneath the gas-lamps of Pall Mall, watching with pale, upturned faces the lighted blinds of the War Office. Stillness reigned, impressive and heavy. Only if a figure moved momentarily across the blinds did a thrill of expectation ripple from man to man, causing the crowd to sway in a continuous, fluid motion from edge to edge.

Lieutenant Sutch, mindful of his wounded leg, stood on the outskirts, his back against the parapet of the Junior Carlton Club, when a hand touched his arm. He turned to see Harry Feversham standing beside him. Feversham's face worked with agitation, unnaturally white, his eyes bright and feverish, like those of a man burning with internal heat. At first, Sutch was unsure if he recognized the man, or if it mattered.

"I might have been out there in Egypt tonight," Harry said, his voice quick and troubled. "Think of it! I might have been sitting by a campfire in the desert, talking over the battle with Jack Durrance; or dead, perhaps. What would it have mattered? I might have been in Egypt tonight!"

Feversham's sudden appearance, no less than his wandering tongue, signaled to Sutch that his fortunes had taken a disastrous turn. Questions surged in Sutch's mind, but he asked none. He took Feversham's arm and guided him straight out of the throng.

"I saw you in the crowd," Feversham continued. "I thought I would speak to you, because—do you remember, a long time ago you gave me your card? I have always kept it, fearing I would have reason to use it. You said that if one was in trouble, the telling might help."

Sutch halted his companion. "We will go in here. We can find a quiet corner in the upper smoking-room." Harry, looking up, realized they stood by the steps of the Army and Navy Club.

"Good God, not there!" he cried in a sharp, low voice, moving quickly into the shadowed roadway where no light fell directly on his face. Sutch limped after him. "Not there, nor tonight. It is late. Tomorrow, if you will, in some quiet place, after nightfall. I do not venture out in daylight."

Again, Lieutenant Sutch asked no questions. "I know a quiet restaurant," he offered. "If we dine there at nine, we shall meet no one whom we know. I will meet you just before nine tomorrow night at the corner of Swallow Street."

Accordingly, they dined the following evening at a table in the corner of the Criterion grill-room. As Harry entered, he scanned the room quickly.

"I dine here often when I am in town," Sutch noted, smiling. "Listen!" The throbbing of the engines driving the electric lights was distinct, their vibrations palpable.

"It reminds me of a ship," Sutch remarked. "I can almost fancy myself in the gun-room again. We will have dinner. Then you shall tell me your story."

"You have heard nothing of it?" Feversham asked, suspicion edging his tone.

"Not a word," Sutch replied, and Feversham breathed a sigh of relief. He had imagined contempt on every face that passed him in the street.

This evening, Sutch was even more concerned than the night before. Under the full light, Harry Feversham appeared clearly: his face thin and haggard from sleeplessness, black hollows beneath his eyes. He drew breath and moved in a restless, feverish rhythm, his nerves seemingly strung to breaking point. Once or twice between courses, he began to speak, but Sutch waited until the cloth was cleared.

"Now," Sutch said, extending his cigar-case. "Take your time, Harry."

Feversham then told the whole truth, without exaggeration or omission. He forced himself into a slow, careful, matter-of-fact speech, so that by the end, Sutch almost fell into the illusion that he was hearing the story of a stranger recounting mere trivia to pass the time. He began with the Crimean night at Broad Place and concluded with the ball at Lennon House.

"I came back across Lough Swilly early that morning," Feversham said in conclusion. "I traveled immediately to London. Since then, I have stayed in my rooms all day, listening to the bugles calling in the barrack-yard beneath my windows. At night, I prowl the streets or lie in bed waiting for the Westminster clock to sound each new quarter. On foggy nights, I hear the steam-sirens on the river. Do you know when the ducks start quacking in St. James's Park?" He laughed bitterly. "At two o'clock to the minute."

Sutch listened without interruption. However, halfway through the narrative, his attitude shifted significantly. Until the moment Harry described concealing the telegram, Sutch had sat with his arms on the table, his gaze fixed on his companion. Thereafter, he raised a hand to his forehead, shielding his face while the remainder of the tale unfolded. Feversham needed no explanation; he sensed that Lieutenant Sutch wished to conceal the scorn he felt, unable to trust his facial muscles. Feversham, however, mitigated nothing, continuing steadily and truthfully to the end.

Even after the story concluded, Sutch did not lower his hand, nor did he speak for some moments. When he finally did, his words struck Feversham with a shock of surprise. There was no contempt in them; though his voice shook, it vibrated with profound contrition.

"I am much to blame," Sutch said. "I should have spoken that night at Broad Place, and I held my tongue. I shall hardly forgive myself." The knowledge that it was Muriel Graham's son who had brought ruin and disgrace upon himself dominated the lieutenant's mind. He felt he had failed in a self-imposed, yet very real, obligation. "You see, I understood," he continued remorsefully. "Your father, I am afraid, never would."

"He never will," Harry interrupted.

"No," Sutch agreed. "Your mother, of course—if she had lived, would have seen clearly; but few women, I think, except your mother. Brute courage! Women make a god of it. That girl, for instance"—and again Harry interrupted.

"You must not blame her. I was defrauding her into marriage."

Sutch removed his hand suddenly from his forehead. "Suppose you had never met her. Would you still have sent in your papers?"

"I think not," Harry said slowly. "I want to be fair. Disgracing my name and those dead men in the hall, I think I would have risked. I could not risk disgracing her."

Lieutenant Sutch thumped his fist despairingly upon the table. "If only I had spoken at Broad Place. Harry, why didn't you let me speak? I might have saved you many unnecessary years of torture. Good heavens! What a childhood you must have spent with that fear all alone with you. It makes me shiver to think of it. I might even have saved you from this last catastrophe. For I understood. I understood."

Sutch saw more clearly into the dark recesses of Harry Feversham's mind than Harry did himself. Because he saw so clearly, he felt no contempt.
