Sally sat with her back propped against a mound of golden sand, her eyes half-closed as she surveyed the morning routine of Roville-sur-Mer. Like most French seaside towns, Roville woke up early, and the beach was already teeming with life. Fathers with bushy beards formed colorful clusters in the foreground, while their female companions gathered beneath bright, cheery parasols. Dogs trotted aimlessly, and children dug furiously with spades, occasionally pausing their labor to strike one another with these handy tools.

One particularly distinguished canine—a poodle sporting a military bearing—wandered over to Sally. Spotting her box of sweets, he wisely decided to wait and see what developments might arise.

Anticipation is usually delightful, but Sally's vacation had proven to be an exception to the rule. It had been a magical month of lazy bliss. She had drifted luxuriously from one French town to another until the sheer charm of Roville—with its azure skies, grand Casino, snow-white hotels lining the Promenade, and general sparkle—had finally grounded her. She could have stayed forever, but the voice of America was calling her back. Gerald had sent a frantic note stating that *The Primrose Way* was opening in Detroit ahead of its New York run. If she wanted to catch the premiere, she needed to leave immediately. It was a messy, hurried letter from a busy man, yet one Sally couldn't ignore. Tomorrow, she would depart.

For today, however, was simply today. She sat and watched the bathers, enjoying the familiar peace of having nothing to do but soak up the warmth and listen to the gentle lapping of the waves.

Of course, there was one downside to mornings on the Roville beach: the overwhelming urge to drift off to sleep. Doing so so soon after breakfast—even on holiday—was undignified. Usually, Sally fought this temptation fiercely, but today the sun was too warm and the whispers of the waves too seductive. She was on the verge of nodding off when voices nearby jolted her back to reality. While the beach buzzed with chatter, these specific voices were speaking English—a rarity in Roville—and hearing the familiar tongue snapped her fully awake. Just a few feet away, two men had taken seats on the sand.

Since the start of her travels, examining strangers and guessing their professions had been one of Sally's favorite pastimes, despite her track record being predictably poor. Glancing from the corner of her eye, she studied the pair.

The first man failed to impress. He was tall and dark, with a tight, precise mouth and high cheekbones that gave him a vaguely sinister air. He possessed the dusty complexion of a man engaged in a perpetual battle with facial hair. Clearly, he shaved twice daily and exercised remarkable self-control not to swear when he nicked himself; Sally could easily imagine him sneering nastily at such moments.

"Hard," Sally diagnosed. "I wouldn't trust him. Probably a lawyer or something similar."

She shifted her gaze to the second man and found herself locked in his stare. He had been fixated on her with intense concentration since arriving. His mouth hung slightly open, giving him the appearance of someone who, after countless disappointments, had finally found something worthy of his attention.

"Rather a dear," Sally decided.

He was a sturdy, thick-set youth with an amiable, freckled face and the reddest hair she had ever encountered. He sported a square chin featuring a small cut at one angle. Sally was convinced that, regardless of how he acquired that wound, he hadn't handled it with superior composure.

"A temper, I suppose," she mused. "Very quick, but short-lived. Not very clever, but nice."

She looked away, finding his hypnotic gaze slightly awkward.

Meanwhile, the dark man—who managed to light a cigarette against a fierce breeze in the impeccably efficient style characteristic of his entire demeanor—threw away his match and resumed the conversation, which had clearly been paused by the act of sitting down.

"And how is Scrymgeour?" he asked.

"Oh, all right," the redhead replied absently. Sally was looking straight ahead, though she felt his eyes were still busy scanning her.

"I was surprised to find him here. He told me he intended to stay in Paris."

A brief pause ensued. Sally tossed a piece of nougat to the attentive poodle.

"Say," the redhead observed in clear, penetrating tones vibrating with deep emotion, "that's the prettiest girl I've ever seen in my life!"

Startled, yet not displeased, Sally accepted this frank confession as a valid commentary on matters of public interest. Her dark companion, however, was utterly shocked.

"My dear fellow!" he exclaimed.

"Oh, it's fine," the redhead said, unfazed. "She doesn't understand. There isn't a single soul in this blasted place who speaks English. If I hadn't remembered a few scraps of French, I'd be starving by now. That girl," he insisted, returning to the topic consuming his mind, "is an absolute topper! I solemnly swear I've never met anyone to compare with her. Look at those hands and feet. You don't get them anywhere else in France. Granted, her mouth is a bit wide," he admitted reluctantly.

Sally's stillness, combined with the other man's assertion regarding the linguistic limitations of Roville's inhabitants, reassured the dark figure. He exhaled audibly. In his entire life, he had never acted with anything less than scrupulous propriety, yet he had panicked at the prospect of being associated—even remotely—with impropriety in another person. It had been a black moment for him when the redhead uttered those few complimentary words.

"Still, you ought to be careful," he said sternly.

He glanced at Sally, who was now splitting her attention between the poodle and a scruffy mongrel that had joined the group, before returning to the mystery of Scrymgeour.

"How is Scrymgeour's indigestion?"

The redhead seemed barely interested in Scrymgeour's internal state.

"Do you notice how her hair curls over her ears?" he asked. "Eh? Oh, pretty much the same, I think."

"What hotel are you staying at?"

"The Normandie."

Sally dipped into her box for another chocolate cream and gave an imperceptible start. She, too, was staying at the Normandie. Presuming he was a recent arrival, she noted she had seen nothing of him at the hotel.

"The Normandie?" The dark man looked confused. "I know Roville quite well by reputation, but I've never heard of a Hotel Normandie. Where is it?"

"It's a little shack down near the station. Not much of a place. Still, it's cheap, and the cooking is decent."

His companion's bewilderment grew.

"What on earth is a man like Scrymgeour doing there?" he asked. Sally felt an urgent curiosity surge within her to learn more about the absent Scrymgeour. The constant repetition of his name had made him feel almost like an old friend. "If there's one thing he's fussy about..."

"There are at least eleven thousand things he's fussy about," the redhead interrupted disapprovingly. "Jumpy old blighter!"

"If there's one thing he's particular about, it's the type of hotel he stays in. Since I've known him, he's always wanted the best. I should have thought he'd go to the Splendide." He pondered this dilemma in a dissatisfied manner for a moment, then seemingly reconciled himself to the notion that a rich man's eccentricities must be endured. "I'd like to see him again. Ask him if he'll dine with me at the Splendide tonight. Say eight sharp."

Sally, distracted by the growing pack of dogs—which now included a white terrier with a black patch over its left eye—couldn't see the young man's face. However, his tone revealed that something was amiss. There was a forced lightness to his voice.

"Oh, Scrymgeour isn't in Roville."

"No? Where is he?"

"Paris, I believe."

"What!" The dark man's voice sharpened. He sounded like he was cross-examining a reluctant witness. "Then why aren't you there? What are you doing here? Did he give you a holiday?"

"Yes, he did."

"When do you rejoin him?"

"I don't."

"What!"

The redhead's manner wasn't exactly submissive.

"Well, if you want to know," he said, "the old blighter fired me the day before yesterday."

Sand shifted as the dark man sprang to his feet. Sally, absorbed in the unfolding drama beside her, absent-mindedly handed the poodle a piece of nougat that rightfully belonged to the terrier. Shooting a quick sideways glance, she saw the dark man standing in a pose reminiscent of a stern melodramatic father about to banish his erring daughter into the snow. The redhead, outwardly stoic, stared fixedly down the beach at a portly bather in an orange suit who, after six false starts, was finally in the water, floating with the dignified grace of a deflated balloon.

"Do you mean to tell me," the dark man demanded, "that after all the trouble the family took to secure you a position that was practically a sinecure, with endless possibilities if you only behaved yourself, you have deliberately thrown away..." A despairing gesture completed the sentence. "Good God, you're hopeless!"
