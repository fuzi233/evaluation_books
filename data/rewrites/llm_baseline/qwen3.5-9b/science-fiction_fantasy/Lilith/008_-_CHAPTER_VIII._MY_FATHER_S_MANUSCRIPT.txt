A profound awe settled over me as I contemplated the task ahead. Above, the sun burned with a golden intensity; below, the sea lay in sapphire stillness under its gaze. This same world sent its greenery reaching upward and its creatures soaring into the air I had breathed since infancy. Yet, I perceived the splendor spreading before me as merely a fleeting display—a stage set ready to be raised at any instant to reveal wonders far surpassing the current view.

Shortly after my father's death, I found myself seated one morning in the library. With a listless demeanor, I had been staring at a portrait hanging among the volumes, a distant ancestor I knew only by reputation, wishing desperately to learn of his origins. Turning my attention to a book on the shelf, I began to read. Glancing up, I noticed a figure approaching—not from the doorway, but emerging from the space between me and the portrait.

He was a thin, pale man dressed in rusty black. His expression was sharp and eager, marked by a prominent nose that reminded me of a specific jug my sisters affectionately dubbed "Mr. Crow."

"Finding myself in your vicinity, Mr. Vane, I have taken the liberty of calling," he said, his voice peculiar yet pleasant. "Your honored grandfather treated me—as I may say without presumption—as a friend. He knew me from childhood as his father's librarian."

At the time, I failed to register just how ancient the man truly was. "May I inquire where you reside now, Mr. Crow?" I asked.

He offered an amused smile. "You nearly hit my name," he replied. "Which demonstrates a keen familial insight. You have seen me before, though only once, and could not then have recognized it!"

"Where was that?"

"In this very room. However, you were quite a child then!"

I hesitated, unsure if I recalled him, though for a fleeting moment, a recognition sparked within me. I implored him to clarify his identity.

"There exists a phenomenon of remembering without recognizing the memory itself," he observed. "Regarding your name—which you guessed close enough—it was formerly Raven."

The name struck a chord; marvelous tales had carried it to my ears. "It is very kind of you to visit," I said. "Won't you sit?"

He seated himself immediately. "Did you know my father, then?"

"I knew him," he answered with a curious smile. "But he cared little for my acquaintance, and we never met. That gentleman, however," he added, gesturing to the portrait, "Old Sir Up'ard—so his people called him—was in his day a friend of mine far more intimate than your grandfather ever became."

Slowly, I began to perceive the strangeness of the encounter. Yet, in truth, it was less bizarre that my visitor remembered Sir Up'ard than the fact that he had served as my great-grandfather's librarian!

"I owe him much," he continued. "Although I had read countless books compared to him, through the special direction of his studies, he revealed to me a certain relationship of modes I could never have discovered alone, nor likely learned from anyone else."

"Would you mind explaining that?" I asked.

"By no means—at least as much as I am able. There are no such things as wilful secrets," he responded, launching into his tale.

"That closet housed his library—a collection of a hundred manuscripts, for printing had not yet been invented. One morning, I sat there cataloging them when he entered and simply said, 'Come.' I laid down my pen and followed him across the great hall, down a steep, rough descent, and along an underground passage to a tower he had recently constructed. It consisted of a staircase leading to a single room at the summit. The door possessed a formidable lock, undone by the smallest key I had ever seen.

Scarcely had I crossed the threshold behind him when, to my eyes, he began to diminish, shrinking until he was barely visible. Suddenly, my vision corrected itself. I realized he was moving swiftly away from me. Within a minute, he was a mere speck in the distance, framed by blue mountain peaks against a sky of paler blue. I recognized the landscape; I had traveled there and back many times, unaware that this was the route.

Many years later, when the tower had long vanished, I taught one of his descendants what Sir Up'ard had shown me. To this day, whenever I wish to return home via the shortest path, I utilize your house. Indeed, without your permission—for which I beg your pardon—I have firmly established a right of way through it. Not from front to back, but from bottom to top!"

"You imply, Mr. Raven," I said, "that you pass through my house into another world, disregarding the intervening space?"

"That I traverse it is an incontrovertible acknowledgment of space," the old librarian retorted.

"Please do not quibble, Mr. Raven," I pressed gently. "Take my question as I intend it."

"There is a door in your house. One step through it carries me into a world vastly different from this one."

"A better one?"

"Not entirely; but so distinct that most of its physical laws, and many of its mental ones, differ from ours. As for moral laws, they must remain fundamentally identical everywhere."

"You test my capacity for belief!" I exclaimed. "Do you take me for a madman?"

"You do not appear to be one."

"A liar, then?"

"You offer me no grounds to suspect that."

"Perhaps you simply do not believe me?"

"I will walk out that door with you if you wish. I possess enough faith in you to risk the attempt."

"The blunder all my children make!" he murmured softly. "The only exit is the entrance!"

I began to fear he had lost his sanity. He sat silently for a moment, his head resting on his hand, elbows propped on the table, eyes fixed on the books before him.

"A book," he declared loudly, "is a door inward, and therefore a door outward. I see Old Sir Up'ard," he continued, closing his eyes, "and my heart swells with love for him. What world resides there?"

"The world of your heart!" I replied. "That is, the concept of him exists there."

"There is at least one world to which your hall-door does not lead?"

"I concede that point; however, the entities within that world are not objects to be owned or held."

"Think further," he rejoined. "Has anything ever truly become yours unless you entered that world? The thought eludes you for the moment, but I assure you: there are more worlds, and more doors to them, than you can conceive in many years!"

He rose, left the library, crossed the hall, and ascended directly to the garret, clearly familiar with every twist and turn. I followed, studying his back. His hair hung long, dark, straight, and glossy. His coat was wide, trailing to his heels, while his shoes seemed comically oversized.

In the garret, light filtered through the edges of the massive roofing slabs, illuminating gaps where no flooring existed. We stepped carefully from joist to joist. In the center of one such void rose a partition with a door. Through it, I followed Mr. Raven into a small, shadowy chamber whose ceiling contracted as it rose, slanting sharply through the roof.

"That is the door I mentioned," he said, pointing to an oblong mirror standing on the floor, leaning against the wall.

I approached it, seeing our figures dimly reflected in its dusty surface. Something about it unsettled me. It appeared old-fashioned and neglected, yet despite its mundane appearance, the eagle perched atop with outstretched wings seemed menacing.

"As a mirror," the librarian noted, "it has grown dull with age, but that matters not; its clarity depends solely on the light."

"Light?" I countered. "There is no light here!"

He ignored me and began tugging at a small chain on the opposite wall. I heard a creaking sound; the top of the chamber began to rotate slowly. He paused, checked his watch, and pulled again.

"We arrive almost precisely to the moment!" he announced. "It is exactly noon!"

The ceiling groaned and revolved for a minute or so. Then he pulled two additional chains, alternating between them, before returning to the first. Moments later, the chamber brightened significantly. A patch of sunlight fell upon a mirror on the opposite wall, casting a beam across the dust. I traced the path of the reflected rays to the mirror on the floor. Yet, from that lower mirror, no reflection returned; the rays seemed to pass cleanly through it. There was no second patch of light anywhere in the room!

"Where have the sunbeams gone?" I cried.

"That I cannot tell," Mr. Raven replied. "Back, perhaps, to their origin. They now belong, I suspect, to a sense not yet developed in us."

He then discoursed on the relationships between mind and matter, and senses and qualities, in ways I could only partially grasp, proceeding to even stranger concepts I could not comprehend at all. He spoke extensively of dimensions, asserting there were many more than three, some governing powers inherent in us yet utterly unknown to us. His words, I confess, held little sway over me, much like the light held no power for the mirror, for I suspected he hardly understood what he was saying himself.
