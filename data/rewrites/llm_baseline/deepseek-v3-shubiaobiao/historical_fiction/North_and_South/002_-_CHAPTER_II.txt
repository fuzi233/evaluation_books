The train rattled southward, carrying Margaret Hale back to Helstone, her father dozing beside her. The weight of the last few days—the hurried farewells, the flurry of her cousin’s wedding—still pressed upon her, a dull ache beneath her ribs. She had longed for home, yet now, as the fields blurred past, an unexpected melancholy settled over her.

Her father stirred, his face softening briefly before the familiar lines of worry reclaimed it. He had aged since she last saw him properly, his once-dark hair now threaded with silver, his features sharper. Margaret wondered, not for the first time, what burdens he carried that etched such weariness into his expression.

*If only Frederick were here,* she thought. Her brother’s absence was a hollow space in their family, a wound no one dared name too loudly.

Helstone welcomed her in a rush of green—the dense woods, the sunlit ferns, the wide commons alive with darting insects and the hum of summer. She walked with her father, crushing fragrant bracken beneath her boots, laughing as the wind tugged at her skirts. Here, she was free.

But home was not without its shadows. Her mother fretted over small grievances—the dampness of the parsonage, the bishop’s neglect, the quiet isolation of their life. "If only we lived nearer to the Stansfields," she sighed, as though a mile’s distance might mend all discontent.

Margaret bit back impatience. She loved the roughness of the land, the honest faces of the villagers, the simplicity that others might call poverty. Yet evenings were long. Her father retreated to his books; her mother’s conversation circled restlessly between nostalgia and complaint.

One rainy afternoon, as they sat by the fire, Margaret nearly asked about Frederick—where he was, when they might hear from him—but the words stuck in her throat. Some sorrows were too deeply buried to disturb.

Then October came, bright and golden, and the world seemed to mend. Margaret took to sketching, determined to capture the forest’s fleeting beauty before winter stripped it bare.

She was bent over her drawing board when the maid announced, with a flourish, *"Mr. Henry Lennox."*

The name startled her. London—and all its complications—had followed her home.
