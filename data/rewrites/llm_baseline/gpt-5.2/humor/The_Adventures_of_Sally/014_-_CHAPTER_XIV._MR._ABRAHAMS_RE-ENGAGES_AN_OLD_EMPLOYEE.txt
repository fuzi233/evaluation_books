The only real happiness, the uplift experts tell us, is the kind you manufacture in other people and then hand round like hot rolls. By that rule, Bugs Butler, thirty hours after the Jersey City ring had become his temporary bedroom, ought to have awakened in a glow of saintly contentment. After all, his failure to stop a short left-hook, followed by a right hand deposited with neat precision on the point of his jaw, had improved the day of a considerable slice of humanity.

Unfortunately, Bugs was not a man who could be cheered by the knowledge that strangers were laughing and counting money because he had gone to sleep in public.

Mr. Lew Lucas, for one, was shining with satisfaction. So were Lucas’s seconds, one of whom, carried away by the poetry of pugilism, kissed him. Most of the crowd, having backed the champion with that touching faith which only ever attends other people’s risks, were delighted. Even the general air of Jersey City seemed brighter, as if the very traffic had started to purr instead of honk.

Bugs slouched from the ring wearing a frown large enough to shade an orchard.

And still the happiness went on spreading, like the smell of coffee in a good kitchen: unstoppable, everywhere at once, and reaching people who hadn’t even been present when the percolator began to sing.

The two Wise Guys, unable to attend, got the news off the ticker and proclaimed themselves five hundred dollars richer. A pimpled office-boy at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises, Ltd., gave a whoop in the Subway when he read his morning paper, for he too had profited by the brittleness of Mr. Butler’s chin. And in a small New York apartment, Sally—breakfasting with the deliberate pleasure of a person who thinks food should be listened to as well as eaten—read the sporting page with fierce satisfaction.

Her coffee, strong enough to stand up and argue, made a small, contented sound as she stirred it: a domestic whisper against the larger city roar outside, and Sally’s mind, always sensitive to the world’s noises, took comfort in that little private rhythm. Down in the street, a delivery truck banged a tailgate like a gong. Somewhere a radio bleated an advertisement at the morning. The contrast only made her apartment feel more her own—quiet, human, and properly scented with toast.

She wasn’t a girl who disliked many people, but she had acquired a brisk distaste for Bugs Butler, and the report of his downfall tasted as pleasant as marmalade.

Lew Lucas, she decided, was a man after her own heart. If he’d been Ginger’s personal friend, he couldn’t, given the brief time available, have avenged him more thoroughly. In the first round he had done a number of diverting, artistic things to Bugs’s left eye. In the second he had continued the good work lower down, where it discourages a man’s vanity most. In the third he had put him to sleep.

Could anyone have done more?

Sally thought not. She lifted her cup—warm porcelain against her fingers, steam carrying that bitter, toasted perfume—and drank Lew Lucas’s health. She hoped his old mother was proud of him. Also, incidentally, she hoped his old mother was somewhere peaceful, with birds outside the window instead of motor horns, because if there was one modern plague Sally loathed it was the city’s habit of making noise in place of conversation.

The telephone bell rang at her elbow, sharp and metallic, like a spoon dropped on tile. She unhooked the receiver.

“Hullo?”

“Oh, hullo,” said a voice.

“Ginger!” cried Sally.

“I say, I’m awfully glad you’re back. I only got your letter this morning. Found it at the boarding-house. I happened to look in there and—”

“Ginger,” interrupted Sally, “your voice is music, but I want to see you. Where are you?”

“I’m at a chemist’s shop across the street. I was wondering if—”

“Come here at once!”

“I say, may I? I was just going to ask.”

“You miserable creature, why haven’t you been round to see me before?”

“Well, as a matter of fact, I haven’t been going about much for the last day. You see—”

“I know. Of course.” Her voice softened at once. She glanced, approvingly and gratefully, at the large photograph of Lew Lucas grinning up from the paper. “You poor thing! How are you?”

“Oh, all right, thanks.”

“Well, hurry.”

There was a pause at the other end.

“I say.”

“Well?”

“I’m not much to look at, you know.”

“You never were. Stop talking and hurry over.”

“I mean to say—”

Sally hung up firmly. If Ginger was going to begin apologizing for existing, it was better to cut him off at the source.

She waited, restless in the way one is restless when the kettle has started to murmur and you know in a moment it will boil. Then footsteps came along the passage. They stopped. The bell rang.

Sally ran to the door, flung it open—and recoiled.

“Oh, Ginger!”

He had been painfully accurate about his appearance. His right eye was clear and devoted; his left had vanished altogether beneath a puffy swelling of dull purple, like a plum that had met with harsh criticism. A bruise disfigured his cheek-bone, and his lips were thick enough to make speech seem like work.

“It’s all right, you know,” he assured her, heroically.

“It isn’t. It’s awful! Oh, you poor darling!” Sally’s hands clenched, and she looked at him as if her gaze could turn into a weapon. “I wish he had killed him!”

“Eh?”

“I wish Lew Lucas—or whatever his name is—had murdered him. Brute!”

“Oh, I don’t know, you know.” Ginger, even with half his face rearranged, could not resist fairness. “He isn’t a bad sort of chap, really. Bugs Butler, I mean.”

“Do you seriously mean to stand there and tell me you don’t loathe the creature?”

“Oh, he’s all right. See his point of view and all that. Can’t blame him, if you come to think of it, for getting the wind up a bit in the circs. Bit thick, I mean to say, a sparring-partner going at him like that. Naturally he didn’t think it much of a wheeze. It was my fault right along. Oughtn’t to have done it, of course, but somehow, when he started making an ass of me and I knew you were looking on—well, it seemed a good idea to have a dash at doing something on my own. No right to, of course. A sparring-partner isn’t supposed—”

“Sit down,” said Sally.

Ginger sat down carefully, like a man making peace with furniture.

“Ginger,” said Sally, “you’re too good to live.”

“Oh, I say!”

“I believe if someone sandbagged you and stole your watch and chain you’d say there were faults on both sides or something. I’m just a cat, and I say I wish your beast of a Bugs Butler had perished miserably. I’d have gone and danced on his grave.” She paused, then added, practical even in fury: “But whatever made you go in for that sort of thing?”

“Well, it seemed the only job that was going at the moment. I’ve always done a goodish bit of boxing and I was very fit and so on, and it looked to me rather an opening. Gave me something to get along with. You get paid quite fairly decently, you know, and it’s rather a jolly life…”

“Jolly? Being hammered about like that?”

“Oh, you don’t notice it much. I’ve always enjoyed scrapping rather. And, you see, when your brother gave me the push—”

Sally made a sound like a plate chipped unexpectedly.

“What an extraordinary thing it is—I went all the way out to White Plains that afternoon to find Fillmore and tackle him about that and I didn’t say a word about it. And I haven’t seen or been able to get hold of him since.”

“No? Busy sort of cove, your brother.”

“Why did Fillmore let you go?”

“Let me go? Oh, you mean—well, there was a sort of mix-up. A kind of misunderstanding.”

“What happened?”

“Oh, it was nothing. Just a—”

“What happened?” Sally’s voice had the crisp edge of a cook with a knife and no patience.

Ginger looked about the room with the guilty helplessness of a man asked to explain a stain.

“It’s not worth talking about.”

“It is worth talking about. I’ve a right to know. It was I who sent you to Fillmore—”

“Now that,” said Ginger, “was jolly decent of you.”

“Don’t interrupt! I sent you to Fillmore, and he had no business to let you go without saying a word to me. What happened?”

Ginger twiddled his fingers as if trying to wind himself out of the conversation.

“Well, it was rather unfortunate. You see, his wife—I don’t know if you know her?…”

“Of course I know her.”

“Why, yes, you would, wouldn’t you? Your brother’s wife, I mean,” said Ginger, brightening briefly with logic. “Though, as a matter of fact, you often find sisters-in-law who won’t have anything to do with one another. I know a fellow—”

“Ginger,” said Sally, “it’s no good your thinking you can get out of telling me by rambling off on other subjects. I’m grim and resolute and relentless, and I mean to get this story out of you if I have to use a corkscrew. Fillmore’s wife, you were saying…”

Ginger returned unwillingly to the pit.

“Well, she came into the office one morning, and we started fooling about…”

“Fooling about?”

“Well, kind of chivvying each other.”

“Chivvying?”

“At least I was.”

“You were what?”

“Sort of chasing her a bit, you know.”

Sally stared at him.

“What do you mean?”

Ginger’s embarrassment deepened until it seemed determined to compete with his bruising.

“The thing was, you see, she happened to trickle in rather quietly when I happened to be looking at something, and I didn’t know she was there till she suddenly grabbed it…”

“Grabbed what?”

“The thing. The thing I happened to be looking at. She bagged it… collared it… took it away from me, you know, and wouldn’t give it back and generally started to rot about a bit, so I rather began to chivvy her to some extent, and I’d just caught her when your brother happened to roll in. I suppose,” said Ginger, adding up events with that scrupulous justice of his, “he had really come with her to the office and had happened to hang back for a minute or two, to talk to somebody or something… well, of course, he was considerably fed to see me apparently doing jiu-jitsu with his wife. Enough to rattle any man, if you come to think of it.”

Sally, who could think of it perfectly well, waited.

“Well, he didn’t say anything at the time, but a bit later in the day he called me in and administered the push.”

Sally shook her head.

“It sounds the craziest story to me. What was it that Mrs. Fillmore took from you?”

“Oh, just something.”

Sally rapped the table like a gavel.

“Ginger!”

“Well, as a matter of fact,” said the battered martyr, “it was a photograph.”

“Who of? Or, if you’re particular, of whom?”

“Well… you, to be absolutely accurate.”

“Me?” Sally blinked. “But I’ve never given you a photograph of myself.”

Ginger’s face, where it was not purple, became scarlet.

“You didn’t exactly give it to me,” he mumbled. “When I say give, I mean—”

“Good gracious!” Sally saw the whole comedy at once. “That photograph we were hunting for when I first came here! Had you stolen it all the time?”

“Why, yes, I did sort of pinch it…”

“You fraud! You humbug! And you pretended to help me look for it.” She gazed at him with reluctant admiration. “I never knew you were so deep and snaky. I’m discovering all sorts of new things about you.”

Ginger, having made his confession, seemed almost lighter, like a man who has finally admitted he ate the last slice.

“I hope you’re not frightfully sick about it?” he said after a pause. “It was lying about, you know, and I rather felt I must have it. Hadn’t the cheek to ask you for it, so…”

“Don’t apologize,” said Sally warmly. “It’s a compliment. So I have caused your downfall again, have I? I’m certainly your evil genius, Ginger. I’m beginning to feel like a regular rag and a bone and a hank of hair. First I egged you on to insult your family—oh, by the way, I want to thank you about that. Now that I’ve met your Uncle Donald I can see how public-spirited you were. I ruined your prospects there, and now my fatal beauty—cabinet size—has led to your destruction once more. It’s certainly up to me to find you another job.”

“No, really, I say, you mustn’t bother. I shall be all right.”

“It’s my duty. Now what is there that you really can do? Burglary, of course, but it’s not respectable. You’ve tried being a waiter and a prize-fighter and a right-hand man, and none of those seems to be just right. Can’t you suggest anything?”

Ginger shook his head.

“I shall wangle something, I expect.”

“Yes, but what? It must be something good this time. I don’t want to be walking along Broadway and come on you suddenly as a street-cleaner. I don’t want to send for an express-man and find you popping up. My idea would be to go to my bank to arrange an overdraft and be told the president could give me two minutes and crawl in humbly and find you prezzing away to beat the band in a big chair. Isn’t there anything in the world that you can do that’s solid and substantial and will keep you out of the poor-house in your old age? Think!”

“Of course, if I had a bit of capital…”

“Ah!” said Sally. “The business man! And what would you do, Mr. Morgan, if you had a bit of capital?”

“Run a dog-thingummy,” said Ginger promptly.

“What’s a dog-thingummy?”

“Why, a thingamajig. For dogs, you know.”

Sally nodded slowly, as if this clarified everything.

“Oh, a thingamajig for dogs. Now I understand. You do put things so obscurely at first. Ginger, you poor fish, what are you raving about? What on earth is a thingamajig for dogs?”

“I mean a sort of place like fellows have. Breeding dogs, you know, and selling them and winning prizes and all that. There are lots of them about.”

“Oh, kennels?”

“Yes, a kennels.”

“What a weird mind you have. You couldn’t say kennels at first, could you? That wouldn’t have made it difficult enough. I suppose if anyone asked you where you had your lunch, you’d say, ‘Oh, at a thingamajig for mutton chops.’” She leaned forward, suddenly intent. “But Ginger, my lad, there is something in this. I believe for the first time in our acquaintance you have spoken something very nearly resembling a mouthful. You’re wonderful with dogs, aren’t you?”

“I’m dashed keen on them, and I’ve studied them a bit. As a matter of fact, though it seems rather like swanking, there isn’t much about dogs that I don’t know.”

“Of course. I believe you’re a sort of honorary dog yourself. I could tell it by the way you stopped that fight at Roville. You plunged into a howling mass of about a million hounds of all species and just whispered in their ears and they stopped at once.” Sally’s eyes brightened the way they did when a recipe finally made sense. “Why, the more one examines this, the better it looks. I do believe it’s the one thing you couldn’t help making a success of. It’s very paying, isn’t it?”

“Works out at about a hundred per cent on the original outlay, I’ve been told.”

“A hundred per cent? That sounds too much like something of Fillmore’s for comfort. Let’s say ninety-nine and be conservative. Ginger, you have hit it. Say no more. You shall be the Dog King, the biggest thingamajigger for dogs in the country. But how do you start?”

“Well, as a matter of fact, while I was up at White Plains, I ran into a cove who had a place of the sort and wanted to sell out. That was what made me think of it.”

“You must start to-day. Or early to-morrow.”

“Yes,” said Ginger doubtfully. “Of course, there’s the catch, you know.”

“What catch?”

“The capital. You’ve got to have that. This fellow wouldn’t sell out under five thousand dollars.”

“I’ll lend you five thousand dollars.”

“No!” said Ginger, with the startled horror of a man offered a second helping when he believes he doesn’t deserve the first.

Sally stared at him.

“Ginger, I’d like to slap you,” she said calmly, which meant she felt it intensely. It was maddening, this sentimental nonsense barging into business like a street-organ into a quiet room. Why, because he happened to be a man and she happened to be a woman, should she be prevented from putting money into a sound enterprise? If Columbus had taken this attitude with Queen Isabella, America would still be an inconvenience rumoured by fish.

“I can’t take five thousand dollars off you,” said Ginger firmly.

“Who’s talking of taking it off me, as you call it?” Sally snapped. “Can’t you forget your burglarious career for a second? This isn’t the same thing as going about stealing defenceless girls’ photographs. This is business. I think you would make an enormous success of a dog-place, and you admit you’re good, so why make frivolous objections? Why shouldn’t I put money into a good thing? Don’t you want me to get rich, or what is it?”

Ginger became confused, a state in which he always looked like a man trying to remember whether he left the gas on in someone else’s house.

“But it’s such a lot of money.”

“To you, perhaps. Not to me. I’m a plutocrat. Five thousand dollars! What’s five thousand dollars? I feed it to the birds.”

Ginger considered this literally for a moment, as if picturing pigeons pecking at banknotes. He knew only that Sally had once inherited something, and he had been impressed by Fillmore’s general air of wealth. The Nicholas family, it seemed, belonged to that special class of people who could afford not only food but silence—if they wanted it.

“I don’t like it, you know,” he said at last.

“You don’t have to like it,” said Sally. “You just do it.”

A thought, comforting as a bandage, came to him.

“You’d have to let me pay you interest.”

“Let you? My lad, you’ll have to pay me interest. What do you think this is—a round game? It’s a cold business deal.”

“Topping!” said Ginger, visibly relieved. “How about twenty-five per cent.”

“Don’t be silly,” said Sally quickly. “I want three.”

“No, that’s all rot,” protested Ginger. “I mean to say—three. I don’t,” he added, trying to compromise, “mind saying twenty.”

“If you insist, I’ll make it five. Not more.”

“Well, ten, then?”

“Five!”

“Suppose,” said Ginger, coaxing like a man bargaining for a puppy, “I said seven?”

“I never saw anyone like you for haggling,” said Sally. “Listen. Six. And that’s my last word.”

“Six?”

“Six.”

Ginger did sums, lips pursed, as if tasting the number.

“But that would only work out at three hundred dollars a year. It isn’t enough.”

“What do you know about it? As if I hadn’t been handling this sort of deal all my life. Six! Do you agree?”

“I suppose so.”

“Then that’s settled. Is this man you talk about in New York?”

“No, he’s down on Long Island at a place on the south shore.”

“I mean, can you get him on the ’phone and clinch the thing?”

“Oh, yes. I know his address, and I suppose his number’s in the book.”

“Then go off at once and settle with him before somebody else snaps him up. Don’t waste a minute.”

Ginger paused at the door, lingering as if his gratitude had made him heavy.

“I say, you’re absolutely sure about this?”

“Of course.”

“I mean to say—”

“Get on,” said Sally.

When he had gone, the apartment seemed to settle, like a kitchen after a visitor: the air still holding the trace of another person, the quiet returning in layers. Sally carried her newspaper to the window-sill and read the fight report a third time, savoring it as one savors a good sauce—knowing what is coming, loving it anyway. Between paragraphs she let her eyes drift to the street below.

It was a beautiful day, bright and clean in the light, though the city kept trying to ruin it with clatter. A streetcar screeched on its rails with the tortured cry of metal forced into obedience. Somewhere a man shouted an advertisement as if volume could replace meaning. Sally listened, half irritated, half amused; modern life was always so proud of its noise, as if the point of living were to drown out your own thoughts.

She had not been there long when a taxi stopped at the apartment-house. She was surprised to see her brother Fillmore heave himself out. He paid the driver, and the cab moved off, leaving him on the sidewalk with his hat brim cutting a decisive line of shadow.

Sally was just about to call down when Fillmore began to behave in a manner that made her freeze with curiosity.

From where she sat, he looked like a man rehearsing steps for a dance he did not trust. He advanced towards the front door with determination, then stopped and scuttled back. He did it again. Then he stood for a moment as if arguing with an invisible opponent, made a third dash, halted abruptly as though he’d bumped into an unseen wall—and finally wheeled and bustled away down the street, disappearing.

Sally could make nothing of it. If Fillmore had taken the trouble to come in a taxi, obviously intending to call, why had he retreated at the last moment as if the door were a wild animal?

She was still turning this over when the telephone rang again, brisk and officious.

“Sally?” said Fillmore’s voice, husky.

“Hullo, Fill. What are you going to call it?”

“What am I—Call what?”

“The dance you were doing outside here just now. It’s your own invention, isn’t it?”

“Did you see me?” Fillmore demanded, sounding as if he’d been caught reciting poetry.

“Of course I saw you. I was fascinated.”

“I—er—I was coming to have a talk with you. Sally…”

His voice trailed off into a silence full of discomfort.

“Well, why didn’t you?”

A pause. Something was plainly upsetting Fillmore Nicholas, which, in Sally’s experience, generally meant either money or emotion, the two things most likely to make him lose his usual imperial smoothness.

“Sally,” he said at last, and coughed hollowly into the receiver.

“Yes.”

“I—that is to say, I have asked Gladys… Gladys will be coming to see you very shortly. Will you be in?”

“I’ll stay in. How is Gladys? I’m longing to see her again.”

“She is very well. A trifle—a little upset.”

“Upset? What about?”

“She will tell you when she arrives. I have just been ’phoning to her. She is coming at once.” Another pause, heavier. “I’m afraid she has bad news.”

“What news?”

Silence.

“What news?” Sally repeated sharply. She hated mysteries. They were like tins without labels: you never knew whether you were opening peaches or something with tentacles.

But Fillmore had rung off.

Sally hung up and stood a moment, uneasy. Then, since there was no sense in worrying the air thin, she carried her breakfast things to the kitchen and washed up, letting the warm water and the simple, domestic noises steady her—the scrape of plate on plate, the small rush of the tap, a far kinder soundscape than the city’s constant banging.

A ring at the doorbell brought her out. Gladys stood there.

Marriage—even marriage to the Hope of the American Stage—had done nothing to alter the former Miss Winch in any important way. As Mrs. Fillmore Nicholas she remained the same square, friendly creature, solid as a good table. She hugged Sally with muscular enthusiasm and marched into the sitting-room.

“Well, it’s great seeing you again,” she said. “I began to think you were never coming back. What was the big idea, springing over to England like that?”

Sally had expected this and had her answer ready.

“I wanted to help Mr. Faucitt.”

“Who’s Mr. Faucitt?”

“Hasn’t Fillmore ever mentioned him? He was a dear old man at the boarding-house, and his brother died and left him a dressmaking establishment in London. He screamed to me to come and tell him what to do about it. He has sold it now and is quite happy in the country.”

“Well, the trip’s done you good,” said Mrs. Fillmore. “You’re prettier than ever.”

There was a pause. Already Sally had noticed something different in Gladys. The old careless, mischievous lightness was dulled, as if some heavy hand had pressed on it. Gladys’s honest eyes looked troubled.

“What’s the bad news?” Sally asked abruptly. She wanted it out, like a bone stuck in a throat. “Fillmore was telling me over the ’phone that you had some bad news for me.”

Mrs. Fillmore scratched at the carpet with the tip of her parasol, making a faint, rasping sound. When she spoke, it wasn’t an answer.

“Sally, who’s this man Carmyle over in England?”

“Oh, did Fillmore tell you about him?”

“He told me there was a rich fellow over in England who was crazy about you and had asked you to marry him, and that you had turned him down.”

“Yes,” said Sally. “That’s true.”

“You couldn’t write and say you’ve changed your mind?”

Sally’s independence rose up hot and quick.

“I suppose I could if I had—but I haven’t. Did Fillmore tell you to try to talk me round?”

“Oh, I’m not trying to talk you round,” said Mrs. Fillmore hurriedly. “Goodness knows, I’m the last person to try and jolly anyone into marrying anybody if they didn’t feel like it. I’ve seen too many marriages go wrong to do that. Look at Elsa Doland.”

Sally’s heart jumped, as if some tender nerve had been touched.

“Elsa?” she stammered. “Has—has her marriage gone wrong?”

“Gone all to bits,” said Mrs. Fillmore. “You remember she married Gerald Foster, the man who wrote ‘The Primrose Way’?”

Sally managed not to laugh, though the sound tried to claw its way out of her throat like hysteria.

“Yes,” she said. “I remember.”

“Well, it’s all gone bloo-ey. I’ll tell you about that in a minute. Coming back to this man in England, if you’re in any doubt about it… I mean, you can’t always tell right away whether you’re fond of a man or not. When first I met Fillmore, I couldn’t see him with a spy-glass, and now he’s just the whole shooting-match… But that’s not what I wanted to talk about. I was saying one doesn’t always know one’s own mind at first, and if this fellow really is a good fellow… and Fillmore tells me he’s got all the money in the world…”

Sally stopped her.

“No, it’s no good. I don’t want to marry Mr. Carmyle.”

“That’s that, then,” said Mrs. Fillmore. “It’s a pity, though.”

“Why are you taking it so much to heart?” Sally asked, with a nervous little laugh that sounded thin in her own ears.
