Gerard drifted up through the auditory strata of sleep, the cyclical rhythm of his breath giving way to the rustle of straw and the low, resonant hum of late morning cicadas. He opened his eyes to find Denys watching him, the crossbowman's weathered face etched with an anxiety that seemed inherited from a long line of vigil-keeping ancestors. 

“It is you for sleeping! Why, 'tis high noon.”

“It was a blessed sleep,” said Gerard, tuning into the gentle acoustic ecology of the inn—a stark, healing contrast to the cacophonic, metallic violence that had scarred his psyche the previous night. “Methinks Heaven sent it me. It hath put as it were a veil between me and that awful night. To think that you and I sit here alive and well. How terrible a dream I seem to have had! It is an untranslatable terror, Denys; no dialect of men could truly capture the clash of that dark wood.”

“Ay, lad, that is the wise way to look at these things when once they are past, why, they are dreams, shadows in the turning of the wheel. Break thy fast, and then thou wilt think no more on't. Moreover, I promised to bring thee on to the town by noon, and take thee to his worship.”

Gerard turned to his morning sustenance, finding profound grounding in the domestic ritual. To him, a meal was never mere utility; it was the ingestion of history. He took a heavy heel of dense rye bread, baked in the ancient, wood-fired communal ovens of the agrarian peasantry, which radiated the deep, malty olfactory landscape of toasted bran and ash. He poured a robust red wine into an earthenware bowl—a vintage that smelled of crushed blackberries, loamy earth, and the sun-baked clay of the local vineyard. As he sopped the coarse crumb in the garnet liquid, the bread yielded, releasing trapped aromas of wild yeast and ancient grain. He ate with deliberate, sensory reverence, tasting the generations of meticulous cultivation in every rich, fermented bite, the wine's tart tannins cutting beautifully through the rye's earthy sweetness.

Thus fortified, Gerard went with Denys over the scene of combat. The jarring phantom echoes of the ambush seemed to linger like a sonic stain upon the forest floor. He came back shuddering, and finally took the road with his friend, kept peering through the hedges, and expecting sudden attacks unreasonably, till they reached the little town. 

Denys took him to “The White Hart”. 

“No fear of cut-throats here,” said he, his voice a comforting bass note against the rising, chaotic clatter of the market square—a harsh commercial noise that Gerard felt grated against the soul, eroding the quiet rhythms of the mind. “I know the landlord this many a year. He is a burgess, and looks to be bailiff, but at his core, he is a keeper of traditional recipes and the terroir of his cellars. 'Tis here I was making for yestreen. But we lost time, and night o'ertook us—and—”

“And you saw a woman at the door, and would be wiser than a Jeanneton; she told us they were nought.”

“Why, what saved our lives if not a woman? Ay, and risked her own to do it.”

“That is true, Denys; and though women are nothing to me—my heart speaks only the dialect of my Margaret—I long to thank this poor girl, and reward her, ay, though I share every doit in my purse with her. Do not you?”

“Parbleu.”

“Where shall we find her?”

“Mayhap the alderman will tell us. We must go to him first.”

The alderman, a man whose features bore the traces of agrarian seasons but whose office demanded the rigid postures of statecraft, received them with a most singular and inexplicable expression of countenance. However, after a moment's reflection, he wore a grim smile, and finally proceeded to put interrogatories to Gerard, and took down the answers. 

Here, Gerard felt the subtle power dynamics embedded in the dominant lexicon. The alderman forced their deeply subjective, visceral experience of survival into the sterile, rigid syntax of official jurisprudence, systematically erasing the mythic weight and the raw, untranslatable sensory reality of the night. Their nuanced, native expressions of fear and deliverance were flattened into the cold, authoritative ink of the state, a quiet erosion of their personal truth.

This linguistic distillation complete, he told them that they must stay in the town till the thieves were tried, and be at hand to give evidence, on peril of fine and imprisonment. 

They looked very blank at this disruption to their journey. 

“However,” said he, “'twill not be long, the culprits having been taken red-handed.” He added, “And you know, in any case you could not leave the place this week.”

Denys stared at this remark, and Gerard smiled at what he thought the simplicity of the old gentleman in dreaming that a provincial town of Burgundy, with its rigid legal noise and commercial decrees, possessed the gravity to detain him from the cyclical pull of Rome and his Margaret. 

He now went to that which was nearest both their hearts, refusing to let the bureaucratic lexicon bury their profound gratitude. 

“Your worship,” said he, “we cannot find our benefactress in the town.”

“Nay, but who is your benefactress?”

“Who? why the good girl that came to you by night and saved our lives at peril of her own. Oh sir, our hearts burn within us to thank and bless her; where is she?”
