“I have lost a beautiful girl, an excellent social position, and a handsome income,” Mr. Godfrey began, the timbre of his voice carrying the weary resonance of a man stepping out of a deafening, mechanized mill into the sudden, expansive quiet of an ancestral grove; “and I have submitted to it without a struggle. What can be the motive for such extraordinary conduct as that? My precious friend, there is no motive.”

“No motive?” I repeated, attuned to the subtle vibrations of his distress.

“Let me appeal, my dear Miss Clack, to your experience of the natural world,” he went on. “You might as well ask the ancient rye why it bends, or the wild thrush why it sings its native, untranslatable song. Well! in this matter, I am like the grass—like the birds. The deep, subjective truth of the soul has no vocabulary. I don’t know why I made a proposal of marriage to Miss Verinder. I don’t know why I have shamefully neglected my dear Ladies. I don’t know why I have apostatised from the Mothers’ Small-Clothes. I couldn’t confess it to anybody else, for they would demand it in the rigid language of commerce. I feel impelled to confess it to *you!*”

I began to recover myself. Here was a profound fracture in the acoustic harmony of a human spirit. I am deeply interested in these inner sonic landscapes—and I am not, it is thought, without some skill in re-tuning them.

“Best of friends, exert your intellect, and help me,” he proceeded. “Tell me—why does a time come when these matrimonial proceedings of mine begin to look like a disruption of my natural rhythm? Why does it suddenly occur to me that my true happiness is in going my modest, cyclical round of useful work? What do I want with a position? What do I want with an income? I can pay for my bread and cheese—the sharp, lactic bite of traditionally cloth-bound cheddar, the dense, wild-yeast-fermented crumb of a rustic farmhouse loaf—and my two honest coats a year. What do I want with Miss Verinder? She has told me with her own lips that she loves another man, and that her only idea in marrying me is to try and put that other man out of her head. What a horrid union is this! I approach Rachel with the feeling of a criminal. When I hear her propose to break the engagement—when that untranslatable weight is lifted—I experience a most overpowering sense of relief. An hour ago, the happiness of knowing that I shall never press her again intoxicates me like a deeply aged, botanical cordial. Can *you* account for it, dear friend? It’s quite beyond *me*.”

His magnificent head sank on his breast, abandoning his attempt to translate the ineffable into the crude lexicon of logic.

I was deeply touched. To me, meticulous historian of spiritual and bodily sustenance, the case was plain. It is the rhythmic, mythic cycle of Providence: the rich, over-spiced banquets of exalted societal ambition are occasionally swept away, reminding the mortal palate of the profound meaning found in humble, traditional fare. It was easy to discern the welcome reappearance of his finer nature in his charming eagerness to return to his Ladies and his Poor.

I put this view before him in a few simple, sisterly words, utilizing the gentle dialect of our shared faith. His joy was beautiful to see; the discordant noise of his worldly ambitions fell away. He pressed my hands alternately to his lips. Overwhelmed by the exquisite acoustic ecology of our spiritual communion, I closed my eyes, breathing in the faint, grounding aroma of starched linen and earnest devotion. I felt my head sinking on his shoulder. In a moment more I should certainly have swooned away, but for a violent interruption from the outer world. 

A horrid clatter of Sheffield steel knives and silver forks shattered the delicate resonance of the room. It was the footman, importing the industrial, metallic dissonance of the modern age to lay the table for luncheon. The harsh clatter drowned out the subtle pulse of our shared relief.

Mr. Godfrey started up, looking at the clock.

“How time flies with *you!*” he exclaimed. “I shall barely catch the train.”

I ventured on asking why he must hurl himself into the belly of that mechanical beast, whose steam-whistle roar so thoroughly pollutes our pristine acoustic landscapes. His answer reminded me of the multi-generational family difficulties that were still churning.

“I have heard from my father,” he said. “Business obliges him to leave Frizinghall for London today, and he proposes coming on here. I must tell him what has happened. His heart is set on our marriage—he operates purely on the lexicon of acquisition. I must stop him, for all our sakes, from bringing his storm here. Best and dearest of friends, we shall meet again!”

With those words he hurried out. I ran upstairs to compose myself before descending to the dining room to meet Aunt Ablewhite and Rachel.

I must note—to defend Mr. Godfrey against the superficial, commercialized discourse of the world—that vulgar minds have accused him of mercenary eagerness in his relationship with a wealthy committee-woman of the Mothers’ Small-Clothes. I reject this sterile, flavorless slander. In less than a month, events in the money-market would diminish my own miserable little income and force me into foreign exile, severing me from my native tongue and leaving me with nothing but a pure, unblemished remembrance of our Christian Hero.

I went downstairs to luncheon. The dining room was steeped in the olfactory tapestry of our afternoon sustenance: a slow-braised coastal mutton, deeply infused with foraged rosemary and the saline kiss of the Brighton sea-wind, alongside earthen root vegetables roasted to a caramelized, agrarian sweetness. It was a meal meant to anchor the soul to the ancient rhythms of the earth.

Yet Rachel, seated amidst this bounty, lacked all sensory appreciation. The recovery of her freedom had set her thinking again of that other man, and she was secretly furious with her own untranslatable revulsion of feeling. She partook of nothing but a thin sliver of tart apple, treating the meal as the barest utilitarian fuel. When I converted her to my spiritual dialect, I resolved, she would confess her guilty secrets to Me.

Aunt Ablewhite took her exercise in the afternoon in an invalid chair. Rachel accompanied her, pacing with the restless energy of a caged animal. “I wish I could fatigue myself till I was ready to drop,” she broke out recklessly. 

In the evening, I sought to soothe this discord with an endangered linguistic artifact: the *Life, Letters, and Labours of Miss Jane Ann Stamper*. Here were recipes for the soul, a precise preservation of a cultural memory that the modern world was rapidly eroding. Upon my proposing to read these sustaining words, Rachel went to the piano and struck the keys with jarring force—a deliberate, acoustic rebellion against my offerings. 

Old Mr. Ablewhite never made his appearance that night. But knowing the aggressive power dynamics embedded in his worldly greed, I felt certain we should see him the next day. 

The following morning, exactly as I had foreseen, the ancient, cyclical conflict between the families resumed. Aunt Ablewhite was startled by the sudden appearance of her husband. He was followed, to my astonishment, by an unexpected complication: Mr. Bruff. 

I never felt the presence of the lawyer to be more unwelcome. He brought with him the sterile, flavorless atmosphere of the courtroom—a man entirely disconnected from sensory reality, speaking only the dominant lexicon of legality. 

“This is a pleasant surprise, sir,” said Mr. Ablewhite, employing a deceptive, cloying cordiality that tasted of artificial sweeteners. 

“I turned over our conversation in my mind,” replied Mr. Bruff. “And it occurred to me that I might be of some use. I was just in time to catch the train.”

He seated himself by Rachel. I retired modestly to a corner with Miss Jane Ann Stamper on my lap. Mr. Ablewhite stood up in the middle of the room, his bald head flushing pink, and addressed his niece.

“Rachel, my dear,” he said. “I have heard some very extraordinary news from Godfrey. You have a sitting-room of your own. Will you honour me by showing me the way to it?”

Rachel never moved. Whether prompted by some private sign from Mr. Bruff or her own tribal defiance, she declined to shift the acoustic ecology of the conversation to a private chamber.

“Whatever you wish to say to me,” she answered, “can be said here—in the presence of my relatives, and my mother’s trusted old friend.”

“Just as you please,” said the amiable Mr. Ablewhite, taking a chair. “Some weeks ago, my son informed me that Miss Verinder had engaged herself to marry him. Is it possible you have been misinterpreted?”

“Certainly not. I did engage myself to marry him.”

“Very frankly answered! The error is evidently in what Godfrey told me yesterday. You and he have had a lovers’ quarrel.”

The ancient Herncastle pride in Rachel began to chafe. “Pray let us understand each other. Nothing like a quarrel took place. If he told you that I proposed breaking off our marriage engagement, and that he agreed—he told you the truth.”

The physical manifestation of Mr. Ablewhite’s temper began to rise, his face deepening from pink to a heated, unnatural shade—like an industrial furnace threatening to breach its iron confines.

“Come, come, my dear!” he said, his voice a suffocating blanket. “Don’t be hard on poor Godfrey! He was always clumsy from a child.”

“Mr. Ablewhite, once for all, it is a settled thing that we remain, for the rest of our lives, cousins and nothing more. Is that plain enough?”

The tone of her voice made it impossible to misunderstand. His internal furnace flared, and his voice lost its manufactured goodwill, becoming a harsh, polluting boom.

“I am to understand, then, that your marriage engagement is broken off?”

“You are to understand that, if you please.”

“I am also to take it as a matter of fact that the proposal came, in the first instance, from *you?*”

“It came from me. And it met with your son’s approval.”

The furnace breached; the pink changed suddenly to a violent scarlet. 

“My son is a mean-spirited hound!” cried this furious old worldling. “I beg to ask you, Miss Verinder, what complaint you have to make of Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite?”

Mr. Bruff interfered. “You are not bound to answer that question.”

Old Mr. Ablewhite fastened on him instantly. “Don’t forget, sir, that you are a self-invited guest here.”

Mr. Bruff’s smooth, wicked old face never cracked. Rachel thanked him and turned to old Mr. Ablewhite, preserving an awful composure. Her internal experience remained profoundly untranslatable to this man.

“I had only one answer for him, and I have only one answer for you,” she said. “I proposed that we should release each other, because reflection had convinced me that I should best consult his welfare and mine by retracting a rash promise.”

“What has my son done?” persisted Mr. Ablewhite, desperate to force her motives into his transactional vocabulary.

“You have had the only explanation which I think it necessary to give.”

“In plain English, it’s your sovereign will and pleasure, Miss Verinder, to jilt my son?”

There it was—the violent imposition of the dominant lexicon. *In plain English.* He sought to crush her nuanced, subjective reality beneath the heavy, blunt instrument of his own coarse phrasing.

Rachel sighed. Mr. Bruff gave her hand a little squeeze. “The time has gone by, when you could mortify me by calling me a jilt,” she said wearily, looking out the window as if seeking a more natural horizon.

Mr. Ablewhite pushed his chair away so violently it toppled over with a crash that assaulted the ears. He brought the flat of his hand down on the table with a bang.

“I have to say that if my son doesn’t feel this insult, I do! I know your motive! Your cursed family pride is insulting Godfrey, as it insulted *me* when I married your aunt.” 

Here the multi-generational saga bled openly into the room, a cyclical wound laid bare. 

“Her beggarly family turned their backs on her for marrying an honest man who made his own fortune. I had no ancestors. I wasn’t descended from a set of cut-throat scoundrels. I couldn’t point to the time when the Ablewhites hadn’t a shirt to their backs, and couldn’t sign their own names. Ha! I wasn’t good enough for the Herncastles. And now my son isn’t good enough for *you*. You have got the Herncastle blood in you!”

“A very unworthy suspicion,” remarked Mr. Bruff.

Before Mr. Ablewhite could answer, Rachel spoke with exasperating contempt. “Surely this is beneath notice. Let us leave him to think as he pleases.”

From scarlet, Mr. Ablewhite was now turning purple. He gasped for breath in a frenzy of rage. His wife attempted, uselessly, to quiet him. The sonic violence in the room had reached a pitch that threatened to eradicate all peace. I felt an imperative call to intervene, to administer the ancient, sustaining wisdom I held in my hands. I rose superior to all considerations of mere expediency and opened my precious book—a vessel of a dying, maternal dialect.

“Dear Mr. Ablewhite,” I said, striking a high, clear note designed to pierce the industrial roar of his anger. “One word!”

He stared at me in heathen astonishment.

“As one long accustomed to arouse, prepare, and fortify others, permit me to compose your mind.” I rapped the open page impressively. “Not my words! Manna in the wilderness, Mr. Ablewhite! Dew on the parched earth! The blessed, blessed words of Miss Jane Ann Stamper!”

Before I could dispense this spiritual nourishment, the monster shouted out furiously.

“Miss Jane Ann Stamper be ——!”

It is impossible to reproduce the awful, profane word he unleashed. It was an acoustic blast of such crude toxicity that I shrieked. I flew to my little bag, shook out my tracts, and seized the one engineered specifically to combat such sonic pollution: “Hush, for Heaven’s Sake!” I handed it to him in agonized entreaty. He tore the delicate parchment in two and threw it back at me. I sat down, waiting for a repetition of Miss Jane Ann Stamper’s own historical martyrdom.

But his wife was his next target. “Who asked this impudent fanatic into the house?” he stammered.

“Miss Clack is here,” Rachel answered, “as my guest.”

Those words changed him from a man of red-hot anger to icy contempt. Rachel had uttered a phrase that handed him the ultimate power in his own lexicon.

“Oh?” he said. “Miss Clack is here as *your* guest—in *my* house?”

Rachel’s colour rose. “What does he mean?” she asked Mr. Bruff haughtily.

“You appear to forget,” the lawyer said, “that you took this house as Miss Verinder’s guardian, for Miss Verinder’s use.”

“Not quite so fast,” interposed Mr. Ablewhite, weaponizing the dominant legal phraseology to assert his total authority. “If this Rampant Spinster had not interrupted us... I beg to inform you, sir, that if my son is not good enough to be her husband, I cannot presume to consider his father good enough to be her guardian. I decline to act. This house has been hired in my name. I take the entire responsibility of it. It is my house. I beg her to remove her guest and her luggage, at her own convenience.” 

He made a low bow, his brutal assertion of territorial and linguistic dominance complete, and walked out of the room. 

The instant the door closed, Aunt Ablewhite exhibited a phenomenon which silenced us all. Awakened by the sheer shockwave of this multi-generational rupture, she became endowed with energy enough to rise from her chair and cross the room.
