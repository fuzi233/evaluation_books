Margaret stepped out with a heaviness in her heart, reluctant to leave the shelter of the room. Yet, the moment she crossed the threshold onto Milton Street, the grey pallor seemed to lift from her countenance. The crisp air of the northern town, sharp and invigorating, seemed to wake something dormant within her youthful blood. Her steps, previously dragging, grew lighter; a faint flush returned to her lips. For the first time since arriving, her thoughts drifted outward, away from the internal turmoil that had consumed her.

The street presented a scene of chaotic vitality. Men walked with hands deep in their pockets, sauntering with a casual defiance, while groups of girls clustered together, their laughter ringing with a boisterous independence that unsettled her. Among them were figures of questionable repute, lingering near the entrances of public houses, smoke curling from their lips as they offered unkind commentary on passersby. Margaret shrank from the prospect of a long journey through this labyrinth of noise and suspicion before reaching the open fields she had intended to visit. Instead, a sudden impulse seized her. She would seek out Mrs. Higgins' cottage. It promised less the serene refreshment of a country walk, yet it offered a human connection, a kindness she desperately needed.

Inside the humble dwelling, Nicholas Higgins sat by the fire, a pipe clenched between his teeth. On the opposite side of the hearth, his daughter Bessy rocked gently, a rhythmic motion that betrayed a restless soul.

Nicholas removed his pipe, rising to push a chair toward Margaret. He leaned against the chimney-piece, adopting a lounging posture as she approached Bessy. "How fares you, child?" Margaret asked, her voice soft.

"Hoo's rather down i' th' mouth in regard to spirits," Bessy replied, her accent thick and melodic, "but hoo's better in health. Hoo doesn't like this strike. Hoo's a deal too much set on peace and quietness at any price."

"This is the third strike I've seen," Bessy sighed, the weight of history settling on her small frame. "Well, the third time pays for all," her father interjected, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "See if we don't damn the masters this time. See if they don't come begging us to return at our own price. That's the game. We missed the mark before, I grant you; but this time, we've laid our plans deep, deeper than before."

"Why do you strike?" Margaret asked, genuinely perplexed. "To stop work until you secure a higher wage, is that not the definition? I confess, where I come from, I have never heard of a strike."

"I wish I were there," Bessy murmured, her eyes distant. "It is not for me to grow weary of strikes. Let me tell you, this shall be the last I witness. Before it ends, I shall be in the Great City—the Holy Jerusalem."

"And what of the present?" Nicholas countered, drawing deeply on his pipe. "Hoo's so full of the life to come, she cannot think of the dust beneath her feet. Now, I, you see, am bound to do the best I can right here. A bird in the hand is worth two in the bush. That is the difference in our views."

"But," Margaret pressed, "if the people strike, as they do in my homeland among the field laborers, the seed goes unsown, the hay remains uncut, the corn unreaped."

"Well?" Nicholas challenged, exhaling a cloud of blue smoke. "What becomes of the farmers?"

"They would either forfeit their lands," Margaret reasoned, "or they would be compelled to offer fair rates."

"Suppose they neither could nor would do the latter?" Margaret continued, tracing the logic. "They cannot abandon their farms instantly, no matter how much they desire to. Yet, without harvest, they have no grain to sell, and from where would the funds arise to pay the laborers' wages next season?"

Still puffing, Nicholas finally spoke. "I know naught of your ways down South. I have heard they are a pack of spiritless, downtrodden men, utterly crushed by poverty, so bewildered by their misery that they do not know when they are being exploited. Here, it is not so. We know when we are put upon. And we have too much blood in our veins to tolerate it. We simply take our hands from our looms and declare, 'You may crush us, but you shall not oppress us, my masters!' And be damned to them, they shall not succeed this time!"

"I wished I lived down South," Bessy whispered.

"There is much to endure there," Margaret observed. "Sorrows exist everywhere. There is grueling physical toil, performed with scant sustenance."

"But it is outdoors," Bessy insisted. "Away from the endless, deafening noise and the sickening heat."

"It rains heavily, it freezes bitterly," Margaret argued. "A young person might withstand it, but an old man is racked with rheumatism, bent and withered before his time, yet he must work, or else succumb to the workhouse."

"You said you were so enamored with the South," Nicholas remarked sharply.

"So I am," Margaret smiled, caught in the trap. "I only meant, Bessy, that there is both good and evil in all things. Since you feel the evil here, I thought it fair you should know the evil there."

"And you say they never strike down there?" Nicholas asked abruptly.

"No!" Margaret answered quickly. "I believe they possess too much sense."

"And I think," Nicholas retorted, dashing the ashes from his pipe with such violence that it shattered, "it is not that they lack sense, but that they lack spirit."

"Oh, Father!" Bessy cried. "What have you gained by striking? Consider the first strike, when Mother passed away. How we all had to cease work—you suffered most of all. Yet, many entered the mills weekly at the same wage until the jobs vanished, leaving some to wander as beggars forever."

"Aye," Nicholas admitted, his expression darkening. "That strike was poorly managed. Fools, or men without honor, seized control. You will see, this time shall be different."

"But you have not yet told me what you are striking *for*," Margaret persisted.

"Why," Nicholas explained, leaning forward, "there are five or six masters who have doubled their wealth over the last two years, flourishing on the sweat of our brows. Now, they approach us, demanding a reduction. We refuse. We shall grind them to dust first, and then see who employs them. They will have slain the goose that laid the golden eggs, I wager."

"And so you plan to die in order to take revenge upon them?"

"No!" Nicholas shook his head vigorously. "I do not intend to die. I merely anticipate the possibility of falling at my post sooner than yielding. That is what men call noble in a soldier, and why not in a poor weaver?"

"But a soldier dies for the Nation," Margaret pointed out. "For the sake of others."

He laughed, a grim, dry sound. "My lass, you are but a young wench. Can you not see that I support three mouths—Bessy, Mary, and myself—on sixteen shillings a week? Do you not realize I strike not for myself, but as much for others as any soldier? Perhaps the cause he dies for belongs to strangers he has never seen, while I champion John Boucher, who lives next door, a broken man with a sick wife and eight children under factory age. I do not champion him alone, though he is a poor soul who can barely tend two looms. I champion the cause of Justice itself. Why should we accept less now than two years ago?"

"Do not ask me," Margaret said, feeling the ground shift beneath her. "I am ignorant. Ask your masters. Surely they must have a reason. It cannot be mere arbitrary whims."

"You are but a foreigner," Nicholas sneered, his eyes flashing. "Ask the masters! They will bid us mind our own business, and they will mind theirs. Our business is to accept the diminished wage and be grateful; theirs is to drive us down to starvation, to swell their coffers. That is the truth."

"But trade conditions may be such that they cannot afford the previous rates," Margaret tried, holding her ground despite seeing the irritation rising in Nicholas.

"Trade conditions!" Nicholas spat the words. "That is merely the masters' humbug. I speak of the rate of wages. The masters hold trade in their own hands; they march it forward like a phantom horse to frighten naughty children into submission. Their cue is to beat us down, to enrich themselves. Our duty is to stand and fight—not for ourselves alone, but for those around us, for justice and fair play. We help create their profits, and we ought to help spend them. It is not that we crave gold so fiercely as before; we have saved. We are resolved to stand or fall together. Not a single man among us will accept less than the Union dictates. So I cry, 'Hooray for the Strike,' and let Thornton, Slickson, Hamper, and their ilk look to it!"
