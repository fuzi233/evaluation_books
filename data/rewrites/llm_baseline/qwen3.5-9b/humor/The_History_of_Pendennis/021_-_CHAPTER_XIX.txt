Our hero, Pen, breathed a sigh of relief when his mentor finally departed on the second day of their arrival at Oxford. One can safely assume the Major felt exactly the same way; having successfully discharged his duty, he was eager to see it behind him. Think of it: more than three months of prime time sacrificed by this "martyr" of a Major for his nephew! Was there ever a more extreme case of selflessness? How many men, or even majors, could claim such devotion? A man will gladly risk his neck or his life for honor, but try asking him to surrender his comfort or his deepest desires, and you'll find most shrink from the task. Few of us possess the stamina for such trials. So, worthy reader, if you happen to sport a beard, would you have done the same? I won't argue the case for women—they are accustomed to sacrifice; we train them well enough. But let's be honest: the tally of self-denial you've accumulated over a lifetime likely doesn't fill the ledger much. Enough said on such grim topics; you're too polite to dwell on the vulgar. Still, I must declare my admiration for the Major's conduct over the last quarter. He has earned every bit of his well-deserved holiday.

Foker and Pen saw him off in the carriage, with the younger gentleman issuing specific instructions to the driver to ensure the safety of the senior Pendennis inside. The elder Pendennis was delighted to have his nephew accompanied by a young man who would introduce him to the university's finest circles. While the Major bolted back to London and then to Cheltenham, descending upon local estates where the families hadn't fled abroad, seeking good shooting and society, the stage was set for Pen's academic odyssey.

We have already dedicated a quarter of the serial's usual space to this single episode in Pen's life. It is obvious that recounting every adventure in such detail is impossible unless a future chronicler picks up the pen after his passing to carry the tale forward. We aren't going to dissect his entire university career with this level of granularity. Alas, the lives of such boys don't lend themselves to total narration. I wish they did! Ask yourself: does your life bear telling? If your honor remains intact, your conscience is likely at peace. Women are often portrayed as pure and unselfish, while men... well, not so much. I wouldn't dare claim Arthur Pendennis was worse than his peers, only that his peers, unfortunately, are mostly flawed. Let's have the courage to admit it. Can you name ten truly spotless men among your acquaintances? My circle is vast, yet I struggle to find ten saints.

During Pen's first term, he attended classical and mathematical lectures with reasonable diligence. However, realizing quickly that he lacked the aptitude or passion for exact sciences, and annoyed by a few boorish youths who couldn't even manage to strap their trousers properly—let alone hide their abominably thick, coarse shoes and stockings—he abandoned math entirely. He informed his doting parent that he intended to focus exclusively on Greek and Roman literature.

Mrs. Pendennis was thrilled by this decision, seeing it aligned with her son's inclinations. Her only concern was his health; she had heard harrowing tales of students succumbing to brain fevers due to overwork. She insisted that his delicate constitution mattered more than any vain honors. Pen, unaware of any lurking illness, promised not to stay up late reading. Surprisingly, he kept this promise with a resolve he rarely displayed in other areas, occasionally slipping up when his guard was down.

Soon, however, Pen discovered that the classical lectures offered little value. His fellow students were dull, and in mathematics, they were far too advanced for him. Mr. Buck, the tutor, proved no better scholar than a typical fifth-former at Grey Friars. He might possess some dry knowledge of Aeschylus or Aristophanes' meter, but he understood poetry no better than Mrs. Binge, his bed-maker. Pen grew weary of watching dull minds stumble through passages he could recite in a fraction of the time they spent. He concluded that private study was the only truly profitable pursuit and announced to his mother that he would read far more independently and attend fewer public lectures. His mother, knowing nothing of Homer herself, accepted his plan with confidence, trusting her boy would secure the position he deserved.

Pen didn't return home until after Christmas, disappointing his mother and Laura, who had been eagerly planning a magnificent snow fortress—a tradition he had enjoyed three winters prior. Instead, he was whisked away to Logwood, Lady Agnes Foker's estate, for private theatricals and a lavish Christmas party filled with high society figures the Major would never want his nephew to ignore. Nevertheless, he stayed home for the final three weeks of the break. Laura noted the impressive array of new clothes he brought, while his mother admired his refined appearance and matured demeanor.

He skipped Easter entirely. Upon returning for the long vacation, however, he arrived decked in even smarter attire: morning outfits featuring extraordinary shooting jackets with remarkable buttons, and evenings dominated by gorgeous velvet waistcoats, richly embroidered cravats, and exquisite linens. Peering into his room, his mother gasped at a stunning dressing case with silver mounts, overflowing with lovely rings and jewelry. Most notably, he had acquired a new French watch and gold chain, replacing the bulky old chronometer inherited from John Pendennis—the very instrument by which the late doctor had monitored countless patients' pulses. Just months ago, Pen had coveted this watch, declaring it the most splendid timepiece in existence. Before leaving for college, Helen had retrieved it from her trinket box (unwound since her husband's death) and gifted it to him, delivering a solemn speech on her father's virtues and the sanctity of time. Now, Pen deemed the heirloom outdated, comparing it disrespectfully to a warming pan. Laura thought this rude, but Pen simply shoved the watch into a drawer alongside soiled primrose gloves, discarded cravats, and another school watch previously mentioned in the family saga.

His old mare, Rebecca, also received the axe. Pen declared her insufficient for his needs, swapping her for a more powerful steed at a hefty price tag funded by Mr. Pendennis. Laura wept as Rebecca was led away.

Furthermore, Pen introduced a large crate of cigars bearing exotic names like *Colorados*, *Afrancesados*, *Telescopios*, and *Fudson Oxford Street*. He smoked them everywhere—from the stables and greenhouses (beneficial for Helen's plants) to his own study. Initially disapproving, his mother soon relented. Pen claimed he was composing a prize poem and required the cigar to concentrate, quoting the late Lord Byron's endorsement of smoking. One day, finding him engrossed in a novel (which he studied to cultivate an appreciation for both domestic and foreign light literature), Helen entered quietly. Rather than disturb him, she fetched a lighter and his case from the adjacent bedroom, lit the match, and placed the cigar in his mouth. Pen laughed, kissing her hand as it rested fondly on the sofa back. "Dear old mother," he quipped, "if I told you to burn the house down, I think you'd do it." Given her devotion, he was likely right; she might have burned the house down for him.

Beyond English light literature, Pen imported volumes of French fiction. When Helen dipped into them, she encountered content that left her wide-eyed with surprise. Pen explained that while he wasn't the author, maintaining fluency in French required engaging with contemporary masters, including the eminent Paul de Kock, just as seriously as Swift or Molière. Mrs. Pendennis sighed in perplexity but acquiesced. Miss Laura, however, was strictly barred from these books by both her anxious mother and the rigid moralist, Arthur Pendennis himself. Despite his broad curriculum requirements, he drew a hard line against prescribing such reading material to a young lady whose societal role differed vastly from his own.

Throughout this long vacation, Pen consumed the entire bin of claret his father had stored—a reserve the son had once praised for lacking headaches. Once depleted, he ordered a fresh supply from "wine merchants" Messrs. Binney and Latham of Mark Lane, following the recommendation of Old Doctor Portman. The honest rector had advised him to host wine parties for his young friends at Boniface, suggesting reputable London suppliers over unreliable Oxbridge traders whose liquor was often poisonous and overpriced. Obeying this counsel, Pen patronized Binney and Latham.

When he requested a shipment to be sent to the cellars at Fairoaks, he cleverly instructed the merchants to include his university wine accounts with the delivery bill. The poor widow was terrified by the total. Pen laughed off her frugality, insisting the bill was moderate and noting that everyone nowadays drank claret and champagne. Reluctantly, the widow paid, sensing her household expenses were swelling beyond her narrow income, though she knew these costs were merely occasional. During his short visits, they pinched when he was gone, but reasoned that while apart, they should maximize happiness together.

Arthur's allowances were remarkably generous, surpassing those of sons from wealthier families. Years prior, the thrifty and loving John Pendennis, determined to provide his son with the university education denied to his own father by previous extravagance, had established an "Education Fund." Year after year, executors found entries labeled "A.E.F." in his books. Even after his death, the widow contributed additional sums, ensuring that by the time Arthur arrived at Oxford, the fund amounted to a significant sum.
