Nine elephants lay cold in the dust, their tusks severed and stripped with a labor that spanned two weary days. We buried the ivory beneath the canopy of a great, solitary tree, marking the spot for future retrieval, though the odds against returning alive seemed already to outweigh the value of the loot. The tusks were magnificent, averaging fifty pounds, a stark contrast to the pair belonging to the great bull that claimed Khiva—a hundred and seventy pounds of pure, unblemished white.

Khiva himself, broken and bleeding, received a burial beside the termite mound where his spirit might find rest, accompanied by an assegai to serve as a companion in the afterlife. By the third day, we moved again. Hope was a fragile thing, hanging by a thread, but we pressed on, dreaming of the day we might exhume our treasure. The journey was a trial of endurance, a long and grinding tramp that swallowed our spirits until, finally, we emerged from the chaos of the bush to find Sitanda's Kraal nestled near the Lukanga River. This was the true gateway, the edge of the fertile lands where the savages tilled their meager fields and grazed their cattle. To the left, however, the landscape dropped away abruptly into the vast, silent expanse of the desert.

Just below our encampment, a thin ribbon of water trickled away, crossing a stony slope. Twenty years prior, I had seen Silvestre creep down this very path, a ghost of a man fleeing his failure, before vanishing into the waterless wastes beyond. That slope marked the limit of the known world, the border where the lush veld ended and the Karoo scrub, hard and gray, began.

Night fell, painting the sky in hues of orange and violet as the sun sank behind the dunes. I left Good to organize the camp and walked with Sir Henry to the crest of the opposing ridge. From there, the desert stretched out like a frozen ocean, bathed in the fading light. Far in the distance, faint blue outlines emerged, capped with snow—the Suliman Mountains.

"There!" I pointed, my voice barely a whisper against the vast silence. "That is the wall surrounding Solomon's Mines. Whether we ever scale it remains to be seen."

"My brother should be there," Sir Henry replied, his tone steady and unshaken by the darkness. "And if he is, I shall find him."

"I pray it so," I answered, turning back toward the shadows, only to pause. Standing behind us, silhouetted against the twilight, was Umbopa, the great Kafir warrior. He gazed intently at the distant peaks, his expression unreadable.

Seeing my attention, he addressed Sir Henry, speaking directly to the man he had sworn allegiance to. "Is it to that land thou wouldst journey, Incubu?" He pointed his spear toward the snow-capped heights.

A chill ran through me. To address a white man by a native name, especially one so familiar, was an affront. Yet, Umbopa's presence commanded a strange respect. "How dost thou know my name?" I demanded sharply. "Thou speakest to me as an equal."

Umbopa laughed, a soft, enigmatic sound. "Why should I not? Look at him, the Inkosi whom I serve. He is of royal blood, visible in his stature and bearing. Why may not I be of kindred spirit? Speak for me, O Macumazahn, to the Inkoos Incubu. I wish to speak to both of thee."

Anger flared, but curiosity held it back. I translated, noting Umbopa's audacity. "Tell him," Sir Henry said softly, "that I intend to journey there."

"The desert is wide and holds no water," Umbopa warned, his voice echoing strangely in the stillness. "The mountains are high, crowned with snow, and beyond them lies the unknown. How shalt thou reach it, Incubu, and why?"

"Tell him," Sir Henry continued, "that I go because I believe a brother of my blood has gone before me. I journey to seek him."

"That is true," Umbopa agreed. "A Hottentot I met on the road spoke of a white man entering the desert two years past, accompanied by a hunter named Jim. They did not return."

"How do you know it was my brother?" Sir Henry asked.

"Nay, I know not the truth of it," Umbopa admitted. "Yet the Hottentot described the man as having thy eyes and a black beard. And he said the hunter was a Bechuana clad in clothes."

"There is no doubt," I interjected, recognizing the description of Jim instantly. "I knew him well."

Sir Henry nodded, a grim determination settling over his features. "George, when he set his mind on a thing, he accomplished it. If he meant to cross the Suliman Berg, he crossed it, unless fate intervened. We must look for him on the other side."

Umbopa stepped forward. "It is a far journey, Incubu."

"Yes," Sir Henry agreed, his gaze unwavering. "But there is no journey on earth that a man cannot undertake if his heart is set upon it. No mountain is too high, no desert too barren, if love guides him and he counts his life as nothing."

Umbopa listened, his eyes reflecting a depth of wisdom that surprised me. "Great words, my father," he murmured. "Listen, what is life? A feather, a seed of grass, blown hither and thither. Sometimes it dies in flight, sometimes it soars. But if the seed is heavy, it travels. Man must die. At worst, he dies a little sooner. I will go with thee across the desert and over the mountains, unless the earth claims me first."

His speech shifted, growing poetic, a burst of eloquence that transcended simple barbarism. "Out of the dark we came, into the dark we go. Like a storm-driven bird at night, we fly from the Nowhere. For a moment, our wings catch the fire-light, and then we vanish back into the void. Life is nothing, yet it is all. It is the hand that wards off Death, the glow-worm shining in the night, the breath of the oxen in winter."

Sir Henry, usually so reserved, smiled slightly. "You are a strange man."

"And perhaps," Umbopa replied, "you seek a brother over the mountains."

Suspicion pricked at me. "What dost thou know of those mountains?" I asked sharply.

"A little," Umbopa said, his voice dropping. "There is a strange land yonder, a realm of witchcraft and beauty, of brave souls and snowy peaks. I have heard tales. But what is the use of words? Darkness falls. Those who survive will see."

He raised his spear in salute and departed, leaving us with a sense of unease. He knew too much, and his silence was heavy.

The following day, preparations began. Heavy rifles and bulky gear could not be dragged across the shifting sands. We entrusted them to an old native keeper nearby, securing them with a mixture of threats and superstitions. I swore to curse his family with madness and witchcraft if he touched a single piece, a bluff that worked perfectly on the superstitious old man. He treated the weapons as if they were ancestral spirits, terrified to approach them.

We pared our supplies down to the bare minimum, a cruel necessity for the wasteland ahead. Three express rifles, two Winchesters, five water bottles, blankets, dried meat, beads for trade, and essential medical stores. The weight was a burden, roughly forty pounds per man, but it was the price of survival. We persuaded three local natives to join us for the initial leg, carrying large gourds of water, lured by the promise of hunting knives. They seemed resigned to their fate, perhaps believing our eventual demise was inevitable, yet they accepted the deal.

Rest came briefly at sunset, a feast of fresh beef and tea—the last we would taste for many days. Then, as the moon rose, bathing the rolling dunes in silver light, we stood ready. Hesitation, that natural instinct, gripped us for a moment on the threshold of this irreversible step. Sir Henry spoke, his deep voice cutting through the silence. "Gentlemen," he said, "we embark on a journey stranger than any dream. Success is doubtful. But we stand together, for good or ill, to the end. Let us pray to the Power who shapes destiny."

For a minute, hats removed, hands over faces, we bowed our heads. The prayer was simple, born of the terror of the unknown, yet it brought a strange peace to our hearts.

"Trek!" Sir Henry commanded.

We moved forward, guided only by Silvestre's ancient, fragmented map and the distant silhouette of the mountains. The sand was treacherous, working into our boots, forcing frequent stops to shake it free. The air was thick, heavy with a creamy stillness that oppressed the soul. Silence reigned, broken only by the crunch of boots and the occasional cry of an ostrich.

Suddenly, a commotion erupted. Good, leading the column with the compass, vanished into a cloud of dust. Snorts, groans, and wild shouts filled the air. In the dim light, we saw galloping forms, half-hidden by swirling sand. The natives, convinced it was the work of ghosts, threw down their loads and crawled to the ground.
