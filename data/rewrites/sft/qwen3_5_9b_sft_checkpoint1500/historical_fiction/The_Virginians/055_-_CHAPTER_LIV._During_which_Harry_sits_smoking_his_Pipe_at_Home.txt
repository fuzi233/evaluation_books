The Colonel Esmond, the maternal grandfather of our Virginians, had spent a significant portion of his long leisure in America composing memoirs of his early life. In these volumes, Madame de Bernstein—formerly Miss Beatrice Esmond before her spinsterhood—played a considerable role. George Warrington, having read his grandfather's manuscript countless times, knew his kinswoman intimately long before their physical meeting; he understood the spirit of the young, beautiful, and wilful lady from half a century prior, even as he now encountered her in the twilight of her days. One wonders whether it is sad or sweet for a woman whose bloom has faded to recall the vibrant era of her youth. When the heart grows withered, do the elderly truly love remembering how it once beat with warmth? When spirits grow languid, do we delight in thinking of former buoyancy? Thus do the buds of prime and the roses of beauty fall and wither, leaving bare branches to shiver in the winter chill.

"And that was a beauty once!" thought George Warrington as his aunt emerged from her evening rout, powdered and diamond-clad. "And that ruin was once a splendid palace. Crowds of lovers have sighed before those decrepit feet, and been bewitched by the brilliance of those eyes." His mind drifted to a fireworks display at Williamsburg on the King's birthday, where he later examined the skeletal remains of exploded Roman candles. To George, the dazzling career of Aunt Beatrice's youth mirrored the charred remnants of the fireworks. While honest Harry had witnessed those same spectacles, he lacked his brother's penchant for deep reflection; George, melancholy and introspective, drew profound lessons from the journals, whereas Harry simply enjoyed the pageantry.

After dinner, the Baroness tapped her cane toward her escritoire, instructing her attendant to retrieve a letter concealed beneath the inkstand. George recognized the superscription instantly—it was the correspondence he had carried from home.

"It appears by this letter," observed the old lady, fixing a scrutinizing gaze upon her nephew, "that since your return, differences have arisen between you and my sister."

"Indeed?" George replied smoothly, feigning ignorance. "I did not realize Madam Esmond had alluded to them."

The Baroness adjusted a large pair of spectacles over eyes that had once ignited passions, glanced over the document, and handed it to George. Reading aloud, he heard the words of his mother-in-law, dated Richmond, Virginia, December 26th, 1756:

*"Honoured Madam! And Sister!--I have received, and thankfully acknowledge, your ladyship's favour... My reply will be brought to you by my eldest son, Mr. Esmond Warrington, who returned to us so miraculously out of the Valley of the Shadow of Death..."*

The letter detailed Harry's miraculous rescue from captivity and his subsequent return to England, masquerading as the heir while George was presumed dead. The mother expressed profound grief at Harry appearing before the King and the family under false pretenses, yet noted that Harry's tender nature meant he would willingly relinquish his inheritance to his elder brother without a pang. However, the letter turned to financial matters, lamenting that an advantageous opportunity to invest Harry's funds in land and slaves in Virginia had been missed due to legal formalities and her elder son's scruples. Consequently, Harry remained dependent on his slender income, a situation exacerbated by his recent European travels and the necessity of maintaining appearances suitable for a grandson of the Marquis of Esmond.

"My mother intends to send additional funds," George read, noting a postscript regarding a proposed marriage to Lady Maria Esmond. *"Let my task now be to save for his less fortunate younger brother... The match is altogether unsuitable."*

"It took him out of prison yesterday, madam," George interrupted gently. "I think that was the best use to which we could put it."

"Nay, sir! I don't know any such thing!" cried the old lady, agitated. "Why not have kept it to buy a pair of colours for him, or to help towards another estate and some negroes? Besides, I had a fancy to pay that debt myself."

"I hope you will let his brother handle that," George countered diplomatically. "I ask leave to act as banker for this matter, considering I have borrowed so much from my mother, to be repaid to my dear Harry."

"Do you say so, sir?" The Baroness poured a glass of rich Burgundy, contrasting sharply with her grandfather's preferred claret. "Read on, and you will see your mother agrees. I drink to your health, nephew George!"

As George continued reading, the letter revealed further complications. The mother expressed concern over Harry's lack of humility during his captivity and hinted at a scandal involving an Indian woman named Silverheels. George recalled the painful memory vividly: the woman had traveled from the wilderness bearing a token from Museau, the French officer who had aided his escape, along with a dying request to send funds to Museau's relatives in Normandy. Upon returning home, the community had treated the poor woman with suspicion and scorn, turning her grief into ridicule. George had successfully petitioned the Governor for a pass to send her back to her people, but the incident had left a bitter stain on the family's reputation, a fact not yet reconciled with Madam Esmond.

Turning to the topic of Harry's marriage, the letter reiterated the mother's disapproval of Lady Maria. Despite Lady Maria's noble behavior during Harry's imprisonment—bringing jewels and gold to support him—the disparity in wealth and station rendered the union impossible. The mother, acknowledging her own limited resources compared to the vast estates of the Virginia planters, urged Harry to return to England to avoid further distress.

"As far as regards myself, madam," George said, folding the paper, "my mother's letter conveys no news to me. I always knew that Harry was the favorite son with Madam Esmond, as he deserves indeed to be. He possesses a hundred good qualities which I lack."

"Nay, that is not your fault," the Baroness teased, eyeing him slyly. "And, but that he is fair and you are brown, one might almost pass for the other."

George bowed, a faint blush coloring his pale cheek. "His disposition is bright, and mine is dark," he admitted. "Harry is cheerful, and I am otherwise. He knows how to make himself beloved by everyone, and it has been my lot to find but few friends."

"My sister and you have pretty little quarrels," the Baroness mused, recalling similar dynamics from her own youth. "There were such in our family in old days. If Madam Esmond takes after our mother—"

"My mother has always described hers as an angel upon earth," George interjected.

"Eh! That is a common character for people when they are dead!" the Baroness exclaimed dismissively. "Rachel Castlewood was an angel, if you like—at least your grandfather thought so. But let me tell you, sir, that angels are sometimes not very commodious to live with. They may be too good for us sinners, and the air down here doesn't agree with them. My poor mother was so perfect she could never forgive me for being otherwise. Ah, mon Dieu! How she oppressed me with those angelical airs!"

George lowered his eyes, reflecting on his own melancholy youth. He hesitated to reveal further family secrets to this cynical old worldling, who nonetheless seemed to understand him despite her reticence.

"I quite comprehend you, sir, though you hold your tongue," the Baroness continued. "A sermon in the morning, a sermon at night, and two or three on Sundays. That is what people call being good. Every pleasure cried 'fie' upon; all worldly people excommunicated; a ball an abomination of desolation; a play a forbidden pastime; and a game of cards perdition! What a life! Mon Dieu, what a life!"

"We played at cards every night, if we were so inclined," George smiled, defending his upbringing. "And my grandfather loved Shakespeare so much that my mother had not a word to say against her father's favorite author."

"I remember," the Baroness agreed. "He could recite whole pages by heart. Though, for my part, I like Mr. Congreve a great deal better. And then, there was that dreadful, dreary Milton, whom he and Mr. Addison pretended to admire!" she cried, tapping her fan.

"If your ladyship does not like Shakespeare, you will not quarrel with my mother for being indifferent to him, too," George said softly. "And indeed, I think—and I am sure—you do not do her justice. Wherever there are any poor, she relieves them; wherever there are any sick, she—"

"She doses them with her horrible purges and boluses!" the Baroness interrupted sharply. "Of course, just as my mother did!"

"She does her best to cure them! She acts for the best and performs her duty as far as she knows it."

"I don't blame you, sir, for doing yours and keeping your own counsel about Madam Esmond," the old lady conceded. "But at least, there is one point upon which we all three agree—that this absurd marriage must be prevented. Do you know how old the woman is? I can tell you, though she has torn the first leaf out of the family Bible at Castlewood."
