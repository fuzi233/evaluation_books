September 1811. The post-chaise halted before the iron gates of Aswarby Hall, Lincolnshire, and a small boy tumbled onto the gravel, his eyes wide with a curiosity that cut through the gathering dusk. Before him rose a formidable structure of red brick, a relic of Queen Anne's reign, crowned by a classical porch of 1790 and flanked by wings housing the stables, their cupolas gleaming like eyes in the twilight. The setting sun turned the glass of the tall, narrow windows into a grid of fiery squares, while the distant church tower, half-swallowed by the oak-studded park, struck six. The sound rolled down the wind, heavy and mournful, settling a chill of early autumn melancholy upon the boy's shoulders.

He was Stephen Elliott, an orphan from Warwickshire, now cast into the orbit of his elderly cousin, Mr. Abney. To the neighbors, Abney was a recluse, a man wrapped in the dust of ancient texts, a Professor of Greek whose library held the secrets of Mithras and the Orphic mysteries. It was baffling, even to the local gossips, that this austere scholar should have volunteered to take in a stray child. Yet, when the front door swung open, Abney did not emerge with the cold demeanor his reputation suggested. Instead, he darted from his study, his hands rubbing together in genuine delight.

"How are you, my boy?" he asked, his voice warm. "How old are you?"

"Twelve, sir," Stephen replied, surprised by the repetition of the question.

"Eleventh of September," Abney noted, scribbling furiously in a leather-bound book. "I like to get these things down. Take him to Mrs. Bunch."

Mrs. Bunch was the heartbeat of the house, a woman born in the neighborhood fifty-five years prior. She made Stephen feel at home within a quarter-hour, her knowledge of the Hall's history vast and her explanations eager. She spoke of the strange happenings that had preceded Stephen's arrival: a little girl found wandering the grounds, vanished after three weeks amidst the singing of gypsies; a foreign boy named Giovanni, who played a hurdy-gurdy and disappeared just as mysteriously, leaving his instrument behind. These stories, told with a mixture of pity and superstition, planted seeds of unease in Stephen's young mind.

That night, the house seemed to hold its breath. Stephen dreamed of a disused bathroom at the top of the stairs, its door glazed with muslin curtains long gone. Through the glass, he saw a figure lying in a lead-lined bath, pale and dusty, wrapped in a shroud. Its lips curved in a dreadful, faint smile, and its hands were pressed tightly over its heart. A low moan drifted from the corpse, and the arms stirred. Stephen woke with a gasp, the moonlight flooding the passage, and rushed to the bathroom door. It was empty. Yet, the memory burned.

The following morning, Mrs. Bunch replaced the curtain, her face grave. At breakfast, Abney listened intently to Stephen's dream, making notes in his book with a scholarly detachment that unsettled the boy. As the spring equinox approached, Abney warned Stephen of the critical nature of the season, citing Censorinus and urging him to keep his windows shut.

Then, the marks appeared. One Friday night in March 1812, Stephen woke to find his nightgown torn with long, parallel slits on the left side of his chest. He pointed them out to Mrs. Bunch, noting they matched the scratches on his bedroom door. "Too high for a cat," she whispered, her voice trembling. "Like a Chinaman's fingernails." She advised him to lock his door, a precaution that did little to soothe the dread coiling in his stomach.

The atmosphere grew heavier. Mr. Parkes, the butler, who usually kept to his pantry, arrived flustered, claiming the wine cellar was infested with rats—or worse, speaking rats. He urged Stephen to listen at the bins, a tale Stephen dismissed as nonsense, though the hairs on the back of his neck stood erect.

March 24, 1812. The wind howled through the grounds, carrying the sensation of an endless procession of unseen spirits, desperate and aimless. After lunch, Abney summoned Stephen to his study at eleven o'clock, promising a revelation concerning Stephen's future. The library was prepared: a brazier burning bright, a silver cup filled with red wine, and papers scattered on the table. As Stephen entered, Abney sprinkled incense, his movements ritualistic.

Outside, the night was still, save for the eerie cries drifting from the woods—sounds neither owl nor bird, but the lamentations of the lost. Then, Stephen saw them. Standing on the gravel terrace were two figures: a girl and a boy. The girl smiled, her hands clasped over her heart, mirroring the specter from his dream. The boy, thin and ragged, raised his arms in a gesture of hungry menace. The moonlight illuminated his transparent hands, revealing nails so long they seemed to glow. On his chest, a black, gaping rent opened, and a desolate cry tore through the silence. They vanished as quickly as they had appeared.

Terror seized Stephen. He grabbed his candle and raced to the study. The door was unlocked, but Abney was inside, speaking to someone unseen. Stephen tried to scream, but the cry died in his throat. He pushed the door open.

The scene was a tableau of horror. Papers lay scattered, detailing Abney's experiments in spiritual ascension through the absorption of human souls. The text spoke of Simon Magus and the absorption of three hearts to gain dominion over elemental forces. It listed the first victim, Phoebe Stanley, and the second, Giovanni Paoli. The final entry was stark: "The final 'victim'... must be my cousin, Stephen Elliott. His day must be this March 24, 1812." The instructions were chilling: remove the heart from the living, reduce it to ashes, and mix it with red wine.

Abney sat in his chair, head thrown back, his face a mask of rage and mortal agony. A terrible wound lacerated his left side, exposing his heart. No blood stained his hands; a clean knife lay on the table. The coroner would later rule it a wild animal attack, but Stephen, reading the papers, understood the truth. The boy who had arrived in September was now dead, his heart claimed by the man who had welcomed him with open arms.
