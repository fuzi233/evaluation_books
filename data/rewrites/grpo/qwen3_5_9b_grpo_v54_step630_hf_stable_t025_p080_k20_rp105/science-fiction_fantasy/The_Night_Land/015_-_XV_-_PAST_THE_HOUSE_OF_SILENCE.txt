By the tenth hour of that day, we launched our raft. The paddles moved with surprising ease, balanced perfectly by the rests I had rigged, allowing the vessel to glide forward with minimal exertion. I took the rear oar while the Maid manned the bow, and together we pushed steadily, achieving a speed that surpassed our walking pace across the rugged terrain.

Around the twelfth hour, we paused for food and drink before resuming our labor. The motion became almost rhythmic, a gentle rocking of our feet rather than strenuous rowing. Hours drifted by in a constant, soft conversation. The Maid frequently glanced back at me with affection, pressing her lips together in a silent invitation for me to join her, yet she always shook her head gently when I attempted to leave my paddle to walk toward her.

At the eighteenth hour, we pulled in our paddles. The Maid carefully arranged her cloak to serve as our bedding. After eating and drinking, we fell into a sound, happy slumber instantly. Eight hours later, we awoke simultaneously. For a fleeting moment, we were disoriented, unsure of our location, but quickly realized we were safe and unharmed by anything during our sleep. We laughed at each other's faces, overwhelmed by the joy of waking together. After a brief embrace, we washed in the sea water and ate. We resumed paddling, proceeding along the coast peacefully and contentedly for the rest of the day.

In total, this voyage consumed four full days of twenty-four-hour cycles. We made no great haste, moving slowly enough for me to regain my strength. Nothing occurred during this time except for one instance where we spotted a large beast emerging clumsily from the sea onto the shore to graze on the vegetation. Though we were too distant to be certain, it appeared entirely natural, lacking any monstrous odor or aura that would disturb our spirits. This creature, like all fauna in that region, seemed reminiscent of the Early World. It suggested that extreme conditions can breed primal forms, perhaps driven by circumstance or a deeper spiritual force inherent to life itself. Whether this force is the cause of life or a result of evolution, it remains distinct; where untainted, it defines humanity. Even if man had once been a fish, his spirit would remain fundamentally human, merely modified physically by necessity. I hold no walls against reason, accepting facts while remaining open to the possibility that man has always existed in his current form, perhaps brutish but possessing the same core spirit.

I do not speak of what lies beyond our current life, for I harbor a wondrous hope for the beautiful things awaiting us in that Glad World we glimpsed while standing in holiness with the Beloved.

Returning to the narrative, another notable feature of our sea journey was a great Fire-Hill rising from the ocean. The sea boiled around it, punctuated by a dozen massive jets roaring upward. Strange grunts emanated from the base, likely caused by bursting gases, signaling the immense energy awakening in that deep country. We lingered in awe, staring back at the Hill of Fire and Force long after passing it.

Beyond this, the only concern arose as we approached where the Great Sea fractured into smaller seas, though passages of water connected them, allowing our passage. I showed the Maid the two locations where I had slept upon my initial arrival; she listened with sweet interest, eager to learn everything.

After four days on the water, we reached the shore at a flat place where the land sloped upward toward the mouth of the First Gorge. It was the tenth hour, mirroring the start of our voyage. It had been a sweet, peaceful journey, and I felt fortunate that what remained of our path held such pleasantness and safety. Yet, we knew danger awaited. Our hearts were set in courage, determined to conquer. If we succeeded in reaching our Mighty Home safely, we would spend our lives together in loveliness—a worthy prize to repay our suffering.

We beached the raft as best we could, wondering if anyone would ever see it again through eternity. We exchanged a glance, and the Maid cut a small piece of wood from the raft as a memento. Gathering our gear on the shore, she helped me don my armor. I strapped the Scrip and Pouch to my back, gripped the Diskos, and prepared for action. She adjusted her belt, her knife ready, her bundle now small.

Then, the Maid knelt and kissed the raft. Memories gathered upon her; she was breaking from the first part of her life. Her heart stirred with a strange, sorrowful trouble. I stooped gently, lifting her to her feet, and led her from the raft. She needed to stay close, and so we moved forward toward the dark mouth of the Great Gorge.

To our right stood the grim, utter-huge Mountain, perched upon which were the four fire-hills I had described. Below them ascended great hills of ash cast down throughout eternity. The Maid gazed upon this wonder for a long time, never ceasing her amazement, nor did I, nor any human ever should.

We climbed to the high mouth of the Gorge and ventured into the gloom until we reached the point where the gorge bent sharply to the left, plunging into darkness. Here, we paused and turned back for a final look at the Country of Seas, the Deep and living Land hidden far downward in the everlasting night.

It was a solemn realization that we might be the last old humans to view that country. I wondered if the Humpt Men would ever evolve, in some far eternity, to the full sweetness of the human spirit I believed lay within them. It was an odd yet natural thought. That land seemed like the Olden Time renewed, viewed through new eyes.

We looked awhile in silence, listening to the far mutter of the Great Fire-Mountains and the noise of life over that land. We were but a few paces from the silence of the Great Gorge, which would lead us toward the Eternal Strangeness of the Night Land. The Maid held my arm tightly as we looked our last into the red light of that Deep and Hid Country, where we had come so utterly near to death.

Presently, I turned, and the Maid slipped her hand into mine. Tears flowed silently down her face, pressed by the weight of her heart. Yet, amidst the sorrow, there was happiness and a vague emotion of loss for the dear island where she had nursed me back to life. She remembered the places where her memory would wander in afterlife, perhaps telling tales to children of a country they would never see, yet forever wonder at.

We passed the Mighty Corner-place of the Gorge and stumbled forward into the gloom.

For sixteen hours, we walked steadily, troubled only by the great darkness. By then, we had gone twenty-six hours without sleep—a foolishness, as I needed my full strength before reaching the Night Land. The Maid had spoken against this, and indeed, we soon found a safe place to rest. Eating and drinking, we calculated our progress and decided to limit our daily journey through the Gorge to sixteen hours, sleeping eight hours each night. We adhered to this schedule until we emerged from the Great Gorge, a journey taking five days in total.

Once in the light places of the Gorge, we were more cheered, though often half-smothered by the horrid gases rising from various points. My strength grew constant as we journeyed. The Maid refused to be carried, moving lightly and cleverly, accustomed to this constant wandering.

At intervals, I stopped to show her the places where I had slept, and she insisted on standing where I had lain so lonely in my despairful search. Always tender with me, she was often speechless due to the calling of her heart.

She constantly asked when we would reach the Night Land, how far it was, and expressed growing excitement. I felt almost as eager as she, wondering what she thought of the Mighty Redoubt and the strange, monstrous land. Above all, I was shaken to my heart by the thought of bringing the Maid swiftly to safety, lest woe befall us despite our progress. This made it difficult to rush beyond our set hours, but we possessed the wisdom to sleep after the sixteenth hour.

We saw no life in that great, desolate Gorge—only gas-burnings, boulders, stark rocks, and the rank smell of gases. There was an utter, everlasting quiet, broken only by the occasional moan or whistle of a lone gas-fire, sounding drearily across the waste and heightening the loneliness in our hearts.

The Maid always thought in her heart that I had come through that place alone to search for her into the unknown lands of the world. Surely, I was a natural man to feel happy that she pondered and remembered this; thereby, her love seemed ever to grow. A man is glad in his spirit and natural pride when his Maid knows he has done wholesomely for her need. Think of the love-days and the echoes of those dear, proud thoughts swelling in you; does not all go strangely with familiar pain in the old way?

On the fifth day, around the seventh hour, I heard odd sounds in the gorge, as if the rocks themselves were making hushed, strange noises at us. I brought the Maid instantly close, took the Diskos into my hand, and we proceeded with great caution.

We passed three places where gas-fires burned and danced, emitting low moans and occasional whistles. Other strange sounds came from the rocks, unfamiliar yet somehow familiar. Suddenly, I realized there was a far-away noise within these sounds, though they seemed to originate near my elbow. I knew I was hearing little echoings caught by the near rocks, originating from some mighty, distant sound. This must be the monstrous piping of the Great Gas Fountain, which you will recall. I told the Maid immediately; she was eager, for this was both a wondrous thing and a landmark indicating we were nearing the end of the Gorge.

We looked ahead earnestly. So many strange, leaping fires were before us that we could not be sure which was the far, monstrous dance of the Great Gas Fountain. It was so distant that the near gas-fires dominated the weary length of the Gorge visually, overshadowing the great dance of the far-off fire, which appeared small due to distance.

Soon, we saw a lightening and darkening far along the Gorge, causing the background of the night to lose some intensity, as if with constant shudders of light. This was surely the distant dance of the Great Gas Fountain's flame. We watched as we journeyed, seeing the vague shuddering of light grow in the distance, merging into a strange, uplifting and falling blue flame.

The sound became steadier, growing over a long while into a monstrous piping, great and wonderful, with a constant change in note.

We passed the last of the lesser fires and entered the fireless part of the Gorge, save for the great upward dance of the Gas Fountain, now grown huge and plainly seen, casting a quaking light over the entire Gorge.

In the end, we came very near to the dance of the monstrous flame. We were half-stunned by the noise, now an utter, furious roar. The Maid and I stood as two lone strangers in the mouth of that deep, desolate Gorge, staring voiceless at the great flaw. My arm was about the Maid; she stood very near me. Neither spoke; how could we, given the sheer magnitude of the noise?

After staring for a great while, we turned to look at each other and kissed very soberly in the light of the monster flame. We stared at the Flame again, then turned to look all ways, marveling at the great throw of the light as it went blue, spreading, and strange to great distances.

For a while, we watched how the far-off side of the Gorge came into plain sight when the Flame leaped. Truly, that seemed a far, lone place, like a lost and forgotten world of desolate mountains.

We looked to see the way our journey would go. Nothing was clearly shown save when the Flame rose to a monstrous height, revealing the huge rocks surrounding it. Yet, I was able to show the Maid the bottom part of the Mighty and Utter Monster Slope, the last leg of our journey before reaching the Night Land.

We proceeded for about a mile to avoid being deafened by the noise of the Gas Fountain. It was now beyond the seventeenth hour, so we ate, drank, and rested in a secure place among the great boulders.

When we woke, we ate and drank again, gazing silently at the Flame dancing monstrously, lonesomely, surrounded by stark, mighty rocks like giants of silence watching forever. Soon, we geared up and moved toward the utter dark of the Mighty Slope, beginning the huge climb that would last through days in an eternity of night.

Often in the first hours, we turned from our blind stumblings to gaze downward from the long height at the loom of the Flame, shuddering far below in the night, casting a quaking light into that far darkness. We left it to dance forever in that deep, lost place, bending all our will and strength to the climb.

Thus, we stumbled for sixteen great hours, reaching a pace proper for the task, feeling somewhat numbed and unreal due to the effect of the Darkness.

For eight days, we went upward forever through that most dreadful night. After the first day, we crept on hands and knees. I went in front, the Diskos ready on my hip. I took two straps from the pouch and scrip, creating a certain length, and attached them from the Maid's waist-belt to my own, ensuring she followed closely.

We made sixteen-hour journeys, eating and drinking at the sixth and twelfth hours, as well as before sleep and upon waking. Our slumber lasted about eight good hours, so that I might preserve our strength for the dreadfulness of the journey yet to come across the fear and horrid terror of the Night Land.

Often, I was utterly sickened and weary of reaching forward and upward forever, making blind fumbles to find a way past great boulders, rocks, and holes in our path in the dark. It seemed we were lost from all life and knowing in a blackness that would never slacken.

I would pause and call softly to the Maid, asking her to creep up near me. Then I would take her into my arms out of the utter blackness of that night, giving and receiving comfort.
