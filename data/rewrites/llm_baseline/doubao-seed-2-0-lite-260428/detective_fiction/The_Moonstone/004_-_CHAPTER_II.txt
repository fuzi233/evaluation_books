A page or two back, I made mention of my lady. It stands to reason the cursed Moonstone could never have ended up in our house to be lost if it hadn’t been gifted to my lady’s daughter, Miss Rachel—and Miss Rachel never would’ve drawn breath to receive that accursed present if my lady herself hadn’t laboured and brought her into the world. So starting our story with my lady means we’ve gone back far enough to not miss any loose threads, and let me tell you, when you’ve got a messy, tangled job like this mystery on your hands, that’s a real comfort right at the start.

If you know anything of London’s fashionable set, you’ve surely heard tell of the three beautiful Miss Herncastles: Adelaide, Caroline, and Julia—the youngest, and by far the best of the three, in my humble opinion, and I’ve got 50 odd years of acquaintance to back that judgement up. I entered the service of their old father, Lord Herncastle, when I was fifteen, hired on as pageboy to wait on the three young ladies. Thank the Good Lord we’ve no business with that old hothead in the Moonstone affair—he had the sharpest tongue and shortest temper of any man I ever met, high or low. I stayed on in that household till the day Julia married the late Sir John Verinder.

Sir John was an excellent, soft-hearted man who only needed someone sensible to steer him, and between us, he found exactly that in my lady. He thrived under her care, grew plump, happy, and died peacefully, every step from the day she walked down the aisle with him to the day she closed his eyes for the last. Of course I went with the new bride down to Sir John’s country estate. “Sir John,” she told him straight, “I can’t do without Gabriel Betteredge.” “My lady,” he laughed back, “neither can I.” That was how he was with her, and that’s how I came to be settled at the Verinder Park for good. I didn’t care where I went so long as my mistress and I were in the same household.

Seeing how my lady took a great interest in the estate farms and outdoor work, I did too—all the more so because I was the seventh son of a small farmer myself. She got me apprenticed to the head bailiff, I worked hard, pleased everyone, and climbed the ranks fast. Some years later, one Monday my lady said, “Sir John, your bailiff is a dull old fool. Pension him generously, and let Gabriel take his post.” Come Tuesday, Sir John came and found me to say the deed was done. You hear endless tales of miserable, bickering married couples, but these two were the perfect opposite. Let that be a warning to the quarrelsome, and encouragement to anyone thinking of tying the knot.

Well, you’d say I was living in clover then, wouldn’t you? A position of trust and respect, my own little cottage to live in, rounds of the estate to keep me busy in the mornings, accounts to sort through in the afternoons, and my pipe and my tattered, dog-eared copy of *Robinson Crusoe* for the evenings. What more could a man want? Remember what Adam pined for when he was alone in Eden, and if you don’t blame him for it, don’t blame me either.

The woman I set my sights on was Selina Goby, the housekeeper I’d hired to tend my cottage. I agree entirely with the late William Cobbett’s rule for picking a wife: make sure she chews her food slow and plants her feet firm on the ground when she walks, and you’ve no troubles ahead. Selina checked both those boxes, which was one reason to marry her. I had another, entirely my own discovery: as a single housekeeper, she charged me a weekly wage for her board and services. As my wife, she couldn’t charge me a penny. Pure economy, with a little dash of fondness thrown in. I told my mistress the whole plan exactly as I’d thought it out. “I’ve turned Selina Goby over in my mind,” I said, “and it’ll work out far cheaper to marry her than keep her on as hired help.”

My lady burst out laughing so hard she had to sit down, saying she didn’t know whether to be more shocked at my blunt language or my unromantic principles. I didn’t understand the fine joke of it, only that she didn’t forbid the match, so I went straight to Selina and proposed. What did she say? Lord, you don’t know women at all if you even need to ask. Of course she said yes.

A week before the wedding, once people started talking about making me a new suit for the ceremony, the jitters hit me. I’ve talked to plenty of other men in my position, and every last one of them admitted that a week out from the wedding, they secretly wished they could wriggle out of it. I went further: I actually tried to get out of it. Not that I was unfair! The laws of England say a man has to compensate a woman for breaking a marriage bargain, and I followed them to the letter. I offered Selina a brand new feather bed and fifty shillings to call the whole thing off. You’ll hardly believe it, but the silly fool turned me down flat.

After that, there was no getting out. I bought the new suit as cheaply as I could, and got through the rest of the ceremony and married life as frugally as possible. We weren’t a happy couple, and we weren’t a miserable couple. We were six of one and half a dozen of the other. We kept getting in each other’s way, no matter how hard we tried not to: if I wanted to go up the stairs, Selina was coming down, and if she wanted to go down, I was tramping up. That’s married life, as far as my experience goes. After five years of constant stairwell run-ins, a merciful Providence took Selina from me, leaving me with our little girl Penelope and no other children. Not long after that, Sir John died too, leaving my lady with her little daughter Rachel, and no other children. If you know anything of my lady, you won’t be surprised to hear Penelope was raised right under her own eye, sent to school, taught all sorts of useful skills, and promoted to be Miss Rachel’s own lady’s maid the second she was old enough.

I kept on as head bailiff, tramping the estate every day, right up to Christmas 1847, when everything changed. My lady turned up at my cottage one evening to take tea with me alone. She pointed out that counting from the day I started as a 15 year old pageboy for old Lord Herncastle, I’d served her family for more than fifty years, and pressed a beautiful hand-knit wool waistcoat into my hands, stitched all by herself, to keep me warm through the harsh winter. I was half choked up with gratitude, till I realized the waistcoat wasn’t an honour at all—it was a bribe. She’d noticed I was growing old before I’d noticed it myself, and had come to wheedle me into giving up the hard outdoor tramping of the bailiff’s job, to take the soft indoor post of house steward, and take it easy for the rest of my days.

I fought back as hard as I could against the indignity of being “retired” while I still thought I was perfectly fit for the farm rounds. But my lady knew my soft spot: she framed it not as a favour to myself, but as a favour to her. After that, I was done for. I wiped my streaming eyes on that brand new wool waistcoat like a daft old man, and said I’d think on it.

Once she was gone, my mind was all in a tangle, so I turned to the remedy I’ve never known fail me in any emergency: I lit my pipe, and flipped open *Robinson Crusoe*. Before I’d read five pages, I landed on the most comforting line I could have asked for, on page 158: “Today we love, what tomorrow we hate.” The path cleared right up for me. Today, I hated the very thought of giving up my bailiff rounds. Tomorrow, on Crusoe’s authority, I’d be all for the soft steward’s post. I’d just grab tomorrow’s frame of mind when it came, and the whole thing would be settled easy. That night I fell asleep the Verinder’s farm bailiff, and woke up the next morning their new house steward. No fuss, no regrets—all thanks to *Robinson Crusoe*.

Just now, my daughter Penelope peeked over my shoulder to read what I’ve written so far. She says it’s all beautifully told, every word true. But she’s raised a very valid objection: I was asked to tell the story of the Moonstone, and so far I’ve told nothing but the story of myself. Curious, isn’t it? I can’t account for it. I wonder if those fancy gentlemen who make their living writing books ever find their own silly selves getting in the way of their grand important subjects, the way I just did? If they do, I feel for them.

Well, that’s another false start, and a whole lot of good writing paper wasted. There’s nothing for it, far as I can see, except for you lot to keep your tempers, and for me to start all over again for the third time. This time, I swear, I’ll get to the Diamond.
