Let it be known to all students of heraldry and aristocratic lineage that the noble family from which Helen Pendennis hailed bore a rather peculiar crest: a mother pelican, generously bleeding into the beaks of her ravenous offspring. A touching allegory, no doubt, though one might question the life choices of a bird willing to be pecked to death by its own children. But such is the way of mothers—human ones, at least—who seem to derive some inexplicable joy from sacrificing themselves for their wayward brood. Whether it’s a reckless husband, a dissipated son, or a particularly troublesome brother, they’ll gladly bleed themselves dry if it means keeping their beloved reprobates afloat. And let’s be honest, gentlemen, how many of us have indulged them in this pastime? Who hasn’t, at some point, bestowed upon his female relations the exquisite pleasure of forgiving him?

Now, our young Arthur Pendennis had, until recently, been the pride of his uncle Major Pendennis—a man not ordinarily prone to pride unless it reflected well upon himself. The Major had watched with satisfaction as Arthur strolled through Oxbridge in the company of young lords, basking in borrowed nobility. He had even gone so far as to host modest dinners for these illustrious youths, serving up his finest claret and his most polished anecdotes (some unfit for repetition, others simply unfit). The Major, who had never been accused of excessive generosity, found himself playing the lavish host, all for the sake of Arthur’s social advancement.

But alas! The boy had squandered it all—his prospects, his mother’s money, his uncle’s pride—in a spectacular display of idleness and extravagance. And now, having failed his examinations with a resounding thud, Arthur found himself in the unenviable position of a man who has publicly tripped over his own feet. The shame! The humiliation! The sheer melodrama of it all!

Convinced that all of England would soon be whispering his disgrace, Pen fled the scene like a tragic hero—or, more accurately, like a man who had just realized he’d left the stove on. He wandered the countryside in a daze, his academic gown flapping pathetically in the January rain, until he was scooped up by an unlikely savior: his old acquaintance Spavin, who had, against all odds, managed to scrape through his own exams.

“Plucked, are you?” said Spavin cheerfully. “Happens to the best of us! I’ve been plucked three times myself. You’ll get used to it.”

Pen, who had not considered failure something one could simply *get used to*, stared at Spavin with the hollow-eyed gaze of a man who has just been told to cheer up after losing his hat, his wallet, and his dignity in quick succession. Nevertheless, he allowed himself to be bundled into Spavin’s garish postchaise, where he was deposited at a coaching inn to catch the mail to London.

Once in the city, he skulked about like a man expecting to be recognized at every turn—though in truth, London had far more pressing scandals to discuss than one plucked university student. He endured a painful interview with his uncle, who alternated between outrage at the debts and bewilderment at the concept of academic failure.

“*Plucked?*” the Major repeated, aghast. “Is this some barbaric university ritual? Were you publicly stripped? Flogged?”

Pen, momentarily distracted from his despair, had to explain that no, it simply meant he had failed his exams. The Major, somewhat relieved that his nephew hadn’t been subjected to medieval torture, swiftly moved on to the more pressing matter of the bills—seven hundred pounds’ worth of them.

“You’ve managed to spend a small fortune and have nothing to show for it but a collection of IOUs,” the Major observed dryly. “A talent, I suppose, though not one I’d recommend cultivating further.”

Pen, in a fit of remorse, declared he would confess everything to his mother but would ask her for no more money—a noble sentiment, albeit one that ignored the rather glaring issue of how he intended to live without funds.

After a few miserable days of pacing London’s streets, dodging acquaintances, and wallowing in self-pity, Pen received two letters: one from his tutor, urging him not to abandon his studies, and another from home—a letter that reduced him to his knees in penitent tears. And so, with a heart full of remorse and a stomach full of breakfast (for even the most dramatic crises must yield to hunger), Arthur Pendennis boarded the coach back to Chatteris, ready to face the music.

Or at least, ready to sit very quietly while others played it for him.
