It had been roughly ten months since we last encountered him, yet that span of time had wrought an alteration akin to years upon his visage. He had wasted away; a heavy cloak of gloom and anxious foreboding had supplanted the cordial serenity that once defined his countenance. His dark blue eyes, perpetually sharp and penetrating, now burned with a sterner, harder light beneath his shaggy grey brows. This was no transformation born merely of sorrow; darker, angrier passions seemed to have carved these lines into his face.

We had not driven far before the General began to speak, employing his customary soldier's directness to discuss the "bereavement"—as he termed it—that he had suffered through the death of his beloved niece and ward. Suddenly, his composure shattered. In a tone of intense bitterness and fury, he launched into an invective against the "hellish arts" to which she had succumbed. With more exasperation than piety, he lamented how Heaven could possibly tolerate such a monstrous indulgence of the lusts and malignity of hell itself.

My father, perceiving immediately that something profoundly extraordinary had befallen the man, asked him, if the pain allowed, to detail the circumstances that warranted such vehement language.

"I should recount it all with pleasure," the General replied, "but you would not believe me."

"Why not?" my father pressed gently.

"Because," he answered testily, "you believe in nothing save what conforms to your own prejudices and illusions. I recall when I was like you, but I have learned better."

"Test me," my father said calmly. "I am no such dogmatist as you imagine. Furthermore, I know full well that you generally demand proof for your convictions; thus, I am strongly predisposed to respect your conclusions."

"You are correct in assuming I have not been led lightly into a belief in the marvelous—for what I have witnessed *is* marvelous—and I have been compelled by extraordinary evidence to credit that which runs diametrically opposed to every theory I hold. I have been made the dupe of a preternatural conspiracy."

Despite his confident assertions regarding the General's insight, I observed my father cast a glance toward him that, in my opinion, carried a marked suspicion of his sanity. Fortunately, the General failed to notice it. He stared gloomily and curiously into the glades and vistas of the woods unfolding before our carriage.

"You are heading to the Ruins of Karnstein?" he asked abruptly.

"Yes, it seems a fortunate coincidence," my father replied. "In fact, I was just about to request that you accompany me to inspect them. I have a specific objective in mind. Is there not a ruined chapel there, surrounded by numerous tombs belonging to that extinct family?"

"There are indeed—highly interesting sites," my father confirmed. "Do you intend to claim the title and estates?"

My father spoke this gaily, but the General appeared oblivious to the jest, failing to register the polite smile or chuckle that courtesy demands of a friend. Instead, he looked grave, bordering on fierce, lost in rumination over a matter that stirred both his rage and his horror.

"Something vastly different," he growled. "I intend to unearth some of those fine people. May God's blessing guide me, I aim to accomplish a pious sacrilege here, one that shall rid our earth of certain monsters and allow honest folk to sleep undisturbed in their beds, free from the threat of murderers. I possess strange tales to tell you, my dear friend, stories that I myself would have scoffed at as impossible mere months ago."

My father studied him again, but this time not with suspicion, but with an expression of keen intelligence and deep alarm.

"The House of Karnstein," my father stated firmly, "has been extinct for generations—a century at least. My late wife was maternally descended from the Karnsteins, yet the name and title have vanished long ago. The castle lies in ruins; the very village is deserted. It has been fifty years since a single wisp of smoke rose from its chimneys; not a roof remains standing."

"That is entirely accurate," the General conceded. "I have heard much about this since our last meeting; much that will astonish you. However, I believe it best to relate everything in the chronological order of events," he continued. "You saw my dear ward—my child, if I may call her. No creature could ever have been more beautiful, and only three months prior, none more blooming."

"Yes, poor soul!" my father exclaimed. "When I last saw her, she was certainly quite lovely. I was grieved and shocked beyond words, my dear friend; I knew precisely what a devastating blow this must have been to you."

He took the General's hand, and they exchanged a warm, sympathetic pressure. Tears welled in the old soldier's eyes, unashamed and unrestrained.

"We have been very old friends," the General whispered, his voice thick with emotion. "I knew you would feel for me, childless as I am. She had become an object of profound interest to me, and she repaid my care with an affection that brightened my home and made my life happy. All of that is gone. The years remaining to me on this earth may not be many; but by God's mercy, I hope to accomplish a service to mankind before I pass, and to subserve the vengeance of Heaven upon the fiends who murdered my poor child in the spring of her hopes and beauty!"

"You mentioned earlier that you intended to relate everything exactly as it occurred," my father reminded him softly. "Please, go on; I assure you, it is not mere curiosity that drives me."

By this time, we had arrived at the junction where the Drunstall road, by which the General had approached, diverged from the path leading toward Karnstein.

"How far is it to the ruins?" the General inquired, peering anxiously ahead.

"Approximately half a league," my father answered.

"Then pray, let us hear the story you were so gracious as to promise."
