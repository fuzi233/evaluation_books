1

It wasn’t until the following Friday that Sally could finally make her way to Detroit. She arrived on Saturday morning and, after a brief ride in a cab that felt like it had been driven by someone who’d just lost a bet, checked into the Hotel Statler. After confirming Gerald was indeed staying there (a fact she verified with a phone call to his room), she decided to take charge of her own destiny and ordered breakfast.

She sat at a corner table, staring blankly at the menu like it held the secrets of the universe—or at least the location of decent coffee. Her mood was low. Nursing Mr. Faucitt through what amounted to a mild case of pneumonia had left her emotionally drained and physically resembling a dishrag that had seen better days. The train ride hadn’t helped—she’d spent most of it trying not to fall asleep while simultaneously avoiding eye contact with anyone who looked remotely interested in conversation. But the real reason for her melancholy? Gerald’s voice on the phone.

He’d sounded… flat. Like a balloon that had been stepped on by a very unenthusiastic person. His greeting had been as lively as a damp firecracker. “Oh, hi,” he’d said, without even a hint of surprise. As if her return from Europe—which had taken her three weeks to complete, mind you—was merely an inconvenience rather than a triumph of modern travel and personal resilience. It stung. Not because she needed dramatic welcomes, but because she’d hoped for something… warmer. Something that didn’t sound like he was reading from a script written by a depressed squirrel.

A cup of coffee, however, worked wonders. Men were always like this in the morning, she reminded herself. They emerged from their rooms like zombies who’d forgotten how to smile. But surely, once he’d endured the ritual of cold showers and existential dread, Gerald would be back to normal—energetic, charming, possibly even slightly flirtatious. In the meantime, food was food, and she needed it like a drowning woman needs a life preserver made entirely of pancakes.

She was pouring her second cup when a large, round-faced young man entered the dining room. He’d caught her attention earlier, lurking near the lobby like a confused penguin who’d wandered too far from home. Now, as he scanned the room with the intensity of a man searching for a missing tooth, she realized with a start: it was Fillmore.

Sally blinked. What on earth was *he* doing here? She’d assumed he was permanently ensconced in New York, living off the proceeds of minor stock transactions and the occasional well-placed yawn. But then again, men of affairs—especially those whose affairs seemed to consist mostly of lunches and vague promises—tended to flit about like moths drawn to neon lights. Still, here he was, and she called out, “Fillmore!”

He froze mid-scan, turned slowly, and saw her. His face registered a mix of shock, relief, and something vaguely resembling guilt. Sally thought: *Ah yes. The guilty conscience. I see it now.* He must have been planning to break the news that he’d secretly become engaged to someone named Winch (or possibly Wench, though that seemed less likely) without consulting her first. And now he was trapped between the desire to confess and the fear of being disowned by his sister.

“Why, Sally!” he exclaimed, stepping forward. “What are you doing here? I thought you were still in Europe.”

“I got back a week ago,” she said, “but I’ve been nursing poor old Mr. Faucitt ever since. He’s been ill—poor dear. I came here to see Mr. Foster’s play, *The Primrose Way*, you know. Is it a success?”

“It hasn’t opened yet.”

“Oh, don’t be ridiculous, Fill. Pull yourself together. It opened last Monday.”

“No, it didn’t. Haven’t you heard? They’ve closed all the theatres because of this infernal Spanish influenza. Nothing’s playing this week. You must’ve seen it in the papers.”

“I haven’t had time to read the papers. Oh, Fill, what an awful shame!”

“Yes, it’s pretty tough. Makes the company all on edge. I’ve had the darndest time, I can tell you.”

“Well, what have you got to do with it?”

Fillmore coughed. “I—er—oh, I didn’t tell you that. I’m sort of—er—mixed up in the show. Cracknell—you remember him? College buddy?—suggested I come down and look at it. Might want me to put money into it or something.”

“You thought he had all the money in the world.”

“He does. But these guys like to let a pal in on a good thing.”

“Is it a good thing?”

“The play’s fine.”

“That’s what Mr. Faucitt said. But Mabel Hobson…”

Fillmore’s face darkened. “She’s an awful woman, Sally! Can’t act, throws her weight around like a spoiled child. The other day there was a fuss about a paper-knife…”

“A fuss about a paper-knife?”

“One of the props. It got mislaid. I’m certain it wasn’t my fault…”

“How could it have been your fault?” asked Sally, bewildered. Love clearly had no positive effects on Fillmore’s mental faculties.

“Well—er—you know how it is. Angry woman… blames the first person she sees… This paper-knife…”

His voice trailed off into silence, as if he’d suddenly remembered something deeply shameful.

“Mr. Faucitt said Elsa Doland was good.”

“Oh, she’s all right,” said Fillmore dismissively. Then his eyes lit up. “But—the girl you want to watch is Miss Winch. Gladys Winch. She plays the maid. Only in the first act, doesn’t say much—just ‘Did you ring, madam?’ and stuff like that. But it’s the way she says it! Sally, that girl’s a genius! The greatest character actress in a dozen years! You mark my words—in a darned little while, you’ll see her name up on Broadway in electric light. Personality? Ask me! Charm? She wrote the words and music! Looks?…”

“All right! All right! I know all about it, Fill. And will you kindly explain how you dared get engaged without consulting me?”

Fillmore blushed furiously. “Oh, do you know?”

“Yes. Mr. Faucitt told me.”

“Well…”

“Well?”

“Well, I’m only human,” he argued weakly.

“I call that a very handsome admission. You’ve got quite modest, Fill.”

He had certainly changed since their last meeting. It was as if someone had punctured him and let out all the pomposity. If this transformation was due, as Mr. Faucitt had suggested, to the influence of Miss Winch, Sally couldn’t help but approve. Romance, especially when it involved a woman who chewed gum during rehearsals, seemed like a step in the right direction.

“I’ll introduce you sometime,” said Fillmore.

“I want to meet her very much.”

“I’ve got to go now. I’ve got to see Bunbury. Thought he might be in here.”

“Who’s Bunbury?”

“The producer. Probably breakfasting in his room. I’d better go up.”

“You’re busy, aren’t you? Little marvel! It’s lucky they’ve got you to look after them.”

Fillmore retreated, and Sally settled back into her chair, no longer offended by Gerald’s earlier tone. Poor Gerald! No wonder he’d seemed upset.

A few minutes later, he arrived.

“Oh, Jerry darling,” said Sally, “I’m so sorry. I’ve just been hearing about it.”

Gerald sat down. His appearance matched his voice perfectly—a man wrapped in a shroud of nervous dullness. He looked like someone who’d just discovered that the entire world was running on a faulty clock.

“It’s just my luck,” he muttered gloomily. “The kind of thing that couldn’t happen to anyone but me. Damned fools! Where’s the sense in shutting the theatres, even if there is influenza? They let people jam against each other all day in stores. If that doesn’t hurt them, why should going to the theatre?”

“It’s not that,” said Sally. “Poor Mr. Faucitt had it badly. That’s why I couldn’t come earlier.”

Gerald didn’t seem to care. He dug his spoon into his grapefruit like it had personally insulted him.

“We’ve been hanging around here day after day, getting bored to death. The company’s falling apart. They’re sick of rehearsing when nobody knows if we’ll ever open. They were all excited a week ago, and now they’re sagging. It’ll ruin the play, of course. My first chance! Just chucked away.”

Sally listened, feeling increasingly desolate. She tried to be fair—surely he was under immense pressure—but self-pity was one of her least favorite traits in men. And then there was the matter of her own pride. Clearly, her arrival had done nothing to lift his spirits. She couldn’t help remembering what Mr. Faucitt had said about Gerald. He was remarkably self-centered—and now he was proving it.

“That Hobson woman is beginning to make trouble,” Gerald continued, stabbing at his scrambled eggs. “She shouldn’t have gotten the part. Elsa Doland could play it a thousand times better. I wrote Elsa in a few lines the other day, and Hobson went ballistic. You don’t know what a star is until you’ve seen one of these promoted clothes-props from the Follies trying to be one. Took me an hour to talk her down.”

“Why not let her throw up her part?”

“For heaven’s sake, talk sense,” Gerald snapped. “Do you think Cracknell would keep the play going without her? He’d close it in a second. And where would I be then? You don’t realize this is a big chance for me. I’d look like a fool throwing it away.”

“I see,” said Sally, tersely. She’d never felt so wretched in her life. Foreign travel, she decided, was a mistake. It might broaden the mind, but it also left you utterly disconnected from everyone you knew. She realized what she was really resenting: Gerald was trying to have it both ways. A man in crisis could either be noble and stoic, or broken and in need of comfort. Gerald wanted both—he demanded sympathy while simultaneously pushing it away. It was like offering someone a sandwich and then refusing to let them eat it.

“By the way,” said Gerald, “there’s one thing. I have to keep her jollying along, so for goodness’ sake don’t go letting it out that we’re engaged.”

Sally’s chin shot up. That was too much.

“If you find it a handicap being engaged to me…”

“Don’t be silly,” Gerald said, resorting to pathos. “Good God! It’s tough! Here I am, worried to death, and you…”

Before he could finish, Sally’s mood shifted—swiftly, dramatically, like a switch flipped by a particularly vindictive electrician. A simple thought struck her: maybe it wasn’t Gerald who’d changed. Maybe it was her. She’d just come off the train—tired, grubby, hair in disarray. Of course he wouldn’t be thrilled. She felt instantly calmer. If her appearance was the problem, all was well.

She reached across the table and took his hand.

“I’m so sorry,” she said. “I’ve been a brute, but I do sympathize, really.”

“I’ve had an awful time,” mumbled Gerald.

“I know, I know. But you never told me you were glad to see me.”

“Of course I’m glad to see you.”

“Why didn’t you say so, then, you poor fish? And why didn’t you ask me if I enjoyed myself in Europe?”

“Did you enjoy yourself?”

“Yes, except that I missed you so much. There! Now we can consider my lecture on foreign travel finished, and you can go on telling me your troubles.”

Gerald accepted the invitation. He spoke at length, though with little variety. It was clear he believed Providence had invented Spanish influenza specifically to sabotage his career. But now he seemed more open, less aloof. The storm had passed. Sally felt included again.

“Well,” said Gerald, checking his watch, “I suppose I’d better be off.”

“Rehearsal?”

“Yes, confound it. It’s the only way of getting through the day. Are you coming along?”

“I’ll come directly I’ve unpacked and tidied myself up.”

“See you at the theatre, then.”

Sally rose and called for the elevator.

2

The rehearsal had already begun when she arrived. As she entered the dark auditorium, voices echoed with that thin, reedy quality that comes from speaking in empty buildings. She found a seat at the back, and as her eyes adjusted, she spotted Gerald sitting beside a man with a bald head and orange hair—Mr. Bunbury, the producer. Scattered throughout the house were members of the cast not needed in the first act. On stage, Elsa Doland—looking lovely—was acting a scene with a man in a bowler hat.

“Why, what do you mean, father?”

“Tiddly-omty-om,” replied the bowler-hatted man. “Tiddly-omty-om… long speech ending in ‘find me in the library.’ And exit.”

He started to leave.

For the first time, Sally sensed the tension. Mr. Bunbury, clearly a man of temperament, picked up his walking stick and hurled it across the room.

“For God’s sake!”

“What now?” asked the bowler hat, pausing halfway across the stage.

“Speak the lines, Teddy,” Gerald snapped. “Don’t skip them like that.”

“You don’t want me to go over the whole thing?”

“Yes!”

“Not the whole damn thing?”

“This is a rehearsal,” Mr. Bunbury snapped. “If we’re not going to do it properly, what’s the point?”

Teddy, seemingly convinced, delivered the line with injured dignity and shuffled off. The atmosphere was thick with anxiety. The theatre world, Sally realized, was just a giant nursery full of easily frustrated children. The waiting, the uncertainty, the endless rehearsing—it had worn down the company’s nerves. One spark could ignite chaos.

Elsa moved to the door, rang a bell, picked up a magazine, and sat down. Moments later, a young woman entered—Miss Winch.

“Miss Winch!” bellowed Mr. Bunbury.

She stopped, looked out over the footlights—not with pain, but with the amused indulgence of someone attending a party for toddlers. She was square, wholesome, good-natured, with a serious face and a faint, knowing smile. Not conventionally pretty, but Sally understood immediately why Fillmore had fallen for her. Deep down, beneath the surface, there was intelligence.

“Hello?” said Miss Winch, amiably.

Mr. Bunbury was visibly shaken.

“Miss Winch, did I or did I not ask you to refrain from chewing gum during rehearsal?”

“That’s right, so you did,” she admitted cheerfully.

“Then why are you doing it?”

Miss Winch rolled the gum around her mouth thoughtfully. “Bit o’ business.”

“What do you mean?”

“Character stuff,” she explained, drawling pleasantly. “Thought it out myself. Maids chew gum, you know.”

Mr. Bunbury ruffled his orange hair in despair. “Have you ever seen a maid?”

“Yes, sir. And they chew gum.”

“I mean a parlour-maid in a smart house,” he moaned. “Do you imagine she’d be allowed to walk into the drawing-room champing that disgusting, beastly stuff?”

Miss Winch considered. “Maybe you’re right.” Then she brightened. “Listen! Great idea! Mr. Foster can write in a line for Elsa calling me down, another giving me a comeback, another for Elsa saying something else, and then something really funny for me. We can work it into a big comic scene. Five or six minutes, all laughs.”

The producer was speechless.

Just then, a dazzling figure in blue velvet and an impossibly stylish hat burst from the wings.

“Say!”

Miss Mabel Hobson had every natural advantage—except a voice. Her figure was perfect, her face flawless, her hair spun gold. But when she spoke in moments of emotion, it sounded like a peacock having a nervous breakdown.

“Say, listen to me for just one moment!”

Mr. Bunbury regained composure. “Miss Hobson! Please!”

“Yes, that’s all very well…”

“You’re interrupting the rehearsal.”

“You bet your sorrowful existence I’m interrupting it,” she agreed. “And if you want to make a little easy money, go ahead and bet somebody ten seeds that I’ll interrupt it again every time there’s any talk of writing up any darned part in the show except mine. Write up other people’s parts? Not while I have my strength!”

A young man with butter-colored hair—Reggie, presumably—tried to calm her.

“Now, sweetie!”

“Oh, can it, Reggie!” she snapped.

Reggie obediently shut up. He wasn’t a brute; he was just resigned.

“I’m the star,” she declared. “And if you think anybody else’s part’s going to be written up… well, pardon me while I choke with laughter! If so much as a syllable is added to anyone else’s part, I walk straight out on my two feet. You won’t see me go—I’ll be so quick.”

Mr. Bunbury stood up, waving his hands. “For heaven’s sake! Are we rehearsing, or is this a debating society? Miss Hobson, nothing is going to be written into anyone’s part. Are you satisfied?”

“She said…”

“Oh, never mind,” Miss Winch said calmly. “It was just a random thought. Working for the good of the show all the time. That’s me.”

“Now, sweetie!” Reggie pleaded.

Hobson relented. “Oh, well, that’s all right, then. But don’t forget—I know how to look after myself.”

She left, followed by Reggie, swallowed by the wings.

“Shall I say my big speech now?” Miss Winch asked.

“Yes, yes! Get on with it. We’ve wasted half the morning.”

“Did you ring, madam?” she said to Elsa, who had been reading her magazine placidly.

The rehearsal continued, and Sally watched with growing dismay. It was all wrong. Even she, a novice, could see it. Miss Hobson was stunning, yes, but in a role requiring depth and nuance, her beauty only highlighted her inability to act. Sally recalled Mr. Faucitt’s story about the lady who got the bird at Wigan. History, she feared, was repeating itself.

A sudden cry from the front row—Mr. Bunbury was on his feet again.

“Miss Hobson!”

She’d just crossed the stage to the desk, a key prop symbolizing her husband’s obsession with business. He loved his desk more than her, and no wife can stand that.

“Oh, gee!” she cried, abandoning her role. “What’s it this time?”

“I suggested at the last rehearsal—and the one before—that you pick up the paper-knife and toy negligently with it. You did it yesterday. Today, you forgot.”

“My God!” she shrieked. “If this doesn’t beat everything! How can I toy negligently with a paper-knife when there’s no paper-knife for me to toy negligently with?”

“The paper-knife is on the desk.”

“It’s not on the desk.”

“No paper-knife?”

“No paper-knife. And it’s no good picking on me. I’m the star, not the assistant stage manager. If you’re going to pick on anyone, pick on him.”

Mr. Bunbury paused, then roared like a wounded bear.

From the wings, a stout figure shuffled out—script in hand, face pale with terror. It was Fillmore.

3

Alas, poor Fillmore! He stood in the middle of the stage, lightning flashing around his head, and Sally, recovering from her shock, sent a wave of sisterly pity across the theatre. She didn’t often feel sorry for Fillmore. He’d always been a bit lush in prosperity, and his past hardships—coffee and buckwheat cakes, frugal fashion choices—had been healthy lessons. But this? This was tragedy.

Somehow, disaster had struck the Fillmore bankroll, and he was back where he started. His presence here could mean only one thing: he was broke. And yet, he’d still managed to bluff his way through breakfast, pretending he was just “mixed up in the show.” How typical.

She remembered his words: “Cracknell suggested I come down and look at it.” Of course. A lie to save face. But now, standing under the glare of Mr. Bunbury’s wrath, he looked like a man who’d just been handed a bill for the entire city.

She felt for him. Truly.
