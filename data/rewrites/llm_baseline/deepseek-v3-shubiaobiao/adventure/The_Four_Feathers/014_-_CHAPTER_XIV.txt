The summer months slipped by, each day thickening the unease in Ethne’s heart. One morning, as sunlight spilled through the open windows of Mrs. Adair’s house overlooking the Salcombe estuary, the words she had long held back finally broke free.

"I’m afraid," she murmured, her fingers tightening on the window ledge.

Behind her, Mrs. Adair did not glance up from her book, though a faint smile touched her lips. "Afraid of what? That some mishap befell Colonel Durrance in London yesterday?"

"No," Ethne replied slowly. "He’s here now—crossing the lawn toward us."

Mrs. Adair’s smile deepened, though she kept her eyes on the page. "I thought as much," she said, so softly the words barely carried.

Ethne barely heard them. Her gaze remained fixed on the figure approaching—steady, unhurried, his stick brushing idly against the grass as though out of habit rather than necessity.

"Do you think," she asked abruptly, "that he still hopes to regain his sight?"

Mrs. Adair arched a brow. "Why else would he travel to London so often to consult his oculist?"

"I’m afraid," Ethne repeated, turning sharply toward her friend. "Haven’t you noticed how quick he’s grown? How he reads silences, fills in unspoken words, deciphers every hesitation? Sometimes I think he sees straight into the corners of my mind. He reads me like a child’s primer."

Mrs. Adair’s reply was cool. "You’re at a disadvantage now. You no longer have your face to shield your thoughts."

"And his eyes tell me nothing at all," Ethne added.

The truth of it was undeniable. Once, Durrance’s gaze had been as open as her own, his expressions easy to decipher. Now, blindness had reversed their roles—where she had once been transparent, she now felt laid bare before a man who had grown unnervingly perceptive.

Mrs. Adair, however, was more intrigued by Ethne’s rare moment of vulnerability. The girl who had once been frankness itself was becoming something else—restless, guarded, a creature of evasions.

"There’s something, then, you must hide from him?" she pressed.

"Yes."

"Something important?"

"Something I *must* keep from him," Ethne insisted, though even as she spoke, she wondered if it was already too late.

She stepped onto the terrace, the morning air doing little to steady her nerves. Below, the lawn sloped toward the creek, where sunlight dappled the water and a lone sailboat tacked lazily against the breeze. She drew a breath, marshaling her composure before descending to meet Durrance.

His steps were sure, his stick a mere prop as he moved toward her. She matched his pace, though each step felt weighted.

"You saw your oculist yesterday?" she asked quickly. "What did he say?"

Durrance shrugged. "That we must wait. Only time will tell if a cure is possible."

Ethne searched his face, as though doubting his words. "But must *we* wait? Surely—"

"It’s wiser," he cut in smoothly, then, with a sudden shift: "It was Mrs. Adair, wasn’t it, who suggested I return to Guessens while you stayed here?"

The question caught her off guard. "I was distraught when I heard of your accident. Laura proposed it. Of course, I agreed."

Her voice held a plea—for understanding, for reassurance. Durrance obliged with a smile. "I know you did, Ethne."

The tension in her shoulders eased slightly, but Durrance was not finished. "Kind of her," he mused, "though it keeps you from your own country. I remember something Harry Feversham once said—"

He spoke the name without hesitation, but Ethne felt the pause that followed like a held breath. Testing, listening—for what? A flinch? A tremor? She gave none.

"—that you could never be truly happy far from Donegal."

"I wasn’t thinking of that when I asked why we must wait," Ethne protested. "That would make me selfish. I only wondered—why delay?"

Durrance’s answer was slow in coming. "I was thinking of Feversham just then. There’s something I’ve long wanted to ask you. Years ago, at Glenalla, you said you might one day tell me what happened that night at Lennon House. What ended your engagement. What sent him away."

Ethne’s fingers curled into her palms. "I’d rather not. It’s done with. Please don’t ask me."

Durrance relented at once. "Very well. I’d never wish to pain you."

"It’s not pain," she corrected quietly. "It’s just... it was so long ago. I think of him now as someone who’s dead."

A movement caught her eye—a small boat nosing into the bank downstream, a stranger stepping ashore, peering uncertainly toward the house. Relief flooded her.

"There’s a visitor," she said quickly. "I’ll see to him."

She hurried forward, grateful for the interruption. The man was of middling height, with a blunt nose, thick mustache, and an air of vague determination.

"Can you tell me if this is The Pool?" he called as she approached.

"Yes. Mrs. Adair is on the terrace."

"I came to see Miss Eustace."

Ethne turned back, startled. "I am Miss Eustace."

The stranger studied her, then gave a satisfied nod. "I’ve had some trouble finding you. Went all the way to Glenalla for nothing. Hard luck, with my leave so short."

Ethne frowned. "I’m sorry, but why were you looking for me?"

His mustache twitched. "You’ve forgotten my name, no doubt. But you’ve heard it before—five years ago. I’m Captain Willoughby."

The name struck like a blow. Ethne’s breath froze. Her hands clenched at her sides.

Willoughby remained unperturbed. "I understand your displeasure, Miss Eustace. None of us foresaw you’d be present when the feathers reached Feversham."

"How do you know I was?"

"Feversham told me."

"You’ve *seen* him?" The cry tore from her before she could stop it, raw and startled.

Willoughby blinked. "A year ago, at Suakin. Else why would I be here?"

Ethne’s pulse hammered. "Why *are* you here?"

From his coat, he produced a small, stained white feather. "To give you this."

Ethne recoiled. "Why?"

"Three feathers were sent to Feversham—three accusations of cowardice. This is one of them. I withdraw mine. I take it back."

"And you bring it to *me*?"

"He asked me to."

The world tilted. The feather in her palm was weightless, yet it carried the burden of years. Willoughby’s voice seemed to come from a distance, but she clung to his words, desperate to hear them all.

"Come," she managed, her voice steadier than she felt. "Tell me everything."

She led him to a secluded spot by the creek, where the water murmured and the hedges enclosed them like a secret.

"Begin," she said. "Leave nothing out. Not even his words—if you remember them."

She held the feather tightly, her mind reeling. Somewhere, somehow, Harry Feversham had reclaimed his honor. And now, at last, she would learn how.

For now, that was enough. The past was alive again, and with it, a hope she had long buried.
