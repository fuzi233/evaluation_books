Between the silver ribbon of the morning sky and the green glittering ribbon of the sea, a boat touched the quay at Harwich, releasing a swarm of passengers like flies from a jar. Among this throng, the man we must follow was by no means conspicuous—nor did he wish to be. There was nothing remarkable about him, save for a striking contrast between the holiday gaiety of his attire and the official gravity of his countenance. He wore a slight, pale grey jacket, a crisp white waistcoat, and a silver straw hat adorned with a grey-blue ribbon. His lean face was dark by comparison, ending in a curt black beard that looked distinctly Spanish and suggested an Elizabethan ruff. He smoked a cigarette with the serious demeanor of an idler. Nothing about him indicated that the grey jacket concealed a loaded revolver, that the white waistcoat hid a police identification card, or that beneath the straw hat rested one of the most powerful intellects in Europe. This was Aristide Valentin, the head of the Paris Police and the world's most celebrated investigator. He had traveled from Brussels to London with a singular purpose: to make the greatest arrest of the century.

His quarry, Flambeau, was in England. The police forces of three nations had finally tracked the great criminal from Ghent to Brussels, and thence to the Hook of Holland. It was widely conjectured that Flambeau would exploit the unfamiliarity and confusion surrounding the Eucharistic Congress currently taking place in London. Likely, he would travel disguised as a minor clerk or secretary connected with the event; but, of course, Valentin could not be certain. Nobody could ever be certain about Flambeau.

It had been many years since this colossus of crime had suddenly ceased to keep the world in a turmoil. When he stopped, following the death of Roland, there was a profound quiet upon the earth. Yet, in his prime—or rather, his worst incarnation—Flambeau was a figure as statuesque and international as the Kaiser himself. Almost every morning, the daily papers announced that he had escaped the consequences of one extraordinary crime only to commit another. He was a Gascon of gigantic stature and boundless daring, and the wildest tales circulated regarding his outbursts of athletic humor. Stories were told of how he overturned a *juge d'instruction* and stood him on his head "to clear his mind," or how he ran down the Rue de Rivoli with a policeman tucked under each arm. It must be said to his credit that his fantastic physical strength was generally employed in such bloodless, though undignified, scenes. His real crimes were chiefly those of ingenious and wholesale robbery. Each of his thefts was almost a new sin, capable of standing as a story in itself. He had once run the great Tyrolean Dairy Company in London, possessing no dairies, no cows, no carts, and no milk, yet serving a thousand subscribers. He accomplished this simply by moving the little milk cans outside his customers' doors to the doors of his own clients. He maintained an unaccountable correspondence with a young lady whose entire letter-bag was intercepted by the extraordinary trick of photographing his messages infinitesimally small upon the slides of a microscope. Many of his experiments, however, were marked by a sweeping simplicity. It is said he once repainted all the numbers on a street in the dead of night merely to divert a traveler into a trap. It is quite certain he invented a portable pillar-box, placing it at corners in quiet suburbs on the chance that strangers would drop postal orders into it. Lastly, he was known to be a startling acrobat; despite his huge frame, he could leap like a grasshopper and melt into the tree-tops like a monkey. Thus, the great Valentin knew perfectly well that his adventures would not end merely when he found Flambeau.

But how was he to find him? On this point, the great Valentin's plans were still in flux.

There was one thing Flambeau, with all his dexterity of disguise, could not conceal: his singular height. If Valentin's keen eye had spotted a tall apple-seller, a tall grenadier, or even a tolerably tall duchess, he might have arrested her on the spot. However, throughout the train journey, there was no one who could possibly be a disguised Flambeau, any more than a cat could be a disguised giraffe. Regarding the people on the boat, he had already satisfied himself. Those picked up at Harwich or during the journey were limited to six individuals: a short railway official heading to the terminus, three fairly short market gardeners collected two stations later, one very short widow traveling from a small Essex town, and a very short Roman Catholic priest arriving from a small Essex village. When it came to the last case, Valentin gave up and almost laughed aloud. The little priest was the very essence of those eastern flats; he possessed a face as round and dull as a Norfolk dumpling, eyes as empty as the North Sea, and several brown paper parcels which he was quite incapable of managing. The Eucharistic Congress had doubtless drawn many such creatures from their local stagnation, blind and helpless like moles disinterred. Valentin was a skeptic in the severe French style and held no affection for priests. Yet, he could feel pity, and this particular specimen might have provoked pity in anyone. He carried a large, shabby umbrella that constantly slipped from his grasp onto the floor. He seemed unsure which end of his return ticket was correct. With a simplicity akin to that of a moon-calf, he explained to everyone in the carriage that he had to be careful because he possessed something made of real silver "with blue stones" hidden in one of his brown-paper parcels. His quaint blend of Essex flatness and saintly simplicity continuously amused the Frenchman until the priest somehow managed to arrive at Tottenham with all his parcels, only to return for his umbrella. When he finally retrieved it, Valentin even took the kindness to warn him not to advertise the silver to anyone. Yet, while talking to whoever he chose, Valentin kept his eyes wide open for someone else; he scanned steadily for anyone, rich or poor, male or female, who stood well over six feet tall, for Flambeau was four inches taller than that.

He alighted at Liverpool Street, quite conscientiously secure that he had not missed the criminal thus far. He then proceeded to Scotland Yard to regularize his position and arrange for assistance should he need it. After lighting another cigarette, he took a long stroll through the streets of London. As he walked through the squares beyond Victoria, he paused suddenly and stood still. It was a quaint, quiet square, very typical of London, filled with an accidental stillness. The tall, flat houses around it looked simultaneously prosperous and uninhabited; the shrubbery in the center appeared as deserted as a green Pacific islet. One of the four sides was significantly higher than the rest, like a dais, and the line of this side was broken by one of London's admirable accidents—a restaurant that looked as though it had wandered in from Soho. It was an unreasonably attractive object, featuring dwarf plants in pots and long, striped blinds of lemon yellow and white. It stood unusually high above the street, and in the usual patchwork fashion of London, a flight of steps from the street ran up to meet the front door almost as a fire escape might lead to a first-floor window. Valentin stood and smoked in front of the yellow-and-white blinds, considering them for a long time.

The most incredible thing about miracles is that they happen. Occasionally, a few clouds in the heavens coalesce into the staring shape of a single human eye. Sometimes, a tree stands upright in the landscape of a doubtful journey in the exact and elaborate shape of a question mark. I have witnessed both these phenomena myself within the last few days. Nelson dies in the instant of victory; and a man named Williams quite accidentally murders a man named Williamson—it sounds like a sort of infanticide. In short, there exists in life an element of elfin coincidence that those who calculate only on the prosaic perpetually miss. As it has been well expressed in the paradox of Poe, wisdom must reckon on the unforeseen.

Aristide Valentin was unfathomably French; and the French intelligence is intelligence that is special and solely its own. He was not a "thinking machine"; for that is a brainless phrase of modern fatalism and materialism. A machine is only a machine because it cannot think. But he was a thinking man, and a plain man at the same time. The French electrify the world not by starting any paradox, but by carrying out a truism. They carry a truism so far—as in the French Revolution. Only a man who knows nothing of motors speaks of motoring without petrol; only a man who knows nothing of reason speaks of reasoning without strong, undisputed first principles. Here, he had no strong first principles. Chief Aristide Valentin of the Paris Police was running late for dinner, and his guests were beginning to gather in the entrance hall before he arrived. However, they felt reassured by the presence of his confidential servant, Ivan. An old man with a scarred face and moustaches as grey as his skin, Ivan always occupied a seat in the hall—a space dominated by an impressive display of weaponry. Valentin's residence was as peculiar and legendary as its master. It was an ancient house surrounded by high walls and towering poplars that nearly touched the Seine. Its strangest feature, and perhaps its greatest security asset, was architectural: there was no exit from the house other than the front door, which was guarded by Ivan and the arsenal. While numerous doors led from the interior into the vast, elaborate gardens, there was no way out of the garden itself. Encircling it was a tall, smooth, unclimbable wall topped with specialized spikes. It was hardly a welcoming environment for reflection, especially for a man in whom a hundred criminals had sworn to exact vengeance.

As Ivan explained to the arriving guests, their host had delayed ten minutes due to urgent matters. In truth, Valentin was finalizing arrangements concerning executions—duties that, though deeply repugnant to his nature, he executed with chilling precision. Ruthless in hunting criminals, he was surprisingly mild regarding their punishment. Having shaped French—and largely European—police methodology, his profound influence had been honorably directed toward mitigating sentences and purifying prisons. He was considered one of France's great humanitarian freethinkers; the only flaw in his philosophy, critics argued, was that he made mercy even colder than justice.

When Valentin finally appeared, he was already attired in black with the red rosette of his office. Should you happen to encounter a member of the exclusive "Twelve True Fishermen" as he enters the Vernon Hotel for their annual banquet, you will notice, as he removes his overcoat, that his evening coat is green rather than black. If you possess the audacity—though it requires defying the stars—to inquire why, he will likely reply that he does so to avoid being mistaken for a waiter. You will then withdraw, crushed by the absurdity, leaving behind an unsolved mystery and a tale worthy of telling.

Alternatively, imagine meeting a mild, hardworking little priest named Father Brown. If you asked him what the greatest stroke of luck in his life had been, he would probably answer that, on the whole, his finest feat occurred at the Vernon Hotel. There, he averted a crime and perhaps saved a soul merely by listening to a few footsteps in a hallway. He might be slightly proud of this wild notion, but since it is virtually impossible for you to rise high enough in society to join the Fishermen or sink low enough among the criminal underworld to meet Father Brown, you will never hear this story unless I tell it to you now.

The Vernon Hotel, where the Twelve True Fishermen held their annual dinners, was an institution that could only exist in an oligarchic society obsessed with good manners. It was a topsy-turvy creation—an "exclusive" commercial enterprise that paid not by attracting patrons, but by turning them away. In the heart of a plutocracy, traders had become so cunning they grew more fastidious than their customers. They deliberately created obstacles so that their wealthy, weary clients would spend money and diplomatic effort overcoming them. If there were a fashionable London hotel that admitted no one under six feet tall, society would meekly assemble parties of tall men to dine there. If an expensive restaurant were open only on Thursday afternoons due to the proprietor's whim, it would be packed exclusively on Thursdays.

The Vernon Hotel stood inconspicuously in the corner of a Belgravia square. It was small and inconvenient, yet these very flaws served as protective walls for a specific class. One inconvenience was deemed vital: the capacity to seat only twenty-four people simultaneously. The sole large dining table was the famous terrace table, situated on a veranda overlooking one of London's most exquisite old gardens. Consequently, even the twenty-four seats at this table could only be utilized in warm weather; this scarcity made the experience even more desirable. The owner, a Jew named Lever, amassed nearly a million pounds by making entry so difficult. Combining this exclusivity with impeccable service, he ensured the wines and cuisine rivaled the best in Europe, while the staff mirrored the fixed demeanor of the English upper class. Lever knew his fifteen waiters intimately; becoming a waiter there was harder than becoming a Member of Parliament. Each was trained in terrifying silence and smoothness, acting as a gentleman's personal servant. Indeed, there was often at least one waiter assigned to every gentleman diner.

The Twelve True Fishermen would dine nowhere else, insisting on luxurious privacy and being utterly disturbed by the mere thought of another club sharing the building. For their annual dinner, they displayed all their treasures as if in a private home, including their celebrated set of fish knives and forks—the insignia of the society. Each utensil was exquisitely crafted in silver, shaped like a fish, and studded at the handle with a large pearl. These were reserved for the fish course, which was invariably the most magnificent part of the meal. The society possessed numerous ceremonies and observances but lacked any history or purpose; this lack of substance was precisely what made it so aristocratic. Membership was hermetic; unless one was already a certain type of person, one never even heard of them. Founded twelve years prior, the club's president was Mr. Audley, and its vice-president was the Duke of Chester.

How I came to know of this appalling hotel, or how my friend Father Brown found himself in that golden galleon, may seem like a natural query. My story is simple, even vulgar. There exists an aged rioter and demagogue who breaks into refined retreats proclaiming that all men are brothers; wherever this leveller goes on his pale horse, Father Brown follows. That afternoon, an Italian waiter suffered a paralytic stroke. Lever, marveling mildly at such superstitions, agreed to summon the nearest Catholic priest. Father Brown, displaying a meek impudence he would show equally in Buckingham Palace, requested a room and writing materials.

Lever was torn. A kind man with a distaste for scenes, he nonetheless viewed the presence of an unusual stranger as a speck of dirt on a freshly polished floor. The Vernon Hotel operated without borders or anterooms; there were no waiting crowds, no chance visitors. With fifteen waiters and twelve guests, finding a new arrival that night would be as shocking as seeing a new brother take tea in one's own family. To the right lie passages to the public rooms; to the left, a similar route leads to the kitchens and offices. "The most beautiful crime I ever committed," Flambeau would later declare in his pious old age, "was also, by a singular coincidence, my final transgression. It occurred at Christmas. As an artist, I had always endeavored to craft crimes suited to the specific season or landscape in which I found myself, selecting terraces or gardens for catastrophes as one might choose a setting for a sculptural group. Thus, squires should be swindled within long rooms paneled with oak; whereas Jews, conversely, should discover themselves unexpectedly destitute amidst the lights and screens of the Café Riche. Similarly, in England, if I wished to deprive a dean of his riches—a task not as simple as it appears—I sought to frame him against the green lawns and grey towers of a cathedral town. Likewise, in France, when I managed to extract funds from a wealthy and wicked peasant (an endeavor nearly impossible), it gratified me to see his indignant head relieved against a grey line of clipped poplars, beneath the solemn plains of Gaul where the mighty spirit of Millet broods.

"Well, my last crime was a Christmas offense: a cheerful, cozy, English middle-class crime; a crime straight out of Charles Dickens. I committed it in a fine old middle-class house near Putney, featuring a crescent of carriage drive, a stable adjacent to it, a nameplate on the outer gates, and a monkey tree. You know the type. I truly believe my imitation of Dickens's style was dexterous and literary. It seems almost a pity I repented the very same evening."

Flambeau would then recount the tale from the inside; viewed from within, it remains peculiar. Seen from without, however, it is perfectly incomprehensible, and it is from this external vantage point that the stranger must study it. From this perspective, the drama commenced when the front doors of the house with the stable opened onto the garden containing the monkey tree, and a young girl emerged with bread to feed the birds on the afternoon of Boxing Day. She possessed a pretty face with brave brown eyes, yet her figure was beyond conjecture, for she was so enveloped in brown furs that it was difficult to distinguish hair from fur. Without her attractive visage, she might have been mistaken for a small, toddling bear.

The winter afternoon was reddening toward evening, and a ruby light was already rolling over the bloomless beds, filling them, as it were, with the ghosts of dead roses. "Oh, don't jump, Mr. Crook," she called out in alarm; "it's much too high."

This pallor was accentuated by an aggressive red tie, the sole element of his costume he seemed to take any care with. Perhaps it was a symbol. The village of Bohun Beacon sat atop a hill so precipitous that the church's tall spire appeared merely the summit of a miniature mountain. At the base of this sacred peak stood a smithy, perpetually glowing with fire and cluttered with hammers and scrap metal. Directly opposite, separated by a rough cobblestone crossroads, lay "The Blue Boar," the village's sole inn. The Reverend and Honourable Wilfred Bohun was deeply pious, hastening toward austere devotions at daybreak. His elder brother, Colonel the Honourable Norman Bohun, possessed no such devotion. Dressed in evening wear, he sat on a bench outside the inn, consuming what a philosophical observer might deem either his final drink of Tuesday or his first of Wednesday. The Colonel cared little for distinction.

The Bohuns were among the rare aristocratic families tracing their lineage directly to the Middle Ages; their banner had once flown over Palestine. Yet, it is a grave error to assume such houses remain bastions of chivalric tradition. Traditions survive best among the poor; aristocrats live in fashion. Father Brown possessed a peculiar insight into human nature, honed by both his vocation and his experience: he knew better than most that death confers a strange dignity upon every man. Yet, even he felt a jolt of incongruity when roused at dawn to learn that Sir Aaron Armstrong had been murdered. There was something absurd, almost unseemly, about secret violence inflicted upon a figure so thoroughly entertaining and popular. Sir Aaron Armstrong was a man of comedy; his popularity was legendary. To hear that "Sunny Jim" had hanged himself, or that Mr. Pickwick had perished in Hanwell, would have been tragic irony. But Sir Aaron, though a philanthropist who tackled society's darkest corners, approached them with a brightness that bordered on the theatrical. His political speeches cascaded with anecdotes and loud laughter; his health was robust to the point of bursting; his ethics were pure optimism; and he addressed the Drink Problem—his favorite topic—with that immortal, monotonous gaiety characteristic of the prosperous total abstainer.

The story of his conversion was well-known on puritanical platforms: how, as a boy, he had drifted from Scotch theology to Scotch whisky, only to rise above both and become, as he modestly claimed, what he was today. Yet, seeing his wide white beard, cherubic face, and sparkling spectacles at countless dinners, it was difficult to believe he had ever been as morbid as a dram-drinker or a Calvinist. He seemed the most seriously merry of mankind.

He resided on the rural fringe of Hampstead in a handsome, modern tower: high but narrow, its smallest side overhanging a steep green bank beside a railway line, shaken daily by passing trains. Sir Aaron boasted he had no nerves. That morning, however, the dynamic reversed; it was the house that delivered a shock to the train.

The engine slowed and halted just beyond the point where the house's angle impinged upon the sharp turf slope. Mechanical arrests are usually gradual, but the living cause here was instantaneous. A man dressed entirely in black, including dreadful black gloves, appeared on the ridge above the engine, waving his gloved hands like a sable windmill. Alone, this might not have stopped a lingering train. Instead, a cry erupted from him—a sound described later as utterly unnatural. It was a shout so distinct that one could hear its horror even without catching the words. The word was "Murder!"

Even the engine driver swore he would have stopped regardless, sensing the dread in the accent alone. Once halted, the scene revealed itself. On the green bank stood Magnus, Sir Aaron's manservant, clad in black. The baronet had often laughed at these gloves, but no one laughed now. Below, rolled nearly to the bottom of the bank, lay the body of an elderly man in a yellow dressing gown with a vivid scarlet lining. A scrap of rope tangled around his leg, suggesting a struggle. There was little blood, yet the body was contorted in a posture impossible for a living creature. It was Sir Aaron Armstrong.

Soon, Patrick Royce, the dead man's secretary and a former Bohemian celebrity, emerged. A big, fair-bearded Irishman, Royce mirrored the servant's agony. Finally, Alice Armstrong, the daughter, arrived, tottering and waving into the garden. By then, the engine driver had resumed his journey, whistling for help from the next station.

Father Brown had been summoned swiftly by Patrick Royce. Though Royce was a casual Catholic who only remembered his faith when in trouble, his plea was expedited because one of the official detectives, Merton, admired the unofficial "Flambeau"—a nickname for Father Brown. As Merton led the priest across the fields toward the railway, their conversation grew intimate.

"There is no sense to be made of it," Merton admitted candidly. "Magnus is a solemn fool, too foolish to be an assassin. Royce has been the baronet's best friend for years, and his daughter undoubtedly adored him. Besides, it's absurd. Who would kill such a cheery old chap? Who could dip their hands in the gore of an after-dinner speaker? It's like killing Father Christmas."

"Yes, it was a cheery house," Father Brown agreed. "Cheery while he lived. Do you think it will remain cheery now he is dead?"

Merton paused, regarding his companion with renewed interest. "Now he is dead?" he repeated.

"Yes," the priest stated stolidly. "He was cheerful. But did he communicate that cheerfulness? Frankly, was anyone else in the house cheerful besides him?"

A window in Merton's mind opened to a strange light of realization. He had visited the Armstrongs often for minor police work and hadn't realized until now how depressing the house truly was. The rooms were high and cold; the decor mean and provincial; the drafty corridors illuminated by electricity bleaker than moonlight. While Sir Aaron's scarlet face and silver beard had blazed like a bonfire throughout the house, he left no warmth behind. His vitality was so intense he required no stoves, carrying his own heat with him. But the others were merely shadows of their lord. Magnus, the moody servant with his monstrous black gloves, was almost a nightmare. Royce, the secretary, was a solid, big man in tweeds, yet his salt-and-pepper beard and premature wrinkles marked him as a failure, possessing a sad, heartbroken good-nature. As for Alice, it was hard to believe she was Sir Aaron's daughter; she was so pallid and sensitive, her graceful form quivering like an aspen leaf. Merton had often wondered if she learned to tremble at the crash of the passing trains.

"You see," Father Brown said, blinking modestly, "I'm not sure the Armstrong cheerfulness is so cheerful for *others*. You say nobody could kill such a happy old man, but I doubt it. *Ne nos inducas in tentationem* [Lead us not into temptation]. If I ever murdered someone," he added simply, "I dare say it might be an Optimist."

"Why?" cried Merton, amused. "Do you think people dislike cheerfulness?"

"People like frequent laughter," Father Brown answered. "But I don't think they like a permanent smile. Cheerfulness without humor is a trying thing."

They walked in silence along the windy grassy bank until reaching the shadow of the tall Armstrong house. Suddenly, Father Brown dropped a thought as casually as discarding a burden: "Of course, drink is neither good nor bad in itself. But I can't help feeling that men like Armstrong want an occasional glass of wine to sadden them."

Their guide, Detective Inspector Gilder, waited on the bank, speaking to Patrick Royce. Gilder was a grizzled veteran, while Royce loomed over him with his powerful stoop, moving like a buffalo pulling a cart. Royce greeted the priest with unusual pleasure, stepping aside to allow them space. Merton, meanwhile, addressed Gilder with respectful but impatient curiosity.

"Well, Mr. Gilder, have you solved the mystery?"

"There is no mystery," Gilder replied, staring dreamily at the rooks.

"Well, there is for me, at any rate," Merton smiled.

"It is simple enough, my boy," Gilder observed, stroking his grey beard. "Three minutes after you fetched Mr. Royce's parson, everything came out. You know that pasty-faced servant in black gloves who stopped the train?"

"I'd know him anywhere. He gave me the creeps."

"When the train moved on, that man vanished," Gilder drawled. "Rather cool, isn't it, to escape via the very train heading for the police?"

"Are you sure he killed his master?" Merton asked.

"Yes, my son, I'm pretty sure," Gilder said dryly. "For the trifling reason that he fled with twenty thousand pounds in papers from his master's desk. The real difficulty is *how* he killed him. The skull appears broken by a heavy weapon, yet no weapon lies nearby. The murderer would have struggled to carry it away unless it was too small to notice—or perhaps too big."

"Perhaps the weapon was too big to be noticed," Father Brown interjected with an odd little giggle.

Gilder turned sharply. "What do you mean?"

"Silly phrasing, I know," Father Brown apologized. "Sounds like a fairy tale. But poor Armstrong was killed with a giant's club—a great green club, too large to be seen. We call it the earth. He was broken against this green bank."

"How do you mean?" Gilder demanded quickly.

Father Brown turned his moon-like face upward, blinking hopelessly at the narrow facade of the house. They followed his gaze to the top of the otherwise blind rear section, where an attic window stood open.

"Don't you see?" he explained, pointing awkwardly like a child. "He was thrown down from there?"

Gilder frowned, examining the window. "Possible, yes. But why are you so sure?"

Father Brown's grey eyes widened. "There's a piece of rope around the dead man's leg. Don't you see the other end caught at the corner of that window?"

From that height, it looked like a speck of dust, but the shrewd investigator nodded. "Quite right, sir. One of yours."
