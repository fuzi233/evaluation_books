A Very Strange Agony

The drawing room smelled of roasted nuts and heavy velvet, a scent that usually soothed me, but tonight it felt cloying. We had settled for coffee and chocolate, though Carmilla sat with her cup untouched, her gaze fixed on the fire as if searching for something beneath the flames. Soon, Madame and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine arrived, their chatter rising to fill the silence, turning the afternoon into a frantic card game. My father eventually emerged from his study, claiming a "dish of tea" as his excuse to join us.

When the cards were stacked away, he moved to the sofa and sat beside Carmilla. His voice, usually booming with authority, trembled slightly with anxiety. "Have you heard from your mother since you arrived?" he asked.

"No," she replied, her voice smooth as polished glass.

He pressed further, asking where a letter might reach her now. "I cannot tell," she answered, her smile not reaching her eyes. "But I have been thinking of leaving you. You have been too hospitable, too kind. I have caused you an infinity of trouble, and I wish to take a carriage tomorrow to pursue her. I know where I shall find her, though I dare not say where yet."

My father's relief was palpable, a sudden tension in his shoulders. "You must not dream of such a thing!" he exclaimed, gripping her arm. "We cannot afford to lose you. I will not consent to your departure unless your mother directs it herself. She agreed to stay until her return. I feel a terrible responsibility, unaided by her counsel, especially as the reports of the mysterious disease ravaging our neighborhood grow more terrifying by the hour. But rest assured, you shall not leave without her explicit command. We would suffer too greatly to let you go."

"Thank you, sir," she whispered, bowing her head with a bashful, practiced grace. "You have all been too kind. I have never known such happiness as in your chateau, under your care, amidst your daughter's company."

He kissed her hand, a gallant, old-world gesture, pleased by her words.

Later, I escorted Carmilla to her chamber as was our custom, sitting with her while she prepared for sleep.

"Do you think," I asked, trying to keep my voice steady, "that you will ever confide fully in me?"

She turned, a radiant, enigmatic smile playing on her lips, offering no verbal answer.

"You won't answer?" I pressed, feeling foolish. "You can't answer pleasantly; perhaps I shouldn't have asked."

"You were right to ask," she said softly, her eyes gleaming in the dim light. "You do not know how dear you are to me, or you would not think any confidence too great to seek. But I am bound by vows, far stricter than those of a nun. I dare not tell my story yet, not even to you. But the time is near. You will think me cruel, selfish. Yet love is always selfish; the more ardent, the more consuming. How jealous I am, you cannot comprehend. You must come with me, loving me, to death; or hate me and still follow me, carrying that hatred through death and beyond. There is no such word as indifference in my nature."

"Now, Carmilla," I interrupted hastily, "you are going to talk your wild nonsense again."

"Not I, silly little fool that I am," she laughed, a sound like dry leaves skittering on stone. "Full of whims and fancies. For your sake, I shall speak like a sage. Have you ever been to a ball?"

"No. How do you run on? What is it like? How charming it must be."

"I almost forget; it was years ago."

I laughed nervously. "You are not so old. Your first ball can hardly be forgotten yet."

"I remember everything about it—with an effort. I see it all, as divers see the world above them through a medium, dense and rippling yet transparent. That night, something occurred that confused the picture, faded its colors. I was nearly assassinated in my bed, wounded here," she pressed a finger against her breast, "and I have never been the same since."

"Were you near dying?"

"Yes, very. A cruel love, a strange love that would have taken my life. Love demands sacrifices. No sacrifice without blood." She yawned, curling her tiny hands into her rich, wavy hair, resting her head on the pillow. Her glittering eyes followed my every movement, tracking me with a shy, unreadable smile.

"Good night," I murmured, retreating from the room with a sensation of unease that settled deep in my stomach.

I often wondered if our pretty guest ever said her prayers. I had never seen her on her knees. In the morning, she descended only after our family prayers had concluded; at night, she never left the drawing room to join our brief vigil in the hall. Had it not been for the casual revelation that she had been baptized, I would have doubted her faith entirely. Religion was a subject she never touched. If I knew the world better, this neglect would not have surprised me.

The precautions of the nervous are contagious. Persons of a like temperament inevitably imitate them. I had adopted Carmilla's habit of locking my bedroom door, succumbing to her whimsical alarms about midnight invaders and prowling assassins. I had also begun the ritual of searching my room, ensuring no lurking thief or murderer was "ensconced" within the shadows.

With these wise measures in place, I climbed into bed and fell asleep. A candle burned steadily in my room, an old habit I had never broken, a beacon against the dark. Thus fortified, I thought I could rest in peace. But dreams slip through stone walls, illuminate dark rooms, or extinguish light ones; their figures enter and exit at will, laughing at locksmiths.

That night, I dreamed of a very strange agony.

It was not a nightmare, for I remained fully conscious of being asleep. Yet I was equally aware of being in my room, lying in bed, precisely as I was. I saw the furniture, the shadows, the darkness pressing in, exactly as I had seen it, save for the oppressive gloom. At the foot of the bed, something moved. At first, I could not distinguish it, but soon I saw a soot-black animal resembling a monstrous cat. It was immense, stretching four or five feet long, measuring the full length of the hearthrug as it paced back and forth with the lithe, sinister restlessness of a beast trapped in a cage.

I could not cry out, though terror seized my throat. Its pace quickened, the room plunging into deeper darkness until I could see nothing but its eyes. Two broad, luminous orbs approached my face. Suddenly, I felt a stinging pain, as if two large needles had darted an inch apart deep into my breast.

I woke with a scream. The room was illuminated by the candle that had burned all night. Standing at the foot of the bed, slightly to the right, was a female figure. She wore a dark, loose dress, her hair hanging down to cover her shoulders. She was as still as a block of stone, breathing not a whisper. As I stared, transfixed, she seemed to shift her position, moving closer to the door. Then, the door opened, and she passed out into the night.

Relief washed over me, allowing me to breathe and move. My first thought was that Carmilla had played a trick, that I had forgotten to secure my door. I rushed to it, finding it locked firmly on the inside. Fear, cold and sharp, gripped me. I was horrified. I threw myself back into the bed, pulling the covers over my head, lying there more dead than alive until the gray light of morning broke.
