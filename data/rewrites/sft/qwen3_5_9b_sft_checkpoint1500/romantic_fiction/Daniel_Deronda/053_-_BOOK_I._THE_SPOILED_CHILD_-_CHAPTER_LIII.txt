A single day intervened between the summons to a second audience and the arrival itself. During that time, the Princess sent no further word than a brief note stating she was not yet well enough to receive him. But on the third morning, a fresh letter arrived, commanding: "I leave to-day. Come and see me at once."

Deronda was ushered into the same chamber as before, yet the atmosphere had shifted profoundly. Blinds and heavy curtains darkened the room until it resembled a twilight sanctuary. The Princess was absent at first, but soon she entered, wrapped in a loose gown of soft, dusky orange silk. Black lace floated about her head, framing features that appeared even more striking in the somber gloom; her eyes seemed larger, her lines more vigorous. In the dim light, she possessed the air of a sorceress who might stretch forth a magical hand to brew potions for others, yet who scorned to mix them for herself, having already drunk her fill of youth.

As Deronda stepped forward, she threw her arms around his shoulders, kissing him on both cheeks with a sudden, fierce warmth. Then, seating herself among cushions with an air of assured firmness and regal dignity—a stark contrast to the erratic volatility of their first meeting—she gestured for him to sit beside her.

"You are quite relieved now, I trust?" he asked softly.

"Yes, I am at ease again," she replied, her tone carrying the matter-of-fact gravity of a queen rather than the intimacy of a mother. "Is there anything more that you would like to ask me?"

"Can I find the house in Genoa where you used to live with my grandfather?" Deronda ventured.

"No," she answered, waving her arm dismissively. "It is pulled down—not to be found. As for the details of our family, and where my father lived at various times, you will find everything among the papers in the chest. It is better than I can tell you. My father was a physician. My mother was a Morteira. I used to hear all those stories without truly listening. You will find them all. I was born amidst them against my will, but I banished them the moment I could."

Deronda struggled to conceal the pang that struck his heart. "Anything else that I should desire to know from you could only be what it is some satisfaction to your own feelings to tell me."

"I think I have told you everything that could be demanded of me," the Princess said, her gaze turning cold and meditative. It seemed she believed she had exhausted every emotional reservoir during their previous encounter. In truth, she had told herself, *"I have done it all. I have confessed all. I will not go through it again. I will save myself from agitation."* And thus, she played out this rigid script.

Yet, for Daniel Deronda, the moment was cruel. It transformed his lifelong filial yearning into a disappointed pilgrimage to a shrine stripped of its sacred symbols. It felt as though all the woman lacking within her mother now dwelt entirely within him. With a tremor in his voice, he asked, "Then are we to part, and I never be anything to you?"

"It is better so," the Princess said, her voice suddenly softening to a mellower hue. "There could be nothing but hard duty for you, even if it were possible for you to take the place of my son. You would not love me." She cut him off abruptly, raising a hand to forestall denial. "Don't deny it. I know what is the truth. You don't like what I did. You are angry with me. You think I robbed you of something. You are on your grandfather's side, and you will always harbor a condemnation of me in your heart."

Deronda felt placed under a ban of silence. Rising from his seat, he preferred to stand, obeying whatever imperious prohibition forbade any display of tenderness. But then his mother looked up at him, her gaze shifting to a new, startled admiration.

"You are wrong to be angry with me," she said. "You are the better for what I did." After a pause, she added sharply, "And now tell me what you shall do?"

"Do you mean now, immediately," Deronda asked, "or as to the course of my future life?"

"I mean in the future. What difference will it make to you that I have told you about your birth?"

"A very great difference," Deronda affirmed emphatically. "I can hardly think of anything that would make a greater difference."

"What shall you do then?" the Princess pressed, her tone sharpening. "Make yourself just like your grandfather—be what he wished you—turn yourself into a Jew like him?"

"That is impossible," Deronda replied, his voice gaining tenacity. "The effect of my education can never be undone. The Christian sympathies in which my mind was reared can never die out of me." He continued, his conviction unshaken, "But I consider it my duty—it is the impulse of my feeling—to identify myself, as far as possible, with my hereditary people. And if I can see any work to be done for them to which I can give my soul and hand, I shall choose to do it."

The Princess fixed her eyes on him with a wondering speculation, examining his face as if trying to read a difficult, ancient language. He met her gaze steadily, sustained by a resolute opposition that expressed his fullest self. She leaned closer, speaking with decisive emphasis, "You are in love with a Jewess."

Deronda blushed. "My reasons would be independent of any such fact."

"I know better. I have seen what men are," she said peremptorily. "Tell me the truth. She is a Jewess who will not accept anyone but a Jew. There *are* a few such," she added, touching her lips with a hint of scorn.

Deronda faced the difficulty of answering those who interpret reality with such absolute certainty that enlightenment becomes impossible. Yet, beyond this, the immediate point was one on which he felt a profound repugnance to either deny or affirm. He remained silent. Presently, the Princess spoke again, "You love her as your father loved me, and she draws you after her as I drew him."

Those words struck deep into Deronda's filial imagination. A tenderness flickered in his glance, which his mother seized as an assent. Her passion rose. "But I was leading him the other way. And now your grandfather is getting his revenge."

"Mother," Deronda remonstrated gently, "let us not think of it in that way. I will admit that there may come some benefit from the education you chose for me. I prefer cherishing that benefit with gratitude to dwelling with resentment on the injury. I think it would have been right that I should have been brought up with the consciousness that I was a Jew, but it must always have been a good to me to have as wide an instruction and sympathy as possible. And now, you have restored me my inheritance—events have brought a fuller restitution than you could have made. You have been saved from robbing my people of my service and me of my duty. Can you not bring your whole soul to consent to this?"

Deronda paused in his plea. His mother listened intently, as if the cadence of his voice were captivating her ear, yet she shook her head slowly. He began again, with growing urgency.

"You have told me that you sought what you held the best for me. Open your heart to relenting and love toward my grandfather, who sought what he held the best for you."

"Not for me, no!" she cried, shaking her head with absolute denial and folding her arms tightly. "I tell you, he never thought of his daughter except as an instrument. Because I had wants outside his purpose, I was to be put in a frame and tortured. If that is the right law for the world, I will not say that I love it. If my acts were wrong—if it is God who is exacting from me that I should deliver up what I withheld—then who is punishing me because I deceived my father and did not warn him that I should contradict his trust? Well, I have told everything. I have done what I could. And *your* soul consents. That is enough. I have, after all, been the instrument my father wanted. 'I desire a grandson who shall have a true Jewish heart. Every Jew should rear his family as if he hoped that a Deliverer might spring from it.'"

Uttering these final sentences, the Princess narrowed her eyes, waved her head up and down, and spoke slowly with a strange, resonant chest-voice, as if quoting words she barely wished to utter.

"Were those my grandfather's words?" Deronda asked.

"Yes, yes; and you will find them written. I wanted to thwart him," the Princess admitted, with a sudden outburst of the passionate fury she had shown in their first interview. Then, more slowly, "You would have me love what I have hated since the time I was so high"—here she held her left hand a yard from the floor—"That can never be. But what does it matter? His yoke has been on me, whether I loved it or not. You are the grandson he wanted. You speak as men do—as if you felt yourself wise. What does it all mean?"
