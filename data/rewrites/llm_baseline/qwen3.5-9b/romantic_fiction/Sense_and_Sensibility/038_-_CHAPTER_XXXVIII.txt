Mrs. Jennings spoke warmly of Edward's conduct, yet only Elinor and Marianne truly grasped its worth. They alone knew how scant the temptation had been for him to disobey, and how meager the solace remained to him beyond the simple knowledge that he had acted rightly, given the loss of both friends and fortune. Elinor took pride in his integrity; Marianne, moved by compassion for his suffering, forgave all his past faults. Though the breach of confidence between the sisters was healed by this public revelation, neither wished to dwell upon it during private moments. Elinor avoided the subject on principle, fearing that Marianne's overly warm and absolute assurances of Edward's enduring love would only cement a belief she secretly hoped to dispel. For Marianne, the courage to discuss the topic quickly faded; the inevitable comparison between her own actions and Elinor's left her more dissatisfied with herself than ever. She felt the full weight of this contrast, but not as her sister intended—to spur her to action. Instead, it bred only relentless self-reproach. She bitterly regretted her failure to act sooner, yet this realization brought only the torment of penitence without the promise of improvement. Her spirit was so weakened that she believed further effort impossible, leaving her utterly disheartened.

For a day or two thereafter, no news arrived regarding events in Harley Street or Bartlett's Buildings. Yet, despite knowing so much already, Mrs. Jennings had resolved from the outset to visit her cousins with comfort and inquiry as soon as possible. Only the unusual influx of visitors had delayed her journey. On the third day following their discovery of the truth, the weather proved so fine and beautiful—a rare occurrence in early March—that many flocked to Kensington Gardens. Mrs. Jennings and Elinor were among them. Marianne, however, aware that the Willoughbys had returned to London and harboring a constant dread of encountering them, preferred to stay indoors rather than risk appearing in such a crowded public space.

Shortly after entering the gardens, an intimate friend of Mrs. Jennings joined them. Elinor was relieved that this companion engaged Mrs. Jennings's attention, allowing Elinor a moment of quiet reflection. She saw neither the Willoughbys, nor Edward, nor anyone else who might hold interest for her, serious or cheerful. Eventually, however, she was surprised to be approached by Miss Steele. Although Miss Steele appeared somewhat shy, she expressed immense delight at meeting them. Encouraged by Mrs. Jennings's evident kindness, she temporarily left her own group to join theirs.

Mrs. Jennings immediately whispered to Elinor, "Extract everything from her, my dear. If you ask, she will tell you anything. Besides, I cannot leave Mrs. Clarke." Fortunately for both Mrs. Jennings's curiosity and Elinor's peace of mind, Miss Steele possessed the habit of volunteering information without prompting; otherwise, nothing would have been learned.

"I am so delighted to meet you!" Miss Steele exclaimed, taking Elinor's arm familiarly. "I wanted to see you above all things in the world." Lowering her voice, she added, "I suppose Mrs. Jennings has heard the whole story. Is she angry?"

"Not at all, I believe, with you," Elinor replied.

"That is wonderful. And Lady Middleton—is she angry?"

"I cannot imagine it possible," Elinor said.

"I am monstrously glad of that! Good gracious! I have had such a time of it! I never saw Lucy in such a rage in my entire life. She swore initially she would never trim a new bonnet for me or do anything else for me again so long as she lived. But now she has completely recovered, and we are as good friends as ever. Look, she made me this bow to my hat and inserted the feather last night. Now, *you* are going to laugh at me too. But why shouldn't I wear pink ribbons? I don't care if it is the Doctor's favorite color. I am certain, for my part, I would never have known he preferred it to any other shade if he hadn't happened to mention it. My cousins have been plaguing me endlessly! I declare sometimes I don't know which way to turn before them."

She drifted off onto a subject where Elinor had nothing to contribute, so Elinor soon deemed it prudent to steer the conversation back to its original course.

"Well, but Miss Dashwood," Miss Steele said triumphantly, "people may say whatever they please about Mr. Ferrars declaring he wouldn't have Lucy, but it isn't true—I can tell you that myself. It is quite shameful for such malicious rumors to circulate. Whatever Lucy might think privately, you know, it was no business of others to assume certainty."

"I never heard anything of the sort suggested before, I assure you," Elinor stated.

"Oh, did you not? But it *was* said, I know, quite clearly, by more than one person. Miss Godby told Miss Sparks that no sensible person expected Mr. Ferrars to abandon a woman like Miss Morton, with thirty thousand pounds to her fortune, for Lucy Steele, who had nothing at all. I received this from Miss Sparks myself. Furthermore, my cousin Richard admitted that when it came to the point, he feared Mr. Ferrars would back out. When Edward didn't appear near us for three days, I couldn't make sense of it. I believe in my heart Lucy thought it all lost. We left your brother's house on Wednesday, and we saw nothing of him throughout Thursday, Friday, and Saturday, unsure of his whereabouts. Once Lucy considered writing to him, but her spirits rebelled against that idea. However, this morning he arrived just as we returned from church, and then everything came out. He had been summoned Wednesday to Harley Street, spoken to by his mother and everyone else, and had declared before them all that he loved no one but Lucy and would have no one but Lucy. He was so distressed by these proceedings that as soon as he left his mother's house, he mounted his horse and rode into the countryside, somewhere or other. He stayed at various inns all Thursday and Friday, deliberately trying to recover his composure. After pondering it repeatedly, he remarked that since he had no fortune and nothing at all, it would seem unkind to maintain the engagement, as it would inevitably lead to her ruin. With only two thousand pounds and no hope of more, and considering that if he entered the clergy—as he was contemplating—he could secure nothing but a curacy, how could they survive on that? He couldn't bear the thought of her settling for less, so he begged, if she had the slightest inclination, to end the matter immediately and let him manage his own affairs. I heard him say all this as plainly as possible. It was entirely for *her* sake, and on *her* account, that he mentioned withdrawing, not for his own reasons. I swear on my life he never dropped a syllable about being tired of her, or wishing to marry Miss Morton, or anything similar. But, naturally, Lucy wouldn't listen to such reasoning. She told him directly—with plenty of talk about sweetness and love—you know, and all that—Oh, la! one can't repeat such things, you know—that she had not the slightest desire to break off. She insisted that she could live with him on a trifling amount, however little he might possess, and she would be very glad to accept it all, or something of that nature. Consequently, he was overjoyed and talked for some time about their future plans. They agreed he should take holy orders immediately and wait until he secured a living before marrying. Just then, I couldn't hear anymore, as my cousin called from downstairs to inform me that Mrs. Richardson had arrived in her carriage and would take one of us to Kensington Gardens. I was forced to go upstairs and interrupt them to ask Lucy if she wished to go, but she didn't care to leave Edward. So, I simply ran upstairs, put on a pair of silk stockings, and came down with the Richardsons."

"I do not understand what you mean by 'interrupting them,'" Elinor said. "You were all in the same room together, weren't you?"

"No, indeed, not us. La! Miss Dashwood, do you really think people make love when someone else is present? Oh, for shame! To be sure, you must know better than that. (Laughing affectingly.) No, no; they were locked away in the drawing-room together, and all I heard was merely by listening at the door."

"How!" cried Elinor. "Have you been recounting to me what you learned solely by eavesdropping at the door? I am sorry I wasn't aware beforehand, for I certainly would not have allowed you to share details of a conversation you ought not to have known yourself. How could you behave so unfairly toward your sister?"

"Oh, la! there is nothing in *that*. I simply stood at the door and heard what I could. And I am sure Lucy would have done exactly the same by me. A year or two ago, when Martha Sharpe and I shared so many secrets, she never hesitated to hide in a closet or behind a chimney-piece on purpose to overhear what we said."
