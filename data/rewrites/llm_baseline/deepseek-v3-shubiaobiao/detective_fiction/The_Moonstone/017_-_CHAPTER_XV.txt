Sergeant Cuff fell into silence again, his sharp mind turning over the puzzle before us, until we reached the edge of the fir plantation leading down to the quicksand. There, as if arriving at some private conclusion, he roused himself and addressed me with renewed focus.

“Mr. Betteredge,” he began, “since you’ve been good enough to assist me—and since you may prove useful before the night is through—I see no reason to dance around the truth any longer. You refuse to speak against Rosanna Spearman out of loyalty and pity. Admirable sentiments, but entirely wasted in this case. Rosanna is in no danger—none at all, even if I prove her involvement in the Diamond’s disappearance as clearly as I see your nose before me.”

“You mean my lady won’t press charges?” I asked.

“I mean she *can’t*,” the Sergeant corrected. “Rosanna is merely a pawn in another’s game, and she’ll be shielded for that person’s sake.”

His tone brooked no argument. Yet something in me still resisted. “Can’t you name this other person?” I pressed.

“Can’t *you*, Mr. Betteredge?”

“No.”

Sergeant Cuff stopped dead, studying me with something between pity and fascination.

“I’ve always had a soft spot for human weakness,” he mused. “At this moment, Mr. Betteredge, I feel particularly tender toward *you*. And you, in turn, feel tender toward Rosanna, don’t you? Tell me—has she had new linen lately?”

The question caught me off guard. Seeing no harm in honesty, I admitted that Rosanna had arrived with scant wardrobe and that my lady, pleased with her conduct, had gifted her fresh linens not two weeks prior.

“A wretched world indeed,” sighed the Sergeant. “Life’s a target, Mr. Betteredge, and misfortune never misses. Without that new linen, we’d have found a paint-stained nightgown among her things and had our proof. You follow me, don’t you? You’ve seen what the maids discovered outside her door. You know what she did after falling ‘ill’ yesterday. Must I spell it out? It’s plain as daylight. When Seegrave showed the smear on the door, Rosanna—suspecting her own garments—slipped away to replace the stained item. She crafted a new one in secret, lit a fire (not to burn evidence, mind you—too risky with prying eyes nearby), and now, mark my words, she’s disposing of the original somewhere along that desolate shore ahead of us. I tracked her to Cobb’s Hole tonight, to a particular cottage. She left with something concealed beneath her cloak—and mark this, Mr. Betteredge—a cloak hides many sins. She headed north along the coast. Tell me, do you consider this shoreline picturesque?”

“Yes,” I replied curtly.

“Tastes differ,” he said dryly. “From a detective’s view, it’s a miserable place to tail someone—no cover at all. I had to choose between confronting her or letting her play out her hand. For reasons I’ll keep to myself, I chose the latter. Now, sand is an excellent witness—it remembers every step. If we don’t intercept Rosanna, the sand may yet tell her secrets. Shall I take the lead?”

A feverish curiosity had taken hold of me. I nodded, and the Sergeant moved ahead, scanning the ground with hawk-like precision. I followed, my pulse quickening.

The fading light cast long shadows over the Shivering Sand, a place that had always unnerved me. The tide was turning, the quicksand’s surface rippling faintly—the only movement in that dead, silent expanse.

The Sergeant stiffened as the quivering sand caught his eye. After a moment’s study, he turned back.

“Treacherous ground,” he muttered. “No sign of Rosanna—yet.”

He led me farther down, where only our own footprints marked the shore.

“Where lies Cobb’s Hole from here?” he asked.

“Due south,” I answered.

“Then she walked *toward* this spot,” he mused. “Can we reach the village by the beach now?”

I confirmed we could.

“Then let’s move briskly,” he said. “I want to find where she left the shore before dark.”

We hadn’t gone far when the Sergeant dropped suddenly to his knees.

“Your marine landscape has its uses,” he remarked. “Here—a woman’s footsteps. Rosanna’s, unless proven otherwise. Notice how they’re deliberately muddled. She knows sand’s betraying nature as well as I do. But haste made her careless. See here—steps toward the water’s edge, then returning. Heel marks near the rocks. She waded out, circled back, then resumed her path further down. Why? Because she carried something she meant to hide—not destroy, or why such pains to conceal her route? Let’s see what the cottage reveals.”

My enthusiasm waned. “What good am I there?”

“Modesty becomes you, Mr. Betteredge,” he said, almost amused. “But your presence loosens tongues. Alone, they’d clam up. With you, conversation flows.”

Reluctantly, I agreed.

The cottage belonged to the Yollands—decent folk, parents to Limping Lucy, Rosanna’s friend. If she had visited them, surely it was innocent? Perhaps the Sergeant’s suspicions were misplaced.

Mrs. Yolland welcomed us, starry-eyed at hosting London’s famed detective. The Sergeant, ever indirect, steered talk from fish prices to Rosanna’s hardships, painting himself as her champion. Within minutes, Mrs. Yolland was pouring him gin, singing Rosanna’s praises.

Then came the revelation: Rosanna meant to leave us. No warning to my lady or me—a breach of propriety. And she’d bought traveling supplies, paid for them herself, though Mrs. Yolland fretted over taking the girl’s savings.

“She wrote a long letter upstairs,” Mrs. Yolland added. “Wouldn’t say to whom. But she’s got a friend somewhere—mark my words.”

I bristled. “She’d have told *me* first.”

Mrs. Yolland laughed. “She bought chains and a tin case from me tonight—for her ‘cuffs and collars,’ she said. Odd choices, but there’s no fathoming Rosanna.”

The Sergeant’s eyes gleamed. He examined the items—cheap, yet telling. My heart sank. Without meaning to, I’d led him straight to damning clues.

When we finally left, the Sergeant was pensive. “Mrs. Yolland has given me much to ponder,” he admitted.

I said nothing. The night’s discoveries weighed heavily, and I couldn’t shake the feeling that I’d unwittingly betrayed Rosanna—and myself.
