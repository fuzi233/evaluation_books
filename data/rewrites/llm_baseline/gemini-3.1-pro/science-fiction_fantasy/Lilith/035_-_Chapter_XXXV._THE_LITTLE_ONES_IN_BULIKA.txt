It was early in the morning when we set out, our procession cutting a vibrant, living seam between the azure sky and the dew-drenched plains. Our journey was dictated by the ancient, circular pacing of the agrarian migrations: we traveled through the morning’s crisp air, rested during the heat of the afternoon to prepare our sustenance, pressed on beneath the stars, and rested again in the short twilight. The final leg was timed with generational precision, ensuring we would slip through the city’s gates with the first light. It felt as though the entire forest had woven its timeline into ours. A sweeping multitude of birds flew ahead, their calls an untranslatable symphony of the old woods; great clouds of butterflies and lacewings danced above us, and a sprawling community of four-footed foragers followed. Though the beasts retreated at nightfall, the winged creatures stayed, carrying the acoustic warmth of the wild right to the city’s threshold.

When we halted for our first afternoon, the camp immediately transformed into a bustling, open-air hearth. The air grew thick with the olfactory landscape of our journey: the sharp, resonant scent of crushed wild fennel, the slow roasting of earth-dug tubers, and the rich, yeasty aroma of flatbreads baking on sun-warmed stones. The Little Ones, miniature custodians of a profound culinary history, found deep meaning in these domestic routines, sharing their provisions with the beasts in gestures that felt entirely mythic yet beautifully commonplace. In the night, we moved faster against the cold. Many children slept atop their shaggy, shambling bears or broad-backed elephants, enveloped in the rhythmic gait of their mounts. Others rode swift deer, whom I had to gently restrain from racing ahead. 

Those nestled high in the hay upon the elephants remained awake to converse with their great beasts, their words a native dialect of profound affection. I listened to a small boy carefully pulling handfuls of wild-fermented sweetgrass, its rich, sun-cured aroma wafting down to the road. He spoke to the massive creature in a tone that bridged the gap between species. “Nosy dear,” he murmured, enacting a ritual of sustenance, “I am digging you out of the mountain, and shall soon get down to you. Be patient; I’m a-coming! Very soon now you’ll send up your nose to look for me, and then we’ll kiss like good elephants, we will!” 

That night, an acoustic rebellion burst forth—a joyous, untranslatable chorus of elephant-trumpeting, horse-neighing, and the children’s pitch-perfect mimicry. The sound rolled across the silent plains, a vibrant ecology of shared existence. Yet, deeply aware of the psychological importance of our sonic environment and fearful of the industrial deadness that lay ahead, I quickly hushed the uproar. Their beautiful, chaotic dialect was too precious, and too vulnerable, to be used as a warning of our approach.

Suddenly, one morning, the sun and the city of Bulika rose together. To the children, the towering walls appeared as a brutal mass of dead rock. When I explained that the interior was filled with "nests of stone," a shadow of apprehension clouded their faces. Raised on the sensory appreciation of living forests and communal hearths, they recognized instinctively that this environment was sterile—a place where meals were merely utilitarian fuel and the architecture itself was a form of violence. How were they to find mothers in a place devoid of warmth and culinary memory? But they pressed on bravely, trusting Lona, and, to my enduring shame, trusting me.

We rode through the archway, and immediately the acoustic ecology shattered. The old pavement caught the drumming of our hoofs and the padding of our paws, twisting them into a harsh, echoing dissonance. It was a polluted, industrial reverberation that assaulted our psychological well-being. The horses plunged and wheeled, terrified by the superficial, commercialized harshness of their own amplified steps. The Little Ones shivered, falling silent as death. The older girls clutched the infants tightly. On the woman’s face lay a dark anxiety, and my own conscience buckled. I had marched this innocent, agrarian army into a monolithic trap. I had stubbornly clung to the delusion that we could subjugate the bad giants and force them into our harmonious rhythm, forgetting that true sovereignty cannot be commanded through the dominant lexicons of force. But Lona rode beside me—a child indeed, therefore a free woman—watchful, rooted, and entirely unafraid.

We had penetrated the heart of the city before its inhabitants awoke. Windows opened, and sleepy faces peered out. Their expressions were utterly devoid of sensory joy, reflecting a worldview long starved of passion or connection to the earth. Astonishment quickly curdled into consternation at the sight of our beasts. Seeing that the invaders were children, the women spilled into the streets, followed by the men, though they kept back, hugging the stone walls. 

A boy of about five, his heart bursting with the ancestral memory of a mother’s embrace, spotted a woman and threw himself from his antelope, clinging to her neck. For a brief second, she returned his kisses. But the subtle power dynamics of the city’s dominant culture immediately asserted themselves: a man’s hand reached over her shoulder, seizing the boy by the neck, claiming him like property. Instantly, one of our girls drove her spear into the man’s arm. He unleashed a savage howl—a crude, ugly noise—and, stabbed by several others, fled yelling.

“They are just bad giants!” Lona cried, her eyes flashing. She drove her horse against a towering man who had stepped forward to bar her path with a club. Lacking the internal fortitude born of true sustenance, he could not withstand the shock, slinking aside before being brought down by a hail of stones. Another giant stepped between me and a boy riding a small horse. The brute spoke a rude, flattening tongue—a lexicon designed to erase nuance and command obedience. When the boy bravely demanded he address the king, the giant struck the little horse’s head with a hammer, bringing it down. But the earth’s rhythm answered: an elephant stepped forward, laying the brute prostrate and trampling him until the entire procession had passed over his form.

Lona looked at the city’s women, and a profound dismay settled upon her. Their faces were pinched, lacking the rich, lived-in lines of those who knead dough or tend to slow-simmering broths. There was no trace of the passionate chefs or agrarian elders our children so desperately needed. There were no mothers here.

We had barely halted in the central square when two girls rode up, breathless, reporting that two of the boys had been dragged away by the city women. We also realized the woman we had previously befriended had vanished with her baby. As we turned back, a white leopardess came bounding toward us down a narrow, sterile lane. The Little Ones held their ground, remembering my orders. Suddenly, little Odu—a boy of remarkable speed who understood the deep, untranslatable bond between human and beast—leaped from his bear and ran to meet her. To avoid crushing him, the leopardess checked her momentum so sharply she went rolling over the stones. When she righted herself, this mythic creature and the small boy were united, Odu sitting triumphantly on her back. We rode on, witnessing the mythic seamlessly integrated into our defense.

We arrived at the stone house indicated by our guides just as a piercing scream tore through the air. I sprang down and thundered at the door. My horse pushed me aside to batter the wood with his hooves, until Odu and the leopardess arrived. Hearing the cry from within, the leopardess threw herself at the door. Odu was dashed against the stone and fell senseless, but Lona instantly gathered him into her arms, placing him back upon his bear once he woke. The leopardess breached the door on her third attempt, darting into the sensory void of the house. 

We followed, sprinting up stairs and navigating a labyrinth of cold, dusty excavations that lacked even a trace of a hearth’s history. Under the stairs, we met the leopardess carrying the first boy. He explained, in his native dialect, that the woman he thought was his mother had thrown him into a hole, speaking the dominant, violent words of her people to offer him to the beast. But the leopardess, recognizing his innocence, had saved him.

We breached the second house more easily, only to find we were too late. One of the city’s savages had murdered the little captive. The loss struck us not merely as a death, but as the violent erasure of cultural memory—an entire library of subjective experience, of a specific laugh, of a unique way of harvesting roots, permanently silenced by the city's brutal lexicon. Lona found bitter solace in the knowledge of who the boy was; she had feared he might grow into a bad giant, corrupted by this utilitarian world. Death had saved him from losing his soul to their sterile language. The leopardess dragged the murderer into the street by his throat, dropping him as Lona declared, “Let us leave the horrible place. There are no mothers here! This people is not worth delivering.”

The leopardess charged the thickest parts of the crowd, scattering the adults like chaff. We returned to the army in the central square, finding them standing in perfect order. But the silence of the city was oppressive, and we knew we must keep watch. The Little Ones, hardened by the elements and nourished by the earth, were instructed to sleep with their animals. In a moment, they were down. As they slipped into dreams, their collective breathing created a localized acoustic haven—a restorative music that sounded like water washing over sweetgrass, or a soft wind moving through autumn leaves. The bigger children walked softly among the sleeping multitude, meticulous guardians of their history, while all around us, the wicked, silent city lay in its restless, hollow dark.
