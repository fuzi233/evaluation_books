The morning sun broke gently over the Downs, illuminating the path where Elinor and Marianne walked side by side. It was during this quiet stroll that Marianne, with a brightness in her eyes that seemed to outshine the day itself, shared a revelation with her sister. Despite Elinor's long familiarity with Marianne's impulsive nature, the sheer extravagance of the news struck her with genuine surprise. Willoughby, Marianne announced with rapturous delight, had gifted her a horse—a fine animal he had bred personally on his Somersetshire estate, perfectly suited to carry a lady.

Marianne spoke of the gift without a single hesitation regarding the practicalities. She had not considered that her mother had no intention of keeping a horse, nor that accepting such a present would necessitate hiring an additional servant to ride it, purchasing a second mount for that servant, and constructing a stable to house them all. Yet, she had accepted the offer immediately and now described the prospect of daily rides across the Downs to Elinor with such fervor that she seemed to live entirely within the dream.

"He intends to send his groom to Somersetshire at once," Marianne continued, her voice rising with excitement. "And when the horse arrives, we shall ride every single day. You must share it with me, Elinor! Imagine the joy of a gallop on these very hills!"

Elinor found herself reluctant to shatter this vision of happiness with the sobering realities that inevitably followed. For a moment, she tried to dismiss the obstacles as trivial. The cost of an extra servant would be negligible; her mother would surely agree to it; any ordinary horse would suffice for the servant, who could likely find one at the park; and a simple shed would serve as a stable. However, Elinor eventually dared to voice her true concern: the impropriety of accepting such a substantial gift from a man she knew so little of, or at least, so recently.

"You are mistaken, Elinor," Marianne replied, her tone warming with defensive passion. "You suppose I know Willoughby very little. While I have not known him long, my acquaintance with him surpasses that of any other person in the world, save yourself and Mama. Intimacy is not determined by time or opportunity, but by disposition alone. Seven years might fail to bring two people close, while seven days can be enough for others. I would consider it far greater impropriety to accept a horse from my brother John, whom I have known for years yet know so little of, than from Willoughby, whose judgment I have long formed."

Realizing that opposing Marianne on such a tender subject would only bind her more tightly to her own opinion, Elinor wisely chose to tread lightly. Instead, she appealed to Marianne's deep affection for their mother, painting a picture of the inconvenience and burden such an expansion of their household would place upon their indulgent parent. Slowly, Marianne's enthusiasm cooled. She promised not to tempt her mother into imprudent kindness by mentioning the offer and vowed to decline Willoughby's gift the next time she saw him.

True to her word, when Willoughby visited the cottage later that day, Elinor heard Marianne express her sorrow in a low voice, explaining why she was forced to refuse his present. Her reasons were stated clearly enough to render further persuasion on Willoughby's part futile. His distress was evident; after voicing his regret earnestly, he added, still speaking softly, "But, Marianne, the horse remains yours, even if you cannot use it now. I shall keep it merely until you can claim it. When you leave Barton to establish your own home in a more permanent dwelling, Queen Mab shall welcome you."

These words, spoken in a hushed tone, were overheard by Miss Dashwood. In the cadence of his voice, the intimacy of his address—calling her sister by her Christian name alone, and the possessive nature of his promise—Elinor instantly perceived a connection so profound and direct that it signaled a complete understanding between them. From that moment, she ceased to doubt that they were engaged. The only surprise this realization caused was that two individuals of such frank tempers should remain hidden from their friends until discovered by accident.

The following day, Margaret provided confirmation that cast the matter in an even clearer light. Willoughby had spent the previous evening with the family, and Margaret, having been left alone in the parlour with just the two of them, had ample opportunity for observation. With a grave and important expression, she approached her eldest sister when they were finally alone.

"Oh, Elinor!" she exclaimed. "I have a secret to tell you about Marianne. I am certain she will be married to Mr. Willoughby very soon."

"You have said almost every day since they first met on High-church Down," Elinor replied dryly. "They had not known each other a week before you were convinced Marianne wore his picture around her neck; yet it turned out to be merely the miniature of our great uncle."

"But this is quite different," Margaret insisted. "I am sure they will marry soon because he possesses a lock of her hair."

"Be careful, Margaret. It may be the hair of some great uncle of his."

"But, indeed, Elinor, it is Marianne's. I am almost certain, for I saw him cut it off. Last night after tea, when you and Mama left the room, they were whispering and talking as fast as possible. He seemed to be begging something of her, and then he took her scissors and cut a long lock from her hair, which was tumbling down her back. He kissed it, folded it carefully into a piece of white paper, and placed it inside his pocket-book."

Given the specific details and the authority of her witness, Elinor could not withhold her belief, nor was she inclined to do so, for the circumstances aligned perfectly with what she had observed herself.

Yet, Margaret's sagacity was not always displayed to her sister's satisfaction. When Mrs. Jennings one evening pressed the family to name the young man who was Elinor's particular favorite—a question that had long been a source of great curiosity—Margaret responded by looking at her sister and asking, "Must I not tell, may I, Elinor?"

This response naturally drew laughter from everyone present, and Elinor attempted to join in, though the effort was painful. She was convinced that Margaret had selected a person whose name she could not bear to have become a standing joke among Mrs. Jennings' circle.

Marianne felt deeply for her younger sister but inadvertently caused more harm than good by turning a deep red and speaking angrily to Margaret. "Remember that whatever your conjectures may be, you have no right to repeat them."

"I never had any conjectures about it," Margaret retorted. "It was you who told me of it yourself."

This exchange only increased the mirth of the company, and Margaret was eagerly pressed to reveal more.

"Oh! pray, Miss Margaret, let us know all about it," urged Mrs. Jennings. "What is the gentleman's name?"

"I must not tell, ma'am. But I know very well what it is; and I know where he is too."

"Yes, yes, we can guess where he is; at his own house at Norland, I suppose. He is the curate of the parish, I dare say."

"No, *that* he is not. He is of no profession at all."

"Margaret," said Marianne with great warmth, "you know that all this is an invention of your own, and that there is no such person in existence."

"Well, then, he is lately dead, Marianne, for I am sure there was such a man once, and his name begins with an F."

At this critical moment, Elinor felt immense gratitude toward Lady Middleton for observing, "that it rained very hard." She believed the interruption stemmed less from any attention paid to her than from her ladyship's distinct dislike of the inelegant subjects of raillery that delighted her husband and mother. Nevertheless, the idea introduced by Lady Middleton was immediately pursued by Colonel Brandon, who was ever mindful of the feelings of others. Much was said on the subject of rain by both of them. Willoughby then opened the piano-forte and invited Marianne to sit, and thus, amidst the various efforts of different people to shift the topic, the conversation finally fell to the ground. Yet, Elinor did not so easily recover from the alarm into which the speculation had thrown her.

That evening, a party was organized to visit the following day a magnificent estate located about twelve miles from Barton. The property belonged to a brother-in-law of Colonel Brandon; without his influence, it could not be seen, as the proprietor, currently abroad, had issued strict orders prohibiting visits. The grounds were described as highly beautiful, and Sir John, who was particularly enthusiastic in his praise, could certainly be considered a tolerable judge, having hosted parties there at least twice every summer for the past decade. The plans included a noble piece of water where sailing would form a major part of the morning's amusement. Cold provisions were to be carried, only open carriages employed, and everything conducted in the usual style of a complete party of pleasure.

To some members of the company, however, the undertaking seemed rather bold, considering the season and the fact that it had rained every day for the last fortnight. Mrs. Dashwood, who already suffered from a cold, was persuaded by Elinor to remain at home.
