A Pedigree and Other Family Matters

In the early days of the reign of George the Magnificent, a certain gentleman named Pendennis resided in the quiet town of Clavering, tucked away in the west of England. To those with long memories, his name was etched onto a signboard above a humble shop in Bath, crowned by a gilded pestle and mortar. Here, Mr. Pendennis practiced the dual arts of apothecary and surgeon. He was a man of considerable versatility: attending to the sick in their chambers, assisting ladies during their most delicate moments, and yet, with a condescension that bordered on the comical, selling brown-paper plasters to farmwives or vending toothbrushes and London perfumes to anyone who wandered across his counter.

Yet, beneath the apron and the mortar, John Pendennis was a man of high breeding and ancient lineage. His Cornish pedigree traced the Pendennises back to the Druids, intermingled with the Normans and kin to the great houses of Wales and Brittany. He possessed a university education that might have launched him into glory, had not fate intervened. In his second year at Cambridge, his father died insolvent, forcing poor Pen to abandon his scholarly dreams for the pestle and apron. He loathed the trade, viewing it as a necessary evil imposed by circumstance and the marriage of his father to a London apothecary of lowly stock.

After a brief apprenticeship with a coarse-minded relative, John set up his own modest practice in Bath. Poverty gnawed at him for some time; he struggled to maintain his shop's gilt ornaments and keep his bedridden mother comfortable. Then came the fateful encounter with Lady Ribstone. Passing through with an intoxicated Irish chairman who carelessly drove a chair-pole through a pink bottle in his window, she collapsed screaming. John, with manners uncommonly soothing for a man of his trade, revived her with cinnamon and sal-volatile. Lady Ribstone, the wife of Sir Pepin Ribstone of Codlingbury, was so charmed by his gentleness that she appointed him her personal apothecary. When her son, Master Ribstone, fell ill with a fever after overeating at Eton, John treated him with such skill and tenderness that he secured the enduring favor of the Codlingbury family.

From that day, his fortunes turned. The good company of Bath took notice; he was beloved by the ladies. His humble shop transformed into a smart establishment, then shed its unworthy merchandise of toothbrushes and perfumes. Soon, he shuttered the shop entirely, retaining only a genteel surgery. He acquired a gig, and before his mother's passing, she watched with joyous tears as her beloved John stepped into a private carriage, albeit a one-horse affair, proudly displaying the Pendennis arms. "What would Arthur say now?" she lamented, thinking of her younger son, who had never visited during his father's struggles.

"Captain Pendennis is with his regiment in India, mother," John replied gently, requesting she cease calling him "Johnny" before Mr. Parkins.

When his mother eventually passed, the house grew lonely without her querulous voice. John altered his night-bell to her former room and took up residence in the great bed there. He was over forty, a man who had lived before the war and before the current history began, yet he was a gentleman with a pedigree to match. He displayed his framed genealogy prominently in his drawing-room, alongside pictures of Codlingbury House and St. Boniface's College, treating his ancestry with the same reverence a soldier treats his sword.

Around the time of his mother's death, another patient, the virtuous Lady Pontypool, also passed away. She was the widow of the second Lord Pontypool and the great-grand-aunt of the present Earl of Bareacres. For her final years, she had been cared for by Miss Helen Thistlewood, a distant relative who endured the elder lady's tyranny with angelic patience. It was during the journey to the abbey for Lady Pontypool's funeral that John Pendennis, a collected thirty-four-year-old in black stockings and a bald head, resolved to ask Miss Thistlewood a question that made his pulse race.

Older than her by more than twenty years and not naturally ardent, John approached the situation with the gravity of a man who had perhaps strangled a love affair in his youth. He asked Miss Thistlewood where she intended to live now that the Pontypool fortune had been packed into boxes and buried under straw. When she tearfully admitted she did not know, having only a thousand pounds left, John proposed that she come and live with him. He was old compared to her, he admitted, but of good birth, good principles, and good temper. He needed a companion, and she, he reasoned, needed security.

Though she might have preferred a younger man, she recognized his worth, his prudence, and his honor. Blushing deeply, she made a low curtsey and asked to consider his kind proposal. They were married during the dull Bath season, which was the height of the season in London. John secured lodgings in Cavendish Square, took his wife to the theaters and parks, and introduced her to society. He left calling cards for the Earls and Lords, though the Earl of Bareacres ignored them, while Lord Pontypool merely sent a footman with a delayed invitation.

John's secret ambition had always been to be a gentleman. Through frugality, a lucky investment in copper-mining shares, and the sale of his Bath business for a handsome sum, he achieved his dream. He purchased a house and small estate near Clavering. He walked the acres he now owned with a pride he never confessed aloud. He dressed as an English gentleman should, in a bottle-green coat and brass buttons, sinking the black breeches of his medical past. He founded the Clavering Book Club, improved the local charities, and redirected the mail route through his town. At market, he inspected oats and weighed geese with a knowing air, demanding to be called "Squire" rather than "Doctor."

His dining room was adorned with portraits he claimed were Lelys and Vandykes, ancestors of his bloodline. While his wife, skeptical of these legends, tried to steer the conversation elsewhere, their young son, Arthur, believed them completely. To little Arthur, Roger Pendennis of Agincourt and General Pendennis of Blenheim were as real as Robinson Crusoe.

With an income of eight hundred pounds a year, John lived comfortably among the second-best sort of society. They dined in moonlight, hosted festivals, and enjoyed the company of the town, though they often blessed themselves that their home, Fairoaks, was a mile away from the prying eyes of Clavering. The house itself was a splendid freestone palace, built by a grandfather who ruined the family by constructing it, and inhabited by a successor who ruined it further by living in it. The present Sir Francis Clavering was abroad, leaving the mansion to decay, its halls moldy and its galleries dismal. Yet, at sunset, the lawn of Fairoaks and the park of Clavering glowed with a rich golden light, a scene that moved young Arthur to recite poetry about the "Parent of Good."

John Pendennis was a man of rigid order. His hat was always brushed, his meals served punctually, and his household ran like a well-oiled machine. He napped after dinner with the *Globe* on his knee, often accompanied by a yellow bandanna handkerchief sent by his brother, Major Pendennis, from India. While the sun set and his wife and son enjoyed the view, John remained aloof, preferring the *Gardener's Gazette* or a game of picquet.

The Major, John's brother, was the family's link to the great world. Having sold out from the army and settled on half-pay, he was the bearer of news from London and the fashion plates. He dined with lords, boasted of invitations to pheasant shoots, and famously rejected the hand of Miss Hunkle, a wealthy attorney's daughter, declaring that as a bachelor, he preferred the company of the highly placed over a fortune. Time proved the Major correct; Miss Hunkle married a French nobleman and became Baroness de Carambole.

Mrs. Pendennis was a woman of tranquil beauty and natural sweetness, revered by her husband and praised by her brother. She moved through the world with a perfection that enraged some country ladies, who found her too proud for her station. Yet, she remained calm, praying, loving her family, and doing her duty. Her only flaw, as perceived by her neighbors, was a pride that vested itself not in her person, but in her family. She spoke of her husband with the reverence due a Pope and of her son with an idolatrous ardor. This superstition, this worship of her family, would later become the source of much misfortune for the young gentleman who was to be the hero of this history.

At Greyfriars School, Arthur Pendennis was neither a dunce nor a scholar. He did exactly what was required and no more. He was distinguished for verse-writing, composing just enough lines to satisfy the regulations, unlike his more zealous peers. He devoured novels and plays outside of school hours, avoided flogging by sheer luck, and spent his money royally on tarts. He was averse to fighting and Greek grammar, telling the truth but never bullying others. When his masters called him a profligate destined for ruin, he simply grew accustomed to the insult, having not yet committed any act worthy of transportation.

While other boys smoked cigars and kept carriages, young Pen remained modest and timid, though by twelve he had heard enough talk to be awfully wise on certain points. At sixteen, he made his public appearance in a coat with a tail, anxiously checking his study-glass for whiskers that never seemed to grow. His voice had dropped into a deep bass, a change that amused his masters.

It was at the close of a forenoon session that his academic life ended abruptly. Called to construe a Greek play, Pen failed miserably, stumbling over the words despite the prompting of his form-fellow. The Doctor, a man of terrifying wrath, launched into a harangue, declaring Pen's idleness incorrigible and his stupidity beyond example. He spoke of the "root of all evil" and the crimes of filial ingratitude, threatening the rod for the next mistake.

Just as the Doctor reached his crescendo, a gentleman entered the room. It was Major Pendennis. He had written on his card, "I must take A. P. home; his father is very ill." The Doctor, startled, stopped his oration. The boys burst into laughter, then silence at the command. Pen, seeing his uncle, was delivered from the classroom.

As the Major drove Pen away in a postchaise, the hostler winked ominously, and the gardener shook his head. Upon arriving at Fairoaks, they found the blinds drawn and the atmosphere heavy with gloom. The old footman's face was blank. Inside, the room was darkened, surrounded by the gloomy portraits of the Pendennises. The doctor and a lawyer from Clavering arrived shortly after, confirming the news: John Pendennis had seized and died.

Mrs. Pendennis bore the loss wonderfully, comforted by the arrival of her son. The Major, as executor and guardian, took charge of the household affairs. The lawyer discussed business, and the Major assumed total superintendence. The old footman, John, followed the Major to bed with the candle and returned the next morning with the key to the hall clock, a ritual he had performed for years.

What passed between the mother and son in those sacred moments of grief is best left veiled. Mrs. Pendennis, who always revered her husband as the most upright and awful of men, found solace in her maternal love. She maintained the memory of her husband with the most precious oil, ensuring his memorial remained handsome.

As for Arthur Pendennis, the shock of his father's death was profound. Yet, amidst the tears and the embrace of his mother, a feeling of secret triumph sprang up in his breast. He was now the chief, the lord of the house. With his mother's blessing and the servants' deference, he resolved that his school days were over. He would not get up until he liked, and he would never again endure the bullying of the Doctor. Walking through the kitchen and kennels, he was greeted with hushed reverence by the servants, who bowed and curtseyed as they never had before. He patted the dogs, inspected the hens, and looked at the orchard, a young master dreaming of a future of endless holidays and absolute freedom.

John Pendennis, Esquire, was buried with one of the most handsome funerals the county had seen since the days of Sir Roger Clavering. A fair marble slab was erected in the abbey church, bearing the Pendennis coat of arms and the motto *nec tenui penna*. The Doctor preached a touching sermon, and Arthur Pendennis reigned in his stead, the new master of Fairoaks.
