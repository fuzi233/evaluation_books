The smoke in the club's smoking-room hung thick and yellow, a fog that seemed to settle permanently around Captain Willoughby. To the regulars, he was a fixture of irritation, a man whose presence acted like a steam-roller on conversation, flattening any spark of wit into dull ash. He was a relentless raconteur, spinning tales of strangers with a vacant, amicable smile, utterly deaf to the polite snubs that usually forced him out. Yet, Willoughby was irresistible in his own way; his sheer, unadulterated boredom was a trap. Those who didn't know him would eventually flee, but those who did, like the members of his club, simply rose and scattered, leaving him with a large, empty corner of the room.

It was early October when the waiter approached this solitary island of silence, handing Willoughby a card. Willoughby took it eagerly; he craved company, and his usual circle had vanished into urgent engagements. But as he scanned the name, his expression darkened. "Colonel Durrance!" he muttered, scratching his head. Durrance was a man who had never visited him with anything resembling friendship. Why would he come now? It felt as though Durrance had somehow tracked him down to Kingsbridge.

"Does Colonel Durrance know I'm here?" Willoughby asked the waiter.

"Yes, sir."

"Show him in."

Willoughby braced himself. Diplomacy would be required, a delicate dance to deflect questions about Miss Ethne Eustace. He wanted Feversham and Durrance to fight their own battles, not his. As Durrance entered, looking calm and composed, Willoughby watched him with suspicion. To his surprise, Durrance offered no immediate inquiries. Instead, Willoughby rose and guided his visitor to the deserted corner, offering a cigarette with a nervous check.

"Oh, I'll smoke," Durrance replied smoothly. "A man can't truly enjoy tobacco unless he sees the smoke. If I let my cigar go out, I'd know at once. But don't worry, I won't." He lit his cigar with deliberate care and leaned back.

"I'm lucky to find you, Willoughby," Durrance said. "I'm only in town today, coming up from Devonshire to see my oculist. Mather told me you were away in the country on my last visit. You remember Mather? We were together in Suakin."

"Of course," Willoughby said, his tone instantly warmer. Talking about Mather was a safe harbor; perhaps it would distract Durrance from the matter that clearly troubled him.

"We're both curious about one thing," Durrance continued. "Did you ever encounter an Arab named Abou Fatma?"

"Abou Fatma," Willoughby repeated slowly. "One of the Hadendoas?"

"No, a Kabbabish."

"Abou Fatma?" Willoughby feigned ignorance, stopping abruptly. It wasn't a natural pause; he expected a follow-up question. Instead, he was internally panicking, wondering how Durrance knew of a name he himself had only heard once a year prior on the verandah of the Palace at Suakin. He had told the truth; he had never met Abou Fatma, though Feversham had.

"That makes me even more curious," Durrance pressed. "Mather and I were on the last reconnaissance in '84. We found Abou Fatma hiding near Sinkat fort. He spoke of the Gordon letters hidden in Berber. Ah, you remember the name now."

"I was just retrieving my pipe," Willoughby lied, pulling it from his pocket. "But the name rings a bell now that you mention the letters."

"They were brought to you in Suakin some fifteen months ago," Durrance noted. "Mather showed me the report in the *Evening Standard*. I'm curious if Abou Fatma returned to Berber and retrieved them. But since you've never seen him, it follows he wasn't the man."

Willoughby felt a pang of regret for his hasty denial. "No, it wasn't Abou Fatma," he admitted with an awkward hesitation, dreading the next question. He filled his pipe, searching for an answer, but Durrance offered none.

"I wondered," Durrance mused. "I thought Abou Fatma wouldn't return to Berber. After all, whoever took that job did so at the risk of his life, and with Gordon dead, for no obvious reason."

"Quite so," Willoughby breathed, feeling a wave of relief. "Since Gordon was dead, for no reason."

"For no obvious reason, I think I said," Durrance corrected calmly. Willoughby glanced at him suspiciously, wondering if Durrance knew about his trip to Kingsbridge. Durrance, however, seemed satisfied, leaning back with his face tilted toward the ceiling, having lost interest in the Gordon letters. He put no further questions to the trap.

Thinking of how Harry Feversham might redeem himself, Durrance realized the only path was the recovery of those letters from the ruined wall in Berber. There had been no danger in Suakin since that last reconnaissance; the great troop-ships had sailed safely to Suez. Willoughby had risked only his health in that white palace on the Red Sea. There was no moment where Feversham could claim, "Your life was forfeit but for me." Durrance, reviewing the gossip from Wadi Halfa and his furloughs, was convinced Feversham had repeated his story at Suakin within Willoughby's hearing. Willoughby's reticence confirmed it. Durrance, a master of observation, had seen the wariness in Willoughby's voice—the same caution Ethne Eustace often used. He had set traps, specifically the comment about "no obvious reason," and Willoughby had stumbled into them.

Durrance had no desire for Willoughby to warn Ethne. He needed to guard against that possibility.

"I want to tell you why I sought you out," Durrance said. "It was because of Harry Feversham."

Willoughby, congratulating himself on escaping the Abou Fatma interrogation, jumped in his seat. Durrance ignored the agitation. "Something happened to Feversham. Five years ago. He did something, or left something undone—the secret has been kept—and he disappeared. Now, you're returning to the Sudan, Willoughby?"

"Yes, in a week."

"Well, Harry Feversham is in the Sudan," Durrance leaned in. "I met him this spring at Wadi Halfa. He had fallen rather low. It's strange, isn't it? A man known well going under in a second, disappearing as if down an oubliette in an old French castle. I want you to look out for him. Let me know if you find him. Harry Feversham was a friend—one of my few real friends."

"All right," Willoughby said cheerfully, his suspicion momentarily quieted. "I'll look out for Feversham. I remember he was a great friend of yours."

He reached for a match. Durrance heard the scrape of phosphorus and the flare. Willoughby lit his pipe, a well-seasoned briar that bubbled as he sucked on the stem.

"Yes, a great friend," Durrance said. "You and I dined with him in his flat above St. James's Park just before we left England."

At that casual sentence, Willoughby's pipe ceased bubbling. A moment of silence passed, then Willoughby swore violently and stamped his foot on the carpet. Durrance's imagination sparked. He pictured the scene: himself smoking, a round table between them, and Willoughby frozen mid-light, staring suspiciously at the blind man until the match burned his fingers, causing the curse and the stamp. Durrance had never given that dinner a thought until now. It deserved scrutiny.

"There were you, me, and Feversham," Durrance continued. "Feversham asked us there to announce his engagement to Miss Eustace. He had just returned from Dublin. That was almost the last we saw of him." He took a drag and added, "By the way, there was a third man present."

"Was there?" Willoughby asked, feigning forgetfulness. "It's so long ago."

"Yes—Trench."

"To be sure," Willoughby said, his voice carefully careless. "We won't dine with poor old Trench again soon."

"And we shall never dine with Castleton again," Durrance said slowly.

"Castleton wasn't there," Willoughby exclaimed, the slip betraying that the memory was vivid despite the years.

"No, but he was expected," Durrance countered.

"No, not even expected," Willoughby corrected quickly. "He was dining elsewhere. He sent the telegram, you remember."

"Ah, yes, a telegram came," Durrance acknowledged.

That dinner party was the key. Willoughby, Trench, and Castleton were the architects of Harry Feversham's disgrace. Durrance tried to recall the details; he remembered leaning against the window above St. James's Park, hearing the tattoo from Wellington Barracks, and receiving the telegram. He visualized another scene: Feversham at the table, reading and re-reading the message, while Trench and Willoughby waited silently, expectant, and Durrance staring out into the cool park, indifferent.

"Castleton was dining with a big man from the War Office that night," Durrance said. A slight movement at his side warned him he was getting too close. He sat a while longer discussing the Sudan's prospects before rising.

"Well, I can rely on you, Willoughby, to help Feversham if you find him. Draw on me for money."

"I will do my best," Willoughby said. "You're going? I could have won a bet off you this afternoon."

"How?"

"You said you never let your cigars go out. This one is stone cold."

"I forgot; I was thinking of Feversham. Good-bye."

Durrance took a cab and drove away. Willoughby was glad to see him go, satisfied with his diplomatic performance. He had hoodwinked the old colonel, he thought, and returned to the smoking-room for a whiskey and potass.

But Durrance had not been hoodwinked. The final puzzle piece had clicked into place. He recalled that no one at the dinner had identified the sender of the telegram. Feversham had read it, crumpled it, and tossed it into the fire without a word. But now Willoughby claimed it came from Castleton, who was dining with a high War Office official. The act of cowardice that earned the three white feathers at Ramelton became clear. Almost immediately after the telegram arrived, Feversham had resigned his commission. Durrance knew he hadn't resigned when the news came. The telegram must have announced that Feversham's regiment was ordered on active service. The more Durrance reflected, the more certain he became. Nothing was more natural than for Castleton to share good news in confidence.

He now had the complete sequence of facts. Why Feversham had panicked, why he had played the coward the moment after his engagement to Ethne Eustace—a time when every manly quality should have been at its peak—remained an inexplicable mystery. But Durrance set that question aside, dismissing it as trivial. The simple, true reality emerged clear: Harry Feversham was atoning for his single act of cowardice with a full and overflowing measure of penance.

"I shall astonish old Sutch," Durrance chuckled to himself. He took the night mail to Devonshire that same evening, reaching home before midday.
