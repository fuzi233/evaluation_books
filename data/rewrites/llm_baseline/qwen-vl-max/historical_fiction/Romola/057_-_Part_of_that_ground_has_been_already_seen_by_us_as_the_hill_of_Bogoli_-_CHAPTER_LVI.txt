The morning air was already thick with warmth when Romola, returning from San Marco, turned toward the intersecting streets near the gate of Santa Croce. The Borgo La Croce lay quiet and still, so hushed that she could hear the soft thud of her own footsteps on the sunlit pavement. As she neared a bend in the narrow road, a small child—no more than three years old, bare except for a white shirt—caught her eye. He had paused mid-waddle, his head turning slowly as if searching for something lost.

At first, all she saw was his back—a sturdy little boy’s back, broad and firm, topped by a cloud of reddish-brown curls. Then he turned, and she saw his face: dark eyes wide with tears, lower lip trembling, fat brown fists gripping his shirt helplessly. A shadow fell over him—the sudden loom of a tall figure—and at once his fear broke into full cry, great sobs shaking his small frame.

Romola, moved instantly by the instinctive tenderness that lived beneath her composed nature, uncovered her head and knelt beside him. She wrapped her arms around him, pressing her cheek to his, speaking softly, soothingly. At first, the sobs grew louder, but he did not struggle. Then, as abruptly as grief can end in a child, the crying ceased. His face relaxed, his mouth opened in wonder, and he stared up at her.

“You’ve wandered away, little one,” she said, kissing his forehead. “Don’t worry—we’ll find your home again. Perhaps your mother will come to meet us.”

She sensed at once that he had slipped out while his mother’s attention had been elsewhere. She thought it likely he would soon be missed.

“Oh, you’re such a heavy boy!” she teased, trying to lift him. “I can’t carry you. Come then, let’s walk together.”

He remained silent, lips parted in awe, one fist still clenched tight against his shirt. But the other hand reached out without hesitation, grasping her pale, strong fingers.

“You have a mother?” Romola asked gently as they began to move, looking down at him with a quiet yearning. He didn’t answer. A girl might have chattered endlessly under such circumstances; but this little man, square-shouldered and curly-haired, was reserved.

Yet he was alert. At the corner near San Ambrogio, he pulled her arm, pointing ahead.

“That’s the way home, is it?” she smiled. He only tugged harder, urging her forward.

Another turn came, and then Romola found herself in a short street leading to open garden ground. At the end stood a house, and there the boy stopped, pulling her toward stone steps. He clung tightly to her hand, unwilling to let go. She wouldn’t leave him unless she knew he was safe.

They climbed the stairs, stepping into dimness after the bright sunlight. At the top, light poured through an open door. They passed into a small lobby, then into a room where Romola paused—her presence unnoticed.

On a low chair at the far end sat Tessa, one hand resting on the edge of a cradle, her head slightly tilted, fast asleep. Near a window, with her back to the doorway, Monna Lisa worked quietly, preparing salad, deaf to everything. For just a moment, Romola took in the scene before her—then Lillo wrenched his hand free and ran to his mother’s side. He didn’t wake her, only leaned his head against her arm and looked at Romola with solemn curiosity.

Tessa stirred, opened her eyes, and gasped in surprise. Her gaze landed on Romola, and she jumped up, blushing deeply, trembling, unable to speak or move.

“Ah! We’ve met before,” Romola said, smiling. “I’m glad it was your son. He was crying in the street—I suppose he’d run off. So we walked together, and then he remembered the way. You hadn’t missed him? That’s good—else you’d have been frightened.”

The shock of realizing Lillo had escaped overwhelmed Tessa. Her color faded, and she grabbed his arm, rushing to Monna Lisa.

“Oh, Lisa, you’re wicked! Why do you stand with your back to the door? Lillo ran so far!”

“Holy Mother!” Monna Lisa murmured in her soft, thick voice, dropping her spoon. “Where were *you*, then? I thought you were watching him.”

“But you know I fall asleep when I rock,” Tessa snapped, petulant. “I can’t help it.”

“Well, well,” said Monna Lisa, “we must shut the outer door—or tie him up. He’ll be as cunning as Satan before long, and that’s the truth. But how did he get back?”

That brought Tessa’s attention back to Romola. She turned toward her, blushing again, timid. Monna Lisa followed her gaze and bowed low.

“Doubtless the most noble lady brought him back,” she said. Then, stepping closer, she added, “It’s my shame he was found in only his shirt. He kicked this morning—he wouldn’t wear his clothes. And the mother, poor thing, won’t let him be beaten. What can an old woman do without a stick when the boy’s legs grow so strong? Let your nobleness look at them.”

Lillo, aware he was being discussed, lifted his shirt slightly and studied his olive, round limbs with calm interest. Romola laughed, bent down, shook him gently, kissed him. This small gesture calmed Tessa, who had already begun to feel reassured by Monna Lisa’s respectful words.

Naldo had once told her about the Carnival incident—how a heavenly woman had appeared just when she needed help, then vanished. Tessa had asked if she might have been the Holy Madonna. Naldo had replied, “Not exactly—only one of the saints.” So in her simple mind, Romola had become mingled with sacred images. When she returned now, the memory of her kindness was tinged with quiet awe—not deep fear, for Tessa feared only ugly spirits like Nofri and the devil. Good beings, she believed, wouldn’t punish her.

Now, with Monna Lisa speaking plainly and Romola laughing, Tessa felt easier.

“Ninna’s in the cradle,” she said. “*She’s* pretty too.”

Romola approached the cradle. Monna Lisa, one of the rare deaf women who never expected to be addressed, returned to her work.

“Ah! She’s waking—look, her blue eyes are open,” Romola said. “Take her up, and I’ll sit here—may I?—and hold Lillo. Come, Lillo!”

She sat in Tito’s chair, arms outstretched. The boy hesitated, then pointed at her with a puzzled frown.

“That’s Babbo’s chair,” he said.

“But Babbo isn’t here,” Romola replied. “And I’ll be gone soon. Come, let me hold you as he does.”

She wondered silently what kind of man Babbo was—one whose wife dressed like a peasant yet carried herself with quiet grace, hinting at ease and comfort. Lillo agreed to be lifted, and once settled in her lap, began to examine her hands and dress, searching for ornaments beyond the rosary.

Tessa, who had been coaxing Ninna from her grumpiness, now sat nearby, arranging the baby carefully, anxious that the strange lady not favor the boy more than she did.

“Lillo was angry because I sat in Babbo’s chair,” Romola said, leaning forward to kiss Ninna’s foot. “Will he come soon and want it?”

“No,” Tessa answered. “You can stay a long time. I’ll be sorry when you go. When you came to care for me at the Carnival, I thought it was wonderful—you came and went so quickly. Naldo said perhaps you were a saint, and that made me tremble, though saints are good. You were good to me, and now you’ve helped Lillo. Maybe you’ll always come to take care of me. That’s how Naldo used to be—long ago, when I was scared on San Giovanni. I couldn’t tell where he came from—he was so beautiful, so kind. And so are you,” she finished, gazing up at Romola with devout admiration.

“Naldo is your husband,” Romola said, studying the boy’s dark, finely drawn eyebrows—unusual for his age. “His eyes are like Lillo’s.”

She spoke with quiet certainty, not asking—knowing.

“Ah! You know him!” Tessa whispered, amazed. “Perhaps you know Nofri and Peretola, our house on the hill, everything. Yes, like Lillo’s—but not his hair. His hair is dark and long—” she continued, growing excited. “Ah! If you know, *ecco!*”

She touched a thin red silk cord around her neck and drew out a tiny parchment _Breve_, a piece of red coral, and a long, dark curl tied neatly at one end. She held it toward Romola, away from Ninna’s reaching fingers.

“It’s fresh,” she said, placing it against Romola’s pale fingers. “I cut it lately. See how bright it is! They fade, and then he lets me cut another when his hair grows. I keep it with the _Breve_—sometimes he’s gone so long, and I think it helps to protect me.”

A shiver passed through Romola as the curl touched her skin. When Tessa first mentioned her husband coming mysteriously from nowhere, Romola had felt a sudden surge of possibility—something that quickened her pulse. To one seeking answers, even faint clues burn with significance. Now, holding the lock, it seemed for a fleeting second like a mocking echo of the one she herself had cut years ago, winding it with her own hair. Yet she kept her composure. She wanted truth—but not at the cost of hurting this innocent, trusting soul.

“It’s a beautiful curl,” she said, resisting the urge to pull her hand away. “Lillo’s curls may be like it—his cheek is dark too. And you don’t know where your husband goes when he leaves?”

“No,” Tessa said, tucking the treasures safely away. “But I know Messer San Michele watches over him—he gave him a coat made of little chains. If he wears it, no one can kill him. And perhaps—if you *were* a saint—you’d watch over him too, since you’ve watched over me and Lillo.”

A flush rose on Romola’s cheeks—her first sign of certainty. But she pressed her face against Lillo’s head, hiding her emotion. A strange joy surged within her—the thought that her burden might soon be lifted, that this simple woman might be Tito’s lawful wife. A feeling both exultant and humbling for a proud woman to experience. Yet she heard Tessa’s plea, and raised her head.

“I’ll always come if you need me,” she said clearly. “But that chain-coat—your husband didn’t wear it when you married? Perhaps he wasn’t gone so long then?”

“Ah, yes,” Tessa replied. “Much longer. So long, I thought he’d never return. I used to cry. Oh me! I was beaten then—so long ago at Peretola, when we had goats and mules.”

“And how long had you been married before he got the chain-coat?” Romola asked, her heart pounding.

Tessa counted on her fingers. Romola watched them closely—as if they might reveal her fate.

“The chestnuts were ripe when we married,” she said. “Then again when he came back at Peretola. Then again on the hill. Then the soldiers came, the trumpets sounded—and Naldo got the coat.”

“You’d been married more than two years,” Romola said, her mind racing. “In which church were you married?”

Tessa flinched at the tone. Until now, she’d spoken freely, unaware she was revealing anything significant.

“Naldo said I was never to tell,” she said, hesitant. “Do you think he’d be angry if I told you?”

“It’s right that you should,” Romola said, fixing her with quiet authority. “Tell me everything.”

If Naldo’s warning had been fresher, Romola’s presence might have silenced her. But the weight of telling something never shared before made her begin in a low voice.

“It wasn’t in a church,” she said. “It was at the Nativita, during the fair. Everyone went overnight to see the Madonna in the Nunziata. My mother was ill, so I took her cocoons. Then he came to me in the church—I heard him call ‘Tessa!’ I knew him—he’d taken care of me on San Giovanni. We went to the piazza where the fair was. I had some _berlingozzi_—I was hungry, and he was kind. At the end of the piazza, there was a holy father and an altar like the ones in processions. He married us. Then Naldo took me back into the church and left me. I went home—my mother died. Nofri beat me worse. Naldo never came back. I used to cry. Once at the Carnival, I saw him and followed him. He was angry, said he’d come someday—I had to wait. So I waited. It was a long time, but he would have come if he could, for he was good. Then he took me away—because I cried and said I couldn’t stay with Nofri. Oh! I was so happy. Since then, I’ve been always happy. I don’t mind the goats and mules—I have Lillo and Ninna. Naldo isn’t angry often, but I think he doesn’t love Ninna as much as Lillo. And she *is* pretty.”

She forgot her earlier seriousness and began showering kisses on Ninna. Romola sat in silence, eyes distant. In that moment, she saw them not as individuals, but as pieces in her own life’s puzzle. The chill of disappointment settled over her—her troubles wouldn’t be solved by law. She had let go of Lillo, rested her cheek on her hand, seeing nothing.

Lillo noticed the change. He hadn’t responded to her affection, but now he objected to its absence. He raised both arms, pulled her head toward him.

“Play with me again!”

Romola roused herself, clasped him close, and looked at Tessa, who had paused from her kisses and now gazed at Romola with quiet reverence. Romola’s face softened, as if bathed in gentler light. Then she took her scissors from her purse and cut a long, wavy strand of her own hair. The three pairs of wide eyes watched, curious as kittens.

“I must go now,” she said. “But I leave this lock so you’ll remember me. If you’re ever in trouble, think God may send me again. I can’t tell you where to find me, but if I learn you need me, I’ll come. Addio!”

She set Lillo down quickly, offered her hand. Tessa kissed it—half in awe, half in sorrow. Romola’s mind was heavy with thoughts. She needed solitude. Yet, true to her nature, she turned to touch Monna Lisa’s shoulder in farewell. Before the old woman finished her bow, Romola had vanished.

Monna Lisa and Tessa moved toward each other, drawn by mutual impulse. The children clung to their mother’s skirt, sensing the hush.

“Do you think she *was* a saint?” Tessa whispered, showing the lock.

Lisa shook her head firmly, then stroked the golden hair.

“She’s a great and noble lady. I’ve seen such in my youth.”

Romola returned home and sat alone through the sweltering afternoon, the weight of certainty crushing her. Her path remained unchanged. She was caught between the demands of outward law—widely binding, sacred—and inner truths growing ever more urgent. She had absorbed Savonarola’s teaching: that duty was not merely obligation, but the expression of moral order. The sanctity of bonds, especially marriage, was the outward form of deeper human virtue. To break ties lightly, as Tito had done to Baldassarre, was to destroy social and personal integrity.

Savonarola had taught her that her life was part of a greater whole—that even small duties became sacred. She was marching with an army, bearing the weight of a shared destiny. If sacrifice was needed, she would stand ready. She had endured long, struggled hard. But the conditions that once made her bond possible had eroded. All her efforts had only revealed the impossibility of union. The marriage had become a degrading servitude.

The law was sacred. But rebellion might be sacred too. It struck her—this was the same dilemma Savonarola faced: where obedience ended, and rebellion began. To act on one’s own conviction, not guided by law, but facing a law armed with divine judgment—lightning that might strike if the conviction was false.

Before sunset, she made her decision. She would seek no counsel from her godfather or Savonarola until she had spoken directly to Tito. She would ask his consent to live apart. She would not flee secretly or abandon Florence. She would tell him that if he ever truly needed her, she would return. Wasn’t that the highest faithfulness?

She shuddered at the thought he might mock her, suggesting she enter a convent—the only respectable way to leave him. But she knew she could not accept that. Not only did she reject the narrow rule, but her father’s memory forbade it. He had despised monastic life, and she could not honor him by embracing what he abhorred.

Tito had said he’d return that evening. She would wait. She would speak at once—difficult to reach him during the day. When she sent Maso to inform him, she noticed the old man hesitate, his eyes full of concern. But she asked no questions. Perhaps he already knew.

It was late when Tito arrived. Romola had paced the long former library, windows open, wearing a loose white robe instead of her usual black. The change brought relief after hours of heat and stillness, but also sharpened her awareness. Holding a lamp, she opened the door.

He stepped in, startled by the intensity in her eyes—the fierce resolution replacing her usual quiet restraint. But he seemed to expect it.

“Ah, it’s you, Romola,” he said, closing the door behind him. “Maso has gone to bed.” He turned, studying her. “You’ve heard it all, I see.”
