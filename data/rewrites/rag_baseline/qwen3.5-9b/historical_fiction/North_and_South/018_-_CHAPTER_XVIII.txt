The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the Hale parlor windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Margaret, like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of a city determined to crush the quiet suffocation of its inhabitants. She sat at the mahogany table, the heavy scent of beeswax and damp wool clinging to the room, her gaze fixed upon the stack of correspondence that had arrived with the morning post. One was a hurried scrawl for her mother; the other, crisp and silvery, bore the foreign postmarks of a journey interrupted by heat, clearly from Aunt Shaw.

Margaret lifted the letter from Naples, her fingers tracing the thin paper. It was then that her father burst into the room, his movements jerky, his face already etched with a gravity that betrayed the turmoil beneath his composure.

"So your mother is tired, and gone to bed early!" he exclaimed, his voice tight. "I'm afraid such a thundery day was not the best in the world for the doctor to see her. What did he say? Dixon tells me he spoke to you about her."

Margaret hesitated, the weight of the unspoken pressing down on her chest. Her father's eyes narrowed, searching hers for confirmation of a catastrophe he refused to acknowledge.

"He does not think her seriously ill?"

"Not at present," Margaret replied, her voice steady despite the ache in her throat. "He says she needs care. He was very kind, and he promised to call again to see how his medicines worked."

"Only care?" Mr. Hale leaned forward, his knuckles white as he gripped the back of his chair. "He did not recommend a change of air? Did he not say this smoky town is doing her any harm, Margaret?"

"No! Not a word," she lied, the falsehood tasting like ash. "He was anxious, I think."

"Anxious," he muttered, dismissing the sentiment as mere professional affectation. "Doctors have that manner. It's business."

Yet, Margaret watched him, seeing the first tremor of genuine terror take root in his mind. He could not let the subject slide; he could not walk away from the shadow of illness that loomed over his wife. Throughout the evening, he returned to the topic with an unwillingness to entertain even the slightest unfavorable idea, a desperate denial that left Margaret feeling profoundly isolated in her sadness.

"This letter is from Aunt Shaw, papa," she finally said, breaking the silence. "She has reached Naples, found it too hot, and has taken apartments at Sorrento. But I don't think she likes Italy."

"He did not mention diet, did he?"

"It was to be nourishing and digestible. Mamma's appetite is pretty good, I think."

"Yes! And that makes it all the more strange he should have spoken of diet."

"I asked him, papa." Another pause, filled with the sound of rain against the glass. Then, softer, "Aunt Shaw says she has sent me some coral ornaments. But," she added, a faint, forced smile touching her lips, "she fears the Milton dissenters won't appreciate them. She has formed all her ideas of Dissenters from the Quakers, hasn't she?"

"If ever you hear or notice that your mother wishes for anything, be sure you let me know immediately. I am so afraid she does not tell me always what she would like. Pray, see after that girl Mrs. Thornton named. If we had a good, efficient house-servant, Dixon could be constantly with her, and I'd answer for it we'd soon set her up amongst us if care will do it. She's been terribly tired lately, with the heat and the difficulty of finding help. A little rest will put her quite to rights—eh, Margaret?"

"I hope so," Margaret said, but her tone was so laden with sorrow that her father noticed instantly. He reached out, pinching her cheek with a rough, affectionate gesture.

"Come, if you look so pale, I must rouge you up a little. Take care of yourself, child, or you'll be needing the doctor next."

But he could not settle. He moved restlessly, pacing on laborious tiptoes, peering into the bedroom to check if his wife was still asleep. Margaret's heart ached at his agitation—his futile attempt to strangle the hideous fear rising from the dark corners of his soul.

Finally, he returned, slightly relieved.

"She's awake now, Margaret. She smiled as she saw me standing by her. Just her old smile. She says she feels refreshed and ready for tea. Where's the note for her? She wants to see it. I'll read it to her while you make tea."

The note was a formal invitation from Mrs. Thornton, extending hospitality to Mr., Mrs., and Miss Hale for dinner on the twenty-first. Margaret was surprised to find an acceptance contemplated after the grim probabilities of the day, yet so it was. The prospect of her husband and daughter attending had captivated Mrs. Hale's imagination, an event to diversify the monotony of her invalid confinement. She clung to the idea with fretful pertinacity, refusing to listen to Margaret's objections.

"Nay, Margaret: if she wishes it, I'm sure we'll both go willingly. She never would wish it unless she felt herself really stronger—really better than we thought, eh, Margaret?" Mr. Hale asked anxiously as Margaret prepared to draft the response.

"Eh, Margaret?" he questioned, his hands making a nervous fluttering motion. It seemed cruel to deny him the comfort he craved. Moreover, his passionate refusal to admit fear almost inspired Margaret with a flicker of hope.

"I do think she is better since last night," she said softly. "Her eyes look brighter, and her complexion clearer."

"God bless you," her father whispered earnestly. "But is it true? Yesterday was so sultry everyone felt ill. It was a most unlucky day for Mr. Donaldson to visit on."

With that, he departed to his duties, which had expanded to include preparing lectures for the working people at a neighboring Lyceum. He had chosen Ecclesiastical Architecture, a subject closer to his own tastes and knowledge than the crude desires of his audience, hoping to offer a gratis course to an institution drowning in debt.

Later that night, the atmosphere shifted as Mr. Thornton entered the dining room.

"Well, mother," he asked, "who have accepted your invitations for the twenty-first?"

"Fanny, where are the notes?" Mrs. Thornton snapped, her voice sharp with fatigue. "The Slicksons accept, Collingbrooks accept, Stephenses accept, Browns decline. The Hales—father and daughter come, though mother is too great an invalid. Macphersons come, and Mr. Horsfall, and Mr. Young. I was thinking of asking the Porters since the Browns can't come."

"Very good. Do you know, I'm really afraid Mrs. Hale is very far from well, based on what Dr. Donaldson said."

"It's strange of them to accept a dinner invitation if she's very ill," Fanny remarked, her voice small and nervous.

"I didn't say *very* ill," her brother replied sharply. "I said far from well. They may not know it either." Then, abruptly, he remembered that Margaret, at least, knew the exact state of affairs from the doctor's previous words.

"Very probably they are quite aware of what you said yesterday, John—of the great advantage it would be to them, to Mr. Hale specifically, to be introduced to people like the Stephenses and Collingbrooks."

"I'm sure that motive would not influence them. No! I think I understand how it is."

"John!" Fanny laughed, a weak, brittle sound. "How you profess to understand these Hales, and how you never allow that we can know anything about them. Are they really so very different to most people one meets?"

She did not intend to vex him, but if she had, she could not have succeeded more thoroughly. He chafed in silence, refusing to dignify her question with a reply.

"They do not seem to me out of the ordinary," Mrs. Thornton interjected. "He appears a worthy kind of man, though rather too simple for trade—it's perhaps as well he was a clergyman first and now a teacher. She's a bit of a fine lady, with her invalidism. As for the girl... she's the only one who puzzles me. She seems to have a great notion of giving herself airs. I can't make out why. I could almost fancy she thinks herself too good for her company at times. And yet they're not rich; from all I can hear, they never have been."

"And she's not accomplished, mamma. She can't play."

"Go on, Fanny. What else does she want to bring her up to your standard?"

"Nay! John," his mother said, turning to him. "That speech of Fanny's did no harm. I myself heard Miss Hale say she could not play. If you would let us alone, we could perhaps like her and see her merits."

"I'm sure I never could!" murmured Fanny, retreating behind her mother's shield. Mr. Thornton heard her but cared not to respond. He walked up and down the dining room, wishing his mother would order candles so he could retreat to reading or writing, escaping the conversation.
