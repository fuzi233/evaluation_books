The eyes in the dark widened, reflecting a shock that mirrored my own. "My son!" The words tumbled from my lips before reason could check them. Slowly, I rose, turning to face the handsome youth who stood before me. As my gaze locked onto his features, the pieces of a fractured puzzle began to align in my mind. There was an undeniable echo of his mother's incomparable beauty etched into his clear-cut face, yet it was tempered by a rugged, masculine strength that was unmistakably mine. Those grey eyes, piercing and intense, bore the exact expression of my own soul.

The boy stood motionless, his posture a tense mixture of half-hope and profound uncertainty.

"Tell me of your mother," I commanded softly, my voice thick with emotion. "Speak to me of all the years I have been robbed by a relentless fate of her dear companionship."

With a cry of pure, unbridled pleasure, he sprang toward me, throwing his arms around my neck. For a fleeting, suspended moment, holding my son close, the dam broke; tears welled to my eyes, hot and stinging, threatening to choke me in the manner of some maudlin fool. Yet, I did not pull away, nor did I feel shame. A lifetime of experience has taught me that a man may appear fragile when confronted with the vulnerability of women and children, yet remain an iron fortress in the sterner avenues of survival.

"Your stature, your manner, the terrible ferocity of your swordsmanship," the boy murmured against my shoulder, "are exactly as my mother has described them to me a thousand times. But even with such evidence, I could scarce credit the truth of what seemed so improbable to me, however desperately I wished it to be true. Do you know what single thing convinced me more than all the others?"

"What, my boy?" I asked, keeping him close.

"Your first words to me—they were of my mother. None but the man who loved her as she has told me my father did would have thought first of her."

"For long years, my son, I can scarce recall a moment when the radiant vision of your mother's face has not hovered before me. Tell me of her."

"Those who have known her longest say that she has not changed, unless it be to grow more beautiful—a miracle, were that possible. Only, when she thinks I am not about to see her, her face grows very sad, oh, so wistful. She thinks ever of you, my father, and all Helium mourns with her and for her. Her grandfather's people love her; they loved you also, and they fairly worship your memory as the savior of Barsoom."

"Each year that brings its anniversary of the day you raced across a near-dead world to unlock the secret of that awful portal, behind which lay the mighty power of life for countless millions, a great festival is held in your honor. But there are tears mingled with the thanksgiving—tears of real regret that the author of this happiness is not with them to share the joy of living he died to give them. Upon all Barsoom, there is no greater name than John Carter."

"And by what name has your mother called you, my boy?" I asked.

"The people of Helium asked that I bear your name, but my mother said no. She insisted that you and she had chosen a name for me together, and that your wish must be honored above all others. So the name she calls me is the one you desired, a combination of hers and yours—Carthoris."

Xodar had been at the wheel while I conversed with my son, and now his urgent voice cut through the reunion. "She is dropping badly by the head, John Carter," he warned. "So long as we were rising at a stiff angle, it was not noticeable, but now that I am trying to maintain a horizontal course, it is critical. The wound in her bow has opened one of her forward ray tanks."

It was true. Upon examining the damage, I realized it was a far graver matter than I had initially anticipated. Not only was the forced angle required to keep the bow level severely impeding our speed, but at the rate we were losing our repulsive rays from the forward tanks, it was merely a question of an hour or more before we would float stern-up, helpless and drifting.

We had slightly reduced our speed with the dawning of a false sense of security, but now I seized the helm once more, pulling the noble little engine wide open. Again, we raced north at terrific velocity. Meanwhile, Carthoris and Xodar, tools in hand, frantically worked at the great rent in the bow in a hopeless endeavor to stem the tide of escaping energy.

It was still dark when we passed the northern boundary of the ice cap and the swirling area of clouds. Below us lay a typical Martian landscape: a rolling ochre seabottom of long-dead seas, low surrounding hills, and here and there, the grim, silent cities of the dead past. Great piles of mighty architecture stood tenanted only by age-old memories of a once-powerful race and by the great white apes of Barsoom.

Maintaining our vessel in a horizontal position became increasingly difficult. Lower and lower sagged the bow until it became necessary to cut the engine entirely to prevent our flight from terminating in a swift, fatal dive to the ground.

As the sun rose, sweeping away the darkness of night with its pale light, our craft gave a final, spasmodic plunge. It turned half upon its side, and then, with the deck tilting at a sickening angle, swung in a slow, agonizing circle, its bow dropping further below its stern with every passing second.

Clinging to handrails and stanchions, we watched the end approach. Finally, snapping the buckles of our harnesses to the rings at the ship's sides, we prepared for impact. In another moment, the deck reared at a ninety-degree angle, and we hung suspended in our leather straps, feet dangling a thousand yards above the alien earth.

I was swinging quite close to the controlling devices, so I reached out to the lever directing the rays of repulsion. The boat responded instantly to my touch, and very gently, we began to sink toward the ground.

It was fully half an hour before we touched down. Directly north of us rose a rather lofty range of hills, toward which we immediately decided to make our way, since they offered greater opportunities for concealment from the pursuers we were confident might stumble in this direction.

An hour later, we found ourselves in the time-rounded gullies of the hills, amidst the beautiful flowering plants that abound in the arid wastes of Barsoom. Here, we discovered numerous huge milk-giving shrubs—that strange flora which serves in great part as food and drink for the wild hordes of green men. It was indeed a boon to us, for we were all nearly famished.

Beneath a cluster of these shrubs, which afforded perfect concealment from wandering air scouts, we lay down to sleep—for me, the first time in many hours. This marked the beginning of my fifth day upon Barsoom, since I had found myself suddenly translated from my cottage on the Hudson to Dor, the valley beautiful, the valley hideous. In all this time, I had slept but twice, though once the clock had rung within the storehouse of the Therns.

It was mid-afternoon when I was awakened by someone seizing my hand and covering it with kisses. With a start, I opened my eyes to look into the beautiful face of Thuvia.

"My Prince! My Prince!" she cried, in an ecstasy of happiness. "'Tis you whom I had mourned as dead. My ancestors have been good to me; I have not lived in vain."

The girl's voice roused Xodar and Carthoris. The boy gazed upon the woman in surprise, but she seemed oblivious to anyone other than me. She would have thrown her arms about my neck and smothered me with caresses had I not gently but firmly disengaged myself.

"Come, come, Thuvia," I said soothingly. "You are overwrought by the danger and hardships you have passed through. You forget yourself, as you forget that I am the husband of the Princess of Helium."

"I forget nothing, my Prince," she replied, her voice trembling. "You have spoken no word of love to me, nor do I expect that you ever shall; but nothing can prevent me from loving you. I would not take the place of Dejah Thoris. My greatest ambition is to serve you, my Prince, forever as your slave. No greater boon could I ask, no greater honor could I crave, no greater happiness could I hope."

As I have before stated, I am no lady's man, and I must admit that I seldom felt so uncomfortable and embarrassed as I did in that moment. While I was quite familiar with the Martian custom which allows female slaves to Martian men, whose high and chivalrous honor is always ample protection for every woman in his household, I had never myself chosen other than men as my body servants.

"And I ever return to Helium, Thuvia," I said, "you shall go with me, but as an honored equal, and not as a slave. There you shall find plenty of handsome young nobles who would face Issus herself to win a smile from you, and we shall have you married in short order to one of the best of them. Forget your foolish gratitude-begotten infatuation, which your innocence has mistaken for love. I like your friendship better, Thuvia."
