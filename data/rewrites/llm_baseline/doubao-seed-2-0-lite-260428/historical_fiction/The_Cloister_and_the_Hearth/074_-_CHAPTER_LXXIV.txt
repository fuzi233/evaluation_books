The newly elected pope held the Dominican order in special favour, and soon a messenger arrived at the convent from the Vatican, calling for a learned, capable friar to take up a teaching post at the University of Basle. Brother Clement was the very man for the task: fluent in half a dozen languages, and long ago, in his worldly life, he had sat in the lecture halls of Guarini the younger. His planned visit to England was put on hold, though not abandoned, and he was dispatched towards Basle – with three months to fill before his post began, tasked with preaching in every village and town along his road.

He passed out of the northern gate with his eyes cast low, his whole being wrapped in quiet, pious contemplation. If we could paint the full story of a man’s soul as a living, breathing work of art, what a walking fresco this barefoot friar made! Hopeful, bright young love, shattering bereavement, bottomless despair, blasphemy, reckless vice, a suicide attempt, gnawing remorse, religious terror, penitence, a total death to worldly ties, and at last soft resignation. All of that, crammed into twelve short months. Now he was a traveller on foot again – but everything had changed. There were no more perilous adventures waiting for him on the road. Even common thieves and robbers bowed low to the ground when they spotted him, and instead of robbing him, they forced their stolen coins into his hands and begged him to pray for their souls. His journey thus held almost no grand, melodramatic incidents. But for the readers who care less for swashbuckling plots and more for the quiet, unspoken movements of a man’s heart, these undramatic moments speak volumes, marking the slow, steady growth of his soul.

The first Sunday of his journey brought a quiet, profound milestone: he prayed openly for the soul of Margaret. He had never dared do that before. It was not that her eternal welfare was not dearer to him than anything else on earth – it was his own crushing humility. The terrible blasphemies he had screamed in his frenzy when he heard news of her death horrified his pious readers, yes, but they horrified him far more, as he turned them over again and again in his thoughts. For months during his novitiate, he was crushed by religious despair, convinced he had committed the sin against the Holy Spirit that dooms a soul forever. Slowly, under the gentle guidance of old Anselm, that dark cloud lifted, but a deep, searing sense of his own unworthiness remained. He feared his own salvation was far from secure, and that it would be a mocking insult to Heaven for a man so stained with sin to pray for a soul as innocent, comparatively, as Margaret’s. So he had coaxed good Anselm and other kindly, soft-hearted old monks to pray for her in his stead, and they did it with all their heart. The older friars of the convent, for all their flaws, bore a pure, tender love for their younger brethren that could never have sprung from the world.

That Sunday, after he had preached to a rapt crowd in a small Italian town and felt his spirit carried high on the power of his own words, a balmy, soft sense of God’s forgiveness and loving kindness settled over him. He prayed for Margaret’s soul then, and from that day forward, it became his daily habit – the last, purified tie that bound his heart to the earth. All ties to his own blood family had faded as if they had never existed: the Church would not share a man’s devotion with the world, and even the Church could not cure the pain of his great, lifelong love for Margaret without erasing all smaller earthly attachments along with it.

For most of his journey, Clement rarely felt any flicker of warm life stir in him except when he stood in the pulpit. The only other exceptions came when he stopped to relieve the suffering of some fellow traveller. Once, he found a young man in the grip of what the villagers said was tarantula bite – or what might equally well have been a ferocious hysterical delusion. For two days, the boy had not slept, thrashing, leaping, and beating the walls of his cottage in convulsions, with the village musicians only making his agony worse by blaring loud, wild tunes. Left to run its course, the illness would end in exhaustion and death. Clement sent for a psaltery and tried soft, soothing melodies at first, but instead of calming him, the notes crushed the boy into groaning, huddling on the dirt floor. Then Clement noticed his lips moving in a constant, quiet whisper. He leaned in, and found the boy was murmuring a strange, unwritten tune no one else had ever heard. He learned the melody, and began to play it. The boy’s face brightened instantly, and he began to dance lightly about the room in perfect contentment. By the time Clement’s fingers ached so badly he could barely pluck the strings, the young man sank down peacefully into a deep, healing sleep that saved his life. After teaching the strange tune to the village musician for future use, Clement went on his way, his heart a little warmer than it had been.

On another occasion, he found a mob dragging a well-dressed stranger down the street, the man struggling and shouting in a language no one in the crowd could understand. He had walked into the town sweltering in the Italian heat, holding a mulberry bough over his head for shade, and the villagers had stared stupidly, uncomprehending, before pouncing on him to drag him before the podesta. Clement followed, trying every language he knew – Italian, French, German, Dutch – until the stranger replied back in tolerable, sharp-accented Latin. He was an Englishman, a Kentish squire named Rolfe, of Betshanger, educated at Cambridge. He had plucked the nearest leafy bough to shade his head, he said, because in England anyone was welcome to take fruit and branches that grew by the public highway. He would happily pay for the twig, he protested, he could not fathom why the whole town was up in arms over a handful of leaves. The stubborn podesta was ready to throw the islander in jail, until Clement mediated, carefully explaining that mulberry leaves were sacred property, for they fed the silkworms that were the Duke of Italy’s most valuable source of income. Rolfe, unapologetic, pulled out a long purse and declared the whole matter simply mercantile: “What damages?” he said. “Name the sum, and I will pay it at once.” The podesta’s eyes lit up at the glint of gold, fined him a single ducat – worth far more than the entire mulberry tree – and pocketed the coin.

Free at last, Rolfe laughed off his anger immediately and shook his head at the absurdity of the whole situation. He was deeply grateful to Clement, and pressed him to come back to England with him. “You are too good for this fool’s country, father,” he said. “England is the only place worth living. I was a fool to leave it to wander among mulberry trees and idiots. My house is always open to you. We sit forty people down to dinner every day at Betshanger – one more guest will never be missed. You can end your days with me and my heirs, if you wish. What an Englishman says, he means.” Clement promised he would visit one day, not to overstay his welcome. Rolfe then begged Clement to hear his confession, saying he had lived like a heathen ever since he left England. While he knelt on the roadside to confess, Clement asked if the rumours were true that England was overrun with Lollards and Wycliffites. Rolfe flushed a little, then spoke gravely. “There are black sheep in every land, holy father. But the English love their own ways, and above all their own mother tongue. The Normans conquered our weapons, but they never conquered our speech – they tried for a hundred years to stamp it out by law and proclamation, and failed. Foreign priests come to us and preach the word of God in mangled Latin or bad French, that sounds no more sense to a plain English farmer than the bleat of a sheep. Then Wycliffe and his men come and read the scripture out of its own book, in plain, simple English, that speaks straight to every man’s heart. Who could resist that? I swear the English would turn deaf ears to St. Peter himself, if he did not speak to them in the tongue their mothers lulled them with, along with their kisses.” He hastily added that this was for the common folk, not himself, who had been educated at Cambridge and knew Latin well. “The key to the English heart is the mother tongue, father.”

Rolfe offered his dual-language English and Latin Book of Hours to Clement, to help him learn the language, and the two men swapped books: Clement gave him his own Latin and Italian devotional manuscript, refusing to “sell” his promised daily prayers for Rolfe’s soul, saying he would offer them freely. The delighted Englishman then stuffed a heavy purse of coins into Clement’s hands, refusing to listen when Clement protested his vow of poverty. “Lay it out for the good of the Church and the good of my soul,” he insisted. “You do not have to keep it for yourself, but you will take it.” The two men embraced, blessed each other, and went their separate ways.

A mile down the road, Clement found two tired German workmen resting in the deep shade of a great chestnut tree beside their cart, which carried a rough, clumsy printing press no bigger than a wine press, drawn by a jaded, half-dead mule. He stared at the machine, the old bitter hatred he had once felt for the craft of printing fading into a faint, distant dream. “Sweynheim!” he called softly.

The two men jumped to their feet, panicked, and scuttled off into the trees, thinking a sorcerer had called their names out of nowhere. Clement laughed, and called to them to come out, explaining he had seen their printed copy of *Lactantius* in Rome, and knew they were the disciples of Johann Fust, the first printers. The two men came forward sheepishly, and complained that their mule was too tired to make it to the next town, and they had no money left to pay for his food or their own. Clement freed the mule of all its harness but a thin halter, and the creature immediately rolled happily in the dust, refreshing himself. He then handed them the purse Rolfe had given him, telling them it was a gift from a charitable English stranger, to pass the kindness on to some other traveller in need someday. The two workmen’s eyes filled with tears. Before they parted, they dragged their press out of the cart, and in a few moments, pulled a fresh printed sheet of eight pages – already set type for Augustine’s *City of God* – for Clement to keep. He stared in wonder at the words, fixed fast to the paper, and warned them never to abuse their great craft, never to print wicked books, for those would spread across the world like locusts to devour men’s souls. They promised they would sooner lay their own hands under the press screw than do that, and the three parted as friends.

Later, at an inn in Tuscany, Clement had a strange, sudden encounter with his past. He was sitting alone in a corner of the big barn the inn had lent to a crowd of travelling pilgrims, poring over his new Book of Hours and working out the English words with the help of his old Dutch, when a servant set a bucket of water at his feet, and a masked lady of high rank – performing public penance – knelt down without a word, rolled up her sleeves, and began to wash his feet. Her mask was barely an inch wide, but it completely hid her face, and she handled his bare feet with a soft, gentle touch no servant could match. When he gently asked if he could do anything to help her soul, she broke down, and confessed her terrible secret.

She had loved a handsome young Dutch stranger named Gerard once, she said, and when he rejected her for a poor girl back in his homeland, she had flown into a jealous rage, hired a bravo to kill him, and then lied to his sweetheart that he had died unfaithful. She had thought him dead for years, his body never found, and her soul was crushed under the weight of the murder. Clement slowly, gently revealed the truth: the bravo she had hired was the very man whose life Gerard had saved at sea, along with his wife and infant child. The assassin had not killed Gerard – he had dragged him out of the Tiber, after Gerard had thrown himself in to die in his grief over Margaret’s supposed death. Gerard had renounced the world then, to become a Dominican friar.

The lady froze. She raised her head slowly, peering through the tiny slit in her mask at Clement’s face. She recognized his voice. The next moment, she let out a faint, soft shriek, and collapsed forward, her brow pressed to his bare feet, weeping so hard her whole body shook.
