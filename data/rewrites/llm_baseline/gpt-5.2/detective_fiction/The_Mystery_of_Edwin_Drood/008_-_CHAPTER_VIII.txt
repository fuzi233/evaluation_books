They waited until the two young ladies had passed the Nuns’ House gate and were swallowed by its enclosed quiet, leaving behind them the cold stare of the polished door-plate, bright as an unblinking eye. The street lay long and pale under moonlight, and in that light the stones seemed to keep their own counsel. Somewhere beyond, Cloisterham’s night-sounds were at work: the measured drip in a gutter, the far drag of a cart-wheel dying away, the Cathedral’s vast hush holding all the smaller noises as if in a hand.

Edwin Drood and Neville Landless looked after the vanished figures, looked at each other, and then, without agreement spoken, fell into step and walked slowly away.

“Do you stay here long, Mr. Drood?” Neville asked.

“Not this time.” Edwin’s answer had the offhand air of a man shrugging on a coat. “I go up to London again to-morrow. But I shall be here, off and on, until next Midsummer. Then I take my leave of Cloisterham—and England too—for many a long day, I expect.”

“Are you going abroad?”

“Going to wake up Egypt a little,” Edwin said, with the easy superiority of someone who has already seen his name written neatly into the future.

“Are you reading?” Neville asked, as if that word—reading—were a rung that might help him climb.

“Reading?” Edwin repeated, and the sound carried contempt, like a spoon striking an empty plate. “No. Doing. Working. Engineering. My small patrimony was left—part of it—as capital in the Firm I’m with, by my father, a former partner. I’m a charge upon the Firm till I come of age, and then I step into my modest share. Jack—you met him at dinner—is, until then, my guardian and trustee.”

“I heard from Mr. Crisparkle of your other good fortune.”

Edwin stopped as if a pebble had been kicked against his toe on purpose. “What do you mean by my other good fortune?”

Neville had spoken in that odd manner of his—advancing, yet wary; like a man who expects both to strike and to be struck. Edwin’s sudden edge was not polite, and the air between them tightened.

“I hope,” Neville said, watching Edwin’s face, “there is no offence, Mr. Drood, in my innocently referring to your betrothal?”

“By George!” Edwin broke out, moving on again, quicker now, as if pace could scrape off irritation. “Everybody in this chattering old Cloisterham refers to it. I wonder no public-house has been set up with my portrait for the sign of The Betrothed’s Head. Or Pussy’s portrait. One or the other.”

“I am not accountable for Mr. Crisparkle’s mentioning the matter to me, quite openly,” Neville began.

“No; that’s true; you are not,” Edwin returned. “But—”

“But I am accountable for mentioning it to you,” Neville said, and there was a flush of earnestness in him, like heat rising too fast from a pan. “And I did so, on the supposition that you could not fail to be highly proud of it.”

In that moment two secret irritations—each fed by a different face—met and took fire. Neville, already stirred by Rose Maylie’s quiet sweetness, could not bear to hear her treated as if she were a settled portion in a ledger. Edwin, already struck by Helena Landless’s presence, could not bear her brother’s tone, as if Edwin were easily appraised and easily dismissed.

Edwin answered at last, with a mock-urbanity that stung all the more for its tune. “I don’t know, Mr. Neville”—he adopted the address he had heard from Mr. Crisparkle—“that what people are proudest of they usually talk most about; I don’t know either that what they are proudest of they most like other people to talk about. But I live a busy life, and I speak under correction by you readers, who ought to know everything, and I daresay do.”

The night received those words and held them. They walked under a strip of moon, then through shadow; and Edwin, under the transparent cover of a half-hummed popular air, would stop now and again to pretend admiration for some “picturesque effect,” as if scenery were a dish set before him to be tasted and praised, while Neville grew hotter at every leisurely pause.

“It does not seem to me very civil in you,” Neville said at length, “to reflect upon a stranger who comes here not having had your advantages, to try to make up for lost time. But, to be sure, I was not brought up in ‘busy life,’ and my ideas of civility were formed among Heathens.”

“Perhaps the best civility,” Edwin retorted, “whatever kind of people we’re brought up among, is to mind our own business. If you will set me that example, I promise to follow it.”

“Do you know that you take a great deal too much upon yourself?” Neville flung back, “and that in the part of the world I come from you would be called to account for it?”

“By whom, for instance?” Edwin demanded, halting and surveying him with disdain.

A hand came down on Edwin’s shoulder—startling, steady—and before either young man could gather himself, John Jasper stepped between them. He had approached from behind, keeping to the shadowy side of the road as noiselessly as a practised listener. Cloisterham’s night had its own acoustic rules: a footfall on stone could be thunder or nothing, depending on how you placed it, and Jasper placed his as if he meant never to be heard.

“Ned, Ned, Ned!” Jasper said. His voice was low, but it cut cleanly through the tension, the way a knife parts twine. “We must have no more of this. I don’t like this. I have overheard high words between you two. Remember, my dear boy, you are almost in the position of host to-night. You belong, as it were, to the place, and in a manner represent it towards a stranger. Mr. Neville is a stranger, and you should respect the obligations of hospitality.”

Then, laying his left hand on Neville’s inner shoulder, and keeping his right on Edwin’s, he walked on between them, holding them as if he held two restive dogs by the collar.

“And, Mr. Neville,” he went on, “you will pardon me; but I appeal to you to govern your temper too. Now, what is amiss? But why ask! Let there be nothing amiss, and the question is superfluous. We are all three on a good understanding, are we not?”

There was a small silent wrestle between the two young men as to who should speak last. Edwin won it.

“So far as I am concerned, Jack, there is no anger in me.”

“Nor in me,” Neville said, though his words came less freely, as if he had to force them past something sharp in his throat. “But if Mr. Drood knew all that lies behind me, far away from here, he might know better how it is that sharp-edged words have sharp edges to wound me.”

“Perhaps,” Jasper soothed, “we had better not qualify our good understanding. We had better not say anything having the appearance of a remonstrance or condition; it might not seem generous. Frankly and freely, you see there is no anger in Ned. Frankly and freely, there is no anger in you, Mr. Neville?”

“None at all, Mr. Jasper.”

“All over then!” Jasper said, brightening his tone as if drawing a curtain on the scene. “Now, my bachelor gatehouse is a few yards from here, and the heater is on the fire, and the wine and glasses are on the table, and it is not a stone’s throw from Minor Canon Corner. Ned, you are up and away to-morrow. We will carry Mr. Neville in with us, to take a stirrup-cup.”

“With all my heart, Jack,” Edwin answered.

“And with all mine, Mr. Jasper,” Neville said, because he could not say less; though something in him resisted going, the way the body resists a room where it knows a quarrel will warm itself back to life.

Jasper, still between them, kept his hands upon their shoulders and, with a musician’s ease, turned the refrain of a drinking song, letting its familiar cadence smooth the air. Cloisterham’s night, which had been a tense, listening thing, seemed for an instant to accept the tune as part of domestic order: fire, glass, a late cup, and men pretending good humour.

They went up to Jasper’s rooms.

When Jasper added the light of a lamp to the fire’s glow, the first object to assert itself was the portrait above the chimneypiece. It stood there like a witness—Rose Maylie’s face, caught and fixed. It was not the sort of witness that improves an understanding already strained. Both young men glanced at it, and both said nothing.

Jasper, who seemed to have only an imperfect clue to the cause of their earlier heat, called attention to it at once, shading the lamp to throw a stronger light upon the painted features.

“You recognise that picture, Mr. Neville?”

“I recognise it,” Neville returned, “but it is far from flattering the original.”

“O, you are hard upon it!” Jasper said. “It was done by Ned, who made me a present of it.”

“I am sorry for that, Mr. Drood.” Neville’s apology was real. “If I had known I was in the artist’s presence—”

“O, a joke, sir, a mere joke,” Edwin cut in, and yawned, provokingly at ease. “A little humouring of Pussy’s points! I’m going to paint her gravely one of these days, if she’s good.”

He spoke with leisurely patronage and indifference, and threw himself back in his chair, clasping his hands behind his head to make a rest for it, as if he sat before an empty hearth in a club and not in a room where every word was food for offence. To Neville—whose feelings ran on quick, sharp heat—this ease was exasperating. Jasper’s eyes moved observantly from one to the other; a slight smile touched his mouth, and he turned his back to the two young men to mix a jug of mulled wine by the fire.

The warm scent began to build: wine meeting heat; spice bruised and waking; something sweet—perhaps sugar—melting down into the dark. Jasper compounded it as if he were balancing a recipe that must come out right: stirring, pausing, tasting the air rather than the liquid. The fire gave a soft, living crackle, and the kettle—or heater—made its small domestic sighs. Against that soundscape, the clink of glass and the scrape of chair-legs were sudden, bright intrusions, like metal on stone.

“I suppose, Mr. Neville,” Edwin said, quick to resent what he saw plainly in Neville’s face, “I suppose that if you painted the picture of your lady love—”

“I can’t paint,” Neville interrupted, too fast.

“That’s your misfortune, and not your fault. You would if you could. But if you could, I suppose you would make her—no matter what she was in reality—Juno, Minerva, Diana, and Venus, all in one. Eh?”

“I have no lady love, and I can’t say.”

“If I were to try my hand,” Edwin went on, the boyish boast getting up in him like froth, “on a portrait of Miss Landless—in earnest, mind you; in earnest—you should see what I could do!”

“My sister’s consent to sit for it being first got, I suppose?” Neville answered, stiff with anger held back by manners. “As it never will be got, I am afraid I shall never see what you can do. I must bear the loss.”

Jasper turned from the fire, filled a large goblet for Neville, filled a large goblet for Edwin, and handed each his own; then filled for himself.

“Come, Mr. Neville,” Jasper said, “we are to drink to my nephew, Ned. As it is his foot that is in the stirrup—metaphorically—our stirrup-cup is to be devoted to him. Ned, my dearest fellow, my love!”

He set the example by nearly emptying his glass. Neville followed. Edwin, with, “Thank you both very much,” followed their example.

The mulled wine went down warm and spiced, its heat spreading quickly, too quickly; the head took it as a sudden blaze takes dry kindling. Edwin’s cheeks grew notably flushed; so did Neville’s. The room felt closer, the fire louder, the night outside farther away—its clean silence shut out by the thickened air and the drink’s swift encouragement.

“Look at him,” Jasper cried, stretching out a hand admiringly, tenderly, rallyingly. “See where he lounges so easily, Mr. Neville! The world is all before him where to choose. A life of stirring work and interest, a life of change and excitement, a life of domestic ease and love! Look at him!”

Edwin still sat thrown back, hands clasped behind his head.

“See how little he heeds it all!” Jasper bantered on. “It is hardly worth his while to pluck the golden fruit that hangs ripe on the tree for him. And yet consider the contrast, Mr. Neville. You and I have no prospect of stirring work and interest, or of change and excitement, or of domestic ease and love. You and I have no prospect—unless you are more fortunate than I am, which may easily be—but the tedious unchanging round of this dull place.”

“Upon my soul, Jack,” Edwin said, complacently, “I feel quite apologetic for having my way smoothed as you describe. But you know what I know, Jack, and it may not be so very easy as it seems, after all. May it, Pussy?”

He snapped his thumb and finger toward the portrait, as if calling a dog to heel.

“We have got to hit it off yet; haven’t we, Pussy? You know what I mean, Jack.”

His words had thickened, as though his tongue were coated. Jasper, quiet and self-possessed, looked to Neville as if expecting his comment. When Neville spoke, his own speech was thick too, blurred at the edges.

“It might have been better for Mr. Drood to have known some hardships,” he said defiantly.

“Pray,” Edwin retorted, turning only his eyes, “pray why might it have been better for Mr. Drood to have known some hardships?”

“Ay,” Jasper assented, interested; “let us know why?”

“Because they might have made him more sensible,” Neville said, “of good fortune that is not by any means necessarily the result of his own merits.”

Jasper’s eyes flicked to Edwin, waiting for the blow to be returned.

“Have you known hardships, may I ask?” Edwin said, sitting upright at last.

Jasper’s eyes turned to Neville, waiting again.

“I have.”

“And what have they made you sensible of?”

“I have told you once before to-night.”

“You have done nothing of the sort.”

“I tell you I have. That you take a great deal too much upon yourself.”

“You added something else to that, if I remember?”

“Yes, I did say something else.”

“Say it again.”

“I said that in the part of the world I come from, you would be called to account for it.”

“Only there?” Edwin cried, with a contemptuous laugh. “A long way off, I believe? Yes; I see! That part of the world is at a safe distance.”

“Say here, then,” Neville rejoined, rising in a fury that shoved his chair back with a harsh scrape. “Say anywhere! Your vanity is intolerable, your conceit is beyond endurance; you talk as if you were some rare and precious prize, instead of a common boaster. You are a common fellow, and a common boaster.”

“Pooh, pooh,” Edwin said, equally furious but more collected. “How should you know? You may know a black common fellow, or a black common boaster, when you see him—and no doubt you have a large acquaintance that way—but you are no judge of white men.”

The insult, flung as carelessly as a slop thrown from a doorstep, struck Neville like boiling liquid. His dark face, already heated by wine and rage, turned a deeper, dangerous red. The room’s sounds sharpened: the fire’s crack, the glass’s faint ring in Jasper’s hand, the heavy beat of blood in the ears as if the house itself had acquired a hammering heart.

Neville flung the dregs of his wine in Edwin’s face, and snatched up the goblet to hurl after it. Jasper caught his arm in the nick of time.

“Ned, my dear fellow!” Jasper cried aloud. “I entreat you, I command you, to be still!”

There was a rush of all three, a clatter of glass, a violent overturning of chairs.

“Mr. Neville, for shame! Give this glass to me. Open your hand, sir. I will have it!”

Neville threw him off and stood for an instant with the goblet still uplifted, trembling in murderous passion. Then he dashed it down under the grate with such force that broken splinters sprang out again in a shower, glittering and flying like brief bright insects. He left the house.

When he first emerged into the night air, nothing around him was still or steady. The street reeled; the moon seemed to whirl; the Cathedral tower leaned and righted itself like a great ship. He only knew that he stood bareheaded in the midst of a blood-red turning, waiting to be struggled with and to struggle to the death.

But nothing happened. The moon looked down upon him with that impartial brightness that makes rage feel foolish, as if he were already dead after his fit. He held his head, where his heart seemed to beat up into his skull like a steam-hammer, and staggered away.

Half-conscious, he heard himself bolted and barred out behind him, as if he were a dangerous animal. The sound of metal sliding home—small, domestic, final—had in it a kind of cruelty: not loud, not theatrical, but decisive. For a few moments he did not know what to do. Wild thoughts of the river rose and sank; the river’s dark hush offered itself like an end to noise. But the moonlight lay on the Cathedral and the graves, washing them into a cold, pale calm; and remembrance of his sister came over him, and the thought of what he owed to the good man who that very day had won his confidence and given him his pledge.

He went to Minor Canon Corner and knocked softly.

It was Mr. Crisparkle’s custom to sit up last of his early household, very softly touching his piano and practising his favourite parts in concerted vocal music. The very air there seemed trained to gentleness; the night wind that slipped along the Corner on a still night was not more subdued than the Minor Canon’s careful touch, out of regard for sleepers, and for the china shepherdess who presided over the domestic peace. Against such quiet, Neville’s knock was a harsh little thing, yet he made it as light as shame would allow.

It was answered immediately by Mr. Crisparkle himself. He opened the door with a candle in his hand. His cheerful face fell into disappointed amazement.

“Mr. Neville! In this disorder! Where have you been?”

“I have been to Mr. Jasper’s, sir. With his nephew.”

“Come in.”

The Minor Canon took him by the elbow with a strong hand, in a strictly scientific manner worthy of his morning trainings, and guided him into his little book-room, shutting the door upon the rest of the house’s sleep.

“I have begun ill, sir,” Neville said. “I have begun dreadfully ill.”

“Too true. You are not sober, Mr. Neville.”

“I am afraid I am not, sir—though I can satisfy you at another time that I have had very little indeed to drink, and that it overcame me in the strangest and most sudden manner.”

“Mr. Neville, Mr. Neville,” the Minor Canon said, shaking his head with a sorrowful smile, “I have heard that said before.”

“I think—my mind is much confused, but I think—it is equally true of Mr. Jasper’s nephew, sir.”

“Very likely,” was the dry rejoinder.

“We quarrelled, sir. He insulted me most grossly. He had heated that tigerish blood I told you of to-day, before then.”

“Mr. Neville,” Mr. Crisparkle returned, mildly but firmly, “I request you not to speak to me with that clenched right hand. Unclench it, if you please.”

“He goaded me, sir,” Neville said, instantly obeying, “beyond my power of endurance. I cannot say whether or no he meant it at first, but he did it. He certainly meant it at last. In short, sir”—and now the confession burst from him like a sob turned to speech—“in the passion into which he lashed me, I would have cut him down if I could, and I tried to do it.”

“You have clenched that hand again,” Mr. Crisparkle quietly observed.

“I beg your pardon, sir.”

“You know your room, for I showed it you before dinner; but I will accompany you to it once more. Your arm, if you please. Softly, for the house is all a-bed.”

He scooped his hand into the same trained elbow-rest as before, backing it with the inert strength of his arm, as skilfully as a Police Expert and with a repose that steadied the unsteady. Thus he conducted Neville to the pleasant, orderly old room prepared for him.

There Neville threw himself into a chair and, flinging his arms upon the reading-table, laid his head on them with wretched self-reproach. The Minor Canon had it in mind to leave without a word. But he looked back, saw the dejected figure, and returned to the door; touched it with a mild hand and said simply, “Good night!”

A sob was Neville’s only answer.

Down-stairs, another soft knock came to the outer door. Mr. Crisparkle opened it to John Jasper, who stood there holding Neville’s hat.

“We have had an awful scene with him,” Jasper said in a low voice.

“Has it been so bad as that?”

“Murderous!”

Mr. Crisparkle remonstrated at once. “No, no, no. Do not use such strong words.”

“He might have laid my dear boy dead at my feet. It is no fault of his that he did not. But that I was—through the mercy of God—swift and strong with him, he would have cut him down on my hearth.”

The phrase smote home; it had Neville’s own words in it, turned back as evidence. Mr. Crisparkle felt the blow inwardly, though his face stayed composed.

“Seeing what I have seen to-night, and hearing what I have heard,” Jasper added with great earnestness, “I shall never know peace of mind when there is danger of those two coming together, with no one else to interfere. It was horrible. There is something of the tiger in his dark blood.”

Again—what Neville had said; again, repeated with a different emphasis, made to sound like a verdict. Mr. Crisparkle heard, beneath Jasper’s solicitude, the power of words to brand; how certain phrases, once adopted by a dominant tongue and repeated with confidence, can steal a man’s whole history and reduce it to a convenient creature.

“You, my dear sir,” Jasper pursued, taking his hand, “even you, have accepted a dangerous charge.”

“You need have no fear for me, Jasper,” Mr. Crisparkle returned with a quiet smile. “I have none for myself.”

“I have none for myself,” Jasper answered, with an emphasis on the last pronoun, “because I am not, nor am I in the way of being, the object of his hostility. But you may be, and my dear boy has been. Good night!”

Mr. Crisparkle went in with the hat—so easily, so almost imperceptibly acquiring the right to be hung up in his hall—hung it on its place, and went thoughtfully to bed, while the house settled again into its careful hush: piano silenced, candle out, only the small domestic creaks and the far, steady presence of the Cathedral’s night pressing on, as if listening to remember what words had been spoken and what words had been used to name a man.
