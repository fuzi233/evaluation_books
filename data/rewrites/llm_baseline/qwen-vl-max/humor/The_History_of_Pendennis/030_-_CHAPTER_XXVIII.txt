The ballroom at Baymouth was in full swing—music, laughter, the occasional cry of “He’s got her!” from a gentleman who’d just managed to catch his partner before she fell into the punch bowl. But then, disaster struck. A minor war broke out near the window, where the air was thick with sarcasm, ill-temper, and one very offended French chef.

Outside, the street crowd—probably bored with their own lives—cheered on the drama like it was a boxing match. “Pitch him in!” they yelled. “Where’s the police? Or better yet, *where’s the fire brigade*?” Inside, two factions had formed: one around Monsieur Alcide Mirobolant, the Chevalier de Juillet (which, if you’re not careful, sounds like a brand of fancy vinegar), and the other around Arthur Pendennis, who was currently trying not to lose his temper, his dignity, or possibly his footing.

And then, like a knight riding in on a donkey with a horn, Captain Strong burst through the door. He didn’t so much enter as *shoulder* his way in, nearly knocking over Madame Fribsby, who shrieked, “Save him! Save him!”—as if he were about to be sacrificed to an ancient god of bad manners.

Now, what caused this ruckus? Let’s rewind. Mr. Pen, still smarting from his recent tumble during a waltz (a fall that had made him look like a startled badger caught mid-dance), was attempting to regain composure by gazing out the window at the sea. The ocean, sparkling under the moonlight, seemed to say, “Hey, buddy, chill out.” But just as Pen was about to whisper, “You’re right,” a hand landed on his shoulder.

He turned—and there stood Mirobolant, eyes wide, face pale, hair like a stormy night, and clearly furious. To be tapped on the shoulder by a French cook? That was worse than being called “sir” by someone who meant “you’re fired.”

“You speak French?” asked Mirobolant, in fluent, dramatic French.

“What’s it to you?” Pen replied in English, because he was not going to play along with any foreign language games unless they involved romance or a duel.

“Ah, but you understand it?” Mirobolant pressed, bowing with theatrical flair.

“Yes,” said Pen, stamping his foot. “I understand it *very* well.”

“Then listen carefully, Monsieur Pendennis,” Mirobolant declared, rolling his Rs like a cannonball down a hill. “Vous êtes un lâche. A coward. A *lache*. Do you know what that means?”

Pen blinked. “Yes, I do. It means you’re about to get punched.”

“No,” said Mirobolant, putting a hand on his hip like a man who had seen many duels and lost none. “It means *you are a coward*, and therefore must fight me.”

“I’ll fling you out the window,” bellowed Pen, lunging forward. “You impudent little *coq*!”

At which point, Captain Broadfoot (who looked like he’d been born to stop fights) and another officer jumped between them. The ladies screamed. The fiddles stopped. Laura, looking like she’d just witnessed a tragedy in a soap opera, leaned over the crowd and whispered, “What in Heaven’s name is happening?”

Strong arrived just in time—like a divine intervention dressed in a tailcoat and a slightly crooked hat. He found Mirobolant grinding his teeth and muttering in a language that sounded suspiciously like French with extra rage.

“What happened?” Strong asked, speaking in Spanish, probably to avoid confusion.

“I am Chevalier de Juillet,” Mirobolant announced, slapping his chest like a man who’d once fought Napoleon personally. “And he insulted me.”

“What did he say?” Strong asked.

“He called me… *Cuisinier*,” hissed the chef, as if the word alone could cause national trauma.

Strong tried not to laugh. “Come, poor Chevalier,” he said, leading him away. “We can’t have duels in front of ladies. And frankly, the poor fellow’s not right in the head.” He whispered this to the crowd, and everyone nodded sagely—especially Laura, whose face lit up with concern.

Pen, meanwhile, was being interrogated by a group of concerned onlookers.

“I don’t know,” he said, puffing his chest. “He was offering his arm to a young lady, so I told him he was a cook. He called me a coward and challenged me. I was so shocked I nearly threw him out the window—hadn’t you all stepped in?”

The gentlemen around him nodded gravely. “D—— him,” one said. “Serve him right. Foreigners! Always thinking they’re better than us. Should’ve been thrown out *first*.”

“But—I’m sorry if I hurt his feelings,” Pen added, suddenly sounding less like a hero and more like a man who’d just realized he’d accidentally insulted a minor nobleman.

Laura rushed to his side. “You’ll shake hands with him before you go to sleep, won’t you, Pen?” she asked, eyes soft as butter. “Foreigners are sensitive. They have different customs. If you hurt someone’s feelings, you should apologize.”

She looked like an angel—maybe even a saint. Pen took her hands, stared into her kind eyes, and said yes, he would.

“How fond that girl is of me,” he thought, smugly. “Should I tell her now? No. Not until I’ve settled this business with the cook.”

Laura, ever the persistent one, asked if he wouldn’t stay and dance with her. “I’m not afraid to waltz with you,” she said, sweetly.

This was a mistake.

Pen immediately saw himself falling again—this time, dramatically, into Miss Roundle, sending her crashing into the wall, while Pynsent laughed. “I shall never dance again,” he declared, darkly. “Never. I’m surprised you’d ask me.”

“Is it because you can’t get Blanche?” Laura asked, with a mischievous glint in her eye.

“No,” Pen snapped. “Because I don’t want to make a fool of myself. For *you* to laugh at me, Laura. I saw you and Pynsent. By Jove! No man shall laugh at me.”

“Pen, Pen, don’t be so wicked!” she cried, wounded by his sudden fury. “Who thinks worse of you for stumbling? Not me. Not anyone.”

Just then, Pynsent appeared. “Lady Rockminster sends her regards,” he said smoothly. “May I escort you to supper?”

“I—I was going with my cousin,” Laura said.

“Oh, pray, no,” Pen interrupted. “You’re in good hands. I’ll leave you. I’m going home.”

“Goodnight, Mr. Pendennis,” Pynsent said, dry as dust—a clear signal that he wanted to box Pen’s ears and call him “pompous idiot.”

Pen didn’t reply. He just bowed and left, ignoring Laura’s pleading eyes.

As soon as he was gone, Pynsent sighed. “What a lovely night,” he said. “The sea is murmuring like a contented cat. I’d rather be walking on the beach than in this stifling room.”

“Very,” said Laura.

Pynsent continued. “Such a strange mix of people. I’ve had to charm the attorney’s daughters, the apothecary’s wife, and a man in the refreshment-room who insisted on buying me champagne. He looked like a pirate who’d just robbed a ship and was trying to impress the locals.”

Laura glanced at him. “I wasn’t listening. I was worried about your cousin and that… that French person.”

“Your cousin has been having a rough night,” Pynsent said. “He hasn’t pleased anyone—Captain Broadwood, the officer, the girl in red, Blanche, the cook, and… me.”

“Didn’t he leave me in your care?” Laura asked, blushing.

“Indeed, I forgive him for that,” Pynsent said warmly. And off they went to supper, Laura clinging to his arm like a prize she’d won in a game show.

But Laura wasn’t hungry. She was distracted. She couldn’t stop thinking about Pen—his jealousy, his anger, his ridiculous pride. How could her mother possibly expect her to marry such a man? And if she did, would it make him happy?

Half an hour later, a waiter handed her a note in pencil:

> *I met Cooky below, ready to fight. I asked his pardon. I’m glad I did. I wanted to speak to you tonight, but will wait till you come home. God bless you. Dance all night with Pynsent and be happy. —PEN.*

Laura smiled. There was still goodness in her mother’s boy.

Meanwhile, Pen descended the stairs, heart heavy. He regretted how he’d treated Laura. Her gentle eyes haunted him. He almost turned back—but then remembered he’d left her with Pynsent. He couldn’t apologize in front of him. So instead, he decided to patch things up with the Frenchman.

Mirobolant was pacing the hall when Pen arrived. He looked cheerful, like a man who’d just discovered chocolate.

“I’ve got him in the coffee-room,” Strong said, grinning. “With pistols and a candle. Or swords on the beach? Mirobolant’s a dead shot with foils—he killed four guards in the July barricades.”

“Confound it,” Pen growled. “I can’t fight a cook!”

“He’s a Chevalier of July,” Strong reminded him. “They present arms to him in France.”

“And you expect me to go out with a servant?” Pen asked, outraged.

“You’ll invite me to a hair trigger?” Strong laughed. “No, I’m joking. I came to settle things. I’ve told Mirobolant you didn’t mean ‘cook’ as an insult. In England, it’s improper for a hired man to offer his arm to a daughter of the house.”

Then Strong dropped the bombshell: Mirobolant was secretly in love with Miss Blanche.

Pen burst out laughing. So did Strong. His anger vanished. He’d been jealous all evening—jealous of Pynsent, jealous of everything. He remembered his own past jealousy over Oaks. He could forgive anything in a man driven by such passion.

So he marched into the coffee-room, extended his hand, and delivered a speech in French so smooth it might have passed for Shakespeare translated.

“I am sincerely sorry for using a word that may have offended Monsieur Mirobolant,” he said. “And I give you my word as a gentleman that I never—*jamais*—intended…”

He paused, proud of his use of “intended,” though he hadn’t actually used the correct word. But Mirobolant was impressed.

“Bravo!” Strong cheered. “You speak French like Chateaubriand!”

“I’ve practiced since childhood,” Pen said.

Strong held back laughter for five minutes—then exploded. Pen never quite understood why.

By daybreak, they reached the Brawl. The ball was over. Madame Fribsby and Mirobolant were heading home. Laura was asleep, heart light. The Claverings were resting. And Blanche? She’d just returned from the refreshment-room, pale as a lemon ice, and told her maid about meeting the most romantic man ever—one who knew her father, the heroic, persecuted, murdered author of her being. She even began writing a sonnet to his ghost before bed.

Pen returned to Fairoaks, still without saying a word to Laura. But he didn’t mind. He’d wait until she came home. He wasn’t really jealous of Pynsent. After all, he was sure Laura would give in—just like her mother always did.

When Helen asked what happened, Pen laughed it off. “No declarations tonight,” he said. “Too much chaos. We’ll save sentiment for later.”

His mother kissed his forehead and patted his hair. She was convinced no woman could resist him.

That night, Pen packed books, cleaned his gun, and smoked pipes while talking to his dog, Ponto.

“I’ll take chambers,” he declared. “Enter the Inn of Court. With two hundred pounds, I’ll survive the first year. Then my pen will support me—tragedy, comedy, novel—all nearly done. I’ll live well without touching Mom’s money. Then, someday, I’ll return, marry Laura, and make her happy. She’s sweet, beautiful, and has given me her money. It’ll steady me, won’t it, Ponto?”

Ponto wagged his tail. Pen patted him. “Yes, she’ll steady me. Won’t you miss me, old boy?”

Ponto licked his hand. Pen smiled.

Laura returned late, driven by Pynsent. Pen’s brow darkened. Laura noticed and felt guilty. Helen, oblivious, rose to go to bed—only for Laura to say she was tired too. So the plan was delayed.

The next morning, Helen woke Pen early.

“Rouse up, dear Pen!” she said. “It’s a beautiful day. Laura’s in the garden.”

Pen laughed. He knew what she was up to.

“Ah, you artful creature,” he said, kissing her. “Can no one escape your tricks?”

Helen blushed, fluttered, and left him.

Pen shaved, dressed, and walked to the garden, feeling grim. “I’m about to tie myself to life,” he thought. “For my mother. Laura’s wonderful. She gave me her money. I wish I hadn’t taken it. But both women want this. So I’ll do it. A man may do worse than make two good people happy.”

In the garden, Laura wore her usual uniform: straw hat, ribbon streamer, blouse, belt, and gauntlets—looking like a soldier sent to rescue a damsel in distress.

She smiled—the same smile that had mocked him at the ball.

Pen scowled. “Why do you laugh at me?” he asked.

“My dear Arthur,” she said, “you looked so funny when you fell. I couldn’t help it.”

“Confound Miss Roundle,” he roared.

“She looked terrible,” Laura said, giggling. “But you were up in seconds. Poor Miss Roundle, sitting on the ground in her red crape dress—can I forget her?”

She mimicked Miss Roundle’s expression—then stopped. “Well, we shouldn’t laugh at her. But we should laugh at you, Pen, if you’re angry about a stumble.”

“You shouldn’t laugh at me, Laura,” Pen said, bitter.

“And why not? Are you such a great man?”

“No,” he said. “I’m such a poor one. Haven’t you baited me enough?”

“Dear Pen,” she said, holding out her hand. “If I’ve hurt you, I beg your pardon.”

“It’s your kindness that humiliates me more than your laughter,” Pen said. “You’re always my superior.”

“Superior to the great Arthur Pendennis?” she asked, teasing. “Impossible.”

“Those who confer benefits should not sneer,” he said. “I don’t like my benefactor laughing at me. It makes the debt harder to bear. You scorn me because I took your money. And I deserve it. But the blow hurts more coming from you.”

And so, beneath the morning sun, the future Mr. Pendennis stood in the garden, torn between duty, pride, and the fact that his fiancée was both adorable and terrifyingly honest.
