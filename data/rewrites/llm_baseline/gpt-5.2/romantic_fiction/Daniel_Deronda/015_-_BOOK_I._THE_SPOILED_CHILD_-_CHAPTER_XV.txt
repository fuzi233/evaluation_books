Gwendolen had gone abroad and, like a child loosed into a fair, learned too quickly the bright trick of chance. The saloons with their green tables had become her new drawing-room; the sharp click of ivory, the thin metallic rustle of gold, the murmurs broken by sudden cries—these were the household sounds she carried in her blood. There were hours when she fancied herself crowned by luck, as if Fate were a lady-in-waiting obliged to smile and hand her winnings; and with that intoxication there crept in a blurred, perilous notion—an idea that in this muddled world nothing mattered so long as one was amused.

Yet the world has its hidden creditors. While she played at reigning, other hands—mysteriously summarized, like a firm-name stamped on a bill, as Grapnell & Co.—had also chosen their amusement, and the cost fell where it was easiest to bruise: on her home. So she returned to England, carrying against her will the visible emblem of her own entanglement: a necklace she had pawned and that another had redeemed, lying on her like a cold chain that refused to become ornament.

As she travelled back, Grandcourt set out to find her—after his fashion. He did not fly with the ardor of a lover who cannot sleep for missing a woman’s face. He drifted. He let Baden-Baden detain him with Russian acquaintances, appointments made and broken with the languid irresponsibility of a man to whom even intention is a kind of pastime. His passions were of that intermittent sort that flicker rather than burn—enough to light a cigar, not enough to warm a house. The common business of living goes on very well without a blazing soul: cravats are tied with scrupulous care, dinners are attended, speeches made to august healths, all at a low expenditure of inward fire. Grandcourt could be persistent too on the same economy—stubborn not from intensity, but from the mere absence of any channel that would carry his will into generous habit. Under pressure such a nature does not rise like a spring; it oozes, then spurts.

Oddly, he had not been displeased by Gwendolen’s sudden flight from the splendid chance he had held out to her. It had piqued him. It pleased him to think she had resented his coolness at Cardell Chase—his having drawn her to the edge of an admission and then walked away, as if consent were a trifle he could postpone. A girl worth mastering ought to have spirit; and if she meant him to follow, it was what he meant too. But for a whole week he did nothing—not even the small act of inquiring where Miss Harleth had gone.

Mr. Lush, watching him, tasted a triumph with his suspicion, like a sauce too rich to trust. Grandcourt said nothing; his face might have belonged to an alligator that has no need to move. Still, delay meant waste—Grandcourt’s slow-churning mind losing momentum, as water loses force in a marsh.

At Diplow the guests were more curious than the host. Was it credible that Miss Harleth had refused Grandcourt? Lady Flora Hollis, lively and inquisitive, found a reason to make a round of calls with Mrs. Torrington—rectory, Offendene, Quetcham—so that she might collect and distribute, twice over, the newest morsels of report. By then Mrs. Davilow and the Gascoignes, after their first sharp agitation, had resolved to prefer any interpretation that did not paint Gwendolen as eccentric or secretive; they wanted her absence to sound as ordinary as a visit, as harmless as a change of air.

The rector’s mind—practical, yet crusted with maxims as a baking-stone with old flour—still entertained the notion that the marriage might only be deferred. He did not know the bitter firmness with which Gwendolen had spoken at home. He could quote to himself that Amaryllis flees only wishing her hiding-place known; and that love, even now, finds its way “over the mountain and over the wave,” steam and timetables making the old hyperbole almost literal. He conceived Gwendolen an Amaryllis with excellent sense and coquettish daring; the question was whether she had dared too much.

Lady Flora, returning from her visits with the news that Miss Harleth had gone to Leubronn with old friends, the Baron and Baroness von Langen, could not resist trying to electrify Grandcourt by dropping the fact at table with a few artful hints that rumor had him for a disappointed adorer. Grandcourt received it quietly—quietly, but not blankly. The next day he ordered Lush to contrive a decent reason for breaking up the Diplow party within another week: he meant to go yachting to the Baltic or somewhere. It was impossible, he implied, to remain at Diplow as if on parole among people he had never wanted.

Lush needed no clearer notice that the yacht would, by some leisurely tacking, drift toward Leubronn. His fear was only that Grandcourt might adhere to his plan with the manner of a creeping billiard-ball and stick on the way. Lush, however, had his own plan: to make himself indispensable and thus accompany him. He succeeded. Gwendolen’s repulsion to Lush was a fact that amused Grandcourt, who liked his creatures obedient and disliked the idea of any creature being outside his reach.

And so it happened that Grandcourt arrived at the Czarina on the fifth day after Gwendolen had left Leubronn, and found there, by one of those crossings that feel like ill-timed repetitions in the long story of families, his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger, with his household, including Deronda.

It is not necessarily a pleasure either to the reigning power or to the heir presumptive when their separate affairs—gout in one, willfulness in the other—bring them to the same watering-place. Sir Hugo was easy-tempered and tolerant; he could bear many differences, many defects. But one difference fretted him more than any debate on Church discipline or ballots: the settlement of the family estates. Faults, too, are less venial when they belong to the person whose existence inconveniences you.

Grandcourt had never been a nephew after Sir Hugo’s heart; but as presumptive heir to the Mallinger lands he was the sign and embodiment of Sir Hugo’s chief grievance—the lack of a son to inherit an estate in which Sir Hugo had only life-interest. Sir Francis had made an ill-advised will: even Diplow, with its modest acres, had been tied up under the same conditions as the ancient wider inheritance of the two Toppings—Diplow, where Sir Hugo had lived and hunted in his younger years, and where his wife and daughters ought to have been able to retire. The grievance had thickened with time. Lady Mallinger had borne three daughters quickly, and then eight silent years had followed; she was now over forty. Sir Hugo, nearly twenty years older, was in that stage when hope wears thin unless some second childhood refreshes it. He had begun to despair of a son; and that made Grandcourt’s interest feel more like a personal affront.

Yet the very pressure of that fact made Sir Hugo careful to keep politeness between them. For he had a plan—grown like a graft alongside his disappointment—to secure Diplow as a future home for Lady Mallinger and the girls. He meant, if he could, to buy off Grandcourt’s prospective interest in Diplow and its attached land with a good lump sum of ready money. If, by some miracle, a son appeared at last, the money would have been wasted; Grandcourt would have been paid to surrender an interest that would have turned to nothing. But Sir Hugo set that risk at nil. He had husbanded his fortune well of late—mines working, leases sold—so that he was prepared for the outlay. The plan required that he should not quarrel with Grandcourt.

Some years earlier, when he wanted Grandcourt’s concurrence to fell an obstructive mass of timber on the demesne, he had congratulated himself on finding no active spite in his nephew’s peculiar mind. Since then nothing had occurred to make them hate each other beyond the limits of perfect politeness, or beyond what was compatible with any strictly mutual accommodation.

Grandcourt, for his part, thought Sir Hugo superfluous and tiresome, and felt the world would be improved when Sir Hugo could be expunged. But he had been made aware through Lush—always a useful medium—of the baronet’s inclinations regarding Diplow, and was secretly gratified to have the alternative of money in his thoughts. Even if he never meant to accept it, there was a pleasure in being able to refuse what Sir Hugo desired. That hinted transaction had counted among the motives which made him ask for a year’s tenancy of Diplow—a request that had annoyed Sir Hugo to grant, because the excellent hunting might tempt Grandcourt to keep his future grasp on the place. A man who already has two houses, one with worse hunting, might naturally covet a third with better. And Lush had thrown out to Sir Hugo a probability that Grandcourt would woo and win Miss Arrowpoint; in that case, ready money might tempt him less. Thus, meeting unexpectedly at Leubronn, Sir Hugo was full of curiosity to learn how things had gone at Diplow, bent on being civil, and looking forward to a private talk with Lush.

Between Deronda and Grandcourt there was also a relation—fainter in its outward marks, but peculiar—depending on circumstances not yet spoken. Still, at the first meeting in the table d’hôte, an hour after Grandcourt’s arrival, no one showed suppressed chagrin. Later, when the four gentlemen met on the terrace without Lady Mallinger, they drifted together through the rooms, Sir Hugo saying as they entered the large saal, “Did you play much at Baden, Grandcourt?”

“No; I looked on and betted a little with some Russians.”

“Had you luck?”

“What did I win, Lush?”

“You brought away about two hundred,” Lush answered.

“You are not here for the sake of play, then?” Sir Hugo asked.

“No; I don’t care about play now. It’s a confounded strain,” said Grandcourt, his diamond ring catching the light as he moved, his fingers idly at his whisker while rouged foreign faces turned to stare at the new milord.

“The fact is, somebody should invent a mill to do amusements for you, my dear fellow,” said Sir Hugo, “as the Tartars get their praying done. But I agree with you; I never cared for play. It’s monotonous—knits the brain up into meshes. And it knocks me up to watch it now. I suppose one gets poisoned with the bad air. I never stay here more than ten minutes. But where’s your gambling beauty, Deronda? Have you seen her lately?”

“She’s gone,” said Deronda, curtly.

“An uncommonly fine girl—a perfect Diana,” Sir Hugo went on, turning again toward Grandcourt. “Really worth a little straining to look at. I saw her winning, and she took it as coolly as if she had known it all beforehand. The same day Deronda happened to see her losing like wildfire, and she bore it with immense pluck. I suppose she was cleaned out, or was wise enough to stop in time. How do you know she’s gone?”

Deronda’s answer came with a slight shrug, as if he would have shut the subject into a drawer. “Oh—by the Visitor-list. Vandernoodt told me her name was Harleth, and she was with the Baron and Baroness von Langen. I saw by the list that Miss Harleth was no longer there.”

To Lush this added nothing beyond the fact that Gwendolen had been gambling—something he had already inferred. He too had looked at the list and learned she was gone, but had no intention of forcing that knowledge on Grandcourt before Grandcourt chose to ask for it. Grandcourt had not asked. It had been enough, apparently, to believe that the object of search would turn up somewhere sooner or later.

Now, though, something piquant had been said, and Grandcourt did not miss a syllable. After a pause he turned to Deronda. “Do you know those people—the Langens?”

“I have talked with them a little since Miss Harleth went away. I knew nothing of them before.”

“Where is she gone—do you know?”

“She is gone home,” Deronda answered, coldly, as if drawing a line.

But another impulse crossed him—sharp, inexplicable even to himself. He turned and looked markedly at Grandcourt, adding, “But it is possible you know her. Her home is not far from Diplow: Offendene, near Winchester.”

The sudden directness of Deronda’s gaze could make silence seem like speech. There was in his face a calm intensity, a richness of tint and life, that startled attention as if something had been uttered. It irritated Grandcourt—not enough to show more than a slight movement of eyelids—that Deronda should turn on him with such intentness when no more was demanded than a simple exchange of information.

“Yes, I know her,” Grandcourt drawled, and with his shoulder toward Deronda paused to look again at the gambling as if that were a better conversation.

Sir Hugo, as the three moved on a little, turned to Lush. “What of her, eh? She must be a new-comer at Offendene. Old Blenny lived there after the dowager died.”

“A little too much of her,” Lush said in a low, significant tone, not sorry to let Sir Hugo smell the truth of it.

“Why—how?” Sir Hugo asked.

They passed out of the salon into an airy promenade, where footsteps fell with that hollow politeness particular to such places, and the music from within thinned into a distant, constant tremor.

“He has been on the brink of marrying her,” Lush went on. “But I hope it’s off now. She’s a niece of the clergyman—Gascoigne—at Pennicote. Her mother is a widow with a brood of daughters. This girl will have nothing, and is as dangerous as gunpowder. It would be a foolish marriage. But she has taken a freak against him; she ran off here without notice when he had agreed to call the next day. The fact is, he’s here after her; but he was in no great hurry, and between his caprice and hers they are likely enough not to get together again. But of course he has lost his chance with the heiress.”

Grandcourt joined them, cutting the thread of confidence. “What a beastly den this is!—a worse hole than Baden. I shall go back to the hotel.”

When Sir Hugo and Deronda were alone, the baronet began again, his tone half amused, half calculating. “Rather a pretty story. That girl has something in her. She must be worth running after—has de l’imprévu. I think her appearance on the scene has bettered my chance of getting Diplow, whether the marriage comes off or not.”

“I should hope a marriage like that would not come off,” Deronda said, with a note of disgust that was more than mere disapproval; it was as if he heard, behind the words “marriage” and “like that,” an unwholesome noise in the world’s soundscape, a harsh grinding where something living ought to breathe.

“What! are you a little touched with the sublime lash?” Sir Hugo said, lifting his glasses to make out Deronda’s expression more clearly.

“On the contrary,” said Deronda, “I should rather be inclined to run away from her.”

“Why, you would easily cut out Grandcourt. A girl with her spirit would think you the finer match of the two,” Sir Hugo persisted, enjoying his own impossible counsel, the way a man enjoys a jest that costs him nothing.

“I suppose pedigree and land belong to a fine match,” Deronda returned, coldly.

“The best horse will win in spite of pedigree, my boy. You remember Napoleon’s mot—Je suis un ancêtre.”

“I am not sure that I want to be an ancestor,” said Deronda. “It doesn’t seem to me the rarest sort of origination.”

“You won’t run after the pretty gambler, then?” Sir Hugo asked, letting the glasses drop.

“Decidedly not.”

He spoke truth. And yet the truth had a shadow. Deronda knew—because he could not quite prevent knowing—that under other circumstances he might have given way to the interest this girl had stirred in him and tried to learn what lay beneath her daring, beneath that quick ear for the world’s applause and that reckless deafness to the world’s warning. But his own history, still unspoken, had biased him strongly in another direction. He felt himself in no sense free.
