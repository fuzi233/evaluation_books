“Ol—ol—how d’you spell it, anyhow?” the sharp young woman behind the brass-grilled window asked, her pencil poised as if she were about to score a careless cook for muddling a recipe.

Newland Archer pushed his wife’s telegram nearer. The office smelled of damp wool, metal dust, and the faint, singed sweetness of the electric wire—an acrid modern odor that seemed to live on its own thin hiss. Outside, Broadway’s wheels and hoofbeats kept up their restless, urban percussion, a racket that made his temples throb.

“Olenska—O-len-ska,” he repeated, drawing the message back and printing the syllables carefully above May’s slanting hand, as though spelling were a way of keeping the name from being mishandled.

“It’s an unlikely name for a New York telegraph office—at least in this quarter,” an unexpected voice remarked close to his shoulder.

Archer turned and found Lawrence Lefferts beside him, fingers smoothing his moustache with the same practiced care one might use to skim froth from a consommé—showing skill while pretending it was effortless. Lefferts’s eyes, deliberately polite, affected not to glance at the telegram.

“Hallo, Newland. Thought I’d catch you here. I’ve just heard of old Mrs. Mingott’s stroke; and as I was on my way to the house I saw you turning down this street and nipped after you. I suppose you’ve come from there?”

Archer nodded and slid the telegram under the lattice.

“Very bad, eh?” Lefferts went on. “Wiring to the family, I suppose. I gather it is bad, if you’re including Countess Olenska.”

Something hot and bitter rose in Archer—like the first scorch when butter is left too long in the pan. His lips hardened; for an instant he wanted, with an almost primitive clarity, to strike the long, vain, handsome face beside him.

“Why?” he asked.

Lefferts, who prided himself on never being caught in open debate, lifted his brows in an ironic grimace that reminded Archer, without words, of the girl behind the grill and her listening pencil. Nothing could be worse form than temper on public display. Archer had never felt less in awe of form; but the impulse to do Lefferts bodily harm passed as quickly as it came. To handle Ellen Olenska’s name with Lefferts—here, now, at the very lip of Catherine Mingott’s sick-room—was unthinkable, like letting a vulgar street tune blare through a house where someone lay in pain.

He paid for the telegram. The two men went out together into the street, where the city’s clangor pressed on them—iron-rimmed wheels over cobbles, a vendor’s cry, the harsh bell of a passing car—each sound jostling for dominance as if noise itself were the new social order.

Archer, regaining control as one regains a knife’s edge after it slips, said evenly: “Mrs. Mingott is much better. The doctor feels no anxiety whatever.”

Lefferts, instantly relieved, overflowed with the proper phrases; then, as if the conversation must have its fashionable sauce, asked if Archer had heard the beastly bad rumors again about Beaufort.

That afternoon the announcement of Beaufort’s failure was in all the papers. It fell on New York like soot—dulling everything it touched, even the earlier report of Mrs. Manson Mingott’s stroke. Only the few who knew the obscure thread that bound the two events wondered if old Catherine’s seizure had been something more than age and flesh rebelling at last.

The tale of Beaufort’s dishonor darkened drawing-rooms and club-corners alike. There had never, Mr. Letterblair said, been a worse case in his memory—nor, for that matter, in the memory of the far-off Letterblair who had given his name to the firm. The bank had continued to take in money for a whole day after failure was inevitable; and since so many of its clients belonged to one or another of the ruling clans, Beaufort’s duplicity tasted doubly rancid—like finding stale meat hidden beneath fresh herbs.

If Mrs. Beaufort had not struck the note that such misfortunes (the word was her own) were “the test of friendship,” compassion might have softened the general indignation against her husband. As it was—especially after the object of her nocturnal visit to Mrs. Manson Mingott had become known—her cynicism was judged to exceed his. And she had not the excuse, nor her detractors the satisfaction, of pleading that she was “a foreigner.”

It comforted those whose securities were safe to remind themselves that Beaufort was foreign; but when a Dallas of South Carolina took Beaufort’s view and glibly spoke of his soon being “on his feet again,” the argument lost its savor, and nothing remained but to accept this dreadful proof of the indissolubility of marriage. Society must manage without the Beauforts; that was all—except for the hapless victims of the disaster: Medora Manson, the poor old Miss Lannings, and certain other misguided ladies of good family who might have been spared if only they had listened to Mr. Henry van der Luyden.

“The best thing the Beauforts can do,” Mrs. Archer pronounced, as if diagnosing a fever and prescribing broth, “is to go and live at Regina’s little place in North Carolina. Beaufort has always kept a racing stable, and he had better breed trotting horses. I should say he had all the qualities of a successful horsedealer.”

Everyone agreed, with that easy unanimity by which New York seasoned its judgments, but no one stooped to inquire what the Beauforts meant to do. They were already being scraped from the social platter.

The next day Mrs. Manson Mingott was much better. Her voice returned enough for her to order that no one should mention the Beauforts to her again, and when Dr. Bencomb appeared she demanded to know what her family meant by making such a fuss.

“If people of my age will eat chicken-salad in the evening what are they to expect?” she asked.

It was said with her usual imperious humor, but there was something revealing in the very domesticity of it: as though the grand dramas of money and disgrace could be reduced, in the end, to the stubborn facts of appetite and digestion. Bencomb, seizing the opening like a cook who knows how to rescue a dish, modified her diet, and the stroke was transformed—conveniently, almost miraculously—into an attack of indigestion.

Yet in spite of her firmness old Catherine did not wholly recover her former attitude toward life. The remoteness of age, though it had not diminished her curiosity, had blunted a compassion never very lively; and she had no difficulty in letting Beaufort’s ruin slide out of her mind. But for the first time she grew absorbed in her own symptoms, and began to take a sentimental interest in family members she had formerly dismissed.

Mr. Welland, in particular, became—by a strange turn of fortune—the object of her attention. Of her sons-in-law he was the one she had most consistently ignored; and all his wife’s efforts to paint him as forceful and clever “if he had only chosen” had been met with Catherine’s derisive chuckle. Now, however, his eminence as a valetudinarian made him precious to her, and she issued an imperial summons that he should come and compare diets as soon as his temperature permitted—for old Catherine was suddenly the first to recognize that one could not be too careful about temperatures.

Twenty-four hours after Madame Olenska’s summons a telegram announced that she would arrive from Washington on the evening of the following day.

At the Wellands’—where the Newland Archers happened to be lunching—the question of who should meet her at Jersey City sprang up at once, sharpened by the Welland household’s continual struggle with material difficulties, as if it were a frontier post improvising meals from scant stores. Servants, horses, carriages, appointments—everything had to be portioned out like rations.

It was decided that Mrs. Welland could not possibly go, because she was to accompany her husband to old Catherine’s that afternoon, and the brougham could not be spared: if Mr. Welland were “upset” by seeing his mother-in-law for the first time after her attack, he might have to be taken home at a moment’s notice. The Welland sons would, of course, be “down town”; Mr. Lovell Mingott would be hurrying back from shooting, and the Mingott carriage would be engaged to meet him; and one could not ask May, at the close of a winter afternoon, to go alone across the ferry to Jersey City—even in her own carriage.

And yet it might appear inhospitable—contrary to old Catherine’s express wishes—if Madame Olenska were allowed to arrive with no one at the station. Mrs. Welland’s tired voice implied that it was just like Ellen to place the family in such a dilemma.

“It’s always one thing after another,” the poor lady grieved, in one of her rare little revolts; “the only thing that makes me think Mamma must be less well than Dr. Bencomb will admit is this morbid desire to have Ellen come at once, however inconvenient it is to meet her.”

The words were thoughtless, as impatience so often is; and Mr. Welland pounced on them instantly.

“Augusta,” he said, turning pale and setting down his fork with the gravity of a man laying down a surgical instrument, “have you any other reason for thinking that Bencomb is less to be relied on than he was? Have you noticed that he has been less conscientious than usual in following up my case or your mother’s?”

Mrs. Welland, going pale in her turn as the consequences of her blunder unrolled before her, managed to laugh, and even helped herself to a second portion of scalloped oysters, as if butter and breadcrumb crust could shore up her cheerfulness. Then, struggling back into her old armor, she said: “My dear, how could you imagine such a thing? I only meant that, after the decided stand Mamma took about its being Ellen’s duty to go back to her husband, it seems strange that she should be seized with this sudden whim to see her, when there are half a dozen other grandchildren she might have asked for. But we must never forget that Mamma, in spite of her wonderful vitality, is a very old woman.”

Mr. Welland’s brow stayed clouded; his anxious imagination had latched on to that last phrase as a tooth catches on gristle.

“Yes—your mother’s a very old woman; and for all we know Bencomb may not be as successful with very old people. As you say, my dear, it’s always one thing after another; and in another ten or fifteen years I suppose I shall have the pleasing duty of looking about for a new doctor. It’s always better to make such a change before it’s absolutely necessary.”

Having arrived at this Spartan decision, he firmly took up his fork again.

“But all the while,” Mrs. Welland began, as she rose from table and led the way into the wilderness of purple satin and malachite called the back drawing-room, “I don’t see how Ellen’s to be got here tomorrow evening; and I do like to have things settled for at least twenty-four hours ahead.”

Archer, who had been staring with an odd fascination at a small painting of two cardinals carousing—its reds like spilled wine—set in octagonal ebony with onyx medallions, turned.

“Shall I fetch her?” he proposed. “I can easily get away from the office in time to meet the brougham at the ferry, if May will send it there.”

His heart beat with a sudden, illicit liveliness, as though the very prospect of seeing Ellen had thrown open a window in him. Mrs. Welland sighed with gratitude; May, who had moved to the window, turned and beamed approval.

“So you see, Mamma, everything will be settled twenty-four hours in advance,” May said, bending to kiss her mother’s troubled forehead.

May’s brougham waited at the door, and she was to drive Archer to Union Square so he could catch a Broadway car to the office. As she settled into her corner, she said, carefully: “I didn’t want to worry Mamma by raising fresh obstacles; but how can you meet Ellen tomorrow, and bring her back to New York, when you’re going to Washington?”

“Oh, I’m not going,” Archer answered.

“Not going? Why—what’s happened?” Her voice was clear as a bell, full of the bright correctness of wifely concern.

“The case is off—postponed.”

“Postponed? How odd! I saw a note this morning from Mr. Letterblair to Mamma saying that he was going to Washington tomorrow for the big patent case that he was to argue before the Supreme Court. You said it was a patent case, didn’t you?”

“Well—that’s it: the whole office can’t go. Letterblair decided to go this morning.”

“Then it’s not postponed?” she persisted—an insistence so unlike her that Archer felt the heat rise to his face, as if he were blushing for her lapse from the old, trained delicacies. It wasn’t the question that hurt; it was the sight of her making believe she hadn’t caught him.

“No; but my going is,” he said, cursing the needless particulars he had offered when he first spoke of Washington, and remembering how he had once read that clever liars give details, but the cleverest do not.

It stung him less to lie to May than to watch her struggle to keep the lie from naming itself between them. He took refuge, meanly, in sarcasm.

“I’m not going till later on—luckily for the convenience of your family.”

As he spoke he felt her looking at him. He forced himself to meet her eyes, not wishing to seem to evade them. Their glances touched, and in that instant something passed—some knowledge, sharp as the chime of a bell heard too near—that perhaps neither of them wanted.

“Yes; it is awfully convenient,” May answered brightly, “that you should be able to meet Ellen after all; you saw how much Mamma appreciated your offering to do it.”

“Oh, I’m delighted to do it.”

The carriage stopped. As he sprang down, she leaned forward and laid her hand on his.

“Good-bye, dearest,” she said; and her eyes were so intensely blue that afterward he wondered if they had shone through tears.

He turned away and hurried across Union Square, where the city’s din broke against him in hard waves, and repeated to himself in an inward chant—measuring time as a cook measures heat, as a lover measures possibility: “It’s all of two hours from Jersey City to old Catherine’s. It’s all of two hours—and it may be more.”
