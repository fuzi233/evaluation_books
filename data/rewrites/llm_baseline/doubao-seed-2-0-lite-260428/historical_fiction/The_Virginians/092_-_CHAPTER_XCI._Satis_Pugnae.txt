It has always struck me—speaking with all deference to men who have made war their lifelong trade—that the earthen ramparts thrown up on Breed’s Hill shaped the entire course of our American conflict for the British Crown. The carnage those ragged colonists dealt us from behind those rough defences was so brutal, their stand so stubbornly unyielding, that every British general who came after moved through the rest of the war with a lingering, unnecessary awe of any rebel barricade. By the time of the White Plains engagement, when Mr. Washington’s half-starved army lay completely at the mercy of our victorious troops, we learned later that our advance halted dead in its tracks before a makeshift fortification built of nothing but cornstalks and loose straw.

Another chance to crush the rebellion slipped through our fingers entirely: the whole long winter that the dwindling, demoralized Continental force huddled freezing and unarmed at Valley Forge, under the very nose of Sir William Howe’s perfectly provisioned, grandly quartered army in Philadelphia. Our men feasted, gambled, danced to fiddle tunes, and threw mock medieval tournaments where fine ladies wove wreaths for the “clement” general, inscribing their favors with absurd verses promising his laurels would never fade. I have always wondered why the grateful rebels never erected a statue to him: no officer in their whole army served their cause half so well, fumbling every chance we had to strike a decisive blow, wrapping our defeats in soft cotton so the rebels barely felt the hurt of them, helping their broken, outnumbered forces back to their feet again and again.

Think of Wolfe, eighteen years prior, the fiery young warrior England sent to seize Quebec: pacing his lines day and night, sleepless, eaten up with eagerness, prowling midnight forests, climbing craggy precipices before dawn, watching until his great heart was worn thin before he struck, and died victorious on the Plains of Abraham. Compare him now to Howe, laughing mid-dance at a Philadelphia ball, his fiddles sawing out tunes while the rebel army he should have crushed froze to death in their huts a few dozen miles away.

The grand scheme hatched by ministers in London and our top generals in New York was simple: drive a military corridor from Canada down the Hudson to New York City, split New England off from the rest of the colonies, and crush the rebellion piecemeal. Gentleman Johnny Burgoyne was given command of the expedition, and set out from Quebec with all the pomp of a conquering hero, firing off grand proclamations threatening the colonists with England’s full might, summoning the “misguided rebels” to lay down their arms. He brought with him a fearsome regular British force, a corps of hardened German veterans, a savage band of Indian auxiliaries, and a full park of heavy artillery. Everyone assumed the Loyalist populace along his march would flock to the royal standard. The Continental force opposing him was small at first, and Washington’s main army was weakened by the thousands of men he was forced to rush north to block Burgoyne’s advance. A second British detachment was supposed to fight its way up the Hudson from New York, clear the river of all rebel resistance, and meet Burgoyne at Albany. At that point, Washington’s depleted army could have been crushed once and for all— but Providence willed it otherwise, and I for one do not regret how the war ended.

Looking back across the board now, every blunder our side made is plain as day. We were perpetually too late. Supplies and reinforcements from home arrived too late. Succor to our beleaguered outposts reached them too late. Our fleet hove into view off Yorktown only hours after Cornwallis had already struck his colors. When an escape route opened for Burgoyne, he delayed retreating until it was already gone. I have heard disgraced old officers argue for hours over wine, proving that a single favorable wind would have kept America in the empire, that one unseasonable tempest alone stopped us from destroying the entire French fleet, that nightfall alone snatched Washington and his whole army out of our grasp no less than three separate times. Who that loves history has not speculated on all the “what ifs” that change the course of the world? I pull my tattered old war map from the shelf sometimes, stare down at the battleground, and marvel at the string of silly, trivial turns of luck that lost us a game we held all the best cards to win.

I cannot deny the old fight still stirs my languid, aging blood. My brother Hal, settled safe on his Virginia plantation, is perfectly content with his quiet lot: the company of his neighboring planter friends, tending his tobacco crops and his enslaved laborers, fishing the creeks, shooting ducks, hunting the hardwood forests, then settling in for a rubber of cards and a hearty supper by his fireside. Happy Hal, dozing on his porch surrounded by hounds at his feet, his sharp little wife running the whole household and her easygoing husband, who obeyed her every word when she lived and grieved her only a short while before he found his peace. His lot would have felt like prison to me, but how lucky the man whose foot fits the exact shoe fortune gives him!

My own income is five times Hal’s, my English house built of fine brick faced with freestone, my wife— I would not trade her for any other woman alive, my children all healthy and bright. But for all the plums and peaches that spill out of Plenty’s horn into my lap, I fear I have been something of an ingrate. My gatekeeper Hodge, sharing his crust of bread and scrap of bacon with a family as large as my own, seems to enjoy his simple meal far more than I enjoy Mrs. Molly’s finest sweetmeats and Mr. Gumbo’s choicest claret from the cellar. Sweetmeats have lost their savor for me, and the scent of old wine palls on an old man’s nose. Our parson Joe Blake has preached many a sermon at me over the years, though I confess I did not always listen closely when he first delivered them. You remember that October evening, honest Joe? (He does, for he has told the story over a bottle as many times as he has preached his sermons, and my Lady Warrington pretends she has never heard it a hundred times.) The night we scrambled through an embrasure at Fort Clinton, and a rebel soldier lifted his musket to dash my brains out with the clubbed end, and your sword ran him through before he could strike. Back then you wore the red coat of the brave 63rd Foot, where your captain Sill died pierced through with a dozen wounds beside you. Now you wear black, and preach in my pulpit, your sermons short, your doctrine sound, and your belly so round I doubt your old 63rd uniform could ever button shut again across it. The other day we read the papers together over our wine: of old friend Howe’s glorious naval victory on the first of June, of our old comrade Lord Rawdon joining the Duke of York’s force, of the duke’s full retreat before Pichegru, and my son Miles helping to take Valenciennes with barely a fight. Oh, Joe, would you not give a year of your quiet parson’s life to be young again, to leave this gouty toe of mine at home, saddle my old mare, and ride back out to feel your blood pound with the thrill of the game once more?

The last engagement I saw played out was a gallant little victory for our side, though it came far too late to change the war’s outcome, and could not stop the catastrophe that was about to swallow Burgoyne’s whole army. At the end of September 1777, after weeks of frustrating delays that weakened the blow we were meant to strike, an expedition finally sailed out of New York harbor. Had it set sail a fortnight earlier, it could have saved Burgoyne’s doomed force— but the fates willed otherwise. The delay was no fault of Sir Henry Clinton’s, who dared not leave New York City unprotected; contrary winds simply held up the long-awaited reinforcements sailing from England. Those ships brought us our first letters from home in months, full of news of our children safe with their Aunt Hetty and their grandfather. My Lady Warrington never spoke a word of reproach, but I could read the longing in her anxious eyes: why stay here, fighting your own countrymen, risking your life and all our happiness? I promised her we would not be facing any serious danger. Sir Henry would only accompany the expedition for the first leg, I told her, and we would sail back for England the moment the reconnaissance was done, and please God we would all be reunited for Christmas.

Three thousand men embarked: two thin regiments of American Loyalists, a corps of New York militia (with whom our kinsman Will Esmond served as a captain), and a force of hard regulars. We sailed up the Hudson, which to my mind is far more wild and beautiful than the famous Rhine in Europe, and no rebel shot greeted us from the beetling, craggy palisades lining the shore. For once, the enemy had no idea we were coming. We landed first on the eastern bank at Verplancks Point, where the small rebel garrison withdrew after a token resistance. Their commander, old Israel Putnam, the gruff fighting general everyone knew, assumed our whole force was marching east to storm the Highlands passes, so he pulled all his men back to defend them. We left a small detachment behind to pretend we were in pursuit, and before dawn the next day, ferried two thousand men with no artillery across to Stoney Point on the western shore, at the foot of the huge, steep mountain the old Dutch settlers named the Dunderberg. A small stream cuts between the base of the Dunderberg and the next height, where Fort Clinton stood—named not for Sir Henry, but for one of two prominent New York gentlemen of that name, who were both in command of the rebel forces opposing us that very day. A short march away stood Fort Montgomery, and behind them both rose Bear Hill, while across the river jutted the great peak the Dutch had drolly named Saint Anthony’s Nose.

We attacked both forts almost simultaneously. Half our men were sent to storm Fort Montgomery under brave Colonel Campbell, who fell dead before the rampart. Sir Henry himself led the rest against Fort Clinton, declaring he hoped we would have better luck than we had at Sullivan’s Island. The narrow path winding up the Dunderberg was so steep and narrow only three men could walk abreast, and we scaled it in utter silence, stunned step after step to meet no resistance at all. The rebels had not even posted a watch there. We were not spotted until we began descending the other side, where a small company sent hurriedly to stop us was scattered easily. The shooting put any chance of surprise out of the question. The fort stood before us, its guns firing down at the exposed slope. The men had strict orders not to fire a single shot. Sir Henry grabbed the regimental colors of the 63rd, held them high over his head, and climbed right up the hill with the stormers. The slope was so steep that the men hauled each other up, pushing one another over the ramparts and through the gun embrasures. That was where Lieutenant Joseph Blake saved my worthless life, and earned the living at Warrington that now supports him and his family.

A quarter of the fort’s garrison escaped into the woods; the rest were killed, wounded, or taken prisoner. Fort Montgomery fell just as quickly. That night, as we stood on the heights where the king’s standard now flew, we were treated to a magnificent sight down on the river. The rebels had stretched a massive chain and heavy boom across the Hudson just below Fort Montgomery, all the way to Saint Anthony’s Nose, to block our ships. Tied up behind that barrier were several American frigates and galleys. When the forts fell, the rebel crews tried to escape upriver under cover of dark, but the wind was against them, and they knew our guns would sink them at dawn. They abandoned ship, set all the vessels ablaze with their sails still fully unfurled, and we watched those great pyramids of flame drift across the dark water, their light reflecting red on the surface, until they exploded one by one in roiling clouds of sparks and smoke and sank beneath the waves.

The next morning a rebel parlementaire came under flag of truce to ask after their wounded and captured men, and he brought me a note that made my heart leap straight up into my throat. It was dated October 7, from Major-General George Clinton’s divisional headquarters, and it read simply: “Colonel H. Warrington, of the Virginia line, hopes that Sir George Warrington escaped unhurt in the assault of last evening, from which the Colonel himself was so fortunate as to retire without the least injury.” I prayed more fervently that night than I had in all the war, thanking God that my dearest brother had been spared, and making a solemn vow then and there that I had had enough fighting. No more of this fratricidal war. My duty was done.

I asked Sir Henry for leave to visit General Clinton’s camp under flag of truce, and he granted it easily. A small boat carried me up the river, and rebel pickets met me on the shore. Hal came running to me almost immediately. He had only just joined Clinton’s division with dispatches from Washington’s headquarters at Philadelphia, and heard only after the fort fell that I had been part of the assault. We spent a long, perfect night together in his rough little hut. His old servant Sady, who had followed Hal to war from Castlewood, cooked up a feast fit for two long-parted brothers. There was only one straw bed in the hut, and we slept side by side on it, just as we had when we were small boys at home. He was overjoyed when I told him I was leaving the army, that I would take off my red coat and go home to my own estates in England. He threw his arms around me, weeping. “Thank God!” he cried. “Oh George, think what could have happened if we had run into each other on those ramparts two nights ago!” He turned pale just thinking of it. He told me our mother was safe back at Castlewood, staunch old Tory that she was: Washington himself had given special orders that no harm should come to her, and Hal’s clever, bold wife Fanny watched over her like a guard. He rhapsodized over Fanny for an hour, never pausing for a single reply, before laughing and saying what a lucky escape he had had when he almost married the foolish Maria Esmond years before. Even old sour cousin Hagan prays for King George every morning and night behind locked doors, he said, and no one bothers him.

Morning came far too soon. Hal walked me down to the boat waiting at the shore, and we embraced long and hard before I stepped aboard. He stood on the bank waving until his figure blurred in the river mist, and the tears in my eyes made it impossible to see him any longer.

For all its gallantry, our little victory at the two forts was useless. It came far too late to save Burgoyne. Multiple messengers we sent through to him were captured, and we never learned their fates. One poor wretch sent with a personal letter from Sir Henry fell in with a rebel regiment that was wearing captured British red coats taken off a prize merchant ship. He thought he had reached friendly lines, walked right up to the sentries, and was hanged as a spy when his dispatches were found on him. Ten days after we took the forts, the terrible news of Burgoyne’s surrender at Saratoga reached us. We sailed home to England not long after. My Lady Warrington shed no tears for the disaster. She had her children, her father, her sister to greet, and a thousand nightly prayers to offer God that her husband was finally safe, and the fratricidal war was no longer any business of his.
