"The first lesson in any grand undertaking," the old sailor Robinson Crusoe once wrote, "is to count the cost before you lay the foundation stone." These were words that had sat gathering dust on my shelf until just yesterday, when I foolishly chose to open them again. It happened right after young Master Franklin Blake dropped by this morning.

It was May twenty-first, 1850. A bright, crisp day, though the air still hummed with the unease of that terrible business in Yorkshire two years prior—the affair of the diamond. My young master, Mr. Franklin, came to me with a serious air. He spoke of a meeting with the lawyer, Mr. Bruff, regarding some family papers.

"Betteredge," he began, his voice steady but earnest, "we've decided something. The story of that missing gem needs to be set down in black and white. Not just a whisper, but a record. For the sake of truth, and perhaps for the peace of mind of those who have been wrongly suspected, it must be told."

I nodded, trying to look wise and agreeable, as lawyers' clerks are trained to do. "Aye, sir," I murmured. "Truth is a good thing, surely."

"But there is a method to it," Mr. Franklin continued, stepping closer as if sharing a secret. "We cannot rely on memory alone, nor on hearsay. The plan is simple: every person who has known the tale must write their piece. We shall weave the whole history together, like threads in a tapestry, limited only to what each of us personally witnessed."

He paused, looking around the room as if expecting the moonstone itself to materialize from thin air. "First, we need the prologue. How the jewel first came into the possession of my Uncle Herncastle, back in India fifty years ago. I have a manuscript for that, written by an eye-witness. Then, the main event: how it arrived at Lady Verinder's house in Yorkshire, and how, within barely twelve hours, it vanished without a trace. Now, who knows the house, the servants, the comings and goings better than you, Betteredge?"

He pointed a finger at me, a gesture that made my heart skip. "You must begin."

And so, the trap was sprung.

If you should ask me what I thought at that moment, I shall tell you the honest truth. I feigned humility. I bowed my head and declared, with a show of deep regret, that I was "quite unequal to the task." Inside, however, I was bubbling with pride. *Of course* I could do it! Who else in England knew the layout of that house, the habits of the staff, the secrets whispered in the corridors? To refuse would be cowardice; to accept was destiny. Young Master Franklin saw straight through my act, I'm sure of it. His eyes twinkled with mischief, and he pressed me further, insisting I give my abilities a fair trial.

Two hours have now passed since he left. I have retreated to my desk, where I sit paralyzed, staring at a blank sheet of paper. The ink bottle sits untouched, the quill rests idle. And why? Because, like poor Robinson Crusoe, I have realized the folly of starting a job before assessing the sheer weight of it.

But let us not mistake this hesitation for fear. I am not a superstitious man. I have read many books, and I am a scholar in my line. I am seventy years old, blessed with a sharp memory and legs that still carry me well. Yet, I hold a peculiar reverence for a certain volume bound in blue leather, costing four shillings and sixpence, featuring a picture on the front cover. They call it *Robinson Crusoe*. Some say it never existed, others claim it will never be written again. I have tested it, year after year, usually paired with a pipe of tobacco, and it has been my constant companion.

When my spirits were low, it lifted me. When I needed counsel, it offered guidance. In days gone by, when my wife plagued me with her endless chatter, it was my refuge. Even now, when I have taken a drop too many, *Robinson Crusoe* has brought me back to earth. I have worn out six copies in my service, and on my lady's last birthday, she gifted me a seventh. That night, I indulged slightly, and the book worked its magic once more.

So, does this ramble about books and tobacco sound like the start of a mystery novel? Does it feel like I am wandering off into the mist, searching for something I cannot name? Perhaps it does. Let us try again. Let us take a fresh leaf, wipe away the hesitation, and address you, dear reader, with my highest regards. For the story of the Moonstone awaits.
