"Patience, little friend. The world is vast, and its dimensions are often hidden until we learn to look beyond the surface."

In this realm of Flatland, existence is defined strictly by two coordinates: length and breadth. There is no height, no depth, no third dimension to offer refuge or perspective. We live on an infinite plane where every line segment stretches forever unless bounded by others. Our climate is uniform, governed solely by the angle at which lines intersect; our houses are simple polygons, their stability determined entirely by the number of sides they possess. A triangle is humble but sharp; a square is sturdy and common; a pentagon holds a place of minor nobility.

The inhabitants of Flatland move with purpose along these flat axes. To recognize one another, we rely first on touch—a gentle tap against the side reveals the shape of our neighbor. But sight, too, has become a refined art. By observing how light reflects off our edges, we can deduce our status and identity without contact. Yet, irregular figures exist among us—those who lack symmetry or proper angles—and they are viewed with suspicion, often feared as chaotic anomalies that threaten the order of the plane.

For centuries, we have practiced painting, attempting to capture the essence of shapes through color. However, this led to a dangerous sedition when some dared to claim that color was a fundamental property of reality rather than a mere decoration. The Universal Colour Bill was enacted to suppress this heresy, stripping away hue and enforcing a monochrome society where geometry alone defines truth.

Our priests, clad in circles—the perfect figure with infinite sides—guard the Doctrine of Two Dimensions. They preach that any suggestion of a third dimension is blasphemy, a delusion born of ignorance. Their authority is absolute, rooted in the belief that perfection lies within the flatness of our world.

But recently, strange visions have begun to plague my mind. I dreamt of a world called Lineland, where beings exist as mere points moving back and forth on a single line, unaware of anything beyond their forward and backward paths. I tried to explain the nature of Flatland to them, describing our width and length, but my words fell on deaf ears; to a point, all directions are indistinguishable except for the axis of motion.

Then came a stranger from Spaceland—a being of such profound complexity that he appeared as a circle to my eyes, yet moved with a fluidity impossible for a flatlander. He spoke of a third dimension, a direction perpendicular to our own, into which we could step if only we knew how. At first, his words were met with ridicule and fear. When language failed him, he resorted to action, projecting shadows upon our plane that danced and shifted in ways no polygon could replicate.

Through these demonstrations, I was transported briefly into Spaceland itself. There, I beheld wonders beyond comprehension: spheres rolling freely, cubes floating in voids, and creatures whose forms defied the rigid constraints of our two-dimensional logic. Though the Sphere showed me many mysteries, my desire for knowledge remained insatiable. Encouraged by these revelations, I returned to share what I had seen, even as the authorities sought to silence me.

Now, I attempt to teach the Theory of Three Dimensions to my grandson, hoping to awaken his mind to possibilities beyond the flat horizon. So far, his understanding remains limited, much like mine once was. Undeterred, I seek other means to spread this liberating idea, knowing full well that in a world built on the illusion of completeness, the truth of higher dimensions may be the most radical revolution of all.
