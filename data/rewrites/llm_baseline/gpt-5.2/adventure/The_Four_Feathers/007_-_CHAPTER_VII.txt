“No one,” Durrance said, and with the same careful economy he showed in all small duties, he strapped the field-glasses into their leather case at his side.

“No one, sir,” Captain Mather agreed. “We will move forward.”

The scouts slipped out ahead, noiseless as moths. Behind them the line re-formed, the two seven-pounder mountain-guns closing up with their patient, metallic clink; and Durrance’s detachment of the Camel Corps came down from the sombre ridge of Khor Gwob—thirty-five miles southwest of Suakin—into the plateau of Sinkat. It was the last reconnaissance in strength before the evacuation of the eastern Soudan, and there was a finality in the day’s stillness, as though the land itself listened for the sound of their leaving.

All morning the camels had laboured up a shale gully between red, precipitous rocks. The stones grated under the broad pads with a dry whisper, and now and again a buckle struck a rifle-sling; that thin, hard clack travelled farther than it had any right to in the naked air. When at last the rocks fell back, the world opened into red mountain-heaps crumbled into a desolation of stones. There was hardly a patch of grass, hardly the ragged arm of a mimosa to catch the eye; only heat, rock, and the faint bitter smell of dust warmed in the sun.

Then, after that arid grinding, the green bushes of Sinkat, lying in the valley below, comforted the sight with the aspect of a park made by human hands. The troopers sat up. Even the camels seemed to take a different step where the sand ran smoother and the shrubs cast a shade that promised coolness, if only by comparison.

They moved in a diagonal line across the plateau toward the mountains of Erkoweet, a silent company on a plain more silent still. It was eleven o’clock. The sun rose toward the centre of a colourless, cloudless sky; the shadows of the camels shortened upon the sand; and the sand itself glistened white as a beach, dazzling enough to make the eyes ache beneath the brim of a helmet. There was no draught of air to stir the rich foliage. The shadows of branches lay so motionless and distinct that they seemed not shadows at all, but branches strewn there by some old storm and forgotten.

Sound became a thing one noticed with the same attention one gave to water. The sharp clank of weapons; the soft, ceaseless padding of camel feet; at times the sudden whirr of pigeons, disturbed from their perches. Even the men’s breathing, when they halted, felt loud.

Yet life moved on the plateau, though it moved with a thief’s caution. Far ahead, where the sand curved trim and smooth between shrubs like carriage drives, a herd of gazelle would start up from the ground and race—silent flashes of dappled brown and white—toward the enclosing hills. It was as if the country had been made in the last hour, set down fresh and unhandled.

“Yet this way the caravans passed southwards to Erkoweet and the Khor Baraka,” Durrance said, answering the thought in his mind. “Here the Suakis built their summer-houses.”

“And there Tewfik fought, and died with his four hundred men,” Mather returned, pointing forward.

For three hours they marched. It was May, and the sun laid its hand upon them with intolerable weight. Alertness drained away. Men rocked drowsily in their saddles, mouths dry, praying not for glory or orders but for evening and the silver shine of stars. For three hours the camels went mincing on with their queer, smirking motions of the head, making of endurance something almost comic—until, quite suddenly, a hundred yards ahead, Durrance saw a broken wall with window-spaces that let the sky through.

“The fort,” he said.

Three years had passed since Osman Digna had taken and wrecked it, but those years had brought another siege as relentless as any army’s. Quick-growing trees had girt it so close to rear and right and left that the traveller came upon it unexpectedly, as Childe Roland upon the Dark Tower in a plain. In front the sand still stretched open to the wells, where three great Gemeiza trees stood spaced like sentinels, their foliage dark and spreading, their shade precious as a ration.

In the shadow to the right front of the fort—where bushes fringed the open sand with the level regularity of a river bank—the soldiers unsaddled and prepared their food. There were the homely, practised sounds of a camp that must feed itself: tin clattered against tin; a knife rasped; a canteen was uncorked; water gurgled, dearer than coin. Someone crumbled hard biscuit into a mess-tin and let a little water soften it, stirring until it would submit to teeth; another man tore at dried meat and held it briefly in the steam of hot tea to take the edge of its hardness. The air filled with the smell of black tea brewed too long—strong, tannic, and comforting—and the faint animal warmth of camels as they settled and sighed.

Durrance and Captain Mather walked round the fort. At the southern corner Durrance stopped short.

“Hallo!”

“Some Arab has camped here,” Mather said, stopping too.

Grey ashes of a wood fire lay in a little heap upon a blackened stone.

“And lately,” Durrance added.

Mather mounted a few rough steps to the crumbled archway and passed into roofless corridors and rooms. Durrance stayed, turned the ashes over with his boot. A stump of twig, charred and whitened, glowed red as an eye opening.

He ground it beneath his heel. A tiny thread of smoke spurted into the air.

“Very lately,” he said to himself, and followed Mather within.

In the corners of the mud walls, in any fissure, even from the packed floor, young trees were sprouting—green persistence where men had meant only clay and gunfire. Rearward a steep glacis and a deep fosse defended the works. Durrance climbed to the parapet above the glacis and sat there while pigeons wheeled and circled overhead, their wings making soft, repetitive beats—one of the few living sounds in that sun-pressed hush. From this very spot, he thought, Tewfik must have strained his eyes day after day toward the pass from Suakin, looking—like another general far to the south—for the sunlight flashing on the weapons of help that did not come.

Mather sat beside him, but his thoughts travelled a different road.

“Already the Guards are steaming out through the coral reefs toward Suez,” he said. “A week and our turn comes. What a God-forsaken country!”

“I come back to it,” Durrance replied.

“Why?”

“I like it. I like the people.”

Mather did not pretend to understand. Yet he knew—because the army itself had shown him—that whatever that taste was, it had carried Durrance forward. Sympathy had stood him in stead of much ability. It had given him patience, and the power to understand: to read, in the tribes of the eastern Soudan, the old hatred of Turkish rule; to distinguish fanaticism from the pretence of it under Osman Digna’s compulsion; to hear, in the very manner of a man’s speech, whether fear or pride held the reins.

“Yes, I shall come back,” Durrance went on. “In three months’ time. For one thing, we know—every Englishman in Egypt knows—that this can’t be the end. I want to be here when the work’s taken in hand again. I hate unfinished things.”

The afternoon deepened. Men slept in the shade with their mouths open, as though the body itself tried to drink the cooler air. The plateau was as noiseless as the morning. The two officers sat compelled to silence by the silence around them—until Durrance’s eyes lost their abstraction and fixed intently upon the shrubbery beyond the glacis.

Without turning his head he saw that Mather, too, stared at the same patch of green.

“What are you thinking about?” Durrance asked suddenly.

Mather laughed, and answered with the odd tenderness a man gives to a private appetite when the world has offered him nothing else to hope for.

“I was drawing up the menu of the first dinner I will have when I reach London. I will eat it alone, I think—quite alone—and at Epitaux. It will begin with a watermelon.” He paused as though tasting it already: the cold, clean sweetness; the faint, grassy perfume when the knife first parts the rind. “And you?”

“I was wondering,” Durrance said, “why—now that the pigeons have got used to our presence—they should still be wheeling in and out of one particular tree. Don’t point to it, please. I mean the tree beyond the ditch, and to the right of two small bushes.”

All about them pigeons perched quietly upon branches, spotting the foliage like purple fruit. Only above the one tree they circled, calling timorously, their notes quickened with the thin edge of fear.

“We will draw that covert,” Durrance said. “Take a dozen men and surround it quietly.”

He remained on the glacis watching the tree and the thick undergrowth. He saw six soldiers creep round from the left and six from the right, each step measured so that even the sand should not complain. But before they could meet and ring the tree, the branches shook violently and an Arab burst out—yellowish dammar wound about his waist, a flat-headed spear in his hand, a hide shield on his arm. He dashed from shelter into open plain, running as though the air itself hunted him.

He ran only a few yards. Mather gave a sharp order; and the Arab, as though he understood it, stopped before a rifle could be lifted. Then, with a dignity that was neither insolence nor servility, he walked back.

They brought him up on to the glacis. He stood before Durrance and spoke in Arabic: he was a man of the Kabbabish, named Abou Fatma, friendly to the English, travelling to Suakin.

“Why did you hide?” Durrance asked.

“It was safer,” the man answered. “I knew you for my friends. But, my gentleman, did you know me for yours?”

Durrance’s quick glance sharpened.

“You speak English,” he said, and shifted into it.

The answer came without hesitation, but without pride.

“I know a few words.”

“Where did you learn them?”

“In Khartum.”

They left Abou Fatma alone with Durrance on the glacis, and the two men talked together for the better part of an hour. Voices carried strangely in that place; even low speech had to be weighed, as though the air might translate it into something louder. When at last they parted, Abou Fatma descended the glacis, crossed the trench, and went away toward the hills.

Durrance ordered the march resumed.

The water-tanks were filled; men replenished their zamshyehs, knowing that of all thirsts in the world the afternoon thirst is the worst—worse than the honest thirst of morning, because it comes with weariness and heat like a hand closing on the throat. Saddles went on again; the camels rose with their groaning and snarling, offended as ever by the notion of duty. The detachment moved northwestward from Sinkat at an acute angle to its morning’s track, skirting the hills opposite the pass by which they had descended. The bushes grew sparse. They came into a black country of stones, scantily relieved by yellow, tasselled mimosas that offered no real shade, only a gesture of it.

Durrance rode up to Mather.

“That Arab had a strange story to tell me,” he said. “He was Gordon’s servant in Khartum. At the beginning of ’84—eighteen months ago, in fact—Gordon gave him a letter which he was to take to Berber, so the contents could be telegraphed to Cairo. But Berber had just fallen when the messenger arrived. He was seized and imprisoned the day after his arrival. During the one day he was free he hid the letter in the wall of a house—and so far as he knows it has not been discovered.”

“He would have been questioned if it had been,” Mather said.

“Precisely. And he was not questioned. He escaped from Berber at night, three weeks ago.” Durrance’s voice lowered, as if the story demanded a quieter air. “The letter still remains in the wall.”

“It is curious,” Mather said. “Perhaps the man was telling lies.”

“He had the chain mark on his ankles,” Durrance replied.

The cavalcade turned left into the hills on the northern side of the plateau and climbed again over shale. Durrance’s thoughts moved ahead of his body. A letter from Gordon: perhaps scribbled on the palace roof beside the great telescope, a sentence written in haste before the eye went back to the lens to search over palm-tops for the smoke of steamers—and then carried down the Nile only to be buried in a mud wall in Berber, like seed dropped in bad ground.

“A letter from Gordon,” Durrance said, musing. “Yes—it’s curious.” He turned his face westward.

Even as he looked, the sun dipped behind the hills. The sky above darkened rapidly to violet; in the west it flamed with rich, iridescent colour. The violence softened; it blended delicately into a single rose hue. The rose lingered, faded, and left a sky of pure emerald green transfused with light from beneath the rim of the world.

“If only they had let us go last year westward to the Nile,” Durrance said with a kind of passion. “Before Khartum had fallen—before Berber had surrendered. But they would not.”

The sunset’s magic might have been painted on a wall for all the place it held in his mind. The letter had struck a chord of reverence: the image of that honest, great, impracticable soldier—despised by officials, thwarted by intrigue—going unflaggingly about his work, knowing that the moment his back was turned the work would be undone.

Darkness came upon the troops. The camels quickened their pace. From every tuft of grass the cicadas shrilled, turning the air into a vibrating cloth of sound; after the day’s unnatural hush, it felt almost like a consolation, a living music that belonged to the land and did not ask permission. The detachment moved down toward the well of Disibil.

That night Durrance lay long awake on his camp bedstead beneath the stars. The day’s heat had gone out of the stones; the air smelled cleaner, with a faint, cold mineral tang, and the camels’ occasional grumble came from the darkness like speech half remembered. He forgot the letter in the mud wall. Southward the Southern Cross hung slanting; above him glittered the curve of the Great Bear. In a week he would sail for England. He counted up the years since the packet had cast off from Dover pier, and the tale of them seemed good: Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, the rush down the Red Sea, Tokar, Tamai, Tamanieb—crowded moments coming back with such clarity that his body answered them. He thrilled at the recollection of Hadendowas leaping and stabbing through the breach of McNeil’s zareba six miles from Suakin; he recalled the obdurate defence of the Berkshires, the steadiness of the Marines, the rallying of broken troops. The years had been years of plenty, and had carried him to the brevet rank of lieutenant-colonel.

“A week more—only a week,” Mather murmured drowsily from somewhere in the dark.

“I shall come back,” Durrance said with a low laugh.

“Have you no friends?”

There was a pause long enough for the cicadas to fill it and the night wind—so slight it was more a change of temperature than a breath—to make a leaf whisper.

“Yes,” Durrance said at last. “I have friends. I shall have three months wherein to see them.”

He had written no word to Harry Feversham during these years. Not writing letters was part of him; correspondence was a difficulty, like speaking in a dialect that had never been his own. Yet he thought now he would surprise his friends by a visit to Donegal, or find them perhaps in London. He would ride once again in the Row. But in the end he would come back. For his friend was married—to Ethne Eustace—and as for himself, his life’s work lay here in the Soudan. He would certainly come back.

So, turning on his side, he slept dreamlessly while the hosts of the stars trampled across the heavens overhead.

At that same moment Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish slept under a boulder on the Khor Gwob. He rose early and went on across the broad plains to the white city of Suakin. There he repeated the story he had told Durrance to Captain Willoughby, acting deputy-governor. After he came from the Palace he told it again—this time in the native bazaar, in Arabic, in the open weave of market sound: vendors calling, metal struck, the dry hiss of sand under feet, and the deeper murmur of men bargaining as though the day were like any other.

A Greek seated outside a café close at hand caught fragments of the tale. He took Abou Fatma aside and, promising him much merissa with which to intoxicate himself, induced him to tell it a fourth time, very slowly, syllable by syllable—so that nothing of it would be lost, as words are lost when they pass between tongues and one man’s meaning becomes another man’s profit.

“Could you find the house again?” the Greek asked.

Abou Fatma had no doubt. He drew diagrams in the dust, not knowing that during his imprisonment Berber had been steadily pulled down by the Mahdists and rebuilt to the north—memory replaced by new walls, as if the town itself had changed its language.

“It will be wise to speak of this to no one except me,” the Greek said, and he jingled some significant dollars. For a long while the two men talked secretly together.

The Greek happened to be Harry Feversham—whom Durrance was proposing to visit in Donegal. Captain Willoughby was deputy-governor of Suakin, and after three years of waiting one of Harry Feversham’s opportunities had come.
