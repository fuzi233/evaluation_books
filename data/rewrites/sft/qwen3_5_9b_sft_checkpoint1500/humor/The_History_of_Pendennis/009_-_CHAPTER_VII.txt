Major Arthur Pendennis arrived at Fairoaks exactly when the schedule demanded, emerging from a journey that had tested the limits of human endurance. For twenty-five hours, he had been packed like sardines into a mail-coach moving at eight miles an hour. His companions included a burly fellow who swelled with layers of greatcoats and snored with the volume of a collapsing cathedral, a widow who treated the vehicle as a private distillery, filling the air with the acrid perfume of Jamaica rum and water, and two schoolboys heading home for the midsummer holidays. These lads, sitting beside the Major's servant on the roof, had somehow managed to transform the coach into a floating pantry, devouring a cargo of ham, eggs, pies, pickles, tea, coffee, and boiled beef with a ferocity that left the Major, sipping weak tea with a rheumy eye, wondering if Lord Steyne was dining at that very moment on something far superior.

Yet, despite the jolting, the horn blasts at turnpike gates, the crushing weight of neighbors, and the Widow's feet constantly nudging the Major's toes, the travelers remained merrily alive. When the coach finally ground to a halt at the little lodge-gate of Fairoaks, the Major descended with a yellow complexion, a bristly beard, a wig that had lost its curl, and joints that screamed with gouty grief. The porteress and gardener's wife bowed deeply, and his man, Morgan, performed his duties with respectful silence, though the Major suspected the man of keeping secrets regarding the wig situation.

Inside, Helen watched from her window. Knowing her brother's vanity, she did not rush to meet him. Arthur Pendennis, even in his prime, required a certain preparation before appearing before his family; he was notoriously particular about his dentition and his coiffure. Once inside his apartment, the transformation began. He groaned, wheezed, and cursed Morgan profusely as he struggled to retrieve his missing front teeth and restore his hair to a state of "majestic" gravity. Finally belted, curled, and set, he emerged into the drawing-room with an air of grave business, looking every inch the man of fashion and commerce.

However, Pen was not there. Only Helen and little Laura sat sewing at her knees. To Laura, the Major offered only a single forefinger, a gesture that caused the girl to tremble, drop the digit, and flee the room in terror. Arthur, wishing to avoid the child entirely, retreated to the orchard, where he found Pen pacing furiously alongside Mr. Smirke. Smirke, Pen's tutor, was so engrossed in the young man's fiery discourse that he failed to notice Laura approaching until she interrupted with a clear cry.

"Come in, Pen!" she urged, gripping his hand. "There's somebody come; Uncle Arthur's here."

"He is, is he?" Pen replied, his grip tightening. He glanced at Smirke with a look of fierce readiness, as if daring anyone to challenge him. Smirke sighed gently and nodded.

"Lead on, Laura," Pen commanded, his voice a mix of comedy and deep, internal anxiety. "Say I wait upon my uncle." He laughed, a nervous sound, as he prepared himself for the ordeal ahead.

Over the preceding forty-eight hours, Pen had confided everything to Smirke, speaking endlessly of Miss Fotheringay—Emily, the goddess of his dreams. Smirke, a man smitten by the same enchantment, listened with rapt attention. He had been convinced by Pen's passionate rhetoric and poetic flourishes that the plan was not only feasible but prudent: Emily settling at Fairoaks, Captain Costigan in the yellow room, and Pen married at eighteen. Even Pen's mother, the Widow Pendennis, had nearly been swayed by her son's indignant logic, though she harbored lingering fears about the consequences of a union between a sixteen-year-old aristocrat and a thirty-year-old actress who had long ceased to blush.

While Pen composed verses late into the night, his mother crept quietly past his door. Hearing the rhythmic tossing and the murmuring of lines, she entered softly. Finding him buried in manuscripts, surrounded by scattered papers, she sat beside him, taking his hot hand. "Why don't you go to sleep, my dear?" she asked tenderly.

"I couldn't sleep," Pen confessed, flinging his arms around her neck. "I love her, Mother! I love her!"

His mother, a woman of immense kindness, soothed him with a strange mixture of pity and nostalgia, recalling how recently he had been a child in that very bed. Later, when the Major, fully restored, confronted the Widow, he discovered her distress was compounded by fear for her son's happiness. She begged him to be gentle, citing Pen's high spirits and the rough treatment he had received from Dr. Portman.

The Major, however, played the role of the seasoned diplomat perfectly. With a gallant kiss on her hand, he feigned shock at her alarm. "Bon Dieu!" he thought internally, marveling at how easily the boy had charmed the woman. "If I were a king, I'd lock this boy away." He pressed her hand, forcing a smile, and then launched into a lecture on the perils of mismatched unions.

"My dear madam," he declared, "we cannot allow the head of the house to marry a strolling mountebank from a booth. We shall not marry into Greenwich Fair."

"But if the match breaks off suddenly..." Helen interjected, her eyes lowering.

"The issue shan't be marriage," the Major countered resolutely. "My dear sister, I've no doubt Arthur will suffer terribly before he gets over this little disappointment. But consider my own brother. He courted a wealthy girl, faced obstacles, and ended up in India as secretary to Lord Buckley. They parted ways, exchanged letters, and eventually, she became a baronet's wife. We suffered, yes, but we recovered."

"And look at my brother Pen," the Major continued, gesturing broadly. "He had a chance with Miss Balls, the apothecary's daughter. Difficultities arose. Regrets? None. Look at this hand!" He pressed Helen's fingers again, emphasizing the point.

"The marriages between people of such different rank and age," Helen sighed, "produce so much unhappiness. Think of Laura's father."

"Injudicious!" the Major cut in sharply. "To marry a woman of low station is to invite misery. Imagine a wife attached to a mother who drops her 'h's and calls Maria 'Marire!' You cannot introduce her into society. I have seen men cut from the best circles solely because of their wives' vulgar connections."

He painted a vivid picture of social ruin, citing examples of Lady Snapperton rejecting a druggist's daughter and the tragic history of Lord Clodworthy, whose wife looked like his mother. "Marry at eighteen an actress of thirty?" he scoffed. "Bah! I would as soon see him marry the cook."

As the conversation wound down, Helen, overwhelmed by the weight of these warnings and her own youthful regrets, realized that the matter of little Laura needed to be addressed separately. The stage was set: the Major, armed with cynical wisdom and a flair for dramatic storytelling, had successfully delayed the inevitable collision, leaving the young lovers in a state of suspended animation, waiting for the storm to break.
