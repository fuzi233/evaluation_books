The morning after Harry’s arrival at Castlewood dawned bright and tranquil, the air crisp with the promise of autumn. Sunday had come, and with it, the quiet rituals of worship. The chapel adjoining the castle served as the village church, its ancient stones steeped in the prayers of generations. A private door led from the house to the family pew, where the Esmonds took their places with solemn propriety, while the villagers filled the humble benches below.

Above them, faded banners hung like forgotten sentinels, their colors dulled by time. Harry’s imagination kindled at the sight—these very standards might have been borne by loyal retainers in the Civil Wars, men who fought for king and creed. Behind the altar rail stood the effigy of their common ancestor, Sir Francis Esmond, forever kneeling in stone, his marble hands clasped in eternal devotion.

Madame de Bernstein, the dowager widow of a bishop, observed the service with stately reverence, her demeanor as composed as the sculptured knight before her. The young clergyman, a handsome man with a voice rich and resonant, read the Scriptures with such animation that the words seemed to leap from the page. The music swelled as one of the Castlewood daughters played the organ, though the harmony was nearly undone when a burst of laughter erupted from the servants’ pew. The cause? Harry’s manservant, Gumbo, whose exuberant voice soared above the congregation, his untrained but magnificent singing drowning out even the clerk. The parson stifled a grin behind his handkerchief, while the London footmen gaped in astonishment.

The sermon that followed was anything but dull. The clergyman, with the flair of a seasoned storyteller, painted a vivid scene: the execution of a horse-thief he had witnessed at the recent Assizes. He spoke not with the fire-and-brimstone fervor Harry knew from his mother’s favored preachers, but as a man of the world, warning his flock against vice with a narrative both gripping and grim. He conjured the thief’s downfall—from honest farmer to drunkard, gambler, and finally, gallows’ prey. The law, he declared, was ever watchful, its reach long and its grip unrelenting.

Harry, rapt, scarcely noticed the passage of time. The ladies of Castlewood listened with polite interest, though the village folk shuffled out afterward as unmoved as if they had heard a weather report.

As the congregation dispersed, Lord Castlewood remarked dryly, *"Sampson has thundered today. The Philistines are well smitten."*

*"Magnificent,"* Harry agreed, still under the spell of the sermon.

*"Bah! That’s his Assize speech,"* scoffed Will. *"He trots it out whenever he’s been to Winchester."*

The organ’s final notes faded into the crisp air. Only Sir Francis Esmond’s stone figure remained, frozen in prayer. The parson emerged from the vestry, nodding amiably to the lingering gentry.

*"Come, tell us of the dogs,"* Will demanded, and with a chuckle, the clergyman obliged.

In the gallery connecting chapel to house, introductions were made. Mr. Sampson bowed deeply to Harry, offering pious hopes that the young Virginian might learn virtue from his English kin. Then, with a swift pivot, the talk turned to horses—specifically, the odds at Huntingdon’s races.

*"Five to four on Brilliant,"* the parson declared.

*"Jason’s the better horse,"* countered Will.

*"Do you wager in Virginia, Mr. Warrington?"* the chaplain inquired.

*"On occasion,"* Harry admitted, grinning.

*"Then I’ll take Brilliant against the field—for ponies!"* Will challenged.

*"Three to one on Jason,"* the parson added.

Harry hesitated. *"I don’t bet on horses I don’t know."*

Will sneered. *"Best write home and ask your mother first."*

Harry’s jaw tightened. *"I manage my own affairs, cousin. Done and done."*

The bets were struck, though Harry later questioned his haste. *"A Virginian doesn’t back down,"* he told himself, even as his purse felt lighter at the thought.

Dinner was a lively affair, the parson regaling the table with London gossip—who had fled with whom, the latest scandal at court, the most outrageous wagers of the town. The ladies laughed at jests that would have scandalized a later age, their cheeks unblushing. Harry, wide-eyed, absorbed it all.

Cards followed, the clatter of ivory counters mingling with the evening birdsong. Madame de Bernstein, sharp as ever, dominated at piquet. The chaplain matched her glass for glass, returning from a swift evening prayer to drain another bottle.

Night fell unnoticed. The moon rose, the stars blinked awake, and still they played on, until supper was announced.

Then came the postboy’s horn, heralding news. Lord Castlewood’s paper held a revelation: *Jason* had won at Huntingdon.

Will swore. The parson groaned. Harry, flush with triumph, waved off his winnings. *"It was in jest, cousin."*

*"Do you think I can’t pay?"* Will snapped, drowning his irritation in wine.

Harry sighed. For all its grandeur, Castlewood was proving a place of sharp edges. Sunday had ended—not in prayerful quiet, but in the rattle of dice and the clink of glasses. And as the candles guttered low, he wondered if this was the England he had dreamed of, or something far more complicated.
