—**The Ancient Mariner**

The morning sun cast golden fingers across the terraced gardens of Stow House, where Sir Richard Grenville and young Amyas Leigh paced side by side. The crisp October air carried the distant lowing of cattle, the restless whinny of colts in the paddock, and the defiant roars of stags clashing in the valley below. It was a scene of peace, yet beneath it thrummed the tension of a turning point—the moment when a boy’s path is set toward manhood.

Amyas had slept late, his limbs heavy with the exhaustion of recent travels, but Sir Richard had been awake since dawn, chasing badgers through the mist-laden combes with his terriers. Now, as they walked, the elder man listened intently to the boy’s tale—of love, of honor, of a brother’s sacrifice.

*“You have chosen the better part,”* Sir Richard said at last, his voice firm. *“Only be strong, lad, and trust that God will make a man of you.”*

Amyas squared his shoulders. *“I do trust.”*

*“Good.”* Sir Richard’s stern face softened. *“For love, Amyas—true love—should lift a man to heaven, not drag him down to hell. You’ve learned that lesson young, and it will serve you well.”*

Below them, Lady Grenville sat amidst a swirl of children and spaniels, her laughter like music on the breeze. Sir Richard’s gaze lingered on her, and for a moment, the soldier’s hardness melted into something tender.

*“But enough philosophy,”* he said abruptly. *“You sail for Ireland tomorrow.”*

Amyas blinked. *“So soon?”*

*“The ‘Mary’ leaves for Milford Haven at first light. Winter commands there—yes, I know he’s no friend of yours after Magellan, but duty cares little for grudges. Deliver these letters to the marshal or the Lord Deputy, and they’ll set you to work. Hard work, I warrant.”*

Sir Richard clapped him on the shoulder. *“Spoken like a Grenville. The best reward for labor is more labor. Rest is for men who’ve earned it—and those who seek it too soon are cowards.”*

Their talk was interrupted by the steward, who announced a ragged stranger at the gate—a wild-eyed, sunburnt man covered in strange tattoos, demanding to speak with Sir Richard.

*“A heathen rogue, by the look of him,”* the steward muttered.

But Amyas, ever curious, went to meet him. The man’s bearing was proud despite his rags, and when he spoke, his voice carried the weight of hard years.

*“I am no rogue,”* he said. *“Only a mariner returned from hell. My name is Salvation Yeo.”*

At that name, Amyas started. *“Yeo? You were with Oxenham!”*

The man’s eyes darkened. *“Aye. And I bring tidings—of him, and of things that will make your ears tingle.”*

Sir Richard, ever wary, pressed the man for proof of his honesty. Yeo bared his arm, revealing a scar that twisted like a serpent from wrist to elbow.

*“The Inquisition’s work,”* he said flatly. *“I’ll not swear an oath, for my conscience forbids it. But I’ll tell my tale true, before God and before you.”*

And so, with the firelight flickering across his weathered face, Salvation Yeo began his story—of golden cities and blood-stained sands, of treachery and courage, and of John Oxenham’s doomed dream.

But that, as they say, is another tale.
