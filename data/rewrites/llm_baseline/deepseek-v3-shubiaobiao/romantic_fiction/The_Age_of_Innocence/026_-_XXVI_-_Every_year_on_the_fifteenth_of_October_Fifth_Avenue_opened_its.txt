Every October, Fifth Avenue awoke with the rustle of rolled-out carpets and the parting of heavy drapes. By November, the ritual was complete—the city had shaken off its summer languor, and society turned its gaze inward, appraising itself with a mixture of pride and unease. The opera season bloomed; engagements piled upon engagements; ballrooms echoed with the latest gossip. And, as predictably as the turning of leaves, Mrs. Archer would sigh and declare that New York was not what it once was.

From her vantage as an observer rather than a participant, she—with the help of Mr. Sillerton Jackson and Miss Sophy—could trace every fissure in the social edifice, every unruly sprout amid the carefully tended rows of respectability. To Archer, it had once been an amusement to listen to her annual lament, to hear her catalog the decay his own careless eye had missed. For in Mrs. Archer’s view, change was never for the better, and Miss Jackson agreed with a fervor that bordered on relish. Mr. Jackson, ever the diplomat, listened with detached amusement, though even he could not deny the shifting tides.

This year, however, even Archer had to concede the truth in their observations. At the Thanksgiving dinner, where gratitude was demanded by tradition but rarely felt, the conversation turned, as it always did, to the state of society—which, if it could be said to exist at all, seemed more a subject for lamentation than celebration.

Dr. Ashmore, the new rector of St. Matthew’s, had chosen a particularly grim text for his Thanksgiving sermon—something from Jeremiah about the sins of the people. Mrs. Archer found it chilling, yet thrilling, to hear society described as something with a *trend*, as though it were a force barreling toward some inevitable ruin.

"The extravagance in dress!" Miss Jackson began, launching into a familiar refrain. "Why, at the opera, I scarcely recognized a single gown from last season—except Jane Merry’s, and even that had been altered."

"Jane is one of the last of the old guard," Mrs. Archer sighed, as if mourning a lost era when ladies had the decency to let their Paris imports mellow in a trunk before wearing them.

"Boston was never so reckless," Miss Jackson sniffed. "There, a proper woman waited two years before donning her French dresses. Mrs. Pennilow had forty-eight Worth gowns still in tissue when she died—now *that* was refinement."

A murmur of approval circled the table before the talk turned, inevitably, to the Beauforts. Rumors swirled about his finances—rumors too dark to be indulged in openly, yet too tantalizing to ignore. Regina’s fate, should the worst come to pass, was too dreadful to contemplate.

Then came the mention of Ellen Olenska—a name that sent a flush across May’s cheeks, sharp enough to startle Archer. The family’s disapproval of the countess was no secret, but May’s reaction was new, unreadable.

"Madame Olenska has always been popular with gentlemen," Miss Sophy offered, with the air of someone planting a carefully aimed dart.

"A danger for any woman in her position," Mrs. Archer agreed mournfully.

When the ladies withdrew, Archer and Mr. Jackson retreated to the library, where the older man lit a cigar and grew ponderous.

"If Beaufort falls," he said, "there will be revelations."

Archer stiffened. The thought of Beaufort’s ruin carried with it an unsettling implication—what would become of Ellen, if her meager support was tied to his fortunes?

Jackson’s gaze was needle-sharp. "She refused her husband’s money when she refused to return to him. Without Beaufort, what does she live on?"

Archer’s fist came down on the desk. "What are you implying?"

Jackson only smiled. "I imply nothing. But others talk. And if she stays now, with Beaufort crumbling, it will only confirm what they say."

Archer burned with helpless fury. He knew Jackson was baiting him, but he could not stop himself. "She won’t go back. Not now. Not ever."

Jackson’s eyes gleamed. "A pity, then."

Later, as May trimmed a smoking lamp, her movements precise, her voice serene, Archer watched her and felt a strange dread. How young she was. How endless their life together stretched before them.

"I may go to Washington soon," he said abruptly. "On business."

She turned, the light catching her face. "Of course. You must see Ellen while you’re there."

Her smile was flawless, her tone light. But beneath the words lay an entire unspoken edict—an understanding, a challenge, a warning.

He stared at her, struck by the terrible efficiency of their shared language, where whole battles were waged in a single polite phrase.

She blew out the lamp. "They smell less if you extinguish them properly," she said, and left him in the dark.
