It was the universal consensus of old New York society that the Countess Olenska had lost her looks.
Years before, in Newland Archer’s boyhood, she had first appeared in the city as a brilliant, flame-pretty little girl of nine or ten, of whom every acquaintance declared she “ought to be captured on canvas.” Her parents were lifelong continental wanderers, and after a childhood spent traipsing from one European capital to the next, she lost them both, and was placed in the care of her aunt Medora Manson: another unrepentant wanderer, who had returned to New York to “settle down” for the seventh time. Poor Medora, repeatedly widowed, always came home to plant roots (each time in a smaller, less expensive house), dragging with her a new husband or newly adopted child—only to part from her husband or quarrel with her ward a few months later, sell the house at a steep loss, and vanish back onto the open road. Because her mother had been a Rushworth, and her last ill-fated marriage tied her to one of the famously eccentric Chivers clan, New York looked on her oddities with indulgence; but when she stepped off the steamer with her little orphaned niece, whose parents had been well-loved despite their regrettable taste for constant travel, the whole family sighed that it was a tragedy to leave that sweet pretty child in Medora’s erratic care.
Everyone was ready to be kind to little Ellen Mingott, even when her dusky red cheeks and tight springy curls lent her a giddy, unruly air that seemed wholly improper for a child who ought still to be wearing mourning black for her parents. Flouting the ironclad unwritten rules of American mourning was just one of Medora’s many peculiarities: when they arrived, her crape veil for her dead brother was a full seven inches shorter than her sisters-in-law’s, while little Ellen was dressed all in crimson merino strung with amber beads, looking less like a grieving orphan than a gipsy foundling. New York had long grown used to Medora, so only a handful of elderly matrons shook their heads over Ellen’s gaudy clothes, and all the rest of her relatives fell quickly under the spell of her bright color and unselfconscious high spirits. She was a fearless, unshy little thing who asked disconcertingly direct questions, made comments far too old for her years, and could do all sorts of outlandish tricks: dance a rousing Spanish shawl dance, and sing smoky Neapolitan love songs while playing accompaniment on a guitar. Under Medora’s guidance—who, having acquired a Papal title, had reverted to her first husband’s last name to style herself the Marchioness Manson, because in Italy she could twist it to sound like Manzoni—Ellen received an expensive, thoroughly incoherent education, one that included “drawing from the live model,” a practice no proper New York girl had ever dreamed of, and playing piano in quintets with professional European musicians.
Of course no good could come of it. A few years later, poor mad Chivers died in an asylum, his widow wrapped in bizarre foreign mourning weeds, packed up their things and left New York again, taking Ellen with her—now a tall, lanky girl with eyes so bright they were impossible to ignore. For a long time no one heard a word of them, then news drifted back: Ellen had married an immensely wealthy Polish nobleman of legendary repute, met at a ball at the Tuileries, who was said to own palaces in Paris, Nice and Florence, a yacht docked at Cowes, and thousands of acres of hunting grounds in Transylvania. She vanished in a kind of sulphurous, gilded apotheosis, and when Medora returned to New York some years later, subdued, penniless, mourning a third husband and searching for an even smaller cottage to rent, everyone wondered why her fabulously rich niece hadn’t lifted a finger to help her. Then came the news everyone had whispered of behind fans: Ellen’s marriage had collapsed in ruin, and she was coming home at last, to seek rest and anonymity among her own kinsfolk.
All of these memories drifted through Newland Archer’s head a week later, as he watched the Countess Olenska step into the van der Luyden drawing room on the night of that fateful, much-anticipated dinner. It was an occasion of immense gravity, and he found himself nervous, wondering if she would manage to carry herself through the scrutiny of New York’s most exclusive, unforgiving circle. She was a little late, one hand still ungloved, fumbling to fasten a bracelet around her wrist, yet she crossed the threshold with no trace of haste or embarrassment, right into a room where all of New York’s most chosen guests sat assembled like a jury.
She paused dead in the center of the drawing room, her mouth grave, her eyes soft and smiling, and in that single instant Archer rejected the whole city’s verdict on her looks. It was true her early girlish radiance was gone. The bright rose flush had faded from her cheeks, she was thin, worn, a little older-looking than her age, which could not be more than thirty. But there hung about her the quiet, unassailable authority of real beauty: a sureness in the way she held her head, the slow, deliberate movement of her eyes, that was not the least bit theatrical, yet struck him as finely honed, brimming with conscious, gentle power. She was simpler in her manners than almost all the other ladies in the room, too, and many guests (as he heard his sister Janey report later) were bitterly disappointed she was not more “stylish”—for stylishness was the highest virtue New York knew. Archer suspected it was because her old quick vivacity had burned away: she was so quiet, quiet in her movements, quiet in her low, warm, unhurried voice. New York had expected something far louder, far more dramatic from a young woman with such a checkered past.
The dinner was a formidable affair at best. Dining with the van der Luydens was never a light, casual experience, but dining there with a Duke who was their direct cousin was little short of a religious solemnity. Archer smiled to himself, pleased that only a born old New Yorker could spot the fine distinction: any stray foreign nobleman might be received politely, but the van der Luydens’ Duke was welcomed with a warm, old-fashioned reverence no visiting lord would ever mistake for mere awe of their title. It was for these tiny, unspoken social nuances that Archer cherished his old New York, even as he laughed at its absurdities.
The van der Luydens had pulled out every stop to mark the occasion. The rare du Lac Sevres china was on the table, alongside the Trevenna George II silver, the van der Luyden’s prized East India Company Lowestoft set, and the Dagonet family Crown Derby. Mrs. van der Luyden looked more than ever like a Cabanel portrait, and Archer’s own mother, decked in her grandmother’s seed pearls and emeralds, reminded him of a delicate Isabey miniature. Every lady wore her finest heirloom jewels, all set in the heavy, old-fashioned mountings the family treasured, and even the reclusive old Miss Lanning, who had been persuaded at great length to attend, wore her mother’s carved cameos and a fine Spanish blonde shawl.
The Countess Olenska was the only young woman at the dinner, yet as Archer glanced around at all the smooth, plump elderly faces framed by diamond necklaces and towering ostrich plumes, they all struck him as curiously shallow, curiously immature, next to hers. It frightened him, a little, to try to imagine all the sorrow, all the joy, all the wild experience that had gone into making her eyes look the way they did.
The Duke of St. Austrey, seated at his hostess’s right hand, was supposed to be the star of the evening. But if the Countess Olenska was less dramatic than everyone had hoped, the Duke was almost entirely invisible. He was too well-bred to show up in a shooting jacket like a recent ducal visitor to the city, but his evening clothes were shabby and baggy, and he wore them with such an air of being no different from homespun that, paired with his stooped posture and the thick beard that spilled over his shirtfront, he hardly looked like he was dressed for dinner at all. He was short, round-shouldered, sunburnt, with a thick nose, small twinkling eyes and a warm, sociable smile, but he rarely spoke, and when he did his voice was so low that even during the frequent expectant silences around the table, no one but his immediate neighbors could hear a word he said.
When the men joined the ladies in the drawing room after dinner, the Duke walked straight over to the Countess Olenska, and the two sat down in a far corner and launched into animated, easy chatter. Neither of them seemed to notice that etiquette dictated the Duke ought to have first paid his respects to the elder matrons Mrs. Lovell Mingott and Mrs. Headly Chivers, and that the Countess ought to have sat perfectly still in her chair, waiting for the amiable hypochondriac Mr. Urban Dagonet—who had broken his decades-long rule of never dining out between January and April just for the chance to meet her—to come and call on her. They chatted for nearly twenty minutes before the Countess stood up, walked alone clear across the width of the drawing room, and settled herself right on the sofa beside Newland Archer.
It was no custom in New York drawing rooms for a lady to get up and leave one gentleman’s side to seek out the company of another. Etiquette demanded she sit immovable as an idol, while suitors and callers took turns sitting beside her. But the Countess seemed utterly unaware she had broken any rule at all. She sat perfectly at ease, a soft, unguarded smile on her face, and looked at him with the kindest, warmest eyes.
“I want you to talk to me about May,” she said.
Instead of answering, he asked: “You knew the Duke before, then?”
“Oh, yes—we used to see him every winter at Nice. He’s very fond of gambling—he used to come to the house all the time.” She said it as simply as if she had said “he is very fond of wildflowers,” then added candidly a moment later: “I think he is the dullest man I have ever met in my life.”
It pleased him so much that he forgot the faint, sharp shock her offhand remark had given him. It was undeniably, thrillingly exciting to meet a woman who found the van der Luydens’ own duke dull, and dared to say so out loud. He ached to question her, to hear all about that wild, far-off European life her careless words had given him such a vivid, glowing glimpse of, but he feared to brush against the painful memories of her failed marriage, and before he could frame a careful question she had drifted back to her original subject.
“May is a darling. I have not seen any young girl in New York so handsome and so clever. Are you very much in love with her?”
Archer reddened and laughed. “As much as any man can be.”
She regarded him thoughtfully, tilting her head as if she did not want to miss a single shade of meaning in his words. “Do you think, then, that there is a limit?”
“To being in love? If there is, I have not found it.”
Her face lit up with soft, unfeigned sympathy. “Ah—so it really and truly is a romance?”
“The most romantic of all romances,” he smiled.
“How wonderful. And the two of you found it all out for yourselves? It was not arranged for you at all?”
Archer blinked at her, a little incredulous. “Have you forgotten that in our country we do not let other people arrange our marriages for us?”
A dark dusky blush rose to her cheek, and he regretted his words instantly.
“Yes,” she said, her voice soft. “I’d forgotten. Forgive me if I make these mistakes sometimes. I do not always remember that everything that was bad where I came from is good, and right, here.” She looked down at the Viennese fan made of eagle feathers in her hand, and he saw her lips tremble just a little.
“I am so sorry,” he said, leaning forward impulsively. “But you are among friends here, you know that.”
“Yes. I know. Wherever I go tonight, that is the feeling I get. That is why I came home. I want to forget everything else, to become a complete American again, like the Mingotts and Wellands, and you and your delightful mother, and all the other good people here tonight. Ah, here is May arriving now,” she added, but she did not move to stand. Her eyes drifted from the door, where May had just appeared, and rested soft and steady on his face.
The drawing rooms were filling up with the rest of the after-dinner guests, and following her gaze, Archer saw May Welland step in with her mother. Dressed in white and silver, a wreath of tiny silver blossoms twisted in her hair, the tall girl looked like Diana just alighted from a night’s hunt in the woods.
“Oh,” Archer laughed. “I have so many rivals. Look, she is already surrounded. The Duke is being introduced to her now.”
“Then stay with me a little longer,” Madame Olenska said in a low, quiet tone, and the tip of her feather fan brushed, light as a breath, against his knee. It was the faintest possible touch, but it thrilled him straight through like a caress.
“Yes, let me stay,” he answered, his voice soft, hardly knowing what he was saying—just then Mr. van der Luyden came up to them, followed by old Mr. Urban Dagonet. The Countess greeted them with her grave, gentle smile, and Archer, feeling his host’s quiet admonitory glance on him, rose and surrendered his seat.
Madame Olenska held out her hand to him as if to say goodbye.
“Tomorrow, then, after five. I shall expect you,” she said—and then turned to make space for Mr. Dagonet on the sofa beside her.
“Tomorrow,” Archer heard himself repeating, even though there had been no engagement, no prior hint all through their conversation that she wanted to see him again.
As he wandered away, he saw Lawrence Lefferts, tall and resplendent in his evening wear, leading his wife up to be introduced, and heard Gertrude Lefferts beam at the Countess with her large, unperceiving smile, gushing that they must have gone to dancing school together as little girls. Behind her, waiting their turn to introduce themselves, Archer spotted all the stubborn couples who had refused to meet the Countess at Mrs. Lovell Mingott’s dinner party a few weeks prior. As his mother had often said: when the van der Luydens chose to make a point, they knew exactly how to teach all of New York a lesson. The wonder was that they chose to do it so rarely.
A soft hand touched his arm, and he looked up to see Mrs. van der Luyden looking down at him, radiant in black velvet and the family diamonds. “It was so good of you, dear Newland, to devote yourself so unselfishly to Madame Olenska. I told your cousin Henry he absolutely had to come to the rescue.”
He smiled at her vaguely, and she added, as if indulging his natural shyness: “I have never seen May looking lovelier. The Duke thinks she is the handsomest girl in the whole room.”
Archer nodded, but his mind was already far away, fixed on the light brush of eagle feathers against his knee, and the quiet, unspoken promise of five o’clock the next afternoon.
