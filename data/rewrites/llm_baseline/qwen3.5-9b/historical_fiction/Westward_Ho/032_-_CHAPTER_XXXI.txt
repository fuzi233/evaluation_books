"Britannia needs no bulwarks, No towers along the steep; Her march is o'er the mountain wave, Her home is on the deep."

Thus sang Campbell of the mariners of England, and now the prophecy hung heavy in the air as the great sea-fight commenced. It was a contest destined to decide whether Popery and despotism or Protestantism and freedom would become the law for half of Europe and the entirety of future America. It was a twelve-day epic, worthy not of dull prose but of the thunderous roll of Homer's verse. Yet, to recount it faithfully, I must rely wherever possible on the words of contemporary witnesses rather than my own invention.

"The Lord High Admiral of England, sending a pinnace named *Defiance* ahead, declared war by firing her cannons. Immediately following, he approached within musket range, unleashing a storm of thunder from his flagship, the *Ark Royal* (also known as the *Triumph*), and launched the initial assault upon what he believed to be the Spanish Admiral's vessel—though it turned out to be the ship of Alfonso de León. Shortly thereafter, Drake, Hawkins, and Frobisher engaged stoutly with their artillery against the rear squadron, commanded by Recalde."

The Spaniards quickly discerned the superior swiftness of the English ships. Realizing they were giving more than they took, despite Recalde's efforts, his squadron hurried forward to join the main fleet. Admiral Medina, seeing his formation scatter rapidly, gathered them into a crescent shape. The Armada pressed onward with solemn, stately purpose, resembling a massive herd of buffalo marching across a prairie, disdainfully ignoring the wolves snarling around their path. But these were no mere wolves; they were cunning hunters, swift of horse and keen of blade, intent on shaming that vast herd from the Lizard to Portland, and from Portland to Calais Roads. Even in this brief two-hour engagement, many a Spaniard began to question the boasted invincibility of their Armada.

One of the four great galliasses was already riddled with shot, her pulpits and chapels shattered, the friars within thrown into disarray. The fleet was forced to circle around her to prevent Drake and Hawkins from sinking her. In executing this maneuver, the "principal galleon of Seville," carrying Pedro de Valdez and a host of blue-blooded nobles, ran aground against her neighbor, tearing away her foremast. Despite Spanish chivalry, she was left to her fate. This did not look like victory. Yet, courage! Though Valdez was abandoned, "Our Lady," the saints, and the bull *Caena Domini*—dictated by one I dare not name here—were still with them. To doubt would be blasphemy. Meanwhile, if they fared poorly against merely a third of the Plymouth fleet, how would they withstand the forty belated ships already appearing on the horizon, whitening the blue waters between them and the Mewstone?

Thus ended the first day. Not an English ship, nor hardly a single man, was injured. The event had forever destroyed the prestige of boastful Spain in English minds and fully justified the policy Lord Howard had adopted on the advice of Raleigh and Drake: to maintain a running fight rather than clamping ships together without consideration. As Raleigh noted, had he done otherwise, he would have been lost, unlike the "great many malignant fools" who later faulted his conduct.

As it was, the day concluded with Amyas Leigh and the other Bideford ships busy for two hours hammering holes in a colossal galleon bearing the name *Sta. Catharina*. A maiden with a wheel adorned her stern, and her flag displayed the arms of the soldier-commandant, though these were shot away early in the fray, leaving Amyas unable to confirm if they belonged to Don Guzman. Still, there was ample opportunity for private vengeance. Finally recalled by the Admiral's signal, Amyas retired to his hammock and slept soundly.

However, before an hour had passed, he was awakened by Cary descending to request orders.

"We were to follow Drake's lantern, Amyas," Cary reported, "but I cannot see it, unless he has been taken aloft among the stars to become a new *Drakium Sidus*."

Amyas grumbled, turning out his lamp, but no lantern appeared. Instead, there was a sudden explosion and a great fire on a Spanish vessel, which was gradually extinguished. Consequently, the entire fleet lay-to for the night.

The next morning found them off Torbay. Amyas was hailed by a pinnace bearing a letter from Drake. Saving the somewhat arbitrary spelling common to men of those days, it read roughly as follows:

*"DEAR LAD,—I have been wool-gathering all night after five great hulks, which the Pixies transfigured overnight into galleons, and this morning again into German merchantmen. I let them go with my blessing; and returning, fell in (God be thanked!) with Valdez's great galleon. There I found good booty, which the Dons, his faithful and valiant comrades, had left behind, though Lord Howard had let her slip past him, thinking her deserted by her crew. I have sent to Dartmouth a sight of noblemen and gentlemen, perhaps fifty; and Valdez himself, who, when I sent my pinnace aboard, insisted on his punctilios and proposed conditions. I answered him that I had no time to bargain; if he wished to die, I was the very man for him; if he wished to live, then *buena guerra*. He sent again, boasting that he was Don Pedro Valdez and that it stood not with his honor—or the honor of the Dons in his company—to surrender. I replied that for my part, I was Francis Drake, and my matches burning. Whereupon he found in my name a salve for the wounds of his own, and came aboard, kissing my fist, with Spanish lies of considering himself fortunate to fall into the hands of fortunate Drake, and much more which he might have kept to cool his porridge. But I have much news from him (for he is a leaky tub); among other things, this: your Don Guzman is aboard the *Sta. Catharina*, commandant of her soldiery, with his arms flying at her sprit alongside the image of Sta. Catharina at the poop. She is a lofty-built ship of three tiers of ordnance. God preserve you, and send you like luck with her.*

"She sails in this squadron of Recalde," the message continued. "The Armada intended to smoke us out of Plymouth; and God's grace it was they did not try. Their orders from home are too strict, and so the slaves fight like a bull in a tether, no farther than their rope, finding thus the devil a hard master, as most do in the end. They cannot match our quick handling and tacking, and take us for very witches. So far so good, and better to come. You and I know the length of their foot of old. Time and light will kill any hare, and they will find it a long way from Start to Dunkirk."

"The Admiral is in a gracious humor, Leigh, to have vouchsafed you such a long letter."

"St. Catherine! Why, that was the galleon we hammered all yesterday!" Amyas stamped on the deck.

"Of course it was. Well, we shall find her again, doubt not. That cunning old Drake! How he has contrived to line his own pockets, even though he had to keep the whole fleet waiting for him."

"He has given the Lord High Admiral the dowry, at all events."

"Lord Howard is too high-hearted to stop and plunder, Papist though he is, Amyas."

Amyas answered with a growl, for he worshipped Drake and was not overly just toward Papists.

The fleet did not locate Lord Howard until nightfall; he and Lord Sheffield had held their position steadfastly all night, tracking the Spanish lanterns with only two ships. At least, there was now no doubt regarding the loyalty of English Roman Catholics. Indeed, throughout the fight, the Howards demonstrated a desperate courage that might have led less prudent men to destruction but ultimately guided them to victory. Soon, a large Spanish ship drifted by, deserted and partially burned. Some men urged leaving their posts to board her, but Amyas stoutly refused. "I have come out to fight, and not to plunder," he declared. "Let the nearest ship to her have her luck without grudging." They passed on, and the men pulled long faces as they watched the galleon snap up by their neighbor and towed off to Weymouth. There, it proved to be the ship of Miguel d'Oquenda, the Vice-Admiral, which they had seen the previous night, almost blown up by a desperate Netherland gunner who, feeling "misused," sought to settle old scores with his tyrants.

Thus ended the second day, while the Portland hills rose higher and clearer every hour. The next morning found them off the island. Would they attempt Portsmouth, though they had spared Plymouth? The wind had shifted to the north, blowing clear and cool off the white-walled downs of Weymouth Bay. The Spaniards turned to face the English, intending to stand off and on until the wind changed, hoping then to reach the Needles. At least, they would have some work to do before rounding Purbeck Isle.
