From the earliest days, I have harbored a profound affection for sustenance, particularly the act of consuming it. As a youth, I possessed both a voracious appetite and a robust digestive system. I recall a specific evening when a gentleman, distinguished by his dull eyes and complexion resembling a bruised plum, arrived for dinner at our residence. For approximately five minutes, he scrutinized my meal with an expression bordering on hypnotic fascination. Eventually, turning to my father, he asked, "Does your boy ever suffer from dyspepsia?"

My father responded, "Never, so far as I am aware. Does it trouble you, Colly Wobbles?" (Though 'Colly Wobbles' was my moniker, it bore no resemblance to my actual name).

"No, Father," I declared. Immediately following this assurance, I inquired, "Father, pray, what exactly is dyspepsia?"

The gentleman, observing me with a mixture of astonishment and covetousness, delivered his verdict in a tone dripping with infinite pity: "You will find out... some day."

My late mother often expressed her delight in witnessing me devour a meal, a sentiment that remains a cherished reflection for me. A vigorous, healthy adolescent, engaged in ample physical activity and diligently avoiding excessive study, naturally possesses the capacity to satisfy even the most demanding gastronomic expectations.

There is a distinct amusement in observing boys dine when the bill is not one's responsibility. Their concept of a substantial repast involves a staggering array of provisions: a pound and a half of roast beef accompanied by five or six robust potatoes (the soapy varieties being deemed more substantial), a generous portion of greens, four thick slices of Yorkshire pudding, followed by currant dumplings, green apples, a handful of nuts, several jumbles, and a bottle of gingerbeer. Post-prandially, they engage in games of "horses."

Conversely, adult men are relegated to a sedentary existence, required to remain motionless for hours after consuming mere clear soup and a chicken wing. It is easy to imagine how boys might view us with disdain. Yet, the youth lack certain luxuries. A boy never experiences the true ecstasy of satiety. He cannot stretch his limbs, rest his hands behind his head, and surrender to the ethereal bliss that envelops the fully fed man. To a boy, dinner is a mundane event; to a man, it is akin to a fairy's potion, rendering the world suddenly brighter and kinder. A man who has dined well develops an immediate, tender affection for all his fellow creatures. He strokes his cat with gentle reverence, cooing "Poor pussy" with emotion, feels genuine concern for the musicians playing outside regarding their exposure to the elements, and momentarily forgets to resent his wife's relatives.

A fine dinner reveals the gentler aspects of male nature. It transforms the gloomy and irritable into jovial conversationalists. Those sour individuals who spend the day appearing as if subsisting solely on vinegar and Epsom salts break into broad smiles after dinner, exhibiting a sudden urge to pat small children on the head and speak vaguely of sixpences. Serious men soften, becoming mildly cheerful, while snobbish young men with heavy mustaches conveniently forget to be obnoxious.

Personally, I am invariably sentimental post-prandially. It is the sole occasion when I can genuinely appreciate a love story. When the protagonist embraces his beloved in a final, desperate hug, stifling a sob, I feel a sorrow comparable to losing a trick at Whist. When the heroine ultimately meets her demise, I weep openly. Had I read such a tale in the morning, I would likely sneer at its melodrama. Digestion—or perhaps indigestion—has a remarkable influence on the heart. Should I wish to compose something truly pathetic, I consume a large plate of hot buttered muffins about an hour prior. By the time I sit to work, an overwhelming melancholy descends upon me. I envision heartbroken lovers parting at lonely stiles amidst deepening twilight, broken only by the distant toll of a sheepbell. I picture old men staring at withered flowers until their vision blurs with tears, and maiden ladies waiting at open windows for a lover who never returns, watching their golden hair turn to white and thin strands. The infants they once cradled have grown into adults burdened by their own woes, while childhood playmates lie silent beneath the grass. They wait and watch until the dark shadows of the unknown encroach, and the world, with its trivial troubles, fades from their aching eyes.

In my imagination, I see pale corpses cast onto frothing waves and deathbeds stained with bitter tears. I hear the wild wails of women and the low moans of children. It is all attributable to the muffins. I could not conjure a single melancholic fancy on a mutton chop and a glass of champagne.

A full stomach is a potent catalyst for poetry, and no sentiment whatsoever can endure upon an empty one. We possess neither the time nor the inclination to indulge in fanciful sorrows until we have dispensed with our real misfortunes. We do not sigh over deceased birds when the bailiff is present, nor do we fret over the temperature of our mistress's smiles when we are unsure of our next shilling.

Those foolish individuals—who, I admit, usually refers to anyone holding an opinion divergent from my own—will assert that mental distress is far more agonizing than physical hunger. This romantic theory offers solace to the lovesick youth who gazes patronizingly upon a starving figure, thinking, "How fortunate I am compared to you!" and comforts the obese gentleman who boasts of the virtues of poverty. However, it is all nonsense, pure cant. An aching head swiftly renders one indifferent to an aching heart. A broken finger banishes all memories of an empty chair. And when a man is truly ravenous, nothing else registers in his consciousness.

We, the sleek and well-fed, struggle to comprehend the agony of hunger. We know the sensation of lacking an appetite despite fine victuals being served, but we fail to grasp the sickness induced by starvation—the desire to die for bread while others squander it, the gaze fixed with famished intensity on coarse food steaming behind grimy windows, longing for a penny's worth of pea pudding but lacking the coin, believing a crust would be divine and a bone a feast.

For us, hunger is a luxury, a piquant sauce that enhances flavor. It is worthwhile to succumb to hunger and thirst merely to rediscover the gratification derived from eating and drinking. If one wishes to fully savor a dinner, a thirty-mile country walk after breakfast, abstaining from all food until return, is recommended. One's eyes will glisten at the sight of the white tablecloth and steaming dishes, and the sigh of contentment upon setting down the empty tankard will be profound. Subsequently, one feels immensely comfortable pushing back the chair, lighting a cigar, and smiling benevolently at everyone.

However, caution is advised when employing this strategy: ensure the promised dinner actually exists, for the resulting disappointment is trying. I recall an occasion with a dear friend, Joseph Taboys. Alas, life's mist obscures us from one another; it has been nearly eight years since I last beheld his jovial countenance. To encounter his hearty laugh and clasp his strong hand again would be delightful. Furthermore, he owes me fourteen shillings. We spent a holiday together, breaking fast early for a lengthy trek. We had ordered a duck for dinner, instructing the hostess to procure a large specimen, reasoning that we would arrive home ravenously hungry. As we departed, the landlady approached in high spirits, presenting a bird roughly the size of a door-mat. "If you finish that, you'll do well," she remarked. We chuckled with self-congratulatory pride, confident in our stamina, and set off.

As expected, we became disoriented. I invariably lose my way in the countryside, a situation that provokes frustration, as seeking directions from locals is futile. Asking a lodging-house servant how to make beds is as sensible as expecting a country bumpkin to know the path to the next village. One must shout the query three times before penetrating the listener's skull. On the fourth attempt, the individual repeats it, pauses to ponder for hundreds of counts, and eventually advises, "You couldn't do better than..." before spotting another bewildered traveler and consulting him. These two debate the matter for a quarter-hour, concluding that one should proceed straight down the lane, turn right, cross via the third stile, navigate past Old Jimmy Milcher's cowshed, traverse seven acres, pass through Squire Grubbin's hay-stack gate, follow the bridle-path until opposite the former windmill site, turn right, leaving Stiggin's plantation behind, and then express gratitude before departing with a splitting headache and zero sense of direction, save for the vague knowledge that a stile awaits somewhere. Upon the next bend, four stiles appeared, radiating in different directions.

Having endured this trial multiple times, we traversed fields, waded brooks, scrambled over hedges, and quarreled regarding blame. Exhausted and footsore, the vision of the duck sustained us—a fairy-like illusion floating before our tired eyes, drawing us forward. It served as a trumpet call to the fainting. We spoke of it, cheering one another with memories. "Come along," we urged, "lest the duck spoil."
