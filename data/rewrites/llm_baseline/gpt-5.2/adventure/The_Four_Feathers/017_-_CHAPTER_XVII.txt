Mrs. Adair asked for nothing and so, by her own crooked creed, learned nothing. Behind her pale, well-bred composure lived a mind that preferred the scrape of a keyhole to the honest swing of a door. A fact won by trapping someone into offering it felt to her like a delicacy reduced in a careful sauce: richer because it had been coaxed, not served up plainly. All through luncheon, though the small white feather in Ethne’s possession prickled her curiosity like a burr caught in lace, though Captain Willoughby’s tidings about Harry Feversham set her thoughts tapping and turning, though the old night at Ramelton—years ago now—hovered at the edge of her guesses like a name half-forgotten in a dying dialect, she showed no sign. She poured out talk on indifferent things with practiced brightness.

It did no good.

“I don’t believe you hear a single word I am saying!” she broke out at last.

Ethne laughed, and admitted it as frankly as if she had been caught seasoning a dish absent-mindedly while someone read aloud a menu.

As soon as luncheon was done she escaped to her room and claimed the afternoon for solitude. From her window the estate lay mild and orderly, yet her thoughts ran far from it, running and returning like a tune that will not let itself be translated into speech. She repeated, slowly, the story Willoughby had brought her in the morning, and it went through her like music played with the right pressure and the right pause—thrilling not because it argued, but because it sounded true.

The small regret—that he had not come home and told it a year ago, when she had been free—was there, but it lay light, like a crumpled rose-leaf under a mattress that could not ruin sleep. The story itself overwhelmed it. Harry Feversham, who had struck the blow at Lennon House, had also, at peril to himself, restored the pride that blow had broken. It was a strange restitution: the hand that had shamed her had, without asking her faith or permission, laboured to give her back her own regard. Gratitude warmed her with a quickening like sunlight on skin after long cloud. Youth, which she had thought spent, came back on that August afternoon in the way a lost taste returns suddenly to the tongue.

She unlocked a drawer in her dressing-case and took out the one portrait—of all his gifts—the only one she had kept. She was clear with herself. The man in the picture was dead to her in the only way that mattered; there was no thought of disloyalty towards Durrance. Yet the dead may still be friends. She looked at the likeness with contentment not sharpened by longing, but softened by respect. He had needed no encouragement from her to keep him on the level of a hard inspiration. When she put the portrait away, she laid the little white feather beside it, as if sealing together two tokens whose meanings could not be rendered into any plain, dominant vocabulary, and locked the drawer.

She returned to the window.

A light breeze made the shadows of the high trees dance in broken patterns over the lawn. The sun mellowed and reddened; the creek beyond held the sky’s colour as a pan holds a slow-browned glaze. Yet, for all the richness of the scene, her heart tugged homewards. She was of her county—Harry Feversham had found that out long ago—and great sorrow and great joy drew her alike to Donegal, where endurance came easier and gladness felt complete. It was August: the first heather would be out on the hillsides, and she could almost smell it, a dry sweetness over peat and granite. She wished the news had found her there, among the brown mountains and the voices of streams that spoke an older language than any drawing-room ever managed. Here, in this strange land, she could not help feeling that even happiness had a different accent.

But Donegal was not wholly absent. Dermod’s old collie had become her shadow; his warm, patient presence was a kind of hearth. To him she spoke her confidence, and if her voice broke, he would never carry the tale beyond her knees. With him at her feet she filled in, from Willoughby’s omissions and from what Feversham had never told, the missing parts as one reconstructs a vanished recipe from scent and texture. Those three years of concealment in Suakin, for instance: the small crowded streets, the troops marching out and coming back dust-stained, bleeding, crowned with victory. She saw Feversham slipping away at their approach, his whole body tuned to danger, afraid an old friend—Durrance, perhaps, or Willoughby, or Trench—might catch a familiar movement and pierce a disguise. She saw the first panic when Berber’s dark brown walls rose ahead; the night in the ruined acres; the stumbling search for the well among Obak’s shifting sandhills. Each picture came with its own taste of fear—metal on the tongue, sand in the throat—and with each she asked, not as a reproach but as a startled accounting, Where was I then? What was I doing?

She sat in that golden drift of thought until the light changed on the creek, until rooks wheeled noisily from the treetops to settle themselves for the night, their calls stitching the evening together. The sound drew her back as surely as a bell recalls a cook from the far end of the market.

At dinner she brought a buoyancy that startled her companions. Mrs. Adair, peering sideways as ever, had to admit that seldom had Ethne’s eyes shone so starrily, or colour sat so freshly on her cheeks. She grew more certain that Willoughby’s news had been of the stirring sort; she grew more tortured by her failure to name it. Yet the meal passed with an ease they had not known since Durrance’s return to Guessens. He, too, threw off some restraint. His spirits rose to meet Ethne’s; he answered laugh with laugh, and that habitual tension—the look of a man listening with his whole life, making his ears stand in for lost eyes—fell away from his face for once, as if the house’s soundscape had softened enough to let him rest inside it.

“You will play on your violin to-night, I think,” he said when they rose.

“Yes,” she answered, “I will—with all my heart.”

He laughed and held the door.

The violin had stayed locked in its case these last two months. Durrance had come to treat that case like a gauge. When the world went well with Ethne, the lid opened and the instrument was allowed to speak; when it went ill, it stayed shut, lest it say too much, lay open old wounds, and expose them to eyes that had no right. Ethne herself knew it for an indiscreet friend—like some cherished dish that reveals, by its very aroma, what has been happening in the kitchen.

But to-night it would be brought out.

Mrs. Adair lingered until Ethne was out of earshot.

“You have noticed the change in her to-night?” she asked.

“Yes. Have I not?” Durrance replied.

“One has waited for it, hoped for it, despaired of it.”

“Are you so glad of the change?”

Durrance tipped his head back.

“Do you wonder that I am glad? Kind, friendly, unselfish—these things she has always been. But there is more than friendliness evident to-night, and for the first time it’s evident.”

A look of pity crossed Mrs. Adair’s face. She went out without another word.

Durrance took all of that change to himself.

Mrs. Adair, restless as a kettle just before boiling, drew up the blinds in the drawing-room, opened the window, and admitted the moonlight. Then, seeing Ethne unlocking the violin-case, she stepped out onto the terrace; she could not sit patiently in the room and watch a secret unfold without her.

So when Durrance entered the drawing-room, he found Ethne alone. She sat in the window, already tightening the strings, turning each peg with the careful, exact ear of someone who understands that a world can be made unbearable by the wrong pitch. Outside, the night’s sounds lay clear: the faint lap from the creek, a distant insect’s steady rasp, leaves shifting with the smallest wind. No harsh machinery intruded; no bought entertainment shouted over the quiet. The house held an older peace, the sort modern noise tries to sell back to people in bottles.

Durrance took a chair behind her in the shadows.

“What shall I play to you?” she asked.

“The Musoline Overture,” he said. “You played it on the first evening when I came to Ramelton. I remember how you played it then. Play it again to-night. I want to compare.”

“I have played it since.”

“Never to me.”

They were alone. The windows stood open on moonlight. Ethne rose suddenly, crossed to the lamp, and put it out. The room deepened; the moon became their only light. She returned to her seat. Durrance remained behind her, leaning forward, hands on his knees, listening—not with the relaxed pleasure he had shown at dinner, but with an intentness he had hidden, like a man tasting for a secret ingredient.

He was making, as he believed, a final test on which his life and hers should turn. The violin would tell him what words could not. Would it speak only friendship—or the thing beyond friendship?

Ethne began the overture, and as soon as the bow drew its first clear line of sound, she forgot he was there.

The garden lay still, warm with summer; scent rose from the borders, thick and sweet as if crushed leaves had been rubbed between fingers. The creek held the moonlight like a solid floor of silver. Trees stood dreaming up at the stars. The music floated out over the lawn into the clean, attentive night, and with it a fancy took her—swift, irrational, irresistible—that the sound might travel down the creek, over Salcombe Bar, and across the bright seas until it struck, small but piercingly clear like fairy music, on the ears of a man sleeping far away under southern stars, the cool desert wind on his face.

If he could only hear, she thought. If he could only wake and know it was a message of friendship.

And with that in her mind she played with a skill she had never used before, not for display but for meaning. She made the violin a voice of sympathy, as if tenderness could be tuned and carried.

The fancy grew as the overture unfolded. The music became a bridge swung in mid-air across the world, and for a few minutes she and Harry Feversham might meet upon it and clasp hands. They would part at once, each returning to the path allotted—yet those minutes would strengthen both to continue.

The chords rang out into silence, and it seemed to her they declared the pride that had returned to her that day. Her fancy hardened into belief. It was no longer If he should hear; it was He must hear. And then, swept past caution, a strange hope sprang up and took her whole attention:

If he could answer.

She lingered on the last bars, holding them as one holds back the final taste of a dish, waiting for the moment after when the mouth understands what it has been given. When the music died, she sat with the violin across her knees and stared out across the moonlit garden, listening for an impossible reply.

An answer came.

Not from the creek, not across the lawn, not from any distance that could be crossed by wind and water.

It came from the dark behind her, spoken in Durrance’s voice.

“Ethne, where do you think I heard that overture last played?”

She started, as if shaken awake.

“Why—you told me. At Ramelton, when you first came to Lennon House.”

“I have heard it since,” he said. “Though it was not played by you. It was not really played at all. But a melody of it—and not even that really, but a suggestion of a melody—I heard stumbled out on a zither, with many false notes, by a Greek in a bare little whitewashed café, lit by one glaring lamp, at Wadi Halfa.”

“This overture?” she whispered. “How strange!”

“Not so strange after all. For the Greek was Harry Feversham.”

So the answer had come.

Ethne sat very still in the moonlight. Anyone bending close might have seen her eyelids closed, as if she were trying to keep the moment intact against the bluntness of sight. A long silence followed. She did not ask why Durrance, having kept such knowledge for so long, chose to speak of it now. She did not ask what had driven Harry Feversham to scrape at a zither in a mean café at Wadi Halfa. The details did not yet want naming; the experience felt untranslatable, like some private dialect of the heart that would only be damaged by forcing it into common words.

It seemed to her simply that he had spoken to her as she had played to him. The music, after all, had been a bridge. It did not even feel strange that he had used Durrance’s voice.

“When was this?” she asked at last.

“In February of this year. I will tell you about it.”

“Yes,” she said. “Please, tell me.”

And Durrance, out of the shadows, began to speak.
