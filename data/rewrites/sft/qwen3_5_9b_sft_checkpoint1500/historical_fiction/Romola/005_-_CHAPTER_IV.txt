First Impressions.

"Good day, Messer Domenico," Nello greeted the first visitor stepping into the shop, offering a silent nod to the second. "You arrive with the timing of cheese upon macaroni. Ah, you are in haste, seeking a shave without delay—eccolo! And today is a morning where every soul carries heavy burdens upon their mind. Florence bereaved—the very hinge of Italy torn away—and heaven itself bewildered as to its next move. *Oimè*!" Nello sighed, though the sun was already climbing steadily toward dinner hour. "As I remarked, you arrive like freshly grated cheese. This young stranger seeks an honorable merchant willing to advance funds against a specific ring of value. If I were to count every goldsmith and money-lender in Florence upon my fingers, I could not find a finer name than Menico Cennini. Moreover, he possesses other wares wherein you trade—Greek learning and youthful eyes, a dual implement that printers like yourselves are perpetually in want of."

The grave elderly gentleman, son of Bernardo Cennini, who two decades prior had heard tales of the German printing press and subsequently cast his own typefaces in Florence, sat in a lathered, passive silence while Nello poured forth this torrent of conversation. Yet, the old man slowly turned a sideways glance toward the stranger.

"This handsome youth offers you unlimited Greek, Latin, or Italian," Nello continued, delighting in expansive paraphrase. "He is a marvel of juvenile learning, akin to Francesco Filelfo or our own unparalleled Poliziano. A second Guarino, too, though fate has dealt him a cruel blow; shipwrecked, he has likely lost a treasury of precious manuscripts that might have lent even greater correctness to your editions, Domenico. Fortunately, he has salvaged a few gems of rare worth. His name is—what did you call yourself, Messer?"

"Tito Melema," the stranger replied, sliding the ring from his finger and presenting it to Cennini. Nello, equally swift with his razor as with his tongue, had just freed the goldsmith from the shaving cloth.

Meanwhile, the man who had entered with the goldsmith—a tall figure of about fifty, sporting a short, trimmed beard, an old felt hat, and a threadbare mantle—had kept his gaze fixed upon the Greek. Now, he spoke abruptly.

"Young man, I am painting a depiction of Sinon deceiving old Priam, and I should be grateful for your likeness to serve as my Sinon, provided you grant me a sitting."

Tito Melema started, his face flushing with pale astonishment, as if struck by a sudden accusation. But Nello gave him no moment to flounder for a response. "Piero," the barber interjected, "you are the most extraordinary mixture of humors and fancies ever encased in human skin. What sorcery do you intend to work upon the visage of this learned youth to fashion it into a traitor? Pray ask him to lift his eyes upward; then, behold, you could mold a Saint Sebastian that would draw throngs of devout women to weep. Or, if you prefer a classical tone, drape myrtle around his curls and transform him into a young Bacchus—or, more accurately, a Phoebus Apollo—for his countenance burns with the warmth and brightness of a summer morn. It won me as a friend within the span of a single 'credo.'"

"Aye, Nello," the painter assented, his speech broken by abrupt pauses. "And if your tongue could cease its endless chirping long enough for your understanding to ponder the matter, you would see precisely why the face of Messere suits my traitor. A perfect traitor must possess a visage upon which vice leaves no mark—lips capable of lying with a dimpled smile, eyes of agate-like brilliance and depth that no infamy can tarnish, cheeks that remain un-haggard after a murder. I speak not that this young man is a traitor; I merely assert that he bears a face which would render him a more consummate villain if coupled with a treacherous heart. To state otherwise is to say nothing more nor less than that he possesses a beautiful face, animated by rich young blood, sufficient to maintain its color through simple nourishment without much aid from virtue. He may harbor the heart of a hero alongside it; I aver nothing to the contrary. Ask Domenico there if the lapidaries can always discern a gem solely by sight. And now, I shall stuff tow into my ears, for your chatter combined with the street bells is more than I can endure. Speak no further, but trim my beard."

With these final words, Piero—known as "di Cosimo" after his master, Cosimo Rosselli—took two pieces of tow, stuffed them firmly into his ears, and settled into the chair before Nello. The barber shrugged, casting a grimace of feigned intelligence at the Greek, as if to declare, "A whimsical fellow, do you see? Everyone takes his ramblings for mere jests."

Tito, standing transfixed with his long, dark eyes resting on the unknown man who had addressed him so ambiguously, seemed to regain his composure only when Piero shifted his position. Satisfied with the explanation, he returned his attention to Cennini, who now observed closely.

"This is a curious and valuable ring, young man. This intaglio, depicting a fish with a crested serpent above it, carved into the black stratum of the onyx—or rather, nicolo—is beautifully highlighted by the surrounding blue of the upper layer. Does this ring, doubtless, possess a history?" Cennini asked, looking up with keen scrutiny.

"Yes, indeed," Tito answered, meeting the gaze frankly. "The ring was discovered in Sicily. From those who devote themselves to gems and seals, I understand that both the stone and the carving possess virtues to bring fortune to the wearer, particularly at sea, and to restore whatever one may have lost." He smiled then. "However, although I have worn it constantly since departing Greece, it has not rendered me entirely fortunate at sea, as you perceive, unless escaping drowning counts as sufficient proof of its virtue. It remains to be seen whether my lost chests will surface. But to ensure no opportunity is missed, Messer, I implore you to hold the ring briefly as pledge for a small sum far below its true value. I shall redeem it the moment I can dispose of certain other gems secured within my doublet, or indeed as soon as I can earn something through any scholarly employment, if fortune deigns to allow me such luck."

"That shall be arranged, young man, if you will accompany me," Cennini said. "My brother Pietro, who judges scholarship far better than I, may perhaps provide you with a task testing your capabilities. In the meantime, return your ring until I can deliver the necessary florins, and if you wish, follow me."

"Yes, yes," Nello chimed in. "Go with Messer Domenico; you could not seek better company. He was born under the constellation that grants skill, riches, and integrity, whatever that celestial alignment may be—a detail of lesser consequence, for infants cannot select their own horoscopes, and indeed, if they could, there might be a disastrous rush of babes at particular epochs. Besides, our Phoenix, the incomparable Pico, has demonstrated that horoscopes are all a nonsensical dream—which is the least troublesome of opinions. *Addio, bel giovane!* Do not fail to return to me."

"No fear of that," Tito replied, bowing farewell as he turned his bright face toward the door. "You owe me a great service; that is the most positive guarantee of our future meetings."

"Say what you will, Piero," Nello muttered as the young stranger vanished. "I shall never view such an exterior without taking it as a sign of a lovable nature. Next, you will claim that Leonardo, whom you incessantly rave about, ought to have crafted his Judas as beautiful as Saint John! But you are as deaf as the summit of Mount Morello with that accursed tow in your ears. Well, well: I shall extract a bit more of this young man's history before escorting him to Bardo Bardi."
