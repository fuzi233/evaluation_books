The heavy leather-bound manuscript lay upon the desk, a silent testament to a truth too terrible for the light of day. Attached to its first page was a note from Doctor Hesselius, a man whose mind was a labyrinth of cold logic and obscure knowledge. In it, he referred to his own treatise, an essay written with the precision of a surgeon dissecting a corpse, detailing the strange phenomena this document illuminated. It was but a single volume in his collected works, a footnote in a life spent chasing shadows.

I had intended to publish this case merely to satisfy the curiosity of the uninitiated, the "laity" who shudder at the edge of the known world. Yet, in doing so, I would not diminish the account given by the woman herself. She was a lady of sharp intellect and terrifying care, her voice preserved here in ink. After much deliberation, I resolved to spare the reader the dry summary of Hesselius's reasoning. His words spoke of "arcana involving the profoundest mysteries of our dual existence," a duality between the living and the dead that chilled the marrow. To reduce such a revelation to a précis would be to insult the gravity of what followed.

My heart ached when I first held this paper, yearning to revive the correspondence Hesselius had begun so long ago with his informant. I hoped to hear her voice again, to question her further, to peel back another layer of the veil. But regret, sharp and final, settled over me. The woman was dead. She had passed away in the years since those letters were last exchanged.

Perhaps, in death, she could offer no new insight. Perhaps her silence was the only thing left to speak. Yet, as I turned to the pages she had entrusted to me, I knew that her narrative, delivered with such conscientious particularity, contained everything necessary. The story did not need her breath to live; it needed only to be read. And so, I opened the book, ready to listen to the tale of the one who walked between worlds.

An Early Fright

In the shadowed valleys of Styria, we dwell within a fortress of stone and silence, a *schloss* that seems carved from the gloom itself. Here, wealth is measured not in gold but in isolation; a mere eight or nine hundred florins a year stretches our lives to the breaking point, yet in this primitive land where labor costs nothing and fear is currency, it suffices. My father, an Englishman retired from the Austrian service, bought this feudal relic and the desolate acres clinging to it for a song. He is a kind man, growing brittle with age, and I am his only child, bearing an English name though I have never seen the land of my birth. To us, this lonely eminence, surrounded by a forest that stretches fifteen miles to the right and twelve to the left, is the entire world.

The castle sits atop a slight rise, overlooking a moat choked with white water-lilies and swans that glide like ghosts over the dark water. A narrow, ancient road passes beneath a drawbridge that has never risen in my lifetime, leading to a gate framed by a steep Gothic bridge spanning a stream that winds through the deep shadows of the woods. We are utterly alone. The nearest village is seven miles away, a mere speck on the horizon, while the nearest seat of power belongs to General Spielsdorf, twenty miles distant. Between us lies only ruin: three miles west, a dead village with a roofless church, its aisles filled with the moldering tombs of the Karnsteins, an extinct family that once ruled a chateau now swallowed by the trees. Legends speak of why they fled, but I know none of them yet.

Our household is a tiny circle of souls. My mother, a Styrian, died when I was an infant, a memory erased before it could take root. In her place stood Madame Perrodon, a Swiss woman of benevolent bulk and broken English, who became my surrogate mother. Beside her was Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, a "finishing governess" who spoke French and German, while my father and I insisted on speaking English daily to keep the tongue alive. We formed a babel of languages that amused strangers, accompanied occasionally by young ladies of my own age who visited for fleeting terms. Yet, despite these few companions, my life was one of profound solitude.

It was in this quiet isolation that the first true terror of my life took root, an incident so trivial in retrospect that it should perhaps remain unrecorded, yet one that haunts my dreams to this day. I was no more than six years old when the nightmare began. The nursery, a vast chamber with a steep oak ceiling, belonged solely to me. One night, I awoke to find the room empty. The maid was gone, the nurse absent. I was not afraid, for I was shielded from the stories of ghosts and fairies that make grown women tremble at a creaking floorboard. Instead, I felt neglected, insulted by the abandonment. I began to whimper, preparing for a tantrum, when a figure emerged from the shadows beside my bed.

A young woman knelt there, her face solemn yet strangely beautiful, her hands hidden beneath the heavy coverlet. She looked at me with a strange, placid wonder, and as she reached out to caress me and drew me close, a wave of inexplicable delight washed over me, silencing my tears. I drifted back to sleep, lulled by her presence. Then, without warning, a sharp, stinging pain pierced my breast, as if two needles had driven deep into my flesh. I screamed.

The woman recoiled, her eyes wide and fixed on me, before slipping silently to the floor and vanishing beneath the bed.

For the first time, true fear seized me. I yelled until my voice broke. Soon, the nurse, the maid, and the housekeeper rushed in. They soothed me with gentle words, but their faces were pale, etched with an anxiety I had never seen. They searched frantically—under the bed, behind the tables, inside the cupboards. The housekeeper leaned close to the nurse and whispered, "Feel along that hollow in the bed. Someone *did* lie there. The warmth remains."

They examined my chest, searching for the wound I claimed to feel, but found nothing but smooth skin. That night, the three servants sat vigil in the nursery, their eyes never closing. From that moment until I was fourteen, a servant was always present in the room.

The days that followed were a blur of terror. A doctor, an elderly man with a saturnine face pitted by smallpox and a chestnut wig, arrived every second day to prescribe bitter medicines I despised. I could not bear to be left alone, even in the broad daylight.

One morning, my father entered the room. He stood by my bedside, laughing cheerfully at something the nurse said, patting my shoulder and kissing my forehead. "It was only a dream, my dear," he assured me, his voice bright. "It cannot hurt you."

But I knew better. The woman in the bed was not a dream. The cold touch of her fingers and the sudden, needle-like pain were real. I was awfully frightened.

Only the nursery maid offered a sliver of comfort, insisting that it was she who had knelt beside me, and that I must have been half-asleep to fail to recognize her. But this explanation did not satisfy the dread coiling in my stomach. Later that day, a venerable old man in a black cassock entered the room. His face was sweet and gentle, and he took my hands, guiding me to join them as he and the women prayed aloud.

"Lord hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus' sake," he intoned, his voice quavering with earnestness.

I remember the stark contrast of his white hair against the black fabric, the clumsy furniture of three centuries surrounding us, and the scanty light filtering through the small lattice window. He knelt with the women, praying for what seemed an eternity. I forget everything before that moment, and for some time after, my memories dissolve into obscurity. Only the scene of that prayer remains vivid, an isolated picture in a phantasmagoria of darkness, marking the beginning of a life forever altered by the unseen.

II. A Guest

I am about to recount a tale so singular that it demands every ounce of your faith in my honesty to accept it as true. Yet, it is undeniably real—a truth witnessed by my own eyes.

It was a sweet summer evening, and my father invited me, as he often did, to accompany him on a stroll through the magnificent forest vista bordering our estate.

"General Spielsdorf cannot arrive sooner than I had hoped," my father remarked as we walked.

He was scheduled to visit for several weeks, with his arrival expected the following day. He was to bring with him a young lady, his niece and ward, Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt. I had never met her, though I had heard her described as a creature of immense charm, a prospect that had filled my days with dreams of delightful companionship. I was far more disappointed than a young woman accustomed to the bustle of a city could possibly imagine. This anticipated visit had been the centerpiece of my reveries for weeks.

"And when does he come?" I asked.

"Not until autumn," he replied. "Perhaps not for two months. And I am truly glad, my dear, that you never met Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt."

"And why?" I asked, feeling a sharp sting of mortification mixed with curiosity.

"Because the poor young lady is dead," he answered simply. "I merely forgot to mention it, but you were not in the room when I received the General's letter this evening."

Shock washed over me. In his first correspondence, written six or seven weeks prior, the General had noted that Bertha was unwell, yet there had been no hint of peril.

"Here is the letter," he said, handing it to me. "He is in profound affliction; the writing seems dictated by sheer distraction."

We sat upon a rough bench beneath a cluster of majestic lime trees. The sun was setting with a melancholy brilliance behind the sylvan horizon, casting long shadows as the stream beside our home wound through noble trees, reflecting the fading crimson of the sky. The General's letter was extraordinary, vehement, and riddled with contradictions. I read it twice, the second time aloud to my father, still unable to make sense of it except to assume that grief had shattered his composure.

*"I have lost my darling daughter, for as such I loved her,"* it began. *"During the final days of dear Bertha's illness, I was unable to write. Before then, I knew nothing of her danger. I have lost her, and now learn _all_ too late. She died in the peace of innocence, and in the glorious hope of a blessed futurity. The fiend who betrayed our infatuated hospitality has done it all. I thought I was welcoming innocence, gaiety, a charming companion for my lost Bertha. Heavens! What a fool have I been! I thank God my child died without suspicion of the cause of her sufferings. She is gone without even conjecturing the nature of her illness or the accursed passion of the agent of this misery. I devote my remaining days to tracking and extinguishing a monster. I am told I may hope to accomplish this righteous purpose. At present, there is scarcely a gleam of light to guide me. I curse my conceited incredulity, my despicable affectation of superiority, my blindness, my obstinacy—all—too late. I cannot write or talk collectedly now. I am distracted. As soon as I recover somewhat, I intend to devote myself to inquiry, which may lead me as far as Vienna. Some time in the autumn, two months hence, or earlier if I live, I will see you—that is, if you permit me; I will then tell you all that I scarce dare put upon paper now. Farewell. Pray for me, dear friend."*

Though I had never seen Bertha Rheinfeldt, tears filled my eyes at the sudden news. I was startled, profoundly disappointed.

The sun had set, and twilight deepened as I returned the letter to my father. It was a soft, clear evening. We loitered, speculating on the violent, incoherent sentences we had just read. We had nearly a mile to walk before reaching the road passing the front of the schloss, and by then the moon was shining brilliantly. At the drawbridge, we encountered Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, who had emerged without their bonnets to enjoy the exquisite moonlight.

We heard their voices gabbling in animated dialogue as we approached. Joining them at the drawbridge, we turned to admire the scene together.

The glade through which we had walked lay before us. To our left, the narrow road wound away under clumps of lordly trees, vanishing into the thickening forest. To the right, the road crossed the steep, picturesque bridge near a ruined tower that once guarded the pass; beyond the bridge, an abrupt eminence rose, covered in trees and showing grey, ivy-clustered rocks in the shadows. Over the sward and low grounds, a thin film of mist stole like smoke, marking distances with a transparent veil, while here and there the river flashed faintly in the moonlight.

No softer, sweeter scene could be imagined. The news I had heard made it melancholy, yet nothing could disturb its profound serenity or the enchanted glory of the prospect. My father, who loved the picturesque, and I, stood in silence gazing over the expanse below. The two governesses stood a little behind us, discoursing eloquently on the moon.

Madame Perrodon was fat, middle-aged, and romantic, talking and sighing poetically. Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, claiming descent from a German father, assumed a psychological and metaphysical tone, declaring that when the moon shone with such intensity, it indicated special spiritual activity. She spoke of the full moon's manifold effects: acting on dreams, on lunacy, on nervous people, exerting marvelous physical influences connected to life. She recounted how her cousin, a merchant's mate, had woken from a nap on deck during such a night to find an old woman clawing at his cheek in his dream; his features had been horribly drawn to one side, and his countenance had never fully recovered its equilibrium.

"The moon, this night," she said, "is full of idyllic and magnetic influence—and see, when you look behind you at the front of the schloss, how all its windows flash and twinkle with that silvery splendor, as if unseen hands had lit the rooms to receive fairy guests."

There are indolent styles of spirits in which, indisposed to speak ourselves, the talk of others is pleasant to our listless ears. I gazed on, pleased by the tinkling of the ladies' conversation.

"I have fallen into one of my moping moods tonight," my father said after a silence, quoting Shakespeare to maintain our English:

*"In truth I know not why I am so sad.
It wearies me: you say it wearies you;
But how I got it—came by it."*

He forgot the rest. "But I feel as if some great misfortune were hanging over us. I suppose the poor General's afflicted letter has something to do with it."

At that moment, the unwonted sound of carriage wheels and many hoofs on the road arrested our attention. They seemed to approach from the high ground overlooking the bridge. Soon, the equipage emerged. Two horsemen crossed the bridge first, followed by a carriage drawn by four horses, with two men riding behind.

It appeared to be the traveling carriage of a person of rank, and we were immediately absorbed in watching this unusual spectacle. Moments later, it became far more interesting. Just as the carriage passed the summit of the steep bridge, one of the leading horses took fright, communicating its panic to the rest. After a few plunges, the whole team broke into a wild gallop, dashing between the horsemen ahead and thundering along the road toward us with hurricane speed.

The excitement was made more painful by the clear, long-drawn screams of a female voice from the carriage window. We all advanced in curiosity and horror; I moved in silence, the others with various ejaculations of terror.

Our suspense did not last long. Just before the castle drawbridge, on the route they were taking, stood a magnificent lime tree on one side and an ancient stone cross on the other. At the sight of the cross, the horses, moving at a pace that was perfectly frightful, swerved violently, causing the carriage wheel to strike the projecting roots of the tree.

I knew what was coming. I covered my eyes, unable to bear the sight, and turned my head away; at the same moment, I heard a cry from my lady friends, who had stepped forward.

Curiosity forced my eyes open, revealing a scene of utter confusion. Two horses lay on the ground. The carriage rested on its side, two wheels in the air. The men were busy removing the traces, while a lady with a commanding air and figure had dismounted, standing with clasped hands, raising the handkerchief within them to her eyes periodically.

Through the open carriage door, a young lady was lifted out, appearing lifeless. My dear old father was already beside the elder lady, hat in hand, evidently offering aid and the resources of our schloss. The older lady did not appear to hear him, nor to have eyes for anything but the slender girl being placed against the slope of the bank.

I approached. The young lady was apparently stunned, but certainly not dead. My father, who prided himself on being something of a physician, had just checked her wrist and assured the lady—who declared herself her mother—that her pulse, though faint and irregular, was undoubtedly still distinguishable. The lady clasped her hands and looked upward, as if in a momentary transport of gratitude; but immediately, she broke out again in that theatrical manner I believe is natural to some people.

She was a fine-looking woman for her time, must have been handsome; tall, but not thin, dressed in black velvet, and possessing a proud, commanding countenance, though now strangely agitated.

"Who was ever born to such calamity?" I heard her say, with clasped hands, as I came up. "Here am I, on a journey of life and death, in prosecuting which to lose an hour is possibly to lose all. My child will not have recovered sufficiently to resume her route for who can say how long. I must leave her: I cannot, dare not, delay. How far on, sir, is the nearest village? I must leave her there; and shall not see my darling, or even hear of her, till my return three months hence."

I plucked my father's coat and whispered earnestly in his ear: "Oh! papa, pray ask her to let her stay with us—it would be so delightful. Do, pray."

"If Madame will entrust her child to the care of my daughter and her good governess, Madame Perrodon, and permit her to remain as our guest, under my charge, until her return, it will confer a distinction and an obligation upon us, and we shall treat her with all the care and devotion which so sacred a trust deserves."

"I cannot do that, sir; it would be to task your kindness and chivalry too cruelly," the lady said, distractedly.

"It would, on the contrary, be to confer on us a very great kindness at the moment when we most need it. My daughter has just been disappointed by a cruel misfortune in a visit from which she had long anticipated great happiness. If you confide this young lady to our care, it will be her best consolation. The nearest village on your route is distant and affords no inn suitable for placing your daughter; you cannot allow her to continue her journey for any considerable distance without danger. If, as you say, you cannot suspend your journey, you must part with her tonight, and nowhere could you do so with more honest assurances of care and tenderness than here."

There was something in this lady's air and appearance—so distinguished and imposing, and in her manner so engaging—as to impress one, quite apart from the dignity of her equipage, with a conviction that she was a person of consequence.

By this time, the carriage was replaced in its upright position, and the horses, now tractable, were back in the traces.

The lady threw a glance at her daughter which I fancied was not quite so affectionate as one might have anticipated from the beginning of the scene. Then she beckoned slightly to my father and withdrew two or three steps with him out of hearing. She talked to him with a fixed, stern countenance, utterly unlike the manner in which she had hitherto spoken.

I was filled with wonder that my father did not seem to perceive the change, and also unspeakably curious to learn what she was saying, almost in his ear, with such earnestness and rapidity.

Two or three minutes at most she remained thus employed, then she turned. A few steps brought her to where her daughter lay, supported by Madame Perrodon. She knelt beside her for a moment and whispered, as Madame supposed, a little benediction in her ear; then, hastily kissing her, she stepped into her carriage. The door was closed, the footmen in stately liveries jumped up behind, the outriders spurred on, the postilions cracked their whips, the horses plunged and suddenly broke into a furious canter that threatened soon again to become a gallop, and the carriage whirled away, followed at the same rapid pace by the two horsemen in the rear.

We Compare Notes

Our eyes tracked the _cortege_ until the carriage vanished into the mist-shrouded woods, the rhythmic clatter of hooves and wheels dissolving into the suffocating silence of the night. Nothing remained but the sudden, sharp intake of breath from the young lady beside me. Her eyes fluttered open. Though her face was turned away, I saw her lift her head, scanning the darkness with a frantic urgency. A voice, sweet yet trembling with terror, cut through the air: "Where is mamma?"

Madame Perrodon responded with a soothing tenderness, offering assurances that soothed the immediate panic. Then came the next questions, rising in a crescendo of confusion: "Where am I? What is this place? Where is the carriage? And Matska?"

Madame answered what she could, and slowly, the girl pieced together the nightmare of the abduction. Relief washed over her when she learned no one in the carriage was hurt, but despair returned when she realized her mother had abandoned her there, promising a reunion in three months. Tears streamed down her face.

I moved to offer my own comfort, but Mademoiselle De Lafontaine gently restrained me. "Do not approach," she warned softly. "One person is all she can handle now; too much excitement might shatter her completely."

Once she was settled in bed, I resolved to visit her later. Meanwhile, my father had dispatched a servant on horseback to fetch the physician, who lived two leagues away, while a chamber was prepared for the stranger.

The girl rose, leaning heavily on Madame's arm, and walked slowly across the drawbridge and through the castle gates. Servants awaited in the hall to guide her to her quarters. Her room was the very drawing room we occupied, a long space with four windows overlooking the moat, the drawbridge, and the dark forest beyond. It was furnished in heavy, carved oak, filled with large cabinets and chairs upholstered in crimson Utrecht velvet. The walls were draped in tapestry framed in gold, depicting hunting scenes and festive gatherings in ancient costumes. It was stately yet comfortable, and here we drank tea, for my father insisted on the national beverage accompanying our coffee and chocolate.

That night, with candles burning low, we recounted the evening's events. Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine were present, while the stranger had already succumbed to a deep sleep under the watch of a servant.

"How do you like our guest?" I asked as Madame entered. "Tell me everything."

"I like her immensely," Madame replied. "She is, I believe, the prettiest creature I have ever seen; your age, so gentle and lovely."

"She is absolutely beautiful," Mademoiselle interjected, having peeked into the room.

"And such a sweet voice!" Madame added.

"Did you notice a woman in the carriage after it started again?" Mademoiselle asked. "Someone who didn't exit but merely stared from the window?"

"No, we saw none."

Then she described a horrifying figure: a hideous black woman wearing a colored turban, gazing from the carriage window with a derisive grin. Her eyes gleamed unnaturally, the whites vast and pale, her teeth bared in a snarl of fury.

"Did you notice how ill-favored the servants were?" Mademoiselle inquired.

"Yes," my father said, entering just then. "Ugly, hang-dog fellows I have never seen. I hope they do not rob the poor lady in the forest. They are clever rogues, however; they managed everything in a flash."

"They must be exhausted from the long journey," Madame suggested.

"Beyond their wicked appearance, their faces were strangely lean, dark, and sullen. I am very curious," my father admitted, though he dared not elaborate. "Perhaps the young lady will tell us tomorrow if she recovers."

"I doubt she will," my father said, a mysterious smile playing on his lips, a slight nod indicating he knew more than he cared to share.

This fueled our curiosity regarding his brief, earnest exchange with the lady in black velvet before her departure. We were scarcely alone when I pressed him for details. He yielded easily.

"There is no reason I should not tell you," he began. "She expressed reluctance to burden us with her daughter, citing delicate health and nervousness, yet explicitly stating she was not subject to seizures or illusions—in fact, perfectly sane."

"How odd to claim that!" I interjected. "It seemed unnecessary."

"At least it was said," he laughed. "Since you wish to know, I will recount it. She declared, 'I am making a long journey of vital importance—she stressed the word—rapid and secret. I shall return for my child in three months; meanwhile, she will remain silent as to who we are, whence we come, and whither we travel.' That was all. She spoke pure French. When she uttered the word 'secret,' she paused, staring sternly at me, her eyes locked on mine. I suspect she places great emphasis on that. You saw how swiftly she departed. I hope I have not acted foolishly in taking charge of the young lady."

For my part, I was delighted. I longed to see and speak with her, waiting only for the physician's approval. Those living in towns cannot comprehend how monumental an event the arrival of a new friend is in such profound solitude.

The doctor did not arrive until nearly one o'clock, but I could not sleep, unable to outrun the carriage of the princess in black velvet on foot. When the physician descended to the drawing room, he reported favorably. The patient was sitting up, her pulse regular, seemingly well. She had sustained no injuries, and the shock to her nerves had passed harmlessly. There was no objection to my visiting her, provided we both wished it. I sent a servant immediately to inquire if she would permit a few moments of my company.

The servant returned promptly to say she desired nothing more.

I was not long in accepting this invitation.

Our visitor lay in one of the finest rooms in the schloss. Perhaps it was slightly too stately. Opposite the foot of the bed hung a somber tapestry depicting Cleopatra with asps at her bosom, alongside other faded classic scenes. Yet, the gold carvings and rich, varied colors of the other decorations redeemed the gloom. Candles burned at the bedside. She sat up, her slender, pretty figure wrapped in a soft silk dressing gown embroidered with flowers, lined with thick quilted silk—the very garment her mother had thrown over her as she lay upon the ground.

What struck me dumb, causing me to recoil a step or two as I approached the bedside, was the face itself. It was the very visage that had haunted my childhood nights, fixed in my memory, the object of my horrified rumination for years when no one suspected my thoughts.

It was pretty, even beautiful, bearing the same melancholy expression I remembered. But this expression instantly transformed into a strange, fixed smile of recognition.

A silence stretched for a full minute before she spoke; I could not.

"How wonderful!" she exclaimed. "Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since."

"Wonderful indeed!" I repeated, struggling to overcome the horror that had momentarily silenced me. "Twelve years ago, in vision or reality, I certainly saw you. I could not forget your face. It has remained before my eyes ever since."

Her smile softened. Whatever was strange in it vanished, leaving her dimpling cheeks delightfully pretty and intelligent.

Feeling reassured, I continued in the vein of hospitality, welcoming her and expressing how much pleasure her accidental arrival had brought us, and especially how happy it made me. I took her hand, shy as a lonely person might be, yet the situation made me eloquent and bold. She pressed my hand, laying hers upon it, her eyes glowing as she looked hastily into mine, smiling again and blushing.

She answered my welcome prettily. I sat beside her, still wondering, as she began to speak.

"I must tell you my vision about you; it is so strange that we should have had such vivid dreams of each other, seeing one another as we do now, when we were both mere children. I was about six years old. I awoke from a confused, troubled dream to find myself in a room unlike my nursery, wainscoted in dark wood, with cupboards and benches scattered about. The beds were empty, and the room seemed devoid of anyone but myself. After looking around, admiring especially an iron candlestick with two branches that I would certainly recognize, I crept under a bed to reach the window. As I emerged, I heard someone crying. Looking up while I was still on my knees, I saw you—most assuredly you—as I see you now: a beautiful young lady with golden hair, large blue eyes, and lips exactly like yours.

"Your beauty won me. I climbed onto the bed and embraced you, and I think we both fell asleep. I was aroused by a scream; you were sitting up, screaming. I was frightened, slipped down to the ground, and seemed to lose consciousness for a moment. When I regained it, I was back in my nursery at home. Your face I have never forgotten. I could not be misled by mere resemblance. _You_ are the lady I saw then."

Now it was my turn to relate my corresponding vision, which I did to the undisguised wonder of my new acquaintance.

"I don't know which should fear the other," she said, smiling again. "If you were less pretty, I think I would be very afraid of you, but being as you are, and we both so young, I feel only that I made your acquaintance twelve years ago and already possess a right to your intimacy. At all events, it seems as if we were destined, from our earliest childhood, to be friends. I wonder if you feel as strangely drawn to me as I do to you. I have never had a friend—shall I find one now?" She sighed, her fine dark eyes gazing at me with passionate intensity.

Now the truth is, I felt rather unaccountably toward the beautiful stranger. I did feel, as she said, "drawn toward her," but there was also a sense of repulsion. In this ambiguous feeling, however, the attraction overwhelmingly prevailed. She interested and won me; she was so beautiful and indescribably engaging.

I perceived a growing languor and exhaustion stealing over her and hastened to bid her good night.

"The doctor thinks," I added, "that you ought to have a maid to sit with you tonight; one of ours is waiting, and she is a very useful and quiet creature."

"How kind of you, but I could not sleep; I never could with an attendant in the room. I shall require no assistance—and, shall I confess my weakness, I am haunted by a terror of robbers. Our house was robbed once, and two servants murdered, so I always lock my door. It has become a habit—and you look so kind I know you will forgive me. I see there is a key in the lock."

She held me close in her pretty arms for a moment and whispered in my ear, "Good night, darling, it is very hard to part with you, but good night; tomorrow, but not early, I shall see you again."

She sank back onto the pillow with a sigh, her fine eyes following me with a fond, melancholy gaze as she murmured again, "Good night, dear friend."

Young people like, and even love, on impulse. I was flattered by the evident, though as yet undeserved, fondness she showed me. I liked the confidence with which she accepted me. She was determined that we should be very near friends.

The next day arrived, and we met again. I was delighted with my companion; that is to say, in many respects.

Her looks lost nothing in the daylight—she was certainly the most beautiful creature I had ever seen, and the unpleasant remembrance of the face from my early dream had lost its initial shock. She confessed that she had experienced a similar shock upon seeing me, along with the same faint antipathy that had mingled with my admiration. We now laughed together over our momentary horrors.

Her Habits—A Saunter

I told you that I was charmed with her in most particulars.

There were some that did not please me so well.

She was above the middle height of women. I shall begin by describing her.

She was slender, and wonderfully graceful. Except that her movements were languid—very languid—indeed, there was nothing in her appearance to indicate an invalid. Her complexion was rich and brilliant; her features were small and beautifully formed; her eyes large, dark, and lustrous; her hair was quite wonderful, I never saw hair so magnificently thick and long when it was down about her shoulders; I have often placed my hands under it, and laughed with wonder at its weight. It was exquisitely fine and soft, and in color a rich very dark brown, with something of gold. I loved to let it down, tumbling with its own weight, as, in her room, she lay back in her chair talking in her sweet low voice, I used to fold and braid it, and spread it out and play with it. Heavens! If I had but known all!

I said there were particulars which did not please me. I have told you that her confidence won me the first night I saw her; but I found that she exercised with respect to herself, her mother, her history, everything in fact connected with her life, plans, and people, an ever wakeful reserve. I dare say I was unreasonable, perhaps I was wrong; I dare say I ought to have respected the solemn injunction laid upon my father by the stately lady in black velvet. But curiosity is a restless and unscrupulous passion, and no one girl can endure, with patience, that hers should be baffled by another. What harm could it do anyone to tell me what I so ardently desired to know? Had she no trust in my good sense or honor? Why would she not believe me when I assured her, so solemnly, that I would not divulge one syllable of what she told me to any mortal breathing.

There was a coldness, it seemed to me, beyond her years, in her smiling melancholy persistent refusal to afford me the least ray of light.

I cannot say we quarreled upon this point, for she would not quarrel upon any. It was, of course, very unfair of me to press her, very ill-bred, but I really could not help it; and I might just as well have let it alone.

What she did tell me amounted, in my unconscionable estimation—to nothing.

It was all summed up in three very vague disclosures:

First—Her name was Carmilla.

Second—Her family was very ancient and noble.

Third—Her home lay in the direction of the west.

She would not tell me the name of her family, nor their armorial bearings, nor the name of their estate, nor even that of the country they lived in.

You are not to suppose that I worried her incessantly on these subjects. I watched opportunity, and rather insinuated than urged my inquiries. Once or twice, indeed, I did attack her more directly. But no matter what my tactics, utter failure was invariably the result. Reproaches and caresses were all lost upon her. But I must add this, that her evasion was conducted with so pretty a melancholy and deprecation, with so many, and even passionate declarations of her liking for me, and trust in my honor, and with so many promises that I should at last know all, that I could not find it in my heart long to be offended with her.

She used to place her pretty arms about my neck, draw me to her, and laying her cheek to mine, murmur with her lips near my ear, "Dearest, your little heart is wounded; think me not cruel because I obey the irresistible law of my strength and weakness; if your dear heart is wounded, my wild heart bleeds with yours. In the rapture of my enormous humiliation I live in your warm life, and you shall die—die, sweetly die—into mine. I cannot help it; as I draw near to you, you, in your turn, will draw near to others, and learn the rapture of that cruelty, which yet is love; so, for a while, seek to know no more of me and mine, but trust me with all your loving spirit."

And when she had spoken such a rhapsody, she would press me more closely in her trembling embrace, and her lips in soft kisses gently glow upon my cheek.

Her agitations and her language were unintelligible to me.

From these foolish embraces, which were not of very frequent occurrence, I must allow, I used to wish to extricate myself; but my energies seemed to fail me. Her murmured words sounded like a lullaby in my ear, and soothed my resistance into a trance, from which I only seemed to recover myself when she withdrew her arms.

In these mysterious moods I did not like her. I experienced a strange tumultuous excitement that was pleasurable, ever and anon, mingled with a vague sense of fear and disgust. I had no distinct thoughts about her while such scenes lasted, but I was conscious of a love growing into adoration, and also of abhorrence. This I know is paradox, but I can make no other attempt to explain the feeling.

I now write, after an interval of more than ten years, with a trembling hand, with a confused and horrible recollection of certain occurrences and situations, in the ordeal through which I was unconsciously passing; though with a vivid and very sharp remembrance of the main current of my story.

But, I suspect, in all lives there are certain emotional scenes, those in which our passions have been most wildly and terribly roused, that are of all others the most vaguely and dimly remembered.

Sometimes after an hour of apathy, my strange and beautiful companion would take my hand and hold it with a fond pressure, renewed again and again; blushing softly, gazing in my face with languid and burning eyes, and breathing so fast that her dress rose and fell with the tumultuous respiration. It was like the ardor of a lover; it embarrassed me; it was hateful and yet over-powering; and with gloating eyes she drew me to her, and her hot lips traveled along my cheek in kisses; and she would whisper, almost in sobs, "You are mine, you _shall_ be mine, you and I are one for ever." Then she had thrown herself back in her chair, with her small hands over her eyes, leaving me trembling.

"Are we related," I used to ask; "what can you mean by all this? I remind you perhaps of someone whom you love; but you must not, I hate it; I don't know you—I don't know myself when you look so and talk so."

She used to sigh at my vehemence, then turn away and drop my hand.

Respecting these very extraordinary manifestations I strove in vain to form any satisfactory theory—I could not refer them to affectation or trick. It was unmistakably the momentary breaking out of suppressed instinct and emotion. Was she, notwithstanding her mother's volunteered denial, subject to brief visitations of insanity; or was there here a disguise and a romance? I had read in old storybooks of such things. What if a boyish lover had found his way into the house, and sought to prosecute his suit in masquerade, with the assistance of a clever old adventuress. But there were many things against this hypothesis, highly interesting as it was to my vanity.

I could boast of no little attentions such as masculine gallantry delights to offer. Between these passionate moments there were long intervals of commonplace, of gaiety, of brooding melancholy, during which, except that I detected her eyes so full of melancholy fire, following me, at times I might have been as nothing to her. Except in these brief periods of mysterious excitement her ways were girlish; and there was always a languor about her, quite incompatible with a masculine system in a state of health.

In some respects her habits were odd. Perhaps not so singular in the opinion of a town lady like you, as they appeared to us rustic people. She used to come down very late, generally not till one o'clock, she would then take a cup of chocolate, but eat nothing; we then went out for a walk, which was a mere saunter, and she seemed, almost immediately, exhausted, and either returned to the schloss or sat on one of the benches that were placed, here and there, among the trees. This was a bodily languor in which her mind did not sympathize. She was always an animated talker, and very intelligent.

She sometimes alluded for a moment to her own home, or mentioned an adventure or situation, or an early recollection, which indicated a people of strange manners, and described customs of which we knew nothing. I gathered from these chance hints that her native country was much more remote than I had at first fancied.

As we sat thus one afternoon under the trees a funeral passed us by. It was that of a pretty young girl, whom I had often seen, the daughter of one of the rangers of the forest. The poor man was walking behind the coffin of his darling; she was his only child, and he looked quite heartbroken.

Peasants walking two-and-two came behind, they were singing a funeral hymn.

I rose to mark my respect as they passed, and joined in the hymn they were very sweetly singing.

My companion shook me a little roughly, and I turned surprised.

She said brusquely, "Don't you perceive how discordant that is?"

"I think it very sweet, on the contrary," I answered, vexed at the interruption, and very uncomfortable, lest the people who composed the little procession should observe and resent what was passing.

I resumed, therefore, instantly, and was again interrupted. "You pierce my ears," said Carmilla, almost angrily, and stopping her ears with her tiny fingers. "Besides, how can you tell that your religion and mine are the same; your forms wound me, and I hate funerals. What a fuss! Why you must die—_everyone_ must die; and all are happier when they do. Come home."

"My father has gone on with the clergyman to the churchyard. I thought you knew she was to be buried today."

"She? I don't trouble my head about peasants. I don't know who she is," answered Carmilla, with a flash from her fine eyes.

"She is the poor girl who fancied she saw a ghost a fortnight ago, and has been dying ever since, till yesterday, when she expired."

"Tell me nothing about ghosts. I shan't sleep tonight if you do."

"I hope there is no plague or fever coming; all this looks very like it," I continued. "The swineherd's young wife died only a week ago, and she thought something seized her by the throat as she lay in her bed, and nearly strangled her. Papa says such horrible fancies do accompany some forms of fever. She was quite well the day before. She sank afterwards, and died before a week."

"Well, _her_ funeral is over, I hope, and _her_ hymn sung; and our ears shan't be tortured with that discord and jargon. It has made me nervous. Sit down here, beside me; sit close; hold my hand; press it hard-hard-harder."

We had moved a little back, and had come to another seat.

She sat down. Her face underwent a change that alarmed and even terrified me for a moment. It darkened, and became horribly livid; her teeth and hands were clenched, and she frowned and compressed her lips, while she stared down upon the ground at her feet, and trembled all over with a continued shudder as irrepressible as ague. All her energies seemed strained to suppress a fit, with which she was then breathlessly tugging; and at length a low convulsive cry of suffering broke from her, and gradually the hysteria subsided. "There! That comes of strangling people with hymns!" she said at last. "Hold me, hold me still. It is passing away."

And so gradually it did; and perhaps to dissipate the somber impression which the spectacle had left upon me, she became unusually animated and chatty; and so we got home.

This was the first time I had seen her exhibit any definable symptoms of that delicacy of health which her mother had spoken of. It was the first time, also, I had seen her exhibit anything like temper.

Both passed away like a summer cloud; and never but once afterwards did I witness on her part a momentary sign of anger. I will tell you how it happened.

She and I were looking out of one of the long drawing room windows, when there entered the courtyard, over the drawbridge, a figure of a wanderer whom I knew very well. He used to visit the schloss generally twice a year.

It was the figure of a hunchback, with the sharp lean features that generally accompany deformity. He wore a pointed black beard, and he was smiling from ear to ear, showing his white fangs. He was dressed in buff, black, and scarlet, and crossed with more straps and belts than I could count, from which hung all manner of things. Behind, he carried a magic lantern, and two boxes, which I well knew, in one of which was a salamander, and in the other a mandrake. These monsters used to make my father laugh. They were compounded of parts of monkeys, parrots, squirrels, fish, and hedgehogs, dried and stitched together with great neatness and startling effect. He had a fiddle, a box of conjuring apparatus, a pair of foils and masks attached to his belt, several other mysterious cases dangling about him, and a black staff with copper ferrules in his hand. His companion was a rough spare dog, that followed at his heels, but stopped short, suspiciously at the drawbridge, and in a little while began to howl dismally.

In the meantime, the mountebank, standing in the midst of the courtyard, raised his grotesque hat, and made us a very ceremonious bow, paying his compliments very volubly in execrable French, and German not much better.

A Wonderful Likeness

That evening, the heavy wheels of a cart groaned upon the gravel drive, bringing from Gratz the grave, dark-faced son of the picture cleaner. His horse, weary from the ten-league journey, stumbled slightly as the cart, laden with two massive packing cases, halted before the Schloss. In our secluded quarters, such arrivals always sparked a feverish curiosity; we would crowd the hall, breathless, to hear the latest news from the capital.

This time, however, the silence was heavier. The cases remained unopened in the hall while the messenger was ushered away by the servants to eat his supper. Only when the dust settled did the artist, armed with hammer, chisel, and screwdriver, return to meet us. We gathered once more, drawn by the promise of revelation, while Carmilla sat listlessly apart, her eyes fixed on nothing in particular.

One by one, the old portraits were brought to light, emerging from their shrouds of dust and decay. My mother belonged to an ancient Hungarian lineage, and these images, mostly portraits, had passed through her hands over generations. My father held a ledger, reading off numbers as the artist rummaged through the crates. The paintings were not masterpieces of technique, but they were undeniably ancient, some bearing the curious, distorted features of forgotten eras. For the most part, I saw them for the first time; the smoke and grime of centuries had nearly erased their faces.

"There is one picture I have not yet seen," my father murmured, his voice tight with anticipation. "In the corner, barely legible, is the name 'Marcia Karnstein,' dated 1698. I am curious to see how it has survived."

I remembered it. It was a small canvas, roughly a foot and a half high, square and unframed, blackened by age until it seemed to swallow the light.

The artist produced it then, with a sudden, evident pride. The darkness vanished. The image was not merely beautiful; it was startling, vibrating with a terrifying vitality. It was the effigy of Carmilla.

"Carmilla, dear," my father exclaimed, his voice cracking. "Here is an absolute miracle. You are alive, smiling, ready to speak. Look at the detail—see even the little mole on her throat."

My father laughed, a nervous sound, and turned to the cleaner, praising his skill with intelligent discourse about the restoration process. But I was paralyzed, lost in a wonder that grew colder with every glance.

"May I hang this in my room, Papa?" I asked, my voice trembling.

"Certainly, dear," he replied, smiling warmly. "If it pleases you, it must be prettier than I thought."

Carmilla did not answer. She leaned back, her fine eyes hidden beneath long lashes, staring at me with a gaze that felt less like observation and more like possession. She smiled, a rapturous, hollow expression.

"And now," she said softly, her voice cutting through the artist's chatter, "you can read the name clearly. It is not Marcia. Look—the gold leaf is fresh. It reads *Mircalla, Countess Karnstein*, with a coronet above and below the date 1698. I am descended from the Karnsteins, you know. Mamma was."

"Ah!" she sighed, the sound languid and heavy. "So am I. A very long descent, very ancient. Are there any Karnsteins living now?"

"None who bear the name, I believe," my father said, brushing aside the weight of history. "The family was ruined in civil wars long ago. The ruins of the castle lie only three miles away."

"How interesting," she murmured, her eyes drifting toward the open hall door where the moonlight spilled across the stone floor. "But look at that beautiful moonlight. Suppose you take a little ramble around the court? Look down at the road and the river."

"It is so like the night you came to us," I whispered, the memory surfacing with uncanny clarity.

She sighed, a sound of pure delight, and smiled. Rising, she took my arm, her fingers digging in slightly too hard, and we walked out onto the cold pavement.

In silence, we descended toward the drawbridge, where the landscape opened up before us, bathed in silver.

"So you were thinking of the night I came here?" she whispered, her breath warm against my ear.

"Are you glad I came?"

"Delighted, dear Carmilla," I answered, though my heart hammered against my ribs.

"And you asked for the picture you think resembles me to hang in your room," she murmured, drawing my arm tighter, resting her head heavily upon my shoulder. "How romantic you are, Carmilla. Whenever you tell me your story, it will be made up chiefly of some great romance."

She kissed me, silent and chilling.

"I am sure, Carmilla, you have been in love; that there is, at this moment, an affair of the heart going on."

"I have been in love with no one, and never shall," she whispered, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial hush, "unless it should be with you."

How beautiful she looked in the moonlight! Yet there was a strangeness to her beauty, a predatory stillness. Shy and strange was the look with which she quickly hid her face in my neck and hair, emitting tumultuous sighs that seemed almost to sob, pressing into me a hand that trembled with a strange, electric tension. Her soft cheek glowed against mine, burning hotter than the moonlight.

"Darling, darling," she murmured, her words slurring slightly. "I live in you; and you would die for me. I love you so."

Suddenly, I started away from her.

She was gazing at me now with eyes from which all fire, all meaning had drained away. Her face was colorless, apathetic, a mask of porcelain perfection.

"Is there a chill in the air, dear?" she said drowsily, her voice flat. "I almost shiver; have I been dreaming? Let us come in. Come; come; come in."

"You look ill, Carmilla; a little faint. You certainly must take some wine," I said, panic rising in my chest.

"Yes. I will. I'm better now. I shall be quite well in a few minutes. Yes, do give me a little wine," answered Carmilla, as we approached the door.

"Let us look again for a moment; it is the last time, perhaps, I shall see the moonlight with you."

"How do you feel now, dear Carmilla? Are you really better?" I asked, fear gripping me. I was beginning to take alarm, lest she had been stricken by the strange epidemic that had recently invaded the countryside.

"Papa would be grieved beyond measure," I added, my voice rising. "If he thought you were ever so little ill, without immediately letting us know. We have a very skilful doctor near us, the physician who was with Papa today."

"I am sure he is. I know how kind you all are; but, dear child, I am quite well again. There is nothing ever wrong with me, but a little weakness. People say I am languid; I am incapable of exertion; I can scarcely walk as far as a child of three years old: and every now and then the little strength I have falters, and I become as you have just seen me. But after all, I am very easily set up again; in a moment I am perfectly myself. See how I have recovered."

So, indeed, she had. She and I talked animatedly, laughing and chatting, and the remainder of the evening passed without any recurrence of those infatuations that had frightened me. I meant her crazy talk and looks, which had unsettled me so deeply.

But there occurred that night an event which gave my thoughts quite a new turn, and seemed to startle even Carmilla's languid nature into momentary energy.

A Very Strange Agony

The drawing room smelled of roasted nuts and heavy velvet, a scent that usually soothed me, but tonight it felt cloying. We had settled for coffee and chocolate, though Carmilla sat with her cup untouched, her gaze fixed on the fire as if searching for something beneath the flames. Soon, Madame and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine arrived, their chatter rising to fill the silence, turning the afternoon into a frantic card game. My father eventually emerged from his study, claiming a "dish of tea" as his excuse to join us.

When the cards were stacked away, he moved to the sofa and sat beside Carmilla. His voice, usually booming with authority, trembled slightly with anxiety. "Have you heard from your mother since you arrived?" he asked.

"No," she replied, her voice smooth as polished glass.

He pressed further, asking where a letter might reach her now. "I cannot tell," she answered, her smile not reaching her eyes. "But I have been thinking of leaving you. You have been too hospitable, too kind. I have caused you an infinity of trouble, and I wish to take a carriage tomorrow to pursue her. I know where I shall find her, though I dare not say where yet."

My father's relief was palpable, a sudden tension in his shoulders. "You must not dream of such a thing!" he exclaimed, gripping her arm. "We cannot afford to lose you. I will not consent to your departure unless your mother directs it herself. She agreed to stay until her return. I feel a terrible responsibility, unaided by her counsel, especially as the reports of the mysterious disease ravaging our neighborhood grow more terrifying by the hour. But rest assured, you shall not leave without her explicit command. We would suffer too greatly to let you go."

"Thank you, sir," she whispered, bowing her head with a bashful, practiced grace. "You have all been too kind. I have never known such happiness as in your chateau, under your care, amidst your daughter's company."

He kissed her hand, a gallant, old-world gesture, pleased by her words.

Later, I escorted Carmilla to her chamber as was our custom, sitting with her while she prepared for sleep.

"Do you think," I asked, trying to keep my voice steady, "that you will ever confide fully in me?"

She turned, a radiant, enigmatic smile playing on her lips, offering no verbal answer.

"You won't answer?" I pressed, feeling foolish. "You can't answer pleasantly; perhaps I shouldn't have asked."

"You were right to ask," she said softly, her eyes gleaming in the dim light. "You do not know how dear you are to me, or you would not think any confidence too great to seek. But I am bound by vows, far stricter than those of a nun. I dare not tell my story yet, not even to you. But the time is near. You will think me cruel, selfish. Yet love is always selfish; the more ardent, the more consuming. How jealous I am, you cannot comprehend. You must come with me, loving me, to death; or hate me and still follow me, carrying that hatred through death and beyond. There is no such word as indifference in my nature."

"Now, Carmilla," I interrupted hastily, "you are going to talk your wild nonsense again."

"Not I, silly little fool that I am," she laughed, a sound like dry leaves skittering on stone. "Full of whims and fancies. For your sake, I shall speak like a sage. Have you ever been to a ball?"

"No. How do you run on? What is it like? How charming it must be."

"I almost forget; it was years ago."

I laughed nervously. "You are not so old. Your first ball can hardly be forgotten yet."

"I remember everything about it—with an effort. I see it all, as divers see the world above them through a medium, dense and rippling yet transparent. That night, something occurred that confused the picture, faded its colors. I was nearly assassinated in my bed, wounded here," she pressed a finger against her breast, "and I have never been the same since."

"Were you near dying?"

"Yes, very. A cruel love, a strange love that would have taken my life. Love demands sacrifices. No sacrifice without blood." She yawned, curling her tiny hands into her rich, wavy hair, resting her head on the pillow. Her glittering eyes followed my every movement, tracking me with a shy, unreadable smile.

"Good night," I murmured, retreating from the room with a sensation of unease that settled deep in my stomach.

I often wondered if our pretty guest ever said her prayers. I had never seen her on her knees. In the morning, she descended only after our family prayers had concluded; at night, she never left the drawing room to join our brief vigil in the hall. Had it not been for the casual revelation that she had been baptized, I would have doubted her faith entirely. Religion was a subject she never touched. If I knew the world better, this neglect would not have surprised me.

The precautions of the nervous are contagious. Persons of a like temperament inevitably imitate them. I had adopted Carmilla's habit of locking my bedroom door, succumbing to her whimsical alarms about midnight invaders and prowling assassins. I had also begun the ritual of searching my room, ensuring no lurking thief or murderer was "ensconced" within the shadows.

With these wise measures in place, I climbed into bed and fell asleep. A candle burned steadily in my room, an old habit I had never broken, a beacon against the dark. Thus fortified, I thought I could rest in peace. But dreams slip through stone walls, illuminate dark rooms, or extinguish light ones; their figures enter and exit at will, laughing at locksmiths.

That night, I dreamed of a very strange agony.

It was not a nightmare, for I remained fully conscious of being asleep. Yet I was equally aware of being in my room, lying in bed, precisely as I was. I saw the furniture, the shadows, the darkness pressing in, exactly as I had seen it, save for the oppressive gloom. At the foot of the bed, something moved. At first, I could not distinguish it, but soon I saw a soot-black animal resembling a monstrous cat. It was immense, stretching four or five feet long, measuring the full length of the hearthrug as it paced back and forth with the lithe, sinister restlessness of a beast trapped in a cage.

I could not cry out, though terror seized my throat. Its pace quickened, the room plunging into deeper darkness until I could see nothing but its eyes. Two broad, luminous orbs approached my face. Suddenly, I felt a stinging pain, as if two large needles had darted an inch apart deep into my breast.

I woke with a scream. The room was illuminated by the candle that had burned all night. Standing at the foot of the bed, slightly to the right, was a female figure. She wore a dark, loose dress, her hair hanging down to cover her shoulders. She was as still as a block of stone, breathing not a whisper. As I stared, transfixed, she seemed to shift her position, moving closer to the door. Then, the door opened, and she passed out into the night.

Relief washed over me, allowing me to breathe and move. My first thought was that Carmilla had played a trick, that I had forgotten to secure my door. I rushed to it, finding it locked firmly on the inside. Fear, cold and sharp, gripped me. I was horrified. I threw myself back into the bed, pulling the covers over my head, lying there more dead than alive until the gray light of morning broke.

VII. Descending

The horror of that night clung to me with a viscosity that no time could wash away. It was not the fleeting chill of a nightmare, which fades like mist at dawn; rather, it seemed to seep into the very grain of the wood, staining the furniture and the walls themselves. The room retained the phantom's imprint, a silent witness to the terror that had consumed me.

The following day, solitude became unbearable. I hesitated to confide in my father, torn between two fears: that he would dismiss my tale as a childish jest, or worse, that he would believe I had fallen victim to the mysterious plague ravaging our village. I knew I was not sick, yet the thought of alarming his fragile health kept my tongue silent. Instead, I sought comfort in the presence of Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle Lafontaine. Their kindness was a balm, though they quickly sensed the shadow that had settled over my spirit.

When I finally unburdened my heart, Mademoiselle laughed, a bright, careless sound that grated against my nerves. "By the way," she teased, "the long lime tree walk behind Carmilla's window is haunted!"

"Nonsense!" Madame Perrodon interjected sharply, sensing the inappropriateness of the topic. "Who tells such stories?"

"Martin claims he saw a female figure walking down that avenue twice before sunrise, while the yard gate was being repaired," Mademoiselle insisted.

"As long as there are cows to milk in the river fields, he will see ghosts," Madame dismissed with a wave of her hand. "Martin is merely frightened. He is the most superstitious fool I know."

"Do not speak of this to Carmilla," I warned, my voice trembling slightly. "She can see down that path from her window, and she is, if anything, more terrified than I."

Carmilla descended later than usual, her face pale and drawn. "I was so frightened last night," she whispered, her eyes wide with a mixture of relief and lingering dread. "I am certain I would have seen something dreadful if not for the charm I purchased from that poor hunchback. I dreamed of a black shape circling my bed, and when I awoke, I felt a dark figure looming near the chimneypiece. But when I reached for the charm beneath my pillow, the vision vanished. I knew, with absolute certainty, that without it, something frightful would have seized me, just as it did those poor souls we heard of."

I recounted my own ordeal, describing the chilling encounter that had left me shaken. Carmilla listened with growing horror. "Did you keep the charm near you?" she asked, her voice earnest.

"No," I admitted, "I had dropped it into a vase in the drawing room. But I shall take it with me tonight, given your faith in it."

In retrospect, I cannot fathom how I summoned the courage to lie alone that night. I pinned the charm tightly to my pillow and fell into a sleep so profound it bordered on oblivion. The next night was equally restful, a dreamless void that offered temporary respite.

Yet, when I awoke, a heavy lassitude weighed upon me, a melancholy so intense it felt almost luxurious in its depth. "As I predicted," Carmilla said, a note of triumph in her voice. "I slept beautifully last night. I pinned the charm to my nightdress; last time it was too far away. I am convinced it was all fancy, save for the dreams. My doctor told me evil spirits do not exist; he said such terrors are merely fevers knocking at the door, unable to enter, and thus wandering on."

"What do you think the charm is?" I asked, seeking rationality.

"It has been treated with some drug, an antidote for malaria," she explained calmly. "It acts only on the body. You do not believe ribbons or perfumes frighten spirits? These ailments begin by attacking the nerves, infecting the brain. But the antidote repels them physically. It is natural science, not magic."

I tried to agree with her, to let the logic dull the edge of my fear, but the impression of the supernatural lingered, refusing to fade completely.

For several nights, I slept deeply, yet every morning brought the same crushing lethargy. I felt myself changing, a stranger in my own skin. A strange, sweet melancholy stole over me, accompanied by dim thoughts of death. An idea took gentle possession of my mind: that I was slowly sinking. Though sad, this acceptance was strangely comforting. My soul acquiesced to the decline. I refused to admit I was ill, refusing to summon my father or the doctor.

Carmilla, however, became more devoted than ever. Her paroxysms of languid adoration grew more frequent, her gaze glazing with an increasing ardor as my strength waned. It shocked me, a momentary glare of insanity, to see her gloat over my fading vitality.

Unknowingly, I had entered the advanced stages of a malady unlike any known to mortals. Its early symptoms held a fascinating allure that reconciled me to the incapacitation, but soon, a sense of the horrible mingled with the fascination, deepening until it perverted the very fabric of my existence.

The first change was deceptively pleasant. Near the precipice of Avernus, I began to experience vague, strange sensations in my sleep. The prevailing feeling was a peculiar, thrilling cold, akin to moving against a swift river current. These were followed by interminable dreams so vague I could recall no scenery or faces, yet they left an awful impression, a sense of exhaustion as if I had endured days of mental torment and danger.

Upon waking, I remembered being in a place of near-darkness, speaking to invisible figures. Most haunting was a single, clear female voice—deep and resonant—that spoke from a distance with slow, solemn terror. Sometimes I felt a hand tracing my cheek and neck; other times, warm lips kissed me, lingering longer and more lovingly until they reached my throat. There, the caress fixed itself. My heart hammered, my breath rose in rapid gasps, and a sobbing convulsion seized me, turning into a dreadful struggle for air before my senses fled into unconsciousness.

Three weeks had passed since this unaccountable state began. My suffering had etched itself onto my face; I was pale, my eyes dark and dilated, the languor visible in every line of my countenance. My father asked repeatedly if I were ill, but with an obstinacy that now seems inexplicable, I assured him I was perfectly well. In a sense, I was right. I felt no physical pain, no derangement of the organs. My affliction was of the imagination, of the nerves. Yet the horrors I endured were so profound I kept them close, guarding them with a morbid reserve.

It could not be the *oupire*, the terrible complaint of the peasants, for victims of that plague rarely survived more than three days. Carmilla complained of dreams and feverish sensations, but hers were mild compared to the abyss I inhabited. Had I understood my condition, I would have begged for aid on my knees. An unsuspected narcotic influence numbed my perceptions, binding me to its will.

It was then that a dream led to a discovery that shattered my remaining sanity. One night, instead of the distant, solemn voice, I heard one sweet, tender, and utterly terrible.

"Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin."

Simultaneously, a light flared in the darkness. I saw Carmilla standing at the foot of my bed, clad in her white nightdress, bathed from chin to feet in a great, spreading stain of blood.

I woke with a shriek, convinced Carmilla was being murdered. Springing from my bed, my next memory was standing in the lobby, screaming for help. Madame and Mademoiselle burst out in alarm; the lobby lamp burned brightly, illuminating our panic.

Desperate, I insisted we knock on Carmilla's door. Our knocking went unanswered. It escalated to pounding and uproar, our cries of her name echoing uselessly. Fear gripped us as we realized the door was locked. In a panic, we retreated to my room, ringing the bell furiously. My father's room was too far away to reach him in time.

Servants soon arrived, and I, dressed in my gown and slippers, joined them. Renewing our summons at Carmilla's door in vain, I ordered the men to force the lock. They complied, and we stood in the doorway, lights raised, staring into the room.

"Carmilla!" we called. No reply.

We scanned the chamber. Everything was undisturbed, exactly as I had left it. But Carmilla was gone.

Search

The dust motes dancing in the shafts of light seemed to hold their breath as we stepped back into the room. It was undisturbed, save for the violent breach we had made moments before. The shock of our entry slowly receded, allowing reason to reclaim its footing. We dismissed the men, their faces grim with the weight of the unknown. Mademoiselle, her mind racing, proposed a chilling hypothesis: perhaps Carmilla had been roused by the clamor at her door. In a primal panic, she might have leaped from her bed, retreating into the deep shadows of a wardrobe or behind heavy drapes, hiding until the majordomo and his minions withdrew. We resumed our frantic search, calling her name with voices cracking under the strain.

But the silence that answered us was absolute. Our confusion curdled into dread. We checked the windows; they were bolted shut, iron bars cold against the glass. I pleaded with her spirit, if she were hiding, to cease this cruel game, to reveal herself and end our torment. It was futile. By now, I was certain she was not in the bedroom, nor in the adjoining dressing room, whose door remained locked from the inside. She could not have slipped through that barrier. A knot of terror tightened in my chest. Had Carmilla discovered one of the secret passages whispered about in the old housekeeper's tales, paths where the traditions of their location had long since faded into myth? Only time could unravel this, yet for now, we were paralyzed by the void.

The darkness stretched past four o'clock, a suffocating blanket. I chose to spend these agonizing hours in Madame's room, hoping daylight might offer a clue where the night had failed.

By morning, the entire household, led by my father, was a hive of agitated energy. Every corner of the chateau was turned over; the grounds were scoured. No trace of the missing lady existed. The stream was about to be dragged, a macabre ritual my father seemed resigned to, his mind fractured by the thought of the tale he would have to tell the poor girl's mother upon her return. I, too, teetered on the edge of madness, though my grief was a sharp, personal blade rather than a paternal despair.

The morning dissolved into alarm. Then, at one o'clock, the air shifted. I rushed to Carmilla's room and froze. She stood before her dressing table, alive and breathing. Astonishment stole my breath; I could scarcely believe my eyes. She beckoned me with a delicate finger, silent and commanding. Her face, however, wore an expression of profound, trembling fear.

I flew to her, an ecstasy of joy surging through me, kissing and embracing her as if to anchor her to the earth. I rang the bell violently, summoning others to share the relief and ease my father's crushing anxiety.

"Dear Carmilla, what has become of you?" I gasped, my voice breaking. "We have suffered agonies. Where were you? How did you return?"

"Last night was a night of wonders," she whispered.

"For mercy's sake, explain."

"It was past two," she said, her voice steady despite her fear. "I went to sleep as usual, my doors locked—the dressing room, the one opening onto the gallery. My sleep was uninterrupted, dreamless. Yet I awoke just now on the sofa in the dressing room. I found the door between the rooms ajar, and the outer door forced. How could this happen without waking me? It must have been accompanied by immense noise. I am particularly easily startled; even the slightest stir wakes me. And how could I have been carried from my bed without my consciousness being shattered?"

Soon, Madame, Mademoiselle, my father, and a throng of servants filled the room. Carmilla was besieged by inquiries, congratulations, and warm embraces, yet she appeared the least capable of explaining the miracle. She was overwhelmed, a vessel for questions she could not answer.

My father paced the room, his brow furrowed in deep thought. I caught Carmilla's eye following him, a sly, dark glint passing beneath her lashes. When the servants were finally dismissed, and Mademoiselle had fetched a bottle of valerian and salvolatile, the room settled into a tense quiet. Only my father, Madame, and I remained with Carmilla. He approached her thoughtfully, taking her hand with gentle kindness and guiding her to the sofa.

"Will you forgive me, my dear, if I risk a conjecture and ask a question?"

"Who can claim a better right?" she replied softly. "Ask what you please, and I will tell you everything. But my story is one of bewilderment and darkness. I know absolutely nothing. Put any question you like, but you know, of course, the limits Mother has placed upon my speech."

"Perfectly, my dear child. I need not tread upon the topics she desires our silence regarding. Now, the marvel of last night lies in your removal from your bed and room without waking, while the windows remained secured and the doors locked from within. I will offer my theory and ask a question."

Carmilla leaned on her hand, dejected. Madame and I listened, breath held tight in our throats.

"Now, my question is this: Have you ever been suspected of walking in your sleep?"

"Never, since I was very young indeed."

"But you did walk in your sleep when you were young?"

"Yes; I know I did. My old nurse has told me so often."

My father smiled, a weary nod acknowledging the truth.

"Well, here is what happened. You rose in your sleep, unlocked the door—not leaving the key in the lock as you usually do, but removing it and securing it from the outside. You took the key with you, carrying it to one of the twenty-five rooms on this floor, or perhaps upstairs or below. This old house is a labyrinth of rooms, closets, and heavy furniture, an accumulation of lumber that would take a week to search thoroughly. Do you see now?"

"I do, but not entirely," she answered, her voice hollow.

"And how, Papa, do you account for her finding herself on the sofa in the dressing room, which we had searched so carefully?"

"She came there after you had searched it, still in her sleep, and finally awoke spontaneously. She was as surprised to find herself there as any of us." He laughed, a sound of profound relief. "I wish all mysteries were as easily and innocently explained as yours, Carmilla. Thus, we may congratulate ourselves: the most natural explanation involves no drugging, no tampering with locks, no burglars, poisoners, or witches—nothing to alarm Carmilla or anyone else for our safety."

Carmilla looked radiant, her beauty enhanced by a graceful languor that seemed to defy the horror of the night. I thought my father was silently comparing her features to my own, for he sighed, "I wish my poor Laura looked more like herself."

So our alarms were happily ended, and Carmilla was restored to her friends, the mystery wrapped in the comforting, terrifying logic of sleepwalking.

IX. The Doctor

My father, refusing to allow Carmilla to remain unattended, stationed a servant outside her chamber door. This precaution was intended to intercept her should she attempt another nocturnal flight, ensuring she could not slip away unnoticed. That night passed in silence, undisturbed by her usual wanderings.

Early the following morning, before I had even risen, the doctor arrived. My father had summoned him in strict confidence, revealing nothing of his purpose to me. Madame escorted me to the library, where the physician awaited. He was a grave figure, distinguished by white hair and spectacles, a man I had noted previously.

I recounted my tale, and as the words left my lips, his expression darkened, growing heavier with each sentence. We stood in the deep recess of a window, facing one another. When I finished, he leaned back against the stone wall, his gaze fixed upon me with an intensity that bordered on horror. After a moment of silent reflection, he requested to see my father.

My father was summoned and entered with a smile, remarking, "I dare say, doctor, you are going to tell me that I am an old fool for having brought you here; I hope I am." But his smile vanished instantly as the doctor beckoned him forward, his face a mask of solemnity.

For some time, the two men conversed in the window recess where I had just spoken. They appeared engaged in a heated, earnest debate. From the far end of the vast room, Madame and I watched, burning with curiosity yet silenced by distance. The thick walls and deep alcove obscured the doctor entirely, leaving only my father's foot, arm, and shoulder visible. Their voices were hushed, rendered unintelligible by the acoustic isolation of the space.

Eventually, my father's face emerged into the light, pale and thoughtful, tinged with agitation. "Laura, dear, come here for a moment," he called softly. "Madame, we shall not trouble you; the doctor requires my presence."

I approached, feeling a sudden tremor of alarm. Though I felt weak, I did not feel ill; strength, one assumes, is a resource available on demand. My father extended his hand as I drew near, yet his eyes remained locked on the physician. "It certainly is very odd; I do not fully comprehend it," he murmured. "Laura, listen to Doctor Spielsberg now, and compose yourself."

"You mentioned a sensation akin to two needles piercing your skin, somewhere near your neck, during your first terrible dream," the doctor asked, his voice low. "Is there still any soreness?"

"None at all," I replied.

"Can you indicate with your finger the precise point where this occurred?"

"Very little below my throat—here," I answered, pointing to the spot covered by my morning dress.

"Now you can verify it yourself," the doctor said. "You will not mind your father lowering your dress slightly. It is necessary to detect the symptom underlying your condition."

I acquiesced. The fabric fell only an inch or two below my collar.

"God bless me!—so it is!" my father exclaimed, his complexion draining of color.

"You see it now with your own eyes," the doctor stated, his triumph grim and absolute.

"What is it?" I cried, fear seizing my heart.

"Nothing, my dear young lady, but a small blue spot, no larger than the tip of your little finger," he replied, turning to my father. "Now, the question is: what course of action is best?"

"Is there any danger?" I urged, trembling.

"I trust not, my dear," the doctor answered calmly. "I see no reason why you should not recover. Indeed, I see no reason why you should not begin to improve immediately. That is the point where the sensation of strangulation begins?"

"Yes," I whispered.

"And—recall as clearly as you can—that same point served as the center of that thrill you described, like the current of a cold stream rushing against you?"

"It may have been; I think it was."

"Ay, you see?" he added, addressing my father. "Shall I speak to Madame?"

"Certainly," my father agreed.

He summoned Madame and instructed her, "I find my young friend here in a precarious state. While I hope it will not prove serious, immediate steps must be taken, which I shall explain later. For now, Madame, you must ensure Miss Laura is never alone for a single moment. This is indispensable."

"We may rely upon your kindness, Madame, I know," my father added.

Madame responded with eager assurance. "And you, dear Laura, I know you will heed the doctor's instructions."

"The doctor has another patient whose symptoms slightly resemble those of my daughter," my father interjected smoothly. "They are milder in degree, but undoubtedly of the same nature. She is a young lady, our guest. Since you are passing this way again this evening, you cannot do better than take your supper here and examine her. She does not descend until the afternoon."

"I thank you," the doctor said. "I shall be with you at approximately seven this evening."

With these final directives issued to both of us, my father departed. I watched him walk out with the doctor, pacing together along the grassy platform between the road and the moat, deeply absorbed in their serious discourse. The doctor did not return. I saw him mount his horse, bid farewell, and ride eastward into the forest.

Almost simultaneously, a man arrived from Dranfield bearing letters. He dismounted and handed the bag to my father.

Meanwhile, Madame and I were lost in conjecture regarding the singular and urgent nature of the doctor's orders. As she later confessed to me, she feared the physician anticipated a sudden seizure, believing that without prompt assistance, I might lose my life in a fit or suffer grave injury. Such an interpretation did not occur to me. Perhaps, fortunately for my nerves, I assumed the arrangement was merely a precautionary measure to prevent me from overexerting myself, consuming unripe fruit, or indulging in the myriad foolish habits attributed to youth.

About half an hour later, my father returned, holding a letter. "This correspondence was delayed; it is from General Spielsdorf. He might have arrived yesterday, or he may not come until tomorrow, though he could be here today."

He placed the open letter in my hand, yet his countenance lacked the usual pleasure he reserved for the arrival of a beloved guest. On the contrary, he seemed to wish the General lost in the depths of the Red Sea. There was clearly something troubling his mind that he refused to disclose.

"Papa, darling, will you tell me this?" I asked suddenly, placing my hand on his arm and gazing imploringly into his face.

"Perhaps," he answered, smoothing my hair caressingly over my eyes. "Does the doctor think me very ill?"

"No, dear," he replied, his tone somewhat dry. "He believes that if proper measures are taken, you will be quite well again, or at least on the high road to complete recovery within a day or two. I wish our good friend, the General, had chosen another time; that is, I wish you had been perfectly well to receive him."

"But do tell me, papa," I insisted, "what does he think is the matter with me?"

"Nothing; you must not plague me with questions," he snapped, displaying more irritation than I had ever witnessed before. Seeing my wounded expression, he kissed me and added, "You shall know all about it in a day or two; that is, all that I know. In the meantime, do not trouble your head about it."

He turned to leave but returned before I could dwell further on the oddity of the situation. He announced his intention to travel to Karnstein, ordering the carriage ready at noon. He insisted that I and Madame accompany him to consult a priest residing near the picturesque grounds on business. Since Carmilla had never visited the site, she could follow when she descended, accompanied by Mademoiselle, who would prepare provisions for what you might call a picnic, to be laid out in the ruined castle.

At noon, I was prepared. Shortly thereafter, my father, Madame, and I set out on our projected drive. Crossing the drawbridge, we turned right, following the road over the steep Gothic bridge westward toward the deserted village and the ruined castle of Karnstein.

No sylvan drive could be imagined more picturesque. The terrain broke into gentle hills and hollows, all draped in beautiful woodland, utterly devoid of the formal rigidity imposed by artificial planting and cultivation. The irregularities of the land frequently diverted the road from its straight course, causing it to wind beautifully around the sides of broken hollows and the steeper slopes of the hills, traversing varieties of ground almost inexhaustible.

Turning one of these bends, we suddenly encountered our old friend, the General, riding toward us, attended by a mounted servant. His portmanteaus followed in a hired wagon. The General dismounted as we pulled up. After the customary greetings, he was easily persuaded to accept the vacant seat in the carriage, sending his horse and servant ahead to the castle.

Bereaved

Ten months had passed since we last beheld him, yet the interval felt like a lifetime, carving years into the man before us. He had wasted away; the cordial serenity that once defined his features had curdled into a deep, suffocating gloom. His dark blue eyes, usually sharp with intelligence, now burned with a sterner, colder light beneath his shaggy grey brows. This was no ordinary decay of spirit; the marks of angrier passions, darker than grief alone could leave, etched themselves upon his face.

We had not driven far when the General spoke, breaking the silence with the blunt, soldierly directness he favored. He launched into a tirade against the "hellish arts" to which his beloved niece and ward had fallen prey. With a bitterness that bordered on fury, he inveighed against Heaven's tolerance, wondering how such a monstrous indulgence of lust and malignity could be permitted to fester.

My father, sensing immediately that something extraordinary had transpired, asked him to detail the circumstances that warranted such vehement language.

"I would tell you all with pleasure," the General replied, "but you would not believe me."

"Why not?" my father pressed gently.

"Because," the General snapped, his voice tight with irritation, "you believe only in what fits your own prejudices and illusions. I remember when I was like you, but I have learned better."

"Try me," my father said calmly. "I am no dogmatist. Besides, I know you demand proof for your beliefs, and thus, I am predisposed to respect your conclusions."

"You are right," the General admitted, his gaze drifting gloomily over the opening glades of the forest. "I have not been led lightly into a belief in the marvelous—for what I have experienced *is* marvelous. Extraordinary evidence has forced me to credit what runs diametrically counter to all my theories. I have been made the dupe of a preternatural conspiracy."

I watched my father exchange a glance with the General, a look heavy with suspicion regarding the soldier's sanity. Fortunately, the General did not notice. He stared ahead, consumed by a grim curiosity.

"You are heading for the Ruins of Karnstein?" he asked suddenly. "A lucky coincidence. I was about to ask you to guide me there. I have a special purpose in exploring. There is a ruined chapel, isn't there? With many tombs of that extinct family?"

"So there are," my father said cheerfully. "Highly interesting. Are you thinking of claiming the title and estates?"

My father spoke with a lightness that betrayed no malice, but the General missed the courtesy of a friend's jest entirely. He did not smile; instead, his face grew grave and fierce, ruminating on thoughts that stirred both his horror and his rage.

"Something very different," he growled. "I intend to unearth some of those fine people. By God's blessing, I aim to accomplish a pious sacrilege here, relieving our earth of certain monsters so that honest folk may sleep without fear of assassins. I have strange tales to tell you, my dear friend, things I myself would have scouted as impossible just months ago."

My father looked at him again, but this time not with suspicion, but with keen intelligence and alarm.

"The house of Karnstein," my father corrected softly, "has been extinct for over a century. My late wife was maternally descended from them, but the name and title have long ceased to exist. The castle is a ruin; the village is deserted. It has been fifty years since a chimney smoked there; not a roof remains standing."

"Quite true," the General acknowledged. "I have heard much of that since I last saw you—much that will astonish you. But I had better relate everything in the order of its occurrence." He paused, his voice dropping. "You saw my dear ward—my child, I may call her. No creature could have been more beautiful, and only three months ago, none more blooming."

"Yes, poor thing!" my father exclaimed, his voice thick with emotion. "When I saw her last, she was indeed lovely. I was grieved and shocked beyond words, my dear friend; I knew the blow you suffered."

He took the General's hand, and they exchanged a firm, sympathetic pressure. Tears gathered in the old soldier's eyes, unashamed and raw.

"We have been old friends," the General whispered, his voice trembling. "I knew you would feel for me, childless as I am. She became an object of near interest to me, repaying my care with an affection that cheered my home and made my life happy. That is all gone. The years remaining to me on earth may be short; but by God's mercy, I hope to accomplish a service to mankind before I die, and to subserve the vengeance of Heaven upon the fiends who murdered my poor child in the spring of her hopes and beauty!"

"You said you intended to relate everything as it occurred," my father reminded him. "Pray do; I assure you, it is not mere curiosity that prompts me."

By this time, we had reached the fork where the Drunstall road, by which the General had arrived, diverged from our path toward Karnstein.

"How far is it to the ruins?" the General inquired, his eyes scanning the road ahead with anxious intensity.

"About half a league," my father answered. "Now, pray let us hear the story you promised."

The Story

"With all my heart," the General rasped, the words tearing from him with visible effort. He paused, gathering his scattered thoughts, before launching into the strangest narrative I have ever endured.

"My dear child," he began, his voice thick with emotion, "was looking forward with great pleasure to the visit you so kindly arranged for her to your charming daughter. In the interim, we received an invitation from my old friend, Count Carlsfeld, whose castle lies six leagues beyond Karnstein. It was to attend the series of fetes held in honor of his illustrious guest, the Grand Duke Charles."

"Yes; and very splendid, I believe, they were," my father murmured.

"Princely! Yet his hospitality is truly regal. He possesses Aladdin's lamp. The night from which my sorrow dates was consumed by a magnificent masquerade. The grounds were thrown open, the trees draped in colored lamps, and the sky erupted in fireworks unseen even in Paris. And the music... music is my weakness. Such ravishing sounds! The finest instrumental band in the world, singers gathered from every great opera house in Europe. As one wandered through these fantastically illuminated grounds, the moonlight casting a rosy glow from the chateau's long rows of windows, one would suddenly hear these enchanting voices stealing from the silence of a grove or rising from boats upon the lake. I felt myself, looking and listening, transported back into the romance and poetry of my early youth."

"When the fireworks faded and the ball began, we returned to the noble suites opened to the dancers. A masked ball is a beautiful sight, but I had never witnessed such a brilliant spectacle before.

"It was an assembly of pure aristocracy. I was myself almost the only 'nobody' present.

"My dear child looked quite beautiful. She wore no mask. Her excitement and delight added an unspeakable charm to her features, always lovely. I noticed a young lady dressed magnificently, yet wearing a mask, who appeared to be observing my ward with extraordinary interest. I had seen her earlier in the great hall, and again, for a few minutes, walking near us on the terrace beneath the castle windows, similarly employed. A lady, also masked, richly and gravely dressed with a stately air, like a person of high rank, accompanied her as a chaperon.

Had the young lady not worn a mask, I could, of course, have been much more certain regarding whether she was really watching my poor darling.

I am now well assured that she was.

"We were now in one of the salons. My poor dear child had been dancing and was resting in a chair near the door; I stood nearby. The two ladies I had mentioned approached, and the younger took the seat next to my ward, while her companion stood beside me, addressing herself in a low tone to her charge.

"Availing herself of the privilege of her mask, she turned to me, speaking in the tone of an old friend and calling me by my name, opening a conversation that piqued my curiosity greatly. She referred to many scenes where she had met me—at Court, at distinguished houses. She alluded to little incidents I had long ceased to think of, but which, I found, had only lain dormant in my memory, instantly springing to life at her touch.

"I became increasingly desperate to ascertain her identity. She parried my attempts to discover her very adroitly and pleasantly. The knowledge she displayed of many passages in my life seemed all but unaccountable; she appeared to take a not unnatural pleasure in foiling my curiosity, watching me flounder in eager perplexity, leaping from one conjecture to another.

"In the meantime, the young lady, whom her mother called by the odd name of Millarca when she addressed her, had, with the same ease and grace, engaged my ward in conversation.

"She introduced herself by saying her mother was a very old acquaintance of mine. She spoke of the agreeable audacity a mask rendered practicable; she talked like a friend, admired her dress, and insinuated very prettily her admiration of her beauty. She amused her with laughing criticisms upon the people crowding the ballroom and laughed at my poor child's fun. She was very witty and lively when she pleased, and after a time they had grown very good friends, and the young stranger lowered her mask, displaying a remarkably beautiful face. I had never seen it before, neither had my dear child. But though it was new to us, the features were so engaging, as well as lovely, that it was impossible not to feel the attraction powerfully. My poor girl did so. I never saw anyone more taken with another at first sight, unless, indeed, it was the stranger herself, who seemed quite to have lost her heart to her.

"In the meantime, availing myself of the license of a masquerade, I put not a few questions to the elder lady.

"'You have puzzled me utterly,' I said, laughing. 'Is that not enough? Won't you, now, consent to stand on equal terms, and do me the kindness to remove your mask?'

"'Can any request be more unreasonable?' she replied. 'Ask a lady to yield an advantage! Beside, how do you know you should recognize me? Years make changes.'

"'As you see,' I said, with a bow, and I suppose, a rather melancholy little laugh.

"'As philosophers tell us,' she said; 'and how do you know that a sight of my face would help you?'

"'I should take chance for that,' I answered. 'It is vain trying to make yourself out an old woman; your figure betrays you.'

"'Years, nevertheless, have passed since I saw you, rather since you saw me, for that is what I am considering. Millarca, there, is my daughter; I cannot then be young, even in the opinion of people whom time has taught to be indulgent, and I may not like to be compared with what you remember me. You have no mask to remove. You can offer me nothing in exchange.'

"'My petition is to your pity, to remove it.'

"'And mine to yours, to let it stay where it is,' she replied.

"'Well, then, at least you will tell me whether you are French or German; you speak both languages so perfectly.'

"'I don't think I shall tell you that, General; you intend a surprise, and are meditating the particular point of attack.'

"'At all events, you won't deny this,' I said, 'that being honored by your permission to converse, I ought to know how to address you. Shall I say Madame la Comtesse?'

"She laughed, and she would, no doubt, have met me with another evasion—if, indeed, I can treat any occurrence in an interview every circumstance of which was prearranged, as I now believe, with the profoundest cunning, as liable to be modified by accident.

"'As to that,' she began; but she was interrupted, almost as she opened her lips, by a gentleman dressed in black who looked particularly elegant and distinguished, with this drawback, that his face was the most deadly pale I ever saw, except in death. He was in no masquerade—in the plain evening dress of a gentleman; and he said, without a smile, but with a courtly and unusually low bow:—

"'Will Madame la Comtesse permit me to say a very few words which may interest her?'

"The lady turned quickly to him, touching her lip in token of silence; she then said to me, 'Keep my place for me, General; I shall return when I have said a few words.'

"And with this injunction, playfully given, she walked a little aside with the gentleman in black, talking for some minutes, apparently very earnestly. They then walked away slowly together into the crowd, and I lost them for some minutes.

"I spent the interval in cudgeling my brains for a conjecture as to the identity of the lady who seemed to remember me so kindly, thinking of turning about to join the conversation between my pretty ward and the Countess's daughter, trying whether, by the time she returned, I might not have a surprise in store for her, having her name, title, chateau, and estates at my fingers' ends. But at this moment she returned, accompanied by the pale man in black, who said:

"'I shall return and inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage is at the door.'

"He withdrew with a bow."

XII. A Petition

"Then we are to lose Madame la Comtesse, but I hope only for a few hours," I said, bowing low.

"It may be that only, or it may be a few weeks," she replied, her voice carrying a chilling weight. "It was very unlucky his speaking to me just now as he did. Do you now know me?"

"I assured her I did not."

"You shall know me," she said, "but not at present. We are older and better friends than, perhaps, you suspect. I cannot yet declare myself. In three weeks, I shall pass your beautiful *schloss*, about which I have been making enquiries. I shall then look in upon you for an hour or two, and renew a friendship which I never think of without a thousand pleasant recollections. This moment, a piece of news has reached me like a thunderbolt. I must set out now, and travel by a devious route, nearly a hundred miles, with all the dispatch I can possibly make. My perplexities multiply. I am only deterred by the compulsory reserve I practice as to my name from making a very singular request of you. My poor child has not quite recovered her strength. Her horse fell with her, at a hunt which she had ridden out to witness; her nerves have not yet recovered the shock, and our physician says that she must on no account exert herself for some time to come. We came here, in consequence, by very easy stages—hardly six leagues a day. I must now travel day and night, on a mission of life and death—a mission the critical and momentous nature of which I shall be able to explain to you when we meet, as I hope we shall, in a few weeks, without the necessity of any concealment."

She proceeded to make her petition, and it was in the tone of a person from whom such a request amounted to conferring a favor, rather than seeking one. This was only in manner, and, as it seemed, quite unconsciously. In the terms in which it was expressed, nothing could be more deprecatory. It was simply that I would consent to take charge of her daughter during her absence.

"This was, all things considered, a strange, not to say, an audacious request." She in some sort disarmed me, by stating and admitting everything that could be urged against it, and throwing herself entirely upon my chivalry. At the same moment, by a fatality that seems to have predetermined all that happened, my poor child came to my side, and, in an undertone, besought me to invite her new friend, Millarca, to pay us a visit. She had just been sounding her, and thought, if her mamma would allow her, she would like it extremely.

"At another time I should have told her to wait a little, until, at least, we knew who they were. But I had not a moment to think in. The two ladies assailed me together, and I must confess the refined and beautiful face of the young lady, about which there was something extremely engaging, as well as the elegance and fire of high birth, determined me; and, quite overpowered, I submitted, and undertook, too easily, the care of the young lady, whom her mother called Millarca."

"The Countess beckoned to her daughter, who listened with grave attention while she told her, in general terms, how suddenly and peremptorily she had been summoned, and also of the arrangement she had made for her under my care, adding that I was one of her earliest and most valued friends."

"I made, of course, such speeches as the case seemed to call for, and found myself, on reflection, in a position which I did not half like."

"The gentleman in black returned, and very ceremoniously conducted the lady from the room. The demeanor of this gentleman was such as to impress me with the conviction that the Countess was a lady of very much more importance than her modest title alone might have led me to assume."

"Her last charge to me was that no attempt was to be made to learn more about her than I might have already guessed, until her return. Our distinguished host, whose guest she was, knew her reasons."

"But here," she said, "neither I nor my daughter could safely remain for more than a day. I removed my mask imprudently for a moment, about an hour ago, and, too late, I fancied you saw me. So I resolved to seek an opportunity of talking a little to you. Had I found that you had seen me, I would have thrown myself on your high sense of honor to keep my secret some weeks. As it is, I am satisfied that you did not see me; but if you now suspect, or, on reflection, should suspect, who I am, I commit myself, in like manner, entirely to your honor. My daughter will observe the same secrecy, and I well know that you will, from time to time, remind her, lest she should thoughtlessly disclose it."

"She whispered a few words to her daughter, kissed her hurriedly twice, and went away, accompanied by the pale gentleman in black, and disappeared in the crowd."

"In the next room," said Millarca, "there is a window that looks upon the hall door. I should like to see the last of mamma, and to kiss my hand to her."

"We assented, of course, and accompanied her to the window. We looked out, and saw a handsome old-fashioned carriage, with a troop of couriers and footmen. We saw the slim figure of the pale gentleman in black, as he held a thick velvet cloak, and placed it about her shoulders and threw the hood over her head. She nodded to him, and just touched his hand with hers. He bowed low repeatedly as the door closed, and the carriage began to move."

"She is gone," said Millarca, with a sigh.

"She is gone," I repeated to myself, for the first time—in the hurried moments that had elapsed since my consent—reflecting upon the folly of my act.

"She did not look up," said the young lady, plaintively.

"The Countess had taken off her mask, perhaps, and did not care to show her face," I said; "and she could not know that you were in the window."

"She sighed, and looked in my face. She was so beautiful that I relented. I was sorry I had for a moment repented of my hospitality, and I determined to make her amends for the unavowed churlishness of my reception."

"The young lady, replacing her mask, joined my ward in persuading me to return to the grounds, where the concert was soon to be renewed. We did so, and walked up and down the terrace that lies under the castle windows."

"Millarca became very intimate with us, and amused us with lively descriptions and stories of most of the great people whom we saw upon the terrace. I liked her more and more every minute. Her gossip, without being ill-natured, was extremely diverting to me, who had been so long out of the great world. I thought what life she would give to our sometimes lonely evenings at home."

"This ball was not over until the morning sun had almost reached the horizon. It pleased the Grand Duke to dance till then, so loyal people could not go away, or think of bed."

"We had just got through a crowded saloon, when my ward asked me what had become of Millarca. I thought she had been by her side, and she fancied she was by mine. The fact was, we had lost her."

"All my efforts to find her were vain. I feared that she had mistaken, in the confusion of a momentary separation from us, other people for her new friends, and had, possibly, pursued and lost them in the extensive grounds which were thrown open to us."

"Now, in its full force, I recognized a new folly in my having undertaken the charge of a young lady without so much as knowing her name; and fettered as I was by promises, of the reasons for imposing which I knew nothing, I could not even point my inquiries by saying that the missing young lady was the daughter of the Countess who had taken her departure a few hours before."

"Morning broke. It was clear daylight before I gave up my search. It was not till near two o'clock next day that we heard anything of my missing charge."

"At about that time a servant knocked at my niece's door, to say that he had been earnestly requested by a young lady, who appeared to be in great distress, to make out where she could find the General Baron Spielsdorf and the young lady his daughter, in whose charge she had been left by her mother."

"There could be no doubt, notwithstanding the slight inaccuracy, that our young friend had turned up; and so she had. Would to heaven we had lost her!"

"She told my poor child a story to account for her having failed to recover us for so long. Very late, she said, she had got to the housekeeper's bedroom in despair of finding us, and had then fallen into a deep sleep which, long as it was, had hardly sufficed to recruit her strength after the fatigues of the ball."

"That day Millarca came home with us. I was only too happy, after all, to have secured so charming a companion for my dear girl."

XIII. The Woodman

But soon, the shadows lengthened into something far more sinister. Millarca fell into a state of profound lethargy, a lingering weakness that clung to her like a second skin since her recent illness. She rarely ventured from her chamber until the afternoon sun had begun to wane. Yet, a deeper unease gnawed at me: despite locking her door from within and never removing the key until the maid arrived to assist with her toilette, she was frequently missing. Witnesses reported seeing her in the pale, pre-dawn grey, wandering through the eastern groves in a trance-like stupor. It became clear she walked in her sleep, yet the mechanics of her disappearance defied logic. How did she slip past a locked door? How did she vanish from the castle without opening a single window?

My confusion was soon eclipsed by a terror far more acute. My beloved child was wasting away, her beauty and health fading in a manner that bordered on the horrific. First came nightmares of appalling intensity, followed by visions of a specter—sometimes resembling Millarca herself, other times a shapeless beast—that paced the foot of her bed. Then came physical sensations: the chilling flow of an icy stream against her breast, the sharp, needle-like pain piercing just below her throat, and finally, a convulsive strangulation that led to unconsciousness.

I listened, transfixed, as the kind old General recounted these exact symptoms. We drove along the short grass flanking the road, approaching the roofless village that had not seen a chimney smoke in half a century. Hearing my own afflictions mirrored so precisely in the fate of the girl who would have been my guest at my father's chateau sent a chill through my marrow. To hear him describe the habits of Carmilla, my beautiful and doomed visitor, was to feel the trap closing around my own neck.

Suddenly, the forest parted, revealing the chimneys and gables of the ruined settlement and the dismantled castle towers looming above us, shrouded by giant trees. In a frightened dream, I alighted from the carriage. Silence hung heavy between us, filled only with the weight of our shared dread. We climbed the ascent, stepping into the spacious chambers, winding staircases, and dark corridors of the castle.

"This was once the palatial residence of the Karnsteins!" the old General declared, gazing from a high window across the vast, undulating forest. "A bloodstained family, whose annals were written in this very soil. It is a cruelty that their atrocious lusts should plague the living even after death." He pointed downward to the grey walls of a Gothic chapel visible through the foliage. "That is their chapel. I hear the axe of a woodman busy among the surrounding trees. Perhaps he holds the information I seek, or can guide us to the grave of Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein. These rustics preserve the traditions of great houses long forgotten by the titled elite."

"We possess a portrait of Mircalla at home," my father offered. "Would you care to see it?"

"Time enough, dear friend," the General replied. "I believe I have seen the original. One motive for my hasty arrival was to explore this chapel."

"She has been dead for over a century!" my father exclaimed.

"Not so dead as you imagine," the General answered cryptically.

"You puzzle me utterly," my father admitted, his eyes narrowing with a flicker of suspicion. Yet, beneath the old man's anger and detestation, there was no madness, only a grim resolve.

"There remains but one purpose for my remaining years on earth," the General said as we passed under the heavy arch of the Gothic church. "To wreak upon her the vengeance which, thank God, a mortal arm can still accomplish."

"What vengeance?" my father asked, bewildered.

"To decapitate the monster," the General answered, his face flushing with fierce rage. He stamped his foot, the sound echoing mournfully through the hollow ruin, and raised a clenched hand as if gripping an axe handle, shaking it ferociously in the air. "To strike her head off!"

"With a hatchet! With a spade! With anything that can cleave through her murderous throat!" he shouted, trembling with fury. "You shall hear."

Hurrying forward, he gestured to a squared block of wood lying on the grass-grown pavement. "Sit, your child is fatigued. Let me close this dreadful tale in a few sentences."

I gratefully claimed the wooden block as a seat. Meanwhile, the General called to a woodman who had been clearing branches from the ancient walls. The hardy old man, axe in hand, stepped forward. He knew nothing of the monuments, but spoke of an elderly ranger staying with the priest two miles away, who could identify every grave of the Karnstein line. For a small fee, he promised to fetch the ranger within half an hour if we lent him a horse.

"How long have you served this forest?" my father asked.

"I have been a woodman here," the man replied in his rough patois, "under the forester all my days, as did my father before me, and so on for generations I can count. I could show you the very house in the village where my ancestors lived."

"Why was the village deserted?" the General inquired.

"It was plagued by revenants," the woodman explained. "Several were tracked to their graves, identified by the usual tests, and extinguished by decapitation, staking, and burning. Yet many villagers perished in the process. Even after these lawful proceedings, after so many graves were opened and vampires deprived of their animation, the village remained tormented."

He continued, his voice dropping. "Then a Moravian nobleman, traveling this way, heard of our plight. Skilled in such affairs, he offered to deliver us from our tormentor. On a night of bright moon, shortly after sunset, he ascended the chapel towers. From there, he watched the churchyard below. He saw the vampire emerge from his grave, placing the linen clothes in which he had been folded nearby, before gliding toward the village to plague its inhabitants.

"The stranger descended, took the vampire's linens, and carried them back to the tower's summit. When the vampire returned, furious at the loss of his clothes, he cried out to the Moravian, who beckoned him to climb. Accepting the invitation, the vampire began to scale the steeple. As soon as he reached the battlements, the Moravian struck his skull in twain with his sword, hurling him down into the churchyard. The stranger followed, descending the winding stairs, and severed the creature's head once more. Next day, he delivered the head and body to the villagers, who impaled and burned them."

"The Moravian had authority from the head of the family to remove the tomb of Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein," the woodman concluded. "He did so effectually, and soon the site was forgotten."

"Can you point out where it stood?" the General demanded eagerly.

The forester shook his head, a sad smile playing on his lips. "Not a soul living could tell you that now. Besides, they say her body was removed, though no one is certain."

With that, he dropped his axe and departed, leaving us alone to listen to the remainder of the General's strange and terrifying story.

XIV. The Meeting

"My beloved child," he resumed, his voice trembling with a mixture of grief and exhaustion, "was now growing rapidly worse. The physician who attended her had failed to produce the slightest impression on her disease, for such I then supposed it to be. He saw my alarm, and suggested a consultation. I called in an abler physician, from Gratz.

Several days elapsed before he arrived. He was a good and pious, as well as a learned man. Having seen my poor ward together, they withdrew to my library to confer and discuss. I, from the adjoining room, where I awaited their summons, heard these two gentlemen's voices raised in something sharper than a strictly philosophical discussion. I knocked at the door and entered. I found the old physician from Gratz maintaining his theory. His rival was combating it with undisguised ridicule, accompanied with bursts of laughter. This unseemly manifestation subsided and the altercation ended on my entrance.

"Sir," said my first physician, "my learned brother seems to think that you want a conjuror, and not a doctor."

"Pardon me," said the old physician from Gratz, looking displeased, "I shall state my own view of the case in my own way another time. I grieve, Monsieur le General, that by my skill and science I can be of no use. Before I go I shall do myself the honor to suggest something to you."

He seemed thoughtful, and sat down at a table and began to write. Profoundly disappointed, I made my bow, and as I turned to go, the other doctor pointed over his shoulder to his companion who was writing, and then, with a shrug, significantly touched his forehead.

"This consultation, then, left me precisely where I was. I walked out into the grounds, all but distracted. The doctor from Gratz, in ten or fifteen minutes, overtook me. He apologized for having followed me, but said that he could not conscientiously take his leave without a few words more. He told me that he could not be mistaken; no natural disease exhibited the same symptoms; and that death was already very near. There remained, however, a day, or possibly two, of life. If the fatal seizure were at once arrested, with great care and skill her strength might possibly return. But all hung now upon the confines of the irrevocable. One more assault might extinguish the last spark of vitality which is, every moment, ready to die.

"And what is the nature of the seizure you speak of?" I entreated.

"I have stated all fully in this note, which I place in your hands upon the distinct condition that you send for the nearest clergyman, and open my letter in his presence, and on no account read it till he is with you; you would despise it else, and it is a matter of life and death. Should the priest fail you, then, indeed, you may read it."

He asked me, before taking his leave finally, whether I would wish to see a man curiously learned upon the very subject, which, after I had read his letter, would probably interest me above all others, and he urged me earnestly to invite him to visit him there; and so took his leave.

The ecclesiastic was absent, and I read the letter by myself. At another time, or in another case, it might have excited my ridicule. But into what quackeries will not people rush for a last chance, where all accustomed means have failed, and the life of a beloved object is at stake?

Nothing, you will say, could be more absurd than the learned man's letter. It was monstrous enough to have consigned him to a madhouse. He said that the patient was suffering from the visits of a vampire! The punctures which she described as having occurred near the throat, were, he insisted, the insertion of those two long, thin, and sharp teeth which, it is well known, are peculiar to vampires; and there could be no doubt, he added, as to the well-defined presence of the small livid mark which all concurred in describing as that induced by the demon's lips, and every symptom described by the sufferer was in exact conformity with those recorded in every case of a similar visitation.

Being myself wholly skeptical as to the existence of any such portent as the vampire, the supernatural theory of the good doctor furnished, in my opinion, but another instance of learning and intelligence oddly associated with some one hallucination. I was so miserable, however, that, rather than try nothing, I acted upon the instructions of the letter.

I concealed myself in the dark dressing room, that opened upon the poor patient's room, in which a candle was burning, and watched there till she was fast asleep. I stood at the door, peeping through the small crevice, my sword laid on the table beside me, as my directions prescribed, until, a little after one, I saw a large black object, very ill-defined, crawl, as it seemed to me, over the foot of the bed, and swiftly spread itself up to the poor girl's throat, where it swelled, in a moment, into a great, palpitating mass.

For a few moments I had stood petrified. I now sprang forward, with my sword in my hand. The black creature suddenly contracted towards the foot of the bed, glided over it, and, standing on the floor about a yard below the foot of the bed, with a glare of skulking ferocity and horror fixed on me, I saw Millarca. Speculating I know not what, I struck at her instantly with my sword; but I saw her standing near the door, unscathed. Horrified, I pursued, and struck again. She was gone; and my sword flew to shivers against the door.

I can't describe to you all that passed on that horrible night. The whole house was up and stirring. The specter Millarca was gone. But her victim was sinking fast, and before the morning dawned, she died."

The old General was agitated. We did not speak to him. My father walked to some little distance, and began reading the inscriptions on the tombstones; and thus occupied, he strolled into the door of a side chapel to prosecute his researches. The General leaned against the wall, dried his eyes, and sighed heavily. I was relieved on hearing the voices of Carmilla and Madame, who were at that moment approaching. The voices died away.

In this solitude, having just listened to so strange a story, connected, as it was, with the great and titled dead, whose monuments were moldering among the dust and ivy round us, and every incident of which bore so awfully upon my own mysterious case—in this haunted spot, darkened by the towering foliage that rose on every side, dense and high above its noiseless walls—a horror began to steal over me, and my heart sank as I thought that my friends were, after all, not about to enter and disturb this triste and ominous scene.

The old General's eyes were fixed on the ground, as he leaned with his hand upon the basement of a shattered monument. Under a narrow, arched doorway, surmounted by one of those demoniacal grotesques in which the cynical and ghastly fancy of old Gothic carving delights, I saw very gladly the beautiful face and figure of Carmilla enter the shadowy chapel.

I was just about to rise and speak, and nodded smiling, in answer to her peculiarly engaging smile; when with a cry, the old man by my side caught up the woodman's hatchet, and started forward. On seeing him a brutalized change came over her features. It was an instantaneous and horrible transformation, as she made a crouching step backwards. Before I could utter a scream, he struck at her with all his force, but she dived under his blow, and unscathed, caught him in her tiny grasp by the wrist. He struggled for a moment to release his arm, but his hand opened, the axe fell to the ground, and the girl was gone.

He staggered against the wall. His grey hair stood upon his head, and a moisture shone over his face, as if he were at the point of death.

The frightful scene had passed in a moment. The first thing I recollect after, is Madame standing before me, and impatiently repeating again and again, the question, "Where is Mademoiselle Carmilla?"

I answered at length, "I don't know—I can't tell—she went there," and I pointed to the door through which Madame had just entered; "only a minute or two since."

"But I have been standing there, in the passage, ever since Mademoiselle Carmilla entered; and she did not return."

She then began to call "Carmilla," through every door and passage and from the windows, but no answer came.

"She called herself Carmilla?" asked the General, still agitated.

"Carmilla, yes," I answered.

"Aye," he said; "that is Millarca. That is the same person who long ago was called Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Depart from this accursed ground, my poor child, as quickly as you can. Drive to the clergyman's house, and stay there till we come. Begone! May you never behold Carmilla more; you will not find her here."

XV. Ordeal and Execution

The air in the chapel grew heavy, thick with the scent of damp stone and ancient dread, when a figure emerged from the shadows of the very archway through which Carmilla had vanished. He was a specter of a man, tall yet hunched, his chest narrow and shoulders hiked as if bearing an invisible yoke. Clad in somber black, his skin was the color of cured leather, etched with deep, rain-worn furrows. Upon his head sat a grotesque hat crowned with a broad, withered leaf, while long, grizzled hair cascaded down his back like dead moss. Gold-rimmed spectacles perched on his nose, magnifying eyes that seemed to dart between the heavens and the earth in a frantic, perpetual dance. A smile stretched across his face, fixed and unnatural, while his long, thin arms swung with a shambling rhythm. His hands, encased in oversized black gloves, waved in a silent, abstract pantomime, disconnected from the reality around him.

"The very man!" roared the General, his voice cutting through the silence with a thrill of triumph. "My dear Baron, what joy to find you! I dared not hope to meet you so swiftly." He gestured to my father, who had just returned, and ushered the eccentric stranger—the Baron—forward. Formal introductions were exchanged, but the pleasantries dissolved instantly into urgent discourse. The Baron produced a rolled parchment from his coat, unspooling it across the weathered surface of a nearby tomb. With a pencil case in hand, he sketched frantic lines, connecting points on the paper that mirrored the architecture of the chapel itself. Occasionally, he would dip into a tattered, yellowed tome, reading aloud passages that seemed to summon the ghosts of the past.

Together, they drifted down the side aisle, opposite my own frozen position, their voices low and conspiratorial. They began to measure distances by pacing, stopping abruptly before a section of the sidewall. There, they scrutinized the masonry with microscopic intensity, stripping away clinging ivy and tapping the plaster with the tips of their sticks. *Scrape. Knock. Tap.* Finally, they revealed a broad marble tablet, its letters carved in high relief, waiting to be read.

With the help of a woodman who had been summoned, the stone was cleared. Beneath the debris lay the inscription and the carved escutcheon of the long-lost monument of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein.

Though the General was no man of prayer, he raised his hands and gaze to the vaulted ceiling, offering a silent, fervent thanksgiving that lasted several moments.

"Tomorrow," he whispered, his voice trembling with anticipation, "the commissioner shall arrive. The Inquisition will proceed according to the law."

Turning to the Baron with gold-rimmed spectacles, the General seized his hands in a warm, shaking grip. "Baron, how can we repay you? How can this entire region thank you? You have delivered us from a plague that has festered for over a century. Thank God, the horrid enemy is finally tracked."

My father led the two men aside, and the General followed close behind. I knew they had withdrawn to discuss my case in private; I saw them glance at me frequently, their expressions shifting rapidly as they conferred.

When my father returned, he embraced me, kissing me repeatedly before leading me from the chapel. "It is time to return," he said, his voice tight with emotion. "But before we leave, we must summon the good priest, who resides nearby. We must persuade him to accompany us to the castle."

Our quest was successful. Exhaustion claimed me as we reached home, a fatigue so profound it felt like a physical weight. Yet, my relief curdled into sheer terror upon discovering the silence regarding Carmilla. No explanation was offered for the chaotic scene in the ruined chapel; my father had decided to keep this secret from me for now.

Her sinister absence made the memory of the chapel all the more horrifying. The preparations for the night were bizarre and chilling. Two servants and Madame were to remain awake in my room, while the priest and my father kept vigil in the adjoining dressing room. That night, the priest performed solemn rites, their meaning lost on me, just as I could not comprehend the necessity of such extreme precautions for my safety.

It was only a few days later that the truth became clear to me.

With Carmilla gone, my nightly torments ceased.

You have likely heard of the appalling superstitions that plague the lands of Upper and Lower Styria, Moravia, Silesia, Turkish Serbia, Poland, and even Russia. The belief in the Vampire. If human testimony, gathered with judicial solemnity before countless commissions of intelligent and upright men, holds any weight, then the existence of such a phenomenon cannot be denied. For my part, having witnessed and experienced what I did, I possess no other theory to explain my suffering than the ancient, well-attested beliefs of my countrymen.

The following day, the formal proceedings commenced within the Chapel of Karnstein.

The grave of the Countess Mircalla was breached. The General and my father stared into the darkness of the coffin, recognizing their perfidious and beautiful guest in the face now exposed to the light. Though one hundred and fifty years had passed since her burial, the features were flushed with the warmth of life. Her eyes were wide open; no cadaverous stench emanated from the box. Two medical men—one official, one representing the prosecutor—attested to the marvel: a faint but distinct respiration, a beating heart. Her limbs were supple, her flesh elastic. The leaden coffin floated in a pool of blood, her body submerged to a depth of seven inches.

Here were all the undeniable signs of vampirism. In accordance with ancient custom, the body was raised, and a sharp stake was driven through the vampire's heart. She let out a piercing shriek, a sound of agony indistinguishable from that of a living soul in its final moments. Then, her head was severed. A torrent of blood gushed from the wound. The body and head were cast onto a pyre of wood, consumed by flames until nothing remained but ash. These ashes were scattered into the river, carried away by the current, and that territory has never since known the visitation of a vampire.

My father possesses a copy of the report from the Imperial Commission, signed by every witness present, verifying this account. It is from this official document that I summarize this final, shocking scene.

XVI. Conclusion

I write these words with a facade of composure, though the truth is, my hands tremble as I hold the pen. There is no calm in this recounting; only your repeated, earnest pleas could have dragged me from my sanctuary to undertake a task that has left my nerves frayed for months. It forces me to revisit the unspeakable horror that, even years after my deliverance, casts a shadow over my days and makes solitude a terror I cannot endure.

Before I close this account, I must speak of the quaint Baron Vordenburg. It was his curious, obsessive lore that led us to the grave of Countess Mircalla. He had retreated to Graz, surviving on a meager pittance from the shattered princely estates of his family in Upper Styria, dedicating his life to the minute investigation of vampirism. He knew every great and obscure work on the subject: *Magia Posthuma*, *Phlegon de Mirabilibus*, *Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis*, and John Christofer Herenberg's *Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris*. From a voluminous digest of judicial cases, he had extracted a system of principles governing the vampire's condition.

He taught me that the deadly pallor attributed to these revenants is a melodramatic fiction. In the grave, or when they emerge into human society, they possess the appearance of healthy life. Only when exposed to light within their coffins do they reveal the true signs of their undead existence. How they escape their tombs and return within specific hours, leaving not a single trace of disturbance in the clay or the shrouds, remains inexplicable. Their amphibious life is sustained by daily slumber in the earth, fueled by a terrible hunger for living blood that grants them vigor while awake.

They are prone to a fascination resembling the passion of love, driven by an engrossing vehemence toward specific individuals. To reach such a victim, they exercise inexhaustible patience and stratagem, overcoming a hundred obstacles. They will never cease until they have satiated their passion, draining the very life from their chosen prey. Yet, in these cases, they refine their murder with the taste of an epicure, heightening the torment through the gradual approaches of artful courtship, seemingly yearning for a semblance of sympathy or consent. In ordinary instances, however, they strike directly, overpowering with violence and exhausting the victim at a single feast.

The vampire is also subject to peculiar conditions. As you know, Mircalla was limited to names that were anagrams of her true identity—Carmilla, Millarca.

My father recounted to the Baron the story of the Moravian nobleman and the vampire at the Karnstein churchyard. When asked how the Baron had located the concealed tomb, his grotesque features puckered into a mysterious smile. He looked down at his worn spectacles, fumbling with the case, before speaking.

"I possess many journals," he said, "written by that remarkable man. The most curious treats of the visit you speak of. Tradition distorts the tale slightly. He might have been called a Moravian nobleman, having moved there, but he was truly a native of Upper Styria. In his youth, he was a passionate, favored lover of the beautiful Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Her early death plunged him into inconsolable grief. Vampires multiply according to a ghostly law: a person who ends their own life, or is haunted by such demons, may become a vampire. This happened to Mircalla. My ancestor, Vordenburg, soon discerned this. He feared that suspicion would eventually fall upon her remains, leading to a posthumous execution that would profane her. He believed that expelling a vampire from its amphibious existence projected it into a far more horrible life. Driven by a horror for her desecration, he devised a stratagem: a pretended removal of her remains and the real obliteration of her monument.

"When age stole upon him, looking back from the vale of years, he filled with remorse. He traced the deception he had practiced, making notes that would guide a descendant to the spot. Death prevented him from acting further, but my hand, too late for many, has followed his clues to the beast's lair."

We spoke further, and he revealed another sign: the power of the hand. The slender fingers of Mircalla had closed like a vice of steel upon the General's wrist when he raised the hatchet. That grasp left a numbness in the limb, a paralysis that is rarely, if ever, fully recovered.

That spring, my father took me on a tour through Italy. We remained away for more than a year. Long before the terror of recent events subsided, the image of Carmilla returned to my memory with ambiguous alternations. Sometimes she was the playful, languid, beautiful girl; sometimes the writhing fiend I saw in the ruined church. Often, waking from a reverie, I start in fear, fancying I hear the light step of Carmilla pausing just outside the drawing-room door.
