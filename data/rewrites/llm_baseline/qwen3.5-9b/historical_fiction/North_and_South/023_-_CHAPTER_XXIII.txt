Margaret had scarcely been gone five minutes when Mr. Thornton burst into the dining-room, his face flushed with a heat that seemed to glow from within. "I couldn't come sooner," he panted, his eyes darting around the room. "The superintendent would have delayed me. Where is she?"

He scanned the empty chairs, then turned his gaze almost fiercely upon his mother, who was calmly rearranging the displaced furniture. She offered no immediate answer.

"Where is Miss Hale?" he demanded again.

"Gone home," Mrs. Thornton replied, her tone somewhat curt.

"Gone home!" he repeated, pacing restlessly. "Yes, she was much better. I don't believe it was anything serious; some people simply faint at the slightest shock."

"I'm sorry she's gone," he muttered, unable to settle. "She wasn't fit to travel."

"She insisted she was," his mother countered. "Mr. Lowe confirmed it. I fetched him myself."

"Thank you, Mother." He paused, extending his hand in a gesture of gratitude. She ignored it entirely.

"What have you done with the Irishmen?" she asked abruptly.

"Sent them to the Dragon for a meal, poor wretches. Fortunately, I managed to catch Father Grady and persuaded him to stay and talk to them, preventing a mass departure. But how did Miss Hale manage to get home? I'm certain she couldn't have walked."

"She had a cab. Everything was handled properly, right down to the fare. Let's discuss something else; she's already caused enough commotion."

"I wouldn't have known where to turn but for her," Mr. Thornton insisted.

"Have you become so helpless that you require a girl to defend you?" Mrs. Thornton retorted with disdain.

He blushed deeply. "Few girls would have taken the blows intended for me—with such genuine goodwill, too."

"A girl in love will do wonders," she replied dryly.

"Mother!" he cried, stepping forward, then halting as he struggled to contain a surge of passion. The intensity of his emotion startled her. She sensed the storm brewing within him but could not decipher its nature. Was it rage? His eyes burned, his frame expanded, and his breathing grew ragged. It was a chaotic blend of joy, fury, pride, relief, and desperate uncertainty. Yet, she remained baffled. The mere presence of such raw, unshared feeling unsettled her profoundly.

Turning her back to him, she moved to the sideboard, retrieved a duster from a drawer, and pretended to polish a spot of eau de Cologne on the sofa arm. She lingered longer than necessary, keeping her back turned. When she finally spoke, her voice sounded strained and artificial.

"You've taken steps regarding the rioters, I assume? Do you anticipate further violence? Where were the police? Always absent when needed!"

"On the contrary," he answered, "I saw several of them struggling ineffectually near the gate, and more arrived just as the yard cleared. I could have arrested some of them then, had I been quicker-witted. But there will be no trouble; plenty of witnesses can identify them."

"But won't they return tonight?"

"I intend to arrange a sufficient guard. I'll meet Captain Hanbury at the station in half an hour."

"You ought to have some tea first."

"Tea! Yes, I suppose so. It's half-past six, and I may be gone for a while. Don't wait up for me, Mother."

"Do you expect me to retire before ensuring your safety?"

"Well, perhaps not." He hesitated. "But if I have time, I'll stop by Crampton after settling matters with the police and speaking to Hamper and Clarkson."

Their eyes locked, staring intently at one another for a full minute.

"Why are you going via Crampton?" she asked softly.

"To inquire after Miss Hale."

"I will send someone. Williams can deliver the water-bed she requested and check on her condition."

"I must go myself."

"Not merely to ask how she is?"

"No, not just that. I wish to thank her for standing between me and the mob."

"What brought you down there at all? You were throwing your head into the lion's mouth!"

He glanced sharply at her, noting her ignorance of the private exchange he'd shared with Margaret in the drawing-room, and deflected with another query: "Will you be frightened to remain alone until I summon the police? Or should we send Williams now so they arrive before we finish tea? Time is critical. I must depart in fifteen minutes."

Mrs. Thornton withdrew from the room. Her servants noted the confusion in her usual sharp commands; she appeared uncertain and distracted. Mr. Thornton remained in the dining-room, ostensibly planning his visit to the police office, though his thoughts were entirely consumed by Margaret. The memory of her arms around his neck, the soft clinging that sent waves of color through his cheeks, seemed to blur everything else into vagueness.

The tea service was stiflingly quiet, broken only by Fanny's incessant narration of her own terror—how she had been alarmed, then relieved, then seized by sickness and trembling limbs.

"There, that's enough," Mr. Thornton interrupted, rising. "The reality was sufficient for me."

He prepared to leave when his mother placed a hand on his arm.

"You will return here before going to the Hales'," she whispered anxiously.

Fanny murmured to herself, "I know what I know. Why? Will it be too late to disturb them?"

"John, come back to me for this one evening. It will be late for Mrs. Hale. But that isn't why. Tomorrow, you will... Come back tonight, John!"

She rarely pleaded with her son; her pride forbade it. Yet, she had never failed.

"I will return immediately after finishing my business. You will surely inquire after them? After her?"

Mrs. Thornton was neither a conversationalist nor a patient listener while her son was away. However, upon his return, her attention sharpened keenly to every detail of his precautions against future outrages. He saw his objective clearly: punishment and suffering were the natural consequences for the rioters. All that was required to protect property and assert the proprietor's will was a clean, sharp execution of justice.

"Mother!" he exclaimed suddenly, breaking a silence in which she had momentarily forgotten Margaret. "You know what I have to say to Miss Hale tomorrow?"

"Yes!" she replied, looking up. "You can hardly avoid it."

"Do otherwise? I don't understand."

"I mean that, having allowed her feelings to overwhelm her, you are bound by honor—"

"Honor?" he scoffed. "I'm afraid honor has nothing to do with it. 'Her feelings overwhelmed her!' What feelings do you mean?"

"Nay, John, no need to be angry. Didn't she rush down and cling to you to save you from danger?"

"She did!" he admitted.

"But, Mother," he continued, stopping abruptly before her. "I dare not hope. I have never been faint-hearted before, yet I cannot believe such a creature cares for me."

"Don't be foolish, John. 'Such a creature'! Why, she might be a duchess's daughter in your estimation. What greater proof could you desire of her affection? I suspect she has struggled against her aristocratic upbringing, but I admire her for seeing clearly at last. It is a significant concession on her part," Mrs. Thornton said, smiling slowly as tears welled in her eyes. "For after tonight, I stand second. It was to have you to myself, exclusively, for a few more hours, that I begged you not to leave until tomorrow."

"Dearest Mother!"

Yet, love remains inherently selfish. In an instant, he retreated into his own hopes and fears, casting a cold shadow across his mother's heart.

"But I know she doesn't care for me. I shall place myself at her feet—I must. If it is but one chance in a thousand, or a million, I will seize it."

"Don't fear!" his mother urged, suppressing her own stinging mortification at his lack of acknowledgment of her fleeting maternal joy, and the pang of jealousy that revealed the depth of her neglected love. "Don't be afraid," she said coolly. "As far as love goes, she may be worthy of you. It must have taken considerable strength to overcome her pride. Don't worry, John," she added, kissing him as she bid him goodnight.

Then she departed slowly and majestically. Once inside her own room, however, she locked the door and sat down to shed tears she had rarely allowed herself to shed.

Margaret entered the sitting room, where her parents were engaged in hushed conversation. She looked exceedingly pale and wan. Approaching them cautiously, she found her voice difficult to summon.

"Mrs. Thornton will send the water-bed, Mama."

"My dear, how exhausted you look! Is it terribly hot?"

"Very hot, and the streets are rough due to the strike."

Margaret's complexion flared brightly, only to drain away instantly.

"There has been a message from Bessy Higgins asking you to visit her," Mrs. Hale said gently. "But I'm sure you look too tired."

"Yes," Margaret whispered. "I am tired. I cannot go."
