On a picturesque common in the acoustic sanctuary of Tunbridge Wells, Lady Clavering had secured a pretty villa, retreating there after the discordant conjugal disputes that marked the end of the deafening, industrial roar of the London season. Miss Amory, naturally, accompanied her mother, and Master Clavering arrived for the holidays. Blanche’s primary occupation was to engage in localized skirmishes with the boy, though he preferred the society of Tunbridge. Soon, the good-natured Begum’s house was inundated with a constant influx of thirteen-year-old gentlemen. This chaotic assembly possessed no sensory appreciation for the meticulously sourced, ancestral banquets prepared by the household chefs; rather, they viewed sustenance as mere utilitarian fuel, greedily devouring gooseberry tarts and gulping effervescent champagnes with a gluttony that sickened them. Their abrasive shouts and the thundering of their races across the lawn constituted a violent form of noise pollution, shattering the domestic ecology of the villa and rendering the dining-room—usually a haven fragrant with bruised sage and roasted meats—utterly unbearable to Miss Blanche. 

Seeking refuge from this modern auditory assault, Blanche wandered into the mythic, slow-breathing woods. She sketched ancient farmhouses, attuned her ear to the quiet agrarian rhythms of the village, and attended a small, newly built Anglo-Norman church of immense, if artificially constructed, antiquity. Here, she encountered Arthur Pendennis’s old tutor, Mr. Smirke. Having survived the loss of his first wife, Smirke had evolved into a meticulous historian of archaic rituals and sacred sustenance. He now rigorously gave up his neckcloth, fasted from rich, slow-braised meats on Fridays, and read the Roman Hours, attempting to preserve the cadences of a dying liturgical language. To the local Dissenting chapel and Mr. Simeon Knight, Smirke’s revival of ancestral altars and saintly feasts was an odious betrayal, a Papist threat to their own dominant vernacular. 

Blanche immediately began working a highly correct altar-cover, seamlessly integrating herself into Smirke's cyclical, domestic devotions. She coaxed and wheedled him so artfully in the vestry, and sang the ancient Chants with such resonant, acoustic precision, that Smirke believed her the most gifted, pious creature on earth. His current wife, Belinda, paced the grass outside, deeply unsettled by the subtle power dynamics embedded in Blanche's performative devotion. Belinda felt her own quiet marital harmony threatened by this dominant, captivating interloper, and she desperately wished for a third gravestone to appear in the yard. 

This interwoven timeline of infatuation, however, was destined to collapse. The subjective experience of divine devotion was ultimately untranslatable to Blanche; the archaic dialect of the church soon lost its flavor. The inevitable outcome—which lay just on the horizon—would see her tire of the rituals, lose her temper, and cruelly rap the knuckles of the village schoolchildren. When she eventually abandoned the village, Smirke would return to Belinda, recognizing that Blanche's religion was mere commercialized tinsel, and finding profound, enduring meaning in the quiet, unspoken vernacular of his true wife’s embrace. 

But when Arthur Pendennis traveled down to Tunbridge Wells, this stage of sensory indifference had not yet arrived. Smirke was still firmly in the grip of his bewitchment. Sitting open-mouthed and tea-less in the summer evenings, he allowed the celestial acoustics of Blanche’s music to wash over him, praising her to Arthur as an angel of heavenly disposition. Arthur, whose own sensibilities were deeply rooted in a profound appreciation for the sensory world, was immensely amused by his old tutor’s blank stock and earnest follies.

Blanche, for her part, was genuinely delighted by Arthur’s arrival. He brought with him the dominant lexicon of the great city—that darling, sharp London jargon so vital to their shared identity, yet utterly untranslatable to the rural folk whose cultural memory was tied to the soil. On his first day, Pen kept Blanche laughing for hours after a meticulously prepared dinner of slow-simmered partridge and spiced preserves. She did not scold her mother, but fondled her to the Begum’s surprise, and sang her songs with a reinvigorated, melodic spirit that harmonized beautifully with the evening's acoustic ecology. When bedtime arrived, she whispered, “Déjà!” with a pretty air of regret, squeezing Arthur’s hand. 

“She is very much improved,” Pen thought later, looking out into the fragrant night. He struck a match and lit a cigar, drawing in the rich, fermented notes of the cured tobacco. The olfactory landscape of the garden—wet earth, night-blooming jasmine, and his spiced smoke—was intoxicating. “How jolly a cigar is at night! How pretty that little Saxon church looks in the moonlight!” He hummed a tune Blanche had adapted to his own verses, the notes vibrating softly against the stone walls. 

“Oh, heavenly!” a girl’s voice broke through the scent of the clematis that wreathed the nearby casement. It was the author of *Mes Larmes*. 

Pen burst into a laugh. “Don’t tell about my smoking,” he said, leaning out, savoring the gustatory richness of the moment. 

“Oh! go on! I adore it,” she cried, her voice a delicate counterpoint to the quiet rustle of the woods. “Heavenly night! heavenly, heavenly moon! but I must shut my window, and not talk to you on account of *les mœurs*. How droll they are, *les mœurs*! Adieu.” And Pen began to sing the Goodnight to Don Basilio into the dark. 

The following days were a circular return to the rhythms of their youth. They rambled through the woods to Penshurst, walking beneath the ancestral oaks, foraging for wild herbs, and speaking of Laura. Blanche’s voice dropped into a sentimental register as she recalled the old, untranslated songs they used to sing. When Pen visited Smirke’s school, he found Blanche seated among the children, seemingly unspoiled, deeply integrated into the village's domestic routines. 

“And do you really like the country?” he asked her, as they walked together, breathing in the scent of crushed pine needles and damp loam. 

“I should like never to see that odious city again. O Arthur—one’s good thoughts grow up in these sweet woods and calm solitudes, like those flowers which won’t bloom in London. I don’t think I shall bear to look London in the face again—its odious, smoky, brazen face! But, heigho!” 

“Why that sigh, Blanche?” 

“Never mind why.” 

“Yes, I do mind why. Tell me, tell me everything.” 

“I wish you hadn’t come down;” and a second edition of *Mes Soupirs* escaped her lips. 

“You don’t want me, Blanche?” 

“I don’t want you to go away. I don’t think this house will be very happy without you, and that’s why I wish that you never had come.” 

Here, *Mes Soupirs* were laid aside, and *Mes Larmes* began to flow. Ah! What answer is given to those tears? What is the method employed for drying them? O ringdoves and roses, O dews and wild-foraged flowers, O waving greenwoods and the acoustic perfection of the summer air! Here were two battered London rakes, steeped in the dominant power dynamics of their metropolitan lexicon, taking themselves in for a moment. They fancied they were in love with each other, playing the mythic roles of Phillis and Corydon amidst a landscape they could admire, but never truly belong to.
