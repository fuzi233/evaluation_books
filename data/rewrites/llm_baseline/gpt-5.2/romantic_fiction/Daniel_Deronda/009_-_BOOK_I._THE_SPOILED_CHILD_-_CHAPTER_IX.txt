Eight months after the Davilows had settled at Offendene, when June had ripened into that late sweetness which makes even hedgerows seem lavish, a rumor began to run—soft at first, like a cart-wheel heard far off on a chalk road, then louder as it passed from market to alehouse, from rectory parlor to dairy-yard. It was not the sort of rumor that came stamped with the red wax of war and Parliament. It was domestic, local, and yet it put a stir into every calling as surely as the first smell of new-mown hay wakes hunger in a village street.

Diplow Hall—Sir Hugo Mallinger’s great place—had been standing for two years with its white shutters closed in a way that made the windows look blind and out of humor with the sun. The deer had grazed as if the lawns were made for them alone; the lilies had opened and shut on the pool without reflection from any passing ribbon or parasol. Now the shutters were to be thrown back; the stables to be aired; the house to be inhabited “in a fitting style,” as if a house itself could taste the difference between neglect and ceremony.

The tenant would not be Sir Hugo. It would be his nephew, Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt—“young Grandcourt,” people called him with that comfortable intimacy which the world allows itself toward other people’s wealth. He was the presumptive heir to the baronetcy, Sir Hugo having daughters and no son; and fortune, never content with a single hook, had more than one to catch admiration with. Through his mother there ran a baronial streak—so that, should certain figures in the middle distance obligingly vanish, he might one day be a peer, and then, by the old conjuring of English custom, any wife he chose would find herself lifted too.

The thought of that unchosen wife rose in minds with the same inevitability as the thought of dinner rises in a kitchen at noon. It was not spoken of openly—not in a neighborhood that could flatter itself on “good taste” and visiting terms with persons of rank. Yet the speculation moved everywhere, threading itself into talk about corn-prices and colts, just as the smell of baking bread will creep into the driest discourse.

Corn-factors, brewers, horse-dealers, saddlers—men who measured life by barley and leather—approved on principle. A landlord in residence meant horses in the stable, mouths at the table, wages paid, ale drawn, shoes hammered; it meant that money would circulate like broth in a large house, flavoring even the poorest crust. The blacksmith at Diplow heard the tidings with a grin that showed his teeth white against soot: there would be hooves to nail, chains to mend, perhaps even an idle gentleman’s whim for a new set of bright fittings. Laborers’ wives thought of their nimble boys in livery, small hands promoted from dung-fork to brush, from hedging to harness. Farmers admitted, with a stiffness that could not quite conceal hope, that straw and hay might find a better market if the Hall were full and hunters were to be fed through the season.

And if those were the practical anticipations of people “not in society,” one could guess the sort of satisfaction among their betters—satisfaction less bluntly spoken, more prettily disguised, and often more hungry. Marriage belongs to both worlds: it is at once a private drama and a public transaction, with servants to hire and dinners to give. So the rumor sent, like a loose feather on a current, an indeterminate vision into more than one well-bred imagination.

At Quetcham, in the beautiful Arrowpoint place, the matter was contemplated from a height at which money does not need to look eager. No one could accuse Mr. and Mrs. Arrowpoint of sordid designs: they could leave their Catherine at least half a million, and fortune makes a virtuous face more readily when it is not pinched. Yet affectionate anxieties are not abolished by a large income. Catherine had refused Lord Slogan—an unimpeachable Irish peer with an estate that wanted nothing but drainage and population—and her parents, without wishing anybody dead (for decent people must always add that clause), could not help thinking it would be agreeable if the intervening persons between Grandcourt and the title were obliging enough to be removed by time. They wondered what sort of man he was: handsome? sound? “reformed,” if there had been anything to reform? and, since politics can be seasoning or poison at a dinner-table, was he liberal-conservative without being too liberal-conservative?

If the Arrowpoints had their private ruminations, it was not surprising that Mr. Gascoigne’s were stirred. Being a clergyman did not exempt him from the parental uneasiness of a man who feels himself responsible for young creatures with wills like unbroken colts. Yet even to his wife he did not speak plainly of matrimonial conjectures. On such subjects, people are as cautious as if they were discussing a new acid or the fixed stars: either your neighbor will seize on your idea and forestall you, or he will take a different view and think the worse of you. Besides, Grandcourt’s past—whatever it was—did not invite in Mr. Gascoigne the suspicious curiosity he might have felt toward a man applying to be butler. A young gentleman of birth and money is forgiven many things as naturally as a rich sauce is forgiven its fat. “Whatever he has done,” the world says, “he has not ruined himself,” and that is the convenient test by which reformation is pronounced genuine.

Mrs. Gascoigne, however, had said to her sister with that interjectional sigh which tries to sound like piety and cannot help sounding like impatience, “It would be a mercy, Fanny, if that girl were well married!” Mrs. Davilow, who could be mild in everything except where her child was touched, made no audible reply; but inwardly the thought rose quick and sharp: You will not get her to marry for your pleasure. The gentle mother could grow saucy in defense of Gwendolen, just as a quiet hen will peck if her chick is threatened.

Then, with her husband, Mrs. Gascoigne said, “I hear Mr. Grandcourt has got two places of his own, but he comes to Diplow for the hunting. It is to be hoped he will set a good example in the neighborhood. Have you heard what sort of a young man he is, Henry?”

Mr. Gascoigne said that he had not heard—at least not in any shape he was disposed to repeat. He carried within him that comfortable doctrine which makes the moral life of a rich man seem a different species of thing from the moral life of the poor. In any case, it was the present—what Grandcourt actually was—that would matter, not the stories men told for want of better talk.

Mrs. Davilow, too, could not be indifferent. The mere mention of an unknown person sets the mind to sketching, as a cook, hearing of a guest, begins to imagine what he likes to eat. Mrs. Davilow’s first picture of Mr. Grandcourt was inevitably flattering: handsome, accomplished, excellent—a man she could be satisfied with as husband for her daughter. But the next thought came like a chill draft under a door: would Gwendolen be satisfied? That girl’s taste was a mystery: excellence might not be her bait. The mother’s image of perfection wavered, dissolved, re-formed into a less certain combination—something that might, by some strange chemistry, touch Gwendolen’s affections. And in the end, pressed by her own anxious love, Mrs. Davilow let herself think what, for her, was almost a hard thought: It would not signify about her being in love, if she would only accept the right person.

Yet she dared not dwell on marriage with her daughter, not since that scene after Rex’s farewell, which had made her feel as if touching Gwendolen’s heart were like touching a wound that might bleed afresh at the lightest pressure. She was ashamed even of her own airy castle-building; for she knew how easily a hint of desire in a mother becomes, in such a daughter, an instant repugnance. With Gwendolen, to recommend a thing was often to condemn it.

The safe subject was dress—the immediate, sensible business of how a girl should appear at the coming Archery Meeting. There was comfort in it: cloth and color are manageable; you can lay them out and decide. They agreed that on her white cashmere, nothing suited her complexion like a touch of pale green. A feather tried in the hat before the glass settled it. The room was quiet except for the small domestic sounds—the brush of fabric, the faint crackle as a ribbon was tightened, the distant clink from below where some spoon or pan was being put away after tea. Even those little noises, regular and known, seemed to steady Mrs. Davilow’s thoughts; the house had its own harmless music.

But then Gwendolen, turning suddenly, flung herself into the attitude of drawing a bow—her whole body alive with that quick, theatrical grace which made play look like power. Her eyes glittered with comic enjoyment.

“How I pity all the other girls at the Archery Meeting—all thinking of Mr. Grandcourt! And they have not a shadow of a chance.”

Mrs. Davilow felt her ears tingle, as if the words had stung them. Before she could answer, Gwendolen wheeled round, wicked with perception.

“Now you know they have not, mamma. You and my uncle and aunt—you all intend him to fall in love with me.”

Mrs. Davilow, piqued into a little stratagem—her timid conscience borrowing a moment’s slyness—said, “Oh, my dear, that is not so certain. Miss Arrowpoint has charms which you have not.”

“I know,” said Gwendolen lightly, “but they demand thought. My arrow will pierce him before he has time for thought.” Her voice had that bright ring which could make cruelty sound like wit. “He will declare himself my slave—I shall send him round the world to bring me back the wedding ring of a happy woman—in the meantime all the men who are between him and the title will die of different diseases—he will come back Lord Grandcourt—but without the ring—and fall at my feet. I shall laugh at him—he will rise in resentment—I shall laugh more—he will call for his steed and ride to Quetcham, where he will find Miss Arrowpoint just married to a needy musician, Mrs. Arrowpoint tearing her cap off, and Mr. Arrowpoint standing by. Exit Lord Grandcourt, who returns to Diplow, and, like M. Jabot, change de linge.”

It was impossible not to feel that she saw too much—saw it with the same instinct by which birds know weather. You could hide a secret as you might sit on a purse and pretend innocence; she would know by the corner of your eye the exact sum you were pressing down. Mrs. Davilow had the uneasy fancy that by sheer divination Gwendolen already knew more about Mr. Grandcourt than anyone else did.

And that fancy, as such fancies will, pushed her into a question that seemed to come merely because she had a tongue and did not know what else to do with it.

“Why, what kind of a man do you imagine him to be, Gwendolen?”

“Let me see!” Gwendolen put her forefinger to her lips, frowning as if in solemn invention, then stretched out the finger with decision. “Short—just above my shoulder—trying to make himself tall by turning up his mustache and keeping his beard long—a glass in his right eye to give him an air of distinction—a strong opinion about his waistcoat, but uncertain and trimming about the weather, on which he will try to draw me out. He will stare at me all the while, and the glass in his eye will cause him to make horrible faces, especially when he smiles in a flattering way. I shall cast down my eyes in consequence, and he will perceive that I am not indifferent to his attentions. I shall dream that night that I am looking at the extraordinary face of a magnified insect—and the next morning he will make an offer of his hand; the sequel as before.”

“That is a portrait of some one you have seen already, Gwen,” said Mrs. Davilow, trying to keep her voice steady. “Mr. Grandcourt may be a delightful young man for what you know.”

“Oh, yes,” Gwendolen returned, with a careless assent pitched high, taking off her hat and turning it round on her hand as if it were a dish she meant to judge from every angle. “I wonder what sort of behavior a delightful young man would have? I know he would have hunters and racers, and a London house and two country-houses—one with battlements and another with a veranda. And I feel sure that with a little murdering he might get a title.”

The jest was of the kind that carries a grain of belief like pepper hidden in sweetness. It left a sharpness. Mrs. Davilow’s brow drew together in distress.

“Don’t talk in that way, child, for heaven’s sake! you do read such books—they give you such ideas of everything. I declare when your aunt and I were your age we knew nothing about wickedness. I think it was better so.”

“Why did you not bring me up in that way, mamma?” said Gwendolen.

The words were quick; their cruelty was quicker than she meant. She saw at once the crushed look, the sob rising like a wave that can’t be held back. The sound of it changed the room—made every small domestic noise beyond the door seem suddenly too loud, too indifferent, as if the world went on clattering its cups while a heart broke.

Gwendolen tossed down her hat and knelt, catching her mother’s hands.

“Mamma, mamma! I was only speaking in fun. I meant nothing.”

“How could I, Gwendolen?” Mrs. Davilow sobbed, the effort to speak making her voice broken. “Your will was always too strong for me—if everything else had been different.”

The logic was disjoined, but the meaning struck home. Gwendolen understood with that swift, uncomfortable clarity that was her nearest approach to self-distrust.

“Dear mamma, I don’t find fault with you—I love you,” she said, truly compunctious. “How can you help what I am? Besides, I am very charming. Come, now.”

With her handkerchief she dabbed and rubbed gently at her mother’s tears, as if soothing could be done by the same careful touch with which one wipes a misted glass. And then, to bring back the ordinary world, she added with coaxing mischief, “Really—I am contented with myself. I like myself better than I should have liked my aunt and you. How dreadfully dull you must have been!”

That blend—tenderness made into a little comedy—quieted Mrs. Davilow as it had often done after collisions. The collisions were rarely repeated on precisely the same spot; both of them carried away from such moments a dread of the topics that had kindled them. Gwendolen dreaded the unpleasant compunction she felt toward her mother; Mrs. Davilow dreaded any hint that she had been reproached. So, by tacit agreement, Mr. Grandcourt’s name was shut out of their talk as one shuts a door against a draft.

When Mr. Gascoigne referred to Grandcourt once or twice, Mrs. Davilow listened with a fearful attention lest Gwendolen’s dangerous perspicacity should betray what might be in her uncle’s mind. But she was not betrayed. Gwendolen knew differences of character with an instinct as sure as an ear that distinguishes woodsong from the thin metallic rasp of a distant machine. And precisely because she meant to evade her uncle’s control, she meant also not to clash with him. She prized the shelter of his indulgence.

Their good understanding was helped by archery. Mr. Gascoigne, being one of the best bowmen in Wessex, was pleased to find in his niece the same elements of skill: the poised elbow, the steadiness of aim, the pleasure in the clean flight of an arrow through air that, on such June days, carried every sound—birdcall, footstep, faint laughter—so distinctly that the world seemed held together by its own hearing. Gwendolen, too, was careful not to forfeit the fatherly kindness that made her freer.

For since Rex’s trouble, both Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna could not hide what Gwendolen felt—unreasonable—alienation. Toward Anna, Gwendolen made efforts at a regretful affectionateness. But neither dared to mention Rex’s name; and Anna, for whom the thought of him was as constant as breath, was constrained beside the cousin whose lightness had ruined his happiness. She tried dutifully to repress all sign; but pain cannot mimic the glance and hand-touch of pleasure. That dumb change, unfair as it was, hardened Gwendolen, and she grew more defiant in secret.

Sometimes she felt as if she were being trained like a performer, expected always to please—mother, aunt, uncle, the whole circle of approving and disapproving eyes. And one day, with that sudden leap from thought to speech which made her so difficult to guard against, she said, “Mamma, I see now why girls are glad to be married—to escape being expected to please everybody but themselves.”

Happily, Mr. Middleton was gone without any avowal. And though admiration for the handsome Miss Harleth spread over thirty square miles—among families that included several disengaged young men, each glad to sit near the lively girl who made conversation as easy as breathing—the fact remained: there had been no explicit offers. Providence, or nature, or whatever name one gives the order that keeps the world from burning itself out in universal passion, had taken care that admiration should not always become love, and love should not always declare itself. Many hidden conditions—some as coarse as the price of corn—help to decide why a girl, perfect in person, accomplishment, and costume, is not besieged by proposals.

It was only eight months since Gwendolen had come to Offendene. Some inclinations grow slowly, like plants edging sunward day by day. And if none of the eligible young men already near at hand had spoken, why should Mr. Grandcourt be expected to do what they had left undone? Perhaps because he was thought still more eligible; and a great deal of what passes for likelihood in the world is only a wish turned into prophecy.

The Arrowpoints, having no anxiety that Miss Harleth should make a brilliant marriage, formed their “likelihoods” differently. But in the quiet rooms at Offendene, where pale-green feathers were chosen and tears were wiped away, the rumor of Diplow Hall being opened again continued to hover—like the scent from a kitchen window that promises something rich without telling you whether it will nourish or poison.
