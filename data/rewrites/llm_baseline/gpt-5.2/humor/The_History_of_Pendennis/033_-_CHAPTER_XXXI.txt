Pen, having once caught the London fever, must needs taste the whole city as a greedy boy tastes a fair—one stall after another, one sweetmeat after another, vowing each last mouthful the finest he ever knew, and instantly forgetting it for the next. He prowled into a hundred queer haunts with the air of a young gentleman who had read of “Life” in books and was resolved to have it served up hot, with sauce. Coalheavers he visited in their tap-rooms, where the beer was brown as the river at low tide and the talk rolled heavy as casks. Boxers he watched in inn-parlours, where the walls were smoky with old oaths and newer pipe-fumes. Honest citizens he observed in suburban gardens, taking their pleasure as if it were a Sunday joint—cut carefully, praised loudly, and meant to last. And had Fate but offered him a celebrated pickpocket in a convenient corner, Pen would willingly have clinked pewter with him, merely for the novelty, as a child will eat a strange berry to boast he has eaten it.

The merriest part of this expeditionary cookery of experience was Warrington, who went through these houses as a man goes through his own pantry: knowing what shelf held the best relish, which drawer the sharpest mustard, and which landlord could be trusted not to water the gin. Pen delighted to see the gravity with which Warrington listened to the Tutbury Pet or the Brighton Stunner at the Champion’s Arms, and the downright affection he showed the coalheaving company at the Fox-under-the-Hill. His acquaintance with public-houses was not so much wide as intimate; he was greeted at the bar as at a club, and nodded to by tapsters with the respectful ease with which cooks salute a man who tips well and never complains of his steak.

“In society,” Warrington would say—sanding his words as the floor was sanded—“everybody is the same: same coat, same grin, same talk, same everything. One young dandy at the club looks and chatters just like another; one Miss at a ball resembles the next, like loaves turned out of the same tin. But here there’s flavour. I like to talk with the strongest man in England, or the man who can drink the most beer in England, or with that tremendous republican hatter who thinks Thistlewood the greatest character in history. I like better gin-and-water than claret. I like a sanded floor in Carnaby Market better than a chalked one in Mayfair. I prefer Snobs, I own it.”

And indeed he was a sort of social republican; it never entered his head, while talking with Jack and Tom, that he was in any respect their better—though, to do him justice, the deference they paid him might have been as pleasant to his vanity as a warm roll to a hungry man.

Pen followed him with immense glee and enormous assiduity; yet Pen, being younger, wore his humility as young men wear their first dress-coat—stiffly, and as if it proved nobility. He moved among these fellows like a young prince in disguise visiting the poor of his father’s kingdom; and they received him accordingly, as a high chap, a fine fellow, a swell with good humour. He had an imperious frankness, a kind of royal condescension; though, in strict revenue, he was heir-apparent to twopence-halfpenny, and but one in descent from a gallypot. But if the world lays a cushion for us to sit higher, we drop onto it readily enough; and there is nothing so touching and so ridiculous as the conceit of a clever generous lad—so frank, so certain, so wrong.

They read pretty hard of a morning—Pen, I fear, not law alone, but politics and history and literature and, above all, the newspaper, that great steaming dish in which the whole town is boiled daily and served to whoever can swallow it. Then, when dinner drew nigh and appetite with it, they sallied out upon the town with spirits sharpened like knives, bent upon a merry night as they had passed a busy forenoon. There is a season—four-and-twenty—when the world is new bread and you believe it will never go stale. Afterwards you may fancy yourself young again with the comrades of that time; the tunes you hum in old age are the tunes you learned then. Yet the same pleasure-garden, revisited, looks dingy: garlands tattered, company thin, and half the lamps out. Grey hairs come on like daylight streaming in—daylight and a headache with it. Pleasure goes to bed with the rouge still on her cheeks. Well: let us walk through the day, sober if we must, but friendly.

Laura and Helen—had they chanced to be up in London—might sometimes, at very early morning when the bridges blushed in sunrise and the city streets shone clean in the dawn, have seen Mr. Pen and Mr. Warrington rattling over the echoing flags toward the Temple after one of their wild nights. Wild—but not so wicked as such nights often are. Warrington hated women; and Pen, lofty even in his faults, was too proud to stoop to a vulgar intrigue. He could not speak to a woman but with instinctive courtesy, and shrank from a coarse word as from a sour smell in a clean room. If he fell in love with a fool—as wiser men have done—he still, for the time of the delusion, set her up as a goddess and waited upon her. Men serve women kneeling; when they get on their feet, they go away.

That hard homely aphorism was delivered to Pen, not by a philosopher in a book, but by an old acquaintance met again in London—honest Mr. Bows of the Chatteris Theatre—now reduced (or promoted, according to how you rate the dignity of labour) to be pianoforte player to the eminent lyrical talent which nightly delighted the public at the Fielding’s Head in Covent Garden; where, behind the scenes and beyond the decencies, flourished the little club called the Back Kitchen.

A number of Pen’s friends frequented this merry meeting. The Fielding’s Head had been a house of entertainment almost since the days when the famous author of Tom Jones sat as magistrate hard by in Bow Street. Fielding’s place was pointed out; Fielding’s chair was said to survive, and in it sat nightly the president of the revels. Most often that president was Cutts, the landlord: a jolly man with a voice like a well-corked bottle—strong, cheerful, and certain to go off with a pop. He sang profusely; his songs belonged to the British Brandy-and-Water School—“The Good Old English Gentleman,” “Dear Tom, this Brown Jug,” and others of that comfortable tribe wherein hospitality and pathos are kneaded together and baked in a baritone.

Often have I marvelled, in my youth, at Cutts’s double capacity: how he would work a room to patriotism by singing of brave Abercrombie receiving his wound, or melt us with the autumn leaf proclaiming the old man must die—Cutts shedding tears liberally, voice faltering, fists thumping approval on the tables—when, before the applause had ended, he would become instantly Cutts the landlord, and call out, brisk as a cook at service: “Now, gentlemen, give your orders; the waiter’s in the room—John, a champagne cup for Mr. Green. Sausages and mashed potatoes? John, attend on the gentleman.”

“And I’ll thank ye give me a glass of punch too, John, and take care the wather boils,” would cry, not unfrequently, a voice Pen knew too well; a voice that made him blush and start when he heard it first. It was the venerable Captain Costigan—now established in London, and one of the great pillars of the harmonic meetings at the Fielding’s Head.

Costigan’s very ruin had a kind of gusto to it. He was never merely drunk; he was steeped. He would insist on punch as if it were a medicinal broth, and on the water boiling as if the kettle were the last outpost of discipline in a besieged life. His manners and his talk brought many young men to the Back Kitchen, for he was a character, and fame has a way of making even folly sound like entertainment when it is delivered loudly enough. He talked to the friend of the moment—his neighbour drinking by his side—about “me daughther”; about her marriage; about the grandeur before and after it; about the carriages she kept; about Mirabel’s adoration for her and for him; about the hundred pounds he was at liberty to draw from his son-in-law whenever necessity urged him.

Then—having sworn, with the solemnity of a man blessing a joint, that he would “dthraw next Sathurday—the fourteenth—at Coutts’s—the very instant I present the cheque”—he would as solemnly ask to borrow a half-crown until that day of Greek Calends, pledging on the honour of an officer and gentleman to “repee the thrifling obligetion.”

Sir Charles Mirabel did not in truth adore his father-in-law as Costigan sometimes boasted, though in other moods Costigan would weep over his daughter’s ingratitude and the wealthy old man’s stinginess. Yet the Mirabels had not been unkind: they had settled a small pension on him, paid with regularity, and forestalled with still greater regularity by poor Cos. The period of payment was always known at the Fielding’s Head; where the Captain, bank-notes in hand, would demand change at the top of his voice amid the songs and suppers, as if money were a trumpet meant to be blown. “I think ye’ll find that note won’t be refused at the Bank of England, Cutts, my boy,” he would say. “Bows, have a glass? Ye needn’t stint yourself to-night, anyhow; and a glass of punch will make ye play con spirito.” He was lavish when he had money—buttons were not invented for his pocket until the coin was gone; and sometimes, indeed, not even then, unless a creditor approached within smelling distance.

It was in one of these moments of exultation that Pen found the old man swaggering at the singers’ table in the Back Kitchen, ordering brandy-and-water for any acquaintance who appeared. Warrington, who was on confidential terms with the bass singer, made his way up to that quarter; Pen followed close.

Pen started and blushed to see Costigan. He had just come from Lady Whiston’s party, where he had met and spoken with the Captain’s daughter again for the first time since very old days; and the sight of the father brought back, with the suddenness of a taste on the tongue, a whole remembered season. He came up with outstretched hand, kindly and warmly; for though Pen might be capricious in his attachments, he always respected the place where Love had dwelt, and—like the Sultan who has once flung the royal handkerchief—desired honours paid to the lady he had once chosen.

The Captain, tipsy, returned the clasp with all the strength of a palm made shaky by constant lifting of tumblers. He looked hard at Pen, as if trying to focus him out of a double world, and cried, “Grecious Heavens, is it possible? Me dear boy, me dear fellow, me dear friend!” Then, with a look of muddled curiosity, he fairly broke down: “I know your face, me dear dear friend, but, bedad, I’ve forgot your name.”

Five years of constant punch had passed since Pen and Costigan met; Arthur was changed, and when a man at that actual moment sees things double, we can pardon his past being muzzy.

Pen laughed, though perhaps a little mortified. “Don’t you remember me, Captain? I am Pendennis—Arthur Pendennis, of Chatteris.”

The sound of the young man’s voice steadied the old remembrance as a spoon steadies a spinning cup. “Pendennis!” cried Costigan, and now the floodgates opened. Pen was his dearest boy, his gallant young friend, his noble collagian, held in his inmost heart ever since they had parted. How was his fawther—no, his mother—and his guardian, the General, the Major? “I preshoom, from your apparance, you’ve come into your prawpertee; and, bedad, yee’ll spend it like a man of spirit—I’ll go bail for that. No? not yet come into your estete? If ye want any thrifle, heark ye, there’s poor old Jack Costigan has got a guinea or two in his pocket—and, be heavens! you shall never want, Awthur, me dear boy. What’ll ye have? John, come hither, and look aloive; give this gentleman a glass of punch, and I’ll pay for’t.—Your friend? I’ve seen him before. Permit me to have the honour of making meself known to ye, sir, and requesting ye’ll take a glass of punch.”

“I don’t envy Sir Charles Mirabel his father-in-law,” thought Pen; for Costigan’s affection, like his punch, came in quantities that might drown discretion. Aloud, he asked after another old friend. “And how is my old friend, Mr. Bows, Captain? Have you any news of him, and do you see him still?”

“No doubt he’s very well,” said Costigan, jingling his money and whistling “The Little Doodeen,” for which ditty he was celebrated at the Fielding’s Head. Then, with that treacherous loop of his memory, he blinked and said, “Me dear boy—I’ve forgot your name again—but my name’s Costigan, Jack Costigan, and I’d loike ye to take as many tumblers of punch in my name as ever ye loike. Ye know my name; I’m not ashamed of it.” And he maundered on, proud as a drunken rooster.

“It’s pay-day with the General,” said Mr. Hodgen, the bass singer, to whom Warrington was listening; “and he’s a precious deal more than half seas over. He’s already tried that ‘Little Doodeen’ of his, and broke it, too, just before I sang ‘King Death.’ Have you heard my new song, ‘The Body Snatcher,’ Mr. Warrington?—angcored at Saint Bartholomew’s the other night—composed expressly for me. Per’aps you or your friend would like a copy of the song, sir? John, just ’ave the kyndness to ’and over a ‘Body Snatcher’ ’ere, will yer?—There’s a portrait of me, sir, as I sing it—as the Snatcher—considered rather like.”

“Thank you,” said Warrington. “Heard it nine times—know it by heart, Hodgen.”

At this moment the gentleman at the pianoforte began to play, and Pen, turning toward the sound, beheld the very Mr. Bows for whom he had been inquiring, and whose existence Costigan had mislaid. There sat the little old man before the battered piano—an instrument that had injured its constitution by sitting up so many nights, and now spoke in a voice at once hoarse and faint, like a kettle that has boiled too often and been scoured too little. Bows accompanied the singers, or played with taste and grace between songs, coaxing music out of that weary box as a frugal housewife coaxes flavour from bones.

Bows had seen and remembered Pen the moment he entered, and had marked the young man’s eager warmth toward Costigan. Now he played an air Pen instantly knew—one sung by the village chorus in The Stranger, just before Mrs. Haller came in. The tune struck Pen like a remembered smell: dusty boards, lamp-oil, cheap oranges, and a heart beating too fast for a boy’s ribs. Nobody else heeded Bows’s playing; the air was scarcely heard amid the clatter of knives and forks, calls for poached eggs and kidneys, the tramp of guests and waiters, the hiss of ale drawn, the scrape of pewter. In that room, music had to fight not only tobacco-smoke but the whole harsh orchestra of appetite.

Pen went up and shook the player’s hand at the end, kindly. Bows greeted him with respectful cordiality. “What, you haven’t forgot the old tune, Mr. Pendennis?” he said. “I thought you’d remember it. I take it, it was the first tune of that sort you ever heard played—wasn’t it, sir? You were quite a young chap then. I fear the Captain’s very bad to-night. He breaks out on a pay-day; and I shall have the deuce’s own trouble in getting home. We live together. We still hang on, sir, in partnership, though Miss Em—though my lady Mirabel has left the firm.—And so you remember old times, do you? Wasn’t she a beauty, sir?—Your health and my service to you,”—and he took a sip from the pewter measure of porter at his side, as naturally as another man might wet a finger to turn a page.

Pen, after this, had many opportunities of seeing the old musician again, and renewing acquaintance with Costigan; for London is wide, but it is stitched together by certain tables and certain tunes, and a man may wander the whole town and still come back to the same battered piano.

As Pen and Bows sat thus in friendly colloquy, men of all sorts drifted in and out of the Back Kitchen: healthy country tradesmen and farmers in London on business, refreshing themselves with song and supper; squads of young apprentices and assistants, the shutters closed over their day’s labour, coming for “fresh air,” which they took chiefly through pipes; rakish young medical students, loudly dressed and (must it be owned) somewhat dirty, applauding with vociferous hands; young university bucks with that genteel simper learned at Alma Mater’s knees; handsome young guardsmen; florid clubmen from St. James’s Street; nay, senators English and Irish; and even members of the House of Peers—who, after all, must eat and drink like the rest of us, though they may pretend their appetites are hereditary.

Mr. Hodgen had made an immense hit with “The Body Snatcher,” and the town rushed to hear it, as the town rushes to any noise that promises a thrill. The curtain drew aside; Hodgen appeared as the Snatcher, seated on a coffin, a flask of gin before him, a spade at hand, and a candle stuck in a skull. The song was sung with an admirable terrific humour: his voice went down so low it seemed to rumble under the floorboards into the listener’s soul; and in the chorus he clamped with his spade and gave a demoniac “Ha! ha!” that made the glasses quiver on the tables, as if the sound itself had struck them. One could scarce tell whether the shiver came from fear or from that gross pleasure by which men pay themselves for imagining horrors at a safe distance. The louder the song, the better London liked it; the more it imitated a graveyard’s racket, the more the Back Kitchen rang with applause—so that you might have thought the city could not bear silence, nor let the dead lie quietly even in music.

None of the other singers, not even Cutts (as that high-minded man owned), could stand up before the Snatcher; Cutts commonly retired to Mrs. Cutts’s private apartments, or into the bar, before that fatal song extinguished him. Poor Cos’s ditty, “The Little Doodeen,” which Bows accompanied charmingly, was sung to but a few admirers who chose to remain after the tremendous resurrectionist chant. The room was commonly emptied after “The Body Snatcher,” or left to a small stubborn remnant of pleasure’s votaries, who sat over cooling plates and thinning punch, as if unwilling to let even the night’s last noise die out.

It was while Pen and Warrington sat there one night—or rather one morning—that two habitues entered almost together.

“Mr. Hoolan and Mr. Doolan,” whispered Warrington to Pen, saluting them; and in the latter Pen recognized the gentleman of the Alacrity coach, who had declined dining with Pen on that former day, being compelled, by professional duties, to decline dinner engagements on Fridays.

Doolan’s paper, the Dawn, lay on the table, much bestained by porter and pressed cheek-by-jowl with Hoolan’s journal, which we shall call the Day. The Dawn was Liberal; the Day ultra-Conservative. Many of our journals are officered by Irish gentlemen; and their gallant brigade does the penning among us as their ancestors did the fighting in Europe—engaging under many a flag, and being good friends again when the battle is over. In their mouths, even the same English word could mean a different insult, according to the paper that paid for it; and there are tones and turns of phrase—native dialect, half-suppressed, half-performed—that carry more power than any argument, because they tell you at once who commands the language and who must borrow it.

“Kidneys, John, and a glass of stout,” said Hoolan. “How are you, Morgan? how’s Mrs. Doolan?”

“Doing pretty well, thank ye, Mick, my boy—faith she’s accustomed to it,” said Doolan. “How’s the lady that owns ye? Maybe I’ll step down Sunday, and have a glass of punch, Kilburn way.”

“Don’t bring Patsey with you, Mick, for our Georgy’s got the measles,” said the friendly Morgan; and straightway, without ceremony, the two fell to talk of their trade: foreign mails, correspondents at Paris and Madrid, the expense of couriers, the circulation of rival sheets—news handled like meat in a market, weighed, wrapped, and shouted over.

Warrington, laughing, took up the Dawn and pointed to a leading article, beginning thus:

“As rogues of note in former days who had some wicked work to perform,—an enemy to be put out of the way, a quantity of false coin to be passed, a lie to be told or a murder to be done—employed a professional perjurer or assassin to do the work, which they were themselves too notorious or too cowardly to execute: our notorious contemporary, the Day, engages smashers out of doors to utter forgeries against individuals, and calls in auxiliary cut-throats to murder the reputation of those who offend him. A black-vizarded ruffian (whom we will unmask), who signs the forged name of Trefoil, is at present one of the chief bravoes and bullies in our contemporary’s establishment. He is the eunuch who brings the bowstring, and strangles at the order of the Day. We can convict this cowardly slave, and propose to do so. The charge which he has brought against Lord Bangbanagher, because he is a Liberal Irish peer, and against the Board of Poor Law Guardians of the Bangbanagher Union, is—”

“How did they like the article at your place, Mick?” asked Morgan. “When the Captain puts his hand to it he’s a tremendous hand at a smasher. He wrote the article in two hours—in—whew—you know where, while the boy was waiting.”

“Our governor thinks the public don’t mind a straw about these newspaper rows, and has told the Docthor to stop answering,” said the other. “Them two talked it out together in my room. The Docthor would have liked a turn, for he says it’s such easy writing, and requires no reading up of a subject: but the governor put a stopper on him.”

“The taste for eloquence is going out, Mick,” said Morgan.

“’Deed then it is, Morgan,” said Mick. “That was fine writing when the Docthor wrote in the Phaynix, and he and Condy Roony blazed away at each other day after day.”

“And with powder and shot, too, as well as paper,” said Morgan. “Faith, the Docthor was out twice, and Condy Roony winged his man.”

“They are talking about Doctor Boyne and Captain Shandon,” Warrington said to Pen, “the two Irish controversialists of the Dawn and the Day—Boyne the Protestant champion, Shandon the Liberal orator. Best friends in the world, I believe, in spite of their rows; and though they cry out against the English for abusing their country, by Jove they abuse it themselves more in a single article than we should take pains to do in a dozen volumes. How are you, Doolan?”

“Your servant, Mr. Warrington—Mr. Pendennis, I am delighted to have the honour of seeing ye again,” said Doolan; and instantly the man’s whole accent shifted, like a cook changing sauce for company. “The night’s journey on the top of the Alacrity was one of the most agreeable I ever enjoyed in my life, and it was your liveliness and urbanity that made the trip so charming. I have often thought over that happy night, sir, and talked over it to Mrs. Doolan. I have seen your elegant young friend, Mr. Foker, too, here, sir, not unfrequently. He is an occasional frequenter of this hostelry, and a right good one it is. Mr. Pendennis, when I saw you I was on the Tom and Jerry Weekly Paper; I have now the honour to be sub-editor of the Dawn, one of the best-written papers of the empire”—and he bowed very slightly to Mr. Warrington.

His speech was unctuous and measured, his courtesy almost oriental; and his tone, when talking to the two Englishmen, differed as much from his talk with his comrade as a parlour dish differs from a kitchen one. Yet the same man sat in the same room, with the same stout and kidneys before him, under the same din of knives and laughter and song; and Pen, watching him, could not but feel—though he might not have put it into words—that in London, as in Ireland, a man’s very language may be a livery he puts on, and that power often lies not in what is said, but in who is allowed to say it, and how.
