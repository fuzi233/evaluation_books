Our benevolent Begum, Lady Clavering, was initially so incensed by yet another instance of her husband's duplicity and foolishness that she flatly refused to assist Sir Francis Clavering in meeting his "debts of honor." She declared her intention to separate from him immediately, leaving him to face the dire consequences of his incorrigible weakness and wastefulness. Following the disastrous financial transactions at the Derby, the unfortunate gambler was in such a state of mental collapse that he sought to avoid everyone. This included his turf associates, whom he owed money to and feared he couldn't repay, and his wife, his long-suffering banker, whom he now doubted would allow him further credit.

When Lady Clavering inquired the next morning if Sir Francis was home, she was informed that he had not returned. Instead, he had dispatched a messenger to his valet, instructing him to send clothes and letters via the courier. Captain Strong anticipated a summons from the Baronet within the day or two. True to his nature, Strong received a frantic note begging him to visit his distraught friend at the Short Hotel in Blackfriars and inquire for "Mr. Francis." Sir Francis possessed a peculiar psychological trait: he would rather fabricate a falsehood than admit the truth, often beginning a contest with Fortune by fleeing and hiding.

The bootboy of the Short Hotel, who delivered Clavering's message to Grosvenor Place and retrieved his carpet-bag, instantly recognized its owner. He whispered this revelation to the footman setting the breakfast table, who relayed it to the servants' hall, then to Mrs. Bonner, the lady's housekeeper and confidante, who finally passed it to Lady Clavering. Consequently, every member of the Grosvenor Place staff knew that Sir Francis was holed up under the alias "Francis" at an inn in the Blackfriars Road. Furthermore, Sir Francis's coachman spread the news among other gentlemen's drivers, who shared it with their masters and the neighboring Tattersalls, where grim predictions arose that Sir Francis Clavering was preparing for a tour of the Levant.

That day, the volume of mail addressed to Sir Francis Clavering, Bart., arriving at his hall table, was extraordinary. The French cook submitted his bill to Lady Clavering. Suppliers for her household, along with Messrs. Finer and Gimcrack (mercers and ornament dealers) and Madame Crinoline (the renowned milliner), forwarded their invoices to her, alongside Miss Amory's substantial private accounts from each establishment.

By the afternoon following the Derby, after a conversation with his principal at the Short Hotel—who was found sobbing and drinking Curaçao—Strong visited Grosvenor Place to conduct business as usual. He discovered all these suspicious documents arrayed on the Baronet's study desk. With a rueful expression, he began opening and examining them.

Just then, Mrs. Bonner, Lady Clavering's maid and housekeeper, descended upon him. As integral to the family as the Chevalier was to Sir Francis, Mrs. Bonner naturally sided with her mistress in the marital dispute, harboring anger equal to, if not exceeding, Lady Clavering's own.

"She won't pay if I advise her," Mrs. Bonner declared. "Please go back to Sir Francis, Captain, and tell him we refuse to pay his debts anymore. Look at him, lurking in a low public house, lacking the courage to face his wife like a man! We made a gentleman of him; we pulled him out of gaol (perhaps saving others too); we've paid his debts repeatedly. We set him up in Parliament, provided him with a town and country house. Yet here he is, a shabby sneak, daring not show his face! We furnished the horse he rides, the dinner he eats, and the clothes on his back. We will provide nothing more. Our remaining fortune belongs to ourselves; we shall not squander it on this ungrateful man. We'll give him enough to survive and leave him. Tell him that from Susan Bonner."

At this moment, Lady Clavering summoned Strong. Hoping to find his mistress more pliable than her factotum, the Chevalier approached her. On previous occasions, he had successfully appealed to Lady Clavering's generosity. He painted a dismal picture of Sir Francis's plight, warning of potential catastrophic consequences if his engagements remained unpaid.

"Let him kill himself!" Mrs. Bonner laughed harshly. "Dying would be the best thing for him." Strong insisted he had found the Baronet surrounded by razor blades. At this, Lady Clavering laughed bitterly. "He won't harm himself as long as there is a shilling left for him to steal from a poor woman. His life is safe, Captain; mark my words. Ah! What a terrible day it was when I first laid eyes on him."

"He's worse than the first man," cried Lady Clavering's aide-de-camp. "Before, he was a wild devil, yes, but he had the courage of a man. This fellow? Why bother paying his bills, selling your diamonds, and forgiving him? Next year he'll be just as bad. The first chance he gets, he'll cheat and rob her again. Her money will sustain a pack of rogues and swindlers—I don't mean you, Captain; you've been a good friend, barring our wish to never have met you."

Seeing Mrs. Bonner's mention of the diamonds, the Chevalier realized the kind-hearted Begum might soften slightly, offering hope for his client.

"Upon my word, ma'am," he said, feigning deep sympathy and admiration for her tireless goodness, "any criticism of Clavering or Mrs. Bonner is deserved by us both. It was indeed an unlucky day for you to encounter either. He has treated you cruelly, and unless you are the most generous and forgiving woman in existence, there is no hope for him. But you cannot allow the father of your son to remain a disgraced man, sending little Frank into the world with such a stain. Bind him; tie him down with any promises you like. I vouch for him; he will sign them."

"And break 'em," retorted Mrs. Bonner.

"And keep 'em this time!" cried Strong passionately. "He must keep them. Have you seen him weep, ma'am? 'Oh, Strong,' he said, 'it isn't for myself I suffer now; it's for my boy—it's for the finest woman in England whom I have treated basely. I know I have.' He didn't intend to bet on this race, ma'am—he truly didn't. He was cheated; the entire ring was rigged. He believed he could place the bet safely, without risk. Let this serve as a lifelong lesson. To see a man weep... oh, it is dreadful."

"He doesn't seem to care much about making my dear missus cry," Mrs. Bonner remarked, pointing toward the room. "Poor soul! Look if he does, Captain."

"If you possess the soul of a man, Clavering," Strong said later, recounting the scene to his principal, "you will keep your promise this time. So help me Heaven, if you cross her word, I will turn against you and expose everything."

"What exactly?" shrieked Mr. Francis, to whom Strong delivered the message back at the Short Hotel, where the Baronet was currently crying and drowning his sorrows in Curaçao.

"Pshaw! Do you imagine I am a fool?" Strong exploded. "Do you think I could navigate this world for so long without keeping my eyes open? Know this: I need only speak, and tomorrow you are a beggar. Moreover, I am not the only one who knows your secret."

"Who else knows?" gasped Clavering.

"Old Pendennis does, or I am vastly mistaken. He recognized the man the very first night he arrived drunk in your house."

"He knows it, does he?" Clavering shrieked. "Damn him—kill him!"

"You'd like to kill us all, wouldn't you, old boy?" Strong sneered, puffing his cigar.

The Baronet slapped his weak hand against his forehead; perhaps Strong had correctly interpreted his desire. "Oh, Strong!" he cried, "If only I dared, I would end it all. I am the damnedest miserable dog in all England. It is this misery that drives me wild and reckless. It compels me to drink"—(he trembled as he raised a bumper of Curaçao)—"and to associate with these thieves. I know they are thieves, every single one of them, damned thieves. And how can I help it? I didn't know! By Gad, I am innocent! Until I saw that damned scoundrel, I knew less than the dead! I will flee, I will go abroad, far from these confounded hells, and bury myself in a forest! I will hang myself from a tree! Oh, I am the most miserable beggar in all England!" Thus, amidst groans, despair, and blasphemy, the impotent wretch vented his grief, lamenting his fate while vowing his miserable repentance.
