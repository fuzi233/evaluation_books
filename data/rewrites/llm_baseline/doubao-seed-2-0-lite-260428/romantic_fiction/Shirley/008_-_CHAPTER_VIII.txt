The sun had not yet pricked the frosted February horizon when Robert Moore swung himself into his saddle, galloping out towards Whinbury before his sister Hortense had even set the kettle to simmer for their café au lait, or laid out the crisp buttered tartines that were his usual breakfast. The errand that took him out so early he kept locked tight behind the quiet reserve that ran in his mercantile bones: the heavy, tangled miseries of his mill business were a secret he rarely breathed to anyone save to startle poor Joe Scott half out of his wits, or send a sharp, brisk warning to some far-flung foreign correspondent. Hortense never asked questions; it was not her way to pry, nor his to render accounts for his comings and goings.

By the time he trundled back down the mill drive, the pale golden morning was stretching over the Yorkshire moors, and the faint, warm memory of the previous evening lingered at the back of his mind – of Caroline’s soft cheek resting against the cushion beside him as he read Shakespeare aloud, of her quiet little gasp when he recited lines about untamed, enduring love – even as he hung his riding coat by the counting-house door and settled at his desk.

Joe Scott’s small son Henry trundled in with the post and the daily papers, and Moore broke the seals one by one, scanning their contents quickly. None of the news was good: every letter reeked of sour, uncompromising bad tidings, so that when he set the last one down, he huffed a sharp, defiant breath through his nose, a flicker of dark exasperation crossing his usually placid face. He did not pace or mutter oaths, but his fingers stripped the feather quill off the top of his pen in one quick, sharp spasm of barely controlled fury, before he dashed off a stack of terse replies, sealed them, and strode out to walk the length of his mill floor, his boots clapping on the wooden planks, to burn off the edge of his frustration.

When he returned, he picked up the paper, but he could not focus. More than once he laid it across his knee, folded his arms, and stared into the crackling fire in the hearth, glancing out the open door at the mild, sweet air that blew in – no spring bloom yet, of course, only the occasional faint whiff of sulphur from the mill’s tall black chimney. He checked his pocket watch for the third time, and had just begun to wonder if Sugden the constable had got lost on the road, when a figure dark as a midnight sky popped around the doorframe: it was Joe Scott, fresh from the dyeing vats, his face and arms streaked deep indigo. “He’s comed, sir,” he said, and vanished before Moore could even nod.

A moment later, in strode Sugden – broad-shouldered, thick-limbed, dressed in rough fustian coat and grey worsted stockings, his official constable’s staff clutched in one big hand. He pulled off his scuffed old hat, stuffed it under his chair, wiped his forehead on a spotted cotton handkerchief, and announced that it was “raight dahn warm for Febewerry.” Moore made a small, noncommittal sound that passed for agreement, and gestured for him to sit.

“I reckon you’ve got all the papers in order?” Moore asked.

“Ay, ay! All’s right and tight,” Sugden replied, whistling softly under his breath to look at his ease. Moore reached to the cupboard at his elbow, pulled out the same black bottle of hollands he’d shared with the curate Malone a few days prior, set a tumbler and a jug on the table, and nodded for Sugden to help himself. “Water’s in that jar in the corner.”

“Dunnut knaw there’s mich need, but a body’s niver too dry of a morning,” Sugden said, grinning as he mixed himself a generous drink, took a long, satisfied draught, and leaned back in his chair. “Yah’d as good have a sup yourself, Mr. Moore. Uncommon good stuff, that. Got it from furrin parts, I’se warrant? Them lads that’s coming are going to keep you talking for hours, you’ll need propping up.”

Moore shook his head. “Seen Mr. Sykes this morning?”

“I seed him a quarter hour back, just before I set off,” Sugden said. “He said he’d be straight over here, and I wouldn’t wonder if old Helstone comes too. I saw ‘em saddling his little nag out the back of the rectory just now.”

He was right. Five minutes later, the sharp clip-clop of a small pony’s hooves echoed across the mill yard, and a nasal, familiar voice called out, “Boy! Take my horse and lead him to the stable!” The Reverend Helstone marched in, brisk and upright, his cheeks tanned from riding, his eyes sharper and brighter than ever.

“Beautiful morning, Moore! How do, my boy? Ha! Sugden! What, you’re ready to get straight to business? I like that,” he said, nodding at the constable, then leaned in, curious. “Your message got to me safe. Are you sure you’re on the right track here? Have you got the warrant?”

“Sugden has it,” Moore said.

“Then I’m coming with you to make the arrest,” Helstone declared.

“No trouble needed, sir,” Moore smiled, dry and quiet. “They’re coming to me. I’m sat here waiting for them now.”

“Who is it, then? One of my parishioners?”

Joe Scott had slipped in unobserved, leaning against the desk, half his person dyed the deepest indigo, a sly little grin on his face. “It’s an old friend of yours, Mr. Helstone,” he said. “The gentleman you’re always joking about, the tub orator, the Reverend Moses Barraclough.”

Helstone pulled out his snuff box and took a very long pinch, blinking. “Ah! I never would have guessed it! That pious hypocrite was never your workman, Moore! He’s a tailor by trade!”

“Which is exactly why I hold it against him,” Moore said, his voice sharpening. “He’s the one who’s been stirring up my sacked workers against me, inciting all that rioting.”

“And he was there at Stilbro’ Moor, leading the lot? On his wooden leg?” Helstone asked.

“Ay, sir,” Joe cut in, happy to tell the tale. “He rode there on horseback so no one would notice his bad leg, wore a mask the whole time, while the rest just blacked their faces. And we found him out because he courted Sarah, our master’s servant lass, and she wouldn’t have a single thing to do with him. She was sweet on Fred Murgatroyd, one of our mill lads, instead. A couple of months back, Fred and Moses ran into each other lingering round the cottage after dark, both trying to persuade Sarah to go for a walk with them. They tussled, and Fred got the worst of it – for all he’s got one leg, Moses is near as strong as Sugden there. Then last night, Fred and his mate took shelter in a barn from a rain shower, and heard Moses plotting with his mates – not just leading the riot at Stilbro’, but the attack on Sykes’s mill too. They’re coming here this morning, a whole deputation, to ‘persuade’ our master to get rid of his new machinery, all under the guise of pious peace and goodwill.”

Before he could say another word, the sound of a gig rolling into the yard made them all turn. Mr. Sykes stumbled in, tall and stout, about fifty years old, his pleasant face tight with anxiety. “Have they been? Are they gone? Did you arrest him? Is it all over?” he panted.

“Not yet,” Moore said, calm as ever. “We’re waiting for them.”

“They won’t come,” Sykes fretted. “It’s near noon. We should give this up, it’ll stir up bad blood, cause who knows what harm. I’ve got a wife and three little girls at home, Moore, a wife and family – a man can’t be too cautious.”

“You don’t have to show your face at all,” Moore told him. “I’ll meet them in the yard. You can stay here by the fire.”

“But my name will be in all the legal papers!” Sykes wailed. Helstone pushed the black bottle across the table to him, and Sykes mixed himself a brimming tumbler of hollands and water, swallowed it down, and within two minutes, colour flooded back to his cheeks, and he was suddenly bold as brass. He announced he wasn’t going to be trampled on by a bunch of common rioters, he’d spend his last penny to see them punished, no one was going to push him around. He poured himself a second glass, coughing a little to excuse it, saying he had a slight chill that morning, and turned to Helstone, puffing out his chest. “You’re a man of peace, sir, your nerves couldn’t stand this sort of peril! But us manufacturers, we get belligerent! When my wife lays awake every night scared the house will get broken into, I get all fired up – I’d enjoy a bit of a scuffle, I would, that’s how spirited I am!”

Helstone let out a low, brief, amused laugh, and stopped Moore before he could press a third glass on the man, shaking his head. “Enough is as good as a feast, Mr. Sykes, isn’t it?” he said, and Sykes nodded, grinning, as Joe Scott slipped the bottle away out of sight.

For half a second, Moore’s dark face softened, unguarded, as he thought of Caroline. What would she have said if she could see him now, this sharp, unyielding side of him, the man who wasn’t the gentle companion who’d sat with her last night reading poetry? Would she still see the Coriolanus she’d looked up at with such soft love? Would she recognise the same man who’d bent over her hand last night, his touch light as a feather? The thought crossed his mind, and he smiled faintly – he knew her gentle heart well enough to guess she would not flinch. She would see the integrity under his stubbornness, the fact he never lied, never cheated, never stooped to the kind of meanness that would make true love wither. He was no slave to petty vices, no weak fool like Sykes – he was a man she could respect, no matter what rough edges he had.

A tramping of feet and murmur of voices rumbled from the yard outside. Moore walked to the window, Helstone at his shoulder, both of them peeking out from behind the frame, and shared a quick, cynical little smile at the sight that met their eyes.

A loud, performative cough echoed, followed by a sharp “Whisht!” to hush the crowd. Moore pushed the casement open an inch, to hear every word.

“Joseph Scott?” came a snuffling, pious voice from the yard. “Might we inquire if your master be within, and might we speak to him?”

“He’s within, ay,” Joe called back, nonchalant.

“Would you be so good as to tell him that twelve gentlemen wish to see him, for a purpose.”

Joe came back inside, grinning. “Twelve gentlemen wanting to see ye ‘for a purpose,’ sir.”

“Good,” Moore said, and he whistled once, soft and quiet, to signal Sugden to stand by. He strolled out into the yard, one hand in his coat pocket, the other tucked in his waistcoat, his cap brim pulled low over his eyes, a faint glint of scorn dancing in his gaze. Twelve men stood waiting, some in their shirt sleeves, some in blue work aprons, two leading the pack: one small, dapper, strutting man with a turned-up nose, and the other broad-shouldered, with a demure, untrusting cat-like look in his eyes, a wooden leg propped on the cobblestones, a thick crutch under his arm – Moses Barraclough himself, a sly leer on his lips.

“Good morning, Mr. Barraclough,” Moore said, polite and debonair.

“Peace be unto you!” Moses intoned, shutting his half-lidded eyes for full pious effect.

“I’m obliged to you, peace is a very fine thing, I wish nothing more for myself,” Moore said. “But I don’t imagine that’s all you’ve come to say to me, is it? Peace isn’t exactly your purpose today, is it?”

“Our purpose may sound strange to the ears of the children of this world, who are wiser in their generation than the children of light,” Moses whined.

“Get to the point, then,” Moore said.

Moses opened his mouth to speak, but the other leading man stepped forward, impatient to show off his fine, grand speech. “Mr. Moore,” he began, drawing out every word slow and deliberate, as if his phrases were the height of elegant rhetoric, “it may be said that reason, not peace, is our purpose. We come to request you hear reason, and if you refuse, sir, I must warn you in very decided terms that measures will be had recourse to, that will bring you to a sense of the foolishness that guides your proceedings. You are a foreigner, sir, come from a distant hemisphere, cast out upon the cliffs of Albion, and you do not understand our ways, the ways of the working classes. If you would but do the sensible thing, give up this mill, and go straight home to where you belong, no harm will come to you. What do you say, lads?”

The rest of the deputation cheered, “Hear, hear!”

Joe Scott snickered behind Moore’s shoulder. “Brayvo, Noah o’ Tim’s! Moses’ll niver top that speech! Cliffs of Albion! Did you come from the Antarctic, maister?”

Moses glowered at Noah, and pushed past him to speak again, his voice suddenly grave and earnest. “Before you came and set up your tent among us, Mr. Moore, we all lived in peace, in loving kindness. I’m a tailor, not a cloth dresser, but I am a feeling man, and I cannot bear to see my brethren oppressed. I urge you, sir, to cast aside your new infernal machinery, and hire more hands.”

“And what if I don’t take your advice, Mr. Barraclough?” Moore asked.

“The Lord pardon you! The Lord soften your heart!” Moses cried, lifting his hands to the sky.

“I saw you drunk in the gutter by Stilbro’ Market a week ago,” Moore said, sharp and clear. “You are no more a friend to the poor than I am. You’re a hypocrite, stirring up rioting for your own petty selfish ambition, you and Noah there both. The rest of the lads behind you are honest men, just led astray, but you two are scoundrels.”

He lifted two fingers to his lips and whistled shrill and loud. Sugden stepped forward, his staff in one hand, the warrant in the other.

“You were at Stilbro’ Moor, Barraclough,” Moore said, nodding at him. “You wore a mask, you knocked one of my men down. Sugden – arrest him.”

The crowd cried out and surged forward to rescue him, but Moore pulled his hand out from his coat, holding a pistol steady in his grip.

“Both barrels are loaded,” he called out, his voice unshaking. “Stand back.”
