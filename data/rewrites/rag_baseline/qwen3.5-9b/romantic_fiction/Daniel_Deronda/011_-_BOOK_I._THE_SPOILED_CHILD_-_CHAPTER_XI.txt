The initiation of any acquaintance, whether with a person or an object, begins with the desperate attempt to sketch a definitive outline for one's ignorance. For Gwendolen, Mr. Grandcourt's request for introduction possessed no suddenness; it was a predictable tide. Yet, when Lord Brackenshaw stepped aside to allow the prefigured stranger to advance, and she found herself face-to-face with the living reality, a sharp shock struck her. It flushed her cheeks and deepened with a vexation born of her own awareness. The impact stemmed from a total reversal of expectation: Grandcourt could scarcely have been more unlike the caricatures she had constructed in her mind.

He stood slightly taller than herself, their eyes seemingly aligned on a horizontal plane. There was not the faintest smile upon his face as he regarded her, nor a trace of self-consciousness or anxiety in his posture. As he raised his hat, he revealed an extensive baldness fringed only by sparse, reddish-blonde hair, yet simultaneously displayed a hand of perfect proportion. The line of features from brow to chin, undisguised by beard, was decidedly handsome, adhering strictly to the vertical with only moderate deviations. Even the slight whisker hung perpendicularly. It was impossible for a human visage to be freer from grimace or solicitous wriggling; equally, it was improbable for a waking man to appear less animated. The stereotypical Englishman, drawing himself up from a bow into rigid stiffness, assenting with severity, projects a suppressed vivacity, hinting at violent release once freed from parade. Grandcourt, however, possessed no such rigidity; his bearing inclined rather toward the flaccid. His complexion held a faded fairness reminiscent of an actress stripped of artificial rouge and powder. His long, narrow gray eyes expressed nothing but a profound indifference.

Attempts at immediate description are futile; who can encapsulate a human being in a single breath? Even in presentation, we merely commence the knowledge of appearance, a process requiring completion through countless impressions under varying circumstances. We recognize the alphabet but remain unsure of the language. Gwendolen perceived this truth illuminated by a prepared contrast within the first minutes of their meeting. They were summarized in the stark realization: *He is not ridiculous.*

Immediately thereafter, Lord Brackenshaw departed, and what is termed conversation commenced. Its primary and constant element was that Grandcourt looked at Gwendolen persistently with a slightly exploring gaze, devoid of any shift in expression, while she returned his attention only occasionally with a flash of observation, softened marginally by coquetry. Following her replies, there ensued a pause, variable in duration, before he spoke again.

"I used to think archery was a great bore," Grandcourt began. His voice carried a fine accent, undercut by a certain broken drawl, suggestive of a distinguished figure suffering from a distinguished chill.

"Are you converted today?" asked Gwendolen.

*(A pause ensued, during which she conjured various degrees and modes of opinion regarding herself that Grandcourt might entertain.)*

"Yes, since I saw you shooting. In matters of this sort, one generally observes people missing and simpering."

"I suppose you are a first-rate shot with a rifle."

*(Another pause, during which Gwendolen, having conducted a rapid survey of Grandcourt, mentally drafted a brief, graphic description of him for an indefinite audience.)*

"I have left off shooting."

"Oh, then you are a formidable person. Those who accomplish things once and abandon them make one feel exceedingly contemptible, as if one were utilizing discarded fashions. I trust you have not abandoned all follies, for I practice a great many."

"What do you term 'follies'?"

"Well, in general, I believe whatever is agreeable is classified as a folly. However, I understand you have not ceased hunting, I hear."

*(Here, Gwendolen recalled reports concerning Grandcourt's standing and concluded he was the most aristocratically appearing man she had ever encountered.)*

"One must do something."

"And do you care for the turf—or is that among the pursuits you have relinquished?"

*(In this pause, Gwendolen reasoned that a man of extremely calm, cold manners might prove less disagreeable as a husband than others, unlikely to interfere with his wife's preferences.)*

"I run a horse now and then; but I do not engage in the sport as some men do. Are you fond of horses?"

"Yes, indeed; I never enjoy my life so fully as when I am on horseback, having a great gallop. I think of nothing. I only feel myself strong and happy."

*(Gwendolen paused, wondering if Grandcourt approved of her statement, yet reassuring herself that she would not disguise her tastes.)*

"Do you like danger?"

"I do not know. When I am on horseback, I never consider danger. It seems to me that if I broke my bones, I should not feel it. I should go at anything that comes in my way."

"You would perhaps enjoy tiger-hunting or pig-sticking. I witnessed some of that for a season or two in the East. Everything here is poor stuff compared to that."

"_You_ are fond of danger, then?"

*(Gwendolen speculated on the probability that men of the coldest manners were the most adventurous, feeling the strength of her own insight as if the question required judicial decision.)*

"One must have something or other. But one gets accustomed to it."

"I begin to think I am very fortunate, because everything is new to me: it is only that I cannot get enough of it. I am not used to anything except being dull, which I should like to leave off as you have left off shooting."

*(It occurred to Gwendolen that a man of cold, distinguished manners might potentially be a dull companion; yet, on the other hand, she considered that most persons were dull, that she had not observed husbands to function as companions—and that, after all, she was not going to accept Grandcourt.)*

"Why are you dull?"

"This is a dreadful neighborhood. There is nothing to be done in it. That is why I practiced my archery."

*(Gwendolen reflected that the life of an unmarried woman, unable to move freely and lacking command over anything, must inevitably become dull through all degrees of comparison as time progresses.)*

"You have made yourself queen of it. I imagine you will carry the first prize."

"I do not know that. I have great rivals. Did you not observe how well Miss Arrowpoint shot?"

*(Gwendolen thought that men had been known to choose someone other than the woman they most admired, recalling several instances of this phenomenon in novels.)*

"Miss Arrowpoint. No—that is, yes."

"Shall we go now and hear what the scoring declares? Everyone is proceeding to the other end now—shall we join them? I think my uncle is looking toward me. He perhaps wishes to speak with me."

Gwendolen found relief in altering the situation. Not that the tête-à-tête was entirely disagreeable to her; rather, while it persisted, she apparently could not dispel the unwonted flush in her cheeks and the sense of surprise that rendered her less mistress of herself than usual. This Mr. Grandcourt, who seemed to feel his own importance far more acutely than hers—a sort of unreasonableness few of us can tolerate—must not presume that he was of great moment to her, or that because others speculated on him as a desirable match, she placed herself wholly at his beck. How Grandcourt had filled the pauses would become more evident later.

"You have just missed the gold arrow, Gwendolen," said Mr. Gascoigne. "Miss Juliet Fenn scores eight points above you."

"I am very glad to hear it. I should have felt that I was making myself too disagreeable—taking the best of everything," said Gwendolen, quite easily.

It was impossible to harbor jealousy toward Juliet Fenn, a girl as mediocre as a midday market in every attribute save her archery and plainness, in which latter trait she was noticeable, much like her father: underhanged and possessing a receding brow resembling that of the more intelligent fish. (Certainly, considering the significance accorded to such accidents in female offspring, marriageable men—or what the new English term "intending bridegrooms"—should examine themselves dispassionately in the mirror, since their natural selection of a mate prettier than themselves is not guaranteed to negate the effect of their own ugliness.)

A lively movement rippled through the mingling groups, carrying the conversation along with it.
