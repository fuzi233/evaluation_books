For Mr. Bell, the vision of Helstone had taken root in the quiet hours following his conversation with Mr. Lennox, blooming uncontrollably throughout the sleepless nights that followed. In these dreams, he returned to the college where he once served as a tutor, now holding the distinguished rank of Fellow. Again, it was a long vacation; again, he was a guest at the home of his newly married friend, the proud husband and happy Vicar of Helstone. Together, they leaped across babbling brooks, impossible jumps that seemed to suspend them whole days in the air, defying gravity and logic. Time and space dissolved, leaving only the emotional truth of the moment. Every event was measured not by clockwork or geography, but by the depth of feeling.

In this realm, the trees blazed with the glorious crimson and gold of autumn, heavy with leaves that whispered secrets of the past. The warm, heady scents of flowers and herbs drifted through the open windows, filling the senses with a sweetness that felt ancient and real. Within the house, the young wife moved with a specific, poignant grace—a mixture of mild annoyance at her station regarding wealth, tempered by a deep, abiding pride in her handsome and devoted husband. This was exactly as Mr. Bell had observed her nearly forty years prior, a ghost from a lifetime ago.

The dream was so vivid, so indistinguishable from reality, that when he finally awoke, his waking life seemed like a phantom illusion. Where was he? In a cramped, handsomely furnished room of a London hotel. Who were those who had spoken to him moments before, moved around him, and touched his skin? They were gone—dead, buried, lost forever, vanishing beyond the reach of any earthly eternity. He was an old man, so recently exulting in the full, roaring strength of manhood. The crushing solitude of his existence became unbearable to contemplate. With a start, he rose, hurrying to dress, desperate to bury himself in the frantic preparations for breakfast at Harley Street.

Yet, the lawyer's business awaited him, a mountain of documents that caused Margaret's eyes to widen in terror and her lips to turn pale. As page after page was read, fate seemed to decree that every single piece of evidence capable of clearing Frederick's name slipped from her grasp, crumbling into dust. Even Mr. Lennox, whose professional voice had always been steady and regulated, softened. His tone grew tender, almost broken, as he approached the final extinguishing of their last hope.

Margaret had known the likely outcome before this hour, but the relentless, minute-by-minute detailing of each successive failure drained the light from her spirit until she could hold back no longer. Tears fell freely. Mr. Lennox paused, lowering his gaze from the papers.

"I had better not proceed further," he said, his voice thick with concern. "It was a foolish proposal on my part." He looked at her, offering a gentle gesture. "Lieutenant Hale..." He hesitated, granting him the title of the service from which he had been so harshly cast aside, a small kindness that seemed to soothe Margaret's raw nerves. "...Lieutenant Hale is happy now. More secure in fortune and future prospects than he could ever have hoped within the navy. And he has, doubtless, adopted his wife's country as his own."

"That is it," Margaret whispered, forcing a tremulous smile. "It seems so selfish in me to regret it... and yet, he is lost to me, and I am so terribly lonely."

Mr. Lennox turned the pages of his folder, silently wishing that he possessed the riches and prosperity he believed awaited him someday. Mr. Bell blew his nose, remaining silent beside them. In a few minutes, however, Margaret appeared to regain her usual composure. She thanked Mr. Lennox with profound courtesy, all the more graciously because she knew her behavior might lead him to believe he had needlessly pained her. Yet, it was pain she would not have been without.

Then, Mr. Bell stepped forward to bid her farewell.

"Margaret!" he said, fumbling slightly with his gloves. "I am planning to go down to Helstone tomorrow to inspect the old place. Would you care to join me? Or would it cause you too great a pain? Speak plainly, do not fear."

"Oh, Mr. Bell," she began, but her voice failed her. Instead, she reached out and took his old, gout-ridden hand, pressing a kiss to it.

"Come, come; that is quite enough," he said, flushing red with awkwardness. "I suppose your Aunt Shaw will trust you with me. We shall depart tomorrow morning and arrive there by two o'clock, I fancy. We'll take a snack, then order dinner at the little inn—the Lennard Arms, it used to be—and wander off to get an appetite in the forest. Can you manage it, Margaret? I know it will be a trial for both of us, but it will be a pleasure to me, at least. There, we shall dine—it will be nothing but doe-venison, if we can even procure it—and then I shall take my nap while you go out and visit old friends. I shall return you safely and sound, barring any railway accidents, and I shall insure your life for a thousand pounds before we start, which may offer some solace to your relations. Otherwise, I promise to bring you back to Mrs. Shaw by lunchtime on Friday. So, if you agree, I shall just ascend to propose it formally."

"It is no use for me to try and explain how much I shall enjoy it," Margaret murmured, tears still glistening in her eyes.

"Well, then, prove your gratitude by keeping those fountains dry for the next two days," Mr. Bell chided gently. "If you do not, I shall feel quite queasy about the lachrymal ducts, and I dislike that sensation."

"I won't cry a single drop," Margaret promised, winking vigorously to shake the moisture from her lashes and forcing a radiant smile.

"There is my good girl." With that, they ascended together to settle the arrangements. Margaret stood in a state of almost trembling eagerness as Mr. Bell discussed his plan with her aunt Shaw. First, the aunt was startled; then, doubtful and perplexed; and finally, she yielded—not necessarily to her own conviction, but to the rough, forceful charm of Mr. Bell's words. To the very end, whether it was right or wrong, proper or improper, she could not find peace in her heart until Margaret's safe return and the happy fulfillment of the project gave her enough certainty to declare, "She was sure it had been a very kind thought of Mr. Bell's, and precisely what she herself had been wishing for Margaret, affording her the very change she required after all the anxious times she had endured."
