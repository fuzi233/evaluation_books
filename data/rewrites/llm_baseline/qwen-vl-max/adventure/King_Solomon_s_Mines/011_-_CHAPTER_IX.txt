There was no need to recount every detail of our journey to Loo. It took two full days, following Solomon’s Great Road—a wide and steady path that cut straight through the heart of Kukuanaland. As we traveled, the land grew richer, the fields more abundant, and the kraals—each surrounded by broad bands of cultivated soil—more frequent. All were built like the first camp we had seen, guarded by strong detachments of warriors. In Kukuanaland, as among the Germans, Zulus, and Masai, every able-bodied man is a soldier. The entire population can be called upon for war, whether offensive or defensive.

We were overtaken by thousands of warriors marching toward Loo for the great annual review and festival. They moved in proud formations, their plumes waving, spears gleaming, shields polished. I had never seen troops so magnificent.

At sunset on the second day, we halted atop a range of hills where the road passed. Below us lay Loo—an immense native town, perhaps five miles around, with outlying kraals extending like military camps on grand occasions. A curious horseshoe-shaped hill stood about two miles north, one we would soon come to know well. The town was beautifully situated, bisected by a river that ran through its center, crossed at several points by bridges—the same river we had glimpsed from Sheba’s Breasts. Sixty or seventy miles away, three snow-capped mountains rose sharply from the plain, forming a triangle. Unlike Sheba’s Breasts, these peaks were sheer and jagged, not smooth and rounded.

Infadoos noticed us gazing at them and spoke up.

“The road ends there,” he said, pointing to the mountains known as the “Three Witches.”

“Why does it end?” I asked.

“Who knows?” he shrugged. “The mountains are filled with caves, and between them lies a great pit. That is where the wise men of old came to find what they sought—and now, it is where our kings are buried in the Place of Death.”

“What did they seek?” I pressed.

“I do not know,” he replied. “My lords who have fallen from the Stars should know.” He glanced at me quickly—evidently, he knew more than he would say.

“Yes,” I said, “you’re right. From the Stars, we learn many things. I’ve heard the wise men came here for bright stones, pretty playthings, and yellow iron.”

“My lord is wise,” Infadoos answered coldly. “I am but a child, unable to speak with my lord on such matters. You must speak with Gagool the old, at the king’s place. She is as wise as my lord.” And he walked away.

As soon as he was gone, I turned to the others and pointed at the mountains.

“There,” I said, “are Solomon’s diamond mines.”

Umbopa stood nearby, lost in one of his silent reveries. He caught my words.

“Yes, Macumazahn,” he said in Zulu, “the diamonds are surely there. And you shall have them, since white men love toys and money.”

“How do you know that?” I asked sharply, uneasy at his cryptic ways.

He laughed. “I dreamed it last night, white men.” Then he turned and left.

“Well,” Sir Henry said, “what’s our dark friend trying to tell us? He knows more than he lets on. By the way, Quatermain—has he heard anything of my brother?”

“Nothing,” I replied. “He’s asked everyone he’s met, but all say no white man has ever been seen here before.”

“Do you think he made it?” Good asked. “We reached this place by miracle. How could he have come without the map?”

“I don’t know,” Sir Henry said grimly. “But somehow, I feel I’ll find him.”

The sun sank slowly, then suddenly—like a physical force—darkness swept over the land. There was no twilight here, no gentle fade from day to night. One moment it was light; the next, the world was wrapped in shadow. But not for long. In the west, a glow appeared—then silver rays, and finally the full moon rose, flooding the plain with radiant light. Its beams pierced the darkness, turning the earth into a shimmering expanse. We stood in awe, watching the stars grow pale beneath the moon’s brilliance, feeling uplifted by beauty too vast to describe. My life has been rough, but I am grateful for moments like this—one of them being the sight of that moon rising over Kukuanaland.

Our silence was broken by Infadoos.

“If my lords are rested, we may proceed to Loo. A hut awaits you tonight. The moon is bright—no danger of falling.”

We agreed. Within an hour, we reached the outskirts of the city. The sprawl of fires lit the landscape, making the town seem endless. Good, ever fond of wit, dubbed it “Unlimited Loo.” Soon we came to a moat with a drawbridge. Arms rattled, a sentry bellowed a challenge. Infadoos gave a password I couldn’t catch. It was answered with a salute, and we passed through the central street of the grass city.

After nearly half an hour of walking past countless huts, Infadoos stopped at a small cluster of dwellings surrounding a courtyard of powdered limestone. These, he said, were our “poor” quarters.

Inside, each of us had a hut. They were better than any we’d seen—each with a comfortable bed of tanned hides laid over mats of aromatic grass. Food was ready: roasted meat and mealie cobs served on wooden platters by young women of striking appearance, who bowed deeply as they presented the meals.

We ate and drank. Then, at our request, the beds were moved into one hut—a precaution that brought amused glances from the attendants. Exhausted, we fell asleep.

When we woke, the sun was high. The female servants, unbothered by modesty, were already inside, ordered to help us “make ready.”

“Make ready indeed,” Good grumbled. “With just a flannel shirt and boots, it won’t take long. Quatermain, ask for my trousers.”

I asked. I was told they’d already been taken to the king—he would see us soon.

To the surprise of the girls, we insisted on dressing ourselves. Good even shaved the right side of his face again—the left, now thick with whiskers, we warned him not to touch. For the rest of us, a good wash and combing sufficed. Sir Henry’s golden hair now reached his shoulders—he looked more like an ancient Dane than ever. My own grizzled beard was fully an inch long, far beyond my usual limit.

By the time we’d eaten breakfast and smoked a pipe, Infadoos arrived with a message: Twala, the king, was ready to receive us—if we wished to come.

We replied we’d prefer to wait until the sun climbed higher—we were still weary from travel. It’s always wise, when dealing with uncivilized peoples, not to rush. They often mistake politeness for fear. Though eager to meet the king, we sat and waited an hour, using the time to prepare gifts: the Winchester rifle once used by poor Ventvögel, and some beads.

We decided to give the rifle and ammunition to the king, and the beads to his wives and courtiers. We’d already given a few to Infadoos and Scragga, who adored them—never having seen such things before.

At length, we declared ourselves ready. Umbopa carried the rifle and beads as we followed Infadoos toward the audience.

After walking a short distance, we came to a vast enclosure—fifty times larger than the one housing our huts. It covered six or seven acres. Along the outer fence stood rows of huts, homes of the king’s wives. Opposite the gate, across the open space, stood a large hut—Twala’s residence. The rest was bare ground, except for the eight thousand warriors standing in perfect silence, their spears gleaming, plumes fluttering, ox-hide shields polished.

In front of the king’s hut stood several stools. At Infadoos’s signal, we sat on three of them. Umbopa stood behind us. Infadoos took position near the door.

We waited—ten minutes, more—in complete silence, aware that every eye of the eight thousand warriors was fixed upon us. It was tense, but we held our composure.

Then the hut door opened. A towering figure stepped out, draped in a tiger-skin cloak. Behind him came Scragga and a strange, shriveled creature wrapped in fur, resembling a monkey. The giant seated himself. Scragga stood behind. The creature crawled into the shade and crouched low.

Still silence.

Then the giant removed his cloak and rose—a terrifying sight. He was enormous, with a face so repulsive we had never seen its like. His lips were thick, his nose flat. One eye gleamed black; the other was a hollow. His expression was cruel, sensual. A plume of white ostrich feathers crowned his head. He wore chain armor, ox-tail ornaments at waist and knee. In his hand, a massive spear. Around his neck, a thick gold torque. On his forehead, a single, dull, uncut diamond shone dimly.

Silence again—until the king raised his spear.

Instantly, eight thousand spears rose in answer. From eight thousand throats roared the royal salute: “_Koom!_” Three times it echoed, shaking the ground like thunder.

“Be humble, O people,” piped a thin voice from the shadow—coming from the monkey-like figure.

“It is the king!” thundered the crowd.

“Be humble, O people—it is the king!”

Then silence returned.

Suddenly, a shield clattered to the ground.

Twala turned his single eye toward the noise.

“Come here,” he said coldly.

A young warrior stepped forward.

“It was your shield that fell, clumsy dog. Do you mock me before these strangers from the Stars? What do you say?”

The youth paled.

“It was chance, O Calf of the Black Cow.”

“Then pay for that chance. You have made me look foolish. Prepare to die.”

“I am the king’s ox,” he whispered.

“Scragga,” the king roared, “show me how you wield your spear. Kill this fool.”

Scragga grinned, lifted his spear. The victim covered his eyes. We froze.

“Once… twice…” the boy waved the weapon—and struck. The spear emerged a foot from his back. He threw up his hands and fell dead.

A murmur rippled through the crowd—then died. The body lay there. Sir Henry leapt up, swearing violently, then collapsed back, overwhelmed by silence.

“The thrust was good,” the king said. “Take him away.”

Four men carried the corpse off.

“Cover the blood,” the thin voice cried. “The king’s word is spoken. The king’s doom is done!”

A girl brought a jar of lime and scattered it over the stain.

Sir Henry seethed with rage. We barely kept him quiet.

“Sit down,” I whispered. “Our lives depend on it.”

He obeyed.

Twala remained silent until the blood was hidden. Then he addressed us.

“White people,” he said, “who come here from lands unknown, greeting.”

“Greeting, Twala, King of the Kukuanas,” I replied.

“Where do you come from? What do you seek?”

“We come from the Stars. Ask not how. We come to see this land.”

“And that man beside you—does he also come from the Stars?”

“Indeed. There are people of your color in the heavens. But ask not of things too high for you, Twala.”

“You speak loudly, people of the Stars,” the king replied, uneasily. “Remember—Stars are far. You are here. What if I make you like the one they bore away?”

I laughed aloud, though my heart was heavy.

“O king,” I said, “be careful. Walk on hot stones, lest you burn your feet. Hold the spear by the handle, lest you cut your hands. Touch one hair of our heads, and destruction will fall upon you. Have not Infadoos and Scragga”—pointing to the boy, who was scrubbing blood from his spear—“told you what kind of men we are? Have you seen the like of us?” I gestured to Good, certain the king had never seen anyone quite so bizarre.

“It is true,” the king admitted, studying Good.

“Have they told you how we strike death from afar?”

“They have. But I do not believe. Show me. Kill one of those men yonder,” he pointed across the kraal, “and I will believe.”

“No,” I said. “We kill only in justice. But if you wish, send a young ox through the gate. Before it runs twenty paces, I will strike it dead.”

“No,” the king laughed. “Kill a man, and I will believe.”

“Very well, O king,” I said coolly. “Walk across the open space. Before your feet reach the gate, you shall be dead. Or—if you prefer—send your son Scragga.” (At that moment, I would have gladly shot him.)

Scragga let out a howl and fled into the hut.

Twala frowned.

“Bring a young ox,” he said.

Two men ran off.

“Now, Sir Henry,” I said. “You shoot. I want to show this brute I’m not the only magician here.”

Sir Henry took his “express” rifle, prepared.

“I hope I hit,” he groaned.

“You must,” I said. “If you miss the first barrel, use the second. Aim for 150 yards. Wait until the beast turns broadside.”

We waited. Then we saw an ox charging through the gate. It paused, turned, bellowed.

“Now!” I whispered.

The rifle rose.

Bang! _Thud!_ The ox fell, kicking, dead—shot through the ribs. The semi-hollow bullet had done its work. A gasp of astonishment rose from the crowd.

I turned calmly.

“Have I lied, O king?”

“No,” he said, awed. “It is truth.”

“Listen, Twala. You have seen. Now know—we come in peace, not war. See,” I said, holding up the Winchester repeater. “Here is a hollow staff that lets you kill as we do—but I place a charm upon it. You shall not kill a man with it. If you aim at a man, it will kill you. Watch—I will prove it.”

I instructed the king to have a soldier stand forty paces away and plant a spear upright, blade facing us.

It was done.

“Now,” I said, “I will break that spear.”

I took careful aim and fired. The bullet struck the blade—shattering it into fragments.

Another wave of astonishment swept through the crowd.

“Now, Twala,” I said, handing him the rifle, “we give you this magic tube. I will teach you how to use it—but beware. Do not turn the magic of the Stars against a man of earth.”

The king took the rifle cautiously and placed it at his feet. As he did, the wizened figure crept from the shadows. It moved on all fours, but when it reached the king, it rose—then threw off its furry covering.

Revealed was a woman of terrible age, her face so shriveled it seemed no larger than a child’s. Deep yellow wrinkles formed a grotesque mask. Her mouth was a sunken slit; her chin curved outward sharply. No nose—only a hollow. Yet her eyes—large, black, fiery—glowed like jewels in a charnel house. Her skull was bald, yellow, and constantly twitching like a cobra’s hood.

She stood still. Then, with a clawed hand, she touched the king’s shoulder and began to speak in a thin, piercing voice:

“Listen, O king! Listen, O warriors! Listen, O mountains, plains, rivers—home of the Kukuana race! Listen, O skies, sun, rain, storm, mist! Listen, O men, women, youths, maidens, unborn babes! Listen, all things that live and die! Listen, all dead things that shall rise again—only to die! Listen—the spirit of life is in me, and I prophesy. I prophesy! I prophesy!”

Her voice trailed into a faint wail. Fear gripped us all.

“Blood! Blood! Blood! Rivers of blood—everywhere! I see it, smell it, taste it—salt! It flows red on the ground, rains from the sky!

“Footsteps! Footsteps! Footsteps! The tread of the white man comes from afar. It shakes the earth. The earth trembles before its master.

“Blood is good—red blood is bright. No scent like new-shed blood. Lions will lap it and roar. Vultures will bathe their wings and shriek with joy.

“I am old! I am old! I have seen much blood—_ha, ha!_ But I shall see more before I die—and laugh. How old am I? Your fathers knew me. Their fathers knew me. Their fathers’ fathers’ fathers knew me. I have seen the white man. I know his desires.

“I am old—but the mountains are older. Who made the great road? Who painted the rocks? Who raised the Three Silent Ones yonder, who gaze across the pit?” She pointed at the mountains.

“You do not know—but I do. A white people came before you. They will come again when you are gone. They will eat you up and destroy you. _Yea! yea! yea!_

“And why did they come? The White Ones, the Terrible Ones, skilled in magic and learning, strong and unswerving? What is that bright stone on your forehead, O king? Whose hands forged the iron on your chest? You do not know—but I do. I, the Old One. I, the Wise One. I, the _Isanusi_, the witch doctor!”

She turned her vulture-head toward us.

“What do you seek, white men of the Stars—ah, yes, of the Stars? Do you search for a lost one? You will not find him here. He is not here. Never in ages has a white foot trod this land—except once. And I remember: he left only to die.

“You come for bright stones. I know it. You will find them when the blood dries. But will you return? Or stay with me? _Ha! ha! ha!_

“And you—thou with dark skin and proud bearing,” she pointed at Umbopa, “who art thou? What dost thou seek? Not shining stones, not yellow metal—those you leave to the ‘white men from the Stars.’ I know you. I smell the blood in your heart. Strip off the girdle—”

Her features contorted. She fell to the ground, foaming, seized by an epileptic fit. She was carried into the hut.

The king rose, trembling. He waved his hand. Instantly, the regiments filed away. In ten minutes, only we, the king, and a few attendants remained.

“White people,” he said, “it crosses my mind to kill you. Gagool has spoken strange words. What say you?”
