One crisp sunlit morning, Mrs. Bretton swept into my room without preamble, requesting I open my drawers and show her my full collection of dresses. I obeyed, no questions, no protest, letting her run a decisive, experienced hand over every hem and fold.

“That will do,” she declared, shutting the last drawer with a firm click. “You must have a new one.”

She was gone before I could frame a reply, and returned ten minutes later with a dressmaker on her arm, who measured me for fabric before I had quite processed what was happening. “I’m following my own taste here,” my godmother said, a glint of unshakable amusement in her eye. “No arguments, Lucy, I’m getting my way on this little matter.”

Two days later, the finished dress arrived: soft, rose-pink, the color of wild apple blossom in May.

“That cannot be for me,” I blurted, stumbling back a step. I felt as though she had handed me the elaborate embroidered robes of a high-ranking Chinese court lady, a garment entirely too bright, too foreign, too un-Lucy for my quiet, shadowed life.

“We shall see about that,” she smiled, unassailable, as she added with that tone that never brooked pushback: “Mark my words. You will wear it this very evening.”

I was certain I would not. I thought no force on earth could make me slip that bright fabric over my shoulders. Pink was a color that had never known me, that I had never dared to claim. But my godmother had already settled our plans: we were to attend a grand concert that same night, hosted in the great gilded hall of Villette’s most prestigious musical society. Advanced students from the city Conservatoire would perform, followed by a charity lottery for the poor, and as the final, glittering surprise, the King, Queen, and young Prince of Labassecour were all to be in attendance. Graham had sent the tickets over, urging us to dress with proper respect for royalty, and to be fully ready and waiting by seven o’clock.

At six, I was ushered up to the dressing chamber, and without the slightest show of force, I found my will gently overruled, my protests melting away under their cheerful, unrelenting care. The pink dress slipped on, softened around the neck and sleeves by delicate draperies of black lace, and they declared me properly en grande tenue, pushing me to face the mirror. I met my own reflection with trembling nerves, and turned away almost immediately, too flustered to linger.

Seven o’clock struck. Dr. John Bretton arrived, and we trooped down the stairs. Mrs. Bretton wore a deep brown velvet gown, its heavy, dignified folds the very picture of quiet grace. I walked in her shadow, searingly envious of that dark, unassuming majesty. Graham stood in the drawing room doorway, and my heart twisted with unease, certain he would think I had decked myself out in bright colors deliberately to draw his eye.

“Lucy, these are for you,” he said, pressing a small, sweet-smelling bouquet into my hands. He said no word about the dress, only gave me a soft, kind smile and a small, satisfied nod, and in that single gesture all my shame and fear of ridicule melted away. The dress, I soon noticed, was cut with perfect simplicity, no frills, no over-the-top furbelows: it was only the light, bright tint that had scared me, and with Graham’s quiet approval, my own eye soon grew happy with it.

People who go to public amusements every night can never know the giddy, gilded thrill of a first gala for someone who has barely ever left the quiet walls of a schoolroom. I had no grand expectations for the music, but the drive to the hall was pure magic: the snug warmth of our closed carriage on the crisp, clear night, the joy of riding out with two companions so cheerful, so unreservedly kind, as if I were their own blood kin. Stars glinted fitfully through the tree boughs over the avenue, and when we rolled out onto the open high road, the whole vast night sky stretched above us, dark and spangled. We passed through the city gates, past the posted guards who made a playful show of inspecting our carriage, a small silly formality that made us all laugh.

We drove through Villette’s brightest streets, shops glowing with gaslight, the pavements thronged with far more life than you would ever see at high noon. As I stared, the quiet shadow of Rue Fossette crossed my mind: the walled school garden, the great empty classrooms where, at this very hour, I would usually wander alone, watching the stars through the high uncurtained windows, listening to the monotonous reader intone the evening pious lecture in the refectory. I would be back to that quiet solitary life soon, I knew, and the thought laid a soft, sobering weight across all the bright joy of the present.

By then we were part of a long stream of carriages all heading the same direction, and soon the great illuminated facade of the concert hall blazed up before us. I had never stepped foot in any public place of entertainment before, so every new sight felt like a fairy tale come to life. We alighted under a bustling crowded portico, and climbed a wide, shallow staircase carpeted entirely in deep crimson, leading to tall solemn doors draped in the same rich fabric.

Graham worked some small bit of magic to make those doors roll silently open, and the great hall revealed itself to me: wide, high, domed, its sweeping circular walls finished in what looked like burnished dead gold, trimmed with snow-white alabaster garlands and gilded leaf wreaths. Every drapery, every carpet, every cushion was that same rich, warm crimson. Hanging from the center of the dome was a great mass of crystal, flashing like scattered rainbows, so dazzling I half expected a dark genie’s hand to hover in the scented glowing air, guarding a treasure plundered from some eastern sultan’s palace.

We moved forward, half lost in the crowd, until we rounded a pillar and nearly ran headlong into what looked like a small group of strangers: a handsome middle-aged lady in dark velvet, a tall gentleman with the finest face I had ever seen, and a quiet third woman in a pink dress and black lace mantle. I took them all in for a split second before I realized we were looking at ourselves in a great full-length mirror set between two pillars. For the first and only time in my life, I saw myself exactly as others saw me. It was not a perfectly flattering view, but it was not nearly as bad as I had feared, and I turned away quietly grateful.

We reached our seats, which gave us a perfect view of the vast, warm, cheerful hall, already nearly full of a splendid, elegant crowd. The local women were not all classically beautiful, but their gowns were immaculate, and every one of them carried herself with that easy, practiced public grace that makes even the most ordinary person look regal on a gala night. Scattered here and there were specimens of that distinct, sculptural Labassecour beauty I had never seen at home in England: solid, rounded, no sharp angles, still and stately as marble caryatids, their faces calm and passionless as a polar snowfield. Right in our direct line of sight sat the most striking of these beauties, a Juno-like blonde in pale blue satin, so cold and self-contained she did not even flicker an eyelash at the hundreds of gazes turned her way.

I noticed Graham’s attention linger on her, and I leaned over to whisper in his ear. “For heaven’s sake, guard your heart well. You could die at that lady’s feet, and she would never care for you in return.”

“Fair enough,” he laughed. “But what if that very grand insensibility is the thing that makes me want to worship her all the more? You know nothing of these romantic impulses, Lucy. Mamma! I’m in terrible danger of falling in love.”

“As if that’s any of my concern,” Mrs. Bretton sniffed, not looking up from adjusting her gloves.

“Cruelty!” he mock-lamented. “No man ever had a more unsentimental mother. She never even considers the possibility that I might bring home a daughter-in-law one day.”

“That’s only because you’ve been threatening to do it for ten years,” she shot back. “You were yelling ‘Mamma, I’m getting married soon!’ before you were even done wearing children’s jackets.”

“One day it will happen,” he grinned. “I’ll head off like Jacob or Esau, and bring home a wife from the local girls here, just to shock you.”

“At your own peril, John Graham,” she said, but her eyes were crinkling with amusement. “That little cottage of ours will never hold two mistresses, especially not a great big statuesque doll in satin and wax.”

The good-natured bickering went on while the hall filled to bursting, every last seat packed tight, and the great temporary platform at the front began to swarm with life. Around two grand pianos at its center, a flock of young Conservatoire girls in white muslin gathered, marshalled by two men I recognized. One was M. Josef Emanuel, the long-haired, artistic piano teacher who visited Madame Beck’s school twice a week to give lessons to the wealthiest pupils. The other, strutting about bossing all hundred girls as if they were his personal army, was his fiery little half-brother M. Paul Emmanuel.

I bit back a smile watching him. He was so perfectly in his element, ordering everyone around with absolute, unapologetic authority, even though I knew full well he could barely tell one musical note from another. It was only his naive love of being in charge that had dragged him here, and soon it was clear he even had his famous pianist brother wrapped around his little finger. He was a tiny hawk of a man, and the moment the actual famous professional performers stepped onto the platform, he vanished out of sight — he could not stand being anywhere he was not the center of attention.

A signal was given. The great crimson doors at the back of the hall rolled open, the whole assembly stood to their feet, and the orchestra burst into a rousing welcome fanfare. The King, the Queen, and the entire court of Labassecour filed in.

I had never seen a real living monarch before, so I strained my eyes to take in every detail, half-disappointed that they did not sit permanently on thrones wearing crowns and clutching sceptres. The King was a man of fifty, slightly bowed, with lines carved deep into his face as if by an iron stylus. I could read at once he was a quiet lifelong sufferer, a man haunted by the dark, constant ghost of hypochondria that follows some people their whole lives, freezing their blood and clouding their sight in the middle of even the brightest celebrations. The Queen beside him was mild, thoughtful, graceful, her face soft with gentle pity for her husband. Again and again I saw her nudge his arm, point out their little son the young Prince sitting on her knee, try to draw him out of his dark abstraction. He would start, smile for a moment at their boy’s chatter, and then slip right back into his somber quiet. No one else in the whole crowd seemed to notice the quiet tragedy playing out right before their eyes.

Right beside the Queen, on the benches reserved for the noblest young girls of Villette, I spotted Ginevra Fanshawe, her hair curled and flowing instead of braided tight like the local girls, chattering away to the daughter of an English peer beside her. I did not need to look at Graham to know he had seen her too. He went suddenly quiet, his answers to his mother growing short, every now and then he suppressed a small, soft sigh. Soon Ginevra’s roving gaze landed on our little group. She looked steadily at Graham, lifted her lorgnette to examine Mrs. Bretton, and then laughed and whispered a joke to her friend, turning her attention to the platform as the concert began.

The music itself was imperfect, charming in its flaws: the nervous young Conservatoire girls played a little tremulously on the grand pianos, M. Josef not having half the firm hand his half-brother would have used to bully them into courage. A great dramatic soprano in white satin ran through a series of incredible vocal tricks that felt more like a conjurer’s show than real music, and a dramatic tenor wailed for ten full minutes about his lost “false Isabelle” until I was quite relieved to see him leave the stage. The best part of the whole night, by far, was the rousing, hearty choruses sung by stolid, barrel-shaped provincial singers from all across Labassecour, their voices loud and unapologetic, ringing through the great gilded hall with a raw, unmistakeable warmth.

For one long perfect night, all the quiet loneliness of my usual life felt very far away, suspended in the soft golden glow of that evening I would remember for years after.
