The first warning of the snow that would come was no more than a whitening in the far air—like breath on glass—making the blue beyond look cleaner by contrast. In the rectory the morning sounded as it always did before the house fully woke: the low clink of a spoon against a cup in the nursery, the soft rasp of a coal being coaxed into flame, the brief hush that followed when someone opened an outer door and the cold came in like a living thing. Nothing in those familiar noises told of the private storm that had already begun in two young hearts.

Anna knew Rex’s secret, though for the first time in all their brother-and-sister history he had never once spoken it aloud to her. He had only carried it with a careless confidence, as if she must know what he knew because she had always known him. And for the first time, too, Anna could not say the thing that pressed on her continually, because to speak it would be to stab him with it.

She admired her cousin with the docile reverence that had been bred into her. She would have said, sincerely, “Gwendolen is always very good to me,” and thought it natural that she should be entirely under Gwendolen’s law. Yet she looked at her with a fear she could not explain, as one watches a splendid creature whose beauty is undeniable and whose hunger is unreadable—as if Gwendolen might, without meaning to be cruel, swallow up whatever small, tender things belonged to Anna’s keeping. And now a heavier conviction sat in her: Gwendolen would never care for Rex.

It was not that Anna had any sharp judgment; it was rather that she had a sense for what was fed and what was starved. She watched how certain words—words that meant reverence and home and steady affection—fell dead when they touched Gwendolen, like good seed cast on stone. Gwendolen’s mind always ran toward something extraordinary, as if ordinary love were a dish too plainly seasoned for her palate. And poor Rex—Papa would be angry if he knew. And Rex, of course, was too young to be in love in that grown way; it ought to have been years off; Anna had imagined herself his housekeeper for ever so long, ordering his shirts and keeping his keys, as if that were her marriage. But what heart could refuse him? In her dread of his pain she began, for the first time, to feel a faint, ashamed dislike of the cousin who charmed them all.

To Rex and Anna those weeks at Pennicote had seemed to throb with a life that must be visible to everyone; and yet the elders—over their newspapers, over the rustle of the Guardian and the Clerical Gazette—were as blind to it as they were to the busy arguments of ants in a gravel-walk. The young ones acted out their drama in pantomime, utterly clear to themselves, and missed by those who had learned to look past what did not threaten the day’s propriety.

It was on a gray morning, after Mr. Gascoigne had driven off to the sessions with Mrs. Gascoigne, that Anna noticed Rex coming down the stairs in his antigropelos—his nearest approach to hunting finery—and with that air of purposeful ease which in him always meant a plan that was not to be discussed.

“Where are you going, Rex?” she said, and the question came out too quickly, as if it had been waiting behind her teeth.

“Going to see the hounds throw off at the Three Barns.”

Anna’s heart gave a little thud, as if the house itself had knocked.

“Are you going to take Gwendolen?” she asked, timidly, as though she were asking whether he meant to take fire into the hayloft.

“She told you, did she?”

“No,” said Anna, and her cheeks warmed. “But I thought—Does papa know you are going?”

“Not that I am aware of. I don’t suppose he would trouble himself about the matter.”

“You are going to use his horse?”

“He knows I do that whenever I can.”

Anna’s mind filled with pictures she could not bear—of flying hooves, of hedges, of sudden falls, of her father’s face.

“Don’t let Gwendolen ride after the hounds, Rex,” she said, and fear sharpened her voice into something like second sight.

Rex smiled—half amused, half provoked. “Why not?”

“Papa and mamma and aunt Davilow all wish her not to. They think it is not right for her.”

“Why should you suppose she is going to do what is not right?”

“Gwendolen minds nobody sometimes,” Anna said, gaining boldness from a surge of anger.

“Then she would not mind me,” Rex returned, perversely turning her distress into play.

“Oh, Rex, I cannot bear it.” Her throat tightened; the words came out with a sob. “You will make yourself very unhappy.” And then she cried outright, the child’s helpless crying that seems all the more unreasonable because it is true.

“Nannie, Nannie—what on earth is the matter with you?” Rex said, impatient because he was already delayed—hat on, whip in hand, the whole of him leaning toward the door.

“She will not care for you one bit—I know she never will!” Anna whispered, brokenly. “I know she never will.”

A flush sprang up in Rex’s face, and he turned away as if her words had been a slap he would not acknowledge. He went out through the hall door quickly, leaving Anna with the sick feeling of having been disagreeable—and still useless.

As he rode, her sentence clung to him with that peculiar unpleasantness which any prophecy of ill fortune has, even when one laughs at it. Yet he explained it away as only Anna’s tenderness, her frightened exaggeration. And then he was sorry he had left her so. But the stronger movement in him was a stubborn belief in the opposite—an urgent need to prove her wrong. His certainty had just enough kinship to doubt to drive him toward confession; a secure man can wait, but a man with a tremor in him wants an answer.

Gwendolen was already mounted and riding up and down the avenue when Rex appeared at the gate. She had, with her usual foresight against inconvenience, kept a groom ready behind her so that she need not be made ridiculous by waiting. But Rex came in time; the groom was dismissed; and they rode off together with the delicious lightness of being unchaperoned, as if the whole world were a lane made for them alone.

She was in her highest spirits, and Rex, looking at her, felt that she had never before been so entirely—herself. Her riding dress, compact and severe, made a frame for the long white throat and the proud line from cheek to chin; everything in her seemed finished and decisive, as if nature had not merely formed her but signed her. Rex could not conceive a more perfect girl. And to a young lover the good, the true, and the beautiful appear not as separate virtues to be weighed, but as one substance shining in the beloved.

It was a mild winter morning—January in name, but not in cruelty. The sky was gray, not menacing but calm, and the landscape beneath it had that subdued richness which makes even bareness look nourished: grass borders damp and green, hedgerows hung with red berries, elms standing purple in their leafless strength, furrows of earth as brown as crust. Their horses’ hooves rang a musical chime along the road, and the sound of it—clean, hollow, rhythmic—seemed to steady the air, so that even their laughter had a place to rest. In that acoustical comfort, the mind grows more daring; the young feel as if happiness were a natural law.

Gwendolen laughed at Rex’s equipment—for he was no dandy, and his antigropelos and practical air made him look as if he had ridden out of a schoolbook illustration. Rex loved her laughter; it was like bright water poured over him. The freshness of the morning mingled with the freshness of their youth; each glance, each careless word, seemed to bubble up without effort. It was morning for them, within and without.

“Anna had got it into her head that you would want to ride after the hounds this morning,” Rex said at last, because Anna’s frightened prophecy made this remark feel dangerously close to the real thing he wanted to say.

“Did she?” Gwendolen answered, laughing. “What a little clairvoyant she is!”

“Shall you?” Rex asked, not quite believing she would do what everyone had objected to, trusting in her having good reasons for whatever she chose.

“I don’t know. I can’t tell what I shall do till I get there. Clairvoyants are often wrong: they foresee what is likely. I am not fond of what is likely: it is always dull. I do what is unlikely.”

“Ah—there you tell me a secret,” Rex said, eager, trying to turn her banter toward the point where his heart was waiting. “When once I knew what people in general would be likely to do, I should know you would do the opposite. So you would have come round to a likelihood of your own sort. I shall be able to calculate on you. You couldn’t surprise me.”

“Yes, I could. I should turn round and do what was likely for people in general,” she replied, her laugh like bells in clear air.

“You see you can’t escape some sort of likelihood. And contradictoriness makes the strongest likelihood of all. You must give up a plan.”

“No, I shall not. My plan is to do what pleases me.”

In another girl the words might have sounded hard, like a plate set down sharply. But her head and neck had that sovereign poise which made audacity seem only a kind of grace. Rex, who was hungry for any entrance into her real feeling, only smiled, anxious to lead her on.

“Can you manage to feel only what pleases you?” he asked.

“Of course not; that comes from what other people do. But if the world were pleasanter, one would only feel what was pleasant. Girls’ lives are so stupid: they never do what they like.”

“I thought that was more the case of the men,” Rex said, honestly. “They are forced to do hard things, and are often dreadfully bored—and knocked to pieces too. And then, if we love a girl very dearly, we want to do as she likes—so after all you have your own way.”

“I don’t believe it. I never saw a married woman who had her own way.”

“What should you like to do?” Rex asked, with real anxiety under his simplicity, as if the answer might show him the path to her happiness.

“Oh, I don’t know—go to the North Pole, or ride steeple-chases, or go to be a queen in the East like Lady Hester Stanhope,” Gwendolen said, lightly. The words were born on her lips; deeper reasons, if they existed, did not come with them.

“You don’t mean you would never be married?”

“No; I didn’t say that. Only when I married, I should not do as other women do.”

“You might do just as you liked if you married a man who loved you more dearly than anything else in the world,” Rex said, poor youth, stumbling into a lesson he had never been taught and yet was ready to recite with his life. “I know one who does.”

“Don’t talk of Mr. Middleton, for heaven’s sake,” she said hastily, and a quick blush spread over her face and neck like a sudden warmth under pale skin. “That is Anna’s chant. I hear the hounds. Let us go on.”

She put her chestnut into a canter. The sound changed at once: the brisker beat of hooves, the faint creak of leather, the breath of the horse coming warmer into the cold air. Rex had no choice but to follow, and yet he felt encouraged. That blush—was it annoyance, was it shame, was it something nearer to what he longed for? He took it as hope.

Gwendolen knew perfectly well that Rex loved her. But love, to her, was as yet a pretty story that filled the gaps in time; she had never suffered it, never been scorched by it, never had her pride made helpless by it. She wanted the small romance of Rex’s devotion to be a pleasant ornament to his stay at Pennicote, and she wished to avoid any explanation that would end it too soon. Besides, she had an almost physical repulsion to being directly made love to. She liked adoration as she liked music heard from a little distance—delightful, but not to be pressed close against her skin. There was, in her, a fierce maiden reserve that could turn suddenly into resistance.

All other thoughts were soon swept away for her by the scene at the Three Barns. Here the countryside gathered into color and sound: horses stamping, bits clinking, men’s voices calling across one another, and beneath it all the restless, thrilling undercurrent of the hounds—an eager, breathing music that seemed to make the ground itself impatient. It was the kind of soundscape that intoxicates: it draws the blood forward and makes caution feel like a dull custom. Even the air had a different smell—damp earth, leather warmed by bodies, a faint animal tang, and, somewhere, the sharp sweetness of crushed hedge leaves.

Several gentlemen of the hunt knew Gwendolen, and she exchanged greetings easily, with that air of belonging to any scene she entered. Rex could not get another word with her. The field took possession of her, and it did so with a strength not due to habit—for she had never yet ridden after the hounds. She had only said she wished to, and had been forbidden. Her mother dreaded danger; her uncle declared the violent exercise unseemly in a woman, and insisted that whatever might be done elsewhere, no lady of good position followed the Wessex hunt. No one but Mrs. Gadsby, the yeomanry captain’s wife—who had been a kitchenmaid, and still spoke like one.

That last argument had weighed with Gwendolen more than the first two. She was torn between the desire to assert her freedom and the horror of being classed with Mrs. Gadsby. Yet today none of the unexceptionable women were present to abstain and so make abstaining the right thing, and Mrs. Gadsby herself was not visible to render following vulgar. So the check of social sound—those little murmurs and pauses by which a neighborhood governs itself—was absent. In that lack of restraining voices, the raw, animal stimulus of the hounds and horses rose unchecked.

“Glad to see you here this fine morning, Miss Harleth,” said Lord Brackenshaw, a middle-aged peer with aristocratic seediness in stained pink, as if grandeur had been worn too often without careful brushing. His manner was easy, as if the threatened deluge would be only a change of weather. “We shall have a first-rate run. A pity you didn’t go with us. Have you ever tried your little chestnut at a ditch? you wouldn’t be afraid, eh?”

“Not the least in the world,” said Gwendolen—truthfully, for fear did not readily enter her in action or companionship. “I have often taken him at some rails and a ditch too, near—”

“Ah, by Jove!” said his lordship, in that quiet notation with which men break off talk when something more exciting begins; and as he reined away, Rex was bringing up his sober mount to Gwendolen’s side when—suddenly—the hounds gave tongue.

It was as if the world had been struck like a bell. The whole field moved at once. No one looked to see who followed. The roar and yelp, the cries of men, the thunder of hooves—sound became command. Gwendolen went with everything, borne by the rush, and Rex without a second thought went too. Could he let her go alone? Under other circumstances he would have enjoyed the run, but now he was perturbed—the confession pressing on him and being checked again and again, like breath held under water. And he was on his father’s gray nag, a good horse enough in his way but of sober years and ecclesiastical habits, a creature bred for decent roads and measured paces, not for flying recklessness.

Gwendolen on her spirited chestnut was up with the best and felt as secure as an immortal, because at her core was the confidence that ill luck did not happen to her. Indeed, she did not think of risk at all—certainly not of risk for Rex. If she had thought of him, she might have laughed at the image of him searching for gates, a fine lithe youth with the heart of a beagle trapped, as if by a spell, on a stiff clerical hackney. But Gwendolen was apt to think more of those who saw her than of those she could not see; and soon Rex was so far behind that even if she had looked, she would not have found him.

For in the search for a gate along a lane lately mended—earth still raw where it had been packed and trodden—Primrose stumbled. There was a moment of sickening tilt, the sudden violent give of a body losing its certainty, then the crack of knees against ground. The horse went down. Rex, pitched forward, was thrown over Primrose’s head.

Pain returned to him as sensation, like an ugly guest arriving late but insisting on being noticed. His shoulder burned and sickened; the world rang in his ears with a dull, far-off roar, as if the hunt were happening inside a shell.

A blacksmith’s son, Joel Dagge, who followed the hounds on foot—one of those rear-guard hunters whose very method some frivolous minds judged immoral—had been naturally in the rear and saw Rex go down. He ran up and was of immediate use in that particular way which belongs to people whose knowledge is not ornamental but fitted to emergencies. He knew what was wrong with the horse, how far they were from the nearest public-house, and exactly how to talk about a dislocated shoulder as if it were no more than a button off a coat.

He looked Rex over with the brisk pity of a practised witness.

“Lord, sir, let me shove it in again for you! I’s seen Nash, the bone-setter, do it, and done it myself for our little Sally twice over. It’s all one and the same, shoulders is. If you’ll trusten to me and tighten your mind up a bit, I’ll do it for you in no time.”

“Come then, old fellow,” Rex said, because he could tighten his mind better than he could keep his seat.

Joel set his hands with the confident precision of someone who has watched a thing done and repeated it until it became his own craft. The pain was sharp enough to whiten Rex’s face to the lips; his breath drew in with a thin, involuntary sound. He did not cry out, but his eyes filled in spite of him, and he hated himself for it.

“Ah, sir, you aren’t used to it—that’s how it is,” Joel observed, not unkindly. “I’s see lots and lots o’ joints out. I see a man with his eye pushed out once—that was a rum go as ever I see. You can’t have a bit o’ fun wi’out such sort o’ things. But it went in again. I’s swallowed three teeth mysen, as sure as I’m alive. Now, sirrey”—this to Primrose—“come alonk—you musn’t make believe as you can’t.”

Joel helped Rex as quickly as possible toward home. There was no other course, for Primrose was injured and Rex battered. Yet all the while Rex’s mind was not on himself but on Gwendolen, on the possibility—unthinkable—that she too might have fallen. That misery was worse than his bruises, worse even than the dread of his father’s anger. He tried to comfort himself with the thought that she rode well, that there would be men around her eager to take care of her, that some acquaintance would see her home. Still the uncertainty gnawed like a bad taste that will not be rinsed away.

Mr. Gascoigne was already back, writing letters in his study, when the interruption came: poor Rex entering with a face not less handsome for its pallor and distress. Rex was, secretly, the favorite son and looked like a young portrait of his father; but Mr. Gascoigne never allowed his feeling to soften his rule—rather he was stricter, as if he must guard himself against indulgence.

He did not at first lay down his pen.

“What is the matter?” he asked, hastily.

“I’m very sorry, sir; Primrose has fallen down and broken his knees.”

“Where have you been with him?” Mr. Gascoigne said, and though he rarely gave way to temper, the severity came like an edge in the voice.

“To the Three Barns to see the hounds throw off.”

“And you were fool enough to follow?”

“Yes, sir. I didn’t go at any fences, but the horse got his leg into a hole.”

“And you got hurt yourself, I hope, eh!”

“I got my shoulder put out, but a young blacksmith put it in again for me. I’m just a little battered, that’s all.”

Mr. Gascoigne laid down his pen. “Well, sit down.”

“I’m very sorry about the horse, sir; I knew it would be a vexation to you.”

“And what has become of Gwendolen?” Mr. Gascoigne asked abruptly.

Rex had not imagined his father had made inquiries about her. He flushed—an awkward, sudden blush against the pallor.

“I am anxious to know—I should like to go or send at once to Offendene—but she rides so well, and I think she would keep up—there would most likely be many round her.”

“I suppose it was she who led you on, eh?” Mr. Gascoigne said, leaning back and examining him more closely.

“It was natural for her to want to go: she didn’t intend it beforehand—she was led away by the spirit of the thing. And, of course, I went when she went.”

There was a short silence; it had the charged stillness of a room after a sound has been cut off. Then Mr. Gascoigne spoke with quiet irony.

“But now you observe, young gentleman, that you are not furnished with a horse which will enable you to play the squire to your cousin. You must give up that amusement. You have spoiled my nag for me, and that is enough mischief for one vacation. I shall beg you to get ready to start for Southampton to-morrow and join Stilfox, till you go up to Oxford with him. That will be good for your bruises as well as your studies.”

Rex felt his heart swell in his throat, behaving, as he would have bitterly said, like a girl’s—tears ready, unbidden.

“I hope you will not insist on my going immediately, sir.”

“Do you feel too ill?”

“No, not that—but—” He bit his lip hard, angry with himself as he felt the tears spring; then he forced the words out more firmly. “I want to go to Offendene, but I can go this evening.”

“I am going there myself. I can bring word about Gwendolen, if that is what you want.”

That calm arrangement, that shutting of doors with a sentence, broke him. He read in it—not merely authority, but an intention fatal to him. He had always believed in his father’s penetration; now he feared it had seen everything.

“Father,” he said, and his voice shook in spite of his effort, “I can’t go away without telling her that I love her, and knowing that she loves me.”

Mr. Gascoigne felt a quick inward rebuke that he had not been more wary; he was sorry for the lad. Yet he put all tenderness under what he judged to be wisdom, and answered quietly, as if stating a plain case.

“My dear boy, you are too young to be taking momentous, decisive steps of that sort. This is a fancy which you have got into your head during an idle week or two: you must set to work at something and dismiss it. There is every reason against it. An engagement at your age would be totally rash and unjustifiable; and moreover, alliances between first cousins are undesirable. Make up your mind to a brief disappointment. Life is full of them. We have all got to be broken in; and this is a mild beginning for you.”

“No—not mild. I can’t bear it. I shall be good for nothing. I shouldn’t mind anything, if it were settled between us. I could do anything then,” Rex cried, impetuously, as if the very air had become too tight for him. “But it’s of no use to pretend that I will obey you. I can’t do it. If I said I would, I should be sure to break my word. I should see Gwendolen again.”

“Well, wait till to-morrow morning, that we may talk of the matter again—you will promise me that,” Mr. Gascoigne said, still quietly.

And Rex—beaten not by argument but by the tone which had governed him all his life—did not, could not, refuse.
