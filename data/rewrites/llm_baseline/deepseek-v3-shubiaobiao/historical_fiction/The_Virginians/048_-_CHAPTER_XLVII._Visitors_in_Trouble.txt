Mr. Gumbo’s loyalty had been a balm to his master’s wounded spirit, and Harry Warrington’s second night in the spunging-house passed more peacefully than the first. Yet though the young man’s anger had cooled toward his faithful servant, it burned all the fiercer against the world beyond his prison walls. His Aunt Warrington’s sanctimony, Lord March’s indifference, Castlewood’s mocking detachment—all had proven hollow. Never again, he swore, would he debase himself by begging favors from such false friends.

Fool that he had been, to trust their promises! This cold, grasping England had no room for honesty. Better to flee—to Germany, to fight beside the King; or home to Virginia, where he might vanish into the wilderness, hunt, tend his mother’s lands, wed some plain, good-hearted girl, and leave these perfumed aristocrats to their scheming.

Morning found him dressed with care, his pride armor against humiliation. Gumbo arrived with letters from Bond Street—mostly bills, and a curt note from his landlord, threatening seizure of his belongings unless debts were settled. The servant himself had been turned away, finding shelter with friends at Lord Wrotham’s.

"Colonel Lambert’s people," Gumbo added meaningfully. "Miss Hetty took ill when she heard you was taken up. The Colonel says if you need him, send word, and he’ll come."

Hetty’s distress touched Harry, though he doubted the tale of her swooning. But the mention of Lambert reignited his bitterness. Ask help from the man who had scorned him? Never—even if he starved.

Breakfast dispatched, he penned a terse summons to his attorney, Mr. Draper, whose arrival did little to soothe his temper. The lawyer, affronted by Harry’s unbroken hauteur, resolved to humble him.

"Your friends are deeply concerned," Draper began unctuously, "that a gentleman of your station should face such disgrace."

"Disgrace?" Harry’s jaw tightened. "I’ve been reckless, not dishonest."

"Yet pawning watches you never paid for might suggest otherwise to some."

The insinuation struck like a slap. Harry surged to his feet. "You dare accuse me of fraud?"

Draper retreated a step but pressed on, relishing his advantage. "Merely stating how the law may view it. Your aunt, however, stands ready to settle your debts—on conditions."

Conditions. The word hung like a blade.

"Name them."

"The marriage—that unfortunate promise to a certain lady—must be renounced."

Harry flushed. The memory of his boyish pledge to Maria Esmond was torment enough without Draper’s smirk.

"Her ladyship has your written vow, recovered by chance," the lawyer continued. "Burn it, and your freedom is bought. Refuse, and you rot here."

The choice was clear, yet Draper’s gloating made acceptance bitter. Harry turned away. "I need an hour."

"An hour?" Draper’s smile curdled. "Most men would leap at such mercy."

"Then come back, and I shall answer."

Snubbed, the lawyer departed, fuming.

When he returned, dusk had fallen. A cloaked figure brushed past him on the stairs—a woman, swiftly gone. Inside, Harry stood firm.

"Well?" Draper demanded.

"I’ll not be bargained with like a coward. Tell my aunt I thank her—but my debts are my own."

Draper gaped. "You fool! You’ll hang rather than bend?"

"Better hanging than dishonor."

The door slammed on the lawyer’s curses. Alone, Harry smiled grimly. Chains might hold his body, but his spirit remained unbroken.
