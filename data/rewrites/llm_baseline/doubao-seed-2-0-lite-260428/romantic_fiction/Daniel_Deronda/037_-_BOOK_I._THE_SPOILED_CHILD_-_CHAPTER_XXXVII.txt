Daniel Deronda had been burning to return to London for weeks, his quiet anxiety for the vulnerable young Jewish singer Mirah tangled tight with his burning curiosity to learn more of the enigmatic, learned Mordecai who had spoken of shared heritage and unfulfilled purpose. He failed to beat Sir Hugo there, though—his guardian had hurried ahead of the rest of the family to be ready for the parliamentary opening on the sixth of February, so Deronda took temporary lodgings in Park Lane, fully expecting his old bachelor chambers would be overrun by his old Cambridge friend Hans Meyrick. That much he had prepared for. What he had not braced for was the tangled, unnameable tangle of feeling that awaited him there.

Most of us have seen that old Retzsch sketch: destiny as Mephistopheles, leaning over a chessboard, playing man for his soul, making fake, unthreatening moves to lure his opponent into moving all his defenses away from the real point of attack. The fiend loves to mock careful preparation, luring us to leave our waterproofs at home when he knows full well the skies are about to pour, just to make us stubbornly distrust all weather forecasts later. It is a cruel test of a man’s spirit, when he has carefully mapped out all his plans, steeled his heart to meet one set of hardships, only to find every bit of his precaution slightly misaligned, his good intentions no better than perfectly carved dovetails cut from the wrong starting point. His magnanimity has readied itself to meet cruelty, only to be called to a completely different kind of trial. That was exactly the situation Deronda walked into.

His first thought was bright, uncomplicated amusement. His familiar sitting room had been turned entirely into an artist’s atelier: canvases propped against every wall, loose sketches scattered across every surface, two uncrated chests shipped all the way from Rome spilling over with plaster casts and small antiquities, the lower half of the windows draped in baize to soften the harsh daylight for painting. Blonde, wild-haired Hans was there at the center of the mess, longer locks falling into his eyes, his face crinkling with that familiar impish charm, his high, quick voice rising higher the more excited he got. Their friendship had stayed warm and steady since their Cambridge days, stitched together by long letters and little shared adventures across England and the continent, that old dynamic of Hans spilling every last feeling and secret, and Deronda offering quiet, unjudging indulgence, deepening the longer they knew each other.

“I knew you’d be dying to see my Roman casts and trinkets,” Hans said, after the first hearty clap on the shoulder and round of questions about each other’s travels, “so I didn’t even feel bad dumping all my stuff here. But I’ve found two perfect little rooms in Chelsea, not five minutes’ walk from my mother and sisters. I’ll be out of your hair as soon as they scrape the walls and put in new skylights for me. That’s all I’m waiting for. But you can’t stop great art, can you? The seed of immortality already sprouted in my bones.”

“More like a fungoid growth,” Deronda teased, the way an older brother teases his favorite sibling. “I’d lay odds it’s a growing disease in your lungs.” He wandered over to the sketches propped on his bookcase ledge: five quick charcoal studies of the exact same face, each capturing a different turn of the head, a different soft expression. He stopped a little ways back to study them, and said nothing. Hans went quiet too, picking up his palette to dab idly at the half-finished canvas on his easel.

“Well? What do you think?” Hans finally prompted.

“The full face is a little too massive, the proportions off,” Deronda said, colder than he ever meant to sound. “Otherwise the likenesses are perfect.”

“No, the proportions are right,” Hans said, decisive. “People are just always surprised when they move from a profile to a full face. But I’m going to make her grander, bigger, for my Berenice series. Look at the rest of the sketches over there—this is going to be my masterpiece, and you know what? You’re exactly the model I need for Agrippa.” He moved to Deronda’s side, palette still in hand, then winced like he’d said something stupid. “Wait no, no—forgot you hate sitting for portraits, curse you. Don’t pout. I already found a perfect bloke to pose for Titus. There’s five paintings total, for the series. First one’s Berenice on her knees, clinging to Gessius Florus’s robes, begging him to spare her Jewish people. That’s the one on the easel right now. The second’s her standing on the Xystus with Agrippa, pleading with her people not to get themselves slaughtered for a hopeless rebellion.”

“Agrippa’s legs look ridiculous,” Deronda said.

“They’re perfectly realistic,” Hans said, his face crinkling into a silly grin. “Public men are always wobbly on their pins, aren’t they? ‘Their legs, the emblem of their various thought,’ as some fellow wrote in The Rehearsal.”

“They’re as impossible as the legs on Raphael’s Alcibiades,” Deronda countered.

“Which makes them ideal,” Hans shot back. “Art doesn’t copy life, it intensifies it. Agrippa probably did have terrible, wobbly legs. I just make them impossibly bad, so no one can miss it. But forget the stupid legs for a minute. Third painting: Berenice glowing with joy, when the news arrives that Vespasian is named Emperor, her lover Titus is his heir, and she’s about to be Empress of Rome.”

“You’d better give her a little scroll tucked in her mouth or something, no one’s going to guess that backstory from just a canvas,” Deronda said.

“Good. Let them feel ignorant. It’s an excellent aesthetic effect. Fourth painting: Titus forcing Berenice to leave Rome, even after she shared his palace for ten years. Neither of them wants to part, both heartbroken—invitus invitam, as Suetonius wrote, the unwilling man sending away the unwilling woman. I already found the perfect brutal Roman centurion to pose for Titus.”

“Won’t Berenice look fifty then? She’d have been that old by then.”

“Nah. Just a few mature lines, to show the years have passed. Dark-eyed Jewish beauty wears like solid gold, especially hers. Fifth and last painting: Berenice sitting all alone on the ruins of Jerusalem. Total pure imagination, that’s what should have happened, even if no history book wrote it down. There’s no sixth painting. Nobody knows what happened to her after that, right? The story just cuts off, ragged edge and all, dissolving into nothing—le néant. Sublime, isn’t it? The common crowd would want to see her funeral, her will read, her linen distributed to her servants. Boring. Come look at the easel piece, I’ve made loads of progress.”

“That beseeching pose is really lovely,” Deronda said after a long moment of quiet study. “You’ve been remarkably productive these Christmas holidays, I take it, picking this whole project up when you got back to London.” Neither of them had said Mirah’s name out loud, not yet.

“Nah,” Hans said, dabbing more paint onto the canvas. “I dreamed the whole thing up way earlier. I ran into this spectacular woman in Trastevere, you know—half the grandest, most striking women in Rome are half Jewish. That made me hunt for the perfect story of a Jewish woman in ancient Rome. Like all great learned men, I took the best story that was right there under my nose. I’ll show you the sketch I did of that Trastevere woman as soon as I can find it.”

“Surely she’d make a better model for Berenice than anyone else,” Deronda said, fumbling to put his strange, growing unease into words.

“Not a bit. The model should be the most beautiful Jewess in the whole world. And I found her.”

“Have you even stopped to ask if she’d want to be cast in that role? I can’t imagine any Jewish woman would be happy to be portrayed as the queen who abandoned her people for a Roman conqueror. Does she even know what the whole series is about?”

“Of course she does. I had her throw herself right into that begging pose. My own little mother Mrs. Meyrick sat for Gessius Florus, and Mirah knelt right there, clinging to her knees.” Hans stepped back a little to judge the effect of his latest brushstroke.

“I’m sure she doesn’t even know Berenice’s real history,” Deronda said, a hot, unnameable indignation rising in him that he couldn’t quite justify.

“Oh she does, she read the soft ladies’ history of the Roman emperors. She thinks Berenice was a fiery patriot who got tricked by love and ambition into loving her people’s worst enemy, the Nemesis following her back to the smoldering ruins of Jerusalem all alone. That was her own exact phrase. I couldn’t bear to tell her I made up that whole ending, the part where she sits grieving on the ruins.”

“Show me that Trastevere sketch,” Deronda said, just to give himself something to do before he said something rash.

“Would you mind flipping through that big folio over there?” Hans said. “All my head studies are in there. They’re a total mess, though. You’ll probably find her sketch right next to a drawing of some crop-eared Cambridge undergraduate.”

Deronda flipped through the pages for a minute, then frowned. “These are all portraits of Cambridge blokes, and little landscapes of the countryside. I’ll start from the other end.”

“Nah, she’s somewhere in the middle. I emptied one folio into the other a week ago.”

“Is this one of your undergraduates?” Deronda asked, holding up a soft portrait of a young man’s kind, open face. “It’s a very lovely face.”

“That? That’s Rex Gascoigne. Great guy. I tutored him before he won his scholarship. Should’ve gotten top honors last Easter, but he got sick, so he’s got to stay at Cambridge another year. I’ve got to look him up soon, see how he’s getting on.”

“Here’s the Trastevere woman, I assume,” Deronda said, holding up the dark, vivid sketch of the Roman woman.

“Pfft,” Hans said, glancing at it dismissively. “Too coarse. I was a total unregenerate idiot back then.”

Deronda closed the folio, leaving the Roman sketch sitting out on the table. He hooked a finger under the edge of his coat collar, turned to face Hans, and said, “My scruples might be stupid, Meyrick, but I need to ask you to do me a favor and drop this whole Berenice project with Mirah as the model.”

Hans threw himself into an over-the-top tragic pose, voice rising to a dramatic scream. “What? My series? My immortal, world-famous Berenice masterpiece? Think about what you’re saying, man! You’re not just killing a life, you’re killing an immortality, as Milton would say. Wait one second, let me set down my paints so I can tear my own hair out properly.”

He laid down his palette and brush, flopped backward into a big overstuffed armchair, hung half over the edge, let his long hair fall over his face, hooked his fingers on either side of his head, and stared up at Deronda with comic wide-eyed terror. Deronda couldn’t help but smile.

“Paint as many Berenices as you want,” he said, “but I wish you’d understand—you will, once you think on it—you need to pick a different model.”

“Why?” Hans said, standing up, all his silly playfulness fading into seriousness.

“Because Mirah’s about to step out into the world as a professional singer. Mrs. Meyrick and I have been working so hard to help her build that reputation, make her independent, give her a safe public standing. She’s already got one very important introduction lined up, and I’m planning to speak to Klesmer soon, the great musician, to help launch her career. Soon her face will be everywhere, known by thousands. And… well, I can’t explain all of it, unless you feel what I feel. I’m convinced if Mirah knew all the details, she’d hate the idea of being portrayed this way, as a queen who betrayed her own people for a Roman lover.”

Hans stood listening, thumbs hooked in the belt of his paint-splattered blouse, his face first showing surprise, then amusement, until a huge laugh almost burst out of him. But he saw Deronda’s grave, offended expression, and reined it in. “I’m so sorry for laughing, Deronda. You’ve never given me such a perfect, sweet little advantage over you before. If you’d been talking about anything other than my own paintings, I’d have believed every single word you said, just because you said it. Do you seriously think my five sad little canvases are ever going to get hung on the line at the Royal Academy, all the public lining up to stare at them? C’mon, man! Cider and wild conceit never gave me a dream half that pretty. My work’s going to stay as private as you could possibly wish, no one but my family and a couple of artist friends will ever see it.”

He turned back to his easel to fill the awkward silence. Deronda stood perfectly still, accepting he’d been stupid about the public attention angle—but his core repugnance hadn’t faded one bit. He was cross with himself, cross with Hans, but his practiced calm carried him through the awkward moment. Hans revered his friend too much not to feel a little shy about Deronda being in the wrong for once, but he was never one to back down easily from a whim, and he kept talking while he painted.

“Even if I did have a public mad for my work, lining up to buy every painting like they’re cheap prints of railway scenes with nurses and babies and bonnet boxes, I still don’t see what’s unfair about what I’m doing. Every great painter worth remembering painted the face he admired more than any other, over and over again. He pours his soul right out into those canvases. He paints the people he hates as caricatures. He paints the people he adores as something sacred, something heroic. If a man could paint the woman he loves a thousand times as the Stella Maris, the star of the sea, to give courage to a thousand sailors on a thousand different ships, that would be the greatest honor he could possibly do her. Isn’t that a thousand times better than painting some leering, immodest nude and calling it by some holy, worshipped name?”

“You can weasel out of any objection with big, broad generalities,” Deronda said, a little sharp, “but no question of personal conduct ever gets settled right that way. I can agree with every last grand thing you just said, and still be right that you shouldn’t make Mirah’s face the public face of Berenice. Fine, I admit I was being unreasonable about the public attention.” He hesitated for a long moment. “Even if it’s all just private, though, there are very good reasons you shouldn’t spend all your time painting her through that specific narrative. You know Mirah’s situation is incredibly delicate right now. Until she’s fully independent, with her career solid under her feet, she’s as fragile as a piece of thin Venetian glass—we can’t risk jarring her out of the safe, happy little home she’s found. Are you sure you can be discreet? Forgive me, Hans. I found her half-dead on the river bank, I promised her I’d look after her. You understand that, right?”

“Perfectly,” Hans said, breaking into a warm, good-humored grin. “You think I’m exactly the kind of clumsy oaf who’d shatter every piece of glass I walk near, and break my own skull in the process. Fair enough. Every single thing I’ve loved my whole life has landed me in some scrape, sooner or later. My painting’s the latest scrape, and I’ll be spending the rest of my life clawing my way out of it. You think I’ll mess things up at home with Mirah? No. I’m regenerate. You think I’m head over heels in love with her, and I’ll make a fool of myself, scream and plunge and ruin everything. You’re half right, I am completely head over heels. But you’re wrong about the rest. I’ve been baptized by awe, man. I won’t mess this up. Ask my mother.”

“So you don’t think that love is a hopeless scrape for you, then,” Deronda said, his voice dropping a little deeper, unbidden.

“I don’t call it hopeless,” Hans said, infuriatingly cool, laying down his brushes, shoving his thumbs in his belt, stepping back to squint at his half-finished painting.

“My dear fellow, you’re only setting yourself up for heartbreak,” Deronda said, firm. “Mirah would never marry a Christian, even if she loved him. You’ve heard her talk about her people, her faith, right?”

“That won’t last,” Hans shrugged. “She’ll never meet a tolerable Jewish man. All of them are insupportably advancing, their noses leading the way.”

“She’s desperate to find her lost family, her mother and brother. They’re almost certainly strict, observant Jews.”

“I’ll convert, if that’s what she wants,” Hans laughed.

“Don’t be stupid,” Deronda said, getting heated. “You said this was a serious love.”

“It is serious. You think it’s desperate, but I don’t.”
