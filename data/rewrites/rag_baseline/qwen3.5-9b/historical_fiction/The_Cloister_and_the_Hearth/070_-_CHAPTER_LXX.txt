To truly comprehend the architecture of Gerard's redemption, one must first confront the visceral horror of his initial encounter with Lodovico. It was a penance born of self-imposed necessity, yet tainted by a revulsion so potent it felt akin to handling a slimy serpent. Yet, in the economy of grace, there is no greater sacrifice than the denial of one's natural instincts; and like all rational acts of contrition, this abhorrence quickly transmuted into acceptance. At first, the assassin's gaze upon the single life he had spared was a grotesque tableau—a mixture of ludicrous arrogance and pathetic vulnerability. But to open a door in a hardened heart is to invite a flood; once the aperture is breached, light inevitably pours through. Finding the act of preserving life strangely sweet, the miscreant gradually grew averse to extinguishing it. From this aversion sprang remorse, and from remorse, a nascent, trembling penitence.

Teresa, ever vigilant, reinforced this transformation. She threatened, not for the first time, to withdraw her presence entirely unless he vowed to lead an honest life. The good fathers of the convent lent their weight to this moral realignment, sending Lodovico and Teresa by sea to Leghorn, where Teresa held friends, while the assassin settled into the mundane rhythm of a porter. Initially, he confessed the work to be miserably dull, a far cry from the adrenaline-fueled chaos of his past. But methinks this drudgery of plodding labor was infinitely superior to the brief, violent excitement of being hewn in pieces by the Princess Claelia's myrmidons. His exile had saved the unconscious penitent from that gruesome fate; conversely, the princess, balked in her quest for vengeance, succumbed to a profound, brooding melancholy. She dismissed her old confessor and summoned a new one renowned for his piety, entrusting to him what she termed her "griefs." This new confessor was none other than Fra Jerome. She could scarcely have fallen into worse—or rather, better—hands.

Fra Jerome listened to her grievances with grim detachment, then seized her by the shoulders and shook the delusions from her mind with the rough efficiency due a kitchen-maid. For, on the path of divine duty, he feared neither the wrath of princes nor the fury of the sea. In a torrent of thunder and lightning, he exposed her as the criminal among criminals. "Thou art the devil," he declared, his voice cracking like a whip, "who with thy gold hast tempted one man to slay his fellow, and then, blinded by self-love, instead of blaming and punishing thyself, art thirsting for more blood of guilty men, though theirs is not so foul as thine."

At first, she resisted, claiming she was unaccustomed to being reprimanded by her confessors. But Jerome overpowered her spirit. He threatened her with the Church's curse, both temporal and eternal, tearing the scales from her eyes until she sank down, groveling in remorse and terror at the feet of this gigantic Boanerges. "Oh, holy father, have pity on a poor, weak woman," she cried, "and help me save my guilty soul. I was benighted for want of ghostly counsel like thine, good father. I wake as from a nightmare."

"Doff thy jewels," commanded Fra Jerome sternly.

"I will. I will."

"Doff thy silk and velvet; and clad in humbler garb than thy meanest servant, wend thou instant to Loretto."

"I will," said the princess, her voice faint.

"No shoes; but a bare sandal."

"No, father."

"Wash the feet of pilgrims, both those departing and those arriving. And to such as be holy friars, tell thy sin, and abide their admonition."

"Oh, holy father, let me wear my mask."

"Humph!"

"Oh, mercy! Bethink thee! My features are known throughout Italy."

"Aye. Beauty is a curse to most of ye. Well, thou mayst mask thine eyes; no more."

On this reluctant concession, she seized his hand, intending to kiss it, but he snatched it away with rude force. "What would ye do? That hand handled the Eucharist but an hour ago; is it fit for such as thou to touch it?"

"Ah, no. But oh, go not without giving your penitent daughter your blessing."

"Time enough to ask it when thou comest back from Loretto."

Thus, that marvelous occurrence by the banks of the Tiber left its indelible mark upon all the actors, much as prodigies are said to do. The assassin, softened by the miracle of having saved the life he was paid to take, turned from the stiletto to the porter's knot. The princess went barefoot to Loretto, weeping her crime and washing the feet of the base-born. And Gerard, carried from the Tiber into that convent as a would-be suicide, now passed for a young saint within its walls. Eyes loving yet experienced watched over him. Upon a probation shorter than usual, he was admitted to priest's orders. Soon thereafter, he took the monastic vows and became a friar of St. Dominic. Dying to the world, the monk parted with the very name by which he had lived in it, thereby severing the final link to earthly feelings. Here Gerard ended, and Brother Clement began.
