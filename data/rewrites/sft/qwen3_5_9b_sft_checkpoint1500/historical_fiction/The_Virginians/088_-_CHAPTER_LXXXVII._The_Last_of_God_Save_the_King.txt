"What twisted law of destiny conspires to cast me perpetually into the minority?" I mused, looking out over the chaotic scene unfolding around me. "Were a bill introduced to surrender this realm to the Pretender of Rome or the Grand Turk, and to bow the neck of the kingdom to a foreign creed, it would surely pass—with me, naturally, casting the solitary vote against it. Yet, here in Virginia, I find myself a pariah twice over: to the Patriots, I am a disloyal Tory; to the Tories, a treacherous republican. The times are utterly unhinged. Oh, cursed fate! Within a year of landing in Virginia, I longed for the tranquil banks of the Waveney. But circumstances rendered flight impossible. To abandon my mother, a solitary woman, to face the looming specter of civil war would have been unthinkable, and she, steadfast and proud, refused to flee. Nor could a man of spirit desert his post. Thus, amidst the clamor, a consensus formed among the well-disposed at His Excellency's table, fueled by generous claret and overflowing tankards. We swore an oath, a *statue signum*, a rallying cry: *Signifer!* All Cavaliers would unite behind it. And there, over wine, His Excellency spoke with the fervor of the bravest soul alive.

Of all my acquaintances in Virginia, none stood more consistently firm than Madam Esmond. Following the influx of gentry to Williamsburg, many proposed a grand ball to honor Her Excellency, the Governor's lady. However, word of the Boston Port Bill swept through, prompting the House of Burgesses to adopt a furious protest, decreeing a solemn day of fasting and humiliation to pray against the calamity of civil strife. Yet, the invitations for the ball had already been sent and accepted. The gentlemen resolved to proceed with the festivities on the appointed eve, intending to don sackcloth and ashes only in the aftermath.

"A ball!" Madam Esmond exclaimed, her voice sharp with disbelief. "I am to attend a gathering hosted by rebels who plan to insult His Majesty openly within a week! I would rather die!" She promptly dispatched a letter to the stewards, stating that, given the perilous state of the nation, she could not in conscience attend.

Her surprise, however, proved immediate and profound. The following time she ventured abroad in her carriage, she was greeted by a hundred voices—white and black alike—shouting "Huzzah, Madam!" and "Heaven bless your ladyship!" They clearly interpreted her refusal as an act of supreme patriotism. Determined to dispel any misunderstanding, Madam leaned from her chair and cried out, "God save the King" as loudly as her lungs would permit. The crowd echoed her cry. Everyone, it seemed, shouted "God save the King" those days.

That same night, my poor Harry, serving as a Burgess and a host of the feast, donned his scarlet coat of Wolfe's Regiment—a garment he would soon be forced to discard—and departed with Madam Fanny to the ball. My Lady Warrington and her humble servant, being strangers and essentially English in spirit, were granted permission to attend the assembly from which Madam Esmond had wisely abstained. I, too, held the honor of dancing a country dance with the lady of Mount Vernon, a partner I found most lively, pretty, and amiable. Yet, I must confess, my wife's glowing praise of her reception was met with a grim silence by my mother when Lady Warrington recounted the evening's events. Could not Sir George Warrington have danced with Lady Dunmore or her daughters, or anyone else but Mrs. Washington? Indeed, the Colonel seemed to consider himself and his wife the apex of society, forgetting that he had once been a road surveyor earning a guinea a day. One cannot measure the pride of these provincial upstarts, nor explain away Lord Dunmore's evident infatuation with the man. As for Mr. Washington, I remain unsure of the depth of his pride, but I know that my good mother harbored no affection for him, nor anything connected to him.

Nor did my mother improve her temper toward him for attending the ball, nor for his conduct three days later, when the day of fasting and humiliation fell. He attended the service performed by our new clergyman, while Madam Esmond, having invited Mr. Belman and sundry colonial authorities to dinner, received an excuse from the clergyman. Undeterred, Madam Esmond tossed her head and declared he might do as he pleased. She staged a magnificent dinner, lighting her house brightly while the rest of the city succumbed to darkness and gloom. She requested Mr. Hardy, one of His Excellency's aides-de-camp, to sing "God save the King," believing the passersby to be listening to a religious observance. Instead, when the simple young gentleman, fresh from Europe, began "Britons, strike home!", a deafening yell erupted from the street. A massive stone, hurled by a rebellious hand, struck the punch-bowl before me, scattering its contents across our dining room.

My mother, undaunted, stepped to the window. I can still see her rigid, tiny figure standing tall, arms outstretched in frills, the twinkling stars her backdrop, singing in chorus, "Britons, strike home! Strike home!" The crowd before the pales roared, "Silence! For shame! Go back!" yet she refused to retreat. "Fling more stones, if you dare!" she challenged the brave little lady. More might have followed, but gentlemen emerging from the Raley Tavern intervened. "You must not insult a lady," a voice I recognized intoned. "Hurrah, Colonel! Hurrah, Captain! God bless your honor!" shouted the people, and thus the enemies were pacified.

Though my mother protested that the disturbance was merely a blip, she insisted on another performance by Mr. Hardy. He offered a sickly grin, claiming he disliked singing under such accompaniments, and the concert ended. Yet, scoundrels returned that night, frightening my poor wife nearly to madness and shattering every window in the front of our tenement. "Britons, strike home!" was simply too much; Madam should have contented herself with "God save the King." Militias were drilled, bullets cast, ammunition stockpiled, and cunning schemes devised to frustrate the royal ordinances. Still, "God save the King" remained the universal cry. When I objected to the gentlemen-patriots, accusing them of plotting separation and inviting the wrath of the greatest power on earth, the retort was always the same: "We mean no separation! We yield to no men in loyalty! We glory in the name of Britons!" Powder barrels were heaped in the cellars, trains laid, yet Mr. Fawkes persisted in his dutiful petitions to King and Parliament, feigning innocence. True, when I spoke of the Empire's power, I naively believed it would be exercised with restraint, avoiding the humiliation of deploying Hessian mercenaries and Indian warriors against thirteen colonies. Did we not wish to subdue them without insulting them further? Why bring in these hordes? Afterward, when the war was lost, it became easy to scrutinize the map and declare how we ought not to have failed. Long Island should have annihilated Washington's army; he should never have emerged from Valley Forge as anything other than a prisoner. The South was ours after Camden, undone only by the inexplicable meddling of the Commander-in-Chief at New York, who paralyzed the efforts of the only capable British general and sent him to a cul-de-sac at York Town, leading to his eventual capture as a prisoner. Oh, for a few more hours of darkness or light! Reading over our American campaigns from their ill-fated beginning to their ignoble end, now that we can see the enemy's movements as clearly as our own, I believe we can discern how a timely advance might have placed enemies in our power, altering the entire issue of the struggle.

Yet, it was ordained by Heaven—for the good, as we now realize, of both empires—that the great Western Republic should separate. The gallant soldiers who fought on her side, led by their indomitable and heroic chief, earned glory by facing not only veteran troops accustomed to war but also the scourges of wretchedness, cold, hunger, dissension, and internal treason. Where everything else would have collapsed, it survived solely because of the pure, unquenchable flame of patriotism burning in the bosom of that heroic leader. What constancy, what magnanimity, what persistence against fortune! Washington, before the enemy, was no greater or braver than the hundreds who fought beside or against him—who had not heard the repeated sneers at "Fabius"? But Washington, the Chief of a nation in arms, battling distracted factions, remained calm amidst conspiracy, serene before the open foe and the darker enemies at his back. He inspired order and spirit into troops hungry and ragged, stung by ingratitude yet showing no anger, ever ready to forgive. Invincible in defeat, magnanimous in conquest, he was never so sublime as on that day when he laid down his victorious sword to seek his noble retirement. Here indeed is a character worthy of admiration and reverence; a life without stain, a fame without flaw. *Quando invenies parem?* In that larger work I have planned and partly written concerning this great war, I hope I have done justice to the character of its greatest leader.
