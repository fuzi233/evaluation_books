From the dawn of time, when the Gods shaped man upon the earth, there have always been lands unfit for his survival—but none so treacherous as the fringes of Atlantis that brush against the wild continents of Europe and Africa. The common folk shun these regions, whispering of spirits that walk in daylight, slaying any who dare trespass. Even criminals, fleeing justice, would sooner face execution than brave its horrors.

The learned dismiss some tales as myth, yet enough truth remains to make life here a gauntlet of peril. The earth trembles daily; mountains seethe with molten fury. Rivers run clear but carry death in their currents. Fissures split the ground, spewing scalding steam or swallowing waters only to erupt in violent geysers. Boiling mud and rivers of fire carve the land anew without warning.

And yet, life clings here—ancient forests loom, their roots drowned in steaming swamps, their canopies veiled in mist. The air is thick, damp, and suffocating, sapping strength from flesh. But strength is needed, for these lands belong to the lizards.

Not the small, skittering things of kinder realms, but titans. Some a man might slay with spear and wit. Others—like the leviathan I once saw rise from a lake, its footfalls shaking the earth—could swallow Phorenice’s mammoth whole. Its armored hide shrugged off boulders; spines along its back tore through trees like parchment. These beasts were no dull-witted monsters. They knew when the mountains would heave, fleeing to deep waters or burying themselves in mud before the cataclysms came. Fire and boiling streams meant nothing to them.

This was the world I now called home. Fear gnawed at me—true fear, not the fleeting unease of battle—but I refused to let it take root. The Gods watched over me. Still, the first months were a torment. My clothes rotted to rags in the damp. Fever wracked my bones. I ate grubs and bitter leaves, or worse, poisoned meat that left me writhing. But survival teaches swiftly.

I found a great hollow tree, carved footholds inside, and built a refuge high above the reach of prowling jaws. Later, I lined the chamber with sand for a hearth, slit windows for light and air. I learned which fish could be speared, which turtles trapped. My ribs no longer pressed against my skin. But some hungers could not be fed.

With the ivory tusk of a river beast, I tried to carve Nais’s likeness—her curve of cheek, the fall of her hair—but my hands were clumsy. Still, the crude shapes stirred memory, and memory fanned longing. I ached to know her fate.

Others of the Priests’ Clan had come to these lands before, seeking enlightenment through solitude and suffering. Some returned with wisdom that seemed like magic to the vulgar. But my mind was not free for such mysteries. Nais owned it. She was my solace and my torment.

At last, I could bear it no longer. From the arrow-slit of my tree, I saw a distant peak—snow-crowned, bathed in the Sun’s bloody glow each dusk. Once, as a novice, I had knelt in adoration as He rose behind those very slopes. Now, I knew that from its summit, I could gaze across the continent to the Sacred Mountain, to the city where Nais lay entombed.

I set out.

The journey was a nightmare. Mists blinded me; I stumbled through bogs, circled lost for days. Lizards stalked the swamps—some large enough to crush me with a step. Twice, rivers of scalding water forced me to halt, hacking timber for rafts. But time meant nothing now. After years of waiting, even agony felt swift.

The air thinned as I climbed, the chill biting deep. I wove grass into cloak and boots, but still the cold gnawed. Then came the snowfields—a labyrinth of ice, where crevasses waited like jaws. Overhead, a great carrion bird wheeled, patient for my death.

Yet the peak drew me on.

When at last I reached the summit, the Sacred Mountain stood clear in the distance, its ring of fires pale against the day. The city below was hidden, but I knew the square, the granite throne, the tomb beneath.

I stood, spear planted, drinking in the sight, deaf to all but memory—until a voice called my name.

Zaemon stood upon the snow, aged yet impossibly swift, the Symbol of the Sun glowing in his grip. He gave the sign of the Seven; I returned it. Then he spoke as emissary of the High Council.

"You are summoned. Return at once. Phorenice besieges the Sacred Mountain. Atlantis bleeds beneath her heel. We need your blade."

"A command I obey. But first, swear to me Nais is safe."

His eyes hardened. "She remains beneath the throne. Phorenice keeps her as bait, knowing you live. But mark this, Deucalion—even were she my own flesh, I would sacrifice her if it served Atlantis."

"Then we differ. Promise me Nais, whole and free, or I march not as your general, but as her avenger."

Zaemon’s lip curled. "So the steadfast Deucalion now bargains with his oath? This is an age of crumbling faith. But we have no better. Very well. Crush Phorenice, and Nais is yours."

He turned and strode down the slope—faster, lighter, until his footprints faded to nothing on the untrodden snow.

I did not marvel. Zaemon’s arts were beyond mine. By now, he would already be back at the Mountain, breathless, preparing.

And I?

I would follow.

But my heart had shifted. Once, I would have placed Atlantis above all. Now, I knew the truth: Nais came first. Even if the world burned for it.
