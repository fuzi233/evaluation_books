Margaret returned to Rotterdam long before Gerard awoke, leaving her boy behind—a bold stroke of maternal strategy. She dispatched the faithful Reicht to Gouda with a vicar’s gray frock and broad felt hat, armed with meticulous instructions on managing her new master.

Then she sought out Jorian Ketel, reasoning that if secrecy was the game, he was her man. Together, they executed two cunning maneuvers—neither cruel, though Margaret fancied them so. But as she carried those deeds to her grave, so shall we.

She stayed away from Gouda parsonage.

Words spoken in the heat of argument often fester in silence. Turning over their exchange, she took offense at Gerard’s assumption that she could ever confuse the priest with the lover. *What did he think of me?* Pride stiffened her resolve. *No more visits to Gouda.*

Little Gerard remained behind, a living anchor for his father’s soul—a sacrifice that tore at her heart. She had never been parted from him for an hour, and his absence left her hollow. By nightfall of the first day, she could bear it no longer. Under cover of darkness, she stole to the parsonage, lurking like a thief until she spotted Reicht alone in the kitchen. A soft tap on the window, a whispered plea: *"Bring him to me, unseen."*

Reicht obliged, and mother and child melted into each other, joy and sorrow mingling in their embrace. A plan was hatched: Reicht would bring the boy halfway each day, where Margaret would meet them. Between the laughter and gamboling, the women spoke in hushed tones—of Luke Peterson.

*"I near promised to marry him,"* Margaret confessed. *"I said, ‘Say the word, and I’ll wed him.’"*

*"Poor Luke,"* Reicht sighed.

*"You’ve no fancy for him yourself, have you?"* Margaret teased.

Reicht’s head lifted sharply. *"Me?"*

And so the plotting began—two women against one unsuspecting boy.

But after five days of stolen reunions, guilt pricked Margaret. She wept—just as a cart arrived at her door. In it sat the Vicar of Gouda, clean-shaven, bright-eyed, clad in the gray frock and hat she had sent.

She fled upstairs to scrub away tears, don a fresh cap, and compose herself before descending. He seized her hands, kissed them, a tear falling upon her skin. She turned her face away, hiding her own.

*"Sweet Margaret,"* he murmured, *"why stay apart from your own good work? We’ve waited for you every day."*

*"What—when I was a hermit and a fool? Forget my ravings. No mortal could have dragged me from that madness but you. And now, I beg you—come to Gouda."*

She feigned indifference (though her heart clamored to go). *"We’ll see. Tell me first of your flock."*

He laughed. *"Two who’d feuded for years came to me, each angling for my favor. By God’s grace, I turned them to friends instead."*

She waved a hand. *"Tell me of yourself, not others."*

*"At first, sunlight blinded me. Now Reicht—"* He caught himself.

*"Between us—her voice cuts like a blade. All voices seem loud now but yours."* He smiled. *"But here’s news: An old man swears no parish in Holland is so plagued by the devil as Gouda. ‘We had a holy hermit,’ says he, ‘but the fiend came and took him, leaving naught but a scrap of skin—like a hedgehog’s.’"*

Margaret’s lips curved.

*"Stranger still,"* Gerard mused, *"the cave collapsed. Had I resisted you, I’d lie buried there. Providence, surely—condemning my solitude, blessing my new path. What say you?"*

He relented. *"Take the cart, then. I’ll walk ahead—a man again, bearing joyful tidings."*

When she arrived at the manse, she found father and son together—the boy a whirl of motion, the man sketching patiently, though *"he’s quicksilver, impossible to fix."*

That night, by the peat fire’s glow, Gerard marveled at the beauty in simple things—the shine of brass, the linen sheets like snow. *"Dives never fretted as I do, lying in such luxury."* He turned to Margaret. *"You were ever fair, but never so bright as now."*

She scoffed.

*"And you, mother—"* He praised Catherine’s aged beauty, her eyes holding love’s quiet light.

*"Enough, flatterer,"* Catherine laughed. *"To bed—you preach at dawn."*

The next day, his sermon held the crowd rapt—two hours that felt like moments. From terror to solace, he wove scripture into their hearts. In a corner, two women wept.

*"Where did he learn such power?"* Catherine whispered.

Margaret kissed her. *"I may not be happy, mother—but I am proud."*

She vowed silently never to let her love come between Gerard and Heaven.

The storm had passed. Now, like waves after a gale, their lives still rolled, but toward inevitable calm.

Bound by love, by an irregular but binding marriage, yet severed by Church and conscience, they lived in a delicate balance. She visited Gouda often, welcomed like a queen. Catherine and Eli, drawn by Rotterdam’s bustle, left Tergou to settle near her.

Yet Margaret was not happy. Her smiles held pensiveness; at times, she snapped. If monks visited Gerard, she turned heel and left. She despised them for parting her from him—a bitterness she passed to her son.

Gerard bore it patiently. He had found his calling, loved his flock, and they adored him. His cheerfulness returned, though part was artifice—a peacemaker’s tool. He reconciled enemies with laughter, tamed animals (even a mule named Jack), and played the jester when needed.

Once, hearing a braggart claim he could outdrink any in Gouda, Gerard challenged him—then pitted him against Jack. *"Which of these asses drinks more?"*

Another time, farmers arguing over hay sought his judgment. He summoned Jack. *"Here’s the finest critic in Holland."*

Years slipped by. In 1471, Denys, marching to England’s wars, offered to marry Margaret—*"for a comrade’s honor."* She smiled, gave him silver, and sent him off.

In 1473, Sybrandt died, blessing the two who had reunited him with love.

In 1474, Luke Peterson wed Reicht—Margaret’s scheming having steered him gently away from herself.

By 1476, Gerard clashed with the Pope over doctrinal excesses, drawing Franciscan ire. Margaret, ever his counsel, guided him through storms.

And in 1477, little Gerard—spoiled by doting women—was sent to Deventer’s famed school, Margaret’s heart torn but yielding to necessity.

So passed eight years. Gerard, content in duty, was happy.

Margaret was not.

But love, tempered by sacrifice, held them—guardians of each other’s souls.
