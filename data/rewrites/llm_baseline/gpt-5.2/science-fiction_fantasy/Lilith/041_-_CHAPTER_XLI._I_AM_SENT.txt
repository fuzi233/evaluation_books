I turned from the stillness of Lona’s couch to where Eve sat, her presence like a hearth that had never once gone out.

“Mother,” I said, and the word tasted strange—older than any tongue I had spoken in my own world—“one couch beside Lona stands empty. I know well enough I have no claim; yet may I not sleep this night in your chamber with my dead? Pardon both my cowardice and my presumption. Take me in. I give myself up. I am sick of myself, and would fain sleep the sleep.”

“The couch next to Lona,” Eve answered, “is the one already made for you. But something waits to be done ere you sleep.”

“I am ready,” I said, though my body wanted nothing but to fold itself down like dough at last proved and spent.

She looked at me with a smile that had the gentleness of hands dusted in flour. “How do you know you can do it?”

“Because you require it.”

“What is it?”

She turned her head to Adam, as if the question belonged to him by right of long keeping, like a seed passed from palm to palm. “Is he forgiven, husband?”

“From my heart,” said Adam.

“Then tell him what he has to do.”

Adam turned not to me first, but to his daughter—Mara—who sat near, quiet as a well whose depth you cannot measure by sight. “Give me that hand, Mara, my child.”

She held it out in her lap. He took it as tenderly as one lifts a sprig of green from a bed after rain—no tearing, no haste, only the certainty of care.

“Let us go to the cottage,” he said to me. “There I will instruct you.”

As we went, the air itself seemed to remember an old violence: a sudden stormful blast rose, and with it a great flapping on the roof—like wings in panic, like torn canvas—then it sank again, not into peace but into a deep moan, as if the world, having cried out, laid its cheek back on the pillow of night.

When the door of the death-chamber was closed behind us, Adam sat, and I stood before him. The small room held a scent of earth and wood and something clean as water in a clay jar. It steadied me more than any reasoning could have done.

“You will remember,” Adam said, “how, after leaving my daughter’s house, you came to a dry rock, bearing the marks of an ancient cataract; you climbed that rock, and found a sandy desert.”

I remembered it as a mouth gone dry—an old river’s speech silenced, its grammar erased to a stony stutter. In my own land we spoke of dead languages as if they were books shut; here, the very land had lost its words.

“Go to that rock now,” he continued, “and from its summit walk deep into the desert. But go not many steps ere you lie down, and listen with your head on the sand. If you hear the murmur of water beneath, go a little farther, and listen again. If you still hear the sound, you are in the right direction. Every few yards you must stop, lie down, and hearken. If, listening thus, at any time you hear no sound of water, you are out of the way, and must hearken in every direction until you hear it again.”

He spoke as if teaching a child to bake by smell rather than by clock—to trust the quiet, the living signs, not the loud and false assurances. Not sight, not argument: ear to ground, cheek to the world.

“Keeping with the sound, and careful not to retrace your steps, you will soon hear it louder, and the growing sound will lead you to where it is loudest: that is the spot you seek. There dig with the spade I will give you, and dig until you come to moisture: in it lay the hand, cover it to the level of the desert, and come home.”

He paused, and his gaze sharpened, like a knife laid flat and true.

“But give good heed, and carry the hand with care. Never lay it down, in what place of seeming safety soever; let nothing touch it; stop nor turn aside for any attempt to bar your way; never look behind you; speak to no one, answer no one, walk straight on. It is yet dark, and the morning is far distant, but you must set out at once.”

He placed the hand in mine. It was Lilith’s hand—severed, light with a dreadful lightness, and yet heavy with the weight of what it meant. He brought me a spade, the handle smooth where years of use had polished it, the blade showing the honest wear of earth-work rather than battle.

“This is my gardening spade,” he said; “with it I have brought many a lovely thing to the sun.”

The words were simple, and they struck deeper than any oath. A tool that had opened ground for food and flower was now to open ground for judgment and mercy. The same iron for both: the difference lay in the hand that held it, and the intention that guided it.

I took it, and went out into the night.

It was very cold, and pitch-dark. The kind of dark that presses on the eyelids, that makes the world feel padded and uncertain, as if sound itself might be smothered before it reached you. To fall would be a dread thing, and the way I had to go was difficult even in broad sunlight.

But I had not set myself the task; and the minute I started I learned I was left to no chance. A pale light broke from the ground at every step, not shining far, only enough—like a cook’s lamp kept low so as not to wake a sleeping house—showing me exactly where next to set my foot. Through the heather and the low rocks I walked without once even stumbling. The night had its own economy: no waste, no glare—only what was necessary, given at the instant of need.

I found the bad burrow quite still. Not a wave arose, not a head appeared as I crossed it. The silence there was not health but a held breath. In my own world, noise had become a kind of tyranny—engines and shrieks and the constant hammered rhythm that leaves no room for a human mind to settle. Here, in this other place, there were silences that were also tyrannies: gaps where a river’s song ought to have been, emptiness where a natural choir had been silenced long ago.

A moon came, and herself showed me the easy way. Toward morning I was almost over the dry channels of the first branch of the river-bed, and not far, I judged, from Mara’s cottage.

The moon was very low and the sun not yet up when I saw, before me in the path—here narrowed by rocks—a figure covered from head to foot as with a veil of moonlit mist.

I kept on my way as if I saw nothing. I held Lilith’s hand close, and the spade in the other, the two burdens balancing like salt and bread: one for preservation, one for hunger.

The figure threw aside its veil.

“Have you forgotten me already?” said the princess—or what seemed she.

I neither hesitated nor answered; I walked straight on, jaw clenched on silence as on a bitter rind you must chew without spitting.

“You meant then to leave me in that horrible sepulchre! Do you not yet understand that where I please to be, there I am? Take my hand: I am alive as you!”

The words were sweetened like deceitful wine, meant to warm the will and loosen it. I was on the point of saying, Give me your left hand—but some memory not of thought but of touch restrained me. I held my peace, and steadily advanced.

“Give me my hand,” she suddenly shrieked, “or I will tear you in pieces: you are mine!”

She flung herself upon me.

I shuddered, but did not falter. Nothing touched me, and I saw her no more—as if the threat had been only a loudness, a blast of sound meant to drown out the quieter, truer murmur I had been sent to seek.

With measured tread along the path, filling it for some distance, came a body of armed men. The sound of their march struck the rocks with a hard, metallic certainty; the clash of their armour was like a brutal music, a parade meant to impress itself on the nerves and make obedience to fear feel like wisdom.

I walked through them—nor know whether they gave way to me, or were bodiless things. But they turned and followed. I heard and felt their march at my very heels, the iron rhythm trying to take possession of my breathing; but I cast no look behind, and, as if starved by my refusal, the sound of their steps and the clangour died away.

A little farther on, the moon now close to the horizon and the way in deep shadow, I descried, seated where the path was so narrow I could not pass her, a woman with muffled face.

“Ah,” she said, “you are come at last! I have waited here for you an hour or more! You have done well! Your trial is over. My father sent me to meet you that you might have a little rest on the way. Give me your charge, and lay your head in my lap; I will take good care of both until the sun is well risen. I am not bitterness always, neither to all men!”

Her voice had the cadence of a lullaby, the promise of a kitchen chair pulled out, a bowl of broth set down, a head allowed to drop into safe linen. And I was very weary. The temptation was terrible, not because it sounded false, but because it sounded plausible. What more likely, indeed, than that I was meant to meet the Lady of Sorrow on the road? What if, through slavish obedience to the letter of the command and lack of pure insight, I should trample under my feet the very person of the Lady?

My heart grew faint at the thought, then beat as if it would burst my bosom. I could almost smell rest—like warm bread, like water set by the bed.

Nevertheless my will hardened itself against my heart, and my step did not falter. I took my tongue between my teeth lest I should unawares answer, and kept on my way. If Adam had sent her, he could not complain that I would not heed her. Nor would the Lady of Sorrow love me the less that even she had not been able to turn me aside.

Just ere I reached the phantom, she pulled the covering from her face. Great indeed was her loveliness—but those were not Mara’s eyes. No lie could truly or for long imitate them: Mara’s gaze had the steadiness of a field known by generations, the quiet accuracy of one who measures by seasons rather than by hurry.

I advanced as if the thing were not there, and my foot found empty room. I had almost reached the other side when a Shadow—I think it was The Shadow—barred my way.

He seemed to have a helmet upon his head, but as I drew closer I perceived it was the head itself I saw—so distorted as to bear but a doubtful resemblance to the human. A cold wind smote me, dank and sickening—repulsive as the air of a charnel-house. It took hold of my breath and turned it sour; firmness forsook my joints, and my limbs trembled as if they would drop in a helpless heap.

For a moment the world was only that stench and that oppression, like being forced into a place where all the songs have been silenced and replaced by a single dead note—an industrial hum of despair.

I seemed to pass through him, but I think now that he passed through me: for an instant I was as one of the damned.

Then, as suddenly as a door opened onto morning, a soft wind like the first breath of a new-born spring greeted me, and before me arose the dawn.

My way now led me past the door of Mara’s cottage. It stood wide open, and upon the table I saw a loaf of bread and a pitcher of water. The loaf sat there like a vow made visible—crust browned, the cut lines on top opened in the bake, as if it had spoken in the oven and been heard. The pitcher held its plainness with dignity. In or around the cottage was neither howl nor wail. The absence of that noise was itself a mercy; it let the small domestic offerings be what they were: sustenance set out without barter, without spectacle.

I did not enter. I did not touch. My mouth ached with want, my throat with dryness, and yet Adam’s words lay on me like a binding cloth: let nothing touch it; stop not; turn not aside.

I came to the precipice that testified to the vanished river. The rock-face was scarred with the old water’s handwriting—channels, smoothings, hollows where the flood had once translated mountain into music. I climbed its worn face, and went on into the desert.

From its summit the sand lay like a page wiped clean—too clean, too uniform, the way a dominant tongue tries to make all other speech disappear. Yet beneath it, Adam had said, water murmured.

There at last, after much listening to and fro, I did as commanded. I went not many steps before I lay down and pressed my head to the sand. At first I heard nothing but my own blood in my ears, and the faint outer hush of dawn. Then—thin, uncertain, like a remembered word on the edge of forgetting—I caught a murmur far below, a living syllable.

I rose, went forward, lay down again. The sound was there. Again I moved, and again listened. Sometimes it faded, and I would lie with my cheek to the ground and turn my attention as one turns a cup to catch the last drops, until I found it again. I began to understand that this was not merely direction but discipline: to be guided by what is quiet and constant, not by what shouts.

At length the murmur grew fuller, nearer, almost eager—as if the hidden water strained toward being found. I determined the spot where it was loudest. Then I hung Lilith’s hand about my neck, as one might hang a bitter amulet that must not be set down, and began to dig.

It was a long labour. The sand would not hold an edge; it slid back upon itself, swallowing the space I made as quickly as I made it, so that I was forced to widen the hole, to work like one digging for a spring in a land that has forgotten how to keep water. The spade bit, lifted, shed; the grains rasped; my breath came in dry pulls. Yet Adam’s gardening spade was honest in my grip. Each thrust felt like an argument with the desert: you are not all there is; you are not the last word.

At length my blade threw up a damp spadeful. The smell rose—faint, exquisite, unmistakable: the scent of moisture in earth, the promise of life, like the first steam from a pot when water begins to wake. I flung the sexton-tool on the verge, and laid down the hand.

A little water was already oozing from under its fingers.

I sprang out, and made haste to fill the grave. Sand poured in, soft and relentless; I pressed it down, levelled it to the desert’s face, hiding what I had done as if to protect it from the loud and the cruel.

Then, utterly fatigued, I dropped beside it, and fell asleep.
