All the next day they sat together—three figures gathered as if the room itself had learned to hold its breath. The house was hushed in the strange way a home becomes after death has passed through it: not only emptied of a voice, but altered in its very sounds. The clock seemed louder, the coal settling in the grate sharper, the street beyond a nearer, harsher murmur; every wheel on the stones came with an edge, every distant shout from Milton’s throats struck the ear like a blow. Even the kettle—set on out of habit, left to cool without purpose—gave no kindly promise; it only clicked and fell silent, like something that had forgotten its own language.

Mr. Hale hardly spoke, except when his children questioned him and forced him, by sheer tenderness, to step into the present. He looked as if his thoughts had narrowed to one long corridor where fear walked up and down: fear of recognition, fear of a knock at the door, fear of a name spoken too loud. Since Mrs. Hale’s death that fear had grown more tyrannical; it filled the spaces she had once softened. He started at any unusual sound, and was never at ease unless Frederick sat where no one entering could see him at once.

Frederick’s grief was no longer audible. The first violent storm had spent itself, leaving a stillness that was almost more dreadful. He was ashamed of having been mastered so openly, as men are taught to be ashamed of tears; and though sorrow sat deep in him, it was a sorrow that would be carried like a sealed thing. He would not speak of his mother again. Margaret, who had been steadier at first, suffered more now. Her eyes would overflow without warning, and even when she spoke of something indifferent there was a mournful softness in her voice, as if every word touched the bruise underneath. Whenever she looked at Frederick, she saw, not only her mother’s absence, but his swiftly approaching departure; it made even the daylight feel temporary.

She was glad he was going, for her father’s sake, however much she grieved for her own. The anxious terror in which Mr. Hale lived lest his son should be detected and captured outweighed all the comfort of having him near. Presence had become another kind of danger.

Towards evening Mr. Hale said, with the constrained decision of a man taking refuge in arrangements because feeling is unmanageable, “You will go with Frederick to the station, Margaret? I shall want to know he is safely off. You will bring me word that he is clear of Milton, at any rate?”

“Certainly,” said Margaret. “I shall like it, if you won’t be lonely without me, papa.”

“No, no! I should always be fancying some one had known him, and that he had been stopped, unless you could tell me you had seen him off. And go to the Outwood station. It is quite as near, and not so many people about. Take a cab there. There is less risk of his being seen. What time is your train, Fred?”

“Ten minutes past six; very nearly dark. So what will you do, Margaret?”

“Oh, I can manage. I am getting very brave and very hard. It is a well-lighted road all the way home, if it should be dark. But I was out last week much later.”

She was thankful, when the time came, that she could hurry him away. There are partings that multiply if you let them—at the chamber door, at the stair, at the threshold; she would have none of that. The last look at his mother’s body was bitter enough for her father without prolonging the scene. She got Frederick into the cab quickly, and made her father remain behind, with the gentlest firmness she could command, as if her briskness were only care for his comfort and not the desperate need to keep fear from growing larger.

The cab’s wheels struck the roadway with a hollow clatter that seemed to echo in Mr. Hale’s nerves. Margaret, sitting close to her brother, listened to every sound outside as if the town itself were an eavesdropper—Milton with its quick feet, its constant grind, its rough-voiced life. Even here, on the way out, the air had the city’s familiar taste: smoke laid on the tongue like stale bitterness, and somewhere, faint and far, the sour-sweet breath of fermenting grain or spirit. Sounds and scents—both were warnings; both could carry a name.

Partly because she had hurried, and partly because one of those common errors in the Railway Guide had shifted the times at the smaller stations, they found, on reaching Outwood, that they had nearly twenty minutes to spare. The booking-office was not open; they could not even take the ticket. The platform lights flared with a raw, impatient glare, throwing hard shadows and making the darker spaces beyond seem fuller of watchers than of mere night.

So they went down the flight of steps to the level ground below the railway. A broad cinder-path crossed a field diagonally beside the road, and they walked there, back and forth, as if movement could keep mischance from finding them. The cinders, black and crisp, gave under their feet with a dry, gritty sound—so different from the soft, honest hush of country earth that Margaret had known as a girl. Even Outwood bore the railway’s voice: a place taught to listen for the long metallic sigh of an approaching train.

Margaret’s hand lay on Frederick’s arm. He took it, holding it with a warmth that made her think, suddenly and painfully, of the way her mother’s hands had always moved about the teacups and bread, the small daily ministries by which a household remembers itself. Now all those acts were stopped; the memory of them felt like a dialect no one spoke aloud for fear it might die altogether.

“Margaret!” Frederick said. His voice had steadied; he spoke now with that controlled earnestness which comes after we have been ashamed of weakness. “I am going to consult Mr. Lennox as to the chance of exculpating myself so that I may return to England whenever I choose, more for your sake than for the sake of anyone else. I can’t bear to think of your lonely position if any thing should happen to my father. He looks sadly changed—terribly shaken. I wish you could get him to think of the Cadiz plan, for many reasons. What could you do if he were taken away? You have no friend near. We are curiously bare of relations.”

She could hardly keep from crying at the anxious tenderness with which he spoke of a future she dared not examine too closely. The last months had hollowed her father; she knew it. She tried to rally herself, because Frederick needed courage from her.

“There have been such strange unexpected changes in my life during these last two years,” she said, “that I feel more than ever that it is not worth while to calculate too closely what I should do if any future event took place. I try to think only upon the present.”

She paused. They had stopped for a moment near the stile that led into the road. The low sun, slipping towards its end, fell on their faces in a colour like diluted wine. Frederick held her hand and searched her expression as if he could read, in the set of her mouth and the shadow about her eyes, what she would not give him in words. She went on, with effort:

“We shall write often to one another, and I will promise—for I see it will set your mind at ease—to tell you every worry I have. Papa is—”

She started—a small, involuntary start—but Frederick felt it, and turned his head sharply. Along the road, passing the very stile where they stood, a horseman rode slowly by. Hoofbeats—measured, controlled—came and went with a distinctness that made Margaret’s heart stumble, for in that moment sound was fate.

Margaret bowed. Her bow was stiffly returned.

“Who is that?” Frederick asked, almost before the rider was out of hearing.

Margaret’s colour rose and fell; she looked drooping and flushed as she answered, “Mr. Thornton; you saw him before, you know.”

“Only his back. He is an unprepossessing-looking fellow. What a scowl he has!”

“Something has happened to vex him,” said Margaret, apologetic in spite of herself. “You would not have thought him unprepossessing if you had seen him with mamma.”

Frederick glanced towards the station as if time, too, had begun to watch them. “I fancy it must be time to go and take my ticket. If I had known how dark it would be, we wouldn’t have sent back the cab, Margaret.”

“Oh, don’t fidget about that. I can take a cab here, if I like; or go back by the rail-road, when I should have shops and people and lamps all the way from the Milton station-house. Don’t think of me; take care of yourself. I am sick with the thought that Leonards may be in the same train with you. Look well into the carriage before you get in.”

They went back up to the station. Margaret insisted on going into the full light of the flaring gas to take the ticket, as if brightness could protect them. The air in the office had the smell of oil and smoke and damp woollen coats; the very place seemed made of irritation and impatience. Some idle-looking young men lounged about with the station-master. Margaret thought she had seen the face of one of them before. He stared at her with an impertinent, undisguised admiration—the bold look of a man who believes every woman in a public place has been put there for his entertainment. She met it with a proud, offended dignity that made his mouth twitch, and went hastily out to Frederick, taking his arm as though the touch anchored her.

“Have you got your bag? Let us walk about here on the platform,” she said. She tried to keep her voice steady, but her courage was already leaking away, drop by drop, at the thought of being left alone, and at the thought of what the next few minutes might hold.

She heard a step behind them along the flagged stones: it stopped when they stopped. The sound was too deliberate, too attentive; it pricked her like a thorn. They stood near the edge and looked out along the line. Far off there was the first thin whizz—like a knife drawn quickly—of the coming train. They did not speak. Their hearts were too full; and besides, speech felt dangerous, as if words might carry further than they intended.

Another moment and the train would be here; a minute more and Frederick would be gone. Margaret almost repented the urgency with which she had pressed him to go to London; it flung him into more chances of being seen. If he had sailed for Spain from Liverpool, he might have been out of reach in hours, with only water between him and his pursuers. Here, on land, everything could stop him: a look, a name, a hand on his sleeve.

Frederick turned round, facing the lamp. The gas shot up in vivid, expectant flames, making his features sharp and pale. At that instant a man in the dress of a railway porter started forward—a bad-looking man with the heavy, brutal face of one who has drunk until coarseness has settled into him like a habit. Yet his senses were not dulled; there was a grim precision in his movement that was more alarming than mere drunken clumsiness.

“By your leave, miss!” he said, pushing Margaret rudely aside. He seized Frederick by the collar with a grip meant to hold, not to jostle. “Your name is Hale, I believe?”

Time broke. In an instant—how, Margaret could not afterwards tell, for everything leapt and reeled before her eyes—Frederick wrenched and wrestled. There was a quick, practised movement; the porter was tripped, thrown off balance, and fell from the platform’s height—three or four feet—down onto the soft ground beside the line. He lay there.

“Run, run!” Margaret gasped, her voice tearing out of her throat. “The train is here. It was Leonards, was it? oh, run! I will carry your bag.”

She snatched at the bag, seized Frederick’s arm, and pushed him along with all the force her fear could lend her. The iron scream of brakes, the rush of air, the shuddering approach of the train came together like one monstrous breath. A door opened; Frederick sprang in. He leaned out for the briefest second—just long enough for a face and a voice to be made memory.

“God bless you, Margaret!”

Then the train rushed past her; the wind of it struck her skirt and hair, and she was left standing alone with the platform lights glaring and the dark beyond swallowing the last sound of the wheels.

Sickness rose in her like a tide. She felt herself turn cold and empty, as if the fear had scooped out her insides. She was thankful to reach the ladies’ waiting-room and sit down for an instant. At first she could do nothing but gasp. It had been such a hurry, such a sickening alarm, such a near chance—so near that the taste of it remained in her mouth, bitter as soot.

If the train had not been there at that very moment, the man might have scrambled up and called assistance to arrest Frederick. She tried to remember whether she had seen him stir. Had he been badly hurt? The thought of leaving a man broken by her brother’s struggle—even a brutal, drunken informer—clung to her, and yet the greater dread was still Frederick’s safety.

She forced herself up and ventured out. The platform was all alight, but it was deserted now; the soundscape had thinned to distant, indifferent noises. She walked to the end and looked over with a fear she hated in herself. No one was there. The ground below was empty. Relief loosened her tight breathing; at least, she told herself, she would not be haunted by the image of a body lying unseen in the dark.

Even so she trembled, and the road home, which she could see from the blaze of the station, looked lonely and dark. The quiet out there was not the gentle quiet of fields; it was a silence made ominous by the memory of sudden voices. She knew she could not walk it. She would wait for the down train and take her seat in it.

But what if Leonards—if it was Leonards—recognized her as Frederick’s companion? Her heart began to hammer again, listening for heavy footsteps, for a thick laugh, for that particular swaggering cadence drunken men have when they believe they are still masters of themselves. She peered about before she dared go into the booking-office for her ticket. Only railway officials were there, standing in a knot and talking loud to one another, their voices bouncing off the bare walls.

“So Leonards has been drinking again!” said one, in the tone of an authority disgusted by a habitual offender. “He’ll need all his boasted influence to keep his place this time.”

“Where is he?” asked another.

Margaret stood with her back towards them, counting her change with trembling fingers. She did not dare turn her head until she heard the answer, as if her very glance might summon him.

“I don’t know. He came in not five minutes ago, with some long story or other about a fall he’d had, swearing awfully: and wanted to borrow some money from me to go to London by the next up-train. He made all sorts of tipsy promises, but I’d something else to do than listen to him; I told him to go about his business; and he went off at the front door.”

“He’s at the nearest vaults, I’ll be bound,” said the first speaker. “Your money would have gone there too, if you’d been such a fool as to lend it.”

“Catch me! I knew better what his London meant. Why, he has never paid me off that five shillings—”

Their talk slid on into common complaint. Margaret’s hands still shook, but the knowledge that Leonards had gone—gone away from the station, away from her—was like finding a door unbarred. Now all her anxiety fixed itself on the coming train. She hid again in the ladies’ waiting-room, where the air smelled faintly of damp shawls and extinguished fires, and where every creak of wood, every passing voice, made her start. In such a state, sound did not merely enter the ear; it ruled the mind. She found herself longing, absurdly, for some remembered domestic noise—her mother’s gentle stir in the kitchen, the soft scrape of a knife spreading butter, the quiet clink of a cup set down with care—anything that spoke of a world in which danger did not announce itself with a boot-step and a shout.

No one came near her until the down train drew up. A porter helped her civilly into a carriage. She did not dare look full into his face until they were in motion; then, as the wheels took their rhythm and the station’s glare slid away behind her, she saw it was not Leonards’. She let her breath out, and sat rigidly still, listening to the train’s long, steady sound as it carried her back towards Milton, where even the lights and crowds and noise could feel, for that night at least, like safety.
