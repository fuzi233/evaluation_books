When Harry and I were larking through our boyhood days on the Virginian plantation, forever playing pranks on our poor long-suffering tutor, who on earth would have guessed that Mr. Esmond Warrington would end up as a “bearleader” himself, paid to chaperone and tutor another man’s son? When my mother and I were reconciled at last, she never could bring herself to name that stretch of my life outright, only ever referring to it as “that terrible time, my love, which I can scarce bear to look back on,” “those dreadful years when there was strife between us,” and so on. And though my former pupil, a most worthy and grateful soul, shipped barrels of the famed ale that built his family’s vast fortune all the way out to Jamestown as gifts for me, Madam Esmond always spoke of him only as “your friend in England,” “your wealthy Lambeth acquaintance,” never so much as breathing his name, and never once sipped a drop of his beer. We brew our own ale down at Warrington Manor, of course, but the good Mr. Foker never fails to send two full butts of his unadulterated strongest brew every year out to Ipswich for us. His son is now a fine dandy of the ton, wed to an earl’s daughter, while the old brewer himself remains as kind and generous a gentleman as one could hope to meet. It was the happy luck of making his acquaintance that put the most welcome guineas I ever earned into my own empty pockets.

It was never the size of the stipend he paid me, mind, that made the post so precious to me in those dark days, but the steady work, the small daily purpose, the glimmer of hope it gave our little household. Mr. Foker’s brewery, later moved to a larger site, stood back then near Pedlar’s Acre in Lambeth, and the surgeon who attended my wife through our first confinement also tended to the brewer’s large family. He was a Bavarian man born, named Voelker, and our shared acquaintance Mr. Lance the surgeon must have told him my name and my ruined fortunes. The good doctor would smoke pipe after pipe of our homegrown Virginia leaf in our tiny back garden, and grew very fond of me and my family. He brought his patron Mr. Foker round to call one afternoon, and when the brewer learned I could speak a smattering of his native tongue, and could even sing that old ballad “Prinz Eugen the Noble Ritter” — a song my own grandfather had brought home with him after Marlborough’s wars on the Continent — he conceived an immediate great affection for me. His lady put her own sedan chair and family chariot at my wife’s full disposal; his young daughter grew utterly besotted with our baby (and to do the boy justice, though he is now as rough a plain-looking fellow as any man who ever wore a pigtail queue, he was as beautiful as a cherub in those days) [The very image of the Squire at thirty, every soul on the plantation swears so. M.W. — Note in the original manuscript]; and his heir, young Master Foker, was being hounded and tormented mercilessly at Westminster School by his peers for the low trade his father made his fortune in, so his parents came to ask if I would take charge of his education, and paid me a sum that kept our household comfortable without the need for begging favors from distant noble relatives.

Mr. Foker was a shrewd man of business, and since he and his family had grown to care for us genuinely, I laid out all my tangled, straitened pecuniary affairs before him with almost no reserve. He said my honesty only deepened the respect and kindness he felt for me. He laughed outright when I told him of the paltry “charity” my noble kin had doled out to us: my aunt’s moth-eaten old coverlid, Lady Castlewood’s mouldy pot of jelly, the haughty cold shoulder Lady Warrington had turned on us when we called. But he cried real tears when I told him the story of poor little Miles’s lost moidore, and as for our friends Parson Sampson and the actor Hagan, “I vow,” he said in his thick rolling accent, “they shall have as much beer as they can ever drink, for nothing.” He sent his own wife to call on Lady Maria, and treated her with the utmost deference and respect whenever she visited his house. It was at the Fokers’ home that Lady Maria stayed when Hagan went off to Dublin to finish his university degrees, and the brewer’s generous purse supplied all his outfit and living costs while he was away.

Once Mr. Foker knew the full extent of my circumstances, he began to speak of me in the most enthusiastic terms, as if my small conduct had been some act of extraordinary virtue. I have written before of how my mother saved all her profits for Harry, and how both she and my brother were deep in my debt. But Harry never once thought of the money he spent as anything but his own, and Madam Esmond could never be made to see she had treated me unfairly — once the coin was spent, as far as she was concerned, the matter was closed for good. Then at the end of 1762, I remember, Harry sent a very considerable sum across the Atlantic to purchase his army promotion, writing in the same letter to remind me I was welcome to draw on his agents at any time if I found myself in need. He had no idea how small my own little remaining capital had already been eaten through.

Of course, I could not touch that money meant for his advancement — that would have delayed his promotion for months, even years — and though I own I was sorely tempted at times, I never drew on him. Nor did I dare ask our good General Lambert for funds, knowing as I did how small his own pension was, how little he had to spare. These simple acts of forbearance my worthy brewer insisted on treating as marks of almost saintly virtue. And what did the man do but write privately to my brother out in Virginia, praising me and my wife as the most perfect couple alive, then call unannounced on Madame de Bernstein — who never once told me of his visit, though I soon noticed she began treating us with a singular gentleness and respect that astonished me, for I had always thought her nothing but a selfish, worldly old woman. Years later I asked him how he had ever talked his way into the Baroness’s front door. He laughed loud and long. “De Baroness!” he said. “I knew her husband de Baron when he was no more than a walet in Munich, and I was a poor brewer’s apprentice.” It seemed best not to dig too deep into that particular branch of our family tree, after that.

And so the stretch of my life that ought to have been the most miserable of all was softened by a host of good friends, small happy moments, and strokes of unexpected luck. The “bear” I was hired to lead was a gentle, docile little cub, who danced to my tutelage without complaint. Far better to guide that boy about his lessons, I thought, than hang about the lobbies of booksellers waiting for a publisher to glance at my manuscripts, or dance attendance on theatre managers who might deign to give my play a reading. My wife and I, in what we jokingly called our “Lambeth exile,” spent dozens of warm, pleasant evenings with these kind benefactors. We were not short of small intellectual joys, either: Mrs. Foker and Theo had both fine singing voices, and would perform duets by the hour together. On nights when I felt in good humour, I would read my unperformed play *Pocahontas* aloud to our small circle, and Mr. Foker swore I gave a better performance even than the tragedian Hagan himself.

I saw almost nothing of my namesake young Miles Warrington after that little scrape of his, and heard very little news of my father’s side of the family for months on end. Sir Miles was assiduous in his attendance at court — I do believe he would have fawned as diligently on Nero himself — and I laughed out loud one day when Mr. Foker told me he had heard rumor on the Exchange that “they are going to make your uncle a Beer.” “A Beer?” I said, confused. “Can’t you make out the word, man?” the testy old brewer huffed. “Well then, a Lort!” Sir Miles was indeed the most obedient, humble servant of whatever minister held power at the time. I half expected him to start speaking with a thick Highland accent during the short reign of Lord Bute, the Scottish favorite. I ran into him and his wife once, just after Mrs. Claypool had been presented to the Queen as Lady Warrington, stepping out of their gilded gilt coach. I had our little boy perched on my shoulder. My uncle and aunt stared resolutely straight through me through the coach window, as if I were no more than a paving stone. The footmen holding their nosegays stared blankly ahead. If I had worn the fairy’s magic cap and turned invisible, my father’s own brother could not have passed me by with less notice.

We rarely took up Lady Castlewood’s odd, casual invitation to come take tea and sup at her house when no other company was present. Old Mr. Van den Bosch, shrewd as he was and brilliant at making a fortune, had no gift for small talk — save with his own daughter, who would rattle on for hours about household accounts, stocks and shares, the ups and downs of the city market, buying and selling farm stock, every bit as sharply as her father. My Lord Castlewood was almost never at home, and his wife did not seem to miss him when he was gone, nor was he ever very comfortable in his father-in-law’s company. The Countess told all this to my wife plainly, in her simple, forthright way. “Guess my lord and my father don’t get on together at all, nohow,” she said. “My lord is forever begging for money, and father keeps the key to the strongbox — and quite right too. If he could get his hands on all our fortune, he’d gamble it all away before the month was out, and then what would become of our fine noble family? We pay every last bill, my dear — except play debts, and those we won’t settle for him, not ever. We pay the cooks, the grooms, the wine merchants, the tailors, everyone — and they’re lucky to get their money, for my lord would never have paid them a farthing! And we always make sure he has a guinea or two in his pocket, so he can go about looking like a proper gentleman. Goodness only knows what he owed, and what he was up to, before we came along! My father and I do our best to make him respectable, but it’s no easy job, let me tell you. Law! He’d melt down every piece of family silver if father didn’t keep the key to the strong room, and when we go down to Castlewood House in the country, father travels with me, and he’s armed, too, along with all the servants.”

“Good heavens!” my wife cried. “Your ladyship does not mean to say you suspect your own husband of wanting to —”

“To what? Oh, nothing, of course! And I’d trust our brother Will with untold sums of money, wouldn’t I? Just as much as I’d trust a cat left guarding a cream pan! I tell you, my dear, being a great lady of rank isn’t all fun and games. If I bought myself a countess’s coronet, I paid a pretty dear price for it — that I did, sure enough.”

And my Lord Castlewood had paid a steep price, too, to have his family estate cleared of every last debt and encumbrance, his houses and stables newly furnished, all his old gambling bills wiped clean. He was the complete slave of his little wife and her shrewd old father. No wonder he could not bear the old man’s company, and snuck away from his own grand fine house as often as he could, to drink and feast at his club if he had the coin, or seek out any company at all rather than the stifling dependence he found at home. To work as a bearleader, as I did, was no grand position, I will own. To cool my heels in a bookseller’s antechamber waiting for the man to finish his dinner and deign to give me audience was often a bitter pill for a man of my name, with all my old Virginian pride. But would I have traded my small, honest poverty for Lord Castlewood’s public ignominy? Would I have chosen his miserable bought dependence over my own? Never. At least I earned every penny of my wages. No man can say I ever flattered my patrons, or groveled before them, or was anything but stiff-necked and proud in all my dealings.

And Fate had given me a life partner who bore all our small privations with such sweet, uncomplaining grace that even niggardly Fortune herself seemed to soften before her, like the wild ogre in the old fairy tales who melts at the kindness of a gentle soul. Poor as we were, every soul who knew my wife saw that she was a true fine lady through and through. The little tradespeople, our landlady, all the humble folk round our Lambeth cottage treated her with far more respect than they showed many of the grandest folks in the parish. “My dear,” good-natured Mrs. Foker said once, when they rode out in her chariot, “you look for all the world like the mistress of the carriage, and I only like your maid.” Our landladies adored her. The shopkeepers ran to fill her small orders as eagerly as if a duchess had placed them, as if waiting on her would make their fortunes. I thought often of the Lady in Comus, moving serene and untainted through all the ruck and rabble of the world around her.

As often as we asked, the kind Fokers would lend us their chariot to drive out into the country or call on the few friends we had. To tell the truth, we drove out once to the Protestant Hero tavern and had a syllabub in their gardens, and the landlady insisted on calling my wife “your ladyship” the entire afternoon. We took tea once with Mr. Samuel Johnson, too, and the clever Mr. Oliver Goldsmith was there in the company, and the good doctor escorted my wife all the way out to our carriage when it was time to leave. But our most frequent visits were to Aunt Bernstein, and I was never in the least jealous that she soon developed a great, adoring fondness for Theo.

That fondness grew so fierce that she would send for her three or four days a week, begging her to stay for days at a time, and complained endlessly that Theo’s husband and baby were nothing but pests, keeping her favorite away from her. Not that my wife was not worthy of all anyone’s affection, of course, but the constant small difficulty of getting away from our cottage to visit the old baroness only fanned her affection into something near a passion. She poured out notes to Theo that read like love letters, and her servants were forever knocking at our kitchen door at odd hours of the day. If my wife did not go to her, she would send heartbreaking appeals, and scold me roundly whenever I called on her for keeping Theo away. Once our little boy fell ill — Fate has seen fit to spare that Captain to be a considerable trial to our tempers these many years — and Madame Bernstein turned up at our cottage three days running, vowing there was nothing at all wrong with the baby, that we were only pretending he was sick to vex her.

The current Countess of Castlewood was just as brash and unceremonious with her old aunt as she was with everyone else, high or low. “What are you all scraping and bowing to that old woman for, I can’t make heads nor tails of it!” she would say. “Her a fine lady! Nonsense! She ain’t no finer than any other lady walking this earth, and I’m just as good as any of them with their high heels and their grand airs! Her a beauty once? Take away her wig, her rouge, her false teeth — and what’s left of that beauty, I’d like to know? You could tuck the whole lot in a bandbox, and all you’d find left is a shrivelled up old woman!” And the plain-spoken little Countess spoke nothing but the truth. All beauty must fade at last, wither on the vine or rot underground. Here was old age with no reverence left to it, grey hairs hidden under wigs and painted over, the old Baroness still clinging to the world with her crutch at fourscore years old. She had had beauty, pleasure, flattery in her time — but what secret rages, disappointments, humiliations she must have suffered! What thorns hidden under all those roses! What stinging bees tucked inside the sweetest fruit! “You are no beauty, my dear,” she would say to my wife, “and you may thank your stars for it. Don’t tell me your husband adores your face and you care for no other man’s admiration! Every woman in the world would rather be beautiful than anything else — richer than Croesus, perfectly good, gifted with every fairy blessing, it matters not! Look at that portrait yonder — it’s a poor enough painting, that vain fool Kneller never could capture my eyes, my hair, my skin. What a figure I had then! And look at me now, this wrinkled old neck! Why must our time of beauty be so short? I remember seeing Mademoiselle de l’Enclos when she was far older than me, still fresh and lovely. We can’t hide our ages. They are written down in the parish books for all to see. I was born the very last year of King James’s reign. I am not old, not really — only seventy-six. But what a wreck I am, my dear — isn’t it cruel that our time should run out so fast?”

My wife would reply with the soft, gentle truth that the time of every living soul on this earth is short, no matter how high or low their station.

“Ah!” the Baroness would cry. “Did not Adam live nigh on a thousand years, and was not Eve beautiful every single day of it? I used to puzzle that poor creature Mr. Tusher with that argument, years ago! What have we done since then, that our lives are shrunk so small, I ask you?”
