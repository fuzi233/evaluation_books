I went after him into the pine-forest, and the world narrowed to resin and shadow.

The air there had a clean bite, as if someone had split fir-logs all night and left their pale wounds to sweeten the dark. Needles carpeted the ground, giving back our footfalls softened and swallowed, while above us the boughs held their own low, chapel-like hush. Only the raven’s wings now and then made a dry, deliberate sound—paper on paper—so that I began to feel the forest not merely as a sight but as an instrument, tuned to quiet; a place where any careless noise would bruise the mind.

We went deeper. The trunks grew thicker, older, each tree more like a face that had lived long enough to forget how to pretend. Some were knotted into grotesque gestures, as if age had turned their limbs into a language of its own. Then, gradually, the pines stood farther apart; the shade thinned and the scent changed—less pitch, more open air, with a faint dryness like heather warmed in sun.

My guide halted on the edge of a heath and pointed with his beak.

“You see that hawthorn?” he said.

I looked where the wood broke and the open land began, and I answered what my eyes insisted on offering me: “I see a gnarled old man, with a great white head.”

“Look again,” he returned. “It is a hawthorn.”

“It seems indeed an ancient hawthorn,” I said, still half resisting what he required of my seeing; “but this is not the season for the hawthorn to blossom!”

“The season for the hawthorn to blossom,” he replied, “is when the hawthorn blossoms.”

The way he said it was not argument but an overturning, like tipping a pot and watching the contents obey gravity without caring what the label claims. The blossoms were there all the same—white as sifted flour, dense as foam. Their smell reached me even at a distance, not the thin prettiness I expected out of season, but a thick, living sweetness edged with something sharp, like apple peel bruised on a stone.

“That tree,” he went on, “is in the ruins of the church on your home-farm.”

The words landed with the strange certainty of a remembered taste returning to the tongue before the mind admits it is hungry.

“You were going to give some directions to the bailiff about its churchyard, were you not, the morning of the thunder?”

I found the moment in me as if I had opened a cupboard and touched a familiar bowl in the dark. “I was going to tell him I wanted it turned into a wilderness of rose-trees,” I said, “and that the plough must never come within three yards of it.”

“Listen!” said the raven, and for an instant even he seemed to hold his breath.

I listened.

At first there was only the forest’s own careful silence, the kind that has weight. Then—so faint that I could not have sworn to it in any other place—I caught something that might have been the sighing of a far-off musical wind, or else the ghost of music that had once been glad and had not yet learned to be sad. It hovered like a melody half remembered after waking, when the mind can still taste it but cannot speak it.

Or did I indeed hear anything?

“They go there still,” said the raven.

“Who goes there?” I asked. “And where do they go?”

“Some of the people who used to pray there,” he answered, “go to the ruins still.”

“But they will not go much longer, I think.”

“What makes them go now?”

“They need help from each other to get their thinking done, and their feelings hatched,” he said, as if speaking of something as ordinary as kneading dough until it learns to hold together. “So they talk and sing together; and then, they say, the big thought floats out of their hearts like a great ship out of the river at high water.”

The image would not leave me—hearts as harbours, thought as a vessel that cannot be launched alone. And behind it, like a warning I could taste as bitterness under sweetness, was the sense that such gatherings were becoming rare: that whatever words they used when they met, whatever old turns of phrase their grandparents’ mouths had carried, were thinning, being replaced by the loud, easy currency of elsewhere. Even a hymn, shouted in a borrowed tongue, can become a kind of noise.

“Do they pray as well as sing?” I asked.

“No,” said the raven. “They have found that each can best pray in his own silent heart.—Some people are always at their prayers.—Look! look! There goes one!”

He pointed right up into the air.

A snow-white pigeon was mounting, with quick and yet quicker wing-flap, the unseen spiral of an ethereal stair. The sunlight caught it and made the air around its wings tremble, flashing and quivering, as if brightness itself were beating time.

“I see a pigeon,” I said, foolishly literal, as if naming it could keep my world from being altered.

“Of course you see a pigeon,” rejoined the raven, “for there is the pigeon! I see a prayer on its way.—I wonder now what heart is that dove’s mother! Some one may have come awake in my cemetery!”

“How can a pigeon be a prayer?” I asked. “I understand, of course, how it should be a fit symbol or likeness for one; but a live pigeon to come out of a heart!”

“It MUST puzzle you!” he returned. “It cannot fail to do so!”

“A prayer is a thought,” I insisted, clinging to my definitions the way a man clings to a recipe he has never actually cooked from. “A thing spiritual!”

“Very true!” said the raven. “But if you understood any world besides your own, you would understand your own much better.—When a heart is really alive, then it is able to think live things.”

His voice had that dry certainty again, like a knife that knows the grain of wood.

“There is one heart,” he went on, “all whose thoughts are strong, happy creatures, and whose very dreams are lives. When some pray, they lift heavy thoughts from the ground, only to drop them on it again; others send up their prayers in living shapes, this or that, the nearest likeness to each. All live things were thoughts to begin with, and are fit therefore to be used by those that think. When one says to the great Thinker:—‘Here is one of thy thoughts: I am thinking it now!’ that is a prayer—a word to the big heart from one of its own little hearts.—Look, there is another!”

This time he pointed not upward but down—toward the foot of a block of granite, half sunk into the earth as if the heath had tried to swallow it.

There, in a crease of soil and stone, was a little flower.

I had never seen one like it. I felt it before I understood it, as you can feel a fragrance and be caught by a memory you cannot translate into speech. Its form was gracious and trusting, its colour not loud but certain—pale rose-hue like the inside of a shell, and in the centre a golden heart, warm as if it had stored sunlight for leaner hours. It carried an odour as of a new world that was yet the old: something like crushed strawberry leaf and young honey, with an undertone of rain on warm dust. The scent did not merely enter the nose; it seemed to enter the attention, making every other thought fall quiet so that this one could be heard.

“That is a prayer-flower,” said the raven.

“I never saw such a flower before!” I said, and the words sounded blunt beside what I felt.

“There is no other such,” he answered. “Not one prayer-flower is ever quite like another.”

“How do you know it a prayer-flower?”

“By the expression of it,” he said. “More than that I cannot tell you. If you know it, you know it; if you do not, you do not.”

His refusal was not unkind; it was like telling a man you cannot give him your tongue and ear and childhood, cannot lend him the secret weight of a word that has only ever lived in one mouth.

“Could you not teach me to know a prayer-flower when I see it?” I asked.

“I could not,” said the raven. “But if I could, what better would you be? you would not know it of YOURSELF and ITself! Why know the name of a thing when the thing itself you do not know? Whose work is it but your own to open your eyes?”

Then, as if the universe itself had a voice and it used his, he added, “But indeed the business of the universe is to make such a fool of you that you will know yourself for one, and so begin to be wise!”

I stood there, looking down at the flower tucked against the granite, and I did see—at least I saw that it was different from any flower I had ever seen before. And because I could not make it fit any remembered category, I knew I must be seeing, in its tenderness and its scent, a shadow of a prayer in it.

A great awe came over me to think of the heart listening to the flower.
