About ten o'clock on the morning of February 27th, the flow of pedestrians in the Florentine streets shifted decisively toward San Marco. It was the final day of the Carnival, and everyone knew that a second Bonfire of Vanities was being prepared before the Old Palace; yet, at this hour, it was plain that the center of public attention lay elsewhere. The Piazza di San Marco teemed with a multitude exhibiting no movement save that caused by newcomers pressing forward from every opening, crowding against the tightly packed front ranks. These ranks formed a dense semicircle before the church, enclosing the Dominican friars of San Marco as they assembled. However, the temporary wooden pulpit erected above the church door remained empty.

Soon, it would be occupied by the man whom the Pope's decree had banished from the Duomo's pulpit, whom fellow clergy were forbidden to associate with, and whom the citizens dared not listen to without fear of excommunication. This man had declared, "A wicked, unbelieving Pope, who seized the pontifical chair through bribery, is not Christ's Vicar. His curses are broken swords; he grasps a hilt without a blade. His commands contradict the Christian life: it is lawful to disobey them—nay, *it is unlawful to obey them*." Despite the Pope hanging terrible threats over Florence, demanding the city renounce this "pestilential schismatic" and send him to Rome to be "converted," the people still flocked to hear him preach in his own church of San Marco. On this very morning, they accepted communion from his excommunicated hands.

For what if this Brother truly held sway over divine lightnings greater than that official successor of Saint Peter? It was a momentous question that the masses could never resolve by the Friar's ultimate test: determining what aligned with the highest spiritual law. No. In such a crisis, if God had chosen the Friar as His prophet to rebuke the High Priest wearing the mystic raiment unworthily, He would attest His choice with an unmistakable sign. So long as belief in the Prophet offered no threat of external calamity, but rather the confident hope of exceptional safety, no sign was required; his preaching was merely music to which the people marched willingly. But now, since belief threatened their commerce, shook their standing among the Italian states, and risked an interdict on their city, the inevitable question arose: *"What miracle showest thou?"*

Slowly at first, then faster, this fatal demand swelled in Savonarola's ear, provoking a response. Outwardly, he declared that the miracle would come at the fitting time; inwardly, he clung to a faith—not entirely unwavering, for what faith ever is?—that if the need for a miracle became urgent, the magnitude of his task was too great for Divine power to abandon. His faith wavered, but not his speech. It is the lot of every speaker addressing a crowd to often speak in virtue of yesterday's faith, hoping it returns tomorrow.

Prepared for a scene designed to answer the popular impatience for a supernatural guarantee of his mission, the wooden pulpit stood ready. Yet, while ordinary friars in black mantles entered and arranged themselves, the multitude's faces were not yet eagerly fixed on the pulpit. There was a sense that Savonarola would not appear immediately, and curiosity lingered in identifying the various monks—some belonging to high Florentine families, others connected to the artisans and shopkeepers forming the bulk of the crowd. Only when the procession of monks concluded, fluttering their books and beginning to chant, did people whisper, "Fra Girolamo must be coming now."

That expectation, rather than any spell cast by the familiar wail of psalmody, spread a silence and anticipation over the multitude like a pale, solemn light. All faces turned upward toward the empty pulpit. Instantly, the space was no longer empty. A figure cloaked head-to-foot in black cowl and mantle stepped inside, kneeling with bowed head and face averted. To the eager crowd, the time seemed endless as the black figure knelt and the monks chanted. Yet the stillness held; the Friar's private audiences with Heaven were charged with an electric awe that unnerved even those who might have stoned him.

Finally, a vibration rippled through the crowd, each person seemingly touching their neighbor with an aspen-leaf tremor, as watchers in the sky perceive the silent arrival of the awaited presence. The Friar rose, turned toward the people, and partially pushed back his cowl. The monotonous chanting ceased. To those nearest the pulpit, it seemed as if the sounds that had filled their ears moments before suddenly merged into the force of Savonarola's flashing glance as he surveyed the silence.

He stretched out his hands. In their exquisite delicacy, they appeared transfigured—from mere organs for grasping into vessels of sensitivity too acute to require rough contact. They emerged like an appealing speech from the part of his soul masked by his strong, passionate face, now etched with deeper lines around the mouth and brow than forty-four years of ordinary life would warrant. At the first extension of these hands, some in the front ranks fell to their knees, and scattered devout disciples further back followed suit. But the great majority stood firm. Some resisted the impulse to kneel before this excommunicated man (fearing that a great judgment might fall upon them in this act of blessing); others bristled with scorn and hatred for the ambitious deceiver staging this new comedy, before which they nonetheless felt powerless, as before the triumph of a fleeting fashion.

Then came the voice, clear and low at first, uttering the words of absolution—"*Misereatur vestri*"—and more fell to their knees. As it rose higher and clearer, the upright heads grew fewer and fewer. By the time he spoke "*Benedicat vos omnipotens Deus*," it erupted into a masculine cry, as if protesting its power to bless under the grip of a demon seeking to stifle it. It rang like a trumpet to the farthest reaches of the Piazza, and beneath its sound, every head bowed.

After the benediction, Savonarola himself fell to his knees, hiding his face in temporary exhaustion. Such great jets of emotion were a necessary part of his existence; he had told the people long ago, "Without preaching I cannot live." But it was a life that shattered him. Minutes later, some rose to their feet, though a larger number remained kneeling, all faces intent on watching him. He took a crystal vessel containing the consecrated Host into his hands and addressed the crowd.

"You remember, my children, three days ago I besought you," he said, holding the Sacrament openly before them all, "to pray fervently to the Most High that if this work of mine does not come from Him, He will send a fire to consume me, that I may vanish into eternal darkness, away from His light which I have hidden with my falsity. Again I beseech you to make that prayer, and to make it *now*."

It was a breathless moment. Perhaps no one truly prayed, unless some, in a spirit of devout obedience, made the effort. Every consciousness was dominated by the sense that Savonarola was praying—in a voice not loud, but distinctly audible in the wide stillness. "Lord, if I have not wrought in sincerity of soul, if my word cometh not from Thee, strike me in this moment with Thy thunder, and let the fires of Thy wrath enclose me."

He ceased speaking and stood motionless, holding the sacred Mystery, eyes uplifted, his entire aspect quivering with excitement. Everyone else was equally motionless and silent. Sunlight, which for the last quarter-hour had pierced the greyness in fitful streaks, struck the convent wall, causing some awe-stricken spectators to startle timidly. Soon, however, a wider parting occurred. With a gentle quickness, like a smile, a stream of brightness poured onto the crystal vase and then spread over Savonarola's face with mild glorification.

An instantaneous shout rang through the Piazza: "Behold the answer!" The warm radiance thrilled through Savonarola's frame, as did the shout. It was his last moment of untroubled triumph. In his rapturous confidence, he felt carried to a grander scene yet to come, before an audience representing all Christendom, where he would once again be sealed as the messenger of supreme righteousness, feeling himself fully charged with Divine strength. It was but a moment that expanded into a vast vision.

While the shout still echoed in his ears, he turned away within the church, feeling the strain too great to endure longer. When the Friar had disappeared and the sunlight seemed to lose its special illumination, spreading impartially over both clean and unclean alike, a confusion of voices began alongside the crowd's general movement. Certain strong discords and varying scales of laughter revealed that during the previous silence and universal kneeling, hostility and scorn had only submitted unwillingly to a momentary spell.

"It seems to me the plaudits are giving way to criticism," observed Tito, who had watched the scene attentively from an upper loggia in a house opposite the church. "Nevertheless, it was a striking moment, was it not, Messer Pietro? Fra Girolamo is a man to make one understand that there was a time when the monk's frock was a symbol of power over men's minds rather than over the keys of women's cupboards."
