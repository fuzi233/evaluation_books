About a week later, the two friends sat side by side, yet the air between them hummed with a dissonance that shattered any pretense of camaraderie. Pietro attacked his canvas with the erratic violence of a storm-tossed ship; he wrought miracles in moments, only to collapse into sullen inertia, cursing the very medium he had just conquered, before resuming his assault in a fresh fury to end it with a groan of defeat. In stark contrast, the shrewd Dutchman worked with a serene, unbroken rhythm, his hands moving with the calm precision of a clockmaker, a quiet smile fixed upon his lips as he observed the chaos. Plainly, Gerard, who had mastered every fool he ever befriended, had successfully harnessed the wild Onagra. Together, they painted playing cards—a trivial craft designed merely to boil the pot. Once finished, the indignant master seized his larger work to begin his daily pilgrimage in search of a buyer. Gerard implored him to take the deck as well, urging him to test the market. The merchant eyed the cards with the suspicion of a rattlesnake, but eventually, embracing Gerard in the warm, open style of Italy, he accepted them. First, however, he dried the final pieces under the fierce, golden sun of that blessed climate.

Left alone, Gerard sketched a few Greek letters and then stitched a tear in his hose. His landlady discovered him thus occupied and queried, with ironic amusement, whether there were no women in the house. "When you have finished that," she said, "come and speak with Teresa, my friend—the woman I mentioned to you, whose husband is a man of little worth, yet who boasts acquaintance with the great." Gerard descended, and who should meet him but the Roman matron. "Ah, Madam," he greeted her, "is it truly you? The good dame told me nothing of this. And the little fair-haired boy—is he well? Is he none the worse for his voyage in that strange boat?" "He is well," replied the matron. "But pray, what are you two discussing?" demanded the landlady, staring alternately at each of them with wide, suspicious eyes. "And why do you tremble so, Teresa mia?" "He saved my child's life," Teresa declared, struggling to compose her features. "Saved your child?" the landlady exclaimed. "My lodger? And he never uttered a word of such a thing! Are you not ashamed to look me in the face?" "Alas, speak not harshly to him," interjected the matron gently. Turning to her friend, she poured forth a glowing eulogy of Gerard's conduct, during which the young painter stood flushed like a maiden, scarcely recognizing his own actions, as gratitude painted them in hues far more radiant than reality. "To think you should ask me to serve your lodger, of whom I knew naught save that he bore your good word, oh, Fiammina; and that was sufficient for me. Dear youth, in serving you, I serve myself." What followed was an eager discourse between the two women regarding the strategies required to breach the impenetrable wall of fees, commissions, and legal chicanery that separated the patrons of art from the unknown artist in the Eternal City. Teresa smiled with a sad pity at Gerard's naive simplicity in having left specimens of his skill at the doors of the mighty without offering bribes. "What!" she cried. "Without promising the servants a share? Without even tipping them to allow the Signori to view your merchandise! It might as well have been thrown into the Tiber." "Well-a-day!" sighed Gerard. "Then how is an artist to find a patron? For artists are poor, not rich." "By fleeing to some city nobler and less greedy than this," Teresa advised. "The Roman court does not wish for sheep without wool." She fell into contemplation and promised to return the next day. The landlady congratulated Gerard warmly. "Teresa has something brewing in her head," she noted.

Scarcely had Teresa departed when Pietro returned with his picture, his countenance black as a thunderhead. Gerard exchanged a glance with the landlady and followed him upstairs to offer comfort. "What? Have they allowed you to bring home your masterpiece?" "As heretofore," Pietro grumbled. "More fools they, then." "That is not necessarily a bad thing." "Pray, what is the matter?" "They have bought the cards!" Pietro shouted, hammering the empty air furiously left and right. "All the better," Gerard replied cheerfully. "They flew at me for them. They were utterly enraptured. They tried desperately to conceal their longing, but failed miserably. I saw, I feigned, I pillaged; curse these boobies." He flung a dozen small silver coins onto the floor, leaped upon them, dancing with the basilisk-like eyes of a gambler, kicking them assiduously until they spun and scattered across the room. Down went Gerard on his knees, following the maltreated innocents directly, transferring them tenderly into his purse. "You should rather smile at their ignorance and put it to profit," he suggested. "And so I will," Pietro retorted, his indignation now concentrated and steaming. "The brutes! We shall paint a pack a day; we shall set the entire city gambling and ruining itself, while we live like princes upon its vices and stupidity." "There was one of the queens, though," Pietro continued, his tone softening, "that I had fain kept back. 'Twas you who limned her, brother. She had lovely red-brown hair and sapphire eyes, and above all, a soul." "Pietro," said Gerard softly, "I painted that one from my heart." The quick-witted Italian nodded, his eyes twinkling with understanding. "You love her so dearly, yet you leave her." "Pietro, it is precisely because I love her so dear that I have wandered this weary road." This intimate colloquy was abruptly interrupted by the landlady's cry from below: "Come down, you are wanted." He descended, and there stood Teresa once more. "Come with me, Ser Gerard."
