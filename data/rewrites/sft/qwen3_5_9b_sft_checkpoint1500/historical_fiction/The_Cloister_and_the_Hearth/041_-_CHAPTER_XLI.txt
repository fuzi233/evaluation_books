Denys, positioned squarely between his companions to prevent any desperate flight, was borne away in a torment of grief and regret. Because of him, Gerard had forsaken the direct German road to Rome; and what recompense awaited him? To be stranded utterly alone in the heart of Burgundy. This crushing realization drove Denys to madness, causing him to rage against the heavens and the earth, alternating with a silence so bleak and ominous that his captors felt compelled to subdue him. They seized their leader just beyond the town limits, bringing the entire cavalcade to a halt at the "Tête d'Or" inn.

Though the young landlady was deeply preoccupied with the Count and far more so with the Bastard, her gaze fell upon Denys, and she asked anxiously what had become of his youthful companion. With a sudden outburst of sorrow, Denys recounted everything, imploring her to dispatch someone after Gerard. "Now that he is severed from me, he may perhaps heed my counsel," he pleaded. "Poor wretch, he cannot bear solitude."

The landlady gave a sharp shake of her head. "I believe I have already shown you kindness enough," she replied, flushing slightly.

"You will not?"

"Not I."

"And then"—and he unleashed a torrent of curses and vituperation upon her.

She turned her back on him, departing with whimpers and complaints that she was unaccustomed to such treatment, before ordering her servant to saddle two mules. Denys rode north with his troop, silent and dejected over his saddle, unaware that this truthful young woman had transformed herself into a rider within merely forty minutes. Driven by genuine urgency, she spurred away with her servant in the exact opposite direction.

By nightfall, after a grueling march, the Bastard and his men arrived at "The White Hart." Their entrance stirred a tremendous commotion, and it took some time before Manon spotted her old friend amidst the throng. When she finally did, her reaction betrayed itself only through flushed cheeks. She did not acknowledge the acquaintance openly. The poor soul seemed already to be hardening his heart, scorning the connection.

Denys saw her but could not smile. The inn brought memories of Gerard too vividly to allow for cheer. Before the night fully closed, the wind shifted. Manon entered the room and beckoned to him with a finger. He rose sullenly, followed by his guards.

"Nay, I wish to speak a word to thee in private."

She led him to a shadowed corner of the chamber and whispered urgently if he would perform her a kindness. He answered aloud, "No, I will not; I am not in the mood to bestow kindness upon man or woman. If I do grant a favor, it shall be to a dog—and not even that, unless necessity demands it."

"Alas, good Archer, I once aided thee and thy fair comrade," said Manon humbly.

"You did, Dame, you did; well then, for his sake—what is required?"

"Thou knowest my tale. I have known better fortune. Now I am honored, yet a woman has hurled insults at me today regarding him, and thus it shall remain until he hangs there. I desire him removed; alas!"

"With all my heart."

"And no other dares I beseech save thee. Wilt thou do it?"

"Not I, even were I not a captive."

Upon this stern refusal, the gentle Manon sighed, pressing her palms together in despair. Denys assured her not to worry. There were soldiers of a lesser caliber who would not hesitate to take two pieces of silver. It was a simple transaction of coin; if she could procure two angels, he would locate two men to perform the dirty work of "The White Hart."

This proposition was not entirely palatable, yet Manon reflected that soldiers were transient birds, drinking deeply tonight to be silenced tomorrow. Softly, she agreed, "Send them to me. But pray, instruct them that this is for a friend of mine; let them not believe it is for me. I would sink into the earth; times have changed."

Denys secured warriors eager to win an angel apiece with such ease. He dispatched them and immediately dismissed the subject with contempt, sitting instead to brood over his lost friend. Soon, Manon and the warriors reached a mutual understanding regarding the operation. However, a question lingered: What of the body once removed? She murmured, "The river runs near—the spot."

"Fling him in, then?"

"Nay, nay; be not so cruel! Might ye not lay him gently—with some heavy stones?"

She must have pondered the logistics in depth, for she was not a mind given to swift ideas. All arrangements were swiftly concluded, barring the timing of payment. The armored men itched for their due and sought it in advance. Manon hesitated at this demand. Did she doubt their word? Then let her accompany them, or watch from afar.

"Me?" cried Manon in horror. "I would sooner die than witness it."

"Which yet thou wouldst have done."

"Ay, for sore is my need. Times have changed."

She had already forgotten her earlier admonition to Denys. An hour later, the disagreeable remnant of their caterpillar-like existence ceased to mar the respectable matron's public life, and the grim eyes of the past stopped casting malignant glares downward into the white hind's clover field. Her total tally stood at a sound average: she became the landlord's wife, a devoted house-hound, and outlived everyone around her.

Her troops, returning from executing the medieval axiom, "Away with the old love," received a jarring shock. They found the marketplace black with clusters of people, whereas it had been vacant an hour prior. Conscience struck them. Such trouble stemmed from meddling with the dead. Nevertheless, the bolder of the pair, emboldened by the darkness, slipped forward alone and stealthily merged into a crowd. He soon returned to his companion, speaking in a tone of reproach that was hardly reasonable:

"You born fool, 'twas nothing but a miracle."
