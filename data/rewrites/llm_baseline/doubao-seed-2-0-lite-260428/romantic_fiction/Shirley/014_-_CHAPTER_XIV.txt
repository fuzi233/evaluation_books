The first thought that drifted through Caroline Helstone’s sleepy head when the rectory rooster crowed her awake was soft, unarguable, and sharp as a shard of thin winter ice. Of course he will marry Shirley.

She sat up in her chintz-curtained bed, fingers twisting in the frayed linen of her nightgown, and breathed in the familiar scent of lavender sachets tucked under her pillow. It was true, and it was right, she told herself firmly. Shirley was wealthy enough to shore up all of Robert Moore’s struggling mill ambitions, clever enough to stand at his side against every hardship the hungry, restive workers of Briarfield threw his way. But the next thought cut deeper, unbidden: I shall be forgotten, the second they are wed.

Oh, she would be nothing to him then. Not his cousin, not the quiet girl who sat up half the night mending his torn cuffs when he came in soaked from a storm, not the one who knew every quirk of his grim, kind smile. She was no match for Shirley, after all: she had nothing but a small dowry, a quiet tongue, no grand fortune to offer. What was the point of lingering, playing the dear platonic friend, pretending she did not burn with a love hot enough to consume her when he was in the room? She would not stoop to that hypocrisy. She could not be half of anything to Robert Moore—she had to be all, or nothing at all. Sunder us then, Providence, she thought, pressing a hand to her aching chest. Part us speedily.

That sharp, heavy ache was still coiled tight under her ribs late that afternoon, when a familiar figure drifted past the parlour window. Shirley Keeldar wandered by slow, her indigo wool skirts swishing over the gravel path, that usual half-wistful, half-careless look on her face that Caroline had long since come to know better than her own reflection. When Shirley was animated, that distant wistfulness melted into a bright, warm gaiety no one else could muster, her laugh never harsh or sharp, always sweet with some quiet undercurrent of sentiment that made every moment in her company feel like a gift.

The parlour door opened, and Shirley stepped in, bonnet askew.
“What do you mean by skipping our afternoon walk, just as you promised?” she demanded, not angry, just amused, her eyes already sharp on Caroline’s drawn face.

“I wasn’t in the humour,” Caroline said, and it was no lie.

Shirley’s gaze held hers for a long moment. “No. I can see you’re not in the humour for loving me right now. You’re in one of those sunless, cold moods, where you’d rather no one at all bothered you. Do you even realize how quiet you get when you’re hurt, little wounded deer?” She did not wait for an answer, just untied her bonnet strings and tossed it onto the side table, making herself at home without being invited. “Do you think I won’t notice you’re bleeding, if you hide it from me?”

“I never feared you would hurt me, Shirley,” Caroline said softly.

“Then why do you avoid me half the time? I can feel when I’m being shunned, you know. You walked home last night with Moore, the two of you lingering so long over a single mile you didn’t get back to the rectory till ten. Which of you was dragging your feet, hmm?”

Caroline’s face flushed. “You talk nonsense, Shirley.”

“Of course he talked nonsense, or worse—looked nonsense, that soft, half-stern look he gets when he’s not guarding his face. I can see the reflection of his dark eyes on your forehead right now. I was half tempted to march out last night and challenge him to a duel, if I could find a trustworthy second. I’ve got pistols, you know, and I can use them.”

“Stuff and nonsense! Who would you have shot, me or Robert?” Caroline laughed, despite herself.

“Neither. Probably a bat, or a tree branch. Your cousin is a puppy, you know—quiet, serious, ambitious, sensible, but a puppy all the same. I cannot stand the way he keeps intruding between us. If it weren’t for him, we’d be the dearest friends in the world. But that six feet of stubborn man keeps eclipsing the friendship I want to keep bright and clear forever. You didn’t want my company this afternoon, and it stings. I’m tired of being nothing more than a nuisance to you, whenever he’s nearby.”

Caroline’s throat felt tight. She reached out, and caught Shirley’s cold hand in hers. “No. That’s not true. I like you, Shirley. More than like you. I was never the sort to rush into a loud, fiery friendship all at once, the way other girls do, but you’re nothing like any ordinary young lady. I esteem you. I value you. There’s no one else in the world I’d rather sit in silence with, no one else who makes me feel safe when my heart feels like it’s tearing in two. Love hurts so, Shirley. It burns you up from the inside out, racks you with pain you can never speak of. But this—this affection between us, soft, no fire, no torment—this is balm. This is sustenance. I never had a sister, but I think this is what it would feel like. Nothing could break it. Not even him.”

Shirley’s face softened, all that sharp edge melting away. She squeezed Caroline’s hand back. “All right, then. We really are friends, no matter how much that troublesome man tries to get between us?”

“Chance what may,” Caroline promised, tugging her down to sit on the sofa beside her.

They were just about to turn their talk to lighter things, when the rector, Mr. Helstone, stepped into the room, and the moment passed. Shirley delayed in the passage to say goodbye later, her voice quiet and heavy.
“Caroline, I’ve got a great weight on my mind. Not my private conscience, the stupid, over-serious conscience that comes with being a lady of the manor. There’s a stern, hard influence settling over this valley, something I don’t approve of but can’t quite fight. There’s going to be trouble soon, I fear—trouble that will hurt him, too. I’m going to try to head it off by good works. Don’t be surprised if you see me turn outrageously charitable all of a sudden. I don’t know the first thing about running relief schemes, so you’ll have to help me. We’ll ask that sweet, sensible Miss Ainley over to Fieldhead tomorrow. I’m putting myself under her tutelage. Drop her a hint that I’m a very ignorant pupil when it comes to clothing societies and soup kitchens, won’t you? She won’t be half so shocked at my stupidity then.”

The next morning, Caroline found Shirley hunched over her desk at Fieldhead, a pile of banknotes, a fat purse, and a household account book spread out in front of her, her expression so gravely serious it was almost comical.
“I tried to do the accounts,” she confessed, looking up, a little sheepish. “I lectured Mrs. Gill the cook on economy till I was practically eloquent—then I looked at the books, and I couldn’t bring myself to cut a single pound of butter, or ask where all the dripping went, or question the stacks of soap and candles that could keep half the parish going for a month. I couldn’t even hint to her that she’s been cheating me a little. I’m a coward, Caroline. Physical danger doesn’t faze me—last week I faced down Wynne’s great red bull in the cowslip lea without flinching—but I couldn’t bear to see Mrs. Gill humiliated. But the books add up. I’ve got three hundred pounds spare, and the poor of Briarfield are starving. I have to help them.”

“Don’t go throwing shillings out the door willy-nilly,” Caroline protested. “You’ll get taken advantage of. Miss Ainley is exactly the right person to run the scheme for you. The scruples of all those rich people who say charity degrades the poor are just nonsense to anyone who’s ever gone hungry. Life’s too short, and too bitter, to let people suffer when we have the means to help them.”

Shirley’s eyes flashed, suddenly sharp, fire under the soft surface. “I know. The poor are suffering now, and their anger is building. If that anger turns into a mob, if they try to burn down Robert’s mill, hurt him, I’ll turn on them like a tigress. I’ll defend what’s mine—what’s his—with every bit of strength I have. But I’m going to do everything I can to make sure it never comes to that. I know their hatred comes from pain. I’m going to ease that pain, before it turns to fire.”

Caroline’s own eyes glowed back, her hand tightening on Shirley’s arm. “If I had half your power to help him, I’d use every last bit of it to stand by his side, till the day I died.”

Off they went to fetch Miss Ainley, who was near to tears with joy when she arrived at Fieldhead, to be seated at the head of the table with paper, pen, ink, and the full three hundred pounds laid out before her. She knew every poor family in three parishes by name, knew exactly what each of them needed to get back on their feet, and she drew up a simple, brilliant relief plan in no time at all, already seeing the hungry fed, the naked clothed, the sick warmed by the money. She insisted they consult the three rectors of Briarfield, Whinbury, and Nunnely before they took a single step, for the clergy were sacred in her eyes, their station making them holy even when the curates were silly, arrogant boys barely worthy to carry her cotton umbrella.

Shirley laughed, and made one strict stipulation: the snot-nosed young curates were to have no hand in the money whatsoever, their meddling fingers were to stay far away from the charity pie. The rectors were sensible enough, could be trusted, but the boys were to be kept far from the till. It was only Caroline’s soft, gentle defence of poor sweet Mr. Sweeting—Miss Ainley’s favourite curate, who she always fed sponge cake and cowslip wine when he visited her cottage—that calmed the good lady’s horror at such a rule.

By that evening, Shirley had managed to gather all three senior clergymen at Fieldhead for a small, private council. She walked stubborn old Welsh Dr. Boultby and gruff Mr. Helstone around the rose gardens, tucking bunches of sweetbriar into their buttonholes, as warm and affectionate as a favourite daughter. The gentle Mr. Hall she left to Caroline, who slipped her arm through his, and led him slowly towards the parlour where Miss Ainley waited, all her papers spread out, the warm light of the fire gilding the banknotes laid out on the table. No one spoke of the dark clouds gathering on the horizon, of the restive workers, of the quiet, shared love the two young women bore the same man. For that one soft evening, they only spoke of good things, of bread for the hungry, of coats for the cold, of peace that might stretch across the valley, unbroken.
