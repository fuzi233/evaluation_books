An ancient English Cathedral Tower—massive, grey, square, planted like a stubborn old root in the landscape—rose where it had no right to be. It stood behind everything the eye could name, behind the daybreak haze, behind the very thought that summoned it. Yet between the watcher and that familiar tower there thrust up a spike: thin, rusty, upright, insolent.

Who had put the spike there?

In the same breath, the air broke into clangour. Cymbals struck hard as tin trays slapped in a scullery. A Sultan went by in long procession, as if the alley outside had become a parade-ground; scimitars winked like knives lifted to a kitchen lamp; dancing-girls scattered flowers that might have been herbs torn up too roughly—mint and marigold, bruised to scent. White elephants followed, caparisoned in colours like spilled spice—saffron, paprika, verdigris—endless, attended by endless feet. The Cathedral Tower would not yield its place in the background. The spike waited for a writhing body that never came.

Stay. The spike was lower, meaner. Not a stake for impalement at all, but the rusty iron upright of a broken bedstead—one corner kicked awry, as if some heavy sleeper had fought his own dreams and lost.

There should have been laughter at that discovery; there was, somewhere in the fog of thought, a vague period of drowsy mirth. Then the man—shaking from head to foot as if he had been held too long over a fire that gives no warmth—pushed himself up, braced on his arms, and looked about.

He was in the meanest, closest of small rooms: a box of stale air and stained plaster. Through a ragged curtain the early light seeped in, not clean and gold, but thin and grudging, like broth poured out after the meat is gone. It came from a miserable court below, where damp lay in corners and the day’s first sounds were not birdsong but the scuffle of rats and the clack of a distant latch.

He lay dressed, across a large unseemly bed, upon the very bedstead that had given way. The iron post he had dreamed as a murderous spike was beside him indeed, jutting up with the dull red-brown of old rust.

Not longwise across the bed but athwart it too, dressed as he was, were three others: a Chinaman; a Lascar; and a haggard woman whose thinness made her seem made of string and ash. The two men were sunk in sleep or stupor. The woman bent over a little pipe, blowing at it to kindle it, shading the ember with her lean hand so that the red spark glowed brighter—an ugly kind of hearth, a small private dawn. With every breath she drew the room’s sourness through her lungs and sent it back out with a sickly sweetness that clung to the throat and hair and clothes.

“Another?” she whispered—querulous, rattling, as if her voice had been kept too long in a cupboard with broken crockery. “Have another?”

He stared, his hand to his forehead, trying to gather the scattered pieces of himself as one might try to gather spilt flour with wet fingers.

“Ye’ve smoked as many as five since ye come in at midnight,” the woman went on in her chronic complaint. “Poor me, poor me, my head is so bad. Them two come in after ye. Ah, poor me, the business is slack, is slack! Few Chinamen about the Docks, and fewer Lascars, and no ships coming in, these say! Here’s another ready for ye, deary. Ye’ll remember like a good soul, won’t ye, that the market price is dreffle high just now? More nor three shillings and sixpence for a thimbleful! And ye’ll remember that nobody but me—(and Jack Chinaman t’other side the court; but he can’t do it as well as me)—has the true secret of mixing it? Ye’ll pay up accordingly, deary, won’t ye?”

As she spoke she blew again, and now and then bubbled at the mouthpiece, taking in the opiate vapour herself. It was a strange domestic industry: a cookery of poison, carefully portioned, pride taken in the “true secret” as if it were a family pickle handed down. The smell had layers—burnt sweetness, damp ash, an undernote like old oranges left too long in a drawer—yet it did not belong to any honest kitchen. It stole appetite by imitation, tricked the belly into silence, as if hunger could be smothered like a flame under a lid.

“O me, O me, my lungs is weak, my lungs is bad!” she continued, half-coughing, half-whispering. “It’s nearly ready for ye, deary. Ah, poor me, poor me, my poor hand shakes like to drop off! I see ye coming-to, and I ses to my poor self, ‘I’ll have another ready for him, and he’ll bear in mind the market price of opium, and pay according.’ O my poor head! I makes my pipes of old penny ink-bottles, ye see, deary—this is one—and I fits-in a mouthpiece, this way, and I takes my mixter out of this thimble with this little horn spoon; and so I fills, deary. Ah, my poor nerves! I got Heavens-hard drunk for sixteen year afore I took to this; but this don’t hurt me, not to speak of. And it takes away the hunger as well as wittles, deary.”

She held the nearly-emptied pipe towards him, and as she did the little coal at its heart winked, lighting the hollows of her face with a subterranean glow, like a lantern shown up through a skull. Then, as if her words had wound her down, she sank back and rolled upon her face.

The man rose unsteadily, his joints protesting like hinges that have not been oiled. He set the pipe down upon the hearth-stone as one might set down a dirty utensil one cannot bear to wash, drew the ragged curtain aside, and looked with a fastidious repugnance at the three bodies strewn across the bed.

He noticed, with the startled precision of a mind that can still take measurements, that the woman had smoked herself into a strange likeness of the Chinaman. The form of cheek and eye and temple, and even the colour that should have belonged to different blood and different weather, were repeated in her. It was as though the drug had translated her into another face—but poorly, inaccurately, leaving the original meaning behind. No language could carry what she had been into what she had become. The room itself seemed full of such mistranslations: words half-heard through thin walls; foreign tongues turned to noise; names worn away into nicknames until no one knew what anything was called.

The Chinaman wrestled convulsively with one of his many Gods or Devils, perhaps, and snarled horribly. The sound did not fit the place—too fierce for the little room—yet it was muffled by the low ceiling and filthy hangings, made small and ugly as a caged animal’s rage. The Lascar laughed and dribbled at the mouth, a soft wet chuckle that made the skin shrink. The hostess was still.

“What visions can she have?” the waking man mused, and turned the woman’s face towards him. He stood looking down at it as if it were evidence of some crime he could not name. “Visions of many butchers’ shops, and public-houses, and much credit? Of an increase of hideous customers, and this horrible bedstead set upright again, and this horrible court swept clean? What can she rise to, under any quantity of opium, higher than that!—Eh?”

He bent his ear to her mouth to catch her mutterings. Her lips moved; the sounds were shapes without meaning, as if her tongue had forgotten its own dialect and could only mouth the dominant babble of the street—unowned, unrooted, unintelligible.

“Unintelligible!” he said under his breath.

As he watched the spasmodic shoots and darts that broke out of her face and limbs—fitful lightning in a dark sky—some contagion seized on him. It was not pity; it was a wretched sympathy of nerves, a twitching echo. He had to withdraw to a lean arm-chair by the hearth, placed there, perhaps, for such emergencies, and grip the arms of it hard, holding tight until the unclean spirit of imitation passed.

When he had got the better of it, he came back sharply, pounced upon the Chinaman, and seizing him with both hands by the throat turned him violently on the bed. The Chinaman clutched at the hands, resisted, gasped, protested in a rapid stream of sounds that might have carried sense in another room, another world.

“What do you say?” the man demanded, pausing watchfully.

The answer poured out—jargon to his ear, syllables without foothold.

“Unintelligible!” he concluded again, with a listening frown, slowly loosening his grasp as if the effort were not in the hands but in the mind.

He turned then to the Lascar and fairly dragged him down upon the floor. As he fell, the Lascar started up half-risen, eyes glaring, arms lashing, and drew a phantom knife. For an instant the room’s soundscape changed: not the soft bubbling of the pipe, not the rat-scratch beneath the boards, but a sudden sharpness—an imagined blade cutting air. Then it became apparent that the woman had taken possession of the real knife for safety’s sake; for she too started up, restraining and expostulating with him in her harsh whisper, and the knife was visible in her dress, not in his, when they drowsily dropped back side by side. Their chattering and clattering—different accents, different cadences, the drowned remnants of far coasts—filled the room and came to nothing. When any distinct word flew out it had no sense or sequence, as if the room itself ate meaning.

Wherefore “unintelligible!” was again the watcher’s comment, with some reassured nodding of his head and a gloomy smile, as though he had verified that no confession could be had here, no coherent clue, nothing to carry forward into daylight.

He laid certain silver money on the table—payment rendered with the same exactness as he had listened—found his hat, groped down broken stairs, and gave a good morning to a rat-ridden doorkeeper who lay in bed in a black hutch beneath the steps, breathing the close air like an animal penned. Then he passed out.

That same afternoon the massive grey square tower of an old Cathedral rose before the eyes of a jaded traveller, not as dream-work now but as masonry and age and authority set upon the earth. The town around it hummed with its ordinary commerce—carts, boots on cobbles, voices advertising wares—yet the Tower held a different register, a deep note in the day’s hearing. Above all the smaller noises, bells were going for daily vesper service: measured bronze speech rolling through streets and over roofs, calling the scattered mind to a single place. The sound did not clang like cymbals; it did not rattle like a whisper in a foul room; it held its own air cleanly and asked the listener to match it.

He must needs attend, one would say, from his haste to reach the open Cathedral door. He went in among the choristers as they were getting on their sullied white robes in a hurry, their hands busy with ties and buttons as if this too were a kind of preparation of food—a daily ritual done whether the heart is in it or not. He took his own robe, put it on with practiced speed, and fell into the procession filing in to service.

Then the Sacristan locked the iron-barred gates that divided the sanctuary from the chancel; and when all the procession had scuttled into place, they hid their faces; and then the intoned words—

“WHEN THE WICKED MAN—”

—rose among groins of arches and beams of roof, waking a muttered thunder that was not the street’s noise at all but the Cathedral’s long memory speaking in a tongue older than any single man’s.
