Harry Warrington, once so proud and untouchable, now found himself locked within the cold walls of a debtor’s prison, his arrogance humbled by iron bars and the mocking indifference of the world. Remorse gnawed at him—not for the debts, but for the indignity of capture. Surely, he thought, his friends would rally. His uncle, Sir Miles, who had welcomed him as a son; his cousin Castlewood, who had bled him dry at cards; his noble acquaintances at White’s—any one of them would surely intervene. Yet beneath this confidence lurked a deeper dread: the fear of becoming a spectacle.

The world, after all, cared little for a gentleman’s disgrace. A man might stumble, might even fall, and the crowd would scarcely pause before moving on. Harry knew this, yet vanity still whispered that his ruin would echo through London’s drawing rooms. He imagined the sneers of Selwyn, the whispered jests at the Savage’s expense. The thought was unbearable.

At dawn, he dispatched a messenger to his one reliable confidant—the ever-resourceful, ever-indebted chaplain, Sampson. If anyone understood the art of evading creditors, it was he. But as the hours crawled by, no Sampson appeared. Instead, a letter arrived, its ink barely dry.

*"Oh, sir, I have done all a man can for his patron!"* Sampson wrote, his desperation palpable. *"Did you not know to what you subjected me? Else, why am I locked but three doors away, seized by the same wolves who hunt you?"*

The irony was bitter. Sampson, his would-be savior, was now as trapped as he.

Meanwhile, Gumbo arrived, wailing like a tragedian, swearing eternal loyalty—though his tongue had already wagged the tale to every servant in Mayfair. The news had reached Lady Maria, who, upon hearing of Harry’s plight, turned her fury upon her brother.

"You took his last shilling, and now you leave him to rot?" she raged.

Castlewood merely shrugged. "Every man plays his own game, sister. Yours was to snare him as a husband. Mine was to take his money. Neither of us is a saint."

Maria’s face flushed with fury, but Castlewood’s smirk never wavered. "A clever woman," he mused, "might turn even this to her advantage. Suppose a certain promise of marriage were to be… reconsidered?"

Her eyes narrowed. "You think me so mercenary?"

"I think you practical," he replied.

Across town, Madame Bernstein received the news with icy calculation. Harry’s recklessness was no surprise, but his arrest was an inconvenience—one she would resolve, though not without conditions.

"Pay his debts," she instructed her lawyer, Draper. "But when he walks free, let him walk clear. No more liabilities. No more folly."

Draper bowed. "And if there are other creditors?"

"Then let them wait," she said. "A lesson must be learned."

Harry, pacing his cell, knew none of this. He knew only the slow passage of time, the sting of humiliation, and the dawning realization that pride was a luxury he could no longer afford.

The world had not ended. It had merely moved on without him.
