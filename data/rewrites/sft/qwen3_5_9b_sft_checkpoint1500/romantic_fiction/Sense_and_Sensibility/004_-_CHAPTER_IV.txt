"What a shame it is, Elinor," sighed Marianne, "that Edward possesses no talent for sketching."

"No talent!" cried Elinor, looking up with mild surprise. "Why should you suppose that? While he does not hold a pencil himself, he takes immense pleasure in viewing the works of others. I promise you, his natural sensibility is quite unimpaired; he simply lacks the chance to cultivate it. If he had ever been guided properly, I am convinced he would have become a fine artist. He trusts his own judgment so little in matters of art that he rarely offers an opinion, yet he possesses an innate correctness and simplicity of taste that invariably leads him aright."

Marianne, fearing to wound her sister's feelings, fell silent on the matter. However, the kind of approval Elinor claimed stirred within Edward by the drawings of others was worlds away from the rapturous ecstasy that Marianne believed constituted true taste. Though she smiled inwardly at the error, she honored her sister for the blind devotion to Edward that caused it.

"I trust, Marianne," Elinor resumed, "that you do not view him as lacking in general taste. Indeed, I venture to say you cannot, for your conduct toward him is so warm and cordial. If that were not your sentiment, I am sure you could never treat him with such kindness."

Marianne hesitated, unsure of what to utter. To hurt her sister's feelings was unthinkable, yet to voice a falsehood was equally difficult. Finally, she replied:

"Do not take offense, Elinor, if my praise falls short of your full appreciation of his virtues. I have had fewer occasions to gauge the subtle workings of his mind, his specific inclinations and tastes, than you have. But I hold the highest opinion in the world of his goodness and intellect. To me, he embodies everything that is noble and lovable."

"I am certain," Elinor answered with a gentle smile, "that even his dearest friends would not be displeased with such glowing testimony. I struggle to imagine how you could speak more warmly."

Marianne was delighted to see how easily her sister was content.

"As for his intelligence and his virtue," Elinor continued, "there can be no doubt among anyone who has spent enough time engaging him in open conversation. The brilliance of his understanding and the soundness of his principles are hidden only by that reserve which keeps him so often silent. You know him well enough to do justice to his substantial worth. As for those 'minuter propensities' you mentioned, peculiar circumstances have kept you less informed on them than I have. He and I have spent considerable time together, whereas you have been wholly absorbed by maternal duties. I have observed him closely, studied his thoughts, and listened to his views on literature and aesthetics. On the whole, I venture to declare that his mind is well-educated, his love for books profound, his imagination vivid, his observations sharp and accurate, and his taste refined and pure. His abilities grow more impressive with familiarity, just as his manners and appearance do. Initially, his address may seem unremarkable, and his features hardly handsome until one perceives the extraordinary gentleness of his eyes and the overall sweetness of his countenance. Now, knowing him as I do, I consider him truly handsome—or at least nearly so. What do you say, Marianne?"

"I shall very soon deem him handsome, Elinor, if I do not already," Marianne declared. "When you urge me to cherish him as a brother, I shall see no flaw in his face any more than I now see any defect in his heart."

Elinor recoiled slightly at this declaration, regretting the warmth she had inadvertently shown in describing him. She knew Edward held a high place in her affections and suspected his feelings were mutual, yet she required absolute certainty before finding comfort in Marianne's conviction of their attachment. She recognized that her mother and sister tended to leap from conjecture to belief, where wishing equated to hoping, and hoping to expecting. She endeavored to clarify the reality of their situation.

"I do not deny," she said softly, "that I think very highly of him—that I greatly esteem and like him."

Here, Marianne erupted in indignant protest.

"Esteem him! Like him! Cold-hearted Elinor! Oh, worse than cold-hearted! Ashamed to feel otherwise! Say those words again, and I shall leave the room this instant!"

Elinor could not help laughing. "Forgive me," she said. "Be assured I meant no offense by speaking quietly of my own feelings. Believe them to be stronger than I have expressed; believe them, in short, to be commensurate with his merit and the hope—however faint—that his affection for me might warrant. But go no further. I am by no means certain of his regard. There are moments when its depth seems doubtful, and until his sentiments are fully revealed, you cannot blame me for avoiding any encouragement of my partiality by believing or declaring it more than it truly is. In my heart, I feel little—if any—doubt of his preference. Yet, other factors must be weighed. He is far from independent. What his mother truly is, we cannot fully know, but based on Fanny's occasional comments regarding her conduct and opinions, we have never deemed her amiable. And I am greatly mistaken if Edward does not realize himself that marrying a woman without great fortune or high rank would encounter many obstacles."

Marianne was stunned to discover how much the imaginations of her mother and herself had outrun the truth.

"And you are not engaged to him!" she exclaimed. "And yet, it surely will soon happen! But two benefits arise from this delay. First, I shall not lose you so quickly. Second, Edward will have ample opportunity to improve that natural taste for your favorite pursuit, which must be indispensable to your future happiness. Oh, if he were so inspired by your genius as to learn to draw himself, how delightful it would be!"

Elinor had laid bare her true thoughts to her sister. She could not consider Marianne's partiality for Edward as thriving as her sister believed. At times, a certain lack of spirit about him suggested something far less promising than mere indifference—a shadow of depression that hinted at deeper troubles. Doubt regarding her own regard, if he felt it, would not necessarily cause such profound melancholy. A more probable cause lay in his dependent position, which forbade the free indulgence of his affections. She knew that his mother neither conducted herself kindly enough to make his current home comfortable nor offered any assurance that he could establish a home of his own without strictly adhering to her ambitions for aggrandizement. Armed with this knowledge, it was impossible for Elinor to remain at ease. She was far from relying on the happy conclusion her mother and sister still assumed certain. Indeed, the longer they remained together, the more uncertain the nature of his regard appeared; and sometimes, for a few painful minutes, she feared it was nothing more than friendship.

However, whatever its true limits, the fact that it was noticed by his sister was enough to disturb her peace, and simultaneously—to her frequent dismay—to render her uncivil. Seizing the first opportunity, she affronted her mother-in-law, speaking expressively of her son's great prospects, of Mrs. Ferrars's resolution that both her sons must marry well, and of the peril facing any young woman who dared to "_draw him in._" Mrs. Dashwood could neither pretend ignorance nor maintain composure. She received an answer marked by contempt and immediately departed, resolving that, regardless of the inconvenience or expense of such a sudden departure, her beloved Elinor should not be exposed another week to such insinuations.

In this state of spirits, a letter arrived from the post, offering a proposal particularly timely. It was an offer of a modest house, available on very easy terms, belonging to a relative of her own, a gentleman of substance and property in Devonshire. The letter came directly from him and was written in the true spirit of friendly accommodation. He understood her need for a dwelling; although the house he offered was merely a cottage, he assured her that everything necessary could be done to make it suitable if the location pleased her. He earnestly urged her, along with her daughters, to visit Barton Park, his own residence, from whence she could judge for herself whether Barton Cottage, situated in the same parish, could be rendered comfortable through alterations. His letter was written with such genuine anxiety to accommodate them, and in a style so friendly, that it could not fail to bring pleasure to his cousin, especially at a moment when she suffered under the cold and unfeeling behavior of her closer kin. She needed no time for deliberation or inquiry. Her resolution was formed as she read. The situation of Barton, in a county so distant from Sussex as Devonshire—which, but a few hours prior, would have been a sufficient objection to outweigh every possible advantage—now became its first recommendation. To leave the neighborhood of Norland was no longer an evil; it was an object of desire, a blessing compared to the misery of remaining her daughter-in-law's guest. To remove forever from that beloved place would be less painful than to inhabit or visit it while such a woman ruled it. Instantly, she wrote to Sir John Middleton acknowledging his kindness and accepting his proposal. Then, she hastened to show both letters to her daughters, securing their approbation before sending her reply.
