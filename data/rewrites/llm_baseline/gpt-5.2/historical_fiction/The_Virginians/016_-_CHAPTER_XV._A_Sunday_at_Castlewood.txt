The second day after Harry Warrington’s coming to Castlewood fell upon a Sunday; and Sunday, in that ancient house, had its own measured way of entering—less by any sudden hush than by a change in the very sounds and smells of the place. The kitchens roused earlier than common: the dull thump of a cleaver on a block, the iron whisper of spits turning, the soft, unhurried speech of women counting eggs and weighing flour by feel. From the court came the steady rasp of a groom’s currycomb, and, beyond the walls, the rooks rehearsed their morning clamor in the elms as if the day itself were a parish tune.

The chapel that belonged to the castle was likewise the village church. A door from the house opened straight into the great state pew where the family sat, raised above the honest benches below, so that the household might pass from corridor to consecration without once crossing the green. Harry, entering with the rest, drew in the scent particular to old churches—stone that had held damp through centuries, beeswax, and a faint ghost of winter smoke—mingled, oddly and homely, with the breakfasting air still clinging to coats and hair: a trace of toasted bread, a last pinch of nutmeg, the sour-sweet tang of ale.

Above, dusty banners drooped from the roof-beams like old stories half-forgotten; and Harry pleased himself with imagining they had once been carried by men of his blood in the Commonwealth wars, those loyal swords whose names were now as mute as the embroidery. Within the altar-rails lay, in marble, the Esmond of King James’s time—square-bearded, red-gowned, forever kneeling on a stone hassock before a great stone desk and book, under a shield of arms painted as if memory itself could be made to last by colour and Latin.

Madame de Bernstein, as a bishop’s widow, never failed attendance. She composed her devotions with a gravity that might have suited the marble ancestor; yet there was life enough in her, too, to observe all the human play of the morning with half an eye, as a housekeeper notes the temper of her servants even while she says her prayers.

The clergyman, Mr. Sampson, tall and high-coloured, read the service in a lively, agreeable voice, giving the Scriptures a point almost theatrical. The organ was touched by one of the young ladies; and the music might have flowed very well indeed, had it not been for an interruption from the servants’ pew—an eruption of laughter checked too late and too clumsily.

For Mr. Warrington’s lacquey Gumbo, hearing the psalm-tune given out, began to sing in a voice so loud and so sweet that heads turned as if pulled by a string. The very air in the church changed at once: the organ’s breath, the clerk’s dutiful droning, the parson’s cadence—all were suddenly made small beside that rich, ringing sound. Harry, who had heard field-songs carried over water in Virginia and hymns flung up in log-chapels, felt the thing in his chest like the stroke of a drum. The London footmen stared, offended out of all propriety; Mr. Sampson himself brought a handkerchief to his mouth. Pleased by the stir he made, Gumbo held on; what began as help turned almost into a solo, and the clerk’s voice was smothered.

The truth was, though Gumbo held the book along with pretty Molly, the porter’s daughter—who had been first to welcome the strangers—he sang by ear and memory, not by note or reading. The black letters before him were no more than marks; the tune was his own inheritance. There was, in that, something both triumphant and melancholy: a tongue of music alive in him while the printed words might as well have been in Greek.

The psalm ended at last; the church settled back into its accustomed noises—the faint creak of pews, a cough stifled in a hand, the rustle of a sleeve—and then came the sermon. Harry, accustomed in his mother’s company to more doctrinal thunder from ministers of a Calvinistic cast, expected a long exhortation; but Mr. Sampson’s was brisk, familiar, and strangely vivid, as though he addressed not saints and sinners in the abstract but neighbours whose faces he knew and whose appetites—of drink, sport, and mischief—he named without shrinking.

He told of an execution he had witnessed after Assizes: a horse-stealer, a man once of good character, with a wife and children who loved him, and the agony of his parting from them. Mr. Sampson painted it so that one might almost smell the crowd—sweat, damp wool, stale beer—and see the cart’s rough boards and the rope’s pale twist. He spoke not in a patchwork of Scripture phrases, but as one man of the world warning others who might be caught by the same slippery pleasures: drinking, card-playing, horse-racing, cock-fighting—the vices of the age, at which he was generously indignant.

He drew the poacher at midnight, the law’s eye watching, traps laid; he galloped the stolen horse over common and highway, from one county into another, with Retribution ever behind—closing, seizing, carrying the man before the justice, never unlocking the manacles until they dropped at the gallows-foot. And then—startlingly—he acted the scene. He leaned, as if to whisper to the criminal at the cart; he dropped his handkerchief so that it fell upon the clerk’s head like a cue in a play. Harry started as it fell. The parson had been in the pulpit more than twenty minutes, and Harry would gladly have heard him for an hour. It seemed to him five minutes, so keenly did the young divine command voice and picture.

The gentlefolks in the great pew were enlivened. Harry, glancing toward the servants, saw them attentive too—especially Gumbo, whose face bore an attitude of intense consternation at the sermon’s talk of traps and law and midnight watchers. But the smockfrocks below took the matter as they took weather: Gaffer Brown and Gammer Jones sat stolid; village lasses in red cloaks and broad hats remained rosy and unmoved.

When the service ended and the organ played them into sunshine, Sir Francis Esmond, temp. Jac. I., still knelt in marble, forever in the same posture, his stone prayer-book as unreadable as Gumbo’s printed page. My lord, before quitting his pew, nodded slightly to the pulpit as the divine’s wig and head rose above the cushion.

“Sampson has been strong to-day,” his lordship said. “He has assaulted the Philistines in great force.”

“Beautiful—beautiful!” Harry cried, his heart warmed by voice and scene.

“Bet five to four it was his Assize sermon,” said Mr. William. “He’s been over to Winton to preach, and to see those dogs.”

Mr. Sampson came out presently in cassock, nodding to the gentlemen who lingered. “Come up and tell us about those dogs,” William called; and the clergyman, laughing, signified assent.

They passed from the church by the gallery that joined it to the house—sacred and domestic stitched together by a covered walk, as if the family meant never to be quite out in the parish air. Mr. Sampson crossed the court to join them and was presented to Harry. He bowed profoundly and hoped Mr. Warrington would benefit by the virtuous example of his European kinsmen. He asked if Harry were related to Sir Miles Warrington of Norfolk—Sir Miles being Harry’s father’s elder brother—and opined it a pity Sir Miles had a son, since the estate was a pretty one and a baronetcy might have sat well upon Harry.

“Tell me about my uncle,” Harry cried.

“Tell us about those dogs!” William demanded in the same breath.

“Two more jolly dogs—two more drunken dogs, saving your presence, Mr. Warrington—than Sir Miles and his son I never saw,” said the chaplain. Sir Miles, he declared, could drink down any man in the county save his son and a few others. “The other dogs Mr. William is anxious about—Heaven hath made him a prey to dogs and all kinds of birds, like the Greeks in the Iliad—”

“I know that line in the Iliad,” Harry said, colouring. “I only know five more—but I know that one.”

His head fell a little as he thought, with an ache like homesickness, of his brother George, who knew the Iliad and the Odyssey and almost every book besides, as if learning were another sense in him.

“What on earth”—William named the place under the earth—“are you talking about now?” he demanded.

Mr. Sampson returned to the hounds, then slipped naturally, as men will, from dogs to horses. William was eager about the Six Year Old Plate at Huntingdon.

“Have you brought news, Parson?”

“The odds are five to four on Brilliant against the field,” the chaplain said gravely, “but mind you—Jason is a good horse.”

“Whose?” asked my lord.

“The Duke of Ancaster’s. By Cartouche out of Miss Langley.”

“Have you horse-races in Virginia, Mr. Warrington?” Mr. Sampson asked, eyes bright.

“Haven’t we!” Harry cried. “But oh—I long to see a good English race.”

“Do you—do you—bet a little?” the chaplain pursued, with a worldly innocence.

“I have done such a thing,” Harry answered, smiling.

“I’ll take Brilliant even against the field, for ponies with you, cousin!” William shouted.

“I’ll give or take three to one against Jason,” said Mr. Sampson.

“I don’t bet on horses I don’t know,” Harry replied, surprised to hear such traffic in odds so soon after that sermon of poachers and manacles.

“Hadn’t you better write home and ask your mother?” William sneered.

“My brother,” my lord said sharply, “our cousin Warrington is free to bet or not as he likes. Have a care, Harry. Will is an old file in spite of his smooth face; and as for Parson Sampson, I defy our ghostly enemy to get the better of him.”

“Him and all his works, my lord,” said Mr. Sampson, with a bow.

The allusion to his mother pricked Harry like a spur. “I’ll tell you what, cousin Will,” he said, “I manage my own affairs in my own way, without asking any lady to arrange them for me. I make my own bets upon my own judgment. But as I am your guest—and, no doubt, you mean to show me hospitality—I’ll take your bet. There. Done and done.”

“Done,” William said, looking askance. “It’s the regular odds in the paper, of course?”

“Well—no—it isn’t,” William muttered; then, forced, “The odds are five to four; you may have them, if you like.”

“Nay— a bet is a bet,” Harry returned. “And I take you too, Mr. Sampson.”

“Three to one against Jason. I lay it. Very good,” said the chaplain.

“Is it ponies too, Mr. Chaplain?” Harry asked, with an air as if he carried all Lombard Street in his pocket.

“No, no. Thirty to ten. It is enough for a poor priest to win.”

Here goes, Harry thought, a great slice out of my quarter’s hundred. Yet pride would not let him draw back. He had not begun the quarrel, but for the honour of Old Virginia he would not seem afraid.

When the bargains were made, William went away scowling toward the stables, where he liked to smoke with the grooms. The brisk parson went to pay his court to the ladies and to partake of the Sunday dinner that was preparing. Lord Castlewood and Harry remained together for a while.

Since Harry’s arrival, my lord had scarcely spoken with him. He was perfectly friendly in manner, yet so silent that he would sit at the head of his table and leave it without uttering a word, as if words were coin and he hoarded them. Now, in this pause between chapel and dinner, he spoke at last with an ease that was almost careless.

“I suppose yonder property of yours is a fine one by this time?” he asked.

“I reckon it’s almost as big as an English county,” Harry answered, “and the land’s as good too—for many things.” He would not have Virginia, nor his share in it, underrated.

“Indeed!” My lord’s look sharpened with surprise. “When it belonged to my father it did not yield much.”

“Pardon me, my lord. You know how it belonged to your father,” Harry cried with spirit. “It was because my grandfather did not choose to claim his right.”

“Of course—of course,” my lord said hastily.

“I mean,” Harry continued, “we of the Virginian house owe you nothing but our own—but our own, and the hospitality you are now showing me.”

“You are heartily welcome to both,” said my lord. “You were hurt by the betting just now?”

Harry hesitated, then spoke with the sudden plainness of his country. “Well—I am sort o’ hurt. Your welcome is different to our welcome, and that’s the fact. At home we’re glad to see a man, hold out a hand, and give him of our best. Here you take us in, give us beef and claret enough, to be sure—and don’t seem to care when we come, or when we go. That’s the remark I’ve been making since I’ve been here. I can’t help telling it out now it’s on my mind; and I think I’m a little easier now I’ve said it.”

As if to cover the heat in his cheeks, he knocked a billiard-ball across the table with a sharp click that echoed in the room, and then laughed, looking up at his elder kinsman.

“A la bonne heure!” my lord said. “We are cold to the stranger within and without our gates. We don’t take Mr. Harry Warrington into our arms and cry when we see our cousin. We don’t cry when he goes away—but do we pretend?”

“No,” Harry answered. “But you try to get the better of him in a bet.”

“Is there no such practice in Virginia? Do not sporting men there try to overreach one another? What was that story I heard you telling our aunt—of the British officers and Tom somebody of Spotsylvania?”

“That’s fair!” Harry cried. “It’s usual practice; a stranger must look out. I don’t mind the parson; if he wins, he may have it, and welcome. But a relation! To think my own blood cousin wants money out of me!”

“A Newmarket man would get the better of his father,” my lord replied. “My brother has been on the turf since he rode over to it from Cambridge. If you play at cards with him—and he will, if you let him—he will beat you if he can.”

“Well, I’m ready!” Harry cried. “I’ll play any game with him I know, or I’ll jump with him, or ride with him, or row with him, or wrestle with him, or shoot with him—there—now.”

Lord Castlewood was amused; he offered his hand. “Anything—but don’t fight with him,” he said.

“If I do, I’ll whip him—hanged if I don’t!” Harry burst out; and then, seeing my lord’s surprise and displeasure, he recollected himself as if a cold cloth had been laid upon his brow. “A hundred pardons, my lord,” he said, flushing deep. He seized his cousin’s hand. “I talked of ill manners, being angry and hurt. ’Tis doubly ill-mannered of me to show anger, and boast of prowess to my own host. It’s not the practice with us Americans to boast—believe me, it’s not.”

“You are the first I ever met,” said my lord, smiling. “I take you at your word. And I give you fair warning about the cards and betting—that is all, my boy.”

“Leave a Virginian alone,” Harry returned stoutly. “We are a match for most men—we are.”

My lord’s smile did not broaden. His eyebrows arched a moment, his grey eyes went down as if he listened to something within. “So you can bet fifty guineas and afford to lose them? So much the better for you. Those great Virginian estates yield a great revenue, do they?”

“More than sufficient for all of us—for ten times as many as we are now,” Harry said; and even as he spoke he suspected he was being sounded, as a man sounds a cask by tapping.

“And your mother makes her son and heir a handsome allowance?”

“As much as ever I choose to draw, my lord!” Harry answered quickly.

“Peste! I wish I had such a mother,” my lord said. “But I have only the advantage of a stepmother, and she draws me. There is the dinner-bell. Shall we go into the eating-room?”

Taking Harry’s arm, he led him in where the meal waited; and there the day’s piety, which had rung out in psalm and organ, took its second shape—meat and wine, the old sacrament of the table.

If the chapel had smelled of stone and wax, the dining-room was a warmer creed: roast beef giving up its fat in a fragrant sigh as the knife went through; gravy dark as polished walnut; bread with a crackling crust and a soft, steaming heart; claret breathing from decanters; and, beneath it all, the steady background of the house—fire ticking in the grate, plate set down and lifted, chairs shifting, voices weaving in and out like a reel.

Mr. Sampson proved the delight of the entertainment. Besides being chaplain to his lordship, he preached in London at the new chapel in Mayfair, endowed by my Lady Whittlesea; and he brought with him the choicest stories of clubs and coteries, the newest scandals—who had run away with whom—the last bon-mot of Mr. Selwyn, the wild bet of March and Rockingham; how the old King had quarrelled with Madame Walmoden; how a duke was suspected of a new love; who stood in favour at Carlton House with the Princess of Wales; and who had been hanged last Monday, and how well he behaved in the cart. He seasoned all with such plain terms and lively jokes as made Harry stare; fresh from the colonies, he was unused to these London elegances, where a man might preach damnation in the morning and laugh over vice at dinner as easily as he dipped bread into sauce.

The ladies laughed quite cheerfully. They did not play at being shocked; they had, like the house itself, their established measures of what was proper, and Mr. Sampson’s breezy worldliness sat upon those measures as naturally as pepper upon beef.

In due time the chapel bell clinked for afternoon service, summoning Mr. Sampson away for half an hour. There was no sermon then; he would be back before they were far into a bottle of Burgundy. Mr. Will called for a fresh one; the chaplain tossed off a glass and ran out. Ere the half-hour was done, he was back again, bawling cheerfully for another bottle. The business of salvation, it seemed, could be accomplished briskly when dinner was waiting.

When they rose from table, they returned to the ladies, and the card-tables were set out as they were for many hours each day. The whole party engaged. Madame de Bernstein could beat any of them at piquet; there was only the chaplain in the circle who proved at all her match. In this easy fashion the Sabbath slipped away, not in silence but in the steady click of pasteboard, the soft scold and laughter of players, the occasional call for wine, and—always—beneath it, the great house’s own music: distant servants’ feet, a door drawn to, the muted creak of beams, the rooks outside keeping their rough chorale over the trees.

The evening was beautiful; someone proposed a cool tankard and a game of whist in a summer-house. But the company voted to sit indoors. The ladies declared the aspect of three honours in the hand, and a few good court-cards, more beautiful than the loveliest scene of nature; and so the sun went down behind the elms while they still bent over their hands. The rooks came home cawing their evensong, and no one stirred save to change partners. The chapel clock tolled hour after hour unheeded. The moon and stars came out, pale and patient at the windows, and at nine the groom of the chambers announced supper.

They sat again to food and drink—lighter fare now, but still abundant—and as they ate, the postboy’s horn was heard twanging as he trotted into the village with his letter-bag: a thin, bright sound that cut through the comfortable room like a reed-pipe across a dance. My lord’s bag was brought in; he set the letters aside and opened his newspaper.

He smiled at a paragraph, looked at Harry, and handed the sheet to William, who by this time was very comfortable from good luck at the tables and a great deal of liquor.

“Read that, Will,” said my lord.

William read the line pointed out, and burst into an exclamation that made the ladies cry out at once.

“Gracious heavens, William! What has happened?”

“Mercy, child, why do you swear so dreadfully?” his fond mamma asked.

“What’s the matter?” inquired Madame de Bernstein, dozing after her usual punch and beer.

“Read it, Parson!” William cried, thrusting the paper at the chaplain, his face fierce as a Turk’s.

“Bit—by the Lord!” roared Mr. Sampson, dashing the paper down.

“Cousin Harry, you are in luck,” my lord said, taking up the sheet and reading aloud. “The Six Year Old Plate at Huntingdon was won by Jason, beating Brilliant, Pytho, and Ginger. The odds were five to four on Brilliant against the field, three to one against Jason, seven to two against Pytho, and twenty to one against Ginger.”

“I owe you a half-year’s income of my poor living, Mr. Warrington,” groaned the chaplain. “I will pay when my noble patron settles with me.”

“A curse upon the luck!” William growled. “That comes of betting on a Sunday”—and he took consolation in another great bumper.

“Nay, cousin Will—it was but in jest,” Harry cried, startled by how sharp pleasure and displeasure could taste in the same mouthful. “I can’t think of taking my cousin’s money.”

“Curse me, sir, do you suppose, if I lose, I can’t pay?” William cried. “That I want to be beholden to any man alive? That is a good joke. Isn’t it, Parson?”

“I think I have heard better,” Mr. Sampson replied; and William, with an oath, called for another bowl.

Let us hope the ladies did not remain for that last replenishment; for it was plain enough that in Castlewood, even on Sunday, the day’s holy sounds—the organ’s breath, the chapel bell, the posthorn’s twang—must compete with the hard bright music of cards, and with wine poured again and again until speech grew louder and meaning, like a language unspoken for too long, began to fray at the edges.
