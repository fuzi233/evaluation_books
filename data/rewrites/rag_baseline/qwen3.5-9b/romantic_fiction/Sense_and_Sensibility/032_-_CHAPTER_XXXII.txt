When Elinor recounted the particulars of the conversation to her sister, the impact was not the serene resolution she had anticipated. Marianne did not question the veracity of the words; she absorbed them with a rigid, silent submission, offering no defense for Willoughby and allowing her tears to confirm the impossibility of his innocence. Yet, while this behavior signaled to Elinor that the truth had finally struck home—evidenced by Marianne ceasing to avoid Colonel Brandon, addressing him with a newfound, albeit pained, respect, and displaying a calmer demeanor—Elinor saw no corresponding reduction in her sister's suffering. Marianne's mind had settled, but it rested in a profound, gloomy dejection. The knowledge of Willoughby's moral collapse weighed heavier on her spirit than his abandonment; the image of Miss Williams' misery, coupled with the terrifying suspicion of what his intentions toward her once truly were, consumed her thoughts. She retreated into a silence so absolute it inflicted more agony on Elinor than any confession could have.

Mrs. Dashwood, receiving and responding to her daughter's frantic correspondence, felt a sting of humiliation mixed with despair. To speak of fortitude in the face of such betrayal felt like a cruel mockery. She resolved, however, that Marianne's individual comfort was secondary to her preservation. Returning to Barton meant constant exposure to the memory of Willoughby, a place where every corner held the ghost of the man who had broken her heart. Therefore, she urged her daughters to extend their stay in London, a city where a variety of distractions might, by sheer chance, divert Marianne's gaze from her own ruin. In the crowded anonymity of the capital, the risk of encountering Willoughby was negligible; his reputation was now so tarnished that no friend would introduce them, and negligence could not expose them to surprise. Furthermore, a letter from her son-in-law indicated the arrival of the Ferrars family in mid-February, necessitating a prolonged urban residence. Though Marianne internally rebelled against this exile, recognizing it as a mistake born of maternal error that stripped her of her mother's direct sympathy, she submitted. For Elinor, the arrangement offered a perverse solace: while it threatened her own happiness by ensuring further proximity to Edward, it was the lesser of two evils compared to Marianne's potential collapse in Devonshire.

Elinor's vigilance in shielding Marianne from the name of Willoughby proved effective. Neither Mrs. Jennings, Sir John, nor Mrs. Palmer dared utter the forbidden topic before her. Elinor, however, was forced to endure the relentless indignation of her circle daily. Sir John, unable to comprehend how a man of such reputed virtue could fall, declared him a scoundrel and a deceitful dog, vowing never to speak another word to him or even look upon him, even if they were trapped together in Barton Covert for hours. Mrs. Palmer echoed this sentiment with equal fervor, resolving to cut all ties and ensure Combe Magna remained too distant for any future visits. Her sympathy manifested in a gossipy cataloging of Willoughby's new life: the coachmaker constructing his carriage, the painter capturing his likeness, and the warehouse stocking Miss Grey's garments. Amidst this clamorous kindness, Lady Middleton provided a rare refuge. Her calm, polite indifference was a balm to Elinor's oppressed spirits. It was a profound comfort to know at least one person in their circle possessed the breeding to feel no curiosity about the scandal and no anxiety for Marianne's health. By expressing her shock only in brief, ritualistic exclamations—"It is very shocking, indeed!"—Lady Middleton maintained her social dignity while condemning the act, eventually moving on to her own assemblies.

Colonel Brandon's inquiries, delivered with delicate discretion, were always welcome. Having earned the right to discuss Marianne's sorrow through his zealous efforts to soothe it, they shared confidences freely. His greatest reward was the pitying glance Marianne occasionally cast his way and the softening of her voice when she addressed him. These small signs assured Brandon of growing goodwill and gave Elinor hope for the future. Meanwhile, Mrs. Jennings, oblivious to these subtle shifts, grew increasingly convinced that the Colonel's suit was failing. Seeing him as grave as ever, and noting his inability to propose or delegate the task, she began to predict a delay until Michaelmas, then abandoned the idea of marriage entirely, assuming the honors of the mulberry tree and the yew arbour belonged solely to Elinor.

Early in February, barely two weeks after receiving Willoughby's letter, Elinor performed the painful duty of informing Marianne of his marriage. She ensured the news reached her privately, sparing her sister the shock of reading it in the morning newspapers. Marianne accepted the intelligence with resolute composure, shedding no initial tears. Only after a pause did they burst forth, leaving her in a state of wretchedness nearly indistinguishable from her initial despair. With the Willoughbys departing town immediately after the wedding, Elinor hoped to gradually reintroduce her sister to the outdoors.

Coinciding with this somber period, the two Miss Steeles returned to their relatives in Conduit Street, greeted with effusive cordiality by everyone except Elinor. Their presence was a source of acute pain, particularly Lucy's triumphant discovery that Elinor had stayed in London. "I should have been quite disappointed if I had not found you here *still*," Lucy exclaimed, emphasizing the word with malicious delight. She feigned surprise that Elinor hadn't departed sooner, mocking her previous promise to leave within a month, and celebrating her decision to break her word. Elinor, mastering her self-control, masked her resentment with a facade of grace.

Miss Steele, boasting of their journey via post-chaise accompanied by Dr. Davies, played the role of the coquettish debutante. She laughed off accusations that the doctor was her "beau," claiming she thought of him not at all, while her cousin insisted he clearly was. Mrs. Jennings, delighted by the charade, promised to contradict anyone who suggested otherwise, rendering Miss Steele completely happy. The conversation inevitably turned to the Dashwood sisters. When Lucy asked if Elinor would visit her brother and sister upon their arrival, Elinor demurred. Lucy persisted, and when Mrs. Jennings interjected that the visit was only just beginning, Lucy fell silent. Turning to Elinor, Miss Steele expressed regret at not seeing Marianne, citing her recent "nervous headaches" as a barrier to socializing. Elinor politely declined the invitation, explaining that her sister was resting in her dressing gown. Undeterred, Miss Steele declared they would simply go to see her personally. This impertinence pushed Elinor to the brink of losing her temper, but she was rescued from the confrontation by Lucy's sharp reprimand, a habit that, while lacking sweetness, effectively governed her sister's manners.
