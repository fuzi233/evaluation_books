"Breakfast for Love," the locals whispered, though the sentiment was lost on the young stranger. Having failed to decipher the source of the city's feverish agitation—and finding the gossip of native Florentines irrelevant to his own plight—he grew impatient with Bratti's slow pace. Abandoning the knot of talkers, he drifted toward the piazza, scanning the stalls for a vendor willing to trade sustenance for coin rather than curiosity.

His hand plunged into the leather pouch at his waist, fingers digging through the emptiness with increasing desperation. "Not an obolus, by Jupiter!" he muttered, the Latin phrase rolling off a tongue that betrayed neither Tuscan nor Italian roots. "I thought I'd saved a single piece. Well, I shall starve for love today."

He took only a few paces before locating a quarter of the market immune to his destitution. In a secluded corner, shielded from the prying eyes of the crowd, two mules stood laden with red tassels and collars. One bore wooden vessels of milk; the other, panniers overflowing with fresh herbs and greens. Leaning her elbow against the milk-mule's neck was a girl, no older than sixteen, her face framed by a crimson hood that concealed her hair entirely. Her features possessed a doll-like innocence, accentuated by the total obscurity of her locks. Perhaps exhaustion from the early morning trek from a distant castle had claimed her, for she slumped in a half-standing, half-leaning posture, seemingly asleep.

Yet, the stranger felt no hesitation in breaking her rest. His approach was so gentle, however, that to the sleeping girl, it felt merely like a sprig of thyme brushing her lips as she bent to gather herbs. The dream shattered instantly. She jolted awake, blue eyes widening in astonishment and confusion as the stranger loomed close. His voice was soft yet alien; even if she had been composed, she would have assumed he spoke gibberish. Instinctively, she turned her head slightly and lifted the corner of her green serge mantle to form a screen between them.

He repeated his words, choosing the phrase *"muoio di fame"* (I die of hunger) knowing it would resonate in her ears, delivered with the playful cadence of a mendicant. This time, comprehension bridged the gap. The mantle lowered, and within moments, a large cup of fragrant milk was pressed into his hands. He drank without further pleasantries. As the liquid warmed his throat, the maiden found the courage to gaze upward at the long, dark curls of this singular figure. Though his garments were tattered, he bore none of the squalid marks of a true beggar.

While this silent appraisal occurred, her hand darted into a bag hanging beside the mule. When the stranger set down his cup, he saw a thick slice of bread extended toward him, accompanied by a flash of those same blue eyes—an implicit invitation to accept.

"But perhaps that is your own breakfast," he said gently. "No, I have had enough without payment. A thousand thanks, my gentle one."

There was no verbal response, but the bread was nudged closer, impatient at his refusal. As the stranger's long, dark eyes met the girl's baby-face, her resolve hardened. "Ah, then, if I must take the bread," he declared, placing his hand upon it, "I shall grow bolder still, and beg for another kiss to sweeten the meal."

Despite the strangeness of his voice—which initially made her wish to recite prayers—his speech was becoming remarkably intelligible. She flushed violently, lifting the mantle to her mouth once more. Just as the presumptuous stranger leaned forward, his fingers grazing the arm holding the screen, a harsh voice erupted beside his ear.

"Who are *you*—murrain take you? No honest buyer, I'll warrant, but a hanger-on of the dice-houses—or something worse. Go! Dance off and find fitter company, or I'll give you a tune faster than you can handle!"

The stranger recoiled, meeting the speaker's glare with a look devoid of alarm or contrition. Instead, a saucy amusement spread across his face, blossoming into a broad, beaming smile as he assessed the threat. She was a stout, brawny woman, a man's jerkin draped over her green serge gown, the peaked hood of a discarded mantle framing a sunburnt face marred by coarse lines and premature wrinkles. Yet, beneath the coarseness, there lingered a half-sad, half-ludicrous maternal resemblance to her daughter—a kinship that often serves as a more chilling prophecy than the skull of Death.

There was something undeniably captivating in that bright young smile, but Monna Ghita possessed no weakness to surrender. Her exasperation intensified.

"Yes, yes, you can grin like any monkey in a cap and jerkin. You're a minstrel or a mountebank, I'll be sworn; you look as silly as a tumbler who has fallen and scrambled back onto his feet. What fool's tricks have you been playing, Tessa?" she snapped, turning to her daughter, whose frightened countenance invited further abuse. "Giving away milk and victuals? Ay, ay, you'd carry water in your ears for any idle vagabond unwilling to stoop for it, you silly staring rabbit! Turn your back and lift the herbs from the panniers, or I'll make you chant a dozen *Aves* without counting!"

"Nay, Madonna," the stranger pleaded with a winning smile, "do not anger your lovely Tessa for sparing a hungry traveler who found himself unexpectedly without a *quattrino*. Your handsome face looks so severe when frowning that I long to see it illuminated by a smile."

*"Va via!"* (Go away!) Monna Ghita retorted. "I know what paste you are made of. You may tickle me with that straw until the end of time before I laugh, mark my words. Get along with a bad Easter, or I'll brand a beauty spot or two on that face of yours that will ruin your kissing prospects before Advent arrives."

As Monna Ghita raised her formidable talons to enforce her decree, Bratti arrived, having trailed behind for a moment. Addressing his companion, he remarked, "What think you of this pretty parrot, Nello? Doesn't his tongue smack of Venice?"

"Nay, Bratti," the barber whispered, his tone laced with skepticism, "thy wisdom holds much of the ass, as I told thee earlier; especially regarding the ears. This stranger is a Greek, else I am not the barber who has exclusively shaved the excellent Demetrio and extracted more than one sore tooth from his learned jaw. This youth appears to have descended straight from Olympus—at least since I last applied my razor."

*"Orsù!"* (Enough!) Nello interjected, seizing the opportunity for sport. "What tempest has caused this uproar? Has this young gentleman been misbehaving?"

"By San Giovanni!" cried the cautious Bratti, shaking off his lingering suspicion of the shabbily clad jewel-bearer. "He did right to flee from *me*, if he sought trouble. I can swear I found him beneath the Loggia de' Cerchi, wearing a ring such as I've seen on Bernardo Rucellai himself. Not another rusty nail's worth do I know about him."

"The truth is," Nello observed, eyeing the stranger with good humor, "this *bello giovane* has been overly presumptuous in admiring Monna Ghita's charms, attempting to kiss her while her daughter's back was turned. Observe how busy the pretty Tessa is; she cannot spare a glance this way. Was it not so, Messer?"

"You have divined the offense like a soothsayer," the stranger laughed. "Only I lacked the fortune to encounter Monna Ghita first. I begged a cup of milk from her daughter and accepted this gift of bread, offering humble gratitude, before enjoying the privilege of facing these riper charms, which I perhaps admired too boldly."

*"Va, va!"* (Go, go!) Monna Ghita roared, fiercely elbowing Nello and driving her mule forward to force the stranger aside. "Tessa, thou simpleton, move the mule forward; the cart is coming upon us."

As Tessa turned to grasp the bridle, she cast a timid glance at the stranger, who was now stepping aside to avoid an approaching market cart. Her gaze lingered just long enough to catch the beckoning motion of his hand, signaling his readiness for a parting word.

*"Ebbene,"* Bratti called out, raising his voice above the rumble of the wheels. "I leave you with Nello, young man, for I cannot push my bag and basket any farther, and I have business at home. Remember our bargain, for if you found Tessa without me, it was not my fault. Nello will show you my shop in the Ferravecchi, and I shall not turn my back on you."

"A thousand thanks, friend!" the stranger laughed, then turned away with Nello up the narrow street leading directly to the Piazza del Duomo.
