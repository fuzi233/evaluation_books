There was another house besides the white one at Pennicote, another breast besides Rex Gascoigne’s, in which the tidings from Genoa fell like a stone dropped into a still pot—suddenly, sounding all the way down, and stirring what had seemed to have settled quiet at the bottom.

In the Meyricks’ little parlour, the day had been moving with its accustomed homeliness: the slight scent of last night’s extinguished fire still clung in the grate; and under it the room held the gentler smells of women’s work—warmed wool, a faint soapiness from washed hands, and the thin, clean odour of paper and ink that came of letters opened and folded often. All that was threaded through by the living sound that made the house happiest: the piano, and above it Mirah’s voice, guiding Mab through _Lascia ch’io pianga_. Mab, earnest and a little tragic in her imitation, had just reached one of those pleading turns where the air seems to ask for mercy with every note, when the latch-key clicked—then a sharp, crackling flourish, like the tearing of stiff brown paper that wraps sugar or dried figs, but louder and more imperious.

Hans came in with the _Times_ held up and shaken as if it were a flag. The paper’s harsh rustle cut straight across the music.

Mirah, who had been playing the accompaniment, started involuntarily. The sound, by that strange deceit of startled senses, had the weight of thunder to her. Mab let her voice die away with a groan of injury and cried—

“O-o-o, Hans! why do you bring a more horrible noise than my singing?”

Mrs. Meyrick, who sat near with her work and her quick eyes, looked up, all appetite for the world’s happenings awakened as it always was. She read news the way some people taste sauces—eager for everything, from great politics to the small garnish of weddings, which, she said, gave her the pleasant sense of finishing fashionable novels without the trouble of reading them, and of seeing the heroes and heroines made happy without knowing what poor creatures they were.

“What on earth is the wonderful news?” she said. “Anything about Italy—anything about the Austrians giving up Venice?”

“Nothing about Italy,” Hans returned, but with a tone that had an odd, anticipative tightness in it, as if he were holding a secret that pleased him too much to keep perfectly quiet—“but something from Italy.”

His mother’s mind, swift in anxiety, leapt at once to the name she most feared to hear connected with danger.

“Nothing bad?” she asked, the colour changing on her cheeks. Mirah’s heart clutched at the same thought before it had even taken words.

“Not bad for anybody we care much about,” Hans said quickly, and with his habitual twist, as if jesting were a cord to cling to when emotion pulled him. “Rather uncommonly lucky, I think. I never knew anybody die conveniently before. Considering what a dear gazelle I am, I’m constantly wondering to find myself alive.”

“Oh me, Hans!” Mab broke in, impatient as a child at a door kept shut. “If you must talk of yourself, let it be behind your own back. What _is_ it that has happened?”

Hans threw the paper before his mother and pressed a finger on the paragraph.

“Duke Alfonso is drowned, and the Duchess is alive, that’s all. But more than all is—Deronda was at Genoa in the same hotel with them, and he saw her brought in by the fishermen who had got her out of the water time enough to save her from any harm. It seems they saw her jump in after her husband—which was a less judicious action than I should have expected of the Duchess. However Deronda is a lucky fellow in being there to take care of her.”

At “Deronda,” Mirah’s breath seemed to stop. She sank back on the music-stool; her eyelids fell, and her hands clasped so tightly that the knuckles whitened. Mrs. Meyrick’s face softened with that ready pity which in her was as natural as the readiness with which she would cut bread for a hungry guest.

“Poor thing!” she said. “She must have been fond of her husband to jump in after him.”

“It was an inadvertence—a little absence of mind,” Hans answered, creasing his face roguishly and dropping into a chair near Mirah, as if the chair were an accomplice. “Who can be fond of a jealous baritone with freezing glances, always singing asides?—that was the husband’s _rôle_, depend upon it. Nothing can be neater than his getting drowned. The Duchess is at liberty now to marry a man with a fine head of hair, and glances that will melt instead of freezing her. And I shall be invited to the wedding.”

Mirah sprang up as if the words had struck her bodily. Her eyes—usually so gentle in their darkness—flashed with a sudden, fierce light; her face was pale, even to the lips that were commonly so rich in colour. She fixed Hans with a look that made him, for all his careless manhood, blush as if he had been a girl caught at some cruel trick.

“Mr. Hans,” she said, and her voice, shaken to its depth, had indignation in it like a sob turned to fire, “you ought not to speak in that way. Mr. Deronda would not like you to speak so. Why will you say he is lucky—why will you use words of that sort about life and death—when what is life to one is death to another? How do you know it would be lucky if he loved Mrs. Grandcourt? It might be a great evil to him. She would take him away from my brother—I know she would. Mr. Deronda would not call that lucky to pierce my brother’s heart.”

The room was struck silent, as if Mirah’s voice had stilled not only them but the very air. Hans sat transfixed, his usual quickness gone. Even when pain took him, his mind could not help producing a ludicrous shape for it, and he stammered, nervously—

“I am a fool and a brute, and I withdraw every word. I’ll go and hang myself like Judas—if it’s allowable to mention him.”

But Mirah’s anger had come like a jet from deep pain; it could not be soothed by his half-mocking penitence. She said no more. She sat down again at the piano and pushed the sheet of music before her with an air as if she meant to begin at once—only her hands trembled slightly, and the paper made a faint rasping sound under her fingers, a small, dry noise that seemed to accuse her of agitation.

It was Mab who spoke, and even her childish scorn had a seriousness in it, as if she felt the sanctity of names.

“Mirah is quite right to scold you, Hans. You are always taking Mr. Deronda’s name in vain. And it is horrible, joking in that way about his marrying Mrs. Grandcourt. Men’s minds must be very black, I think.”

“Quite true, my dear,” Hans said low, rising and turning away toward the back window, as if he could walk out of the shame in his own chest.

Mirah lifted her voice higher than usual, striving after firmness as after a straight seam when the cloth pulls.

“We had better go on, Mab; you have not given your full time to the lesson. Will you sing this again, or shall I sing it to you?”

“Oh, please sing it to me,” Mab answered, glad to slip back into the familiar track.

And Mirah sang _Lascia ch’io pianga_—but not now as a mere lesson. The melodious sobs and cries poured from her with a fuller, more urgent life, as if the old Italian plea were suddenly her own breath turned into sound. The piano, too, had that richer pressure which comes when a player’s heart is heavy and will have the keys give it speech. Hans, leaning against the mantel-piece, kept his eyes carefully away from his mother’s, and listened as one listens to the wind after having slammed a door in anger—ashamed to have made the peace of the house shake.

When Mirah had let her last note float away and had pressed the last chord, she rose quickly.

“I must go home now. Ezra expects me.”

She gave her hand silently to Mrs. Meyrick. She hung back a little, not daring to look at her, and—unlike her usual way—did not offer the kiss that had become a daily sweetness between them. But the little mother understood too much to rebuke. She drew Mirah’s face down to hers, and with a soft, steady touch said—

“God bless you, my dear.”

The words had the warmth of bread set down before one who is faint; and Mirah, with all her suffering, felt a new sharpness of compunction. She had been angry in Mrs. Meyrick’s house; she had rebuked Hans, who loved her; and mixed with jealousy and fear was the sting of thinking she had shown something like proud ingratitude, an unbecoming assertion of superiority. Her friend divined that self-reproach and met it with tenderness.

Hans had seized his wide-awake and stood ready at the door.

“Now, Hans,” Mab said, with what was really a sister’s tenderness cunningly disguised as severity, “you are not going to walk home with Mirah. I am sure she would rather not. You are so dreadfully disagreeable to-day.”

“I shall go to take care of her, if she does not forbid me,” Hans said, opening the door.

Mirah said nothing. The air outside had that cool edge which makes every sound sharper: distant wheels on the road, the brief cry of a bird, the click of their own steps on the paving. Hans walked by her side, unforbidden.

She had not courage to begin speaking again. She was aware that perhaps her words had been too severe, and yet behind them her heart held words far severer still. It was as if the crackle of that newspaper had burst open something in her that could not be folded down smooth again. And Hans, too, was busy with his own thoughts.

Mirah’s sudden flash of anger had waked in him a perception which made him feel dull not to have had it sooner. Suppose Mirah’s heart were preoccupied with Deronda in another character than that of benefactor to her and her brother? The supposition brought anxieties into Hans that, to do him justice, were not wholly selfish. He had long held, with a stubborn persuasion, that there was a serious attachment between Deronda and Mrs. Grandcourt. He had pieced together fragments of observation; he had gathered what his sisters had heard from Anna Gascoigne; and all had seemed to him to say plainly that Mrs. Grandcourt had a passion for Deronda, and that Deronda’s own austere self-repression covered a susceptibility which meant concealed love.

Many men, having such a belief, would have avoided every allusion that could irritate that susceptibility; but Hans’s talk naturally fluttered toward mischief, and he had a boyish taste for experimenting on live creatures—irritating his friends playfully to see what would stir. His experiments had ended by satisfying him that what he suspected was true.

Meanwhile, any susceptibility Deronda had shown about a lover’s attentions to Mirah, Hans had explained away by the alleged reason—her dependent position—for he credited his friend with unselfish anxiety for those he could rescue and protect. And Deronda’s insistence that Mirah would never marry one who was not a Jew necessarily seemed to exclude himself, since Hans shared the common opinion, undisturbed by anything he knew, that Deronda was Sir Hugo Mallinger’s son.

So Hans had thought himself clear about Deronda’s affections. Yet now—these events, which to him had looked like a step toward the union with Mrs. Grandcourt, had drawn from Mirah a betrayed passion which made him melancholy for her as well as for himself—though, on the whole, less melancholy than if he had imagined Deronda’s hopes fixed on her. It is not sublime, but it is common, that a man can bear more fortitude and milder jealousy at seeing the beloved object unhappy because his rival loves another, than at seeing her entirely happy in his rival. The mercurial Hans fluctuated between being wounded because Mirah was wounded, and being almost obliged to Deronda for loving somebody else.

He could not give Mirah any direct sign of how he understood her anger; and yet he longed that his speechless companionship might be eloquent in penitence and tender sympathy—an admissible form of wooing a bruised heart. Thus they went side by side in a companionship that seemed, for all its silence, like an agitated communication, as if two strings were vibrating together beyond the range of hearing, and the air between them were full of unheard sound.

At Mirah’s door Hans stopped. “Good-bye,” he said, holding out his hand with an appealing look.

Mirah met that look with melancholy gentleness and answered, quietly—

“Will you not come in and see my brother?”

Hans could not but read this as pardon. He did not understand how Mirah’s nature, wrought upon by early experience, could pass quickly from violent excitement into resolute acceptance of pain—as if pain were an old companion who, having been absent, had come back to sit in its accustomed chair.

“If you will let me,” Hans said; and they went in together. Half his grief was gone, and already his fancy was spinning a small romance: how his devotion might make him indispensable to Mirah in proportion as Deronda gave his devotion elsewhere. It seemed fair: his friend, Hans thought, was being provided for according to his own heart. And on the question of Judaism Hans felt thoroughly fortified. Who ever heard in tale or history that a woman’s love went in the track of her race and religion? Moslem and Jewish damsels were always attracted toward Christians; and now, if Mirah’s heart had gone too swiftly toward Deronda, here was another case.

Hans was accustomed to make merry with his own arguments, to call himself a Giaour, and antithesis the sole clue to events; but he believed a little in what he laughed at. His bird-like hope, built on the lightest principles, soared again in spite of heavy circumstances.

They found Mordecai looking singularly happy, holding a closed letter in his hand. His eyes glowed with a quiet triumph which, in his emaciated face, gave the impression of a conquest over assailing death—like a man who, with breath failing, yet holds up a cup of water to another’s lips.

After Hans’s greeting, Mirah put her arm round her brother’s neck and looked down at the letter without daring to ask about it, though she felt sure it was the source of his happiness.

“A letter from Daniel Deronda,” Mordecai said, answering her look. “Brief—only saying that he hopes soon to return. Unexpected claims have detained him. The promise of seeing him again is like the bow in the cloud to me,” he continued, turning to Hans; “and to you it must be a gladness. For who has two friends like him?”

While Hans answered, Mirah slipped away to her own little room—not to indulge in any outburst. If angels, once fancied to watch women at their toilette, had entered with her and let her shut the door, they would have seen only quiet, practical motions: she took off her hat and sat down, pressing her hands against her temples as if she had suddenly remembered a headache; then rose and dashed cold water on her eyes and brow and hair, until her backward curls held crystal beads and her face looked out again like a flower opened freshly after a shower. She sighed deeply—more than once—and put on her little slippers. Then she sat still for a couple of minutes which seemed to her long, as if full of things that were coming; and at last she rose with the air of one recollecting a duty and went down to make tea.

Something of her old life had returned. She had once been used to remembering she must learn her part, go to rehearsal, act and sing in the evening, hide her feelings from her father; and the more painful her life grew, the more she had been trained to hide. The force of her nature had found its chief action in resolute endurance; and to-day the violence of feeling which had broken out in anger had quickly transformed itself into a steady facing of trouble—the well-known companion of her young years.

Yet while she moved about and spoke as usual, a close observer might have discerned a difference between this apparent calm, born of restraining energy, and the sweet, genuine calm of the months when she had first felt a return of infantine happiness. Those whom fortune has indulged, who have always believed calamity to be what happens to others, feel a blind incredulous rage when their lot is reversed, and half believe their cries will alter the storm. Mirah felt no such surprise when familiar Sorrow came back from brief absence and sat down with her according to old use. But this habit of expecting trouble rather than joy hindered her from clinging persistently to any belief contrary to the probabilities which were not only suggested by Hans, but supported by her own private knowledge and long-growing presentiment.

An attachment between Deronda and Mrs. Grandcourt, ending in marriage, had the aspect of certainty to her feeling. There had been no fault in him: facts had ordered themselves into a tie between him and a woman who belonged to another world than hers and Ezra’s—nay, who seemed another sort of being than Deronda: something foreign that would disturb his life instead of blending with it. Well, well—if only it could have been deferred so as to make no difference while Ezra was there! Mirah did not know all the momentousness of the relation between Deronda and her brother, but she had seen and felt enough to forebode its incongruity with any close tie to Mrs. Grandcourt. That was the first clothing her mind gave to her mortal repugnance.

But beneath her habitual acts, her thoughts moved quickly and silently, like changing sensations. Soon the inward language said distinctly: the mortal repugnance would remain even if Ezra were secure.

“What I have read about and sung about and seen acted, is happening to me—this that I am feeling is the love that makes jealousy;” thus impartially Mirah summed up the charge against herself.

But what difference could her pain make to any one else? It must remain exclusively her own and hidden, as her early yearning and devotion to her lost mother had been hidden. And unlike that devotion, this new feeling seemed a misfortune of her nature: a discovery that what should have been pure gratitude and reverence had sunk into selfish pain; that the feeling she had delighted to pour out in words was degraded into something she was ashamed to betray—an absurd longing that she, who had received all and given nothing, should be of importance where she was of no importance—an angry feeling toward another woman who possessed the good she wanted.

Yet what vain reliance had lain darkly within her and now burned into sight as disappointment and jealousy? It was as if her soul had been steeped in poisonous passion in forgotten dreams of deep sleep, and now flamed out in this unaccountable misery. For with her waking reason she had never entertained what seemed the wildly unfitting thought that Deronda could love her. The uneasiness she had felt before had been vague, easily explained as regret that he was only a visitant in her and Ezra’s world—that his home-world, with its lights and lacqueys, was as different from hers as a portico is from the door of a tent, where the only splendour comes from inaccessible stars. But her pain was no longer vague. The image of Mrs. Grandcourt by Deronda’s side, drawing him farther and farther away, was as definite as pincers on her flesh.

In the Psyche-mould of Mirah’s frame there rested a fervid quality of emotion—sometimes rashly supposed to require the bulk of a Cleopatra. Her impressions had the thoroughness and tenacity that make the first selection of passionate feeling take on the character of a lifelong faithfulness. And now a selection had declared itself which gave love the cruel heart of jealousy. She had been used to strong repugnance toward objects around her and to walking inwardly aloof while they touched her senses; and now that repugnance concentrated itself on Mrs. Grandcourt, of whom she involuntarily conceived more evil than she knew.

“I could bear everything that used to be—but this is worse—this is worse,—I used not to have horrible feelings!” she whispered aloud to her pillow, as if the sound of her own words might keep her from drowning in them.

Strange that she should have to pray against any feeling concerned with Deronda.

But this conclusion had not come in an idle hour. It had been reached through an evening spent attending to Mordecai—whose exaltation, in the prospect of seeing his friend again, disposed him to speak much to her. Often his speech was broken by silences that seemed full of inward utterance, animating his eyes and moving his lips without sound, as if he listened to something beyond the room’s quiet noises—the small tick of the clock, the faint murmur of the kettle, the soft settling of coal ash—as if that humble domestic soundscape were only a threshold to greater voices.

One thought especially occupied him. Once, after a long silence, he said—

“Seest thou, Mirah, the _Shemah_, wherein we briefly confess the divine Unity, is the chief devotional exercise of the Hebrew; and this made our religion the fundamental religion for the whole world: for the divine Unity embraced as its consequence the ultimate unity of mankind. See, then—the nation which has been scoffed at for its separateness, has given a binding theory to the human race. Now, in complete unity a part possesses the whole as the whole possesses every part: and in this way human life is tending toward the image of the Supreme Unity. For as our life becomes more spiritual by capacity of thought, and joy therein, possession tends to become more universal, being independent of gross material contact; so that in a brief day the soul of man may know in fuller volume the good which has been and is, nay, is to come, than all he could possess in a whole life where he had to follow the creeping paths of the senses. In this moment, my sister, I hold the joy of another’s future within me: a future which these eyes will not see, and which my spirit may not then recognize as mine. I recognize it now, and love it so, that I can lay down this poor life upon its altar and say: ‘Burn, burn indiscernibly into that which shall be, which is my love and not me.’ Dost thou understand, Mirah?”

“A little,” Mirah had answered faintly, “but my mind is too poor to have felt it.”

“And yet,” Mordecai had said, rather insistently, “women are specially framed for the love which feels possession in renouncing, and is thus a fit image of what I mean. Somewhere in the later _Midrash_, I think, is the story of a Jewish maiden who loved a Gentile king so well, that this was what she did:—she entered into prison and changed clothes with the woman who was beloved by the king, that she might deliver that woman from death by dying in her stead, and leave the king to be happy in his love which was not for her. This is the surpassing love, that loses self in the object of love.”

“No, Ezra, no,” Mirah had said, with low-toned intensity, as if the story itself hurt her. “That was not it. She wanted the king, when she was dead, to know what she had done, and feel that she was better than the other. It was her strong self, wanting to conquer, that made her die.”

Mordecai had been silent a little, and then had argued gently, pressing the thought like a seal on wax.

“That might be, Mirah. But if she acted so, believing the king would never know.”

“You can make the story so in your mind, Ezra, because you are great, and like to fancy the greatest that could be. But I think it was not really like that. The Jewish girl must have had jealousy in her heart, and she wanted somehow to have the first place in the king’s mind. That is what she would die for.”

“My sister,” Mordecai had answered, “thou hast read too many plays, where the writers delight in showing the human passions as indwelling demons, unmixed with the relenting and devout elements of the soul. Thou judgest by the plays, and not by thy own heart, which is like our mother’s.”

Mirah had made no answer then. And now, making tea with hands that did not shake—measuring, pouring, listening to the small domestic sounds as if they were duties to hold on by—she felt how the very words about renouncing love and losing self had turned into a hard, bitter taste within her: she could not translate her own experience into the noble dialect her brother spoke. There was something in her that would not become pure by being named; and the name she feared most was the one she could not help hearing, again and again, like a refrain under every ordinary sound: Deronda.
