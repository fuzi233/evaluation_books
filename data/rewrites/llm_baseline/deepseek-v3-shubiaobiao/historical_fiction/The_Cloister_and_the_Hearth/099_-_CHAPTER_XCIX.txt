Jerome remained as stern as ever, yet a quiet change had softened his heart toward Gerard. During his time in England, he had felt the absence of his brother more than he cared to admit, and since his return, he often wondered what had become of him. The tales from Gouda had lifted Gerard in his esteem—especially the defiance of Pope and Minorites over what Jerome called *"the damnable heresy of the immaculate conception."* But when a young monk, eyes wet with tears, revealed the true cause of Gerard’s decline, the old disdain flared anew.

He resolved to halt this disgrace. Twice daily, he visited Clement in his cell, wielding all his old authority and eloquence to shake him from this earthly despair. The Church needed his piety, his voice—now more than ever. Gerard listened, praised Jerome’s logic, admired his strength, confessed his own frailty—yet still, day by day, he faded like mist before the dawn.

One afternoon, Jerome spoke bluntly. *"I know your tale—and I was proud of it. But now, Clement, you undo it all. This is not virtue; it is the victory of flesh over spirit. Better to have yielded and repented than to resist while she lived, only to surrender now."*

Clement smiled, gentle as ever. *"Dear Jerome, you wrong me. There is no passion left—only love, and I will not blush for it, not here, not in heaven. Think of the faithful hound that dies upon his master’s grave—is that beast guilty of passion? Neither am I. Passion might have saved my life, but damned my soul. She was my strength, my solace. Her face lifted me in the pulpit; her voice soothed me when men proved ungrateful. She was woven into every good deed I ever did—and when she was gone, I could not walk alone."*

He paused, then added, softer still: *"And do not think me rebellious, Jerome. It is God’s mercy that calls me. When the earth fell upon her coffin, something broke within me—something that cannot mend. I heard it snap. From that hour, bread lost its taste, meat its savor. Were my eyes bandaged now, I could not tell one from the other."*

*"Holy saints!"* Jerome muttered.

*"And from that same hour,"* Gerard continued, *"my sorrow lifted. I smile often now—do you know why? I believe it is thus: He who holds the keys of life and death touched me that moment and said, 'Enough. You have fought, you have wept. Now come home.' And so I come, gladly."*

Jerome groaned. *"The Church has ever had her gentle, fragile servants,"* he said at last. *"I would give ten years of my own life to save yours—but I see it cannot be. Die in peace."*

And so it was. In the days that followed, Gerard slipped toward death with a serenity that drew even the oldest monks to his side, eager to catch his final whispers. He spoke to Giles—begged him to care for Jack, to watch over little Gerard’s inheritance, to kiss their parents in his stead, to send Denys home to Burgundy. *"Poor soul,"* he murmured, *"he will be lost here without me."*

Then came a private hour with Jerome. None knew what passed between them, but when Jerome left, his head was bowed, his hands clasped upon his breast, his sighs heavy.

That night, the monks who kept vigil heard Gerard murmur praises, thanks, fragments of joy. Once, his voice drifted, as if in vision: *"She walks in green fields… white-robed… she smiles… she beckons…"*

Near dawn, three soft taps struck the wall—slow, deliberate. Gerard stirred. *"I come, love. I come."*

When the brothers gathered at his side, he spoke once more, clear and strong. *"Mark this well: I die holding no faith in man’s own merit. I stand with the Apostles, with the fathers of the Church—we are saved not by our works, not by our wisdom, but by faith alone."*

A hush fell. Then, at his request, they swept the floor, strewed ashes in the shape of a cross, and laid him upon it. *"Shall we stretch out your arms?"* one asked.

*"God forbid,"* he whispered. *"I am not worthy of that."*

As dawn broke, his face turned upward, rapt, his hands folded—like Margaret’s. And as the sun rose, at the very hour she had died, he breathed his last word:

Then—he was gone.

They prepared his body. Beneath his robe, they found a hair shirt. *"A saint!"* the younger monks cried. But beneath *that* lay a long, auburn tress. Horror and debate erupted—until Jerome silenced them. *"Who are you to judge?"* he thundered at the loudest accuser. *"For all you know, it is a relic. To your cell—a week’s penance for your haste."*

Then, when the coffin was sealed, Jerome laid the lock upon Gerard’s breast. *"There, Clement,"* he said—and set himself a penance for the act.

The next day, they buried him in Gouda’s churchyard, beside a leaden coffin. Jerome spoke over the grave as the people wept. Later, in the refectory, he addressed the brotherhood:

*"We have buried a saint. Feast, do not mourn—for he is free. I alone shall fast, for I wronged him in life. And you, young brothers, pry not into whether that lock was love’s token or a holy relic. Look instead to the heart that bore it—and follow his example, if you can."*

And so the lovers were united at last—not by the Church, but by the earth. A kinder fate than life had granted.

Now, as another hand sets this stone upon their memory, ask not for pity. Theirs was no tragedy. Life is ever barred by sorrow; death is but a door. If you cannot bear to hear that these two died young—who would now be dust regardless—how will you endure when your own beloved slip from your arms into the sky?

Take from their tale not grief, but strength. For the purest hearts are often gathered first—not lost, but lifted.
