Within Buck, the ancient, primal beast was awakening and swelling with power. Fueled by the brutal demands of trail life, this inner force grew stronger every day, yet it remained a secret. His newly forged cunning granted him a steady poise and absolute control. He was consumed by the effort of adapting to this harsh new existence, leaving no room for carelessness. Consequently, he avoided conflict rather than seeking it, moving with a deliberate caution. He possessed no inclination for rashness, and despite the burning hatred simmering between him and Spitz, he showed no impatience and refrained entirely from aggression.

Conversely, sensing a dangerous rival in Buck, Spitz seized every opportunity to display his teeth. He actively bullied Buck, constantly provoking a fight that could only end in the death of one or the other. Had circumstances allowed earlier in the journey, such a confrontation surely would have occurred, but fate intervened through an unusual accident.

That evening, the team established a bleak and miserable camp on the shore of Lake Le Barge. Blinding driving snow, a wind sharp enough to cut like a white-hot knife, and encroaching darkness had forced them to stumble blindly for a spot to rest. Their situation could scarcely have been worse. Behind them rose a sheer wall of rock, compelling Perrault and François to build their fire and spread their sleeping robes directly on the frozen surface of the lake. Having discarded their tent at Dyea to travel lighter, they relied on a few pieces of driftwood to kindle a fire that slowly melted a hole through the ice, allowing them to eat their supper in the dark.

Tucked close beneath the sheltering rock, Buck constructed his nest. It was so snug and warm that he hesitated to leave it when François began distributing the thawed fish. However, upon returning for his share, he found his sanctuary occupied. A warning snarl signaled that the intruder was Spitz. Until now, Buck had sidestepped trouble with his enemy, but this intrusion was too much. The beast within him roared. He launched himself at Spitz with a fury that startled everyone, especially Spitz, whose entire history with Buck taught him that his rival was merely a timid giant who survived only due to his immense size and weight.

François was equally shocked as the two dogs erupted from the disrupted nest in a tangled frenzy. "A-a-ah!" he shouted at Buck. "Give it to him, by God! Give it to him, the dirty thief!"

Spitz was ready for battle, circling back and forth in a frenzy of rage and eagerness, waiting for a chance to strike. Buck matched him in intensity, circling cautiously for the tactical advantage. Just as the struggle for supremacy seemed imminent, the unexpected occurred—an event that would project their rivalry far into the future, past countless weary miles of trail and toil.

An oath from Perrault, the thunderous crash of a club striking a bony frame, and a high-pitched yelp of pain shattered the tension. Pandemonium broke loose. The camp was revealed to be teeming with skulking, furry shapes—dozens of starving huskies who had tracked the scent of food from a nearby Indian village. They had slipped in unnoticed while Buck and Spitz fought. When the two men charged among them wielding stout clubs, the invaders snapped their jaws and fought back. Driven mad by the smell of food, chaos ensued. Perrault struck one with its head buried in the grub box; the club landed heavily on its gaunt ribs, sending the box crashing to the ground. Instantly, a dozen famished brutes scrambled for the bread and bacon. The clubs fell upon them indiscriminately. They yelped and howled under the rain of blows but struggled on with frantic determination until the last crumb was devoured.

Meanwhile, the astonished team dogs burst from their nests only to be attacked by these fierce invaders. Buck had never seen dogs like these. It appeared their bones were bursting through their skin; they were mere skeletons draped loosely in ragged hides, with blazing eyes and drooling fangs. Hunger-madness rendered them terrifying and irresistible. There was no opposition. The team dogs were swept back against the cliff at the first onslaught. Buck was surrounded by three huskies, and in moments, his head and shoulders were ripped and slashed. The noise was frightful. Billee cried as usual. Dave and Sol-leks, bleeding from numerous wounds, fought bravely side by side. Joe snapped like a demon; once, his teeth clamped onto the foreleg of a husky, crunching through the bone. Pike, the malingerer, leaped upon a crippled animal, breaking its neck with a swift flash of teeth and a jerk. Buck grabbed a frothing adversary by the throat and was sprayed with blood as his teeth sank through the jugular. The warm taste of it fueled his ferocity. He threw himself upon another, only to feel teeth sink into his own throat simultaneously. It was Spitz, treacherously attacking from the flank.

Perrault and François, having cleared their portion of the camp, rushed to save their sled dogs. The wild wave of famished beasts rolled back before them, and Buck shook himself free. But his liberation lasted only a moment. The two men were forced to retreat to save the remaining grub, prompting the huskies to resume their assault on the team. Billee, terrified into a sudden bravery, sprang through the savage circle and fled across the ice. Pike and Dub followed on his heels, with the rest of the team trailing behind. As Buck prepared to spring after them, he saw Spitz rushing at him with clear intent to knock him down. If Buck fell and became trapped under that mass of hungry animals, there would be no hope. Bracing himself for the impact of Spitz's charge, Buck joined the flight out onto the lake.

Later, the nine team dogs gathered in the forest for shelter. Though unpursued, they were in a dire state. Not one was untouched; most bore four or five wounds, while some suffered grievously. Dub was badly injured in a hind leg; Dolly, the last husky added at Dyea, had a severely torn throat; Joe had lost an eye; and Billee, usually so gentle, cried and whimpered all night with an ear chewed and shredded. At daybreak, they limped warily back to camp to find the marauders gone and the two men in foul tempers. Nearly half their food supply was missing. The huskies had gnawed through the sled lashings and canvas covers. Nothing edible had escaped them—not even a pair of Perrault's moose-hide moccasins, chunks of leather traces, or two feet of lash from François's whip. François broke from his mournful inspection of the damage to check on his wounded dogs.

"Ah, my friends," he said softly, "perhaps so many bites will make you mad dogs. Perhaps all mad dogs, sacredam! What do you think, eh, Perrault?"

The courier shook his head doubtfully. With four hundred miles of trail still separating him from Dawson, he could ill afford for madness to break out among his dogs. After two hours of cursing and exertion, the harnesses were repaired, and the wound-stiffened team set off, struggling painfully over the most difficult section of the trail they had yet faced—and indeed, the hardest between them and Dawson.

The Thirty Mile River was wide open. Its wild waters defied the frost, holding ice only in the eddies and quiet spots. Six days of exhausting labor were required to traverse those thirty terrible miles. Terrible they were, for every step was taken at the risk of life for both dog and man. A dozen times, Perrault, probing the way, broke through ice bridges, saved only by the long pole he carried, which he positioned so it fell across the hole created by his own body. But a severe cold snap had arrived, the thermometer reading fifty degrees below zero. Each time he broke through, he was compelled, for the sake of his life, to build a fire and dry his clothes.

Nothing daunted him. It was precisely because nothing daunted him that he had been chosen as a government courier. He took all manner of risks, resolutely thrusting his weather-beaten face into the frost and pushing on from dim dawn to dark. He skirted the frowning shores on rim ice that bent and crackled underfoot, daring not to halt. Once, the sled broke through, taking Dave and Buck with it. By the time they were dragged out, they were half-frozen and nearly drowned. A fire was essential to save them. Coated solidly in ice, the two men kept them running around the flames, sweating and thawing, so close they were singed by the heat.

Another time, Spitz went through the ice, dragging the entire team after him up to Buck, who strained backward with all his might, his forepaws on the slippery edge while the ice quivered and snapped all around. Behind him was Dave, straining backward, and behind the sled was François, pulling until his tendons cracked.

Again, the rim ice broke away before and behind, offering no escape except up the cliff. Perrault climbed it by a miracle, while François prayed for exactly that miracle. Using every thong, sled lashing, and piece of harness tied into a long rope, the dogs were hoisted one by one to the cliff's crest. François came up last, hauling the sled and load. Then came the search for a place to descend, eventually achieved via the rope, leaving them back on the river with only a quarter-mile credit for the day.
