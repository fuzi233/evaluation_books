Mrs. Leigh stood at the edge of the windswept terrace, her eyes drifting out across the grey waters of the Atlantic as she had done countless times before. It was a habit formed over three long, arduous years – a ritual of hope and despair, of watching and waiting. Her hair, once lustrous and dark, now silvered with age, danced in the sea breeze, its wisps tangled with the whispers of forgotten dreams. The sun had dipped low, casting a golden glow over the hazy sandhills and the distant sea, where the silhouette of ships glided effortlessly across the horizon. But none of these vessels held the promise she sought – the promise of her sons, lost to the vast expanse of the ocean.

In the silence of her heart, a refrain echoed – a lamentation born of the weight of uncertainty. News had come of triumphs and conquests: Drake's victories in the Spanish Main, the relief of Raleigh's colony in Virginia. Yet, amidst the fanfare, no whisper of the Rose, no echo of Amyas and Frank's laughter, their brave hearts beating in time with hers. Mrs. Leigh's eyes, red-rimmed from the ceaseless vigil, seemed to hold a world of sorrow, a universe of longing. Still, she walked the familiar path, driven by an unyielding instinct, as the tide rose and fell with the rhythm of her grief.

Below, the pebbles of the shore thrummed beneath the pounding surf, a reminder of the tempests that had ravaged the coast years past. Tonight, however, the noise was not just the sea's. The villagers of Northam had kindled bonfires, their voices raised in jubilation and defiance, as the tower's bells tolled out a triumphant peal. The air vibrated with the sound of laughter and song, the scent of smoke and woodsmoke carried on the wind. In every village, the hills blazed with bonfires, a fiery testament to the fervor of the people, their loyalty to the queen, and their fervent wish for peace.

Far away, the bells of Bideford responded to the peals of Northam, their melancholy tones weaving a somber counterpoint to the revelry below.
