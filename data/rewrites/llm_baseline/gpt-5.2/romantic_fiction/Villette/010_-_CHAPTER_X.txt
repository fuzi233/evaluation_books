Madame Beck never startled you by inconsistency. She could be forbearing with everybody, and tender to nobody; and in her the two habits went hand in hand, as naturally as a well-swept floor goes with a locked pantry. Her own little ones did not soften her. She was vigilant for their health, provident for what she called their future, careful of their clothes, their lessons, their portion of air and exercise; but she seemed not to know the impulse which makes a woman draw a child to her bosom as one draws bread to the mouth when hunger bites.

I have seen her seated in the garden, where the gravel made a discreet, dry sound underfoot and the clipped leaves kept their own stillness, while, far down an alley, her “little bees”—the children—went and came under Trinette’s eye. In Madame’s posture there was always prudence—an attitude of mind more than of body. If the youngest, a puny, delicate darling, chanced to spy her, and, breaking away, came tottering along the path, laughing and panting with that breathy eagerness which is half joy, half distress, and would have flung itself at her knee, Madame only extended a hand—an even, intercepting hand—to avert what she would have called an inconvenient concussion.

“Prends garde, mon enfant!” she would say, unmoved.

She let it stand near her a moment, as one lets a warm dish cool before covering it; then, without smile, kiss, or fond word, she rose, took the small fingers, and led it back to Trinette.

To the eldest girl, Désirée, she bore herself in another fashion; yet the same trait—calm without candour—ruled it. That child was a plague. In kitchen and class-room alike one heard: “Quelle peste que cette Désirée! Quel poison que cet enfant-là!” and the epithet was justified. Désirée had the genius of provocation; she would creep to the servants’ garrets, lift lids and turn keys, tear their best caps with a quick, spiteful hand, soil shawls as neatly as if malice were a needle and she were skilled in hemming it. She watched for her chance at the dining-room buffet, where a glass would fall and shiver, or porcelain crack with a sharp little cry that made the ear wince; she haunted the storeroom cupboard, where jars stood ranked—preserves glowing in their own sugar, sweet wine sleeping in its bottle—and there she plundered and broke, and—most finished of all—she contrived that suspicion should drip, drop by drop, upon the cook and kitchen-maid.

When Madame saw, or when report reached her, her sole observation, uttered in matchless serenity, was, “Désirée a besoin d’une surveillance toute particulière.”

So she kept that promising olive-branch much at her side. Never once, I think, did she sit the child down and tell her plainly what she was—thief, liar, spoiler; never did she speak of the evil of the habit, the after-taste of such indulgence, the bitterness it leaves when youth is done. Surveillance was to do the whole work, as if watching were seasoning enough to change the nature of a dish.

It failed, of course. Shut out from the servants, Désirée pillaged her mother. Whatever lay on Madame’s work-table, whatever glittered at her toilet, the child’s fingers sought and conveyed. A brooch vanished; scissors went; a ring was missing. Madame saw—and pretended not to see. She had not the rectitude to confront vice in its small face and name it aloud. When the value of what was gone made restitution necessary, she would affect to suppose Désirée had taken it “in play,” and would beg her to restore it.

Désirée was too practised to be caught in so mild a net. She brought falsehood promptly to the aid of theft, and denied she had touched brooch, ring, or scissors. Then Madame, maintaining her hollow system with admirable composure, assumed belief—calm, bland belief—after which she watched and dogged the child with untiring patience, tracked her to a hiding-place—some hole in the garden-wall, some cranny in garret or out-house. When she had found the cache, she sent Désirée out walking with Trinette, and, profiting by the absence, robbed the robber.

Désirée, true daughter of an astute parent, never let cheek or eye betray mortification on discovering her loss. She would come back with the same composed face, as if she had mislaid nothing, as if it were all part of the game.

The second child, Fifine, they said resembled her dead father. Madame had given her healthy frame, blue eye, ruddy cheek; but not from Madame came the moral creature. Fifine was honest, gleeful, warm-tempered; she bustled like a little cook about a hot hearth—apt to knock things over, apt to burn her fingers, but meaning no harm. She had, too, a frank gourmandise: the most direct road to her heart lay through her palate.

One day that bustling instinct led her into calamity. She fell—top to bottom—down a steep flight of stone steps; the sound was of flesh and bone against hard edges, and it rang through the house in a way that made every ear flinch. Madame, who always heard every noise—heard them as some people hear a false note in music—issued from the dining-room, lifted the child, and said quietly, like one naming a fact on an inventory:

“Cet enfant a un os de cassé.”

At first we hoped she was wrong; we were not. One small, plump arm hung powerless, a helpless thing, as if it belonged to another body. Madame turned to me.

“Let Meess take her,” she said, in the children’s English. “Et qu’on aille tout de suite chercher un fiacre.”

In a fiacre she departed at once, cool, prompt, self-possessed, to fetch a surgeon. She did not find the family-doctor—Dr. Pillule—at home; but that did not embarrass her. She sought until she laid her hand on a substitute to her mind, and brought him back as decisively as one brings home a needed ingredient from a distant market when the pot is already on the fire.

Meanwhile I had cut the child’s sleeve from the injured arm—cloth yielding with that quick, tearing sound scissors make when they work under haste—and I undressed her and put her to bed. The little chamber was small and heated; it smelt of warm linen, of human breath and fear, of camphor from some old drawer, and—faintly—of the kitchen below where supper had been begun and abandoned. We were gathered there—the bonne, the cook, the portress, and I—yet none of us, I suppose, looked very closely at the new doctor when he entered. I, at least, had my whole soul taken up with Fifine’s cries. She had good lungs; her wail filled the room, broke against the ceiling, beat back upon us, and made the very air seem sharp.

Those cries redoubled when the stranger approached the bed. When he lifted her, she fought him with all the passionate indignation of pain.

“Let alone!” she screamed, in her broken English. “I will not you: I will Dr. Pillule!”

“And Dr. Pillule is my very good friend,” came the answer—in perfect English, clear and untroubled—“but he is busy at a place three leagues off, and I am come in his stead. So now, when we get a little calmer, we must commence business; and we will soon have that unlucky little arm bandaged and in right order.”

He called for a glass of eau sucrée. The spoon chimed softly against the glass; the sugar-water smelt thin and comforting, like childhood’s simplest bribe. He fed her a few teaspoonfuls, speaking with that easy mastery which quiets by taking for granted that quiet will come. Fifine, true little gourmand, swallowed between sobs; her rage softened, not through reason but through sweetness.

“More when it is over,” he promised; and then he went to work.

He needed help. He demanded it of the cook, a robust woman with strong arms—arms fit to knead heavy dough, to lift iron pots. But at the thought of touching that tortured little limb, courage deserted the stoutest. The cook fled; the portress fled; Trinette, too, gave way and vanished, leaving behind only the rustle of skirts and the shameful silence of retreat.

I did not like to touch the broken arm. I had that shrinking which is not cowardice but a sort of reverence for pain; yet there was no alternative. My hand was already extended—trembling—to do what was requisite, when I was anticipated. Madame Beck put out her own hand. Hers was steady while mine shook.

“Ça vaudra mieux,” said the doctor, turning from me to her.

He chose wisely. My firmness would have been forced; hers was neither forced nor feigned. She stood there, composed as a woman tasting a bitter draught without a change of face.

The doctor’s fingers were quick, skilful, decisive; he set, adjusted, bound. The bandage, when finished, lay neat and sure, as if his hands had a memory for order. When it was done, he looked up, satisfied.

“Merci, Madame; très bien, fort bien!” he said. “Voilà un sang-froid bien opportun, et qui vaut mille élans de sensibilité déplacée.”

He was pleased with her firmness; she was pleased with his praise. And indeed his whole appearance—voice, mien, manner—was such as to work in his favour with women accustomed to measure men by their bearing. When a lamp was brought in—for it was evening and the room had begun to darken—you saw him better. He was young. In that small chamber, among those Dutch-made women, he looked imposingly tall. His profile was clear, fine, expressive. His eye glanced from face to face a little too vividly, too quickly, too often; yet the character of it was pleasant, and so was his mouth. His chin was full, cleft, Grecian, perfect.

As to his smile, one could not at once decide what epithet suited it. It pleased—and yet it carried with it a power of summoning up, like a sudden sound in a quiet wood, all one’s weak points, all one’s little ridiculousness, all that might lay one open to a laugh. Still, Fifine liked that dubious smile and called its owner genial. He had hurt her; nevertheless, when he prepared to go, she stretched out her hand to bid him good-night, as if sweetness and skilful mastery had made him friend.

He patted her hand kindly. Then he and Madame went down-stairs together—she talking with an unwonted tide of spirits and volubility, he listening with good-natured amenity dashed with an unconscious roguish archness hard to define. I noticed he spoke French well, yet English better; he had, too, an English complexion, eyes, and form.

And I noticed more.

As he passed me in leaving the room, turning his face in my direction for a moment—not to address me, but to speak to Madame, yet so standing that I could not but look up—a recollection which had struggled in my mind since the first sound of his voice started up perfected. This was the very gentleman I had spoken to at the bureau; who had helped in the matter of my trunk; who had guided me through the dark wet park. When he went down the long vestibule and out into the street, I recognised even his tread—that firm, equal stride I had followed under dripping trees, while the night’s water pattered on leaves with a cold, incessant whisper.

It seemed likely that this young surgeon-physician’s first visit to the Rue Fossette would be his last. Dr. Pillule, respectable and established, was expected home next day; why should a substitute return? But Fate—if one must call by a large name the small concatenations by which lives are bent—had written otherwise.

Dr. Pillule was summoned to attend a rich old hypochondriac in the antique university town of Bouquin-Moisi. He prescribed change of air and travel, and, having prescribed, was retained to accompany the timid patient on a tour of some weeks. Thus it remained for the new doctor to continue his attendance in the Rue Fossette.

I often saw him when he came; for Madame would not trust the little invalid wholly to Trinette, and required me to spend much time in the nursery. He was, I think, skilful. Fifine recovered rapidly under his care; yet even convalescence did not hasten his dismissal. Destiny and Madame Beck seemed in league, and both had ruled that he should make deliberate acquaintance with the vestibule, the private staircase, the upper chambers.

No sooner did Fifine emerge from his hands than Désirée declared herself ill.

That possessed child had a genius for simulation, and, captivated by the indulgences of a sick-room—pillows like little thrones, sheets to build tents, broths and sweet draughts as tributes—she concluded an illness would suit her perfectly and took to bed. She acted well, and her mother better; for though the whole case was transparent to Madame Beck as day, she treated it with astonishing gravity and good faith. What surprised me was that Dr. John—for so the young Englishman had taught Fifine to call him, and we all, taking the name from her, fell into the habit until it became established, and he was known by no other in the Rue Fossette—what surprised me was that Dr. John consented, tacitly, to adopt Madame’s tactics and fall in with her manoeuvres.

At first he betrayed a moment of comic doubt; he cast one or two rapid glances from the child to the mother, paused as if consulting himself; then, with a good grace, resigned himself to play his part in the farce.

Désirée ate like a raven, and her appetite was a kind of insolence. She gambolled day and night in bed, pitched tents with sheets and blankets, lounged like a Turk among pillows and bolsters; she amused herself by throwing shoes at her bonne and grimacing at her sisters. In short, she overflowed with unmerited health and evil spirits; she only languished when mamma and the physician paid their daily visit.

Madame Beck, I knew, was glad—at any price—to have her daughter in bed and out of mischief; but I wondered that Dr. John did not tire. Every day, on that mere pretext, he came punctually. Madame received him always with the same empressement, the same sunshine for himself, the same admirably counterfeited concern for her child. Dr. John wrote harmless prescriptions for the patient—tisanes that smelled of steeped herbs, innocuous powders, a regimen as empty of danger as it was full of ceremony—and he viewed the mother with a shrewdly sparkling eye.

Madame caught his rallying looks without resenting them; she had too much good sense for that. Supple as the young doctor seemed, one could not despise him. This pliancy was not adopted to curry favour: while he liked his office at the pensionnat and lingered strangely about the Rue Fossette, he carried himself there with independence—almost carelessness; and yet he was often thoughtful and preoccupied, as if the house held for him not merely patients and politenesses, but some question his mind kept chewing like a hard crust.

It was not my business to observe the mystery of his bearing, or to search out its origin or aim; but placed as I was, I could hardly help it. He laid himself open to my observation, because in my presence he accorded me precisely that degree of notice a person of my exterior habitually expects: about what is given to unobtrusive articles of furniture—chairs of common joiner’s work, carpets of no striking pattern. Often, while waiting for Madame, he would muse, smile, watch, or listen like a man who thinks himself alone. The house, at such times, had its own sound-world: the far-off clink of a saucepan neglected on the kitchen range; the faint scrape of a chair-leg in the schoolroom; the hush and flutter of a child’s restless turning among sheets; in the garden below, the thin, tired chirp of a sparrow persevering against the city’s muffled roar. I, meantime, was free to puzzle over his face and movements, to wonder what peculiar interest and attachment—mixed with doubt, strangeness, and ruled by some presiding spell—wedded him to this demi-convent buried in the built-up core of a capital, where voices were trained into one dominant tongue and small native tones were daily rubbed away like names from an old spoon.

He, I believe, never remembered that I had eyes in my head, much less a brain behind them.

Nor would he ever have found it out, but that one day, while he sat in the sunshine and I was observing the colouring of his hair, whiskers, complexion—the whole being of such a tone as a strong light brings out with perilous force (I recollect comparing his beamy head, in thought, to that “golden image” which Nebuchadnezzar set up)—an idea, new, sudden, startling, riveted me with over-mastering strength. It was not admiration that fixed me; it was recognition sharpened into certainty, and something beyond recognition—some sense of what names do, what they hide, what they proclaim. Here, in this house where we called him “Dr. John” because a child’s tongue had christened him so, was a man whose very appellation seemed a translation, a dominance—English set upon French soil; and I felt, with a quick inward recoil, how easy it is for the strong language to take the place of the weak, for one sound to drown another, as carriage-wheels drown birdsong in a lane.

I know not to this day how I looked at him. Surprise and conviction made me forget myself. I only recovered my usual consciousness when I saw his notice arrested—when I perceived that he had caught my movement in a clear little oval mirror fixed in the side of the window recess, by aid of which reflector Madame often secretly spied persons walking in the garden below.

Though of so gay and sanguine a temperament, he was not without a nervous sensitiveness that made him ill at ease under a direct, inquiring gaze. On surprising me thus, he turned and spoke in a tone courteous, yet with just so much dryness as marked annoyance and gave what he said the character of rebuke.

“Mademoiselle does not spare me,” he observed. “I am not vain enough to fancy that it is my merits which attract her attention; it must then be some defect. Dare I ask—what?”

I was confounded; yet not beyond recovery. I knew that neither incautious admiration nor unjustifiable inquisitiveness had drawn my look. I might have cleared myself at once, but I would not. I did not speak. I was not in the habit of speaking to him. So I let him think what he pleased and accuse me as he would. I resumed some work I had dropped, and kept my head bent over it during the remainder of his stay, while his voice, and Madame’s, moved on above me—two currents in the same room, one dominant, one yielding, both careful, both practised.

There is a perverse mood of mind which is rather soothed than irritated by misconstruction; and in quarters where we can never be rightly known, we take, I think, a strange pleasure in being consummately ignored. What honest man, casually taken for a housebreaker, does not feel rather tickled than vexed at the mistake?
