Gerard dragged himself back to consciousness and to raw, unvarnished despair before his eyes even fluttered open. The acrid tang of fever herbs and leech’s poppy draught clung to his tongue, and every thud of his pulse sent a white-hot lancing pain through his skull. By the second day, he was raving, brain on fire, thrashing so hard the neighbors could hear his broken cries for Margaret through the thin oak walls. On the table hard by his bed, coiled like a stray pelt, lay his rich auburn hair, long as a woman’s, shorn off by the leech while he thrashed unaware.
Worse symptoms stacked on each other fast: the cold sweats, the glassy unseeing stare, the rattle in his chest that made the gossips huddle on the landing and cross themselves. On the fifth day, the leech snapped his satchel shut, shook his head at the landlady, and bade her make ready for the end. But the very same sunset, Gerard fell slack into a deep, unyielding sleep so still the maids thought he’d already drawn his last breath.
Most of the town said he would wake only to die. But an old gossip whose opinion carried weight — she’d nursed plague victims and battlefield wounded for thirty years — declared his young, hard-worn body might yet pull through, if only he could sleep twelve unbroken hours.
His old landlady cleared every last soul out of the room that instant, barred the door, and settled herself in the chair by his bedside. She vowed a wax candle to the Virgin for every hour he should sleep, lit the first, and watched.
He slept twelve hours. The good soul fell to her knees, weeping, and thanked Our Lady out loud.
He slept twenty-four hours. She began to fidget, checking his even breath every ten minutes, the little pile of burnt candle stubs piling up by the statue in the corner.
At the thirtieth hour, she sent a runner for the old nurse. “Thirty hours! Shall we wake him?”
The woman leaned over Gerard, inspected his half-parted lips, his soft damp palm, and shook her head. “Learned leeches would rouse him to prod at his tongue and scribble nonsense on their parchments, and be none the wiser. But we that be women know better than to meddle when bon Nature is doing the healing. When did sleep ever harm a racked brain or a torn heart?”
By the forty-eighth hour, word of the man who slept two full days straight leaked out, and they had to bar the stairwell to keep the gawking neighbors from crowding the sickroom. They let only two visitors pass: Fra Colonna, the gentle humanist friar, and his friend the gigantic, stolid Fra Jerome, who had sailed through a hundred North Sea storms at Gerard’s side. The two relieved the exhausted women, sitting silent by the bed: Colonna’s eyes glistened with tears as he gazed at his young friend, while Jerome, his rosary wrapped tight in his big calloused hand, watched Gerard as calmly as he had watched the towering waves the day they nearly drowned together.
At last, as the first pale streaks of dawn seeped through the room’s leaded window, the landlady nudged Fra Colonna’s elbow. He looked up. Gerard had opened his eyes, soft and slow, as if he had only been dozing by a cottage fire.
He stared, bewildered, for a long minute. He drew himself up a little in the bed, lifted a shaking hand to his head, and found his hair was gone, cropped close to his scalp. He spotted Colonna, and a flicker of old warmth crossed his face, a smile of quiet pleasure at seeing a friend. But halfway through the smile, the memory of Margaret’s death, of the cruel weeks he’d spent wandering before the news found him, crashed over him all at once. His face convulsed with anguish so sharp it looked like it might tear him apart. He gave a great, raw cry that rattled the window panes, and twisted to shove his face hard against the rough plaster wall.
His landlady wept aloud at the sight. She had been widowed young, she knew what it felt like to wake and remember the person you loved most would never walk through the door again. Fra Jerome began reciting canticles and prayers from his breviary, his deep voice low and steady. Gerard rolled himself tight in the bedclothes, as if he could hide from the world.
Fra Colonna stepped to his bedside, sniffling, and reminded him all was not lost. The divine Muses were immortal, he said. He must turn his affection to them; they would never betray him nor fail him like creatures of clay. The simple, good father could not finish his plea before his own emotion overcame him, and he hurried out of the room to compose himself.
Fra Jerome remained, his broad frame filling the doorway. “Young man,” he said, solemn as a judgment, “the Muses exist only in the brains of pagans and idle visionaries. The Church alone gives repose to the heart on earth, and happiness to the soul hereafter. Hath earth deceived thee, hath passion torn thy heart to rags? The Church opens her arms: consecrate thy gifts to her! The Church is peace of mind.”
He spoke the words and turned to leave before Gerard could reply.
“The Church!” Gerard screamed, leaping barefoot out of bed and shaking his fist at the friar’s retreating back. “Malediction on the Church! But for the Church I should not lie broken here, and she lie cold, cold, cold in the dirt of Holland. Oh, my Margaret! Oh, my darling! My darling! And I ran from thee the very last months thou hadst to live. Cruel! Cruel! The monsters around thee, they let thee die. Death never comes without signs. Those blind, selfish wretches saw not, or recked not — but I would have seen them, I that loved thee. Oh, had I been there, I had saved thee! I had saved thee! Idiot! Idiot, to leave her for a moment!”
He wept bitter, heaving sobs for a long time. Then all that grief curdled into white-hot rage, and he cried out so loud the rafters trembled: “The Church! For whose sake I was driven from her! My malison be on the Church, and the hypocrites that name it to my broken heart. Accursed be the world! Ghysbrecht the thief lives, and Margaret dies. Murderers, harlots, thieves thrive to grey old age. Only angels die. Curse life! Curse death! And whosoever made them what they are!”
Fra Jerome never heard the heretical words, only the roar of raw rage that echoed down the stairwell. It was a mercy. If he had heard them, he would have dragged his old shipmate to the forum stake to be burned with no more hesitation than he would have roasted a kid for his supper.
The landlady, who had followed Colonna down to the landing, heard the raised voice and hurried back up, frightened. She found Gerard yanking his best velvet doublet over his shoulders, tears still streaming down his face. She remonstrated with him.
“What avails my lying here?” he said, his voice hollow and bleak. “Can I find that which I seek beneath these sheets?”
“Saints preserve us! Art distraught again? What is it you seek, my poor lad?”
“Oblivion.”
“Oblivion, my little heart? Thou art far too young to talk of that.”
“Young or old, what else have I to live for?” He buckled on his fine leather belt. The good dame fussed, trying to stop him. “My pretty Gerard, know that it is Tuesday, not Sunday. Thou never wearest thy brave clothes on working days. Bethink thee.”
“What I did when she lived, I did. Now I shall do whatever erst I did not. The past is dead. There lies my hair, and with it my whole old way of life. I served one Master as well as I could. You see my reward. Now I’ll serve another, and give him a fair trial too.”
“Alas!” the woman gasped, turning pale. “What dark words are these? What new master is this whose service thou wouldst try?”
And with that horrible declaration on his lips, the miserable creature walked out of the house, his cap and feather set jauntily lopsided on his head, his fever-weak limbs wobbling under him, his face pale as ashes, all drawn and lined as if he had lived sixty years in the space of a few weeks.
