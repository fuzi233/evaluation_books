The evening meal at Tergou was interrupted by the frantic entry of Luke Peterson, a man coated in the dust of the road. "Good people," he gasped, "Mistress Catherine is required instantly at Rotterdam."

"My name is Catherine, young man," the woman corrected sharply, though her heart leapt. "Call me Kate, or Margaret."

"Ay, dame," Luke replied, breathless. "She said to me, 'Good Luke, hasten to Tergou and summon Eli the hosier's wife, Catherine, for God's love.' I did not wait for dawn."

"Saints preserve us!" cried the hostess, her mind racing with wild theories. "He has returned? No, she would surely have announced such news. What calamity could compel this?"

"Perhaps the youth can enlighten us," Kate ventured timidly.

"I can," Luke declared. "Her infant is dying. She was consumed by worry for it."

Catherine sprang to her feet. "What is the ailment?"

"Nay, I know not the specifics, but it has wasted away steadily."

A fleeting, furtive exchange of satisfaction passed between Cornelis and Sybrandt, unnoticed by Catherine, whose gaze was fixed on her husband. "Now, Eli," she commanded fiercely, "if you utter a single word against this, we shall quarrel after all these years."

"Who contradicts you, foolish woman?" Eli retorted cheerfully. "Quarrel with your own shadow while I fetch Peter's mule for you."

"Bless thee, my good man! Didst thou ever fail me in a pinch?" she exclaimed, already moving. "Eat your dinner, whoever can, while I prepare."

She drove Luke away in the cart, interrogating him with a mixture of severity and seduction until he confessed everything he knew. Upon arriving, Margaret met them at the threshold, pale and trembling, throwing her arms around Catherine's neck in a desperate embrace.

"Come," Catherine said, scanning the woman's face. "He lives, thank God."

She surveyed the failing child and the hollow-eyed mother. "Fortunate you summoned me," she observed clinically. "The child is poisoned."

"Poisoned! By whom?"

"By you. You have fretted."

"Nay, indeed, mother. How can I avoid fretting?"

"Do not feign ignorance. A nursing mother must turn her mind from grief to the comfort in her lap. Does she not know that the child wastes if the mother vexes herself? This stems from your reading and writing—idle crafts suited to men, which deprive women of practical wisdom. The child must be weaned."

"Oh, cruel woman!" Margaret cried vehemently. "I regret sending for you. Would you steal from me the only solace I possess? While nursing Gerard, I forget I am the unhappiest creature under the sun."

"That you do not," Catherine replied sharply. "Or he would not be as he is."

"Mother!" Margaret pleaded.

"'Tis hard," Catherine softened, yet remained firm. "Consider: would it not be harder to look down upon his lovely face gazing up at you from a small coffin?"

"Oh, Jesu!"

"And how could you face your other burdens with a heart full and a lap empty?"

"Oh, mother, I consent to anything. Only save my boy."

"That is a good lass. Trust me; I see clearer than you."

Yet another consent was required—the babe's—and he proved more stubborn than his mother. Margaret attempted to feign regret at his behavior, claiming he loved her too well, but Catherine was a match for both. Having noticed a healthy young woman nearby with an infant, Catherine approached her, explaining the crisis. The stranger agreed with a smile, placing her own child in the cradle and entering Margaret's home. She curtsied, and Catherine handed the pining infant to her. The stranger examined the baby with pity, cooed over it, and began to nurse it as if it were her own.

Margaret, paralyzed by the stranger's assurance, cast a rueful glance at Catherine before bursting into tears. The visitor looked up. "What is the matter? Wife, you told me the mother was unwilling."

"She is not; she is merely a fool. Never heed her. And you, Margaret, I am ashamed of you."

"You are a cruel, hard-hearted woman," Margaret sobbed.

"Those who undertake to guide the weak must be stern. And forgive me; you are not my flesh and blood, but your boy is."

Allowing her words to settle, Catherine added, "Come now. She sacrifices her own to save yours, and you can think of nothing better than weeping in the woman's face. Out, fie, for shame!"

"Nay, wife," the nurse replied gently. "Thank Heaven, I have enough for myself and hers to spare. Pray, do not blame her; perhaps life's troubles have soured her milk."

"And her heart as well," Catherine remarked without mercy.

Margaret stared at Catherine, her eyes dropping to the floor. Turning to the nurse, she sobbed, "I know I ought to be very grateful to you," then turned her head away, unable to bear the sight of another woman nursing her Gerard, who showed no distinction between the new mother and the one who had abandoned him.

The nurse responded, "You are very welcome, my poor woman. And so are you, Mistress Catherine, my townsfolk, though you seem unaware."

"What, are you from Tergou? All the better. I cannot recall your face."

"Oh, you know me not. My husband and I are humble folk compared to you. Yet true Eli and his wife are known and respected throughout the town. So, I am at your call, dame; at yours, wife; and at yours, my pretty poppet, night or day."

"There is a woman of the right old sort," Catherine murmured as the door closed behind her.

"I HATE her. I HATE her. I HATE her," Margaret whispered with fervent intensity.

Catherine merely laughed at the outburst. "That is right," she said. "Better to speak it openly than think it slyly. It is very natural, after all. Come, here is your bundle of comfort. Take it and hate it, if you can," placing the child in Margaret's lap.

"No, no," Margaret protested, turning her head halfway away. She could not bring herself to turn fully. "He is not my child now; he is hers. I do not know why she left him here, but it was kind of her not to take him to her own house, cradle and all. Oh! oh! oh!"

"Ah! Well, one comfort remains: he is not dead. This gives me light: some other woman has taken him from me. Like father, like son," Margaret wailed.

Catherine felt pity for her and allowed her to cry in peace. Later, when assistance was needed, Catherine would take Gerard out for fresh air. Margaret never objected, expressing no incredulity, yet she was always in tears upon their return.

This tacit agreement lasted briefly. Margaret became entirely eager to wean little Gerard. Once done, the child recovered his health and vigor, only to face a new trial immediately upon teething. Here, Catherine's experience proved invaluable. Amidst her grief regarding the father, Margaret found moments of bliss watching her son's tiny teeth emerge. "Teeth, mother? I call them not teeth, but pearls of pearls." Each pearl that appeared on his red gums seemed to Margaret the greatest achievement of Nature. Her companion shared the illusion. If told that standing corn was equally admirable, Margaret might have transformed into a reproachful gazelle and Catherine would have thrown them both out; thus, every new tooth was announced with a shriek of triumph by whichever woman discovered it first.

Catherine conversed with Joan and learned she was the wife of Jorian Ketel of Tergou, formerly a servant to Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, who had fallen out of favor and returned to Rotterdam. His friends had secured him the position of sexton at the parish, and combined with carpentry, he managed quite well.

In return, Catherine told Joan the identity of the child she had nursed, recounting the tale of Margaret and Gerard and the deep anxiety caused by the boy's silence. "Ay," Joan said, "the world is full of trouble." One day she advised Catherine, "I believe my man knows more about your Gerard than anyone here; but he has grown closer to his duties lately. Drop in some day just before sunset and set him talking. For our Lady's sake, do not say I sent you. The only hiding he ever gave me was for babbling his business, and I do not wish for another. Gramercy! I married a man for the comfort of the thing, not to be hidden."

Catherine visited. Jorian was eager to explain how he had befriended her son and perhaps saved his life, but this was no news to Catherine. The moment she began cross-examining him about why her lost boy neither came nor wrote, he cast a grim look at his wife, who received it with a calm, stolid candor and innocent unconsciousness. His answers grew short and sullen.

"What should he know more than another?" he deflected. He added, after a pause, "Do you think the burgomaster keeps such as me in his secrets?"

"Oh, then the burgomaster knows something?" Catherine asked sharply.

"Likely. Who else should?"

"I'll ask him."

"I would."

"And tell him you say he knows."

"That is right, dame. Go make him mine enemy. That is what a poor fellow always gets if he speaks to you women."

From that moment, Jorian withdrew, becoming impenetrable as a hedgehog, almost as prickly. His conduct caused both women agonies of mind, alarm, and irritated curiosity. Ghysbrecht was, for some reason, Gerard's mortal enemy; he had stopped his marriage, imprisoned him, and hunted him. Here was his former servant, who, when off guard, had hinted that this enemy held the key to Gerard's silence. Despite sifting every word and look from Jorian, everything remained dark and mysterious. Catherine then urged Margaret to go herself. "You are young and fair. You may extract more from him than I could."

The conjecture proved reasonable. Margaret went with her child, tapping timidly at Jorian's door just before sunset. "Come in," a sturdy voice called. She entered, finding Jorian by the fireside. At her sight, he rose, snorted, and burst out of the house. "Is that for me, wife?" Margaret asked, turning very red.

"You must excuse him," Joan replied somewhat coldly. "He lays it to your door that he is a poor man instead of a rich one. It concerns a piece of parchment. One was missing, and he received nothing from the burgomaster regarding it."

"Alas! Gerard took it."

"Likely. But my man says you should not have let him; you were pledged to keep them all safe. And truly, I do not blame my Jorian for being angry. 'Tis hard for a poor man to be so near fortune and lose it through those he has befriended. However, I tell him another story. Says I, 'Folk out of trouble like you and me should not be too hard on folk in trouble; and she has plenty.' Going already? What is all your hurry, mistress?"

"Oh, it is not for me to drive the goodman out of his own house."

"Well, let me kiss the bairn before you go. He is not in fault anyway, poor innocent."

Upon receiving this cruel rebuff, Margaret made a resolution she did not even confide to Catherine.

After six weeks, the good woman returned home. On the child's birthday, which occurred shortly thereafter, Margaret performed no work. She donned her Sunday clothes, took her boy in her arms, and went to the church to pray long and fervently for Gerard's safe return.

That same day and hour, Father Clement celebrated a mass and prayed for Margaret's departed soul in the minster church at Basle.
