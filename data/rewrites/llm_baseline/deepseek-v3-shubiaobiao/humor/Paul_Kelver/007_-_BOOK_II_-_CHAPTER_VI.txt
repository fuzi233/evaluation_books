“Nothing’s missing,” my mother declared, scanning the room with a housekeeper’s precision. “Mark my words, she took fright and bolted.”

“But what on earth was there to frighten her?” My father paused mid-pour, decanter in one hand, bottle in the other, his brow furrowed in bewilderment.

“The sheer horror of the idea,” my mother sighed. “She’d never waited at table in her life. Last night, she was near tears just thinking about it.”

“Then what’s to be done?” My father’s voice rose in alarm. “They’ll be here within the hour!”

“They’ll have no dinner,” my mother said grimly, “unless I don an apron and serve it myself.”

“Where does she live?”

“Ilford.”

“Then we must laugh it off,” my father decided.

But my mother, sinking into a chair, burst into tears. The week had been merciless—a whirlwind of scrubbing, rearranging, and last-minute purchases. The kitchen range, as if unionized, had staged a mutiny, refusing to bake unless granted “permanently improved conditions.” Cookbooks, long neglected, had been dusted off, consulted, and promptly cursed. Experiments had ended in disaster, triumphs in anxiety. And now, on the brink of victory, the reward had been snatched away.

Downstairs, the dinner waited. Upstairs, the guests would soon assemble. And between them? A yawning chasm, bridged only by a parlour-maid who had fled to Ilford.

A knock at the door.

“Surely not already?” My father lunged for his coat.

“Barbara, likely,” my mother sniffed. “She promised to help me dress. Not that it matters now.”

But it was Barbara, swift as sunlight, kissing them all in one fluid motion. And with her came laughter, as shadows do when light enters.

“There’ll be no dinner,” my father announced cheerfully. “We’ll pretend it was yesterday. Imagine their faces!”

“There *will* be dinner,” my mother corrected, “but it’s stranded in the kitchen with no way up.”

Barbara’s face lit with inspiration. “I’ll find someone.”

Before protest could form, she had snatched the latchkey and vanished.

Silence fell, heavy as regret.

“If only there were a hotel nearby,” my father muttered.

“You’ll entertain them alone,” my mother decided. “I’ll serve.”

“Absurd!”

“Cook can’t cook *and* serve.”

“Fan?”

A look silenced him.

“Paul?”

“I’ll not turn my son into a footman.”

“Then dress, at least.”

“An apron will suffice.”

“I’d hoped to see you in that new frock.”

The words, clumsy as they were, struck true. My mother softened. “I’d looked forward to it too.”

Then—the key in the lock.

“She’s failed,” my mother sighed.

The door swung open.

“Will I do?”

There she stood, framed in the doorway, golden hair crowned with the daintiest of caps, apron crisp as new snow. Every objection withered before her.

“*You* shall be footman,” she commanded me—and this time, my mother laughed. “Wait here.” Then, to my mother: “Ready?”

It was my first glimpse of my mother in evening dress, and I confess—I was scandalized. The gleaming whiteness of her shoulders seemed improper, and I must have shown it, for she flushed and reached for a shawl.

But Barbara cried out, “A sin to hide such beauty!” And my father, with a conjurer’s deftness, swept the shawl aside and knelt, pressing my mother’s hand to his lips as though she were royalty. Barbara, behind her chair, insisted I follow suit. “The Queen is receiving,” she whispered.

And so I knelt, shyly gazing up at this radiant stranger who was my mother—and glimpsed, for the first time, the philosophy of clothes.

---

Soon, my parents’ voices would clash like cymbals—hers sharp as shattered glass, his low and sullen. Doors would slam; silence would fester. I lay awake, listening through thin walls to whispers that cut deeper than shouts.

Some cruel, unseen thing had crept into our home, slipping between them, whispering malice. I fancied I could see it—a grinning shadow, laughing as it turned love to venom.

To this day, I see it still—lurking behind every whispered sweetheart, mocking every love tale. Does it spare any of us? Or must we all pass through this valley where love’s light dims?

---

My duties as footman left little time for peeking. The guest of honor, one Mr. Teidelmann (or Tiedelmann—memory falters), was a snuffling, deaf old titan of industry, his name emblazoned on Limehouse warehouses. My mother shouted pleasantries at him; he cupped his ear and demanded repetition.

Hasluck, ever the salesman, bellowed praise for Teidelmann’s disinfectant. “My wife swears by it!”

“Our drains!” chimed Mrs. Hasluck. “You’d hardly know they existed.”

“Eh? What’s that?” Teidelmann squinted.

“Your disinfectant,” my mother translated.

“Delighted,” he mumbled, bored.

Hasluck persisted. “Reminds me of coconuts!”

“Does it?”

“Doesn’t it you?”

“Can’t say. Never use the stuff.”

A pause. Then, sotto voce: “Silly fool.”

The table froze.

Hasluck, unruffled, laughed. “He’s right. I *am* a fool—talking shop like a tradesman.”

But soon, he had Teidelmann’s ear again—this time on art. “Pearsall says you’re the best judge in Europe.”

Teidelmann chuckled. “He’d know. Tried to palm off rubbish on me often enough.”

“That last purchase made young —’s career.”

“Paid two thousand. They’ll sell for twenty.”

“But you’d never sell?” my father asked.

“No. My widow will.”

A soft laugh floated from the corner—the second Mrs. Teidelmann, a languid beauty. “Anderson’s despair is my indifference to art. He’ll pay thousands for a ragged child in a painting. Such waste.”

“Yet you’d pay thousands for a diamond,” my father countered.

“To enhance my neck.”

“An even greater waste.”

Again, that musical laugh.

“Who is she?” I whispered to Barbara as we shared a jelly on the stairs.

“The second Mrs. Teidelmann. Married him for his money. Beautiful, but I don’t like her.”

“Not as beautiful as you.”

She scoffed. “I’m just a child. No one notices me—except other children.” A rare note of discontent.

“Dr. Hal thinks you beautiful.”

Her voice brightened. “What did he say?”

“I forget exactly—but he admires you.”

“Ask him again. Properly. I’m curious.”

---

For no one at that dinner shone brighter than the parlour-maid.

“Wherever did you find her?” Mrs. Florret marveled.

“A neat-handed Phillis,” Dr. Florret approved.

“She’d never wait at *my* table,” laughed Mrs. Cottle, our minister’s wife.

“She’s temporary,” my mother confessed.

“Thank Mrs. Hasluck,” my father added.

“And me!” Hasluck grinned.

Later, my father absentmindedly called Barbara “my dear,” drawing a sharp glance from Mrs. Cottle. Then Hasluck pinched her elbow, eliciting a yelp. For the house’s honor, my mother explained the ruse—earning Barbara even greater admiration.

By dessert, cap still perched atop her golden head, Barbara sat among the guests, the evening’s darling.

“Kind hearts,” Dr. Florret intoned, “are more than coronets.”

Only my aunt struck a discordant note. “When I was a girl, I told myself, ‘Fanny, you must be amiable.’ And I was. For *years*.”

“Did it pay?” Hasluck asked.

“It attracted,” she said dryly, “no attention whatever.”

“What do you respectable folk know of life?” Washburn flung out. “You’re marionettes, dancing to etiquette’s strings. You weep, sin, and die by rule. Only in death do you face life—leaving others to don black and pretend grief.”

“Assume a virtue,” Dr. Florret murmured.

“Like the ass in the lion’s skin,” Washburn retorted. “Here, at least, men live—kill what they hate, die for what they love. Savages, perhaps, but not bloodless dolls.”

“I prefer the dolls,” Dr. Florret said.

“They *are* pretty,” Washburn conceded.

“Like my first masked ball,” my father mused. “Everyone identical. I fled to the street just to see faces again.”

“But didn’t they unmask at midnight?” purred Mrs. Teidelmann.

“I didn’t stay.”

“Pity. I’d have liked to see what lay beneath.”

“You might,” my father said, “have been disappointed.”
