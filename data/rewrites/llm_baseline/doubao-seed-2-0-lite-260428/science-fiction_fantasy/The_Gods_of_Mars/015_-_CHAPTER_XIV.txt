“My son!”
I could not believe my ears. I rose slow, stiff from the long hours at the ship’s helm, and turned to face the lithe, handsome youth standing before me. Now that I looked close, the reason his face had tugged at some half-forgotten, cherished part of my soul hit me like a blow to the chest. Dejah Thoris’s incomparable grace was etched in every clean line of his features, but it was hard, bright, unmistakeably masculine—those sharp grey eyes, the set of his jaw, the quiet, unyielding fire in his gaze, they were mine, mirror-perfect.
The boy stood rooted, hope warring with stark uncertainty in the tilt of his shoulders.
“Tell me of your mother,” I said, my voice rougher than I intended. “Tell me everything of the years a cruel, unyielding fate stole from me, the years I missed at her side.”
He cried out, sharp with unlooked-for joy, and sprang to throw his arms around my neck. For a long, breathless moment I held him tight, and hot tears stung my eyes, thick enough that I nearly choked like some maudlin old fool. I have never regretted that moment, never been ashamed. A lifetime of fighting across two worlds has taught me a man may seem soft where his wife and child are concerned, and still be unbreakable when the swords are drawn for sterner things.
“Your height, the way you move, that terrible, unmatched ferocity when you fight with a blade,” Carthoris said, pulling back to look at my face, “my mother told me those things a thousand times. But even hearing all that, I scarce dared believe it could be you. What convinced me more than all the rest?”
“What, my boy?”
“The first words you spoke to me. They were of her. No man on Barsoom, no stranger, no loyal soldier, would think first of Dejah Thoris before his own safety, his own glory—save the one who loved her, the one she told me I was named for.”
“For every day of the ten long years I was trapped away from Barsoom,” I told him, “I cannot think of a single waking moment where her face was not shining bright before me. Tell me how she fares.”
“All who have known her longest say she has not changed at all—if anything, she grows more beautiful, even after all this time. Only when she thinks no one is watching, her face goes so sad, so full of longing. She never stops waiting for you. All of Helium mourns with her. Her grandfather’s people love her, they loved you too, and worship your memory as the saviour of Barsoom. Every year on the anniversary of the day you raced across that near-dead world to unlock the life-giving portal that saved billions, we hold a great festival in your honour. But the thanksgiving is mixed with tears—tears that the man who gave us all that new life is not here to share it. There is no name greater on all of Barsoom than John Carter.”
“And what name did you and she choose for me, my boy?” I asked.
“The people of Helium begged to name you after me,” he smiled, “but mother said no. That you two had picked my name together, that your wish came before all else. It is woven from the two names we love best: Carter, and Thoris. Carthoris.”
Xodar had manned the helm the whole time we spoke, and now he called over to us, voice tight with alarm.
“She’s dropping badly at the bow, John Carter. So long as we were climbing at a sharp angle you could not tell, but now that I try to hold a level course it’s impossible. The wound in her hull tore open one of the forward ray tanks.”
I ran to check the damage, and it was far worse than I feared. Not only did we have to tip the nose of the ship sharply upward to keep from diving headlong into the ground, bleeding speed every second, but the repulsive rays pouring out of the cracked tank meant in an hour or less we would be floating completely helpless, stern-down, plummeting to the ochre sea beds below.
We had eased our pace earlier, lulled into a false sense of safety, but I wrenched the engine throttle wide open the second I realized how bad the damage was, and the little craft tore north at blistering speed, while Carthoris and Xodar scrambled to patch the ragged gash in the hull, fighting a losing battle to stop the rays from escaping.
It was still dark when we cleared the northern edge of the ice cap and the thick layer of roiling clouds that hung over it. Below us stretched the familiar Barsoom landscape: rolling, ochre bottoms of seas that had dried a million years prior, low crumbling hills, and dotted across the horizon the grim, silent ruins of cities dead for longer than the human race had walked Earth, great piles of masterful architecture now only home to ancient ghosts, and the ferocious great white apes that roamed the empty wastes.
It got harder and harder to keep the craft level. The bow sagged lower and lower until I had to kill the engine entirely, lest we dive straight down and shatter against the rock below. When the sun crested the horizon and washed away the last of the night dark, the ship gave a final, lurching plunge, rolled half onto its side, and began to spin slow in sickening circles, the bow dropping further below the stern with every turn.
We clung to the handrails and stanchions as the deck tilted steeper and steeper, until all three of us snapped our safety harnesses to the iron rings bolted to the hull sides. A moment later the deck reared to a full ninety-degree angle, and we hung there suspended by our leather straps, our feet dangling a full thousand yards above the ground.
I swung close enough to reach the control levers, and my fingers closed around the bar that regulated the ship’s repulsion rays. The craft responded soft to my touch, and we began to sink slow, gentle, toward the ground below.
It took half an hour for us to touch down softly on the earth. Directly north of us rose a range of high, jagged hills, perfect for hiding from any air patrols that might stumble across our crash site, so we struck out for them at once.
An hour later we were hidden deep in the rounded, wind-carved gullies of the hills, surrounded by the bright flowering shrubs that grow even in Barsoom’s driest wastes. We found thick clusters of the great milk-yielding bushes that serve the nomadic green hordes as both food and drink—exactly the boon we needed, for all three of us were half-starved. We curled up in the shade of the bushes, completely hidden from any passing air scout, and fell asleep. It was the first real rest I had had since I woke back on Barsoom, five days prior, after being pulled straight from my cottage on the Hudson to the bizarre, beautiful, cursed valley of Dor. I had only managed two short naps the entire time, one of them within the therns’ stone storehouse.
I woke mid-afternoon to find someone pressing their lips to my hand, kissing it over and over. I jolted awake, and stared straight into Thuvia’s bright, tear-streaked face.
“My Prince! My Prince!” she cried, light bursting from her. “I mourned you dead. My ancestors have heard my prayers. I have not lived for nothing.”
Her voice woke Xodar and Carthoris. The boy stared at her in open surprise, but Thuvia did not even notice the two men standing beside me. She would have thrown her arms around my neck, smothering me in affection, if I had not disengaged her gentle but firm.
“Easy, Thuvia,” I said, soothing her. “You are worn thin by all the danger and hardship you have survived. You forget yourself, and you forget that I am the husband of Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium.”
“I forget nothing, my Prince,” she replied, soft and steady. “You have never spoken a word of romantic love to me, and I never expected you to. But nothing can stop me loving you. I would never dream of taking Dejah Thoris’s place. All I want, all I ever ask, is to serve you forever as your slave. There is no greater honour, no greater happiness I could ever hope for.”
I have never been the sort of man who fancies himself a favourite of women, and I was more awkward and embarrassed in that moment than I have ever been in my life. I knew the Martian custom well, that a man of honour will always protect any woman in his household, even a slave, but I had never taken any servant but men before.
“If we make it back to Helium, Thuvia,” I told her, “you will come with us as an honoured equal, never a slave. There you will find dozens of handsome young nobles who would face Issus herself just to win one smile from you, and we will have you married to the best, bravest of them all. Forget this foolish infatuation born of gratitude and fear, that your innocent heart mistook for love. I would far rather have you as a dear friend.”
“You are my master,” she said, simply, but a note of deep sadness hung in her voice. “It shall be as you say.”
“How did you end up here, Thuvia?” I asked. “And where is Tars Tarkas?”
“The great Thark, I fear, is dead,” she said, her voice falling. “He fought like no man I have ever seen, but hundreds of green warriors from a rival horde overwhelmed him. The last I saw of him they were dragging him, bleeding and wounded, toward the dead city they sallied from to attack us.”
“You are not certain he is dead?” I pressed. “Where is this city?”
“It sits just beyond this ridge of hills. The escape craft you stayed behind to let us board was far too complicated for us to pilot, so we drifted aimless for two full days. Finally we decided to abandon it and strike out on foot for the nearest waterway. Yesterday we crossed these hills and came upon the dead city below. We were walking down its main street toward the central plaza when a party of green warriors rounded the corner. Tars Tarkas was leading the way, and they saw him, but not me. He shoved me into a shadowed doorway and told me to hide, to run for Helium if I could.
“‘There is no escape for me now,’ he said. ‘These are the South Warhoons. Once they have seen my metal, it is a fight to the death.’
“Then he stepped out to meet them. My Prince, you have never seen fighting like it. For an entire hour they swarmed him, the bodies of dead Warhoons piling higher and higher around his feet until they formed a small hill. But at last there was no more room for him to swing his great longsword. The ones behind pushed the front ranks straight onto him, and he stumbled, and went down, and they rolled over him like a great, living wave. When they dragged him away toward the heart of the city, he was still, and I think he was dead.”
“I will not leave Tars Tarkas to die in Warhoon hands if there is even a sliver of a chance he lives,” I said, standing and buckling my sword belt tight. “I go into that city tonight to confirm it, or rescue him.”
“I go with you,” Carthoris said at once.
“And I,” echoed Xodar.
“Neither of you will come,” I told them flat. “This is work for stealth, not brute force. One man alone can slip through their shadows unseen where three would get caught before we even crossed the city wall. I go alone. If I need your help, I will come back for you.”
They did not like it, but both were good soldiers, and we had already agreed I held command. The sun was already dipping low, and Barsoom’s short, sudden night fell fast.
I gave them final instructions for what to do if I did not return, bade them all farewell, and set off at a long, fast jog toward the dead city. As I came out of the hills, the nearer of Barsoom’s two moons tore across the sky, its bright silver light gilding the jagged, barbaric splendour of the ancient metropolis. The city had been built on these gentle foothills that once sloped down to meet the long-gone sea, which meant I could slip into its streets completely unobserved from the rear hillside, away from the main sea-facing approaches the Warhoons always used.
The green hordes that occupy these dead cities never spread far past the central plaza, so I hugged the dense, pitch-black shadows of the walls the whole way, pausing at every intersection to make sure no warrior was in sight before darting across to the shadowed side opposite. I made it all the way to the edge of the inhabited part of the city without being spotted, alerted to the horde’s presence only by the grunts and squeals of their thoats and zitidars corralled in the hollow walled courtyards of the surrounding blocks.
Those old familiar sounds of green Martian life made my chest tight, like coming home after a decade away. It was among these exact sounds that I had first courted Dejah Thoris, long ago, in the marble halls of the dead city of Korad.
I hunkered in the shadows at the far corner of the first inhabited square, and saw a stream of warriors pouring out of the surrounding buildings, all heading for the great domed structure at the centre of the plaza. I knew Warhoon customs well enough to know that was either the jeddak’s private quarters or the audience hall where he met his sub-chiefs, and whatever gathering they were holding almost certainly involved their new prisoner, Tars Tarkas.
To reach that building I had to cross the full length of one city block, a wide main avenue, and a stretch of the main plaza itself. The south side of the nearest block was completely dark, no torches burning, no sounds of life, so I slipped through the empty, abandoned building there to cut through to the inner courtyard. No one stopped me, no one spotted me, and I emerged into the courtyard right behind the occupied east-facing buildings. A whole herd of thoats and zitidars wandered there, cropping the low ochre moss that grows everywhere on uncultivated Barsoom. The wind blew from the northwest, so they could not scent me, and I crept along the east wall, tucked tight beneath the overhanging second-floor balconies, all the way to the north end of the block. The first three floors of those buildings were lit up, teeming with people, but everything above the third was pitch black.
There was no way to sneak through the lit rooms, packed full of green men and women, so my only path was up through the dark upper floors. It was trivial for me to scale the wall; one quick, agile leap, and my hands closed around the stone handrail of the second-floor balcony. I hauled myself up in a single motion.
Through the open windows I could see the green Martian squatting on their silk and fur sleeping mats, grunting short, single words at each other, their telepathic powers more than enough for conversation without long speeches. A warrior stepped through the doorway into the hall, and called to one of the men sitting there.
“Tan Gama, come. We are taking the Thark before Kab Kadja. Bring one other with you.”
The warrior named rose, gestured to a comrade beside him, and all three left the apartment.
If I followed them, I knew I would find exactly where they were keeping Tars Tarkas. I slipped through a dark unlit doorway on the balcony that led into the building’s corridor, and crept after the three warriors. They turned right at the end of the hall, vanishing from sight, and I raced after them as fast as I dared, not caring that my pace was reckless. Fate had handed me a perfect opportunity, and I could not waste it.
At the end of the corridor I found a spiral stairway leading up and down. I knew Warhoon customs well, and these ancient buildings: the Thark would not be held in an above-ground cell. He was down in the dark dungeon pits beneath the city.
I had been a prisoner of the northern Warhoons once, long ago, and I still remembered every inch of that dark underground pit they had locked me in. I crept down the stairs, and at the landing below I saw the faint glimmer of the three warriors’ torch far down the shaft, leading into the maze of underground corridors. I followed a safe distance behind them, keeping to the edge of the torchlight, as they wound through a hundred twisting, turning passages that had not seen sunlight in a million years.
After a hundred yards, they turned sharp right through an open doorway. I ran to catch up, and peered inside to see them unlocking the heavy chains that bound Tars Tarkas to the cell wall. They herded him rough out of the cell so fast I barely managed to dart back down the corridor ahead of them, out of the thin circle of their torchlight, before they spotted me.
I had assumed they would turn back the way they came, away from me, but to my horror, they turned straight toward me the second they stepped out of the cell. There was nothing for it but to run as fast as I could stay out of their torchlight, not daring to duck into any random side passage, lest they turn right into it after me. I raced through the dark, and I knew at any second I might stumble straight into a hidden pit, or run face-first into the ghoulish things that haunt the dark pits beneath Barsoom’s dead cities. The faint glow of their torch behind me was just bright enough that I could see the turns in the corridor ahead, and keep from slamming headfirst into the walls.
Suddenly I rounded a corner and came to a five-way intersection, and the faint glow of the torch vanished completely. The silence around me was total, as heavy as a tomb. The warriors must have taken a different corridor at the junction while I was still moving ahead of their light.
I groped my way back to the five-way crossroads, my hand pressed flat to the wall to guide me, fumbling until I counted five separate corridor entrances, but not a single glimmer of light peeked out of any of them. I held my breath and listened, and far down the middle corridor I thought I heard the faint clink of their side arms against their blades. I ran down that passage, pausing every few steps to listen again, but soon the silence swallowed even that faint sound. I had followed a false lead.
