As Jorian tugged his coarse linen doublet smooth and settled his thick wool jerkin over it, ready to lead the visitor to Peter Brandt’s grave, his rough, good-humoured tongue did not idle.
“Folks out Sevenbergen way used to call old Peter a bit of a magician, you know. And they swear he left them with a parting taste of his craft: told them he saw Margaret’s lad coming down the Rhine in fine velvet clothes and a purse heavy with coin, but his face scarred by a foreign sword, and short a limb or two from what he left with. But dear heart, none of it ever came to pass. Margaret still wears herself thin waiting for her Gerard, and old Peter lies as quiet in the clay as any of his neighbours—though she did pay to put a carved stone slab over him, to keep him settled where he belongs, as you shall soon see.”
He braced both hands on the edge of the open grave to heave himself up out of the dirt, but the friar laid a trembling, ice-cold hand on his shoulder, and spoke in a strange, hoarse whisper.
“How long has Peter Brandt been dead?”
“Some two months now. Why?”
“And his daughter buried him, you say?”
“Nay, I dug the grave for him, but she paid all the church dues and had that stone raised to mark the spot. He never had more than the one daughter, Margaret, leastways none he ever owned to.”
“Then—you think Margaret is still alive?”
“Think? Man, I’d be dead in her place else. Riddle that one, will you? She saved my life not six weeks back. I’d come down with a wasting sickness, couldn’t keep a crumb on my stomach, and all the town doctors couldn’t do a thing for me. My Joan was already measuring me for a shroud, and then Margaret—who folks round here said died three years back, mark you—turned up, threw open my stuffy windows, had them move me out of my damp bed, and cured me in the blink of an eye. And what d’you think she used? The scurviest little weed growing wild in my very own garden: feverfew! I used to call it a nuisance, but now every time I pass that patch I doff my cap and give it a civil nod.” Jorian stopped short, staring at the friar as the man’s face flushed bright then drained ashen pale in quick succession. “What ails you, father? You look like you’ve seen a ghost.”
“Surprise—joy—wonder—terror,” gasped Clement, his chest heaving. “I knew a man who loved her so deeply the false news of her death nearly tore his soul clean from his body. You say she lives? I believe you.”
Jorian stared long and hard, then spoke very gravely. “By Our Lady, answer me this: did you truly know the lad that loved poor Margaret? Where?”
Clement nearly threw off his friar’s cowl right there to reveal his own face, but he remembered Jerome’s warning letter, and shrank back from the old name he had left behind in the secular world. “I knew him in Italy. His name was Gerard Eliassoen. I was with him when a letter arrived from Margaret Van Eyck, telling him the woman he loved was dead and buried. Let me sit down, my strength fails me. Foul play. Foul, cruel play.”
“Father, Heaven itself must have sent you here to me.” Jorian guided the staggering friar to a half-rotted grave marker to rest on, then spat on his palms and drove his spade furiously into the loose grave dirt, grinding his teeth. “I’ve held the riddle of that lie locked in my head for years, sick at heart for the both of them. Two knaves in this town I’d gladly bash sense into right this minute if I had the chance. You say you want to know who tricked your Gerard? It was Ghysbrecht van Swieten, the old burgomaster of Tergou, and those two worthless hounds Cornelis and Sybrandt, Gerard’s own blood brothers.
“I was doing carpentry work up in the burgomaster’s upper chamber once, patching the floor, and I’d lain flat on my stomach to sneak my tools away before they saw me loiter, when I heard the whole plot. They planned to swap the true letter that painter Hans Memling was carrying out to Gerard in Italy, for a forged one that said Margaret was dead. The old man was nervous they wouldn’t go through with it, so he opened a drawer brim full of silver, and said if they pulled the trick off they could each plunge a hand in and keep every coin they could hold.
“Next dawn I spotted those two rascals slinking out of their house, each sporting a fresh black eye. I followed them to the burgomaster’s, and hid in the rafters. I saw them open that drawer, every silver piece glinting, and you never saw two fools strain so hard to hold more coins than their fists were meant for. Sybrandt had even daubed the back of his hand in glue to stick extra pennies to his skin, the greedy lout! They did the foul deed, swapped the letters, and ran off—so giddy they even left their own silver rings behind in the drawer, the devil got the better end of that bargain.
“They’ve got a fancy new shop in the Hoog Straet now, sign hanging out front all gilded: a sheep and a lambkin. A pair of wolves sucking their dam would be closer to the mark. The whole family’s feasting there today, celebrating their wicked little victory. All these years Margaret’s sat by her window staring down the street, crying her eyes out for Gerard, never once guessing he’d been tricked into thinking she was rotting in the ground. If it weren’t for those two cowards, the lad whose life I fished out of the river would be here beside her now, the happiest man in all Holland, instead of wasting away in a foreign country, and she’d have her head on his shoulder instead of pining alone day after day—”
The rest of his rant died unspoken. The friar had sprung to his feet, livid with unchained, wordless fury, fists clenched high over his head, and tore headlong out of the churchyard as if the hounds of hell were at his heels. Jorian’s own anger fizzled out to nothing at the sight of that raw, unhuman tempest of grief, and he stood gaping after him, bewildered, until a small, trembling hand closed around his arm.
It was Margaret Brandt. She had waited for the friar on old Peter’s tombstone for so long she’d wandered into the church to look for him, only to see his enormous shadow tear across the south aisle floor and brush right past her, spurring her after him into the yard. She stared after the vanishing wild figure down the lane, then turned to Jorian, white-lipped.
“What did he say to you? He spoke to you, do not deny it.”
“Girl, he asked me where you were buried in this churchyard. I told him nowhere, thank the Lord, that you were alive and going about saving fools like me from the grave. Then he told me he knew your Gerard in Italy, and got a letter that said you were dead. I told him the truth: that letter was a forgery, those two knaves Cornelis and Sybrandt swapped it, I saw the whole thing.”
Margaret gasped, half laughing through tears. “He lives! The worst fear of all is gone, thank God.” But her face blanched again a heartbeat later. “Oh, Jorian—what have you done? That friar is no stranger. That is Gerard. Himself! You do not know him like I do. He once struck the burgomaster Ghysbrecht so hard the man dropped senseless to the ground. He’s heading straight for that shop on Hoog Straet. Quick! Help me run, before there is bloodshed.”
Jorian’s blood turned to ice. The tonsured friar’s sunken, fasting face snapped into place in his memory, that was the lad he’d dragged out of the Rhine all those years ago. He scrambled out of the grave, yanking his jerkin on as he ran beside her, their boots thudding down the cobblestones.
They rounded the corner onto Hoog Straet, and spotted the gilded sheep sign swinging over the new shop barely twenty yards ahead, when a roar like a wounded wild beast burst out from inside the house. The door flew off its latch, and the friar burst out, still white with unspent rage, fists raised high, deaf to Jorian’s shouts to stop, and tore down the far end of the street before they could even call his name.
Margaret screamed, and fainted dead away against Jorian’s chest. A moment later she stirred, her face calm and cold as winter frost.
“Help me inside, Jorian. Let me see what horror lies there. Let me know the worst, and die if I must.”
He supported her shaking limbs across the threshold, the house unnaturally, deathly still around them. No sound of shouting, no cry of pain. He pushed the unlatched inner door open softly with his palm, and the two of them stood frozen, side by side, on the threshold of the great back chamber. What they saw there, you shall soon know.
