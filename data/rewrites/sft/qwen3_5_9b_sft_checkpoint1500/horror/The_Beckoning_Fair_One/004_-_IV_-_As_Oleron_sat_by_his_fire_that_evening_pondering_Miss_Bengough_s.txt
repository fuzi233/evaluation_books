By the time the embers of Oleron's fire glowed like dying stars against the encroaching dark, his mind was already a battlefield. He sat hunched over the heat, wrestling with Miss Bengough's prophecy that shadows waited for him in his work. A cold, hard certainty crystallized within him: it would have been infinitely kinder if she had kept her warnings locked inside her head. To speak of difficulty, he realized with a sinking stomach, was to conjure it. Words themselves became a kind of mortar, binding together the doubts and fears that otherwise might remain scattered. By voicing the obstacles, she had built them into walls. She had inadvertently birthed an enemy dedicated to the ruin of *Romilly*.

And, in a manner both absurd and irrefutable, women possessed a strange power to attach this malignancy directly to the surroundings where one lived. *"You'll never finish *Romilly* here,"* she had declared. But why? Was it the sheer luxury of the house—the gentle, suffocating comfort—that drained the vital juices of ambition and reduced a man to sloth? The cottage was undeniably charming, a haven of beauty, yet it held no such seductive poison. No, Elsie had simply missed the mark.

Driven by a sudden need to refute her, Oleron shifted his chair, turning to survey the room. It seemed to smile at him, a benevolent, knowing grin illuminated by the firelight. He mirrored the expression, though it tasted of pity for a space that had somehow been maligned in her eyes. Even the slight pallor he had noted earlier vanished under the soft, amber glow. The chintz curtains, heavy with floral patterns and trellises, hung in long, silent folds; the leather-bound volumes in the cases drank the light with a richness that spoke of age and wisdom. If anything had been lacking, it was Elsie herself, standing out of step with the quiet dignity of the room.

A strange thought seized him, and he allowed it to expand. Yes, the room had, quite unintentionally, played a trick on Miss Bengough that afternoon. It had acted as a mirror, reflecting a stark contrast between her nature and the atmosphere around her. If one were to grant the ridiculous notion that the room lacked vigor—though it hardly did—it stood in opposition to Elsie's overwhelming abundance. She was a creature of redundancy, of "muchness," whereas Oleron, in his soul, leaned toward the austere.

In that moment, a startling revelation struck him. He visualized Elsie again as she had stood before him: large, showy, a moist, pink flush radiating from her skin like the bloom of a prize flower. Instantly, the image suffered in his mind. He recognized now, with a jolt of horror, that he had been critiquing her all along, unconsciously dissecting her machinery. Her humid, melting appearance was not natural; it was the product of analyzable processes. Lurking behind that façade, he saw now, was a vague, shadowy shape emblematic of mortality—a grim counterpoint to the vitality he needed. For ten years, they had shared intimacies, yet he had never dared propose. Now, sitting in the firelight, a profound gratitude washed over him for having never taken that step.

Sudden shame flushed his face. Had Elsie been there, witnessing this internal scrutiny, he would have crumbled before her. What right did he have to dissect the woman who had been his anchor, his loyal comrade, the one whose support he would count on if the world fell away? To treat her thoughts as a puzzle to be solved was to betray a sacred trust. He was an ingrate, a cad.

For ten minutes, he sat frozen, staring into the flames as the humiliating red faded from his cheeks. The only sound in the silence was a faint, musical tinkling drifting from the kitchen—the rhythmic drip of water from a poorly tightened tap into a vessel below. His fingers began to beat mechanically against his knee, counting the falls, trying to drown out the rhythm with a trivial motion. Slowly, the shame receded, leaving him cool and detached. When he finally resumed his meditation, he was unaware that he had returned to exactly where he had left off.

His thoughts drifted back to the root of his unease. It was not merely her physical floridity that he had judged; it was the vast chasm between their minds. There was no comfort in realizing how different they truly were. He felt no relief in remembering that they had once walked side by side in understanding; instead, a torrent of questions surged forth. Their intellects diverged sharply. Looking back, he could no longer deny that their paths had never truly coincided. True, he had read his writings to her, and she had spoken with apparent comprehension, but what happens when the foundation of friendship is revealed to be built on a lie? He questioned everything now. For a fleeting second, the thought crossed his mind that a man who demands more from a friend than they can give risks losing them forever, but he pushed the idea aside.

Silence returned, broken only by the distant tap. And then, slowly, the truth emerged. If these observations applied to Elsie Bengough, they applied with even greater force to the creation she inspired: Romilly Bishop. Since he could not bring himself to speak harsh words of Elsie aloud, he gave free rein to his thoughts regarding the fictional character.

There was no doubt lingering in his heart anymore. He hated the central figure of his novel. As he had described her, she overwhelmed the senses; she was coarse-fibred, over-colored, rank. The moment he articulated this hatred, it became absolute. Just as Gulliver had mocked the Brobdingnagian maids-of-honour, so Oleron now despised Romilly. Mentally and spiritually, she was a void—unsensitive, limited, common. The model, which had stuck out through fifteen vulgar and blatant chapters, had blinded him to the flaws until now. He marveled that it had taken so long for dawn to break upon him.

And *this* was meant to be his Beatrice, his vision! As Elsie, she was destined to enter the furnace of his art and emerge transformed into the Woman all men desire. Her thoughts were to be distilled from his finest imaginings, her form carved from his dearest dreams, set in a landscape worthy of her worth. He had brooded over her for months, feeling her stir within him like a mother sensing a child's life, before penning chapter after chapter.

And those fifteen sodden chapters were what he had produced!

A surge of determination shook him. They must go. All fifteen chapters of her. It was settled. For what would replace them, his mind was a blank canvas, but he focused on the immediate necessity. A man was not absolved of taking the wrong path simply because the right one had not yet revealed itself. Something better would surely follow if it was fated to arrive; meanwhile, action was required.

He rose, retrieved the manuscript pages, and spread them on his desk. Before casting them into the fire, he read them over once more, letting the weight of his failure settle over him. Then, instead of tossing them, he let them slip from his hand, catching nothing but the air.

His consciousness snapped back to the kitchen tap. It had a gamut of four or five notes, ringing irregular changes that were foolishly sweet, like a dulcimer. In his mind, Oleron could see every gathering drop, the tiny tremble on the lip of the brass, the percussion of its fall—"Plink... plunk..."—minimized almost to silence. Following the lowest note, there seemed to be a brief phrase, repeated irregularly, and soon Oleron found himself waiting for its return. It was quite pretty, a haunting melody born of water.

Yet it offered no wakefulness. Instead, it pulled him under. Oleron dozed over the fire, the rhythm lulling him into sleep.

When he awoke, the fire had burned low, its embers glowing like coals in the hearth, while the candleflames licked the rims of the Sheffield iron stands. With sluggish movements, he rose, yawned, and performed his nightly ritual of locking doors and fastening windows. He passed into his bedroom and sank into a deep, soundless slumber.

But the following morning brought a curious sequel. Mrs. Barrett did not knock at his door, as she often did, but tapped gently on the wooden panel beyond it, where his bed lay. Oleron rose, donned his dressing gown, and admitted her. He was not aware that, as the door swung open, he had been humming a tune. Mrs. Barrett lingered, her hand resting on the knob, her face averted slightly, a soft smile playing on her lips.

"My dear!" her soft falsetto trilled. "But that will be a very *old* tune, Mr. Oleron! I shall not have heard it for forty years!"

"What tune?" Oleron asked, bewildered.

"The tune, indeed, that you was humming, sir."

Oleron had his thumb buried in the flap of a letter, and it remained there. "_I_ was humming?... Sing it, Mrs. Barrett."

Mrs. Barrett *prut-prutted*, producing a sound like dry leaves skittering across stone. "I have no voice for singing, Mr. Oleron; it was Ann Pugh who sang for our family. But the tune is very *old*, and it is called 'The Beckoning Fair One.'"
