Wallack’s was packed to the cornice, the whole house breathing together as if the velvet, the gaslight, and the press of bodies had made one great animal of it. “The Shaughraun” was in full tide—Boucicault nimble as ever in the title role, the English company at that sweet crest of popularity where even the stale turns of a familiar scene could be made to taste new. From the galleries came the hearty, unembarrassed roar of delight; below, in stalls and boxes, the applause wore gloves—people smiled at the old contrivances and yet surrendered to them, because the crowd itself, with its layered murmur and sudden storms of clapping, made surrender easy.

There was one passage that always stilled the house into a kind of listening silence, a hush so exact that Newland Archer could hear the faint creak of dress-fabrics and the soft percussion of a dropped programme as distinctly as a drum in a small room. It was the parting: Montague, after a brief, dry exchange with Ada Dyas—words pared down to something like bone—turning to go while she stood by the mantel, her face angled toward the fire as if warmth were a language she understood better than speech.

She wore a grey cashmere gown, unlooped, untrimmed, falling in long clean lines that seemed to make her stillness more eloquent. At her throat, a narrow velvet ribbon—black, and so simple it looked almost severe—its ends trailing down her back. When he turned from her, she braced her arms on the mantel-shelf and hid her face in her hands. He reached the threshold, paused, then stole back with the caution of a man entering a sanctuary; he lifted one end of the velvet ribbon and pressed his lips to it—no more than a touch, like tasting a remembered thing—and slipped away while she remained motionless, unalerted. The curtain dropped upon that dumb surrender, and the audience, caught in the net of it, held its breath a moment longer than the stage required.

It was for that scene that Archer came, again and again. He had seen great actors in Paris and London spend themselves in torrents of speech and gesture; yet this reticence—the refusal to translate grief into display—moved him more deeply than any famous outpouring. Tonight it struck him with a particular ache, as if the theatre’s hush had made room for another silence entirely: his own leave-taking from Madame Olenska after their talk a week or ten days earlier.

He could not have explained the likeness, and if one had set the two tableaux side by side, they would have seemed to belong to different worlds. He was no romantic young actor with a handsome anguish; and Ada Dyas, tall and red-haired, had a monumental plainness, her pale face pleasantly ugly in a way that was the very opposite of Ellen Olenska’s vividness. Nor had he and Madame Olenska been lovers parting in broken-hearted quiet: they had been client and lawyer separating after a consultation that had left the lawyer with the worst possible sense of the client’s case.

And yet his heart beat with a retrospective excitement, a faint, dangerous pleasure in remembering. The resemblance lay, perhaps, in Ellen Olenska’s mysterious capacity to suggest a life beyond the daily run of things—tragic and moving possibilities that hovered at the edge of common rooms like music heard through a closed door. She hardly ever said anything to produce that effect; it was simply part of her, as if her outlandish past clung to her like an incommunicable scent, or as if something dramatic, passionate, unusual, lived quietly in her very nerves.

Archer had always inclined to think that chance and circumstance mattered less than the inward tendency some people had for events—for collisions, for upheavals—to seek them out. In Madame Olenska he had felt this from the first. She seemed to him precisely the kind of woman to whom things were bound to happen, no matter how she shrank from them. The singular point was that she had lived in an atmosphere so thick with drama that her own propensity to provoke it had apparently passed unnoticed. The odd absence of surprise in her—her way of taking certain facts for granted—was what gave him the sense of her having been plucked out of a maelstrom. What she accepted without comment measured what she had had to rebel against.

He had left her that evening with the conviction—painful, reluctant—that Count Olenski’s accusation was not unfounded. The shadowy figure in her past, “the secretary,” had probably not gone unrewarded for his part in her escape. The conditions she had fled were intolerable—past speaking of, past believing. She was young, frightened, desperate. What more natural than gratitude to a rescuer? And what more cruel than that gratitude’s power, in the law’s eyes and the world’s, to level her with the husband she abhorred?

He had made her understand this, because he was bound to; and he had made her understand something else, harder still: that simple-hearted, kindly New York—on whose broader charity she had counted—was precisely the place where she could least hope for indulgence. To have to tell her that, and to watch her accept it with a quiet resignation, had hurt him in a way he had not expected to feel. It drew him to her with obscure currents of jealousy and pity, as if her dumbly confessed error had placed her—humbled, and therefore dear—in his hands. He was glad it was to him she had spoken, and not to the cold professional scrutiny of Mr. Letterblair, nor under the embarrassed gaze of her family.

And, as was inevitable in that world, he had at once made it his business to reassure the others: to assure them she had abandoned her idea of divorce, basing the decision on her having understood its uselessness. With infinite relief they had all turned their eyes away from the “unpleasantness” she had spared them.

“I was sure Newland would manage it,” Mrs. Welland had said proudly of her future son-in-law; and old Mrs. Mingott, having summoned him for a confidential interview, had congratulated him on his cleverness and then snapped out impatiently: “Silly goose! I told her myself what nonsense it was. Wanting to pass herself off as Ellen Mingott and an old maid, when she has the luck to be a married woman and a Countess!”

Those voices—cheerful, complacent, incurious—ran now like an under-tune beneath the theatre’s applause. When the curtain fell on the stage-parting, Archer’s eyes filled unexpectedly, and he stood to go. In rising he turned toward the boxes behind him—and there, as if the evening had been arranged by some director with an ironical taste for symmetry, he saw the woman he had been thinking of.

Madame Olenska was seated in a box with the Beauforts. Lawrence Lefferts was there, and one or two other men, and the whole little group seemed framed in gilt and plush as neatly as the play itself. Archer had not spoken with Ellen alone since their evening together; indeed he had tried to avoid being with her in company. But their eyes met; at the same moment Mrs. Beaufort recognized him and made her languid gesture of invitation—an airy motion that managed to be both a welcome and a sign that she need not exert herself further. He could not, without awkwardness, refuse. He went in.

Beaufort and Lefferts shifted, giving him space, and after a few words to Mrs. Beaufort—who always preferred to look beautiful rather than to talk—Archer seated himself behind Madame Olenska. The only other person in the box was Mr. Sillerton Jackson, bent toward Mrs. Beaufort and confiding, in that carefully modulated undertone which suggested both secrecy and the pleasure of being overheard, details of Mrs. Lemuel Struthers’s last Sunday reception—where, some people reported, there had actually been dancing.

Under cover of this narrative, to which Mrs. Beaufort listened with her perfect smile and her head held at just the angle that gave the stalls her most flattering profile, Madame Olenska turned slightly and spoke to Archer in a low voice.

“Do you think,” she asked, glancing toward the stage, “he will send her a bunch of yellow roses tomorrow morning?”

The question struck him like the sudden lifting of a lid from something he had kept covered. He reddened; his heart gave a leap of surprised delight. He had called twice on Madame Olenska, and each time had sent her a box of yellow roses—each time without a card. She had never alluded to them; he had assumed she had never connected them with him. Now, with that simple, almost playful sentence, she showed that she had known; and by linking the roses to the tenderness on the stage she gave his small anonymous act a meaning it had not dared to claim for itself.

“I was thinking of that too,” he said, his voice a little unsteady. “I was going to leave the theatre in order to take the picture away with me.”

To his surprise, colour rose in her face—reluctantly, duskily, as if it came from some deeper source than mere amusement. She lowered her eyes to the mother-of-pearl opera-glass in her smoothly gloved hands and, after a pause, said quietly: “What do you do while May is away?”

“I stick to my work,” he answered, and felt, faintly, an annoyance at the question—as though it pried at the careful partitions he had been building.

The Wellands, in obedience to a habit as established as a religion, had left the previous week for St. Augustine. Mr. Welland’s bronchial tubes—so often discussed and so seldom visibly afflicted—required, it was believed, the southern air for the latter part of the winter. Mr. Welland was a mild, silent man with no opinions and many habits, and his habits were treated as laws of nature. One of them demanded that his wife and daughter should accompany him on his annual migration. An unbroken domesticity was essential to his peace of mind; he would not have known where his hair-brushes were, nor how to obtain stamps for his letters, without Mrs. Welland there to tell him. Since all the members of the family adored one another, and since he was the central object of their idolatry, it never occurred to his wife and May to let him go alone. The sons—both in the law, and therefore winter-bound to New York—would join for Easter and travel back with him.

Archer could not decently question the necessity of May’s accompanying her father. The family physician’s reputation rested largely on the pneumonia Mr. Welland had never had; and Mr. Welland’s insistence on St. Augustine was inflexible. Originally, May’s engagement to Archer had been meant to remain unannounced until her return from Florida. The fact that it had been made known sooner was not expected to disturb Mr. Welland’s plans.

Archer would have liked to go south—sunshine, boating, May’s clear presence made brighter by open air. But custom and convention held him as firmly as any legal obligation. Little arduous as his professional duties were, he would have been convicted of frivolity by the whole Mingott clan if he had asked for a holiday in mid-winter. So he accepted May’s departure with the resignation he was beginning to recognize as one of the principal ingredients of married life.

He was conscious, now, that Madame Olenska was watching him from under lowered lids.

“I have done what you wished—what you advised,” she said abruptly.

“Ah—I’m glad,” he returned, embarrassed that she should broach it here, with Beaufort’s broad back near and Sillerton Jackson’s murmuring gossip filling the air like a thin, persistent instrument.

“I understand—that you were right,” she went on, a little breathlessly; “but sometimes life is difficult… perplexing…”

“I know.”

“And I wanted to tell you that I do feel you were right; and that I’m grateful to you,” she ended; and as she spoke she lifted her opera-glass quickly to her eyes, because the door of the box opened and Beaufort’s resonant voice broke in on them with the noise of a larger world reclaiming its place.

Archer stood up and left the box and the theatre. The sounds of Wallack’s—applause, voices, the stirring of the crowd—followed him out into the corridor and down the stairs, thinning as he went, until the night air received him with its own harsher notes: wheels on paving-stones, a distant call, the city’s perpetual unrest.

Only the day before he had received a letter from May Welland. With her characteristic candour she had asked him to “be kind to Ellen” during their absence.

“She likes you and admires you so much—and you know, though she doesn’t show it, she’s still very lonely and unhappy. I don’t think Granny understands her, or uncle Lovell Mingott either; they really think she’s much worldlier and fonder of society than she is. And I can quite see that New York must seem dull to her, though the family won’t admit it. I think she’s been used to lots of things we haven’t got; wonderful music, and picture shows, and celebrities—artists and authors and all the clever people you admire. Granny can’t understand her wanting anything but lots of dinners and clothes—but I can see that you’re almost the only person in New York who can talk to her about what she really cares for.”

His wise May—how he had loved her for that letter, for the generous confidence in it, for the way her innocence could sometimes strike straight to a truth. Yet he had not meant to act on her request. He was too busy, for one thing; and he did not care, as an engaged man, to play too conspicuously the part of Madame Olenska’s champion. Besides, he had an idea that Ellen knew how to take care of herself far better than May imagined. Beaufort was visibly at her feet; Mr. van der Luyden hovered above her like a protecting deity; and any number of candidates—Lawrence Lefferts among them—waited their opportunity in the middle distance.

And yet, as he walked away into the city’s clamour, he could not deny what he felt each time he saw her, each time her voice slid through the din and found him: May’s ingenuousness, after all, came close to divination. Ellen Olenska was lonely, and she was unhappy.
