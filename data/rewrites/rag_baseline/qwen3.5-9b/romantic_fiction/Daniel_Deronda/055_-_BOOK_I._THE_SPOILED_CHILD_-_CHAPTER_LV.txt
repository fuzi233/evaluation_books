When Deronda encountered Gwendolen and Grandcourt upon the staircase, his mind was already a storm-tossed sea, preoccupied by the shadow of a recent summons to his mother. Only two hours prior, he had parted from her, and shortly thereafter learned that the Princess Halm-Eberstein had departed the hotel. Logically, the path to Mainz lay clear; he could have delivered Joseph Kalonymos's letter and claimed the family chest, fulfilling the practical mandate of his journey. Yet, a paralyzing mixture of emotions, refusing to crystallize into reason, held him fast. Long after the farewell, he remained passive, weighed down by a retrospective gravity. In the solitude of his waiting, he relived the preceding scenes with a renewed, piercing intensity, as if they were mere preludes to their inevitable arrival within his soul. He allowed himself to weep, with a compassion sharper than most women's, over a life so intimately near yet agonizingly distant. He perceived the world shifted irrevocably by the certainty of bonds that had altered the balance of his hopes and fears, granting him a strange, newfound fellowship. It was as though, hidden beneath the cloak of night, he had mistakenly joined a band of wanderers, only to discover at dawn that the tents of his true kin stood miles apart. A quivering imagination seized him, sensing a profound kinship with a grandfather animated by fierce impulses and cherished thoughts, now perhaps stirring from sleep within his own heart. Through this torrent of passion, Mordecai and Mirah remained ever-present, silent partners clasping hands with him in understanding. Such was the texture of Deronda's spirit: quick, responsive fiber wrapped in a mantle of self-controlled reserve forged by early hardship.

Then, the persistent ringing of a bell shattered the trance, reminding him of the hour. He considered consulting the *Bradshaw* and making the necessary preparations for the next train to Mainz, thoughts of the letter and the chest flickering briefly alongside images of London and the beings who constituted the deepest attachments of his life. But other desires, rooted in Genoa, anchored him. These were the forces that urge us to linger when an interview carries the presage of finality or overwhelming sorrow. He did not consciously decide to stay until Friday's evening service at the synagogue, nor did he explicitly vow to seek the Grandcourts again despite Grandcourt's likely displeasure. Instead, he simply ceased moving, sitting amidst the stillness while his mind wandered to the sanctuary. He visualized the congregation: faces scarcely different from those of his grandfather's era, hearing the Spanish-Hebrew liturgy endure through seasons of wandering like a plant with drifting seeds, binding far-off lands to the exile's home. His thoughts drifted to Gwendolen, haunted by anxious recollections of the past and a half-formed conviction that it would be cruelty to depart without attempting, against Grandcourt's probable objections, to demonstrate his continued sympathy following their abrupt separation.

In this suspended state, he delayed his departure, eating dinner without taste, then rising abruptly to locate the synagogue. Passing the porter, he inquired if Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt remained in the hotel and requested their apartment number. The porter supplied the number but added that they had gone out boating. This single piece of information possessed a potency sufficient to fracture Deronda's focus, dividing his attention between the sparse *talithim* and the solemn, dark faces of worshippers who approached their prayers with an easy familiarity—a Hebrew dyed Italian—that suggested his grandfather, per the Princess's hints, must have been nearly as exceptional a Jew as Mordecai himself. Were not men of ardent zeal and far-reaching vision inherently exceptional? Those who, as Mordecai declared, created and fed the world, molding the passive lives that otherwise would have shriveled into the narrow tenacity of insects, unshaken by thoughts beyond the reach of their antennae.

A mournful impatience mingled with his solicitude for Gwendolen, fueled by his current release from immediate cares, urging him to hasten from the synagogue. He chose instead to take his evening walk toward the quay, a favored haunt that now held special allure due to the possibility of intercepting the Grandcourts returning from their boating trip. Resolving to approach them deliberately and disregard any grounds Grandcourt might have for excluding him, Deronda moved forward. The sun had sunk behind a bank of clouds, casting only a faint, dying yellow light upon the waves, which churned under an active breeze. Sauntering slowly within sight of the strand, he observed groups concentrating their attention on a sailing-boat advancing swiftly toward the shore, propelled by two men. Amidst the clamorous chatter in various tongues, Deronda deemed it safer not to ask questions but to push his way to the forefront, becoming an unobstructed witness to the unfolding drama.

Telescopes were raised, and loud declarations erupted: the boat carried someone who had drowned. One claimed it was the *milord*; another insisted the prostrate figure was *miladi*. A Frenchman, lacking a glass, ventured that it was the *milord* who had likely taken his wife out to drown her, citing a national practice—a remark an English skipper immediately dismissed in their native idiom as nonsense that had "undergone a mining operation," before decisively ruling that the reclining figure was a woman. For Deronda, terribly agitated by fluctuating terrors, the rhythm of the oars was punctuated by swift visions of possible and impossible events that might have led to this tragic conclusion, or this broken fragment thereof, with a darker half concealed—if the woman seemingly snatched from the waters was indeed Mrs. Grandcourt.

Soon, however, doubt vanished. The boat was drawn to land, and Deronda saw Gwendolen half-raising herself on her hands, driven by her own effort beneath a heavy shroud of tarpaulins and pea-jackets. She appeared pale as one of the sheeted dead, shivering, with wet hair streaming down her face, her eyes wide with a wild, amazed consciousness, as if she had awakened in a world where judgment was imminent and the surrounding beings were there to seize her. The first rower, drenched through, leaped onto the sand and ran off; the sailors crowding the boat blocked Deronda's advance, allowing him only to watch as Gwendolen cast terrified glances, shrinking in horror as she was carefully, tenderly extricated and led away by the strong arms of rough, bronzed men. Her wet clothes clung to her limbs, exacerbating the impediments of her weakness.

Suddenly, her wandering eyes fell upon Deronda, standing before her. Immediately, as if she had been expecting him and searching for him, she tried to stretch out her arms, restrained by her supporters, speaking in a muffled voice, "It is come, it is come! He is dead!"

"Hush, hush!" Deronda commanded, his tone authoritative. Turning to the assistants, he declared, "I am a connection of this lady's husband. If you will get her on to the *Italia* as quickly as possible, I will undertake everything else."

He remained to learn from the remaining boatman that her husband had gone down irrecoverably and that his vessel floated empty nearby. They had heard a cry, arrived in time to see the lady leap in after her husband, and managed to extract her swiftly, saving her from severe injury. With this confirmed, Deronda hastened to the hotel to ensure the best medical aid was secured. Once satisfied, he telegraphed the event to Sir Hugo, begging his immediate arrival, and also notified Mr. Gascoigne at the rectory, finding therein the nearest known channel to reach Gwendolen's mother. Words Gwendolen had spoken in moments of agitated confession returned to him with the force of inspiration: she had often mentioned her mother's presence as a potential aid, had she but been able to secure it.
