In Which Pendennis Counts His Eggs

Arthur Pendennis had just landed in London, though his stay was meant to be fleeting. After seeing off some fellow travelers at an inn near the West End, he hurried to Chambers in Lamb Court. Even the dreariest legal chambers manage to catch a little sunlight, and for the barrister, freedom itself is the sun. Pen pushed open the door to his friend Warrington's room and found it, as ever, fragrant with pipe-smoke. George Warrington sat hunched over his newspapers and reviews, the quintessential image of a busy lawyer.

The two friends exchanged a greeting that was rough around the edges but warm at the core, a distinctly British form of affection. "Well, young one!" Warrington grinned, pulling his pipe from his mouth. "How are you, old boy?" Pen replied, shaking his hand vigorously. This was the ritual of two men who might have died for each other yesterday and merely nod at breakfast tomorrow. They shared a purse, a soul, and a fierce loyalty, yet their interaction remained a study in stoic decorum. They did not embrace like the French, nor kiss like the Germans; they simply acknowledged each other's existence with a grunt and a handshake, confident in a bond that required no words.

"Switzerland?" Pen asked, settling into the chair opposite.

"Yes," Warrington confirmed. "Couldn't find tobacco fit to smoke until Strasbourg, where I finally grabbed some Caporal."

Though Warrington's mind was clearly filled with grand sights and sublime emotions, his enthusiasm remained shy, veiled behind a cloud of tobacco smoke. He would save his true effusions for private letters later; for now, he contented himself with a list of places visited: Switzerland, Northern Italy, the Tyrol, Vienna, Dresden, and the Rhine. He spoke of them with a peculiar sulky tone, as if mentioning them made him slightly miserable.

Turning the conversation back to himself, Pen confessed to being bored stiff in the country, canvassing with agonizing slowness. He mentioned plans for Tunbridge Wells and the inevitable slowness of his friends there. "It is terribly difficult for an Englishman to admit happiness," he sighed.

"And the seat in Parliament, Pen? Is it settled?" Warrington asked, sharpening his pencil.

"As soon as Parliament sits and a new writ is issued, Clavering steps down, and I slide into his shoes," Pen answered confidently.

"Do we operate under a specific King? Are we Liberals, Conservatives, or Independents?"

"Hem!" Pen chuckled. "Politics are dead. Every man thinks alike. I haven't enough acreage to be a Protectionist, nor could I afford to be if I owned the whole county. I'll stick with the Government but push ahead on social reforms. I've been busy compiling Blue Books on sanitation and colonization. Don't look at me like that, old cynic. I'm serious."

"We reserve the right to vote against the government whenever we feel like it," Warrington noted dryly. "But we are friends of the people first. We lecture at the Clavering Institute, shake hands with mechanics, and argue for a wider franchise. Still, we're ready to accept office once the House hears our speeches and realizes we aren't nuisances."

"I am not Moses," Pen said, his voice dropping to a melancholy hum. "I have no divine laws to descend from Sinai. I don't belong to the mountains, nor do I aspire to lead humanity. My faith isn't strong enough for that, nor is my vanity. I won't lie, George, unless it's a necessary fiction to keep the wheels turning. If I hear a good idea in the House, I'll support it. If a decent spot opens up, I'll take it. But I wouldn't flatter a giant any more than a mob. As for labels, why bother with Whiggism? It's not a holy order. The Radicals and the countryside built the Liberal Conservatives. Sometimes *The Morning Post* is right, sometimes *Punch*. I don't have a calling; I just seize chances. Let's talk about something else."

"The next thing after ambition is love, surely?" Warrington pressed gently. "How are our young hearts doing? Are we going to trade our chambers for a drawing-room? Are you divorcing me, Arthur, to marry?"

"I suppose so," Pen admitted, sighing deeply. "She's kind, lively, sings well, and doesn't mind smoking. She'll have a fortune—Uncle says the Begum will shower us with gold. And Blanche seems smitten."

"That implies we accept both her caresses and her cash," Warrington observed sagely.

"Life is a transaction, isn't it?" Pen mused. "I haven't broken my heart over her. I've told her plainly what I feel, and we're engaged. Since I last saw her, especially these past two months, I think she's grown fonder. Her letters to me and to Laura prove it. Ours have been plain—no rapturous vows, nothing theatrical. Just treating it as a done deal, neither rushing nor delaying."

"And Laura? How is she?" Warrington asked, his tone shifting abruptly.

"Laura, George," Pen said, staring fixedly at his friend, "by heaven, she is the best, noblest, and dearest girl the sun has ever warmed." His voice cracked. He reached across the table, gripping Warrington's hand.

"You only realized that now, didn't you?" Warrington asked softly after a heavy pause.

"Who hasn't learned lessons too late?" Arthur cried, his impatience flaring again, gathering emotion as he spoke. "Whose life hasn't been a series of disappointments? Who buries their heart intact? We all pay a ransom to Fate. If we survive paying the fine, that's lucky. I thought I'd lost the greatest prize in the world—an angel living under my roof—and let her slip away. Am I the only fool? Does it hurt less because I admit I deserved it? She's gone. God bless her! It's like *Undine*, isn't it, George?"

"She was in this room once," Warrington whispered. "I saw her. Heard her soft voice. Saw that sweet smile and those kind eyes. I think of her in every nightmare, blessed and loved forever. Now... nothing remains but a glass that once held a bouquet and a Bible with Helen's handwriting. A brief flower of a life."

They sat in silence, the weight of loss hanging between them. Finally, Pen broke the quiet. "I must go see Uncle. He's in a terrible mood, writing nasty letters. He's getting old, and he wants me in Parliament with a house and an heir before I fade away. 'Show me these,' he wrote, 'then let old Arthur Pendennis make room for the younger fellows.' He walked the Pall Mall pavement long enough."

"There's a kindness in that old heathen," Warrington remarked. "He cares for his lineage, for us. He blesses us from the club window at Bays's and gets buried beneath the flags of St. James's, watching the cabs roll to the Levee. An edifying end."

Pen mused, "The new blood I'm bringing in is a bit tainted. If I'd chosen differently, Father-in-Law Amory wouldn't have been the ancestor I wanted. Nor Grandfather-in-Law Snell. Or those Oriental forebears. Speaking of Amory, who was he? Lieutenant of an Indiaman. Blanche wrote verses about him—the storm, the mountain wave, the seaman's grave, the gallant father. Drowned commanding a country ship between Calcutta and Sydney. Amory and the Begum weren't exactly happy. She's been unlucky with husbands, the poor old lady. Between us, Sir Francis Clavering is a despicable creature—"

"Never legislated for his country," Warrington cut in sharply. Pen flushed.

"At Baden," Warrington continued, changing the subject, "I met Chevalier Strong in full regalia. He and Clavering are at odds; Strong thinks Clavering is an utter scoundrel. Your old card teacher, Bloundell, was with Strong. Time has polished him into a more accomplished rogue. But the real king of the castle was Colonel Altamont. He was breaking banks and giving flies to everyone."

"My uncle knows a thing or two about Altamont," Pen said. "And Clavering knows something about him. There's a certain *louche* quality to the man. Anyway, I must dash off to Bury Street like a dutiful nephew."

Taking his hat, Pen prepared to leave. "I'll walk with you," Warrington offered. They stopped at Pen's chambers, now situated on the lower floor. Pen immediately began dabbing himself with eau-de-cologne, meticulously scenting his hair and whiskers.

"What's wrong? Haven't you smoked? Did my pipe poison you?" Warrington grumbled.

"I'm dining with some ladies," Pen explained, adjusting a gorgeous shirt-front and neckcloth. "They're passing through town, staying at an hotel in Jermyn Street. I slept at Bath last night. Come along, George! I can invite anyone I please, and the old lady talks about you constantly."
