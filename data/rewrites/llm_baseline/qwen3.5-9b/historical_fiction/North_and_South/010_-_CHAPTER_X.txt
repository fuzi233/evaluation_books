Mr. Thornton departed the house without entering the dining-room a second time. Being somewhat late, he hurried briskly toward the Hales' residence at Crampton. He was acutely conscious of the need to honor his new acquaintance; any display of disrespect born of tardiness would be unforgivable. As he stood at the gate, waiting for Dixon's inevitable slowness—a delay compounded by her reluctance to answer the bell—he heard the church clock strike half-past seven.

Dixon eventually opened the door, ushering him into a modest drawing-room where Mr. Hale received him with warmth. His host guided him to his wife, whose pallid countenance and shawl-clad silhouette offered a silent apology for her cool reception. Margaret was in the process of lighting the lamp as Mr. Thornton entered, the twilight already deepening outside. The flame cast a gentle glow across the center of the dim room, a space defined by rural habits that left the starry sky visible through the windows, blending the interior with the vast, outer darkness.

To Mr. Thornton, this room presented a stark contrast to the one he had just left. The latter was handsome and ponderous, devoid of any trace of feminine presence save for his mother seated therein, serving primarily as a hall for consumption rather than comfort. This room, however, was vastly finer—perhaps twenty times so—though lacking in physical comfort. There were no mirrors, not even a fragment of glass to catch and multiply the light, nor any gilding. Instead, it possessed a warm, sober breadth of color, beautifully accented by the faded, cherished chintz curtains and slipcovers of old Helstone. An open davenport sat by the window opposite the door; elsewhere stood a pedestal holding a tall white china vase, from which cascaded wreaths of English ivy, pale green birch, and copper-toned beech leaves. Baskets of needlework dotted the corners, while books lay scattered on a table, seemingly abandoned mid-read. Behind the door, another table was dressed for tea, draped in a crisp white cloth and laden with coconut cakes, a basket overflowing with oranges and ruddy American apples resting atop fresh leaves.

Mr. Thornton perceived that these graceful attentions were the habit of the household, particularly of Margaret. She stood beside the tea table in a light muslin gown touched with pink. Though she appeared oblivious to the conversation, her sole focus was the arrangement of the teacups, her round ivory hands moving with a dainty, noiseless precision. A bracelet adorned one slender arm, threatening to slide down over her rounded wrist. Mr. Thornton found himself watching the removal of this "troublesome" ornament with greater intensity than he paid heed to his host's words. It seemed to captivate him to observe her pushing it up impatiently until it constricted her soft flesh, followed immediately by the loosening and its subsequent fall. He nearly cried out, "There it goes again!"

With the preparations for tea complete, there was little left to occupy him, and he found himself regretting that the necessity of eating and drinking would soon interrupt his observation of Margaret. She served him his tea with the dignified air of an unwilling servant, yet her eyes betrayed her anticipation the moment he signaled readiness for another cup. He almost wished to request that she perform for him the task she was compelled to perform for her father, who currently held her little finger and thumb in his broad hand, forcing them to function as sugar tongs. Mr. Thornton witnessed Margaret lift her beautiful eyes to her father, filled with a mixture of light, laughter, and love, as this silent pantomime unfolded between them, unnoticed by either party.

Though Margaret's head still throbbed, evidenced by her pale complexion and silence, she resolved to bridge any awkward pauses rather than allow her father's guest to feel neglected. However, the conversation flowed uninterrupted. Once the tea service was cleared, Margaret retreated to a corner near her mother with her embroidery, allowing her thoughts to wander freely.

Mr. Thornton and Mr. Hale remained deeply engrossed in a topic begun during their previous meeting. Margaret was abruptly pulled back to the present by a trivial remark from her mother. Looking up from her work, she was struck by the profound divergence in appearance—and thus nature—between her father and Mr. Thornton. Her father was slight of build, appearing taller than he actually was when not juxtaposed with the tall, massive frame of the other man. The lines on his face were soft and wavering, traversed by a constant, tremulous movement that revealed every shifting emotion. His eyelids were large and arched, lending his eyes a languid, almost feminine beauty, while his finely arched brows were raised high by the weight of those dreamy lids.

In contrast, Mr. Thornton's brow was straight and fell low over clear, deep-set, earnest eyes. These eyes, though not sharply cutting, seemed intent on penetrating the very core of his subject. The lines on his face were few but firm, carved as if in marble, concentrated chiefly around lips that were slightly compressed. Beneath them lay teeth of such flawless whiteness that they suggested a sudden burst of sunlight. When a rare, bright smile flashed forth from his eyes, transforming his entire demeanor from severe resolution to keen, honest enjoyment, it was a joyfulness displayed with a fearless immediacy usually reserved for children. Margaret admired this smile; it was the first feature she had found attractive in her father's new friend. The opposition of their characters, evident in these physical details, seemed to illuminate the mutual attraction they clearly shared.

She quietly rearranged her mother's woolwork and sank into her own reverie, effectively forgetting her presence in the room entirely. Mr. Thornton was so thoroughly occupied explaining to Mr. Hale the majestic power yet delicate calibration of the steam-hammer—that it could stretch from earth to sky, filling the horizon, and then instantly compress into a vessel small enough for a child to carry—that he ignored her completely. He recounted how this realization of power emerged from a single man's mind in their town, capable of ascending step by step to ever-greater marvels. He declared his belief that many in their midst could step forward to sustain the struggle compelling all material power to bow to science.

"This imagination of power, this practical realization of a gigantic thought, sprang from one man's brain in our good town," Mr. Thornton asserted. "That very man possesses the capacity to climb, step by step, upon each wonder achieved, reaching higher marvels still. And I wager we have many among us who, were he gone, could spring into the breach and carry on the war that demands all material power yield to science."

"Your boast recalls the old lines: 'I've a hundred captains in England,' he said, 'As good as ever was he.'"

At his father's citation, Margaret looked up with inquisitive wonder. How had they drifted from cogwheels to *Chevy Chase*?

"It is no boast of mine," Mr. Thornton replied. "It is plain matter-of-fact. I will not deny that I am proud to belong to a town—or perhaps better, a district—whose necessities give birth to such grandeur of conception. I would sooner be a man toiling, suffering—even failing and unsuccessful—here, than lead a dull, prosperous life in the old, worn grooves of what you call more aristocratic society down in the South, with its slow days of careless ease. One may become clogged with honey and lose the ability to rise and fly."

"You are mistaken," Margaret interjected, stirred by the slight against her beloved South into a fervent defense that flushed her cheeks and brought angry tears to her eyes. "You know nothing of the South. If there is less adventure or less progress—if I must say less excitement—due to the gambling spirit of trade required to force out these wonders, there is also less suffering. I see men here walking the streets who look crushed by some pinching sorrow or care—not merely sufferers, but haters. In the South, we have our poor, but there is not that terrible expression of sullen injustice on their faces that I see here. You do not know the South, Mr. Thornton," she concluded, collapsing into a determined silence, furious with herself for speaking so freely.

"And may I say you do not know the North?" he asked, his tone possessing an inexpressible gentleness as he sensed he had wounded her.

She remained resolutely silent, yearning passionately for the lovely haunts she had left behind in Hampshire, feeling her voice would tremble if she attempted to speak.

"At any rate, Mr. Thornton," Mrs. Hale intervened, "you will admit that Milton is a much smokier, dirtier town than you will ever encounter in the South."

"I'm afraid I must concede its cleanliness," Mr. Thornton replied, flashing a quick, gleaming smile. "But we are commanded by Parliament to burn our own smoke; so I suppose, like good little children, we shall obey—eventually."

"But I recall you telling me you had altered your chimneys to consume the smoke, did you not?" Mr. Hale asked.

"My alterations were made by my own volition before Parliament interfered," Mr. Thornton explained. "It was an immediate expense, but it repaid itself in coal savings. I am uncertain whether I would have acted had I waited for the Act. At least, I would have waited to be informed against and fined, creating all the legal trouble possible. But all laws relying on informants and fines become inert due to the odiousness of their machinery.
