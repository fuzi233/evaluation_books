The evening was black as ink—no moon, no stars, only a heavy sky choked with rain-laden clouds that looked like smudged charcoal. Malone wasn’t the sort to notice such things. Nature’s moods passed him by without stirring a thought. He could walk for miles through April showers and never see the sun catch the hills in golden light or feel the hush of a soft rain washing over them. He didn’t pause to wonder where the stars had gone or mourn the absence of the clear, deep blue night he might have known on a colder, clearer evening. He simply trudged forward, head down, hat pulled back on his head in the way Irishmen often wore it, stepping steadily along the causeway—"tramp, tramp"—then splashing through muddy ruts where the paving stones gave way to wet earth—"splash, splash." His eyes were fixed only on landmarks: first, the spire of Briarfield Church; then, farther ahead, the flickering lights of Redhouse Inn.

He came close to it, and the sight of firelight glowing through a half-drawn curtain, glasses on a round table, and voices laughing from an oak settle nearly made him turn aside. A tumbler of whisky-and-water sounded tempting. But this wasn’t just any inn—it was Mr. Helstone’s parishioners gathered there. They knew him. He sighed, shook his head, and kept walking.

Now the highroad ended. To reach Hollow’s Mill, a shortcut across open fields would save time. These fields were flat and bare, offering no shelter or beauty. Malone cut straight through them, leaping over hedges and stone walls. He passed one building—a large, odd-shaped house, like a hall built without plan. There was a high gable, then a long front, then another low one, topped by a thick chimney stack. Trees stood behind it, but no light shone from any window. It was utterly still—the only sounds were the rain trickling from the eaves and the quiet whistle of wind around the chimneys and through the branches.

Beyond that, the land sloped sharply downward. Below lay a valley, where water murmured through the dark. One small light glimmered far off—his destination.

He reached a little white cottage, visible even in the dense gloom, and knocked. A young servant opened the door, holding a candle. The narrow passage led to a narrow staircase. Crimson baize doors and a strip of crimson carpet contrasted with pale walls and a white floor, making the place look clean and warm.

"Mr. Moore is at home, I suppose?"

"Yes, sir, but he’s not in."

"Not in? Where is he?"

"At the mill—in the counting-house."

Then one of the red doors opened.

"Are the wagons come, Sarah?" asked a woman’s voice—and a woman’s face appeared at the same moment. She wasn’t exactly beautiful—curl-paper wound around her temples spoiled any claim to goddess-like grace—but she wasn’t frightening either. Still, Malone flinched as if he’d seen something dreadful. Though big and sturdy, he stepped back into the rain, muttering, "I’ll go to him," and hurried down a short lane toward the looming shadow of the mill.

Work hours were over. The workers had gone. The machinery was silent. The mill was shut. Malone walked around its soot-blackened side until he found a crack of light. He knocked with the thick end of his shillelah, beating out a sharp rhythm.

A key turned. The door opened.

"Is it Joe Scott? What news of the wagons, Joe?"

"No," said Malone. "It's me. Mr. Helstone sent me."

"Oh! Mr. Malone." The voice held a faint note of disappointment. After a pause, it continued politely, though stiffly:
"I beg you will come in. I regret deeply that Mr. Helstone should have thought it necessary to send you so far. There was no need—I told him so—and on such a night. But please step inside."

Malone followed through a dim, indistinct room into a brighter one within. It seemed dazzling after the darkness outside—brightened by a good fire and a lamp with a vivid glow on the table. Yet the room itself was plain: wooden floor, no carpet; a few stiff green chairs that once belonged to a farm kitchen; a solid desk, a simple table, and framed plans on the walls—buildings, gardens, machines.

Plain, yes—but it suited Malone. Once he hung up his wet coat and hat, he dragged a creaky chair to the hearth and planted his knees almost inside the bars of the red-hot grate.

"Comfortable quarters you’ve got here, Mr. Moore. All snug to yourself."

"Yes, but my sister would be glad to see you, if you’d prefer coming into the house."

"Oh no!" Malone laughed. "Ladies are best left alone. I’m no lady’s man. You don’t mistake me for Sweeting, do you?"

"Sweeting? Which one? The gentleman in the chocolate coat—or the little one?"

"The little one—Nunnely’s boy. The cavalier of the Misses Sykes, who’s in love with all six of them, ha! ha!"

"Better to love them all than just one," Moore replied quietly.

"But he’s specially in love with one. When I and Donne pressed him to choose, he named—guess who?"

Moore smiled faintly. "Dora, of course. Or Harriet."

"Ha! Ha! You guessed well. But how did you know?"

"Because they’re the tallest, the handsomest—and Dora, at least, is the sturdiest. And since your friend Mr. Sweeting is slight and small, I assumed he'd prefer a contrast."

"You're right—Dora it is. But he has no chance, does he?"

"What does Mr. Sweeting have besides his curacy?"

That question made Malone burst out laughing—for three full minutes before he could speak.

"What does Sweeting have? Why, David has his harp—or flute, which is much the same. A pinchbeck watch, ditto ring, ditto eyeglass. That’s his fortune."

"How would he support Miss Sykes in gowns?"

"Ha! Ha! Excellent! I’ll ask him next time. I’ll roast him for his presumption. But surely he thinks old Christopher Sykes will help out. He’s rich, isn’t he? They live in a big house."

"Sykes runs a large business."

"So he must be wealthy?"

"He must have plenty to do with it. In these times, he’d be about as likely to pull money from his trade to give dowries as I’d be to tear down that cottage and build a mansion as big as Fieldhead."

"Do you know what I heard, Moore, the other day?"

"No. Perhaps that I *was* planning such a thing. Your Briarfield neighbors can say anything—sillier things, even."

"That you were going to lease Fieldhead—and marry a Miss Sykes there. Dora, I’m sure. You said she was the handsomest."

Moore sighed. "How many times have I been betrothed since I came to Briarfield? Every eligible woman in the district has been assigned to me in turn. First the two Misses Wynns—one dark, one fair; then Miss Armitage, red-haired; then Ann Pearson. Now it’s the whole clan of the Misses Sykes. On what grounds? Heaven knows. I visit no one. I avoid female society as thoroughly as you do, Mr. Malone. If I ever go to Whinbury, it’s only to call on Sykes or Pearson in their counting-houses—where we talk business, not marriage. We discuss cloth we can’t sell, hands we can’t employ, mills we can’t run. The world turns against us. Our minds are filled with troubles—not with courtships or dowries."

"I agree completely, Moore. If there’s one idea I hate more than any, it’s marriage—as people usually mean it. Two poor fools tying themselves together in some sentimental knot, pretending it’s love. Humbug! But a marriage based on dignity, mutual respect, lasting interests—that’s different."

"No," Moore answered, distracted. He seemed lost in thought. After a while, he stared into the fire, then suddenly turned.

"Hark! Did you hear wheels?"

He rose, went to the window, opened it, listened. Then closed it again.

"Just the wind rising. The stream’s swollen, rushing down the hollow. I expected the wagons at six. It’s nearly nine now."

"Seriously," Malone asked, "do you think installing this new machinery puts you in danger? Helstone seems to think so."

"I only wish the frames were safely inside. Once they’re up, I defy anyone. Let the frame-breakers come. My mill is my castle."

"One despises such scoundrels," Malone said solemnly. "I almost wish a gang would show up tonight. But the road seemed quiet when I came. Saw nothing moving."

"You came by Redhouse?"

"Yes."

"There’d be nothing there. The risk lies toward Stilbro’."

"And you think there’s real danger?"

"They’ve done it to others. They may do it to me. The difference is—most manufacturers crumble when attacked. Take Sykes: when his dressing-shop burned, when cloth was torn from his tenters and left in ruins, he did nothing. Gave up like a rabbit under a ferret. But I—well, if I know myself—I’ll stand by my mill, my trade, my machinery."

"Helstone says these three are your gods. That 'Orders in Council' are your seven deadly sins. Castlereagh your Antichrist, and the war-party his legions."

"Yes. I hate them because they ruin me. They block my path. I can’t move forward. I can’t carry out my plans. At every turn, I’m thwarted by their effects."

"But you’re rich and thriving, Moore?"

"Rich in cloth I can’t sell. Come see my warehouse—stacked to the roof. Roakes and Pearson are the same. America used to take their goods. But the Orders in Council cut that off."

Malone yawned, knocking his boots together.

"And yet," Moore went on, unaware of his guest’s boredom, "they keep pestering me about marriage! As if life were only about paying attention to some young lady, then marrying her, taking a honeymoon tour, visiting relatives, and starting a family. Oh, que le diable emporte!" He broke off abruptly, then added calmer, "I believe women only talk about such things. So naturally, they assume men do too."

"Of course," Malone agreed. "But never mind them." He whistled, glanced around impatiently, clearly wanting something.

This time Moore noticed.

"Mr. Malone," he said, "you must be hungry after that walk. I’ve forgotten hospitality."

"Not at all," Malone replied—but his eyes lit up. Moore rose, opened a cupboard.

"I like having everything ready," he said. "No need to depend on the women in the cottage for food or drink. I often spend evenings here alone, sleep with Joe Scott in the mill. Sometimes I’m my own watchman. I don’t sleep much. On fine nights, I wander with my musket around the hollow. Mr. Malone—can you cook a mutton chop?"

"Try me. I’ve done it hundreds of times at college."

"There’s a dishful. And the gridiron. Turn them quickly. Know how to keep the juices in?"

"Never fear. Hand me a knife and fork."

Malone rolled up his sleeves and set to work with energy. Moore placed plates, bread, a black bottle, and two tumblers on the table. Then he took out a small copper kettle, filled it from a stone jar, set it beside the sizzling gridiron, fetched lemons, sugar, and a china punch bowl. While brewing, a knock came.

"Is it you, Sarah?"

"Yes, sir. Will you come to supper?"

"No. I won’t be in tonight. I’ll sleep in the mill. Lock the doors. Tell your mistress to go to bed."

He returned.

"You’ve your household in order," Malone said, cheeks flushed from the heat, turning the chops with care. "You’re not under petticoat rule, like poor Sweeting—whew! How the fat spits! Burnt my hand—there’s a fine brown one for you, full of gravy. You and I, Moore—we’ll have no gray mares in our stables when we marry."

"I don’t know," Moore said. "If the gray mare is handsome and gentle, why not?"

"The chops are done. Is the punch ready?"

"There’s a glass. Taste it. If Joe and his crew return with the frames intact, they’ll share this."

Malone grew loud and cheerful over the meal. He laughed at trifles, made jokes and laughed at them himself, growing boisterous. Moore remained quiet, absorbed.

Reader, it’s time you saw the man who sat opposite him.

At first glance, Moore looked unusual—thin, dark, sallow, with foreign features and hair falling carelessly across his forehead. He paid little attention to grooming. His face, though finely shaped with southern symmetry, was marred by a look of worry and weariness. His eyes were large, gray, grave—thoughtful rather than warm, searching rather than kind. When he smiled, it softened his expression slightly—not cheerfully, but with a quiet charm that hinted at patience, kindness, perhaps loyalty. He was young—no more than thirty—tall and slender. His speech grated, with a foreign accent that clung despite his careful diction. It unsettled Yorkshire ears.

For Moore was not fully English. His mother was foreign-born. He was born abroad, raised partly overseas. A hybrid, perhaps, in nature—he felt alien to parties, sects, customs. He likely preferred isolation, focused solely on Robert Gérard Moore’s success, not on broader causes. Trade ran in his blood—the Gérards of Antwerp had been merchants for centuries. Once wealthy, they fell through bad luck and war. The French Revolution shattered them. With them collapsed the English branch of Moore, tied to the Antwerp firm. Robert Moore, son of the Antwerp partner, married Hortense Gérard, hoping to inherit her father’s share. He inherited only debts. Though creditors settled, some believed Robert accepted those debts as legacy, vowing one day to repay them and rebuild the house of Gérard and Moore to its former glory. Others said he carried childhood grief and adult hardship deep in his soul.

Yet he lacked the means to act grandly. When he came to Yorkshire, his ancestors’ grand estates were gone. He rented a dilapidated cloth-mill in a remote corner, a cottage nearby, and a few rough acres for pasture and cloth-tenters—all from the trustees of Fieldhead estate, then owned by a minor.

In two years, he’d shown energy. The dingy cottage became neat and tasteful. He turned part of the rugged land into a garden, cultivated with Flemish precision. The mill, old and outdated, he despised from the start. He reformed it slowly, limited by funds. Poverty frustrated him. “Forward” was his creed—but he was held back.

So he didn’t stop to consider how his progress hurt others. Not being native or long-settled, he didn’t care when new machines displaced workers. He never asked where the unemployed would eat. And in that, he was no different from thousands who ignored the starving poor of Yorkshire.

The time was bleak in British history—war at its peak, Europe engulfed. England, though not broken, was worn. Many begged for peace, no matter the cost. National honor meant little when hunger gnawed. For a bite of meat, they’d sell their birthright.
