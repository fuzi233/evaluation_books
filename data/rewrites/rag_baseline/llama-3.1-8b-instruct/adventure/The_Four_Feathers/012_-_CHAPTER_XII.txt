In the velvety darkness of a May night, three officers congregated on a grassy knoll overlooking the Nile, their smoke curling upwards like wisps of incense. The moon hung full in the sky, its luminescence washing over the landscape with an ethereal glow, rendering the planets mere specters. The stars, shrouded in a veil of silver, twinkled with a soft intensity, their gentle sparkle like the first hints of morning dew on a verdant meadow. Beneath them, the Nile flowed sluggishly, its tranquil surface reflecting the starry expanse above like a polished mirror. From the opposite bank, the desert stretched out, an endless expanse of white sand, dotted with hummocks that shimmered like diamonds scattered across a velvet cloth. The mess-room veranda cast a dark silhouette behind the officers, a stark contrast to the vibrant tapestry of night unfolding before them.

As one of the officers struck a match to light his cigar, the flame danced in the darkness, casting an otherworldly glow on a face etched with worry. "I fear the worst," he said, exhaling a plume of smoke, "though I dare not voice my doubts aloud." The speaker was Colonel Dawson, a man of middle age and seasoned commander, his eyes haunted by the prospect of loss.

His words were met with a somber nod from Lieutenant Calder, a man whose hair had begun to gray, but whose unlined face spoke of a vitality that belied his years. Calder's voice was tinged with a hint of trepidation as he spoke. "He departed eight weeks ago, sir. I fear we may have reason to worry."

Major Walters, the third officer, offered a counterpoint to the prevailing mood. "We mustn't jump to conclusions just yet, gentlemen. Durrance is no stranger to the wilderness. Give him a campfire and a pair of sheiks to share it with, and he'll spin tales for hours on end. He's a man of simple tastes, after all."

Colonel Dawson's expression remained skeptical, his eyes clouded by doubt. "He's three weeks past due, Major. And Durrance, as you know, is a man of habit. I fear for the worst."
