Spring arrived, bringing a deceptive bloom to the English countryside. Fields turned green, hills refreshed, and gardens burst into flower, yet beneath this verdant skin, the nation remained unchanged in its suffering. Poverty persisted among the poor, and hardship weighed heavily on employers. While commerce faltered under the shadow of continuing war—England's blood spilled and wealth squandered for seemingly futile ends—the occasional news of victories in the Peninsula arrived too slowly to offer comfort. Instead, long silences were broken only by Bonaparte's arrogant boasts of triumph. For those bearing the brunt of the conflict, this protracted struggle against an apparently invincible foe felt tedious and hopeless. Desperation drove men like Yorke and Moore, along with countless others teetering on the brink of bankruptcy, to demand peace on any terms whatsoever. They organized meetings, delivered impassioned speeches, and circulated petitions, caring little for the conditions attached to such a boon.

Human nature, whether viewed individually or collectively, leans toward selfishness; the mercantile class exemplifies this trait with striking clarity. These merchants prioritize profit above all national concerns, viewing England's interests solely as extensions of their own wallets. Chivalry, disinterest, and honor have largely died within their hearts. Under their rule, a nation might submit ignominiously—not out of Christian principle, but driven by the greed of Mammon. During recent conflicts, English traders would have endured insults from the French, offering cloak after cloak to Napoleon until only a single garment remained, worn solely to protect the purse within its pocket. Only when that beloved purse was seized would they transform into fierce defenders, biting at the thief's throat until the treasure was restored. Yet, when speaking against war, they claim to despise it for its brutality, presenting themselves as uniquely civilized and kind. In reality, many are narrow-minded and cold-hearted, harboring no affection for anyone outside their own trade, viewing others as burdensome parasites who deserve neither air nor sustenance. To them, anyone not involved in commerce eats the bread of idleness. Long may it be before England truly becomes a nation of shopkeepers!

We have established that Sir Walter Moore was no self-sacrificing patriot, guided instead by a keen focus on personal advancement. Consequently, when pushed once again toward financial ruin, he fought fiercely against forces threatening his stability. While he stirred anti-war sentiment in the North and leveraged the influence of wealthier allies, he occasionally sensed the irrationality of his party's demands. Hearing of Europe arming against Bonaparte, witnessing Russia rise to defend her frozen lands and serfdom from foreign tyranny, Moore realized that a free nation like England could not simply concede to an unjust aggressor. Furthermore, reports of Lord Wellington's steady, unyielding progress in the Peninsula convinced him that a genuine, enduring power resided within the British army—a force destined eventually to secure victory. However, that "end" felt impossibly distant. Meanwhile, as an individual, Moore faced imminent destruction; his hopes were grinding to dust. His priority shifted entirely to self-preservation and fulfilling his own destiny.

His vigorous pursuit of this goal led to a definitive break with his former Tory friend, the rector, Mr. Helstone. Their dispute erupted at a public meeting and escalated into scathing newspaper exchanges. Mr. Helstone denounced Moore as a Jacobin, severed all contact, and forbade his niece, Caroline, from visiting Hollow's Cottage or receiving French lessons. He dismissed the language as frivolous and harmful to female minds, questioning who first made it fashionable to teach women such nonsense. He declared Caroline's cousins dangerous influences and ordered her immediate cessation of correspondence with them.

Mr. Helstone anticipated resistance, expecting tears. Yet, he had noticed subtle differences in Caroline's behavior depending on her guests. When the lively Malone visited, engaging in boisterous antics with the household cat or firing guns in the garden, Caroline would vanish upstairs, remaining hidden until summoned for supper. Conversely, when Robert Moore stayed, despite his quiet demeanor and lack of loud entertainment, Caroline remained seated in the room, finding profound contentment in stitching pin-cushions and knitting missionary socks.

Helstone, sharp-eyed and alert, had witnessed them bidding farewell once. It was a fleeting exchange of glances—clear and gentle in Moore's eyes, manly and searching in Caroline's. Neither displayed deceit, yet the interaction unsettled him. He imagined whipping Moore and boxing Caroline's ears, storming against the folly of flirtation under his roof. Political disagreements compounded these private suspicions, solidifying his resolve to separate the cousins.

One evening, as Caroline worked near the drawing-room window, Helstone delivered his decree. He noted her pallor and silence, observing that Robert Moore's name had vanished from her speech and his presence from the rectory for weeks. Suspecting clandestine meetings, he commanded her to stop visiting Hollow's Cottage and cut off all communication with her cousin Hortense, citing the family's Jacobinical principles.

"Yes, uncle," Caroline replied quietly.

There was no flush of anger, no gathering tears. The thoughtful shadow that had settled over her features remained undisturbed. She obeyed perfectly, not out of fear, but because the command aligned with her own judgment. Going to Hollow's Cottage had become painful; hope and love had abandoned that small cottage, for Robert seemed to have deserted it. Whenever she dared ask after him, the answer was invariably that he was away or consumed by business. Hortense worried he was destroying himself with overwork; he barely ate at home, living instead in his counting-house.

Church offered the only chance to see him, yet she rarely looked. To gaze upon him was both excruciating pain and overwhelming pleasure, an emotion she knew was ultimately wasted. Once, on a bleak, rainy Sunday, she caught sight of him alone in his pew. Dressed in sombre olive tones, he sat with folded arms, lost in deep abstraction. Instinctively, Caroline understood that his thoughts were miles away, wrapped in complexities of responsibility and duty that excluded her entirely.

She pondered his condition, speculating on his fears and the mysteries of "business." She strove to see things as they were, rejecting romance for truth. "Different indeed," she concluded, "is Robert's mental state from mine. I think only of him; he has no room for me. Love has been the dominant emotion of my heart for two years, yet his faculties are absorbed by other pursuits. He rises now, leaving the church. Will he turn his head? No. He offers not one look. That is cruel. A kind glance would have sustained me until tomorrow. Grief chokes me because another human being's eye refused to meet mine."

That evening, after Malone departed and the study bell rang for prayers, Caroline retreated to her chamber. Alone with Fanny, who had lit a small fire against the chill, Caroline paced silently. The house was mute; the servants read devotional books in the kitchen, and the study door dampened the voices of the gentlemen. Caroline opened a theological volume but found it unreadable, her mind too turbulent to follow another's logic.

Instead, her imagination filled with images of Moore: winter firesides, summer afternoons in Nunnely Wood, spring mornings listening to cuckoos in Hollow's Copse, and autumn evenings sharing baskets of blackberries. He fed her berries like a parent feeding a fledgling. But these visions were hollow. The pictures faded, the voice silenced, and the warmth of his touch dissolved into the chill of reality. Returning from her enchanted realm to the present, she stared at her own pale reflection on the wall.

"I have to live, perhaps, until seventy," she murmured. "With good health, half a century may lie ahead. How shall I occupy it? What shall I do to fill the time between me and the grave?"

Reflecting further, she realized, "I shall not be married. Since Robert does not care for me, I will likely never have a loving husband or children. I assumed marriage was my inevitable destiny, but now I see clearly I may be mistaken. I will probably remain an old maid. I will watch Robert marry someone wealthy while I stand alone. What was I created for? Where is my place in the world?"

Suddenly, a solution emerged. "Ah! I see," she continued. "That is the puzzle old maids face. Others tell us our place is to do good—to be helpful whenever needed. This is partly true, a convenient doctrine for those who preach it. Yet, I suspect some claim others should serve them, repaid only with praise, calling such devotion virtue. Is this enough? Is it truly living? Isn't there a terrible hollowness in giving oneself away to others when there is nothing of one's own to bestow? Does virtue lie in self-abnegation? I doubt it. Excessive humility breeds tyranny; weak concession fosters selfishness. The Roman Catholic Church, for instance, teaches renunciation and submission, yet produces so many grasping tyrants among its priests. Every human being deserves their share of rights. Perhaps happiness lies in knowing one's allotment and holding to it firmly. These are strange thoughts. Are they right? I am unsure."

Life, she mused, is brief anyway. Seventy years pass like a vapor or a dream. All paths lead to the grave, a small hole in the earth where the mighty husbandman deposits the seed of humanity. The body decays and returns to the soil, while the soul ascends, folding its wings at the edge of the fiery sea to gaze upon the vision of the Trinity. Words like these attempt to describe the indescribable. Who can truly guess the soul's eternal destination?
