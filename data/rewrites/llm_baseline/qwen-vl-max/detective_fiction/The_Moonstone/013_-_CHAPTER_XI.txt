When the last of the guests had departed, I returned to the inner hall and found Samuel stationed at the side table, tending to the brandy and soda water. My lady and Miss Rachel emerged from the drawing-room, followed by the two gentlemen. Mr. Godfrey accepted a glass of brandy and soda; Mr. Franklin declined anything. He sat down, looking utterly drained—no doubt the evening’s festivities had worn him out.

My lady turned to bid them farewell, her gaze lingering on the glittering diamond in her daughter’s dress—the very stone that had once belonged to the cursed Indian temple.

“Rachel,” she asked quietly, “where are you planning to keep the Diamond tonight?”

Miss Rachel was in high spirits, caught in that playful, almost defiant mood young women sometimes fall into after an exciting day—when nonsense becomes a kind of art. First, she claimed she didn’t know where to put it. Then she declared, with mock seriousness, “On my dressing-table, of course—alongside all my other trinkets.” But then she remembered: what if the Diamond began to glow on its own? What if its eerie moonlight lit up the room in the dead of night and frightened her half to death?

Then, suddenly inspired, she recalled the Indian cabinet in her sitting-room. “Ah!” she exclaimed, “I’ll put the Indian diamond in the Indian cabinet—so they can admire each other!”

She let this absurd idea run its course, but before she could go further, her mother interrupted.

“My dear,” said my lady, “your Indian cabinet has no lock.”

“Good heavens, mamma!” Miss Rachel protested. “Is this a hotel? Are there thieves lurking in our own house?”

Ignoring the remark, my lady wished the gentlemen goodnight, then turned to her daughter and kissed her forehead. “Why not let *me* keep the Diamond for you tonight?” she suggested.

Miss Rachel reacted as though someone had proposed taking away her favorite doll. My lady saw at once that reason would get nowhere. “Come to my room first thing tomorrow morning,” she said. “I have something important to discuss with you.” With that, she left us slowly, lost in thought—and clearly unsettled by the path her reflections were leading her down.

Next, Miss Rachel said goodnight. She shook hands with Mr. Godfrey, who stood near a painting at the far end of the hall. Then she turned toward Mr. Franklin, still seated in silence, weary and withdrawn.

What passed between them, I cannot say. But standing beside the old oak frame that held our large mirror, I saw her reflection—how she slipped the locket Mr. Franklin had given her from beneath her dress, held it up for a moment with a smile that carried more meaning than mere amusement, and then darted off to bed.

That small act struck me deeply. It made me question my own judgment. Suddenly, Penelope’s suspicions about Miss Rachel’s feelings seemed less like idle gossip and more like something worth considering.

As soon as Miss Rachel was gone, Mr. Franklin noticed me. His usual fluctuating temper had shifted again—this time toward caution.

“Betteredge,” he said, “I’m beginning to think I took Mr. Murthwaite too seriously when we spoke in the shrubbery. Do you suppose he’s been spinning tales about his travels just to frighten us? And do you really mean to release the dogs?”

“I’ll remove their collars, sir,” I replied, “and let them roam freely if they sense anything amiss.”

“All right,” said Mr. Franklin. “We’ll decide what to do tomorrow. I don’t intend to alarm my aunt unless absolutely necessary. Goodnight.”

He looked pale and exhausted as he nodded to me and headed upstairs with his candle. I ventured to suggest a drop of brandy-and-water before bed. Mr. Godfrey, walking over from the other end of the hall, agreed. He urged Mr. Franklin, in the kindest way possible, to accept something warm before retiring.

I mention these small details because, after everything I’d seen and heard that day, it pleased me to note that the two gentlemen remained on cordial terms. Their earlier exchange of sharp words—overheard by Penelope in the drawing-room—and their rivalry for Miss Rachel’s favor had not caused any real rift between them. They were both level-headed men of the world, and there’s certainly this advantage to people of position: they’re rarely as prone to quarrel as those without it.

Mr. Franklin refused the drink and went upstairs with Mr. Godfrey, whose room was next door. But on the landing, either Mr. Godfrey persuaded him or he changed his mind—again. “Perhaps I’ll need it later,” he called down. “Send some brandy-and-water up to my room.”

I sent Samuel with the drink, then went outside to unfasten the dogs’ collars. Both animals were stunned by the sudden freedom—they leapt at me like puppies. But the rain soon cooled them. Each drank a little water and retreated back into their kennels. As I re-entered the house, I observed signs in the sky hinting at better weather ahead. For now, however, the storm raged on, drenching the ground until it was soaked through.

Samuel and I inspected every corner of the house, locking and securing everything as usual. This time, I checked everything myself—I trusted nothing to my deputy. By midnight, I was finally able to rest.

The strain of the day had taken its toll. I suppose I felt the effects of Mr. Franklin’s exhaustion too. It wasn’t until sunrise that I finally drifted into sleep. The whole night, the house had been silent as a tomb—only the rain splashing and the wind sighing among the trees broke the quiet.

Around half-past seven, I woke and opened my window to find a bright, sunny morning. The clock struck eight. I was about to go out and chain the dogs again when I heard a sudden rustling of petticoats behind me on the stairs.

I turned—and there was Penelope, racing down like a whirlwind. “Father!” she shrieked. “Come upstairs—quick! *The Diamond is gone!*”

“Are you mad?” I asked.

“Gone!” she repeated. “Vanished—no one knows how! Come see!”

She dragged me into Miss Rachel’s sitting-room, which opened directly into her bedroom. There stood Miss Rachel herself, nearly as pale as the white dressing-gown she wore. The doors of the Indian cabinet stood wide open. One drawer was pulled out completely.

“Look!” Penelope insisted. “I saw her put the Diamond in that drawer last night.” I approached the cabinet. The drawer was empty.

“Is this true, miss?” I asked.

With a face and voice unlike her own, Miss Rachel answered exactly as Penelope had:

“The Diamond is gone!”

After saying that, she stepped into her bedroom and shut and locked the door.

Before we could react, my lady arrived, drawn by my voice and alarmed by the commotion. The news of the theft seemed to freeze her. She rushed straight to Miss Rachel’s bedroom and demanded entry. Miss Rachel admitted her.

The alarm spread like wildfire through the house. Soon, the two gentlemen were alerted.

Mr. Godfrey came out first. At the news, he simply raised his hands in bewilderment—a poor show of strength under pressure. Mr. Franklin, whom I had counted on for clarity and direction, appeared equally helpless at first. Yet he had slept well—unusually so—and the rest had, as he confessed, left him groggy. But once he’d finished his coffee—taken early, as he always did, in the continental fashion—his mind cleared. The rational side of him resurfaced, and he took charge with calm determination.

He summoned the servants and instructed them to leave all lower doors and windows untouched—except the front door, which I had opened. He then suggested checking whether the Diamond might have fallen somewhere unseen—perhaps behind the cabinet or beneath the table it stood upon. We searched thoroughly. Nothing. We questioned Penelope again—she revealed nothing new.

Then Mr. Franklin proposed speaking to Miss Rachel. He sent Penelope to knock at her bedroom door.

My lady answered. She closed the door behind her. Moments later, we heard it lock from inside. When she emerged, she looked deeply troubled. “Rachel is completely overwhelmed by this,” she told Mr. Franklin. “She refuses even to speak to me. I fear she may be too upset to see anyone.”

Having added to our confusion, my lady regained her composure and acted decisively.

“I suppose there’s no choice?” she said calmly. “Must I send for the police?”

“And the first thing they’ll do,” Mr. Franklin interjected, “is arrest the Indian jugglers who performed here last night.”

My lady and Mr. Godfrey both started—neither knew of the suspicion Mr. Franklin and I harbored.

“I can’t explain now,” Mr. Franklin continued. “But I’m certain the Indians stole the Diamond. Give me a letter of introduction to one of the magistrates in Frizinghall—just stating that I represent your interests—and let me ride off immediately. Every minute counts.”

(Nota bene: Whether it was the French or English side of him, the rational part of Mr. Franklin was now firmly in control. The only question was: How long would it last?)

He placed pen, ink, and paper before his aunt. She wrote the letter reluctantly. If it had been possible to ignore such a loss—especially of a jewel worth twenty thousand pounds—I suspect she might have welcomed the chance to let the thieves escape unscathed. Her distrust of her brother’s legacy ran deep.

I accompanied Mr. Franklin to the stables and used the opportunity to ask how the Indians could possibly have entered the house.

“One of them might have slipped into the hall during the chaos as guests left,” he said. “He could have hidden under the sofa while my aunt and Rachel discussed where to keep the Diamond. Once the house grew quiet, he’d have had free access to the cabinet.”

With that, he called to the groom, opened the gate, and galloped off.

It seemed the most logical explanation. But how had the thief escaped? I had found the front door locked and bolted, just as I’d left it. All other doors and windows remained secure. And the dogs? If the thief had jumped from an upper window, how had he avoided them? Had he brought drugged meat? As I pondered this, the dogs themselves burst around the corner, rolling playfully in the wet grass, full of life and energy. I struggled to calm them and chain them back. The more I thought about it, the less convincing Mr. Franklin’s theory became.

Breakfast was served—no matter what happens in a house, breakfast must be had. Afterward, my lady summoned me. I had no choice but to reveal everything I’d kept hidden—the Indians’ plot, their intentions, their threats. Though startled, she recovered quickly. Her concern was less for the stolen gem and more for her daughter.

“You know how peculiar Rachel can be,” she said. “But I’ve never seen her like this. The loss of her jewel seems to have unhinged her. Who would have thought that cursed Diamond could hold such power over her so quickly?”

Indeed, it was strange. Miss Rachel was never overly fond of toys or trinkets. Yet now she remained locked in her room, inconsolable. And she wasn’t alone. Mr. Godfrey, usually so composed, wandered aimlessly through the house and gardens, unsure of his role. Should he leave, sparing the family the burden of his presence? Or stay, hoping his modest assistance might help? He chose to stay—but the strain showed. Circumstances test character. Mr. Godfrey, tested, proved weaker than I’d believed. The female servants—except Rosanna Spearman, who kept to herself—began whispering and staring nervously, as women often do when faced with mystery. Even I felt restless, irritable. The Moonstone had turned us all topsy-turvy.

Just before eleven, Mr. Franklin returned. The strong side of him had evidently collapsed under the weight of events. He had left at a gallop, but returned at a walk. Gone was the iron resolve; now he seemed stuffed with cotton, limp and defeated.

“Well,” my lady asked, “are the police coming?”

“Yes,” he replied. “They’ll follow me in a fly. Superintendent Seegrave and two officers. A mere formality. The case is hopeless.”

“What?” I asked. “Have the Indians fled?”

“The poor, innocent Indians have been unjustly imprisoned,” Mr. Franklin said. “They’re as guiltless as newborn babes. My theory—that one of them hid in the house—has burned to ashes like all the others. It’s been proven impossible.”

After shocking us with this reversal, he sat down at my lady’s request and explained.

He had reached Frizinghall and laid out the facts clearly to the magistrate, who immediately summoned the police. Inquiry revealed the Indians had never attempted to leave town. Further questioning confirmed all three had returned to Frizinghall between ten and eleven, walking straight back after their performance. Later, at midnight, police searching their lodging house saw them all present, along with their boy. I myself had secured the house safely at midnight. The evidence was undeniable: the Indians couldn’t have done it.

The magistrate ruled there was no suspicion against them—but, since the investigation might yet uncover something, he committed them as rogues and vagabonds, holding them under lock and key for a week. They had unknowingly broken a minor law—enough to justify the action. Institutions stretch, if pulled gently. The magistrate was a friend of my lady’s, and so the Indians were detained.

Thus ended the Indian clue. If they were innocent, then who had taken the Moonstone?

Ten minutes later, Superintendent Seegrave arrived. He reported passing Mr. Franklin on the terrace, lounging in the sun (probably with the Italian side of him dominant), warning the police that the case was hopeless—before the investigation had even begun.

For a household in distress, Superintendent Seegrave was a welcome sight. Tall, broad-shouldered, and military in bearing, he had a commanding voice, a firm gaze, and a frock coat that buttoned neatly to his leather stock. “I’m the man you want,” his entire appearance declared. He ordered his two subordinates with stern authority, leaving no doubt that he meant business.

He began by inspecting the entire property—inside and out. His conclusion: no one had broken in. The theft must have come from within the house.

Imagine the servants’ panic when they learned this. The Superintendent decided to examine Miss Rachel’s boudoir first, then question the staff. Meanwhile, he posted one officer at the staircase leading to the servants’ quarters, forbidding anyone passage.

At this, the female servants erupted. They surged from their corners, raced upstairs, burst into the room, and surrounded the Superintendent, all looking equally guilty, demanding to know who he suspected.

He met the chaos with calm authority. He fixed them with his steady gaze and barked, “Now, then, you women—downstairs, every one! I won’t have you here. Look!” He pointed to a smudge on the edge of Miss Rachel’s door, just below the lock. “See what mischief your petticoats have already caused! Clear out!”

Rosanna Spearman, nearest to him, obeyed instantly. The others followed. He completed his search, found nothing, and asked me who had discovered the theft. Penelope had. She was summoned.

The Superintendent began sharply. “Now, young woman, listen carefully. Speak the truth.”

Penelope flared up. “I’ve never been taught to lie, Mr. Policeman! And if father can stand there and hear me accused of stealing, with my own bedroom locked against me, my name ruined—then he’s not the father I thought he was!”

A word from me calmed the situation. Questions followed smoothly. Penelope confirmed she’d seen Miss Rachel place the Diamond in the drawer. She’d entered at eight with tea and found the drawer open and empty. That was all.

Next, the Superintendent requested to see Miss Rachel. Penelope relayed the request. The reply came back: “I have nothing to tell the policeman. I will not see anyone.”

The officer looked both surprised and offended. I explained she was ill and begged for patience. We descended, meeting Mr. Godfrey and Mr. Franklin crossing the hall.

The two gentlemen, being residents, were questioned. Neither had heard anything unusual. Had they heard noises? Only the rain. Had I, awake longer than either, heard anything? Nothing.

Released, Mr. Franklin whispered, “That man will be useless. Seegrave is an ass.”

Mr. Godfrey whispered back, “Clearly a capable man. Betteredge, I have great faith in him.”

Many men, many opinions—an old saying, indeed.

The Superintendent returned to the boudoir, with Penelope and me following. He wanted to check if any furniture had been moved during the night.

While we were examining chairs and tables, the bedroom door opened. Miss Rachel, who had refused everyone, stepped into the room on her own. She picked up her garden hat from a chair and walked directly to Penelope.

“Bring me my parasol,” she said.
