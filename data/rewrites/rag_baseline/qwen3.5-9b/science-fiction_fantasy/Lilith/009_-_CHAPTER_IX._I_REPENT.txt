I laid the manuscript down, a gesture of surrender born not from exhaustion, but from a sudden, clarifying shame. Finding that my father had glimpsed that mysterious realm—and knowing he recognized Mr. Raven—forced a brutal recalibration of my own narrative. The absence of news regarding his death ceased to be a mystery; it became a tactical error on my part. I had assumed he had simply followed the Raven, failed to return, and thus vanished into the void. This realization struck me with the force of a physical blow: I had fled. I had run from the very sanctuary I claimed to seek.

The cognitive dissonance was intolerable. What profound truths regarding mortality and the dimensions beyond ordinary perception might I have already uncovered within the Ravens' domain! They were undoubtedly good people, their household a fortress of integrity. A single night spent under their roof would have cost me nothing, yet I had treated them as conspirators, unworthy of my trust, harboring designs against me. The more I dissected my behavior, the more repulsed I became by my own cowardice. Why fear the dead? To share their holy rest was an honor I had proven myself unfit to receive. What harm could a sleeping king or a lady with a wound in her palm inflict upon me?

A profound longing seized me for the sweet, stately stillness of their countenances. Tears welled, hot and involuntary, blurring the page. I collapsed onto a nearby couch, the grief washing over me, and succumbed instantly to sleep.

Awakening was abrupt, triggered by a sensation akin to a distant summons. The house sat silent, quiet as an empty church. Outside, a blackbird sang on the lawn, its melody sharp against the encroaching twilight.

"I must go," I told myself, the resolve cutting through the fog of regret. "I will confess my shame and submit to whatever command they issue."

I rose and ascended the stairs directly to the garret. The wooden chamber remained unchanged, the mirror dimly reflecting the dust motes dancing in the stagnant air. It was nearly noon; the sun would sit slightly higher than when I first arrived. I needed to raise the hood incrementally and realign the mirrors. If only I had been present to witness Mr. Raven perform the adjustment!

I pulled the chains, allowing the light to cascade onto the primary mirror. Turning to the secondary pane, I saw the shapes of the former vision—they were distinguishable, yet tremulous, like a landscape distorted in a pool disturbed by a gentle breeze. I touched the glass; it was impermeable, a solid barrier between worlds. Suspecting polarization as the requisite variable, I shifted the mirrors, altering their angular relationship. After numerous adjustments, chance finally aligned the optics correctly. Through the fractured prism, the mountains appeared blue, steady, and crystal clear.

I stepped forward, and my boots found purchase on heather. All I recalled of the path to the cottage was a passage through a pine forest. I navigated thickets and small fir-woods, constantly hallucinating recognition of the terrain, yet no forest existed here. The sun dipped toward the horizon, and the air grew chilling with the onset of winter. Then, to my delight, I spotted a small black object approaching. It was the Raven.

"Hastening to meet you," I said, hurrying to intercept him. "I beg your pardon, sir, for my rudeness last night. Will you take me with you now? I heartily confess I do not deserve it."

"Ah!" he replied, tilting his head upward. After a brief pause, he spoke. "My wife does not expect you tonight. She regrets that we ever encouraged your stay last week."

"Take me to her," I pleaded humbly. "Let me tell her how sorry I am."

"It is of no use," he answered flatly. "Your night had not come then, or you would not have left us. It is not come now, and I cannot show you the way. The dead were rejoicing under their daisies—all lying among the roots of the flowers of heaven—at the thought of your delight when winter passes and the morning birds arrive. Ere you departed, they shivered in their beds. When the spring of the universe arrives... but that cannot be for ages yet. How many? I do not know. Nor do I care to know."

"Tell me one thing, I beg of you, Mr. Raven: is my father with you? Have you seen him since he left the world?"

"Yes; he is with us, fast asleep. That was the figure you saw with his arm on the coverlet, his hand half-closed."

"Why did you not tell me? I should have been so near him, and not known!"

"And turn your back on him!" the Raven corrected sharply. "I would have lain down at once had I known!"

"I doubt it. Had you been ready to lie down, you would have known him—" Old Sir Up'ard, he continued, referencing my lineage. "Both your grandfather and your twice-great-grandfather are up and away long ago. Your great-grandfather has been with us for many years; I think he will soon begin to stir. You saw him last night, though of course you did not recognize him."

"Why OF COURSE?" I demanded.

"Because he is so much nearer waking than you. No one who refuses to sleep can ever wake."

"I do not at all understand you!"

"You turned away, and chose not to understand!"

I held my peace. Silence threatened to drive him off.

"And my grandfather—is he also with you?" I asked.

"No; he remains in the Evil Wood, fighting the dead."

"Where is the Evil Wood? Let me find him."

"You will not find it; but you will hardly miss the wood. It is the place where those who refuse to sleep wake up at night to kill their dead and bury them."

"I cannot understand you!"

"Naturally not. Neither do I understand you; I can read neither your heart nor your face. When my wife and I fail to comprehend our children, it is because there is insufficient substance in them to be understood. God alone can understand foolishness."

"Then," I said, feeling stripped bare and utterly worthless, "will you be so good as to show me the nearest way home? There are surely multiple routes, for I have traversed two already."

"There are indeed many ways."

"Tell me, please, how to recognize the nearest."

"I cannot," the Raven answered. "You and I employ the same words with divergent meanings. We are frequently unable to communicate what someone NEEDS to know because they WANT to know something else, thereby ensuring total misunderstanding. Home is perpetually distant, located in the palm of your hand. Telling you how to reach it is futile. Yet you will get there; you must get there; you have to get there. Every entity not currently at home must journey there. You believed you were at home where I found you; if that had truly been your home, you could not have left it. Nobody leaves home. And nobody ever exists at home without having traveled there first."

"An enigma treading upon another enigma!" I exclaimed. "I did not come here to be subjected to riddles."

"No; but you came, and found the riddles waiting for you! Indeed, you are yourself the only riddle. What you term riddles are truths, appearing so only because you are not true."

"Worse and worse!" I cried.

"And you MUST answer the riddles!" he insisted. "They will continue interrogating themselves until you achieve self-understanding. The universe is a riddle attempting to escape, and you are holding your door firmly shut against it."

"Will you not, in pity, tell me what I am to do—where I must go?"

"How should I dictate YOUR to-do, or the path to it?"

"If I am not to go home, at least direct me to some of my kind."

"I know of none. The beings most similar to you reside in that direction." He pointed with his beak. I saw nothing but the setting sun, its glare blinding me.

"Well," I said bitterly, "I cannot help feeling ill-treated—snatched from my home, abandoned in a strange world, and denied instruction on where to go or what to do!"

"You forget," the Raven said calmly, "that when I brought you and you declined my hospitality, you reached what you call home safely. Now you have come of your own volition. Good night."

He turned and walked slowly away, his beak directed toward the ground. I stood dazed. Technically, I had come of my own accord, yet had I not arrived with an intent of atonement? My heart ached, and in my brain, there was neither quest nor purpose, hope nor desire. I watched the Raven depart, intending to follow, but deemed it futile.

Suddenly, he pounced on a specific spot, driving the entire weight of his body onto his bill. For several moments, he dug vigorously. Then, with a flutter of his wings, he threw back his head, and something shot from his beak, arcing high into the air. At that exact moment, the sun set, and the atmosphere plunged into dusk. However, the object opened into a soft radiance, pulsing toward me like a firefly, yet possessing a much larger, yellower luminescence. It flew overhead.

I turned and pursued it.
