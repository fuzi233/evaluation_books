The rumor of Rosanna's vanishing had rippled quickly through the ranks of the outdoor staff. They, too, had scoured the grounds, and their search led them to a small, energetic boy known as "Duffy," who spent his days weeding the gardens. It was Duffy who claimed to have spotted Rosanna Spearman only thirty minutes prior. He insisted she had not merely walked past him in the fir plantation but had sprinted toward the sea-shore.

"Does this boy know the coastline?" Sergeant Cuff asked.

"He was born and raised right here on the coast," I replied.

"Duffy!" the Sergeant barked, his voice cutting through the damp air. "Do you want a shilling? If you do, come with me. Mr. Betteredge, keep the pony-chaise ready until I return."

Without waiting for a second response, the Sergeant launched himself toward the Shivering Sand, moving with a speed that defied my own aging legs. Behind him, the boy let out a wild cry of excitement and scrambled to keep pace.

Once the Sergeant was gone, a strange, paralyzing restlessness seized me. I wandered aimlessly through the house, performing a dozen futile tasks, none of which I could later recall. It was only when Duffy returned, breathless, that I realized the Sergeant had handed the boy a scrap of paper from his pocketbook. The pencil scrawl read: *Send me one of Rosanna Spearman's boots, and be quick about it.*

I dispatched a maid to Rosanna's chamber and sent the boy back to tell me I would follow with the boot. I knew this was not the swiftest route, yet I could not trust another soul to deliver the evidence. A sudden, desperate urge to protect the girl, a feeling that had resurfaced at the eleventh hour, drove me forward. Clutching the boot, I ran as hard as a man of seventy could manage.

As I approached the shore, the sky turned a bruised black, and rain lashed down in heavy sheets. The roar of the ocean crashed against the sandbank at the bay's mouth. Further along, I found the boy cowering beneath the lee of the dunes. Beyond him lay the churning sea, the waves tumbling over the bank, the rain sweeping across the water like a frantic cloak, and the vast, yellow expanse of the beach dominated by a single, dark figure: Sergeant Cuff.

He waved toward the north as I drew near. "Stay on that side!" he shouted, his voice strained. "Come down here!"

I stumbled toward him, gasping for air, my heart hammering against my ribs. I had a hundred questions burning in my throat, yet not one escaped my lips. His face terrified me; his eyes held a look of pure horror. He snatched the boot from my hand and pressed it firmly into a footprint on the sand. The print bore south from our position, pointing directly toward the rocky ledge known as the South Spit. The rain had not yet blurred the impression, and the fit was exact.

The Sergeant said nothing, simply indicating the boot in the track. I grabbed his arm, desperate to speak, but my voice failed me as it had before. He continued, following the trail down where the rocks met the sand. The South Spit was just submerged by the rising tide, the waters heaving over the hidden face of the Shivering Sand. With an obstinate, dreadful patience, Cuff tested the boot against the footprints, confirming they all pointed straight toward the rocks. Yet, no matter how he hunted, he found no marks leading *away* from them.

Finally, he gave up. Silently, he looked at me, then out at the deepening surge of the quicksand. I looked where he looked and saw the truth in his expression. A terrible, silent tremor racked my entire body. I sank to my knees on the wet sand.

"She has returned to the hiding place," Cuff murmured to himself. "Some fatal accident has befallen her on those rocks."

The memory of Rosanna's altered demeanor, her deadened responses when I had found her sweeping the corridor mere hours ago, surged through my mind. Even as Cuff spoke, I knew his guess was close, though perhaps not to the full, dreadful reality. I tried to warn him, to say that the death she had chosen was a death of her own seeking, but the words refused to form. The paralysis held me fast. I felt nothing of the driving rain, saw nothing of the rising tide. In a dreamlike vision, the lost girl returned to me as I had seen her that morning when I fetched her into the house. I heard her again, telling me that the Shivering Sand seemed to pull her against her will, wondering if her grave waited there. The horror struck me with the force of a physical blow, echoing through my own child, who was just Rosanna's age. My own daughter, subjected to such trials, might have lived that misery and met this end.

The Sergeant gently lifted me up and turned me away from the site of her demise.

With that relief, I began to draw breath again, seeing the world as it truly was. Looking toward the dunes, I saw the outdoor servants and the fisherman, Yolland, rushing down to us, shouting inquiries about the girl. In brief words, Cuff explained the footprints and declared that a fatal accident must have occurred. He singled out the fisherman and turned back to the sea. "Tell me," he commanded. "Could a boat have taken her off in weather like this from those rocks where her tracks end?"

Yolland pointed to the crashing rollers and the towering waves foaming against the headlands.

"No boat ever built," he answered, "could reach her through that."

Cuff cast one final glance at the footprints, which the rain was already beginning to wash away.

"There," he said, "is proof she did not leave by land. And here," he added, looking at the fisherman, "is proof she did not escape by sea." He paused, considering. "She was seen running toward this place half an hour before I arrived. Some time has passed since then. Call it an hour ago. How high would the water be on this side of the rocks at that time?" He pointed to the south side, the area not filled by the quicksand.

"As the tide makes today," Yolland replied, "there wouldn't have been enough water to drown a kitten on that side of the Spit an hour ago."

Cuff turned northward, toward the quicksand.

"How much on this side?" he asked.

"Less still," Yolland answered. "The Shivering Sand would have been just awash, and no more."

Turning to me, the Sergeant concluded the accident must have happened on the quicksand side. My tongue finally loosened. "No accident!" I cried out. "When she reached this place, she came weary of life to end it here."

Cuff recoiled slightly. "How do you know?" he asked. The others crowded around. The Sergeant regained his composure instantly. He pushed them back, noting my age and stating that the discovery had shaken me. "Let him alone a little," he ordered. Then he turned to Yolland. "Is there any chance of finding her when the tide ebbs?"

"None," Yolland replied grimly. "What the Sand takes, the Sand keeps forever."

Stepping closer, the fisherman addressed me directly. "Mr. Betteredge, I have a word regarding the young woman's death. Four feet out, broadwise, along the side of the Spit, there is a shelf of rock about half a fathom deep under the sand. My question is: why didn't she strike that? If she slipped accidentally from the Spit, she would have fallen where there is footing at the bottom, at a depth that would barely cover her to the waist. She must have waded out or jumped into the Deeps beyond—or she wouldn't be missing now. No accident, sir! The Deeps of the Quicksand have got her. And they have got her by her own act."

Following the testimony of a man whose expertise was unquestionable, the Sergeant fell silent. We all held our peace. Together, we turned back up the slope of the beach.

At the dunes, we were met by the under-groom, running from the house. He was a good lad with an honest respect for me. He handed me a small note, his face etched with sorrow. "Penelope sent me with this, Mr. Betteredge," he said. "She found it in Rosanna's room."

It was her final farewell to the old man who had, thank God, always done his best to befriend her.

"You have often forgiven me, Mr. Betteredge, in past times. When you next see the Shivering Sand, try to forgive me once more. I have found my grave where my grave was waiting for me. I have lived, and died, sir, grateful for your kindness."

Those were the only words. Yet, small as they were, I lacked the strength to withstand them. Tears come easily when one is young and entering the world; they come easily when one is old and leaving it. I broke down sobbing.

Sergeant Cuff stepped closer, intending kindness, I had no doubt. I shrank back from him. "Don't touch me," I whispered. "It is the dread of you that has driven her to it."

"You are wrong, Mr. Betteredge," he answered quietly. "But there will be time enough to speak of it when we are indoors again."

I followed the others, supported by the groom's arm. Through the driving rain, we returned—to face the trouble and terror that awaited us inside the house.
