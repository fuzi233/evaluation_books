When Flambeau decided to take his monthly leave from the bustling offices of Westminster, he abandoned the grandeur of the Thames for the humble, whispering veins of the Eastern Counties. He commandeered a craft so diminutive it functioned as often a rowboat as a sailer, navigating the narrow, reed-choked waterways where the vessels seemed to glide like spirits over the sleeping land. The boat was cramped, designed for essentials alone, and Flambeau had curated a sparse inventory for his journey: tinned salmon for sustenance, loaded revolvers for defense, a flask of brandy for potential fainting spells, and a priest—a necessity, he reasoned, should death visit him unannounced. With this light burden, he drifted down the tiny streams of Norfolk, aiming eventually for the Broads, yet finding himself enchanted by the overhanging gardens, the mirrored villas, and the quiet corners where he lingered to fish, hugging the shoreline as if seeking refuge from a noisy world.

Like a true philosopher, Flambeau's holiday lacked a specific destination, though he possessed a convenient excuse. He harbored a vague intention, serious enough to give weight to his leisure but light enough that failure would not ruin it. Years prior, during his reign as King of Thieves in Paris, he had received countless letters—wild praises, denunciations, and declarations of love—but one particular note had lodged permanently in his memory. It was a visiting card, encased in an envelope bearing an English postmark. On the reverse, written in French with green ink, read: "If you ever retire and become respectable, come and see me. I want to meet you, for I have met all the other great men of my time. That trick of yours of getting one detective to arrest the other was the most splendid scene in French history." On the front, engraved in formal script, was the address: "Prince Saradine, Reed House, Reed Island, Norfolk."

At the time, Flambeau paid little attention to the prince, aside from noting his reputation as a brilliant, fashionable figure in Southern Italy. Young Saradine had famously eloped with a high-born married woman; while such escapades were commonplace in his circle, they lingered in the public mind due to a subsequent tragedy: the alleged suicide of the insulted husband, who reportedly threw himself from a precipice in Sicily. Saradine had dwelt briefly in Vienna before embarking on a lifetime of restless, perpetual travel. When Flambeau, shedding his European celebrity for anonymity in England, considered paying a surprise visit to this eminent exile, he realized how remote and forgotten Reed Island truly was. Yet, fate conspired, and he located it far sooner than anticipated.

They anchored their boat one night beneath a bank draped in tall grasses and short, pollarded trees. Exhaustion from heavy sculling brought sleep quickly, and they awoke before the true light of day. To be precise, they woke before daylight, for a large lemon moon was merely sinking behind a forest of high grass overhead, leaving the sky a vivid violet-blue, nocturnal yet brilliantly lit. Simultaneously, both men recalled the elfin adventures of childhood, when tall weeds closed over us like woods. Rising against the backdrop of the great low moon, the daisies appeared as giants, the dandelions towering sentinels, evoking the whimsical patterns of a nursery wallpaper. The shallow riverbed allowed them to gaze upward at the grass, and "By Jove!" exclaimed Flambeau, "it's like being in fairyland."

Father Brown sat bolt upright in the boat and crossed himself. His sudden movement caused his friend to stare mildly. "The people who wrote the medieval ballads," the priest answered, "knew more about fairies than you do. It isn't only nice things that happen in fairyland." "Oh, bosh!" dismissed Flambeau. "Only nice things could happen under such an innocent moon. I am for pushing on now and seeing what does really come. We may die and rot before we see such a moon or mood again." "All right," agreed Father Brown. "I never said it was always wrong to enter fairyland. I only said it was always dangerous."

They pushed slowly up the river as the sky shifted from glowing violet to pale gold, fading into a vast, colorless cosmos preceding dawn. Soon, the first stripes of red, gold, and grey splitting the horizon were blocked by the black silhouette of a town or village perched on the riverbank. It was already twilight, a soft gloom where all things were visible, when they passed under the hanging roofs and bridges of a riverside hamlet. The houses, with their long, low, stooping roofs, seemed to lean down to drink from the river like great grey and red cattle. The broadening dawn had turned to working daylight before they spotted a single living soul on the wharves: a placid, prosperous man in his shirt sleeves, with a face as round as the recently set moon and a halo of red whiskers, leaning on a post above the sluggish tide. Driven by an unanalyzable impulse, Flambeau rose in the swaying boat and shouted at the man to inquire if he knew Reed Island or Reed House. The man's smile widened slightly, and he pointed up the river toward the next bend. Flambeau advanced without further speech.

Their boat navigated many grassy corners and reedy silences, but before the search became monotonous, they swung around a sharp angle into the silence of a secluded pool. Instantly arrested by the view was a long, low islet, fringed on every side with rushes, upon which stood a long, low house or bungalow constructed of bamboo or some tough tropical cane. The upright rods forming the walls were pale yellow, while the sloping roof rods were a darker red or brown; the entire structure was a study of repetition and monotony. The early breeze rustled the reeds around the island and sang within the strange ribbed house like a giant panpipe.

"By George!" cried Flambeau. "Here is the place, after all! Here is Reed Island, if ever there was one. Here is Reed House, if it exists anywhere. I believe that fat man with whiskers was a fairy." "Perhaps," remarked Father Brown impartially. "If he was, he was a bad fairy." Before either could elaborate, the impetuous Flambeau ran the boat ashore in the rattling reeds, and they stood on the long, quaint islet beside the odd, silent house.

The house faced away from the river, its back to the landing stage; the main entrance was on the opposite side, overlooking a long garden. Approaching via a small path that circled nearly three sides of the building, close under the low eaves, they peered through three different windows onto the same long, well-lit room. Panelled in light wood and filled with numerous looking-glasses, it was laid out for an elegant lunch. When they reached the front door, flanked by two turquoise-blue flowerpots, it was opened by a butler of the dreariest type—long, lean, grey, and listless—who murmured that Prince Saradine was away, though expected hourly. The house was kept ready for him and his guests. Displaying the card with its green scrawl, the parchments face of the depressed retainer flickered to life. With shaky courtesy, he suggested they wait, warning that His Highness might arrive any minute and would be distressed to miss any gentleman he had invited. They had orders to keep a cold lunch prepared.

Curiosity moved Flambeau gracefully to accept, and he followed the old man ceremoniously into the long, lightly panelled room. Nothing was notably remarkable except the alternating rhythm of long, low windows and long, low oblongs of looking-glass, lending the place a singular air of lightness and unsubstantiality, as if one were luncheoning outdoors. Pictures hung in the corners: a large grey photograph of a very young man in uniform, and a red chalk sketch of two long-haired boys. When Flambeau asked if the soldierly figure was the prince, the butler answered shortly in the negative; it was the prince's younger brother, Captain Stephen Saradine. With that, the old man seemed to dry up, losing all taste for conversation.

After the lunch, which concluded with exquisite coffee and liqueurs, the guests were shown the garden, the library, and the housekeeper—a dark, handsome lady of considerable majesty, resembling a plutonic Madonna. She and the butler were the sole survivors of the prince's original foreign staff; the others were new recruits gathered in Norfolk by the housekeeper, who went by the name of Mrs. Anthony. She spoke with a slight Italian accent, leading Flambeau to suspect Anthony was a Norfolk corruption of a more Latin name. Mr. Paul, the butler, also possessed a faintly foreign air, though his tongue and training were thoroughly English, characteristic of the most polished men-servants of cosmopolitan nobility.

Despite its beauty, the place radiated a curious, luminous sadness. Hours seemed to stretch into days. The long, well-windowed rooms were flooded with daylight, yet it felt like a dead sunlight. Amidst the sounds of talk, clinking glasses, and moving feet, the melancholy roar of the river permeated every corner. "We have taken a wrong turning and come to a wrong place," said Father Brown, looking out at the grey-green sedges and silver flood. "Never mind; one can sometimes do good by being the right person in the wrong place."
