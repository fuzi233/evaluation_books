When Fra Luca’s voice fell into silence, Tito did not at once move away. He stood as if his feet had forgotten their errand, while the last crush of passengers thinned and the friar, gathering his robe about him with a slow, pained care, passed under the shadowed arch and into Santa Felicita. Only then did Tito turn and take the Via de’ Bardi, letting the daylight strike his face into its customary composure.

“If this monk is Florentine—if he means to stay in Florence—then all must come out.”

The thought went with him like a pebble in the shoe: small, but altering every step. Yet he was not so shaken that he could not do what was due in the ordinary order of things. He must go to Bardo, he must excuse himself for having stayed away, he must appear the same bright guest who could make an evening seem warmer by his mere entrance. It was still possible—perhaps in the end unavoidable—that he should accept the changed conditions and speak Baldassarre’s name aloud; but that candour would throw an ugly light backward, showing his first silence not as modest confusion but as a studied evasion, a choice to enjoy safety and settle his florins while his benefactor’s fate was only a rumour in the sea-wind. For the moment it was wiser to behave as if nothing had happened. The night was long. No one could know the exact hour at which a letter had reached his hand. He might postpone the instant when his life must change its taste.

So he climbed to the second storey room where Romola and her father sat apart from the street’s holiday noise as they sat apart from its weekday noise—among parchments that smelt of old glue and dust, among marble that kept the cool breath of the quarry. Outside, Florence rang with bells and shouts and the passing laughter that was like a stream over stones; within, the air had its own hush, broken by the thin rasp of a page turned, the tick of a tool, the murmured Latin that clung to Bardo’s lips as naturally as Tuscan clung to others.

Tito entered with a brightness only a little dimmed—dimmed just enough to be taken for regret at his lateness. He made that regret credible by naming what he had sacrificed: the Corso, the press of faces, the colour and swagger of it. And then he threw more animation than usual into the evening, as if gaiety could be kneaded and set to rise by sheer will. He spoke quickly, lightly; he made his voice a pleasant instrument against the room’s scholastic dryness. Yet all the while his mind worked beneath the talk like a mill whose stones grind on unseen: each word he uttered fell into place above, while below a different labour went on, leaving its own separate deposits—hard conclusions settling out of soft feeling.

By the time he went down the stone stairs and stepped from the grim doorway into starlight, his thoughts had reached a new stage. A purpose, like a sauce long simmered down, had thickened into something that would cling.

The next day, when he had finished the duties of his professorial work and the air began to soften towards afternoon, he turned up the Via del Cocomero toward San Marco. Florence had its usual music: the slap of sandals, the creak of carts, the calls of sellers; and in that weave of sound there were quieter threads—the drip of a fountain somewhere behind a wall, the flutter of pigeons, a far hammer on metal. Tito noticed all as one notices the scent of bread while passing a bakehouse: not as a thing to stop for, but as a thing that sweetens motion. He was going to learn from Fra Luca precisely how much the monk conjectured, on what grounds he had ventured his conjecture, and how long he meant to remain under San Marco’s roof. On that fuller knowledge Tito would mould a statement that should, in any event, save him from the necessity of leaving Florence.

He had never before needed to invent a lie carefully shaped to another man’s mind; yet the occasion had come now—the occasion which circumstance never fails to beget when a man has already consented to a tacit falsity. His ingenuity, trained in pleasing, in adapting, in finding the right phrase for the right ear, was ready. It seemed to him, with a growing ease, that he was not bound to go in search of Baldassarre.

He had once said—had meant, perhaps, as far as a young man means anything in a moment—that on fair assurance of his father’s existence and whereabout he would unhesitatingly go after him. But why was he bound? When he looked steadily, stripping the question of all the fine wrappings that society tied about it, what was the end of life but to draw from it the utmost sum of pleasure? His own life was in bloom. It promised more delight—rich, varied, quick to respond—not for himself alone but for others too, than the wintry remainder of a man past keen enjoyment, a man whose thoughts had stiffened into that rigid bareness which made companionship a demand and not a gift.

And had not Baldassarre already done his work? Those old ideas—rules, histories, habits of mind—were already sown in Tito’s fresh soil; they lived there as germ and sap. That, Tito told himself, was the true order of nature: maturity as a nest for youth. Baldassarre had had his draught. It was Tito’s turn now.

Besides, even the prospect offered no clear path: “I think they are going to take me to Antioch.” A phrase like that opened not a road but a vista of fog. A long voyage; then months, perhaps years, of search with no guarantee it would not end in emptiness. To leave behind at starting a life of distinction and of love, a life seasoned with praise like salt in food, and with tenderness like oil. And if he found anything, what would he find? The old companionship again, exacting, familiar, its claims known by rote in advance—like a dish whose bitterness you can taste before it reaches your mouth.

Certainly the gems and therefore the florins were, in the narrow sense of possession by which ordinary business was settled, Baldassarre’s. But in that larger view—so Tito was teaching his conscience to say—by which the world belonged to youth and strength, they were rather his who could turn them into the richest portion of lived sweetness. He knew that this was not the language of men. Florentines—those loud judges who could turn a man’s name into a proverb in a week—would expect him to snatch at the first chance of rescue, to fling money and self after the lost benefactor as one flings bread to a starving mouth. But what was the sentiment of society? A tangle of traditions and opinions, full of inherited words whose meanings had shifted like dialects under conquest: a dominant lexicon calling one thing “duty” and another “shame,” binding men with syllables that tasted noble on the tongue while they served common envy. No wise man, Tito thought, would let such a web guide him except so far as his own comfort required.

It was not the florins he cared for—perhaps not at all, except for Romola’s sake. He could part with money readily enough. It was the joy, due to him and close to his lips, that he refused to thrust away and so travel on, thirsty. Those maxims that demanded a man fling away the good that made existence sweet—were they not often only the lining of selfishness turned outward? They had been coined by men who desired others to sacrifice themselves for their benefit. Tito would rather Baldassarre did not suffer; he disliked suffering as he disliked a sour smell in a room. But could any philosophy prove he was bound to care for another’s suffering more than for his own? To do that he must have loved Baldassarre devotedly—and he did not. Was that his fault? Gratitude? Looked at closely, it made no valid claim. Baldassarre’s life would have been dreary without him; are we indebted to men for pleasures they have given themselves?

Once he began to dissolve Baldassarre’s claim, his thought acted like a strong acid, eating rapidly through every tissue of sentiment that had once seemed tough. He lacked that dread which, though some called it mere fear for one’s own skin, was in truth something rarer: the awe of a Nemesis, the sense that wrong-doing carried with it an unseen echo in the world’s soundscape, a threat that lived not in any priest’s tale but in the trembling of the soul. Religious pagans had felt it; Christians gave it names and faces; and even the crowd, without theology, carried a shadowy fear at anything called wrong. It was a fear that could crush ordinary cowardice, because it was the first recognition of a law restraining desire—a check upon the bold, cold analysis that can never prove obligation sacred once feeling has been banished.

“It is good,” sang the old Eumenides, “that fear should sit as the guardian of the soul, forcing it into wisdom—good that men should carry a threatening shadow in their hearts under the full sunshine; else, how should they learn to revere the right?”

That guardianship might become needless—but only when outward law was needless too, when duty and love ran together like two streams made one. Tito, as he entered the outer cloister of San Marco and asked for Fra Luca, felt no such shadow. He thought himself too cultivated, too sceptical for it. He had been nurtured in contempt for priests’ tales, made the more contemptible by priests’ impudent lives; he had been fed on learned disputes about the Chief Good until it seemed a matter of taste, like preferring one herb to another.

Yet fear lived strongly in him—the fear of whatever might rob him of pleasure. And he feared, distinctly, that Fra Luca might be the instrument that drove him out of Florence.

A lay brother—the kind called a _fra converso_—answered him. The man’s accent was plainly that of a raw contadino; his slow eyes held no prying curiosity, only the dulled patience of those who have listened all their lives to wind in vines and the creak of carts in rutted roads.

“Fra Luca? Ah, he is gone to Fiesole—to the Dominican monastery there. They took him on a litter in the cool of the morning. The poor Brother is very ill. Could you leave a message for him?”

“Thanks,” Tito said, letting his tone carry polite regret and no urgency. “My business can wait.”

He turned away with a relief that loosened something tight in his chest, as if a cord had been cut. The cloister sounds returned to him—the small footfalls on stone, the faint voice somewhere reading, the distant bell that measured the day like a ladle measuring broth. All seemed to say: wait.

“This friar is not likely to live,” he thought. “I saw he was worn to a shadow. And at Fiesole there will be nothing to recall me to his mind. Besides, if he should come back, my explanation will serve as well then as now. But I wish I knew what it was that his face recalled to me.”
