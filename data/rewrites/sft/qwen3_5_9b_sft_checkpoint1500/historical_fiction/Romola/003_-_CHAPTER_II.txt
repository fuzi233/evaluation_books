Once Bratti had merged into the throng of gossipers, the young stranger, having grown impatient and hopeless of uncovering the cause of the city's agitated humors, decided that lingering for an escort served no purpose. He began to wander the piazza, seeking a vendor of sustenance who possessed less than the average citizen's fervent curiosity regarding public affairs. Suddenly, as if struck by a sudden resolve, he plunged his hand into the pouch hanging at his waist, probing it repeatedly with a furrowed brow.

"Not a single coin!" he muttered, in a tongue that was neither Tuscan nor purely Italian. "I believed I kept one poor fragment. Well, I shall seek my breakfast through charity alone!"

He had not walked far before locating a quarter of the market where such currency would likely go unchallenged. In a secluded corner, distant from the clusters of chattering folk, stood two mules, decked out in vibrant red tassels and collars. One bore wooden vessels for milk; the other, panniers overflowing with fresh herbs and salads. Leaning her elbow upon the neck of the milk-bearing beast, sat a young girl, seemingly no older than sixteen. A red hood enveloped her countenance, rendering her features entirely hidden save for a glimpse of her cheek, which appeared even more infantile in its delicacy due to the total seclusion of her hair. Perhaps the poor child, exhausted by her labor in the pre-dawn gloom preparing for the journey from a castle several miles away, had succumbed to slumber in that semi-reclining posture. Yet, the stranger felt no hesitation in waking her. His approach, however, was so gentle that it seemed to the sleeping damsel merely a twig of thyme brushing against her lips as she bent to gather greens.

The dream shattered instantly. She opened wide, azure eyes and sprang upright, startled and confused by the sudden presence of the youth standing before her. She heard him speak in a voice that seemed strangely soft, so that even if she had been composed, she would have assumed he spoke words utterly unintelligible to her. Her immediate reaction was to turn her head slightly aside and lift a corner of her green serge mantle to use as a screen. He repeated his words gently—"Forgive me, fair one, for disturbing your rest. I am starving, and the aroma of milk renders breakfast more desirable than ever."

He had chosen the phrase "_muoio di fame_" (I die of hunger) knowing it would resonate with her ears, and he delivered it with a playful cadence, mimicking the intonation of a mendicant. This time, understanding dawned. The corner of the mantle fell, and within moments, a large cup of fragrant milk was offered to him. He accepted it without further compliment, lifting it to his lips. As he drank, the little maiden gathered the courage to gaze upward at the long, dark curls of this singular-spoken stranger. Though his garments were tattered, he bore no resemblance to the beggars she had encountered before.

As this observation unfolded, another current of feeling moved her hand into a bag suspended beside the mule. When the stranger finally set down his cup, he saw a large slice of bread extended toward him, accompanied by a fleeting glance from those blue eyes, clearly encouraging him to accept this additional gift.

"But perhaps that is your own meal," he remarked. "No, I have had sufficient without payment. A thousand thanks, my gentle soul."

There was no verbal reply, yet the bread was nudged closer, as if impatient at his refusal. As the stranger's long, dark eyes rested upon the childish face, it seemed the girl was gathering strength to look up and meet his gaze.

"Ah, then, if I must take the bread," he said, placing his hand upon it, "I shall grow bolder still, and beg for another kiss to sweeten the loaf."

Despite the strangeness of his voice, which had initially seemed jarring, his speech grew wonderfully clear. She flushed deeply and lifted a corner of her mantle to cover her mouth. Just as the somewhat presumptuous stranger leaned forward, his fingers touching the arm that held up the shielding cloth, a harsh voice rang sharply in his ear.

"Who are *you*—curse upon you! No honest buyer, I warrant, but a parasite of the dice-house—or something fouler. Go! Dance away and find keener company, or I shall give you a tune to a faster beat than you care for!"

The young stranger recoiled slightly, then regarded the speaker with a gaze provocatively devoid of alarm or contrition. A slight, saucy amusement on his face blossomed into a broad, beaming smile as he took in the figure of his threatener. She was a stout, burly woman, wearing a man's jerkin thrown loosely over her green serge gown, with the peaked hood of some discarded mantle fastened tightly around her sunburnt face. Beneath the coarseness and premature lines of age, there lingered a half-tragic, half-comic maternal likeness to the tender, baby-like visage of her daughter—a resemblance that often serves as a more chilling, shudder-inducing prophecy than the grim visage of Death itself.

There was something undeniably appeasing in that bright, youthful smile, yet Monna Ghita was not a woman to display any weakness. She continued to speak, her tone seemingly sharpened by exasperation.

"Yes, yes, you may grin as readily as any monkey in a cap and jerkin. You're a minstrel or a mountebank, I'll swear; you look as foolish as a tumblers when he has fallen upside-down and managed to land on his heels. And what devilish tricks hast thou played, Tessa?" she added, turning to her daughter, whose frightened countenance was now an open invitation for scolding. "Do you give away milk and provisions? Ay, ay, thou wouldst carry water in thy ears for any idle vagabond who did not wish to stoop for it, thou silly, staring rabbit! Turn thy back and lift the herbs from the panniers, or I shall make thee recite a few Hail Marys without counting."

"Nay, Madonna," the stranger pleaded with a smiling plea, "do not be angry with your lovely Tessa for showing pity to a hungry traveler who found himself unexpectedly without a single coin. Your handsome face looks so striking when it frowns that I long to see it illuminated by a smile."

"_Go via_! I know what paste you are made of. You may tickle me with that straw straw for a long while before I laugh, mark my word. Be gone, with a wretched Easter! Else I shall put a beauty-spot or two on that face of yours that shall ruin your kissing before Advent."

As Monna Ghita raised her formidable hands, ready to enforce the first and final requirement of her eloquence, Bratti, who had arrived a minute prior, addressed his companion. "What dost thou think of this pretty parrot, Nello? Does not his tongue smack of Venice?"

"Nay, Bratti," whispered the barber, lowering his voice, "thy wisdom contains much of the ass, as I told thee but moments ago; especially regarding the ears. This stranger is a Greek, or else I am not the barber who has held the exclusive shave of the excellent Demetrio, and extracted more than one sorrowful tooth from his learned jaw. And this youth appears to have come straight from Olympus—at least, once I have applied my razor."

"_Orsù_! Monna Ghita!" continued Nello, delighted to witness some sport. "What tempest hath caused such a thunderclap? Hath this young stranger behaved ill?"

"By Saint John!" said the cautious Bratti, who had not yet shed his original suspicions regarding the shabbily-clad possessor of jewels, "he acted rightly in fleeing from *me*, if he intended to stir up mischief. I can swear that I found him beneath the Loggia de' Cerchi, wearing a ring upon his finger such as I have beheld upon Bernardo Rucellai himself. Not another rusty nail's worth do I know about him."

"The truth is," said Nello, eyeing the stranger with good humor, "this _bello giovane_ has been somewhat too presumptuous in admiring the charms of Monna Ghita, and attempted to kiss her while her daughter's back was turned; for I observe that the pretty Tessa is currently too busy to look this way. Was it not so, Messer?" Nello concluded in a tone of courtesy.

"You have divined the offense like a prophet," the stranger laughed. "Only that I lacked the fortune to find Monna Ghita here at first. I begged a cup of milk from her daughter and accepted this gift of bread, for which I was offering a humble token of gratitude, before I enjoyed the higher pleasure of facing these riper charms, which I may have been too bold to admire."
