To truly comprehend the architecture of Doctor Florret, one must first dismantle the illusion of his benevolence. He was a man constructed entirely of correctness—a large specimen, neither towering nor diminutive, precisely calibrated to the dimensions required for the headmaster of a respectable middle-class institution. He was stout, suggesting abundance rather than gluttony; his hands were pale and sculpted, adorned on the left with a diamond ring that possessed the dull, steady luster of authority rather than the chaotic sparkle of wealth. He spoke of mundane meteorological phenomena with a cadence that bestowed dignity upon the weather itself. His visage, perpetually clean-shaven, radiated a benignity carefully tempered by discretion.

Every aspect of his existence mirrored this rigid geometry. His wife, the feminine archetype of his own perfection, stood beside him as if she had been carved directly from one of his ribs, a testament to the theory that original methods had been retroactively applied to their union. Their furniture was utilitarian, built for endurance rather than aesthetic flourish; their paintings adhered strictly to the sartorial rules of decorum, adorning the walls without demanding attention. Doctor Florret possessed the uncanny ability to utter the correct sentiment at the precise moment, in the exact tone, rendering doubt impossible. To observe him was to realize that deviation was synonymous with revolution; to disagree with him was to declare oneself an insurgent.

In practice, however, as I soon learned at the cost of several wasted years, he adhered dogmatically to the pedagogical doctrines established by English schoolmen since the reign of Edward VI. Heaven knows I labored under his regime. I possessed an ambition, vast and unfocused, a boy's desire that sought its center in every horizon and refused to anchor itself. Passing a street orator, hatless and sweating, gesturing utopian ideals to hungry eyes, I would immediately project myself as Demosthenes swaying the multitude, a statesman silencing the House of Commons, or the Prime Minister of England, revered by the entire nation. I imagined the opposition papers forced into reluctant admiration. If the echo of a distant drum reached my ear, I visualized vast fields of carnage, with myself, conspicuous and isolated, marching forward. Promoted solely on merit, I envisioned myself as Commander-in-Chief, greeted by white-waved hands from every rooftop and the deep, rolling roar of a populace. Whether my charger was snow-white or coal-black was a question of profound deliberation, for I demanded a day without flaw. Thus, I paced victoriously between waves of upturned faces, only to collide with a lamppost or the toes of an irascible gentleman and wake abruptly.

In these dreams, I was Christopher Columbus, Drake, and Nelson fused into a single entity. I spurned the presumptions of modern geographers to discover new continents; I defeated the French—those useful enemies—and died in the throes of victory. Sometimes a nation mourned me, burying me in Westminster Abbey; other times, I lived to become a Duke. Both outcomes held a peculiar charm, though personally, I remained indifferent. While boys reading letters from their mothers regarding severe toothaches on November the ninth were dismissed by Doctor Florret with a snort, I listened on November the tenth to the fading chatter of Lord Mayor's Show glories, the bells of Bow ringing in my ears. I dreamed of being a merchant prince, lending millions to nobles and marrying their daughters, or sitting unnoticed in a corner listening to discussions on new books until I became a celebrated author. I wrote poetry, fiction, and history, and everyone read and marveled. Yet, a crumpled rose leaf lay beneath my pillow, a symbol of my vexation: penmanship was a torment, spelling a puzzle, forcing me to write with sorrow and endless blotting.

Regardless of the path I chose to the Elysian Fields of fame, I dimly grasped that education was the essential weapon. With an aching heart and a pounding head, I devoured my books. I recall now sitting in my small bedroom, elbows planted on a rickety, one-legged table, startled periodically by the crackle of my hair touching the solitary candle flame. On cold nights, I donned my overcoat, turned up around my neck, wrapped a blanket around my legs, and often sat with fingers stuffed in my ears to block the importunate sounds rising from below. *"A song, of a song, to a song, a song, O! song!"* *"I love, thou lovest, he she or it loves..."* The chants repeated until my own voice sounded like a strange buzzing insect surrounding me, while my head seemed to shrink until I raised my hands in fright, wondering if it remained intact upon my shoulders.

Was I stupider than the average, or is a boy's brain physically incapable of bearing the strain of our educational system? *"Latin and Greek,"* I heard the suave tones of Doctor Florret repeat, echoing the solemn croak of Correctness, *"are useful as mental gymnastics."* My dear Doctor Florret and Company, could you not, from your vast storehouse of truly necessary knowledge, select apparatus better fitted to strengthen rather than overstrain the mental muscles of children aged ten to fourteen? You, gentle reader, with a fully grown brain trained by years of practice to its subtlest uses, take from your shelf a volume of Browning or even Shakespeare. This language is your mother tongue; you have not merely learned it, you inhabit it. Constrain for me this short passage; parse, analyze, resolve it into its component parts! Now, will you maintain that it is beneficial for Tommy, the tear-stained, ink-bespattered little brat, to treat *Aesop's Fables* or *Ovid's Metamorphoses* in the same manner? Would it not be equally sensible to insist that he practice his skinny little arms with hundred-pound dumbbells?

We were the sons of city men, of professionals of modest means, minor officials, clerks, and shopkeepers; our paths led through the grind of the workaday world. Yet, nearly half our time was consumed by studies utterly useless to us. How I hated these youth-tormenting shades! Homer! How I wished the fishermen had asked him that absurd riddle sooner. Horace! Why could not that shipwreck have succeeded? It would have done so for anyone but a classic.

Then, on one blessed day, a wondrous talisman fell into my hands.

"Hearken unto me, ye heavy-burdened little brethren. Waste not your substance upon tops and marbles, nor yet upon tuck (do ye still call it 'tuck'?), but scrape and save. For in the neighborhood of Paternoster Row dwells a good magician who, for silver, will provide you with a 'Key' that shall open wide the gates of Hades."

By its aid, the frogs of Aristophanes became my merry companions; with Ulysses I wandered eagerly through Wonderland. Doctor Florret was charmed by my progress, which was genuine, for now, at last, I studied according to the laws of common sense, understanding first and explaining afterward. Let Youth, lest the folly of Age imprison it in ignorance, provide itself with "Keys."

But let me not claim credit due to another. It was Dan—Dan of the strong arm and soft smile, Dan the wise hater of all useless labor, sharp-witted and easy-going—who made this grand discovery.

Dan followed me a term later into the Lower Fourth, but before a week had passed, he handled Latin verse with an ease and dexterity suggestive of unholy dealings with the Devil. In a lonely corner of Regent's Park, first ensuring no one was within earshot, he revealed his magic to me.

"Don't tell the others," he commanded. "Or it will get out, and then nobody will be any the better."

"But is it right?" I asked.

"Look here, young'un," Dan said. "What are you here for? What is your father paying school fees for—to play a silly game, or to learn something?"

"If it's only a game—we boys against the masters," Dan continued, "then let's play according to the rules. If we're here to learn... well, you've been in the class four months and I've just arrived, and I bet I know more Ovid than you do already." Which was true.

Thus, I thanked Dan and shared his key with him. All the Latin I remember, for whatever good it may be to me, I owe to him.

Knowledge of yet greater value I owe to the good fortune that his sound mother wit was ever at my disposal to correct my dreamy unfeasibility. From start to finish, he was my friend. To be the chosen friend of Dan, a shrewd judge of men and boys, I deemed no unimportant feather in my cap. He "took to" me, he said, because I was so "jolly green"—"such a rummy little mug." No other reason would he ever give, save a sweet smile and the tumbling of my hair with his great hand. But I think I understood. I loved him because he was big, strong, handsome, and kind. No one but a little boy knows how brutal or how kind a big boy can be. I was still somewhat of an effeminate little chap, nervous and shy, with a pink-and-white face and hair that no amount of wetting could straighten. I was growing too fast, consuming the strength I had, and my daily journey, combined with schoolwork and homework, perhaps exceeded my years.
