Happy is the man who possesses twin anchors: Merit and the Favor of Women. Had I but a fraction of Gerard’s charm to draw upon, I might rise to laurels—or at least to some modest professorship. But while I lament my lack of such a chain, let me instead paint for you the portrait of Fra Colonna.

The true torchbearers of antiquity’s revival were not dry scholars, but storytellers—Petrarch and Boccaccio. Their labor did not blaze into immediate glory, yet they sowed seeds that would not perish. By their time, Italy was never without some keeper of Greek wisdom, and every learned Greek who set foot upon her shores was welcomed as a brother. The fourteenth century closed with the birth of Poggio, Valla, and Guarino the Elder; by the dawn of the fifteenth, Florence, under Cosimo de’ Medici, had become a nest of Platonists. Under Gemistus Pletho, these thinkers sought to dethrone Aristotle—for few minds are vast enough to honor one giant without slighting another. Theodore Gaza defended the Stagirite with reason; George of Trebizond with venom. Then Bessarion, cardinal and Greek-born, struck back in his *Adversus calumniatorem Platonis*.

Into this fray of ink and intellect stepped Francesco Colonna, a Florentine noble who, at twenty, took the Dominican habit—not for piety, but for the quiet of the cloister. He was a scholar of tongues, a collector of manuscripts, a connoisseur of art and stone. Inscriptions on ancient ruins shook his faith in written histories; years in the East enriched him with rare texts and mastery of Greek, Latin, Hebrew, Syriac. Returning, he found Italy transformed—Alfonso of Naples, the Estes, and above all, his old friend Thomas of Sarzana, now Pope Nicholas V, who filled the Vatican with five thousand manuscripts and set the learned to translating: Poggio on Xenophon, Gaza on Theophrastus, Trebizond on Eusebius.

Here, in this garden of the mind, Fra Colonna flourished—a skeptic in religion, a zealot in art. He dismissed Christian rites as mummery for the masses, yet revered those with pagan roots, blind to his own inconsistency. A man of many talents—painting, modeling, music, tongues—he lacked only the need to profit by them. His joy lay in appreciation, a rare virtue in any age, for most men are blind to some great art or science, and the dullest are often the proudest.

Fra Colonna took Gerard under his wing, and soon the young scribe’s skill became the talk of Rome’s palaces. "You must not linger with me," the friar insisted. "A man must rise in the world." And so Gerard passed into the service of greater patrons, at thrice the pay. Three hundred crowns for Aristotle’s *Rhetoric*—such was the frenzy for Greek manuscripts. The nobles fawned on him, but Gerard, ever the shrewd Dutchman, kept his head.

Then came the day he dined with Cardinal Bessarion. A mile from the head of the table, he marveled at pheasants served in full plumage, chickens cooked in glass, napkins folded into wings and fans. Afterward, the tables were whisked away, revealing sweets beneath—devoured by the ladies, eyed warily by the men. When all had dispersed, Gerard left, having dined with a prince of the Church.

Greater honor still awaited. Summoned by the cardinal, he was taken to the Vatican, ushered up a private stair, and set to copying Plutarch in a chamber beside a vast, guarded curtain. Once, curiosity moved him to peek—only to meet the crossed halberts of papal guards and glimpse figures bowing before some unseen presence. He returned to his work, chastened.

Then came Jacques Bonaventura, the Pope’s nephew, a lively soul who barged in demanding entertainment. Gerard read him Alexander’s life, and the young captain, inflamed, vowed to seek glory over pleasure.

Through it all, Gerard’s heart stayed fixed on Margaret. His art brought wealth, but he lived simply, hoarding coin for their future—a home in Bruges, Antwerp, or Augsburg, where they might live out their days in peace. He helped Pietro too, securing a noble patron for the artist’s work.

Yet though he resisted vice and luxury, a greater trial loomed. For in Rome’s gilded halls, temptation wore many faces—and the most perilous still lay in wait.
