Mr. Home stayed for two days. During his visit, he refused to go out, choosing instead to sit quietly by the fireplace all day long—sometimes silent, sometimes exchanging light conversation with Mrs. Bretton. Her talk was just right for a man in his fragile state: not too emotional, not too distant, but thoughtful and warm, with a gentle maternal tone that suited their age difference perfectly.

Paulina, meanwhile, was both joyful and quiet, busy yet watchful. Her father often lifted her onto his lap, where she would stay until she sensed—real or imagined—that he was growing restless. Then, with soft precision, she’d say, “Papa, put me down; I shall tire you with my weight.”

And so, like a tiny burden, she’d slide down to the rug, settling herself beside his feet on the carpet or a low stool. There, her white workbox and her red-spotted handkerchief came into play. This handkerchief was meant as a keepsake for “papa,” and it had to be finished before he left. Thus, the demands on her delicate needlework were great—she managed only about twenty stitches in half an hour.

The evening brought Graham home from school, returning to his mother’s care. His arrival added a spark of life to the household—a vitality heightened by the familiar drama that always unfolded between him and Paulina.

From the moment he arrived, Paulina had maintained a distant, almost proud air, a reaction to the slight she felt when he first appeared. When he spoke to her, her usual reply was, “I can’t attend to you; I have other things to think about.” When pressed to name them:

“Business.”

Graham tried to win her attention by opening his desk and displaying its treasures: seals, bright wax sticks, penknives, and a collection of engravings—some vividly colored—gathered over time. The temptation wasn’t entirely without effect: her eyes, though hidden behind her embroidery, would flicker toward the table, drawn by the scattered pictures. One etching slipped to the floor—a child playing with a Blenheim spaniel.

“Pretty little dog!” she exclaimed, delighted.

Graham said nothing. Soon, slipping from her corner, she crept closer to examine it. The dog’s large eyes, floppy ears, the child’s hat and feathers—everything was irresistible.

“Nice picture!” she declared.

“Well—you may have it,” said Graham.

She hesitated. The desire to own it burned strong, but accepting would mean surrendering her dignity. No. She placed it back and turned away.

“You won’t have it, then, Polly?”

“I would rather not, thank you.”

“Shall I tell you what I’ll do with it if you refuse?”

She half-turned, listening.

“Cut it into strips to light the taper.”

“No!”

“But I shall.”

“Please—don’t.”

Graham, undeterred by her plea, took scissors from his mother’s basket.

“Here goes!” he announced, making a show of menace. “Right through Fido’s head—and splitting little Harry’s nose.”

“No! _No!_ NO!”

“Then come to me. Come quickly, or it’s done.”

She paused, lingered—but stepped forward.

“Now, will you have it?” he asked, standing before her.

“Please.”

“But I shall want payment.”

“How much?”

“A kiss.”

“Give the picture first into my hand.”

As she said this, her expression shifted slightly—doubtful, perhaps untrustworthy. He handed it over. She fled, a debtor, darting to her father and taking refuge on his knee. Graham rose, feigning anger, and followed. She buried her face in Mr. Home’s waistcoat.

“Papa—papa—send him away!”

“I’ll not be sent away,” said Graham.

Still hiding her face, she held out her hand to keep him off.

“Then I shall kiss the hand,” he said. But at once, it curled into a tiny fist—and delivered payment in a small coin that was no kiss at all.

Graham, ever clever like his young rival, pretended to be thoroughly discomfited. He threw himself onto a sofa, resting his head against the cushion, appearing wounded. Paulina, seeing his silence, peeked at him. His eyes and face were covered with his hands. She turned on her father’s knee, gazing at him anxiously. Graham groaned.

“Papa, what is the matter?” she whispered.

“You had better ask him, Polly.”

“Is he hurt?” (groan again.)

“He makes a noise as if he were,” said Mr. Home.

“Mother,” Graham suggested weakly, “I think you should send for the doctor. Oh my eye!” (Silence returned, broken only by sighs.)

“If I were to become blind…” he mused.

His tormentor could not bear the thought. She rushed to his side.

“Let me see your eye. I didn’t mean to touch it—only your mouth—and I didn’t think I hit so _very_ hard.”

Silence answered her. Her features trembled. “I am sorry—I am sorry!”

Then came emotion, faltering, and finally tears.

“Have done trying that child, Graham,” said Mrs. Bretton.

“It is all nonsense, my pet,” Mr. Home insisted.

And Graham lifted her again—she punished him once more, pulling his hair while calling him “The naughtiest, rudest, worst, untruest person that ever was.”

On the morning of Mr. Home’s departure, he and Paulina spoke privately in a window alcove. I heard part of their exchange.

“Couldn’t I pack my box and go with you, papa?” she whispered, earnestly.

He shook his head.

“Would I be a trouble to you?”

“Yes, Polly.”

“Because I am little?”

“Because you are little and tender. Only strong, grown people should travel. But don’t look sad, my little girl—it breaks my heart. Papa will soon come back to his Polly.”

“Indeed, indeed, I am not sad, scarcely at all.”

“Polly would be sorry to give papa pain, wouldn’t she?”

“Sorrier than sorry.”

“Then Polly must be cheerful—no crying at parting, no fretting afterward. She must look forward to meeting again and try to be happy in the meantime. Can she do this?”

“She will try.”

“I see she will. Farewell, then. It is time to go.”

“_Now_?—just _now_?”

“Just now.”

She raised quivering lips. Her father sobbed—but she did not. After setting her down, he shook hands with everyone present and left.

When the street door closed, she dropped to her knees beside a chair, crying out—“Papa!”

It was low, long—a cry like “Why hast thou forsaken me?” For several minutes, I saw her endure deep agony. In that brief span of childhood, she experienced emotions some never know. It was in her nature: such moments would return if she lived. No one spoke. Mrs. Bretton, being a mother, shed a few tears. Graham, writing at his desk, looked up and watched her. I, Lucy Snowe, remained calm.

Left alone, the child handled her sorrow as none else could—fighting an unbearable feeling, and eventually, slowly, taming it. That day, she accepted no comfort; nor the next. Afterward, she grew quieter, more still.

On the third evening, she sat on the floor, worn and subdued. Graham entered and gently lifted her without a word. She made no resistance—instead, she nestled into his arms, as if drained. When he sat, she rested her head against him. Within minutes, she fell asleep. He carried her upstairs to bed. I was not surprised when, the next morning, her first question was, “Where is Mr. Graham?”

It happened that Graham was not joining breakfast—he had exercises to complete and had asked his mother to send tea to his study. Paulina offered to bring it. She needed to be busy, to care for someone. The cup was entrusted to her—for, though restless, she was careful. As the study faced the breakfast room across the hall, I watched her.

“What are you doing?” she asked, pausing at the threshold.

“Writing,” said Graham.

“Why don’t you come take breakfast with your mamma?”

“Too busy.”

“Do you want any breakfast?”

“Of course.”

“There, then.”

She placed the cup on the carpet, like a jailer passing water to a prisoner, then retreated. Soon, she returned.

“What will you have besides tea—something to eat?”

“Anything good. Bring me something particularly nice; that’s a kind little woman.”

She returned to Mrs. Bretton.

“Please, ma’am, send your boy something good.”

“You shall choose for him, Polly. What shall my boy have?”

She selected the best item on the table. Shortly after, she came back with a whispered request for marmalade—which wasn’t there. Once obtained (Mrs. Bretton refused them nothing), Graham soon praised her to the heavens, promising that when he had a house, she would be his housekeeper—and possibly, if she showed talent, his cook. When she didn’t return, and I went to check on her, I found Graham and her sharing breakfast—she standing at his elbow, eating alongside him, excepting the marmalade, which she delicately refused, perhaps fearing it seemed she had fetched it for herself as much as for him. Such subtle instincts she constantly displayed.

This bond, once formed, was not easily broken. Time and circumstance seemed to strengthen it rather than weaken it. Though different in age, gender, and interests, they somehow found endless things to say to each other. Paulina, I noticed, revealed her true character only in Graham’s presence. With Mrs. Bretton, she was obedient, even dull—sitting silently at her feet, learning, sewing, drawing figures on slate, never showing originality or spark. I stopped watching her then—she was uninteresting. But the moment Graham knocked in the evening, a transformation occurred. She would rush to the top of the stairs.

Her greeting was often a reprimand or threat.

“You haven’t wiped your shoes properly on the mat. I shall tell your mamma.”

“Little busybody! Are you there?”

“Yes—and you can’t reach me. I’m higher up than you.” (Peering between the banister rails; she couldn’t look over.)

“Polly!”

“My dear boy!” (a term she adopted from his mother.)

“I am fit to faint with fatigue,” Graham declared, leaning against the wall. “Dr. Digby has quite knocked me up with overwork. Just come down and help me carry up my books.”

“Ah! You’re cunning!”

“Not at all, Polly—it’s fact. I’m as weak as a rush. Come down.”

“Your eyes are quiet like the cat’s, but you’ll spring.”

“Spring? Not at all. It isn’t in me. Come down.”

“Perhaps I may—if you promise not to touch, not to snatch me up, not to whirl me round.”

“I? I couldn’t do it!” (Collapsing into a chair.)

“Then put the books down on the first step, and go three yards off.”

Done. She descended cautiously, eyes fixed on the supposedly frail Graham. Of course, her approach revived him instantly—spasms of energy erupted. Playtime was guaranteed. Sometimes she’d be angry; sometimes it passed smoothly, and we’d hear her say, as she led him upstairs: “Now, my dear boy, come and take your tea—I’m sure you must want something.”

It was amusing to see her beside Graham during tea. Alone, she was still and quiet; with him, she became fidgety, officious, endlessly attentive. I often wished she’d mind her own business—but no—herself was forgotten in him. He was more than the Grand Turk in her estimation. She’d gradually gather all the plates before him, and even when everything seemed within reach, she’d find something new: “Ma’am,” she’d whisper to Mrs. Bretton, “perhaps your son would like a little cake—sweet cake, you know—there is some in there” (pointing to the cupboard). Mrs. Bretton usually disliked sweet cake at tea, but the request persisted: “One little piece—only for him—as he goes to school: girls—like me and Miss Snowe—don’t need treats, but _he_ would like it.”

Graham liked it well, and almost always got it. To his credit, he would have shared it with her—had she allowed it. But insisting would ruffle her for the evening. Standing by his knee, monopolizing his talk and attention—that was her reward, not a slice of cake.

She adapted swiftly to whatever interested him. One might think the child had no mind of her own, that she existed solely through another. Now that her father was gone, she clung to Graham, sensing his emotions as her own, living through his life. She learned the names of all his schoolmates instantly, memorizing their traits as told by him. A single description sufficed. She never confused identities. She’d talk for hours about people she’d never seen, imagining their faces, manners, temperaments with astonishing clarity. Some she mimicked—like an under-master Graham disliked, whose quirks she caught and repeated for amusement. But Mrs. Bretton disapproved and forbade it.

They rarely quarreled—but once, a rift occurred, deeply wounding her.

On Graham’s birthday, he invited friends—boys his own age—to dinner. Paulina was eager for their arrival; she’d heard much about them. After dinner, the boys stayed in the dining room, laughing loudly. Passing through the hall, I found Paulina sitting alone on the lowest stair, staring at the polished door where the hall lamp reflected. Her brow was furrowed in thought.

“What are you thinking about, Polly?”

“Nothing particular—only I wish that door were glass, so I could see through it. The boys seem very cheerful, and I want to join them. I want to be with Graham, to watch his friends.”

“What stops you?”

“I feel afraid—but may I try? May I knock and ask to come in?”

I thought they might welcome her as a playmate, so I encouraged her.

She knocked—too softly at first. On a second try, the door opened. Graham’s head appeared—cheerful, but impatient.

“What do you want, you little monkey?”

“To come to you.”

“Do you indeed? As if I’d be bothered with you! Off to mamma and Mistress Snowe, and tell them to put you to bed.”

His red-haired head and flushed face vanished—the door shut firmly. She was stunned.

“Why does he speak so? He never spoke like this before,” she said, horrified. “What have I done?”

“Nothing, Polly. Graham is busy with his friends.”

“And he likes them better than me! He turns me away now they’re here!”

I started to console her, ready with wise words. But she stopped me—plugging her ears at my first sentence, then lying flat on the mat, face against the floor. Neither Warren nor the cook could move her. So she lay there until she chose to rise.

That evening, Graham forgot his irritation and reached for her as usual. But she pulled away, her eyes flashing. She refused to say goodnight, refused to look at him. The next day, he ignored her—she became stone-cold. The day after, he teased her, asking what was wrong. Her lips stayed sealed. He couldn’t truly be angry—his feelings were too uneven. He tried soothing, coaxing: “Why are you so angry? What did I do?” Eventually, tears answered him. He petted her, and they reconciled. But the incident marked her. Afterward, she never sought him, followed him, or begged for his notice.

Once, I told her to deliver a book to Graham in his study.

“I shall wait till he comes out,” she said proudly. “I don’t want to make him get up to open the door.”

Graham had a favorite pony, often ridden out. From the window, she always watched his comings and goings. Her secret dream was to ride it around the courtyard—but she would never ask. One day, she went down to watch him dismount. Leaning on the gate, longing shone in her eyes.

“Come, Polly, will you have a canter?” he asked, half casually.

I suppose she thought he was too casual.

“No, thank you,” she replied, turning away with cool dignity.

“You’d better,” he pressed. “You’ll like it, I’m sure.”

“I don’t care a fig about it,” she said.

“That’s not true. You told Lucy Snowe you longed to ride.”

“Lucy Snowe is a _tatter_-box,” she muttered. (Her imperfect speech was the least precocious thing about her.) And with that, she walked inside.

Graham came in soon after and remarked to his mother: “Mamma, I believe that creature is a changeling. She’s a perfect cabinet of oddities. But I should be dull without her. She amuses me far more than you or Lucy Snowe.”

“Miss Snowe,” Paulina said to me later (now used to chatting with me at night in our room), “do you know on what day I like Graham best?”

“How could I possibly know such a thing? Is there one day when he’s different from the others?”

“To be sure! Can’t you see? Don’t you know? I find him most excellent on Sunday. Then we have him all day. He’s quiet, and in the evening—so kind.”

This wasn’t entirely untrue. Church and Sunday routines kept Graham calm. Evenings, he’d claim the couch and call for Polly.

Graham was not like other boys. Not all his joy came from action. He could sit still, reflect, read—though he rarely commented on what he read. Still, I’ve seen him lost in thought, absorbed. He had a taste, even instinctive preference, in his choices. Occasionally, a quiet mood would settle over him.

With Polly nearby, kneeling on a cushion or the floor, soft murmurs began—quiet, not loud, yet clear enough to catch. I overheard snatches of their talk. Something deeper than daily routine seemed to soothe Graham into gentler thoughts.

“Have you learned any hymns this week, Polly?”

“I’ve learned a very pretty one—four verses long. Shall I say it?”

“Speak nicely—don’t hurry.”

She recited—or half-sang—in a small voice. Graham corrected her delivery, teaching her how to express it. She learned fast, imitated well, and above all, wanted to please him. She became a quick student. Hymns led to Bible readings—perhaps a chapter. Corrections were rare; she read simple narratives well. When the subject moved her, her voice gained depth, emphasis—remarkable.

Joseph cast into the pit. Samuel called. Daniel in the lions’ den—these were favorites. Especially Joseph’s story stirred her.

“Poor Jacob!” she’d whisper, lips trembling. “How he loved his son Joseph! As much,” she once added, “as much, Graham, as I love you. If you were to die…” (She reopened the book, found the verse, and read.) “I should refuse to be comforted, and go down into the grave to you mourning.”

With those words, she gathered Graham into her arms, pressing his long-haired head close. The gesture struck me as oddly bold—like seeing a wild animal, half-tamed, fondled too freely. I didn’t fear Graham would harm her, but I worried she might provoke a careless, impatient push—one worse than a slap. Yet these displays were usually met with passive acceptance. Sometimes, a quiet wonder at her devotion would glimmer in his eyes. Once, he said: “You like me almost as if you were my little sister, Polly.”

“Oh! I _do_ like you,” she said. “I _do_ like you very much.”

But my time for observing her was short. Barely two months after her arrival, a letter came from Mr. Home. He was settled among his relatives on the Continent. England was distasteful to him; he had no plans to return for years. He wished his daughter to join him immediately.

“I wonder how she’ll take this news?” Mrs. Bretton said, reading the letter.

I wondered too. I decided to deliver it myself.

I found her in the drawing-room—her favorite place when alone, where she could trust herself not to touch anything or soil it. She sat on a couch, half-hidden by the drooping curtains near the window. She seemed content. Her tools of occupation surrounded her—the white workbox, scraps of muslin, ribbons collected for doll’s clothes. The doll, neatly night-capped and gowned, lay in its cradle. She rocked it to sleep, fully believing it could feel and dream. At the same time, her eyes scanned a picture book open on her lap.

I stood before her, holding the letter. She looked up—not startled, but calm, composed.
