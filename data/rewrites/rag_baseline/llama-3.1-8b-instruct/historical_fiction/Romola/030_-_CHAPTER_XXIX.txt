A Moment of Triumph.

On the twenty-fourth of November, a week after the arrival of the French, the narrow streets of Florence were abuzz with the promise of a decisive moment. Every piazza was filled, and shopkeepers stood at their doors, their thumbs hooked in their belts, as artisans passed lazily by, waiting for the slightest provocation to spring into action.

In Nello's shop, a gathering of six or seven men had congregated at the unusual hour of three in the afternoon. The air was thick with the scent of freshly baked bread and the sound of Nello's lute drifted out into the piazza as he half-sat on the board against the shop window, keeping watch over the crowd. His fingers moved deftly over the strings, creating a lively melody that seemed to match the rhythm of the city's excitement.

As he finished his tune, Nello laid the lute aside and turned to his companions. "I wouldn't trade that spectacle for all the gold florins in Florence," he exclaimed, his eyes sparkling with mirth. "The sight of the French soldiers shuffling along in their clumsy boots, chasing after their fleeing prisoners, was a sight to behold!" He chuckled, shaking his head. "If only I'd stayed behind the counter to tend to my customers, I might have missed it. But, as it is, I'll never forget the look on the face of that poor Greek scholar when he felt the weight of my apprentice's grasp and saw a wild-eyed stranger looming over him."

Piero di Cosimo snorted in amusement. "You should have been there, Nello, to see your favorite Greek scholar turn as white as a sheet when he felt the touch of those 'claws' and saw a 'madman' bearing down on him." He smiled wickedly. "What color do you think a man's liver turns when he's as scared as a deer in the forest?"

Nello shot back, his voice dripping with sarcasm. "Oh, Piero, do spare us your vinegar-tongued jibes. What's wrong with my dear scholar for looking taken aback when he felt a hand on his shoulder and saw a stranger staring at him with wild eyes? You Florentines are a peculiar lot, aren't you? You value wit and cunning above brute strength and courage. When you realized that, you set your oxen to plowing the fields and your scholars to do the thinking for you."
