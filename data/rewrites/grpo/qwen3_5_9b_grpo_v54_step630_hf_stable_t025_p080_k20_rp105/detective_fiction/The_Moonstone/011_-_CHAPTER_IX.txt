June twenty-first dawned gray and uncertain, the sky heavy with the promise of unsettled weather, yet by midday the clouds had parted to reveal a brave, clear sun. In the servants' hall, we commenced the annual celebration of Miss Rachel's birthday with our customary offerings. As the senior steward, it fell to me to deliver the traditional speech, a ritual I followed with the solemnity of a monarch addressing Parliament. Like Her Majesty's annual address, my words were met with eager anticipation, though once delivered, they were received with the polite grumbling of an audience expecting novelty and finding only tradition. Yet, there is a comfort in predictability; an easy people to govern, whether in the statehouse or the kitchen.

Following breakfast, Mr. Franklin and I held a private consultation regarding the Moonstone. The time had arrived to transfer the gem from the bank at Frizinghall into Miss Rachel's direct custody. Whether Mr. Franklin was thwarted in renewed advances toward his cousin or simply plagued by the insomnia that fractured his resolve, he appeared ill at ease. His mind oscillated wildly regarding the diamond, shifting positions every few minutes. I, however, adhered strictly to the plain facts: no incident had occurred to warrant alarm, yet the legal imperative remained absolute for Mr. Franklin to deliver the jewel. Eventually, logic prevailed over his hesitation. We agreed that after lunch, he would ride to Frizinghall to retrieve the stone, returning with Mr. Godfrey and the two young ladies to accompany him home.

With this arrangement settled, Mr. Franklin returned to Miss Rachel. The morning and early afternoon were consumed by the arduous task of adorning the front door. Penelope mixed the paints under strict direction, while my lady paced the room, handkerchief pressed to her nose against the fumes of the linseed oil, vainly attempting to dismiss the artists. It was not until three o'clock that they finally removed their smocks, leaving Penelope—already compromised by the strong odors of the vehicle they had employed—and the floor covered in pigment. Yet, their labor was complete. The door was transformed, festooned with griffins, cupids, and floral entanglements so intricate and chaotic that they lingered in the mind long after the initial delight faded. I noted with a grim satisfaction that Penelope's contribution to the morning's festivities concluded with a bout of sickness in the back kitchen. If art demands such sacrifices, even from my own daughter, then let art have them.

Mr. Franklin seized a hurried bite from the luncheon table before departing for Frizinghall, ostensibly to escort his cousins, though privately he carried the Moonstone. While he was away, I assumed my station at the sideboard, overseeing the dining arrangements. After ensuring the wine was ready and reviewing the staff, I retired to compose myself. A momentary lapse into reverie, perhaps mistaken for sleep, was shattered by the clatter of hooves outside. I stepped into the hall to greet a cavalcade consisting of Mr. Franklin and his three cousins, accompanied by one of the late Mr. Ablewhite's grooms.

Mr. Godfrey struck me as unusually subdued, lacking his customary buoyancy. He offered a polite handshake and a perfunctory greeting, yet a shadow obscured his features. When I inquired about his father's health, he replied curtly, "Much as usual." In stark contrast, the two Miss Ablewhites were a whirlwind of vitality. Nearly as tall as their brother, these rosy, yellow-haired girls possessed an abundance of health and spirit that caused the horses beneath them to tremble. They dismounted without assistance, bouncing upon the ground with the resilience of india-rubber. Their speech began with a loud "O," their movements were punctuated by a "bang," and their laughter erupted at the slightest provocation. I dubbed them "Bouncers."

Taking advantage of the din, I approached Mr. Franklin in the hall. "Have you secured the Diamond, sir?" I asked. He nodded, tapping his breast pocket. "Have you seen any sign of the Indians?" he countered. "Not a glimpse," he replied, before requesting my lady. Upon learning she was in the small drawing-room, he proceeded immediately. Moments later, the bell rang, and Penelope was dispatched to summon Miss Rachel.

Half an hour later, crossing the hall, I was halted by a sudden eruption of screams from the drawing-room. Recognizing the familiar "O" of the Miss Ablewhites, I was not alarmed, yet curiosity drew me inside under the guise of seeking dinner instructions. The scene was one of mesmerized fascination. Miss Rachel stood at the table, holding the Colonel's diamond, transfixed. On either side of her, the two Bouncers knelt, devouring the gem with their eyes, shrieking with ecstasy as it caught the light. Opposite them stood Mr. Godfrey, clapping his hands like a delighted child and murmuring, "Exquisite! Exquisite!" Mr. Franklin sat by the bookcase, pulling at his beard and staring anxiously toward the window. There, framed by the glass, stood my lady, clutching the extract from the Colonel's Will, her back turned to the assembled company.

She turned to face me as I entered, her expression a mask of familial frowning and suppressed temper. "Come to my room in half an hour," she commanded. "I shall have something to say to you then." She departed, clearly wrestling with the same dilemma that had troubled Mr. Franklin and me at the Shivering Sand. Was the legacy of the Moonstone evidence of her brother's cruel injustice, or proof of his depravity beyond her wildest imaginings? These were weighty questions for my lady to resolve while her innocent daughter held the Colonel's birthday gift.

Before I could withdraw, Miss Rachel, ever considerate of the old servant who had served her since birth, called me back. "Look, Gabriel!" she commanded, flashing the jewel into a shaft of sunlight pouring through the window. Lord bless us, it was a diamond! As large as a plover's egg, it emitted a light akin to the harvest moon. Gazing into the stone was like peering into a yellow abyss that swallowed all else; it seemed unfathomable, a jewel held between finger and thumb that possessed the depth of the heavens. We set it in the sun, then extinguished the room's lights, and it glowed with a moony brilliance from within. No wonder Miss Rachel was captivated; no wonder her cousins screamed. The diamond's hold on me was so profound that I erupted in a roar of "O" rivaling the Bouncers. The only one maintaining his composure was Mr. Godfrey. He draped an arm around each sister's waist, casting a compassionate glance between the gem and me, declaring, "Carbon, Betteredge! Mere carbon, my good friend, after all!"

His intent was likely to instruct me, but he merely reminded me of the impending dinner. I shuffled downstairs to my army of waiters. As I exited, Mr. Godfrey embraced his sisters and ogled Miss Rachel, offering me a token of affection: "Dear old Betteredge, I have the truest regard for him!" It was a stockpile of love to draw upon. By comparison, Mr. Franklin seemed a savage.

At the appointed half-hour, I presented myself in my lady's room. Our conversation largely mirrored our previous discussion at the Shivering Sand, save that I carefully withheld any mention of the jugglers, as nothing had transpired to justify alarming her. Upon receiving my dismissal, I observed that she viewed the Colonel's motives in the darkest light possible and was determined to remove the Moonstone from her daughter's possession at the earliest opportunity.

Returning to my quarters, I encountered Mr. Franklin, who inquired if I had seen his cousin Rachel. I had seen none. He then asked for Mr. Godfrey. I claimed ignorance, though I suspected the cousins were not far apart. Mr. Franklin's suspicions aligned with mine; he tugged his beard and slammed the library door with a sound laden with meaning.

My preparations for the birthday dinner proceeded undisturbed until it was time to dress for the evening. Just as I donned my white waistcoat, Penelope appeared at my dressing table, ostensibly to brush my remaining hair and adjust my cravat. Her spirits were high, and she had news. She kissed the top of my bald head and whispered, "News for you, father! Miss Rachel has refused him."

"Who is 'him'?" I asked.

"The ladies' committee-man, father," she replied. "A nasty sly fellow! I hate him for trying to supplant Mr. Franklin!"

Had I possessed the breath, I would have protested this indecent characterization of an eminent philanthropist. However, Penelope was busy tightening my cravat, and the full force of her indignation found its way into her fingers. I was nearly strangled.

"I saw him take her away alone into the rose-garden," she continued. "I waited behind the holly to observe their return. They had gone out arm-in-arm, both laughing. They returned walking separately, grave as could be, avoiding eye contact in a manner impossible to miss. I have never been more delighted in my life! There is one woman in the world who can resist Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, at any rate; and if I were a lady, I should be another!"

I would have protested again, but Penelope had seized the hairbrush, transferring the entirety of her feelings into that implement. If you are bald, you understand the sacrifice; if not, skip this section and thank providence for the barrier between your brush and your head.

"Just on the other side of the holly," Penelope resumed, "Mr. Godfrey came to a halt. 'You prefer,' he said, 'that I should stop here as if nothing had happened?' Miss Rachel turned on him like lightning. 'You have accepted my mother's invitation,' she said; 'and you are here to meet her guests. Unless you wish to make a scandal in the house, you will remain, of course!' She advanced a few steps, then seemed to soften. 'Let us forget what has passed, Godfrey,' she said, 'and let us remain cousins still.' She offered her hand. He kissed it—an act I would have deemed a liberty—and she withdrew. He waited alone, head bowed, grinding a hole slowly into the gravel with his heel; you never saw a man look more discomfited. 'Awkward!' he muttered between his teeth when he looked up, then went on to the house—'very awkward!' If that was his self-assessment, he was correct. Awkward enough, I'm sure. And the conclusion, as I told you all along," she cried, finishing me off with a final, scorching stroke of the brush. "Mr. Franklin's the man!"

I reclaimed the hairbrush and prepared to deliver the reproof my daughter's language and conduct richly deserved. Before I could utter a word, the crash of carriage wheels outside silenced me. The first of the dinner guests had arrived. Penelope instantly fled. I donned my coat and checked my reflection in the glass. My head burned crimson like a lobster, yet in every other respect, I was impeccably dressed for the evening's ceremonies. I entered the hall just in time to announce the arrival of the first two guests: the philanthropist's parents, Mr. and Mrs. Ablewhite.
