Life at Barton Hall had settled into a rhythm of tolerable comfort for the Dashwood sisters. The house, its gardens, and the rolling landscape surrounding them had ceased to be strangers; they were now woven into the fabric of their daily existence. Where Norland once held charm through its grandeur, the cottage now offered a joy born of necessity and intimacy. Sir John Middleton, a man accustomed to a life of leisure and rarely seen in active pursuit, was genuinely astonished to find the Dashwoods perpetually engaged.

Despite his earnest pleas for them to mingle with the neighborhood and his frequent offers of carriage rides, Mrs. Dashwood's spirited independence proved an insurmountable barrier. She resolved that her daughters would not venture beyond walking distance, a restriction that naturally limited their circle of acquaintances to the few families residing within reach.

Yet, nature provided ample diversion. About a mile and a half from the cottage, nestled in the narrow, winding valley of Allenham, stood an ancient, dignified mansion. Its resemblance to Norland had sparked the girls' imaginations during their earliest walks, fueling a desire to know its inhabitants. Inquiry revealed, however, that the owner, a lady of impeccable character, was too frail to leave her home, rendering the estate a silent monument rather than a social destination.

The countryside itself was a treasure trove of beauty. The high downs, visible from nearly every window, beckoned the sisters to escape the damp valleys below. On a memorable morning, when a showery sky broke into patches of brilliant sunshine, Marianne and Margaret ventured forth. They sought refuge from the lingering rain of the previous two days, drawn by the promise of clear air. While Elinor and their mother remained indoors, absorbed in books and pencils despite Marianne's confident prediction of lasting fair weather, the two younger sisters ascended the downs with glee.

They revelled in the glimpses of blue sky and the invigorating gusts of the south-westerly wind, pitying their mother and sister for missing such delights.

"Is there any felicity in the world," Marianne declared, "superior to this? Margaret, let us remain here for at least two hours."

Margaret assented, and they pressed onward against the wind, laughing with delight for some twenty minutes. Suddenly, the clouds coalesced overhead, and a driving rain descended upon them. Chagrined and surprised, they were forced to turn back, as no shelter lay closer than their own cottage. Their only solace was the prospect of racing down the steep hillside leading to the garden gate.

They launched themselves forward. Marianne initially gained the lead, but a misstep sent her crashing to the ground. Margaret, unable to halt her momentum to offer aid, was swept along by the slope and reached the bottom unharmed.

Just as Marianne fell, a gentleman carrying a gun, accompanied by two pointers, was ascending the hill. Hearing the commotion, he lowered his weapon and rushed to her aid. Marianne had managed to raise herself, but her foot was twisted, leaving her barely able to stand. The gentleman, perceiving her distress and her modesty refusing assistance, lifted her into his arms without hesitation and carried her down the hill. Passing through the open garden gate, he bore her directly into the house, where Margaret had just arrived, and did not release her until she was seated comfortably in the parlour.

Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor rose in astonishment. As the stranger spoke, explaining his intrusion with frankness and grace, a mixture of wonder and secret admiration bloomed in their eyes. His appearance was strikingly handsome, and his voice and demeanor added further charm to his figure. Even had he been older, less attractive, or of common bearing, Mrs. Dashwood's gratitude would have been secured by his kindness to her child. Yet, the combination of youth, beauty, and elegance infused his actions with a magnetic quality that touched her heart deeply.

She thanked him profusely and, with her characteristic sweetness, invited him to sit. He politely declined, citing his wet and disheveled state. When Mrs. Dashwood asked for his name, he replied, "Willoughby," and added that he resided at Allenham, hoping to call the following day to inquire after Miss Dashwood's recovery. The invitation was gladly granted, and he departed into the heavy rain, leaving an indelible impression.

News of the incident spread quickly, and Willoughby's manly beauty and graceful gallantry became the subject of universal admiration. The laughter elicited by his rescue of Marianne was heightened by his striking exterior. Marianne, though her face flushed with confusion prevented her from studying him closely once inside, had witnessed enough to join the chorus of praise with her characteristic fervor. To her, his person and bearing were the embodiment of a romantic hero. The lack of formal ceremony in carrying her indoors appealed to her sense of urgency and passion. Every detail seemed perfect: his name, his residence in their beloved village, and the fact that a shooting-jacket suited him better than any other attire. Her imagination flourished, her thoughts turned pleasant, and the pain of her sprained ankle faded into insignificance.

The next morning, as soon as the weather permitted, Sir John visited. Upon hearing of the accident, he eagerly asked if he knew any gentleman named Willoughby at Allenham.

"Willoughby!" Sir John exclaimed. "Is he in the country? That is excellent news. I shall ride over tomorrow and invite him to dinner on Thursday."

"You know him, then?" Mrs. Dashwood asked.

"Know him! Of course. He visits here every year. He is as good a fellow as ever lived—a decent shot, and perhaps the boldest rider in England."

"And is that all you can say?" Marianne interjected, indignant. "What of his manners in closer acquaintance? His talents? His genius?"

Sir John looked slightly perplexed. "Upon my soul, I know little of those finer points. But he is pleasant, good-humored, and possesses the finest black pointer I have ever seen. Was he out today?"

Marianne could not satisfy him regarding the dog's color any more than he could describe the nuances of Willoughby's mind.

"But who is he?" Elinor asked. "Where does he come from? Does he have a house at Allenham?"

Sir John provided more concrete details. Mr. Willoughby owned no property in the county; he resided at Allenham Court merely to visit an elderly relative, from whom he expected to inherit. "He is certainly worth pursuing," Sir John advised, turning to Elinor. "He owns a small estate in Somersetshire as well. If I were you, I would not let him slip away, regardless of this hill-climbing business. Miss Marianne must not expect to monopolize all the eligible men. Brandon will be jealous if she is not careful."

"I do not believe," Mrs. Dashwood smiled, "that Mr. Willoughby will be inconvenienced by the attentions of my daughters. It is not a pursuit they were raised for. Men are quite safe with us, however wealthy. Nevertheless, I am glad to hear he is a respectable young man, and his acquaintance would be entirely suitable."

"He is a very good sort of fellow," Sir John repeated. "Last Christmas, at a small dance at the park, he danced from eight until four without sitting down."

"Did he indeed?" Marianne asked, her eyes sparkling. "With elegance? With spirit?"

"Yes, and he was back at eight to ride to cover."

"That is precisely what I admire," Marianne declared. "Whatever a young man's pursuits may be, his eagerness should know no moderation, leaving him no sense of fatigue."

"Aye, aye, I see how it will go," Sir John chuckled. "You will be setting your cap at him now, and never thinking of poor Brandon."

"That is an expression, Sir John," Marianne replied warmly, "which I particularly detest. I abhor every cliché used to display wit; 'setting one's cap at a man' or 'making a conquest' are the most odious of all. Their tendency is coarse and illiberal, and even if they were clever, time has long since destroyed their ingenuity."

Sir John did not fully grasp the reproof but laughed heartily nonetheless. "Well, you will make enough conquests, one way or another. Poor Brandon! He is already smitten, and he is very well worth your attention, in spite of all this tumbling and sprained ankles."
