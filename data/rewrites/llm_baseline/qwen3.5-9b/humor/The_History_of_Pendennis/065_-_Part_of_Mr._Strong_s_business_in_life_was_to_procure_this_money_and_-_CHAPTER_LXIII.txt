Part of Mr. Strong's business in life was to procure money, and the rest was to ensure it vanished into the pockets of those least deserving of it. This particular chapter serves as a belated explanation for the events of Chapter LXI, setting the stage for a tale of financial entanglement, hidden secrets, and the peculiar dynamics of a household in crisis.

The information Major Pendennis had gathered regarding the Clavering family—acquired partly through Mr. Strong's diligence and partly through his own benevolent interference as the family's "friend"—was enough to make the old gentleman hesitate. His original plan to bestow a wife upon his nephew, Arthur, seemed suddenly fraught with peril. To marry Arthur to a woman with two such formidable father-in-laws as the current guardians of the Clavering estate was, in the Major's estimation, a recipe for disaster.

One of these guardians, the late Sir Francis Clavering, was a man of volatile temperament who had recently engaged in a duel with a convict named Altamont. While the convict had successfully escaped justice for cutting down the officer in charge, the mere possibility of Altamont resurfacing under British authority meant a swift return to the gallows. No sensible guardian wished to secure a wife whose father was currently awaiting execution, even if the threat of exposure served as a powerful lever to control the fugitive. However, the Major reasoned that Altamont, facing the noose, would naturally avoid recognition, while Clavering, terrified of losing everything if his secret lover appeared, would become a willing puppet in the hands of anyone holding that fatal knowledge.

Yet, there was a darker economic reality looming. The Begum, Clavering's wealthy second wife, had already spent fortunes clearing her husband's debts. Her vast resources were destined to be drained entirely by this "irreclaimable reprobate." Consequently, her heirs would inherit an empty treasury. Furthermore, Miss Amory—the Begum's daughter—would arrive at her husband's side not with a dowry or a parliamentary seat, but with her person and a pedigree tarnished by the infamous legal notation *sus. per coll.* (suspicion of collusion) attached to the name of her deceased father.

Despite these grim prospects, the Major saw a potential opening. He needed to speak privately with Sir Francis Clavering, the Member for Clavering, to discuss a scheme that required absolute confidentiality.

Finding a man in financial distress is akin to finding a lost bird; he vanishes from his usual haunts, only to reappear in the strangest places. One might expect a gentleman of fashion to be found in a club or on Rotten Row, yet such men often surface happily eating cheap food in Billingsgate or seeking refuge in obscure pubs like the "Fox under the Hill" while fleeing the law. Similarly, Sir Francis Clavering, embroiled in domestic strife and debt, had ceased to exist in his own home. As his servant, Mr. Lightfoot, noted, the Baronet pretended to sleep when water was brought and slipped out the moment the footman descended the stairs. Following his quarrel with Mr. Strong, Clavering had fled to unknown haunts, escaping not only the Chevalier's reproaches but also the nagging voice of his own conscience.

The Major, determined to catch this elusive target, faced significant difficulty. Since becoming the family's confidant, Clavering had treated him with the same avoidance he reserved for lawyers and agents. Invitations to dinner at Greenwich were politely declined; requests for brief conversations resulted in appointments made with military precision ("four o'clock precisely") that invariably ended with the Baronet's absence. Clavering was as timid as a borrower who fears the lender, proving that seeking a man is one thing, but finding him is quite another.

Meanwhile, the wheels of deception continued to turn. Despite pledging his honor to stop issuing new bills, Sir Francis had secretly signed a promissory note via his associate, Mr. Moss Abrams. The transaction occurred in the back parlor of Mr. Santiago's cigar shop near St. James's Street. Santiago, a man well-versed in the habits of London's rogues, recognized the handwriting immediately. "He is at his old work again," Santiago observed to a passing customer. "A bill for fifty pounds, dated two months ago. He'll claim he forgot it during his recent settlement with his wife. They'll give him more money now that he's 'clear.'"

Santiago's shop stood directly across from the Major's lodgings. As the Major walked daintily toward his door, key in hand, he encountered Mr. Strong, the Chevalier, striding along the pavement with a cigar glowing ominously in his mouth and a mustache that screamed of disrepute.

"Confound these young men!" the Major muttered internally. "Mustachios and cigars are the trademarks of low fellows." He paused, offering a stiff, dignified bow. "Good evening, Mr. Strong. I trust you are well?"

Strong stopped, extending a hand that the Major barely reciprocated with two ungracious fingers. "What brings you to town, Mr. Strong?" the Major asked, attempting to sound patronizing despite his confusion.

"My news is bad, sir," Strong replied gravely. "It concerns our friends at Tunbridge Wells. Clavering is at his old tricks again, Major Pendennis."

"Indeed! Come inside," the Major urged, leading Strong into his drawing room. Once seated, Strong unloaded his indignation. "No promise binds him, sir. Remember when we met with his lawyer? He refused to give his word unless he could swear on a Bible, kneeling and invoking damnation. Yet today he signed a fresh bill. He will sign as many as you please for ready cash. There is a bill of his and mine coming due next week."

"I thought we had settled all of this," the Major protested.

"Not that one," Strong admitted, flushing slightly. "He asked me not to mention it. I had half the money for it, but... well, they will be down on me. But I don't care; I'm used to it. It is Lady Clavering who angers me. It is a shame that such a good-natured woman, who has paid him out of prison a dozen times, should be ruined by his heartlessness. A pack of bill-steealing boxers and rascals get his money, and he doesn't scruple to throw an honest fellow over. Did you know he took money from Altamont?"

"Altamont?" The Major feigned ignorance. "That singular man who visited your house tipsy once?"

"I don't know who he is if you don't," Strong replied suspiciously.

Seeing the Chevalier's defensiveness, the Major shifted tactics instantly. Adopting a tone of worldly wisdom, he suggested that Clavering likely harbored secrets that Altamont exploited for profit. "Many men of the best families pay through the nose to keep their private affairs quiet," the Major mused smoothly. "But those are not my business, Mr. Strong. I am interested only in the welfare of his wife and that charming daughter. If I am called upon to intervene, I shall do so to the best of my ability. By the way, is this Mr. Altamont in town?"

Strong rose abruptly, grabbing his hat in a huff. "I am not at liberty to disclose his whereabouts, Major Pendennis."

"Precisely!" the Major exclaimed, seizing on the evasion. "Caution is the mark of a true soldier. We cannot know what ears walls may have. You have your ideas, I have mine; let us simply agree that some things are best left unknown until necessity demands otherwise." He patted Strong's arm warmly. "An old brother soldier agrees with me! Good evening, Captain Strong. Thank you for your visit. Morgan, show the door."

As Strong departed, bewildered by the Major's sudden shift from hostility to camaraderie, the Major's valet, Morgan, remained in the shadows. Having overheard the entire conversation, Morgan later approached Mr. Lightfoot at the Club.

"Do you know who Altamont is?" Morgan whispered.

"Strong's man of business," Lightfoot replied casually. "He draws the Governor's bills and does odd jobs. Kite-flying requires a team to lift the paper. Altamont put the pot on at the Derby and won big. I wish the Governor could get some luck, and I'd love to see my book paid up."

Morgan then pressed Lightfoot on whether Lady Clavering would pay the debts again. Intrigued, Morgan revealed a startling secret: he himself had been profiting from the chaos. Through careful speculation and leveraging his position within the Major's household, Morgan had amassed a small fortune. He had purchased numerous bills of exchange bearing Clavering's name, including those endorsed by Edward Strong.

When the Major finally confronted Morgan about his newfound wealth, the valet confessed with humble pride. "Yes, sir, a little," Morgan admitted, looking down. "Having gained some information and acquaintance with the family through your kindness, I put my money on the pot. I bought Sir Francis's bills, many of which bore the name of the gentleman who just left, Mr. Strong. Knowing the scandal in Grosvenor Place, I figured I might as well make my money as anyone else. Please tell me if Lady Clavering intends to pay any more."
