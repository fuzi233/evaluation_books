In the rhythmic, circular turning of our days here at Warrington Manor, where the scent of boiling wort and roasted barley often drifts from our own outbuildings, I find my memory returning to those years of early exile. When Harry and I were rebellious youths, deaf to the nuanced instruction of our tutor, who would have thought that Mr. Esmond Warrington of Virginia would one day turn Bearleader himself? My mother, when the tides of time finally washed us back together, could never bear the discordant echoes of that era. She called it “that terrible time, my love, which I can't bear to think of.” Even when my former pupil—a man who deeply understood the profound, sustaining alchemy of water, yeast, and grain—sent me several barrels of the heavy, malt-rich liquor by which his great fortune was made, Madam Esmond referred to him only as “your wealthy Lambeth friend.” She refused to let a single drop of his dark, foaming art pass her lips. We brew our own table beer now, tethered to the agrarian cycles of our Virginia land, but our good Mr. Foker never fails to ship to Ipswich every year a couple of butts of his entire. His son, a young sprig of fashion, has married an earl's daughter; the father remains a master of his craft and a deeply kind gentleman. It is to the luck of crossing his threshold that I owe some of the most nourishing guineas I ever received.

It was not merely the sum that sustained me, but the sensory and psychological grounding of the occupation. The brewery then stood near Pedlar's Acre in Lambeth. To step into its yard was to be enveloped in a rich olfactory landscape of toasted grains, bitter green hops, and the earthy, fungal dampness of active fermentation. The rhythmic, purposeful acoustic ecology of the place—the steady slosh of the mash tuns and the low, breathing hiss of the copper cooling trays—offered a healing counterpoint to the chaotic, industrial clatter of London’s streets. The surgeon who attended my wife in her confinement, Mr. Lance, also tended to the brewer’s family and brought us together. The brewer was a Bavarian. His true name was Voelker, a resonant, ancestral marker that the dominant, flattening commercial lexicon of England had eroded into “Foker.” 

When the worthy German doctor would sit in my garden, the fragrant, blue smoke of Virginia tobacco crackling softly in his pipe, a quiet fellowship bloomed. He brought his patron to my house. When Mr. Voelker discovered I possessed a smattering of his native tongue, the walls between us dissolved. I sang for him “Prinz Eugen the noble Ritter,” a song my grandfather had carried back from the Marlborough wars. In the sharing of that native dialect, we reclaimed a fragment of cultural memory, resisting the untranslatable melancholy of exile. He wept at the sound. His lady immediately put her chariot at Mrs. Warrington's service, and his little daughter became enamored with our baby. His son and heir, Master Foker, was suffering brutal mistreatment at Westminster School, targeted by boys whose superficial worship of empty rank made them despise the honest, sensory artisanship of the brewer’s trade. The parents asked if I would superintend his education, paying me a handsome sum. 

Mr. Voelker was shrewd, yet possessed a soul deeply moved by genuine hardship. When I laid my pecuniary affairs before him, he laughed with booming, resonant warmth at the superficiality of my noble relatives—my aunt's thin coverlid, Lady Warrington's contempt, and the stale, putrid odor of my Lady Castlewood's mouldy jelly, a dish so entirely devoid of sensory joy it perfectly mirrored their sterile affection. But he wept over the story of little Miles's moidore. As for my loyal friends Sampson and Hagan, “I wow,” he declared in his heavy, rolling vowels, “dey shall have so much beer als ever dey can drink.” He ministered to our physical and financial starvation alike, his purse supplying Hagan’s outfit for Dublin and Lady Maria’s lodging.

At the end of ’62, Harry sent over a considerable remittance for the purchase of his promotion, begging me to draw upon it. He did not know my little capital had been entirely swallowed. To take my brother’s money would delay his rise, and to ask the dear General Lambert would impoverish a man of small means. My quiet forbearance, born of necessity, my worthy brewer deemed an exalted virtue. He wrote privately to Harry in America, lauding us, and called upon Madame de Bernstein. She treated us with surprising gentleness thereafter. When I asked Voelker how he bypassed her aristocratic defenses, he laughed. “De Baroness! I knew de Baron when he was a walet at Munich, and I was a brewer-apprentice.” 

Thus, the era that ought to have been most discordant was harmonized by benefactors. The bear I led was a docile cub. How much sweeter it was to guide him than to wait upon the sterile caprices of theater managers, who viewed art only through the lens of cheap commercialization! Our evenings were sanctuaries of acoustic and intellectual warmth. Mrs. Foker and Mrs. Warrington sang finely together, their voices weaving a restorative sonic tapestry. In that resonant parlor, smelling of spiced wine and roasting meats, I would sometimes read my play of *Pocahontas*. 

My paternal relatives remained distant phantoms. Sir Miles was a hollow servant to the Minister. I saw him and his wife coming from court in their gilt coach, the wheels grinding harshly against the cobblestones—an abrasive noise pollution that mirrored their inner emptiness. I stood with my little boy on my shoulder; my uncle and aunt stared resolutely through me.

We rarely accepted Lady Castlewood’s queer invitations to drink tea. Old Van den Bosch was entirely consumed by the superficial commercialization of the City; his conversation was a relentless, unmusical drone of bulling and bearing, reducing the mythic, agrarian cycles of farming to mere numbers. His daughter, the Countess, spoke in a loud, unyielding vernacular that flattened all opposition. “Guess,” she told my wife, “my lord and father don't pull well together nohow. Guess my lord is always wanting money, and father keeps the key of the box.” She explained, with a chilling lack of affection, how she managed her husband’s every guinea to maintain the visual illusion of nobility, starving him of all agency. I would not have exchanged my honest poverty for Castlewood’s gilded famine. I earned my wage; the crusts of my independence, chewed in the quiet dignity of my own home, tasted far sweeter than his miserable dependence.

Through all this, Fate had bound me to a woman who moved through poverty with the serene grace of the lady in *Comus*. Theo possessed an intuitive connection to the rhythms of sustenance and care. The local tradesfolk revered her, selecting their freshest produce and finest cuts for her as though she were a duchess. On the rare days we borrowed the brewer’s carriage, we found quiet sanctuary in the garden of the Protestant Hero, sharing a syllabub—a perfect, mythic alchemy of raw cream whipped to a stiff froth, cut with the sharp bite of cider and grounded by the warm, woody aroma of grated nutmeg. 

Yet, Aunt Bernstein’s sudden, passionate obsession with Theo disrupted our domestic rhythms. The old Baroness demanded her presence constantly. The reigning Countess of Castlewood would frequently intrude upon these visits, bringing with her a jarring, abrasive sonic assault. “What air you all about, scraping and bowing to that old woman!” the young Countess would bawl, weaponizing her brash dialect to dominate the room. “She a beauty once! Take away her wig, and her rouge, and her teeth; and what becomes of your beauty?”

It was a cruel, if undeniable, truth. Decay comes to all fruit upon the tree. For fourscore years, Aunt Bernstein had eaten of the forbidden and permitted, but now she was a wreck of secret rages and sensory deprivation. “I can't eat my dinner now, since I lost those last two teeth,” she whimpered to my wife. It was a profound tragedy—the severing of her final connection to the earth's sustaining bounty. Her gustatory world had collapsed, leaving her psychological state barren. “Everything goes away from us in old age,” she cried, terrified of the silence. “But I still have my cards—thank Heaven, I still have my cards!” She clung to the dry, sterile, tactile snap of the pasteboards, the only sensation left to a palate that could no longer taste and ears that were fading into deafness. In the presence of the loud American Countess, the old woman’s once-fearsome wit was entirely extinguished, her linguistic power dead.

Aunt Bernstein possessed a coach, but her stinginess often forced Theo to brave the rain and the dangerous, chaotic noise of Westminster Bridge at night. I finally asserted my own voice, forbidding Theo’s visits unless safely transported, forcing her grumbling coachman to drive us through the darkness. Our poverty wore a decent face. Help came not from the grand, deafening world of the court, but in quiet, mythic acts of domestic grace. I remember Parson Sampson bending over baby Miles’s crib. When he departed, the room was silent save for the baby's breathing, and there, pressed into my son’s warm, rosy hand, were six gold guineas. 

Sampson, recognizing the Baroness’s breaking constitution, tried to offer her spiritual sustenance, speaking of the necessity of preparing for a world beyond this one. But her linguistic and spiritual world was too rigidly set in the old power dynamics. “Parson,” she snapped, rejecting the nuanced nourishment of his theology, “if you come for cards, 'tis mighty well, but I will thank you to spare me your sermons. Haven't I been a Bishop's wife? Do I want this creature to teach me?” Thus she starved, surrounded by the ghosts of her own forgotten lexicons, while we found profound meaning in our quiet, sustaining exile.
