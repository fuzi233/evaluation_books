"Much ado there was, God wot; He wold love and she wold not." —Nicholas Breton.

Time, we know, is a deceptive ruler. Its extension is a flawed metric; the vast distance the sun travels across the sky tells us nothing of how deeply life has shifted beneath our feet, just as measuring a field's acreage reveals nothing of the wildflowers bursting forth within it. A man might march south, stumble over a forgotten bone, and spend years unraveling the secrets of anatomy; or turn east, discovering a linguistic key that unlocks the history of entire races. He might lead an expedition that carves new paths across continents, endure physical ruin, and write a heroic epic of resolve. Yet, when he returns after only a few months, he finds his neighbors still grumbling about the same parish grievances, sees the same elderly gentleman pacing the pavement, muttering to himself, shaking his head at the same butcher's boy, and staring longingly at the same shop window displays.

If the sharpest intellect moves with the speed of a greyhound, the slowest must crawl like a limpet, appearing stuck until one realizes it is inching forward. Human experience swings wildly between these poles: from the revolutionary surge of change that births a new inner and outer existence, to the quiet, recurring rhythm of the familiar, marked only by hunger and the turning of the heavens.

Something of this stark contrast defined the year that transformed the brilliant, self-assured Gwendolen Harleth of the Archery Meeting into a crushed penitent, driven to confess her unworthiness—a confession she would have preferred to avoid entirely. Meanwhile, her family at Pennicote Rectory underwent far subtler shifts: merely adjustments to habits, a recalibration of hopes to match a reduced income, fewer social calls, and fading compliments. The rectory remained as charming as ever. The red and pink peonies on the lawn and the rows of hollyhocks along the hedges bloomed with equal vigor this year as last. The rector maintained his cheerful faith in the goodwill of his patrons, resolved to earn it through diligent duty, regardless of whether anyone noticed. He wrote two ecclesiastical articles annually, unsigned and attributed to anonymous scholars, save for the select few patrons who received private copies. They knew the author intimately but rarely bothered to read the content. The rector took no bitter pleasure in this obscurity; instead, he annotated his own copies to make them engaging loans, pleased that the Archdeacon and other authorities found no fault with his arguments. What peaceful authorship! Living amidst the fields and downs, breathing air untouched by the suffocating critique of the city, avoiding the gaunt leanness of Dante's inferno, and finding nourishment in complacency rather than the arduous labor of composing a *Divina Commedia*.

Then there was the father's renewed delight in his favorite son, a joy that outweighed the loss of eighteen hundred pounds a year. Whatever hidden transformation Rex had undergone following the disappointment of his first love appeared secondary to the clear evidence of serious ambition sparked by the family's recent misfortune. Indeed, Mr. Gascoigne tended to view the previous year's crisis as merely an evaporation of excess moisture, a final step in the baking process required to harden the human dough.

Rex had recently returned for a summer visit, bringing Anna home. While he displayed nearly his old vivacity among his siblings, he diligently maintained the habits of the eager student, rising early and retiring early to pursue a rigorous course of study.

"You do not regret choosing the law as a profession, Rex?" his father asked.

"There is no profession I would prefer," Rex replied. "I wish to end my days as a first-rate judge and help draft a legal code. I reverse the famous saying. 'Give me something to do with making the laws,' I'd say, 'and let others compose the songs.'"

"You'll have to stuff yourself full of immense amounts of rubbish, I suppose—that's the drawback," the rector noted.

"I don't see that legal rubbish is worse than any other. It isn't as bad as the trivial literature that clogs people's minds. It doesn't dull the senses. Our wittiest men have often been lawyers. An orderly method of viewing the world as cases and evidence seems preferable to a chaotic wash of disconnected fragments. From a higher perspective, the foundations and evolution of law offer the most fascinating insights into philosophy and history. Of course, there will be much that is tedious, drudgery-inducing, and frustrating. But the greatest prizes in life cannot be won easily—I've learned that."

"Well, my boy, the best omen for success in any career is believing it to be the finest in the world. Most people feel this way when they enter a task with true willpower." The rector recalled a conversation with Brewitt, the blacksmith, whose apprentice lacked enthusiasm for his trade. "'And yet, sir,' Brewitt said, 'what would a young fellow have if he didn't like blacksmithing?' The rector cherished a paternal pride, allowing it to surface only in measured doses. He had easily accepted Warham's departure for India, but Rex represented a different kind of romance—the later-life discovery of a son who surpasses oneself, inspiring visions of future eminence based on famous precedents.

He spoke decisively to his wife: "Rex will be a distinguished man, Nancy, I am certain of it—as sure as Paley was about his son."

"Was Paley an old bachelor?" Mrs. Gascoigne asked.

"That is hardly relevant, my dear," the rector replied, forgetting the irrelevance instantly. Mrs. Gascoigne felt her remark had been somewhat weak.

This tranquil passage of time at the rectory was mirrored in the household of the Jodsons, a modest white house nestled near the rectory, surrounded by evergreens and known locally as "Jodson's." Mrs. Davilow's delicate face bore only a slight deepening of its gentle melancholy, her hair showing just a few more strands of silver due to the trials of the past year. Her four daughters had blossomed slightly, freed from the shadows of their earlier struggles, while the capable Jocosa maintained her neutral stance toward worldly pleasures, reserved for those not "in a situation."

In the low, narrow drawing room, expanded by two quaint projecting windows with lattices wide open to a July afternoon, the air was thick with the scent of monthly roses, the soft murmur of the garden, and the occasional distant clip-clop of hooves or rumble of wheels that only heightened the ensuing silence. With Rex and Anna joining the usual group of six, the scene felt crowded and lively. Anna, always beloved by her younger cousins, eagerly recounted her new experiences in London and the acquaintances she had made. When she arrived alone previously, countless questions had been directed at her regarding Gwendolen's residence in Grosvenor Square, her words, and the opinions of others about Gwendolen.

Had Anna visited Gwendolen after learning about the yacht? No—an answer that left speculation rampant regarding everything connected to that mysterious vessel, aside from the fact that Gwendolen had written shortly before departing to announce that Mr. Grandcourt and she were yachting in the Mediterranean, and again from Marseilles to declare her certainty of enjoying the trip, promising not to write another letter until she had penned a lengthy diary filled with repetitions. Furthermore, Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt's movements had been reported in the newspapers, adding to the intrigue. Thus, this new chapter in Gwendolen's elevated life became a striking element of the sisters' romantic narratives, with the book-obsessed Isabel weaving in a dash of piracy to ensure a happy ending.

However, when Rex was present, the girls, adhering to strict instructions, avoided this captivating topic. Instead, today featured animated discussions of the Meyricks and their extraordinary Jewish friends, prompting astonished queries from minds for whom the concept of living Jews, drawn from books, suggested a difference so profound it bordered on zoological—a strange race akin to those described by Pliny that might sleep beneath the shade of their own ears. Bertha could not conceive what Jews believed nowadays, harboring a vague notion that they rejected the Old Testament because it validated the New. Miss Merry thought Mirah and her brother were "unarguable," while the amiable Alice expressed indifference to their beliefs, admitting she simply "couldn't bear them." Mrs. Davilow gently corrected her, noting that prominent Jewish families in London and Paris were perfectly respectable, though acknowledging that common, unconverted Jews were objectionable. Isabel asked whether Mirah spoke differently, or if one could be in her company without realizing she was a Jewess.

Rex, possessing no partisan loyalty to the Israelites despite his academic cramming on their ancient history, amused himself by playfully exaggerating each speaker's prejudice, until Anna interceded, begging them to understand he was only joking. Their laughter was cut short by the arrival of a letter for Mrs. Davilow. A messenger had delivered it with urgent haste from the rectory. It contained a telegram. As Mrs. Davilow read and reread it in silent agitation, all eyes turned to her with growing anxiety, yet no one dared to speak. Finally lifting her gaze, she saw the young faces painted with fear. Remembering they might be imagining horrors worse than reality, she paused, then began again with a sob that was half-relief: "My dears, Mr. Grandcourt—"

She hesitated an instant, then continued, "Mr. Grandcourt is drowned."
