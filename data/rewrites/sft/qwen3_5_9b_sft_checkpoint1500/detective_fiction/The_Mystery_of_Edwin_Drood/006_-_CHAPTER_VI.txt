The Reverend Septimus Crisparkle had shattered the thin sheet of ice near Cloisterham Weir with the sheer force of his amiable head, a vigorous start to his day. Now, seeking further circulation, he engaged in a spirited bout of shadow-boxing against a mirror in the drawing-room. The glass reflected a picture of health and vigor: Septimus feinted with artistic cunning and countered with the straight, honest power of a man who believed in the righteousness of his blows. His face glowed with a natural innocence, and his benevolent spirit seemed to radiate from beneath his boxing gloves.

"It is scarcely breakfast time yet," observed Mrs. Crisparkle—the old lady, mother though not wife to the Reverend Septimus—as she descended the stairs, waiting for the tea urn. "Indeed, Septimus, you stopped your exercise precisely when I arrived."

She stepped forward, and before the Reverend could resume his left hook, she caught his gloved hands, kissing them with genuine tenderness.

"I say, every morning of my life, Sept," she remarked, watching him recover his stance, "you will eventually break a pier-glass or burst a blood vessel."

"Neither, please God, Mother," Septimus replied, breathing heavily. "Here is the wind!" He demonstrated his speed by delivering a series of rapid, severe combinations that left the old lady's cap disheveled—a technical term in the "Noble Art" known as sending it into "Chancery"—yet not a single strand of lavender ribbon moved. With magnanimity, he released his defeated opponent, tucked his gloves away, and adopted a contemplative pose by the window just as a servant entered to set the breakfast table.

Once the urn was poured and the meal prepared, the two sat alone. It was a scene of profound, quiet intimacy, though unseen by anyone save themselves. Mrs. Crisparkle stood to recite the Lord's Prayer aloud, her voice clear and steady, while her son, a Minor Canon within five years of forty, bowed his head in reverence, just as he had done when he was nearly four.

There is nothing more charming than an elderly woman when her eyes are bright, her figure trim, her face serene, and her attire as delicate as a porcelain shepherdess, perfectly fitted to her form. "My Sept!" were the two words that frequently summed up Mrs. Crisparkle's thoughts whenever she gazed at her son across the table. They were a good pair for breakfast in Minor Canon Corner, a secluded spot in the cathedral's shadow where the cawing of rooks and the tolling of bells created a silence deeper than absolute stillness. The swaggering soldiers, the suffering serfs, and the powerful monks of old were all gone, leaving behind a tranquil air and a romantic melancholy born of stories fully told.

"And what news, Mother?" inquired the Minor Canon, displaying a wholesome appetite.

Mrs. Crisparkle placed a letter on the cloth and passed it to him. She was proud of her sharp vision, requiring no glasses, while her son, equally proud, insisted on wearing a pair of spectacles of immense, comical proportions. This pretense, devised to ensure his mother derived maximum satisfaction from his alleged inability to read clearly, actually hindered his view, as his natural eyesight was microscopic.

"It is from Mr. Honeythunder, surely?" said the old lady, folding her arms.

"Of course," assented her son, sliding his clumsy lenses onto his nose. He struggled to decipher the script, mumbling that his sight grew worse daily.

"'I write,'" read his mother, her voice precise, "'from the chair, to which I shall probably be confined for some hours.'"

Septimus glanced at the row of chairs along the wall with a mixture of protest and relief.

"'We have,'" continued his mother, emphasizing the next phrase, "'a meeting of our Convened Chief Composite Committee... and it is their unanimous pleasure that I take the chair.'"

Septimus let out a breath he hadn't realized he was holding. "If it comes to that, let him."

"'Not to lose a day's post, I take the opportunity of a long report being read, denouncing a public miscreant—'"

"A most extraordinary custom," interrupted Septimus, wiping his ear with a vexed gesture. "These philanthropists seem determined to denounce someone, and equally determined to find them guilty!"

"'Denouncing a public miscreant,'" Mrs. Crisparkle resumed, "'to get our little affair of business off my mind. I have spoken with my two wards, Neville and Helena Landless, regarding their education. They agree to the plan.'

"And it is another strange thing," Septimus noted, "that these gentlemen seize their neighbors by the scruff of the neck and shove them toward peace. Pardon me, Mother."

"'Therefore,' the letter concludes, 'you will prepare your son, the Rev. Mr. Septimus, to receive Neville as an inmate on Monday next. On the same day, Helena will arrive to take up quarters at the Nuns' House...'"

"Well, Mother," Septimus said after a moment's reflection, "we must try it. There is certainly room, and time, and inclination. I confess I am relieved it is not Mr. Honeythunder himself. Though that seems prejudiced, does it not? I have never seen him. Is he a large man?"

"I believe so," Mrs. Crisparkle hesitated. "His voice is far larger than his stature."

"Larger than himself?"

"Larger than anyone."

"Hah!" Septimus chuckled, finishing his breakfast with less enthusiasm, sensing the flavor of the souchong tea fading slightly.

Mrs. Crisparkle's sister, a lady as dainty as Dresden china, lived in London with her husband, a clergyman. It was during her recent annual visit that Mr. Honeythunder, a Professor of Philanthropy, had become acquainted with the sisters following a charitable banquet for orphans.

"I am convinced, Mother," Septimus said, weighing the matter, "that the priority must be to make these young people feel at ease. Disinterest is impossible unless we are comfortable ourselves. Jasper's nephew is here; youth gravitates toward youth. We shall invite him, and Miss Twinkleton, and the intended bride, to join us for dinner. That makes eight guests. Would that trouble you, Mother?"

"Nine would, Sept," the old lady replied, appearing suddenly nervous.

"My dear Mother, I specified eight."

"The capacity of the room and the table, Sept."

The arrangement was settled. Septimus visited Miss Twinkleton to coordinate Helena Landless's stay at the Nuns' House, and invitations extended to the others were eagerly accepted. Instructions were sent to Mr. Honeythunder regarding the travelers' schedule, and the kitchen filled with the scent of soup.

Cloisterham, in those days, lacked a railway. Mr. Sapsea, a local figure, maintained that a train would never arrive, a prophecy that history ironically disproved later, when express trains merely yelped past without stopping, treating the town as insignificant. However, a fragment of line did exist, routing through a back alley labeled "Beware of the Dog."

Through this humble entrance approached Mr. Crisparkle, awaiting a short, squat omnibus piled high with luggage. Seated atop the heap, dominating the driver's box, sat a passenger with a square-jawed, imposing presence, squashing the driver into a tiny space.

"Is this Cloisterham?" the passenger boomed.

"It is, sir," the driver grunted, rubbing his sore back. "And I am glad to see it."

"Widen your box-seat," the passenger commanded. "You are morally, and perhaps legally, bound to provide comfort to your fellow man."

The driver patted his ribs anxiously. "Have I sat upon you?"

"You have," admitted the driver. "Take this card."

"I shall not accept it," the driver refused, eyeing the paper disdainfully. "What use is it?"

"Membership in a Society," the passenger declared fiercely.

"What do I gain?"

"Brotherhood," the passenger roared.

"Thank you," the driver said calmly, stepping down. "My mother was content with me, and so am I. I require no brothers."

"But you must have them!" the passenger insisted. "Whether you like it or not, I am your brother."

"Not too far, I say!" the driver protested. "The worm *will*..."

Before the sentence could finish, Mr. Crisparkle intervened gently. "Joe! Joe! Do not forget yourself, my good fellow!" Once the driver had composed himself, Crisparkle greeted the stranger warmly. "Mr. Honeythunder?"

"That is my name, sir."

"My name is Crisparkle. Reverend Mr. Septimus?"

"Glad to see you, sir. Neville and Helena are inside. Having succumbed somewhat to the pressures of my public labors, I decided to take some fresh air and escort them down, returning tonight. So, you are the Reverend Mr. Septimus?" He surveyed the vicar with disappointment, twisting a ribbon around a monocle he wore but rarely used. "Hah! I expected to see you older, sir."

"I hope you will," Septimus replied with a smile.

"Eh?"

"A mere jest, not worth repeating."

"A joke? Ay, I never see a joke," Mr. Honeythunder retorted sharply
