On a long, dead-straight East End London brick street – the kind where every house is identical enough to look like a toddler stacked them from drab grey Lego, and the perfectly even window sills and doorsteps stretch off into the distance like a bored geometry teacher’s half-finished diagram of a problem no one on earth has ever cared about – stands one oddball house that yanks me back against my will like a badly trained dog on a retractable lead. Half the time I don’t even notice my feet are moving until I’m already trekking past the noisiest naphtha-lit high streets full of weary, work-worn faces, then the dim empty lanes where shadows loiter like blokes who forgot where they live, then the squalid little side streets where gutters are thick with screaming kids and every front door spits out a party that’s gotten far too loud, until I finally skid to a stop in front of my weird little memory magnet, its front railings half-broken and rusty, and catch my breath.

This place is way bigger than its drab little neighbors, built back when this whole area was still a country lane edging the marshes, and it sticks out like a sore thumb in a neighborhood where the entire unwritten rule is that nothing is allowed to have personality at all. Two sides of it wrap around what used to be a fancy garden, but now it’s just a patch of stones and dust where laundry hangs permanently, looking thoroughly confused about how it got there – seriously, who in their right mind thinks hanging washing out in a dust bowl is a good use of time? The front porch is crumbling too, all the fake stucco falling off to reveal the sad lumpy wood under it, like someone tried to dress a pig up as a fancy marble portico and gave up halfway.

I pop by in the daytime sometimes, when the ladies in fuzzy house slippers are gossiping round the area gates and the only other noise is the coal man, who yells “COALS! THREE AND SIXPENCE A SACK! CO-O-O-ALS!” in a tone so mournful you’d think he was announcing his own dog’s funeral, so of course no one ever answers him. But the street never recognizes me in broad daylight, and my old house pal acts like it’s too embarrassed by its own shabbiness to say hi, turning its grimy little windows away like a kid hiding behind their mom’s legs when you wave at them in the supermarket. Not until night – the only thing in the world that’s nice to ugly stuff – pulls her soft dark veil over the whole sordid neighborhood will it greet me like a beloved old nurse you track down decades later, arms wide open. Once all the people currently living inside its walls are asleep, and the flickering pub sign from across the road goes out, it chats with me about the old days, asking questions, reminding me of tiny stupid things I’d completely forgotten.

Then the old familiar footsteps echo down the empty street, the people I knew pass through the creaky old gate without seeing me, and the two of us gush over them like a pair of retirees flipping through a tattered old photo album full of faded, weirdly dressed portraits, sighing and smiling and muttering “Dear, dear!” and “Ah, well, you had to be there.”

That bent, worn man hurrying towards us, pausing every fifty yards to lean heavy on his fancy Malacca cane? “Handsome, isn’t he?” I say, squinting at his shadow.
“Aye, handsome enough,” the old house rumbles back. “Even more so before you and I knew him, when all those stupid little money worries carved their grumpy lines into his face. I never could work out who you took after, you know. Your mum and dad were a very pretty pair – but Lord, what a couple of overgrown children.”

“Children?” I sputter, shocked. My dad must have been well into his late 30s by the time this house met him, and my mum’s face is clear as day in my head, full of grey threads mixed in with her soft brown hair.
“Children,” the house snaps back, testy as any old person who hates being questioned. “The most helpless pair I ever laid eyes on. Who but a kid would decide at 38 that the best way to fix his broken fortune was to become a solicitor? And then who but a total idiot would pick the back end of Poplar to nail up his professional door plate?”

“Everyone said it was a rising neighborhood!” I huff, defensive. No son likes hearing his family’s life choices roasted, even if deep down you know the critic has a point. “All kinds of sea-faring folks were supposed to move here to be near the new docks! And if they’d moved, they’d have spent all their time squabbling over ship stuff, right? That’d have been perfect for a smart solicitor right on their doorstep.”

“Stuff and nonsense,” the house snorts. “The mere smell of this place would have sent any sensible man running a mile. And him? The ‘smart solicitor’ doling out loophole advice to shifty dock hustlers and freight crooks? Can you even picture it? The man starved himself for two years on five shillings a week, all to make sure no one lost a penny for trusting him. Your mum didn’t even find out about it for years. You really think that crowd of chandlers and brokers would have found him a useful man to do business with?”

I’ve got no comeback for that, and the house doesn’t wait for one, just keeps gabbing. “And your mum! Who but a child would take that soft-tongued, obvious little drama queen in and not see through her terrible acting instantly? Who but the silliest kid that ever wandered out unsupervised would fall for all that nonsense? They were both babies, the pair of them.”

“Tell me,” I say, because at this hour I’m daft enough to fully believe this old pile of bricks is alive – just like a violin that’s been played a thousand times eventually makes its own music, how could walls full of old voices and floors trod by thousands of old footsteps not be alive? “I was just a tiny kid back then, life sounded like a weird foreign language to me – was there any truth to that old story everyone whispered about?”

“Truth?” the house snips. “Just enough truth to plant a lie on top of, and Lord knows lies don’t need much soil to grow. I saw what I saw, I know what I know. Your mum was a good woman, your dad was a true husband. But that old problem’s as old as time: men think one way, women think another, so there’s always a little crack of doubt sitting between two people no matter how much they love each other.”

“But they figured it out in the end, right?” I say, remembering.
“Aye, in the end,” the house says. “That’s always when you all figure it out, you humans. Right when you’re almost done.”

A grubby, once-angelic little face peeks shyly at me through the railings, then ducks back when I turn my head. That’s the little girl who used to run around the streets with us.
“What ever happened to her?” I ask.
“Her? Oh, she’s grand,” the house says. “Lives round the corner, you must have passed her shop a dozen times. She’s fourteen stone now, and has nine kids running round her ankles. She’d be chuffed to see you.”

“Thanks, but I don’t think I’ll pop in,” I say, laughing, but it’s not a totally happy laugh. I can still hear the pitter-patter of her tiny seven-year-old feet vanishing down that long street.

More familiar faces float past. A big red-cheeked grinning face makes my chest feel that old familiar mix of fondness and quiet “oh no I really shouldn’t like him” disapproval. Next to him is a massive clean-shaven face, with the ugliest mouth and the softest, loveliest eyes I’ve ever seen on a man.
“Was he really as bad as everyone said he was?” I ask the house.
“Shouldn’t wonder,” the house says. “I never knew a worse man. Nor a better one, neither.”

The wind whips his face away to reveal a tiny old lady, hobbling along fast on a stick, three little corkscrew curls bobbing on each side of her head. She pulls the most ferocious faces at me as she gets close, muttering to herself like it’s a top secret thought: “I’d like to skin ‘em. I’d like to skin ‘em all. Skin ‘em all alive!” It sounds totally fiendish, but I just laugh – this is the same little old lady who used to bring us all stolen jam tarts when our mums weren’t looking.

Then a fair, laughing face drifts past. I saw someone just like her only a few hours ago, but that sweet face was covered in a mess of paint life had slopped over it, like Evil himself was scribbling over a watercolour he hated. I stand there between those two faces: the rogue with the wicked mouth and the tender eyes, the girl who taught a small boy that women were all soft magic and mystery. “Whose fault was it?” I yell at the shadows. “Yours? Hers?” And I can hear his old mocking laugh, answering: “Whose fault? God made us.” For a second I hate him, but then I look into his sad eyes, and the pity is bigger than the hurt, and I echo him: “God made us.”

All the faces, merry and sad, pretty and ugly, spin around me, but the one at the centre is always the same: a little lad with golden curls that looked more at home on a little girl, shy and awkward, quiet as a mouse, with a little old man’s serious face on a seven year old body. I turn to my old brick friend. “If he could see me now, do you think he’d recognise me?”
“How would he?” the house grumbles. “You’re nothing like the version of you he’s carrying around in his head. Do you think you’d recognise your own ghost, if it walked past you on the street?”
“It’s sad he wouldn’t know me,” I say.
“Could be a lot sadder if he did,” the house huffs back.

We’re quiet for a minute, but I know exactly what the house is thinking. It speaks before I can guess. “You, the story writer – why don’t you write a book about him? You know his whole story, after all.” It’s the house’s favourite hobby horse. It brings it up every single time I visit.
“But he never did anything exciting!” I protest.
“He lived,” the house says. “Isn’t that enough?”
“Aye, but he lived in boring old modern London, not in some fairy tale kingdom with knights and dragons,” I argue. “How do you make a story out of that that anyone would care about?”

The house gets properly huffy with me now. “‘The people’?” it snaps. “What are all of you but a bunch of kids in a dimly lit room, waiting for your name to be called to go to sleep. One of you climbs up on a stool and tells a story, and the rest crowd round. Who’s to say what you’ll like, and what you won’t?”

I trudge home through the quiet, soft-breathing streets, turning the house’s words over in my head. Is it just being a daft old mum, thinking the whole world cares about her weird little kid? Or is it actually onto something? Then I think of that small, very specific member of my audience who curls up next to me on evenings and begs for stories after I’ve finished all the ones about giants and gallant knights and magic swords. Even when I’ve told every fairy tale I know, she still snuggles closer and says “Now tell me a real story. You know – about a little girl who lived in a normal house with her mum and dad, and was sometimes a bit naughty.”

So maybe out there in the big wide world, there’s a few more people like her, who’ll step away from the grand epic tales of haughty heroes strutting around palaces and battlefields, to listen to the story of a totally ordinary lad, who lived with totally ordinary people in a boring old London street, and grew up to be a totally ordinary man. Who loved a little, grieved a little, helped a few people, hurt a few people, muddled through, failed, and still hoped. If that’s you, come on over and crowd round.

And don’t get cross halfway through and yell that this is a boring humdrum story where nothing “proper” happens. I’m warning you upfront: I’m only telling you the stuff I actually saw. My villains are just regular sinners, not 100% evil. My good guys are very imperfect saints. My princes don’t always slay their dragons – more often than you’d think, the dragon eats the prince. The bad fairies usually win against the good ones. The magic thread leads you down the wrong street half the time. And even the hero isn’t always brave and perfect.

Only come and listen if you’re happy to hear your own story, told by someone else. The kind of story that makes you nod and say “Oh, right. I dreamed that once. That’s true. I remember.”
