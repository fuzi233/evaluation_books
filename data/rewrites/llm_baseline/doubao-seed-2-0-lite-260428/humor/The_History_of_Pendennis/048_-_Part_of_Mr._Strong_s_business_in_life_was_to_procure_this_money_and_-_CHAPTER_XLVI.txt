We’ve lost track of good old noble Henry Foker for a few pages, so let’s catch up: true to his brand of stubborn, no-restraint constancy, he’s been fully occupied chasing the great all-consuming love that’s taken over every spare brain cell he’s got.

I’d beg all my young readers currently wrapped up in crush-mode to stop and do the math on how much time this little hobby eats up. Turns out pining after someone is easily the most expensive, unproductive pastime a human being can waste their life on. What *don’t* you sacrifice for it, you poor silly over-romantic lot? First off, you lose hours of precious sleep, tossing and turning replaying that one 2-second interaction you had with your crush, then stumble down to brunch at noon when the rest of the family’s long done with breakfast and off to their day. Then when you finally drag yourself to work or class, you can’t focus for a second—all your wits are glued to the thought of that person. When you’re finally done slacking through your tasks, you ditch all your friends and family just to loiter near their house, stare up at their bedroom window, or creep by their carriage in the park to get a 10-second glimpse. At night, home is boring as dirt; your mom’s chit chat makes you want to zone out, and even her best home cooking goes untouched. The whole rest of the banquet of life tastes like cardboard, except for that one single little side dish you’re fixated on. Work, family, your old hobbies, all the stuff you used to care about? Suddenly intolerable. You’re only happy when you’re chasing your flame.

That’s the kind of brain rot common to over-indulged young men, anyway—and that’s exactly where H. Foker was at. The guy was raised to give in to every silly urge he had, so of course he threw himself headfirst into this one with his usual self-absorbed gusto. No need for worldly folks to side-eye him for turning into love’s little doormat, either, just because he once gave his buddy Arthur Pendennis loads of very sensible love advice. Who among us hasn’t handed out perfect, airtight advice to our friends? Who hasn’t stood on a soapbox preaching, then turned around and done the exact dumb thing we warned them against?

Sure, you madam reading this are probably a perfect little saint, never had a wrong thought in your frigid, spotless little life. And you, sir? You’re so level-headed no silly crush could ever throw off your work rhythm at the office or your stock trades. You’re too tough to need anyone’s sympathy, right? Fine, we won’t give you any. We’ll save all our pity for the rest of us regular schmucks, the ones who stumble, mess up, get back up, and trudge along with everyone else. You never trip, so why would you need a hand? Your perfect serenity never gets clouded by passion, tempted by sin, or gnawed at by guilt. Compassion would just be rude to a perfect angel like you—but also, hanging out with you is insufferable. You’re so high up on the pedestal of your own virtue you’re basically all alone, and none of us regular mortals can bother to climb up just to chat. Bye Felicia. We’re sticking to the messy normal folks in this story, none of whom are perfect—okay, maybe there’s one little sweet character who’s almost perfect, but she’s so humble she thinks she’s a giant sinner, so she doesn’t count.

That near-perfect girl’s not in London right now, and she’s definitely not the one Foker’s losing his mind over. But who needs to be an angel to get worshipped like one? The human race has wild, diverse tastes, after all. No one is too ugly, too old, too dumb, too pompous, too much of a total idiot to get adored. I’ve read stories of two elderly convicts, a pickpocket and his wife, both ugly, uneducated, argumentative drunks, whose love was every bit as grand as Antony and Cleopatra’s. Everyone knows the story of the tiny Polish dwarf Count Borulawski who had the most beautiful baroness in Dresden’s court head over heels for him. And just the other day, teen Cornet Tozer ran off with Mrs. Battersby, who was old enough to be his mom. No poverty, no shame, no crime is bad enough that someone won’t cling to it, hug it tighter than they’d ever hug virtue, out of that ridiculous, stubborn, wonderful human capacity for stupid faithful love.

So there was Henry Foker, pining after his crush, cursing every force keeping him away from her. When Lord Gravesend and his family high-tailed it back to their country estate (leaving their proxy with the stuffy old Lord Bagwig), Harry hung around London way longer than he had any business staying. His fiancée, his cousin Lady Ann, couldn’t have cared less that he was lingering—she didn’t miss him for a second. Wherever Miss Blanche Amory went, this smitten fool followed her around. The whole ton knew he was engaged to his cousin, though, so he had to hide his little passion under wraps. It was so pent up inside him it’s a wonder he didn’t blow up one day, screaming his secret love to the whole street and then collapse from the relief.

One beautiful June evening, Gaunt House threw the ball of the century. The next day’s newspapers printed nearly two full columns of all the fancy nobles and rich gals lucky enough to score an invite. Sir Francis and Lady Clavering and Miss Blanche Amory were there—Major Pendennis, our relentless social climber, had wrangled them tickets. So were our two lads Arthur Pendennis and Harry Foker. Both of them showed up ready to dance as many sets with Blanche as humanly possible. The good Major took it upon himself to chaperone Lady Clavering, and immediately herded her straight to the best part of the whole mansion: the refreshment room. There, surrounded by old master paintings, giant solid gold platters, towers of flowers, and thousands of wax candles, an all-night supper was spread that cost more than most people’s annual rent. We don’t need to name exactly how many jellies, creams, peach tarts, grapes, pâtés, cups of tea, and glasses of champagne Lady Clavering polished off. No one knows how much the Major suffered trailing after her, flagging down every butler and maid to bring her more snacks, with the patience of a saint. He never complained, never let the agony show on his face, just kept carrying plate after plate over to the Begum.

Mr. Wagg tried to keep count of all the dishes Lady Clavering ate, but after a few glasses of champagne his math skills went totally out the window, and at the end of the night he told Lady Steyne’s doctor Honeyman to make sure he checked on the Begum the next day to confirm she hadn’t exploded from overeating.

Sir Francis Clavering wandered in, skulked around the fancy rooms for a bit, decided all this pomp and fancy company was not his vibe, tossed back a couple glasses of wine at the buffet, and bailed straight for Jermyn Street, where his buddies Loder, Punter, little Moss Abramns, and Captain Skewball were waiting for him around the green gambling table. The rattle of dice and their friendly banter soon got his usual weak little grin back on his face.

At one point, Mr. Pynsent came over to claim Blanche for a dance he’d asked her for earlier. But he and Arthur had already exchanged nasty glares across the dance floor, so Arthur jumped up first, claimed Blanche was his partner for that exact set. Pynsent bit his lip, glowered even harder, bowed super sarcastically, and backed off, grumbling that he’d give up his spot. Some men are just permanently in your way no matter what. Pynsent and Pen both thought the other was the worst person alive, and acted accordingly.
“Ugh, that stuck-up little provincial idiot,” Pynsent thought. “He wrote a cheap two-penny novel and now his head’s so far up his own arse a good kick would knock that conceit right out of him.”
“That man is the most insufferable idiot on the planet,” Pen said out loud to Blanche, “His soul lives in Downing Street, his neckcloth is made of office foolscap, his hair is dyed with sand, his legs are rulers, his insides are all tape and sealing wax. He was a prig in his cradle, and hasn’t laughed a single time in his life except three times at the exact same terrible joke his boss told. I like that man about as much as I like cold boiled veal.”
Blanche, of course, gasped and called him wicked, mechant, perfectly horrible, and teased him about what mean things he’d say about her when her back was turned.
“Say? I’d say you’ve got the prettiest figure and the tiniest waist in the whole world, Blanche—Miss Amory, sorry, my bad. C’mon, another turn. This waltz music could make a fat alderman get up and dance.”
“Aw, are you not tripping over your own feet mid-waltz anymore?” Blanche teased, looking up at him all arch and cheeky.
“People trip and get back up again in life, you know,” Pen said. “I used to call you Blanche back in the old days, it’s still the prettiest name I know. And yeah, I’ve had lots of practice since then.”
“Loads of different partners, I bet,” Blanche said, with a fake little dramatic sigh and a shrug. And she was right—Pen had gotten a whole lot of waltz practice in the years since they’d known each other, so he was actually pretty good now.

If Pen was chattery and cheeky, Harry Foker, normally the most talkative, friendly guy alive, turned totally silent and mopey the second he was dancing with Blanche. To hold her tiny waist felt like pure heaven, to spin around the room with her was delirium—but what on earth could he possibly say that was good enough for her? What bit of conversation could he bring that was worthy of a witty, beautiful queen like Blanche? She did all the talking, when she was dancing with him. She asked him how his cute little pony was, looked at him with such soft, regretful kindness, sighed so delicately when he offered to let her ride it, and turned him down. “I never have anyone to ride with in London,” she said. “Mamma’s too nervous, and she thinks her figure looks terrible on a horse. Sir Francis never rides with me. He loves me exactly the way a stepfather loves a stepdaughter. Oh, wouldn’t it be wonderful to have a real father, Mr. Foker?”
“Uh, yeah, it’s pretty great,” Harry said, who’d had a very calm, unremarkable relationship with his own dad. Blanche immediately dropped her sad sentimental act, her grey eyes twinkling so hard they both burst out laughing. Harry relaxed completely, and started chattering away nonstop, that sweet silly Foker banter, full of phrases you’d never find in a dictionary, rambling stories about his horses, his life, and all the other people waltzing past them at the ball, who he gossiped about with zero filter and lots of goofy humor.

When the conversation started lulling and Harry’s bashfulness crept back in, Blanche knew exactly how to get him talking again. She asked him about his family estate Logwood, if it was pretty, if he was a hunter, if he thought women should hunt too (she was all ready to say she adored hunting). But Foker said he hated sporty ladies, and pointed out Lady Bullfinch walking past, who he’d once seen out on a hunt smoking a cigar, calling her a total horse godmother. Blanche immediately said she thought hunting was disgusting, that the thought of killing a cute little fox made her shiver, and Foker laughed so hard he started waltzing with more energy and grace than ever.

At the very end of that last waltz, Blanche asked him about his family’s big house Drummington, if it was nice, said she’d heard all his cousins were super accomplished, that she’d met Lord Erith, and asked which of his cousins was his favorite. “Oh it’s Lady Ann, isn’t it?” she teased. “I can tell by the way you’re blushing.” Then she said she was tired of dancing, it was way too late, she had to go find her mamma. Before Harry could say a single word, she slipped right out of his arm, grabbed Pen’s instead (he was strutting across the dance floor at that exact moment), and yelled “Mamma! Take me to mamma right now, dear Mr. Pendennis!” She was already halfway across the room before Harry could process what just happened, left standing there stunned like she’d shot an arrow straight through his chest.

Lord Steyne, decked out in all his state regalia with the garter and sash, bald head and bright shining eyes, and that little ring of red sideburns circling his face, looked incredibly grand on a big occasion like this. He totally bowled over Lady Clavering when he came over to introduce himself, at Major Pendennis’s humble little request. He handed her a glass of wine with his own regal hand, said he’d heard all about her charming daughter, and asked to be introduced to her. Right on cue, Arthur walked over with Blanche on his arm.

The lord bowed super deep, and Blanche curtsied so low you’d think she was going to touch the floor. He shook Pen’s hand, said he’d read his novel, that it was delightfully wicked and clever, and asked Blanche if she’d read it. Pen cringed and blushed so hard his ears turned pink—because Blanche, with little black ringlets and a few details changed, was very obviously the heroine Neaera in Pen’s *Walter Lorraine*.

Blanche had read it. The look in her eyes said she adored every second of it. Once that little bit of performance was done, the Marquis of Steyne bowed again to both Lady Clavering and Blanche, and wandered off to say hi to the rest of his guests. The second his back was turned, both mom and daughter were gushing about how amazing and noble the Marquis was. “He said you two make a very nice couple,” Major Pendennis whispered to Lady Clavering. Did he really? She was over the moon. All the flattery and fancy food and being noticed by a marquis had put her in the best mood ever. She laughed, winked, nodded knowingly at Pen, tapped his arm with her fan, tapped Blanche, tapped the Major—her joy was boundless, and she showed it in the loudest, most unapologetic way possible.

When the party trundled down the giant staircase of Gaunt House, the sun was already up, pale and bright over the black bare trees in the square, the sky streaked soft pink. Half the people who’d been at the ball looked absolutely ghastly in the morning light—especially that poor devoted Major. He’d spent all night running after Lady Clavering, feeding her every snack she wanted and every compliment she loved, and he was a wreck. The circles under his eyes were deep brown, his puffy orbs looked exactly like the plover eggs Lady Clavering had eaten half a dozen of the night before, the wrinkles in his old face were deep furrows, and little silvery stubble glinted on his chin, next to his dyed whiskers which had gone totally lank and lost their curl.

There he stood, suffering in total silence, no complaints, for hours past when he should have been in bed. He knew he looked terrible (he could see all the other partygoers his age looked just as rough, after all). His rheumatism was twinging in his back and knees, his feet were burning inside his fancy tight patent boots, and he’d even forced himself to eat a little bit of the supper even though he knew it gave him indigestion, just to keep Lady Clavering happy. If the gods admire people who suffer through hardship bravely, then the god of social climbing Major Pendennis worshipped must have been looking down at him totally approving. Martyrs exist for every religion, after all.

The two Pendennises (young Pen and old Major) waited with the Clavering women until their carriage pulled up. The Major’s martyrdom was over, because the nice Begum insisted on dropping him off at his place in Bury Street on her way home. He climbed into the back seat after a couple of wimpy little bows and his last polite thank you speech, determined to keep up his perfectly proper persona right up until he collapsed in his bed. The Begum waved her chubby little hand goodbye to Arthur and Foker, and Blanche smiled sleepily at the two boys out the carriage window, wondering if she looked pale and pretty under her pink hood, and if that was just the ball mirrors playing tricks or actual tiredness making her look wan.

Arthur probably noticed that Blanche’s complexion looked a little yellow, but he wasn’t fooled by any mirror tricks. Our young worldling had very sharp eyes, and he saw Blanche’s face almost exactly as it was, no rose-colored glasses. But poor smitten Foker? Blanche’s face glowed so bright to him he couldn’t see a single flaw in it, same as he couldn’t find a flaw in the sun that was just rising over the rooftops.

Moralists will tut-tut that Pen picked up some very bad late-night London habits, often getting into bed at the exact same time that early-rising country folk were pouring their first cup of morning tea. People get used to any schedule, after all. Newspaper editors, Covent Garden market sellers, night cabbies, late-night ball attendees—they’re all wide awake at 3 or 4 a.m. when normal people are snoring their heads off. We mentioned last chapter Pen was in a very giddy, loose mood these days, more than happy to smoke a cigar and say whatever was on his mind.

So Foker and Pen walked away from Gaunt House, smoking, Pen chattering nonstop and Foker looking like he was dying to say something important. Pen got extra snarky and fancy whenever he spent time around rich noble folks, copying their little airs and graces, and his overactive imagination made him fully believe he was a Very Important Person right now. He ranted and made fun of everyone he’d seen at the ball: Lady John Turnbull’s terrible French she insisted on dropping into every conversation, no matter how much everyone hated it, Mrs. Slack Roper’s ridiculous costume and fake jewelry, the old dandies and the young dandies, he roasted everyone in sight.
