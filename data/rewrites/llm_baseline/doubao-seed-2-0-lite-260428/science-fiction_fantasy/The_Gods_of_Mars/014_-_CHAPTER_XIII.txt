Xodar stared at me, jaw slack, as I recounted every second of the arena rites: the sword pressed to Issus’s wrinkled throat, the way she’d screamed not with divine wrath but the petty, spitting fury of a cornered old tyrant. For all his private doubt that the self-proclaimed goddess was anything more than a fraud, he could scarcely wrap his head around the fact any mortal had dared raise a blade to her and not been reduced to smoldering ash on the spot.

“It is the final proof,” he breathed at last. “No lingering scrap of my old superstition survives. Issus is nothing but a cruel, power-hungry crone, propped up by centuries of carefully orchestrated lies that have kept every last soul on Barsoom trapped in religious bondage.”

“For all that, she still rules every inch of this sunken underworld,” I said. “We leave the first chance we get—tonight, if possible.”

He barked a sharp, disbelieving laugh. “Good luck finding that chance. No prisoner of the First Born has ever broken free of Omean in all recorded history.”

“Tonight will be as good a night as any,” I told him. “Dark’s falling fast. Can you swim?”

“A slimy silian that haunts the black depths of Korus couldn’t outswim me,” he grinned. “I was born in these waters.”

“Perfect. Our red companion almost certainly can’t,” I said. “The outer red domains barely hold enough water to float a toy skiff. Their people haven’t seen a river or a sea in a dozen generations—deep water terrifies them. One of us will have to carry him the hundred yards out to the fliers moored off the dock. I’d hoped we could make the whole swim submerged, but there’s no way he’d survive that.”

“The red boy comes with us?” Xodar asked. At my nod, he nodded back. “Wise. Three swords beat two, and I’ve seen that kid fight in the arena a dozen times. Before I saw you move, I’d have sworn no man could fight like that, outnumbered ten to one and still laugh in his enemies’ faces. You could be father and son—you’ve got the exact same grim, unimpressed smirk when you draw a blade.”

“Be that as it may, he’s a deadly fighter,” I said. “If Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, was here with us, we could carve a path from one end of Barsoom to the other even if every living soul on the planet came at us.”

“One day soon, they will,” Xodar said darkly. “That’s just another of Issus’s lies. She never deals directly with the outer world—she works through the Holy Therns, who are every bit as deluded as the red and green Barsoomians they dupe. Her decrees appear written in blood on their most guarded altars, seemingly out of thin air. The fools think they’re receiving divine revelations, but I’ve carried those messages for her for decades. There’s a hidden tunnel dug by our slaves ages ago, running straight from Issus’s temple to Matai Shang’s main temple, a tunnel no thern has ever guessed exists.

“The therns spread their doctrine across all the surface world, preaching the sacred pilgrimage down the River Iss to the Valley Dor and the Lost Sea of Korus, tricking thousands of poor souls into walking voluntarily to their own enslavement, swelling the therns’ coffers and their slave pens. Then the First Born raid the therns whenever we want, seizing their wealth, their artisans, their warships, and their royal red women captives. We’re a non-productive race—labour or invention is a crime for a First Born, fit only for lower castes whose sole purpose is to keep us in idle luxury. We don’t die natural deaths, you know. Our women live forever unless we tire of them and discard them. Only Issus has cheated death entirely, for uncounted millennia.”

“Wouldn’t the rest of Barsoom live just as long, if not for the pilgrimage scam that drags every soul to Iss before they hit their thousandth year?” I asked.

“I’m certain of it,” Xodar said, quiet now. “And I’ll fight for them, to atone for all the harm I helped inflict, born of nothing but generations of lies.”

A weird, trilling call cut across the black waters of Omean—the same one I’d heard the night before, marking the end of the Barsoomian day, when the entire population of the sunken sea spreads their silk sleeping mats across their ship decks and drops straight into dreamless rest.

Our guard stepped in to make his final rounds before the surface world’s new day dawned, checked the three cells in less than a minute, and slammed the heavy iron door shut behind him. We were alone for the night.

I waited long enough for him to trudge back to the guardhouse, then clambered up to our grated window. A quarter mile out, a massive First Born battleship loomed, surrounded by a handful of smaller cruisers and single-man scout fliers. Only the battleship posted a watch, and even he was spreading his sleeping mat on his tiny upper deck platform, and seconds later he was stretched out, dead to the world. No wonder their discipline was so lax—no soul on the surface even suspected the First Born, or the Sea of Omean, existed. There was no possible enemy to guard against.

I dropped back down and Xodar described his personal flier, a custom 5-seater built to be the fastest craft on the entire sea. I recognized the unique gearing he described instantly—Kantos Kan had shown me that exact system back when we served undercover in Zodanga’s navy under Sab Than, prince of Zodanga. The First Born had stolen the design straight from Helium’s shipyards, and I knew no craft on Barsoom could outpace a Helium-engineered flier.

We agreed to wait an hour for every last straggler to fall asleep, before I snuck over the cell partitions to fetch the red youth. He was leaning back against his cell wall, staring up at Omean’s glowing phosphorescent dome, and his eyes went wide when he saw me balancing on the foot-wide wall above his cell. Then a wide, knowing grin spread across his face. He motioned for me to wait, and whispered that he’d spent months practicing jumps, and could almost leap high enough to pull himself up to the top of the partition on his own. I hung over the edge, grabbed his outstretched hand when he launched himself upward, and hauled him up beside me.

The three of us huddled in Xodar’s cell, swearing a blood oath to fight back to back, to the death, no matter what enemies came for us. Even if we escaped Omean, we knew the full weight of Barsoom’s centuries-old religious dogma would be hunting us. We agreed I’d pilot the flier, and head straight for Helium the second we broke through to the surface.

“You’re a prince of Helium?” the red boy asked, his expression strange, like he knew something he wasn’t saying. I didn’t press, preoccupied with our escape plans, and the moment passed.

We moved out then. I used my harness straps as a rope to haul Xodar up onto the partition wall with us, then we climbed the outer prison wall one by one, lowering each other to the ground. The sentry on his slow, snail-paced beat rounded the far end of the prison and vanished from sight, and we walked slow and casual the hundred yards toward the water, right past the guardhouse full of sleeping First Born soldiers.

Xodar and the boy slipped silently over the dock edge first, clinging to the metal mooring rings at the water line, only their noses and mouths sticking above the dark surface of Omean. I hesitated for half a second, staring at the guardhouse door. We needed swords. I couldn’t leave them there, unused. The foolhardy urge won out.

I eased the door open a crack. A dozen black-skinned First Born were stretched out on their silk mats, snoring deep. A weapon rack on the far wall was lined with swords and revolvers. I pushed the door wider, and the hinge gave a high, groaning squeak. One guard stirred, and my heart stopped dead. I froze over him, hands hovering over his throat, ready to silence him before he could yell. Seconds dragged by, and he rolled over, his breathing even and deep again.

I tiptoed across the room, grabbed three long swords off the rack, and fumbled one extra revolver off its pin. Right then, the opposite door burst open. The officer of the guard stood there, face full of rage, his revolver coming up. We fired at exactly the same time. His bullet whizzed an inch past my ear, and I saw him crumple. I dove straight through the open window right behind the weapon rack, and hit the cold water of Omean before the echoes of the shots had faded.

The three of us struck out for Xodar’s flier, 100 yards out. Xodar was towing the red boy, I was carrying all three swords, and we moved slow enough that I was certain we’d be spotted any second. But we clambered over the flier’s rail entirely unseen, right before the watch on the battleship finally noticed us.

The alarm gun boomed, its thunder rolling under Omean’s rocky dome, and thousands of sleeping First Born exploded awake. Decks of hundreds of warships swarmed with armed men, launching every craft they had in pursuit. I wrenched the flier’s controls, and we rose off the water in a surge.

“Climb high!” Xodar yelled over the roar of the engines. “They can’t fire their heavy guns up toward the dome—shell fragments will rain back down on their own fleets. Our steel keel will stop all their rifle fire.”

I climbed, and we looked down to see hundreds of men leaping off their ships, swimming for their scout fliers. A 10-man cruiser rose fast directly in front of us, angled to skim over our deck and drag us down with grappling hooks. There was no way around—every other path was blocked by onrushing pursuers. I feinted like I was climbing higher, tricking the cruiser’s pilot into tilting his craft even steeper to force me up against the dome, then I slammed the throttle to maximum and dove straight for his keel at full speed.

The crash was deafening. The cruiser, already tilted at a suicidal angle, flipped completely backward. Its crew screamed as they tumbled through the air down to the water, and the wreck, propellers still spinning wildly, nosed straight down to the bottom of the sea. Our bows were crushed in, and the jolt nearly threw all three of us over the rail. I caught the red boy by his ankle half a second before he would have vanished into the dark water. Our flier careened wildly, drifting within fifty feet of the glowing dome, until I wrenched the controls back level and aimed for the black, yawning mouth of the escape shaft.

A hundred scout fliers were right on our heels. Xodar yelled that if we used only our anti-gravity rays to climb the narrow shaft, they’d overtake us in seconds, their smaller, buoyancy-aided craft rising faster than ours could. There was only one trick left.

“Hold on!” I screamed. “Both of you, hold the boy tight—we’re not using buoyancy. We’re flooring it straight up.”

We shot under the black lip of the shaft, and I cranked the speed lever all the way to its final notch. The wind screamed in our ears as we hurtled upward at nearly two hundred miles an hour, no light to guide us but the tiny, shrinking circle of phosphorescent Omean glow far below. It was blind luck, more than skill, that kept us from scraping against the rocky shaft walls that would have smashed the flier to pieces in an instant. In forty seconds flat, we burst out of the two-mile deep tunnel, out into the open air of the surface world.

And total darkness wrapped around us. No moons, no stars. I had never seen anything like it on Barsoom, where the sky is always clear enough to see every constellation at night. Then I realized: the southern summer had arrived. The polar ice cap was melting, and the rare, almost unheard of meteorological phenomenon called clouds had rolled in, swallowing the entire sky. We had broken out of the sunken prison of Omean straight into a world of total, unseen shadow.
