"Nay, I have done; you get no more of me;
And I am glad, yea glad with all my heart,
That thus so clearly I myself am free."

Margaret withdrew to her chamber, leaving Mrs. Thornton behind. Once inside, she paced the floor, a rhythmic, agitated movement that had become her habit whenever distress clouded her mind. Yet, mindful that the small house offered no privacy, her steps ceased the moment she heard the latch lift and Mrs. Thornton depart. Only then did she allow herself to sit, forcing her memory to replay the conversation, word by word, until the scene was etched in her mind.

Finally, she rose, her voice heavy with a quiet despair. "At least, her words do not strike me; they slide off, for I am innocent of the motives she imputes. Yet, it is a bitter pill to swallow that any woman—any human soul—could so readily believe such things of another. It is hard, and it is sad. Where I have erred, she does not accuse; she does not know. And he... he never told her. I should have known he would not!"

She lifted her chin, finding a strange pride in the delicacy of Mr. Thornton's conduct. But a new, sharper thought pierced her resolve. "He, too, must suspect poor Frederick of some other attachment." A flush warmed her cheeks at the admission. "I see it now. It is not merely that he knows of my deceit, but that he believes another cares for me; and that I—Oh, dear! Oh, dear! What am I to do? What do I mean? Why does it matter what he thinks, beyond the loss of his regard for my honesty? I cannot say. But I am wretched! Oh, how this past year has drained me! I have skipped from childhood to old age. I have known no youth, no womanhood; the dreams of womanhood have closed forever, for I shall never marry. I anticipate sorrows as if I were already an elder, with a tear-stained spirit. I am weary of this constant demand for strength. I could endure it for my father; that is a natural, holy duty. I could even muster the energy to resent Mrs. Thornton's unjust, impertinent suspicions. But it is crushing to feel how completely he misunderstands me. What has come over me to make me so morbid today? I do not know. I only know I cannot help it. I must yield sometimes. No, I will not," she declared, springing to her feet. "I will not dwell on myself or my station. I will not dissect my own feelings. It serves no purpose now. Someday, if I live to be an old woman, I may sit by the fire, gazing into the embers, mourning the life that might have been."

Throughout this turmoil, she hastily dressed to leave the house, pausing only to wipe her eyes with impatient gestures, knowing the tears would return despite her bravado.

"I suppose many a woman makes as tragic a mistake as I have, only discovering it too late. How proudly and impertinently I spoke to him that day! But I did not know then. It crept upon me slowly, and I cannot pinpoint its origin. Now, I will not yield. It will be difficult to treat him as before, with this miserable burden on my conscience; but I will be calm, quiet, and speak little. Though, to be sure, I may not see him; he keeps his distance, evidently. That would be worse than all. And yet, no wonder he avoids me, believing what he must."

She stepped out, moving rapidly toward the countryside, attempting to drown her reflections in the speed of her motion.

Upon her return to the doorstep, her father approached. "Good girl!" he said. "You've been to Mrs. Boucher's. I was just meaning to go there, if I had time, before dinner."

"No, papa; I have not," Margaret replied, flushing. "I never thought about her. But I will go directly after dinner; I will go while you are taking your nap."

True to her word, she visited the sick woman. Mrs. Boucher was truly ill, not merely ailing. The kind neighbor who had arrived previously had assumed charge of the household. Some children had been sent to neighbors; Mary Higgins had fetched the three youngest at dinner, and since then, Nicholas had sought the doctor. He had not yet arrived; Mrs. Boucher was dying, and all that remained was waiting. Margaret hoped to hear the doctor's opinion later, so she decided to visit the Higginses in the meantime, perhaps learning if Nicholas had managed to reach Mr. Thornton.

She found Nicholas busily spinning a penny on the dresser for the amusement of three small children who clung to him fearlessly. He, along with them, smiled at the coin's long rotation, and Margaret took this happy engagement as a good sign. When the penny finally stopped, little Johnnie began to cry.

"Come to me," Margaret said, lifting the child from the dresser and holding him close. She held her watch to his ear while asking Nicholas if he had seen Mr. Thornton.

The look on Nicholas's face shifted instantly.

"Ay!" he said. "I've seen and heard too much on him."

"He refused you, then?" Margaret asked, sorrowfully.

"To be sure. I knew he'd do it all along. It's no good expecting mercy from masters like him. You're a stranger and a foreigner, unlikely to know their ways; but I knew it."

"I am sorry I asked. Was he angry? Did he not speak to you as Hamper did?"

"He wasn't overly civil!" Nicholas spun the penny again, partly for his own amusement, partly for the children's. "Never you fret. I'm exactly where I was. I'll go on tramping tomorrow. I gave him as good as I got. I told him I didn't hold the good opinion of him that would make me come a second time on my own; but you advised me to come, and I was beholden to you."

"You told him I sent you?"

"I dunno if I called you by your name. I don't think I did. I said a woman who knew no better had advised me to come and see if there was a soft spot in his heart."

"And he—?" Margaret prompted.

"Said I should tell you to mind your own business. That's the longest spin yet, my lads. And those are civil words compared to what he used to me. But never mind. We're where we were; and I'll break stones on the road before I let these little ones starve."

Margaret placed the struggling Johnnie back on the dresser.

"I am sorry I asked you to go to Mr. Thornton's. I am disappointed in him."

A faint noise sounded behind her. Both she and Nicholas turned simultaneously to find Mr. Thornton standing there, his face a mask of displeased surprise. Acting on a swift impulse, Margaret stepped out before him, saying nothing, bowing low to conceal the sudden pallor that washed over her face. He bowed equally low in return before closing the door. As she hurried toward Mrs. Boucher's, she heard the latch click, and it seemed to complete the measure of her mortification. He, too, was annoyed to find her there.

In his heart, Mr. Thornton possessed tenderness—a "soft place," as Nicholas had called it—but he guarded it with jealous pride, keeping it sacred and safe, wary of any circumstance that might intrude. Yet, if he dreaded exposing this tenderness, he was equally eager that all men recognize his justice. He felt unjust in having given such a scornful hearing to anyone who had waited with humble patience for five hours to speak to him. That the man had spoken saucily when given the chance meant nothing to Mr. Thornton; he actually liked him for it. He was conscious of his own irritability at the time, which likely made them even. It was the five hours of waiting that struck him. He had not five hours to spare himself, but he had sacrificed an hour or two of his hard intellectual and physical labor to gather evidence regarding the truth of Higgins's story, the nature of his character, and the tenor of his life. He tried not to be, but was convinced that all Higgins had said was true. Then the conviction settled in, like a spell, touching the latent tenderness of his heart. The patience of the man, the simple generosity of the motive (for he had learned of the quarrel between Boucher and Higgins), made him forget entirely the mere reasonings of justice, leaping over them by a diviner instinct. He came to offer Higgins work, and he was more annoyed to find Margaret there than by hearing her last words; for then he understood she was the woman who had urged Higgins to approach him. He dreaded admitting any thought of her as a motive for what he was doing, simply because it was right.

"So that was the lady you spoke of as a woman?" he said indignantly to Higgins. "You might have told me who she was."

"And then, maybe, you'd have spoken of her more civilly than you did; you'd gotten a mother who might have kept your tongue in check when you were talking of women being at the root of all plagues."

"Of course you told that to Miss Hale?"

"In course I did. Leastways, I reckon I did. I told her she wasn't to meddle again in aught that concerned you."

"Whose children are those—yours?" Mr. Thornton had a pretty good notion whose they were from what he had heard, but he felt awkward turning the conversation from this unpromising beginning.

"They're not mine, and they are mine."

"They are the children you spoke of to me this morning?"

"When you said," replied Higgins, turning around with ill-smothered fierceness, "that my story might be true or might not, but it were a very unlikely one. Master, I've not forgotten."

Mr. Thornton was silent for a moment; then he said, "No more have I. I remember what I said. I spoke to you about those children in a way I had no business to. I did not believe you. I could not have taken care of another man's children myself if he had acted toward me as I hear Boucher did toward you. But I know now you spoke truth. I beg your pardon."

Higgins did not turn or immediately respond. But when he did speak, it was in a softened tone, though the words remained gruff.

"You've no business to pry into what happened between Boucher and me. He's dead, and I'm sorry. That's enough."

"So it is. Will you take work with me? That's what I came to ask."

Higgins's obstinacy wavered, recovered strength, and stood firm. He would not speak. Mr. Thornton would not ask again. Higgins's eye fell on the children.

"You've called me impudent, and a liar, and a mischief-maker, and you might have said with some truth that I was now and then given to drink. And, I've called you a tyrant, and an old bulldog, and a hard, cruel master; that's where it stands. But for the children, Master, do you think we can ever get on together?"

"Well!" said Mr. Thornton, half-laughing, "it was not my proposal that we should go together. But there's one comfort on your own showing. We neither of us can think much worse of the other than we do now."

"That's true," said Higgins, reflectively. "I've been thinking ever since I saw you what a mercy it was you didn't take me on, for that I never saw a man whom I could less abide. But that's maybe been a hasty judgment; and work's work to such as me. So, Master, I'll come; and what's more, I thank you; and that's a deal from me," he said more frankly, suddenly turning round and facing Mr. Thornton fully for the first time.

"And this is a deal from me," said Mr. Thornton, giving Higgins's hand a good grip. "Now mind you come sharp to your time," he continued, resuming the master's tone. "I'll have no laggards at my mill. What fines we have, we keep pretty sharply. And the first time I catch you making mischief, off you go. So now you know where you are."

"You spoke of my wisdom this morning. I reckon I may bring it with me; or would you rather have me about my brains?"

"'Bout your brains if you use them for meddling with my business; with your brains if you can keep to your own."

"I shall need a deal of brains to settle where my business ends and yours begins."

"Your business has not begun yet, and mine stands still for me. So good afternoon."

Just before Mr. Thornton reached Mrs. Boucher's door, Margaret emerged. She did not see him, and he followed her for several yards, admiring her light, easy walk and her tall, graceful figure. Suddenly, this simple emotion of pleasure was tainted, poisoned by jealousy. He wished to overtake her, to speak to her, to see how she would receive him now that she must know he was aware of some other attachment. He wished, too, though he was somewhat ashamed of the wish, that she should know he had justified her wisdom in sending Higgins to him for work, and that he had repented his morning's decision.

He came up to her. She started.

"Allow me to say, Miss Hale, that you were rather premature in expressing your disappointment. I have taken Higgins on."

"I am glad of it," she said coldly.

"He tells me he repeated to you what I said this morning about—" Mr. Thornton hesitated. Margaret took it up:

"About women not meddling. You had a perfect right to express your opinion, which was a very correct one, I have no doubt. But," she went on a little more eagerly, "Higgins did not quite tell you the exact truth." The word "truth" reminded her of her own untruth, and she stopped short, feeling exceedingly uncomfortable.
