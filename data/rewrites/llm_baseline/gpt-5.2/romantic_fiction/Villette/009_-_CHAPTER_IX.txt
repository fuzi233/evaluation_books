My hours, once long and vacant as an unfurnished room, were now hung round with occupation till not a strip of wall showed bare. I taught, I studied; I stitched one day into the next so closely that I could scarcely find the seam. It was pleasant to feel the mind used—scoured clean like a bright copper pan, not left in a damp corner to green with neglect. There lay before me experience, various and abundant; for Villette drew into its streets all tongues and tempers, and Madame Beck’s school was like a market where nations stood elbow to elbow. In Labassecour, equality is a habit of the hand if not a doctrine of the mouth: at the same desk a young countess and a young bourgeoise bent their heads together; and, looking only with the eye, you could not always tell which was which. Sometimes the plain one had the franker courtesy; the titled one carried the prize for a delicately mixed insolence and deceit,—so lightly poised that it might pass, to a careless observer, for mere charm.

To be just, the native Labassecouriennes had a hypocrisy of their own; only it was of a rougher grain, like coarse salt flung in without dissolving. If a lie served the day, they used it with a broad ease, as if conscience were a utensil they did not keep in the kitchen. In Madame Beck’s house—from the scullion who scoured the pots to the directress who scoured souls—no one seemed to blush at invention. “J’ai menti plusieurs fois,” went into the monthly confession like a pinch of pepper: the priest heard, unstartled, and absolved, as readily as a cook gives leave to taste. But to miss mass, or to read a chapter of a novel—that was a spoiled dish, a sin that must be scraped off and punished.

While I was yet only half awake to these rules of the place, and ignorant of the ends to which such easy falsehood could be kneaded, I did well enough. The first lessons had been like cooking over a furnace that threatened to flare up—moral heat under my feet, sparks and fumes flying into my eyes. But for me the eruption abated. I was bent on success with the obstinacy of hunger: I could not bear to be baffled by mere wanton indocility in my first effort to earn bread. Many a night I lay awake, not dreaming, but planning—how to get a hold on these mutineers, how to bring this stiff-necked tribe under steady influence.

In the first place, I saw plainly that Madame’s help was not to be counted on. Her policy was like a shopkeeper’s—keep customers pleased at any cost; and if the cost must be wrung from the teachers, so much the better. To appeal to her in any crisis was to sign one’s own dismissal. In her intercourse with the pupils she took what was pleasant and recommendatory; and left to her lieutenants all that was disagreeable—those moments when to act promptly is to incur dislike. Therefore I must rely on myself.

Imprimis—it was clear as daylight that this swinish multitude were not to be driven by force. They were to be humoured; patience must be the spoon that kept the pot from boiling over. A courteous, sedate manner impressed them; a rare flash of raillery did good, like a dash of vinegar that gives savour. But severe, continuous application—heavy demand on memory, reason, attention—they rejected outright. Give an English girl a theme, and, even if dull, she will set her teeth and labour at it; give a Labassecourienne the same, and she will laugh and toss it back, crying, “Dieu, que c’est difficile! Je n’en veux pas. Cela m’ennuie trop.”

A teacher who knew the country would take it back at once—no contest, no preaching—smooth every difficulty, mince it fine, cook it down to their level, return it thus modified, and then lay on sarcasm like salt on a raw thing that needs curing. They would feel the sting, perhaps toss their heads; but they bore no malice against this sort of attack, provided it was not sour, but hearty, and held up to them in a clear, bold type their incapacity, ignorance, and sloth. They would riot at three additional lines of a lesson; but I never knew them rebel against a wound given to self-respect. The little they had of that quality had been trained to be crushed; it rather liked the pressure of a firm heel.

By degrees, as I grew fluent and free in their language, and could use its more nervous idioms to fit their case, the elder and more intelligent began to like me in their fashion. I noticed that when once a pupil had been roused to a stirring of worthy emulation, or pricked into honest shame, she was won. If I could but once make their usually large ears burn under their thick glossy hair, all went comparatively well. Presently bouquets began to appear on my desk in the morning—little nosegays smelling of shop-flowers and damp paper, with here and there a bruised leaf that told of hasty theft from a conservatory border. By way of acknowledgment I sometimes walked with a select few during recreation.

It happened, once or twice, that in conversation I made an unpremeditated attempt to straighten some of their twisted notions of principle. Especially I spoke my mind of the evil and baseness of a lie. In an unguarded moment I said that, of two errors, I thought falsehood worse than an occasional lapse in church-attendance. These poor girls were trained to carry to Catholic ears whatever the Protestant teacher said, as if each syllable were an herb to be tested for poison. An edifying consequence followed. Something—unseen, indefinite, nameless—stole between me and these my best pupils: the bouquets still came, but speech grew difficult, as if a cloth had been thrust between our mouths. When I paced the alleys or sat in the berceau, no girl could come to my right hand but, as if summoned by a bell I did not hear, a teacher appeared at my left. And, wonderful to relate, Madame’s shoes of silence brought her continually to my back—quick, noiseless, unexpected as a wandering breeze that carries away the last warm scent of bread before you have tasted it.

Once, with naïve directness, the opinion of my Catholic acquaintance concerning my spiritual prospects was set before me. A pensionnaire to whom I had rendered some small service sat beside me and exclaimed:

“Mademoiselle, what a pity you are a Protestant!”

“Why, Isabelle?”

“Parceque, quand vous serez morte—vous brûlerez tout de suite dans l’Enfer.”

“Croyez-vous?”

“Certainement que j’y crois: tout le monde le sait; et d’ailleurs le prêtre me l’a dit.”

Isabelle was blunt, odd, and fearless; she added, under her breath, as if imparting a household counsel:

“Pour assurer votre salut là-haut, on ferait bien de vous brûler toute vive ici-bas.”

I laughed; it was impossible not to.

Has the reader forgotten Miss Ginevra Fanshawe? If so, she must be brought forward again; for she was now a thriving pupil in Madame Beck’s establishment. Two or three days after my sudden settlement in the Rue Fossette, she arrived; and when she encountered me, she showed so little surprise that I could only suppose astonishment was a luxury she had never cultivated. She must have good blood; no duchess could have been more perfectly, radically, unaffectedly nonchalante. Wonder in her was but a weak, transient ripple. Most other faculties seemed made of the same thin stuff—likings and dislikings, loves and hates, cobweb and gossamer. Yet there was one thing about her strong and durable: her selfishness.

She was not proud; and, bonne d’enfants as I was, she would at once have made of me a friend and confidant. She plagued me with vapid complaints—school quarrels, household economy: the cookery was not to her taste; the people about her, teachers and pupils, she held despicable because they were foreigners. She scolded Friday’s salt fish and hard eggs; she poured contempt on the soup, the bread, the coffee. At first I bore it; but iteration is like a bad odour—at last it penetrates through patience. I grew crusty and set her down; and, in truth, I should have done so from the beginning, for a salutary reprimand always agreed with her, as some stomachs are better for a bitter draught.

Longer still I had to endure her demands in the way of work. Her wardrobe, as to what the world sees, was well and elegantly supplied; but other habiliments were less cared for, and what she had needed frequent repair. She hated needle-drudgery, and brought me heaps of hose and the like to be mended. After some weeks, seeing compliance harden into expectation, I told her plainly she must mend her own garments. She cried at the news, accused me of ceasing to be her friend, and tried hysteria as a sauce to soften my resolution; but I held by it and let the tears fall where they would.

And yet—how pretty she was. How charming on a sunny Sunday morning, well dressed and well humoured—pale lilac silk, fair long curls lying on her white shoulders. Sunday was a holiday she always spent with friends in town; and among these friends, she soon let me understand, was one who would fain become something more. By glimpses and hints, and by the buoyancy of her look and manner, it was proved that ardent admiration—perhaps genuine love—was at her command. She called her suitor “Isidore”; not his real name, she hinted, but one she chose because his own was “not pretty.” Once, after she had been boasting of the vehemence of “Isidore’s” attachment, I asked if she loved him in return.

“Comme cela,” said she. “He is handsome, and he loves me to distraction, so that I am well amused. Ça suffit.”

As she carried on longer than her fickle tastes had led me to expect, I one day made serious inquiry whether the gentleman was likely to be approved by her parents, and especially by her uncle—on whom she depended. She allowed that this was doubtful, as she did not believe “Isidore” had much money.

“Do you encourage him?” I asked.

“Furieusement sometimes,” said she.

“Without being certain you will be permitted to marry him?”

“Oh, how dowdyish you are! I don’t want to be married. I am too young.”

“But if he loves you as much as you say, and it comes to nothing, he will be miserable.”

“Of course he will break his heart. I should be shocked and disappointed if he didn’t.”

“I wonder whether this M. Isidore is a fool?” I said.

“He is, about me; but wise in other things, à ce qu’on dit. Mrs. Cholmondeley considers him extremely clever: she says he will push his way by his talents. All I know is, that he does little more than sigh in my presence, and that I can wind him round my little finger.”

Wishing to get a clearer idea of this love-stricken Isidore, whose footing seemed to me insecure, I asked for a personal description. But she could not describe; she had neither words nor the power of putting them in a picture. Indeed she seemed hardly to have noticed him: nothing of his face, no change of expression, had lodged in her memory. “Beau, mais plutôt bel homme que joli garçon”—that was all. My patience often flagged in listening to her; but one thing kept my attention. All her hints, all her careless details, went to prove—unconsciously, to my thinking—that his homage was offered with delicacy and respect. I told her plainly I believed him much too good for her, and with equal plainness that she was but a vain coquette. She laughed, shook curls from her eyes, and danced away as if I had praised her.

Ginevra’s studies were little better than nominal. There were three things only she practised in earnest—music, singing, dancing; also the embroidering of fine cambric handkerchiefs she could not afford to buy ready worked. History, geography, grammar, arithmetic—those she left undone or got others to do. Much of her time was spent in visiting. Madame, aware that her stay was limited and would not be extended for progress, allowed her great licence. Mrs. Cholmondeley—her chaperon—a gay fashionable lady, invited her whenever she entertained, and sometimes took her to evening parties. Ginevra approved this life; it had but one inconvenience: she must be well dressed, and she had not money for variety. All her thoughts ran upon this want. It was strange to see how quickened her otherwise indolent mind became on the matter—how necessity, like a loud street-organ under one’s window, forces attention and drives the same tune through the brain till you cannot help but hum it.

She begged boldly of Mrs. Cholmondeley—boldly, not with reluctant shame, but in this strain:

“My darling Mrs. C., I have nothing in the world fit to wear for your party next week; you must give me a book-muslin dress, and then a ceinture bleu céleste: do—there’s an angel! will you?”

The “darling Mrs. C.” yielded at first; but as requests multiplied with indulgence, she was soon obliged—like all Miss Fanshawe’s friends—to resist encroachment. After a while I heard no more of her gifts; yet the visits went on, and the absolutely necessary dresses still appeared; also many expensive etceteras—gloves, bouquets, even trinkets. These things, contrary to her custom and even nature—for she was not secretive—were kept sedulously out of sight for a time. But one evening, when she was going to a large party where particular care was demanded, she could not resist coming to my chamber to display herself.

Beautiful she looked: so young, so fresh, with that delicacy of skin and flexibility of shape which is English, and not common among continental charms. Her dress was new, costly, perfect—complete in those finishing details that cost so much and make the whole effect tastefully finished, like a dish carried out with its final glaze and garnish.

I viewed her from head to foot. She turned airily round, that I might survey her on all sides. Her small blue eyes sparkled; she was in her best humour, conscious of her power. She was going to bestow on me a kiss—her schoolgirl fashion of sharing triumph—but I held her off.

“Steady!” I said. “Let us be steady, and know what we are about, and find out the meaning of our magnificence.”

“Shall I do?” was her question.

“Do? There are different ways of doing; and, upon my word, I don’t understand yours.”

“But how do I look?”

“You look well dressed.”

She found the praise too cool, and began to point out each ornament—each ribbon, each fold, each sparkle. “Look at this parure,” said she. “The brooch, the ear-rings, the bracelets: no one in the school has such a set—not Madame herself.”

“I see them all.” (Pause.) “Did M. de Bassompierre give you those jewels?”

“My uncle knows nothing about them.”

“Were they presents from Mrs. Cholmondeley?”

“Not they indeed. Mrs. Cholmondeley is a mean, stingy creature; she never gives me anything now.”

I did not choose to ask further; I turned abruptly away, as if a sudden harsh sound had jarred through the room and set my teeth on edge.

“Now, old Crusty—old Diogenes,”—these were her familiar names for me when we disagreed—“what is the matter now?”

“Take yourself away. I have no pleasure in looking at you or your parure.”

For an instant she looked surprised.

“What now, Mother Wisdom? I have not got into debt for it—that is, not for the jewels, nor the gloves, nor the bouquet. My dress is certainly not paid for, but uncle de Bassompierre will pay it in the bill: he never notices items, but just looks at the total; and he is so rich, one need not care about a few guineas more or less.”

“Will you go? I want to shut the door…. Ginevra, people may tell you you are very handsome in that ball-attire; but in my eyes you will never look so pretty as you did in the gingham gown and plain straw bonnet you wore when I first saw you.”

“Other people have not your puritanical tastes,” she retorted, angry. “And besides, I see no right you have to sermonize me.”

“Certainly! I have little right; and you, perhaps, have still less to come flourishing and fluttering into my chamber—a mere jay in borrowed plumes. I have not the least respect for your feathers, Miss Fanshawe; and especially for the peacock’s eyes you call a parure: pretty enough, if you had bought them with money that was your own, and which you could spare; not pretty at all under present circumstances.”

“On est là pour Mademoiselle Fanshawe!” called the portress; and away she tripped.

The semi-mystery of the parure was not solved till two or three days later, when she chose to confess.

“You need not be sulky with me,” she began, “in the idea that I am running somebody—papa or M. de Bassompierre—deeply into debt. I assure you nothing remains unpaid for but the few dresses I have lately had: all the rest is settled.”

There lay the riddle; for I knew her means were a few shillings, and I knew how carefully she hoarded even those.

“Écoutez!” she went on, drawing close, coaxing, for my disapproval inconvenienced her; she liked me to listen, even if I listened only to blame. “Écoutez, chère grogneuse! I will tell you all how and about it; and you will then see not only how right the whole thing is, but how cleverly managed. In the first place, I must go out. Papa himself said he wished me to see something of the world; he remarked to Mrs. Cholmondeley that though I was sweet enough, I had rather a bread-and-butter-eating, schoolgirl air, which he desired me to get rid of by an introduction to society here, before my regular début in England. Well then, if I go out, I must dress. Mrs. Cholmondeley is turned shabby and will give nothing more; it would be too hard upon uncle to make him pay for all the things I need: that you can’t deny—that agrees with your own preachments. Well, but somebody who heard me—quite by chance, I assure you—complaining to Mrs. Cholmondeley of my distressed circumstances, and what straits I was put to for an ornament or two—somebody, far from grudging a present, was quite delighted at the idea of being permitted to offer some trifle. You should have seen what a blanc-bec he looked when he first spoke of it: how he hesitated and blushed, and positively trembled for fear of a repulse.”

“That will do, Miss Fanshawe. I suppose I am to understand that M. Isidore is the benefactor: that it is from him you have accepted that costly parure; that he supplies your bouquets and your gloves?”

“You express yourself so disagreeably,” she complained, “one hardly knows how to answer. What I mean to say is, that I occasionally allow Isidore the pleasure and honour of expressing his homage by the offer of a trifle.”

“It comes to the same thing…. Now, Ginevra, to speak the plain truth, I don’t very well understand these matters; but I believe you are doing very wrong—seriously wrong. Perhaps, however, you now feel certain you will be able to marry M. Isidore; your parents and uncle have consented, and, for your part, you love him entirely?”

“Mais pas du tout!”—she always fled into French when about to be most heartless. “Je suis sa reine, mais il n’est pas mon roi.”

“Excuse me: I must believe this is nonsense and coquetry. There is nothing great about you; yet you are above profiting by the good nature and purse of a man to whom you feel indifference. You love M. Isidore far more than you think, or will avow.”

“No. I danced with a young officer the other night, whom I love a thousand times more than he. I often wonder why I feel so very cold to Isidore, for everybody says he is handsome, and other ladies admire him; but, somehow, he bores me: let me see now how it is….”

She tried, for once, to think; and I encouraged her, for if she could but taste her own motives clearly—name the flavour of them—she might perhaps loathe them.

“Yes,” I said, “try to get a clear idea of the state of your mind. To me it seems in a great mess—chaotic as a rag-bag.”

“It is something in this fashion,” she cried ere long: “the man is too romantic and devoted, and he expects something more of me than I find it convenient to be. He thinks I am perfect—furnished with sterling qualities and solid virtues such as I never had, nor intend to have. Now, one can’t help, in his presence, rather trying to justify his good opinion; and it does so tire one to be goody, and to talk sense—for he really thinks I am sensible. I am far more at my ease with you, old lady—you dear crosspatch—who take me at my lowest, and know me coquettish, and ignorant, and flirting, and fickle, and silly, and selfish, and all the other sweet things you and I have agreed to be part of my character.”

“This is all very well,” I said, striving to keep severity steady, though her whimsical candour shook it, “but it does not alter that wretched business of the presents. Pack them up, Ginevra, like a good honest girl, and send them back.”

“Indeed I won’t,” she answered stoutly.

“Then you are deceiving M. Isidore. It stands to reason that by accepting his presents you make him understand he will one day receive an equivalent—in your regard…”

“But he won’t,” she broke in. “He has his equivalent now, in the pleasure of seeing me wear them—quite enough for him: he is only bourgeois.”

That phrase—so arrogant, so empty—shut the last little gate of indulgence in me. She rattled on, untroubled; and while her voice ran, light and loud as an ill-played air, I heard behind it the sharper things the house was full of: the constant creak of doors opening and closing without kindness; the tap of shoes that came and went as silently as spies; the murmurs of borrowed phrases; the rustle of confessions made habitual till words lost their weight. In such a place even one’s own mother-tongue seemed sometimes to shrink and grow hoarse, as if it could not keep its true meaning alive amid so much practised gloss.
