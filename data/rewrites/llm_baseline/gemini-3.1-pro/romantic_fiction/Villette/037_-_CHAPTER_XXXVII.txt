It was entirely within the cyclical, seasonal rhythms of her lineage for Paulina to let the inkwell dry and decline further correspondence with Graham until her father, the steward of their domestic estate, had sanctioned the intercourse. But Dr. Bretton could not remain sequestered within a league of the Hôtel Crécy; he hungered too deeply for her presence to observe the fallow period. Both lovers meant at first, I believe, to maintain a polite distance, preserving the quiet, ancestral rhythm of the household. They kept their intention so far as demonstrative courtship went, but in the untranslatable subjective experience of feeling, their roots soon entwined.

All that was best in Graham sought Paulina. Whatever in him was noble awoke and ripened in her presence. With his past admiration of Miss Fanshawe, I suppose his intellect had little to do, but his whole mind, and his highest sensory tastes, came into question now. These, like all his faculties, were active, eager for rich nutriment, and profoundly alive to the complex flavors of true companionship. 

I cannot say that Paulina designedly led him to talk of literature, or formally proposed to herself the task of cultivating his mind like a precious heritage seed. She thought him already perfect. It was Graham himself who, at first by the merest chance, mentioned some text he had been digesting, and when her response sounded a welcome acoustic harmony of sympathies—something deeply resonant to his soul—he talked on, pouring forth a richer, more evocative linguistic broth than he had ever offered before. She listened with a gourmand’s delight, and answered with animation. In each successive answer, Graham heard a sonic frequency waxing finer and finer to his ear; in each he found a suggestive, persuasive native dialect that opened a scarce-known cellar of vintage reserves within him, revealing unsuspected power in his own mind, and a latent, slow-simmering goodness in his heart. 

Each savored the way in which the other talked. The unamplified acoustic purity of the voice, the precise diction, the carefully sourced expressions pleased them; each keenly relished the layered flavor profile of the other’s wit. They met each other’s meaning with strange quickness, their thoughts paired like perfectly balanced aromatics. Graham possessed a wealth of natural effervescence; Paulina held no such inherent flow of animal spirits—unstimulated, she inclined to the quietude of a cool, stone larder—but now she seemed merry as a lark. In her lover’s genial presence, she blossomed like sun-warmed fruit. How beautiful she grew in her rich, sensory happiness, I can hardly translate into the dominant lexicon, but I wondered to see her. As to that gentle ice of hers—that native reserve on which she had depended—where was it now? Graham brought with him a generous, consuming heat that soon thawed her timid, self-imposed restriction, melting it like spun sugar into a rich syrup.

Now the old Bretton days were unspooled, interwoven timelines bleeding seamlessly into the present. They spoke of the past brokenly at first, with the smiling diffidence of tasting a new vintage, then with opening candour and a robust, full-bodied confidence. Graham had forged a better opportunity than the one he had wished me to provide; he had earned independence from the collateral help that disobliging Lucy had refused. All his reminiscences of “little Polly” found their proper expression in his own pleasant acoustic tones, seasoned by his own handsome lips. 

More than once when we were alone, Paulina would tell me how wonderful it was to discover the meticulous, food-historian-like accuracy of his memory. While he looked at her, recollections would seem to quicken and ferment in his mind. He reminded her that she had once gathered his head in her arms, caressing his leonine graces, and cried out, “Graham, I *do* like you!” He recalled the tactile sensation of her small hands smoothing his cheek, the scent of sweet milk on her breath as she climbed by a footstool to his knee. He remembered the touch of her small forefinger placed tremblingly in the cleft in his chin, the childish lisp with which she named it “a pretty dimple,” seeking his eyes and telling him he had a “nice, strange face; far nicer, far stranger, than either his mamma or Lucy Snowe.”

“Child as I was,” remarked Paulina, “I wonder how I dared be so venturous. To me he seems now all sacred, his locks are inaccessible, and, Lucy, I feel a sort of fear when I look at his firm, marble chin. Women are called beautiful, Lucy; he is not like a woman, therefore I suppose he is not beautiful, but what is he, then? Do other people see him with my eyes? Do *you* admire him?”

“I’ll tell you what I do, Paulina,” was once my answer to her attempts to translate the untranslatable. “*I never see him*. I looked at him twice or thrice about a year ago, before he recognised me, and then I shut my eyes. If he were to cross their balls twelve times between each day’s sunset and sunrise, except from the deep cellar of memory, I should hardly know what shape had gone by.”

“Lucy, what do you mean?” said she, the sonic vibration of her voice dropping under her breath.

“I mean that I value the quiet acoustics of my own vision, and dread the deafening roar of being struck stone blind.”

It was best to answer her strongly, cutting through the romantic excess to silence for ever the passionate confidences which left her lips like sweet honey, but dropped into my ear like scalding, molten lead. To me, she commented no more on her lover’s physical plating.

Yet speak of him she would. Sometimes she used the quiet, brief phrases of her native dialect; sometimes she spoke with a tenderness of cadence and a musicality of voice exquisite in itself, pure as the resonance of a struck crystal glass, but which chafed my own psychological well-being miserably. I gave her stern looks and words in return; but cloudless happiness had dazzled her native clear sight, and she only thought Lucy—fitful.

“Spartan girl! Proud Lucy!” she would say, smiling at me. “Graham says you are the most peculiar, capricious little woman he knows; but yet you are excellent; we both think so.”

“You both think you know not what,” said I, rejecting the superficial commercialization of their mutual praise. “Have the goodness to make me as little the subject of your mutual talk and thoughts as possible. I have my own quiet harvest, my life apart from yours.”

“But ours, Lucy, is a beautiful life, or it will be; and you shall share it.”

“I shall share no man’s or woman’s life in this world, as you understand sharing. I think I have one friend of my own, but am not sure; and till I *am* sure, I live solitary, preserving my own acoustic ecology.”

“But solitude is sadness.”

“Yes; it is sadness. Life, however, has worse than that. Deeper than melancholy, lies the crushing weight of heart-break.”

“Lucy, I wonder if anybody will ever comprehend you altogether.”

There is, in lovers, a certain infatuation of egotism; they demand a witness to their feast, cost that witness what it may. Paulina had forbidden letters, yet Dr. Bretton wrote; she had resolved against correspondence, yet she answered, were it only to chide. She showed me these letters; with something of the spoiled child’s wilfulness and the heiress’s imperiousness, she *made* me read them. As I read Graham’s, I scarce wondered at her exaction. They were fine letters—steeped in a rich, masculine terroir, modest yet gallant. Hers must have appeared to him beautiful, though she struggled against the subtle power dynamics embedded in his dominant lexicon. She had proposed to herself the task of hiding her deep feeling, bridling her lover’s ardour with cool reserve. But Graham had become the very sustenance of her life; he drew her like a powerful magnet. For her, there was an unspeakable, untranslatable influence in all he uttered, wrote, thought, or looked. With this unconfessed confession, her letters glowed like a slow-roasting hearth fire; it kindled them, from greeting to adieu.

“I wish papa knew; I *do* wish papa knew!” began now to be her anxious murmur, vibrating with a nervous frequency. “I wish, and yet I fear. I can hardly keep Graham back from telling him. There is nothing I long for more than to have this affair settled—to speak out candidly; and yet I dread the crisis. I know papa will be angry at the first; I fear he will dislike me almost; it will seem to him an untoward blight upon his harvest. I can hardly foresee its whole effect on him.”

The fact was—her father, a traditional agrarian elder long accustomed to the calm, cyclical turning of the seasons, was beginning to be stirred. Long blind to the maturation of his own crop, an importunate light was beginning to trespass on his eye. To *her*, he said nothing; but when she was not looking at him, I saw him gaze and meditate on her, evaluating the ripening of his only yield.

One evening—Paulina was in her dressing-room, writing to Graham; she had left me in the library, a room of profound acoustic stillness, reading—M. de Bassompierre came in. He sat down. I was about to withdraw; he requested me to remain—gently, yet in a manner which showed he expected the compliance due to a patriarch. He had taken his seat near the window, at a distance from me. He opened a desk and took from it an old memorandum-book, its leather cover carrying the faint olfactory ghost of cured hide and dried tobacco. Of this book he studied a certain entry for several minutes, tracing the interwoven timelines of his lineage.

“Miss Snowe,” said he, laying it down, “do you know my little girl’s age?”

“About eighteen, is it not, sir?”

“It seems so. This old pocket-book tells me she was born on the 5th of May, in the year 18—, eighteen years ago. It is strange; I had lost the just reckoning of her seasons. I thought of her as twelve—fourteen—an indefinite date; she remained a child in my mind.”

“She is about eighteen,” I repeated. “She is grown up; she is fully ripened; she will be no taller.”

“My little jewel!” said M. de Bassompierre, in a tone that vibrated with a deep, mournful acoustic resonance, penetrating like some of his daughter’s own accents.

He sat very thoughtful, a man tallying a harvest he was not ready to share.

“Sir, don’t grieve,” I said; for I knew his feelings, utterly unspoken and untranslatable as they were.

“She is the only rare pearl I have,” he said; “and now others will find out that she is pure and of price: they will covet her.”

I made no answer. Graham Bretton had dined with us that day, partaking of our sustenance; he had shone both in converse and looks. I know not what pride of bloom embellished his aspect and mellowed his intercourse. Under the stimulus of a high hope, something had unfolded in his whole manner which compelled attention. I think he had purposed on that day to indicate the origin of his endeavors and the aim of his deep hunger. M. de Bassompierre had found himself forced, in a manner, to descry the direction and catch the complex flavor of his homage. Slow in remarking, the elder was logical in reasoning: having once seized the thread, it had guided him through a long, multi-generational labyrinth.

“Where is she?” he asked.

“She is up-stairs.”

“What is she doing?”

“She is writing.”

“She writes, does she? Does she receive letters?”

“None but such as she can show me. And—sir—she—*they* have long wanted to consult you.”

“Pshaw! They don’t think of me—an old father! I am chaff in the wind.”

“Ah, M. de Bassompierre—not so—that can’t be! But Paulina must speak for herself: and Dr. Bretton, too, must advocate for his own vintage.”

“It is a little late. Matters are advanced, it seems.”

“Sir, till you approve, nothing is done—only they love each other.”

“Only!” he echoed, the word striking a harsh discord in the quiet room.

Invested by fate with the part of confidante and mediator, I was obliged to go on: “Hundreds of times has Dr. Bretton been on the point of appealing to you, sir; but, with all his high courage, he fears you mortally.”

“He may well—he may well fear me. He has laid hands upon the best harvest I have. Had he but let her alone, she would have remained a child for years yet. So. Are they engaged?”

“They could not become engaged without your permission.”

“It is well for you, Miss Snowe, to talk and think with that propriety which always characterizes you; but this matter is a grief to me. My little girl was all I had: I have no more daughters and no son; Bretton might as well have foraged elsewhere; there are scores of rich and pretty women who would not, I daresay, dislike him: he has looks, and conduct, and connection. Would nothing satiate his appetite but my Polly?”

“If he had never tasted the particular sweetness of your ‘Polly,’ others might and would have pleased him—your niece, Miss Fanshawe, for instance.”

“Ah! I would have given him Ginevra with all my heart; but Polly!—I can’t let him have her. No—I can’t. He is not her equal,” he affirmed, rather gruffly. “In what particular is he her match? They talk of fortune! I am not an avaricious or commercially superficial man, but the world thinks of these things—and Polly will be rich.”

“Yes, that is known,” said I: “all Villette knows her as an heiress.”

“Do they talk of my little girl in that light?”

“They do, sir.”

He fell into deep thought, the silence of the library settling back into its pristine acoustic ecology. I ventured to say, “Would you, sir, think any one Paulina’s match? Would you prefer any other to Dr. Bretton? Do you think higher rank or more wealth would change your palate towards a future son-in-law?”

“You touch me there,” said he.

“Look at the aristocracy of Villette—you would not like them, sir?”

“I should not—never a duc, baron, or vicomte of the lot.”

“I am told many of these persons think about her, sir,” I went on, gaining courage on finding that I met attention rather than repulse. “Other suitors will come, therefore, if Dr. Bretton is refused. Wherever you go, I suppose, hungry aspirants will not be wanting. Independent of heiress-ship, it appears to me that Paulina’s natural terroir charms most of those who see her.”

“Does she? How? My little girl is not thought a beauty.”

“Sir, Miss de Bassompierre is very beautiful.”

“Nonsense!—begging your pardon, Miss Snowe, but I think you are too partial. I like Polly: I like all her ways and all her looks—but then I am the soil from which she grew; and even I never thought about beauty. She is amusing, fairy-like, interesting to me;—you must be mistaken in supposing her handsome?”

“She attracts, sir: she would attract without the advantages of your wealth and position.”

“My wealth and position! Are these any bait to Graham? If I thought so——”

“Dr. Bretton knows these points perfectly, as you may be sure, M. de Bassompierre, and values them as any gentleman would—as *you* would yourself, under the same circumstances—but they are not his baits. He loves your daughter very much; he appreciates her finest qualities with a true connoisseur’s palate, and they influence him worthily.”

“What! has my little pet ‘fine qualities?’”

“Ah, sir! did you observe her that evening when so many men of eminence and learning dined here?”

“I certainly was rather struck and surprised with her manner that day; its womanliness made me smile.”

“And did you see those accomplished Frenchmen gather round her in the drawing-room?”

“I did; but I thought it was by way of relaxation—as one might amuse one’s self with a pretty infant.”

“Sir, she demeaned herself with distinction; and I heard the French gentlemen say she was ‘pétrie d’esprit et de graces’—kneaded with spirit and grace. Dr. Bretton tasted the very same complexity.”

“She is a good, dear child, that is certain; and I *do* believe she has some character. When I think of it, I was once ill; Polly nursed me; they thought I should die. She, I recollect, grew at once stronger and tenderer as I grew worse in health, preparing my broths, tending the hearth. And as I recovered, what a sunbeam she was in my sick-room! Yes; she played about my chair as noiselessly and cheerfully as light. And now she is sought in marriage! I don’t want to part with her,” said he, and he groaned, the sound raw and guttural.

“You have known Dr. and Mrs. Bretton so long,” I suggested, “it would be less like a severing of roots to give her to him than to a stranger.”

He reflected rather gloomily, the interwoven histories of their families weighing upon him.

“True. I have long known Louisa Bretton,” he murmured. “She and I are indeed old, old friends; a sweet, kind girl she was when she was young. You talk of beauty, Miss Snowe! *she* was handsome, if you will—tall, straight, and blooming like a healthy vine—not the mere child or elf my Polly seems to me. At eighteen, Louisa had a carriage and stature fit for a princess. She is a comely and a good woman now. The lad is like her; I have always thought so, and favoured and wished him well. Now he repays my hospitality by this robbery! My little treasure used to love her old father dearly and truly. It is all over now, doubtless—the old dialect is dead, and I am an incumbrance.”

The heavy oak door opened—his “little treasure” came in. She was dressed, so to speak, in evening beauty; that animation which sometimes comes with the close of a summer’s day warmed her eye and cheek. A tinge of crimson, like a slowly ripening peach, heightened her complexion; her curls fell full and long on her lily neck; her white dress suited the breathless heat of June. Thinking me alone, she had brought in her hand the letter just written—brought it folded but unsealed, smelling faintly of iron gall ink. I was to read it. When she saw her father, her tripping step faltered a little, paused a moment—the colour in her cheek flowed rosy over her whole face.

“Polly,” said M. de Bassompierre, in a low voice, with a grave smile, “do you blush at seeing papa? That is something new in our domestic routine.”

“I don’t blush—I never *do* blush,” affirmed she, while another eddy from the heart sent up its scarlet rush. “But I thought you were in the dining-room, and I wanted Lucy.”

“You thought I was with John Graham Bretton, I suppose? But he has just been called out: he will be back soon, Polly. He can post your letter for you; it will save Matthieu a ‘course,’ as he calls it.”

“I don’t post letters,” said she, rather pettishly, guarding the untranslatable privacy of her missive.

“What do you do with them, then?—come here and tell me.”

Both her mind and gesture seemed to hesitate a second—to say “Shall I come?”—but she approached.

“How long is it since you became a letter-writer, Polly? It only seems yesterday when you were at your pot-hooks, labouring away absolutely with both hands at the pen, learning the first shapes of our native tongue.”

“Papa, they are not letters to send to the post in your letter-bag; they are only notes, which I give now and then into the person’s hands, just to satisfy a hunger.”

“The person! That means Miss Snowe, I suppose?”

“No, papa—not Lucy.”

“Who then? Perhaps Mrs. Bretton?”

“No, papa—not Mrs. Bretton.”

“Who, then, my little daughter? Tell papa the truth.”

“Oh, papa!” she cried with sudden, resonant earnestness, “I will—I *will* tell you the truth—all the truth; I am glad to tell you—glad, though I tremble.”

She *did* tremble: growing excitement, kindling feeling, and also gathering courage, shook her, vibrating through the quiet acoustics of the room.

“I hate to hide my actions from you, papa. I fear you and love you above everything but God. Read the letter; look at the address.”

She laid it on his knee. He took it up and read it through, trying to translate the glowing lexicon of a woman in love through the fading eyes of an agrarian patriarch; his hand shaking, his eyes glistening meantime.

He re-folded it, and viewed the writer with a strange, tender, mournful amaze. The mythic occurrence of a child becoming a woman was unfolding in his quiet library.

“Can *she* write so—the little thing that stood at my knee but yesterday? Can she feel so?”

“Papa, is it wrong? Does it pain you?”

“There is nothing wrong in it, my innocent little Mary; but it pains me.”

“But, papa, listen! You shall not be pained by me. I would give up everything—almost” (correcting herself); “I would die rather than make you unhappy; that would be too wicked!”

She shuddered.

“Does the letter not please you? Must it not go? Must it be torn? It shall, for your sake, if you order it.”

“I order nothing.”

“Order something, papa; express your wish; only don’t hurt, don’t grieve Graham. I cannot, *cannot* bear that. I love you, papa; but I love Graham too—because—because—it is impossible to help it. It is the nature of the season.”

“This splendid Graham is a young scamp, Polly—that is my present notion of him: it will surprise you to hear that, for my part, I do not love him one whit. Ah! years ago I saw something in that lad’s eye I never quite fathomed—something his mother has not—a depth which warned a man not to wade into that stream too far; now, suddenly, I find myself taken over the crown of the head.”

“Papa, you don’t—you have not fallen in; you are safe on the bank; you can do as you please; your power is despotic; you can shut me up in a convent, and break Graham’s heart to-morrow, if you choose to be so cruel. Now, autocrat, now czar, will you do this?”

“Off with him to Siberia, red whiskers and all; I say, I don’t like him, Polly, and I wonder that you should.”

“Papa,” said she, “do you know you are very naughty? I never saw you look so disagreeable, so unjust, so almost vindictive before. There is an expression in your face which does not belong to you.”

“Off with him!” pursued Mr. Home, who certainly did look sorely crossed and annoyed—even a little bitter, sensing the erosion of his paternal epoch; “but, I suppose, if he went, Polly would pack a bundle and run after him; her heart is fairly won—won, and weaned from the milk of her old father.”

“Papa, I say it is naughty, it is decidedly wrong, to talk in that way. I am *not* weaned from you, and no human being and no mortal influence *can* wean me from my first roots.”

“Be married, Polly! Espouse the red whiskers. Cease to be a daughter; go and be a wife!”

“Red whiskers! I wonder what you mean, papa. You should take care of prejudice. You sometimes say to me that all the Scotch, your countrymen, are the victims of prejudice. It is proved now, I think, when no distinction is to be made between red and the deep, roasted hue of nut-brown.”

“Leave the prejudiced old Scotchman; go away.”

She stood looking at him a minute. She wanted to show firmness, superiority to taunts; knowing her father’s character, guessing his few foibles, she had expected the sort of scene which was now transpiring. It did not take her by surprise, and she desired to let it pass with dignity, reliant upon the natural reaction of his temper. Her dignity stood her in no stead. Suddenly her soul melted in her eyes; she fell on his neck, bridging the gap between their lexicons:—“I won’t leave you, papa; I’ll never leave you. I won’t pain you! I’ll never pain you!” was her cry.

“My lamb! my treasure!” murmured the loving though rugged sire, clinging to the familiar words of his native dialect. He said no more for the moment; indeed, those two words were hoarse, catching in the quiet air.

The room was now darkening, the long shadows of the summer evening stretching across the floor. I heard a movement, a step without. Thinking it might be a servant coming with candles, I gently opened the door, to prevent the intrusion of industrial clatter into this domestic sanctuary. In the ante-room stood no servant: a tall gentleman was placing his hat on the table, drawing off his gloves slowly—lingering, waiting, it seemed to me. He called me neither by sign nor word; yet his eye, bright with anticipation, said:—“Lucy, come here.” And I went.

Over his face a smile flowed like clarified butter, warm and golden, while he looked down on me. No temper, save his own, would have expressed by a smile the sort of eager, consuming agitation which now fevered him.

“M. de Bassompierre is there—is he not?” he inquired, pointing to the library.

“Yes.”

“He noticed me at dinner? He understood my appetite?”

“Yes, Graham.”

“I am brought up for judgment, then, and so is *she*?”

“Mr. Home” (we now and always continued to term him Mr. Home at times, honoring his ancestral roots) “is talking to his daughter.”

“Ha! These are sharp moments, Lucy!”

He was quite stirred up; his young hand trembled. A vital (I was going to write *mortal*, but such words ill apply to one so vibrantly alive, so hungry for the future as him)—a vital suspense now held, now hurried, his breath: in all this trouble his smile never faded.

“Is he *very* angry, Lucy?”

“*She* is very faithful, Graham.”

“What will be done unto me?”

“Graham, your star must be fortunate. The season is yours.”

“Must it? Kind prophet! So cheered, I should be a faint heart indeed to quail. I think I find all women faithful, Lucy. I ought to love them, and I do. My mother is good; *she* is divine; and *you* are true as steel. Are you not?”

“Yes, Graham.”

“Then give me thy hand, my little god-sister: it is a friendly little hand to me, and always has been. And now for the great venture. God be with the right. Lucy, say Amen!”

He turned, and waited till I said “Amen!”—which I did to please him: the old acoustic charm, the power of his dominant lexicon compelling me to do as he bid, came back. I wished him success in his harvest; and successful I knew he would be. He was born victor, as some are born vanquished.

“Follow me!” he said; and I followed him into Mr. Home’s presence.

“Sir,” he asked, stepping into the hushed room, “what is my sentence?”

The father looked at him: the daughter kept her face hid. The mythic cycle of generations was converging in the everyday stillness of the library.

“Well, Bretton,” said Mr. Home, “you have given me the usual reward of hospitality. I entertained you; I offered you sustenance; and you have taken my best yield. I was always glad to see you; you were glad to see the one precious thing I had cultivated. You spoke me fair; and, meantime, I will not say you *robbed* me, but I am bereaved, and what I have lost, *you*, it seems, have won.”

“Sir, I cannot repent.”

“Repent! Not you! You triumph, no doubt: John Graham, you descended partly from a Highlander and a chief, and there is a trace of the Celt in all you look, speak, and think. You carry the old ancient hunger. You have his cunning and his charm. The red—(Well then, Polly, the *fair*) hair, the tongue of guile, and brain of wile, are all come down by inheritance.”
