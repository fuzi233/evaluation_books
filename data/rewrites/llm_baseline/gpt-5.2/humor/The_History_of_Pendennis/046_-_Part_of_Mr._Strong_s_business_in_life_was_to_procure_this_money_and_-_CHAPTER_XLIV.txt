Early next morning—at that hour when the milk-carts are still politely whispering over the stones and the city has not yet worked itself up into its customary iron clatter—Colonel Altamont came rolling out of his room in Shepherd’s Inn as if he had been fired from a cannon and was now seeking somewhere soft to land. He wandered into Captain Strong’s sitting-room, where the Chevalier sat planted in an easy-chair with the newspaper, a cigar, and the serene air of a man who could make a bivouac feel like a breakfast-parlour.

Strong had already performed, with the gravity of a priest at an altar, the morning rite of fried eggs and broiled rashers—Mr. Grady having executed them secundum artem, the fat in the pan singing a small crisp hymn, the bacon snapping like applause, the eggs set tender as custard at the centre and just kissed into colour at the edges. The smell had soaked into the room—salt, pepper, browned meat, coffee—and even the old floorboards seemed to creak more contentedly under it. Whatever the world did to Captain Strong—camp, Fleet, prison, battle, bankruptcy—it never managed to get at his digestion; and his digestion, like a good regiment, kept him in order. He had, the night before, seen Altamont safely bedded with a friendliness that cost him nothing, removed the candle like a nurse confiscating a child’s fireworks, discovered a spirit-bottle empty upon which he had been counting, and consoled himself with water and his pipe as placidly as if Providence had expressly brewed it.

Altamont’s first act was not to notice the eggs, the bacon, or any other blessing of the table; he bellowed for a pint of pale ale. Grady brought it. The Colonel poured it into a pewter flagon, and from the flagon into himself. He set the tankard down empty with the air of a man who had just rescued his own life at sea, drew a mighty breath, and wiped his mouth with his dressing-gown. (Strong, who had a soldier’s eye for uniforms and a man’s eye for other men, could not help observing—as he had long observed—that Altamont’s dyed whiskers and the honest colour of his beard were not on speaking terms; and that under the black wig lurked hair of a different story. But Strong, who was tidy in manners as in camp, made no remark.)

“Nothing like that beer,” said Altamont, revived into loquacity. “When the coppers are hot. Many a day I’ve drunk a dozen of Bass at Calcutta, and—and—”

“And at Lucknow, I suppose,” Strong put in, laughing, and flicking ash with military neatness. “I got the beer for you on purpose. Knew you’d want it after last night.”

Altamont beat his great hand against his head as if he would knock the sense back into it by force. “I can’t help myself. I’m a madman when I get the liquor on board me; and ain’t fit to be trusted with a spirit-bottle. Once I begin, I can’t stop till I’ve emptied it; and when I’ve swallowed it, Lord knows what I say or what I don’t say. I dined at home here quiet. Grady gave me just my two tumblers. I meant to pass the evening at the Black and Red as sober as a parson. Why did you leave that confounded sample-bottle of Hollands out of the cupboard, Strong? Grady must go out too, and leave me the kettle a-boiling for tea. It was no use. I couldn’t keep away from it. Washed it all down, sir—by Jove. And it’s my belief I had some more afterwards at that infernal little thieves’ den.”

“What, were you there too?” Strong asked. “Before you came to Grosvenor Place? That was beginning betimes.”

“Early hours to be drunk and cleared out before nine, eh? But so it was. Yes—like a great big fool—I must go there; and I found ’em dining—Blackland, young Moss, and two or three more of the thieves. If we’d gone to Rouge et Noir, I must have won. But we didn’t try the black and red. No—hang ’em—they know’d I’d have beat ’em at that. I must have beat ’em. I can’t help beating ’em, I tell you. But they was too cunnin for me. That rascal Blackland got the bones out, and we played hazard on the dining-table. And I dropped all the money I had from you in the morning—be hanged to my luck.”

He spoke with the wounded splendour of a man who thinks Providence has cheated him at dice.

“That set me wild,” he went on. “I suppose I must have been very hot about the head, for I went off thinking to get some more money from Clavering, I recollect; and then—and then I don’t much remember what happened till I woke this morning and heard old Bows at No. 4 playing on his pianner.”

Strong paused to light his cigar from a coal, and listened a moment—because in a lodging-house you never have silence, you only have the next room’s opinion of music. Somewhere below, Mr. Bows was indeed worrying his instrument in a manner that suggested the keys had personally offended him. The notes, half-stifled by boards and plaster, came up like distant carpentry.

“I should like to know,” Strong said at last, in that tone of mild curiosity he used for asking about cannon as well as consciences, “how you always draw money from Clavering, Colonel.”

Altamont burst out laughing. “Ha, ha! He owes it me.”

“I don’t know that that’s a reason with Frank for paying,” Strong answered, without moving his eyes. “He owes plenty besides you.”

“He gives it me because he is so fond of me,” Altamont said, with a grin that had something sharp in it. “He loves me like a brother; you know he does, Captain.—No?—He don’t?—Well, perhaps he don’t; and if you ask me no questions, perhaps I’ll tell you no lies, Captain Strong—put that in your pipe and smoke it, my boy.”

Then, as if the confession of knavery had made him virtuous by reaction: “But I’ll give up that confounded brandy-bottle. I must give it up, or it’ll be the ruin of me.”

“It makes you say queer things,” Strong said, looking full at him. “Remember what you said last night, at Clavering’s table.”

Altamont’s face changed so quickly it was as if the wig had slipped and shown the bare skull of fear. “Say? What did I say? Did I split anything? Dammy, Strong—did I split anything?”

“Ask me no questions, and I will tell you no lies,” Strong replied, paying him back his own coin with a smile that didn’t ring. He remembered well enough the Colonel’s sudden blanching and hurried retreat the moment he had recognised Major Pendennis—“Captain Beak,” as Altamont had called him, with that drunken instinct for the sore point. But Strong, who could march all night and hold his tongue all day, did not choose to jog Altamont’s memory into sharper mischief.

“No,” he said aloud. “You didn’t split, as you call it. It was only a trap of mine to see if I could make you speak; but you didn’t say a word that anybody could comprehend—you were too far gone for that.”

So much the better, thought Altamont; and he heaved a great sigh, like a man reprieved from hanging because the hangman couldn’t find the rope.

Strong noted the relief—filed it away, as he filed away everything useful—and took no notice. Altamont, now that his neck felt safe for the moment, expanded into narrative again.

“Yes, I own to my faults,” he said, with the grand candour of a sinner who expects admiration for not denying the obvious. “There is some things I can’t, do what I will, resist: a bottle of brandy, a box of dice, and a beautiful woman. No man of pluck and spirit—no man as was worth his salt—ever could, as I know of. There’s hardly p’raps a country in the world in which them three ain’t got me into trouble.”

“Indeed?” said Strong.

“Yes. From the age of fifteen—when I ran away from home, and went cabin-boy aboard an Indiaman—till now, when I’m fifty year old, pretty nigh—them women have always been my ruin. Why, it was one of ’em—with such black eyes, and jewels on her neck, and battens and ermine like a duchess—it was one of ’em at Paris that swept off the best part of the thousand pound as I went off with.”

And so, with the gusto of a man who can taste his own disasters, he began to dish up Paris.

At first, he said, he had lived like a gentleman—Colonel Altamont at Meurice’s, public tables only, cautious as a cook measuring pepper. He described the smart world as if it were a supper: white kid gloves, tufted chins, names with hyphens like skewers. There was a certain Bloundell-Bloundell; there was Madame the Countess de Foljambe—“such an eye, Strong! such a hand at the pianner.” She played and sang, he swore, until she “warbled your soul out of your body a’most,” and he made it sound as if the music were a syrup poured warm down a man’s throat, sweet at first and then cloying and finally poisonous. She had him every Tuesday; he had opera-boxes and dinners at the restaurateur’s—champagne with its bright biting fizz, and afterwards that “confounded brandy,” which, in his telling, always entered like a villain in a melodrama.

It was not the dinners and opera-boxes that took the money, he maintained, but “another way”: one night at the Countess’s supper—Bloundell-Bloundell, the Honourable Deuceace, the Marquis de la Tour de Force, “all tip-top nobs”—champagne, punch mixed by the Countess herself, cards, drink, and then his memory went the way of a candle in a draught. He woke to a headache and visitors.

First came Deuceace—easy, smiling, with that air of a gentleman who can borrow money as if he were doing you a favour. Deuceace told him he had borrowed thirty louis at vingt-et-un, given an I.O.U., and that Altamont had pocketed it. Altamont found the paper in the card-case the Countess had “worked” for him—sure enough. Deuceace paid him in gold on the bed-table—chink, chink, chink—each coin sounding like a small insult.

Then came Bloundell-Bloundell and the Marquis—“the Marky,” as Altamont called him, twiddling moustachios at the chimney-glass and making lunges like a man fencing with his own reflection. They insisted on “affairs.” They produced notes of hand: three hundred and forty louis to the Marquis; two hundred and ten to Bloundell. Altamont sprang out of bed roaring, swore he owed nothing, called them names, and was met with threats—exposure, chastisement, honour, all the theatre of it. Bloundell, being larger and heavier, was not a man to be flung out of windows as easily as a marquis.

Then a fourth gentleman arrived—Altamont’s Boulogne friend, the one who hadn’t played, and who had warned him. This witness, as witnesses will, was inconvenient. He declared Altamont had insisted on the high stakes; the Countess had begged him to stop; the others had offered repeatedly to leave off. And then, with the relish of a man delivering the worst news with the greatest delicacy, he told Altamont the Marquis would shoot him “as sure as”—as sure as his name was whatever it was. He added that he had left the Countess crying, nearly hysterical, terrified there’d be a quarrel and a bullet, and that he believed—indeed he was certain—that she was distractedly in love with Altamont.

“Do you think so?” said Altamont, who could swallow danger more easily than vanity.

His friend described the Countess on her knees—“Save Colonel Altamont!”

“As soon as I was dressed,” Altamont continued, “I went and called upon that lovely woman. She gave a shriek and pretty near fainted. She called me Ferdinand—I'm blest if she didn’t.”

“I thought your name was Jack,” Strong said, laughing.

Altamont blushed—an odd dull red showing through the dye and bravado. “A man may have more names than one, mayn’t he, Strong? When I’m with a lady, I like to take a good one. She called me by my Christian name. She cried fit to break your heart. I can’t stand seeing a woman cry—never could—not whilst I’m fond of her. She said she couldn’t bear to think of my losing so much money in her house. Wouldn’t I take her diamonds and necklaces, and pay part?”

He swore he refused—wouldn’t touch a farthing’s worth. “But what can a woman do more than give you her all? That’s the sort I like.”

She warned him they would shoot him—kill her Ferdinand.

“They’ll kill my Jack wouldn’t have sounded well in French,” Strong said, and laughed again at the absurdity of names changing with borders as if identity were a coin that needed different stamping for different markets.

“Never mind about names,” Altamont said, sulkily. “A man of honour may take any name he chooses, I suppose.”

He went on: he had refused the duel—“field of honour be hanged”—and the Countess, shocked as if he’d spilt sauce on her altar-cloth, had drawn back and questioned whether a British officer could refuse. Her father had been a General of the Grand Army; a soldier should pay “debts of honour.”

“What could I do?” Altamont cried, spreading his hands as if fate itself had shuffled the deck. “Everybody was against me. Caroline said I had lost the money; though I didn’t remember a syllable about the business. Every one of these chaps was a man of fashion and honour; and the Marky and the Countess of the first families in France. And—by Jove, sir—rather than offend her, I paid the money up—five hundred and sixty gold Napoleons, by Jove: besides three hundred which I lost when I had my revenge.”

He sat musing, as if trying to taste in memory whether the thing had been robbery or romance. “And I can’t tell you at this minute whether I was done or not,” he ended. “Sometimes I think I was: but then Caroline was so fond of me. That woman would never have seen me done: never, I’m sure she wouldn’t: at least, if she would, I’m deceived in woman.”

Strong might have let him go on—there was no end to a man’s past when the bottle had edited it—but their door was knocked at. Grady opened it, and in came Sir Francis Clavering.

“The Governor, by Jove,” Strong exclaimed, surprised; while Altamont scowled from under his heavy eyebrows. “It’s no good, I warrant.”

Indeed, good very seldom carried Sir Francis anywhere. Money carried him—as regularly and as ignobly as a sedan-chair. When he entered Shepherd’s Inn, one could almost hear the rustle of protested bills behind him like dead leaves dragged on a boot. He came pale with irritation, his face set in that pinched expression of a man who has spent his life dodging creditors and still fancies himself pursued by injustice rather than arithmetic.

He began at once, addressing Altamont: “So, sir—you’ve been at your old tricks.”

“Which of ’um?” Altamont asked, with a sneer as broad as his shoulders.

“You have been at the Rouge et Noir. You were there last night,” Clavering cried.

“How do you know—were you there?” Altamont said. “I was at the Club, but it wasn’t on the colours I played—ask the Captain—I’ve been telling him. It was with the bones. It was at hazard, Sir Francis, upon my word and honour it was.” And he looked at the Baronet with a mock humility so knowing that it was like being bowed to by a pickpocket.

“What the deuce do I care,” shrieked Clavering, “how a man like you loses his money—whether at hazard or roulette? What I will not have is that you should use my name, or couple it with yours. Damn him, Strong—why don’t you keep him in better order? I tell you he has gone and used my name again—drawn a bill upon me—and lost the money on the table—I can’t stand it—I won’t stand it. Flesh and blood won’t bear it. Do you know how much I have paid for you, sir?”

“This was only a very little ’un, Sir Francis,” Altamont said, with the easy cheerfulness of a man asking forgiveness for setting fire to a curtain. “Only fifteen pound. Captain Strong, they wouldn’t stand another. And it oughtn’t to anger you, Governor. Why, it’s so trifling I did not even mention it to Strong—did I now, Captain? I protest it had quite slipped my memory—and all on account of that confounded liquor I took.”

“Liquor or no liquor, sir, it is no business of mine,” Clavering snapped. “I don’t care what you drink or where you drink it—only it shan’t be in my house. And I will not have you breaking into my house of a night, and a fellow like you intruding himself on my company. How dared you show yourself in Grosvenor Place last night, sir—and—and what do you suppose my friends must think of me when they see a man of your sort walking into my dining-room uninvited, and drunk, and calling for liquor as if you were the master of the house?”

“They’ll think you know some very queer sort of people, I dare say,” Altamont returned, impenetrably good-humoured. “Look here, Baronet, I apologise; on my honour I do. Ain’t an apology enough between two gentlemen? It was a strong measure, I own—walking into your cuddy and calling for drink as if I was the Captain. But I had had too much before, you see—that’s why I wanted some more; nothing can be more simple. And it was because they wouldn’t give me no more money upon your name at the Black and Red, that I thought I would come down and speak to you about it. To refuse me was nothing; but to refuse a bill drawn on you that have been such a friend to the shop, and are a baronet and a member of parliament, and a gentleman and no mistake—damme, it’s ungrateful.”

Clavering’s temper, always thin as watered wine, boiled over. “By heavens, if ever you do it again—if ever you dare to show yourself in my house; or give my name at a gambling-house or any other house—by Jove, at any other house—or give any reference at all to me, or speak to me in the street, by God, or anywhere else until I speak to you—I disclaim you altogether—I won’t give you another shilling.”

“Governor, don’t be provoking,” Altamont said, his good humour thickening into sullenness. “Don’t talk to me about daring to do this thing or t’other, or when my dander is up it’s the very thing to urge me on. I oughtn’t to have come last night, I know I oughtn’t. But I told you I was drunk, and that ought to be sufficient between gentleman and gentleman.”

“You a gentleman! dammy, sir,” cried Clavering, the words flying out as if they’d been waiting years to escape. “How dares a fellow like you call himself a gentleman?”
