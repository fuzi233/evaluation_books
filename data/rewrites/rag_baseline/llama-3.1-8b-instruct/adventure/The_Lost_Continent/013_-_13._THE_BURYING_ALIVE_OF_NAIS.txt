The air was heavy with anticipation in the grand square. I emerged from the shadows of the royal pyramid, my footsteps echoing off the stone walls. The previous night's labor had left its mark – the stench of sweat and dust hung in the air, and the distant clang of hammers still resonated through the stillness. The city's inhabitants had gathered to witness the culmination of Phorenice's will, their faces a mix of awe and trepidation.

As I made my way towards the throne, the sun began to peek over the horizon, casting an otherworldly glow on the massive structure. Seven tiers of stone, each knee-height deep, formed the groundwork, with three steps cut into the front of each level. The uppermost layer held a cavity, designed to contain the body of Nais, while above it, the massive block that would form the seat of the throne loomed, poised precariously by lifting rams.

The architects and engineers had worked tirelessly throughout the night, their skills honed by Phorenice's inventions and their own ingenuity. The stonecutters and masons had labored under the flickering light of torches, their sweat-drenched bodies a testament to their dedication. Whips cracked, and men had fainted, but the pace never faltered. The city's resources had been drained to bring this monolith to life, and now it stood before me, a testament to the power of human endeavor.

As I approached the throne, the chamberlains parted to reveal the spectacle. The carvings on the lower part of the throne depicted the royal snake encircling the chair, its great hooded head looming over the entire structure. The upper part, still supported by the lifting rams, seemed to defy gravity, its weight a marvel of engineering. The people murmured in awe, their eyes fixed on the magnificent creation before them.

I felt a sense of detachment, a disconnection from the emotions swirling around me. The throne was a monument to Phorenice's ambition, a symbol of the city's submission to her will. As I stood before it, I couldn't help but think of the countless lives that had been sacrificed in its creation – the half-dozen workmen whose bodies lay beneath the scaffolding, their labor fueling the construction of this monolith.

The silence was oppressive, punctuated only by the occasional crack of a whip or the distant rumble of the city's machinery. I felt like an outsider, a stranger in a world
