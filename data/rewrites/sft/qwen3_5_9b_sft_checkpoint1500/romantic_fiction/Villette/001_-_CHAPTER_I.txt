My godmother resided in a stately house within the pristine, ancient town of Bretton. Her husband's lineage had anchored itself there for generations, bearing the name of their soil—Bretton of Bretton. Whether this was a fortuitous accident or the legacy of a distant ancestor of considerable standing, I could never discern.

As a child, I visited Bretton roughly twice a year, finding profound delight in the locale. The estate and its inhabitants were tailor-made for my sensibilities. The vast, tranquil chambers, the meticulously arranged furnishings, the expansive windows admitting clear light, and the balcony overlooking a magnificent, antiquated street—all these elements appealed to me deeply. It was a place where Sundays and holidays seemed eternally suspended, bound by an atmosphere of such profound quietude and cleanliness that time itself seemed to slow.

In a household comprised entirely of adults, a single child invariably becomes the center of attention. In this quiet sphere, I was similarly cherished by Mrs. Bretton. She had become a widow, leaving behind one son, prior to my acquaintance with her; her husband, a physician, had passed away while she remained youthful and comely.

Though she was no longer young in appearance, she retained a striking beauty. Tall, well-proportioned, and despite possessing a dark complexion uncommon among Englishwomen, her cheeks wore a luminous vitality that spoke of robust health. This radiance animated a pair of fine, cheerful black eyes. Regrettably, nature had not bestowed this complex upon her son. His eyes were blue—though piercing even in boyhood—and his hair, when illuminated by the sun, appeared golden, a hue friends hesitated to define otherwise. Yet, he inherited his mother's facial lines, her perfect dentition, her stature (or the promise thereof, for he was still growing), and, above all, her flawless health and a spirit marked by a steady, equal temperament that proved more valuable than any fortune.

It was during the autumn of a certain year that I stayed at Bretton, having been summoned personally by my godmother to reclaim me from the relatives with whom I had permanently settled. I suspect she then clearly foresaw approaching events, shadows of which I barely perceived, yet whose faint suspicion instilled within me a restless melancholy, rendering me grateful for the prospect of changing scenes and company.

Time, under her roof, flowed with a gentle, untroubled current. Unlike the tumultuous swiftness of youth, it moved blandly, akin to a broad river gliding through a flatland. My stays there mirrored the pilgrimage of Christian and Hopeful beside a serene stream, flanked by "green trees on each bank, and meadows beautified with lilies all the year round." There was no charm of variety, no thrill of incident; indeed, when stimulation finally arrived, it felt like a disturbance, something I wished to avoid rather than embrace.

One day, a letter arrived that evidently startled and concerned Mrs. Bretton. At first, I assumed it originated from home and trembled, anticipating some catastrophic news. However, no mention was made of it, and the gathering clouds dissipated quickly.

The following day, returning from a lengthy walk, I discovered an unexpected transformation upon entering my bedroom. Beside my own French bed, nestled in its shady alcove, stood a small crib draped in white linen. Next to my mahogany chest of drawers lay a diminutive rosewood bureau. I paused, gazing in bewilderment. "What portents are these?" I wondered. The answer, however, was manifest: "A second guest is arriving; Mrs. Bretton anticipates another visitor."

Descending to dinner, explanations unfolded. A little girl would shortly join me as a companion; she was the daughter of a friend and distant relative of the late Dr. Bretton. It was noted that this little girl had recently lost her mother, though Mrs. Bretton soon qualified this tragedy, suggesting the loss was less severe than initially appeared. Mrs. Home—the mother's name—had been remarkably beautiful yet giddy and careless, neglecting her child and failing to satisfy her husband. Their union, far from congenial, had ended in separation by mutual consent, devoid of legal proceedings. Shortly thereafter, overwhelmed by exertion at a ball, the lady contracted a cold, developed a fever, and succumbed after a brief illness. Her husband, a man of sensitive feelings, was shattered by the suddenness of the news, convinced that his own lack of patience and indulgence had hastened her demise. He had brooded over this guilt until his spirits were severely affected. Medical advice suggested travel as a remedy, and in the interim, Mrs. Bretton had offered to care for his little daughter. "And I trust," concluded my godmother, "that the child will not resemble her mother—a silly, frivolous flirt that every sensible man foolishly marries. For," she confided, "Mr. Home *is* a sensible man in his own way, though not overly practical. He is devoted to science, spending half his life in a laboratory conducting experiments—a pursuit his butterfly-like wife could neither comprehend nor endure. Indeed," she admitted, "I should not have enjoyed it myself."

Addressing my curiosity, she revealed that her late husband had attributed Mr. Home's scientific inclination to a maternal uncle, a French savant. The gentleman was of mixed French and Scottish descent, maintaining connections in France, several of whom held the title *de*, claiming nobility.

That same evening, at nine o'clock, a servant was sent to intercept the coach carrying our anticipated visitor. Mrs. Bretton and I sat alone in the drawing-room awaiting her arrival; John Graham Bretton was absent, visiting a schoolfriend in the countryside. My godmother perused the evening papers while I stitched. Outside, the air was wet; rain lashed against the glass panes, and the wind sounded angry and restless.

"Poor child!" Mrs. Bretton murmured intermittently. "What terrible weather for her journey! I wish she were safely here."

Just before ten, the bell announced Warren's return. As soon as the door opened, I rushed down the hallway. There lay a trunk and several band-boxes, beside them stood a figure resembling a nursemaid, and at the foot of the stairs, Warren carried a shawled bundle in his arms.

"Is that the child?" I asked.

"Yes, miss."

I reached out to open the shawl, eager to glimpse the face, but it was swiftly turned away, pressed close to Warren's shoulder. "Put me down, please," a small voice commanded when Warren opened the drawing-room door. "And remove this shawl," the speaker continued, using a minute hand to extract the pin and removing the clumsy wrapping with a fastidious haste. The creature that now appeared attempted to fold the shawl deftly, but the drapery was far too heavy and cumbersome for those small hands and arms to manage. "Give it to Harriet, please," the direction was given, "and she can put it away." With that, the child turned and fixed her gaze upon Mrs. Bretton.

"Come here, little dear," the lady said warmly. "Come, let me see if you are cold and damp; let me warm you by the fire."

The child advanced promptly. Freed from her wrappings, she appeared exceedingly tiny, yet a neat, perfectly formed little figure, light, slight, and straight. Seated on my godmother's ample lap, she looked like a mere doll; her neck, delicate as wax, and her head of silky curls increased the resemblance.

Mrs. Bretton spoke in soft, affectionate phrases as she rubbed the child's hands, arms, and feet. Initially regarded with a wistful gaze, she soon returned a smile. Though rarely a caressing woman—even with her deeply beloved son, her manner was seldom sentimental, often the opposite—when the small stranger smiled at her, she kissed her, asking, "What is my little one's name?"

"Missy."

"But besides Missy?"

"Polly, papa calls her."

"Will Polly be content to live with me?"

"Not *always*; but until papa returns. Papa is gone away." She shook her head expressively.

"He will return to Polly, or send for her."

"Will he, ma'am? Do you know he will?"

"I think so."

"But Harriet does not think so—at least, not for a long while. He is ill."

Her eyes filled with tears. She drew her hand from Mrs. Bretton's and made a movement to leave her lap. It was first resisted, but she said, "Please, I wish to go: I can sit on a stool."

She was permitted to slide down from the knee, took a footstool, and carried it to a corner where the shade was deepest, seating herself there. Mrs. Bretton, though commanding and peremptory in grave matters, was often passive in trifles; she allowed the child her way. Turning to me, she said, "Take no notice at present." Yet I did take notice. I watched Polly rest her small elbow on her small knee, her head propped on her hand. I observed her draw a square inch or two of pocket-handkerchief from the doll-pocket of her skirt, and then I heard her weep. While other children in grief cry aloud without shame or restraint, this being wept silently. The tiniest occasional sniff testified to her emotion. Mrs. Bretton did not hear it—which was, perhaps, as well. Soon, a voice from the corner demanded, "May the bell be rung for Harriet!"

I rang; the nurse was summoned and arrived.
