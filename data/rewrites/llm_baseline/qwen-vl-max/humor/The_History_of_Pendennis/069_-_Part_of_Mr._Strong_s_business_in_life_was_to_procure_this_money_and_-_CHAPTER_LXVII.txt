CHAPTER LXVII. In Which Pen Begins to Doubt His Election (And Possibly His Entire Life)

Pen, busy plotting his parliamentary rise like a man who’d just discovered the secret of immortality (or at least a good seat in the House), was suddenly interrupted by news that Lady Rockminster had arrived at Baymouth—along with Laura. The mere mention of Laura’s name sent a shiver down Pen’s spine, not from joy, but from the kind of guilt only a morally flexible gentleman can feel when confronted by someone who still believes in angels.

Laura, his sister—his mother’s legacy, his moral compass, and, as he now realized, possibly his conscience—was coming into town. And what was he doing? Plotting to become a Member of Parliament, marry Blanche Amory (a woman whose charm could be measured in inches of lace and yards of coquetry), and generally behave like a man who’d forgotten how to say “no” to ambition. He felt like a schoolboy caught red-handed stealing tarts, except instead of tarts it was honor, and instead of a headmaster it was Laura—whose pure, unblinking gaze could detect hypocrisy from a mile away.

He wrote her a letter full of poetic flattery, witty banter, and enough sentiment to drown a small country estate. But deep down, he knew he was lying. He was acting like a rogue playing dress-up in a borrowed conscience. Because here was the truth: Laura wasn’t just a sister. She was a mirror. And in her reflection, Pen saw not the polished gentleman he pretended to be, but a man who’d traded sincerity for status, affection for advancement, and peace for power.

Why did a simple girl—kind, innocent, and barely aware of the political machinations of the world—frighten him so? Because she didn’t play games. She didn’t lie. She didn’t pretend. And that made her terrifying. For a man whose life was built on cleverness and compromise, honesty was like an open door to a room full of uncomfortable truths.

As he rode from Clavering to Baymouth, he felt like a criminal heading toward a trial where the judge had never been wrong—and had seen right through him before he even stepped off the horse.

Meanwhile, Laura had spent the past year under Lady Rockminster’s care, learning manners, music, and the fine art of being overlooked in polite society. Poor thing. Though she’d been raised with all the tenderness of a pampered pet, she now found herself in a world where kindness wasn’t always returned. Her mother, Helen, had doted on her so much that no one else seemed to notice her existence. Now, without that protective glow, Laura faced the cold reality of social hierarchy: some people ignored her; others treated her like a charity case; and servants, when they weren’t rude, were at least indifferent.

But Laura? She didn’t complain. Instead, she smiled. And that smile—bright, trusting, utterly genuine—was her superpower. She didn’t need manipulation or flattery. She simply *was*. And because she was, everyone around her softened. Even Lady Rockminster’s long-suffering maid, who had endured forty years of scolding, grew fond of her. When Laura fell ill in Paris, the maid nursed her despite her mistress’s fears of fever. The chef wept when she ate chicken. The footmen celebrated her recovery by getting drunk at a wine shop. Even the most grumpy members of the household became sentimental over her.

And then there was Lady Diana Pynsent—once skeptical, now declaring that Laura was “very agreeable.” A revolution in diplomacy, if ever there was one.

When Pen arrived, Laura came rushing downstairs, perhaps even embracing him in front of Calverley and Coldstream. These two giants, normally as expressionless as statues carved from granite, would have witnessed a severed head rolling down the stairs and shown no more emotion than a pair of garden gnomes. But Laura’s warmth? That was something else entirely.

She looked better than ever—healthier, brighter, more radiant. Pen couldn’t help but stare. Her eyes sparkled like sunlight on water. Her cheeks blushed with life. And when she kissed him, he felt like he’d been struck by lightning—only this time, it wasn’t pain, it was shame.

“You’re too kind to me, Laura,” he said, voice thick with emotion. “I don’t deserve it.”

“Mamma left you to me,” she replied, brushing his forehead with her lips. “Remember our pact? You come to me when you’re happy—or when you’re sad. So which is it?”

Pen hesitated. Then, weakly: “I’m… trying to be happy.”

“Oh, I know about the Parliament plan!” she said, grinning. “Everyone knows! Lady Rockminster’s doctor talked about it today. I bet it’ll be in the Chatteris paper tomorrow. And Miss Blanche Amory—she’s going to be your wife, isn’t she?”

Pen blinked. “You know about that?”

“Know? All the world does! Tout se sait, as I intend to say to Blanche when I meet her. She wrote me the sweetest letter. Said she adores you. I hope she means it.”

Pen stared. “So you’re not… upset?”

“Upset?” she laughed. “Why should I be? You’re happy. That’s all that matters. And when you become a minister, I’ll wear my best dress and let Blanche present me at court. I’ve been practicing my curtsey.”

And with that, she performed a perfect Parisian bow—complete with dramatic flourish—just as Lady Rockminster entered.

The old lady gave Pen one stiff finger to shake. He bowed awkwardly. She sighed.

“So you’re marrying, are you?” she said.

“Scold him, Lady Rockminster!” Laura chimed in. “He didn’t tell us!”

“I wanted Sir Francis Clavering to give my grandson his seat,” the dowager snapped. “Why didn’t he? I suppose you’re getting rich with this Miss Amory. I wouldn’t take her unless she came with a fortune.”

Pen winced. “Sir Francis is tired of politics. And I… well, I thought it might be worth trying.”

“Ah, yes,” she sneered. “You prefer Miss Amory to my Laura. Well, I’ve no patience with men who do. She’s crooked, I tell you—affected, artificial. After seeing Laura, how could you choose such a creature?”

Pen smiled tightly. “I’m very sorry Miss Amory doesn’t please you.”

“You mean—you don’t care whether I like her or not? Very well. I don’t care either. I won’t marry her. I’m glad I won’t. A little odious thing. When I asked the three attaches at the Embassy why they didn’t fall in love with Laura, they laughed and said they wanted money. Selfish! Cowards! We ran away with each other in my day. No romance left now.”

Pen raised an eyebrow. “Did you consult Laura before offering her to them?”

“Miss Bell has only a little money,” the dowager declared. “She must marry soon. Someone must make a match for her. A girl can’t offer herself.”

“And what if she doesn’t want to be matched?” Pen asked.

“She will,” Lady Rockminster said firmly. “Because I say so.”

Laura returned, smiling. “Have you been asking Arthur why he won’t marry me?” she asked, taking his hand. “He’s going to marry someone else. But I’ll be very fond of her. And I’ll live with them—if he doesn’t keep asking every bachelor who comes to dinner why he won’t marry me.”

Pen exhaled. Relief washed over him. No reproaches. No judgment. Just love. And that made him feel worse.

From then on, he found himself visiting Baymouth constantly. Lady Rockminster welcomed him with the same authority she used to command the household: “Come often. Grandjean cooks well. And you need to learn manners. You’re always thinking about yourself. Don’t blush. It’s natural. My sons were like that until I cured them. Come here. We’ll teach you how to behave. Hecker will pour you wine. On good days, champagne. But no too much. And no disturbing me during my nap.”

She ruled her little kingdom with iron grace, and everyone—servants, guests, even Pen—bowed to her whims.

Her gatherings were modest: the doctor, the vicar, his curate, occasionally a visitor. But Pen found himself drawn to the quiet moments—when Lady Rockminster napped and Laura played the piano. He’d sit in silence, sipping wine, watching her fingers dance across the keys, wondering how anyone could be so pure in a world so corrupt.

One evening, he asked why she hadn’t written when she was ill.

“What good would it have done?” she said gently. “I had Martha and the doctor. You’re too busy to write to women. You have books, newspapers, politics, railroads…”

Pen looked at her. And blushed again. Because he remembered: he hadn’t written. Not once. Not when she was sick. Not when she needed him.

They walked together—through fields, past Fairoaks, along the downs. And as they rode, Pen began to see her clearly: not just as a sister, but as a person—warm, wise, untouched by pretense. In their mother’s lifetime, she’d held back. But now, with no pressure to be anything but herself, she spoke openly, freely, honestly.

They talked about their mother. About her endless love, her silent devotion. Pen learned things he never knew—how she’d visited every cottage, how she’d given food and comfort to those in need, how she’d loved him not for what he could become, but for who he was.

One day, they visited her grave. Pen stood there, silent. Guilty. He’d come to pay respects, but felt unworthy. A man who sold his soul for a seat in Parliament—what right did he have to stand beside a woman who’d lived for love?

They rode past Fairoaks. Pen stopped and asked a boy for a sprig of honeysuckle. The boy handed it over. Pen gave it to Laura. She smiled.

“You weren’t selfish,” she said softly.

“Only a pretty sacrifice,” he muttered.

“But your heart was kind. That’s all that matters.”

Pen sighed. “I wish I could be more like you.”

“You already are,” she said. “You just forget it.”

Later, as they walked home, she asked him what he planned to do in Parliament.

“Women don’t understand politics,” he said, half-joking.

“Then explain it to us,” she insisted. “Lady Diana says Mr. Pynsent spends all day in committees, all night at the House, votes as he’s told, never speaks, and never rises above a subordinate role. Are you going to follow that path?”

“Possibly,” he admitted.

“But why? What’s so brilliant about it?”

“I don’t know,” he said honestly. “Maybe I want to do good. Maybe I want to matter.”

“Then why not stay at home?” she asked. “Write books. Kind books. Books that help people. If fame is vanity, why chase it? If the world is wicked, why stay in it?”

Pen paused.

“And what would you have me do?” he asked.

“I’d have you bring your wife to Fairoaks,” she said. “Live quietly. Study. Help others. Have children who play on the lawn. Pray in our mother’s church. If the world is a temptation, aren’t we told to pray not to be led into it?”

Pen looked at her. And for the first time in years, he didn’t feel like a politician. He felt like a brother. Like a son. Like a man who might actually be capable of choosing love over ambition.

And for a moment, he wondered: what if he did?
