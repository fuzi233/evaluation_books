Moore was in the saddle before the morning had found its voice.

The night still lay cold and folded in the hollows when he rode out, and the road to Whinbury rang under his horse’s shoes with a sound like struck iron—thin, solitary, and too sharp for peace. The hedges were black laces against a paling sky; here and there a cottage chimney sighed up a first ribbon of peat-reek; a cock tried his call and was answered far off, as if the very air were feeling its way toward day. Moore took in all these small, homely signals without softening. Whatever business he did in Whinbury—under the dim lamp of a clerk’s office, with paper breathed on by cold fingers and sealed with quick impatience—he kept to himself when he returned.

By the time Hortense had warmed the milk and coffee together till it gave up that faint sweet steam—half dairy, half roast—he had already washed the road dust from his hands and put away the look of a man who has been bargaining with something hard. She cut the tartines neatly, her knife making a crisp whisper against crust; she set the café au lait before him, and the smell was gentle, domestic, almost pleading. He ate, but his appetite seemed a duty; he drank, but as if warmth could not travel far in him. Hortense asked nothing. Between brother and sister there was a compact older than affection: his movements were not to be questioned; her silence was not to be interpreted.

When breakfast was done, he went to the counting-house.

Henry—Joe Scott’s lad—brought in the letters and the daily paper. Moore sat down, broke seals, glanced, and the short lines of ink might as well have been drops of sourness: his nostrils gave a little derisive snuff, and in his eye there stirred that keen, almost gay defiance which looks like a jest until it turns to steel. Without changing the placidity of his face, he chose a pen and, in a brief spasm of finger-fury, stripped away the feathered top. He wrote answers swiftly—decisive strokes, as if each word were a nail driven home—sealed them, and went out to walk through the mill.

Within, the day’s work had begun its thick chant: looms thudding like great hearts, wheels making their relentless, circular whir. Sound did not merely fill the place; it ruled it, pressed on ribs and temples, ate into thought. Outside the wide counting-house door stood open to sunshine and mild air, but what the breeze carried was not spring. Now and then, along with the clean edge of cold, there came a sulphur-puff from the gaunt chimney—soot-thick smoke rushing up in a sable column—an industrial breath that soured the very idea of weather.

He came back, sat down, and tried the newspaper. It would not hold him. More than once he laid it across his knee, folded his arms, and stared into the fire. He looked towards the window. He looked at his watch. His attention moved like a restless tongue tasting for something it could not find.

A dark-blue apparition—the ghost of Joe Scott, fresh from a dyeing vat, half man and half indigo—showed itself at the open door.

“He’s comed, sir,” said Joe, and vanished as quickly as if he had been absorbed into the mill’s own blue.

Moore did not lift his eyes from the paper.

A large man entered—broad-shouldered, massive-limbed—clad in fustian, with grey worsted stockings and a hat so battered it looked as if weather had chewed it. He removed the hat, stowed it under his chair with care, and wiped his forehead with a spotted cotton handkerchief that had been living in the hat-crown.

“Raight dahn warm for Febewerry,” he remarked, and sat with the air of one who has a right to be where he is.

Moore made a sound that might serve for assent.

The visitor set, in the corner beside him, an official-looking staff, and then began to whistle—an aimless, comfort-seeking tune, as if to make the air his own.

“You have what is necessary, I suppose?” Moore asked.

“Ay, ay! all’s right.”

Whistling resumed. Paper rustled. Fire ticked. The mill, beyond, ground on—an unceasing, nerve-wearing bass.

After a time Moore reached to the cupboard at his side without rising, and took out a black bottle, a tumbler, and a jug. He placed them on the table with the economy of a man who wastes no motion.

“Help yourself; there’s water in that jar in the corner.”

“I dunnut knaw that there’s mich need, for all a body is dry in a morning,” the fustian gentleman replied, already on his feet. He mixed with a practised hand, the clear water clouding as it took the spirit; he tested it by a deep draught, and sank back with the satisfaction of a man who believes he has made life simpler.

“Will you tak naught yourseln, Mr. Moore?” he asked, mouth mellowed by the burn and sweetness of the hollands.

Moore answered with a slight negative movement; he did not drink.

“Yah’d as good,” urged the visitor. “It ‘uld set ye up. Uncommon good hollands. Ye get it fro’ furrin parts, I’se think?”

“Ay!”

“Tak my advice and try a glass on’t. Them lads ‘at’s coming ‘ll keep ye talking, nob’dy knows how long. Ye’ll need propping.”

“Have you seen Mr. Sykes this morning?” Moore inquired, as if his mind had never touched the bottle at all.

“I seed him—hauf an hour—nay, happen a quarter sin’—just afore I set off. He said he aimed to come here, and I sudn’t wonder but ye’ll have old Helstone too. I seed ’em saddling his little nag as I passed at back o’ t’ rectory.”

Five minutes later the yard answered with the brisk trot of a small horse. Hoofs tapped a quick, bright rhythm on the stones, and then a nasal voice called out, “Boy—take my horse and lead him into the stable.”

Helstone entered as if the cold had sharpened him. He marched nimbly and erect, looking browner, keener, and more alive than his years promised.

“Beautiful morning, Moore. How do, my boy? Ha! whom have we here?” His glance fell on the man with the staff. “Sugden! What! you’re going to work directly? On my word, you lose no time. But I come to ask explanations. Your message was delivered to me. Are you sure you are on the right scent? How do you mean to set about the business? Have you got a warrant?”

“Sugden has.”

“Then you are going to seek him now? I’ll accompany you.”

“You will be spared that trouble, sir; he is coming to seek me. I’m just now sitting in state waiting his arrival.”

“And who is it? One of my parishioners?”

Joe Scott had slipped in without sound—silent as dye sinking into cloth. He leaned on the desk, indigo-dark and watchful. Moore answered Helstone’s question with a smile—one of those brief, dry curves that carried no kindness.

Joe took the word, his pawky quietness like a small knife.

“It’s a friend of yours, Mr. Helstone—a gentleman you often speak of.”

“Indeed! His name, Joe? You look well this morning.”

“Only the Rev. Moses Barraclough; t’ tub orator you call him sometimes, I think.”

Helstone drew out his snuff-box and took a long pinch, as if the tobacco’s sting might guard him against surprise.

“Ah! Couldn’t have supposed it. Why, the pious man never was a workman of yours, Moore. He’s a tailor by trade.”

“And so much the worse grudge I owe him,” Moore returned, “for interfering and setting my discarded men against me.”

“And Moses was actually present at the battle of Stilbro’ Moor? He went there, wooden leg and all?”

“Ay, sir,” said Joe, relishing the telling. “He went there on horseback, that his leg mightn’t be noticed. He was the captain, and wore a mask. The rest only had their faces blackened.”

“And how was he found out?”

“I’ll tell you, sir,” Joe said, glancing sideways at Moore with a look that begged leave and took it all the same. “T’ maister’s not so fond of talking. I’ve no objections. He courted Sarah, Mr. Moore’s sarvant lass, and so it seems she would have nothing to say to him; she either didn’t like his wooden leg or she’d some notion about his being a hypocrite. Happen—for women is queer hands; we may say that amang werseln when there’s none of ’em nigh—she’d have encouraged him, in spite o’ his leg and his deceit, just to pass time like. I’ve known some on ’em do as mich; and some o’ t’ bonniest and mimmest-looking too—ay, I’ve seen clean, trim young things, that looked as denty and pure as daisies, and wi’ time a body fun’ ’em out to be nowt but stinging, venomed nettles.”

“Joe’s a sensible fellow,” Helstone put in drily.

“Howsiver, Sarah had another string to her bow. Fred Murgatroyd—one o’ our lads—is for her; and as women judge men by their faces—and Fred has a middling face, while Moses is none so handsome, as we all knaw—the lass took on wi’ Fred. A two-three months sin’, Murgatroyd and Moses chanced to meet one Sunday night; they’d both come lurking about these premises wi’ the notion o’ counselling Sarah to tak a bit of a walk wi’ them. They fell out, had a tussle, and Fred was worsted—for he’s young and small, and Barraclough, for all he has only one leg, is almost as strong as Sugden there—indeed, anybody that hears him roaring at a revival or a love-feast may be sure he’s no weakling.”

“Joe, you’re insupportable,” Moore cut in, his patience fraying at the edges. “You spin out your explanation as Moses spins out his sermons. The long and short of it is, Murgatroyd was jealous of Barraclough; and last night, as he and a friend took shelter in a barn from a shower, they heard and saw Moses conferring with some associates within. From their discourse it was plain he had been the leader, not only at Stilbro’ Moor, but in the attack on Sykes’s property. Moreover they planned a deputation to wait on me this morning, which the tailor is to head, and which, in the most religious and peaceful spirit, is to entreat me to put the accursed thing out of my tent. I rode over to Whinbury this morning, got a constable and a warrant, and I am now waiting to give my friend the reception he deserves. Here, meantime, comes Sykes. Mr. Helstone, you must spirit him up. He feels timid at the thoughts of prosecuting.”

A gig rolled into the yard, its wheels making a hollow, anxious rattle on the stones. Mr. Sykes entered—a tall, stout man of about fifty, comely enough in feature, but with a physiognomy that seemed made of concessions. His face had the look of a man who has spent many nights listening for footsteps where none should be.

“Have they been? Are they gone? Have you got him? Is it over?” he asked at once, words tripping over each other.

“Not yet,” Moore returned, unruffled. “We are waiting for them.”

“They’ll not come; it’s near noon. Better give it up. It will excite bad feeling—make a stir—cause perhaps fatal consequences.”

“You need not appear,” said Moore. “I shall meet them in the yard when they come; you can stay here.”

“But my name must be seen in the law proceedings. A wife and family, Mr. Moore—a wife and family make a man cautious.”

Moore’s mouth tightened with a disgust he did not trouble to disguise.

“Give way, if you please. Leave me to myself. I have no objection to act alone; only be assured you will not find safety in submission. Your partner Pearson gave way, and conceded, and forbore. Well, that did not prevent them from attempting to shoot him in his own house.”

“My dear sir,” said Helstone, “take a little wine and water.”

The “wine and water” was hollands and water—clean-looking, deceptively mild. Sykes compounded a brimming tumbler and swallowed it. It went down like liquid fire and false courage; in two minutes it painted his cheeks and set his tongue to trumpeting. Noise—chemical, easy noise—rose inside him and drove out fear, as a louder sound drives out a softer one.

He declared he hoped he was above being trampled on by common people; he would endure the insolence of the working-classes no longer; he had considered and made up his mind to go all lengths; if money and spirit could put down these rioters, they should be put down; Moore might do as he liked, but he—Christie Sykes—would spend his last penny in law before he would be beaten; he would settle them, or he’d see.

“Take another glass,” Moore urged, watching him with a sort of cool amusement.

Sykes did not mind if he did. This was a cold morning—though Sugden had called it warm—one must be careful at this season; it was proper to take something to keep damp out; he had a cough already (he produced it obligingly, as proof); something of this sort was excellent taken medicinally; he didn’t make a practice of drinking spirits in a morning, but occasionally it really was prudent to take precautions.

“Quite prudent, and take them by all means,” Moore replied.

Sykes, warmed and inflated, turned to Helstone, who stood on the hearth, his shovel-hat still on his head, his keen eyes watching the whole scene as if it were a lesson in some unpleasant catechism.

“You, sir, as a clergyman,” said Sykes, “may feel it disagreeable to be present amidst scenes of hurry and flurry—and, I may say, peril. I dare say your nerves won’t stand it. You’re a man of peace, sir; but we manufacturers, living in the world, and always in turmoil, get quite belligerent. Really, there’s an ardour excited by the thoughts of danger that makes my heart pant. When Mrs. Sykes is afraid of the house being attacked and broke open—as she is every night—I get quite excited. I couldn’t describe to you, sir, my feelings. Really, if anybody was to come—thieves or anything—I believe I should enjoy it, such is my spirit.”

Helstone’s response was a laugh—hard, brief, low—cut like flint. It did not flatter Sykes; it did not even quite insult him; it simply told the truth as sound.

Moore would have pressed a third tumbler on the heroic mill-owner, but Helstone lifted a hand with quiet authority.

“Enough is as good as a feast, is it not, Mr. Sykes?”

Sykes assented, and sat watching Joe Scott remove the bottle at a sign from Helstone. A self-satisfied simper lingered on his lips, and a regretful glisten in his eye followed the black glass as it vanished. Moore looked, for one instant, as if he would have liked to make sport of him to the very top of his bent.

And somewhere beyond this room—somewhere in a quieter house, where reading by lamplight and gentle music could make an evening seem a refuge—there was a young woman to whom Moore had lately been, for one soft interval, almost tender. If she had seen him now—with that mischievous, sardonic edge showing in his face—would she have recognized the same man? The thought passed like a shadow and was gone. Men, like cloth, showed different sides according to the light.

A gathering sound rose in the yard—a trample, a thickening hum—then a pause, as if the crowd drew breath together. Moore stepped to the window; Helstone followed. They stood so that they could see without being seen: the tall young man behind the undersized old one, both looking out with the caution of those who have long listened to danger and learned its notes.

Their only comment was a cynical smile exchanged—stern eyes flashing it like a signal.

Outside came a flourishing, oratorical cough. Then an interjection—“Whisht!”—aimed at stilling several voices. Moore opened the casement an inch or two, to let sound in more freely. The voices in the yard had a particular texture: not the round speech of the educated, but the thick, vowel-heavy dialect of the district—language carrying old fields, old hearths, old resentments. In it lived a whole memory that no polished lexicon could translate without killing something vital; yet here it was being used as a weapon, turned and sharpened by men who knew how to make words herd other men.

“Joseph Scott,” began a snuffling voice, “might we inquire if your master be within, and is to be spoken to?”

Joe, sentinel at the counting-house door, answered with lazy indifference.

“He’s within, ay.”

“Would you then, if you please”—and the emphasis on “you” was a small act of power—“have the goodness to tell him that twelve gentlemen wants to see him.”

“He’d happen ax what for,” Joe suggested, enjoying himself. “I mught as weel tell him that at t’ same time.”

“For a purpose.”

Joe came in.

“Please, sir, there’s twelve gentlemen wants to see ye, ‘for a purpose.’”

“Good, Joe; I’m their man.—Sugden, come when I whistle.”

Moore went out, chuckling dryly, as if what awaited him were sport rather than peril. He advanced into the yard with one hand in his pocket and the other in his waistcoat; his cap brim shaded his eyes, but could not hide the deep, dancing ray of scorn in them.

Twelve men stood waiting—some in shirt-sleeves, some in blue aprons—faces weathered, hands stained with labour, bodies carrying the posture the mill had taught them: shoulders slightly forward, ears tuned for command. In the van were two conspicuous leaders.

One was a little dapper strutting man with a turned-up nose and a self-important air—as if words themselves were a coat he wore for show. The other was broad-shouldered, and more notable still: a demure face, eyes like a cat’s—watchful and faithless—plus a wooden leg and a stout crutch. A leer lived about his lips, as though he laughed inwardly at the very people he pretended to represent. He did not look like a man who tasted honesty; he looked like a man who tasted advantage.

“Good-morning, Mr. Barraclough,” Moore said, debonairly, in a tone that made courtesy sound like mockery.

“Peace be unto you!” answered Barraclough, closing his already half-shut eyes as he delivered the blessing, as if it were a performance he could do better blind.

“I’m obliged to you. Peace is an excellent thing; there’s nothing I more wish for myself. But that is not all you have to say to me, I suppose? I imagine peace is not your purpose?”

“As to our purpose,” began Barraclough, “it’s one that may sound strange and perhaps foolish to ears like yours, for the childer of this world is wiser in their generation than the childer of light—”

“To the point, if you please, and let me hear what it is.”

“Ye’se hear, sir. If I cannot get it off, there’s eleven behint can help me. It is a grand purpose, and”—his voice shifted from a half-sneer to a whine—“it’s the Looard’s own purpose, and that’s better.”

“Do you want a subscription to a new Ranter’s chapel, Mr. Barraclough? Unless your errand be something of that sort, I cannot see what you have to do with it.”

“I hadn’t that duty on my mind, sir; but as Providence has led ye to mention the subject, I’ll make it i’ my way to tak ony trifle ye may have to spare; the smallest contribution will be acceptable.”

He doffed his hat and held it out like a begging-box; a brazen grin crossed his countenance.

“If I gave you sixpence you would drink it,” Moore said.

Barraclough uplifted the palms of his hands and the whites of his eyes—a burlesque of sanctity so broad it was almost a jest upon religion itself.

“You seem a fine fellow,” Moore returned, cool and dry. “You don’t care for showing me that you are a double-dyed hypocrite, that your trade is fraud. You expect indeed to make me laugh at the cleverness with which you play your coarsely farcical part, while at the same time you think you are deceiving the men behind you.”

Moses’ countenance lowered. He saw he had overshot his mark. He would have answered, but the second leader—impatient at having been kept in shadow—stepped forward.

This man did not look like a traitor; conceit, rather than cunning, sat on him. He spoke through throat and nose, very slowly, as if savouring each phrase before letting it go, and as if his listeners ought to be grateful for the elegance of his speech.

“Mr. Moore,” he commenced, “it might, perhaps, justly be said that reason rather than peace is our purpose. We come, in the first place, to request you to hear reason; and should you refuse, it is my duty to warn you, in very decided terms, that measures will be had resort to which will probably terminate in— in bringing you to a sense of the unwisdom, of the—the foolishness which seems to guide and guard your proceedings as a tradesman in this manufacturing part of the country. Hem! Sir, I would beg to allude that as a furriner, coming from a distant coast, another quarter and hemisphere of this globe, thrown, as I may say, a perfect outcast on these shores—the cliffs of Albion—you have not that understanding of huz and wer ways which might conduce to the benefit of the working-classes. If, to come at once to partic’lars, you’d consider to give up this here miln, and go without further protractions straight home to where you belong, it ’ud happen be as well. I can see naught ageean such a plan.—What hev ye to say tull’t, lads?”

The deputation answered with a unanimous “Hear, hear!”—a sound like a wave breaking.

“Brayvo, Noah o’ Tim’s!” muttered Joe Scott behind Moore, delighting in the absurd grandiosity. “Moses’ll niver beat that. Cliffs o’ Albion, and t’ other hemisphere! My certy! Did ye come fro’ th’ Antarctic Zone, maister? Moses is dished.”

But Moses refused to be dished. He cast an ireful glance at Noah of Tim’s and began again, this time serious, dropping the sly sarcasm that had failed.

“Or iver you set up the pole o’ your tent amang us, Mr. Moore, we lived i’ peace and quietness—yea, I may say, in all loving-kindness. I am not myself an aged person as yet, but I can remember as far back as maybe some twenty year, when hand-labour were encouraged and respected, and no mischief-maker had ventured to introduce these here machines which is so pernicious. Now, I’m not a cloth-dresser myself, but by trade a tailor. Howsiver, my heart is of a softish nature. I’m a very feeling man, and when I see my brethren oppressed, like my great namesake of old, I stand up for ’em; for which intent I this day speak with you face to face, and advises you to part wi’ your infernal machinery, and tak on more hands.”

“What if I don’t follow your advice, Mr. Barraclough?” Moore asked.

“The Looard pardon you! The Looard soften your heart, sir!”

“Are you in connection with the Wesleyans now, Mr. Barraclough?”

“Praise God! Bless His name! I’m a joined Methody!”

“Which in no respect prevents you from being at the same time a drunkard and a swindler,” Moore said, his voice still even, but cutting. “I saw you one night a week ago laid dead-drunk by the roadside, as I returned from Stilbro’ market; and while you preach peace, you make it the business of your life to stir up dissension. You no more sympathize with the poor who are in distress than you sympathize with me. You incite them to outrage for bad purposes of your own; so does the individual called Noah of Tim’s. You two are restless, meddling, impudent scoundrels, whose chief motive-principle is a selfish ambition, as dangerous as it is puerile. The persons behind you are some of them honest though misguided men; but you two I count altogether bad.”

Barraclough made a movement to speak.

“Silence!” Moore snapped, and the word fell like a lash. “You have had your say, and now I will have mine. As to being dictated to by you, or any Jack, Jem, or Jonathan on earth, I shall not suffer it for a moment. You desire me to quit the country; you request me to part with my machinery. In case I refuse, you threaten me. I do refuse—point-blank! Here I stay, and by this mill I stand, and into it will I convey the best machinery inventors can furnish. What will you do? The utmost you can do—and this you will never dare to do—is to burn down my mill, destroy its contents, and shoot me. What then? Suppose that building was a ruin and I was a corpse—what then, you lads behind these two scamps? Would that stop invention or exhaust science? Not for the fraction of a second of time! Another and better gig-mill would rise on the ruins of this, and perhaps a more enterprising owner come in my place. Hear me! I’ll make my cloth as I please, and according to the best lights I have. In its manufacture I will employ what means I choose. Whoever, after hearing this, shall dare to interfere with me may just take the consequences. An example shall prove I’m in earnest.”

He whistled—shrill and loud. The sound sliced through the yard, through the men’s murmurs, through the mill’s distant grind. At that signal Sugden came forward, staff in hand, warrant ready: law embodied in fustian and paper.

Moore turned sharply to Barraclough.

“You were at Stilbro’,” he said. “I have proof of that. You were on the moor, you wore a mask, you knocked down one of my men with your own hand—you! a preacher of the gospel!—Sugden, arrest him!”

Sugden seized Moses.

There was a cry and a surge—a rush forward as if the whole deputation would become one body to pull him free. Boots scraped stone; voices rose—raw, dialect-thick—each man calling from his own fear and fury. It was a sound with history in it, old as hunger, old as threatened hearths; and yet here it met the newer, harsher music of industry and law.

All this time Moore’s right hand had lain hidden in his breast. Now it came out holding a pistol, its metal catching a cold glint of day.

“Both barrels are loaded,” he said, very quietly. “I’m quite determined! Keep off!”
