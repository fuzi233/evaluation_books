When the evening shadows lengthened and the contemplation of his own profound nature grew monotonous despite its vastness, Mr. Sapsea took to the Cathedral Close. He strolled with a swelling air of proprietorship, nursing a benignant landlord's heart for his meritorious tenant, Mrs. Sapsea, whom he had publicly honored with a prize. He relished spotting stray faces peering through the railings, perhaps pausing to read his inscriptions. If a stranger emerged from the churchyard with a hurried step, Mr. Sapsea was morally certain the soul was "with a blush retiring," exactly as his monument directed.

His stature had swelled further; he was now Mayor of Cloisterham. Without mayors, he felt sure, the entire edifice of society would crumble—a theory he believed he himself had forged. While others might be knighted for their speeches, Mr. Sapsea knew he could deliver an address that would explode like a mortar shell into the English Grammar. Rise, Sir Thomas Sapsea! Of such is the salt of the earth.

Since their initial meeting over port, epigrams, backgammon, beef, and salad, Mr. Sapsea's friendship with Mr. Jasper had deepened. He recalled the gatehouse reception warmly, where Jasper had sat at the piano, singing with figurative ear-tickling that left a considerable area of irritation. What Mr. Sapsea admired in the young man was his readiness to absorb the wisdom of elders and his solidity at the core. Evidence abounded: Jasper had not sung the frivolous ditties favored by national enemies, but rather the genuine, home-brewed anthem of King George the Third. He had exhorted the Mayor, addressing him as "my brave boys," to smash all rival nations and continents, reducing every peninsula and isthmus to rubble and sweeping the seas in all directions. It was a clear indictment that Providence had erred in creating so few hearts of oak and so many verminous peoples.

Walking slowly along the damp path beside the churchyard, hands clasped behind his back and scanning for a blushing retiree, Mr. Sapsea rounded a corner to find himself standing in the goodly presence of the Dean, conversing with the Verger and Mr. Jasper. Offering his obeisance, Mr. Sapsea felt instantly transformed, struck by an ecclesiastical gravity surpassing any Archbishop of York or Canterbury.

"You are evidently planning to write a book about us, Mr. Jasper," the Dean remarked. "To chronicle us. Well! We are ancient, and ought to make a fine volume. Our wealth lies less in possessions than in age; perhaps you will include that, calling attention to our grievances."

Mr. Tope, bound by duty, was greatly amused.

"I truly have no intention, sir," Jasper replied, "of becoming an author or archaeologist. It is merely a whim. And regarding that whim, Mr. Sapsea here is more accountable than I."

"How so, Mr. Mayor?" the Dean queried, nodding kindly at his "Fetch." "Explain, Mr. Mayor."

"I am unaware," Mr. Sapsea responded, glancing around for data before returning his gaze to his original, examining it minutely, "what the Very Reverend the Dean means."

"Durdles," Mr. Tope hinted.

"Ay!" the Dean echoed, "Durdles, Durdles!"

"The truth is, sir," Jasper explained, "my curiosity regarding this man was first ignited by Mr. Sapsea. His unique ability to extract the recluse and the odd from mankind first compelled me to give the man a second thought, though naturally, I had encountered him constantly. You would not be surprised, Mr. Dean, if you had witnessed Mr. Sapsea dealing with him in his parlour, as I did."

"Oh!" cried Sapsea, catching a ball tossed to him with ineffable complacency. "Yes, yes. The Very Reverend the Dean refers to that? Yes. I happened to bring Durdles and Mr. Jasper together. I regard Durdles as a Character."

"A character, Mr. Sapsea, that with a few skilful touches you turn inside out," Jasper observed.

"Nay, not quite that," returned the lumbering auctioneer. "I may possess a little influence over him, perhaps; a little insight into his character, perhaps. The Very Reverend the Dean will please to bear in mind that I have seen the world." Here, Mr. Sapsea lagged slightly behind the Dean to inspect his coat-buttons.

"Well!" said the Dean, casting a glance around to locate his copyist. "I hope, Mr. Mayor, you will employ your study and knowledge of Durdles to the noble purpose of exhorting him not to break our worthy and respected Choir-Master's neck. We cannot afford it; his head and voice are far too precious."

Mr. Tope erupted again, falling into respectful convulsions of laughter before subsiding into a deferential murmur implying that any gentleman would deem it a pleasure and honor to have his neck broken for such a compliment.

"I will take it upon myself, sir," Sapsea observed loftily, "to answer for Mr. Jasper's neck. I will tell Durdles to be careful. He will heed what *I* say. How is it currently endangered?" he inquired, surveying the surroundings with magnificent patronage.

"Only by my undertaking a moonlight expedition with Durdles among the tombs, vaults, towers, and ruins," Jasper answered. "You recall suggesting, when you united us, that, as a lover of the picturesque, it might be worthwhile?"

"I remember!" replied the auctioneer. And the solemn idiot genuinely believed he remembered.

"Profiting by your hint," Jasper pursued, "I have taken several day-rambles with this extraordinary old fellow, and tonight we intend a moonlight hole-and-corner exploration."

"And here he is," said the Dean.

Durdles, clutching a dinner-bundle, was indeed seen slouching toward them. Drawing nearer and perceiving the Dean, he removed his hat, swinging it beneath his arm as he prepared to slouch away, until Mr. Sapsea intercepted him.

"Mind you take care of my friend," Sapsea enjoined.

"What friend o' yourn is dead?" Durdles asked. "No orders has come in for any friend o' yourn."

"I mean my live friend there."

"Oh! him?" said Durdles. "He can take care of himself, can Mister Jarsper."

"But do you take care of him too," Sapsea urged.

Whom Durdles, with a tone of command, surveyed surlily from head to foot.

"With submission to his Reverence the Dean, if you'll mind what concerns you, Mr. Sapsea, Durdles he'll mind what concerns him."

"You're out of temper," Sapsea said, winking at the company to demonstrate how smoothly he would manage the situation. "My friend concerns me, and Mr. Jasper is my friend. And you are my friend."

"Don't you get into a bad habit of boasting," Durdles retorted, offering a grave cautionary nod. "It'll grow upon you."

[Illustration: Durdles cautions Mr. Sapsea against boasting]

"You are out of temper," Sapsea repeated, reddening before sinking back into the crowd.

"I own to it," Durdles returned; "I don't like liberties."

Mr. Sapsea winked a third time, signaling to the assembly that he had settled *his* business, before stalking out of the controversy.

Durdles then bid the Dean a good evening. Adding, as he placed his hat back on, "You'll find me at home, Mister Jarsper, as agreed, when you want me; I'm a-going home to clean myself," he soon slouched out of sight. This declaration to "clean himself" was one of the man's incomprehensible compromises with inexorable fact; he, his hat, his boots, and his clothes never showed a trace of cleaning, existing uniformly in a state of dust and grit.

The lamplighter began dotting the quiet Close with specks of light, running rapidly up and down his little ladder—an instrument generations had grown accustomed to, whose removal Cloisterham would have regarded with horror. The Dean withdrew to dinner, Mr. Tope to tea, and Mr. Jasper to his piano. Bathed only in the firelight, he sat chanting choir music in a low, beautiful voice for two or three hours, until darkness had fully descended and the moon approached the horizon.

Softly closing the piano, Jasper changed his coat for a pea-jacket, retrieving a wicker-cased bottle from its largest pocket. Donning a low-crowned, flap-brimmed hat, he moved softly outside. Why did he move so softly tonight? No outward reason appeared. Could a sympathetic reason be crouching darkly within him?

Arriving at Durdles's unfinished dwelling, or hole in the city wall, Jasper saw a light burning within. Moving silently among the gravestones, monuments, and stony debris of the yard, already touched sideways by the rising moon, he knocked.
