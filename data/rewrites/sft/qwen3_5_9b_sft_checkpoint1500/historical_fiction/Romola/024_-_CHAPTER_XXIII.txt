"You are easily frightened, though," Piero remarked, his lips curling in a fresh sneer. "Though my likeness falls short of the original, the old fellow certainly wore a tiger's gaze. I intend to return to the Duomo and study him again."

"It is hardly pleasant to be seized by a lunatic, if indeed he is one," Lorenzo Tornabuoni replied smoothly, offering a polite defense for Tito. "Yet, he may merely be a rogue. Let us observe; I believe we must ascertain whether we possess sufficient authority to quell this unrest between our citizens and these foreigners." Addressing the Frenchman directly, he added, "We must determine if we can halt this disturbance."

With swords unsheathed, the three men stepped forward, flanked by a silent ring of onlookers. Tito followed suit. The initial paralysis of terror within him yielded quickly to the frantic calculations that mortal peril forces upon the cowardly. He needed to know what others knew regarding Baldassarre.

The mob of men and lads, eager mostly to jeer and torment the soldier rather than wound him seriously, parted instantly at the sight of gentlemen bearing drawn steel. The French soldier was questioned. He and his comrades had merely escorted their captives into the city to solicit ransom. Two were Tuscan soldiers captured in Lunigiana; the third, an elderly man, had accompanied a group of Genoese who had clashed with French foragers near Fivizzano. Though the elder appeared mad, he posed no threat. The soldier knew nothing further, unable to comprehend a single word the old man muttered. Tito listened intently, yet remained deaf to all surrounding noise until Tornabuoni addressed him specifically.

"Will you accompany us, Melema? Or, since Messere departs for Signa, might you not wisely align yourself with the current fashion and attend the Friar? He speaks with the force of a torrent at flood today. It is a duty we must all perform to preserve our Medicean standing. *I* myself would go, if only time permitted."

Tito's countenance had regained its hue, and he managed a display of cheerful composure.

"Certainly, I rank among the admirers of the inspired orator," he smiled. "Regrettably, however, I shall remain engaged with the Segretario until the procession commences."

"I shall enter the Duomo to view that savage old man once more," Piero declared.

"Piero mio," Lorenzo interjected gently, "show him kindness and bring him to one of the traveler's hospitals. The monks may determine if he requires confinement."

As the party dispersed, Tito turned toward the Palazzo Vecchio, where he awaited Bartolommeo Scala. The distance was brief, yet for Tito, it stretched into eternity, akin to the fleeting, agonizing minutes of a waking dream. Every street corner and public square held layers of memory, foreboding, and torturous anxieties that could have filled many months of history. A cold serpent seemed to coil around his limbs.

Baldassarre lived, and he resided in Florence—a living vengeance that would not rest, just as a coiled viper refuses to settle until it has crushed its prey. In Baldassarre's nature, injury demanded immediate retribution. His love and hate burned with a feverish intensity that enslaved every fiber of his being, driving him to sacrifice himself to his passions as if worshipping a capricious god. Yet, Baldassarre had loosened his grip and vanished. Tito understood this perfectly: the vengeance was being measured, calculated to ensure absolute success.

Had Tito not uttered those damning words—"He is a madman"—could he not have conjured the mental fortitude required to deny knowing the man? Had he claimed positive proof of Baldassarre's demise, who could contradict him? Only Fra Luca, who was dead, and the crew of the companion galley that delivered the news of the pirate encounter. The odds of Baldassarre crossing paths with any survivor of that shipwreck were infinitesimal. Tito felt a bitter pang of regret; a timely, carefully crafted lie might have spared him catastrophe. Yet, fabricating such a falsehood in the heat of a convulsive shock would have demanded a mastery of self that he did not feel he possessed. Those words had erupted from him like a sudden birth, conceived and nurtured in the darksome recesses of his soul.

Tito was subject to an inexorable law of human nature: we prepare for sudden acts through the repeated selection of good or ill, slowly forging the character that guides our next move.

His sole hope now rested on Baldassarre failing to find his revenge. And then, a thought more actively cruel than any he had previously entertained struck him: Could his own unpremeditated words contain a grain of truth? Enough truth, at least, to shield him against any declaration Baldassarre might later make? The old man looked strangely wild, his eyes burning with an eager, tormented light. Perhaps suffering had driven him to madness. If so, the vengeance seeking to inflict disgrace might be thwarted.

Yet, there existed a form of vengeance that no clever deception could parry. Baldassarre belonged to a stock for whom the thrust of the dagger was as instinctive as the spring of a tiger's paw. Tito shrank back in shuddering horror from the prospect of disgrace, but he also harbored a deep-seated, physical dread inherent to his soft, pleasure-loving nature—a terror that prevented him from facing wounds and death as a mere release from shame. Immediately, his mind raced to discover some hidden armor, some stratagem that could protect him from a wrath no subtlety could deflect.

He marveled at the potency of the fearful passion that gripped him. It felt as though a blight had suddenly struck, transforming the vibrant joy of youth into a landscape of pure pain.

A final resource remained. He could turn back, seek out Baldassarre again, confess everything—to Romola, to the entire world. But he never considered this path. True repentance, severing all ties to evil, demands more than the paralyzing grip of selfish fear. He possessed no conviction that truth offered strength or safety; instead, he clung entirely to his ingenuity and his capacity for dissimulation. With the initial shock of betrayal fading, he hoped to brace himself for any emergency through cool deceit and defensive guile.

A peculiar fact marked Tito's experience at this critical juncture: no direct plan to eliminate Baldassarre ever crossed his mind. Every other possibility ran through his consciousness, even flight from Florence itself, yet he never devised a scheme to remove his enemy. His dread spawned no active malice, nor did he wish to cause pain to any mortal. He had simply sought to make life easier for himself, to navigate his human lot in a way that pinched nowhere. That choice, made repeatedly at various turns, had landed him in precarious positions. The question now stood not whether he should share the burden of destiny with his suffering fellows, but whether the totality of his lies and deceptions would suffice to prevent him from being crushed by the inevitable consequences of his habitual choices.
