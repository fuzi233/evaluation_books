Some grow old in spirit by forty, others remain young at eighty. Margaret Van Eyck’s heart was evergreen—full of warmth, life, and tenderness. She loved her namesake, the young Margaret Brandt, with a passion that felt fresh and unburdened by time. This affection was no fleeting fancy; it was rooted in deep understanding. Margaret Van Eyck had a keen eye for character, and she saw in the younger woman something rare and precious: sincerity. But beyond her own noble qualities, Margaret Brandt had an even greater ally in the artist’s heart.

Human nature.

Strange as it may seem to those who do not watch closely, our hearts often warm more quickly to those we have helped than to those who have helped us. The ancient Greeks noticed this truth long ago, but it was the British Homer who captured it most powerfully in verse:

“I heard, and thought how side by side
We two had stemmed the battle's tide
In many a well-debated field,
Where Bertram's breast was Philip's shield.
I thought on Darien's deserts pale,
Where Death bestrides the evening gale,
How o'er my friend my cloak I threw,
And fenceless faced the deadly dew.
I thought on Quariana's cliff,
Where, rescued from our foundering skiff,
Through the white breakers' wrath I bore
Exhausted Bertram to the shore:
And when his side an arrow found,
I sucked the Indian's venom'd wound.
These thoughts like torrents rushed along
To sweep away my purpose strong.”

Observe: the assassin’s hand is stayed not by kindness received, but by deeds done. Memory recalls what one has given, not what one has gained.

Margaret Van Eyck had been wonderfully kind to Margaret Brandt. She had broken her routine to visit her, nursed her through illness, soothed her fears, petted her, and healed her more effectively than any medicine could have. In return, her heart opened wide—not just to the girl’s charm, but to the gratitude she inspired. She now loved Margaret Brandt more tenderly than she had ever loved Gerard, though in truth, her original visit had been prompted only by concern for him.

When, therefore, she saw the blush rise upon Margaret’s cheeks and read the parchment that had brought it about, she gave up her own plans without hesitation.

“Sweetheart,” she said, “I once hoped he would stay in Italy five or six years, return rich, and above all, become a true master of art. But your happiness comes before all else. And I see you cannot live without him. So let us bring him home as soon as may be.”

“Oh, madam!” replied the young woman, her voice soft and trembling. “You’ve read my very soul.” She lowered her eyes and blushed deeply. “But how shall I tell him? That passes my skill. He has gone to Italy—but I know not where. Wait! He named the cities he meant to visit. Florence was one, and Rome.”

Yet even then, being wise and cautious, she feared that a letter addressed simply “My Gerard—Italy” might never reach its destination. She looked up at her friend, pleading for advice.

“You’ve come to the right place, at the right time,” said the old lady. “Hans Memling was here today—he’s going to Italy, no later than next week. ‘To improve his hand,’ he says. Not before it was needed, I assure you.”

“But how can he find Gerard?”

“Why, he knows him well, child. They’ve dined here together more than once, and were as close as hand and glove. Since their work is the same, they’ll likely travel the same roads. Sooner or later, they must meet. Now go and write a long letter—copy the pardon into it—and leave it with me. I will vouch for the messenger. Within six months, at the latest, Gerard will receive it. And when he does, he’ll kiss it, tuck it into his breast, and fly home. What are you smiling at? And why do your cheeks burn so? You’re smothering me with joy. Yes—happy days are coming for my little pearl.”

Meanwhile, Martin sat in the kitchen, the black-jack before him, while Reicht Heynes spun beside him. And oh! how she questioned him that night.

Hans Memling was an old pupil of Jan Van Eyck and his sister. Though Margaret sneered at him, he was indeed a painter—no mere artisan, but a man of talent and good heart, save for one fault: he loved the “nipperkin, canakin, and the brown bowl” far too well. This weakness kept him poor and often brought him to Margaret Van Eyck’s door—not just for food, but sometimes for a groat. Yet this dependence gave her influence over him. She knew he would never betray a trust placed in his hands.

The letter was written and entrusted to Margaret Van Eyck. The following week, Hans Memling returned from Flanders. She gave him the letter and a piece of gold toward his journey. He seemed eager to depart.

“All the better,” she said. “He’ll reach Italy the sooner.”

But as some horses race at the start and then lag after the first few steps, so did Hans’s haste fade once he reached the main inn in Tergou. There, he found two of his closest companions seated in the bay window. He entered for a farewell drink. When he offered to pay, they refused—no, he was going on a long journey; they would treat him. Everyone would treat him—the landlord included.

This generosity loosened his tongue as if wine were oil. He boasted of showing the Italians how to paint, sang of his battles with the pike, and recounted his romantic triumphs with women (not present to contradict). In short, he was “plenus rimarum erat: huc illuc diffluebat”—full of chatter, spilling out in every direction. Among his ramblings, he revealed he carried a letter for a fellow townsman—Gerard, a good fellow. “You are all good fellows,” he added, slapping Sybrandt on the back so hard it knocked the breath from his body.

Sybrandt scrambled around the table to avoid the blow, but listened closely. For the first time, he learned that Gerard had gone to Italy. Still, to test the truth, he pretended disbelief.

“My brother Gerard is never in Italy.”

“Ye lie, ye cur!” roared Hans, instantly irate, unaware that the man opposite him was the very one he had praised and struck moments before. “If he is ten times your brother, he is in Italy. What call ye this? Read that superscription!” And he flung the letter onto the table.

Sybrandt picked it up, examined it gravely, then laid it down again. “I cannot read,” he said.

But another in the group—by great fortune—could. Proud of this rare skill, he took the letter and read aloud:

“To Gerard Eliassoen, of Tergou. These by the hand of the trusty Hans Memling, with all speed.”

“'Tis excellently well writ,” said the reader, inspecting each letter.

“Ay!” Hans declared grandly. “And small wonder—it was penned by a famous hand: Margaret, sister of Jan Van Eyck. Blessed and honoured be his memory! She is an old friend of mine.”

Then Hans drifted off into forty other topics.

Sybrandt slipped away and sought out Cornelis.

They met in secret, heads bent low over the news. Italy was far. If only they could keep Gerard there?

“Keep him there? Nothing would hold him long from his Margaret.”

“Curse her!” said Sybrandt. “Why didn’t she die when she should have?”

“She die? She’d outlive the plague to torment us.” Cornelis seethed at her selfishness in not dying to ease their burdens.

The two brothers conspired, feeding each other’s malice until, at last, their corrupted hearts formed a plan: to keep Gerard in Italy forever, thus securing his share of their father’s inheritance.

But once devised, the plan stalled. Execution required cunning. Evil hesitated. Then, as if Satan himself whispered into both their ears at once, they both cried out:

They went to Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, who received them immediately—for a man under agony of uncertainty grasps eagerly at knowledge. Certainty is often painful, but suspense is unbearable.

“You have news of Gerard?” he asked, eyes restless.

They told him of the letter and Hans Memling. He listened, tense. “Who wrote it?”

“Margaret Van Eyck,” they answered, naturally assuming the content matched the handwriting.

“Are you sure?” He drew a drawer open and pulled out a document penned by Margaret during negotiations for her house. “Was it written like this?”

“Yes,” said Sybrandt boldly. “It is the same hand.”

“Good,” said Ghysbrecht, heart pounding, yet feigning indifference so convincingly that it unsettled them. They fumbled with their hats, stammered, hesitated—then slowly confessed they wanted a letter forged, one that might keep Gerard in Italy. They intended to substitute it in Hans Memling’s pouch.

As they wavered between respect and suspicion—wondering if this burgomaster was as crooked as they were—conflicting thoughts raced through Ghysbrecht’s mind. Vengeance urged him to let Gerard return and feel the law’s weight. Prudence advised keeping him a thousand miles away. But prudence also warned: why risk dirty work on uncertain grounds? Why give these rogues power to stain his name?

Finally, his conviction that Gerard possessed a secret capable of striking him to the core—combined with caution—formed his reply: “It is my duty to aid citizens who cannot write. But I will not be responsible for their matter. Tell me what I should write.”

“Something about Margaret.”

“Ay, ay! Say she is false, married to another—I’ll guarantee it.”

“No, burgomaster, no!” Sybrandt protested. “Gerard wouldn’t believe it, or only half. Then he’d come back to see. No—say she is dead.”

“Dead? At her age? Will he believe that?”

“Sooner than the other. She was nearly dead—so it’s not a full lie.”

“Humph. Do you think that will keep him?”

“We are certain,” said Cornelis. “Our Gerard will never leave Italy now he is there. It was always his dream. He’d come back for Margaret, but not for us. What does he care for us? He despises his own family—always has.”

“This would be a bitter pill,” said the old hypocrite.

“It will be for his good in the end,” replied the younger.

“What avails Famine wedding Thirst?” said Cornelis.

“And the grief you prepare so calmly?” Ghysbrecht retorted sarcastically, though he tasted his own revenge.

“Oh, a lie is not like a blow with a curtal axe. It hacks no flesh, breaks no bones.”

“A curtal axe?” Sybrandt laughed. “No, nor even like a cudgel.” And he shot a sly, poisoned glance at the burgomaster’s broken nose.

Ghysbrecht’s face darkened with fury. But it worked as intended—the old man bristled with hatred.

“Well,” he said coldly, “tell me what to write, and I must write it. But mark this: you bear the blame if anything goes wrong. Not the hand that writes, but the tongue that dictates, commits the deed.”

The brothers agreed, sneering inwardly. Ghysbrecht drew his inkhorn, placed Margaret’s sample writing before him, and began to ask about the letter’s size—when suddenly Jorian Ketel burst into the room, clearly annoyed not to find the burgomaster alone.

“You see I have business,” said Ghysbrecht.

“Ay, but this is grave. I bring good news—but not for every ear.”

The burgomaster rose and pulled Jorian aside into the deep window embrasure. The brothers heard them speak in low, urgent tones. It ended with Ghysbrecht sending Jorian to saddle his mule. Then, turning to the brothers with sudden chill, he said:

“I value household peace—but this is not to be done hastily. We will see. We will see.”

“But, burgomaster, the man will be gone. It will be too late!”

“Where is he?”

“At the hostelry, drinking.”

“Well, keep him drinking! We will see, we will see.” And he sent them away discomfited.

To understand all this, we must step back. That very morning, Margaret Brandt had met Jorian Ketel near her door. He passed her with a scowl. She remembered him.

“Stay,” she said. “Yes—it’s the good man who saved him. Oh, why haven’t you been near me since? Why haven’t you come for the parchments? Was it not true about the hundred crowns?”

Jorian snorted, but seeing her honest face, he began to suspect a mistake. He told her he had come—and how he had been turned away.

“Alas!” she said. “I knew nothing of it. I lay at death’s door.” Then she invited him to follow her into the garden, showing him where the parchments were buried. “Martin wanted to dig them up, but I stopped him. He put them there—and I said none should move them but you, who earned them so well.”

“Give me a spade!” Jorian cried. “Wait—no. He’s suspicious. Are you sure they’re still there?”

“I’ll take full blame if any hand has touched them.”

“Then keep them two hours longer, good Margaret,” said Jorian, and ran off to the Stadthouse, joyful.

The burgomaster rode toward Sevenbergen, Jorian striding beside him, promising that within an hour the missing parchments would be in his hands.

“Ah, master!” said Jorian. “Lucky it wasn’t a thief who took them.”

“Not a thief? Not a thief? What do you call him then?”

“Well, saving your presence, I call him a jackdaw. This is jackdaw’s work—if ever there was. Take the thing you least need, and hide it—that’s a jackdaw. I should know,” he added oracularly. “I was raised with a chough. He and I were born the same year, but he cut his teeth long before me. My life was a burden for years because of him. If you had a hole in your hose no bigger than a groat, in went his beak like a gimlet. For stealing, Gerard all over. What he wanted least—and what any poor Christian in the house needed most—that went first. Mother was a strong woman—so if she glanced round, away flew her thimble. Father lived by cordwaining—so at sunrise Jack stole his awl, wax, and twine. After that, make your bread how you could! One day I heard my mother tell him to his face he was enough to corrupt half-a-dozen children—and he only cocked his eye at her, then snatched the nurseling’s shoe off his foot. Now this Gerard is tarred with the same brush. The parchments mean nothing to him—like a thimble or awl to Jack. He took them out of mischief and hid them. You’d never have found them but for me.”

“I believe you’re right,” said Ghysbrecht. “I’ve vexed myself more than need.”

When they reached Peter’s gate, he grew uneasy.

“I wish it had been anywhere but here.”

Jorian reassured him.

“The girl is honest and kind,” he said. “She had nothing to do with taking them. I swear it.” He led him into the garden. “There, master—if a face can be believed, they lie there. See, the soil is loose.”

He ran for a spade stuck in the ground and began digging. Soon he uncovered a parchment. Ghysbrecht seized it, knelt, and tore it from the hole. His hands trembled, his face gleamed. He pulled out parchment after parchment. Jorian dusted them clean.

But as Ghysbrecht unearthed many, his face grew darker. When he reached the last, he pressed his hands to his temples, stunned.

“What mystery lies here?” he gasped. “Are fiends mocking me? Dig deeper! There must be another!”

Jorian drove the spade in, throwing up hard earth. In vain. And even as he dug, the burgomaster’s mood shifted.

“Treason! Treachery!” he cried. “You knew of this!”

“Knew what, master, in Heaven’s name?”

“Caitiff—you knew there was another worth twice all these!”

“That’s false!” Jorian cried, now suspicious. “It’s a trick to rob me of my hundred crowns. Oh, I know you, burgomaster!” And he nearly whimpered.

A calm voice fell on them like oil on waves.

“No, good man, it is not false—nor quite true. There was another parchment.”

“There, there, there! Where is it?”

“But,” continued Margaret quietly, “it was not a town record—so you’ve won your hundred crowns. It was a private deed between this burgomaster and my grandfather Flor—is Brandt.”

“Hush, hush!”

“Where is it, girl? That is all we want to know.”

“Have patience, and I shall tell you. Gerard read the title and said, ‘This is as much yours as the burgomaster’s.’ He set it aside, to read with me later.”

“It is in the house, then?” asked the burgomaster, regaining composure.

“No, sir,” said Margaret solemnly. “It is not.” Then, her voice faltering, “You hunted my poor Gerard so hard, so close, that you left him no time to think of anything but his life and his grief. The parchment was in his bosom. He took it with him.”

“Whither? Whither?”

“Ask me no more, sir. What right have you to question me thus? It was for your sake, good man, that I forced myself to come here, to speak to this cruel old man. For when I think of the misery he has caused me and him, the sight of him is more than I can bear.” She shuddered, then walked slowly inside, hand to her head, weeping bitterly.

Remorse, dread, avarice, fear—all tugged at Ghysbrecht’s hardened heart in one moment. He hung his head, arms limp. A coarse chuckle made him turn—there stood Martin Wittenhaagen, leaning on his bow, grinning from ear to ear.

“Ho! Arrest him! Seize him—traitor and thief!” shouted Ghysbrecht. “Dog, thou shalt pay for all!”

Martin said nothing. Calmly, he thrust the duke’s pardon under the burgomaster’s nose. Ghysbrecht stared—speechless.

Martin pressed his advantage.

“The duke and I are soldiers. He won’t let you greasy burghers trample on an old comrade. He bade me carry you a message.”

“The duke send a message to me?”

“Ay! I told him of your masterful ways—of imprisoning Gerard for loving a girl. And he said, ‘Tell him this is to be a king, not a burgomaster. I’ll have no kings in Holland but one. Bid him be more humble, or I’ll hang him at his own door.’”

(Ghysbrecht trembled—he believed the duke capable of it.)

“‘As I hanged the burgomaster of Thingembob.’ The duke couldn’t recall which of you he had hung, or where—such trifles stick not in a soldier’s memory. But he was sure he had hanged one of you for grinding poor folk. ‘And I’m the man to hang another,’ quoth the good duke.”

These insults from such a low man—coupled with his immunity, shielded by the duke—drove the choleric old man into helpless rage. He shook his fist, tried to threaten, but could not speak. Then he gave a shriek, coiled like a rattlesnake ready to strike—and spat violently onto Martin’s doublet.

The thick-skinned soldier treated it with contempt. “Here’s a venomous old toad! He knows a kick from his foot would send him to his final rest—and wants me to cheat the gallows. But I’ve slain too many men in fair fight to raise a limb against anything less than a man. And this I count no man. What is it? An old goat-skin bag full of rotten bones.”

“My mule! My mule!” screamed Ghysbrecht.

Jorian helped him up, trembling. Once mounted, he gathered unnatural strength. The figure racing to Tergou was truly terrifying—so aged and wizened the face, so white and flowing the hair, so fierce the fury that shook his bent frame as he spurred forward, yelling, “I’ll make their hearts ache. I’ll make their hearts ache. All of them. All—All—All!”

The black sheep sat disconsolate among the revelers, eyeing Hans Memling’s wallet. He had taken it off and tossed it on the table. How easily they could have switched the letters. For the first time, they regretted not learning to write like their brother.

Then Hans mentioned leaving. The brothers whispered, deciding to abandon the plan—for now. Before they could finish, Dierich Brower appeared in the doorway and winked.

They stepped outside. “Come to the burgomaster at once,” he said.

They found Ghysbrecht pale and agitated, Margaret Van Eyck’s handwriting before him. “I have written what you desired,” he said. “Now for the superscription. What were the words? Did you see?”

“We cannot read,” said Cornelis.

“Then all this labor is lost!” Ghysbrecht snapped. “Dolts!”

“But,” said Sybrandt, “I heard the words read. I remember: ‘To Gerard Eliassoen, these by the hand of the trusty Hans Memling, with all speed.’”

“Good. Now, how was it folded? How large?”

“Longer than that one, not as long as this.”

“Very well. Where is he?”

“At the hostelry.”

“Go then. Take this groat—treat him. Ask to see the letter, and substitute this. Bring me the original.”

They agreed, took the letter, and hurried to the hostelry.

They had barely left when Dierich Brower emerged from the Stadthouse, ordered to watch them until the true letter was secured. He waited outside.

He did not wait long. They returned almost immediately, heads bowed.

“Too late!” they cried. “Too late! He is gone.”

“Gone? How long?”

“Scarce five minutes. Cursed chance!”

“You must return to the burgomaster at once,” said Dierich.

“To what end?”

“No matter—come!” And he hurried them back.

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was not a man to accept defeat.

“Well,” he said, hearing the news, “suppose he is gone. Is he mounted?”

“No.”

“Then what stops you from catching him?”

“But what use is catching him? There are no inns along the road.”

“Fools!” he snapped. “Is there no way to empty a man’s pockets but liquor and sleight of hand?”

A look passed between Ghysbrecht and Dierich—one that clarified everything. The brothers paled, their resolve crumbling.
