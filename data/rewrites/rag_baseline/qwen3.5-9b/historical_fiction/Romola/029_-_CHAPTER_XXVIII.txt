Four days later, Romola traversed the winding lanes toward the workshop of Piero di Cosimo on the Via Gualfonda. The atmosphere along the route was thick with tension; the thoroughfares were clogged with French soldiers and Florentine citizens locked in a mutual, hostile scrutiny. On one side, the invaders, stripped of their homeland's context and confronted with a culture they deemed inferior, paraded with an arrogant air of pre-eminence. On the other, the Florentines, weary of performing hospitality for guests who treated them like subjects rather than hosts, simmered with resentment. The initial veneer of diplomatic festivity—the grand religious spectacles with their impossible flying machines, the royal courtship rituals, and the hollow discussions of commerce—had evaporated. In its place rose the harsh reality that the new Charlemagne viewed Florence not as a partner, but as a conquered prize, intending to install a viceroy and restore the exiled Medici. This logic was absurd, a grotesque inversion of divine justice, given that the sole grievance of the city was the unpopular policies of Piero de' Medici. Yet Florence refused submission. This defiance manifested in the frantic councils of the Old Palace and in the defiant banners fluttering above the piazzas, mocking every insolent Frenchman. Consequently, the streets became dangerous terrain for noblewomen. However, Romola, cloaked in her somber black veil and mantle, accompanied by the loyal old Maso, moved with a sense of armored security, immune to prying glances. Her destination was urgent: she needed to inspect the progress of her father's portrait.

Piero di Cosimo was a capricious artist, prone to abandoning canvases for months or burying them in coffers until they were lost to oblivion unless driven by strict deadlines. Though Romola enjoyed his favor, she knew that sentiment was no shield against his whims. Having promised completion weeks prior, he had failed to deliver, fueling her nervous anxiety. She possessed a desperate need to possess a likeness of her father during his blindness—a period of profound darkness in his life—and feared that without this visual anchor, his image would fade from her mind entirely. Love, she understood, demanded absolute fidelity; it required the preservation of the beloved's essence in memory. By special dispensation, she was permitted to enter his studio without announcement.

She slid the heavy iron bar and called out in a sharp, flute-like tone, mimicking the little maid who once summoned Tito. Piero responded instantly, though his explanation for his readiness was less flattering than intended. "Ah, Madonna Romola," he said, opening the door. "I thought my eggs had arrived; I was starving for them."

"I have brought you sustenance superior to hard-boiled eggs, Piero," Romola replied, offering a smile as she adjusted her veil. From Maso, she retrieved a small basket brimming with cakes and *confetti*. Stepping across the threshold, she continued, "I know you delight in such treats when they require no effort. Confess it."

"Yes, when they descend upon me as effortlessly as sunlight," Piero admitted, crossing his arms and staring down at the sweets as Romola unveiled them. He looked up then, his eyes tracing the line of her hair and the fall of her hood with the practiced appreciation of a master painter. "Yet I suspect these confections serve a different purpose: to silence your tongue while you lecture me."

"Proceed to the inner chamber," he instructed, gesturing toward the workspace. "You will find I have advanced the composition since your last visit, though it remains unfinished. Remember, I do not bind myself to promises. As the saying goes: 'Whoever promises and fails to keep, shall never have a soul at peace.'"

The door leading to the wild garden was shut tight, sealing the space. Piero was already at work, but not on Romola's commission. Her father's portrait stood propped against the wall, awaiting transport to the optimal light source. As he bent to lift it, he inadvertently revealed another canvas hidden behind it—an oil sketch of Tito, recently updated. The smaller work sat recessed within the larger frame, unnoticed by Piero until he shifted the heavy panel. While examining a detail in the piece he held, he began to move it toward an easel, unaware of the discovery.

Romola gasped, her cheeks flushing with shock. "That is Tito!"

Piero glanced around, offering a silent shrug of vexation at his own lapse in memory. He was annoyed by the oversight. Romola remained transfixed, her expression shifting from surprise to alarmed confusion. "What a peculiar image," she said, her voice trembling slightly. "When did you create this? What signifies it?"

"A mere whim," Piero dismissed, removing his skullcap and scratching his head, adopting his characteristic grimace to deflect emotional exposure. "I required a handsome youthful visage, and your husband's features suited the task perfectly."

He stepped forward, lifting the sketch with its back turned to Romola, feigning a cursory inspection before discarding it as unworthy of display. But Romola, recalling the distinctive chain-mail armor and pierced by the uncanny convergence of symbols linking Tito to fear, moved to his side. "Do not hide it; allow me to view it again. That man with the rope around his neck... I witnessed him. I saw you approach him in the Duomo. What compelled you to include him in a portrait alongside Tito?"

Seeing no viable escape, Piero chose partial honesty. "It was pure chance. The man was fleeing—climbing the steps and seized hold of your husband. Presumably, he stumbled. I happened to be present, observed the scene, and deemed the savage-looking elder a suitable study. But it is worthless; merely a reckless stroke."

With a gesture of finality, he moved the sketch away, placing it high on a shelf. "Come, examine the Oedipus."

His haste to conceal the drawing had achieved the exact opposite of his intent: it confirmed the unsettling nature of the incident. The implication lingered—that there was something inherently negative, something cowardly, about the event involving Tito. This suggestion struck a chord of pride and delicacy in Romola, silencing her further inquiry. She feared that pressing the matter might suggest she harbored even a whisper of doubt regarding her husband. Instead, she spoke softly, her voice steady. "He was a pitiful, strange creature, that prisoner. Know you anything else of him?"

"No further information," Piero replied smoothly. "I merely directed him to the hospital. Observe now; the face of Oedipus is nearly complete. Tell me your assessment."

Romola turned her full attention to the portrait, standing in reverent silence before it. Finally, she spoke. "Ah," she breathed, her eyes brightening with moisture. "You have achieved exactly what I desired. You have imbued it with that look of listening. My dear Piero..." She turned to him, gratitude evident in her expression.

"Now that is precisely what I cannot endure in women," Piero snapped, kicking aside debris cluttering the floor. "You pour forth emotions where none are warranted. Why should you offer thanks for a commissioned work, especially when I make you wait? If I paint, surely it is for my own satisfaction and reputation, yes? Should you thank a man for not being a rogue or a fool? It suffices if he thanks God for granting him such virtue. Women imagine walls are held together with honey."

"You crusty Piero!" Romola laughed through her tears, reaching into the basket to extract a particularly crisp sweetmeat. "I forgot how irritable you are. Here, consume this delicacy."

Piero accepted the treat with the grace of a bear dreaming of pears receiving a swan's egg—he genuinely savored the sweetness, yet remained accustomed to masking his vulnerabilities beneath a rough exterior. "It is excellent, Madonna Antigone," he murmured, reaching for another. He had subsisted on nothing but hard eggs for two weeks.

Romola stood opposite him, her fresh anxieties momentarily suspended by the sight of this naive joy. "Farewell, Piero," she said eventually, setting the basket down. "I promise not to express gratitude if you finalize the portrait promptly and skillfully. I shall remind you that you were obligated to complete it for your own sake."

"Agreed," Piero replied curtly, assisting her efficiently in folding her mantle and veil.

Once the door clicked shut behind her, he exhaled, thinking, "I am relieved she asked no further questions about that sketch. I dread the notion that she might deduce I considered her fine husband a fitting model for a coward. I minimized the matter; she will not dwell on it."

Piero was overly optimistic, a common trait among open-hearted men attempting subtle deception. Yet, as Romola walked home, the image of the sketch weighed heavily upon her mind. She could not reconcile the facts: the chain armor and the encounter described by Piero, both occurring on the morning the armor was acquired. Had the look of terror on Tito's face been genuine? What did it signify?

"It means nothing," she whispered to herself, trying to steady her resolve. "It was merely a coincidence. Shall I inquire of Tito?"

Her reason commanded, "No: I will not interrogate him about actions he did not voluntarily disclose. To do so would violate the trust I owe him."

But her heart countered, "I dare not ask him."

A terrible fracture existed within that trust. Like those who clutch a precious gemstone, fearing it may be cracked, she hesitated, paralyzed by the desire to believe the bond remained intact.
