"What was that remark you made concerning our engagement at seven-thirty?" Mr. Cupples inquired as the pair emerged from the imposing gateway of the residential block. "Do we truly possess such an appointment?"

"Certainly," Trent replied, his voice carrying a peculiar buoyancy. "You are dining with me. There exists but one fitting manner to honor this occasion: a meal where I bear the cost. I sought your company first. Having finally plumbed the depths of a singular mystery—one that has haunted my thoughts for over a year—it demands celebration. Unless a triumph of this magnitude warrants a treat, I fail to comprehend what does. Cupples, we shall bypass the club. Tonight requires a festival, and the sight of a gentleman collapsing beneath pleasurable emotion within the confines of a London club would be fatal to any professional reputation. Furthermore, the culinary offerings there are invariably identical, or at least indistinguishable in flavor, regardless of preparation. The eternal banquet at my club has wearied countless members like myself, and it shall continue to do so. However, tonight, let us spread a feast that remains unseen by the masses. We shall avoid the halls crowded by the satraps. Let us proceed to Sheppard's."

"Who is Sheppard?" Mr. Cupples asked softly, walking beside his companion. Trent moved with an unnatural lightness, and a passing policeman, noting his expression, offered a kindly smile, attributing the man's visible happiness solely to the effects of alcohol.

"Who is Sheppard?" Trent echoed, his tone sharpened by a bitter intensity. "That inquiry, if I may permit myself the observation, Cupples, epitomizes the spirit of aimless curiosity prevalent in this restless age. I propose dining at Sheppard's, and immediately you fold your arms, driven by a frenzied pride of intellect, demanding to know the identity of Sheppard before crossing the threshold. I refuse to cater to the vices of the modern mind. Sheppard's is merely a venue for sustenance. I know no Sheppard. It never entered my consciousness that such a figure existed. Likely, he is a myth born of totemic origins. All I know is that one may procure a portion of saddle of mutton at Sheppard's so superior that it has caused many an American visitor to curse the advent of Christopher Columbus... Hail, taxi!"

A cab glided smoothly to the curb, and the driver accepted his instructions with a dignified nod.

"There is further cause for suggesting Sheppard's," Trent continued, feverishly igniting a cigarette, "namely, that I am soon to wed the most extraordinary woman in existence. I trust the association of ideas is evident."

"You intend to marry Mabel!" Mr. Cupples exclaimed. "My dearest friend, what joyous tidings! Clasp hands, Trent; this is magnificent! I congratulate you both from the depths of my soul. Might I interject, without disrupting your effervescent spirits—which are entirely natural, recalling my own state in similar circumstances decades ago—but might I express how fervently I have desired this union? Mabel has witnessed profound sorrow, yet she is undoubtedly fashioned by the grand design of humanity to exert the most benevolent influence upon the life of a virtuous man. Yet, I was unaware of her sentiments regarding yourself. *Your* sentiments toward her, I have observed for some time," Mr. Cupples continued, a twinkle in his eye that belied his apparent worldly cynicism. "I perceived it immediately when you both dined at my residence; you sat attentively listening to Professor Peppmuller, your gaze fixed intently upon her. Some of us elder gentlemen still retain our faculties, my dear boy."

"Mabel asserts she perceived it prior to that," Trent replied, his countenance falling slightly. "I believed I was enacting the role of a man not utterly infatuated. Alas, I have never excelled at dissimulation. I would not be surprised if even ancient Peppmuller discerned something through his double-convex lenses. Nevertheless, however deranged I may have appeared as an undeclared suitor," he resumed, regaining his vivacious demeanor, "I am destined to be considerably more so henceforth. Regarding your congratulations, thank you a thousand times, for I know they stem from genuine affection. You are the sort of formidable brute who would contort a visage three feet long if you suspected we were erring. Incidentally, I find myself a fool tonight; I am compelled to ramble. You must endeavor to tolerate it. Perhaps it would prove easier if I sang you a verse of one of your cherished old ballads. Which tune did you perpetually sing? Something akin to this, was it not?" "I never sang that melody in my lifetime," protested Mr. Cupples. "I have never heard it before."

"Are you certain?" Trent queried, doubt flickering in his eyes. "Very well, I presume I must accept your testimony. It is a sublime song, nonetheless: not the entire warbling grove assembled in concert could surpass it. Somehow, it articulates my current sentiments better than any other composition; it ascends unbidden to the lips. From the fulness of the heart, the mouth speaks, as the Bishop of Bath and Wells remarked when listening to a speech by Mr. Balfour."

"When precisely was that?" Mr. Cupples asked.

"On the occasion," Trent replied, "of the introduction of the Compulsory Notification of Diseases of Poultry Bill, that ill-starred measure which you, of course, recall. Hullo!" he interrupted abruptly as the cab surged down a side street and curved into a wide, bustling thoroughfare. "We have arrived." The cab halted.

"Here we stand," Trent said as he settled the fare and guided Mr. Cupples into a lengthy, paneled chamber furnished with numerous tables and filled with the murmur of conversation. "This is the sanctuary of fulfilled desire, the bower encircled by roses. Observe there are three bookmakers consuming pork at my favored table. We shall occupy the one in the opposite corner."

He engaged earnestly with a waiter, while Mr. Cupples, lost in a pleasant reverie, warmed himself before the roaring fireplace. "The vintage here," Trent resumed as they took their seats, "is undoubtedly derived from grapes. What shall we consume?"

Mr. Cupples emerged from his contemplation. "I believe," he stated, "I shall have milk and soda water."

"Hush!" Trent urged. "The head-waiter possesses a frail heart and might overhear you. Milk and soda water! Cupples, you may consider yourself robust, and I do not deny your fortitude, but I caution you that this habit of blending beverages has claimed the lives of many a sturdier man than yourself. Prudence dictates you abandon it forthwith. Fill the bowl high with Samian wine; reserve soda for the Turkish hordes. Behold, our repast arrives." He issued another command to the waiter, who arranged the dishes before them and vanished. Trent evidently enjoyed the status of a respected patron. "I have procured," he announced, "wine of which I am assured, and I trust you will sample it. Should you have taken a solemn vow, then in the name of all teetotal saints, drink the water standing at your elbow, but do not seek cheap renown by requesting milk and soda."

"I have never undertaken any pledge," Mr. Cupples said, scrutinizing his mutton with approval. "Simply, I harbor no fondness for wine. I purchased a bottle once and consumed it merely to ascertain its nature, and it rendered me ill. Yet, it was likely inferior vinage. I shall taste a drop of yours, as it is your dinner, and I assure you, my dear Trent, I wish to perform something unconventional to demonstrate the strength of my feelings on this auspicious occasion. I have not experienced such delight in many years. To reflect," he murmured aloud as the waiter filled his glass, "upon the resolution of the Manderson enigma, the exoneration of the innocent, and the crowning of your own and Mabel's felicity—all converging simultaneously! I toast you, my dearest friend." Mr. Cupples lifted his glass and took a mere, tentative sip.

"You possess a magnificent nature," Trent observed, deeply moved. "Your external semblance betrays the vastness of your soul. I anticipated seeing an elephant conduct at the opera before witnessing you drink to my health. Dear Cupples! May his bill remain forever stained with that delicate rose-hue!—No, curses all!" he erupted, startling a fleeting trace of discomfort across his companion's face as he tasted the wine anew. "I have no business intruding upon your preferences. I offer apologies. You shall receive whatever you desire, even if it causes the head-waiter to perish in his pride."
