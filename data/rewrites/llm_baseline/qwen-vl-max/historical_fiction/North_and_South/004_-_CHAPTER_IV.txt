“Cast me upon some naked shore,
Where I may track
Only the print of some sad wrack;
If thou be there though the seas roare,
I shall no gentler calm implore.”

He was gone. The house had closed for the evening. No more clear blue skies or golden hues at dusk. Margaret ascended to prepare for tea, only to find Dixon in a sour mood—irritated by the unexpected interruption of a visitor on a busy day. Her displeasure showed itself in sharp, impatient strokes as she brushed Margaret’s hair, pretending haste to attend to Mrs. Hale. Yet despite this pretense, Margaret waited long into the evening before her mother descended from her room.

She sat alone by the fire, candles unlit behind her, lost in thought. The day had held so much—joyful walks, sketching under open skies, a pleasant dinner with laughter and warmth—and then, the heavy silence of their walk in the garden. How different men were from women! She felt deeply wounded, unsettled by the impossibility of refusal when her heart had stirred in response to his proposal. And yet he—within minutes of being rejected—had spoken as if nothing mattered but success, briefs, fine houses, polished society. As if those were all that defined a man’s life. Oh, how she could have loved him—if only he had been different, not just in manner, but in depth, in soul.

Then came the thought: perhaps his lightness was not real at all, but a mask. A shield against pain too deep to show. If she had loved and been turned away, would not her own heart have cracked beneath such grief? The idea brought a strange comfort—almost a kind of justice.

Her mother entered the drawing-room before she could settle these thoughts. Margaret had to push aside the weight of what had passed, and become an attentive listener as her mother recounted trivial grievances—Dixon’s complaint about a burnt ironing blanket, Susan Lightfoot seen wearing artificial flowers, a sign, she insisted, of vanity and frivolity.

Mr. Hale sipped his tea in silence, lost in thought. Margaret answered for both parents. She wondered how they could forget so completely the man who had shared their day—the one whose presence had once seemed so vital. Then it struck her: he had not proposed to them. He had not asked for their consent. That explained their silence.

After tea, Mr. Hale rose and stood beside the fireplace, elbow resting on the mantel, hand supporting his head. He stared into the flames, sighing deeply now and then. Mrs. Hale stepped out to consult with Dixon about winter clothing for the poor. Margaret prepared her mother’s knitting, dreading the length of the evening, longing for bed, where she might quietly review the events of the day.

“Margaret!” he said suddenly, in a voice broken by urgency, making her start. “Is that tapestry work urgent? Can you leave it and come into my study? I need to speak to you about something very serious—something that concerns us all.”

“Very serious,” repeated Margaret, her mind racing. Mr. Lennox had not spoken privately with her father after her refusal—so this could not be about him. Still, guilt pricked her. She was no longer just a girl, but a woman capable of marriage. And what if her father disapproved of her having refused him?

But soon she realized this was not about a recent event. It was deeper, older. Her father motioned her to sit beside him. He stirred the fire, snuffed the candles, sighed again, and finally spoke—his words forced out like a burden:

“Margaret… I am going to leave Helstone.”

“Leave Helstone, papa? Why?” she whispered.

He did not answer at first. His hands fidgeted with papers on the table, opening his mouth several times, closing it again. Margaret watched, helpless, as the tension tightened around him. She could not bear it.

“But why, dear papa? Please tell me!”

He looked up, his eyes full of sorrow, and said slowly, with effort: “Because I can no longer serve as a minister in the Church of England.”

She had expected news of a new appointment—perhaps a grand rectory far from Helstone, something her mother had long desired. But this—this was worse than any change of residence. This was a severance of faith, of identity.

“What do you mean?” she breathed. “Why not?”

“It is not about Frederick,” he said quickly. “Not about anything he has done. The bishop would not concern himself with that. It is about me. About what I have come to believe.”

He paused, gathering strength. “Margaret, I will tell you now. I will answer your questions—but after tonight, let us never speak of this again. I can face the consequences. But speaking of it… it breaks me.”

“Doubts, papa?” she whispered. “Doubts about religion?”

“No,” he said firmly. “Not doubts about religion. Not harm to that. But… about whether I have the right to hold this living. For years I’ve wrestled with it. I’ve tried to silence my conscience with the authority of the Church. But I cannot go on. I love the Church—I love it dearly—but I cannot stay where I must betray my own truth.”

He broke off, unable to continue. Margaret sat frozen. This was not mere change—it was exile. A fall from grace, perhaps.

“I’ve been reading today,” he said, trying to steady himself, “about the two thousand ministers ejected from their churches. I tried to take courage from their example. But it does no good. I feel it—deeply.”

“Oh, papa,” she cried, tears rising. “Have you thought of this carefully? It seems impossible—horrible.”

The foundation of her world trembled. Her father—her anchor—was choosing a path that shattered everything she knew.

Seeing her distress, he gathered himself. He walked to the bookshelf, took down a volume he had read often lately, and placed it in her hands.

“Listen,” he said, putting an arm around her waist. She clutched his hand, unable to lift her face. She could not focus on the words, so overwhelmed was she by emotion.

“This is from a clergyman named Oldfield—minister of Carsington, over a hundred years ago. He wrote this while facing the same choice. His trials are over. He fought the good fight.”

He read aloud:

*When thou canst no longer continue in thy work without dishonour to God, discredit to religion, foregoing thy integrity, wounding conscience, spoiling thy peace, and hazarding the loss of thy salvation; in a word, when the conditions upon which thou must continue (if thou wilt continue) in thy employments are sinful, and unwarranted by the word of God, thou mayest, yea, thou must believe that God will turn thy very silence, suspension, deprivation, and laying aside, to His glory, and the advancement of the Gospel’s interest. When God will not use thee in one kind, yet He will in another. A soul that desires to serve and honour Him shall never want opportunity to do it; nor must thou so limit the Holy One of Israel, as to think He hath but one way in which He can glorify Himself by thee. He can do it by thy silence as well as by thy preaching; the laying aside as well as thy continuance in thy work. It is not pretence of doing God the greatest service, or performing the weightiest duty, that will excuse the least sin, though that sin capacitated or gave us the opportunity for doing that duty. Thou wilt have little thanks, O my soul! if, when thou art charged with corrupting God’s worship, falsifying thy vows, thou pretendest a necessity for it in order to a continuance in the ministry.*

As he read, he found strength. He believed he could stand firm. But when he finished, he heard her quiet sob. His courage faltered.

“Margaret,” he murmured, drawing her closer, “think of the early martyrs. Think of those who suffered for truth.”

“But father,” she said, lifting her tear-streaked face, “the martyrs died for truth. You—oh, dear, dear papa—”

“I suffer for conscience,” he said, his voice firm despite the trembling in his frame. “I must do what my conscience demands. I have carried shame for too long—shame that would have moved any stronger man to act. Your mother’s wish—granted at last, though in mockery—has brought this moment. And I am thankful for it. A month ago, the bishop offered me another living. To accept it, I would have had to make a fresh declaration of conformity. I tried. I tried to simply refuse the preferment and stay here—strangling my conscience, as I had already strained it. God forgive me.”

He walked the room, muttering words of self-reproach. Margaret listened, grateful for the few she heard.

At last he stopped. “We must leave Helstone.”

“Yes,” she whispered. “But when?”

“I’ve written to the bishop—though I may have told you already, I forget things now. I’ve informed him of my resignation. He was kind. He argued, pleaded. But it was useless. They are the same arguments I’ve used on myself. I must resign. I must go to him in person to say farewell. That will be hard. But worse—far worse—will be leaving my people. A curate, Mr. Brown, has been appointed. He arrives tomorrow. Next Sunday, I preach my farewell sermon.”

It was sudden. And yet—perhaps it was better. Delay would only prolong the pain. To hear it all at once, laid bare, was like being stunned into numbness.

“What does mamma say?” she asked, her voice thick.

To her surprise, her father began pacing again. At length, he stopped.

“Margaret,” he said, “I am a coward. I cannot bear to hurt her. I know her married life has not been all she hoped. This will break her. I have not had the strength to tell her. But she must know. Now.”

Margaret felt a wave of shock. Her mother knew nothing. And yet, everything was already in motion.

“Yes,” she said, “she must know. But—she will be shattered.”

“Where are we going?” she asked, trying to grasp the future.

“To Milton-Northern,” he said flatly.

“The manufacturing town in Darkshire?”

“Yes.”

“Why there, papa?”

“Because I can earn bread there. Because no one knows me. No one will remind me of Helstone.”

“Bread?” she echoed. “But you and mamma have money—”

She stopped herself, seeing the shadow deepen on his face.

He understood. He saw the worry in her eyes, mirrored his own despair. He changed course.

“You shall know everything, Margaret. But help me tell your mother. I cannot do it. The thought of her pain makes me sick. If I tell you, perhaps you can break it to her tomorrow.”

She hesitated. She disliked it more than anything she had ever faced. But then she lifted her head.

“It is painful,” she said, “but it must be done. I will do it as well as I can. You have many hard things to do.”

He nodded, pressing her hand. She nearly wept again.

“Now,” she said, trying to shift her thoughts, “tell me our plans. You and mamma have income beyond the living, don’t you? Aunt Shaw has money.”

“Yes. We have about a hundred and twenty pounds a year. Seventy of that has gone to Frederick since he left. I don’t know if he still needs it. He serves with the Spanish army. He must have some pay.”

“Frederick must not suffer,” she said firmly. “In a foreign land, unjustly treated by his own country. A hundred pounds remains. Could we not live on that in some quiet part of England? I think we could.”

“No,” he said. “That won’t do. I must work. I must keep busy to avoid sinking into gloom. And a country parish would only remind me of Helstone—of what I’ve lost. And a hundred pounds wouldn’t stretch far enough to give your mother the comforts she deserves. We must go to Milton. It is settled.”

He added, almost apologetically, “I decide better when alone—when not influenced by those I love. I cannot endure objections. They make me weak.”

She kept silent. Where they went mattered less than the fact that they were leaving everything behind.

“A few months ago,” he continued, “when my doubt became unbearable, I wrote to Mr. Bell. You remember him?”

“No,” she said. “But I know who he is—Frederick’s godfather, your old tutor at Oxford?”

“Yes. He is a Fellow at Plymouth College. He is from Milton-Northern—or at least, he owns property there. It has grown in value since the town expanded. I wrote to him. I didn’t tell you why. But I trusted him. He has lived a peaceful life. He hasn’t given me strength—but he has been kind. And through him, we are going to Milton.”

“How?” she asked.

“He has tenants, houses, mills. Though he dislikes the place—too noisy for his taste—he must maintain connections. He says there is a position open for a private tutor.”

“A private tutor?” she said, incredulous. “What do manufacturers want with classics? With literature? With the education of gentlemen?”

“Oh,” he said, “some of them are intelligent men, aware of their own limits—more so than many at Oxford. Some want to learn. Others want their children better educated than they were. There is an opening. Mr. Bell has recommended me to Mr. Thornton—a tenant of his, a man of sense, from his letters. In Milton, I shall have a busy life, even if not a happy one. And the people, the scenes—nothing like Helstone. That is why I choose it.”

Margaret understood. It was not just about work. It was about difference. About escape. Though she despised the North—its factories, its grim towns, its harsh people—there was one thing it offered: distance. A place where Helstone could not haunt them.

“When do we go?” she asked, after a pause.

“I don’t know exactly. I wanted to talk to you first. Your mother knows nothing yet. But I think—in a fortnight. After I send my resignation, I have no right to remain.”

“In a fortnight!” she gasped.

“No—not precisely. Nothing is fixed,” he said, seeing the sorrow in her eyes. “But it should be decided soon.”

She regained composure. “Yes, papa. It should be. Only—mamma doesn’t know. That is the hardest part.”

“Poor Maria,” he murmured. “Poor, poor Maria. If I were not married—if I were alone—how easy it would be. But I cannot tell her.”

“No,” she said softly. “I will tell her. Give me until tomorrow evening. Oh, papa,” she cried, “say it’s a dream. Say it’s not real. You cannot mean to leave the Church—to abandon Helstone—to be torn from us—by some illusion? You do not really mean it?”

He remained still. Then he looked at her, his gaze steady, his voice slow and hoarse:

“I do mean it, Margaret. Do not deceive yourself. This is not a dream. It is my resolve. My fixed intention.”

He held her gaze for a long moment. She returned it, pleading, searching—until she could no longer deny the truth.

Then she rose and moved toward the door.

He called her back.

He stood by the fireplace, bent and weary. But as she approached, he straightened, placed his hands on her head, and said solemnly:

“The blessing of God be upon thee, my child.”

“And may He restore you to His Church,” she replied, from the depths of her heart.

She feared her words might be wrong, irreverent. But then she threw her arms around him. He held her close, murmuring to himself: “The martyrs bore greater pain. I will not shrink.”

They were startled by Mrs. Hale’s voice calling for her daughter.

They pulled apart, each knowing what lay ahead.

“Go, Margaret,” he said quickly. “I shall be out all tomorrow. Before night, you will have told your mother.”

“Yes,” she said, and left the room—dazed, stunned, carrying the weight of a world undone.
