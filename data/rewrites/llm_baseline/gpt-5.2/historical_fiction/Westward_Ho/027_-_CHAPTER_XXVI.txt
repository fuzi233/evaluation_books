One last look—so a man might tell himself, and pretend it were merely piety—at that tropic sea which seemed to have been poured from a crucible. Evening came down on the bay of Santa Marta like warm oil, and the land-breeze combed the surface into a million tiny flames. Beyond, the forests crackled with their own quiet light: fireflies winking and drifting as steadily as embers in a kitchen-dusk, when a cook rakes the coals and the room answers with a soft, contented hiss. Mist lay along the inner hills, lazy as steam from a great pot left to settle; and above all, the peak of Horqueta, nineteen thousand feet of frozen white, caught the last rose of sunset and then, when the color died, took up again under Venus a pale gold—ice becoming treasure by mere change of lamp.

Gold. That word had a smell to it here, if a man were willing to swear so in mixed company: a metallic sweetness in the damp air, as if the earth itself sweated coin. It was that very fancy—half poetry, half appetite—that warmed the Bishop of Cartagena as he sat in the state cabin of the great galleon, The City of the True Cross, and gazed shoreward with a scholar’s melancholy.

His stoutness had been arranged to advantage: his body in one easy-chair, his stout ankles on another; before him a table spread as if the ship were not a floating fortress but a banquet-room. Oranges and limes lay split and shining, their oils sharp as a struck match; guavas with their bruised, musky perfume; pine-apples exhaling that fermented, honeyed bite which can make a hungry man angry with his own hunger. All the fruits of Ind crowded together, their skins sweating in the cabin heat. An Indian girl, scarved and chained with gold as with a second bondage, moved a fan of feathers in a patient rhythm to keep the flies from settling on his holiness’s mouth and hands. At his side, in a pail, stood ice hacked from Horqueta—ice bought with Indian backs and delivered as a pious gift—and in the ice more than one flask of Alicant, virtuous and strong.

But the bishop was not, this evening at least, to drink and nibble alone. At his especial request Don Pedro, colonel of the soldiers aboard; Don Alverez, intendant of his Catholic majesty’s customs at Santa Marta; and Don Paul, captain of mariners of The City of the True Cross, had come to keep him company. Two friars sat lower down the table, and between sips and peels and the endless buzz of tropical insects they were doing their best to coax his mind away from the latest ruin of his cathedral town—Cartagena, sacked and burned by Sir Francis Drake.

“We have been great sufferers, señores—ah, great sufferers,” snuffled the bishop, quoting Scripture with that glib irreverence which takes holy phrases and salts them like meat for a traveling table. He complained of Tita and the mosquitoes in the same breath as he spoke of Providence, and yet no one contradicted him. “Great sufferers, truly; but there shall be a remnant—ah, a remnant like the shaking of the olive tree and the gleaning grapes when the vintage is done.—Ah! Gold? Yes, I trust Our Lady’s mercies are not shut up, nor her arms shortened.—Look, señores!”

He pointed with a broad hand out of the stern-window where the bay lay like a poured ingot.

“It looks gold! it smells of gold, as I may say, by a poetical license. Yea, the very waves, as they ripple past us, sing of gold, gold, gold!”

“It is a great privilege,” said the intendant smoothly, “to have comfort so gracefully administered at once by a churchman and a scholar.”

“A poet, too,” said Don Pedro. “You have no notion what sweet sonnets—”

“Hush, Don Pedro—hush! If I, a mateless bird, have spent an idle hour in teaching lovers how to sing, why, what of that? I am a churchman, señores; but I am a man and I can feel, señores; I can sympathize; I can palliate; I can excuse. Who knows better than I how much human nature lurks in us fallen sons of Adam? Tita!”

“Um?” said the girl—more a throat-sound than a word, as if her native tongue had been pressed down under Spanish syllables and would not rise.

“Fill his excellency the intendant’s glass. Does much more treasure come down, illustrious señor? May the poor of Mary hope for a few more crumbs from their Mistress’s table?”

“Not a pezo, I fear. The big white cow up there”—and he jerked his chin toward Horqueta—“has been milked dry for this year.”

“Ah!” The bishop looked up as if he could drain the mountain with his eyes. “Only good to cool wine with, eh? and as safe for the time being as Solomon’s birds.”

“Solomon’s birds? Explain your recondite allusion, my lord.”

“Enlighten us, your excellency, enlighten us.”

“Ah! thereby hangs a tale. You know the holy birds who run up and down on the Prado at Seville among the ladies’ pretty feet—eh? with hooked noses and cinnamon crests? Of course. Hoopoes—Upupa, as the classics have it. Well, señores, once on a time, the story goes, these hoopoes all had golden crowns on their heads; and, señores, they took the consequences—eh? But it befell on a day that all the birds and beasts came to do homage at the court of his most Catholic majesty King Solomon, and among them came these same hoopoes; and they had a little request to make, the poor rogues. And what do you think it was? Why, that King Solomon would pray for them that they might wear any sort of crowns but these same golden ones; for—listen, Tita, and see the snare of riches—mankind so hunted, and shot, and trapped, and snared them, for the sake of these same golden crowns, that life was a burden to bear. So Solomon prayed, and instead of golden crowns, they all received crowns of feathers; and ever since, señores, they live as merrily as crickets in an oven, and also have the honor of bearing the name of his most Catholic majesty King Solomon. Tita! fill the señor commandant’s glass. Fray Gerundio, what are you whispering about down there, sir?”

Fray Gerundio had done no more than murmur to his brother something which sounded like a prayer uttered through clenched teeth.

“O si sic omnia!—would that all gold would turn to feathers in like wise!”

“Then, friend,” replied the other friar, a Dominican too but darker and sterner in the face, “corrupt human nature would within a week discover some fresh bauble, for which to kill and be killed in vain.”

“What is that, Fray Gerundio?” asked the bishop again.

“I merely remarked, that it were well for the world if all mankind were to put up the same prayer as the hoopoes.”

“World, sir? What do you know about the world? Convert your Indians, sir, if you please, and leave affairs of state to your superiors.” He turned to the Dons and lowered his voice with the conspiratorial relish of a man who enjoys his own authority. “You will excuse him, señores. A very worthy and pious man, but a poor peasant’s son; and beside—you understand. A little wrong here; too much fasting and watching, I fear, good man.” And he tapped his forehead to signify that Fray Gerundio’s wits were not, in his judgment, properly salted.

The friar saw and heard and only smiled—one of those quiet men whose charity had been sharpened, like a blade honed against cruelty, by watching his countrymen grind the weak. He was clumsy of court-speech; he could not knit three fine sentences together to please a bishop’s ear. But among Indians and negroes, crucifix in hand, he could make words live. He had been used to rebuffs; he took them like hard bread and swallowed without complaint, sipping his wine in silence while the talk rose and fell around him like surf.

“They say,” observed the commandant, “that a very small Plate-fleet will go to Spain this year.”

“What else?” said the intendant, and his voice cracked like a dry stick. “What have we to send, in the name of all saints, since these accursed English Lutherans have swept us out clean?”

“And if we had anything to send,” said the sea-captain, “what have we to send it in? That fiend incarnate, Drake—”

“Ah!” cried the bishop, as if the name itself were a bad smell at table. “Spare my ears! Don Pedro, you will oblige my weakness by not mentioning that man—his name is Tartarean, unfit for polite lips. Draco—a dragon—serpent—the emblem of Diabolus himself—ah! And the guardian of the golden apples of the West, who would fain devour our new Hercules, his most Catholic majesty. Deceived Eve, too, with one of those same apples—a very evil name, señores—a Tartarean name.—Tita!”

“Um!”

“Fill my glass.”

“Nay,” swore the colonel, slamming his cup down so that the fruit trembled, “this English fellow is of another breed of serpent from that, I warrant.”

“Your reason, señor; your reason?”

“Because this one would have seen Eve at the bottom of the sea, before he let her, or any one but himself, taste aught which looked like gold.”

“Ah, ah!—very good! But—we laugh, valiant señores, while the Church weeps. Alas for my sheep!”

“And alas for their sheepfold!” said Don Alverez. “It will be four years before we can get Cartagena rebuilt again. And as for the blockhouse, when we shall get that rebuilt, Heaven only knows, while his majesty goes on draining the Indies for his English Armada. The town is as naked now as an Indian’s back.”

“Baptista Antonio, the surveyor, has sent home by me a relation to the king, setting forth our defenceless state. But to read a relation and to act on it are two cocks of very different hackles, bishop, as all statesmen know. Heaven grant we may have orders by the next fleet to fortify, or we shall be at the mercy of every English pirate!”

“Ah, that blockhouse!” sighed the bishop, and his sigh had in it both wounded dignity and the thin whine of a man remembering the taste of other men’s wine. “That was indeed a villainous trick. A hundred and ten thousand ducats for the ransom of the town! After having burned and plundered the one-half—and having made me dine with them too, ah! and sit between the—the serpent, and his lieutenant-general—and drunk my health in my own private wine—wine that I had from Xeres nine years ago, señores—and offered, the shameless heretics, to take me to England, if I would turn Lutheran, and find me a wife, and make an honest man of me—ah! and then to demand fresh ransom for the priory and the fort—perfidious!”

“Well,” said Don Pedro, “they had the law of us, the cunning rascals, for we forgot to mention anything but the town, in the agreement. Who would have dreamed of such a fetch as that?”

“So I told my good friend the prior, when he came to me to borrow the thousand crowns. It was Heaven’s will. Unexpected like the thunderbolt, and to be borne as such. Every man must bear his own burden. How could I lend him aught?”

“Your holiness’s money had been all carried off by them before,” said the intendant, who knew, and none better, the exact contrary.

“Just so—all my scanty savings! desolate in my lone old age. Ah, señores, had we not had warning of the coming of these wretches from my dear friend the Marquess of Santa Cruz, whom I remember daily in my prayers, we had been like to them who go down quick into the pit. I too might have saved a trifle, had I been minded: but in thinking too much of others, I forgot myself, alas!”

“Warning or none, we had no right to be beaten by such a handful,” said the sea-captain. “And a shame it is, and a shame it will be, for many a day to come.”

“Do you mean to cast any slur, sir, upon the courage and conduct of his Catholic majesty’s soldiers?” said the colonel, stiffening.

“I?—No; but we were foully beaten, and that behind our barricades too, and there’s the plain truth.”

“Beaten, sir! Do you apply such a term to the fortunes of war? What more could our governor have done? Had we not the ways filled with poisoned caltrops, guarded by Indian archers, barred with butts full of earth, raked with culverins and arquebuses? What familiar spirit had we, sir, to tell us that these villains would come along the sea-beach, and not by the high-road, like Christian men?”

“Ah!” said the bishop, “it was by intuition diabolic, I doubt not, that they took that way. Satanas must need help those who serve him; and for my part, I can only attribute—the misfortune which occurred to art-magic. I believe these men to have been possessed by all fiends whatsoever.”

“Well, your holiness,” said the colonel, “there may have been devilry in it; how else would men have dared to run right into the mouths of our cannon, fire their shot against our very noses, and tumble harmless over those huge butts of earth?”

“Doubtless by force of the fiends which raged with them,” the bishop put in eagerly, glad to have the universe arranged into a simple catechism of good and evil.

“And then, with their blasphemous cries, leap upon us with sword and pike? I myself saw that Lieutenant-General Carlisle hew down with one stroke that noble young gentleman the ensign-bearer, your excellency’s sister’s son’s nephew, though he was armed cap-a-pie. Was not art-magic here? And that most furious and blaspheming Lutheran Captain Young, I saw how he caught our general by the head, after the illustrious Don Alonzo had given him a grievous wound, threw him to the earth, and so took him. Was not art-magic here?”

“Well, I say,” said the captain, “if you are looking for art-magic, what say you to their marching through the flank fire of our galleys, with eleven pieces of ordnance, and two hundred shot playing on them, as if it had been a mosquito swarm? Some said my men fired too high: but that was the English rascals’ doing, for they got down on the tide beach. But, señor commandant, though Satan may have taught them that trick, was it he that taught them to carry pikes a foot longer than yours?”

“Ah, well,” said the bishop, and his voice sagged into self-pity, “sacked are we; and San Domingo, as I hear, in worse case than we are; and St. Augustine in Florida likewise; and all that is left for a poor priest like me is to return to Spain, and see whether the pious clemency of his majesty, and of the universal Father, may not be willing to grant some small relief or bounty to the poor of Mary—perhaps—(for who knows?) to translate to a sphere of more peaceful labor one who is now old, señores, and weary with many toils—Tita! fill our glasses. I have saved somewhat—as you may have done, señores, from the general wreck; and for the flock, when I am no more, illustrious señores, Heaven’s mercies are infinite; new cities will rise from the ashes of the old, new mines pour forth their treasures into the sanctified laps of the faithful, and new Indians flock toward the life-giving standard of the Cross, to put on the easy yoke and light burden of the Church, and—”

“And where shall I be then? Ah, where? Fain would I rest, and fain depart. Tita! sling my hammock. Señores, you will excuse age and infirmities. Fray Gerundio, go to bed!”

The Dons rose to go, while the bishop maundered on, his words thickening as the wine did its work.

“Farewell! Life is short. Ah! we shall meet in heaven at last. And there are really no more pearls?”

“Not a frail; nor gold either,” said the intendant.

“Ah, well! Better a dinner of herbs where love is, than—Tita!”

“My breviary—ah! Man’s gratitude is short-lived, I had hoped—You have seen nothing of the Señora Bovadilla?”

“No.”

“Ah! she promised:—but no matter—a little trifle as a keepsake—a gold cross, or an emerald ring, or what not—I forget. And what have I to do with worldly wealth!—Ah! Tita! bring me the casket.”

When the guests were gone, the cabin’s sounds changed. Their boots and spurs faded into the ship’s creaks; the table’s fruit sat in its own sweet breath; the fan stopped and the flies returned. The bishop, alone now with the girl and his God, mumbled prayers out of his breviary and fingered jewels and gold with the dull, greedy eyes of covetous age—his lips forming Latin while his hands counted in a language older than Rome.

“Ah!—it may buy the red hat yet!—Omnia Romae venalia! Put it by, Tita, and do not look at it too much, child. Enter not into temptation. The love of money is the root of all evil; and Heaven, in love for the Indian, has made him poor in this world, that he may be rich in faith. Ah!—Ugh!—So!”

He clambered into his hammock. Tita drew the mosquito net over him, wrapped another around her own head, and curled on the cabin floor like a shadow cast down and forgotten. Soon master and slave snored a merry bass to the treble whine of mosquitoes—a whole choir of hunger and heat.

It was long past midnight, and the moon was down. The sentinels above, who had tramped and challenged until they believed their officers were deep asleep, had slipped away from what they called the unwholesome rays of the planet to find what they considered their right—health and peace—in slumber. The ship, full-fed with plunder and self-satisfaction, slept too.

Out beyond the galleon’s stern, from behind the isolated rocks of Morro Grande, two long lines glided into the bay. They were almost invisible on the glittering water, for they were perfectly white; only the faint, cold sparks of phosphorescence along their sides betrayed their passage, like fish-fire under a knife.

The bishop woke and shifted, uncomfortable. The wine was dying in him, leaving headache and sour mouth; his shoulders had slipped down, his heels up. He sat upright in his hammock and peered out.

“Tita!”

“Um?” She lifted her head, eyes open and blank.

“Put another pillow under my head, child! What is that? a fish?”

Tita looked. She did not think it was a fish. But she did not choose to say so; an argument with a bishop in the night was like trying to hush a pot that has boiled over—you only burn yourself and make a mess.

The bishop squinted again, decided it must be some white whale or shark or other monster of the deep, crossed himself, prayed for a safe voyage, and snored once more, clutching the casket as if it were a reliquary.

Presently the cabin-door opened without a creak, and the head of the señor intendant appeared. His face shone with sweat though the night was not hot, and his eyes moved as if listening for every small noise the ship made—the click of a spar, the far slap of water against the hull, the nearer, intimate rasp of a rat.

Tita sat up. Then she began to crawl, snake-smooth, among chairs and table-legs toward him, lit by the weak yellow of the cabin lamp.

“Is he asleep?” whispered the intendant.

“Yes: but the casket is under his head.”

“Curse him! How shall we take it?”

“I brought him a fresh pillow half-an-hour ago; I hung his hammock wrong on purpose that he might want one. I thought to slip the box away as I did it; but the old ox nursed it in both hands all the while.”

“What shall we do, in the name of all the fiends? She sails to-morrow morning, and then all is lost.”

Tita bared her white teeth, not in a smile but in the show of an animal cornered too long. She touched the dagger at his side.

“I dare not!” he hissed, and shuddered as if the blade were already in his own flesh.

“I dare!” she breathed. “He whipt my mother, because she would not give me up to him to be taught in his schools, when she went to the mines. And she went to the mines, and died there in three months. I saw her go, with a chain round her neck; but she never came back again. Yes; I dare kill him! I will kill him! I will!”

The señor felt his mind ease as a coward’s does when another offers to carry his sin. He had no wish to do the murder himself; he feared the devil and valued his own soul. But Tita was an Indian, and in his accounting Indians’ souls were cheap, like their bodies. If she were lost, it would be an acceptable loss.

“But we shall be discovered!”

“I will leap out of the window with the casket, and swim ashore. They will never suspect you, and they will fancy I am drowned.”

“The sharks may seize you, Tita. You had better give me the casket.”

Tita’s eyes flashed; she saw his hunger for the box as clearly as she smelled the bishop’s wine on his breath.

“You would not like to lose that, eh? though you care little about losing me. And yet you told me that you loved me!”

“And I do love you, Tita! light of my eyes! life of my heart! I swear, by all the saints, I love you. I will marry you, I swear I will—I will swear on the crucifix, if you like!”

“Swear, then, or I do not give you the casket,” she said, and held out the little crucifix at her neck. Her gaze devoured him—wild, unreasoning, the fierce love of one raised where words of tenderness have been taught in a foreign tongue and paid for in bruises.

He swore, trembling and pale.

“Give me your dagger.”

“No, not mine. It may be found. I shall be suspected. What if my sheath were seen to be empty?”

“Your knife will do. His throat is soft enough.”

She slipped away, soundless as a cat, toward the hammock. The intendant stood shivering at the far end of the cabin and turned his back, so that he might not see the deed and have the picture haunt him at confession.

He waited. One minute. Two. Five. An hour, perhaps. Cold sweat bathed him; his blood hammered in his temples until the whole world seemed to ring. Was that a bell? No—only his own pulse, tolling like a warning no priest could absolve.

There was a struggle—she was at it now—then a stifled cry. The intendant’s gut clenched; of all sounds he dreaded that most, the old man’s voice breaking the night. Another struggle; and Tita’s voice, muffled as if beneath cloth, called for help.

“I cannot help you. Mother of Mercies! I dare not help you!” he hissed. “She-devil! you have begun it, and you must finish it yourself!”

A heavy arm locked his throat from behind. For an instant he believed the bishop’s ghost had risen and taken him, or some fiend had come to drag him down alive. He tried to scream—to wake the ship, to buy his life with others’—but a handkerchief was jammed into his mouth, choking the sound into a wet grunt. In another minute he was bound hand and foot and flung upon the table. The cabin filled with armed men as if the shadows had hatched them: wild figures with bright blades, armor catching starlight from the stern-gallery.

Two men were lashing up the bishop in his hammock; two more had seized Tita, pinning her wrists as easily as one pins a struggling hen for the knife. Over them all loomed a gigantic enemy, whose voice, when it came, cut through the cabin’s tight air like a command in a storm.

“Now, Will,” he whispered, “forward and clap the fore-hatches on; and shout Fire! with all your might. Girl! murderess! your life is in my hands. Tell me where the commander sleeps, and I pardon you.”

Tita looked up into the huge speaker’s face, and something in her—fear, calculation, a sudden sense of power offered and withdrawn—made her obey without a word. She nodded, turned her chin, and pointed.

The intendant, gagged, could only hear. He heard the giant’s steps. He heard the door of the colonel’s cabin open. Then a short scuffle—boots thudding, a strangled oath—and silence.

But only for a moment. For already the alarm had been given, and confusion tore through the ship like fire itself. Feet ran. Doors banged. Voices rose: one man shouting “Fire!” as if flames were at his heels; another “Wreck!” as if the sea had opened its mouth; another “Treason!” as if the word could ward off steel.

Amyas—for it was none other—had gained the poop. The sentinels were gagged and bound before they could find their breath; and every half-naked wretch who came trembling up on deck in his shirt by the main hatchway was hurled into the scuppers and there secured, as briskly as fish are flung into a basket. The ship’s own sounds—the creak of timbers, the slap of water, the high thin singing of rigging—were swallowed by human panic, that ugliest noise of all: a crowd of men who do not know which story to believe, only that their lives depend on believing the loudest.

“Lower away that boat!” shouted Amyas, in Spanish, to his first batch of prisoners.

They were unarmed, bare-legged, blinking like men dragged from ovens into cold air. They could do nothing but obey.

“Now then, jump in. Here, hand them to the gangway as they come up.”

It was done. Each Spaniard who appeared was kicked to the scuppers and bundled down over the side, splashing into the boat with curses and prayers tangled together. The boats filled fast—white shirts in heaps, hands clawing gunwales, mouths open for breath and forgiveness.

“She’s full. Cast loose now and off with you. If you try to board again we’ll sink you.”

“Fire! fire!” shouted Cary, forward. “Up the main hatchway for your lives!”

The ruse worked perfectly. Before half-an-hour was over, all the ship’s boats that could be lowered were crammed with Spaniards in their shirts, pulling for shore as if the sea behind them were boiling.

“Here is a new sort of camisado,” quoth Cary. “The last Spanish one I saw was at the sortie from Smerwick: but this is somewhat more prosperous than that.”

“Get the main and foresail up, Will!” said Amyas. “Cut the cable; and we will plume the quarry as we fly.”

“Spoken like a good falconer. Heaven grant that this big woodcock may carry a good trail inside!”

“I’ll warrant her for that,” said Jack Brimblecombe. “She floats so low.”

“Much of your build, too, Jack. By the by, where is the commander?”
