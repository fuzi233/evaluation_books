The long-guarded secret Martin Lambert had whispered into his wife’s ear at the close of the third-to-last chapter, the very news that sent the whole Oakhurst household into peals of delighted laughter, we finally reveal here. The hay was all stacked dry in the barn, the barley and wheat still too green to cut, and the farm work left no use for the plow horses for days on end. So Colonel Lambert, leaning back in his chair over supper, proposed that they harness those very workhorses to the old family coach, load all the children in, and make a jaunt to Tunbridge Wells, stopping only to pick up their good friend James Wolfe at Westerham along the way.

Mamma Lambert embraced the idea at once, and if she snuck a quick, loving kiss to the honest man who suggested it, no one there was fool enough to tease her for it. The children all jumped and cheered. The two girls darted straight up to their bedrooms to rummage through every chest for their finest finery: heavy calamanco gowns, glossy paduasoy overdresses, frilled falbala hems, furbelow trims, wool capes, cardinal cloaks, loose sacque gowns, soft negligees, lace solitaires, ribbons by the handful, full draped mantuas, clocked silk stockings, and glossy high red-heeled shoes, all chattering a mile a minute as they laid out every article of their toilet. Mamma hauled her own rarely-worn best brocades from the deep cedar presses where they only came out for weddings and rare feasts, before going back to rest between sachets of dried lavender. The Colonel pulled out his gold-laced tricorn, hand-embroidered waistcoat, and silver-hilted smallsword he had stowed away for formal occasions, while young Charley was gifted a holiday suit cut from the very wedding coat his father had worn decades before—Mrs. Lambert snipped it apart with a little soft sigh for the old days. The two plow horses, Ball and Dumpling, had their manes and tails tied with blue satin ribbon, and old Chump, the shaggy white cart horse, was rigged out as a unicorn leader to pull the heavy coach up the first five steep miles from Oakhurst to Westerham.

That ancient family carriage was famed across the whole shire: local legend said it had joined the procession that brought King George I across the Thames from Greenwich on his first arrival to claim the British throne. It had belonged to Lambert’s grandfather, and every member of the family had grown up convinced it was one of the grandest coaches in all three kingdoms. Their plowman Brian, an old ex-redcoat, sat on the driver’s box with Charley perched right beside him. All the precious new gowns were stowed in thick leather imperial trunks strapped tight to the roof, the Colonel’s matched pair of flintlock pistols were tucked in the carriage door pockets, and a heavy blunderbuss hung within Brian’s easy reach behind the box. No highwayman dared molested their little convoy, no overcharging innkeeper dared fleece the thrifty large family, for young Colonel Wolfe had already booked them a cheap, cozy lodging right across the road from Madame Bernstein’s usual Tunbridge Wells residence, and he rode ahead on horseback to escort them the last stretch of the journey.

They pulled up at their new lodgings on a soft golden Saturday evening, just as the Baroness was throwing one of her famous card routs. From their second-floor window, they watched sedan chair after sedan chair draw up at her door, setting down powdered beaux in gold-embroidered waistcoats and patched, brocaded ladies in full court dress. The sun had not yet set—our ancestors of that century started their entertainments early, tucking into suppers, punch, and cards any time after three o’clock in the afternoon right through to midnight or later. Hetty and Theo decided the view was almost as delightful as being invited to the party itself: they could spot every guest as they stepped inside, and peek through the Baroness’s open casements to watch the chatter and laughter unfold, with Colonel Wolfe whispering the names and titles of every notable person they recognized.

When the old Duchess of Queensberry passed by, Martin Lambert immediately burst out reciting lines from his favorite Matthew Prior, about “Kitty, beautiful and young.” “Just think, that old woman was once as young and pretty as you two girls!” he exclaimed.

“Like us, papa?” Hetty tossed her little head playfully. “We never pretended to be great beauties, after all!”

“Aye, just like you, you little baggage,” he laughed, teasing her with more lines from the same poem, about the young heroine wild to sneak off to the party, sick of the gentle restraints wise mamma laid on her. Hetty retorted that they had never been invited anyway, and if that was the best beauty the rout had to offer, the wit could not be much to write home about. They bantered back and forth about Prior’s verses until Hetty gasped, pressing her face to the glass and pointing down to the street below.

Theo waved a handkerchief at the figure they saw: it was Harry Warrington, the young Virginian, walking arm in arm with a plump, fine-robed clergyman, and the pair of them headed straight for Madame Bernstein’s door. Wolfe told them the clergyman, Mr. Sampson, had preached a brilliant if slightly theatrical sermon the previous Sunday. The family teased Wolfe that he seemed to spend every single day of the week at Tunbridge Wells, not just Sundays, and Mrs. Lambert said they would not send across the road to fetch Harry that night—he ought to enjoy his party undisturbed by a crowd of uninvited country relations. Theo murmured softly that she was glad Harry kept the company of such an eloquent clergyman, her quiet expression clear: she had never believed the wild rumors that some of the local gossips spread about him.

Then they spotted the Bishop of Salisbury, wearing his blue Garter ribbon, followed by his chaplain, and right behind him a gilded sedan chair topped with no less than five earl’s coronets, carried by porters dressed in heavy gold lace livery, preceded by three footmen in matching splendid livery. The Bishop, just about to step through the Baroness’s door, hurried back, bowing so low his powdered wig nearly slipped off his head, to offer his hand to the lady stepping down from the sedan.

Martin teased his wife and daughters a little with a few silly German phrases before explaining the heraldry they were looking at: the lady stepping down was the Countess of Yarmouth, the long-favored mistress of King George II. When the girls pressed him to know why the highest lords and bishops in the realm were all kowtowing to her, Mrs. Lambert quickly put a finger to his lips, stopping him before he named the line of royal mistresses stretching back centuries, to save her daughters’ cheeks from blushing bright red. The old republican Lambert muttered under his breath that it was a crying, unforgivable shame to see the highest clergy and proudest aristocrats in the land bowing and scraping to a painted foreign favorite.

Suddenly Wolfe jumped up from his chair, grabbed his hat, and ran straight out of the room. He had just spotted the young lady he was courting, Miss Lowther, walking across the Pantiles with her guardian, heading straight for the Baroness’s party. Theo turned away from the window looking quiet and grave, almost awestruck, while Hetty stayed right where she was, her cheeks flushed bright red with anger. “I’m thinking what I would do if I ever saw my own papa bowing and scraping to a woman like that,” she told her mother, when mamma came to lead her away to supper.

The hissing tea kettle boiled over the fire, and the whole family sat down to their simple evening meal, leaving Hetty in charge of the window sill. Charley ran out to the crowd gathered outside the Baroness’s house, spent his sixpence on a warm fresh cheese cake from Mrs. Brown’s tart shop, and came darting back to report that he had spotted the great Samuel Johnson beating the stone pillars of the Pantiles with his walking stick, drinking cup after cup of tea at twopence apiece. The women all jumped up to crowd the window when Charley added that Johnson was sitting with his friend Samuel Richardson, the famous author of *Clarissa*. The sun dipped below the rooftops, stars pricked out overhead, and the Baroness’s footmen lit the candles in the parlor across the way. The Lamberts watched a group of adoring lady readers tuck a wool shawl around Richardson’s neck and walk him home, and Theo sighed that he did not look half as grand as the hero Sir Charles Grandison from his own novels. Mamma said she almost wished they had not gotten a peek at their favorite writer at all.

Hetty called them back to the glass, and they saw Harry Warrington being formally presented to the Countess of Yarmouth, before the countess settled down to a card table with the Bishop, the Earl, and a fourth guest. Harry soon stepped into the window embrasure across the street, deep in conversation with his cousin Lady Maria Esmond, the very lady they had met briefly during her visit to Oakhurst. “Just look at those grand lace frills on his shirt!” Mamma laughed. “My dear, he’s not wearing any of the linen we stitched for him back home!” Colonel Lambert roused from his doze on the parlor sofa, told the girls they had no right to be prying and spying on their friend, and pulled the heavy curtains shut, bringing that quiet evening of peeking to an end.

It is worth noting, for no other reason than to mark the gentle contrast between the two households across the road from each other, that while Madame Bernstein and her guests—bishops, noblemen, dignitaries, and all the rest—gambled at cards, gossiped over champagne and roast chicken, and fawned over the king’s favorite Countess, the Lamberts knelt around their own plain dining table. Old Brian the coachman tiptoed in as quietly as his creaking old boots would let him, and Martin Lambert stood to read the evening prayer aloud, asking Heaven to light their path through the dark, and keep all of them safe through the perils of the night.

The whole household was up before dawn on Sunday morning, dressed in their new finery, Charley at their heels, tramping across the empty common and the quiet Pantiles long before any other guests in town were awake to eat their breakfast. It was Hetty who spotted Harry’s black servant Gumbo leaning out of an open window across the street, his hair still in curl papers, waving a feather duster to dust the furniture inside, flirting with the pretty cherry-cheeked milkmaid lingering under the sill. His hands sparkled with rings, and his coat was loaded down with trinkets gifted to him by the fine ladies of the town who adored him. Harry never showed his face at that window. Neither of the girls spoke of him as they walked, and when Hetty snapped at Charley for pestering everyone nonstop about breakfast, Theo laughed, pulled her little netted purse that her dearest school friend had stitched for her out of her pocket, and used her last sixpence to buy him another cheese cake when the shop opened.

The church was packed full of fine company: the old Duchess of Queensberry, Madame Bernstein, Lady Maria at her side, Wolfe singing the psalms out of the very same book as Miss Lowther, Mr. Richardson surrounded by a flock of adoring lady readers, and Samuel Johnson sitting amongst the charity children, knocking over a pair of small boys in leather breeches when he turned to bow at the altar for the Creed. Martin Lambert noticed Harry was not in his pew, and grumbled that it was a shame the young Virginian had skipped the service. Hetty tossed her head and pretended she had not noticed, but honest Theo admitted she had been sorry to see his empty seat.

When they got back to their lodgings, James Wolfe stormed in the door, his face dark with indignation. He had gone looking for Harry that morning, he told them, and found him still in his nightgown at a quarter before eleven, sitting cross-legged at a table playing piquet cards with Mr. Sampson the parson, finishing the game they had started the night before, while all the church bells were ringing for service. Hetty flared up at once, defending Harry fiercely, saying plenty of fine people played cards on a Sunday, even the King himself with that very Countess of Yarmouth they had watched from the window the day before. Wolfe told them he had taken the liberty of scolding both the parson and Harry for skipping service, and Hetty got so angry she spun around and ran out of the room, bursting into tears on the staircase.

Wolfe was mortified, and Mrs. Lambert comforted him, calling him the old childhood pet name of “Aunt Lambert” as she pressed his hand, while Martin whistled his favorite old folk tune “Over the Hills and Far Away” and Charley teased his father that whistling on a Sunday was rude, and begged to eat the last of Theo’s cheese cake. Theo ran out of the room after Hetty, and found her weeping so hard she could barely breathe. The two girls clung to each other, Hetty sobbing all her frustrations out against Theo’s soft shoulder.

Hetty claimed she had a headache and stayed in bed that afternoon, while the rest of the family walked out to enjoy the Sabbath sunshine on the common. There they found Madame Bernstein resting on a bench under a gnarled oak tree, Harry and Lady Maria sitting at her side. Harry ran straight over to greet them, his face glowing with pure unfeigned joy at seeing his old friends from Oakhurst. Theo thought he looked taller, and handsomer than she ever remembered seeing him, and she called him by his first name like he was her own brother. “Why did you not come find us earlier today, Harry?” she asked, chiding him gently with a raised hand, no rebuke in her kind face at all. “I never even guessed you were here!” he laughed.

Ah, dear friendly reader, wandering the world and stumbling through temptation, may you also be blessed with at least one or two pure, loving hearts to care for you, and pray for you, even when you are fool enough to stray.
