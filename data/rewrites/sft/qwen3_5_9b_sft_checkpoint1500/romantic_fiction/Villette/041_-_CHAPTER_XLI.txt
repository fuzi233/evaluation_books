Must I, before the curtain falls, recount the liberation I purchased on the night of the festival? Must I detail how I, alongside the two sturdy companions I dragged home from the lantern-lit park, endured the trial of intimate knowledge?

I subjected them immediately to the test. They had loudly proclaimed their physical prowess when they rescued me from the bonds of love, yet when I demanded deeds rather than words, some tangible proof of a liberated existence, some evidence of a life relieved from sorrow—"Freedom" excused himself, claiming temporary poverty and incapacity; "Renovation" remained silent. He had perished during the night, falling suddenly.

Left with nothing but a private prayer that my conjectures had raced ahead of reality, I sought to bolster the crushing hour with memories of the warping, distorting spells of jealousy. After a brief, futile struggle, I found myself dragged back, a captive once more to the ancient rack of suspense, bound and stretched anew.

*Shall I yet behold him before he departs?* *Will he carry me in his memory?* *Does he intend to return?* *Will today—the next hour—bring him?* Or must I again submit to the corroding agony of waiting, the brutal pain of severance at the end, that mute, fatal wrench which, by tearing up hope and doubt simultaneously, shakes the foundations of life? Yet, the hand that inflicts this violence cannot be coaxed to pity, for absence erects her barrier.

It was the Feast of the Assumption; the schools were closed. The boarders and instructors, having attended Mass, had departed on a long excursion into the countryside for their *goûter*, their afternoon meal. I stayed behind, for now only two days remained before the *Paul et Virginie* must sail, and I clung to this final opportunity like a castaway gripping his last raft.

Some repairs awaited in the First Class: benches and desks required mending. Holidays were frequently utilized for such tasks, which could not be performed amidst the noise of pupils. Sitting alone, intending eventually to retreat to the garden to clear the space, I was too listless to fulfill my own design. Then I heard footsteps approaching.

Foreign artisans and servants invariably operate in pairs; I am convinced it takes two carpenters from La Bassée to drive a single nail. While adjusting my bonnet, which had hung idly from my hand, I vaguely marveled at hearing the tread of only one "worker." I noted, too—with the dreary leisure of a prisoner counting the dust motes—that this man wore leather shoes, not wooden sabots. I deduced he must be the master-carpenter, arriving to inspect before dispatching his journeymen. I wrapped my scarf around me.

He advanced. He opened the door. My back was turned. I felt a slight thrill—a sensation too fleeting, too complex for immediate analysis. I turned. I stood in the presence of what I believed to be the master artisan. Gazing toward the doorway, I saw it filled with a figure, and my eyes burned the image of M. Paul onto the canvas of my mind.

Hundreds of prayers offered to wear out Heaven yield no fulfillment. Yet, once in a lifetime, a singular golden gift may fall prone into the suppliant's lap—a boon complete and radiant, minted directly by Fruition.

M. Emanuel wore the attire in which he likely intended to travel—a coat guarded with velvet. I perceived him as prepared for instant departure, though I understood two days still remained before the vessel's sailing. He looked vigorous and cheerful. He appeared kind and benign. He entered with eagerness; within a single second, he was close to me; he radiated friendship. Perhaps it was the mood of a bridegroom that thus brightened him. Whatever the cause, I could not meet his sunshine with a cloud. If this were my final moment with him, I would not squander it in artificial distance. I loved him deeply—too deeply to allow Jealousy herself to obstruct a kind farewell. A cordial word from his lips, or a gentle gaze from his eyes, would sustain me for the entirety of life remaining; it would be comfort in the last strait of loneliness. I would accept it—I would drink the elixir, and prudence should not spill the cup.

The interview would naturally be brief. He would utter to me precisely what he had said to the assembled pupils. He would take and hold my hand for two minutes; he would touch my cheek with his lips for the first, last, and only time—and then... nothing more. Then, truly, the final parting, the vast separation, the great chasm I could not cross to reach him—across which, perhaps, he would not even glance, failing to remember me.

He took my hand in one of his, using the other to push back my bonnet. He looked into my face; his luminous smile faded slightly; his lips articulated something akin to the wordless language of a mother discovering a child greatly and unexpectedly altered, shattered by illness or worn thin by want. A check supervened.

"Paul, Paul!" cried a woman's hurried voice from behind, "Paul, come into the salon; I have many things to say to you—conversations for the whole day—and so has Victor; and Josef is here. Come, Paul, come to your friends."

Madame Beck, summoned by vigilance or an inscrutable instinct, pressed so near she nearly thrust herself between me and M. Emanuel.

"Come, Paul!" she reiterated, her eye grazing me with a hard ray like a steel stylus. She pushed against her kinsman. I thought he would recede; I thought he would depart. Pierced deeper than I could endure, compelled now to feel what defied suppression, I cried—

"My heart will break!"

What I felt seemed a literal heart-break; yet the seal of another fountain yielded under the strain: one breath from M. Paul, the whisper, "Trust me!" lifted a burden, opened an outlet. With many a deep sob, with thrilling shivers, with icy tremors, and yet with profound relief—I wept.

"Leave her to me; it is a crisis: I will give her a cordial, and it will pass," said the calm Madame Beck.

Being left to her and her cordial seemed to me akin to being abandoned to a poisoner and her bowl. When M. Paul answered deeply, harshly, and briefly—

"Laissez-moi!" ("Leave me!")—in the grim sound I felt a strange, strong, yet life-giving music.

"Laissez-moi!" he repeated, his nostrils flaring, his facial muscles quivering as he spoke.

"But this will never do," said Madame, with sternness. Her kinsman rejoined more sternly—

"Sortez d'ici!" ("Get out of here!")

"I will send for Père Silas: on the spot I will send for him," she threatened pertinaciously.

"Femme!" ("Woman!") cried the Professor, no longer in his deep tones, but in his highest, most agitated key, "Femme! Sortez à l'instant!" ("Get out at once!")

He was roused, and I loved him in his wrath with a passion surpassing anything I had yet felt.

"What you do is wrong," pursued Madame; "it is an act characteristic of men of your unreliable, imaginative temperament; a step impulsive, injudicious, inconsistent—a proceeding vexatious, and not estimable in the view of persons of steadier and more resolute character."

"You know not what I have of steady and resolute in me," said he, "but you shall see; the event shall teach you. Modeste," he continued less fiercely, "be gentle, be pitying, be a woman; look at this poor face, and relent. You know I am your friend, and the friend of your friends; in spite of your taunts, you well and deeply know I may be trusted. Of sacrificing myself I made no difficulty, but my heart is pained by what I see; it *must* have and give solace. *Leave me!*"

This time, in the "_leave me_" there was an intonation so bitter and so imperative, I wondered that even Madame Beck herself could, for one moment, delay obedience; but she stood firm. She gazed upon him dauntlessly; she met his eye, forbidding and fixed as stone. She was opening her lips to retort; I saw over all M. Paul's face a quick rising light and fire; I can hardly tell how he managed the movement—it did not seem violent; it kept the form of courtesy; he gave his hand; it scarce touched her, I thought; she ran, she whirled from the room; she was gone, and the door shut, in one second.

The flash of passion was all over very soon. He smiled as he told me to wipe my eyes; he waited quietly until I was calm, dropping from time to time a stilling, solacing word. Soon I sat beside him once more—reassured, not desperate, nor yet desolate; not friendless, not hopeless, not sick of life, and seeking death.

"It made you very sad then to lose your friend?" said he.

"It kills me to be forgotten, Monsieur," I said. "All these weary days I have not heard from you one word, and I was crushed with the possibility, growing to certainty, that you would depart without saying farewell!"
