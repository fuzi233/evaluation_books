As the days passed at Tunbridge Wells, Henry Warrington found himself the object of intense curiosity among the townspeople. His arrival had sent ripples through the social hierarchy, and everyone from the local gentry to the shopkeepers seemed to be abuzz with excitement. The Lord Bishop of Salisbury himself was barely accorded the same level of attention, and the young Virginian's presence was the main topic of conversation at every gathering. People would turn to watch him walk by, and the country folk would stare in wonder as he entered the market. Even the matrons of the town made a point to leave their daughters alone with him, and the girls would smile coyly as they chatted with the charming young man.

Everyone knew the extent of his wealth and the size of his income, and the amounts he won at play were discussed in hushed tones at every tea table in town. It was as if the entire community had taken it upon themselves to keep tabs on the young Virginian's fortunes. Such was the interest in his life that even his loyal servant, Gumbo, was treated with affection and indulgence, and the valet soon found himself at the center of attention, regaling the locals with tales of his master's adventures. Gumbo, never one to resist a good meal, flourished under the attention and became a favorite among the town's valets, spinning tales of his master's exploits with reckless abandon.

Despite his newfound fame, Henry bore his honors with humility, refusing to let his head swell with pride. He had always been taught to hold himself in high esteem, and his current success only reinforced his self-assurance. However, he was not one to brag about his victories or lord it over others. When challenged to games of skill or sports, he accepted with alacrity, feeling a sense of duty to represent the values of his homeland. And represent them he did, proving himself a worthy opponent in every contest he entered.

His aunt, Madame de Bernstein, was overjoyed by her nephew's successes, and his wins at cards, riding matches, and tennis tournaments only served to solidify his position as the darling of the family. She delighted in his company, finding his innocence and good nature a breath of fresh air amidst the stifling formality of high society. She even took an interest in his education, introducing him to the finer things in life, from literature to fashion. Together, they pored over books, and she marveled at his natural wit and humor.
