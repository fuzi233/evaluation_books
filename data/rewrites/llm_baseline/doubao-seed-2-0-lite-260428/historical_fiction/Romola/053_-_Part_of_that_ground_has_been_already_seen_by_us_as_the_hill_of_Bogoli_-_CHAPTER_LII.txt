For Romola, the final echoes of that Carnival day had at first seemed to leave no heavier personal burden than the new, gentle task of tending to her ageing kinswoman Brigida, who swung by turns between complaining bitterly of her grey hairs and making wobbly, half-hearted peace with the slow creep of old age. But those feasting days had ushered in a Lenten season that swept her up in a near-constant whirlwind of sharp mental tension and urgent, ceaseless work.

Bernardo del Nero had just been elected Gonfaloniere. After months of furious campaigning, the Medicean faction had claimed that narrow triumph, and the victory stoked Romola’s unshakable, gnawing premonition: some long-hidden, carefully laid scheme was going to ripen into either glorious success or total ruin during the two months her godfather held supreme authority in Florence. Every dim, pale dawn seeping through her bedroom shutters felt like that old fear stepping in to keep her company. It walked with her through the cobblestone streets at sunrise, as she joined the crowds heading to the Duomo for the early Lenten sermon — but once inside the vast, thrumming cathedral, the cold weight of it faded, the way a soldier’s dread of death vanishes the second he steps onto the battlefield.

In the Duomo, she felt part of a conflict far larger than the walls of Florence could contain. Girolamo Savonarola was preaching the final full cycle of Lenten sermons he would ever be permitted to finish inside the cathedral’s walls. He knew excommunication was only days away, and he had reached the point of defying it openly. He held a merciless, unflinching mirror up to the corruption rotting through the Church, calling every vice and falsehood by its true name, no soft polite periphrases to soften the blow; and with mounting, burning confidence, he prophesied a coming renovation, a mass revolt against institutional greed and moral decay. His own fate straddled two competing visions in his mind, shifting by the minute: sometimes he saw himself taking a glorious lead in that uprising, his voice ringing across every corner of Christendom, rousing the Church’s dead, stagnant body back to life the way Christ’s call had dragged Lazarus out of his sealed tomb. Other times he saw nothing ahead but persecution and martyrdom: this mortal life was nothing more than a long vigil, and the true dawn would only break after he was dead.

The grandeur of that position would have impressed any mind not dulled completely by cynicism — even men like Machiavelli, who were inclined to write the friar off as a calculating opportunist first, could not deny the raw, unignorable power of his speeches. For Romola, who shared his fire and believed unshakably in the purity of his core purpose, the crisis felt as personal as any struggle in her own family. It wove itself into every small, daily labour she performed, labours that demanded not just dogged perseverance, but new reserves of courage. The famine that had gripped Florence for months had never lifted, and the long stretch of unrelenting hardship had made every form of suffering sharper to bear. Disease spread through the overcrowded narrow streets, and word was going round that the Plague would soon take hold. Trudging wearily from hovel to hovel among the sick, the starving, the angry murmuring crowds, Romola drew strength from more than just her own soft pity: she drew it from the belief that a great hero was fighting for a sublime, world-altering cause, toward which her small acts of mercy could only creep slowly, the way morning dew freshening weed-choked soil tends a harvest no one alive now will ever live to see.

But the grand, rousing music of his sermons held jarring, dissonant notes. Those early, glowing days of hope, when the friar had preached peace, charity, and full amnesty for old political grudges, when he believed the French king’s arrival would purify the Republic, were long past. Political intrigue had proved too stubborn to let that amnesty take root. The myth of the French “deliverer”, who had abandoned his grand mission and turned his back on Florence, had done far more harm than good. Hostility against the Prophet, both petty and grand-scale, was mounting, and his enemies wielded new weapons with unrelenting determination.

Bit by bit, the tone of contention and self-defence crept into his sermons. He began to yield to popular demand, speaking in far greater detail of his private visions and revelations, adopting a sharper, more defiant tone against every detractor. Once, he had denounced obsession with miracles, saying true faith had no need for them — now he openly declared that at the exact right moment, divine power would send a public miracle to prove his prophecies true. And every time the promised triumph of good receded further behind the unignorable reality of prevailing evil, his threats of coming vengeance against corrupt priests and vicious tyrants grew sharper, stoked not just by righteous indignation, but by his own personal exasperation. For a great public orator who gives himself entirely to the fire of the moment, that quiet struggle between selfish and unselfish motive that most men hide deep in the private chambers of their souls is written out in letters of fire for the whole world to see.

But if those notes of angry exasperation jarred harshly on Romola’s ears, there was another listener in the crowd for whom those sharp tones were the only ones that stirred his blood, the deep thrumming bass that cuts through all other sound for a man nearly deaf. Baldassarre had learned the great friar was preaching again, and he came to every sermon he possibly could, drinking in that voice full of threats, as if it were a power risen up on the side of long-delayed justice. He came all the more often because he had spotted Romola in the crowds too, waiting and watching for the exact moment — both the right set of outer circumstances, and the right shift in his own wavering, fraying mind — to speak to her face. Twice Romola had caught sight of his dark, fixed stare across the throng in the Duomo. She did not want to see his face ever again, but she found herself searching for it regardless, the way men cannot look away from a terrible portent hanging over their lives. But whatever secret the old man was still holding back felt like a trivial fear next to her present anxieties: it belonged, she thought, only to the past, and her every worry was absorbed by the looming present.

The turbulent Lenten season slipped by, anyway. April, the second and final month of her godfather’s rule, was drawing to a close, and nothing had happened to fulfill her premonition. The wider public had shared her fears, too, with rumours spilling out of Rome that Piero de’ Medici was plotting a violent takeover — but in just a few days, the suspected Bernardo would step down from power, and the danger would pass.

Romola was trying to draw a little courage from the fact that all her worst fears had come to nothing, when on the twenty-seventh of April, as she walked her usual afternoon rounds of mercy visits to the poor and sick, a messenger found her, summoning her at once to the home of Camilla Rucellai, the most famous of Florence’s self-anointed prophetesses, to discuss a matter of the utmost public importance.

Romola felt a deep, unshakable repulsion for the shrill, over-excited volatility of these “illuminated” women, and at that moment she had a particular loathing for Camilla: word had spread that the woman was spouting “revelations” that directly accused Bernardo del Nero of treachery. At first she was fully inclined to send the messenger back with a flat refusal. Camilla’s summons almost certainly related to public affairs, and Romola’s first instinct was to shut out any new information that would only add to the weight she was already carrying. But it had long since become her unshakable habit to set aside her own impulses and yield passively to every outward claim on her sense of duty. She chided herself for letting her vague fears make her cowardly and selfish, and finally complied, turning her steps straight for Camilla’s house.

She found the nervous, grey-haired woman in a chamber arranged to look as bare and ascetic as a convent cell. Camilla’s thin, wiry fingers clamped tight around Romola’s wrist as soon as she sat down, and her eager voice addressed her first in a loud, urgent whisper, the contact sending a physical jolt of revulsion through Romola that made it almost impossible to stay seated. Camilla had a vision to share: her guardian angel, she claimed, had appeared to her, and revealed that Romola held secret knowledge about her godfather Bernardo del Nero that, if disclosed, could save the entire Republic from ruin. Camilla’s voice rose higher and shriller as she told the tale, ending in a fevered exhortation that Romola must obey her angel’s command, and separate herself from this known enemy of God.

Romola’s temper was the slow-burning, massive kind, and save for moments of deep agitation, she was always calm and self-possessed. She had a natural, instinctive disgust for the shallow excitability of women like Camilla, whose entire faculties seemed to have dissolved into wild fantasy, leaving no room for sober thought or genuine feeling. Before the prophetess had finished speaking, Romola jumped to her feet, trying to wrench her arm free of Camilla’s tightening grip. It was useless. Camilla held on like a crab, and Romola’s resistance only spurred her on to wilder, more desperate exhortation, spilling over her own intended limits to scream a string of other corroborating visions. Christ himself, she shrieked, had appeared to her, and ordered her to command the city’s ruling magistrates to throw Bernardo del Nero out of the high window of the Palazzo Vecchio. Fra Girolamo himself knew all about it, she cawed, and this time he did not dare declare the vision unholy.

“And since then!” Camilla cried, straining upward, wild eyes fixed on Romola’s face, “the Blessed Infant came to me, and laid a sweet, sweet wafer on my tongue, to reward me for doing His will!”

“Let me go!” Romola said, her voice deep and shaking with anger. “God grant you are mad — for if you are not, you are abominably wicked!”

This time her furious effort to free herself was too strong for Camilla’s grip. She tore her arm away and rushed out of the house, not pausing till she had hurried far down the street, and found herself standing before the church of the Badia. She slipped behind the heavy curtain under the old weathered stone arch to step into the quiet sanctuary, cut off entirely from the noise and rush of the street, where every object and every custom spoke of eternal peace that persisted even at the heart of turmoil.

She sank down on the step of the altar, in front of Filippino Lippi’s serene painting of the Virgin appearing to Saint Bernard, and waited, hoping the roiling tumult in her chest would soon ebb. The thought that stung her most sharply was the claim that Savonarola sanctioned this murderous, foolish nonsense. Not for a second did she believe he had approved throwing Bernardo out a window as a divine command: his entire political teachings were rooted in absolute respect for law and order, and she knew he could never endorse such a thing. But the painful, unavoidable question rose in her mind: if he knew how deadly and dangerous these false visions of women like Camilla were, if he so distrusted these supposed spirit-seers that he kept his distance from them as much as possible, why did he not denounce them openly from the pulpit? Why did he not shout down these pretended revelations that draped the very idea of a just God in new darkness?

The answer came to her with brutal, cutting clarity: he was trapped inwardly, because he could not separate the basis of Camilla’s visions from his own. He was trapped outwardly too, knowing that if he denounced these revelations, his own followers would turn on him, screaming that he was trying to smother all divine inspiration that did not come straight from his own mouth — or the mouth of his trusted confidant and fellow visionary, Fra Salvestro.

Romola knelt there, her face buried against the cold stone of the altar step, enduring one of those sickening, disillusioning moments when the great enthusiasm that had been the only force strong enough to make life feel worth living suddenly seemed inextricably tangled up in empty fantasy and willful blindness. Her mind turned with new, sharp longing toward the steady, untheoretical prudence, the quiet dignity of her godfather, who was being marked for death as if he were the evil King Agag, simply because he believed a more limited form of government would serve Florence better than the unruly Great Council, and because he would not pretend to forget his old loyalties to the exiled Medici family.

But that thought was no sooner out than the old premonition of a secret Medici plot rose up again, and she acknowledged that the popular party’s fierce suspicion was half-justified. Keeping Florence’s government pure, keeping it free of vicious tyrants, was a sacred cause — the friar had been right about that, and Romola’s reason had always agreed with him. But right then, her reason agreed only reluctantly. Her heart recoiled from that “right” tangled up in so much narrow, rigid judgment, a right that demanded men be judged entirely by shallow markers of assent or denial to political creeds, ignoring the full, complicated humanity inside them. Her affection and respect clung tighter than ever to her godfather, and through him, to the memories of her father, who had spent his whole life rejecting the lazy, cruel habit of sorting all men into sheep and goats based on nothing more than a religious or political label.

Calm would not come, not even kneeling on that altar step, not even looking at that serene painting where Saint Bernard, writing in his quiet rocky solitude, was visited by faces bathed in celestial peace. Romola was trapped in the thick, urgent press of human troubles, and that quiet rocky solitude felt a million miles away. She rose to her feet, determined to go back to the sick people in the courtyard of the poor quarter, to lose herself in small, immediate acts of kindness, and revive that sense that her life held value, now that that larger, grander faith had failed her.

But when she turned around, she was face to face with Baldassarre. He was standing only two yards away from her.
