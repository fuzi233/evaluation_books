Where is the woman who cannot play the part required of her? Where is she who refuses to deceive, even for the sake of the man she adores? Yet nature, in moments of supreme crisis, often grants the simplest of women the wit to cast dust into Solomon's eyes. As soon as the men had withdrawn, Margaret rose from her bed. She stripped off her nightgown, leaving only her skirt, petticoat, and stockings, then dragged the heavy chest—her makeshift bed—against the wall. She paused at the threshold, listening intently. The heavy tread of boots faded through her father's chamber and down the stone stairs until silence reclaimed the house.

Inside the chest lay a secret known only to her. To a stranger, the room appeared sound and solid, but Gerard, pressed for time and materials, had ingeniously cut a section from the floorboards to accommodate her hiding place, seamlessly repairing the gap above. In truth, the chest rested on the rafters a foot beneath the surface, making it nearly two feet deep despite its deceptive shallowness.

Quiet settled over the room. Margaret knelt, offering a silent prayer of gratitude to Heaven. Rising, she leaned over the lid and whispered softly, "Gerard!"

There was no answer.

She increased her volume slightly. "Gerard, all is safe. Thank God! You may rise, but be cautious!"

Silence.

She placed her hand upon his shoulder. "Gerard!"

Still nothing.

"Oh, what is this?" she cried, her fingers flying wildly across his face and chest. She gripped his shoulders, shook him, and lifted him, but he slipped from her trembling grasp, collapsing back not as a living man, but as a lifeless weight. A cold dread seized her. The lid had been lowered; she had lain upon him. The intruders had been in the room for some time. With a frenzy born of sheer terror, she tore him from the chest and carried him to the window. She threw it wide, letting the cool night air rush in. She laid him there, bathed in the silver moonlight. His face was the color of ashes; his limbs were limp and utterly still. She pressed her hand to his heart. Horror! It was as motionless as the rest of him. The horror of horrors: she had suffocated him with her own body.

The human mind cannot instantly accept such a sudden, terrible calamity. Gerard, who had barely crawled into that chest five minutes prior, how could he be dead?

She called upon every endearing name her heart could conjure. She kissed him, caressed him, and implored him to speak.

No response to words of love she had never dared utter before. Trembling violently, she began to babble foolish, terrible things over his ashen countenance.

"Oh, Gerard! I am so sorry you are dead. I am so sorry I have killed you. Forgive me for not letting the men take you; it would have been better than this. Oh, Gerard! I am very, very sorry for what I have done."

Then, suddenly, she began to rave.

"No! No! Such things cannot be, or there is no God. It is monstrous! How can my Gerard be dead? How can I have killed my Gerard? I love him. Oh, God, you know how I love him. He does not. I never told him. If he knew my heart, he would speak to me; he would not be so deaf to his poor Margaret. It must be a trick to make me scream and betray him! But no! I love him too well for that. I'll choke myself first." She clutched her own throat, stifling the wild urge to shriek in her agony.

"If he would but say one word. Oh, Gerard! Don't die without a word. Have mercy on me and scold me, but speak to me! If you are angry with me, curse me! I deserve it—the idiot who killed the man she loved better than herself. Ah, I am a murderess. The worst in all the world. Help! Help! I have murdered him. Ah! Ah! Ah! Ah! Ah!"

She tore at her hair, launching shriek after shriek so piercing and wild that they rang like a funeral knell in the darkened house. Dierich Brower and his men, alerted by the noise, sprang to their feet, staring at one another in confusion and alarm.
