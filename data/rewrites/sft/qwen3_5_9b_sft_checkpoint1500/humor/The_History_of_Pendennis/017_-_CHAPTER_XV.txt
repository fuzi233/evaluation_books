So long as the enemy lingered outside the gates, Major Pendennis swore he wouldn't stir from Fairoaks. He didn't bother to micromanage Pen's every step, yet the lad remained under the hawk-like gaze of the Major and his network of spies; young Arthur's movements were a matter of record to his vigilant guardian.

Let us pause here to address the universal tragedy of the novelist's hero: surely no man who has ever dipped into fiction escapes the cruel twist of fate regarding love. Whether thwarted by circumstance, betrayed by the fair sex, or undone by his own foolishness, the worthy friend must recall his own bruised heart to understand Mr. Pen's agony. Ah, the weary nights! The sickening fevers! Those mad desires battering themselves against the unyielding granite of indifference, only to bounce back shattered. Imagine tonight, in the heart of London, a ledger of groans and curses from tossing lovers. Who among the males of the metropolis will not be awake at two or three, counting the hours like a monk in a monastery, rolling from pillow to pillow, restless and heart-broken? It is a terrible pang. I have never seen a man literally die of love, though I have watched a twelve-stone giant shrink to nine stone and five under the weight of a disappointed passion—a quarter of his mass perishing in the process. Later, he regains his weight, perhaps grows larger; likely, a new affection has wrapped around his ribs like a warm blanket. Young Pen will find comfort eventually, just as the rest of us do. We say this to spare the ladies premature tears or undue alarm. His mother worried, naturally, but what maternal instinct won't invent fears? "Depend on it," the Major would gallantly assure her, "the boy will recover. Once we move her from the country, we'll whisk him away to see some life. For now, ease your mind. Half the pain of losing a woman stems from vanity, not love. Being abandoned by a woman is the devil, to be sure, but consider how easily *we* abandon them."

Mrs. Pendennis, however, remained in the dark. Such complexities lay far beyond her simple sphere. She disliked the subject, having weathered her own private storm and recovered, possessing little patience for the passions of others—with the singular exception of Arthur's. She absorbed his sufferings as her own, feeling perhaps more acutely in his feverish deliriums than he did himself. She watched him through this present grief with a jealous, silent sympathy, though he kept his troubles hidden from her.

One must grant the Major considerable merit and forbearance, displaying a truly creditable degree of familial affection. Life at Fairoaks was profoundly dull for a man accustomed to the whirlwind of London, where he could bow in three or four drawing rooms nightly. Now, his pleasures were limited to a dinner with Doctor Portman or a neighboring Squire, and a dreary game of backgammon with the Widow, who strained her best to amuse him. He longed for the mail bag bearing London letters and devoured every word of the evening papers. He medicated himself assiduously, believing a regimen of quiet living suited him post-London banquet, dressing laboriously twice daily and taking regular exercise along the terrace. Armed with his cane, his meticulous toilette, his medicine chest, his backgammon box, and his newspaper, this worldly philosopher constructed a fortress against ennui. Though he failed to improve every shining hour like the bees in the widow's garden, Major Pendennis succeeded in rendering his captivity merely tolerable, passing moment by moment. Eventually, he grew fond of steering conversations toward the American War, the Massacre of Wyoming, and the exploits at Saint Lucia, largely because he possessed two volumes of the 'Annual Register' in his bedroom which he studied with religious devotion. Thus does a well-regulated man accommodate himself to fortune, showing a calm superiority.

Pen, meanwhile, sometimes sat in for the backgammon box or listened to his mother's simple summer melodies, yet he remained restless and wretched. He was frequently up before dawn, descending to the carp-pond in Clavering Park. It was a dreary pool choked with whispering rushes and green alders, the site where a milkmaid had drowned herself in the time of the Baronet's grandfather, and where her ghost was rumored to still wander. Pen did not attempt suicide, as his mother suspected. Instead, he enjoyed fishing there, thinking deeply as the float quivered in the little eddies and fish flapped nearby. A bite excited him greatly, and in this manner he occasionally hauled in carps, tenches, and eels, which the Major cooked in the continental fashion.

Beneath his favorite tree by this pond, Pen composed a collection of poems suitable to his melancholy state—verses over which he later blushed, wondering how he could ever have invented such rubbish. As for the tree itself, it holds a hollow where he used to stash his tin-box of ground-bait and fishing tackle; it is there, we are led to believe, that he later hid something else, but matters advance quickly. Suffice it to say, he wrote poetry and relieved his soul significantly. When a gentleman's grief reaches this stage, it may be loud, but it is rarely severe. When a man struggles to find a rhyme for sorrow beyond "borrow" and "tomorrow," his woes are nearer an end than he realizes. So it was with Pen. He suffered hot and cold fits, alternating between sullen peevishness, blank resignation, and occasional mad paroxysms of rage and longing. During these fits, his imaginary mistress, Rebecca, would be saddled and galloped fiercely through the countryside or into Chatteris, her rider gesticulating wildly atop her, startling carters and turnpike men as he cried out the name of the false one.

Mr. Foker became a frequent and welcome visitor to Fairoaks during this era, his good spirits and oddities amusing the Major and Pen, while astonishing the Widow and little Laura. His tandem caused quite a sensation in Clavering marketplace, where he overturned a stall, sliced Mrs. Pybus's poodle over its shaven quarters, and drank raspberry bitters at the Clavering Arms. Everyone in the little place knew who he was and consulted their Peers to verify his title. So young, and their books so ancient, that his name appeared in only a few volumes; his mother, now quite an antiquated lady, was listed as Lady Agnes Milton, progeny of the Earl of Rosherville. Yet his youth, wealth, and honorable lineage spread rapidly through Clavering, where, one assumes, poor Pen's little transaction with the Chatteris actress was discussed with equal fervor.

Viewing the tiny old town of Clavering St. Mary from the London road, past the lodge at Fairoaks, with the rapid, shining Brawl winding down from the town and skirting the woods of Clavering Park, alongside the ancient church tower and peaked roofs rising among trees and old walls, backed by sunshiny hills stretching westward toward the sea—it appears so cheery and comfortable that many a traveler's heart must have yearned for it from the coach-top, imagining it a calm friendly nook to shelter in at life's end. Tom Smith, driver of the Alacrity coach, often pointed to a tree near the river commanding a fine view of the church and town, telling his companion on the box, "Artises come and take hoff the Church from that there tree—It was a Habby once, sir." Indeed, it is a pretty view, recommended to Mr. Stanfield or Mr. Roberts for their next tour.

Yet, like Constantinople seen from the Bosphorus, or Mrs. Rougemont viewed from the opposite box, Clavering is rather prettier at a distance than on close acquaintance. The town so cheerful a few furlongs off looks very blank and dreary. Except on market days, the streets are empty. The clack of pattens echoes through half the place, and you may hear the creaking of the rusty old ensign at the Clavering Arms without disturbance from any other noise. There has not been a ball in the Assembly Rooms since the Clavering volunteers gave one to their Colonel, the old Sir Francis Clavering; the stables that once housed that brilliant, now-defunct regiment are now cheerless and empty, save on Thursdays when farmers put up there with their tilted carts and gigs, making a feeble show of liveliness, or on Petty Sessions when magistrates attend in what used to be the old card-room.

On the south side of the market rises the church, with its great grey towers, wherein the sun illuminates delicate carving, deepening the shadows of huge buttresses and gilding glittering windows and flaming vanes. Centuries ago, the image of the Church's Patroness was wrenched from the porch; such statues of saints as were within reach of stones and hammers during that period of pious demolition are now maimed and headless. Of those spared from fire, only Doctor Portman knows their names and histories, for his curate, Smirke, is not much of an antiquarian, and Mr. Simcoe, incumbent and architect of the Chapel of Ease in the lower town, considers them the abomination of desolation.
