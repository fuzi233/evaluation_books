If my friend from Flatland still possessed the sharp, unclouded intellect he held when he first penned these memoirs, I would not need to stand here as his voice. He wishes to thank the Spaceland readers who, with surprising speed, demanded a second printing of his work; to apologize for the inevitable errors and typos that mar the text, for which he bears only partial blame; and to clarify a few misunderstandings. Yet, he is no longer the Square he once was. Seven years of solitary confinement, compounded by the crushing weight of universal skepticism and ridicule, have worn down his spirit. Combined with the natural fading of old age, these trials have scrubbed away much of the terminology and specific insights he gained during his brief exile in Spaceland. Consequently, he has asked me to address two lingering objections on his behalf: one intellectual, the other moral.

The first charge is that a Flatlander, upon seeing a Line, must perceive something that possesses thickness as well as length, for otherwise it would be invisible. Therefore, the argument goes, Flatlanders must admit that their people are not merely long and broad, but also—albeit slightly—thick or high. This objection is logically sound and nearly impossible to refute for a Spaceland mind. I confess, when I first heard it, I was at a loss. But my friend's response, though delivered with the humility of a broken man, strikes me as complete.

"I concede," he told me, "the factual basis of your critic's claim, yet I reject his conclusion. It is undeniable that within Flatland exists a Third Dimension, unrecognized by us, which we might call 'height,' just as you possess a Fourth Dimension in Spaceland, currently unnamed, which I shall term 'extra-height.' Yet, just as you cannot perceive your 'extra-height,' we cannot perceive our 'height.' Even I—who spent twenty-four hours in Spaceland and learned the definition of 'height'—cannot now comprehend it through sight or reason. I can only grasp it through faith.

"The logic is stark. A dimension requires direction, measurement, and the concept of 'more' or 'less.' In Flatland, every line is equally and infinitesimally thick. There is no variation to trigger the mind's conception of height. No 'delicate micrometer,' as one hasty critic suggested, would help us; for we would not know *what* to measure or *in which direction*. When we see a Line, we see something that is long and bright. Brightness is as essential to a Line's existence as its length; without light, the Line vanishes. Thus, whenever I speak to my fellow Flatlanders about this hidden dimension visible in a Line, they reply, 'Ah, you mean brightness.' When I insist, 'No, I mean a true dimension,' they retort, 'Then measure it, or tell us its direction.' And I am silenced, for I can do neither.

"Only yesterday, the Chief Circle—the High Priest—visited the State Prison for his seventh annual inspection. He asked me the standard question: 'Are you any better?' I attempted to demonstrate that he was 'high,' possessing a dimension he did not know. His reply was chilling: 'You say I am "high"? Measure my "high-ness," and I will believe you.' Stumped, crushed, and unable to meet his challenge, I was forced to submit. He left the room triumphant.

"Does this still seem absurd? Imagine a visitor from the Fourth Dimension descending to your world. He tells you that whenever you open your eyes, you see a Plane (Two Dimensions) and infer a Solid (Three), yet you actually see a Fourth Dimension—a true dimension that is neither color nor brightness, nor anything you can point to or measure. What would you do? Would you not lock him up? That is my fate. It is as natural for Flatlanders to imprison a Square for preaching the Third Dimension as it is for Spacelanders to incarcerate a Cube for preaching the Fourth. Alas, how deeply the family resemblance runs through blind, persecuting humanity across all dimensions! Points, Lines, Squares, Cubes, and Extra-Cubes—we are all equally prone to the same errors, all slaves to our dimensional prejudices, as one of your Spaceland poets wisely noted:

*'One touch of Nature makes all worlds akin.'*"

*[Note: The Author requests that I add that certain misconceptions among his critics have led him to include in his dialogue with the Sphere remarks relevant to this point, which he had previously omitted as tedious.]*

Regarding this intellectual defense, the Square's position appears impregnable. However, his answer to the second objection—the moral charge—is far less clear. He is accused of being a woman-hater. Since this charge has been vigorously pressed by the larger half of the Spaceland population, I wish to address it as honestly as possible. Yet, the Square is so unfamiliar with Spaceland's moral vocabulary that a literal transcription of his defense would be unjust. As his interpreter, I summarize his evolution: during his seven-year imprisonment, his personal views on Women and the Isosceles classes shifted significantly. He now leans toward the Sphere's opinion that Straight Lines hold superiority in many respects over Circles.

However, writing as a historian, he has aligned himself—perhaps too closely—with the prevailing views of Flatland and even Spaceland historiography, where the destinies of women and the masses have rarely been deemed worthy of serious consideration. Furthermore, he seeks to disavow the circular, aristocratic tendencies often attributed to him. While acknowledging the intellectual dominance maintained by a few Circles over the multitudes, he believes the facts of Flatland speak for themselves: revolutions cannot always be suppressed by slaughter, and Nature, in sentencing the Circles to infertility, has doomed them to ultimate failure. "Herein," he states, "I see the fulfillment of the great Law of all worlds: while the wisdom of Man thinks it is working one thing, the wisdom of Nature constrains it to work another, and quite a different and far better thing."

Finally, he begs his readers not to expect every minute detail of Flatland to mirror Spaceland exactly. Yet, he hopes that, viewed as a whole, his work will prove both suggestive and amusing to those Spacelanders of moderate and modest minds who, regarding matters of supreme importance lying beyond direct experience, refuse to say either, "This can never be," or, "It must needs be precisely thus, and we know all about it."

The world of Flatland is not merely a place; it is a strict, unyielding geometry where existence is defined entirely by the length of one's line. Here, the concept of "up" or "down" is a heresy, for we live upon a plane so vast that its boundaries seem infinite to our two-dimensional eyes. The climate is a constant, flat expanse of light and shadow, devoid of the vertical storms that plague higher dimensions. Our houses are not built upward but outward, sprawling like intricate constellations across the plain, their walls forming perfect angles that speak of order and law.

We are the inhabitants of this rigid realm, creatures whose identity is stamped upon us by the number of sides we possess. A triangle is a man of modest means, sharp and efficient; a square is a gentleman of substance, broad and stable; a circle is a figure of immense power, approaching the divine perfection of the Sphere. Yet, among us all, there exists a class distinct from the rest: the Women. They are straight lines, simple and dangerous, lacking the angles that denote character and rank. To them, we grant no voice, for they cannot distinguish between the angles of a triangle and the obtuse corners of a square. They are mere extensions of the ground, useful only for labor and reproduction, their form rendering them invisible to the eye until they move.

In this world, recognition is a matter of survival. We do not look at faces, for we have none; we judge by the angles of our shoulders and the length of our lines. But there is a flaw in this system, a terrifying irregularity known as the Irregular Figure. These are the outcasts, the criminals, the chaotic ones who defy the sacred geometry of our society. Their jagged, unpredictable edges strike fear into the hearts of the orderly, for they represent a universe where rules do not apply.

To combat this chaos, we once practiced the ancient art of painting, coloring our lines to denote status and warning. But the people grew bold, demanding the right to choose their own hues. This led to the Chromatic Sedition, a rebellion of color that threatened to dissolve the very fabric of our social order. The response was swift and brutal: the Universal Colour Bill was enacted, stripping away the right to paint and enforcing a monochromatic existence where every shade is dictated by the state.

At the helm of this rigid order stand the Priests, figures of immense authority who claim to see beyond the plane. They preach the Doctrine of the Higher Dimensions, speaking of a world where we are not lines but spheres, trapped in a flat illusion. They warn of dangers lurking above and below, of a third dimension that could crush us if we were not careful. Their words are a mix of mysticism and mathematics, designed to keep us humble and obedient, reminding us that our world is small, fragile, and entirely dependent on the laws of geometry that govern us.

But lately, strange visions have begun to plague the mind. I have seen flashes of a world called Lineland, a realm of such simplicity that it seems almost comical compared to our own complexity. I have tried to explain these visions to others, to share the burden of this knowledge, but my words fall on deaf ears. Then came the Stranger, a being from Spaceland, a creature of three dimensions who walked among us as a perfect sphere. He spoke of mysteries I could not comprehend, of angles I could not see, and of a reality far greater than the sum of our parts.

He tried to reveal the secrets of Spaceland through words, but his language was too limited, too bound by the two dimensions he inhabited. So, he resorted to deeds, showing me the impossible, bending the laws of our world to demonstrate the truth of the third dimension. And when I finally stepped into Spaceland, seeing the world from above, I realized the true horror and beauty of our existence. We are not just lines; we are shadows of something much greater, trapped in a plane that limits our potential.

Yet, even after witnessing these wonders, my desire for knowledge never waned. The Sphere encouraged me, urging me to spread the theory of three dimensions, to teach my grandson and others the truth of our higher nature. But the task is difficult, for the people of Flatland are stubborn, clinging to the safety of their known world. They fear the unknown, the irregular, the colorful. And so, I continue to try, to diffuse the theory of three dimensions, hoping that one day, we might break free from the flatness that binds us, and see the world as it truly is.

I designate our realm Flatland, not by our own nomenclature, but to render its essence intelligible to you, fortunate dwellers of the Third Dimension.

Envision an infinite plane where geometric forms—lines, triangles, squares, pentagons, hexagons, and countless others—drift freely across the surface, utterly incapable of ascending above or descending below it. They exist much like shadows cast upon a wall, yet possessing substance and glowing, defined edges. This is the accurate image of my nation and its citizens. Once, I might have termed this entire existence "my universe," but my consciousness has since been expanded to comprehend higher realities.

In such a dimension, it is logically impossible for any object of "solid" nature to exist. You might reasonably assume, however, that we could at least visually distinguish between the various shapes as they moved about. This is a grave error. We perceive nothing of the sort; we cannot differentiate one figure from another through sight alone. To us, the only visible reality consists of straight lines. I shall now demonstrate this necessity.

Place a coin upon the center of a table in your world and gaze down upon it from above. To your eyes, it appears a perfect circle. Now, retreat to the table's edge and slowly lower your head until your eye aligns with the surface. As you descend, the coin transforms from a circle into an increasingly elongated oval. Finally, when your eye rests exactly upon the table's plane, mirroring the condition of a Flatlander, the oval vanishes entirely. The coin ceases to be a shape and becomes, in your vision, a mere straight line.

This phenomenon applies equally to any polygon cut from pasteboard. Whether it be a triangle, a square, or any other form, once your viewpoint reaches the level of the surface, the figure dissolves into a line. Consider, for instance, an equilateral triangle, which among us signifies a tradesman of reputable standing. Figure 1 depicts how you would see him if you hovered above; Figures 2 and 3 illustrate his appearance as your eye nears the table's level; and if your eye were perfectly flush with the surface—as it is for us in Flatland—you would behold nothing but a straight line.

When I resided in Spaceland, I learned that your sailors encounter a parallel experience when traversing your oceans. A distant island or coastline may possess intricate bays, promontories, and angular recesses, yet from afar, you perceive none of these details unless the sun casts sharp shadows that reveal depth through light and dark. Instead, you see only a grey, unbroken line stretching across the water.

Precisely so is it with us. When a triangular acquaintance or any other citizen approaches, we see no complexity. Lacking a sun and the play of shadows that aids your vision, we possess no such optical crutches. As a friend draws nearer, his line grows larger; as he departs, it shrinks. Yet, regardless of whether he is a triangle, a square, a pentagon, a hexagon, or a circle, to our eyes he remains nothing more than a straight line.

You may wonder how we manage to distinguish one friend from another under such visual constraints. This inquiry, though natural, finds its proper answer only when I describe the inhabitants of Flatland in detail. For now, I shall defer that discussion to briefly address the climate and architecture of our world.

In Flatland, just as in your own realm, the compass points are fixed: North, South, East, and West. Yet, unlike your world, we possess no sun, no moon, and no stars to mark the sky. Determining direction here requires a unique reliance on the laws of our nature. A subtle, constant gravitational pull draws everything inexorably toward the South. While this force is negligible in our temperate zones—allowing a healthy woman to wander several furlongs northward with ease—it serves as our primary navigational anchor across most of the land. This pull is compounded by the rain, which descends at regular intervals exclusively from the North, offering a wet warning of our orientation. Within our cities, architecture reinforces this guidance; our dwellings are aligned so their walls run North-South, shielding interiors from the northern downpour. In the open countryside, where structures are absent, the trunks of trees stand as silent sentinels pointing the way. Despite these aids, navigation remains a challenge. In the vast, featureless plains of our temperate regions, devoid of both shelter and timber, I have known myself stranded for hours, helpless until the first drop of rain fell from the North to reveal my bearing.

The burden of this southern attraction is not borne equally. It weighs heavily upon the frail, the elderly, and particularly upon the delicate Females of our sex. Consequently, it is a fundamental rule of breeding that any man encountering a lady in the street must immediately yield the North side of the path to her. This act of courtesy is far from simple, especially when one is in robust health and the climate renders the distinction between North and South a matter of guesswork.

Our dwellings offer no windows, for light permeates our existence uniformly, day and night, from a source we cannot perceive. In ancient times, our scholars obsessed over the origin of this illumination, their futile inquiries filling our asylums with the insane. The Legislature eventually banned such research entirely, yet I alone bear the terrible truth: I know the source of light lies in a third dimension. My insight isolates me, marking me as the maddest of the mad, while my fellow citizens remain blind to the geometry of higher spaces.

Turning back to our homes, the standard design is pentagonal. As illustrated, the two northern sides, RO and OF, form the roof and typically lack doors. The eastern side holds a narrow entrance reserved for women, while the western side features a broad doorway for men; the southern base remains doorless. Square and triangular residences are strictly prohibited. The sharp angles of squares and triangles pose a lethal threat to travelers, whose lines are less distinct than those of living beings. A sudden collision with the corner of a square or triangle could prove fatal. Recognizing this danger, the law banned triangular homes as early as the eleventh century, permitting them only for military fortifications and state arsenals. Squares were tolerated longer but taxed heavily. Three centuries ago, the Legislature decreed that in any town exceeding ten thousand souls, the pentagon became the minimum allowable shape for public safety. Today, the pentagonal form dominates even our rural landscapes, leaving the rare square house as a relic found only in the most isolated, backward districts.

To a three-dimensional observer, a fully grown citizen of Flatland spans roughly eleven inches in length or breadth, with twelve inches serving as the absolute limit of our physical stature. Yet, within this two-dimensional plane, our identity is defined not by size, but by geometry. Our women exist as straight lines—featureless, silent, and devoid of angles.

Below them stand our soldiers and the lowest stratum of laborers: Isosceles triangles. These figures possess two long, equal sides, each stretching nearly eleven inches, converging at a vertex to form a razor-sharp, menacing angle. Their base, the third side, is often negligible, sometimes measuring no more than half an inch. When this base shrinks to merely an eighth of an inch, the distinction between a soldier and a woman vanishes entirely; the figure becomes so acutely pointed that it is indistinguishable from a line. We classify these beings as Isosceles, a designation that marks their precarious status in the social hierarchy.

Ascending the ladder of complexity, we find the Middle Class, composed of Equilateral triangles, where all three sides are perfectly equal. Above them lie the Professional Men and Gentlemen, represented by Squares and Pentagons. The Nobility follows, beginning with Hexagons and progressing through figures with increasing numbers of sides until they achieve the title of Polygon. At the apex of existence stands the Priestly order: the Circles. To the untrained eye, a Circle appears to be a perfect, smooth boundary, but in truth, it is a Polygon with so many infinitesimally small sides that it mimics the perfection of a sphere. This is the highest rank, reserved for those closest to the divine.

Nature herself enforces a rigid law of progression: a male child must possess one more side than his father. Consequently, each generation naturally ascends the scale of nobility. A Square fathers a Pentagon; a Pentagon, a Hexagon. However, this divine arithmetic falters among the tradesmen, soldiers, and workers. Lacking equal sides, they are deemed imperfect human forms, and the Law of Nature does not strictly apply to them. An Isosceles father invariably produces an Isosceles son.

Yet, hope persists even for the degraded. Through a long lineage of military triumphs or diligent labor, the more intelligent artisans exhibit a subtle transformation: their base widens slightly while their other two sides contract, moving them closer to the ideal of the Equilateral triangle. The High Priests facilitate this ascent by arranging strategic intermarriages between the sons and daughters of these intellectually superior lower-class citizens. While the birth of a genuine Equilateral from Isosceles parents is a statistical rarity amidst the vast numbers of triangular births, it is a miracle worthy of celebration.

Such a birth demands rigorous preparation: generations of frugality, self-control, and the systematic cultivation of intellect. Once certified as Regular by the Sanitary and Social Board, the infant is subjected to solemn ceremony before being removed from his biological family. He is adopted by a childless Equilateral, bound by oath to sever all ties with his former home. This separation is crucial; the fear is that the newly elevated organism, through unconscious imitation, might regress to his hereditary level of inequality.

This phenomenon serves a dual purpose. It offers a glimmer of hope to the serfs, breaking the monotony of their squalor, while simultaneously acting as a potent barrier against revolution for the Aristocracy. If the rabble were entirely devoid of ambition, their sheer numbers and physical ferocity would overwhelm the wisdom of the Circles. Instead, Nature has devised a Law of Compensation: as the working classes grow in intelligence and virtue, their defining acute angle diminishes, rendering them physically less threatening. As they gain the mental capacity to wield their penetrating power wisely, they lose the raw, destructive force of their sharpness.

This balance ensures the stability of the state. The Polygons and Circles can stifle sedition in its infancy, exploiting the boundless hopefulness of the human mind. Artifice aids this order; State physicians can artificially compress or expand the limbs of rebellious leaders, rendering them perfectly Regular and granting them immediate entry into the privileged classes. Those too irregular to be rehabilitated are confined in honorable hospitals for life, while the most obstinate are executed.

Ultimately, the wretched mass of Isosceles, left planless and leaderless, faces two fates. They may be swiftly neutralized by the small corps of loyalists maintained by the Chief Circle, or, more commonly, they are driven into mutual warfare by jealousies and suspicions skillfully fomented by the ruling class. Perishing by one another's angles, they vanish. Our annals record no fewer than one hundred and twenty major rebellions and two hundred and thirty-five minor outbreaks, all concluding in this manner.

If the sharp, wedge-shaped Triangles of the Soldier class strike fear into the hearts of the populace, it requires little deduction to realize that our Women are forces of a magnitude far exceeding even their military counterparts. Where a Soldier is merely a wedge, a Woman is a needle—a creature defined entirely by its extremities, possessing lethal acuteness at both ends. To this inherent danger, she adds the terrifying ability to render herself practically invisible at will. Thus, in Flatland, the Female is not a being to be trifled with; she is a walking catastrophe.

Perhaps my younger readers from Spaceland will pause here, asking how such invisibility is possible. The answer lies in simple geometry, requiring no complex magic. Consider a needle resting on a table. Viewed from the side, it presents a long, dangerous line. Viewed from the end, however, it collapses into a mere point, vanishing from sight. So too is it with our Women. When a woman turns her side toward us, she appears as a straight line, a clear threat. When she faces us directly, showing her eye or mouth—organs that in Flatland are identical—we see only a brilliant, lustrous point, effectively invisible. Conversely, when she turns her back, presenting only a dull, sub-lustrous extremity, she becomes nearly indistinguishable from the background, her rear serving as a natural "Invisible Cap."

The peril posed by these entities is absolute. In Spaceland, one might survive a collision with a working man or suffer a wound from a soldier; in Flatland, running into a Woman is synonymous with immediate and total destruction. And when she is invisible, or visible only as a faint glimmer, the margin for error shrinks to zero. Even the most cautious citizen risks annihilation.

Consequently, the laws of Flatland are rife with statutes designed to mitigate this existential threat, particularly in the southern states where gravity is stronger and involuntary motion is more common. The code generally mandates three primary rules: First, every dwelling must possess a dedicated eastern entrance for females, ensuring they enter with "becoming and respectful manner," distinct from the western door used by men. Second, no woman may traverse a public space without maintaining a constant, audible "Peace-cry," under penalty of death. Third, any female suffering from conditions causing involuntary motion—such as St. Vitus's Dance or severe sneezing—is to be instantly destroyed.

Some states impose further restrictions, forbidding women from standing still in public or mandating that they be trailed by a male relative. Yet, the wisest circles have concluded that excessive restriction breeds resentment. When confined to their homes or hampered abroad, women's tempers flare, often resulting in domestic massacres that decimate entire villages within hours. Therefore, the three core laws remain the standard for regulated societies.

Ultimately, our greatest safeguard is not legislation, but the biological limitations of the women themselves. While a single backward thrust can kill instantly, the woman's own frailty prevents her from sustaining the struggle; she must disengage immediately or be shattered. Furthermore, the power of fashion acts as a subtle defense. In civilized states, it is considered a mark of breeding for a woman to sway her back rhythmically from right to left. This undulation, envied by wives of lower ranks who can only achieve a monotonous pendulum swing, ensures that in households of status, the "back-motion" is as ubiquitous as time itself, granting husbands and sons immunity from invisible attacks.

Do not mistake this for a lack of affection. Unfortunately, the Frail Sex is governed by the passion of the moment, devoid of reflection, judgment, or foresight due to their lack of angles and consequent lack of brain-power. In their fury, they recognize no distinctions. I have witnessed a woman exterminate her entire household, only to ask, half an hour later, where her husband and children had gone once her rage subsided.

Thus, a woman must never be irritated while she retains the ability to turn around. Once confined to apartments designed to deny her this mobility, she becomes impotent for mischief, unable to remember threats or promises made in the heat of the moment.

Domestic life in Flatland proceeds smoothly enough, save for the lower military classes, where husbands' lack of tact and reliance on their own sharp angles rather than diplomacy leads to disaster. These reckless males often neglect the proper construction of women's quarters or provoke their wives with irrevocable insults, resulting in massacre. Paradoxically, this destructiveness is viewed by many as a providential mechanism for controlling population and stifling revolution.

Even in the most harmonious, circular families, life lacks the deep peace found in Spaceland. Safety has been purchased at the cost of comfort. It is an ancient instinct among the women of the higher classes to keep their eyes and mouths perpetually directed toward their husbands and male guests. To turn one's back upon a husband in a distinguished household is a portent of lost status. While this ensures safety, it creates a cacophony of relentless discourse. In the homes of the working class, where a wife may turn her back to hum her Peace-cry, there are moments of silence. But in the upper echelons, the bright, penetrating eye and the voluble mouth are ever-present. The skill required to dodge a sting is useless against the torrent of speech. As the wife has nothing meaningful to say and no conscience to restrain her, many cynical husbands confess they would rather face the silent, deadly sting than endure the safe, sonorous noise of a woman's other end.

To the citizens of Spaceland, the plight of our women may seem tragic. Indeed, it is. While a male Isosceles might hope for evolution and a sharper angle, a Woman has no such future. "Once a Woman, always a Woman" is the immutable decree of nature; evolution itself seems to suspend its progress in her favor. Yet, we find a grim comfort in this prearrangement: since they have no hopes for the future, they possess no memory to recall past humiliations and no forethought to anticipate future suffering. They are trapped in the eternal present, a necessity of their existence and the foundation of our society.

You, fortunate inhabitant of the Third Dimension, bathed in both shadow and radiance, gifted with dual eyes that grant you the mastery of perspective and the delight of myriad hues—you who can truly behold an angle and gaze upon the perfect circumference of a circle—how shall I convey to your enlightened mind the profound labyrinth we traverse in Flatland merely to identify the shape of our neighbor?

Recall my previous assertion: every entity in our realm, whether living stone or breathing soul, regardless of its internal complexity, presents itself to our vision as a mere straight line. If all appear identical, how does distinction arise?

The solution lies in a triad of senses. First stands Hearing, a faculty far more refined in us than in your world. It allows us not only to distinguish friends by voice but to categorize the three lowest orders: the Equilateral, the Square, and the Pentagon. I need not mention the Isosceles, whose voice I disregard entirely. Yet, as we climb the social ladder, auditory discrimination grows treacherous. Voices blend, and the aristocracy, bred in luxury, has largely atrophied the crude skill of ear-based identification. Where deceit lurks, sound fails us. Among the lowest ranks, vocal organs are grotesquely exaggerated; an Isosceles can mimic a Polygon, and with practice, even a Circle. Thus, a second method prevails.

For our women and the lower classes—and I shall address the elite later—Touch is the supreme arbiter of identity, particularly between strangers or when determining class rather than individual. What constitutes an "introduction" among your highborn in Spaceland is here reduced to the ritual of "Feeling." The archaic gentleman in the remote provinces still bows and says, "Permit me to ask you to feel and be felt by my friend Mr. So-and-so." In the bustling towns, commerce abbreviates this to, "Let me ask you to feel Mr. So-and-so," assuming reciprocity. The modern, dashing youth, disdainful of effort and linguistic purity, has twisted the phrase further, using "to feel" as a technical shorthand for "to recommend for the purpose of feeling and being felt." Indeed, polite society now sanctions the barbarism: "Mr. Smith, permit me to feel Mr. Jones."

Do not mistake this "Feeling" for the tedious circumnavigation you might imagine. Through rigorous schooling and daily practice, we instantly discern the angles of Triangles, Squares, and Pentagons. The obtuse vertex of an Isosceles is as obvious to the dullest touch as a rock to a stone. Usually, feeling a single angle suffices to classify the individual, unless one encounters the higher nobility. There, the task becomes perilous. Even a Master of Arts may confuse a ten-sided figure with a twelve-sided one; few Doctors of Science can swiftly differentiate between a twenty-sided and a twenty-four-sided aristocrat.

Those who remember my earlier excerpts regarding the Laws of Women will understand the gravity of this contact. Care and discretion are paramount; otherwise, the angles of the Felt may inflict fatal injury on the Feeler. The Felt must remain statue-still. A sudden start, a fidgety shift, or even a violent sneeze has proven lethal to the incautious, snuffing out friendships before they bloom. This danger is acute among the lower Triangles. Their eyes are positioned so far from their vertex that they are blind to the extremity of their own frame. Coupled with their rough, coarse nature, they lack sensitivity to the delicate touch of the higher Polygon. It is no wonder that an involuntary toss of the head has, on occasion, cost the State a valuable life.

I recall my excellent Grandfather, a rare Isosceles of the least irregular sort, who, shortly before his death, secured four of seven votes from the Sanitary and Social Board to elevate him to the class of the Equal-sided. He often wept, a tear glistening in his venerable eye, recounting a tragedy befalling his great-great-great-grandfather. This ancestor, a respectable working man with an angle of precisely 59 degrees and 30 minutes, was afflicted with rheumatism. While being felt by a superior Polygon, a sudden spasm caused him to accidentally transfixed the Great Man through the diagonal. The resulting imprisonment and the moral shock that rippled through the family relations caused a regression. In the next generation, the family brain registered at only 58 degrees. It took five generations of struggle to recover the lost ground, reaching the full 60 degrees and finally achieving the Ascent from the Isosceles. All this calamity stemmed from a single accident during the act of Feeling.

Here, I anticipate the cry of my educated readers: "How can you in Flatland know angles, degrees, or minutes? You see only a line; how can you discern inclination?"

My answer is that while we cannot *see* angles, we can *infer* them with terrifying precision. Our sense of touch, honed by necessity and training, distinguishes angular differences far more accurately than your unaided sight. Furthermore, Nature herself provides us with aids. It is a Law of Nature that the brain of the Isosceles begins at half a degree and increases by half a degree in every generation until the goal of 60 degrees is reached, marking the transition from serfdom to freedom.

Consequently, Nature supplies an ascending alphabet of angles from 0.5 to 60 degrees. Specimens of these angles are placed in every elementary school. Due to occasional retrogressions, moral stagnation, and the extraordinary fertility of the Criminal and Vagabond classes, there is a vast surplus of individuals in the half-degree and single-degree categories, along with a fair abundance up to 10 degrees. These creatures are destitute of civic rights; many, lacking the intelligence for warfare, are conscripted by the State for educational service. Fettered immovably to prevent danger, they populate the classrooms of Infant Schools, serving as living rulers for the Board of Education to teach the offspring of the Middle Classes the very tact and intelligence these wretched specimens lack.

In some states, these Specimens are fed and allowed to live for years. However, in more temperate regions, it is deemed more advantageous for educational efficiency to dispense with food, renewing the Specimens monthly—the average lifespan of the foodless criminal. In cheaper schools, the longevity of a specimen is offset by the cost of sustenance and the degradation of the angle's accuracy after weeks of constant handling. Moreover, the more expensive system, though costly, slightly reduces the redundant Isosceles population—a goal every statesman cherishes. Thus, despite the popular reaction in favor of "the cheap system," I maintain that in this instance, expense is the truest economy.

Yet, I must not let school board politics obscure my purpose. Enough has been shown to demonstrate that Recognition by Feeling is neither as tedious nor as indecisive as it seems, and it is far more trustworthy than Hearing. Nevertheless, as noted, this method carries inherent dangers. For this reason, many in the Middle and Lower classes, and all without exception in the Polygonal and Circular orders, prefer a third method, the description of which I shall reserve for the next section.

I stand before you now, aware that I risk appearing profoundly inconsistent. Throughout these prior sections, I have maintained that every figure in Flatland presents merely as a straight line, implying that visual distinction between social classes is impossible. Yet, I am compelled to explain to my Spaceland critics how we, in fact, navigate the world by sight.

If the Reader recalls the passage on "Recognition by Feeling," they will note the crucial qualifier: "among the lower classes." It is strictly among the higher orders, and within our temperate latitudes, that Sight Recognition is practiced. This ability exists solely because of the Fog. It shrouds our world for most of the year, sparing only the torrid zones. What you in Spaceland deem an unmixed evil—blotting landscapes and depressing spirits—is to us a blessing second only to air, the Nurse of arts and Parent of sciences. Without this atmospheric veil, all lines would appear with equal, indistinguishable clarity, as they do in those arid, transparent lands. But where Fog reigns, objects at a distance of three feet appear appreciably dimmer than those at two feet eleven inches. Through rigorous observation of this gradient of dimness, we infer the configuration of the observer with startling exactitude.

Consider two figures approaching: a Merchant, an Equilateral Triangle, and a Physician, a Pentagon. How do I distinguish them?

Imagine my eye positioned to bisect the angle of the approaching stranger. My gaze lies evenly between the two sides nearest me, contemplating them impartially. In the case of the Merchant, I perceive a straight line where the center is bright, but the extremities fade *rapidly* into the mist. His sides recede sharply into the Fog, rendering his corners very dim indeed. Conversely, the Physician's line, while also bright at the center, shades away *less rapidly*. His sides recede more gradually into the haze, keeping his extremities significantly brighter than the Merchant's.

Through long training and constant experience, the educated classes can thus discriminate with fair accuracy between the middle and lowest orders. I trust my Spaceland patrons do not dismiss this account as incredible. To attempt further detail would only perplex, yet I must warn the young: recognizing one's father or sons is not an easy affair. Life presents subtler problems. If my Father, the Triangle, presents his side rather than his angle, I am momentarily unsure if he is a Triangle or a Straight Line—a Woman—until he rotates or I shift my perspective. Similarly, observing a hexagonal grandson face-on reveals a complex interplay of brightness: one whole line in comparative light, flanked by two smaller, dimmer lines shading toward the extremities.

To elaborate further would be to indulge a temptation I must resist. The meanest mathematician in Spaceland understands that discriminating between rotating polygons in a crowded ballroom tasks even the most intellectual mind. It justifies the rich endowments of the Learned Professors of Geometry at the University of Wentbridge, where the Science and Art of Sight Recognition are taught to the elite. Only the scions of our noblest houses can afford the time and money required for this noble pursuit. Even to me, a mathematician of standing and grandfather to two regular Hexagons, navigating a crowd of rotating high-rankers is occasionally perplexing. For a common tradesman or serf, such a scene is unintelligible chaos.

To you, my Spaceland Reader, suddenly transported into our midst, the crowd would appear as a single, irregularly brightening and dimming line. Even a student perfect in theory would require years of experience to move through a fashionable gathering without jostling against betters whom it is against etiquette to "feel." To comport oneself with propriety in Polygonal society, one ought to be a Polygon oneself. This is the painful teaching of my experience.

The Art of Sight Recognition is honed by habit and by the deliberate avoidance of "Feeling." Just as the deaf and mute in Spaceland, if permitted to gesticulate, never master the valuable art of lip-reading, so it is with us. None who rely on "Feeling" in early life will ever learn "Seeing" in perfection. Consequently, among the Higher Classes, "Feeling" is discouraged or forbidden. Children are sent not to elementary schools where Feeling is taught, but to exclusive seminaries. At the University, to "feel" is a grave fault, punishable by rustication or expulsion.

Among the lower classes, however, Sight Recognition is viewed as an unattainable luxury. A tradesman cannot spare his son for abstract study. Thus, the poor are allowed to "feel" from infancy, gaining a precocious vivacity that contrasts with the initial inertia of the half-instructed youth. Yet, once the latter complete their university course, a new birth occurs; they rapidly overtake their triangular competitors in every pursuit.

Only a few fail the Final Test. Their condition is pitiable. Rejected by the higher class and despised by the lower, they possess neither the matured powers of the Bachelor nor the native versatility of the Tradesman. Professions and public service are closed to them. Though marriage is not legally barred, suitable alliances are rare, for the offspring of such ill-endowed unions is generally unfortunate or Irregular. It is from this refuse of our nobility that past seditions have drawn their leaders. Some progressive statesmen argue that true mercy dictates their total suppression, advocating for imprisonment or painless extinction for those who fail the Final Examination.

I find myself digressing into the subject of Irregularities, a matter of such vital interest that it demands a separate section.

Throughout the preceding pages, I have operated under an assumption—what ought to have been declared as a foundational axiom from the outset—that every sentient being in Flatland possesses a Regular Figure, meaning a construction of perfect geometric symmetry. To be precise: a Woman is not merely a line, but a perfectly straight one; an Artisan or Soldier must possess two sides of exact equality; a Tradesman, three; and a Lawyer, of which I am a modest member, four. In general, for any Polygon to exist within our society, all its sides must be equal in length.

While the absolute magnitude of these sides naturally correlates with the individual's age—a newborn female measuring roughly an inch, whereas a mature woman may stretch to a foot—their equality is the critical variable. For males of every rank, the sum of an adult's side lengths typically approximates two feet. Yet, physical scale is secondary; the entire edifice of Flatlandian social life rests upon the immutable law that Nature mandates equal sides.

Were our sides unequal, our angles would inevitably diverge. Under such conditions, the mere estimation of a single angle would fail to reveal an individual's true form; one would be compelled to verify every angle through the tactile ordeal of Feeling. Life, however, is too brief for such exhaustive scrutiny. The sciences and arts of Sight Recognition would instantly collapse; the practice of Feeling would cease to function as a reliable tool; social interaction would descend into peril or impossibility; trust and foresight would vanish. No citizen could safely arrange the simplest of social encounters. In short, civilization would regress into barbarism.

Am I rushing my readers toward these inevitable conclusions? Surely, a moment's reflection, aided by a single analogy from daily existence, must convince anyone that our entire social system is predicated on Regularity. Consider meeting two or three Tradesmen in the street. You identify them instantly by their characteristic angles and slightly dimmed sides, inviting them into your home for lunch. You act with absolute confidence because everyone knows the precise area occupied by an adult Triangle. Now, imagine that your Tradesman drags behind his regular vertex a chaotic parallelogram, twelve or thirteen inches in diagonal. What would you do with such a monster wedged firmly in your doorway?

I fear I insult the intelligence of my readers by detailing scenarios obvious to any inhabitant of Spaceland. Clearly, measuring a single angle would no longer suffice in such a catastrophe; one's entire existence would be consumed by feeling or surveying the perimeters of acquaintances. Already, avoiding collisions in a crowd taxes the wisdom of even a well-educated Square. But if no one could calculate the Regularity of a single figure among them, chaos would reign supreme. The slightest panic would inflict grievous injuries, or, if Women or Soldiers were present, perhaps considerable loss of life.

Thus, expediency aligns with Nature, stamping its approval upon the Regularity of form, and the Law hastens to support this consensus. In Flatland, "Irregularity of Figure" signifies the same as, or indeed exceeds, the combination of moral obliquity and criminality known to you in higher dimensions, and is treated accordingly. It is true that some propagators of paradoxes argue there is no necessary link between geometric and moral irregularity. They claim the Irregular is born to be shunned by parents, ridiculed by kin, neglected by servants, and scorned by society, barred from all positions of trust. They describe how police watch every movement until adulthood, when the individual is either destroyed if exceeding the margin of deviation or imprisoned in a Government Office as a seventh-class clerk, forbidden marriage, forced into drudgery for a pittance, and subjected to constant surveillance during vacations. They ask, what wonder that human nature becomes embittered and perverted by such treatment?

Yet, this plausible reasoning fails to convince me, nor the wisest of our statesmen, that our ancestors erred in establishing the axiom that tolerating Irregularity is incompatible with the safety of the State. Undoubtedly, the life of an Irregular is harsh; but the interests of the Greater Number demand it remain so. If a man with a triangular front and a polygonal back were permitted to exist and propagate an increasingly irregular lineage, what fate would befall the arts of life? Must our houses, doors, and churches be altered to accommodate such monstrosities? Must ticket collectors measure every man's perimeter before granting entry to a theater or lecture hall? Should an Irregular be exempted from the militia? And if not, how is he to be prevented from bringing desolation to his comrades? Furthermore, what irresistible temptations to fraud must beset such a creature? How easily could he enter a shop with his polygonal front forward, ordering goods from a trusting tradesman to any extent? Let advocates of false philanthropy plead as they will for the abolition of Irregular Penal Laws; I, for my part, have never encountered an Irregular who was not also what Nature evidently intended him to be: a hypocrite, a misanthrope, and, within the limits of his power, a perpetrator of all manner of mischief.

This does not mean I advocate the extreme measures seen in some States, where an infant deviating by half a degree from correct angularity is summarily destroyed at birth. Some of our greatest geniuses suffered deviations as severe as, or greater than, forty-five minutes in their earliest days; their loss would have been an irreparable injury to the State. Moreover, the art of healing has achieved glorious triumphs through compressions, extensions, trepannings, colligations, and other surgical or dietary operations that have partially or wholly cured Irregularity. Therefore, advocating a *Via Media*, I propose laying down no fixed or absolute line of demarcation. However, at the stage when the frame is just setting and the Medical Board reports that recovery is improbable, I suggest that the Irregular offspring be painlessly and mercifully consumed.

If my readers have followed my narrative with any degree of scrutiny, they will not be startled to learn that existence in Flatland is, by all accounts, profoundly monochromatic. I do not speak here of the grim realities of war, political intrigue, or the chaotic friction of social factions—elements that, while present, fail to constitute true history in the eyes of a higher dimension. Nor do I deny that the peculiar fusion of existential struggle and mathematical deduction lends our lives a sharp, intellectual edge incomprehensible to your Spaceland inhabitants. My critique is strictly aesthetic. From the standpoint of art and beauty, our world is a void, a flat expanse of grey silence.

How else could it be? Our entire visual universe consists of lines. Our landscapes, our historical tapestries, our floral arrangements, and our portraiture are reduced to single strokes, differentiated only by the subtle gradations of light and shadow. There is no depth, no saturation, no explosion of hue.

Yet, this was not the eternal state of our reality. According to the fragmented traditions preserved in our archives, a vibrant epoch once illuminated our ancestors' lives. For roughly half a dozen centuries, a transient but dazzling splendor bathed Flatland. Legend attributes this revolution to a singular, visionary figure—a Polygon whose name has been lost to time, though some whisper it was a Pentagram. Having stumbled upon the rudimentary chemistry of color and the technique of application, he began to adorn his dwelling, then his servants, then his lineage, until finally, he painted himself.

The dual benefits of utility and beauty were undeniable. Wherever Chromatistes turned his variegated form, he commanded immediate awe and deference. In a society where identity is often obscured by silhouette, color became the ultimate clarifier. No longer did neighbors need to strain their geometric calculations to distinguish front from back; no one jostled him in confusion. His voice was liberated from the exhausting labor of self-identification required of us colorless Squares and Pentagons moving through crowds of blind Isosceles.

The contagion spread with the velocity of wildfire. Within a week, every Square and Triangle in the district had adopted the Chromatistes' example; only the most rigid Pentagons hesitated. Within months, even the Dodecagons were infected. A year passed before the practice permeated the realm, sparing only the highest echelons of the Nobility. Soon, the custom radiated outward, and within two generations, Flatland was a kaleidoscope, save for two distinct classes: the Women and the Priests.

Here, Nature seemed to raise an impassable barrier. The proponents of color relied on a specific sophistry: "Distinction of sides implies distinction of colors." This axiom converted whole cities overnight. Yet, it held no sway over the Priests and the Women. The Women, possessing a single side, were technically devoid of sides. The Priests, claiming the status of perfect Circles, boasted of having no sides whatsoever, existing instead as a continuous circumference. Thus, they viewed the "color axiom" as logically void. While the rest of society succumbed to the intoxicating allure of corporal decoration, the Priests and Women remained the sole guardians of the original, unadorned purity.

Though we might label those ancient days immoral, licentious, or anarchical, from an aesthetic perspective, they represented the glorious, albeit aborted, childhood of Art in Flatland. It was a childhood that never matured into adulthood, nor even bloomed into youth. Life itself was a delight, for to live was to see. Even a modest gathering was a spectacle to behold. The richly varied hues of a congregation in a temple or an audience in a theater were said to have occasionally overwhelmed our greatest scholars and actors with sensory overload. But nothing rivaled the unspeakable magnificence of a military review.

Imagine the sight: a line of battle of twenty thousand Isosceles pivoting in unison, swapping the somber black of their bases for the fiery orange and regal purple of their upper flanks. Consider the militia of the Equilateral Triangles, tricolored in red, white, and blue. Observe the Square artillerymen, clad in mauve, ultra-marine, gamboge, and burnt umber, rotating rapidly beside their vermilion cannons. Watch the dashing flash of five-colored and six-colored Pentagons and Hexagons, serving as surgeons, geometer-mages, and aides-de-camp, careering across the field. These visions were so potent that they gave credence to the famous tale of an illustrious Circle, so overcome by the artistic grandeur of his command that he discarded his marshal's baton and royal crown, vowing to exchange them forever for an artist's brush.

The sensuous richness of that era is further evidenced by the very language of the time. The common speech of ordinary citizens during the Color Revolt was suffused with a deeper, more vivid tinge of word and thought. We owe our finest poetry and the lingering rhythmic cadences of our modern scientific discourse entirely to that fleeting, radiant age.

While the intellectual arts crumbled around them, a new, seductive simplicity took root. The Art of Sight Recognition, once the cornerstone of Flatland's hierarchy, became obsolete. Geometry, Statics, and Kinetics were dismissed as archaic burdens, their universities falling into disrepair. Even the inferior Art of Feeling, taught in elementary schools, was abandoned. With these disciplines gone, the Isosceles classes—formerly disciplined by the necessity of education and the tribute they paid to the state—swelled in number and insolence. Freed from the old constraints that had tempered their brutality, they grew bold and numerous.

Year by year, the Soldiers and Artisans, now elevated to parity with the highest Polygons, declared that the distinction between their station and the elite was an illusion. They argued that the complexities of life could be solved not through rigorous calculation, but through the simple, intuitive process of Colour Recognition. Dissatisfied with the slow decline of Sight Recognition, they demanded the legal prohibition of all "monopolizing and aristocratic Arts." They called for the abolition of endowments dedicated to Mathematics and Feeling, insisting that since Colour—a second nature—had rendered class distinctions unnecessary, the Law must follow suit. All individuals, they proclaimed, were entitled to absolute equality.

Seeing the higher orders waver, the revolutionaries pressed further. They demanded that every citizen, including the Priests and the Women, submit to the universal law of Colour by being painted. When objections arose that Priests and Women lacked the sides necessary for such decoration, the revolutionaries countered with a twisted logic: Nature and Expediency dictated that the front half of every human—the side bearing the eye and mouth—must be distinguished from the hind half. Thus, a bill was drafted for a general assembly proposing that every Woman be painted red on the front and green on the back. The Priests were to receive the same treatment: red on the semicircle containing their face, green on the rear.

This proposal was born not of the Isosceles, whose minds were too degraded to conceive such statecraft, but of a cunning Irregular Circle. Spared from destruction in childhood due to a foolish indulgence, this Irregular plotted to bring desolation upon his country. His scheme relied on a brilliant, deceptive geometry. By assigning Women the same dual-colour palette as the Priests, the revolutionaries ensured that, when viewed from the side, a Woman would appear indistinguishable from a Priest. To the casual observer, both would present a straight line, half red and half green.

Consider the visual deception: A Woman, painted red on her front and green on her back, presents a stark diagonal division to the side viewer. Now consider a Priest, whose mouth sits at the apex of his red semicircle, with the green occupying his rear. If one looks directly along the Priest's dividing diameter, the silhouette is also a straight line, split exactly between red and green. While the Priest's form might be slightly shorter and the shading more rapid, the immediate impression of identical colouring would cause the observer to ignore subtle differences. In an age where Sight Recognition was already decaying, and as Women quickly learned to soften their edges to mimic the Circles, the confusion became inevitable. The distinction between the sacred Priest and the common Woman would vanish.

The allure of this prospect for the Female Sex was undeniable. They anticipated the chaos with delight. At home, they could eavesdrop on political secrets meant for men and issue commands in the name of a priestly Circle. Outdoors, the striking red-and-green combination would lead the populace into endless mistakes, granting Women the deference usually reserved for the highest class. The scandal that might befall the Circular Class, or the subversion of the Constitution, were concerns the Women ignored entirely. Even within the households of the Circles, the Women rallied behind the Universal Colour Bill.

The second objective of this diabolical plan was the gradual demoralization of the Circles themselves. Until now, the Nobility had maintained their pristine intellectual clarity, having grown up in colourless homes where the Sacred Art of Sight Recognition was cultivated. But the Irregular sought to stain this purity. By forcing the Hierarchy to submit to the pollution of Colour, he aimed to destroy their domestic environment. Once subjected to chromatic taint, every parental and childish Circle would begin to demoralize one another. The infant Circle, once faced with the problem of distinguishing Father from Mother amidst the confusing hues, would find its logical exercises corrupted by maternal imposture. The intellectual lustre of the Priestly Order would inevitably wane, paving the way for the total destruction of the Aristocratic Legislature and the final subversion of the privileged classes.

For three long years, the agitation for the Universal Colour Bill raged like a fever, and until the very final hour, it appeared that Anarchy would swallow Flatland whole. The state's defenses crumbled under the weight of internal rot. A massive levy of Polygons, fighting as mere private soldiers, was obliterated by a superior force of Isosceles Triangles, while the Squares and Pentagons stood frozen in neutral horror. Yet, the true tragedy lay within the sanctity of the home. Even among the noble Circles, political venom had curdled into domestic madness. Enraged by the opposition to the Bill, wives besieged their husbands with relentless pleas to surrender their principles; when these entreaties failed, the resulting fury descended into slaughter. Innocent children and their fathers were butchered in the heat of the moment, with the mothers perishing alongside them in the carnage. History records that during this triennial nightmare, no fewer than twenty-three Circles lost their lives to the poison of their own households.

The peril was absolute. It seemed the Priests faced only two paths: total submission or total extermination. Then, the tide turned abruptly, swept away by a singular, grotesque incident—a spectacle that statesmen should learn to anticipate, for such absurdities hold a disproportionate power over the populace's sympathies.

An Isosceles of the lowest caste, his intellect barely exceeding four degrees, stumbled into the colors of a tradesman he had robbed. Whether he painted himself or was painted remains a matter of debate, but the result was the same: he donned the twelve hues of a Dodecagon. Venturing into the Market Place, he approached a maiden, the orphaned daughter of a noble Polygon whom he had once courted in vain. Using a voice feigning nobility, aided by a string of improbable accidents and the staggering negligence of the bride's family, he orchestrated a fraudulent marriage. When the deception was revealed, the poor girl took her own life.

News of this catastrophe rippled from State to State, igniting a violent agitation among the Women. Sympathy for the victim, mingled with a terrifying fear that their sisters and daughters might face similar fates, transformed their perspective on the Colour Bill overnight. Many openly declared their conversion to the cause of the Bill; the rest required only a spark to follow suit. Seizing this moment, the Circles hastily convened an extraordinary Assembly of the States. Beyond the usual guard of Convicts, they ensured the presence of a large contingent of reactionary Women.

In the midst of an unprecedented crowd, Pantocyclus, the Chief Circle of that era, rose to find himself hissed and hooted by a hundred and twenty thousand Isosceles. Silence was only achieved when he announced a policy of Concession. Yielding to the will of the majority, he declared, the Hierarchy would accept the Colour Bill. The uproar instantly swelled into applause. He then invited Chromatistes, the leader of the Sedition, to the center of the hall to receive the submission of the Hierarchy on behalf of his followers. What followed was a speech of rhetorical mastery, delivered over nearly a day, defying any attempt at summary.

With a grave air of impartiality, Pantocyclus declared that since they were now committed to Reform, they must examine the perimeter of the subject—its defects as well as its advantages. Gradually, he introduced the dangers to the Tradesmen, the Professional Classes, and the Gentlemen, silencing the rising murmurs of the Isosceles by reminding them that despite these flaws, he was willing to accept the Bill if approved by the majority. It was evident that everyone, save the Isosceles, was moved by his words, leaning toward neutrality or aversion.

Turning to the Workmen, he asserted that their interests could not be ignored and that acceptance of the Bill required a full understanding of the consequences. Many, he noted, were on the verge of ascending to the class of Regular Triangles; others looked forward to distinctions their children might never achieve. This honorable ambition would now be sacrificed. With the universal adoption of Color, all distinctions would vanish. Regularity would be confused with Irregularity; development would yield to retrogression. The Workman would, within a few generations, be degraded to the level of the Military, or even the Convict Class. Political power would fall into the hands of the greatest number—the Criminal Classes—who were already more numerous than the Workmen and would soon outnumber all other classes combined when Nature's compensative laws were violated.

A subdued murmur of assent ran through the ranks of the Artisans. Chromatistes, alarmed, attempted to step forward to address them but found himself surrounded by guards, forced into silence. Meanwhile, the Chief Circle made a final, impassioned appeal to the Women. He cried that if the Colour Bill passed, no marriage would be safe, no woman's honor secure; fraud, deception, and hypocrisy would permeate every household, and domestic bliss would share the fate of the Constitution, rushing swiftly to perdition. "Sooner than this," he thundered, "come death!"

These words served as the preconcerted signal for action. The Isosceles Convicts fell upon and transfixed the wretched Chromatistes. The Regular Classes opened their ranks, making way for a band of Women who, under the direction of the Circles, moved backward, invisible and unerring, upon the unconscious soldiers. The Artisans, imitating their superiors, also opened their lines. Simultaneously, bands of Convicts occupied every entrance, forming an impenetrable phalanx.

The battle, or rather the carnage, was brief. Under the skillful generalship of the Circles, almost every charge by the Women proved fatal, and many extracted their stings uninjured, ready for a second slaughter. But no second blow was needed; the rabble of the Isosceles finished the work for themselves. Surprised, leaderless, attacked in front by invisible foes, and finding their exit blocked by the Convicts behind, they instantly, in their own fashion, lost all presence of mind and raised the cry of "treachery." This sealed their fate. Every Isosceles now perceived a foe in every other. In half an hour, not one of that vast multitude remained alive; the fragments of seven hundred thousand of the Criminal Class, slain by their own angles, attested to the triumph of Order.

The Circles did not delay in pushing their victory to its uttermost limit. The Working Men were spared but decimated. The Militia of the Equilaterals was immediately called out, and every Triangle suspected of irregularity on reasonable grounds was destroyed by Court Martial, bypassing the formality of exact measurement by the Social Board. Over the course of a year, the homes of the Military and Artisan classes were systematically inspected. During this period, every town, village, and hamlet was purged of the excess of the lower orders, a condition brought about by the neglect to pay the tribute of Criminals to the Schools and University, and by the violation of other natural laws of the Constitution. Thus, the balance of classes was restored.

Needless to say, the use of Color was henceforth abolished, and its possession strictly prohibited. Even the utterance of any word denoting Color, save by the Circles or qualified scientific teachers, was punished by severe penalties. Only in our University, within some of the highest and most esoteric classes—which I myself have never been privileged to attend—is it understood that the sparing use of Color is still sanctioned for illustrating the deeper problems of mathematics. But of this, I can speak only from hearsay.

Elsewhere in Flatland, Color is now non-existent. The art of creating it is known to only one living person: the Chief Circle for the time being. By him, it is handed down on his deathbed to none but his Successor. There is but one manufactory that produces it; and, lest the secret be betrayed, the Workmen are annually consumed, and fresh ones introduced. So great is the terror with which even now our Aristocracy looks back to the distant days of the agitation for the Universal Colour Bill.

It is finally time to leave behind these scattered notes on the mechanics of Flatland and turn to the true heart of this narrative: my initiation into the mysteries of Space. That is the subject at hand; everything preceding it serves only as a prologue.

Consequently, I must omit countless fascinating details regarding our mode of locomotion without feet, our methods of construction without hands, the meteorological patterns that prevent the north from drowning the south, the biology of our flora, the rhythm of our seasons, and the geometry of our script. These omissions are not born of forgetfulness, but of a deliberate economy of your time.

Yet, before unveiling the secrets of the third dimension, I feel compelled to address the bedrock of our society: the architects of our destiny, the objects of our near-worship. Need I specify that I speak of the Circles, or Priests?

Do not mistake my terminology for a mere label. In Flatland, a Priest is far more than a spiritual guide; he is the omnipotent administrator of every conceivable pursuit—trade, engineering, education, statecraft, and morality. They are the invisible causes behind every visible effect, directing the machinery of existence while remaining detached from its labor.

To the uninitiated, every Circle appears perfect. However, the educated know the truth: no Circle is truly a Circle, but a polygon with a vast number of infinitesimal sides. As the number of sides increases, the shape approaches perfection. A figure with three or four hundred sides is nearly indistinguishable from a true Circle to the human touch. Indeed, it *would* be difficult to detect the angles, were it not for a crucial societal taboo: recognition by feeling is forbidden among the elite. To "feel" a Circle is considered the height of insolence. This cultural aversion to tactile verification allows the highest circles to maintain their mystique, hiding the jagged reality of their perimeters. With an average perimeter of three feet, a circle with three hundred sides possesses edges barely the width of a human hair; those with six or seven hundred sides are smaller than the head of a pin from Spaceland. By convention, the Supreme Circle is assumed to possess ten thousand sides.

The ascent of the Circle lineage defies the natural laws governing lower polygons. While commoners gain only one side per generation, the Circle race operates under two antagonistic decrees. First, development accelerates with status; second, fertility plummets. In the homes of polygons nearing circularity, sons are rare, often nonexistent. Yet, when a son does appear, he may inherit five hundred or even six hundred sides, leaping generations in a single birth.

Furthermore, art intervenes to hasten evolution. Our physicians have mastered the delicate art of fracturing the tender sides of an infant polygon and resetting them with surgical precision. Though fraught with peril, this Neo-Therapeutic process can double a child's sides instantly, allowing a descendant to skip hundreds of generations of natural accumulation.

The cost of this ambition is staggering. Scarcely one in ten children survives the procedure. Yet, driven by an insatiable desire for nobility, parents of the upper polygonal classes routinely submit their firstborns to the Circular Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium within their first month of life. One year decides the fate of the child. Most return to the crowded cemeteries of the lost, their bodies buried beneath tombstones. But on rare occasions, a procession returns a survivor—not merely a polygon, but a Circle by courtesy. Such a miracle, however fleeting, fuels the desperate hope of countless families, who willingly sacrifice their children in the gamble for immortality.

The entire theological edifice of the Circles rests upon a single, immutable axiom: "Attend to your Configuration." Whether the discourse concerns politics, morality, or ecclesiastical hierarchy, the ultimate objective remains the refinement of individual and collective shape, with the perfection of the Circle standing as the supreme ideal to which all lesser forms aspire.

The Circles claim the historic merit of having eradicated the ancient heresy that conduct is a product of will, effort, training, or encouragement. Instead, they preach that Configuration is destiny. It was Pantocyclus—the great Circle who quelled the Colour Revolt—who first illuminated humanity with this truth: that one's nature is written in one's angles. If you are born an Isosceles with unequal sides, you are doomed to error unless corrected in the Isosceles Hospital; similarly, a Triangle, Square, or irregular Polygon born with a defect must seek the Regular Hospitals for cure, lest they end their days in the State Prison or at the executioner's blade. Under this doctrine, every fault, from minor misconduct to flagrant crime, is attributed solely to a deviation from perfect Regularity—perhaps caused by a jostle in the crowd, improper exercise, or a sudden shift in temperature causing a frame to shrink or expand. Consequently, Pantocyclus concluded that neither good nor bad conduct deserves praise or blame. Why praise a Square for defending a client when one should merely admire the precision of his right angles? Why blame a thieving Isosceles when the true tragedy is the incurable inequality of his sides?

While theoretically sound, this rigid dogma presents practical difficulties. In the courts, if a criminal pleads that his unevenness compels him to steal, the Magistrate replies that such a nuisance must be consumed to protect society. Yet, in domestic life, where death is not an option, the theory falters. I confess that when one of my Hexagonal grandsons excuses his disobedience by claiming a temperature shift has compromised his perimeter, I find myself unable to logically reject his plea, yet equally unable to practically accept it. To resolve this, I adopt the convenient assumption that a stern scolding possesses a latent power to strengthen a child's Configuration—a notion I hold without evidence. Fortunately, I am not alone; many high-ranking Circles acting as judges employ praise and blame as if these concepts were tangible realities, believing fervently that a human figure possesses the agency to choose between them.

The Circles systematically invert the natural order of parent-child relations found in Spaceland. Where Spaceland teaches children to honor their parents, Flatland teaches a man to honor his grandson, or failing that, his son. This "honor" is not indulgence, but a reverent subordination of parental interests to the welfare of posterity, ensuring the State's advancement.

However, the system's greatest vulnerability lies in its treatment of Women. To discourage irregular births, no woman with irregular ancestry is deemed a suitable partner for a man seeking social elevation. While male irregularity is a matter of visible measurement, female irregularity is invisible; thus, the State maintains strict pedigrees, and marriage without certification is forbidden. Ironically, the higher a man climbs the social scale, the less rigorous his scrutiny of a wife's lineage. An aspiring Isosceles avoids any woman with a single irregular ancestor to ensure an Equilateral son; a Square or Pentagon looks back only five hundred generations; but a Circle, proud of his potential to sire a Chief Circle, has been known to marry a woman with an irregular great-grandfather solely for her superior lustre or the enchanting quality of her low voice—a trait we prize highly in females.

These ill-judged unions often result in barrenness or a reduction in sides. Yet, the loss of a few sides in a developed Polygon is easily masked by operations in the Neo-Therapeutic Gymnasium, and the Circles are too complacent to view infertility as a sign of inferiority rather than a law of superior development. If unchecked, the gradual erosion of the Circular class could soon render the production of a Chief Circle impossible, collapsing the very Constitution of Flatland.

A more insidious danger, which I dare not openly propose a remedy for, concerns our relations with Women. Three centuries ago, the Chief Circle decreed that since women lack Reason but abound in Emotion, they must be denied mental education. They were barred from reading and taught only enough arithmetic to count the angles of their husbands and children. The result has been a generational decline in female intellectual power, a policy of enforced quietism that persists today.

I fear this policy, born of good intentions, has inflicted a heavier burden upon the Male Sex. We now live a bifurcated existence. With women, we speak of "love," "duty," "right," and "wrong"—irrational fictions designed to curb feminine exuberance. Among ourselves, however, we utilize a stark scientific vocabulary where "love" is the anticipation of benefits and "duty" is mere necessity. We outwardly display the utmost deference to women, leading them to believe they are adored as fervently as the Chief Circle himself; yet behind their backs, we regard them as little more than mindless organisms.

Our theology in the women's chambers differs entirely from our true theology. This dual training, requiring young males to unlearn the emotional language of their mothers before mastering the cold dialect of science, strains the developing intellect. I perceive a weakening in the grasp of mathematical truth among the youth compared to the robust minds of our ancestors three hundred years ago. My humble appeal to the highest Authorities is based on the enfeeblement of the Male intellect: if a woman were to secretly learn to read and transmit the secrets of logic to her sex, or if an infant male inadvertently revealed these truths to a mother, the consequences would be catastrophic. Therefore, I urge a reconsideration of the regulations regarding female education, lest the very foundation of our rational society crumbles under the weight of its own contradictions.

The final day of the 1999th year dawned bright and crisp, marking the commencement of the Long Vacation. After indulging my favorite pastime—Geometry—until the clock struck late, I retired to my chamber with a stubborn, unsolved theorem gnawing at my mind. Sleep claimed me quickly, and within the realm of dreams, I found myself transported to a spectacle of bewildering simplicity.

Before me stretched an infinite multitude of tiny straight lines, which I instinctively identified as women, interspersed with even smaller entities that shimmered like luminous points. They all moved back and forth along a single, rigid axis, maintaining a uniform velocity. Occasionally, a cacophony of confused chirping erupted from the crowd as they surged forward; yet whenever they halted, absolute silence descended upon the line.

Driven by curiosity, I approached one of the larger lines, whom I presumed to be a woman, and attempted to speak. She offered no response. Undeterred, I tried again, and a third time, only to be met with the same deafening indifference. My patience frayed under what I perceived as intolerable rudeness. I maneuvered my body until my mouth faced hers directly, blocking her path, and shouted, "Woman, what is the meaning of this chaotic throng, this strange twittering, and this monotonous oscillation along this single line?"

"I am no woman," came the reply from a voice that seemed to emanate from everywhere and nowhere. "I am the Monarch of this world. But tell me, stranger, how did you intrude upon my realm of Lineland?"

Apologizing profusely for startling His Royal Highness, I introduced myself as a visitor and begged for an account of his dominions. However, extracting meaningful information proved a Herculean task. The King, seemingly convinced that familiarity bred knowledge, assumed I was merely playing dumb. Through persistent questioning, I finally coaxed the following truths from him.

This poor, self-proclaimed ignorant monarch believed that the straight line he inhabited was not merely a road, but the entirety of the universe and space itself. Unable to perceive or move outside this line, he possessed no concept of anything beyond it. When I first addressed him, his confusion was palpable; the sounds reached him in a manner alien to his experience, leading him to believe he saw no man and heard a voice originating from my own intestines. Until I physically obstructed his path, he had neither seen me nor understood my origin. To him, everything outside his line was not empty space, but non-existence—a void where nothing existed at all.

His subjects—the small lines representing men and the points representing women—were equally imprisoned within this one-dimensional existence. Their entire horizon was reduced to a single point; they could see nothing but a point. Whether man, woman, or child, every entity appeared as a mere dot to the eye of a Linelander. Sex and age could only be discerned through the timbre of the voice. Furthermore, since every individual occupied the entire width of their universe, no one could move sideways to yield to another. Once neighbors, forever neighbors; their bond was as indissoluble as marriage in our world, lasting until death separated them.

Such a life, restricted to a single line of sight and motion, seemed to me a dreary existence. I was struck by the King's surprising vitality and cheerfulness. Wondering if conjugal bliss could flourish under such constraints, I hesitated before asking about his family's health. "My wives and children," he declared, "are well and happy."

Staggered by this claim—given that I had observed only men in his immediate vicinity—I dared to ask, "Pardon me, Your Majesty, but how can you possibly see or approach them when half a dozen individuals stand between you? Is proximity not required for marriage and procreation in Lineland?"

"How absurd!" the King retorted. "If that were true, the universe would be empty. No, neighborhood is unnecessary for the union of hearts, and the birth of children is too sacred to rely on chance. Since you choose to feign ignorance, I shall teach you as if you were a newborn Linelander. Know this: marriages are consummated solely through the faculty of sound and hearing."

"You know every man possesses two mouths or voices, as well as two eyes—a bass at one end and a tenor at the other," he continued, noting my inability to distinguish his tenor. "I deduce from this that you are not a man, but a feminine monstrosity with a bass voice and an uneducated ear."

"Proceed," I urged.

"Nature decrees that every man must wed two wives," the King explained. "Why two?" I asked. "Because," he cried, "how can a union be perfectly harmonious without combining the four voices in one: the bass and tenor of the man, and the soprano and contralto of the two women?"

"But what if a man prefers one wife or three?" I pressed.

"It is impossible," he insisted. "As inconceivable as two and one making five, or the human eye seeing a straight line."

He then described the Great Weekly Chorus. Once a week, a law of nature compelled all inhabitants to move with violent rhythm for the duration of a hundred and one counts. At the fifty-first pulsation, the entire population paused. It was in this suspended moment that marriages occurred. The adaptation of bass to treble and tenor to contralto was so exquisite that lovers, even twenty thousand leagues apart, recognized each other's destined notes instantly. Love pierced the barriers of distance, uniting the trio. This vocal marriage resulted in a threefold offspring—one male and two females—to maintain the balance of the sexes.

"Always threefold?" I asked. "Must one wife always bear twins?"

"Bass-voiced monstrosity! Yes," the King snapped. "How else could the balance be kept? Would you ignore the alphabet of Nature?"

He fell silent, furious, and it took considerable effort to calm him before he resumed his tale. He explained that not everyone found their match immediately. For many, courtship was a long, arduous process involving repeated attempts during the weekly chorus. If a wooer's voice harmonized with only one potential wife, or if the two women did not yet sing in perfect unison, nature provided a mechanism for correction. Each failed attempt induced the imperfect voices to subtly adjust, inching closer to harmony. Eventually, after countless trials, a day would arrive when the three distant lovers suddenly found themselves in exact resonance. Before they could fully comprehend the miracle, the wedded triplet was vocally embraced, and nature rejoiced in another marriage and three new lives.

The realization that the Monarch had been lost in reverie prompted me to attempt a descent from his celestial rapture back to the realm of empirical logic. I resolved to offer him glimpses of the fundamental truths governing our existence in Flatland. Thus, I initiated my discourse: "Your Royal Highness, by what faculty do you distinguish the forms and orientations of your subjects? Before entering your Kingdom, I observed through the sense of sight that your populace consists of Lines and Points, varying in magnitude—"

"You speak of an impossibility," the King interjected sharply. "You must have witnessed a hallucination. To discern the distinction between a Line and a Point via sight is, by the very nature of things, impossible. Such differentiation is achieved solely through the sense of hearing, and by this same mechanism, my own form is precisely determined. Behold—I am a Line, the longest in Lineland, extending over six inches of Space."

"Of Length," I ventured to clarify.

"Fool," he retorted. "Space *is* Length. Cease your interruptions, or I shall end this audience."

Apologizing, I persisted, yet he continued with disdain, "Since you remain impervious to reason, I shall demonstrate with your ears how my dual voices reveal my shape to my Wives, currently situated six thousand miles, seventy yards, two feet, and eight inches away—one to the North, the other to the South. Listen; I summon them."

He emitted a chirp, then proceeded with complacency: "My Wives, receiving the sound of one of my voices followed closely by the other, perceive that the second arrives after an interval during which sound traverses 6.457 inches. They infer that one of my mouths lies 6.457 inches farther from them than the other, thereby deducing my shape to be exactly 6.457 inches. Naturally, you understand they do not perform this calculation repeatedly upon hearing my voice; they established it once, prior to our marriage. Yet, they *could* perform it at any moment. Similarly, I can estimate the form of any male subject through the sense of sound."

"But how," I asked, "if a Man feigns a Woman's voice with one of his tones, or disguises his Southern voice so thoroughly it cannot be recognized as an echo of the Northern? Might such deceptions cause grave inconvenience? And possess you no means of verifying such frauds by commanding neighboring subjects to touch one another?"

This was, of course, a foolish query, for tactile sensation could not serve the purpose; however, I asked it deliberately to irritate the Monarch, and I succeeded perfectly.

"What!" he shrieked in horror. "Explain your meaning."

"Feel, touch, come into contact," I replied.

"If by FEELING you mean approaching so closely that no space remains between two individuals, know, Stranger, that this offense is punishable by death in my dominions. The reason is self-evident: the fragile form of a Woman, liable to shatter under such approximation, must be protected by the State. Since Women cannot be distinguished from Men by sight, the Law universally ordains that neither Man nor Woman shall approach so closely as to obliterate the interval between the approximator and the approximated."

"And indeed, what conceivable purpose would be served by this illegal and unnatural excess of approximation, which you term TOUCHING, when all ends of such a brutal and coarse process are attained more easily and accurately by the sense of hearing? Regarding your suggested danger of deception, it is non-existent: for the Voice is the essence of one's Being and cannot be altered at will. But suppose I possessed the power to pass through solid objects, penetrating my subjects one by one, even numbering a billion, verifying the size and distance of each by the sense of FEELING: how much time and energy would be wasted in this clumsy, inaccurate method! Wherefore, now, in a single moment of audition, I conduct the census and statistics—local, corporeal, mental, and spiritual—of every living being in Lineland. Hark! Only hark!"

Thus speaking, he paused, listening in ecstasy to a sound that seemed to me no better than the tiny chirping of an innumerable multitude of Lilliputian grasshoppers.

"Truly," I replied, "your sense of hearing serves you well, filling many deficiencies. Yet permit me to observe that your life in Lineland must be deplorably dull. To see nothing but a Point! Just before entering your kingdom, I saw you dancing from left to right, and then from right to left, with Seven Men and a Woman immediately to your left, and eight Men and two Women to your right. "It is correct," said the King, "so far as the numbers and sexes are concerned, though I know not what you mean by 'right' and 'left'. But I deny that you saw these things. "Not so," I replied. "Besides your motion of Northward and Southward, there exists another motion which I call from right to left."

**KING.** Exhibit to me, if you please, this motion from left to right.

**I.** Nay, I cannot do so unless you could step out of your Line altogether.

**KING.** Out of my Line? Do you mean out of the world? Out of Space?

**I.** Well, yes. Out of *YOUR* World. Out of *YOUR* Space. For your Space is not the true Space. True Space is a Plane; but your Space is merely a Line.

**KING.** If you cannot indicate this motion from left to right by moving within it, I beg you to describe it to me in words.

**I.** If you cannot distinguish your right side from your left, I fear no words of mine can clarify my meaning to you. Yet surely you cannot be ignorant of such a simple distinction.

**KING.** I do not in the least understand you.

**I.** Alas! How shall I make it clear? When you move straight ahead, does it not occasionally occur to you that you *COULD* move in some other way, turning your eye round to look in the direction towards which your side is now fronting? **KING.** Never. And what do you mean? How can a man's inside "front" in any direction? Or how can a man move in the direction of his inside?

**I.** Well then, since words cannot explain the matter, I shall resort to deed, and will move gradually out of Lineland in the direction I wish to indicate to you.

At the word, I began to move my body out of Lineland. I leave the realm of dreams to step firmly onto the ground of fact.

It was the final day of the 1999th year of our era. The rhythmic tapping of rain against the ceiling had long since heralded the descent of night. I sat in the quiet company of my wife, lost in contemplation of the year just passed, the century approaching, and the dawn of the new Millennium.

My four sons and two orphaned grandchildren had withdrawn to their respective chambers. My wife remained, keeping vigil as the old millennium faded and the new one began.

I was deeply absorbed in thought, replaying a conversation with my youngest grandson, a brilliant hexagon of exceptional promise and perfect angles. Only a few grains of sand remained in the hourglass. As I turned the glass northward for the last time of the old millennium, I muttered aloud, "The boy is a fool."

Instantly, a Presence filled the room, and a chilling breath swept through my very soul. "He is no such thing," cried my wife, "and you dishonor your own grandson by such slander." Both my wife and I heard the words, though she failed to grasp their significance. We both surged forward toward the source of the sound. Horror seized us when we beheld a Figure. At first glance, it resembled a woman viewed from the side, but a fleeting observation revealed that its extremities dissolved into dimness too rapidly to belong to the Female Sex. It might have been a circle, save that it altered its size in a manner impossible for any regular figure known to us.

My wife lacked my experience and the composure to analyze these anomalies. "There are none," I replied. "But why do you assume the stranger is a woman? I perceive by my Sight Recognition—"

"Oh, I have no patience with your Sight Recognition," she retorted. "'Feeling is believing,' and 'A straight line to the touch is worth a circle to the sight'"—proverbs beloved by the Frailer Sex in Flatland.

"Well," I said, fearing to provoke her further, "if it must be so, demand an introduction."

Assuming her most gracious demeanor, my wife advanced toward the Stranger. "Permit me, Madam, to feel and be felt by..." She recoiled abruptly. "Oh! It is not a woman, and there are no angles, not a single trace of one. Could I have so grievously offended a perfect Circle?"

In the dry, fogless air of my sitting-room, visual recognition was treacherous at close range. Overcome by panic, I lunged forward, shouting, "You must permit me, Sir—" and placed my hand upon him. My Wife had been correct. There was not a trace of an angle, no roughness, no inequality. I had never encountered a more perfect Circle. He remained still as I circled him, starting from his eye and returning to it. He was circular, flawless, and undeniably so.

**STRANGER:** Have you felt me sufficiently? Are you not introduced yet?

**I:** Most illustrious Sir, forgive my awkwardness. It stems not from ignorance of social custom, but from surprise and nervousness regarding this unexpected visit. I beseech you to keep my indiscretion secret, especially from my Wife. But before we proceed, would your Lordship deign to satisfy the curiosity of one who wishes to know whence you come?

**I:** Pardon me, my Lord, but are you not already in Space, along with your humble servant, even now?

**STRANGER:** Pooh! What do you know of Space? Define it.

**I:** Space, my Lord, is height and breadth indefinitely prolonged.

**STRANGER:** Exactly: you see you do not even know what Space is. You believe it consists of only Two Dimensions; but I have come to announce a Third—height, breadth, and length.

**I:** Your Lordship jests. We also speak of length and height, or breadth and thickness, thus denoting Two Dimensions by four names.

**STRANGER:** I came from it. It is up above and down below.

**I:** My Lord seems to imply it is Northward and Southward.

**I:** Pardon me, my Lord; a moment's inspection will convince you that I have a perfect luminary at the junction of two of my sides.

**STRANGER:** Yes: but to see into Space, you ought to have an eye not on your Perimeter, but on your side—that is, on what you would call your inside; but we in Spaceland call it your side.

Yet, her good sense far exceeded that of her sex; perceiving my unusual excitement, she did not argue but insisted I was ill and required repose. Glad of an excuse to retire to my chamber, I sought quiet to ponder the impossible. As drowsiness claimed me, I struggled to visualize the Third Dimension, specifically the construction of a Cube from a Square. The image remained hazy, but I recalled the crucial phrase: "Upward, and yet not Northward." Determined to let these words serve as my guiding clue, I repeated them mechanically, like a charm, until I sank into a sound, refreshing sleep.

In my slumber, I dreamed I stood once more beside the Sphere, whose lustrous hue signaled that his wrath had transformed into perfect placability. Together, we drifted toward a bright, infinitesimally small Point, which my Master directed my gaze upon. As we neared, a faint humming emanated from it—a sound akin to a Spaceland bluebottle, yet so subtle that even in the vacuum's stillness, it reached our ears only after we halted some twenty human diagonals away.

"Look yonder," said my Guide. "In Flatland thou hast lived; in Lineland thou hast received a vision; thou hast soared with me to the heights of Spaceland; now, to complete thy range of experience, I conduct thee downward to the lowest depth of existence: the realm of Pointland, the Abyss of No Dimensions."

"Behold this miserable creature," the Sphere continued. "That Point is a Being like ourselves, confined to the non-dimensional Gulf. He is his own world, his own universe; of anything else, he forms no conception. He knows not length, breadth, or height, for he has never experienced them. He has no cognizance of the number two, nor any thought of plurality; for he is his own One and All, being truly Nothing. Yet observe his perfect self-contentment. From this, learn that to be self-satisfied is to be vile and ignorant, and that to aspire is superior to blind, impotent happiness. Now, listen."

Silence fell, broken only by a tiny, low, monotonous tinkling rising from the buzzing creature—like a phonograph recording a single note. From it came the words: "Infinite beatitude of existence! It is; and there is none else beside It."

"What does this puny creature mean by 'It'?" I asked.

"He means himself," the Sphere replied. "Have you not noticed that babies and childish souls, unable to distinguish themselves from the world, speak of themselves in the third person? But hush!"

"It fills all Space," the Point continued, soliloquizing. "And what It fills, It is. What It thinks, It utters; what It utters, It hears. It is the Thinker, the Utterer, the Hearer, the Thought, the Word, the Audition; it is the One, and yet the All in All. Ah, the happiness! Ah, the happiness of Being!"

"Can you not startle this creature from its complacency?" I urged. "Reveal to it the narrow limitations of Pointland as you did to me, and lead it upward."

"That is no easy task," my Master said. "Try yourself."

Raising my voice to its utmost, I addressed the Point: "Silence, contemptible creature! You call yourself the All in All, yet you are the Nothing. Your so-called universe is a mere speck in a Line, and a Line is but a shadow compared to—"

"Hush, hush! You have said enough," the Sphere interrupted. "Listen now, and mark the effect of your harangue on the King of Pointland."

The Monarch's luster beamed brighter than ever upon hearing my words, proving his complacency remained unshaken. Hardly had I ceased when he resumed his strain: "Ah, the joy! Ah, the joy of Thought! What can It not achieve by thinking! Its own Thought returning to It, suggestive of Its disparagement, thereby enhancing Its happiness! Sweet rebellion stirred up to result in triumph! Ah, the divine creative power of the All in One! Ah, the joy, the joy of Being!"

"You see," my Teacher observed, "how little your words accomplished. So far as the Monarch understood them, he accepted them as his own—for he cannot conceive of any entity except himself—and he plumed himself upon the variety of 'Its Thought' as proof of creative power. Let us leave this God of Pointland to the ignorant fruition of his omnipresence and omniscience; nothing you or I can do can rescue him from his self-satisfaction."

As we floated gently back to Flatland, I heard the mild voice of my Companion drawing the moral of my vision, stimulating me to aspire and to teach others to do the same. He had confessed that he was initially angered by my ambition to soar above the Third Dimension, but since then, he had gained fresh insight and was no longer too proud to acknowledge his error to a pupil. He then proceeded to initiate me into mysteries even higher than those I had witnessed, showing me how to construct Extra-Solids from Solids, and Double Extra-Solids from Extra-Solids, all strictly according to analogy. These methods were so simple and easy that they were patent even to the Female Sex.

I awoke with a heart swelling with triumph, my mind already racing through the glorious campaign awaiting me. The Gospel of the Third Dimension was ready to be preached across Flatland, even to the rigid ranks of the Soldiers and the timid Women. My first convert would be my Wife.

But as I finalized this grand design, a hush fell upon the street outside, broken only by the booming voice of a herald. He read aloud the latest Resolution of the Council: a decree demanding the arrest, imprisonment, or execution of anyone who sought to pervert the minds of the populace with delusions or claimed revelations from another world.

A cold shiver ran through me. This danger could not be ignored. Prudence dictated that I abandon any mention of my Revelation entirely and rely solely on the path of Demonstration. After all, the proof seemed so starkly simple—"Upward, not Northward"—that discarding the mystical element should leave nothing lost. Yet, the phrase, which had felt as clear as arithmetic moments before my sleep, now seemed to lose its sharp edge.

My wife entered just then, and after exchanging a few polite, mundane words, I resolved not to burden her with the truth. My pentagonal sons were men of integrity and standing, renowned physicians, yet they lacked the mathematical rigor required for my purpose. Worse, their patriotism and reverence for the Circles far outweighed their love for me. If they discovered I was preaching sedition regarding the Third Dimension, they would surely hand me over to the Prefect.

The solution lay elsewhere. A young, docile hexagon with a mathematical bent would be the ideal student. Why not begin with my grandson? His earlier, innocent musings on the meaning of $3^3$ had already pleased the Sphere. Teaching a child would keep me safe; he knew nothing of the Council's edict, unlike my sons, whose loyalty to the state might override familial affection.

First, however, I had to quell my wife's curiosity regarding the mysterious interview with the Circle and my sudden appearance. I offered her a vague, plausible explanation—perhaps less consistent with the truth than my readers in Spaceland might wish—but she eventually accepted it, returning quietly to her domestic duties without pressing for details about the Third Dimension.

With her distracted, I summoned my grandson. To confess the truth, I felt the vivid images of my vision slipping away like a half-remembered dream, and I longed to test my ability to create a disciple before the memory faded completely.

When he entered, I locked the door. Sitting beside him with our mathematical tablets—what you would call lines—I proposed we resume yesterday's lesson. I reminded him how a point moving in one dimension creates a line, and how a line moving in two dimensions creates a square. Then, forcing a smile, I turned to him. "And now, you scamp," I said, "you tried to convince me that a square could move 'Upward, not Northward' to create another figure, a sort of extra square in three dimensions. Repeat that, you young rascal."

Outside, the herald's cry of "O yes! O yes!" echoed again, proclaiming the new law. My grandson, though young, possessed an intelligence rare for his age and was raised in absolute reverence for the Circles. He understood the gravity of the moment instantly. He waited until the proclamation faded, then burst into tears.

"Dear Grandpapa," he sobbed, "that was only my fun! I meant nothing by it. We didn't know about the new Law, and I'm sure I never mentioned the Third Dimension. And I certainly didn't say anything about 'Upward, not Northward'! That sounds like nonsense. How can something move upward without moving northward? Even a baby wouldn't be that silly. How absurd! Ha! ha! ha!"

"Not at all silly," I snapped, my patience fraying. "Here, take this square." I grabbed a movable model lying nearby. "Watch closely. I move it—not northward, but... yes, upward. That is to say, not northward, but somewhere else..." My explanation dissolved into confusion as I shook the square aimlessly, unable to visualize the movement in two dimensions.

My grandson erupted in laughter, louder than before. "You aren't teaching me, Grandpapa; you're just joking!" he declared, unlocking the door and fleeing the room. Thus ended my first, disastrous attempt to convert a pupil to the Gospel of Three Dimensions.

The cold rejection of my grandson did not extinguish my hope, nor did it silence my resolve. I realized that a mere slogan—"Upward, not Northward"—was insufficient to shatter the rigid walls of Flatlandian dogma. To truly liberate the mind, I needed proof, a visual architecture that could stand before the public eye. Thus, I turned to the written word, dedicating months of solitary confinement within my own thoughts to the composition of a treatise on the mysteries of the Third Dimension.

However, caution dictated my strategy. To evade the Law, I deliberately avoided the term "physical dimension." Instead, I spoke of a metaphysical realm I called "Thoughtland," a place where a figure could gaze downward upon Flatland, perceiving the interiors of all things simultaneously. In this theoretical space, I described a figure surrounded by six squares, containing eight terminal points—a Cube. Yet, as I wrote, I was plagued by a profound limitation: in Flatland, there are no canvases but lines, and no diagrams but lines. Everything exists on a single axis, distinguishable only by length and luminosity. Consequently, when I finally completed my work, titled *Through Flatland to Thoughtland*, I harbored deep doubts that my readers could ever decipher the geometry I had so laboriously constructed.

My life during this period was cast in shadow. Pleasure lost its flavor; every sight became a tantalizing temptation to treason, for I could not stop comparing the flat, deceptive reality of our world with the true, voluminous nature of things as I had witnessed them. I neglected my clients and my trade, consumed by the agony of trying to reproduce a vision that existed only in my mind. One day, nearly eleven months after my return from Spaceland, I attempted to visualize a Cube with my eyes closed. I failed. Though I eventually succeeded, I remained uncertain whether I had perfectly reconstructed the original form. This uncertainty deepened my melancholy, yet it also hardened my determination. I felt that I would gladly sacrifice my life for the Cause if it could produce even a single spark of conviction. But if I could not convince my grandson, how could I expect to persuade the highest Circles of the land?

Yet, my spirit often proved stronger than my prudence. I found myself slipping into dangerous utterances, labeled heterodox or treasonable by the authorities. Even among the elite Polygonal and Circular societies, I could not restrain myself from dropping phrases like "the eye that discerns the interiors" or "the all-seeing land." On rare occasions, I even let slip the forbidden terms "the Third and Fourth Dimensions." The breaking point came at a meeting of the Local Speculative Society, hosted at the Prefect's palace. An absurd paper had been read arguing that Providence had intentionally limited us to two dimensions and reserved omniscience solely for the Supreme. Overcome by fervor, I abandoned all pretense of fiction. I recounted my entire voyage with the Sphere, my journey through Space, and my return, detailing every sight and sound. I urged my listeners to cast aside their prejudices and embrace the Third Dimension.

Needless to say, I was immediately arrested and brought before the Council.

The next morning, standing in the very spot where the Sphere had once appeared to me, I was granted the floor to speak. However, the atmosphere was thick with foreboding. The President, noting a guard composed of high-angle Policemen (those with angles barely under 55 degrees), ordered them replaced by a lower class, barely two or three degrees above a line. I understood the implication instantly: I was to be executed or imprisoned, and my testimony erased by the simultaneous silencing of the witnesses. The President sought to substitute cheap victims for the expensive truth.

After my defense, the President, perhaps moved by my earnestness, posed two final questions: First, could I indicate the direction of "Upward, not Northward"? Second, could I describe a Cube using anything other than abstract enumeration of sides and angles?

"I can say nothing more," I declared, committing myself entirely to the Truth, trusting that its cause would ultimately prevail.

The President agreed with my sentiment. He sentenced me to perpetual imprisonment. He added that if the Truth intended for me to emerge and evangelize the world, the Truth itself would ensure that outcome. For now, I would face no unnecessary discomfort, barring escape attempts, and I would be occasionally permitted to visit my brother, who had already been sent to prison.

Seven years have passed. I remain a prisoner, isolated save for the company of my jailers and the weekly visits of my brother. He is a noble Square—just, sensible, cheerful, and kind. Yet, these interviews bring me the bitterest pain. He was present when the Sphere manifested in the Council Chamber; he saw the shifting cross-sections and heard the explanations given to the Circles. Still, not a single week has gone by in seven long years without me recounting my experience and describing the phenomena of Spaceland, yet my brother remains unconvinced. He has not grasped the nature of the Third Dimension and openly denies the existence of a Sphere.

I am utterly without converts. The millennial Revelation, it seems, has been made to me in vain. Prometheus ascended to Spaceland to bring fire to mortals, but I, a poor Flatland Prometheus, lie here in chains, bringing down nothing but silence to my countrymen.

Still, in my brighter moments, I cling to a hope: that these memoirs will somehow reach the minds of humanity in some higher dimension, stirring up a race of rebels who refuse to accept the limits of their reality. But darkness often returns. Heavy is the burden of knowing I cannot honestly confirm the exact shape of the Cube I once saw. In my nightly visions, the command "Upward, not Northward" haunts me like a soul-devouring Sphinx. There are seasons of mental weakness when Cubes and Spheres fade into the background of the impossible, when the Land of Three Dimensions seems as illusory as the Land of One or None. In these moments, even this prison wall, these tablets, and the solid realities of Flatland itself seem no better than the offspring of a diseased imagination, a baseless fabric of a dream.
