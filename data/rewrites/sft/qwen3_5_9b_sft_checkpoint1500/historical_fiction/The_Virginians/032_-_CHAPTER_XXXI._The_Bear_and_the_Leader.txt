To the discerning eye, the truth concerning Harry Warrington and his entanglement with Cattarina was plain as day. Yet, within the gossipy halls of Tunbridge Wells, a different narrative held sway, particularly among the older matrons who viewed the Virginian through a lens of suspicion. Madame de Bernstein, a woman of considerable stature and ancient lineage, remained steadfast in her conviction that her nephew was not merely dissipated but morally corrupt. To her, an "Old Roman" at heart, the notion that Harry might emulate the vices of Rome was a comforting certainty; she eagerly embraced every scrap of scandal regarding his alleged Lovelace-like exploits. While the reality showed him offering tea and music to the company with genuine gallantry, treating ladies of substance with kindness, Madame Bernstein's vigilance had begun to slacken. The "silly love affair" commenced at Castlewood seemed to fade from her mind, replaced by a self-absorbed focus on her own rheumatic twinges and the management of her social calendar. Like many of her generation, she possessed the wisdom of years, yet those years had softened her once-fierce instincts. The dragon of her youth, capable of tearing out a thousand eyes, now trailed a languid tail, her scales grown soft and her claws reduced to a feeble pat. She could no longer turn swiftly, nor did her venom prove deadly; she was content to crawl home to her cave, admiring how time had tamed the once-devastating forces of female malice.

If the citizens of Oakhurst, despite their collective skepticism, could not resist the tide of rumors painting Harry as a rogue, surely Madame Bernstein, a woman who had witnessed more evil than the entire Oakhurst family combined, would be immune? Alas, the atmosphere of Tunbridge Wells was thick with malice. Every elderly lady in her circle believed every tale spun about Mr. Warrington's debauchery. When the little dancer returned to London, the gossip mills whispered that it was because heartless Harry had abandoned her for some other paramour—a name, or perhaps six, that the society eagerly traded like currency. A century prior, such gatherings at springs like Epsom or Bath were the epicenters of a freer, albeit often reckless, existence where ancestors of all stripes mingled, danced, and gambled. This bad reputation served Madame Bernstein well, assuaging her conscience regarding Harry's passion for Lady Maria. She felt so relieved by the rumors of his wild extravagances that when the chaplain arrived to escort Lady Maria home, the Baroness showed no desire to part. Instead, she kept her niece close, regaling her with tales of her cousin's sins, whispering platitudes about "boys being boys," and plotting to secure a suitable, fortune-rich wife for Harry. Poor Maria endured these pleasantries with quiet fortitude, a necessary shield against the old woman's meddling.

Centuries ago, a certain French Duke and Marquis traversed Europe and America, leaving behind a memoir that posterity wisely ignores. Having played the role of Don Juan across continents, he catalogued the beauties who fell to his charm. For the descendants of the fashionable circles Harry inhabited, it would be a delightful irony to discover their ancestors adorning the pages of such a candid chronicler. During his travels in North America, this nobleman, accustomed to the ease of European conquest, found the colonial life strangely resistant. Unlike the elegant sieges he laid in Europe, he secured not a single victory in Pennsylvania or New England. Travelers of the time often lamented the rudeness and barbarism of the Americans, yet the truth, observed by the narrator, was that American gentlemen conversed with modesty and the women of the colonies lived lives of purity. Harry Warrington, possessing a native colonial modesty, carried this trait to England. Though surrounded by the raucous talk of pleasure-seekers, he remained largely silent, refusing to indulge in double entendres or boast of any triumphs over the fair sex. He appeared shy and awkward whenever others recounted their own conquests.

Mr. Sampson, the chaplain, had noted this reserve early on at Castlewood, where Harry grew visibly uneasy as his cousin Will shared his choice stories. Lord Castlewood had sternly rebuked his brother, commanding him to keep his jokes for the usher's table and avoid offending their kinsman. Thus, the phrase "Reverentia pueris"—reverence for children—became a guiding principle for Sampson, ensuring he did not use his cynicism to wound an innocent youth. Touched by Harry's gift of a horse, the chaplain felt a genuine warmth toward the lad. "You see, sir," Sampson mused, acknowledging his worldly nature, "I must do as the rest of the world does. *Video meliora, deteriora sequor*—I see better things, but follow worse. I have a little sister at school, and while I strive to keep a decent tongue when speaking with her, I trust I may apply similar restraint to you."

Sampson spoke highly of Harry to the Baroness, who delighted in the praise. She adored the boy's good looks, his manly courage, his ready blushes, his bright eyes, and his deep, youthful voice. His blend of shrewdness and simplicity constantly amused her; she would have grown weary of a clever or witty suitor, but Harry's unique nature charmed her. "We must find a good wife for him," she declared to Sampson, already envisioning a match of suitable age and fortune. Sampson, too, harbored personal anxieties and had a candidate of his own in mind. He hoped that if his efforts succeeded in marrying Harry off, Lord Castlewood would reward him with a benefice or provision, as hinted at previously regarding Lady Maria.

Their bond deepened quickly. Sampson was a man of high spirits, good appetite, and unpretentious humor. He avoided sanctimonious hypocrisy, shielding Harry from shocking outbreaks of levity, introducing him only to the "minor mysteries" of the world. To Harry, he was simply a jolly companion, eager for a drink, a cockfight, a shooting match, or a gallop across the commons. Yet, beneath this boisterous exterior, Sampson maintained a decent conversation, striving to amuse rather than mislead his young patron. He was a man of immense animal spirits and natural wit, an experienced "toad-eater"—a term for a professional flatterer who climbed the ladder of success by ingesting the ambitions of the powerful. In Harry's time, a gentleman's toad-eater openly courted his patron, called him "Sir," ran errands, and stood until bidden to sit. Sampson wore this role with pride, referring to Harry as his young Virginian patron, acting as his aide-de-camp, giving orders to tradesmen, and managing his social obligations with the flair of an *ami du prince*.

Returning from the coffee-house, Sampson expressed his shock at Harry's reputation. "What a reputation, sir, they have made for you in this place!" he cried. "Monsieur de Richelieu was nothing to you!"

Harry, confused, asked what he meant by Richelieu, noting he had never served in Minorca. Sampson explained the local frenzy surrounding the Widow Patcham and Mrs. Hooper, both of whom were allegedly desperate for attention from Mr. Warrington. It was the talk of the whole resort.

Indignant, Harry paced the room. "Who says so?" he demanded. "I should like to meet the man who dares say so, and confound the villain!"

Sampson laughed nervously, advising caution. "I should not like to show him to you; it might be the worse for him."

"It's a shame," Harry continued, his face flushed with anger, "to speak with such levity about the character of ladies or gentlemen. Do you know, in our country, a fellow's ears would not be safe? Just before I left home, three brothers shot down a man for speaking ill of their sister."

"Serves the villain right!" Sampson agreed.

"They have started this calumny here already," Harry insisted, referring to the rumors about Cattarina. "And they said the very same thing about my Lord March. Isn't it shameful?"

"Indeed it is," Sampson replied gravely.

"I don't know what I should do if these stories reached my mother's ears. It would break her heart. Why, a mere week ago, a military friend, Mr. Wolfe, told me of the horrible lies circulating. Good heavens! What do they think a gentleman of my name and country can be? A seducer of women? They might as well accuse me of horse-stealing."

Sampson chuckled, referencing the legend of the Turkish Sultan who received bushels of ears as gifts. "If you collected all the ears of those who spread scandal against you, you would fill a basket!"

"And so I would," Harry vowed, his temper flaring. "Any fellow who speaks against a lady or a gentleman of honor shall pay the price."

"If you go down to the Well, you'll find a harvest of them," Sampson noted, describing the high tide of detraction. "But I shall be sorry for anyone you mark, for you're a man of your word."

Agreeing to proceed to dinner and then to Lady Trumpington's tea, the two gentlemen departed.
