It was approximately eight o'clock on the morning following the tragedy. Mr. Nathaniel Burton Cupples stood upon the veranda of the Marlstone Hotel, his mind entirely occupied with the prospect of breakfast. For a man of his disposition, the term "thinking" was literal; he genuinely engaged in deliberation regarding every conscious act of his life whenever time permitted. He reflected that the previous day's excitement—the frantic energy surrounding the discovery of the deceased—had disrupted his natural appetite, leaving him significantly undernourished. Today, however, hunger gnawed at him after an hour of early rising. He resolved to indulge in a third slice of toast and an extra egg, adhering strictly to his usual routine otherwise. Any remaining caloric deficit could be addressed at lunch, a matter for future consideration.

Having settled this culinary strategy, Mr. Cupples devoted a few moments to appreciating the vista before summoning his order. Through the lens of a true connoisseur, he surveyed the rugged coastline where a massive, perforated rock jutted from the glassy sea, contrasting with the orderly splendor of the vast, sloping fields of pasture and woodland that climbed gently toward the distant moors. Mr. Cupples possessed a deep affection for landscapes.

He was a man of medium height and spare frame, nearing sixty. Though constitutionally delicate, he remained wiry and active for his age. A sparse, straggling beard and moustache failed to hide a thin yet kindly mouth; his eyes were keen and pleasant, framed by a sharp nose and narrow jaw that lent him a distinctly clerical appearance. This impression was reinforced by his plain dark attire and soft black hat. The overall effect was undeniably priestly. He was a man of extraordinary conscience, industry, and orderliness, lacking in imagination. His upbringing in his father's household—a home famously recruited via advertisements describing itself as a "serious family"—had forged a fortress of gloom from which he had escaped with two rare gifts: an inexhaustible kindness of heart and a capacity for innocent gaiety derived from nowhere near humor. Had he pursued a clerical career in an earlier era, he might have worn the scarlet hat. Instead, he was a respected member of the London Positivist Society, a retired banker, and a widower without issue. His austere yet contented life was spent largely among books and museum collections. His profound, patiently accumulated knowledge of various disconnected subjects had secured him a place in the quiet, dimly lit world of professors, curators, and research devotees, where he felt most at ease at their amiable, albeit uncheerful, dinner parties. His favorite author was Montaigne.

Just as Mr. Cupples concluded his meal at a small table on the veranda, a large motorcar turned onto the hotel drive. "Who is that?" he asked the waiter. "It's the manager," the young man replied listlessly. "He's been to meet a gentleman arriving by train."

The vehicle halted, and the porter rushed forward. Mr. Cupples let out a cry of delight as a tall, loosely built man, considerably younger than himself, stepped from the car and ascended the veranda, tossing his hat onto a chair. The newcomer's high-boned, Quixotic face bore a pleasant smile; his rough tweed suit, unkempt hair, and short moustache were tolerably disheveled.

"Cupples, by all that's miraculous!" the man exclaimed, lunging forward before Cupples could rise and grasping his hand in a firm grip. "My luck is finally turning today," the newcomer continued spasmodically. "This is the second stroke of fortune within an hour. How are you, my dearest friend? And pray, why are you here? Why sit amidst the ruins of that breakfast? Do you recall its former glory, or ponder its demise? I am truly glad to see you!"

"I was half-expecting you, Trent," Mr. Cupples replied, his face breaking into a warm smile. "You look splendid, my dear fellow. I shall tell you everything. But surely you haven't eaten yet. Would you care to join me at my table?"

"Delighted!" Trent said. "An enormous breakfast, accompanied by refined conversation and tears of recognition that refuse to dry. Could you ask young Siegfried to set a place for me while I freshen up? I won't be more than three minutes." He vanished into the hotel, and Mr. Cupples, after a brief pause, proceeded to the telephone in the porter's office.

Returning moments later, he found his friend already seated, pouring tea and displaying genuine interest in the menu choices. "I anticipate a difficult day ahead," Trent remarked, speaking with his characteristic jerky cadence. "I probably won't eat again until evening. You deduce why I'm here, don't you?"

"Undoubtedly," Mr. Cupples said. "You've descended to investigate the murder."

"That is a somewhat colorless phrasing," Trent corrected, dissecting a sole with precision. "I prefer to state that I have come in the capacity of an avenger of blood to hunt down the guilty and vindicate the honor of society. That is my trade. Families wait upon me at their private residences. By the way, Cupples, I've made a promising start already. Wait, and I'll explain." A silence ensued during which the newcomer ate rapidly and abstractedly while Mr. Cupples watched with evident happiness.

"The manager here," the tall man finally said, "possesses remarkable judgment. He is an admirer of mine and knows more about my finest cases than I do myself. The *Record* telegraphed last night announcing my arrival, and when I disembarked from the train at seven this morning, there he was awaiting me with a motorcar the size of a haystack. He is beside himself with joy at my presence. It is pure fame." Taking a sip of tea, he continued, "His first words were asking if I wished to view the corpse—he believed he could arrange it immediately. He is as keen as a razor. The body remains in Dr. Stock's surgery in the village, exactly as discovered. It is scheduled for autopsy this morning, hence my timely arrival. Well, he drove me straight to the doctor, providing full particulars en route. I was reasonably *au fait* by the time we arrived. I suppose the manager of an establishment like this holds some influence with the physician. Regardless, neither he nor the duty constable offered resistance, though the latter was careful to warn me against revealing my identity in the press."

"I viewed the body prior to its removal," Mr. Cupples noted. "Nothing remarkable struck me, save that the gunshot wound to the eye had scarcely disfigured the countenance or caused significant bleeding. The wrists, however, were scratched and bruised. I imagine your trained faculties discerned other suggestive details."

"Suggestive details, certainly; though I doubt they indicate much. They are merely peculiar. Consider the wrists: how did you perceive bruises and scratches there? Presumably, you encountered Manderson previously before the murder."

"Certainly," Mr. Cupples admitted.

"Did you ever observe his wrists?"

Mr. Cupples pondered. "No. Upon reflection, I recall that when I interviewed Manderson here, he was wearing stiff cuffs extending well over his hands."

"He always did," Trent confirmed. "My friend the manager attests to this. I pointed out to him that you overlooked the fact that no cuffs were visible; indeed, they had been pulled up inside the coat sleeves, much as yours would be if you hurried into a coat without adjusting your cuffs. That explained why you saw his wrists."

"Well, I find that suggestive," Mr. Cupples observed mildly. "One might infer, perhaps, that he hurried through his dressing upon waking."

"Yes, but did he? The manager stated exactly what you surmised. 'He was always a bit of a swell in his dress,' he told me, drawing the conclusion that when Manderson arose in that mysterious fashion before the house awoke and ventured into the grounds, he was in a tremendous rush. 'Observe his shoes,' he added to me: 'Mr. Manderson was invariably meticulous about his footwear. Yet, those shoelaces were tied hastily.' I concurred. 'And he left his false teeth in his room,' the manager reported. 'Doesn't that prove he was flustered and rushing?' I acknowledged it appeared so. But I countered, 'Consider this: if he was so pressed for time, why did he style his hair with such care? That parting is a work of art. Furthermore, why did he don such finery? He wore a complete ensemble of underclothing, studs in his shirt, sock-suspenders, a watch and chain, money, keys, and various items in his pockets. That is what I said to the manager. He could offer no explanation. Can you?"

Mr. Cupples considered. "Those facts might suggest he was hurried only at the final stages of dressing. Coat and shoes typically come last."

"But not false teeth. Ask anyone who wears them. Moreover, I've been informed he hadn't washed upon rising, which in a fastidious man suggests a violent hurry from the outset. Here is another anomaly: one of his waistcoat pockets was lined with wash-leather intended for his gold watch, yet he placed the watch in the pocket on the opposite side. Anyone accustomed to settled habits recognizes how odd this is. Essentially, there are signs of great agitation and haste alongside evidence of deliberate slowness. For now, I refrain from guessing.
