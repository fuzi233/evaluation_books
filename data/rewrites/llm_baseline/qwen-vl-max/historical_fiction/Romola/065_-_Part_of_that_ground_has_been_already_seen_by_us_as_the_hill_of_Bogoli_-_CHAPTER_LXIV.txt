Tito’s visit to San Marco had been announced in advance, and he was promptly conducted by Fra Niccolò, Savonarola’s secretary, up the winding staircase into the long, quiet corridors lined with cells. These halls, though austere in their simplicity, bore traces of divine artistry—Fra Angelico’s frescoes flickered here and there like fleeting glimpses of heaven, delicate as rainbows on dissolving clouds. In them, the Madonna sat crowned in radiant glory, and the Divine Child gazed forth with an eternal promise. Yet the beauty was rare, broken by stretches of bare walls and narrow windows that let in only a sliver of light, illuminating nothing but the hard pallets and crucifixes within each cell.

It was a time of rest in the monastery. Most of the monks were absent, and silence reigned. At the end of one corridor, behind a door leading to a small antechamber where the Prior often sat at his desk or received visitors, the light fell upon a single figure kneeling before a crucifix. The back of the man was turned, clad in the long white Dominican tunic and scapular. He could have been any ordinary friar—someone whose sins were no worse than idle thoughts during choir, or petty satisfaction when another monk spilled ink on his illuminated manuscripts. His aspirations might have been no higher than to climb safely into Paradise through prayer, fasting, and obedience.

But beneath this plain robe beat a heart far more complex, burdened by a consciousness beyond the reach of most men. Savonarola was not merely praying—he was wrestling. Though his lips moved in Latin words—“_Cor mundum crea in me_”—his mind was elsewhere, consumed by visions of the trap his enemies had laid for him. He had entered his cell, fallen to his knees, and begun to pray, seeking peace, seeking assurance that the decisions pressing upon him were not betraying God’s will. But the turmoil within would not be stilled.

Images flooded his mind: the fire in the Piazza, the crowd, the challenge to walk through flames unharmed. He had spoken of miracles before, declaring that God would reveal Himself in due time. To the Florentines, eager for resolution, now seemed that time. They saw it as proof of faith—not just in Fra Domenico, who was ready to enter the fire, but in Savonarola himself. Why did he hesitate?

The answer was not simple. Savonarola believed in miracles—yes, in the sense of divine interventions, of inward revelations, of prophetic inspiration. But the idea of walking through fire, tangible and immediate, pressed too heavily on his imagination. It demanded not only belief but action. And his nature, shaped by both intense emotion and sharp reason, could not reconcile the two. He felt the heat of the flames even in thought, the agony of flesh meeting flame—so vividly that he recoiled from the very notion. Even if he resolved to go, he knew he might falter at the last moment.

Yet he could not dismiss the expectations of his followers. They too desired the trial, not out of malice, but out of hope—a hope that the miracle would confirm their faith and silence their doubters. And he could not satisfy them. Not without betraying something deeper.

He had told himself that the trial must not be a personal test, but a divine one. Only if the Pope’s legate came, if ambassadors from every great power stood witness, and if the fate of Church reform hung on the outcome—then, perhaps, would he step forward. Until then, he reserved himself for greater duties. He could not abandon the chariot of his mission to wrestle with every challenger.

And yet—what if Fra Domenico went? What if the Franciscan, driven by fervor, stepped into the fire? Then the miracle might still happen. Or perhaps not. But the mere act of offering himself could become a sign, a token of divine favor. Still, Savonarola could not allow it to proceed unchecked. He would do all in his power to prevent it. The fuel could be gathered, the people assembled, the rituals performed—but the actual entry could be stopped. If necessary, he would ensure that Fra Domenico carried the consecrated Host into the flames. That, at least, might offer a sacred warrant for protection. Or better yet, the demand itself might provoke resistance, and thus end the trial before it began.

But none of this could be said aloud. He must appear willing, trusting in God’s will. This gap between inner truth and outward appearance troubled him deeply. It was not the simplicity he had preached, nor the purity he once sought. In the heat of the day, when voices roared and passions flared, how could one remember the quiet yearnings of dawn? How could one know whether he had betrayed them?

“O God,” he whispered, “it is for the sake of the people—for they are blind, and their faith depends on me. If I put on sackcloth and cast myself among ashes, who will take up the banner? Have I not been led by a path I did not know, to this work?”

His prayers faded. He remained on his knees, but his mind drifted to visions of Europe transformed—of councils summoned, of corrupt popes deposed, of Christendom renewed. The immediate pain of doubt dissolved in the glow of prophecy.

Then came a knock at the door.

Savonarola rose slowly, drew on his mantle as custom dictated, and met Tito with a calmness that masked the storm within. Tito bowed deeply, not kneeling, but offering a gesture of profound respect. Savonarola nodded quietly, gestured him to sit, and asked at once:

“Your business is weighty, my son—one that could not be entrusted to others?”

“Assuredly, father,” replied Tito. “I shall not waste your time with preliminaries. From Messer Domenico Mazzinghi I learned you might wish to use the next courier sent to France with despatches from the Ten. I beg your pardon if I overstep my bounds—but since Messer Domenico is away at his villa, I wished to inform you that a courier departs for Lyons at daybreak tomorrow.”

Savonarola’s face remained composed, but his eyes—always keen, always alert—dilated slightly, catching the light. Tito had seen this before, and he expected it. He busied himself adjusting his gold fibula, seeming patient, yet watchful.

In truth, Savonarola had anticipated this news. He had already written a letter to the Florentine ambassador in France, urging King Charles to summon a General Council to reform the Church and depose Pope Alexander VI, whom he deemed unworthy, elected through corruption and ruled by simony. This letter lay sealed in his desk. But Tito did not know its contents—only that something important was at stake.

“It is true,” Savonardo said calmly, “that I have letters I would send under safe cover. Our community has affairs in France—among them, a debt owed to Signor Philippe de Comines, for the Medici library we purchased. But I expect Messer Mazzinghi to return before evening. I may gain time by waiting.”

Tito inclined his head. “Indeed, reverend father, that would be wise—except that if Messer Domenico fails to return, I would need to come again late, or you would send the letters through your secretary. And I know you wish to avoid any suspicion of undue closeness between yourself and government officers.”

He spoke carefully, testing the waters. The more hesitant Savonarola appeared, the more certain Tito became of his guess.

Silence followed. Then, a faint flush crept into the Frate’s cheeks. He was holding back—excitement, anticipation, the weight of what was to come.

“It is likely Messer Domenico will return in time,” Tito said, rising as if to leave. “With your permission, I’ll take my leave. But since I may not speak with you again soon, I venture to confide—what is not yet known beyond the Ten—that I intend to resign my secretaryship and depart from Florence.”

Savonarola looked up, surprised.

“I find,” continued Tito, “that I have mistaken my calling. I left the life of letters for politics, but in Florence, one becomes only an instrument—unless one can shape policy. And I cannot. Also, my wife has been alienated from Florence since the events of August. I wish to join her.”

Savonarola nodded. “I understand. You plan to travel, then—to the courts of Europe, to universities? First to Hungary, where scholars are welcomed?”

“Yes, father. I shall leave within ten days. I have not hidden from you that I am not a religious enthusiast. My wife’s faith exceeds mine. But I recognize the justice and grandeur of your vision—of nations and the Church. If you would entrust me with a commission, I would honor it.”

“Stay,” said Savonarola. “When you leave, I will write to your wife—I care for her spiritual welfare, though she departed in anger. As for the letters to France…”

He rose and walked to his desk. He took out a folded letter, its address in his own precise hand, visible but unreadable to Tito. He placed it in a larger envelope, sealed it, and handed it to him.

“Pardon me, father,” Tito said quickly, “but unless you insist, I would rather not carry it. Messer Mazzinghi may return. Or Fra Niccolò can bring it to me later, when I deliver the other despatches.”

“No,” Savonarola said firmly. “I wish you to address this packet yourself. Your handwriting is better suited than my secretary’s.”

Tito sat down to write. Savonarola stood beside him, arms crossed, his face flushed, his lip trembling slightly.

When Tito finished, he rose to leave.

“Take it,” Savonarola said abruptly. “There is no point in delay. I do not wish Fra Niccolò to make unnecessary trips to the Palazzo.”

Tito accepted the letter. Savonarola stood motionless, breath held, tension radiating from him. There is a subtle force, a current, that flows from such passionate souls—when they are inwardly agitated, the air seems to grow still, heavy with unspoken meaning.

Tito bowed low and departed, the letter hidden beneath his cloak.

It reached the courier and left Florence. But before that, another message—encoded, secret—had already been dispatched by Tito’s own agents. It traveled through relays, carried by armed men loyal to Ludovico Sforza, Duke of Milan, who waited along the borders to intercept any correspondence that threatened his interests.
