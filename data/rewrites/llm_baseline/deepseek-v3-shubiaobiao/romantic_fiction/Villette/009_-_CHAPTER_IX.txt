My days were now full and purposeful. Between teaching and my own studies, there was scarcely a moment to spare—and I found satisfaction in this busy rhythm. It was good to feel myself progressing, honing my mind like a blade against the whetstone of labor. Experience, in all its varied shades, stretched before me. Villette was a city of many faces, and Madame Beck’s school mirrored its diversity: young countesses sat beside merchants’ daughters, their differences blurred by the Labassecourien pretense of equality. Yet nobility often revealed itself not in grace but in a finely-woven web of arrogance and deceit, while the bourgeoises, frank and warm, carried a sincerity their high-born peers lacked.

The native girls, too, had their own brand of falsehood—coarse and unapologetic. Lies slipped from their tongues as easily as breath, unburdened by conscience. Confession was a formality; a muttered *"J’ai menti plusieurs fois"* earned no more than an indifferent absolution. But let one skip mass or sneak a novel, and penance followed swiftly. Hypocrisy had its hierarchy.

At first, I navigated this terrain blindly. The early lessons were battles, fought on the trembling crust of rebellion. But I refused to be bested. Night after night, I lay awake, strategizing how to tame these unruly minds. Madame Beck was no ally—her loyalty was to popularity, never to justice. A teacher who begged her aid in quelling mutiny might as well pack her trunks. So I learned to rely on myself.

Force was useless. These girls responded only to patience, to a voice both steady and sly. Heavy demands of memory or logic they rejected outright. Where an English pupil would doggedly pursue understanding, a Labassecourienne would toss her book aside with a laugh: *"Dieu, que c’est difficile! Je n’en veux pas. Cela m’ennuie trop."* The trick was to soften the task, then strike with sarcasm—sharp but not bitter. They could endure mockery, even relish it, so long as it glittered with humor. Insults to their intellect stung but did not fester.

Gradually, fluency bridged the distance. The cleverest among them warmed to me—when I made their ears burn beneath their glossy hair, I knew I had won them. Bouquets appeared on my desk; in return, I sometimes walked with a chosen few during recreation. Once, unthinking, I criticized their casual falsehoods, declaring lies worse than skipped masses. The words were carried to pious ears. After that, an invisible barrier rose. Bouquets still came, but conversation died. If I sat in the garden, a teacher materialized at my side; Madame’s silent shoes glided ever at my back.

One girl, Isabelle, once sighed: *"Mademoiselle, what a pity you are a Protestant!"*

*"Certainly. Everyone knows it—and besides, the priest told me."* Then, sotto voce, she added: *"To ensure your salvation up there, they ought to burn you alive down here."* I laughed—there was nothing else to do.

Then there was Ginevra Fanshawe.

She arrived at the Rue Fossette days after I did, unsurprised to see me. If she possessed a single durable quality, it was her selfishness. She was not proud, merely indifferent, treating me as a convenient listener for her vapid grievances—the dull meals, the foreign inferiority of teachers and peers. I endured it, until repetition wore me thin and I snapped. She cried, accused me of abandoning her, but I held firm.

Her demands extended to labor. Her wardrobe, though elegant, was plagued by neglect. Hosiery piled on my desk for mending until I refused outright. Tears followed, then resignation.

Yet for all her faults—she was lovely. On Sunday mornings, draped in lilac silk, her fair curls spilling over white shoulders, she was radiant. Sundays were spent with friends in town, among them a certain admirer—*"Isidore"* (a name of her invention, his real one being, she hinted, unpleasing).

*"Do you love him?"* I once asked.

*"Comme cela,"* she shrugged. *"He is handsome, and adores me. It amuses me. Ça suffit."*

When I pressed whether her family would approve, she laughed. *"Oh, how dull you are! I don’t want to marry. I’m too young."*

*"About me, yes. But clever otherwise—so Mrs. Cholmondeley says. All I know is that he sighs in my presence and I twist him around my finger."*

Her extravagance soon troubled me. Though she claimed no debt, jewels and gloves appeared—gifts, I suspected, from Isidore. When I confronted her, she preened. *"He enjoys seeing me wear them. It’s enough for him—he’s only bourgeois."*

Her candor was as chilling as it was frank. *"He thinks me perfect—sensible, virtuous. It’s exhausting! With you, I needn’t pretend."*

Arrogance sealed her words. There was no reaching her.

That evening, she burst into my room, adorned for a ball—gowned, bejeweled, triumphant. *"Well? Do I look splendid?"*

She pouted. *"Don’t be sour. Admit it’s exquisite."*

*"Where did these come from?"* I touched a bracelet.

She laughed, twirling. *"I’d sooner dance with an officer than endure his sighs."*

There was no swaying her. As the portress called her name, she swept out, leaving the scent of roses and hypocrisy in her wake.

Days later, she confessed—not out of remorse, but to boast of her cleverness. *"I must go out, mustn’t I? Papa wishes me polished. And if Mrs. Cholmondeley won’t pay, and uncle mustn’t be burdened—why shouldn’t Isidore?"*

No guilt touched her. In her eyes, the world existed to serve her whims. And Isidore? Merely another toy, soon to be discarded.
