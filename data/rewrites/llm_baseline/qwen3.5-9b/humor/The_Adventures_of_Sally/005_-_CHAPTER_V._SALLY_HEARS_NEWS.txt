Sally had meticulously planned her arrival in New York. Her grand design was to check into the St. Regis Hotel immediately, immersing herself in the gilded luxury her fortune entitled her to enjoy before settling into a modest, yet comfortable apartment she intended to secure once she had the leisure to hunt it down. However, when the moment finally arrived and she stood directing the taxi driver at the dock, the whole scheme felt dreadfully cinematic—too much like a scene from a film. It simply wasn't time to sever ties with the boarding house that had been her home for three years until she actually secured her own place. Besides, the decent thing to do, if she wished to avoid branding herself in her own conscience as a "female Fillmore," was to return temporarily to Mrs. Meecher's admirable establishment and reunite with her old friends. After all, home is where the heart is, even if the pantry contains more prunes than a gourmet would deem prudent.

Perhaps it was the inevitable complacency born of knowing she was doing the right thing, or perhaps it was the thrilling anticipation of seeing Gerald Foster again after weeks of separation that made the familiar streets sparkle with brilliance as she drove through them. It was a perfect, crisp New York morning, painted in shades of azure sky and amber sunlight; even the ash cans seemed to possess a stimulating gleam. The streetcars were packed with cheerful souls bounding off to work; policemen directed traffic with jaunty affability; and the white-clad street cleaners performed their poetic duties with a quiet, undeniable relish. It was improbable that any of these citizens knew she had returned, yet somehow, they all behaved as though today were a special occasion.

The first discordant note in this overture of happiness was struck by Mrs. Meecher. After expressing her delight at Sally's return and confirming that her old room was still available, she dropped the bombshell: Gerald Foster had left town that very morning.

"Gone to Detroit, he has," announced Mrs. Meecher. "Miss Doland, too."

She paused briefly to deliver a caustic remark to the handyman, who was currently using Sally's trunk as a weapon to complain about the quality of the hallway wallpaper. Once the handyman had bumped his way up the stairs, Mrs. Meecher resumed.

"There's that play of his being tried out there, you know, on Monday," she continued. "They've been rehearsing ever since you left."

Sally felt a pang of disappointment, but the morning was so beautiful, and New York was so magnificent following the dull voyage across the ocean, that she refused to allow herself to be depressed without cause. After all, she could always go to Detroit tomorrow. It was comforting to have something to look forward to.

"Oh, is Elsa in the company?" Sally asked hopefully.

"Sure. And very good, too, I hear," Mrs. Meecher replied, keeping pace with the latest theatrical gossip. She was an ex-member of the profession herself, having starred in the first production of *Florodora*, though, unlike everyone else, she wasn't part of the original sextette.

"Mr. Faucitt was down to see a rehearsal," Mrs. Meecher explained, "and he said Miss Doland was fine. And he's not easy to please, as you know."

"How is Mr. Faucitt?" Sally asked.

Mrs. Meecher, not unwillingly—for she was a woman who thoroughly enjoyed the tragedies of life—made her second attempt to lower Sally's elevated spirits.

"Poor old gentleman, he ain't over and above well," she said. "Went to bed early last night with a headache, and this morning I stopped by to see him, and he doesn't look well. There's a lot of this Spanish influenza about. Might be that. Lots of people have been dying of it, if you believe what you see in the papers," Mrs. Meecher added buoyantly.

"Good gracious! You don't think..." Sally stammered.

"Well, he ain't turned black," Mrs. Meecher admitted with regret. "They say they turn black. If you believe what you see in the papers, that is. Of course, that may come later," she added with the air of someone convinced everything would eventually work out. "The doctor'll be in to see him pretty soon. He's quite happy. Toto's sitting with him."

Sally's concern deepened. Like everyone who had spent significant time in the house, she held strong opinions regarding Toto. This quadruped, who stained the reputation of the entire canine race by posing as a dog, was a small, woolly creature with a persistent, penetrating yap that was hard to endure in health and absolutely intolerable for a sick man. Her heart bled for Mr. Faucitt. Mrs. Meecher, conversely, maintained a faith in her little pet's amiability and soothing powers that seven years of close association had failed to shake. To her, with Toto on the spot, all possible measures for pampering the invalid had clearly been taken.

"I must go up and see him," cried Sally. "Poor old dear."

"Sure. You know his room. You can hear Toto talking to him now," said Mrs. Meecher complacently. "He wants a cracker, that's what he wants. Toto likes a cracker after breakfast."

As Sally entered the room, the invalid's eyes turned wearily toward the door. At the sight of Sally, they lit up with an incredulous rapture. Almost any intervention would have pleased Mr. Faucitt at that moment, for his little playmate had long outstayed any welcome he might originally have received; but for the caller to be his beloved Sally seemed to the old man something akin to a return of the Age of Miracles.

"Sally!"

"One moment. Here, Toto!"

Struck momentarily dumb by the sight of food, Toto had jumped off the bed and stood with his head cocked to one side, peering questioningly at the cracker. He was a suspicious dog, but he allowed himself to be lured into the passage. Upon reaching it, Sally threw the cracker down, slipped inside, and shut the door. Toto, after a couple of yaps—which may have been gratitude or baffled fury—trotted off downstairs, and Mr. Faucitt drew a deep, relieved breath.

"Sally, you come, as ever, as an angel of mercy. Our worthy Mrs. Meecher means well, and I yield to no man in my respect for her innate kindness of heart: but she errs in supposing that that thrice-damned whelp of hers is a combination of sick-nurse, soothing medicine, and a week at the seaside. She insisted on bringing him here. He was yapping then, as he was yapping when, with womanly resource which I cannot sufficiently praise, you decoyed him hence. And each yap went through me like hammer-strokes on sheeted tin. Sally, you stand alone among womankind. You shine like a good deed in a naughty world. When did you get back?"

"I've only just arrived in my hired barouche from the pier."

"And you came to see your old friend without delay? I am grateful and flattered, Sally, my dear."

"Of course I came to see you. Do you suppose that, when Mrs. Meecher told me you were sick, I just said 'Is that so?' and went on talking about the weather? Well, what do you mean by it? Frightening everybody. Poor old darling, do you feel very bad?"

"One thousand individual mice are nibbling the base of my spine, and I am conscious of a constant need of cooling refreshment. But what of that? Your presence is a tonic. Tell me, how did our Sally enjoy foreign travel?"

"Our Sally had the time of her life."

"Did you visit England?"

"Only passing through."

"How did it look?" asked Mr. Faucitt eagerly.

"Moist. Very moist."

"It would," said Mr. Faucitt indulgently. "I confess that, happy as I have been in this country, there are times when I miss those wonderful London days, when a sort of cozy brown mist hangs over the streets and the pavements ooze with a perspiration of mud and water, and you see through the haze the yellow glow of the Bodega lamps shining in the distance like harbor lights. Not," said Mr. Faucitt, "that I specify the Bodega to the exclusion of other and equally worthy hostelries. I have passed just as pleasant hours in Rule's and Short's. You missed something by not lingering in England, Sally."

"I know I did—pneumonia."

Mr. Faucitt shook his head reproachfully. "You are prejudiced, my dear. You would have enjoyed London if you had had the courage to brave its superficial gloom. Where did you spend your holiday? Paris?"

"Part of the time. And the rest of the while I was down by the sea. It was glorious. I don't think I would ever have come back if I hadn't had to. But, of course, I wanted to see you all again. And I wanted to be at the opening of Mr. Foster's play. Mrs. Meecher tells me you went to one of the rehearsals."

"I attended a dog-fight which I was informed was a rehearsal," said Mr. Faucitt severely. "There is no rehearsing nowadays."

"Oh dear! Was it as bad as all that?"
