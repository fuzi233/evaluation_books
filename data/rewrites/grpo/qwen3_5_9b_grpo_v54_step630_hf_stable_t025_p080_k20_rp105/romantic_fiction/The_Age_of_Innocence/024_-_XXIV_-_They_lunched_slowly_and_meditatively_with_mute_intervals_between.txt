They lunched slowly, the silence between their words heavy and deliberate, as if the air itself were holding its breath. The conversation ebbed and flowed, not in a steady stream, but in sudden rushes of speech followed by long, meditative pauses where speaking felt merely like an accompaniment to the deeper dialogue of their shared presence. Archer guarded his own life from her, not out of malice, but driven by a desperate hunger to hear every detail of hers; he leaned forward, his eyes fixed on her, while she rested her chin on her clasped hands, weaving a tapestry of the year and a half since they had crossed paths.

She spoke of her weariness with the polished veneer of New York society. The city had been kind, almost suffocatingly so, welcoming her back with open arms, yet the initial thrill of novelty had faded. She had found herself, in her own words, "too different" to truly care for the things that mattered to them. That realization had propelled her toward Washington, a place rumored to offer a kaleidoscope of opinions and people. She spoke of settling there, building a home for Medora, whose patience had finally worn thin under the weight of familial neglect and the looming specter of unwanted matrimony.

"But Dr. Carver," Archer interjected, his voice cutting through the quiet. "Aren't you afraid of him? I heard he's been staying at the Blenkers'."

A soft smile touched her lips. "The danger has passed. Dr. Carver is a brilliant man, but he seeks a wealthy wife to fund his grand designs. Medora is merely a convenient advertisement for his conversion."

"To what?" Archer pressed.

"To radical social experiments," she replied, her gaze drifting across the table. "But tell me, do these new ideas interest you more than the blind adherence to tradition that plagues our circle? It seems absurd to discover America only to replicate Europe. Do you think Christopher Columbus sailed the ocean blue just to attend the opera with the Selfridge Merrys?"

Archer's face flushed. "And Beaufort? Do you speak such truths to him?"

"I haven't seen him in ages," she admitted. "But I used to. And he understands."

"Exactly as I've always suspected," Archer said, his tone sharp with a mixture of jealousy and self-loathing. "You dislike us. You favor Beaufort precisely because he is so unlike us." He gestured to the sparse room, the stark white houses lining the shore, the empty beach. "We are dreadfully dull. Colorless. Lifeless. I wonder," he broke out, his voice rising, "why you do not return?"

Her eyes darkened, and he braced himself for an angry retort. Instead, she sat in profound silence, contemplating his question, and a cold fear seized him that she might answer that she wondered, too.

Finally, she spoke. "I believe it is because of you."

The confession hung in the air, delivered with a chilling dispassion that somehow stoked his vanity rather than cooled it. Archer turned crimson to the roots of his hair, paralyzed, terrified that any movement would shatter the fragile moment like a rare butterfly taking flight.

"At least," she continued, "it was you who taught me that beneath the dullness lie things so fine, so sensitive, so delicate that everything I once cherished in my other life now appears cheap by comparison. I struggle to explain," she furrowed her brow, "but it feels as though I never understood until now how much hardness, shabbiness, and baseness one must endure to pay for the most exquisite pleasures."

"Exquisite pleasures..." he felt the urge to retort, to point out the cost, but the pleading in her eyes silenced him.

"I wish," she went on, her voice dropping to a whisper, "to be perfectly honest with you, and with myself. For a long time, I have waited for this moment: to tell you how you have shaped me, what you have made of me."

Archer stared beneath his frowning brows, then laughed, a nervous, brittle sound. "And what do you think you have made of me?"

She paled. "Of you?"

"Yes," he insisted, his voice trembling slightly. "For I am the product of your making far more than you were ever of mine. I am the man who married one woman solely because another commanded him to."

Her pallor gave way to a fleeting flush. "I thought... you promised... you would not say such things today."

"Ah, how typical of a woman!" he exclaimed, masking his pain with sarcasm. "None of you can ever see a bad business through!"

She lowered her voice, her eyes searching his. "Is it a bad business—for May?"

He moved to the window, drumming his fingers against the glass, acutely aware of the wistful tenderness with which she had invoked her cousin's name.

"That is the burden we must always carry," she insisted, turning back to him. "By your own admission."

"My own admission?" he echoed, his eyes fixed blankly on the sea.

"Or if not," she pursued her thought with painful intensity, "if it is not worth sacrificing everything, missing out on everything, so that others might be spared disillusionment and misery—then everything I returned home for, everything that made my previous life seem so barren and poor because no one there acknowledged its value... all of it was a sham, a dream."

He turned from the window, his body still but his spirit shifting. "In that case," he concluded for her, "there is no earthly reason why you should not go back."

Her eyes clung to him with desperate intensity. "Oh, is there no reason?"

"Not if you staked your entire existence on the success of my marriage. My marriage," he said, his voice savage with frustration, "will not be a spectacle worthy of keeping you here." She remained silent, and he pressed on. "What is the point? You gave me my first glimpse of a real life, and at the very same moment, you asked me to continue a sham one. It is beyond human endurance—that is all."

"Oh, do not say that, when I am enduring it!" she burst out, tears welling in her eyes.

Her arms dropped to the table, and she sat with her face exposed to his gaze, vulnerable as if stripped bare, revealing the soul behind the eyes. Archer stood dumbfounded, overwhelmed by the sudden revelation.

"You too... oh, all this time, you too?"

In answer, she let the tears spill over her lashes, tracing slow, silent paths down her cheeks.

Half the room separated them, yet neither moved. Archer felt a strange indifference to her physical presence; he would not have noticed her unless one of the hands she had thrown onto the table had caught his eye, just as it had in the small house on Twenty-third Street, where he had watched it to avoid looking at her face. Now, his imagination spun around that hand as if it were the edge of a vortex, yet he made no effort to cross the distance. He knew the love that feeds on caresses and demands them, but this passion, burning closer than bone, refused to be satisfied superficially. His greatest terror was doing anything that might erase the sound and impact of her words; his only thought was that he should never again feel quite alone.

But soon, a crushing sense of waste and ruin overtook him. There they were, close together, safe and enclosed, yet chained to separate destinies that might as well have placed them worlds apart.

"What is the use," he exploded, a great, hopeless cry rising in his throat, "when you will go back? How on earth can I keep you?"

She sat motionless, her lids lowered. "Oh—I shall not go yet!"

"Not yet? Then some time? A time you already foresee?"

At that, she raised her clearest eyes. "I promise you: not as long as you hold out. Not as long as we can look straight at each other like this."

He sank into his chair. What her answer truly conveyed was: *If you lift a finger, you will drive me back—to all the abominations you know, and all the temptations you half-guess.* He understood it with crystal clarity, and the thought anchored him to his side of the table in a state of moved, sacred submission.

"What a life for you—" he groaned.

"Oh—as long as it is a part of yours."

"And mine a part of yours?"

She nodded.

"And that is to be all—for either of us?"

"Well; it *is* all, isn't it?"

At that, he sprang up, forgetting everything save the sweetness of her face. She rose as well, not to meet him or flee, but quietly, as though the hardest part of the task was done and she merely waited. So quietly that, as he approached, her outstretched hands acted not as a barrier but as a guide. They fell into his, while her arms, extended but not rigid, kept him at a distance that allowed her surrendered face to speak the rest.

They may have stood thus for a long time, or only a few moments; but it was sufficient for her silence to convey everything she had to say, and for him to realize that only one thing mattered. He must do nothing to make this meeting their last; he must leave their future in her care, asking only that she hold fast to it.

"Don't—do not be unhappy," she said, her voice breaking as she withdrew her hands.

He answered, "You won't go back—you won't go back?" as if it were the single possibility he could not bear.

"I won't go back," she said. Turning away, she opened the door and led the way into the public dining room.

Outside, strident schoolteachers gathered their belongings for a straggling flight to the wharf. Across the beach lay the white steamboat at the pier, and over the sunlit waters, Boston loomed in a line of hazy promise.
