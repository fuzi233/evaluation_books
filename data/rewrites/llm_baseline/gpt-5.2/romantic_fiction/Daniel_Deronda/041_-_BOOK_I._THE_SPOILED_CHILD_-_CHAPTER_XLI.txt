The evening after he had left Mordecai—left him with that fever of conviction still warming the very air about the bridge—Deronda found that no later hour, no change of coat, no well-lit room, could quite disperse the encounter from his nerves. He dressed for dinner as custom required; the white tie lay cool against his throat like a bandage; the house moved with its usual soft mechanics—doors shutting with cushioned certainty, the subdued clink of glass, the obedient hush that wealth trains into walls. Yet under that cultivated quiet there ran another sound, remembered and sharper: Mordecai’s voice, with its thin insistence, a note that seemed to vibrate in him long after the speaker had been left behind.

Deronda’s mind, always quick to feel and then to question the feeling, turned upon itself with the same persistence as a tongue returns to a flavor it cannot name. He was alarmed—not at Mordecai’s poverty, nor at his cough, nor at the extravagant shape of his hope, but at the fact that he, Deronda, had been half mastered by another man’s certainty. It is easy to be moved and call it compassion; harder to discover, when one is alone again, that something like consent had begun to kindle under the pity, as if the stranger’s need had found a private hollow in him where it could lodge and echo.

Part of him drew back from a cowardice he despised: the fear of being laughed at by the people who took their own shallowness for wit. There were circles in which a grave emotion was a social indiscretion, and any seriousness about one’s own life a kind of vanity. Deronda had always shrunk from that modish obtuseness. Why should he be ashamed because he still lived among men who dined, and talked, and wore faultless linen, as if history were a far-off theatre and the present only a drawing-room? If the same incident were written in an old chronicle—Rome, or Alexandria, or Jerusalem—if some young man, dissatisfied with his neutral existence, had been caught into a new fellowship by a fervent voice, Deronda knew he would have called it natural, even sacred. The centuries lent dignity by mere distance; they did not alter the substance of the human hunger. Was he to treat his own hour as trivial because it was noisy with carriages and trimmed with etiquette?

And yet—another recoil came with equal force. He feared, too, the easy slavery of contagion: to be hurried by reverential pity into a path dimly seen, merely because a suffering soul clutched at him as at a staff. He repeated to himself, deliberately, the plain worldly verdict which Sir Hugo would have delivered with that good-humored sharpness that made skepticism sound like common sense.

“A consumptive Jew,” Sir Hugo would say—Deronda could hear the tone—“possessed by a fanaticism made more intense by obstacles and by the sense of death at his elbow, has fixed on you as the body into which he would pour his own visionary spirit. Odd in form, but not uncommon in substance. There are plenty of men who are sure they have the key of the universe—mathematical, metaphysical, moral. One believes all physicists conspire to keep his discovery locked up; another is certain that with one hair’s-breadth of alteration he has fitted the old wards of philosophy at last. They lie in wait for the man who must hear them; they carry manuscripts no printer wants; they talk as if fate had appointed an audience. It is right to be kind—indulge him a little, ease his last days if you can—but do not imagine his notions are worth what he thinks them.”

Deronda did not refuse those whisperings; he even gave them their clearest wording, as if he must be just to doubt in order to be just to belief. He had been forced before to face that troublesome family-likeness among heirs of enthusiasm—prophets and dreamers, benefactors and deluded inventors. The same heat that makes one man Columbus may make another the pursuer of perpetual motion. If you strip the grandest life down to bare qualities—immovable conviction in face of hissing incredulity—you can put Galileo beside a charlatan and find the statement fit them both. A mind anxious to be fair could not rest satisfied with such tests. The only safeguard against giving the hemlock to the wrong man, or banishing truth as if it were a nuisance, was the difficult faculty of understanding the matter on which conviction fastened, and the still rarer fellowship with human travail that prevents one from scanning deep experience lightly.

“Let the ages judge,” people say—yet the ages are made out of separate breasts making separate judgments, each one a beginning. Even the making of a steam-engine required that kind of justice in some single head before it could become a common thing in the world; Watt’s thought would have remained a thought if no one had trusted it, tested it, suffered for it. Deronda’s thinking, long trained in that direction, saved him from contemptuous prejudgment. Even if Mordecai’s claim had been no more than the plaint of an eager spirit, Deronda’s reverence for the struggles of men would have made him listen. But Mordecai’s claim was not merely, “Understand me.” It was—strangely, urgently—“Be what I need you to be.”

It was that which turned sympathy into a weight on conscience. Deronda’s conscience was not the clean, hard mechanism some men carry, clicking out judgments by a fixed inner law. It was a voice made of many sensibilities, and those sensibilities had been stretched by the habit—begun early and never abandoned—of imagining himself into lives unlike his own. Mordecai, with the rash right of a soul in extremity, demanded more than kindness: “You must believe my beliefs—hope my hopes—see the vision I point to—behold a glory where I behold it.” Taken as an obligation, it was preposterous. To seem to accept it would have been dishonest. Deronda, in remembering the agitation of the moment, felt grateful that even in his pity he had not let himself be driven into false concessions.

And the claim hung upon a supposition which might be, and most likely was, discordant with fact: that he, Deronda, was of Jewish blood. What appeal could be more hypothetical? Yet his whole life had been threaded with one hypothesis that had bitten into him like a secret tooth: since he was thirteen he had associated the deepest experience of affection, resentment, gratitude, and shame with the supposition that Sir Hugo was his father. That unproved belief had taught him passionate self-command; by its light he had learned to subdue feelings and to cherish them; he had lived in suspense with a conscience that could not rest. He was well used to motives that sprang from conceptions which might one day be overturned; well used to waiting for a revelation that would change the duties belonging to him. Suspense, when it stirred scruple and imagination together, was almost his native climate.

Now, then—suppose this other supposition were a foreshadowing of discovery. Suppose Mordecai’s belief about him proved true, and Mordecai’s ideas made a real conquest over his own convictions. It was not impossible that a mind like Mordecai’s—burning, organizing, intense—might give to Deronda the very shape of duty he had long felt as fragments within him, like broken sculpture that certifies beauty without revealing the whole form. That possibility rose before him with a tremulous attraction, and he found himself beginning to defend it against the mockery he could already foresee.

If the influence had come from an honored professor, an accepted philosopher, a man enthroned in a chair of learning with the sanction of printed name and public homage, would receptiveness have been laughed at? Only by those who think it weakness to be obliged for an idea—who prefer to hint that they always held implicitly, and in a better form, whatever another has made explicit. But what was there, after all, but vulgarity in deciding beforehand that a poor Jewish workman met perhaps on the sanded floor of the Hand and Banner could not contain any spiritual force worth discipleship to a white-handed gentleman? The world reads souls by their hands, and is wrong on both sides: an emperor once dismissed the descendants of David because their palms were those of laborers; a rabbi at Rome believed the Messiah would be found among the destitute. Outward signs are treacherous. Poverty is no guarantee of inspiration; neither is comfort. Yet inspiration has often worn poor clothes, and to dismiss it because of them is only another kind of blindness.

A more plausible objection lay in Mordecai’s visionary excitability—his habit of turning wishes into overwhelming impressions, of reading events as fulfillments. Could such a temperament keep that wise estimate of consequences which guards even noble motives from fatal error? And yet—Deronda could not honestly conclude beforehand. There are natures in which a just estimate of consequence is fused within the fire of belief, not melted away by it—belief that does not merely foresee consequences but helps to make them. Even strict science, with all its measurements, has needed a forecasting ardor—a faith in its preconception strong enough to outlast failures of experiment. And where human motives and actions are concerned, passionate belief has an efficacy that can resemble proof: it may happen first in one soul, and later become the habit of many. Mordecai’s excitement might be the mark of delusion; it might also be the fevered edge of a truth struggling to live.

Besides, if he demanded “strict reasoning” as a credential, would that secure safety? The driest argument has its hallucinations. Men dream in demonstrations; they build an illusory world out of axioms and definitions, then shut out fact with a triumphant Q.E.D. No formula will save mortals from mistake about matter imperfectly apprehended. If an unemotional intellect can carry a man into a mathematical dreamland where nothing is but what is not, why may not an emotional intellect absorb into its vision some portion of what will one day be? Perhaps comprehensive life feeds theory with new material, as an artist’s sensibility seizes combinations which science later explains and justifies. Deronda could not trust presumptions, either way. Human thinking is a makeshift, whether in the sum of it or in each separate mind that helps to make the sum.

Columbus had superstitions about himself, and argued poorly in places; yet he held sound physical conceptions and had the passionate patience of genius to make them tell on mankind. The world now speaks contemptuously of those who were deaf to him. Deronda felt the sting of that example. “My contempt for them,” he said inwardly, “binds me to see that I do not adopt their mistake on a small scale—to make myself deaf by assuming there can be no momentous relation between this Jew and me simply because he has clad it in illusory notions.”

For—whatever Mordecai believed about the way they had met, Deronda could trace the visible chain of links. If he had not found Mirah, it is likely he would never have begun to care especially for the Jews; certainly he would not have lingered in that search after an Ezra Cohen which had made him pause at Ram’s book-shop and ask the price of the Maimon. Mordecai, on his side, had long nursed a vision of a disciple; he saw Deronda by that light; Deronda fitted the longing image. Mordecai took him for one of his race. Suppose—against probability—that this impression should be proved true. Suppose Deronda should one day share the ideas to which Mordecai gave his last breath. That was what concerned the shape their meeting might give to his life.

But if the issue should be different—if there were nothing to share, nothing but illusion? Then something painful lay in wait. He would almost inevitably become the active cause of Mordecai’s crushing disappointment. The thought made his chest feel tight, as if the air had thickened. He feared that no tenderness of his could soften that suffering. And he asked himself, with a kind of reluctant honesty, whether the alternative—that he should not disappoint Mordecai—would be less painful to him.

Here he wavered; and he knew why. Lately there had been feelings at work in him that had altered the reluctance he once had to the notion of being a Jew. Call it romance if you will: that young spirit of adventure which has sent generations of youths to seek the hidden tokens of their birth, and with them an inheritance of tasks, began to quiver in him at the bare possibility that his path might suddenly widen into something ancient and appointed—something that joined thought to action, private life to the long memory of a people. Yet he could not let possibility harden into belief. The conviction that his father was an Englishman only grew firmer under the weak assaults of unwarranted doubt; and he would not say, even to himself, “I should be glad” if some hour came when that conviction was declared a delusion. His lifelong affection for Sir Hugo—stronger than his resentment—made him shrink from wishing for any truth that would wound that bond, even if it also freed him.

Which way the truth lay, he repeated to himself what he had said to Mordecai: he could not, without farther reason, undertake to hasten discovery. Indeed, the longer he pondered, the more he was tempted to cherish uncertainty for the present, as one might keep a flame low to prevent the sudden flare that consumes all. If continued intercourse revealed only illusions in what he was expected to share, the lack of valid evidence that he was a Jew might spare Mordecai the worst shock—the shock of a refused fraternity. Perhaps, even, it might be justifiable to use that uncertainty to keep Mordecai in suspense long enough to accept the offices of friendship Deronda yearned to press on him—help more practical than discipleship, kindness that could be given without lying.

These were the thoughts that occupied him through the interval of four days before he could fulfill his promise to call for Mordecai at Ezra Cohen’s. Sir Hugo’s demands on him often lasted so late that each evening expedition to Holborn was put out of the question; and meanwhile Deronda moved through the ordered domestic hours—voices softened by carpets, laughter trained to the right pitch, the faint percussion of knives and forks like a courteous rainfall—carrying within him another sound, the ragged, earnest cadence of a man who spoke as if the last crumb of his life must be made to nourish a future he would not live to see.
