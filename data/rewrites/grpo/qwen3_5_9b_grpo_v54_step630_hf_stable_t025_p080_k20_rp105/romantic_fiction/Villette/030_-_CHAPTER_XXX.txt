Do not be too quick to judge, nor assume with premature charity that from that day M. Paul underwent a transformation into a man of easy company, stripped of his capacity to radiate discomfort. No, he remained a creature of small stature and unreasonable moods. When his nerves were frayed, as they often were, he descended into acute irritability. Furthermore, his veins ran dark with a livid tincture of belladonna—the essence of jealousy. I speak not of the tender, heart-warming variety, but of that sterner, narrower sentiment rooted in the mind.

Watching him knit his brow or protrude his lip at my exercises—which he delighted in faulting, finding a knot of blunders sweeter to him than a cluster of nuts—I often thought, and still think, that he bore a striking resemblance to Napoleon Bonaparte. In a shameless disregard for magnanimity, he mirrored the Great Emperor. M. Paul would have quarreled with twenty learned women; he would have unashamedly engaged in petty bickering with an entire capital of coteries, never troubling himself about the loss of dignity. He would have exiled fifty Madame de Staëls if they had annoyed, offended, or outrivalled him.

I recall vividly a heated episode involving a certain Madame Panache, a lady temporarily hired by Madame Beck to teach history. She was clever, possessing a vast knowledge and the art of leveraging it with words and confidence. Her appearance was undeniably fine, yet her robust, ample figure and bustling, demonstrative presence clashed with the fastidious tastes of M. Paul. The sound of her voice echoing through the *carré* would unsettle him; her long, striding walk along the corridor would often cause him to snatch up his papers and flee instantly.

One day, with malicious intent, he intruded upon her class. He quickly discerned that her method differed from his own cherished plan. With little ceremony and less courtesy, he pointed out what he termed her errors. Expecting submission, he instead met acrid opposition and a sharp reprimand for his unjustifiable interference. Rather than withdrawing with dignity, he threw down the gauntlet. Madame Panache, bellicose as Penthesilea, picked it up immediately. She snapped her fingers in the intermeddler's face and rushed upon him with a storm of words. M. Emanuel was eloquent, but Madame Panache was voluble. A fierce antagonism ensued. Instead of laughing at his fair foe with her sore *amour-propre*, M. Paul detested her with intense seriousness. He honored her with his earnest fury, pursuing her vindictively and implacably, refusing to rest peacefully in his bed, to enjoy his meals, or to savor his cigar until she was rooted out of the establishment. The Professor conquered, yet the laurels of this victory did not grace his temples. Once I hinted as much. To my surprise, he admitted I might be right, averring that when confronted with men or women of coarse, self-complacent quality like Madame Panache, he had no control over his passions; an unspeakable aversion compelled him to a war of extermination.

Three months later, hearing that his vanquished foe faced reverses and distress, he forgot his hatred. Active in both good and evil, he moved heaven and earth to find her employment. When she came to thank him, the old voice, the old forward manner, acted upon him so strongly that within ten minutes he started up and bowed her—or rather, himself—out of the room in a transport of nervous irritation.

To pursue an audacious parallel: in his love of power and eager grasp for supremacy, M. Emanuel was like Bonaparte. He was a man not always to be submitted to. Sometimes it was necessary to resist; it was right to stand still, look up into his eyes, and tell him that his requirements exceeded reason and his absolutism verged on tyranny.

The first developments of talent within his range excited and disturbed him. He watched its struggle into life with a scowl, holding back his hand, perhaps saying, "Come on if you have strength," but refusing to aid the birth. When the peril of the first conflict was over, when he saw the lungs expand and the heart beat, he did not yet offer to foster. "Prove yourself true ere I cherish you" was his ordinance. How difficult he made that proof! What thorns, flints, and briers he strewn in the path of feet not inured to rough travel! He followed footprints marked sometimes in blood, holding the austerest police-watch over the pain-pressed pilgrim. And when at last he allowed a rest, he opened those same lids wide with a pitiless finger and thumb, gazing deep into the brain and heart to search for Vanity, Pride, or Falsehood. If he finally let the neophyte sleep, it was but a moment; he woke him suddenly to apply new tests, sending him on irksome errands when he was staggering with weariness. Only when every severest test had been applied and endured, when the most corrosive acid had failed to tarnish the ore, did he admit it genuine, stamping it with his deep brand of approval.

I speak not ignorant of these evils. Until the end of the previous chapter, M. Paul had not been my professor. About that time, accidentally hearing me acknowledge an ignorance of arithmetic that would disgrace a charity-school boy, he took me in hand. He examined me, found me abundantly deficient, gave me books, and appointed tasks.

He began with pleasure, indeed with unconcealed exultation, condescending to say he believed I was "bonne et pas trop faible"—well enough disposed and not wholly destitute of parts—but, owing to adverse circumstances, "as yet in a state of wretchedly imperfect mental development."

The beginning of all effort has indeed been marked by preternatural imbecility for me. I could never assert a claim to average quickness, even in forming a common acquaintance. A depressing and difficult passage has prefaced every new page I have turned in life. So long as this passage lasted, M. Paul was very kind, very forbearing. He saw the sharp pain inflicted and felt the weighty humiliation imposed by my sense of incapacity. His own eyes would moisten when tears of shame clouded mine; burdened as he was with work, he would steal half his brief recreation to give to me.

But strange grief! When that heavy, overcast dawn began to yield to day; when my faculties struggled free and my time of energy arrived; when I voluntarily doubled, trebled, and quadrupled the tasks he set to please him, his kindness became sternness. The light in his eyes changed from a beam to a spark. He fretted, opposed, and curbed me imperiously. The more I did, the harder I worked, the less he seemed content. Sarcasms of which the severity amazed me harassed my ears, followed by bitter inuendoes against the "pride of intellect." I was vaguely threatened with some doom if I trespassed the limits proper to my sex and conceived a contraband appetite for unfeminine knowledge. Alas, I had no such appetite. What I loved, I joyed in by any effort to content. But the noble hunger for science in the abstract, the godlike thirst after discovery—these feelings were known to me but by briefest flashes.

Yet, when M. Paul sneered at me, I wanted to possess them more fully. His injustice stirred ambitious wishes in me, imparting a strong stimulus, giving wings to aspiration. In the beginning, that uncomprehended sneer made my heart ache, but by-and-by it only warmed the blood in my veins, sending added action to my pulses. Whatever my powers—feminine or otherwise—God had given them, and I felt resolute to be ashamed of no faculty of his bestowal.

The combat was very sharp for a time. I seemed to have lost M. Paul's affection; he treated me strangely. In his most unjust moments, he would insinuate that I had deceived him when I appeared "faible"—incompetent; that I had feigned a false incapacity. Again, he would accuse me of far-fetched imitations and impossible plagiarisms, asserting that I had extracted the pith out of books I had not so much as heard of—and over which I should infallibly have fallen asleep as deeply as Eutychus.

Once, upon his preferring such an accusation, I turned upon him. Gathering an armful of his books from my desk, I filled my apron and poured them in a heap upon his estrade at his feet.

"Take them away, M. Paul," I said. "And teach me no more. I never asked to be made learned, and you compel me to feel very profoundly that learning is not happiness."

Returning to my desk, I laid my head on my arms and would not speak to him for two days. He pained and chagrined me. His affection had been very sweet and dear—a pleasure new and incomparable; now that this seemed withdrawn, I cared not for his lessons.

However, the books were not taken away. They were restored with careful hands to their places, and he came as usual to teach me. He made his peace somehow—too readily, perhaps. I ought to have stood out longer, but when he looked kind and good, and held out his hand with amity, memory refused to reproduce with due force his oppressive moments. And then, reconciliation is always sweet!

On a certain morning, a message came from my godmother inviting me to attend a notable lecture in the public rooms. Dr. John had brought the message himself, delivering it verbally to Rosine, who had not scrupled to follow the steps of M. Emanuel, then passing to the first class, and, in his presence, stand "carrément" before my desk, hand in apron-pocket, and rehearse the same, saucily and aloud, concluding with the words, "Qu'il est vraiment beau, Mademoiselle, ce jeune docteur! Quels yeux—quel regard! Tenez! J'en ai le cœur tout ému!"

When she was gone, my professor demanded of me why I suffered "cette fille effrontée, cette créature sans pudeur" to address me in such terms.

I had no pacifying answer to give. The terms were precisely such as Rosine—a young lady in whose skull the organs of reverence and reserve were not largely developed—was in the constant habit of using. Besides, what she said about the young doctor was true enough. Graham *was* handsome; he had fine eyes and a thrilling glance. An observation to that effect actually formed itself into sound on my lips.

"Elle ne dit que la vérité," I said.

"Ah! vous trouvez?"

"Mais, sans doute."

The lesson to which we had that day to submit was such as to make us very glad when it terminated. At its close, the released pupils rushed out, half-trembling, half-exultant. I, too, was going. A mandate to remain arrested me. I muttered that I wanted some fresh air sadly—the stove was in a glow, the class overheated. An inexorable voice merely recommended silence; and this salamander—for whom no room ever seemed too hot—sitting down between my desk and the stove—a situation in which he ought to have felt broiled, but did not—proceeded to confront me with a Greek quotation!

In M. Emanuel's soul rankled a chronic suspicion that I knew both Greek and Latin. As monkeys are said to have the power of speech if they would but use it, and are reported to conceal this faculty in fear of its being turned to their detriment, so to me was ascribed a fund of knowledge which I was supposed criminally and craftily to conceal. The privileges of a "classical education," it was insinuated, had been mine; on flowers of Hymettus I had revelled; a golden store, hived in memory, now silently sustained my efforts and privily nurtured my wits.

A hundred expedients did M. Paul employ to surprise my secret—to wheedle, to threaten, to startle it out of me. Sometimes he placed Greek and Latin books in my way, and then watched me, as Joan of Arc's jailors tempted her with the warrior's accoutrements, lying in wait for the issue. Again he quoted authors and passages, and while rolling out their sweet and sounding lines (the classic tones fell musically from his lips—for he had a good voice, remarkable for compass, modulation, and matchless expression)—he would fix on me a vigilant, piercing, and often malicious eye. It was evident he sometimes expected great demonstrations; they never occurred, however. Not comprehending, of course, I could neither be charmed nor annoyed.

Baffled—almost angry—he still clung to his fixed idea; my susceptibilities were pronounced marble, my face a mask. It appeared as if he could not be brought to accept the homely truth and take me for what I was: men, and women too, must have delusion of some sort; if not made ready to their hand, they will invent exaggeration for themselves.

At moments I *did* wish that his suspicions had been better founded. There were times when I would have given my right hand to possess the treasures he ascribed to me. He deserved condign punishment for his testy crotchets. I could have gloried in bringing home to him his worst apprehensions astoundingly realized. I could have exulted to burst on his vision, confront and confound his "lunettes," one blaze of acquirements. Oh! why did nobody undertake to make me clever while I was young enough to learn, that I might, by one grand, sudden, inhuman revelation—one cold, cruel, overwhelming triumph—have for ever crushed the mocking spirit out of Paul Carl David Emanuel!
