Margaret Brandt stood alone, her reserve a fragile bulwark against the whispers that swirled around her. The townspeople of Sevenbergen spoke of her in hushed tones, their jealousy and malice simmering just beneath the surface. She knew it, and the knowledge gnawed at her like a rodent, night and day. For she was certain that when her condition could no longer be concealed, they would revel in her downfall.

As a child, Margaret had been taught that betrothal was a binding contract, one that could not be broken without consequences. Yet, Gerard had possessed the contract, and now he was far away, leaving her vulnerable to the cruel whims of others. She despised herself for the oversight, for allowing the world to believe she was a woman without a husband.

Margaret's mind worked with the clarity of a winter morning, untainted by sentiment or fancy. She knew the world's opinion of her would be shaped by her visible pregnancy, not by the existence of a contract hidden away in Italy. What use were her marriage lines when they could not be produced? No woman would believe her, and even fewer would care to listen. In a world where reputation was currency, Margaret's modesty would be her greatest liability.

The cruel irony was that she was not the first to be slandered. Women had long been at a disadvantage, their reputations fragile as glass. But Margaret's situation was different – her condition was a truth that would soon be impossible to hide. As she sat listlessly, her heart heavy with foreboding, she felt the weight of the world's gaze upon her. Even the gentlest of words from her father could set her cheeks aflame, her mind consumed by the dread of being paraded as a fallen woman.

That evening, Margaret returned from Margaret Van Eyck's household, her secret exposed to her peers. She had hoped that Catherine, a kindred spirit, would be her ally. But Catherine's silence was a cold comfort, and Margaret's heart sank. She waited anxiously for news, but none came. In the end, she resigned herself to the fact that Catherine was not the friend she had hoped for. The line between friendship and enmity had been drawn, and Margaret was left to face the consequences alone.
