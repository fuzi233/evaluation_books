Sally sat in her apartment on the morning of her return to New York, trembling with the electric thrill of a swimmer who has finally conquered the hesitation of a raw, freezing dawn. She ached, yes, but it was a stimulating ache, the kind that proves you are alive and defiant. She had spent months wavering on the brink of happiness, and now, plunging into the mid-stream of reality, she was ready for whatever the water threw at her.

Her first impulse was to run to Ginger. She needed his stolid, unshakeable friendliness to anchor her. She regretted not sending a cable to meet her at the docks; the echoing customs sheds had already drained her courage, leaving her feeling strangely isolated. Glancing at her watch, she realized it was still early. She could catch Ginger at the office and drag him out for lunch.

Stepping out into the city, she found that the relentless hand of change had already reshaped the outer office of Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd. The pimpled boy she had once known, with whom she had shared such warm rapport, was gone. In his place stood a grim, suspicious stripling whose face was a map of acne and whose demeanor was that of a security guard rather than a theater employee. He peered at her with narrowed eyes, as if she were a thief caught in the act of stealing the blotting paper, before demanding her business with icy acerbity.

"I want Mr. Kemp," Sally said.

The boy scratched his cheek dourly with a ruler. Unseen by her, he had been attempting to balance the ruler on his chin, juggling it with paper weights—a secret ambition to enter vaudeville.

"What name?" he asked, coldly.

"Nicholas," Sally replied. "I am Mr. Nicholas' sister."

On previous visits, this declaration had caused chaos. Today, however, it struck the boy like a bullet. He dropped the ruler, convulsing with surprise. His peevish mood, born of frustration with his paper weights, instantly evaporated. The discovery that the visitor was the boss's sister transformed his attitude from hostile to winningly polite. He offered her a seat, dusted her coat sleeve with exaggerated courtesy, and complimented the fine morning.

"I want Mr. Kemp," Sally insisted when he mentioned Mr. Nicholas was out.

"Mr. Kemp?" the boy asked, feigning sorrow. "Name's new to me, lady. Never seen him in here."

"But surely..." Sally hesitated, a grim foreboding settling in her stomach. "How long have you been employed here?"

"Just over a month, miss," the boy answered smoothly.

"Hasn't Mr. Kemp been in the office all that time?"

"Nope. Name's new to me. Does he look like anything? What's he look like?"

"He has very red hair," Sally said.

"Never seen him," the boy confirmed. The truth hit Sally hard. Left to his own devices, Ginger had once again made a mess of things. Surely Fillmore wouldn't have dismissed a man under her special protection so thoroughly.

"Where is Mr. Nicholas?" she asked, desperate for information.

"Really out, miss. He went off to White Plains in his automobile half an hour ago."

"White Plains? What for?"

The boy, eager to impress, launched into a monologue about Bugs Butler, a boxer training at the camp. He described Butler as a "hot sketch" who couldn't possibly beat the lightweight champion, Lew Lucas. He spoke with the fervor of a true fan, detailing Butler's failures against Cyclone Mullins and K-leg Binns, betting his own money that Butler couldn't last six rounds.

"Oh, Bugs—Mr. Butler is one of the boxers in this fight that my brother is interested in?" Sally asked, seeing a glimmer of hope.

"That's right. He's going up against the lightweight champ. Lew Lucas is the champ. He's a bird!" the boy enthused.

Sally pressed him for details about finding Ginger at the camp. The boy, delighted by her interest, directed her to the train station, promising she'd find the camp easily. As she left, he resumed his paper weight juggling, his plastic mind already shifting back to business.

***

Fillmore Nicholas, the manager, was just escaping the training camp, a large barn-like gymnasium filled with expectant onlookers and plutocrats eager to see the final preparations for Bugs Butler's upcoming fight. He had been relieved when the talkative manager, Lester Burrowes, distracted him, allowing Fillmore to slip away to the hotel for a much-needed meal. He hadn't eaten since leaving England.

At the door, he collided with Sally.

"Hallo, Fillmore!" she called softly.

The impact shattered Fillmore's composure. He leaped three inches into the air, his nervous system jolted as if struck by a pole. He stared at her, breathless and bewildered. How could she be here? He had left her in Shropshire, assuming she was safe at Monk's Crofton. Now she was in the heart of a boxing camp in New York.

"Sally!" he croaked. "What... what...?"

"Did I startle you? I'm sorry."

"What are you doing here? Why aren't you at Monk's Crofton?"

"I decided I wanted to get back to America. Circumstances arose which made it pleasanter to leave Monk's Crofton."

"Do you mean to say..." Fillmore persisted, dread filling his voice. "That Carmyle proposed to you and you turned him down?"

Sally flushed. "I don't think it's particularly nice to talk about that sort of thing, but—yes."

Desolation washed over Fillmore. The perfect arrangement, the ideal match between the wealthy, aristocratic Bruce Carmyle and his sister, had been ruined by her caprice. He had envisioned a brother-in-law with a claim on the Carmyle bank balance, a sensible husband for a sensible girl. Now, she had spoiled it all.

"But why?" he asked, his hunger momentarily forgotten.

"Oh, Fill!" Sally sighed, sensing his distress. "I should have thought the reason was obvious."

"You mean you don't like him?"

"I don't know whether I do or not. I certainly don't like him enough to marry him."

Fillmore, starving and heartbroken, urged her to the hotel for food. Sally refused, citing her need to talk. Just then, Lester Burrowes, the manager, approached, wearing a "nobbly" tweed suit and a brown bowler hat.

"Say, Mr. Nicholas, you ain't going'? Bugs is just getting ready to spar." Burrowes glanced at Sally.

"My sister—Mr. Burrowes," Fillmore introduced faintly.

"Pleased to meecher," Burrowes said, his eyes glittering. Before Fillmore could retreat, Burrowes grabbed his coat button, determined to sell his charge. "You've never seen this boy of mine when he was feeling right. Believe me, he's there! He's a wizard. He's a Hindoo!"

Fillmore exchanged a worried look with Sally, who pitied him. She knew she would soon have to explain why she had fired Ginger, but for now, she remembered he was her brother and suffering.

"He's the cleverest lightweight since Joe Gans," Burrowes declared fervently.

"Can he make a hundred and thirty-five ringside without being weak?" Sally asked suddenly.

The effect on Burrowes was instantaneous. He dropped away from Fillmore like an exhausted shellfish, his mouth agape as if a child had asked a mathematician about higher algebra. He had never imagined Sally was a boxing enthusiast.

"It took him fifteen rounds to get a referee's decision over Cyclone Mullins," Sally corrected severely, listing Butler's past defeats.

Burrowes rallied, protesting that the referee was biased and that Butler had won by a mile. He produced a picture postcard of a gaunt, crouching figure and thrust it into Sally's hand. "That's Bugs. Take a slant at that and then tell me if he don't look the goods."

Before Sally could respond, Burrowes called over a youth in a bath-robe. "Bugs, this is Miss Nicholas, come to see you work out."

Bugs Butler, an admirer of the sex, quickened his pace. In the flesh, he was a singularly repellent young man, radiating danger and arrogance. He smirked at Sally, and she forced a polite smile. "I hope you are going to win, Mr. Butler," she said.

"You betcher," he asserted, his chest swelling with complacency.

As the manager ushered him toward the ring, Bugs cast a disparaging glance at the sparse crowd, mostly comprised of unpaid newspapermen. "All right," he said boredly. "I'll go a couple of rounds with Reddy for a starter."

Suddenly, a young man with tousled red hair emerged from the shadows, pulling off a sweater. Sally gasped in astonishment. It was Ginger.

***

The spell was broken by the energetic Mr. Burrowes, who urged his sparring partner, Reddy (Ginger), to hurry. Ginger, embarrassed and awkward, began pulling on his gloves. Sally watched, feeling like an intruder at a strange rite, a puppy wandering into a church.

In the center of the ring, Bugs Butler, now clad in boxing trunks that revealed every rippling muscle, moved with rhythmic nonchalance. To Sally, he looked like a human panther, a machine built for destruction. Two "Wise Guys" in the audience scoffed at his shadow boxing, dismissing it as easy until a real wallop was delivered.

"Ready, Bugs?" Burrowes asked.

Ginger stepped forward.

Bugs attacked with the confidence of a child about to demolish a Noah's Ark. He flicked Ginger lightly over his guard, circling sinuously to show off his footwork. To the experts, it was farcical; to Sally, it was terrifying. She shuddered, gripping the rope tightly.

Then, something solid smote Bugs' nose. He rocked back, astonished and in pain. He had been careless, letting his eyes flicker to check on Sally, and now he paid the penalty. Piqued, he shimmered around the ring, his thoughts turning hard toward Ginger.

Ginger, too, was thinking hard thoughts. He had endured Bugs' pounding before, but today was different. Those careless flicks felt like an insult, a deliberate attempt to humiliate him in front of Sally. He refused to be made an exhibition of. Digging heavily into Bugs' lower ribs, he forced a clinch.

"Say, what the hell d'ya think you're getting at?" Bugs demanded in a heated whisper.

Ginger maintained a pink silence, his jaw set, his temper reaching white heat. He dodged a vicious right and rushed in. A left hook shook him but missed. Suddenly, Bugs, bothered by the ropes and trying to side-step, ran into a swing and fell.

"Time!" shouted the scandalized Burrowes.

The audience gasped. The Wise Guys laughed raucously, urging the fallen warrior to retire. But Sally felt a fierce, cave-womanly rush of happiness sweep through her. She was proud of Ginger. This was not the mild, amiable young man she had known; this was a new Ginger, a stranger who could stand up to a champion.

Bugs rose almost immediately, his motto clear: *A man may be down, but he is never out.* He snarled at his manager, "Like hell it's time!" and launched himself at Ginger in a whirlwind of flying gloves.

To Burrowes, this was a bitter blow. His protégé, usually cool and scientific, was abandoning technique for primitive fury. He was trying too hard, losing his elegance, and making an exhibition of himself. Yet, nothing could stop him. His whole fighting soul was in the effort to destroy Ginger.

Ginger weathered the tempest like a storm-driven ship, bruised and bleeding but fighting hard. Bugs swung wildly, missing vital spots, his own face now streaked with blood. The Wise Guys leaned against each other, one crying in despair.

Then, suddenly, the end came. Fatigue returned to Bugs. His feet, once so subtle, stumbled. A quick feint, a short, jolting stab, and Ginger's guard was down. He swayed in the middle of the ring, hands hanging, knees a-quiver.

Bugs Butler measured his distance, and Sally shut her eyes.
