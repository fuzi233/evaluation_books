I call our world Flatland, not because we possess a name for it, but to strip away the confusion of higher dimensions and reveal its stark, two-dimensional truth to you, my fortunate readers dwelling in the boundless expanse of Space. Envision a vast, infinite plane of paper where straight lines, triangles, squares, pentagons, hexagons, and myriad other geometric forms drift freely across the surface, trapped within its confines, unable to rise above or sink beneath it. These shapes exist like shadows cast by solid objects, yet unlike mere phantoms, they are tangible, possessing hardness and luminous edges. This is the essence of my nation and its citizens. Once, in ignorance, I might have termed this realm "my universe"; but since my mind has been illuminated by superior perspectives, I reserve that grander title for the dimension beyond ours.

In such a realm, the concept of "solidity" is an impossibility, a phantom notion borrowed from your world. One might reasonably assume that despite this lack of depth, we could visually distinguish between a triangle, a square, or any other polygon as they moved about. Yet, this assumption is fundamentally flawed. We cannot see figures as you understand them; we perceive nothing but straight lines. Let me demonstrate this necessity with a simple experiment. Place a copper penny upon the center of a table in your three-dimensional space. Now, lean over it and gaze downward. To your eyes, the coin appears a perfect circle. Draw back until your eye rests at the table's edge, gradually lowering your vision until you align perfectly with the surface, mimicking the condition of a Flatlander. At this precise moment, the penny loses its circular guise, elongating into an increasingly narrow oval until, finally, it resolves into a single, unbroken straight line. This phenomenon holds true for any shape cut from pasteboard—a triangle, a square, or a complex polygon. When viewed from directly above the plane, the figure vanishes, replaced solely by its linear boundary.

Consider, for instance, an equilateral triangle, which among us signifies a tradesman of respectable standing. From your vantage point high above, he presents as a distinct geometric form. As your eye descends, approaching the level of the tabletop, the figure distorts, shrinking in height while widening in breadth. Finally, when your eye lies flush with the surface—as it does for every inhabitant of Flatland—you behold nothing but a straight line. No corners, no vertices, no internal angles are visible to us; only the line remains.

This experience mirrors the plight of your sailors traversing the oceans. When they gaze upon a distant island or coastline on the horizon, the intricate geography—bays, headlands, and recessions—is lost to distance. Unless the sun strikes the land with brilliance, casting shadows that reveal relief, the sailor sees only a grey, unbroken line cutting across the water. So too do we observe our neighbors. Without a sun to cast shadows, and lacking the third dimension to provide cues of depth, we rely solely on the length of the line before us. If a triangular acquaintance approaches, his line grows longer; if he departs, it shrinks. Whether he is a triangle, a square, a pentagon, a hexagon, or even a circle, to our eyes, he is indistinguishable from any other straight line.

You may rightly wonder how we navigate this visual deprivation, distinguishing friend from foe amidst a sea of identical lines. The answer to this profound mystery requires a deeper understanding of our physiology and social codes, a topic I shall address when describing the inhabitants themselves. For now, let us turn our attention to the climate and architecture of our peculiar country.
