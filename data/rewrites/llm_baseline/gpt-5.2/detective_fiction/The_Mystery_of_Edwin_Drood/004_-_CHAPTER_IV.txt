If Cloisterham ever bred a man who could auction off his own reflection and buy it back at a profit, it was Mr. Thomas Sapsea.

He stood for the town’s notion of weight and consequence: broad in the middle, slow in the mind, sonorous in the throat. He “dressed at” the Dean, and was so often mistaken—bowed to, spoken to, half-saluted—for some higher ecclesiastical article, that the mistake had hardened into a private proof of greatness. He had even trained his sale-voice to a mild intonation, as if landed property were to be disposed of with a blessing; and when he finished a public auction, he would spread his hands with a grave, flowing motion—as though the next act must be Confirmation, and the next sound an Amen.

His premises lay in the High Street opposite the Nuns’ House, a building of much the same vintage: old bones irregularly patched, like a household that has survived plagues by gradually admitting air and light. Above Sapsea’s doorway hung a wooden effigy of his father—curled wig, toga, and a little hammer poised—caught in the eternal act of selling. The town admired the chastity of the idea, and the delicacy of the carved little finger, as if that finger had always known precisely where value resided.

Inside, Sapsea sat in his ground-floor room that looked first upon a paved back-yard and then, beyond rails, upon a strip of garden. An early fire burned—a luxury he allowed himself without apology, for it was a cool, raw autumn evening, and the draughts that prowled old Cloisterham stone could chill a man into needless humility. The room smelt of hot coal, dusty paper, and the sweet, raisiny breath of port uncorked and waiting. His portrait watched him; an eight-day clock ticked at him; a weather-glass stood ready to contradict the sky. They were his chosen witnesses: he against mankind, his clock against time, his glass against weather.

On the table by the fire lay a writing-desk and materials, and beside them a tray holding three clean wineglasses. Sapsea glanced at a scrap of manuscript, lifted his chin, and read it in his head with such dignity that only one word escaped him—audible, rounded, and self-satisfied:

“Ethelinda.”

He began to pace with his thumbs in his waistcoat arm-holes, repeating the lines from memory, giving each syllable the weight of a deed of conveyance.

His maid came in.

“Mr. Jasper is come, sir.”

Sapsea, without haste, waved the air as if granting an audience. “Admit him.” He drew two wineglasses from the tray with the air of a man recognizing claims already established by law.

John Jasper entered—Choirmaster and musician, his face composed, his eyes bright with that fixed attention which can look like sympathy until it turns. The warmth of the fire and the red of the port made a small, domestic theatre; outside, the town’s evening sounds pressed faintly at the panes: a cart-rumble dying in the High Street, a distant shout from boys who never learned quiet, and, above all, the Cathedral’s presence—felt even when it held its voice.

Sapsea welcomed him with swelling courtesy.

“Glad to see you, sir. I congratulate myself on having the honour of receiving you here for the first time.”

“You are very good. The honour is mine and the self-congratulation is mine,” Jasper returned smoothly.

“You are pleased to say so, sir. But I do assure you it is a satisfaction to me to receive you in my humble home. And that is what I would not say to everybody.” His tone left hanging, like an unstruck bell, the rest of the meaning: that Jasper might count himself fortunate to be thought satisfiable.

“I have for some time desired to know you, Mr. Sapsea.”

“And I, sir, have long known you by reputation as a man of taste.” Sapsea filled Jasper’s glass, then his own. He raised it as if it were a national banner. “I will give you, sir: When the French come over, may we meet them at Dover!”

It was an old toast, the sort that had survived into the present like a preserved fruit gone leathery with age; but Sapsea drank it as if it had been invented that morning for his lips. Jasper watched him with a slight smile.

“You can scarcely be ignorant, Mr. Sapsea,” Jasper said, letting the firelight play over his face, “that you know the world.”

“Well, sir,” chuckled Sapsea, stretching his legs before the fire, “I think I know something of it; something of it.”

“Your reputation for that knowledge has always interested and surprised me, and made me wish to know you. For Cloisterham is a little place. Cooped up in it myself, I know nothing beyond it, and feel it to be a very little place.”

Sapsea took this as tribute. “If I have not gone to foreign countries, young man—” He checked himself, as if politeness were a rare spice requiring careful measurement. “You will excuse me calling you young man, Mr. Jasper? You are much my junior.”

“By all means.”

“If I have not gone to foreign countries, young man, foreign countries have come to me. They have come to me in the way of business, and I have improved upon my opportunities. Put it that I take an inventory, or make a catalogue. I see a French clock. I never saw him before, in my life, but I instantly lay my finger on him and say ‘Paris!’ I see some cups and saucers of Chinese make, equally strangers to me personally: I put my finger on them, then and there, and I say ‘Pekin, Nankin, and Canton.’ It is the same with Japan, with Egypt, and with bamboo and sandalwood from the East Indies; I put my finger on them all. I have put my finger on the North Pole before now, and said ‘Spear of Esquimaux make, for half a pint of pale sherry!’”

Jasper’s smile widened a fraction, as if the soundscape of the world—wind at sea-edges, market-calls in foreign tongues, the hush of snow—had all been reduced here to labels and prices, and he found the reduction amusing.

“Really? A very remarkable way, Mr. Sapsea, of acquiring a knowledge of men and things.”

“I mention it, sir,” Sapsea returned, brimming with complacency, “because, as I say, it don’t do to boast of what you are; but show how you came to be it, and then you prove it.”

Jasper let the subject fall where it lay, like a heavy key on a table. “Most interesting. We were to speak of the late Mrs. Sapsea.”

“We were, sir.”

Sapsea filled both glasses again and took the decanter back as if securing a treasure. He lifted a folded scrap of paper, the manuscript Jasper had come for, but held it in suspense—like a cook describing a dish at length before permitting the first taste.

“Before I consult your opinion as a man of taste on this little trifle,” he said, holding the paper up to the firelight, “which is but a trifle, and still has required some thought, sir, some little fever of the brow, I ought perhaps to describe the character of the late Mrs. Sapsea, now dead three quarters of a year.”

Jasper, caught mid-yawn behind his wineglass, lowered it quickly and summoned an attentive look—though his watering eyes betrayed the effort.

“Half a dozen years ago, or so,” Sapsea went on, “when I had enlarged my mind up to—I will not say to what it now is, for that might seem to aim at too much—but up to the pitch of wanting another mind to be absorbed in it—I cast my eye about me for a nuptial partner. Because, as I say, it is not good for man to be alone.”

Jasper’s brows lifted slightly, as if storing the phrase for later use in another voice, another setting.

“Miss Brobity at that time kept—I will not call it the rival establishment to the establishment at the Nuns’ House opposite, but I will call it the other parallel establishment down town. The world did have it that she showed a passion for attending my sales, when they took place on half holidays, or in vacation time. The world did put it about, that she admired my style. The world did notice that as time flowed by, my style became traceable in the dictation-exercises of Miss Brobity’s pupils.”

His deep voice rolled like a drum in a small room, pushing aside the softer domestic sounds—the tick of the clock, the fire’s shift and crack, the faint far-off wheel on stone.

“Young man, a whisper even sprang up in obscure malignity, that one ignorant and besotted Churl (a parent) so committed himself as to object to it by name. But I do not believe this. For is it likely that any human creature in his right senses would so lay himself open to be pointed at, by what I call the finger of scorn?”

Jasper shook his head gravely. Not in the least likely.

Sapsea, luxuriating in his own narrative, seemed about to refill Jasper’s glass though it was already full; he did refill his own, which was empty.

“Miss Brobity’s Being, young man, was deeply imbued with homage to Mind. She revered Mind, when launched, or, as I say, precipitated, on an extensive knowledge of the world. When I made my proposal, she did me the honour to be so overshadowed with a species of Awe, as to be able to articulate only the two words, ‘O Thou!’ meaning myself. Her limpid blue eyes were fixed upon me, her semi-transparent hands were clasped together, pallor overspread her aquiline features, and, though encouraged to proceed, she never did proceed a word further. I disposed of the parallel establishment by private contract, and we became as nearly one as could be expected under the circumstances. But she never could, and she never did, find a phrase satisfactory to her perhaps-too-favourable estimate of my intellect. To the very last (feeble action of liver), she addressed me in the same unfinished terms.”

As Sapsea’s voice deepened further, Jasper’s eyes drifted shut, as if the cadence itself were soporific. Then, abruptly, he opened them and answered the deep voice with a deep sound of his own.

“Ah!” he said—almost as if he had checked himself from adding, men!

“I have been since,” Sapsea continued, stretching out before the fire as if warming not only his body but his self-importance, “what you behold me; I have been since a solitary mourner; I have been since, as I say, wasting my evening conversation on the desert air. I will not say that I have reproached myself; but there have been times when I have asked myself the question: What if her husband had been nearer on a level with her? If she had not had to look up quite so high, what might the stimulating action have been upon the liver?”

Jasper’s face assumed a fallen, almost doleful cast. “I suppose it was to be.”

“We can only suppose so, sir,” Sapsea agreed. “As I say, Man proposes, Heaven disposes. It may or may not be putting the same thought in another form; but that is the way I put it.”

Jasper murmured assent, his fingers resting lightly on the warm bowl of his glass, the port’s scent rising—berries and old wood and a faint spirit-bite—mingled with coal smoke.

“And now, Mr. Jasper,” Sapsea resumed, producing the scrap of manuscript as if unveiling a monument in miniature, “Mrs. Sapsea’s monument having had full time to settle and dry, let me take your opinion, as a man of taste, on the inscription I have—(as I before remarked, not without some little fever of the brow)—drawn out for it. Take it in your own hand. The setting out of the lines requires to be followed with the eye, as well as the contents with the mind.”

Jasper took the paper and read it. The letters stood in a kind of carved arrogance even on the page:

ETHELINDA,  
Reverential Wife of  
MR. THOMAS SAPSEA,  
AUCTIONEER, VALUER, ESTATE AGENT, &c.,  
OF THIS CITY.

Whose Knowledge of the World,  
Though somewhat extensive,  
Never brought him acquainted with  
A SPIRIT  
More capable of  
LOOKING UP TO HIM.

STRANGER, PAUSE  
And ask thyself the Question,  
CANST THOU DO LIKEWISE?  
If Not,  
WITH A BLUSH RETIRE.

Sapsea had risen and planted himself with his back to the fire, watching Jasper’s face for admiration as keenly as he watched a bidder’s twitch at an auction. His stance set his face toward the door; and at that moment the maid appeared again.

“Durdles is come, sir!”

Sapsea promptly drew forth and filled the third wineglass, as being now claimed. “Show Durdles in.”

Jasper handed back the paper. “Admirable!”

“You approve, sir?”

“Impossible not to approve. Striking, characteristic, and complete.”

Sapsea inclined his head as if accepting a formal receipt. Then he invited Durdles—entering with a breath of chill stone and damp air clinging to his clothes—to take off the glass of wine, for it would warm him.

Durdles was a stonemason, and seemed made of the very colour of his trade—dusty as a gravestone from head to foot, as if the Cathedral’s powdered centuries had settled upon him and stayed. Everyone in Cloisterham knew him. He was the town’s chartered libertine: praised as a wonderful workman (though he seldom worked), and known without dispute as a wonderful sot. Of the Cathedral crypt he knew more than any living authority—perhaps more than any dead one, too. It was said he had first taken to it as a hiding-place where he might lock out the town boys and sleep away drink, having access through his contracts for rough repairs. However begun, the intimacy endured. In demolition of old wall, buttress, and pavement he had seen strange sights, and recounted them with that half-drunken precision which makes the incredible sound like a thing you could touch.

He often spoke of himself in the third person, either because his identity was always a little fogged, or because Cloisterham’s habit of labeling people had infected his own speech.

“Durdles come upon the old chap,” he would say of some buried magnate, “by striking right into the coffin with his pick. The old chap gave Durdles a look with his open eyes, as much as to say, ‘Is your name Durdles? Why, my man, I’ve been waiting for you a devil of a time!’ And then he turned to powder.”

A two-foot rule lived in his pocket, a mason’s hammer almost always in his hand. He was forever tapping and sounding about the Cathedral, listening for hollow places. Whenever he told Tope, “Here’s another old ’un in here!” Tope dutifully passed the announcement to the Dean as an established discovery, as though stone itself had spoken.

Tonight he wore coarse flannel with horn buttons, a yellow neckerchief with draggle ends, an old hat more russet than black, and boots the colour of quarried grit. He led a hazy gipsy life, carrying his dinner tied in a little bundle and eating it anywhere—often seated on tombstones, as if the dead were simply part of the furniture of his day. That dinner bundle had become one of Cloisterham’s familiar sights; on a few famous occasions it had been taken into custody with him and exhibited before the bench at the Town Hall when he was brought in drunk and incapable. But such occasions were rare, because Durdles was as seldom drunk as sober.

He lived alone in a cramped, unfinished little house—believed to be built, so far, of stones stolen from the city wall. The approach was ankle-deep in chips, like a petrified grove of sculptured tomb fragments—urns, draperies, broken columns—at every stage of becoming. In the yard two journeymen chipped incessantly while two others sawed stone facing one another, dipping in and out of their sentry-box shelters with such mechanical regularity that they might have been figures designed to symbolize Time and Death; their saw’s rasp carried on the air like a harsh, modern interruption to Cloisterham’s older music, as if industry had learned to grind its teeth in public.

Sapsea put the manuscript into Durdles’s hands as if conferring a relic.

Durdles unfeelingly drew out his two-foot rule, and measured the lines as calmly as if they were a paving-stone, dusting them with stone-grit from his fingers.

“This is for the monument, is it, Mr. Sapsea?”

“The Inscription. Yes.”

Sapsea waited for awe. Durdles gave arithmetic.

“It’ll come in to a eighth of a inch,” said Durdles, as though that were the only praise worth having.

He drained the port with the air of a man swallowing warmth rather than wine.

“Your servant, Mr. Jasper. Hope I see you well.”

“How are you, Durdles?” Jasper asked.

“I’ve got a touch of the Tombatism on me, Mr. Jasper, but that I must expect.”

“You mean the Rheumatism,” Sapsea corrected sharply, nettled that his grand composition had met the measuring-stick first.

“No, I don’t. I mean, Mr. Sapsea, the Tombatism. It’s another sort from Rheumatism. Mr. Jasper knows what Durdles means. You get among them Tombs afore it’s well light on a winter morning, and keep on, as the Catechism says, a-walking in the same all the days of your life, and you’ll know what Durdles means.”

“It is a bitter cold place,” Jasper assented, shivering—not with sympathy, but as if the thought of that underworld damp crept into him.

“And if it’s bitter cold for you, up in the chancel, with a lot of live breath smoking out about you,” Durdles returned, “what the bitterness is to Durdles, down in the crypt among the earthy damps there, and the dead breath of the old ’uns—Durdles leaves you to judge.—Is this to be put in hand at once, Mr. Sapsea?”

Sapsea, with an author’s impatience to see himself in print on stone, replied that it could not be out of hand too soon.

“Then you had better let me have the key,” said Durdles.

“Why, man, it is not to be put inside the monument!”

Durdles looked at him with dogged certainty, like a man who knows the habits of stone and the secrets of locks. “Durdles knows where it’s to be put, Mr. Sapsea; no man better. Ask ’ere a man in Cloisterham whether Durdles knows his work.”

Sapsea rose, opened a drawer, and took a key; then unlocked an iron safe let into the wall and drew forth another key—both handled with reverence, as if they were medals of his respectability.

“When Durdles puts a touch or a finish upon his work,” Durdles explained, “no matter where, inside or outside, Durdles likes to look at his work all round, and see that his work is a-doing him credit.”

The key was large. Before accepting it, Durdles slipped his two-foot rule into a side-pocket made for it, opened his flannel coat, and opened the mouth of a vast inner breast-pocket, as deliberately as a man stowing away a tool that might save his life. He took the key and placed it deep within.

“Why, Durdles!” Jasper said, amused, “you are undermined with pockets!”

“And I carries weight in ’em too, Mr. Jasper. Feel those!” He produced two other large keys with a clank of iron. “Hand me Mr. Sapsea’s likewise. Surely this is the heaviest of the three.”

“You’ll find ’em much of a muchness, I expect,” Durdles said. “They all belong to monuments. They all open Durdles’s work. Durdles keeps the keys of his work mostly. Not that they’re much used.”

Jasper took the three keys for a moment and let them weigh his palm. Their cold metal made a blunt music when they touched—an unlovely little chime, like a parody of church bells: not calling anyone to prayer, only to secrecy.

“By the bye,” Jasper said, as if idly, bending over the fire and making the keys click together, “I have been going to ask you, many a day, and have always forgotten. You know they sometimes call you Stony Durdles, don’t you?”

“Cloisterham knows me as Durdles, Mr. Jasper.”

“I am aware of that, of course. But the boys sometimes—”

“O! if you mind them young imps of boys—” Durdles broke in gruffly.

“I don’t mind them any more than you do. But there was a discussion the other day among the Choir, whether Stony stood for Tony.” He clinked one key against another.

“Take care of the wards, Mr. Jasper,” Durdles warned.

“Or whether Stony stood for Stephen.” Jasper changed keys, clinking again.

“You can’t make a pitch pipe of ’em, Mr. Jasper.”

“Or whether the name comes from your trade. How stands the fact?”

Jasper lifted his head and handed the keys back with a face of open friendliness, as if the question were harmless as a sip of wine. But Durdles, gruff as his own stone, lived in a perpetual uncertainty of dignity and offence; his haze could harden in an instant into suspicion. He pocketed his two keys one by one and buttoned them in. He took up his dinner-bundle from the chair-back where he had hung it. He tied the third key into the bundle as if he were an ostrich and liked to dine upon cold iron. Then he went out without answering at all.

Sapsea, the least able man in Cloisterham to recognize a door slammed in his face, proposed a hit at backgammon. Jasper consented. The dice clicked, the pieces slid with small wooden sounds, and Sapsea’s voice poured on—diffuse, improving, self-regarding—until, later, supper was brought: cold roast beef and salad.

The beef had that firm, satisfied look of yesterday’s joint, cut in thick slices that showed their grain; the fat held a faint sheen in the firelight. The salad—crisp leaves dressed sharp—gave off a clean, green smell as it met vinegar, a brief bright note in the room’s heavier perfumes of coal, old wood, and port. Knife against plate made its homely scrape, and the clock’s ticking kept time like a small, stubborn drummer.

So the evening was beguiled, and the good autumn fire did its steady work. When at last Jasper rose to go, he hinted that he would return for more of Mr. Sapsea’s wisdom on future occasions. Sapsea, satisfied to have his portion admired and his inscription approved, allowed him to escape for the present—carrying away, in his own mind, the instalment he had received, and perhaps other weights besides, that chimed faintly when they touched.
