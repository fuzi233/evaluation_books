After Madame Beck’s festive gathering—three weeks of ease, a single day of wild merriment, and one more of utter weariness—came a time of quiet reckoning: two months of unrelenting study, the true heart of the school year. These final weeks were when real effort was demanded, when teachers and students alike poured their energy into preparing for the prize examinations. The burden fell heavily on all, but especially on those who taught subjects central to the curriculum. Professors sharpened their lessons, mistresses urged their pupils forward, and every student labored under the weight of expectation. A public display was expected—a demonstration of learning—and no means were too great to achieve it.

I paid little heed to how others prepared; my own duties absorbed me entirely. My task was among the most demanding: teaching English to ninety students who saw the language as both mysterious and difficult, and training their tongues in sounds they found nearly impossible—the soft hushes and sharp hisses that marked speech in the British Isles. It was a challenge not only of intellect but of patience.

The examination day arrived—so solemn, so charged with consequence. We dressed with care, avoiding anything frivolous or light; modest, composed garments were worn. To me, the day felt like a trial laid solely upon my shoulders. Unlike the other teachers, I was expected to preside over the testing of my subject. M. Paul, the headmaster, took charge of all other disciplines, refusing help from any colleague. He ruled the school with an iron hand, brooking no rivals. Even Madame Beck, who longed to examine in geography—her favorite subject—was forced to yield to his authority. He stood alone on the examiner’s platform, save for one exception: English. He could not manage it himself. Reluctantly, he left it to me—though not without a flash of envy in his eyes.

He had a deep disdain for vanity in others, though he seemed to revel in his own. He loved being seen, admired, acknowledged—but detested the same in anyone else. When he could not suppress someone’s prominence, he seethed like a storm bottled up inside.

On the evening before the exam, I walked in the garden with the other teachers and boarders. M. Emanuel joined me in the forbidden alley. His cigar glowed between his fingers, his dark coat hung loosely, his Greek cap’s tassel shadowing one temple. His whiskers curled like a cat’s in anger, and his blue eyes held a flicker of irritation.

“So,” he said abruptly, stopping before me, “you will sit like a queen tomorrow—beside me? I can see you already savoring the joy of power. Ah, little ambitious one, I see your pride shining!”

But I was not proud. I did not care for praise or recognition. If the audience had been filled with friends, perhaps I might have felt differently. But they were strangers. Their approval meant nothing to me. For him, such moments burned with warmth and meaning. He craved them. I did not. Yet I found something compelling in his jealousy—it stirred him, made him vivid. It lit strange fires in his dull face, sparked shadows in his violet-blue eyes (he once told me his black hair and blue eyes were “one of his beauties”). There was honesty in his rage—no pretense, no mask.

I did not deny his claim. I simply asked where the English exam would fall—early or late?

“I hesitate,” he replied. “Should it come at the start, when few are present and your ambition won’t be flattered by a crowd—or at the end, when everyone is tired and attention is spent?”

“You are harsh, Monsieur,” I said, feigning sorrow.

“One must be harsh with you. You are one of those who must be kept down. I know you. Others pass you by and think you a mere shadow. But I looked at your face once—and that was enough.”

“Do you truly understand me?”

He did not answer directly. Instead, he continued, “Were you not thrilled when you succeeded in that vaudeville? I watched you. Your face blazed with desire to win. Fire, not just light—je me tiens pour averti.”

“The feeling I had then,” I said, “was not triumph. I despised the role you gave me. I had no connection with the audience below. They are decent people, I suppose—but do I know them? Are they mine? Can I care if I stand before them again tomorrow? Will this examination mean anything to me beyond duty? It is merely a task—one I wish done.”

“Shall I take it from you?”

“With all my heart—if you do not fear failure.”

“But I would fail. I know only three English phrases: ‘de sonn,’ ‘de mone,’ ‘de stare’—is that right? Perhaps we should abandon the exam altogether.”

“If Madame agrees, I agree.”

“Heartily?”

“Very heartily.”

He smoked in silence, then turned suddenly.

“Give me your hand,” he said. The bitterness vanished from his face, replaced by something softer—kindness, even generosity.

“Let us not be rivals,” he added. “Let us be friends. The exam shall happen, and I’ll choose the best time. Instead of hindering you—as I nearly did ten minutes ago—I will help. I have my cruel moods. I’ve always had them since childhood. But you are alone, a stranger, making your way. It may be good for you to be known. Let us be friends. Do you agree?”

“From the bottom of my heart, Monsieur. I welcome a friend more than any triumph.”

“Poor thing,” he murmured, turning away.

The examination passed smoothly. M. Paul honored his word and made my part as easy as possible. Then came the prize-giving, the closing of term, and the departure of the students. And so began the long vacation.

That vacation! Could I ever forget it? I doubt it. Madame Beck left the first day to join her children by the sea. The other teachers returned to family or friends. Every professor fled the city—some to Paris, some to Boue-Marine. M. Paul set off for Rome. The house emptied completely—save for me, a servant, and one deformed, mentally impaired girl, a sort of crétin, whom her stepmother refused to take back.

My heart nearly broke. Longings pulled at me, stretching every nerve. How slow the September days felt! How silent, how empty the house seemed! The garden, once vibrant, now lay grey and still, dusted with the residue of summer. At the start of those eight weeks, I scarcely believed I could endure them. My spirits had been sinking for some time; now, without work to sustain me, they sank faster. Even hope was gone. The future offered no comfort, no promise. Existence itself often felt meaningless—a surrender to despair. Life, when viewed with full awareness, appeared a barren desert: endless sands, no greenery, no water, no shelter. The dreams youth clings to—love, purpose, joy—I knew not, nor dared to know. When they knocked at my heart, I barred them out. Sometimes, rejected, they brought tears—but I could not let them in. I feared the sin of presumption too deeply.

Religious reader, you may preach to me about this. Moralists will scold. Philosophers will frown. Stoics will disapprove. Cynics will sneer. Epicureans will laugh. Well, let them. I accept all. Perhaps they are right. But perhaps, in my place, you too would have been wrong. That first month was long, dark, heavy.

The crétin did not seem to suffer. I did what I could to feed and warm her. She wanted only food, sun, or fire when sunlight failed. Her mind slept, content in its emptiness. Her brain, eyes, ears, heart—none woke to pain or labor. Lethargy was her paradise.

For three weeks, the weather remained hot and dry. Then storms broke in the fourth and fifth weeks. I don’t know why the shift in air crushed me more than calm skies ever had—but it did. In that vast, hollow house, my nerves could barely hold. I prayed constantly for strength, for peace. The conviction seized me—Fate was my enemy, unyielding, unchangeable. I did not blame God’s mercy or justice. I accepted that suffering was part of His design. And I trembled knowing I was one of those chosen to bear it.

It was a relief when the crétin’s aunt came and took her away. The girl had been a burden—she couldn’t go outside, couldn’t be left alone. Her mind, like her body, was twisted. A vague impulse toward harm lived within her. She rarely spoke, sat for hours, twisting her face into grotesque expressions. Being with her felt like sharing space with a wild animal. Personal care required the courage of a nurse. My resolve faltered often, sometimes failing utterly. These duties were hard—but my inner pain was worse. Tending her made me lose appetite, sent me weak to the fountain. Yet she never touched my heart, never brought tears.

Once freed, I began walking. At first, I stayed near Rue Fossette. Gradually, I ventured beyond the gates, wandered along roads, through fields, past cemeteries, farmhouses, lanes, woods—nowhere in particular. A restless force pushed me forward. Loneliness gnawed at me like hunger. I walked entire days—through noon heat, afternoon drought, dusk, and moonrise.

Alone, I imagined others. Madame Beck, happy at the seaside with her children and friends. Zélie St. Pierre, in Paris. Ginevra Fanshawe, traveling southward with relatives. Ginevra seemed the happiest. She moved through golden landscapes, beneath autumn suns warming harvests and vineyards. Moons rose above her like crystal crowns.

But it meant nothing. I felt those suns, saw those moons—but wished only to be buried beneath earth, hidden from their glow. I could not live in their light, could not love them. Yet Ginevra had something I lacked—something powerful, gentle, enduring. True Love followed her. She was never truly alone. Was she unaware? Impossible. I pictured her quietly grateful, loving secretly, waiting to confess fully. I saw her beloved half-aware of her affection, comforted by it. Between them, a thread of understanding, invisible yet strong, carried prayers across miles. Ginevra became, in my mind, a kind of heroine.

One day, realizing my thoughts were slipping, I whispered: “I believe my nerves are breaking. My mind has suffered too much. A sickness is growing. What shall I do? How can I stay well?”

There was no way to stay well. Soon, after a night of unbearable sorrow, I fell ill. I took to bed. The Indian summer ended, and equinoctial storms began. For nine dark, wet days—hours rushing past in noise and chaos—I lay in a fever of nerves and blood. Sleep abandoned me. At night, I would rise, search for her, beg her return. Only wind and rain answered. Sleep never came.

But once—once—she came. Not in kindness. In anger. Impatient with my pleas, she brought a dream of vengeance. By the clock of St. Jean Baptiste, it lasted less than fifteen minutes—but it tore me apart. It felt like a visitation from eternity. Between midnight and one, a cup was pressed to my lips—black, strong, strange—drawn not from a well, but from a boundless, seething sea. Suffering mixed in mortal measure tastes nothing like this. After drinking, I woke, thinking it over. The end had come. Trembling, I rose on my knees. Horrors gripped me—indescribable agony, mental torment. The worst was this: I dreamed the dead I loved, who had loved me, met me elsewhere—strangers now. My soul was pierced by despair. No reason remained to live. Yet Death challenged me with a cold, proud voice. When I tried to pray, only these words came: “From my youth up Thy terrors have I suffered with a troubled mind.”

It was true.

The next morning, Goton brought tea and urged me to call a doctor. I refused. I thought no doctor could heal me.

One evening—clear-headed, not delirious—I rose, dressed myself, weak and trembling. The dormitory was unbearable. The white beds turned into specters—each crown a skull, sun-bleached and ancient. Dead dreams of older worlds stared from wide-open eye sockets. That night, I knew Fate was stone, Hope a false idol—blind, cold, unfeeling. I felt the trial God had given me was reaching its peak. I must act—my hands, though feeble, must turn it.

It rained still, but less fiercely. Twilight fell. From the lattice, I saw clouds trailing low like mourning banners. It seemed Heaven grieved for all earthly pain. The weight of my dream lessened. The unbearable thought—that I would no longer be loved—began to yield to hope. If I could leave this house—this tomb-like roof—and walk to a quiet hill far in the fields, perhaps clarity would return.

Wrapped in a cloak, I stepped out. Bells rang from a church nearby. They called me in. I entered. Any sacred ritual, any sign of worship, any chance to speak to God was as vital to me as bread to the starving. I knelt with others on the stone floor. The church was old, dim, purpled by stained glass light.

Few remained after the service. Half left. Those who stayed confessed. I did not move. Doors closed. Silence settled. One by one, penitents approached the confessional. I watched. Whispers passed. Comfort returned. A pale woman near me said softly, “Go now. I am not ready.”

Mechanically, I rose and went. I knew what I was doing. My mind had raced ahead. This step could not make me worse. It might soothe.

The priest inside never looked at me. He simply leaned his ear to my lips. He was likely kind, but this was routine. Habit had dulled him. I hesitated. I didn’t know the confession formula. So instead of beginning, I said: “Mon père, je suis Protestante.”

He turned sharply. He was not a native priest—his features showed he was French. Though aged, he had feeling, intelligence. He asked gently why, being Protestant, I came to him.

“I am dying for a word of comfort,” I said. “I’ve lived alone for weeks. I’ve been ill. A weight presses on my mind. I cannot carry it anymore.”

“Is it a sin?” he asked, startled.

I reassured him. Then, as best I could, I told him the outline of my pain.

He looked thoughtful, puzzled. “You surprise me,” he said. “I’ve never had a case like yours. Confession follows a pattern. Yours breaks it. I’m not prepared.”

Of course I hadn’t expected him to be. But simply speaking—to a human, yet sacred ear—had already eased me.

“Must I leave, father?” I asked.

“My daughter,” he said kindly, “go for now. But your words have struck me. Confession often becomes mechanical. You have poured your heart out—rarely done. I will ponder this. Were you of our faith, I would know what to say. Minds like yours find peace only in retreat, prayer, sacrifice. The world offers no solace for such souls. Holy men tell such penitents to climb upward through penance, self-denial, difficult deeds. Tears become their bread and drink. Their reward comes later. I believe these pains are messages from God—calling you back to the true Church. You were made for it. Protestantism is too dry, too cold for you. The more I reflect, the clearer it seems—your case is extraordinary. I will not forget you. Go now—but return.”

I rose, thanked him, and began to leave. He called me back.

“You must not come here,” he said. “This church is too cold. Come to my house. I live…” He gave me his address. “Be there tomorrow at ten.”

I bowed. I pulled down my veil, wrapped my cloak tighter, and slipped away into the night.
