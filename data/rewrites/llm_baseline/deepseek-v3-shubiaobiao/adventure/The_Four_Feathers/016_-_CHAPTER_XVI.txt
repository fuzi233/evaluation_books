Ethne stood frozen as Colonel Durrance called her name from the entrance of the garden enclosure. Her fingers tightened around the yellowed feather in her palm, her breath stilled. Beside her, Captain Willoughby shifted uneasily, but her grip on his arm silenced him.

Durrance stepped forward, his movements unnervingly sure for a blind man. "Ethne?" he called again, his voice sharpening with uncertainty.

Willoughby whispered, "He can't truly be blind—he's looking right at us."

Ethne barely breathed. "He sees nothing. Stay still."

Durrance paused, his head tilted as though listening. Then, with sudden decision, he turned and strode toward the creek, muttering, "Where in the world can she be?"

Only when his footsteps faded did Ethne release Willoughby. "That was close," he muttered.

She ignored the remark, her mind racing. "You’ve told me everything?"

"Everything I remember."

"And this happened last spring?"

"Yes."

Her next question was quiet, deliberate. "Why didn’t you bring this to me last summer?"

Willoughby shrugged. "Short leave. I was hunting ibex in the hills."

"I see," she said, her voice flat. "Good sport?"

"Fair enough."

A bitter irony twisted in her chest. Last summer, she had been free. If Willoughby had come then—but he hadn’t. The moment was lost, and with it, the future she might have had. Yet her face betrayed nothing.

"You might have written," she suggested.

"Feversham didn’t seem urgent. He left for Suez soon after."

"I must find him," she murmured. "He must come back."

Willoughby hesitated. "Even if he does, there are still two feathers unaccounted for. Castleton is dead—killed at Tamai."

"Dead?" A flicker of satisfaction crossed her face.

Willoughby frowned. "Trench is worse off—captured, a prisoner in Omdurman."

She shrugged. "I don’t care to hear of Trench."

"You would if you knew what Omdurman means," he countered.

But Ethne was already moving on. "Where did Feversham go?"

"Back to England, I assume. He had funds."

"Good." She glanced toward the house. "You must leave now. Durrance mustn’t know you were here."

Willoughby balked. "Durrance was Feversham’s friend. He should know—"

"No." Her voice was steel. "He doesn’t know why my engagement ended, and it must stay that way."

"Why?"

"Because I am engaged to him now."

Willoughby stiffened. "Then I’ve wasted my time bringing this feather."

She curled her fingers around it. "You haven’t. I’m grateful."

Willoughby studied her, then sighed. "It’s a muddle. Rough on Feversham. Rough on Durrance. And—" His gaze softened. "Rough on you, too, Miss Eustace."

She didn’t answer. Instead, she led him to his boat, her thoughts a storm beneath a calm surface.

As Willoughby pushed off, Mrs. Adair appeared on the bank, her eyes alight with curiosity. "Who was that?"

"A friend," Ethne replied smoothly. "Captain Willoughby."

Mrs. Adair’s smile was knowing. "Of Colonel Durrance’s regiment?"

"No. The North Surrey."

"Ah. Feversham’s old regiment."

Ethne met her gaze without flinching. "We were speaking of him, yes."

Willoughby, caught between them, hesitated.

"Goodbye, Captain," Ethne said firmly.

With a resigned nod, he rowed away.

Mrs. Adair watched him go, then turned to Ethne. "Durrance is dining with us tonight."

"How kind of you to invite him," Ethne replied, her tone light. But her fingers tightened around the feather in her hand.

As they walked back to the house, Ethne’s mind was already elsewhere—on a man lost to the desert, on a future she could no longer grasp, and on the quiet, relentless weight of the past.
