I write these lines from India to my relations in England with a single, urgent purpose: to explain why I have refused to extend the right hand of friendship to my cousin, John Herncastle. The silence I have kept regarding this rift has been wrongly interpreted by family members whose regard I hold dear. I ask you to withhold judgment until you have read the following account. Upon my honor, I swear that what follows is the strict and literal truth.

Our estrangement began during a pivotal public event involving both of us: the storming of Seringapatam under General Baird on May 4, 1799. To understand the gravity of the incident, I must briefly recount the rumors circulating in our camp prior to the assault. These tales spoke of immense wealth hidden within the Palace of Seringapatam, specifically a legendary yellow diamond.

Legend described this gem as being set in the forehead of the four-armed Hindu deity associated with the Moon. Due to its unique hue and a superstitious belief that it mirrored the phases of the moon itself, it became known as "The Moonstone." Similar myths existed in ancient Greece and Rome concerning a lesser stone believed to be influenced by lunar tides, though the Indian tradition was far more potent.

According to the lore, the diamond's history began in the eleventh century when the conqueror Mahmud of Ghazni sacked the holy city of Somnath. While the conquerors stripped the temple of its riches, the image of the moon-god, wearing the Yellow Diamond, vanished. Saved by three Brahmin priests, the idol was secretly transported to Benares, installed in a golden hall inlaid with precious stones, and restored to worship. That very night, the god Vishnu appeared to the priests in a dream. He breathed his divine essence onto the diamond and commanded that three priests, rotating in shifts, must guard it day and night forevermore. He warned that any mortal who touched the gem would face ruin, and that this curse would follow the stone through every subsequent owner. This prophecy was inscribed in gold above the shrine's gates.

Centuries passed, guardianship remaining with the descendants of those original priests. Then came the reign of Aurangzeb, the Mughal Emperor, whose campaigns brought fresh devastation to the temples. The shrine was desecrated, idols shattered, and the Moonstone seized by a high-ranking officer. Unable to reclaim it by force, the priestly line continued their vigil in disguise, watching the cursed jewel pass from hand to hand through various Muslim dynasties, always awaiting the day Vishnu would restore it.

By the late eighteenth century, the diamond had fallen into the possession of Tipu Sultan, ruler of Seringapatam. He mounted it as a pommel on a dagger and kept it among his most prized armaments. Yet, even within the Sultan's own palace, the three guardian priests remained hidden, observing the theft of their heritage.

These fantastical stories made little impression on the troops except for my cousin, John Herncastle. His romantic nature led him to accept the legends as absolute fact. On the eve of the assault, he grew absurdly agitated because others dismissed the tale as mere fiction. A heated argument ensued, fueled by Herncastle's volatile temper. In a boastful outburst, he declared that if the British forces captured Seringapatam, the Moonstone would appear on his finger. The comment drew laughter, and the matter seemed settled that night.

However, the reality of battle proved far more chaotic. At the start of the assault, my cousin and I were separated. I did not witness him crossing the river, scaling the breaches, or entering the town. We reunited only at dusk, after General Baird had discovered the corpse of Tipu Sultan beneath a pile of the dead. Both of us were assigned to parties tasked with preventing looting and restoring order.

Camp followers had already committed atrocities, but worse, soldiers had slipped through an unlocked gate into the palace treasury, filling themselves with gold and jewels. It was in the courtyard outside the treasury that we met to enforce discipline. Herncastle's spirit, inflamed by the carnage, had reached a frenzied state; he was entirely unfit for the sober duties required. While the treasury teemed with disorder, the soldiers behaved with a grotesque, good-natured recklessness. Jests and raucous banter filled the air, and inevitably, the rumor of the Moonstone resurfaced as a cruel game. "Who has the Moonstone?" became a rallying cry that triggered fresh waves of plunder wherever it was uttered.

Amidst my futile attempts to restore order, a terrifying scream erupted from the opposite side of the courtyard. Driven by fear of further massacre, I ran toward the noise. I found an open doorway where two Indian guards lay dead across the threshold. Inside, a third Indian, mortally wounded, collapsed at the feet of a man facing away from me. As I entered, the man turned. It was John Herncastle, holding a torch in one hand and a blood-dripping dagger in the other.

As he pivoted, the light caught a stone set like a pommel on the dagger's handle, flashing like a sudden flare of fire. The dying Indian sank to his knees, pointing frantically at the weapon in Herncastle's grip. In his native tongue, he gasped, "The Moonstone will have its vengeance yet on you and yours!" With those final words, he died.

Before I could intervene, the crowd that had followed me poured into the room. My cousin charged forward like a berserker, shouting, "Clear the room! Set a guard on the door!" The men retreated as he brandished his torch and blade. I quickly stationed two trusted sentries to block the exit. For the rest of that night, I saw no more of my cousin.

Early the next morning, while looting continued unabated, General Baird ordered a drumbeat proclamation: any thief caught would be hanged immediately. The provost-marshal was present to ensure enforcement. Amidst the resulting throng, Herncastle and I met again. He extended his hand as usual, saying, "Good morning." I hesitated before returning the gesture.

"First," I demanded, "tell me how the Indian in the armoury died and what those final words meant when he pointed to your dagger."

"The Indian died of a mortal wound, as I assume," Herncastle replied casually. "As for his words, I know no more than you do."

I studied him closely. The madness of the previous day had subsided, replaced by a cold composure. Deciding to grant him another chance, I pressed, "Is that all you have to say?"

"That is all," he answered flatly.

Turning my back on him, I walked away. We have not spoken since.

Please note that unless forced to make this public, this account concerns only my family. Herncastle has offered no justification severe enough to warrant reporting him to our commanding officer. Though he has been mocked repeatedly about the diamond by those who remember his earlier rage, his memory of the scene in the armoury seems sufficient to keep him silent. Rumors suggest he intends to transfer regiments solely to distance himself from me. Whether true or false, I cannot bring myself to accuse him without concrete proof.

I possess no evidence that he killed the two men at the door, nor can I definitively claim he struck the third man inside, as I did not witness the blow itself. True, I heard the dying man's words, but if they were merely the ravings of delirium, how can I refute them?

Let my relatives judge for themselves whether my aversion to this man is justified. While I personally disbelieve the supernatural legends surrounding the gem, I am haunted by a personal superstition: that crime carries its own inevitable fatality. I am convinced of Herncastle's guilt, and I strangely believe that if he retains the diamond, he will live to regret it; and if he passes it on, others will suffer the same fate.
