The afternoon of the seventh of September hung heavy and lazy over Florence, a silence broken only by the distant hum of the city, two months since Romola and Tito had dared to speak their love aloud.

Tito stepped into Nello's shop, finding the barber in a state of profound repose. Stretched upon the bench, a cap served as a blindfold over his eyes, one leg drawn up while the other had slipped toward the floor, dragging a manuscript volume of verse beneath it until the leaves were crushed. In the corner, Sandro sat alone, his fingers moving with solemn, rhythmic precision as he played *mora*, his left hand responding to the arithmetical demands of his right with the gravity of a priest.

Tito moved with a whisper of a step, snatching up the lute. He bent over the sleeping barber, plucking the strings softly as he sang Lorenzo de' Medici's *Carnival Song*:

*"Beauteous is life in blossom!
And it fleeteth—fleeteth ever;
Whoso would be joyful—let him!
There's no surety for the morrow."*

Nello woke as easily as a startled bird. The cap flew from his eyes, and he sprang upright, stretching his arms to their full length before curling them around Tito's curls in a playful grip.

"Ah, my Apollino! I am somewhat late with my siesta on this scorching day," Nello chuckled, rubbing his eyes. "That comes of not sleeping naturally, but taking a potion of potent poetry. Listen to me matching my words by their initial letters, like a *Trovatore*. A bad symptom, I fear; my wine of understanding is running off at the spigot of authorship, leaving me an empty cask smelling of dregs, like many a genius I know. What is it, my Orpheus?"

"It is but a tailor's image of your poet's," Tito replied, letting his fingers drift lightly across the strings. "Yet you have divined my impatience. I seek an extra touch of your art—not on my chin, but on the *zazzera*, which is as tangled as Florentine politics. Your comb works wonders there, stirring the animal spirits, and a hint of your orange-scent would be fitting. I am bound for the Scala palace; the young Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici attends, bringing with him Bernardo Dovizi of Bibbiena, whose wit is so rapid I can only hope to outshine it with the fragrance of orange blossoms."

Nello seized his comb, flourishing it before pushing Tito gently back into the chair and wrapping him in a cloth.

"Never speak of rivalry, *bel giovane mio*," Nello said, working the comb with practiced ease. "Bernardo Dovizi is a keen youth, but he will never cast a net to catch the wind. He bears the sharp-muzzled look of his brother, Ser Piero, the weasel Piero de' Medici keeps to slip through small holes. No! You distance all rivals; you may soon touch the sky with your forefinger. They say you have carried enough honey to sweeten the sour Messer Angelo, who has pronounced you less of an ass than expected, given your understanding with the Secretary."

"And between ourselves, Nello," Tito murmured, "Messer Angelo possesses more genius and erudition than all other Florentine scholars combined. It suits them to praise me now that Poliziano is bowed by grief or illness. I might challenge a sparrow-hawk like Pietro Crinito, but Poliziano is a large-beaked eagle who would swallow me, feathers and all, without noticing the difference."

"I will not contradict your modesty," Nello teased. "But we clever Florentines do not repeat the same truths daily; we cry down Dante and cry up Francesco Cei merely for variety. If we hail you as a new Poliziano, heaven ensures the lie is not quite so great. Are you not a pattern of virtue in this wicked city? With your ears double-waxed against siren invitations that would lure you from the Via de' Bardi and your great work destined to astonish posterity?"

"Posterity," Tito smiled wryly, "whom it will probably astonish as the universe does, by the impossibility of seeing what the plan ever was."

"Yes, something like that was prophesied recently. Cristoforo Landino said the excellent Bardo lies overthrown in his learning like a cavalier in heavy armor, angry because he is over-ridden. A pithy remark, though it seems Messer Cristoforo feeds one with an empty spoon and gags one with vain expectation. *Ecco!* You are perfect now." Nello drew away the cloth. "Impossible to add more grace! But love is not always fed on learning, is it? I shall have to dress the *zazzera* for the betrothal soon, shall I?"

"Perhaps," Tito said, smiling faintly, "unless Messer Bernardo recommends that Bardo require me to yoke a lion and a wild boar to the car of the Zecca before I can win my Alcestis. But I confess he is right: I am unworthy of Romola. She is a Pleiad who may grow dim by marrying any mortal."

"*Gnaffè*," Nello laughed. "Your modesty is in the right place. Yet fate seems to have measured and chiselled you for the niche left empty by the old man's son. By the way, *Cronaca* told me he is now at San Marco. Did you know?"

A slight electric shock passed through Tito as he rose, though he hid it by stooping to flatten the crushed leaves of the fallen book.

"No; I thought he was at Fiesole. Are you sure he has returned to San Marco?"

"Cronaca is my authority," Nello shrugged. "I do not frequent that sanctuary, but he does. Ah," he added, taking the book from Tito's hands, "my poor Nencia da Barberino! It jars your scholarly feelings to see the pages dog-eared. I was lulled by her rustic charms—'prettier than the turnip-flower,' 'with a cheek more savoury than cheese.' But to get such a notion of a *contadina*, one must lie on velvet cushions in the Via Larga, not watch the *Fierucoloni* stumping into the Piazza della Nunziata this evening."

"And pray, who are the *Fierucoloni*?" Tito asked, settling his cap.

"The country women from Pistoia and the Casentino, come to keep vigil in the church of the Nunziata and sell their yarn and dried mushrooms at the *Fierucola*, the little Fair. They make a queer show with paper lanterns, howling hymns to the Virgin on her nativity. If you had the leisure..." Nello paused, his expression darkening slightly. "No? Well, I have had enough. There is wild work in the Piazza tonight. One may get stones about the ears or shins without asking for them. I was never fond of that pressing attention. *Addio*."

Tito left the shop carrying a quiet uneasiness. His visit had ended sooner than anticipated. The boy-cardinal Giovanni de' Medici, the youngest of red-hatted fathers who would later present his broad dark cheek to posterity as Pope Leo the Tenth, had been detained by his favorite pastime, the chase, and failed to appear at the Scala palace.

It was nearly sunset when Tito left the palace, intending to proceed to the Via de' Bardi. He had not gone far when, to his astonishment, he saw Romola advancing toward him along the Borgo Pinti.

She wore a thick black veil and a black mantle, yet her figure and her walk were unmistakable. Beside her walked a short, stout form, recognized instantly as Monna Brigida, despite the unusual plainness of her attire. Romola had not been bred for devotional observances, and her appearances outside the loggia on the roof of her house were so rare, and so heavily discussed beforehand due to Bardo's dislike of being left alone, that Tito felt certain there must be some sudden, urgent cause for her absence.

She saw him through her veil and hastened her steps.

"Romola, has anything happened?" Tito asked, turning to walk beside her.

She did not answer immediately. Monna Brigida broke the silence.

"Ah, Messer Tito, you do well to turn round, for we are in haste. And is it not a misfortune? We are obliged to go round by the walls and turn up the Via del Maglio because of the Fair; the *contadine* block the way by the Nunziata, which would have taken us to San Marco in half the time."

Tito's heart gave a violent bound.

"Romola," he said in a lower tone, "are you going to San Marco?"

They had emerged from the Borgo Pinti and were now under the city walls, where wide gardens lay to their left and all was quiet. Romola lifted her veil to breathe the air, and Tito saw the subdued agitation in her face.

"Yes, Tito mio," she said, looking directly at him with sad eyes. "For the first time I am doing something unknown to my father. It comforts me that I have met you, for at least I can tell *you*. But if you are going to him, it will be well for you not to say that you met me. He thinks I am only gone to my cousin, because she sent for me. I left my godfather with him; *he* knows where I am going and why. You remember that evening when my brother's name was mentioned and my father spoke of him to you?"

"Yes," Tito whispered. A strange complication seized his mental state. His heart sank at the probability of a great change in his prospects, yet his thoughts darted over a hundred details of the course he would take when that change arrived. Still, he returned her gaze with a hungry sense that it might be the last time she would bend it upon him with full, unquestioning confidence.

"The *cugina* heard he had returned. The evening before—the evening of San Giovanni—I afterwards found he had been seen by our good Maso near our door. But when Maso went to inquire at San Marco, Dino, that is, my brother—christened Bernardino after our godfather, but now calling himself Fra Luca—had been taken to the monastery at Fiesole because he was ill. This morning, however, a message came to Maso saying he had returned to San Marco. Maso went to him there. He is very ill, and he has adjured me to go and see him. I cannot refuse, though I hold him guilty. I still remember how I loved him when I was a little girl, before I knew he would forsake my father. Perhaps he has some word of penitence to send by me. It cost me a struggle to act against my father's feeling, which I have always held to be just. I am almost sure you will think I have chosen rightly, Tito, because I have noticed your nature is less rigid than mine, and nothing makes you angry. It would cost you less to be forgiving. Though, if you had seen your father forsaken by one to whom he gave his chief love—by one in whom he planted his labor and his hopes—forsaken when his need was becoming greatest—even you, Tito, would find it hard to forgive."

What could he say? He was not equal to the hypocrisy of telling Romola that such offenses ought not to be pardoned, nor did he have the courage to utter words of dissuasion.

"You are right, my Romola; you are always right, except in thinking too well of me."

There was genuine truth in those words. Tito looked beautiful as he spoke them, his face unusually pale, his lip quivering slightly. Romola, interpreting all things largely, like a mind prepossessed with high beliefs, had a tearful brightness in her eyes as she looked at him, touched with keen joy that he felt so strongly whatever she felt.

"And now, Tito, I wish you to leave me," she said, continuing her walk. "The *cugina* and I shall be less noticed if we enter the piazza alone."

"Yes, it were better you should leave us," Monna Brigida interjected. "To say the truth, Messer Tito, all eyes follow you. Let Romola muffle herself as she will, everyone wants to see what is under her veil, for she has that way of walking like a procession. Not that I find fault with her for it, only it doesn't suit my steps. And indeed, I would rather not have us seen going to San Marco. That is why I am dressed as if I were one of the *Piagnoni* themselves, and as old as Sant' Anna. For if it had been anybody but poor Dino, who ought to be forgiven if he's dying, for what's the use of having a grudge against dead people? Make them feel while they live, say I—"

No one made a scruple of interrupting Monna Brigida. Tito, having just raised Romola's hand to his lips and said, "I understand, I obey you," turned away, lifting his cap. It was a sign of reverence rarely made by native Florentines at that time, exciting Bernardo del Nero's contempt for Tito as a fawning Greek, while to Romola, who loved homage, it gave him an exceptional grace.

He was half glad of the dismissal, half disposed to cling to Romola to the last moment in which she would love him without suspicion. It seemed certain that this brother would before all things want to know, and that Romola would before all things confide to him her father's position and her own after the years that must have brought so much change. She would tell him she was soon to be publicly betrothed to a young scholar, filling the place left vacant long ago by a wandering son. Tito foresaw the impulse that would prompt Romola to dwell on that prospect, and what would follow on the mention of the future husband's name. Fra Luca would tell all he knew and conjectured, and Tito saw no possible falsity by which he could now ward off the worst consequences of his former dissimulation.
