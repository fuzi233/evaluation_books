Though I find little virtue in the tradition of tying loose ends, I yield to it now—not out of conviction, but weariness. Let me gather what fragments remain of those who have crossed this tale, though I owe no accounting to every soul that lined Gerard’s path from Holland to Rome. They served their purpose: to prove his world was peopled not with hollow figures, but with flesh and blood.

Eli and Catherine endured to a hoary age, outliving even memory’s grasp upon their son and Margaret. Giles, too, defied time—gone to Bavaria, where at ninety he still drew breath, though little more than a trumpet-voiced specter of bone and leather. As for Cornelis, freed of rivals and long absolved by his mother (who clung to him as her scattered brood left her hollow), he waited, ever waiting, for his inheritance. But Catherine’s wit outlived his patience: rust consumes the idle blade before the whetstone wears away. At sixty-five, he shriveled in her arms—she, hale at eighty-six—and in his dying gasp, he whispered of renovations he’d make once the house was his. “Yes, my love,” she murmured, soothing a dreamer to his last sleep. His clay was borne to the grave by the very feet he’d hoped to bury.

Denys, heartbroken after Gerard’s death, returned to Burgundy. A meager pension sustained him until an inheritance fell like an unlooked-for bounty into his lap. There he lived out his days, a grizzled soldier, spinning war tales when the mood struck, but more often cursing the faithlessness of women.

Jerome, repelled by northern laxity, sought refuge in Italy. Connections hoisted him to a mitred abbacy, where he ruled with iron discipline—revered, feared, and unloved. A decade passed before he died alone, the Vulgate on his pillow, the crucifix clenched in his hand. Yet on his lips lingered something rare: a smile. Perhaps, in that final hour, he found a kindness he had never shown. *Requiescat in pace.* The Master he served employs many servants—some gentle, some stern—and even the surliest may yet find grace.

As for the fair-haired boy, Gerard Gerardson, his tale belongs not to fiction but to history. The house of his birth in Brede Kirk Straet bears the inscription: *"Hæc est parva domus natus qua magnus Erasmus,"* and volumes have been writ of him. Yet history, like a dwarf measuring a giant, has failed to grasp the fullness of Erasmus. First scholar of his age, theologian, and—unmatched in his time—a dramatist of divine spark. Scenes in this very book draw from his pen, and where they fall, they light the page. The words of such a mind do not perish. They sleep in dust until new eyes awaken them, and then they rise, they bloom, they seed again, immortal.
