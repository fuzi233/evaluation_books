“So on those happy days of yore
Oft as I dare to dwell once more,
Still must I miss the friends so tried,
Whom Death has severed from my side.

But ever when true friendship binds,
Spirit it is that spirit finds;
In spirit then our bliss we found,
In spirit yet to them I’m bound.”

Margaret was ready long before the appointed hour, and had time enough to weep quietly when no one watched, and to smile brightly when someone looked her way. Her last worry was that they might be too late for the train—but no! They arrived in good time, and she sat down at last, heart light and breath free, opposite Mr. Bell in the carriage, as the train pulled away from the station. Past familiar landmarks they raced: southern towns and hamlets bathed in golden sunlight, their red-tiled roofs glowing like embers beneath a clear blue sky—a world apart from the gray slate roofs of the north. Pigeons drifted lazily around crooked gables, fluffing their feathers in the warmth, as if soaking up every ray. At each stop, only a few people lingered—no crowds, no rush. It felt almost as though these quiet souls were too content with home to want to travel. Later in the year, this line would buzz with holidaymakers, but now it moved slowly, gently, unlike the relentless pace of northern rails. At nearly every halt, a man or two stood idle, hands in pockets, staring at the track as if watching the train pass was the most important thing in life. Margaret wondered what he’d do when the train was gone—what could possibly hold his attention beyond the empty platform, the sheds, and distant fields?

The air shimmered over the sun-drenched land. Farm after farm slid past, each one stirring memories—of German idylls, of *Herman and Dorothea*, of *Evangeline*. Then, suddenly, she was roused from her reverie. This was the stop for Helstone. The moment came—and with it, emotions sharp and tangled, neither joy nor sorrow, but something deeper. Every mile carried echoes of the past, cherished beyond measure, yet mourned for being gone. She had last traveled this road with her father and mother—on a bleak day, her own heart heavy with despair, but at least they were there. Now she was alone. An orphan. They had slipped from her life, vanished from the world. And here, the road shone in full summer glory, unchanged, untouched by time. The trees, the turns, the very light—all exactly as they had been years ago. Nature did not age. It only renewed itself.

Mr. Bell sensed what stirred within her and said nothing. He knew better than to intrude.

They drove to the Lennard Arms—part inn, part farmhouse—set slightly apart from the road, as if its owner preferred solitude to show. The house faced the village green, where an ancient lime tree stood, its branches thick with shade, its roots cradling the weathered crest of the Lennard family. The door hung open, but no one rushed forward to greet them. When the landlady finally appeared—after they’d already begun unpacking—she welcomed them warmly, as if they were old friends, and apologized for her delay: “It’s hay-time,” she explained, “and I’ve been packing baskets for the men. Didn’t hear the wheels, the road’s soft grass.”

“Why, bless me!” she exclaimed, catching sight of Margaret in the dim parlour. “It’s Miss Hale, Jenny!” she called to her daughter. “Come here quick—it’s Miss Hale!” Then she stepped forward, clasping Margaret’s hand with maternal warmth.

“And how are you all? How’s the Vicar? Miss Dixon? The Vicar above all! God bless him—we’ve never stopped missing him since he left.”

Margaret tried to speak of her father’s death—her mother’s passing, she saw, the woman already knew. But the words caught in her throat. She touched her black dress and whispered, “Papa.”

“Surely not, sir?” Mrs. Purkis turned to Mr. Bell, eyes wide with dread. “A gentleman passed through last winter—maybe spring—who told us about Mr. Hale and Miss Margaret. Said Mrs. Hale had gone—poor lady. But never a word of the Vicar being ill!”

“It is so,” Mr. Bell replied gently. “He died suddenly while visiting me in Oxford. A good man. Many of us would give much to end as calmly as he did. Come, Margaret, my dear—your father was my oldest friend, and you’re my goddaughter. That’s why I thought we’d come together to see the old place. And I know you’ll find comfort here—good rooms, a fine dinner. You don’t remember me, I see, but my name is Bell. I’ve stayed here once or twice when the parsonage was full. Your mother always kept the best ale.”

“To be sure,” Mrs. Purkis said, stepping back. “I beg your pardon—I was so taken with Miss Hale. Let me show you to your room, miss, where you can take off your bonnet and wash your face. Just this morning I dropped fresh roses into the water jug—thought perhaps someone might come. Nothing smells sweeter than rose-scented water in spring. To think the Vicar is gone! Well, we must all die, but that gentleman said he was recovering after Mrs. Hale’s passing.”

“Come down later, Mrs. Purkis,” Mr. Bell said. “After you’ve seen to Miss Hale, I’d like to talk about dinner.”

Margaret’s bedroom had a small casement window almost blocked by climbing vines and roses. But pushing them aside, she could glimpse the tops of the parsonage chimneys rising above the trees, the lines of the roof still familiar through the leaves.

“Aye,” Mrs. Purkis said, smoothing the bed and sending her daughter for lavender-scented towels. “Times have changed, miss. Our new Vicar has seven children, and he’s building a nursery—right where the arbour and tool-house used to stand. New grates, plate-glass windows in the drawing-room. He and his wife are lively folk—do a great deal of good, or so they say. If not, I’d call it turning things upside down for little reason. The new Vicar’s a teetotaller and magistrate. His wife has recipes for saving money—makes bread without yeast. They both talk at once, like a storm, and knock one down. Only when they’re gone, and peace returns, do you realize what you might have said. He’ll be checking the men’s cans in the hayfield—then there’ll be trouble because it’s not ginger-beer. But I can’t help it. My mother and grandmother sent strong ale to the workers, and took salts when ill. I’ll go on in their ways, even if Mrs. Hepworth wants to give me comfits instead of medicine—pleasant, she says, but I don’t trust it. But I must go, miss, though I’d love to hear more.”

Mr. Bell had strawberries and cream, brown bread, milk, and a Stilton cheese waiting for Margaret when she came downstairs—along with a bottle of port for himself. After their simple meal, they set out walking, uncertain which path to choose, so many memories tugged at them.

“Shall we go past the vicarage?” Mr. Bell asked.

“No, not yet,” Margaret said. “Let’s go this way—make a circle, and return that way.”

Here and there, trees had fallen in the autumn before. A rough cottage, once squat and crumbling, had disappeared. Margaret missed each one—each felt like a lost friend. They passed the spot where she and Mr. Lennox had sketched. The great beech tree, scarred by lightning, was gone. The old man who lived in the ruinous cottage was dead. The cottage had been torn down, replaced by a neat, tidy house. A small garden now grew where the tree had stood.

“I didn’t think I’d grown so old,” Margaret murmured, sighing.

“Yes,” Mr. Bell said softly. “It’s the first changes that make time seem mysterious to the young. Later, we grow numb to it. I accept change as natural. Instability is familiar to me. To you, it’s fresh and painful.”

“Let’s go see little Susan,” Margaret said, leading him up a grassy path under the shadow of a forest glade.

“With all my heart,” he replied. “Though I have no idea who little Susan is. But I have a fondness for Susans—for simple Susan’s sake.”

“My little Susan was disappointed when I left without saying goodbye. It’s haunted me—how I might have spared her pain with just a little effort. But it’s far. Are you sure you won detail?”

“Quite sure. Unless you walk too fast. Here, there’s no view worth stopping for. You’d think it romantic to walk with a person ‘fat and scant o’ breath’—if I were Hamlet, Prince of Denmark. Have mercy on my frailties for his sake.”

“I’ll walk slower—for your sake. I like you twenty times better than Hamlet.”

“On the principle that a living ass is better than a dead lion?”

“Perhaps. I don’t analyze my feelings.”

“I’m content to accept your liking me—without dissecting it. But we needn’t crawl along.”

“Very well. Walk at your own pace. I’ll follow. Or stop and meditate, like the Hamlet you compare yourself to—if I go too fast.”

“Thank you. But since my mother hasn’t murdered my father and married my uncle, I wouldn’t know what to ponder—unless it’s whether we’ll have a well-cooked dinner.”

“What do you think?”

“I’m hopeful. She was famed for cooking, according to Helstone opinion.”

“But have you considered the distraction of haymaking?”

Margaret felt his kindness in trying to keep conversation light, to distract her from the weight of memory. Yet she would have preferred silence—perhaps even solitude—over this gentle companionship.

They reached the cottage where Susan’s widowed mother lived. Susan wasn’t there—she’d gone to school. Margaret was disappointed. The woman noticed and began to apologize.

“Oh, it’s quite right,” Margaret said quickly. “I’m glad to hear it. I should have known. She used to stay home.”

“She did,” the woman said. “And I miss her. I taught her what little I knew at night. Not much, to be sure. But she was becoming such a helpful girl—now I miss her sore. But she’s far ahead of me in learning.” She sighed.

“I’m all wrong,” Mr. Bell muttered. “Don’t mind me. I’m a hundred years behind the times. But I’d say the child was getting a better, simpler, more natural education staying home—helping her mother, reading a chapter of the New Testament each night—than any schooling under the sun.”

Margaret didn’t reply—she didn’t want to prolong the debate in front of the mother. Instead, she asked, “How is old Betty Barnes?”

“I don’t know,” the woman said sharply. “We’re not friends.”

“Why not?” Margaret asked—once the village peacemaker.

“She stole my cat.”

“Did she know it was yours?”

“I don’t know. I reckon not.”

“Well, couldn’t you get it back when you told her?”

“No! For she’d burnt it.”

“Burnt it?” Margaret and Mr. Bell gasped.

“Roasted it!” the woman clarified.

It was no explanation. After questioning, Margaret learned the horrifying truth: Betty Barnes, frightened that her husband would discover she’d lent his Sunday clothes to a gipsy fortune-teller (promising they’d be returned before he missed them), resorted to a local superstition. According to belief, the screams of a cat boiled or roasted alive could compel dark forces to grant a wish. So she sacrificed the animal. The woman believed it worked. Her only regret was that her cat had been chosen. Margaret listened in horror, trying to reason with her—failing utterly. She guided the woman through logical steps, but at the end, she simply repeated: “It were cruel, sure. I wouldn’t do it myself. But nothing like it for getting what you want. I’ve heard it all my life. But it were cruel, for all that.”

Margaret gave up, heart sick.

“You’re a good girl not to mock me,” Mr. Bell said.

“How? What do you mean?”

“I admit I’m wrong about schooling. Anything rather than raise that child in such practical paganism.”

“Oh! Poor little Susan. I must see her. Would you mind calling at the school?”

“Not at all. I’m curious to see what she’s learning.”

They walked on in silence, through shaded glens whose soft greenery could not ease the shock of what they’d heard—the cruelty, the utter lack of empathy for suffering.

As they emerged from the woods, the murmur of voices rose like bees in a hive. The school stood on the village green. The door was open. A brisk woman in black greeted them—Mrs. Hepworth, the current Vicar’s wife, and Margaret’s mother’s successor. Margaret hesitated, then stepped forward, meeting bright eyes and whispers: “It’s Miss Hale.”

Mrs. Hepworth smiled warmly. “Your father, I presume, Miss Hale. I see it in the likeness. I’m delighted to see you, sir, and so will the Vicar be.”

Margaret corrected her gently: “It’s not my father. He’s passed. I’m afraid…”

She trailed off, wondering how Mr. Hale could have borne returning, if he’d believed it was his own daughter. She didn’t hear what Mrs. Hepworth said—left it to Mr. Bell to respond. She scanned the room, looking for old faces.

“Ah! I see you’d like to teach a class, Miss Hale,” Mrs. Hepworth said. “First class, stand up for a parsing lesson with Miss Hale.”

Poor Margaret—her visit was sentimental, not inspecting. But seeing the eager faces, some baptized by her father, she sat down, tracing features, holding Susan’s hand briefly, unseen. The class searched for books. Mrs. Hepworth chatted with Mr. Bell, explaining the Phonetic system, showing him a conversation with the Inspector.

Margaret leaned over her book, hearing only the hum of voices. Memories flooded in. Tears filled her eyes—until a girl stumbled over the word “a.”

“A—an indefinite article,” Margaret said softly.

“I beg your pardon,” Mrs. Hepworth snapped, alert. “We are taught by Mr. Milsome to call ‘a’ an—anyone remember?”

“An adjective absolute!” half a dozen voices shouted.

Margaret sat abashed. The children knew more than she did. Mr. Bell turned away, smiling.

She spoke no more during the lesson. Afterward, she visited a few old favorites—girls growing fast, slipping from childhood into youth. She was fading from their memories as quickly as they were from hers. Still, she was glad to see them again—even if sadness tinged her joy.

When school ended, it was early afternoon. Mrs. Hepworth invited them to the parsonage—to see “alterations” (though “improvements” had nearly slipped out). Margaret cared little for the changes—they clashed with her memories. But she longed to see the old house once more, even knowing the pain it would bring.

The parsonage was transformed—inside and out. The garden, once perfectly trimmed, was scattered with children’s toys: marbles, hoops, a straw hat crushed onto a rosebush, snapping a long flowering stem. The hall, once quiet and orderly, now bore signs of boisterous, healthy childhood.

“Ah!” Mrs. Hepworth said. “You must excuse the mess, Miss Hale. Once the nursery is finished, I’ll insist on order. We’re converting your room into a nursery. How did you manage without one?”

“We were only two,” Margaret said. “You have seven, I suppose?”

“Seven. Look—here we’re adding a window facing the road. Mr. Hepworth is spending a fortune. The house was barely livable when we came—for a family like ours, of course.”

Every room was altered. The former study—Mr. Hale’s—was now open to the road. The new window offered views of sheep straying toward the “Jolly Forester.” The Vicar, ever vigilant, watched from his desk, hat and stick ready to chase them back. The whole family was energetic, loud, kind-hearted, but lacking in subtlety. Margaret feared Mrs. Hepworth would notice Mr. Bell’s irony in praising everything that offended him. But no—she took every word seriously. Margaret reproached him as they walked back to the inn.

“Don’t scold, Margaret. It was all for you. If she hadn’t shown every change with such pride, I could have behaved better. But if you must preach, wait till after dinner. It’ll put me to sleep—and aid digestion.”

They were both tired. Margaret, especially, was weary. She no longer wanted to wander through the woods near her childhood home. And somehow, the visit to Helstone had not been what she expected. Change was everywhere—small, but deep. Families shifted with absence, death, marriage. Time moved on, carrying people from childhood to youth, manhood to age, until they fell, ripe, into the earth. Places changed—trees gone, paths narrowed, light streaming where shadows once reigned. Progress, they called it. But Margaret sighed for the old charm, the quiet gloom, the untamed lanes of her youth.

She sat by the window on the little settle, watching twilight gather, matching the quiet ache in her heart. Mr. Bell slept soundly after his long day. Finally, he stirred as the tea tray arrived, brought by a flushed country girl—evidently taking a break from hay-making.
