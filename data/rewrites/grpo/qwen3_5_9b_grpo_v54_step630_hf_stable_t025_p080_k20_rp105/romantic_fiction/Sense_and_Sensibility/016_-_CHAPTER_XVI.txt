Marianne felt that if she had managed even a single hour of sleep the night following her departure from Willoughby, she would have deemed herself utterly inexcusable. Yet, shame held no sway over her; she rose from her bed not merely rested, but drained, her spirit too raw for the composure that would have been required to face her family. The very emotions that rendered such stoicism a disgrace left her immune to the accusation. She had wept through the darkness, waking with a throbbing headache, speechless and repelled by the idea of food. Every attempt at comfort from her mother and sisters met only with a cold refusal, her sensitivity a fortress against their pity.

Once breakfast concluded, she fled the house alone, wandering the village of Allenham. There, she indulged in the ghost of past joys while drowning her present ruin in tears. The evening offered no respite; instead, she turned her grief into a ritual. She played every melody she had once shared with Willoughby, tracing the lines of his handwriting on the music sheets until her heart grew so heavy it could bear no further weight. Hours were lost at the piano, alternating between song and sobbing, her voice often choked by tears. Even in literature, she sought the same bitter contrast, reading only the books they had cherished together, feeding a hunger for misery that seemed insatiable.

For a few days, this violent affliction could not be sustained; it settled into a quieter, deeper melancholy. Yet, her solitary strolls and silent contemplations still summoned waves of sorrow as fierce as before. No letter arrived from Willoughby, and none was anticipated. Her mother, bewildered, found solace in rationalizations, while Elinor's unease grew.

"Remember, Elinor," Mrs. Dashwood said, "how often Sir John retrieves our mail himself. We agreed that secrecy is paramount; if their correspondence passed through his hands, it could not remain hidden."

Elinor saw the logic but felt the weight of the silence pressing down. "Mother," she ventured, "why do you not simply ask Marianne if she is engaged to Willoughby? From you, her beloved and indulgent mother, such a question could not offend. It would stem from love itself. She was once so open, so trusting with you."

"I would never ask such a thing," Mrs. Dashwood replied, her tone firm. "If they are not engaged, what agony would such a query inflict? It would be cruel. I would never deserve her trust again if I forced a confession of what she intends to keep hidden. I know Marianne's heart; she loves me deeply, and she will tell me everything when the time is right. I will not pry from a child, for duty forbids it."

Elinor found this delicacy excessive, given her sister's youth, yet her protests fell on deaf ears. Common sense and prudence were drowned by her mother's romantic idealism.

Days passed before Willoughby's name was spoken before Marianne, save for the sharp wit of Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, whose jokes only deepened her pain. One evening, however, Mrs. Dashwood, picking up a volume of Shakespeare, remarked, "We never finished *Hamlet*, Marianne. Our dear Willoughby left before we could conclude it. We shall wait until he returns..."

"Months!" Marianne cried, startled. "No—nor many weeks."

The remark brought a flicker of hope to Elinor, confirming Marianne's certainty in Willoughby's return.

A week after his departure, Marianne was finally persuaded to join her sisters for a walk, though she had previously avoided all company, slipping away whenever they planned to go to the downs or the valley. Elinor, disapproving of her isolation, insisted. They walked in silence along the road through the valley, Marianne's mind too turbulent for conversation. As they reached the point where the road opened toward Barton, they paused to survey the landscape.

Suddenly, an animated figure appeared—a man on horseback approaching them. Within moments, they recognized him as a gentleman.

"It is he! It is indeed!" Marianne exclaimed, her eyes lighting up, and she hurried forward.

"Elinor!" she cried, "I know it is!"

"Indeed, Marianne, I fear you are mistaken," Elinor interjected, quickening her pace to shield her sister. "That man is not tall enough, nor does he carry himself like Willoughby."

"He has his air! His coat! His horse!" Marianne insisted, walking eagerly ahead.

Elinor kept close behind, certain of her judgment. As they drew within thirty yards, Marianne's heart sank. She turned abruptly to flee, but her sisters' voices called her back. A third voice joined theirs, familiar and beloved, urging her to stay. Marianne turned in surprise to find Edward Ferrars.

He was the only man in the world who could be forgiven for resembling anyone other than Willoughby at that moment, yet he possessed the power to draw a smile from her. She wiped away her tears to greet him, momentarily forgetting her own disappointment in her sister's happiness.

Edward dismounted, handing his horse to a servant, and walked back with them to Barton, having come specifically to visit. He was received with warmth by all, but Marianne displayed a fervor in her greeting that even Elinor did not match. To Marianne, the encounter felt like a continuation of the coldness she had sensed at Norland. Edward, however, offered little warmth. He appeared confused, barely registering the joy of the reunion, neither rapturous nor cheerful. He spoke little, answering only when pressed, and showed no special affection for Elinor. Marianne watched with growing astonishment, a nascent dislike taking root. The contrast between his reserved demeanor and the vibrant manners of her lost lover sharpened her despair.

After a brief pause for greetings, Marianne asked if he had come straight from London.

"No," Edward replied, "I have been in Devonshire for two weeks."

"Two weeks!" Marianne repeated, surprised he had stayed in the county without seeing Elinor.

"I was staying with friends near Plymouth," he explained, looking slightly troubled.

"Have you been to Sussex lately?" Elinor asked.

"Yes, I visited Norland about a month ago."

"And how does dear, dear Norland look?" Marianne asked eagerly.

"Dear, dear Norland," Elinor said dryly, "likely looks much as it always does this time of year. The woods and paths are thick with dead leaves."

"Oh!" Marianne sighed, her voice filled with longing. "With what transporting sensation have I watched them fall! How I delighted to see them driven in showers by the wind! What feelings the season and the air inspire! Now, there is no one to appreciate them. They are seen only as a nuisance, swept away hastily."

"It is not everyone," Elinor noted, "who shares your passion for dead leaves."

"No," Marianne admitted softly. "My feelings are rarely understood. But sometimes they are." As she spoke, she drifted into a reverie, then rallied herself. "Now, Edward," she said, gesturing to the view, "look at Barton Valley. Try to find tranquility. Look at those hills! Have you ever seen their like? To the left is Barton Park, amidst those woods. You can see the end of the house. And there, beneath that distant, grand hill, lies our cottage."

"It is a beautiful country," Edward replied politely. "Though these valleys must be quite muddy in winter."

"How can you speak of mud with such scenery before you?"

"Because," he smiled faintly, "among the objects before me, I see a very dirty lane."

Marianne muttered to herself, "How strange."

"Do you find the neighborhood agreeable? Are the Middletons pleasant people?"

"No, not all," Marianne answered quietly. "We could not be more unfortunately situated."

"Marianne!" Elinor exclaimed. "How can you say that? How unjust! They are a respectable family, Mr. Ferrars, and have treated us with the utmost kindness. Have you forgotten the many pleasant days we owe them?"

"No," Marianne whispered, "nor the many painful moments."

Elinor ignored the comment, turning her attention to Edward and attempting to sustain conversation by discussing their current residence and its amenities. His continued coldness and reserve mortified her deeply. She felt vexed and half-angry, yet she resolved to treat him with the courtesy due to a family connection, suppressing any outward sign of resentment, guided by the past rather than the present.
