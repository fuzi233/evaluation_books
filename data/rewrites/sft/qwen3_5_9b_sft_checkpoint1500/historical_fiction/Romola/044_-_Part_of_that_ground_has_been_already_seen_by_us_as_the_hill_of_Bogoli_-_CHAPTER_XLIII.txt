The Unseen Madonna.

Returning from the hospital, an hour past, Romola chose a path that widened toward the river, distancing herself from the Ponte Vecchio before turning back toward it. Her intention was to hasten to San Stefano and seek Baldassarre, though dread held her back; yet, in abandoning him, she feared she would lose a vital tether to her own existence.

As she neared the junction where the Por' Santa Maria curved to her right and the Ponte Vecchio lay to her left, she stumbled into a throng that suddenly sank to their knees. Without hesitation, Romola joined them. The Great Cross of the Duomo was passing, leading a solemn procession. Having arrived later than anticipated, she was forced to wait until the cross had vanished behind the buildings.

Rising, the shift from kneeling to standing, coupled with the sudden silence, made her acutely aware of her exhaustion. Beside her, a hosier offered kind words: "Madonna Romola, you must be weary. Gian Fantoni would be pleased to offer you a seat in his home; his door is right there. Let me open it. You know he loves God and the Friar as we do. His house is yours."

Accustomed to such fraternal address from citizens whose faces she knew from the Duomo, Romola accepted. For her, the concept of 'home' had shifted away from the lonely solitude of the Via de' Bardi toward the towering circuits of Florence itself, a city that seemed to greet her at every street corner with appeals and friendship. Gladly, she stepped through the open door, guided by the hosier to an upstairs window. There, a stout woman in the plain garb of the Piagnoni, accompanied by three children, received her with profound reverence, seating her amidst bright draperies. From this vantage point, Romola observed not only the procession moving slowly between the houses on the bridge but also the river and the Lung' Arno stretching toward the Santa Trinita.

The procession advanced in sadness and stillness. No wailing chants broke the silence; only the tramp of feet and the soft sweep of woolen robes echoed. First came a long train of Florentine youth, their faces young and unblemished by beard, bearing aloft the white image of the youthful Jesus, crowned with golden glory, standing beside the tall cross where thorns and nails awaited. Following them were the Companies of Discipline, bound by vows of self-chastisement and piety. Their garments concealed heads and faces save for the eyes, transforming individual citizens of all ranks—shopkeepers, accountants, statesmen—into anonymous symbols of a shared vow. Each company wore a color and a badge, but the complete shrouds erased personal identity, leaving only an expression of collective fellowship.

In stark contrast, the multitude of monks stood out as distinct individuals despite their common tonsures and frocks. A white stream of reformed Benedictines flowed first, followed by the vast numbers of the Frati Minori, or Franciscans, clad in grey with knotted cords around their waists and wooden sandals beneath bare feet. Perhaps the largest order in Florence, they were known for their zeal, their love for mankind, and their hatred of the Dominicans. After the grey came the black of the Augustinians of San Spirito, men of cultivated countenances who had inherited Boccaccio's library and formed Florence's most learned circle during an age of scarcity. Then followed the white-over-dark habit of the Carmelites, and finally the unmixed black of the Servites, an order founded by seven merchants who abandoned worldly gain to worship the Divine Mother.

Now, the hearts of the onlookers quickened, beating either with hatred or love. A stream of black and white mantles emerged from the bridge—black cloaks over white scapularies. All knew these were the Dominicans. Those from Fiesole led the way, one black mantle parting another, one tonsured head following another. Suspense hung heavy in the air. The mantles were coarse, many threadbare or ragged, for the Prior of San Marco had reduced his fraternities to the strictest poverty and discipline. Yet, amidst this sea of worn cloth, one mantle stood out, slightly less tattered than the rest, topped by a tonsured head that a stranger might find unremarkable unless they recognized it from bronze medals depicting the Sword of God, or from scenes of his arrival guarded by an armed retinue, or from the inward flame that transformed him as he surveyed a rapt multitude.

As Savonarola drew near, silence reigned absolute. Even those wishing to speak hesitated, lest their voices break the solemn tramp of the feet and the faint rustle of garments. Nevertheless, his ear—and others'—caught a mingled hiss, a sound longing to become curses, intercut with murmurs yearning for blessings. Perhaps the preponderance of hissing caused two or three of his disciples in the foreground to fall to their knees as if divinity itself were passing. This movement of silent homage spread like a subtle shockwave along the streets, leaving some unmoved while compelling others to bow deeply. Yet hatred gathered intensity as well; as Savonarola ascended the Por' Santa Maria, Romola saw someone at an upper window spit upon him.

Monks returned—the Humble Brothers of Ognissanti, glorious workers in the wool trade—and others of the Vallombrosan and Benedictine varieties, their varied forms marking a spiritual renewal long past. Finally, the shaven crowns ended, giving way to the train of untonsured secular priests.

Then followed the twenty-one incorporated Arts of Florence, arrayed in long lines with banners proclaiming their distinct functions, from bakers to judges and notaries. After them marched the secondary officers of state, ascending from lesser to greater, until the line of seculars was broken by the Canons of the Duomo. They carried a sacred relic: the very head of San Zenobio, immortal bishop of Florence, enclosed in silver, whose virtues were believed to have saved the city a millennium prior.

Here lay the nucleus of the procession. Behind the relic marched the archbishop in a gorgeous cope, sheltered by a canopy, followed by the mysterious, hidden Image. First veiled by rich brocade curtains enclosing an outer painted tabernacle, and within that, by an ancient shrine never opened in living memory, lay the Vision of the Pitying Mother. Found ages ago in the soil of L'Impruneta, she had uttered a cry as the spade struck her. Historically, the unseen Image rarely entered the Duomo without lavish gifts preceding it—veils, curtains, mantles offered by emulous men and communities. The richest, it was said, was woven by a poor abbess and her nuns, who, lacking funds, crafted a mantle of gold brocade with their prayers, embroidering it with supplications until two beautiful youths spread white wings and vanished into the blue sky to present it to the Blessed Virgin in the great Piazza.

But today, no gifts preceded the tabernacle. No donations were to be made except to the poor. This was the counsel of Fra Girolamo, whose preaching never urged gifts to invisible powers but only aid for visible need. Altars had been raised at various church points, laden with oblations for the destitute. Not even a torch was carried. Surely, the hidden Mother cared less for torchlight and brocade than for the wail of the hungry. Florence was in extremity; she had done her utmost and could only wait for a divine intervention beyond human power.

Fra Girolamo, the friar in the torn mantle, had promised help would surely come. Many, hearts faint, clung to his word rather than the virtues of the unseen Image. Yet, many fiercely hearted rejoiced secretly, hoping the Friar's promise might prove false.

Slowly the tabernacle moved forward, knees bending in reverence. Profound stillness enveloped the scene, for the train of priests and chaplains from L'Impruneta stirred no passion. The procession was nearing its close with the Priors and the Gonfaloniere. The long train of companies and symbols, possessing a silent music that stirred the mind like a chorus, was fading from view. Now, a faint, yearning hope struggled against the accustomed despondency.

Romola's heart swelled, half with foreboding, half with the enthusiastic fellowship that the last two years had made habitual to her, as natural as clothing to the vain and idle. Giving a deep sigh, as if released from long mental tension, she remained on her knees, overcome by languor. Suddenly, something brightly colored flashed from between the houses onto the distant bridge. Instantly, Romola sprang up, stretching out her arms, leaning from the window. As the black drapery fell from her head, the golden gleam of her hair and the flush of her face seemed illuminated by a single, brilliant light.

A shout erupted simultaneously. The last troops of the procession paused, and all faces turned toward the distant bridge. But the bridge was already passed; the horseman pressed on at full gallop alongside the Arno. His bay horse, streaked with foam, appeared all white from the speed of its motion. Its cap flew loose beside its red *becchetto*, and the rider waved an olive branch in his hand. It was a messenger—a bearer of good tidings! The blessed olive branch spoke far and wide. Yet the impatient people could not wait. They surged to meet the oncomer, seizing his horse's reins, pushing and trampling.
