One day, as Clement lay in his cave, sighing in the depths of despair, a thought struck him like a spark in the dark. If he could carve holy words upon his walls—sacred invocations, prayers, and verses of scripture—then perhaps the very stones around him might become a bulwark against the tempests of his soul. So he seized his chisel, not as a warrior takes up his sword, but as a laborer grips his tool, and with feverish devotion, he began to etch the words of God into the cold rock.

Like the great sculptors of Italy who shaped marble with divine fury, Clement did not pause to sketch his lines. He carved from memory, from the depths of his faith, letting the chisel dance under his hand as if each stroke were a prayer. He begged candle-ends and rancid oil from his few visitors—"Anything that burns," he said—and by night his cave glowed like a forge, casting flickering light upon the sacred words rising from the stone.

One by one, the holy talismans took shape around him, a fortress of scripture against despair. Beneath the great crucifix fastened to his wall, he carved the words of St. Augustine:

*O Christian soul, look upon the wounds of the suffering Christ, the blood of the dying Redeemer, the price paid for thy salvation! Weigh these things in thy mind, that He may be fixed in thy heart as He was fixed upon the cross. Remember His passion, and no earthly trial shall seem too hard to bear.*

The words soothed him, if only a little. Exhausted at last, he fell into a deep sleep. When he awoke, he heard a soft chirping. There, upon his breviary, perched a robin, its bright eyes darting, its feathers fluffed and smoothed in quick succession. Clement held his breath, afraid to frighten it away. The bird hopped onto his bare foot, its tiny claws barely felt, then flitted back to the book. With slow, careful movements, Clement reached for a crumb of bread and flicked it toward the robin. The bird seized the offering and fled, but soon returned for more.

"Bless you, little one," Clement murmured. "You are a ray of light in my darkness."

And so the days passed. The cold deepened, but his heart grew warmer. Despair faded like a retreating shadow. His prayers filled with fervor, his psalms with joy. More birds came, fluttering in from the snow, finding sanctuary with him. One frosty night, as he sang praises to his psaltery, his voice echoing through the hollow cave, he heard a low, mournful howl outside. Peering through the cracks of his door, he saw a great red wolf, its muzzle lifted to the sky, answering his song with its own wild music.

"My sins are leaving me," Clement whispered. "The creatures of God acknowledge me at last."

But peace was not to last. Visions came—dreams so vivid they blurred the line between waking and sleep. At first, they were celestial: saints with rainbow wings, voices like music. Then they shifted. He wandered ruins with Fra Colonna, saw temples rise from dust, heard philosophers speak in forgotten tongues. And always, beneath the marvels, a dread lurked—something waiting, something terrible.

Then, one night, the dream changed. Margaret stood before him, her hair like a halo, her smile tender. "Gerard," she whispered, "by day be whose you will, but at night be mine." He woke trembling, his heart pounding.

The battle had begun anew. He fasted longer, scourged himself till blood ran, plunged into icy streams to numb his flesh. Yet still she came, radiant in his dreams, more real than memory. He knew it was the Enemy's work, a demon wearing her face. The hermits of old had faced such trials—St. Anthony, St. Macarius—all tempted by phantoms of desire.

He fought with every weapon of asceticism: hunger, cold, pain. One night, desperate, he threw himself upon a bed of thorns, rolling until his skin was torn and bleeding. At last, exhausted, he fell into a dreamless sleep.

When he woke, he was victorious—or so he thought. The visions ceased. The birds grew tamer, perching on his hand, allowing him to gild their claws. He dared to hope.

Then, one day, a woman's voice called to him from outside, asking after Gerard. He answered, "Pray for the soul of Gerard, son of Eli," and she was gone. But the voice haunted him. That night, Margaret returned in his dreams, more vivid than ever. He could count the strands of her hair, see the light in her eyes.

"I must leave this place," he whispered. "Memory chains me here."

He took to sleeping in the woods, shivering beneath the stars. One clear, frosty night, he returned to his cell, marveling at the vastness of heaven. "How can men live for this small world," he murmured, "when that great one shines above us?"

He paused at his door. It was closed. He had left it open. A prickle of unease ran through him. Peering through a crack, he saw his small oil lamp still burning.

"Either I shut it," he muttered, "or the fiend has shut it to trouble me."

He pushed inside, lighting a taper with nervous hands. As the flame caught, a sigh whispered through the cave. His pulse leaped. He turned, shielding the light—and there, in the farthest corner, stood Margaret Brandt.
