"Unwatch'd the garden bough shall sway,
The tender blossom flutter down,
Unloved that beech will gather brown,
The maple burn itself away;

Unloved, the sun-flower, shining fair,
Ray round with flames her disk of seed,
And many a rose-carnation feed
With summer spice the humming air;

*       *       *       *       *

Till from the garden and the wild
A fresh association blow
And year by year the landscape grow
Familiar to the stranger's child;

As year by year the labourer tills
His wonted glebe, or lops the glades;
And year by year our memory fades
From all the circle of the hills."

The final day arrived, and the house stood besieged by the chaos of departure. Packing-cases lined the hall, destined for the railway station just beyond the gate, while the once-pristine lawn lay trampled and strewn with straw drifting through the open windows. The rooms themselves seemed to have lost their substance; light poured in harshly through the uncurtained glass, rendering the familiar spaces alien and stark. In Mrs. Hale's dressing-room, the atmosphere was thick with nostalgia. Mrs. Hale and Dixon worked slowly amidst the clothes, pausing frequently to unearth forgotten treasures—relics of the children's youth—and to handle them with a tenderness that betrayed their reluctance to let go. Their progress was negligible, weighed down by the gravity of the moment.

Downstairs, Margaret stood as a pillar of calm amidst the turmoil. She directed the men hired to assist the cook and Charlotte, her presence a steady anchor against the rising tide of emotion. The servants, weeping intermittently, marveled at her composure, convinced that her long residence in London had hardened her heart against the pain of leaving Helstone. They failed to perceive the heavy, crushing ache that pressed against her chest, a weight so profound that only the constant exertion of her senses could prevent her from breaking down entirely. To yield would be to leave her father and mother leaderless; her father was already deep in the vestry, sorting through papers and registers that only he could organize, while Margaret possessed neither the inclination nor the capacity to succumb to strangers or even to the household friends who surrounded her.

Eventually, the four packers retreated to the kitchen for tea, leaving Margaret to move stiffly through the echoing drawing-room and out into the twilight of an early November evening. A soft, lilac-hued mist veiled the world, obscuring but not hiding the landscape, while a robin sang somewhere nearby—perhaps the very bird her father had crafted a shelter for in his study. Leaves, vibrant and golden in the dying light, drifted down, already touched by frost. Margaret walked beneath the pear-tree wall, a path she had last traversed beside Henry Lennox. Here, at this bed of thyme, he had once spoken of the thoughts she must suppress. Her eyes lingered on a late-blooming rose as she recalled the vivid image of carrot leaves he had described, a memory sharp and painful from only two weeks prior.

Where was he now? Likely in London, navigating the same circular routine of Harley Street dinners and social gatherings. Perhaps even as she walked through this damp, decaying garden, he was enjoying a brisk walk in the Temple Gardens, listening to the roar of the city and catching glimpses of the lights rising from the river. The contrast was stark: here, silence reigned, save for the distant creak of a cottage door opening for a weary laborer. Beyond the garden, the forest offered a stealthy, crunching sound—the tread of poachers. Margaret remembered the thrill she had once felt watching them leap silently over the fence under the moonlight, admiring their wild freedom. But tonight, fear gripped her. A branch snapped heavily in the darkness near the tree line. Instinct took over; she sprinted to the window, tapping urgently until Charlotte, startled from her task of securing the house, rushed to the door.

"Let me in! It is only me, Charlotte!" Margaret gasped, her heart hammering against her ribs. Once safely inside, with the windows bolted and the familiar walls enclosing her, she sank onto a packing-case. The room was cold and cheerless, illuminated only by Charlotte's long, unsnuffed candle. Charlotte looked at her with surprise, but Margaret, sensing the concern rather than seeing it, rose with a faint smile.

"I feared you might shut me out entirely," she said softly. "Then I would never have reached the kitchen, and the doors to the lane and churchyard are already locked."

"Oh, miss, I would have missed you instantly," Charlotte replied, her voice trembling slightly. "The men would have needed your direction. I've put tea in Master's study; it is the most comfortable room, so to speak."

"Thank you, Charlotte. You are a kind girl. I shall be sorry to leave you. Please, try to write to me if I can ever offer help or advice. I shall always welcome a letter from Helstone. I will send my address as soon as I know it."

The study was prepared for tea, a warm sanctuary with a blazing fire and unlit candles waiting on the table. Margaret sat on the rug, seeking warmth against the damp chill of the evening and the exhaustion that weighed upon her. She clasped her hands tightly around her knees, her head bowed in a posture of quiet despondency. But when her father's footsteps approached on the gravel outside, she sprang up, shaking her heavy black hair back and wiping away tears she had not realized had fallen.

He entered looking far more broken than she did. She struggled to engage him in conversation, forcing herself to speak of topics she hoped would interest him, though every effort felt like a final exertion.

"Have you walked far today?" she asked, noting his refusal to eat.

"As far as Fordham Beeches. I visited Widow Maltby; she is deeply grieved that we could not say goodbye. She says little Susan has been watching down the lane for days." The mention of the child, waiting in vain because her father could not leave, broke Margaret completely. She sobbed, her heart feeling as though it would shatter. Mr. Hale paced the room, distressed and perplexed.

"I cannot bear it," he muttered, his voice tight with anguish. "I cannot bear to see the suffering of others. I think I could endure my own with patience. Oh, is there no way to go back?"

"No, father," Margaret said, meeting his gaze with steady resolve, her voice low but firm. "It is bad to believe you in error. It would be infinitely worse to have known you a hypocrite." She lowered her voice at the end, as if the mere suggestion of such a thought bordered on irreverence.

"Besides," she continued, "I am merely tired tonight. Do not think I am suffering from what you have done, dear papa. We cannot discuss it tonight, I believe." Tears threatened again, overwhelming her control. "I will take this tea to Mother. She had hers early while I was busy, and she will surely need it now."

By morning, the relentless rhythm of the railroad tore them away from Helstone. They departed before they could truly settle, leaving behind the parsonage, half-hidden by China-roses and pyracanthus, glowing in the morning sun. Almost immediately, they were gone, returning no more. A sharp pang struck Margaret's heart as she tried to catch one last glimpse of the church tower from the train window, but her father remembered her wish too well, claiming the right to the view. She leaned back, closing her eyes, and tears welled, hanging briefly on her lashes before rolling slowly down her cheeks to fall unnoticed upon her dress.

They spent the night in a quiet London hotel. Mrs. Hale wept throughout the day, while Dixon expressed her grief through irritability, constantly chafing against Mr. Hale, whom she blamed for their misery. As they walked through the familiar streets, passing shops and acquaintances, the morning stretched into an eternity. It was the height of the afternoon bustle in November, a stark contrast to their internal stillness. Mrs. Hale, roused like a child, pointed out old landmarks—"Harrison's, where I bought my wedding things"—and exclaimed at the changes, the vast plate-glass windows that dwarfed those of Southampton.

Suddenly, she stopped. "Margaret, we have just passed Mr. Henry Lennox. Where can he be going?"

Margaret surged forward, then recoiled, a half-smile touching her lips at her own sudden movement. They were already a hundred yards away. To her, he remained a relic of Helstone, tied to a bright morning and an eventful day. She would have loved to see him, unseen and unheard, to preserve the memory of him without the complication of their past.

The evening passed in the heavy silence of the hotel room. Mr. Hale ventured out to visit booksellers and friends, but everyone they encountered seemed to be rushing toward some appointment, bound by the city's frantic pace. To them, the world was a whirlwind of purpose; to the Hales, they were strangers and outcasts. Yet, Margaret knew that within a mile lay homes where she and her mother would be welcomed with open arms, provided they came in gladness or peace. But in their current state of sorrow and complicated trouble, they would be perceived as shadows in those houses of intimacy, unable to find the deep, silent sympathy that true friends offered in times of grief.
