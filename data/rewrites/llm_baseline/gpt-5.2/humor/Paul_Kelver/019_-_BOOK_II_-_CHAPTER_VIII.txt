I went out of London with martial music in my ears, the sort you can’t quite tell whether it’s a band or merely your own vanity marching up and down your ribs. A little more than a year later—wet December, fog that tasted of old pennies and river-mud—I came back the way a cat comes back to a scullery: low, quick, and hoping nobody notices the smell.

Defeat is a thing you can repair with work. Disgrace has the awkward habit of sticking like burnt sugar to the bottom of the pan. I returned to London as to a crowd thick enough to pickle a man in anonymity, and I intended—if I am being honest—to waste away there until I had courage for some final, quiet stupidity.

I had been ambitious. I write “had,” as if ambition were a hat one mislaid in a railway carriage and never claimed again. I had wanted a name, a place, and not merely the admiration of the world, but its respect; the sort of respect you imagine is served to you on a clean plate, with a sprig of honour laid across it like parsley. I had meant to be Galahad; I discovered I was—when the heat came on and the fat began to spit—a very poor cook of my own virtues, a man who turned cowardly the moment temptation cracked its whip.

The first sour ingredient in the mess was Urban Vane.

I discovered, not with any dramatic thunderclap, but with the slow certainty of a food historian finding a modern label pasted over an older one, that Vane was a common swindler. The play we had been touring—our precious “Drama, in five acts, by Mr. Horace Moncrieff”—had been stolen from the desk of a well-known dramatist whose acquaintance Vane had made in Deleglise’s kitchen.

Deleglise’s kitchen—yes, that kitchen again, with its clatter of pans and the singing hiss when stock meets a hot stove, that honest orchestra of labour. How absurd that theft should begin in a place where everything is weighed and tasted and watched, where you can tell who has been at the sauce because the spoon lies a quarter-inch wrong. Yet there Vane had insinuated himself, visiting the dramatist while he was ill, sitting by his bed with the patience of a man waiting for a joint to cool so he can carve it without losing the juices. The poor fellow partially recovered, went off to Italy, and was expected—so we all said, with that theatre cheerfulness that calls death “a bad turn”—to die there. Had he done so, the robbery might have passed unchallenged, like a stale joke repeated in a new town.

But word came to us, in some small northern place where rain sounded like thrown peas on window-glass, that he had taken a fresh lease of life and was on his way back to England.

That night, Vane invited me to a bottle of wine and a cigar, as if we were going to discuss lighting cues and royalties. He told me the bald truth with the airy wit of a man sprinkling pepper over somebody else’s dinner. Six months of companionship with Urban Vane had done what damp does to decent bread: if it hadn’t destroyed the roots of what manhood I had, it had certainly made it soft and liable to mould.

At first I stormed. Vane sat and listened, amused, as though I were a kettle obligingly coming to the boil.

“Your language, my dear Kelver,” he said at last, when my stock of invective had simmered down to a thin gruel, “might wound me were I able to accept you as an authority upon this vexed question of morals. With the rest of the world you preach one thing and practise another. I have noticed it so often. The preaching has ceased to interest me.

“You profess to be indignant with me for making use of another man’s ideas. It is done every day. You yourself were quite ready to take credit not due to you. For months we have been travelling with this play: ‘Drama, in five acts, by Mr. Horace Moncrieff.’ Not more than two hundred lines of it are your own—excellent lines, I admit, but they do not constitute the play.”

The thought struck me like the smell of something left too long on the stove: sharp, undeniable, humiliating.

“But you asked me to put my name to it,” I stammered. “You said you didn’t want yours to appear—for private reasons. You made a point of it.”

He waved cigar-smoke aside as if it were a trivial steam from a saucepan. “The man you are posing as would never have put his name to work not his own. You never hesitated; on the contrary, you jumped at the chance of so easy an opening to your career as playwright. My need, as you imagined it, was your opportunity.”

“But you said it was from the French,” I insisted, clinging like a bad cook to a bad recipe. “You’d merely translated it; I adapted it. I don’t defend the custom—but it is the custom. The man who adapts a play calls himself the author.”

“I know,” he said. “It has always amused me. Our sick friend himself—whom I am sure we are both delighted to welcome back to life—has done it more than once, and made a fair profit. Indeed, from internal evidence I am strongly of opinion that this present play is a case in point. Well, chickens come home to roost: I adapt from him. What is the difference?”

He poured himself more wine with the calm of a man salting his own dish. “Simply this: that whereas, owing to the anomalous state of copyright laws, stealing from the foreign author is legal and commendable, against stealing from the living English author there is a certain prejudice.”

“And the consequences,” I ventured, “may be unpleasant.”

He laughed—a rare sound from him, and all the more disquieting for being genuine. “You mean, my dear Kelver, that you will.”

I stared. He leaned forward, enjoying my confusion the way some men enjoy listening for the snap of a crust they know they’ve overbaked.

“Don’t look so dumbfounded. You cannot be so stupid as you are pretending. The original manuscript at the Lord Chamberlain’s office is in your handwriting. You knew our friend as well as I did, and visited him. Why, the whole tour has been under your management. You have arranged everything—most excellently; I have been quite surprised.”

In that moment my anger was too far away to reach. Fear took up all the room, like a horrible industrial noise that drowns out every softer sound in the mind. I could hear my own blood battering at my ears, as if some unseen drummer had set up in my skull.

“But you told me,” I began. “It was only a matter of form—that you wanted to keep your name out of it because—”

He looked at me with real astonishment, and my own words began to sound absurd, like a sentimental speech delivered over the clatter of plates in a cheap restaurant.

“I am sorry,” he said. “Really sorry. I took you for a man of the world. I thought you merely did not wish to know anything.”

Still—God forgive me—fear was uppermost.

“You’re not going to put it all on to me?” I said, and I hate myself for the pleading.

He rose and laid his hand on my shoulder. Instead of knocking it away, I was grateful for its pressure, as a man in a loud, crowded street is grateful for any touch that seems human.

“Don’t take it so seriously,” he said. “He will merely think the manuscript has been lost. As likely as not he will be unable to remember whether he wrote it or merely thought of writing it. No one in the company will say anything: it isn’t their business.

“We must set to work. I altered it a good deal before you saw it, and changed all the names. We will retain the third act: it is the only thing of real value. The situation is not original; you have as much right to dish it up as he had. In a fortnight we will have the whole thing so different that if he saw it himself he would only imagine we had got hold of the idea and forestalled him.”

Dish it up. That phrase—culinary, domestic—made the crime sound as homely as reheated stew. And perhaps that is how evil gets you: not with horns and sulphur, but with the tone you use when you explain how to use yesterday’s bones for today’s broth.

There were moments in those weeks when my better self—my good angel, if you like old names—spoke plainly. I should go to the dramatist and tell him the truth. I could see it as clearly as a recipe written in honest ink.

But Vane never left my elbow. If I confessed, he hinted, he would deny me, and my tale would be treated as the frightened invention of a young rogue trying to wriggle off a hook. At best I should wear suspicion like a greasy apron for the rest of my career. At worst—well, there are reputations that are murdered in broad daylight and nobody calls it a crime.

Besides, what harm had we done? So Vane argued, and I, already softened, let the arguments enter. A play presented in twenty little towns, soon forgotten. Most plays were “something like.” Our friend would produce his version and reap a rich harvest; ours would vanish. If there was talk, it would advertise him. We’d replace it quickly with a new piece. A young man of my genius could surely write something better than this hotch-potch; all I lacked was experience. And as for conscience—wasn’t “honesty” merely a convention, a word in a dominant lexicon used to police the weak? The world’s real commandment was not “Be good,” but “Don’t get found out.”

So I fell—less like a man falling heroically from a cliff, more like a thing sinking in thick mud, inch by inch.

We worked. Feverishly. Vane had brains; I had diligence and the sort of imagination that can be bent to any purpose, like a good knife in the wrong hand. I tried to persuade him to let me write a new play altogether, offering to give it him for nothing—anything to escape the taste of theft that was always in my mouth, bitter as burnt coffee. He thanked me warmly but declined, his professed faith in my talent never quite robust enough to risk his own profits.

“Later on, my dear Kelver,” he said. “For the present this is doing very well. Going on as we are, we shall soon improve it out of all recognition, while at the same time losing nothing essential. All your ideas are excellent.”

By the end of three weeks we had made a concoction that, in dialogue and character, might pass as our own. Here and there it was good work. In other circumstances I might have been proud. As it was, I felt only the thief’s tight-lipped listening, always expecting the footstep on the stair.

There was one speech in the play—addressed by the hero to the villain—“Yes, I admit it; I do love her. But there is that which I love better—my self-respect!” Our juvenile lead—who in life led a rabbit-like existence, always diving into holes to avoid writs for alimony—would step down to the footlights, slap his chest (stage convention makes the male torso a sort of moral larder crammed with virtues), and bring down the house.

Every night, as the applause rolled over that line like surf over a rock, I would shudder. I remembered writing it with cheeks hot, not from inspiration but from shame—shame that felt like being caught licking the spoon when you’ve been told the pudding is not yours.

There was also a character—a vicious old man—whom Vane had from the beginning wanted me to play. I had refused then, choosing instead a high-souled countryman, the kind of part in which a young fool can preen his virtue in public. Now Vane renewed the argument. My power of resistance had become thin as watered soup. I accepted the exchange.

The old man’s scenes had more snap, and I found, to my further degradation, that I could get a coarse laugh from the audience by giving the role all the ugliness we dared. Under an older actor the effect might have been harmless, but a young man’s nature takes impressions as fresh dough takes a stamp. I found myself enjoying the laughter that rewarded my suggestive leers. I began to call up all the evil in me as if it were a useful spice. Vane praised me, helped me, urged me to make the part nastier. I had genius, he said; I understood human nature. One proof of my deterioration was that I was beginning to like him.

Looking back now, I see him plainly: a very ordinary scamp, his pretensions not even amusing. But then he seemed, to my boyish mind, a superior being by some freak of fate compelled to live among inferiors. He affected a contempt for conventions as though decency were merely a suburban accent. Every decent instinct was, in his vocabulary, “bourgeois,” and therefore unintellectual. It was the first time I had heard that particular dialect—this clever, fashionable language that makes vice sound like art and sincerity sound like a shopkeeper counting coppers.

He talked of Hedonism with the air of discovering a new continent. Pan, coaxed from dark groves, was to sit upon Olympus. There was no evil, only Nature; repression was for dullards; advancement lay through joyous expression of the animal.

My lower nature—always hungry, always eager for permission—rose up and accepted his nonsense as gospel. Boys are impressed by such talk; they do not yet know how often it is merely an excuse for bad manners.

And yet, villains who do wrong for the aesthetic pleasure of it belong more properly to novels than to life. I think Vane believed much of the rubbish he spoke. Since then, I hear, he has turned respectable: an excellent husband and father, a considerate master, a delightful host. I can almost see him now carving a joint with propriety, smiling at his guests, and never once tasting the bitterness he fed to others. I prefer to think of him not as devilish but as foolish. It grants him no grandeur.

Our leading lady was handsome and coarse, over-developed, a creature of loud health and hotter hands than were pleasant. She had begun as a music-hall singer, then married a small tradesman in the south of London. Three or four years before, her Juno-like charms had turned the head of a youthful novelist—refined and sensitive, from whom great things had been expected. He ran away with her and married her; the scandal was still fresh. She had already repented. Such women look on lovers as rungs of a ladder; he had not carried her up as far as she expected.

Under her spell his ambition had collapsed like a soufflé in a draught. He no longer wrote; he no longer cared for anything but his own debasement. He was with us in the company, playing small parts badly, and would have stayed as bill-poster rather than be sent away. It was a domestic tragedy performed nightly in the wings, unheard above the public laughter—the acoustic ecology of misery: the soft, constant drip of a ruined talent, drowned out by clapping.

Vane planned to bring her and me together. To her he represented me as a young gentleman of means, a coming author who would soon earn enough to keep her in luxuries. To me he hinted that she had fallen in love with me. I was never attracted to her by any deeper feeling than one has for a handsome animal. It was my vanity he worked upon: he envied me; any man would envy me; I only needed experience. Great intellects must learn all lessons, even if the lesson involves other people bleeding.

As years earlier I had painfully trained myself to like cigars and whisky—as if bitterness on the tongue were a credential for literature—so I now attempted to cultivate a taste for passion. Vane supplied me with novels and histories in which all “men of note” were sad dogs. That I had neither instinct nor inclination for being a sad dog struck my idiot self as a defect. Her languishing glances irritated rather than inflamed me; her covert pressure of her hot, thick hand left me cold. I felt reproached by my own lack of appetite, as though manhood were measured by what you can swallow.

I would fall in love with her. Surely my blood was as red as other men’s. Besides—was I not an artist, and was not profligacy the hallmark of the artist?

But I grow tired of confession, and Fate—who sometimes acts like a brusque, practical cook snatching your hand away from the flame—saved me from completing the part Vane had written for me in that vulgar comedy. She was rough, but the mud was creeping upward fast, and a quick hand must needs be rough.

Our dramatic friend—the one who had actually written the stolen play—produced his version sooner than we expected. It crept out that something very like it had been seen in the provinces. Talk began, and enquiries. Vane said it would blow over. But the wind seemed to be turning our way, and it carried with it an ominous sound, like distant carts on a cobbled road: the approaching rumble of consequences.

As a rule the salaries were paid by me on Friday night. Vane would bring me the money during the evening for me to distribute after the performance. We were in the north of Ireland. I had not seen Vane all day. As soon as I had changed, I went to look for him. The box-office keeper met me and put a note in my hand.

It was short, neat, and to the point—like the slash of a knife across a string.

Vane had pocketed the evening’s takings and left by the seven-fifty train. He regretted the inconvenience; life was full of little comedies; the wise man attached no seriousness to them. We should probably meet again and laugh over our experiences.

I looked up from the letter to find myself ringed by faces—hard, suspicious, suddenly hungry. Rumour had gone ahead of the truth, as it always does. With dry lips I told them what had happened. They did not believe me. Vane had contrived that, to them, I was the manager, the responsible man. My tired explanations sounded like lies, and perhaps they were: not lies of fact, but lies of character—because my character had indeed agreed to this mess.

They talked of giving me “a damned good hiding.” I doubt whether I would have known by morning if they had. A dull apathy had settled on me; the whole scene felt stupid, ridiculous, like some farce played too long. It dawned on me that they had known, all along, the true history of the play, the reason for the sudden alterations. They had saved their virtuous indignation for the moment it could be most useful to themselves.

Someone suggested I was drunk—whether kindly or not, I cannot tell. It was accepted. No further trains left the town that night. They allowed me to go, with a promise that a deputation would visit my lodgings early in the morning.

Our leading lady had left the theatre the moment the curtain fell; she did not need to wait, her husband acting as her business man. When I reached my rooms, I found her sitting by the fire.

It reminded me that our agent in advance had fallen ill, and her husband, at her suggestion, had been appointed in his place, leaving on Wednesday to prepare the next town. I supposed she had come for money; the thought amused me, in a weary way.

“Well?” she said, with her one smile. I had been labouring for months to see it as soul-consuming, but it never quite succeeded.

“Well,” I said.

“You don’t seem overjoyed to see me. What’s happened?”

I laughed. “Vane’s bolted and taken the week’s money with him.”

“The beast!” she said. “I knew he was that sort. What ever made you take up with him? Will it make much difference to you?”

“It makes a difference all round,” I said. “There’s no money to pay any of you. There’s nothing to pay your fares back to London.”

She rose. “Here, let me understand. Are you the rich mug Vane’s been representing you to be, or only his accomplice?”

“The mug and the accomplice both,” I answered, “without the rich. It’s his tour. He put my name to it because he didn’t want his to appear—for family reasons. It’s his play; he stole it—”

She whistled. “I thought it looked a bit fishy, all those alterations. But funny things do happen in this profession! Stole it, did he?”

“The whole thing. I put my name to it for the same reason.”

She dropped back into her chair and laughed—loud, long, good-tempered. “Well, I’m damned!” she said. “The first man who ever took me in. I should never have signed if I’d thought it was his show. I could see the sort he was with half an eye.”

She sprang up. “Here, let me get out of this. I just looked in to know what time to-morrow; I’d forgotten. You needn’t say I came.”

Her hand on the door, she was seized again with laughter and had to lean against the wall.

“Do you know why I really did come?” she said. “You’ll guess when you come to think it over, so I may as well tell you. It’s a bit of a joke. I came to say ‘yes’ to what you asked me last night. Have you forgotten?”

I stared at her. Last night felt years away, like a meal eaten in another life.

She laughed again. “So help me, if you haven’t. Well, you asked me to run away with you—that’s all, to let our two souls unite. Damned lucky I took a day to think it over! Good-night.”

“Good-night,” I said, without moving. I gripped a chair because a furious impulse came over me to thrust her out and lock the door. Not from desire—Heaven knows—but from the intolerable noise of other people in my misery. I wanted silence. I wanted to hear nothing but my own thoughts, and even those were too loud.

I heard her turn the handle.

“Got a pound or two to carry you over?” The voice was purely practical now; women can do that turn as quickly as snapping shut a cupboard door.

I put my hand in my pocket. “One pound seventeen,” I said, counting it. “It will pay my fare to London—or buy me a dinner and a second-hand revolver. I haven’t quite decided yet.”

“Oh, you get back and pull yourself together,” she said. “You’re only a kid. Good-night.”

I put a few things into a small bag and walked thirty miles that night into Belfast. There is a particular sound to walking in the dark with disgrace for company: your own footsteps, too loud; the wind in hedges, whispering like people at the back of a theatre; and, far off, the steady industrial hum of a world that does not care whether you live or die, only that you do not impede the traffic.

In London I took lodgings in Deptford, where I was least likely to meet any face from the old life. I kept myself alive by giving singing lessons at sixpence the half-hour, evening lessons in French and German—may the Lord forgive me—to ambitious shop-boys at eighteenpence a week, and by making up tradesmen’s books. A few pieces of jewellery I had retained helped over bad patches. For four months I existed there and hardly went beyond the neighbourhood.

The streets in that part of London have their own cuisine of odours: frying fat that never quite freshens, damp bricks, coal smoke, stale beer, and the river’s low, mouldy breath. At night there were other sounds—shouting, cart-wheels, the clink of bottles, the thin crying of infants, the harsh, mechanical laughter of men who have forgotten what joy sounds like. The sonic landscape pressed on the nerves like constant pounding on a door. It is difficult to be good in a place where quiet has died.

Sometimes, wandering listlessly, an object or a vista would strike me as familiar—as if memory were a smell caught unexpectedly in a cold alley: for an instant vivid, then gone. Then I would turn and hurry back into my dim, weltering grave of streets.

Of thoughts and emotions during those days I seemed unconscious. Somewhere in me they may have been stirring and contending; but I lived as in a long, dull dream. I ate and drank and woke and slept and walked and walked, and lounged at corners staring for hours, seeing nothing.

It surprises me now how clearly the mind can photograph what the heart refuses to feel. Tragedies, dramas, farces were played before me in that teeming underworld—domestic rows, drunken reconciliations, children bargaining for crusts, women haggling over scraps as if they were relics of a lost language of plenty. They stand in my memory complete and distinct; yet I have no recollection of ever having seen them at the time, as if my eyes were open only to the spectacle and not to the meaning, and as if the meaning, like a dialect nobody speaks any more, had died in my mouth.
