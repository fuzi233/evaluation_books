I located my mistress in her private sitting room. Upon mentioning that Sergeant Cuff wished to address her, she startled visibly, her expression shifting instantly to annoyance.

"Must I see him?" she demanded sharply. "Cannot you represent me, Gabriel?"

I was at a loss to comprehend this request and betrayed my confusion openly in my countenance. My lady, perceiving my bewilderment, kindly clarified her position. "I fear my nerves are somewhat shaken," she admitted. "There is something in that police officer from London that makes me recoil—I cannot explain why. I possess a presentiment that he is carrying trouble and misery right into this house. It is very foolish, and entirely unlike *me*, but such is the reality."

I struggled for words. In truth, the more I observed Sergeant Cuff, the more I admired him. However, my mistress rallied quickly, displaying the natural courage of a woman of high spirit. "If I must see him, I must," she declared. "Yet, I cannot bring myself to face him alone. Bring him in, Gabriel, and remain here while he speaks."

This marked the first onset of the "megrims"—her recurring headaches—since her youth. I returned to the boudoir where Mr. Franklin was strolling toward the garden to join Mr. Godfrey, whose departure was imminent. Sergeant Cuff and I proceeded directly to my mistress's chamber. I confess, my lady paled noticeably upon seeing him, though she otherwise composed herself with remarkable dignity. She requested that the Sergeant have no objection to my presence, noting that as her trusted adviser and longtime servant, I was the most logical person to consult regarding household matters. The Sergeant graciously accepted, stating that since he intended to discuss the staff generally and had already found my experience useful, my attendance was a favor.

My lady indicated two chairs, and our conference commenced immediately.

"I have already formed an opinion on this case," Sergeant Cuff announced, requesting permission to withhold it for the present. "My immediate purpose is to report what I discovered upstairs in Miss Verinder's sitting-room and to outline my proposed course of action."

He detailed the smear on the paintwork and the deductions drawn from it, echoing his earlier explanation to Superintendent Seegrave but with greater decorum. "One fact is certain," he concluded. "The diamond is missing from the drawer in the cabinet. A second probability is nearly absolute: the marks from the smear must belong to an article of clothing worn by someone within this house. Before we proceed further, we must identify that garment."

"And does that discovery," my mistress interjected, "presumably imply the identification of the thief?"

"I beg your ladyship's pardon; I do not claim the diamond is stolen. Only that it is missing. Locating the stained garment may provide the path to recovering it."

She glanced at me. "Do you understand this?"

"Sergeant Cuff understands it, my lady," I replied confidently.

"How do you propose to locate the stained dress?" she asked, turning her gaze back to the Sergeant.

"The other officers have, I regret to say, already searched the servants' boxes and rooms," Cuff noted with apparent shame. "I neither can nor will allow them to be insulted in such a manner a second time!"

(Indeed, there was a mistress to serve! One could argue she was a woman among ten thousand.)

"That is precisely the dilemma I intended to raise," the Sergeant continued. "The initial officer caused significant damage to this inquiry by allowing the servants to realize they were suspects. If I provoke suspicion a second time, the obstacles they might erect—particularly the women—are unpredictable. Nevertheless, their wardrobes *must* be searched again. The first search sought the diamond; the second must seek the stain. I fully agree with your ladyship that the servants' feelings deserve consideration, yet I am equally convinced their wardrobes require inspection."

This appeared to be a deadlock. My mistress expressed her frustration in refined language.

"I have devised a plan to resolve this impasse," Sergeant Cuff offered, provided my lady consented. "I propose explaining the situation to the servants."

"The women will feel accused immediately," I interrupted.

"They will not, Mr. Betteredge," the Sergeant countered smoothly, "if I inform them I intend to examine the wardrobes of *everyone* who slept in the house on Wednesday night—from your ladyship downward. It is merely a formality," he added, casting a sidelong glance at my mistress, "yet the servants will perceive it as equitable treatment. Rather than hindering the investigation, they will likely uphold it as a matter of honor."

I recognized the logic. My lady, once her initial shock subsided, agreed. "Are you certain the investigation is necessary?" she asked.

"It is the most direct route to our objective, my lady."

Rising to summon her maid, my mistress declared, "You shall speak to the servants with the keys to my wardrobe in your hand."

Sergeant Cuff halted her with an unexpected query. "Shall we not first ensure that the other ladies and gentlemen in the house consent as well?"

"The only other lady is Miss Verinder," my mistress replied, surprised. "The gentlemen are my nephews, Mr. Blake and Mr. Ablewhite. There is no likelihood of refusal from any of them."

At this juncture, I reminded my lady that Mr. Godfrey was departing. As I spoke, Mr. Godfrey knocked at the door to bid farewell, followed closely by Mr. Franklin, who was accompanying him to the station. My lady explained the predicament, but Mr. Godfrey resolved it instantly. Calling to Samuel through the window to retrieve his portmanteau, he handed the key directly to Sergeant Cuff.

"My luggage may follow me to London once the inquiry concludes," he stated.

The Sergeant accepted the key with a polite apology. "I apologize for causing you any inconvenience, sir, for what amounts to a mere formality; however, the example set by those above will undoubtedly ease the servants' acceptance of this search."

After bidding my lady a deeply sympathetic farewell, Mr. Godfrey departed, leaving a message for Miss Rachel that clearly signaled his intent to pursue the marriage proposal again at the earliest opportunity. Following his cousin out, Mr. Franklin informed the Sergeant that all his belongings were open to scrutiny and held no locks. Sergeant Cuff offered his warmest thanks.

As observed, the views of the Sergeant had been met with exceptional readiness by my lady, Mr. Godfrey, and Mr. Franklin. Only Miss Rachel remained to align herself before we assembled the servants to commence the search for the stained dress. Yet, my mistress's inexplicable aversion to the Sergeant seemed to render our conference increasingly unpleasant the moment we were left alone.

"If I send you down Miss Verinder's keys," she said to him, "do you presume I have fulfilled all your requirements for the present?"

"I beg your ladyship's pardon," Cuff responded. "Before we begin, I should like, if possible, to inspect the washing-book. The stained item may be linen. Should the search yield nothing, I wish to account for every piece of linen in the house and every load sent to the washerwoman. If an article is missing, there will be at least a strong presumption that it bore the paint stain and was deliberately destroyed yesterday or today by its owner." Turning to me, he added, "Superintendent Seegrave directed the attention of the female servants to the smear when they crowded into the room on Thursday morning. That, Mr. Betteredge, may prove to be another of Superintendent Seegrave's errors."

My lady instructed me to ring the bell for the washing-book, remaining seated in case the Sergeant required further access. The book was delivered by Rosanna Spearman. The girl had descended to breakfast that morning looking wretchedly pale and haggard, though sufficiently recovered from her recent illness to perform her duties. Sergeant Cuff scrutinized our second housemaid intently—her face upon entry, her stooped shoulder as she exited.

"Do you have anything further to say to me?" my lady asked, eager to escape the Sergeant's company.

The great Cuff opened the washing-book, comprehended it thoroughly within thirty seconds, and closed it again. "I venture to trouble your ladyship with one final question," he said. "Has the young woman who brought us this book served as long as the others?"

"Why do you ask?" my lady queried.

"The last time I encountered her," the Sergeant replied, "she was imprisoned for theft."

Truthfully, there was no choice but to disclose the facts. My mistress emphasized Rosanna's exemplary conduct and the high regard she held among the matrons at the Reformatory. "You do not suspect her, I hope?" my lady added, her tone earnest.

"I have already informed your ladyship that I suspect no one in this house of theft—at least, not at this moment."

With that assurance, my lady rose to ascend the stairs to retrieve Miss Rachel's keys. The Sergeant preceded me in holding the door open, bowing low. My lady shuddered as she passed him. We waited, and waited, but the keys never arrived. Sergeant Cuff made no comment to me. Instead, he turned his somber face to the window, shoved his lanky hands into his pockets, and hummed "The Last Rose of Summer" softly to himself.
