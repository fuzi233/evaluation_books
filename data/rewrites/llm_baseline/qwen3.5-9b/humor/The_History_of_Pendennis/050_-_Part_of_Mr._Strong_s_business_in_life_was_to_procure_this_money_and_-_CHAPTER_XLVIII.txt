Costigan's arrival failed to rouse Pen from his slumber, nor did any hostile missive from Mr. Huxter disturb his peace. When Arthur Pendennis finally opened his eyes, he found himself buoyed by a vitality far exceeding the usual groggy awakening of a weary Londoner.

Consider the City man: as sleep vanishes, the specters of 'Change and the counting-house immediately seize him, demanding his attention before breakfast. Consider the lawyer: dawn brings not hope, but the crushing weight of the day's case and the looming deadline for the attorney who expects his papers by nightfall. Who among us does not feel this immediate, heavy anxiety the moment consciousness returns? Yet, this anxiety is also a "kind strengthener," a divine ordinance that grants rest precisely to fuel labor.

Mr. Pendennis, however, possessed a different constitution. His labors held little fascination for him. The thrill of literary creation fades quickly in the mind of a hired hand, just as the joy of seeing one's name in print lasts only until the third issue. Pegasus, once yoked to the daily grind of the stage, becomes as mundane as any hack horse, requiring a whip or a fresh ear of corn to move. Thus, Arthur performed his duties at the *Pall Mall Gazette*—now bolstered by a novelist's salary—with zero enthusiasm. He wrote adequately, occasionally brilliantly, but mostly mechanically. He was a literary sprinter, naturally fast and flashy, yet devoid of endurance.

Society offered him neither amusement nor comfort. Despite his boasts, he was too young for the refined company of women, which seems attainable only when a man stops worrying about his own image and ceases to view romance as a conquest. Simultaneously, he was too young to be an equal among established men, whose conversations he could only eavesdrop upon, and too old for the reckless revelry of his peers. Destined to be largely solitary, fate had dealt him a hand many envy in practice but few enjoy in theory. Pendennis accepted this solitude with surprising equanimity, though he grumbled about it incessantly.

"What a lovely, artless creature that was," Pen murmured to himself immediately upon waking, recalling the Vauxhall incident. "Such a natural manner! How infinitely preferable to the calculated coquetry of the ballroom belles!" His mind drifted to Miss Blanche and the stiff, artificial graces of polite society. "Who imagined a rose could bloom in a porter's lodge, or flourish in that dreary pot at Shepherd's Inn? Does she learn to sing from Old Bows? If her voice matches her charm, it must be exquisite. I adore those low, veiled tones. 'What would you like me to call you?' Indeed, poor little Fanny! It tempted me to strike a grand pose and demand she call me 'Sir.' But no nonsense of that sort—no Faust and Marguerite drama for me. That old Bows! He taught her to sing, didn't he? A dear soul in those antique garb, a true philosopher with a kind heart. He was wonderful during the Fotheringay affair. Men have their sorrows, too. I must cultivate Old Bows. One sees too much of genteel circles; besides, there's nobody in town anyway. Yes, I shall visit Bows and Costigan. What a rich character Costigan is! Begad, I'll study him for a novel."

With Saturday arriving—a day of leisure for the *Gazette*, allowing contributors a rare reprieve from ink and brainwork—Pendennis decided to act on his impulse. He would visit Shepherd's Inn, ostensibly to see Old Bows.

In truth, even if Arthur Pendennis had been the most cunning rogue or the most artful seducer in history, he could not have devised a finer strategy to captivate poor Fanny Bolton than the one he employed the previous night. His dandified demeanor, his air of protection, his conceit mixed with genuine generosity, and his inherent honesty—all factors that allowed him to gently rebuff her trembling advances without crushing her spirit—conspired to make her adore him. His flaws and virtues worked in tandem.

If one could peek into Fanny's bedchamber—a cramped cupboard shared with two younger sisters currently being fed gingerbread and apples by Mr. Costigan—one might find the poor girl tossing restlessly on her thin mattress. Her dreams were filled with the delights of that eventful night, replaying every glance, word, and gesture of Arthur, her splendid hero. Fanny, having consumed countless novels in secret through the circulating library managed by Miss Minifer alongside her millinery trade, viewed Arthur as the living embodiment of every gallant in those greasy, thrilling volumes.

Pen was undeniably a dandy regarding shirts and accessories. Fanny had gazed with awe at the fineness of his linen, the brilliance of his studs, the elegance of his cambric handkerchief, and the gleaming whiteness of his gloves. Even his boots possessed a jet-black radiance. The Prince had arrived to subjugate the humble handmaid. His image haunted her restless sleep: the tone of his voice, the blue fire in his eyes, the look of benevolent pity, the protective smile, and the winning laugh. She felt his arm around her, saw him filling that delicious glass of champagne.

She contrasted him with her peers: Emma Baker, proud of her engagement to a cheesemonger in a white apron near Clare Market; Betsy Rodgers, fussing over her young man, an attorney's clerk who carried a bag.

At approximately two o'clock, after the Bolton family had finished dinner (with Mr. B., the porter, absent in the countryside attending to the Countess of Estrich's hearse for Messrs. Tressler), a figure appeared beneath the inn archway. A gentleman in a white hat and white trousers halted at the wicket. Fanny was not merely surprised; she was overwhelmed with delight and blushes. She knew it was Him. There stood His Royal Highness, beaming from the gate.

"Mamma, mamma!" she cried, running to open the wicket, shoving aside her siblings. How red her cheeks turned as she handed him her hand! How gracefully he removed his hat as he entered, drawing stares from the children. He asked Mrs. Bolton if she had rested well after last night's exertions and expressed concern for any headaches. Then, feigning chance, he remarked that since he was passing, he couldn't leave without checking on his "little partner."

Mrs. Bolton, initially shy and wary of such advances, melted under the torrent of Pen's inexhaustible good humor. Unable to perceive any unwelcome intent, she welcomed him. Seeking a seat, Pen found none free—covered by dishes or workboxes—so he perched atop a child's chair, an uncomfortable eminence that sent the children into fits of laughter. Fanny, however, was the most amused, marveling at His Royal Highness's condescension. To sit in a child's chair! Years later, she would gaze at that very piece of furniture, her imagination populating it with the smiling face of the man who had sat there.

Pen settled in and launched into a voluble monologue. He inquired about the undertaking business, the number of mourners accompanying Lady Estrich, and the state of the inn. He showed keen interest in Mr. Campion's cab, mentioning he'd met the driver in society. He asked about shares in the Polwheedle and Tredyddlum, whether Mrs. Bolton rented chambers there, and if any were available. He declared the inn superior to the Temple and expressed a desire to reside there permanently.

Turning to the local notables, he asked about Captain Strong and Colonel Altamont. "Wasn't he a stout man?" Pen queried with immense wit, describing the Colonel's jewelry, wig, and "very black" whiskers—which he jokingly described as "blue black," or a rich greenish-purple. This description elicited hysterical giggles from the ladies. Pen claimed to have met the Colonel at Sir Fr——'s house.

"Oh, we know," the ladies replied. "Sir F—— is Sir Francis Clavering; he visits twice weekly with the Captain. My little boy fetched bill-stamps for him." Mrs. Bolton suddenly realized she had breached confidence. "O Lor! I beg pardon, I shouldn't have mentioned no secrets," she stammered, completely charmed by Pen's manner. "But we know you're a gentleman, Mr. Pendennis, for I'm sure you've behaved like one. Hasn't Mr. Pendennis, Fanny?" Fanny loved her mother for this endorsement, casting up her dark eyes to the low ceiling. "Oh, that he has, I'm sure, Ma," she replied with deep feeling.

Curious about the bill-stamps and Strong's chambers, Pen pressed further. When Altamont arrived to join the group, Pen questioned him about his identity and social connections with practiced adroitness, driven by private motives regarding Sir Francis Clavering. The answers were simple and honest, limited by the Boltons' modest understanding of high society.

When Pen found himself at a loss for topics, he invoked his status as a journalist. "Would you ladies like any orders for the play?" he asked. Theater tickets were the ultimate delight for anyone connected to the stage. With Mr. Bolton often away due to his drinking habits and unbecoming behavior, the family longed to escape to the theater, leaving little Barney to guard the lodge.
