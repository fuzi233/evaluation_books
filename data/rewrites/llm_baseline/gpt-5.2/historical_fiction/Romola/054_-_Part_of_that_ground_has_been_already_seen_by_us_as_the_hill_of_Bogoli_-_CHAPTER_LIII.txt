“I would speak with you,” Baldassarre said, when Romola’s eyes at last met his without shrinking.

It was plain he had followed her out of Santa Maria del Fiore and waited, like a shadow that dared not cross the light. The air around them held the day’s last heat—stone warmed through, incense long since thinned—yet his voice had the dry rasp of someone who had breathed too much dust.

“Yes,” Romola answered, with the same meek steadiness with which she might have taken on a hard penance. She felt, in the very muscles of her throat, that she was about to swallow something bitter and must not gag.

“But you would go where no one can hear us?” she added.

“Where he will not come upon us,” Baldassarre murmured, darting a glance behind him as if the very paving-stones carried messages. “Out—in the air—away from the streets.”

“I sometimes go to San Miniato at this hour,” Romola said. “If you will, I will go now. Follow me. It is far; but there we may be solitary.”

He nodded—quickly, hungrily—and she set out at once, choosing the long climb rather than any alley where voices echoed and men’s shoulders brushed too close. To many a woman it would have seemed a peril to lead so wild-looking a stranger toward loneliness; but Romola’s life had lately taught her that danger did not always announce itself with a knife or a shout. Sometimes it came sweetened, served up like sugared almonds at a wedding feast, and only later did the mouth learn it had been fed on poison.

They went upward through the changing sound of Florence: the bright clatter of hoof and cart-wheels thinning behind them, the cries of vendors sinking to a distant chatter, until there was more wind than voice, more leaf than laughter. Cicadas had begun their thin, relentless sawing in the scrub; now and then a bell-note travelled from a lower church and died in the open air like steam off hot bread.

When the afternoon was far advanced and the sun already stooped low in the west, Romola paused on rough ground in the shadow of tall cypress-trunks. The earth here was stony and pale, as if flour had been sifted over it; between stones, a few tough herbs clung on—wild fennel and thyme bruised underfoot, giving off sharp scents that pricked at the nostrils and made the mouth water with the remembered bite of country food.

She looked around for Baldassarre. He was not far behind; but when he reached her he dropped heavily onto an edge of rock and hard soil, as though the hill had taken payment from his bones. The thickset frame that once had fought and endured—the frame that had first shown itself to her with a rope round it in the Duomo—had lost its sturdy vigor. Under the tremor of exertion his eyes seemed vaguer, as if the mind behind them were groping for its own name.

“The hill is steep,” Romola said, sitting near him with compassionate gentleness. “And I fear you have been weakened by want.”

He turned his head and fixed his eyes on her. A long silence followed, not stubborn but helpless: the pause of a man who feels words on the edge of him the way one feels hunger—certain, gnawing, and yet unable to seize a morsel. Romola held herself still, almost holding her breath, as if any impatience in her would make the fragile thing he was trying to bring forth crumble back into dark.

He looked like no more than a common-bred, neglected old man. Yet Romola’s days had been spent near such figures now—near the poor, the sick, the down-trodden—and she had learned to see tragedy lodged in coarse hands and broken nails, to recognize that the great disasters of the world often ended in very small, domestic miseries: empty bowls, thin blankets, the ache of walking too far on too little bread.

Gradually his glance gathered itself, as flame gathers from a wick. At last, with abrupt emphasis, he said, “Ah! you would have been my daughter!”

The color rushed into Romola’s face and fled as swiftly, leaving her lips pale and parted. She sat like a marble image that had heard a verdict.

In that one word—daughter—the whole story leapt into shape inside her, as if someone had thrown a lamp into a closed room. Tito’s smiling evasions, his easy turns of phrase, his practiced tenderness: her mind seized them and held them up to a merciless new light. There could be no check, in the first instant, to the belief that sprang from her knowledge of his nature.

Her face, betraying so much, stirred Baldassarre like wine. For the first time his words had struck where they meant to strike. He leaned toward her, eagerness hardening his voice, and laid his rough hand on her arm.

“You are a woman of proud blood—is it not true?” he said. “You go to hear the preacher; you hate baseness—baseness that smiles and triumphs. You hate your husband?”

“Oh God! were you really his father?” Romola whispered. She was too filled with the images crowding upon her—the boy in Greek curls, the man with gentle eyes—to take note even of the hatred Baldassarre tried to stir. “Or was it as he said? Did you take him when he was little?”

“Ah, you believe me—you know what he is!” Baldassarre cried, exulting. His grip tightened, as if the contact itself fed his strength. “You will help me?”

“Yes,” Romola said—meaning pity, meaning human help, meaning any healing one might give to a ruined soul.

She laid her palm gently on the hand bruising her arm. The skin beneath her touch was hard and cracked, like bark; it reminded her of hands that had dug and lifted and gone hungry. Tears rose in her eyes as she looked at him.

“Oh, it is piteous!” she said. “Tell me—you were a great scholar; you taught him. How is it?”

She broke off, a chill darting through her. Tito had called this man mad. And where, in this broken creature, were the signs of refinement, of past learning? Yet she did not snatch away her hand. She had learned that revulsion can wound worse than a blow. She sat perfectly still, and listened more cautiously.

“It is gone!—it is all gone!” Baldassarre said. “And they would not believe me, because he lied, and said I was mad; and they had me dragged to prison. And I am old—my mind will not come back. And the world is against me.”

His eyes sank as if a wave of despondency passed over him, leaving him cold. Then he looked up again, and the fire returned.

“But you are not against me,” he said. “He made you love him, and he has been false to you; and you hate him. Yes—he made me love him. He was beautiful and gentle, and I was a lonely man. I took him when they were beating him. He slept in my bosom when he was little, and I watched him as he grew, and gave him all my knowledge, and everything that was mine I meant to be his. I had many things; money, and books, and gems. He had my gems—he sold them; and he left me in slavery. He never came to seek me; and when I came back poor and in misery, he denied me. He said I was a madman.”

“He told us his father was dead—was drowned,” Romola said faintly, as if saying it could change it. “Surely he must have believed it then. Oh—he could not have been so base then!”

A vision rose, keen as the smell of new-baked bread that brings back a whole morning: those first days of her marriage, when Tito’s presence seemed a gentle nourishment, and she had swallowed his words as easily as milk. The yearning regret within that memory loosened, for a moment, the rigid horror. Tears broke free; with one great sob they rushed down her face.

“Ah, you are young, and the tears come easily,” Baldassarre said, with an impatient edge. “But tears are no good. They only put out the fire within—and it is the fire that works. Tears will hinder us. Listen to me.”

Romola started slightly. Again the thought flickered—madness. If this were madness, it might be dangerous; if it were truth, it was more dangerous still. Yet she did not dare to show doubt. Better to keep his confidence, to gather facts, to find firm ground beneath her feet in a world that had become slippery.

“Tell me,” she said, keeping her voice as gentle as she could, “how did you lose your memory—your scholarship?”

“I was ill,” Baldassarre answered. “I can’t tell how long—it was a blank. I remember nothing. Only at last I was sitting in the sun among the stones, and everything else was darkness. And slowly, and by degrees, I felt something besides that: a longing for something—I did not know what—that never came. And when I was in the ship on the waters I began to know what I longed for. It was for the Boy to come back—it was to find all my thoughts again, for I was locked away outside them all. And I am outside now. I feel nothing but a wall and darkness.”

As he spoke, his voice went dreamy, and he fell silent, resting his head between his hands. The wind moved through the cypress like a long exhalation. Below, Florence was only a dim spread of roofs and towers, and its human noises were swallowed; up here, sound belonged to leaf, insect, and the far cry of a bird.

Romola’s doubts sank again under the weight of pity. She had watched her own father’s daily pain—how Bardo missed Dino and the future he had laid up for him like carefully stored grain. Loss, she knew, could hollow out the mind, leaving it echoing.

“It all came back once,” Baldassarre said suddenly, raising his head. “I was master of everything. I saw all the world again, and my gems, and my books. I thought I had him in my power, and I went to expose him where—where the lights were and the trees; and he lied again, and said I was mad, and they dragged me away to prison… Wickedness is strong; and he wears armour.”

The fierceness flared. He seized her arm again.

“But you will help me? He has been false to you too. He has another wife, and she has children. He makes her believe he is her husband, and she is a foolish, helpless thing. I will show you where she lives.”

The first shock that shot through Romola turned to visible anger. The indignity to her as a woman rose, instinctive and burning; it was as if her very name had been taken from her and given to another mouth to speak. Baldassarre felt that heat and mistook it for the thirst he wanted.

“You hate him,” he urged. “Is it not true? There is no love between you; I know that. I know women can hate; and you have proud blood. You hate falseness, and you can love revenge.”

Romola sat as if bound, not feeling his bruising grasp. Her thoughts clashed and would not settle: shame, pity for the other woman, horror at Tito, a dim dread of the violence in the man beside her. Even the very words Baldassarre used—wife, rightful, justice—seemed to carry invisible weights. In Florence, words were weapons as much as knives; the wrong word, in the wrong mouth, could strip a person of place and protection. And Romola, who had grown up among scholars, felt with sick clarity how little any phrase could translate the whole truth of what she had lived. The heart’s experience had no clean Latin for it, no Greek that would preserve its exact flavor; it was like trying to keep the scent of crushed thyme in a closed fist.

“You shall contrive it,” Baldassarre said at last, bending toward her in an eager whisper. “I have learned by heart that you are his rightful wife. You are a noble woman. You go to hear the preacher of vengeance; you will help justice. But you will think for me. My mind goes—everything goes sometimes—all but the fire. The fire is God: it is justice: it will not die. You believe that—is it not true? If they will not hang him for robbing me, you will take away his armour—you will make him go without it, and I will stab him. I have a knife, and my arm is still strong enough.”

He thrust his hand beneath his tunic and drew out a hidden knife. In the dimming light the blade caught a cold gleam. He ran his thumb along the edge absentmindedly, as if the sharpness were a thing he must taste to keep his purpose alive.

It seemed to Romola that each hour brought a new hardness, a new test. Her mind was too quick and sound to waste soft, scolding words on a man in this state; she knew they would be like pouring water into cracked earth. She chose not to answer his last speech. Better to turn him aside, gain time, let his heat cool, and—meanwhile—learn what she could.

She spoke, her voice trembling despite her effort at steadiness. “You say she is foolish and helpless—that other wife—and believes him to be her real husband. Perhaps he is. Perhaps he married her before he married me.”

“I cannot tell,” Baldassarre said, pausing, his fingers still on the knife-edge. Bewilderment crossed his face as if a door in his mind had slammed. “I can remember no more. I only know where she lives. You shall see her. I will take you; but not now,” he added hurriedly. “He may be there. The night is coming on.”

Romola started with sudden awareness. The sun had set; the hills were darkening. The wind had cooled, and the cypress shadows lay long and thick. Far off, Florence’s murmurs were almost gone; in their place were the closer sounds—the rasp of insects, the faint crackle of dry leaves—so that even her own breath seemed loud in her ears. The world up here felt watchful, like a kitchen at night when the fire has gone down and every tiny settling of the house can be heard.

“It is true,” she said, rising. “But you will come and take me—when?”

“In the morning,” Baldassarre replied, as if her question meant only impatience for vengeance. “Come to me where you came to me to-day—in the church. I will be there at ten; and if you are not there, I will go again towards mid-day. Can you remember?”

“Mid-day,” Romola repeated, fastening the word as one ties a knot.

“Mid-day,” Baldassarre echoed—then, as if the number slipped from him and only the broader mark remained, he said again, “Only mid-day. The same place, and mid-day.”

He rose too, catching her arm once more with his left hand while he held the knife in his right.

“And after that,” he said, his whisper thick with desire, “we will have our revenge. He shall feel the sharp edge of justice. The world is against me, but you will help me.”

“I would help you in other ways,” Romola said, making a first timid attempt to pierce the illusion. “I fear you are in want; you have to labour and get little. I should like to bring you comforts, and make you feel again that there is someone who cares for you.”

“Talk no more about that,” Baldassarre said fiercely. “I will have nothing else. Help me to wring one drop of vengeance on this side of the grave. I have nothing but my knife. It is sharp; but there is a moment after the thrust when men see the face of death—and it shall be my face that he will see.”

He let go and sank back into a sitting posture, as if the blaze in him had spent itself on those words. Romola stood, feeling a helplessness like hunger: a need that could not be satisfied by any food she knew how to provide. She must defer every intention till the morrow—till she could think, seek counsel, find some way to guard the living without yielding to the deadliness he called justice.

“Mid-day, then,” she said distinctly, putting firmness into the syllables.

“Yes,” Baldassarre answered, exhaustion falling over him like a cloak. “Go; I will rest here.”

She hurried away down the slope, her steps careful on the loose stones, the scent of bruised herbs rising with each footfall. At the last point from which he would likely still be visible, she turned. He sat where she had left him, hunched in the gathering dark—cypress behind him, knife hidden again, the air around him full of the small relentless sounds of the hillside.
