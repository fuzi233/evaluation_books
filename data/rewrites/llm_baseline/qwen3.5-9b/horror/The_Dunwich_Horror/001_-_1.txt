When a traveler in north-central Massachusetts mistakes the fork at the junction of the Aylesbury Pike, just past Dean's Corners, they stumble into a landscape that defies expectation. The terrain climbs steeply, and stone walls edged with thorny briars squeeze tighter against the rutted, dust-choked road. The forests encroaching from the frequent belts of trees seem unnaturally vast, their flora—a riot of wild weeds, brambles, and grasses—possessing a lushness alien to settled lands. Yet, the cultivated fields are sparse and barren, and the few scattered dwellings share a startling uniformity of age, squalor, and decay. Instinctively, one hesitates to seek directions from the gnarled, solitary figures occasionally spotted on crumbling doorsteps or lurking in the rocky meadows. Their silence and furtiveness evoke a sense of confrontation with forbidden entities, things best left undisturbed.

As the road ascends, revealing the mountains looming above the deep woods, a profound unease settles in. The summits are grotesquely rounded and symmetrical, lacking the comforting irregularity of nature, and frequently silhouette against the sky the bizarre circles of tall stone pillars crowning their peaks. Deep gorges of uncertain depth slice through the route, crossed by crude wooden bridges that inspire doubt regarding their structural integrity. When the road descends, stretches of marshland appear, instilling an instinctive dread, especially at twilight when unseen whippoorwills chatter and fireflies emerge in abnormal numbers, dancing to the raucous, creepy rhythms of strident bullfrogs. The thin, shimmering ribbon of the Miskatonic River, winding close to the feet of the domed hills, suggests a serpentine motion. As the hills draw nearer, their dark, precipitous wooded sides loom menacingly, offering no escape route.

Beyond a covered bridge lies a small village huddled between the stream and the sheer face of Round Mountain. Here, a cluster of rotting gambrel roofs hints at an architectural era long past, contrasting sharply with the surrounding region. Closer inspection reveals that most homes are abandoned and collapsing, while the broken steeple of the local church shelters the hamlet's sole, slovenly mercantile store. One cannot help but fear the gloomy tunnel of the bridge, yet there is no alternative. Upon crossing, a faint, malignant odor permeates the village street—the scent of massed mold and centuries of decay. Relief comes only in leaving the place, following the narrow road around the hill bases until it rejoins the main pike. Later, travelers often realize they have passed through Dunwich. Outsiders avoid it whenever possible; since a specific season of horror, all signposts directing toward it have been removed.

Judged by standard aesthetic criteria, the scenery is undeniably beautiful, yet it attracts neither artists nor tourists. Two centuries ago, when superstition held sway, people avoided the area citing witch blood, Satan worship, and strange forest presences. In our rational age, following the suppression of the Dunwich horror of 1928, the town is shunned without anyone fully understanding why. Perhaps one reason, applicable perhaps only to the locals, is their repulsive decadence. Having followed a path of retrogression common to many New England backwaters, they have evolved into a distinct race marked by the clear stigmata of degeneracy and inbreeding. Their collective intelligence is woefully low, and their history reeks of open viciousness, hidden murders, incest, and acts of almost unimaginable perversion.

Among the old gentry, descendants of the two or three armigerous families arriving from Salem in 1692, some maintain a marginally higher plane of existence, though many branches have sunk so deeply into the sordid populace that only their names remain as keys to origins they now disgrace. Some Whateleys and Bishops still send their eldest sons to Harvard or Miskatonic University, though these youths rarely return to the moldering gambrel roofs where they and their ancestors were born. Even those aware of the recent horrors cannot pinpoint the exact ailment of Dunwich. However, old legends speak of unhallowed rites and Indian conclaves where forbidden shadows were summoned from the great rounded hills, accompanied by wild orgiastic prayers answered by loud cracks and earth-shaking rumblings.

In 1747, Reverend Abijah Hoadley, newly arrived at the Congregational Church in Dunwich Village, delivered a memorable sermon on the proximity of Satan and his demons. He declared: "It must be allowed that these blasphemies of an infernal train of demons are matters of too common knowledge to be denied; the cursed voices of Azazel and Buzrael, of Beelzebub and Belial, being heard from underground by over a score of credible witnesses now living. I myself caught a very plain discourse of evil powers in the hill behind my house less than a fortnight ago; wherein there were rattling, rolling, groaning, screeching, and hissing, such as no thing of this earth could raise up, and which must needs have come from those caves that only black magic can discover, and only the devil unlock." Hoadley vanished shortly after preaching this sermon, but the text, printed in Springfield, remains extant.

Noises in the hills persisted annually, baffling geologists and physiographers to this day. Other traditions recount foul odors near the hill-crowning stone circles and rushing airy presences heard faintly from specific points at the bottom of great ravines. There is also the tale of the Devil's Hop Yard, a bleak, blasted hillside where no tree, shrub, or blade of grass grows. Furthermore, the natives hold a mortal terror of the numerous whippoorwills, which become vocal on warm nights. It is vowed that these birds are psychopomps waiting for the souls of the dying, timing their eerie cries to the sufferer's labored breathing. Should they catch the fleeing soul, they flutter away in demoniac laughter; if they fail, they sink into a disappointed silence. Of course, these tales are obsolete and ridiculous, relics of ancient times.

Dunwich itself is ridiculously old—far older than any community within thirty miles. To the south, one can still spy the cellar walls and chimney of the ancient Bishop house, constructed before 1700, while the ruins of the mill at the falls, built in 1806, represent the most modern architecture visible. Industry never flourished here; the nineteenth-century factory movement proved fleeting. Oldest of all are the great rings of rough-hewn stone columns atop the hills, generally attributed to Native Americans rather than settlers. Deposits of skulls and bones found within these circles and around the sizable table-like rock on Sentinel Hill support the popular belief that these were once burial grounds of the Pocumtucks, even though many ethnologists, dismissing the absurd improbability of such a theory, insist the remains are Caucasian.
