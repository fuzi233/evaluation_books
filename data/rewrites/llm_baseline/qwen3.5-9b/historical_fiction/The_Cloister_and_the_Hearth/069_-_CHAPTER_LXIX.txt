In a guest cell within a Dominican convent, a solitary stranger lay prostrate, his strength utterly spent by waves of violent nausea that had finally receded, leaving him as frail as Margaret had been in Holland. Upon the hearth, a massive wood fire crackled warmly, while the patient's garments hung suspended nearby. Beside the bed sat a towering friar, his voice resonating softly as he read aloud from his breviary. At intervals, the sufferer opened weary eyes to gaze at him, listening intently; at others, he shut them tight and emitted low moans. The monk knelt, his forehead pressed to the floor in fervent prayer, before rising to bid his charge farewell. "Day breaks," he declared. "I must prepare for Matins." "Blessed Father Jerome," the stranger whispered, "before you depart, tell me—how did I arrive here?" "By the hand of Heaven," the friar replied gravely. "You cast aside God's gift; yet He has restored it to you. Reflect upon this! You have tested the world and found only bitterness. Now test the Church! The Church offers peace. *Pax vobiscum*." With those words, he departed.

Gerard remained reclining, lost in meditation and wonder, until exhaustion claimed him and he drifted into a doze. When consciousness returned, he found a new attendant seated by his side—a layman whose eyes were small and perpetually restless, a stark contrast to the calm majesty of Friar Jerome. The man inquired anxiously about his condition. "Extremely weak," Gerard murmured. "Where have I encountered you before, sir?" "No worse for my gauntlet?" the other asked, his tone laced with concern. "Indeed, I nearly struck you down, or both of us might now lie at the bottom of the Tiber." Gerard fixed him with a stare. "What? Was it you who saved me? How?" "Well, signor, I happened to be by the banks of the Tiber on some errand, though it matters little now. You approached me, begging desperately for a dagger stroke. Yet, even as you spoke, I recognized you—the nobleman who spared my wife and child upon the sea." "It is Teresa's husband... and an assassin?!" "At your service. Next, you threw yourself into the river, commanding me to stand aside." "I recall that clearly." "If it had been anyone else, I would have obeyed instantly, moving not a muscle. My enemies are many; they might all hang on a single rope or drown in one river for my sake. But when you sank in the Tiber crying out 'Margaret!'..." "Ah!" "My heart screamed 'Teresa!' How could I return home and face her if I allowed you to die, especially since your death was the very act that saved hers? So I entered the water. Fortunately for us both, I swim like a duck. Seeing me approach, intent on mutual destruction, you attempted to seize me, hoping to end us together. Instead, I circled you and, accepting my apologies, struck the nape of your neck with my steel-gloved fist. You lost consciousness. With great effort, fighting the strong current, I dragged your body ashore, insensible and filled with water. I carried you on my back to my home, thinking, 'Teresa will nurse him, and she will be pleased with me.' Hard by this monastery, a holy friar—the largest I have ever seen—intercepted us and questioned the situation. I recounted everything. He scrutinized you closely. 'I know this face,' he stated. 'It is Gerard, a fair youth from Holland.' 'The same,' I confirmed. Then His Reverence said, 'He has friends among our brothers. Leave him with us! This is charity, our divine office.' He also informed me that the convent possessed far greater means to tend to you than I did, which proved true enough. Thus, I merely negotiated permission to visit daily, and here you are." As he spoke, the man cast a look upon Gerard that blended strangeness with deep affection. Gerard offered no response. He felt as though a serpent coiled within the room. Closing his eyes, he dismissed the visitor, who retreated on tiptoe, promising his daily return.

Gerard lay with his eyes shut, awake but profoundly contemplative. Saved from death by an assassin—was this not the finger of Heaven pointing toward salvation? That Heaven he had once insulted, cursed, and defied. A shudder ran through him at the memory of his blasphemies. He attempted to pray, finding the words formed on his lips, yet his spirit refused to engage in true devotion. "I am doomed eternally," he cried out in despair, "doomed, doomed." Suddenly, the organ of the convent church erupted in rich, solemn harmony, followed by the soaring voices of the choir chanting a full service. Amidst the chorus, one voice seemed to float above the rest, ascending toward heaven itself—a sweet boy's tone, full, pure, and angelic. Gerard closed his eyes and listened. The melodies transported him back to the innocent days of his boyhood. There was no earthly dross, no foul, fierce passions tearing and corrupting the soul. Only peace—sweet, balmy peace. "Yes," he sighed, "the Church brings peace of mind. Until I left her embrace, I knew neither sorrow nor sin." Tears streamed down the face of the poor, broken creature. Just as he wept, the radiant and reverend visage of someone he had not expected to see again appeared before him. It was Father Anselm. The good priest had arrived at the convent only the previous night. Gerard recognized him instantly and called out, "Oh, Father Anselm! You healed my wounded body in Juliers; now heal my injured soul in Rome! Alas, you cannot." Anselm sat beside the bed, placing a gentle hand upon Gerard's head to soothe him with tender words. Having learned the circumstances of Gerard's arrival, he spoke kindly yet solemnly, helping the young man confront his crime, urging repentance, and expressing gratitude to the Divine Power that had thwarted his will to save his soul. "Come, my son," Anselm said, "first cleanse your bosom of its heavy burden." "Ah, father," Gerard replied, "in Juliers I could; then I was innocent. But now, an impious monster that I am, I dare not confess to you." "Why not, my son? Do you think I have not sinned against Heaven in my time, and deeply? Oh, how deeply! Come, poor laden soul, pour forth your grief, pour forth your faults; withhold nothing! Do not lie oppressed and crushed by hidden sins." Soon, Gerard knelt at Father Anselm's feet, confessing every sin with sighs and groans of profound penitence. "Your sins are grave," Anselm acknowledged. "Yet your temptation was equally terrible, exceedingly so. I must consult our worthy Prior." The good father kissed Gerard's brow and withdrew to confer with the superior regarding the appropriate penance. Immediately, Gerard found the power to pray. He prayed with all the fervor of his heart. The phase through which this remarkable mind now passed can be summarized in a single word: Penitence. He turned away from the world with terror and revulsion, begging passionately to remain within the convent walls. To him, the monastery was like a wounded, weary bird returning to its gentle nest. He spent his novitiate in prayer, self-mortification, pious reading, and meditation.

Meanwhile, the Princess Claelia's spy returned home to report that Gerard was undoubtedly dead, though the manner of his passing remained unknown. She stood literally stunned. After a long silence, when she finally gathered the breath to speak, her words were filled with lamentation over her fate, cursed with such treacherous instruments. "So swiftly," she sighed, "these monsters commit evil deeds. They come to us in our hour of passion, first tempting us with their mercenary daggers, then executing the mortal acts we never imagined unless provoked by them." Within mere hours, her pity for Gerard and her hatred for his killer boiled into a feverish intensity, fueled by her own foolish rage. "Poor soul! I cannot call you back to life. But he shall never live, that traitor who slew you so treacherously." She immediately placed armed men in ambush, keeping them on guard throughout the day. Her plan was clear: when Lodovico arrived to collect his payment, they would spring from hiding in a specific antechamber and hack him to pieces. "Strike at his head," she commanded, "for he wears a private coat of mail beneath his robes. If he escapes alive, your own heads shall answer for it." Thus, she sat weeping for her victim, simultaneously pulling the strings of deadly machines designed to spill the blood of a second man, solely because he had served as her instrument to kill the first.
