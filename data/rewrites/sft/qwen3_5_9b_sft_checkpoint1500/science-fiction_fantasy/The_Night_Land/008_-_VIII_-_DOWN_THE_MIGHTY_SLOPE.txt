Down the mighty slope I descended, moving with a profound and deliberate silence. The darkness surrounding me was not merely an absence of light; it was a physical weight, a suffocating pressure that seemed to crush my very soul. It was a night unlike anything you have ever witnessed or imagined, so absolute that it threatened to unmoor me from reality itself. At times, I found myself walking with a random, drifting step, as if my feet had left the solid ground of Earth and were striding into the Void, slipping through an endless, unreal ether. Yet, whenever this delusion seized my mind, it was instantly corrected by the harsh, tangible reality of the world beneath me. A kick against a jutting rock, a stumble onto a hidden boulder, a sharp shock to the bones—these were the anchors that dragged me back from the void, reminding me with brutal certainty that I treaded hard, actual earth.

My only guide through this abyssal obscurity was the descent itself. Gravity pulled me inexorably downward, the sole thread connecting me to direction. Progress was agonizingly slow; through sheer reason, I estimated I covered scarcely a mile in two hours, perhaps less, choked by the impenetrable gloom. My strides were halting, my spirit weary, unable to maintain a free stride in the face of such utter negation.

Desperate to pierce the veil, I turned to the Diskos. I spun it occasionally, letting its strange, glistening beam cut a fleeting path through the blackness. I would stare into that tiny cone of illumination, fixing the terrain into my inner memory, marching forward until the darkness swallowed the vision again, leaving me stumbling blindly. In return, the Diskos offered a light that seemed astonishingly brilliant, a testament to the monstrous scale of the surrounding dark. I used it sparingly, only when the pain of my stumbles demanded a glimpse of the "blessedness" of light to orient my steps.

Six long, bitter hours passed, and it was now beyond the twentieth hour since my last slumber. Exhausted, I sat upon the Mighty Slope, in the eternal dark, and consumed two tablets, drinking water from a flask. I wrapped my cloak tightly around me, placing my scrip and pouch beneath my head for a pillow, the Diskos clutched close at hand. Sleep came swiftly, though my thoughts drifted earnestly, if vaguely, to Naani.

I slept for nearly six hours, waking suddenly as if struck by a blow. Something had brushed past me, or perhaps approached too closely. My heart leaped, and I gripped the Diskos, listening intently into the void. Nothing. Not a whisper, not a rustle. Only the silence of the infinite night. I sat up, reaching for my supplies, eating and drinking with trembling hands, fumbling in the dark. Soon, however, I was dressed, the Diskos in my hand, and standing once more.

A strange, persistent unease settled over my spirit throughout the following day. I stopped frequently, listening, convinced that something followed me quietly, silently, in the shadows. But my ears heard nothing. Still, I pressed downward into the night that held the slope.

Early in the seventh hour, after another meal, disaster struck. I stepped suddenly into a small hole in the ground, pitching violently. I tumbled against a sharp rock, shattered by the impact. Though my armor saved me from fatal injury, the fall shook me to my core, leaving me bruised and breathless. After recovering some strength, I realized that standing was too dangerous. I began to crawl, moving on hands and knees. This method allowed me to feel the ground beneath me, reducing reliance on the Diskos, which I rarely needed to illuminate the path when I was so close to the earth.

Thus I crept, a weary traveler in the dark, covering many sore miles. Eighteen hours later, having eaten and drunk three times, I sought a level place to rest. I found a spot relatively free of jagged boulders, clearing away the obstacles that might trip me. After feeding, I composed myself for sleep. Thoughts of Naani drifted through my mind, mingled with a lingering, half-formed fear that something hovered constantly near me in the dark. Twice I rose to my elbows to listen, but heard no sound, convincing myself that it was but a fancy born of exhaustion. Yet, I did not sleep deeply; I listened even as I drifted into unconsciousness.

Waking again after six hours, the sensation returned instantly—a presence, an entity, drawing near. I grasped the Diskos, listening with every faculty, but the silence remained absolute. The day that followed mirrored the last, save for one terrifying incident. Around the eighth hour, I nearly plunged into a monstrous pit in the Great Slope. I caught myself by the breast at the edge, crawling back to safety, circling the chasm in the dark. I emerged shaken, terrified of what else might lurk in the depths, unsure if I was surrounded by dangers or merely haunted by solitude.

Realizing that caution was slowing me to a crawl, I devised a plan. I needed a cord, but I had none. If a boy is not a boy without a cord, surely a man is not a man? Searching frantically, I found salvation in the leather strap of my pouch. Long, thin, and sturdy, it was perfect. I buckled the scrip and pouch together, affixed a heavy stone to the end of the strap, and secured it. As I crawled forward on hands and knees, I held the other end, throwing the stone ahead. By the tension and slack of the line, I could gauge the depth of the slope, ensuring I did not plummet into a hidden cliff.

It was a cumbersome fashion of travel, yet it brought me peace. I was no longer a blind creature stumbling into the abyss; I had a tether to the future. For eighteen hours I slept, waking strangely before the sixth hour, just as I had before. This recurring, unexplained awakening filled me with wonder and unease. No harm befell me, yet the conviction gnawed at me that something was always near. Whether it was evil, I could not know, for it had not harmed me.

Three more days passed, and I continued to creep downward, weary and worn, the Diskos strapped to my hip. Six days had elapsed on the Great Slope, an eternity of utter dark. I knew only that I was descending toward some dire and dreadful destination, a monstrous way downward with no end in sight.

As I traveled, I noticed a shift in the atmosphere. The biting cold had faded from the air, replaced by a heaviness that pressed against my chest. I recalled the air of the Night Land and the Mighty Pyramid. Within the Pyramid, the air was thin and keen, sealed off from the outside world, likely drawn from the underground fields. But here, far below, the air was different. It was thick, dense, almost solid, pressing heavily upon me. It boiled the water in my flask instantly, causing it to fizz violently and spill over the rim, wetting my hand. This density suggested a chemical richness absent in the higher realms, a world where gravity and atmosphere worked in tandem with a terrible intensity.

This observation led me to reflect on the history of the people who built the Pyramid. They possessed broad chests, an adaptation to the thin air of the upper heights. Centuries ago, plans existed to move the populace vertically through the pyramid, from city to city, to acclimate them to the varying densities of air. These movements, lasting over three years, were intended for health and mental training. Yet, humanity naturally resisted such upheaval, preferring the static comfort of their native levels. Consequently, the flying ships, capable of traversing the vertical expanse, eventually fell into disuse. The air of the Night Land, grown too weak to support flight, combined with a primal fear of the Evils dwelling in the night, caused the peoples of the Pyramid to abandon aviation entirely.

Why, I wondered, did the Road Makers of ages past not fly down from the upper world into the deep valley? Perhaps the air of the upper regions had become so thin that they forgot the art of flight. Or perhaps the terror of descending a hundred miles into a chasm, never to rise again, paralyzed them. The bottom of the Great Valley was a realm of monsters, strange beasts unknown to the surface nations. The splitting of the earth, the rushing oceans, the fire and storm—it marked the End of the World. Yet, in truth, it was but the beginning of a new Eternity, where Life arose from Death and Good from apparent Evil.

However, the Circle of Fate had turned. The peoples of the Mighty Pyramid, once masters of the skies, had regressed, losing the glory of the airships and retreating into a darker age. Just as the nations of the past had fallen, so too had these advanced civilizations slipped backward.

Back to the present journey: the air had changed drastically. I had reached a new depth, far beyond the Last Redoubt, in a monstrous night where the air was heavy and abundant. This density explained the fizzing water and the strange behavior of gases. I had been traveling downward for six great days, nearing the middle of the world, where the descent seemed endless.

Suddenly, far off in the deep of the night, a faint, unsure shining appeared. A spark of hope ignited within me, sickening and painful all at once. Was it real? Had I found the end of the slope?
