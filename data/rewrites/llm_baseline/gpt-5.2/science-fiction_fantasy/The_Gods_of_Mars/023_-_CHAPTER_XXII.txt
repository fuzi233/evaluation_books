“John Carter—John Carter,” she sobbed, her cheek against my shoulder as though my tunic were the last warm cloth in a world of stone. “Even now my eyes argue with my heart. When the girl, Thuvia, brought word you had returned to Barsoom, I heard the syllables, but they would not settle into meaning. Happiness like that has no name in the tongue of a woman who has eaten loneliness for years—dry as old pan and bitter on the palate. And when I learned the place they kept me, this dreadful hive, I told myself even you could not break into it.”

Her voice shook, yet beneath it I could hear the temple’s own harsh music: the clank of metal on metal, boots scuffing the corridors, the distant shout that rose and fell like wind in dead reeds. Sound, here, was never clean—always amplified by stone, always twisted by arches until you could not tell a man’s warning from a man’s boast.

“Moon after moon,” she went on, “no rumor came. I taught myself to swallow despair as one learns to swallow bad water. Then, an hour ago, I heard battle in the palace. I did not know what it meant, only that the air changed—like a kitchen before the bread comes out, when everyone begins to move faster. I hoped—foolishly perhaps—that it was Helium at last, and my Prince.”

“And Carthoris,” she whispered, and that single name carried the ache of years. “Our son?”

“He was with me less than an hour since, Dejah Thoris,” I said. “It must have been his men you heard. He is here.”

Her eyes closed for a heartbeat, as though savoring the word here the way a starving woman savors the first taste of real grain.

“Where is Issus?” I demanded.

Dejah Thoris’ shoulders lifted and fell, the smallest weary gesture. “She sent me under guard to this room just before the fighting began. She said she would send for me later. She was…angry, yes—but more than that. Fearful. I have watched her half a year, John Carter. I have seen cruelty done as calmly as a cook skins a fruit. Yet today she moved like one whose own knife has turned in her hand. Now I know why. She had learned you were coming to demand account for my imprisonment.”

From beyond the walls the uproar swelled. It was the wrong kind of sound to live with—too many feet, too many blades. Not the steady, honest noises of a hearth: the hiss of fat meeting heat, the thump of dough, the friendly clatter of bowls. This was a noise that devoured thought.

I was needed there. I knew it with the same certainty a man knows he must snatch a pot from flame before it scorches. Yet I dared not leave Dejah Thoris, and I dared not drag her into the mêlée where every corridor might hide a blade.

Then I remembered the pits—the cold, airless belly beneath this place from which I had crawled like a thing reborn. If a pantry can hide flour from thieves, perhaps the pits could hide my Princess from Issus.

I told her.

For an instant she clung harder, fingers biting through cloth. “To be parted again—even for a moment—John Carter, I cannot. The thought of being alone, of that creature finding me—” She shuddered. “You do not know her. No one can imagine her ferocity who has not watched her daily, as regular as a ritual meal, for over half a year. It took me nearly all that time to force my mind to believe what my eyes saw.”

“I shall not leave you, then,” I said, and meant it.

She was silent, and the temple’s din filled the gap like smoke. Then she drew my face down and kissed me—no ceremony, no hesitation, only the fierce certainty of a woman who has rationed tenderness until it is rare as spice.

“Go,” she said, and the word was command and gift. “Our son is there. Helium’s men are there, fighting for the Princess of Helium. Where they are, you should be. Hide me in the pits and go.”

I led her to the door through which I had come from below. I held her as though I could press my own strength into her bones, then, with a heart that felt flayed, guided her across the threshold, kissed her once more, and closed the door upon her.

The latch clicked—a small domestic sound, like shutting a cupboard, and yet it was the cruelest noise I had ever heard.

I ran toward the greatest tumult.

I had crossed scarcely half a dozen chambers when I found the fight. Black men, thick as a hedge of thorns, jammed the entrance to a great room, trying to bar red men from the inner sacred precincts. I came from within, behind the blacks. No time for counting, no time for caution—only the animal knowledge that if you hesitate, you die.

I charged.

“For Helium!” I shouted, and the words tore out of me like breath from a bellows. My long-sword sang its thin bright note. The first black warrior fell; then another. The reds, hearing my voice, answered with a roar—“John Carter! John Carter!”—and the sound itself seemed to stiffen their arms, to season their courage like salt.

The blacks wavered, broke, and the red men burst into the chamber.

Five hundred fought there—black against red—and no man asked quarter, no man gave it. It was not a battle so much as a butcher’s work, relentless, close, the air thick with iron and sweat. The floor became slick, then flooded, until blood lay ankle-deep and bodies were so many that often we braced our feet upon the dead to strike at the living.

Carthoris fought beside me—my son, my blood—his blade quick, his eyes clear. In him I saw Helium’s future, and in the blacks I saw the old superstition’s strangling hands. Back and forth we surged.

Toward the great windows that overlooked the gardens, my gaze caught movement, and exultation struck me so hard it was almost pain.

“Look!” I cried. “Men of the First Born—look!”

For a heartbeat the fight stilled, as though even hatred paused to breathe. Every face turned.

Across the gardens stood a wavering black line, and behind it—driving it back like a tide of living green—came a horde of green warriors astride their mighty thoats. Even at that distance I could hear the deep-chested thunder of the beasts, the jangling harness, the wild, ragged chorus of riders—a sound like storm over savannah.

One rode forward, more grim than the rest, and bellowed a command. Tars Tarkas—Jeddak of Thark.

He lowered his great metal-shod lance; his warriors followed. Twenty yards…then less. Another word from the Thark, and the green men charged with a battle-cry that clawed at the ear. The black line held one moment—then the thoats punched through it, riders and beasts passing clean as a spear through cloth. After them came uden upon uden of red men.

The greens broke wide, circling the temple; the reds surged for its interior.

We turned back to our interrupted slaughter, but our foes had vanished, melting away in sudden terror. The room, moments before a cauldron, became a hollow, panting space filled with the metallic aftertaste of killing.

My first thought was Dejah Thoris.

I shouted to Carthoris that I had found his mother, and we ran—he beside me, our survivors behind. My boots beat the corridor stones; the temple’s acoustics flung the sound back at us, doubling it, as though the place mocked our urgency.

When I entered the chamber where I had left her, I knew at once someone had been there.

A silk lay on the floor—fresh, gleaming faintly as though torn from a sleeve. A dagger. Metal ornaments scattered as if ripped free in struggle. The air itself felt disturbed, like a room after a pot has boiled over.

Worst of all, the door to the pits stood ajar.

I was across the room in a bound. I flung it open and plunged inside.

Emptiness.

“Dejah Thoris!” I cried, and again, and again—my voice slapped back from stone, useless. No answering breath, no rustle of cloth. For an instant sanity teetered. Rage rose, blind and absolute.

“Issus!” I roared. “Issus! Search the temple for her, but let no man harm her but John Carter. Carthoris—where are Issus’ apartments?”

“This way!” he cried, and sprang off as though propelled by fury alone.

Down we ran, deeper into the bowels of the temple. At last we reached a great carved door. Carthoris burst through, and I was on his heels.

The throne room—again that obscene tableau: the carved sorapus throne, the reclining slaves, the ranks of soldiery.

We gave them no time. I cut down two in the front rank with a single sweep. Then I drove straight through the remaining ranks with the sheer force of my body, leaping onto the dais beside the throne.

Issus squatted there—repulsive, ancient, terror distorting her already hideous features. She tried to wriggle toward a trap behind her, but I caught her by the arm before she could rise.

The guard began to rush from all sides. I drew my dagger and pressed it close to that vile breast.

“Back!” I shouted. “Back! The first black foot that steps upon this platform sends my dagger into Issus’ heart.”

They hesitated. An officer barked an order and they drew back, and in that same moment the outer corridor vomited forth a thousand red men—Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, Xodar—filling the chamber like a flood.

“Where is Dejah Thoris?” I demanded.

Issus’ eyes rolled, wild, trying to comprehend what she saw. The temple—her temple—fallen. The old lie cracking. And then the highest noble of her realm, high priest and prime minister, lifted his voice, rich with the trained cadence of ritual.

“Issus, Goddess of Death and of Life Eternal,” he cried, “arise in thy righteous wrath and with one wave strike dead thy blasphemers! Let none escape! Daughter of the Lesser Moon—save thy people! Strike!”

That was the last thread holding her semblance of divinity.

She went mad.

In my grasp she became a screaming, gibbering thing, biting, clawing, laughing—a laugh so wrong it chilled the blood. The slave girls shrieked and cowered. Issus sprang at them, teeth bared, froth flying. Spat upon them. The room stank of fear and old corruption.

I shook her hard, as one might shake sense into a delirious man.

“Where is Dejah Thoris?” I shouted.

For a moment she mumbled, then a sudden cunning sparked in her close-set eyes.

“Dejah Thoris?” she crooned, and laughed again, shrill and unearthly. “Yes, Dejah Thoris—I know. And Thuvia. And Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang. They each love John Carter. Ha-ah! Droll, droll. Together for a year they will meditate within the Temple of the Sun. But ere the year is gone there will be no more food. Ho-oh! Divine entertainment.”

She licked froth from her lips as though tasting a sauce.

“No more food,” she hissed. “Except each other. Ha-ah! Ha-ah!”

The words struck like poison. I felt my muscles go cold, my rage so sharp it trembled through me. For an instant the dagger lifted of its own will.

“Countermand your orders!” I snarled. “Recall the condemned. Haste, or you die!”

“It is too late,” she shrieked, and fell back into her gibbering.

My hand wanted her heart, wanted it with a simple, brutal certainty. But something—some last restraint of the man I had been before war—stayed me. I would not drive steel into a woman, even this one.

A fitter ending offered itself.

“First Born!” I cried, turning to those in the chamber. “You have seen to-day the impotency of Issus. The gods are impotent. Issus is no god—only a cruel old woman who has deceived you for ages. Take her. John Carter, Prince of Helium, will not contaminate his hand with her blood.”

And I shoved the raving fraud from her throne into the grasping arms of her own people.

I caught sight of Xodar among the red officers and called him. “Lead me—quickly—to the Temple of the Sun.”

I did not wait to learn what vengeance the First Born would take. I ran with Xodar, Carthoris, Hor Vastus, Kantos Kan, and a score of red nobles, through inner passages where the air tasted stale and the stone swallowed sound into a muffled, anxious hush.

We came to the central court—a vast circular space paved with transparent marble, pale as skimmed milk. Ahead rose the golden Temple of the Sun, wrought in fantastical designs, inlaid with gems that caught even the dimness and broke it into colored fire—ruby, emerald, sapphire—like spices scattered on a dark board.

“This way,” Xodar shouted, pointing to a tunnel mouth beside the temple.

We were about to descend when a deep-toned roar burst from behind us—from the Temple of Issus. A red man, Djor Kantos, padwar of the fifth utan, ran from a nearby gate, shouting.

“The blacks have fired the temple! In a thousand places it burns. Haste to the outer gardens or you are lost!”

Smoke began to pour from windows around the court. Above the highest minaret of Issus a pall spread, thickening, blotting the upper air. The temple’s sound changed again—no longer pure battle, but the hungry crackle and boom of fire, the frantic echoes of men trying to outpace it.

“Go back!” I cried to those with me. “Xodar—point the way and leave me. I shall reach my Princess yet.”

“Follow me, John Carter,” he replied, and without waiting he plunged down into the tunnel.

I followed.

Down through tier after tier of galleries we ran, the air growing cooler, then tainted with old dust. At last we reached a level passage ending in a lighted chamber.

Massive bars blocked us.

Beyond them—there she was.

Dejah Thoris, my incomparable Princess. Thuvia beside her. Phaidor, too, the daughter of Matai Shang. Their faces floated in the strange light like figures behind glass.

Dejah Thoris saw me and ran to the bars, hands outstretched. Already the chamber had turned upon its slow axis so that only part of its opening aligned with the corridor. The interval was closing—slow as a millstone, unstoppable as season.

Soon there would be only a thin crack. Then nothing. And for a long Barsoomian year the chamber would revolve until, for one brief day, the opening passed the bars again.

A year.

A year with Issus’ promised “entertainment.”

“Xodar!” I cried. “Can no power stop this revolving? Is there none who holds the secret of these bars?”

“None, I fear, whom we could fetch in time,” he said, eyes tight. “I shall go and make the attempt. Wait for me here.”

He ran.

I stood with my hands on the bars as though my grip could force iron to soften. Dejah Thoris pushed her fingers through, and I took them, pressing them to my mouth, to my cheek, trying to memorize their warmth as one memorizes the smell of a beloved dish from a far homeland. We spoke in low tones—little fragments of life we had been denied, names and memories, the private dialect of two hearts.

Thuvia came close, then, seeing our need for solitude, withdrew to the far side of the chamber with a dignity that tasted of clean water.

Phaidor did not.

She stood near enough that her breathing seemed to share our air.

“John Carter,” she said, voice taut, “this is the last time you shall see any of us. Tell me that you love me, that I may die happy.”

“I love only the Princess of Helium,” I answered. “I am sorry, Phaidor. It is as I have told you from the beginning.”

She turned away, biting her lip, but not before she cast Dejah Thoris a scowl so dark it seemed to stain the light.

We spoke on—urgent, intimate—while the opening shrank. Above us, faint through stone, came the tumult of black and red and green men fighting their way through fire. A draught carried smoke down to us, at first a mere taint, then thicker, until it coated the tongue, bitter as scorched grain.

I watched the gap narrowing, calculating with a soldier’s mind and a lover’s panic. Soon it would be too small even for Dejah Thoris’ slender form.

Why did Xodar not return?

The smoke grew heavier, rolling down the corridor, turning the world to a choking haze. Then through it came shouting and the slap of running feet.

“Come back! Come back!” someone cried. “Even the pits are burning!”

A dozen men broke through the blinding smoke to my side—Carthoris, Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, Xodar, and others who had followed.

“There is no hope, John Carter,” Xodar rasped. “The keeper of the keys is dead, and his keys are not upon his carcass. Our only hope is to quench the conflagration and trust to fate that a year will find your Princess alive. I have brought sufficient food to last them.”

As he spoke he began tossing tiny cans through the narrowing crack—small cylinders that clinked softly as they landed within, like provisions being stocked in a cellar against famine.

“When the crack closes, no smoke can reach them,” he continued. “If we extinguish the flames, I believe they will be safe.”

“Go, then,” I said, voice raw. “Take the others. I shall remain here beside my Princess until a merciful death releases me. I care not to live.”

Even as I spoke, Xodar kept throwing the little cans, quick, methodical, as though he were a careful steward portioning supplies. The crack narrowed to an inch.

Dejah Thoris stood as close as she could, whispering courage to me, urging me to save myself, her voice a thin steady thread in the roar and crackle above.

Then, beyond her shoulder, I saw Phaidor’s face twist—malign, sharpened by hatred. Her eyes met mine and held.

“Think not, John Carter,” she said, “that you may so lightly cast aside the love of Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang. Nor hope to hold thy Dejah Thoris again. Wait you the long year, but know when the waiting ends it shall be Phaidor’s arms which welcome you—not those of the Princess of Helium. Behold, she dies!”

Her hand flashed up.

A dagger caught the light.

Then another figure moved—Thuvia.

As the blade fell toward the unprotected breast of my love, Thuvia was almost between them.

A gust of smoke—thick, blinding—burst down the corridor and swallowed the chamber’s interior. Through it rang a single shriek, one sharp sound like a knife striking bone.

The smoke thinned.

But the wall was blank.

The last crevice had closed.

For a long year that hideous chamber would keep its secret.

Hands seized me. Voices urged, commanded.

“In a moment it will be too late,” Xodar shouted. “There is but a bare chance we can reach the outer garden alive even now. The pumps are started—in five minutes the pits will be flooded. If we would not drown like rats in a trap we must hasten above and dash for safety through the burning temple!”

“Go,” I told them, hollow with it. “Let me die here. There is no hope elsewhere. When they carry her dear body out a year hence, let them find the body of her lord awaiting her.”

After that my memory breaks into fragments, like a shattered platter. I recall struggling—many hands on me, the crush of bodies, the roar of fire, the sting of smoke in my eyes. I recall being lifted, borne away, my boots scraping stone, my voice lost in the din.

No one later spoke of the details. They did not pry open grief for curiosity’s sake. And I did not ask.

If I could know one thing, what weight would lift—what bitterness might dissolve.

But whether the assassin’s dagger found one fair bosom or another, only time will divulge.
