My hand slipped down from the curtain. Do not imagine, oh, do not presume, that the sheer, dreadful embarrassment of my predicament was the dominant thought in my mind! Far from it. So intense was my sisterly concern for Mr. Godfrey that I never paused to question why he was absent from the concert. No! My mind was consumed solely by the words—the startling, seismic words—that had just escaped his lips. "He will do it today," he had declared, with a tone of terrifying resolve. What, oh, what would he accomplish? Could he commit an act even more deplorably unworthy than those he had already performed? Would he renounce his faith? Would he desert us at the Mothers'-Small-Clothes? Had we witnessed the final fading of his angelic smile within the committee room? Had we heard the last echo of his unrivaled eloquence at Exeter Hall?

So agitated was I by the mere specter of such catastrophic outcomes associated with such a man that I believe I would have fled my hiding place, imploring him in the name of every Ladies' Committee in London to clarify his intentions. This was when I suddenly detected another voice entering the room. It pierced through the drapes; it was loud, bold, and utterly devoid of feminine charm. It was the voice of Rachel Verinder.

"Why have you come up here, Godfrey?" she demanded. "Why did you not enter the library?"

He laughed softly. "Miss Clack is in the library."

"Clack in the library!" She immediately settled herself onto the ottoman in the rear drawing-room. "You are perfectly correct, Godfrey. We had far better remain here."

A moment prior, I had been burning with feverish anxiety, unsure of my next move. Now, I grew ice-cold, certain of everything. To reveal myself after overhearing what I had was impossible. To retreat—save perhaps into the fireplace itself—was equally untenable. A martyrdom awaited me. Out of justice to my own conscience, I quietly adjusted the curtains so that I might both witness and listen. Then, I faced my martyrdom with the spirit of a primitive Christian.

"Do not sit on the ottoman," the young lady continued. "Fetch a chair, Godfrey. I prefer people to sit opposite me when we converse."

He took the nearest seat, a low chair. He was exceedingly tall, and many sizes too large for it. I had never before seen his legs held in such disadvantageous view.

"Well?" she pressed. "What did you say to them?"

"Just what you said to me, dear Rachel," he replied.

"That my mother was not well today? And that I did not quite like leaving her to attend the concert?"

"Those were the precise words. They were grieved to lose you at the concert, but they fully understood. Everyone sent their love; everyone expressed a cheerful belief that Lady Verinder's indisposition would soon pass."

"You do not think it is serious, do you, Godfrey?"

"By no means! In a few days, I feel certain, all will be well again."

"I think so too. I was frightened at first, but I agree. It was very kind of you to go and apologize for me to people who are almost strangers to you. But why not have accompanied them to the concert? It seems terribly unfair that you should miss the music as well."

"Do not say that, Rachel! If you only knew how much happier I am—right here, with you!"

He clasped his hands and gazed at her. In that posture, he inadvertently turned toward me. Can words convey the sickness that seized me when I observed the exact same pathetic expression on his face that had once charmed me while he pleaded for destitute millions on the platform at Exeter Hall?

"It is difficult to overcome one's bad habits, Godfrey. But do strive to conquer the habit of paying compliments—for my sake."

"I never paid *you* a compliment, Rachel, in my entire life. Successful love may occasionally employ the language of flattery, I admit. But hopeless love, dearest, always speaks the truth."

He drew his chair closer and took her hand as he uttered the phrase "hopeless love." A momentary silence ensued. He, who thrilled multitudes, had undoubtedly thrilled *her*. I realized then the meaning of the words that had dropped from his mouth when he stood alone in the drawing-room: "I'll do it today." Alas! The most rigorous standards of propriety could scarcely fail to detect that he was performing that deed right then and there.

"Have you forgotten our agreement, Godfrey, when you spoke to me in the countryside?" she asked. "We agreed that we were to be cousins, and nothing more."

"I break that agreement, Rachel, every time I see you."

"Then do not see me."

"Quite futile! I break the agreement every time I think of you. Oh, Rachel! How kindly you told me only the other day that my place in your estimation was higher than it had ever been! Am I mad to build hopes upon those dear words? Am I mad to dream of a future day when your heart may soften to me? Do not tell me so, if I am! Spare me my delusion, dearest! I must possess *that* to cherish, to comfort me, if I have nothing else!"

His voice trembled, and he pressed a white handkerchief to his eyes. Exeter Hall again! Nothing lacked to complete the parallel save for the audience, the cheers, and the glass of water. Even her obstinate nature was moved. I saw her lean slightly closer to him. I detected a new tone of interest in her subsequent words.

"Are you truly certain, Godfrey, that you love me as deeply as that?"

"Certain! You know what I was, Rachel. Let me tell you what I am. I have lost every interest in life except my interest in you. A transformation has overtaken me which I cannot account for. Would you believe it? My charitable endeavors are now an unendurable nuisance. When I encounter a Ladies' Committee, I wish myself at the uttermost ends of the earth!"

If the annals of apostasy contain anything comparable to such a declaration, I can only state that the specific case is not available in the stores of my reading. I thought of the Mothers'-Small-Clothes. I thought of the Sunday-Sweetheart-Supervision. I thought of the other societies, too numerous to list, all constructed upon this man as upon a tower of strength. I thought of the struggling Female Boards, who, metaphorically speaking, drew the breath of their professional lives through the nostrils of Mr. Godfrey—of that very Mr. Godfrey who had just reviled our noble work as a "nuisance" and declared his desire to vanish to the ends of the earth when found in our company! My young female companions will surely feel encouraged to persevere when I mention that this spectacle nearly broke my discipline before I could swallow my righteous indignation in silence. Yet, it is only fair to myself to add that I did not lose a single syllable of the conversation.

Rachel spoke next. "You have made your confession," she stated. "I wonder whether it would cure you of your unhappy attachment to me if I made mine?"

He started. I confess I started as well. Both of us thought she was about to reveal the mystery of the Moonstone.

"Would you think, upon seeing me," she continued, "that I am the wretchedest girl alive? It is true, Godfrey. What greater wretchedness exists than to live degraded in one's own estimation? That is my existence now."

"My dear Rachel! It is impossible that you have any cause to speak of yourself in such terms!"

"How do you know I have no cause?"

"Can you ask me the question! I know it, because I know *you*. Your silence, dearest, has never diminished you in the eyes of your true friends. The disappearance of your precious birthday gift may seem strange; your unexplained connection with that event may seem stranger still—"

"Are you speaking of the Moonstone, Godfrey?"

"I certainly thought you referred—"

"I referred to nothing of the sort. I can hear reports of the loss of the Moonstone, let whoever wishes speak of it, without feeling degraded in my own estimation. Should the story of the Diamond ever come to light, it will be known that I accepted a dreadful responsibility; it will be known that I entangled myself in the keeping of a miserable secret—but it will be as clear as the sun at noonday that I did nothing mean! You have misunderstood me, Godfrey. It is my fault for failing to speak more plainly. At whatever cost, I shall be plainer now. Suppose you were not in love with me? Suppose you were in love with another woman?"

"Yes?"

"Suppose you discovered that woman to be utterly unworthy of you? Suppose you were convinced that it was a disgrace to waste another thought upon her? Suppose the bare idea of ever marrying such a person made your face burn merely from contemplation?"

"Yes?"

"And suppose, despite all that—you could not tear her from your heart? Suppose the feeling she aroused in you (during the time you believed in her) was not a feeling to be concealed? Suppose the love this wretch inspired in you... Oh, how can I find words to express it! How can I make a *man* comprehend that a feeling which horrifies me in myself can simultaneously fascinate me?
