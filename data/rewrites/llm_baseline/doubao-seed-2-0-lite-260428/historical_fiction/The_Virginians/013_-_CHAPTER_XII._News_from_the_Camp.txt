The parting between the two Warrington brothers is long over: George has settled into his place as part of General Braddock’s personal staff, and Harry has drifted back to Castlewood to see to the estate he was left to tend alone. But his heart rides with the army, and none of his old familiar pastimes at home hold an ounce of joy. He will not admit to a soul how deep his disappointment burns, that he was left behind under the quiet, suddenly melancholy roof now that George is gone. He passes his brother’s empty bedroom with his face turned away, takes George’s usual seat at the head of the dinner table, and sighs before he takes a sip from the chipped silver tankard the two boys had carved their initials into as children.

Madam Esmond Warrington calls out the toast to the King with ringing, unshakable stoutness every single day. On Sundays, when Harry leads the household in the service and reaches the line of prayer for all travellers by land and by water, her response of *“We beseech Thee to hear us”* rings out with a weight of solemnity that makes the old footmen hovering in the back hall pause mid-step. She talks of George constantly, but always cheerfully, as if his safe return is a certainty no one could ever question. She marches straight into his vacant room every morning, head held high, no sign of tremor on her face, and sees that his books, linen, and scattered papers are all tended with perfect care. She speaks of him with a special, reverent softness, and constantly reminds the old servants over meals of what they must do “when Mr. George comes home.”

Mrs. Mountain is on the edge of tears the second George’s name is spoken, and Harry’s face settles into a tight, haggard grimace whenever the conversation turns to the camp—though he will never confess to the sharp, cold fear coiled in his chest. His mother, by contrast, is always calm, still. She fumbles at piquet and backgammon far more than her usual sharp self would ever allow, and the servants find her already wide awake and fully dressed, walking the halls by candlelight, no matter how early they rise. She has asked old Mr. Dempster, their long-time Scottish tutor, to come back and take up residence full time at Castlewood. No longer sharp or haughty as her old stubborn temper once made her, she speaks to everyone gently, never presses a point harshly, and spends hours recounting stories of her own father’s old military campaigns, how he came home from every battle with no wound worse than a gash across the sword arm—so she trusts and hopes her eldest son will do just the same.

George sends letters home to his brother as often as he can, and once the army sets out on its march, he scribbles a rough, smudge-stained journal on scrap paper that he forwards at every possible chance. Harry devours every line, reading it over and over until he nearly knows it by heart, and reads whole sections aloud in family gatherings as the long summer evenings stretch out: Madam sits perfectly upright at the tea table (no Esmond would ever stoop to lean against the back of a chair), little Fanny Mountain bends over her sampler, Mr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain linger over their card game, and the old, quiet servants drift near the parlour door in the gloaming, listening to every word of the young master’s adventures. The yellowed pages, covered in George’s slanted, slim handwriting, are handled so carefully you could almost believe the two boys are still there, alive in that fire-lit room—though now their portraits hang, faint and faded, in tarnished gold frames, as if they had never walked the earth at all. But we can almost hear Harry’s warm, eager voice as he reads:
> “It must be said the provinces are treating King George II’s service shamefully, and his Excellency the General is in a proper fury. Virginia is bad enough, and Maryland little better, but Pennsylvania is the worst of all. We begged them to send word they would supply our troops coming over to fight the French, and promised they would sustain the men once they arrived. Now they not only break that promise and make barely any provision for their defenders, but their people charge absurd, exorbitant prices for cattle and stores, and cheat the very soldiers who have come to fight their battles. No wonder the General swears so fiercely, and the men are sullen. Delays have stretched on for weeks and months, while the French have fortified every fort on our frontier unimpeded.
> “Though I will never admit I love Colonel Washington, your favourite (and I swear I am not jealous, Hal), you cannot deny he is a brave man and a fine officer. The General’s whole staff respects him, and he asks his opinion on every matter. He is almost the only man among us who has seen Indians in full war paint, and I own he was right to fire on Monsieur Jumonville last year.
> “That silly quarrel at Benson’s Tavern never came to a duel, rest assured. Captain Waring came into camp eager to pick it up again, bearing a challenge from Captain Grace, and your hot-headed friend here was ready to take it up, and asked Colonel Wingfield, one of the General’s aides, to be my second. But when Wingfield heard the whole story: that the fight started because Grace was drunk, and Waring was tipsy, and the two 44th Regiment men chose to insult a militia officer for no reason at all, he swore he would never let Colonel Washington meet them on the field. He threatened to bring the whole matter straight to his Excellency, who would court martial both captains for brawling, drunkenness, and indecent conduct. The two men were forced to sheathe their swords and swallow their wrath. When sober they were good-natured enough, and they ate their humble pie over a reconciliation dinner hosted by Colonel Washington, who was as calm and proper as any prince could ever be. Hang him, he has no faults at all—that’s why I still dislike him. When he marries that widow, what a dreary life she will lead!”

“I wonder at the taste of some men, and the effrontery of some women,” Madam Esmond says, setting her teacup down sharply. “I wonder at any woman who has been married once, forgetting herself so far as to marry again! Don’t you, Mountain?”
“Monstrous,” Mrs. Mountain agrees, with a quiet, knowing little smirk.
Mr. Dempster keeps his eyes fixed intently on his glass of spiced punch. Harry looks as if he is half choking on suppressed laughter, or some other secret, unnameable feeling. But his mother taps his arm gently. “Go on, Harry. Continue your brother’s journal. He writes well, but ah, will he ever write half so fine as my papa?”

Harry resumes reading.
> “We keep the strictest order in camp here. The rules against drunkenness and disorderly behaviour are severe. Every company’s roll is called at morning, noon, and night, and the officers submit a list of absent or unruly men to their regimental commander, who ensures they are properly punished. The drummers are almost constantly at work. Oh Harry, it made me sick to my stomach the first time I saw the lash cut across a strong, white soldier’s back, and heard the poor man’s piteous scream.”

“Horrid!” Madam cries. “I think I would have murdered my old schoolmaster Ward if he had ever flogged me. Thank Heaven he only got as far as cracking my knuckles with a ruler! The men are well looked after, I see—but I wish the officers were held to the same standard. The local Indians just broke up their camp and left in a huff, because the young officers were forever drinking with the squaws and… and… well, I will not write the rest of that here.”

Harry pauses, clearly not wanting to read that last part out loud in front of prim little Fanny, still bent over her sampler.
“Skip that part about those odious tipsy creatures,” Madam says, and Harry lifts his voice to a bright, more respectable tone.
> “Every regiment holds full Divine Service at the head of its colours every Sunday. The General does everything mortal man can to stop plundering, and encourage local farmers to bring their provisions to our market. He has declared any soldier who dares molest a market woman or interrupt trade will be shot. He has even ordered the price of provisions raised a penny a pound, and lent money out of his own private purse to buy supplies for the camp. He is a strange, contradictory man, this General Braddock. He flogs his men without mercy, but he gives away his own coin without stint. He swears the most tremendous oaths at dinner, and tells stories that Mountain would be shocked to hear after the third glass of punch—but he never misses a single service. He adores the great Duke of Cumberland, and has his name on his lips constantly. Our two regiments both served in Scotland, so I dare say Mr. Dempster knew the colour of their uniform facings.”

“Aye, we saw the tails of their coats as they ran, as well as their facings,” the old Jacobite tutor growls, a dry little smile tugging at his mouth.
> “Colonel Washington has been laid low with a very sharp fever, and has barely been well enough to keep pace with the march. Ought he not to go home and be nursed by that widow of his? When either of us is laid up, we are almost as good friends as we ever were. But I still feel foolish for hating him all those months. Good powers, Harry, I was in such a fury at that tavern we could have fired a few shots off at each other and cleared the air completely! Even if I don’t love him the way you do, I know he is a good soldier, a good officer, and a brave, honest man. At any rate, I shall never dislike him just for refusing to be our stepfather.”

“A stepfather, indeed!” Madam cries out, half laughing and half indignant. “Jealousy and prejudice have quite turned that poor boy’s head! Does he suppose the Marquis of Esmond’s own daughter and heiress could not find far grander men to be stepfathers for her sons than a mere provincial surveyor? If there are any more of those foolish allusions in George’s journal, skip them Harry, my dear. We have heard quite enough about that silly old quarrel already.”

“‘Tis a pretty sight,” Harry continues, “to see the long line of redcoats winding through the pine forests, or forming up camp after a day’s march. We take every possible precaution against surprise, so no prowling Indian can creep up on us unawares. Our advanced sentries and allied native scouts have fallen in with enemy raiding parties more than once, and taken a scalp or two from them first. Those French and their painted allies are such cruel villains that we do not think of showing them any mercy. Only yesterday we found a little boy, scalped but still alive, in an empty lone cottage, where his parents had been murdered and dragged off by the savages. The General was so furious at the cruelty he has offered a reward of five pounds for every Indian scalp brought into camp.
> “When the day’s march ends, you ought to see our camp, laid out with such perfect care. The baggage, the General’s tents, and his guard are placed right in the very centre. We have outlying sentries posted in pairs, in groups of three, groups of ten, and whole companies strung out for miles. At the first sign of an attack, they are ordered to fall back on the main body and rally around the tents and baggage, which are stacked and arranged to form a strong makeshift fortification. Sady my servant and I are marching on foot now, you must know. The Pennsylvanians sent such rickety, useless horses into camp that they dropped dead almost immediately. Any good horses left were ordered to be turned over to the supply train, so Roxana now carries two heavy packs on her back instead of her young master. She still knows me, of course, whinnies the second she sees me, and I walk beside her, chattering about Castlewood all the way down the trail.
> *July 4.* To guard against surprise, every man in the column has been warned to pay strict attention to the drum beats: halt immediately at the long roll, and advance to the beat of the long march. We are all far more alert to the enemy now. Our advanced pickets are doubled, and two sentries stand at every post. The men on the front picket lines stay under arms, bayonets fixed, all through the night, and are relieved every two hours. The men who come off duty lie down right beside their muskets, and are never allowed to leave their posts. It is very clear we are drawing near to the enemy at last. The whole of Virginia lay parched and scorched under the blazing July sun. For ten long days, no news had come back from the column advancing on the Ohio. Their march, slow and toilsome though it was through the tangled forest, was bound to meet the enemy soon. The troops, led by experienced, capable officers, were now accustomed to the wilderness, and no one feared an ambush. Every precaution had been taken. The talk in every small town, every gentleman’s plantation, and every rough roadside tavern across the province was that the French forces would never dare make a stand. Braddock’s two veteran regiments from Britain, plus their Virginian and Pennsylvania allies, were more than a match for any force the French could bring down from Montreal. Madam Esmond swore she would buy George the finest thoroughbred in all Virginia or the Carolinas to replace his beloved Roxana. It was only for the King’s service that no one would part with their good horses for any price, of course.

Though everyone at Castlewood pretended to be cheerful at their meals, never speaking of anything but a certain, triumphant end to the campaign, none of them could rest easy. They rode from neighbour’s house to neighbour’s house constantly, chasing every scrap of news that drifted through the province. The plantation negroes, riding cross-country at midnight to visit friends or sweethearts, spread gossip faster than any post rider could. For three weeks after the army marched out, every report was bright and hopeful. But on July 10, a vast, sudden gloom settled over the whole province. A look of terror and doubt fell on every face. The fiddles fell silent in the slave quarters, the singing and laughter of the usually cheerful black families went quiet. Horsemen crowded every roadside tavern, drinking, yelling, brawling over the bars, all bearing the same terrible story: the army had been ambushed. Cut to pieces almost to a man. All the officers were picked off by hidden French and Indian marksmen. The General had been wounded, carried off the field in his silk sash, and now the word came that Braddock was dead, scalped by a French-allied warrior.

Mrs. Mountain gave a sharp, piercing scream the second Gumbo brought the news back from across the James River, and little Fanny ran crying to her mother’s arms. “Lord God Almighty, watch over us, and defend my boy!” Madam Esmond gasped, sinking straight down on her knees, her rigid hands lifted up to heaven. Harry was as pale as his mother’s fine linen tablecloth. He and old Dempster armed themselves, mounted their horses, and set off north that very hour, with two trusted mounted servants riding behind them. Every mile they travelled along the rough, newly hewn road the expedition had cut through the forest, the terrible scale of the disaster grew clearer. Fugitives from the fatal battle of July 9 had poured into Dunbar’s camp fifty miles back from the field. Every straggler they stopped told the same tale: a thousand men were dead. Two thirds of all the officers were killed. Every last one of Braddock’s aides-de-camp had been hit. Harry’s heart ached so sharp he could barely breathe, recalling every childhood memory he and George shared, every secret vow they had made as boys to ride to war side by side. Wounded men looked up at him and wept at the grief written on his face. Rough backwoodsmen clapped him on the shoulder with tears in their eyes, and even the gruff old Scottish tutor could barely look at him for the tears in his own eyes, grieving far more for the heartbroken boy riding north to find his brother than for the rest of the fallen men.
