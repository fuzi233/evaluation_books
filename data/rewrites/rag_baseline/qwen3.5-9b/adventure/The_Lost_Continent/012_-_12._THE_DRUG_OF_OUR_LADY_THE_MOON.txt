The sun was dying, obscured by the black smoke of a burning mountain. Phorenice wrung water from her clothes until her shoulders shook. "Row harder, Deucalion," she said, her voice thin. "Take me through the water-gate. Let me be restored to my comforts. That merchant will regret ruining my garments, and I regret them, too. Being a woman means I cannot simply replace them." She tossed her short red hair aside, looking at me sideways. "Do you approve of my wisdom in coming here without an escort?"

"The Empress can do no wrong," I recited the old formula, forcing a smile.

"At least I have shown you I can fight," she continued, her tone shifting to something lighter, almost mocking. "I caught you looking at me with pleasant approval once or twice. You are a difficult man to thaw, Deucalion, but you warm perceptibly the longer you stay near me. We shall be a pair of rustic sweethearts soon enough, if this continues. I am glad I devised the plan to approach those smelly fishers."

She had taken me out in the litter solely to invite a fight, to demonstrate her skill, and perhaps to observe how I carried myself under stress. If we were to live together as husband and wife, it was necessary to know each other's limits precisely. Furthermore, I was too accustomed to the glorious handling of arms to quarrel deeply with anyone offering a worthy challenge. Yet, I could not stop comparing Phorenice to another woman. With a similar opportunity presented, Nais had spared me the struggle through pity for the squalid rebels who refused to acknowledge her as chieftain. Here, however, the Empress frittered away twenty of her hardest subjects merely to gratify a whim.

Loyal to my vows as a priest and to the commands of the High Council on the Sacred Mountain, I suppressed these comparisons. As I rowed toward the harbor forts, sending prayers to the High Gods for the dexterity of my tongue, they answered. Through their love for Atlantis and its harassed people, they granted me the power of speech Phorenice craved. Her eyes glowed as I spoke.

The beach where we had fought was protected by a chain of jagged rocks rising from the deep, running from the harbor wall to the city gates. Fishermen knew the passes, navigating between stones or leaping into their light boats if danger threatened. I possessed neither the knowledge nor the dexterity. I prayed silently: *Show me, High Gods, if You wish this woman who has defiled You to reign, and if You wish me to serve as her husband.*

There was no half-answer. A great wave rose in the lagoon behind us, a surge caused only by an earthquake. On its sleek back, we were hurled forward, cleared of the reefs and their clinging seaweed without ever touching a single stone. I bowed my head as we rowed toward the forts. The High Gods had not yet taken vengeance for the insults offered to Them.

Inside the forts, sentries beat drums in their rotation, paying little heed to the fishing boat clamoring for the chain to be lowered. Fortunately, two officers were airing themselves in a turret atop the nearer fort. Recognizing our shouts, they silenced the drums. Torches were lowered to inspect our faces, and with a splash, the great chain descended into the water.

A galley lay moored against the harbor wall. Ropes were let down from the fort, and a crew manned the oars. Customary gifts of dry clothing were handed to us in the cabin, replacing our sodden raiment. Fevers await those who carry wet clothes after sunset; though I know drugs can ward them off, I noted with grim amusement that the Empress possessed little of the Goddess within her regarding the frailties of humanity.

The galley crossed the calm harbor waters swiftly, tying itself to the golden rings of the royal quay. While we awaited litters, I watched a lantern lit in the boat guarding Phorenice's mammoth. The huge red beast stood shoulder-deep in the water, trunk upturned, trembling under the light. I did not intercede for it. If the mammoth sank into the harbor mud, I could bear the loss with equanimity.

Truth be told, the ride on that beast's back had left me with a sense of helplessness nearly intolerable. Perhaps circumstances have bred undue self-reliance in me; others must judge. But I prefer walking on my own feet, as the Gods fashioned us. On my own feet, I can guard my own head and neck—a feat accomplished on four continents, throughout a long and active life, on countless occasions. On the back of that detestable creature, I grew as nervous as a child or a coward.

We had little leisure for such introspection. Waiting for the litters, Phorenice asked the port-captain, who approached officiously, about Nais. He replied that she had been locked in a dungeon beneath the royal pyramid, with a guard officer bonding for her safety.

"It is to be hoped he understands his work," the Empress said. "That pretty Nais knows the pyramid better than most; he may be sent to the tormentors for placing her in a cell with a secret outlet. Would you feel pleasure if the girl escaped, Deucalion?"

"Assuredly," I said, knowing secrecy was useless. "I hold no enmity against Nais."

"But I do," she said viciously. "And I remain minded to lock your faith to me with the wedding gift you know of."

"The thing shall be done," I said. "First, the Empress of Atlantis."

"Poof!" she exclaimed. "Deucalion, you are too stiff and formal. You ought to be honored that I condescend to be jealous of your favors. Take my hand, sir, to help me into the litter. Come in beside me and keep me warm against the night air. Ho! Guards with the torches! Step back against the street walls. The perfume you burn stifles me."

That night, a feast was held in the royal banqueting hall. I sat beside Phorenice on the raised dais beneath the symbols of the snake and the outstretched hand. Our forthcoming relationship, previously implied, was now proclaimed openly. The Empress acknowledged me as her husband-to-be, and the throng of courtiers hailed me as superior to themselves by virtue of her choice. There was method in their salutations. Rumors of my preference for older, simpler habits must have circulated; consequently, no one drank to my health in the new, impertinent manner. Decorously, each lord and lady spilled a goblet at my feet. When I invited anyone, man or woman, to share tit-bits from my platter, they ate simply and thankfully, avoiding the extravagant gestures of kissing or pocketing.

The flaring jets of earth-breath revealed a plainer dress and a more sober demeanor among the banqueters. Phorenice leaned over until her ruddy curls brushed my face, chiding me in a playful whisper for usurping her authority.

"Oh, sir," she pleaded mockingly, "do not make your rule over us too ascetic. I gave no orders for this change. Tonight, there are no perfumes; the food is so plain I half intend to burn the cook. As for the clothes and jewels of these diners, by my face! they might have come straight from the old King's reign before I stepped in to show how tasteful colors and glinting gems can be. It is done by no order of mine, Deucalion. They have swung to this change by sheer courtier instinct. Look at the beards of the men! There is not half the curl today that they showed with such exquisiteness yesterday. By my face! I believe they'd shave their chins tomorrow as smooth as yours if you continue setting the fashions at this prodigious rate and I do not interfere."

"Why hinder them if they feel cleaner-shaven?"

"No, sir. There shall be only one clean chin where a beard can grow in all Atlantis, and that shall be carried by the man who is husband to the Empress. Why, my Deucalion, would you have no sumptuary laws? Would you have these good folk here and the common people outside imitate us in every cut of hair and fold of garment that pleases us? Come, sir, if you and I chose to declare our sovereignty marked only by superior strength of arm and wit, they would hate us instantly for our arrogance. Whereas, if we keep apart to ourselves a few mere personal decorations, these become just objects to admire and pleasantly envy."

"You show me that there is more in the office of a ruler than meets the eye."

"And yet they tell me, and indeed show me, that you have ruled with some success."

"I employed the older method. It requires a Phorenice to invent these nicer flights."

"Flatterer!" she said, smiting me playfully with the back of her small fingers. "You are becoming as great a courtier as any of them. You make me blush with your fine pleasantries, Deucalion, and there is no fan-girl here tonight to cool my cheek. I must choose another. But it shall not be Ylga. Ylga seems to possess more kindness for you than I like. If she is wise, she will move to her palace on the other side of the city, occupy herself with ordering her slaves and making embroideries. I shall not be hard on Ylga unless she forces me, but I will have no woman in this kingdom treat you with undue civility."
