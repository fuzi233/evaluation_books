The whole family was up before the sun peeked over the island’s palm treetops, and the four boys scrambled across the wide branches of their giant tree castle like a troop of overexcited young monkeys.
“Dad! What do we get to do first today?” they whooped, thumping down the ladder one after another. “Give us jobs, we’re ready!”
“Today, we rest,” I told them, grinning at their confused, surprised faces.
“Rest?” Jack echoed, bouncing on the balls of his feet. “Why would we rest? There’s a hundred cool things to build and explore!”
“Remember the rule we followed at home: six days for work, and the seventh for honoring the Creator who’s kept us safe so far. That’s today—Sunday,” I explained.
“Wait, it’s actually Sunday?” Jack’s face lit up. “That’s fun! I won’t do boring work, I promise. But can I bring my bow and shoot, and climb around the trees all afternoon?”
“That isn’t quite the gentle rest we’re supposed to keep for the Lord’s day,” I laughed. “You’re used to worshipping in a stone church back home, but we’ve got something far more beautiful here: the whole island’s green shade, the birds singing, the fresh grass under our feet. We can worship here just as well.”
“But there’s no minister, no organ, no hymn books!” Franz piped up, pouting a little.
“Then we’ll sing the hymns we remember, right here under the leaves. C’mon, boys, down the ladder—let’s turn our tree landing into our little worship space for the morning.”
As the last boy slid down to the soft grass, I leaned over to my wife Elizabeth. “I’ll walk them through a short service with a little allegory story instead of a long sermon, to keep their attention. Once that’s done, they can have whatever quiet, innocent fun they like, and we’ll all take a slow sunset walk later to enjoy the island.”
Elizabeth agreed wholeheartedly. After a quick breakfast, we all sat in the dappled shade, sang the old hymns we could recite from memory, and said prayers of thanks that we’d all survived the shipwreck. When I told the boys I’d tell them a special parable instead of a regular sermon, Franz’s eyes went wide.
“I love parables best of all!” he said. “When can we read from our Bible again, Dad? I miss our old story readings.”
“Your words are such a good reminder, little one,” I admitted, my cheeks warm. “I’ve been so focused on hauling supplies from the wreck to feed our bodies that I completely forgot to grab a copy of the Bible. I’ll make it a top priority on my next trip back to the ship.”
At that, Elizabeth jumped up, rummaged in her little “magic bag” of supplies she’d grabbed from the wreck, and pulled out a full, dry copy of the Holy Scriptures. My chest filled with relief as I took it, read a few beloved passages out loud, and told the boys the story of the Great King who tended a desolate island until it bloomed for his colonists, who fell into rebellion only to be offered free pardon and a chance to return to his perfect, bright kingdom. The boys sat completely silent, rapt, until I finished tying the parable’s lessons to our little new life on the island, and closed the service with a short, quiet prayer.
Once the service ended, everyone drifted off to their own small projects. I grabbed a handful of arrows and set to work tipping them with sharp porcupine quills. Franz hung around begging for his own tiny kid-sized bow and set of arrows, while Fritz asked for help turning a wild tiger-cat hide into waterproof carrying cases. Jack helped me fix the quills to the arrow shafts, and mumbled that he wished he had glue to hold the spines extra tight.
“Mamma’s soup is super sticky!” Franz blurted out. “I can go ask for a chunk of it right now!”
“Nah, silly, that’s not glue,” Jack snickered. “We should look for real glue in the toolbox we hauled off the wreck.”
“Hey, don’t tease your brother,” I told Jack gently. “Some of the most famous, world-changing inventions started out as ideas that sounded just as silly as Franz’s soup glue thought. You never know what random thought might work.”
Just then a crack rang out right over our heads, and two small birds dropped dead at our feet. We looked up to see Ernest grinning from a high tree branch, still holding his smoking gun.
“Did you see that? Perfect shot, right?” he cheered, slipping down the ladder to grab the birds. One was a plump thrush, the other a tiny, sweet ortolan that we knew was a famous delicacy. We realized the fig trees they’d been feeding on would soon be full of flocks of the little birds—if we caught enough of them and preserved them in barrels of melted butter, we’d have piles of ready food for the rainy season ahead.
By the time we finished talking, Jack had made a whole pile of sharp quill arrows, I’d finished Franz’s little bow, and the tiny hunter begged for a quiver to hold all his new gear. I stripped a long sheet of bark off a small sapling, fitted it with a flat bottom and a shoulder strap, and soon Franz was trotting around the clearing, quiver slung over his back, looking exactly like a tiny, cheerful Cupid his mom had laughed at.
We all gathered for lunch soon after, and I pitched a new, fun idea to the boys: let’s name all the different spots we’d explored on the island, so we wouldn’t have to say long, clunky descriptions every time we wanted to talk about a place.
“Oyster Bay!” Fritz shouted first.
“No, Lobster Bay!” Jack yelled over him. “To remember that giant lobster that tried to bite my leg our first day on the beach!”
“I think we should name it Safety Bay,” Elizabeth said softly, “to give thanks that we all made it here alive when the rest of the ship was lost.”
Everyone agreed immediately, and the names came fast after that. Our first old campsite got named Tentholm, the tiny islet in the bay where we found the dead shark became Shark’s Island, the reedy wetland full of flamingos we spotted was Flamingo Marsh. Our tree castle? After a whole minute of arguing, we named it Falconhurst, then quickly ticked off the rest: Prospect Hill for the first high hill we climbed to scan the horizon, Cape Disappointment for the rocky headland where we’d strained our eyes for hours looking for the rest of the ship’s crew and found no one, and Jackal River for the big stream that ran past our landing spot.
The rest of the afternoon was full of silly fun. Fritz finished his hide cases, and he and Jack talked me into helping make a spiky porcupine-skin cuirass for our dog Turk. We cleaned the hide, sewed it to fit snugly around his chest, and soon the big dog was trotting around like a weird, spiky little tank. Juno, our other dog, gave him the widest possible berth, and we all agreed no jackal or wild cat would ever dare take a bite out of him. I used the leftover scrap of porcupine hide to make a spiky little helmet for Jack, who paraded around the clearing so proud he could barely stop grinning.
When the sun started to dip low, we took a slow walk through the flower-dusted glades near Falconhurst, sang another hymn, and climbed up to our tree bunks for a peaceful, restful Sabbath night.
The next morning, I announced we’d be making a trip back to Tentholm—but we’d take a brand new route no one had explored before. We all trundled out fully armed: me and the three older boys carried guns and game bags, Franz had his tiny bow and full quiver. We must have looked hilarious: Fritz was wearing his margay-skin belt, Jack had his spiky porcupine helmet on, and the two of them looked like tiny, friendly young savages. Elizabeth walked beside me, the only unarmed one, carrying an empty jar to bring back a fresh supply of butter from our stores at Tentholm. Turk trotted ahead in his spiky armor, with Juno trailing a few steps behind, still too weirded out by his new look to get too close.
Knips, our pet monkey, immediately tried to hop up on Turk’s back for his usual ride—but one touch of his paw on the spiky porcupine hide made him make the most dramatic, disgusted face we’d ever seen. He chattered furiously, bounded over to Juno, hopped on her back instead, and settled in like he’d never had a better steed. Our pet flamingo, who’d gotten spoiled from all the pets we’d given him, decided he wanted to come too, and walked right at my heel the whole way down the stream bank.
The boys were running ahead, exploring the edges of the treeline, when we heard Ernest yell at the top of his lungs. He came sprinting back to us, red in the face, clutching a leafy green plant in his fist.
“Potatoes! Dad, that’s a potato plant!” he gasped.
“Acres of them, acres!” Jack yelled right behind him. “Come look!”
We all ran to the clearing, and sure enough, a huge stretch of rich soil was thick with the green leaves and little knobbly potato roots we all recognized right away. It was one of the best discoveries we could possibly have made—we’d never go hungry here, now that we had a steady crop of potatoes that would grow year round.
We dug and dug for so long that every game bag, every pocket, every pouch we carried was stuffed full of rough, brown potatoes. The boys begged to turn right back for Falconhurst immediately to cook our first harvest, but I reminded them we still had supplies to pick up at Tentholm, and we kept trundling along, heavy but giddy.
When we got to the stream’s rocky waterfall, we waded across, and stepped out into the most gorgeous tropical grove we’d ever seen: tall palms towered over thick ferns, jasmine and sweet-smelling vanilla blooms perfumed the air, and clusters of ripe pineapples grew all around. The boys immediately started tearing into the sweet, tangy fruit, until Elizabeth warned them there were no doctors on the island if they ate too much and got a stomach ache. We sent Knips scrambling up to the top of the pineapple patches to fetch us all the ripest fruit hidden high in the leaves, and I pointed out the valuable karatas plant nearby—its long fibrous leaves made perfect thread, the soft dry pith was great tinder for starting fires, and the bruised sap from the leaves made a great healing salve for cuts and scrapes. I broke off a dry piece of the karatas pith, struck two pebbles together over it, and a tiny spark caught the tinder immediately. The boys cheered, and I handed Elizabeth a handful of the soft, strong fibers to use for sewing.
Jack spotted an Indian fig tree covered in sharp thorns a minute later, and tried to climb it to grab the sweet fruit, only to come back with pricked, bleeding hands. I showed him how to skewer the fruit off the branches with a sharp wooden stick, no need to touch the thorns at all, and we all sat around eating the sweet, pulpy fruit until our bellies were full. Ernest found tiny bright red bugs clinging to the fruit, and realized immediately they were cochineal—the rare insects that make expensive scarlet dye back in Europe. We all laughed when Jack said we didn’t need fancy red clothes, but we’d definitely bring a bunch of the bugs back home with us when we were rescued.
By the time we finished exploring the grove, we’d reached Tentholm. All our old supplies were still perfectly safe, Elizabeth filled her jar with fresh butter, Fritz checked our stock of ammunition, and Jack and Ernest ran down to the beach to catch the half-wild ducks and geese we’d left penned there. The birds were too shy to come near the shore, so Ernest came up with a genius plan: he tied chunks of cheese to long pieces of string, tossed them out into the water, and gently reeled the hungry ducks back onto the sand one by one.
We packed a big sack of salt on Turk’s back (after taking off his spiky armor so he’d be more comfortable), tied the ducks and geese to our game bags, locked up our Tentholm tent, and started the walk home along the beach. By the time we trundled back up to Falconhurst, we clipped the wings of our new waterfowl to stop them from flying away, and set them free on the little stream near our tree. We had a delicious dinner of boiled new potatoes, fresh milk, and butter that night, then climbed up to our tree bunks full and happy.
I woke up at dawn the next morning, roused Ernest up early with me to pull driftwood off the beach. I wanted to break him of his lazy streak, and he groaned a little at first, but soon perked up, excited to have a secret little adventure before the rest of the boys woke up. We brought our donkey with us, and dragged a big wide palm bough behind him to carry our driftwood load. We pulled armfuls of heavy, smooth driftwood off the sand, stacked it on the palm bough for the donkey to drag home, and I spotted a half-buried old sailor’s chest sticking out of the dunes half-hidden by sand. We dragged that back too.
By the time we got back to Falconhurst, the other boys were blasting away at the ortolan birds in the fig trees, just like Ernest had predicted—they’d been wasting shots, missing most of their targets because they were firing straight up through the thick leaves. Elizabeth pointed out we’d run out of all our gunpowder soon if we kept that up, so we split up work: the younger boys wove simple bird snares out of the tough karatas fibers, while Fritz and Ernest helped me nail the driftwood together into a big, sturdy sledge that we could use to haul heavy loads of supplies back from Tentholm without straining our backs.
We were mid-build when we heard a huge squawking fuss coming from the chicken coop. The hens were flapping and cackling, and Ernest spotted Knips the monkey sneaking off into the grass with a stolen warm egg in his paw. He’d snuck into the coop and eaten half a dozen new laid eggs, and we found the rest hidden under a tuft of grass. Elizabeth decided she’d lock Knips up in the tree every morning until she’d collected all the new eggs, so the little thief couldn’t steal any more.
A little later, Jack was setting his new bird snares high in the fig tree branches, and came running back down to tell us he’d spotted a pair of our pet pigeons building a nest in the branches right above our landing. We all agreed no one would fire a gun near the nest, and the snares would only be set for the wild fruit-eating birds, so the little pigeon family would stay safe. We were building a home, piece by piece, and every new discovery, every new little addition to our little community, made it feel more and more like we belonged right there on our little island.
