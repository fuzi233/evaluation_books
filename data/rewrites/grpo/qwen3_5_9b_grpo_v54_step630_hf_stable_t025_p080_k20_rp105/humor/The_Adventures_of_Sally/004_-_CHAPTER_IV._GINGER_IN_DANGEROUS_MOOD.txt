A few days later, the universe conspired to force a reunion. Bruce Carmyle, having returned from Roville via a route that bypassed Ginger's preoccupied gaze, bumped into his cousin in the bustling chaos of Piccadilly. Ginger, desperate to maintain the separation he craved, was already mid-nod, ready to vanish into the crowd, when Carmyle planted a hand on his arm.

"Just the man I wanted to see," Bruce announced with a smile that felt dangerously out of place.

"Oh, hullo!" Ginger replied, his voice lacking any genuine warmth.

"I was thinking of dropping by your club."

"Yes?"

"Yes. Cigarette?"

Ginger stared at the offered case with the wary suspicion of a man who has been lured onto a train platform only to find a magician pulling rabbits out of thin air. He felt utterly bewildered. In all the years they had known each other, he couldn't recall a single instance where his cousin displayed such unadulterated geniality. It was baffling, especially given that the Scrymgeour affair remained a festering wound, and the Family was currently in high session, dissecting it with surgical precision.

"Been back in London long?"

"Day or two."

"I heard quite by accident you'd returned and were staying at the club. By the way, thank you for introducing me to Miss Nicholas."

Ginger jumped as if he'd been electrocuted.

"What!"

"I was in that compartment at Roville Station, you know. You threw her right on top of me. We agreed to consider that an introduction. She's quite attractive."

Bruce Carmyle hadn't entirely decided if he liked Sally, but one thing was certain: she was not going to fade from his life. Her abrupt departure had left him with that specific, maddening itch of dissatisfaction that often mimics the early stages of love. She had won the first round, and now he felt a burning desire to meet her again and prove that there was more to him than she apparently suspected. Bruce Carmyle was piqued; he was convinced that a future without her would be terribly flat.

"A very attractive girl. We had a very pleasant talk."

"I bet you did," Ginger muttered, his tone dripping with envy.

"By the way, she didn't happen to give you her address?"

"Why?" Ginger asked, his guard instantly rising. His attitude toward Sally's address was akin to a connoisseur guarding a unique masterpiece; he intended to keep it secret and gloat over it privately.

"Well, I—er—I promised to send her some books she was anxious to read..."

"I shouldn't think she gets much time for reading."

"Books which are not published in America."

"Oh, pretty nearly everything is published in America, what? Bound to be, I mean."

"Well, these particular books are not," Bruce said shortly, finding Ginger's reserve increasingly irritating. He wished he had been more creative in his approach.

"Give them to me and I'll send them to her," Ginger offered.

"Good Lord, man!" Bruce snapped. "I'm capable of sending a few books to America. Where does she live?"

Ginger reluctantly revealed the sacred number of the holy street that served as Sally's headquarters. With a persistent devil like his cousin, there seemed no escape, so he surrendered the information grudgingly.

"Thanks." Bruce Carmyle scribbled the details down with a gold pencil in a dapper, morocco-bound notebook. He was the type of man who always carried a pen and never relied on the backs of envelopes.

A silence stretched between them. Bruce cleared his throat.

"I saw Uncle Donald this morning," he said.

The geniality vanished instantly. There was no need for it anymore, and Bruce was a man who despised waste. He spoke coldly, and beneath the words lay a familiar, stinging hint of reproof.

"Yes?" Ginger said moodily. This was the uncle in whose office he had made his debut as a hasher: a respectable figure at the National Liberal Club, but never a favorite of Ginger's. While there were other minor uncles and subsidiary aunts to make up the Family, Uncle Donald was unquestionably the managing director, and Ginger considered him to be a human blister.

"He wants you to dine with him tonight at Bleke's."

Ginger's depression deepened. A dinner with Uncle Donald would hardly be a cheerful affair, even in the exotic setting of the Arabian Nights. There was something about Uncle Donald's personality that would have sobered the orgies of Emperor Tiberius at Capri. To dine with him at a morgue like Bleke's Coffee House—a relic of Old London that catered almost exclusively to regular patrons who hadn't missed an evening there in half a century—was to touch bed-rock. Ginger was extremely doubtful whether flesh and blood could survive it.

"Tonight?" he said. "Oh, you mean tonight? Well..."

"Don't be a fool. You know as well as I do that you've got to go." Uncle Donald's invitations were royal commands within the Family. "If you've another engagement, you must put it off."

"Oh, all right."

"Seven-thirty sharp."

"All right," Ginger said gloomily.

The two men parted ways, Bruce heading east to his chambers at the Temple to see clients, while Ginger headed west, following the path his cousin had taken. There was little sympathy between these cousins, yet oddly enough, their thoughts converged on the same object. Bruce, cutting briskly through the crowds of Piccadilly Circus, was thinking of Sally. So was Ginger, as he loafed aimlessly toward Hyde Park Corner, bumping through pedestrians in a sort of mental coma.

Since returning to London, Ginger had been in a terrible state. He mooned through the days and slept poorly at night. If there is one thing more rotten than another in a pretty blighted world, one thing that gives a fellow the pip and reduces him to the condition of an absolute onion, it is hopeless love. Hopeless love had stirred Ginger up. His soul had once been placid. Even the financial crash that had so drastically altered his life had not bruised him deeply. His temperament had allowed him to bear the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune with a philosophic "Right ho!" But now, everything was different. Things irritated him acutely, which he had previously accepted as inevitable—his Uncle Donald's moustache, for instance, and the owner's habit of using it during meals as a sort of zariba or earthwork against the assaults of soup.

"By gad!" Ginger thought, stopping suddenly opposite Devonshire House. "If he uses that damned shrubbery as a soup-strainer tonight, I'll slosh him with a fork!"

Hard thoughts... hard thoughts! And they were getting harder all the time, for nothing grows more quickly than a mood of rebellion. Rebellion is a forest fire that flames across the soul. The spark had been lit in Ginger, and long before he reached Hyde Park Corner, he was ablaze and crackling. By the time he returned to his club, he was practically a menace to society—to that specific section of it, at any rate, which included his Uncle Donald, his minor uncles George and William, and his aunts Mary, Geraldine, and Louise.

The mood did not dissipate when he began to dress for the dismal festivities at Bleke's. He scowled as he struggled morosely with an obstinate tie. One cannot disguise the fact: Ginger was warming up. And it was just at this moment that Fate, as though it had been waiting for the perfect psychological instant, applied the finishing touch.

There was a knock at the door, and a waiter entered with a telegram.

Ginger looked at the envelope. It had been readdressed and forwarded from the Hotel Normandie. It was a wireless, handed in on board the White Star liner *Olympic*, and it read:

Ginger sat down heavily on the bed.

The driver of the taxi-cab, which pulled up at the dingy door of Bleke's Coffee House in the Strand at twenty-five minutes past seven, was rather struck by his fare's manner and appearance. A determined-looking sort of young bloke, was the taxi-driver's verdict.
