When Maso returned to the threshold and admitted the visitors, Nello, performing a profound courtesy to Romola, gently propelled Tito forward, guiding him toward her father.

"Messer Bardo," Nello announced, his voice adopting a measured, deferential timbre uncommon to his usual boisterous self, "I bring you the Greek scholar who has long sought audience with you. Driven by both the renown of your learning and the value of your collections, and by an urgent necessity arising from a recent shipwreck, he desires your patronage. His name is Tito Melema, at your service."

Astonishment, akin to seeing a panther-clad stranger wielding a thyrsus, gripped Romola. The barber had concealed the youth's age and visage entirely. Among her father's academic guests, she rarely encountered anyone but middle-aged or silver-haired men. Yet, etched in her mind with indelible clarity, remained the singular masculine face of her lost brother: fair-complexioned, with sunny hair mirroring her own. But a defensive wall of proud self-reliance, forged by her father's complaints of worldly injustice, hemmed in the rushing tide of admiration. Tito's countenance, however, radiated a rich, untarnished beauty that required no competition from the color of his black *sajo* or knee-length tunic. To Romola's young, wintry existence—one inherited solely from the dark mines of books containing memories of a dead mother, a vanished brother, and a blind father's happier times—it appeared like a sudden wreath of spring dropped into a frozen landscape. Though she returned his initial bow with the same pale, reserved demeanor, as he approached the ice beneath her feet began to melt. When he glanced toward her again, a fleeting pink flush stained her cheeks, vanishing instantly as if summoned back by her imperious will. Yet, Tito's gaze held a gentle, beseeching adoration, a quality uniquely propitious to a shy, proud spirit, serving perhaps as the only atonement for being too handsome. The spell of his presence stemmed largely from an absence of demand or assumption, evoking the image of a fleet, dark-eyed beast that captivates not by fleeing indifference, but by resting its chin upon one's palm, seeking a stroke as if driven by love.

"Messere, I offer you welcome," said Bardo, his tone tinged with condescension. "Misfortune coupled with learning, particularly Greek learning, serves as a letter of credit earning the ear of every educated Florentine. As you surely know, since the era when Manuelo Crisolora spread his teachings a century ago, no one earns the title of scholar who possesses merely the transplanted, derivative literature of the Latins; such inert students are rightly stigmatized as *opici*, or barbarians, echoing the Roman phrase 'refilling their urns at the fountainhead.' I perceive, as Nello has forewarned, that I am wholly blind. A calamity to which Florentines are peculiarly susceptible, whether due to the biting spring winds from the Apennines, the jarring transition from indoor gloom to summer sunlight—which allegedly produced the multitude of the *lippi* among the ancients—or some occult cause defying our shallow guesses. Pray, be seated: Nello, my friend, sit."

After ensuring the visitors were settled, with Romola taking her customary chair at his right, Bardo inquired, "From which region of Greece do you hail, Messere? I presumed your unhappy homeland had exhausted its sons capable of cherishing any image of her primordial glory, though indeed the barbarous Sultans have recently shown willingness to graft upon their wild stock the precious vine they themselves have trampled. From whence do you come?"

"I sailed last from Nauplia," Tito replied. "However, I have resided in Constantinople and Thessalonica and traveled through regions seldom visited by Western Christians since the triumph of Turkish arms. Let me inform you, Messere, that I was not born in Greece, but at Bari. I spent my first sixteen years in Southern Italy and Sicily."

As Tito spoke, a subtle emotion, like a breeze rippling water, crossed Bardo's delicate features. Leaning forward, he extended his right hand toward Romola and turned his head as if to address her, but correcting himself, he withdrew and spoke in a subdued voice, "Excuse me; is it not true—that you are young?"

"I am twenty-three," Tito answered.

"Ah," Bardo murmured, his tone vibrating with restrained excitement, "and you undoubtedly possessed a father who cherished your early education—one who, perhaps, was himself a scholar?"

A brief pause ensued before Tito's response reached Bardo's ears; for Romola and Nello, it commenced with a sharp shock that caused a momentary tremor of the lip, doubtless reviving a supremely painful memory.

"Yes," Tito replied, "at least a father by adoption. He was a Neapolitan, a man of accomplished scholarship in both Latin and Greek. However," he added after another hesitation, "he is lost to me—lost on a voyage he rashly undertook to Delos."

Bardo sank back, too delicate to press further into a sorrow he sensed was fresh. Romola, acutely aware of the fibers Tito's voice had disturbed in her father, realized that this new acquaintance had, with startling swiftness, breached the barrier separating them from the alien world. Nello, perceiving that the conversation had momentarily stalled, seized the opportunity to relieve the silence.

"In truth, it is as transparent as Venetian glass that this fine youth has received the finest training. The two Cennini have already set him to work on their Greek texts. It seems to me they are not men who begin to cut until they have felt the edge of their tools; they tested him well beforehand, I am sure. And if there are two things that cannot be concealed—love and a cough—I posit a third: ignorance, once a man is compelled to act beyond mere nodding agreement. The *tonsor inequalis* (uneven barber) betrays himself inevitably when he picks up the shears; does not this hold true, Messer Bardo? I speak in the fashion of a barber, yet as Luigi Pulci declares—"

*"Perdonimi s'io fallo: chi m'ascolta / Intenda il mio volgar col suo latino."* ("Forgive me if I err: whoever listens / Must understand my vernacular through his Latin.")

"Nay, my dear Nello," Bardo interjected with an air of friendly severity, "you are not wholly illiterate and could likely achieve more respectable progress in learning if you had refrained somewhat from the *cicalata* and street-corner gossip to which our Florentines are so prone, and especially if you had not clogged your memory with those frivolous productions of which Luigi Pulci offers the most egregious example—a compendium of extravagances and incongruities infinitely distant from the models of a pure age, resembling rather the *grylli* or conceits of a period when mystical meaning served as a warrant for monstrous forms; with this distinction, however, that while the monstrosity remains, the mystic meaning is absent. In contemptible contrast to Virgil's great poem, which, as I long maintained with Filelfo before Landino assumed to expound similar views, embodied the deepest lessons of philosophy within a graceful, well-knit fable. And I cannot help regarding the proliferation of these babbling, lawless works, despite being countenanced by patronage and, to some degree, the example of Lorenzo himself—otherwise a friend to true learning—as a sign that the glorious hopes of this century are to be quenched in gloom; nay, that they serve as a delusive prologue to an age worse than the Age of Iron—an era of tinsel and gossamer, wherein no thought possesses the substance to be molded into consistent, enduring form."

"Again, pardon me," Nello exclaimed, spreading his palms outward and shrugging his shoulders, "I find myself knowing so many things in good Tuscan before I have time to consider the Latin equivalent; and Messer Luigi's rhymes invariably slip off the lips of my clients: that is what corrupts me. Indeed, speaking of clients, I have left my shop and my reputation too long in the custody of my sluggish Sandro, who does not deserve the title of *tonsor inequalis*, but rather to be pronounced simply a bungler in the vernacular tongue. So, with your permission, Messer Bardo, I shall take my leave—understood that I remain at your service whenever Maso calls upon me. It seems a millennium since I dressed and perfumed the damigella's hair, which deserves to shine in the heavens as a constellation, though indeed it would be a pity for it ever to stray so far from reach."

Three voices joined in a fugue of friendly farewells as Nello retreated, bowing to Romola and gesturing to Tito. The astute barber observed that the charming youth, who had somehow enchanted him through some potent magic, was firmly launched in Bardo's favor. Satisfied that his introduction had not failed thus far, he deemed it proper to withdraw.

The brief outburst of wrath provoked by Nello's unfortunate citation had diverted Bardo's mind from the feelings that had previously constrained further speech, and he now addressed Tito with his ordinary calmness.
