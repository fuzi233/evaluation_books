In September of 1811, when the year was already tipping toward its brown-and-gold decline, a post-chaise rolled up the drive and halted before Aswarby Hall. The boy who stepped down—Stephen Elliott, lately orphaned in Warwickshire—stood a moment on the gravel and looked as children do when they have been uprooted: with the whole face turned outward, as if the eyes could drink enough of a place to make it less strange.

Aswarby was a tall, square, red-brick house of Queen Anne time, wearing, like an older body forced into a newer fashion, a later classical porch set on stone pillars. Many narrow windows, each divided into small panes by thick white woodwork, caught the evening light and held it, so that the house seemed full of slow, contained fires. Wings stretched right and left, linked by glazed galleries on colonnades, and each wing carried an ornamental cupola topped with a gilded vane that spoke, in its quiet glitter, of expense spent for the sake of being seen.

Beyond the front lay the flat park—oaks scattered like deliberate punctuation, firs fringing the horizon. The church clock, half-hidden by trees at the park’s edge, struck six; the sound travelled as if it had weight, beating gently down the wind. Even the air seemed to have a flavour: damp earth pricked with fallen needles of pine, and the faint smoke-sweetness that rides from chimneys at supper time. It was a pleasant impression, and yet one touched with the melancholy proper to early autumn, when the light is kind but brief and everything audible seems to be retreating.

Stephen had been brought here because Mr Abney—an elderly cousin of whom he had heard little—had offered him a home. The offer had surprised those who knew anything of Aswarby’s master. Mr Abney’s neighbours pictured a thin, austere recluse, a man folded into his library, unlikely to tolerate a child’s footsteps and questions. Scholars spoke of him with another sort of awe: the Professor of Greek at Cambridge had once said no one knew more of the religious beliefs of the later pagans than the owner of Aswarby. His shelves held the Mysteries, the Orphic poems, Mithras, the Neo-Platonists—books that smelled of old calf and dust and the sour-sweet of ink long dried. In the marble-paved hall stood a group of Mithras slaying the bull, shipped at great cost from the Levant: carved muscle and stone violence under a ceiling that echoed each step.

Yet when the front door opened, it was not a cold figure who received Stephen. Mr Abney came out of his study with a brisk, almost eager energy, rubbing his hands as though delighted to feel his own warmth.

“How are you, my boy—how are you? How old are you?” he said; and then, as if the question were a point of taste that required insisting upon, he asked it again within minutes. He peered, smiling thinly, like a man checking the ripeness of fruit.

“I’m twelve years old next birthday, sir,” Stephen answered.

“And when is your birthday, my dear boy? Eleventh of September, eh? That’s well—that’s very well. Nearly a year hence, isn’t it? I like—ha, ha!—I like to get these things down in my book. Sure it’s twelve? Certain?”

Stephen assured him it was. Mr Abney’s satisfaction had something clipped and private about it, like a cook pleased with the weight of a joint but reluctant to show it.

“Well, well! Take him to Mrs Bunch’s room, Parkes, and let him have his tea—supper—whatever it is.”

The butler, Mr Parkes—steadily spoken, steady-footed—conducted Stephen down into the lower regions where heat lived more comfortably than it did in the high rooms. There, in the housekeeper’s room, he met Mrs Bunch.

Mrs Bunch was everything the Hall’s marble and scholarly coldness was not: broad-hipped, warm-handed, rich with the aromas of domestic work—soap, starch, stored apples, and the faintly greasy comfort of a kettle that has boiled a hundred times. She set Stephen down as if setting a child in the best chair were as natural as laying bread on a table. Tea came—strong, properly drawn, with milk warmed so it did not shock the cup; bread and butter spread thick; a bit of cold meat sliced with the careful thrift of a woman who knows how far provisions must stretch. The simple ceremony of it eased him. His appetite, stirred by travel, found in that hot drink and those plain, well-kept things a kind of anchoring.

Within a quarter of an hour they were friends. Within a week Stephen had discovered that friendship with Mrs Bunch meant access to Aswarby’s living memory. She had been born near the place, had been at the Hall twenty years, and knew the corners of it as one knows the shelves of one’s own pantry. She was not unwilling to tell what she knew.

Stephen, adventurous and inquisitive, plied her with questions while she worked—polishing, mending, portioning, sorting the day into its proper stacks. Who built the temple at the end of the laurel walk? Who was the old man in the picture on the staircase, seated with a skull beneath his hand? Mrs Bunch supplied answers as readily as she might supply a spoonful of jam to a hungry child. But there were subjects on which her explanations softened into unease, as milk does when it is near to turning.

One November evening, with rain whispering at the windows and the fire making a soft crackle that seemed to say the house was alive and listening, Stephen asked suddenly—trusting, as children do, that elders hold the keys to heaven and hell.

“Is Mr Abney a good man, and will he go to heaven?”

“Good?—bless the child!” said Mrs Bunch. “Master’s as kind a soul as ever I see! Didn’t I never tell you of the little boy as he took in out of the street, as you may say, this seven years back? and the little girl, two years after I first come here?”

Stephen leaned forward at once. “No. Do tell me all about them, Mrs Bunch—now, this minute!”

“Well,” she said, drawing her knitting closer as though it were a net she could keep safe things in, “the little girl I don’t seem to recollect so much about. I know master brought her back with him from his walk one day, and give orders to Mrs Ellis, as was housekeeper then, as she should be took every care with. And the pore child hadn’t no one belonging to her—she telled me so her own self—and here she lived with us a matter of three weeks it might be; and then, whether she were somethink of a gipsy in her blood or what not, but one morning she out of her bed afore any of us had opened a eye, and neither track nor yet trace of her have I set eyes on since.

“Master was wonderful put about, and had all the ponds dragged; but it’s my belief she was had away by them gipsies, for there was singing round the house for as much as an hour the night she went, and Parkes, he declare as he heard them a-calling in the woods all that afternoon. Dear, dear! a hodd child she was, so silent in her ways and all, but I was wonderful taken up with her, so domesticated she was—surprising.”

Stephen imagined that singing—human voices in the dark, the kind that carry farther than they ought, as if the trees themselves were throats. The idea frightened him and pleased him at once.

“And what about the little boy?” he asked.

“Ah, that pore boy!” Mrs Bunch sighed, and in the sigh the room seemed to cool. “He were a foreigner—Jevanny he called hisself—and he come a-tweaking his ’urdy-gurdy round and about the drive one winter day, and master ’ad him in that minute, and ast all about where he came from, and how old he was, and how he made his way, and where was his relatives, and all as kind as heart could wish. But it went the same way with him. They’re a hunruly lot, them foreign nations, I do suppose, and he was off one fine morning just the same as the girl. Why he went and what he done was our question for as much as a year after; for he never took his ’urdy-gurdy, and there it lays on the shelf.”

Stephen’s eyes went at once to the hurdy-gurdy—an object that looked absurdly cheerful in its promise of music, like a loaf that refuses to stale. He spent the remainder of the evening cross-examining Mrs Bunch, and then turning the crank and pressing the keys, trying to coax out a tune. The instrument gave him thin, stubborn noises, as if the wood remembered hands other than his and resented the substitution.

That night he dreamed.

At the top of the house, near his bedroom, there was an old disused bathroom. It was kept locked. The upper half of the door was glazed, and the muslin curtain that once softened that pane had long been removed. If you passed in the daytime you could look in and see the lead-lined bath fixed to the wall, its head toward the window.

Stephen dreamed he stood in the passage, looking through the glass. Moonlight lay in the bathroom like cold milk. In the bath lay a figure.

It was a thing of dreadful thinness, the sort of thin that does not suggest hunger alone but a stripping-away of everything that makes a person solid in the world. The skin looked dusty and leaden, as though it had been coloured by the bath itself; the body was wrapped in a shroud-like garment. The thin lips were crooked into a faint smile that seemed both pleading and mocking. The hands were pressed tight over the heart.

As Stephen stared, a distant moan—almost too quiet to be sound—seemed to form at the figure’s mouth, and the arms began to stir. Terror shoved him backward, and he woke—to find he was indeed standing on the cold boards of the passage, in the full moonlight, his feet bare, his throat dry, his skin prickling as if the night air had salted him.

He forced himself to go to the bathroom door. The lock held. He peered through the glass. The bath was empty. Only the moon’s pallor and the faint metallic smell of old plumbing met him.

He went back to bed.

In the morning he told Mrs Bunch, who was so impressed that she replaced a muslin curtain over the glazed door, as if a layer of cloth could keep out what glass had admitted. At breakfast Stephen confided his experience to Mr Abney as well. Mr Abney did not laugh it away. He grew interested—too interested—and made notes in a book he kept, as though Stephen’s fear were a specimen to be pinned and labelled.

As the spring equinox approached, Mr Abney spoke of it often. He said it had been considered by the ancients a critical time for the young; Stephen would do well to take care of himself and shut his bedroom window at night; Censorinus, he added, had valuable remarks on the subject. His counsel might have sounded like kindness, but the way he looked at Stephen when he said it made the boy feel, unreasonably, as if he were being measured.

Two incidents in March deepened that sensation into something worse.

After a night that left him oppressed and uneasy—though no dream clung to him in the morning—Mrs Bunch, the next evening, was mending his nightgown. She held it up to the lamplight, and her comfortable face tightened.

“Gracious me, Master Stephen!” she broke out, fretful. “How do you manage to tear your nightdress all to flinders this way? Look here, sir, what trouble you do give to poor servants that have to darn and mend after you!”

On the left side of the chest were long, parallel slits, six inches in length, as if something had raked and scored the linen. Some had not quite pierced through. They looked not like a tear from snagging on a nail but like marks made on purpose—patient, repeated, hungry.

Stephen stared, then felt his stomach sink with the strangeness of it. “Mrs Bunch, I don’t know. I’m sure they weren’t there last night. But—they’re just the same as the scratches on the outside of my bedroom door; and I’m sure I never had anything to do with making them.”

Mrs Bunch’s mouth fell open. She snatched up a candle and hurried out, her steps quick and heavy on the stairs. Stephen sat and listened to the house’s noises—old wood shifting, the distant faint rush of wind in a chimney—sounds that, on ordinary evenings, belonged to safety. Now they seemed like a language he did not know.

She returned in a few minutes, candle guttering.

“Well,” she said, trying to sound solid, “Master Stephen, it’s a funny thing to me how them marks and scratches can ’a’ come there—too high up for any cat or dog to ’ave made ’em, much less a rat: for all the world like a Chinaman’s finger-nails, as my uncle in the tea-trade used to tell us of when we was girls together. I wouldn’t say nothing to master, not if I was you, Master Stephen, my dear; and just turn the key of the door when you go to your bed.”

“I always do, Mrs Bunch, as soon as I’ve said my prayers.”

“Ah, that’s a good child: always say your prayers, and then no one can’t hurt you.”

She set to mending, drawing her needle in and out with the firm, domestic rhythm of someone stitching a tear in cloth because that is what one does. Yet even as she worked, she fell into pauses—moments where the needle hovered and her eyes went far away, as if she were listening not to Stephen but to something else in the house.

This was a Friday night in March, 1812.

On the following evening Stephen and Mrs Bunch were together again when the butler, Mr Parkes, came in abruptly. Parkes as a rule kept to his pantry; now he was flustered, and his voice had lost its slow steadiness.

“Master may get up his own wine, if he likes, of an evening,” he said at once. “Either I do it in the daytime or not at all, Mrs Bunch. I don’t know what it may be: very like it’s the rats, or the wind got into the cellars; but I’m not so young as I was, and I can’t go through with it as I have done.”

“Well, Mr Parkes, you know it is a surprising place for the rats, is the Hall.”

“I’m not denying that, Mrs Bunch; and, to be sure, many a time I’ve heard the tale from the men in the shipyards about the rat that could speak. I never laid no confidence in that before; but tonight, if I’d demeaned myself to lay my ear to the door of the further bin, I could pretty much have heard what they was saying.”

Mrs Bunch bristled. “Oh, there, Mr Parkes, I’ve no patience with your fancies! Rats talking in the wine-cellar indeed!”

“Well, Mrs Bunch, I’ve no wish to argue with you: all I say is, if you choose to go to the far bin, and lay your ear to the door, you may prove my words this minute.”

“What nonsense you do talk, Mr Parkes—not fit for children to listen to! Why, you’ll be frightening Master Stephen there out of his wits.”

“What! Master Stephen?” Parkes said, suddenly aware. “Master Stephen knows well enough when I’m a-playing a joke with you, Mrs Bunch.”

Stephen did know, and that knowledge made him colder. Parkes had not been joking at first. Yet no amount of questioning could coax a fuller account from him. Parkes retreated into silence, as if speech itself might invite something nearer.

Then came March the twenty-fourth, 1812.

It was a windy, noisy day that seemed to worry the very walls. Air rushed and worried at the house’s corners; branches scraped and sighed; the cupolas’ vanes turned and flicked with metallic impatience. In the gardens, the laurel leaves rattled like dry scales. Even indoors the sound pressed in—restless, infiltrating—so that the Hall felt less like shelter and more like a shell held to the ear, full of distant, moving sea.

Stephen, standing by the fence and looking out into the park, had a queer sensation that the wind was crowded. It felt as if an endless procession of unseen people were being swept past him—borne resistlessly, aimlessly—forever reaching out to catch at something solid, something living, and forever failing. It was not a thought he could have put into fine words, but it struck him in the body: a tightening in the chest, a prickle at the scalp, a longing to run inside and bolt doors against something that had no hands.

After luncheon Mr Abney said, in a tone that tried to sound casual and did not succeed: “Stephen, my boy, do you think you could manage to come to me tonight as late as eleven o’clock in my study? I shall be busy until that time, and I wish to show you something connected with your future life which it is most important that you should know. You are not to mention this matter to Mrs Bunch nor to anyone else in the house; and you had better go to your room at the usual time.”

To a twelve-year-old, permission to sit up late is its own illicit feast. Stephen’s fear and curiosity mingled until he could not tell one from the other. That evening, going upstairs, he looked in at the library door. The room’s familiar smells—dust, leather, cold stone—were overlaid by another: incense, resinous and sharp, like crushed pine and bitter herbs.

A brazier, usually in the corner, had been dragged out before the fire. On the table stood an old silver-gilt cup filled with red wine—dark, viscous-looking in the lamplight, like blood warmed to a civilized gloss. Near it lay written sheets of paper. Mr Abney, intent, sprinkled incense from a round silver box onto the brazier’s coals. The smoke rose in deliberate curls, and Stephen had a sudden, absurd thought of kitchens: how certain spices bloom when heated, releasing what they hold. This was like that, and not like that at all.

Mr Abney did not seem to notice Stephen’s step.

The wind fell as night came. A full moon made the gardens and terraces look bleached and too clean. At about ten, Stephen stood at his open bedroom window and looked out over the country. The air was cold and had the scent of water. Across the mere the woods were a darker band, their outlines cut sharp by moonlight.

Still as the night was, it did not sound empty. From time to time cries rose from across the water—thin, despairing, like lost wanderers calling without hope of answer. They might have been owls. They might have been water-birds. Yet they did not quite resemble either. The sounds did not sit properly in the ear; they seemed to strike the mind directly, a kind of acoustic bruise.

Were they coming nearer?

Now they sounded from the nearer side of the water. Then, in a few minutes, they seemed to float about among the shrubberies beneath Stephen’s window. He could not locate them, could not pin them to any throat or branch. The house, with its many rooms, seemed to hold its breath.

The cries ceased. Stephen, shivering, began to think of shutting the window and returning to his book—he had been reading *Robinson Crusoe*, and the thought of an island with clear edges and ordinary hunger appealed to him—when he caught sight of two figures on the gravelled terrace along the garden side of the Hall.

A boy and a girl, it seemed. They stood side by side, looking up at the windows as if they knew exactly which one belonged to him.

Something in the girl’s shape recalled, with a sick certainty, the figure from the bath. She stood still, half smiling, with her hands clasped over her heart, as though guarding it. The boy beside her inspired sharper fear: a thin shape with black hair and ragged clothing, raising his arms in a gesture not of greeting but of demand, menace, and an unappeasable hunger. The moon shone through his hands—almost transparent—and Stephen saw the nails: fearfully long, catching the light, seeming to let it pass through them like glass.

When the boy lifted his arms, he disclosed a horror in his own body. On the left side of his chest there was a black, gaping rent. It was not a wound that had bled and closed; it was an absence. And into Stephen’s mind—not so much into his ear—fell the impression of one of those desolate cries he had heard over the woods, as if the sound had been searching for a mouth and had found his thoughts instead.

In another moment the pair moved swiftly and noiselessly over the dry gravel, and were gone.

Stephen’s fear became a single command: go to Mr Abney. Whatever waited at eleven, it was at least in a room with a living man—if Mr Abney were living. Candle in hand, he hurried down the passages. The Hall’s sounds changed with his movement: each stair creak, each soft rattle of flame in the candle, each distant tick of a clock seemed magnified, as though the house were listening to him go.

The study door was not locked; the key, as usual, sat on the outside. Stephen knocked. No answer. He knocked again, harder.

Inside, someone was speaking—Mr Abney’s voice, but not in the tones Stephen knew. It was urgent, strained. And then—what was that?—as though a cry had tried to break free and been choked, pressed back into the throat. The sound made Stephen’s hair lift at the nape of his neck.

Then everything went quiet.

He pushed at the door. It yielded.

What Stephen saw that night, he could not fully understand then. Later, when he was older, certain papers found on Mr Abney’s table explained the chain of events with a clarity that turned memory into nausea.

On the table were written sheets—Mr Abney’s hand, neat and cold. There were sentences Stephen would come to know by heart against his will:

“It was a belief very strongly and generally held by the ancients—of whose wisdom in these matters I have had such experience as induces me to place confidence in their assertions—that by enacting certain processes, which to us moderns have something of a barbaric complexion, a very remarkable enlightenment of the spiritual faculties in man may be attained: that, for example, by absorbing the personalities of a certain number of his fellow-creatures, an individual may gain a complete ascendancy over those orders of spiritual beings which control the elemental forces of our universe.

“It is recorded of Simon Magus that he was able to fly in the air, to become invisible, or to assume any form he pleased, by the agency of the soul of a boy whom, to use the libellous phrase employed by the author of the *Clementine Recognitions*, he had ‘murdered’. I find it set down, moreover, with considerable detail in the writings of Hermes Trismegistus, that similar happy results may be produced by the absorption of the hearts of not less than three human beings below the age of twenty-one years. To the testing of the truth of this receipt I have devoted the greater part of the last twenty years, selecting as the *corpora vilia* of my experiment such persons as could conveniently be removed without occasioning a sensible gap in society.

“The first step I effected by the removal of one Phoebe Stanley, a girl of gipsy extraction, on March 24, 1792. The second, by the removal of a wandering Italian lad, named Giovanni Paoli, on the night of March 23, 1805. The final ‘victim’—to employ a word repugnant in the highest degree to my feelings—must be my cousin, Stephen Elliott. His day must be this March 24, 1812.

“The best means of effecting the required absorption is to remove the heart from the *living* subject, to reduce it to ashes, and to mingle them with about a pint of some red wine, preferably port. The remains of the first two subjects, at least, it will be well to conceal: a disused bathroom or wine-cellar will be found convenient for such a purpose. Some annoyance may be experienced from the psychic portion of the subjects, which popular language dignifies with the name of ghosts. But the man of philosophic temperament—to whom alone the experiment is appropriate—will be little prone to attach importance to the feeble efforts of these beings to wreak their vengeance on him. I contemplate with the liveliest satisfaction the enlarged and emancipated existence which the experiment, if successful, will confer on me; not only placing me beyond the reach of human justice (so-called), but eliminating to a great extent the prospect of death itself.”

On the table, too, stood the silver-gilt cup of red wine. The incense smell lay thick, coating the air like a spice meant to mask another odour. The brazier’s coals glowed dull and steady. Everything had the careful arrangement of a ritual—and yet something in the room had been violently interrupted, overturned without moving a single object.

Mr Abney was in his chair. His head was thrown back. His face—so often dry, measured, interested—was stamped now with an expression Stephen could not have imagined on it: rage, fright, mortal pain, all contorted together as though the man had tried to keep his composure to the last and been torn out of it.

In his left side was a terrible lacerated wound, exposing the heart. There was no blood on his hands. A long knife lay on the table, perfectly clean.

It looked, in the simplest telling, as though some savage wild-cat might have done it—some creature from the woods, driven in through the open window by hunger or chance. The study window indeed was open, and the cool night air came in with the damp smell of the gardens.

That was the coroner’s opinion when the body was found: death by the agency of some wild creature.

But Stephen—who had seen, in moonlight, two children on the terrace, and the black rent in the boy’s chest, and the long, shining nails—carried within him another conclusion. The Hall’s domestic sounds, which had once promised safety—the kettle’s murmur in Mrs Bunch’s room, the tick of clocks, the familiar creak of stairs—were afterward never quite the same to his ear. In quiet places, long after, he would find himself listening for cries that did not belong to any bird, and for footsteps that crossed dry gravel without making a sound.
