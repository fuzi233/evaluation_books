*"This is true the poet sings,
That a sorrow’s crown of sorrow
Is remembering happier things."* —TENNYSON: _In Memoriam_

On a quiet evening toward the end of July, Daniel Deronda rowed alone along the Thames. It had been over a year since he returned to England, ostensibly finished with his education and expected to take his place in society. Though he had begun studying law—mostly out of respect for Sir Hugo’s wishes and to avoid idle days—this choice had done little more than deepen his uncertainty about what life meant for him. He was no longer the boy who dreamed in grand gestures; yet he still carried within him a restlessness that refused to settle.

Boating had always drawn him, but now it held an even deeper pull. In London, surrounded by the Mallingers, the river offered something rare: peace, solitude, and silence. At Putney, he kept a boat of his own, and whenever Sir Hugo didn’t need him, he would row until dusk, returning under the stars. He wasn’t lost in sentimentality, not exactly—but he was caught in a mood common among young men of his class: questioning whether it was worth joining the world’s relentless struggle. Not that he lacked means—he had capital, inherited ease, and time enough to wonder. But wonder, without purpose, can become a kind of paralysis.

He wore a dark blue shirt and skullcap, his curls neatly trimmed, a soft beard framing his mouth. The seraphic boy he once was had long faded into a man of quiet strength—still handsome, but grounded, thoughtful. His face bore no trace of childishness now, though there remained a certain depth in his gaze—something Gwendolen had once called “dreadful,” though it was really only a calm, searching look. His voice, when it slipped into song, was not a tenor but a high baritone—pleasant, yes, but not striking. And his hands—long, strong, flexible—were made for work, not for delicate gestures. They spoke of power tempered by refinement, like those painted by Titian in some noble portrait where force and grace met.

As he rowed, he hummed softly—a fragment from *Otello*, Rossini’s setting of Dante’s lines:
*"Nessun maggior dolore
Che ricordarsi del tempo felice
Nella miseria."*
(“No greater pain than to remember happy times in misery.”)

It was a melody that haunted him, one he often sang without meaning to. That evening, as he neared Kew Bridge, the words came again, low and mournful, drifting on the water like a whisper.

The river was not empty. A few people strolled along the towpath, and a barge approached slowly. Deronda steered aside, letting his oar rest as he waited. And then, near the bank, he saw her.

A girl—barely eighteen, slight and pale, wrapped in a woolen cloak. Her dark hair was tucked beneath a large black hat, her face small and fragile, her eyes fixed on the water with a stillness that seemed carved from stone. She stood motionless, as if she had forgotten how to breathe. Something about her stirred Deronda deeply—not just her beauty, but the unbearable weight of sorrow pressing down on her.

He stopped singing. And as he did, she turned her head slightly, startled. Their eyes met.

For a moment, nothing moved. No word passed between them. She looked at him with the wide-eyed fear of a fawn—no blush, no panic, just a quiet trembling, as though she were half awake, half dreaming. Then she turned away and walked slowly to a bench beneath a tree.

Deronda could not stay. He had no right to follow, to intrude. Poor women in distress were not uncommon. But this one—her delicate features, the way the light fell on her—made her seem different. Unforgettable.

He rowed on, but the image stayed with him. Who was she? What had brought her here? He imagined stories—abandoned daughters, broken families, love gone wrong. He smiled at himself for believing that beautiful faces must have tragic lives. Yet he couldn’t deny that such sorrow felt sharper when it touched someone so tender, so young.

“I wouldn’t have remembered her look if she’d been plain,” he thought. “But the beauty makes it harder to forget.”

By the time he reached Richmond Bridge, the sun was sinking. The sky blazed gold and crimson, and the trees cast long shadows across the water. This was his favorite hour—the moment when day slipped into night, and silence deepened. He found a secluded spot opposite Kew Gardens, where the river curved gently, and moored his boat. Lying back on the cushions, he watched the sky fade, the stars begin to blink into existence, like answers to a silent call.

He was lost in thought—half-wondering if one could ever dissolve oneself into the world around, until the self became no more than another part of the landscape—when he noticed movement on the opposite bank.

A figure stepped forward from behind the willows.

His breath caught. It was her.

She looked up, then glanced around, reassured by the emptiness. She pulled off her hat and buried it in the bushes. Then, slowly, she removed her cloak and dipped it into the river. She held it there, letting it soak, before lifting it with effort and standing.

Deronda understood instantly. She meant to drown herself.

He rose without hesitation, seized his oar, and rowed swiftly toward her. She froze, terrified, sank to the edge of the bank, clutching the wet cloth as if it were a shield. She covered her face, hoping he hadn’t seen her.

But he was close now. He steadied the boat against the shore and spoke softly, gently:
“Don’t be afraid. You’re unhappy. I know. Please trust me. Let me help you.”

She raised her head. The light caught her face. She recognized him.

After a pause, she whispered, in a voice so sweet it broke through the silence:
“I saw you before… *nella miseria*.”

Deronda frowned. Was she losing her mind?
“You were singing?” she asked, hesitating. “*Nessun maggior dolore*…”

Her voice gave the melody life. He understood now.
“Yes,” he said quietly. “I sing it often. But please—don’t stay here. Let me take you somewhere safe. And that cloak—let me carry it.”

She clutched it tighter, trembling. But then she looked at him, her eyes full of questions.
“You look good,” she murmured. “Perhaps it’s God’s command.”

He reached out his hand.
“Do trust me. I’ll protect you. I’d rather die than let harm come to you.”

She stepped forward. Slowly, she laid the soaked cloak on the ground. Then, with delicate hands folded at her waist, she leaned forward, as if afraid she might lose sight of him.
“Great God,” Deronda breathed, the words escaping like a prayer.

Something stirred deep within him—a memory, a fear, a longing. *Could my mother have been like this?* The thought came with sudden force, mingling grief and awe.

She stepped into the boat.
“You had a covering for your head,” he said.
“My hat,” she replied, touching her head. “It’s hidden in the bush.”

He jumped out, retrieved it, wrung out the cloak, and threw it into the boat.
“We must take it with us,” he said cheerfully. “So no one thinks you’ve drowned.”

He offered his coat.
“It’s cold. Will you mind wearing it?”

She accepted it with a faint, melancholy smile, wrapping it around her shoulders.

“Do you have any food?” he asked.
“No,” she said. “I still had money for bread.”

They rowed in silence. The twilight thickened. Stars blinked one by one. The moon climbed, half-hidden by trees.

She stared at the oar, leaning forward, as if feeling warmth return to her body.
“I like to listen to the oar,” she said softly.
“So do I,” he replied.

“If you hadn’t come,” she whispered, “I’d be dead now.”

“I can’t bear you saying that,” he said. “I hope you’ll never regret I came.”

“But how can I be glad to live?” she asked. “The *maggior dolore* and the *miseria* have lasted longer than the *tempo felice*.”

She paused, then added dreamily:
“*Dolore—miseria*... I think those words are alive.”

Deronda said nothing. To question her felt intrusive. He didn’t want to act as a savior, or assume authority. He simply wanted her to speak, when she was ready.

At last she did.
“I thought it wasn’t wicked. Death and life are one before the Eternal. Our fathers slew their children and themselves to keep their souls pure. That’s what I meant. But now—I am commanded to live. I don’t know how.”

“You’ll find friends,” he said. “I’ll help you.”

She shook her head.
“Not my mother. Not my brother. I cannot find them.”

“You’re English?” he asked gently. “You speak perfectly.”

She looked at him, straining to see in the dim light.
“You want to know if I’m English?”

He flushed under her gaze.
“I only want to know what you wish to tell me.”

She paused, then said quietly:
“I am English-born. But I am a Jewess.”

Deronda was silent. He had not guessed. Yet now, seeing her—delicate, dark-haired, with a quiet dignity—he wondered why he hadn’t seen it before.

“Do you despise me for it?” she asked, her voice breaking.

“Why should I?” he said. “I’m not foolish.”

“I know many Jews are bad,” she said.

“So are many Christians,” he replied. “But I wouldn’t judge you because of that.”

“My mother and brother were good,” she whispered. “But I shall never find them. I came from abroad. I ran away. I cannot speak of it. I thought God would guide me to my mother. But I despaired. This morning, when the light came, I heard one word: *Never!* But now…” Her voice cracked. “Now I think—I am commanded to live. Perhaps we are going to her.”

And she wept, burying her face in her knees.

Deronda hoped the tears would bring relief. But he also felt a growing unease. Where could he take her? Lady Mallinger’s home? Too formal, too bright, too likely to frighten her. He thought of Mrs. Meyrick’s house in Chelsea—small, quiet, warm. Hans Meyrick was away in Italy, but his wife and daughters lived there. Kind-hearted, gentle, inclined to believe in innocence. They would welcome a wounded bird.

He decided quickly. The cab ride felt endless after the stillness of the river. She slept fitfully, her head lolling, her breathing shallow. He watched her, wondering what had brought her to this brink.

Then, suddenly, a memory surfaced—Plutarch’s tale of the Delphic women. When the Maenads, exhausted from their rites, fell asleep in the marketplace, the townswomen gathered silently around them, guarding their sleep. When they woke, they were cared for, guided home.

Deronda felt a quiet certainty. These women would do the same. They had hearts like that.

He was no longer just a young man rowing the river. He was becoming someone else—someone who had found a life, not by choice, but by chance. And perhaps, in saving her, he was being saved too.

For in that moment, as he carried her into the unknown, he realized: to rescue someone from death is not always to save them. But to offer them a chance—to say, *live*—is the beginning of everything.
