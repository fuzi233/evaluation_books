"All thoughts, all passions, all delights, Whatever stirs this mortal frame, All are but ministers of Love, And feed his sacred flame." —Coleridge.

Deronda's eagerness to declare his love had reached a fever pitch, fueled entirely by Hans's recent revelation that Mirah suffered deeply from jealousy. Driven by this urgency, Deronda returned to Ezra with a steely resolve: he intended to demand, not merely request, a private audience with Mirah. Should she accept his heart, he felt invincible regarding the consequences; moreover, as her betrothed, he believed he could secure a protective authority over her, shielding her from future conflicts with her father.

Yet, beneath his confidence lay a deep-seated dread. Deronda sensed no overt restlessness in Lapidoth, nor any decline in the father's efforts to court favor, but he foresaw a coming storm—a struggle, a humiliation, or a crescendo of domestic turmoil that might leave Ezra and Mirah helpless. These fears would have been confirmed had he known the turmoil brewing in Lapidoth's mind. What appeared to Ezra as a minor torment was, for Lapidoth, an agonizing submission to restraint, endured only because he viewed it as a temporary measure to secure a future, unrestricted freedom.

Lapidoth had originally planned to wait for a favorable opportunity, perhaps to extract a substantial sum from Deronda. Meanwhile, however, he had begun to scrutinize the household with curious, calculating eyes, hunting for clues regarding where Mirah hid her money and her keys. An insatiable gambling instinct drove him, weaving a mental net that trapped every other thought. Had he possessed a tangible fortune to seize, this single-minded focus might have wavered, but Mirah, possessing a sharp, practical clarity, had thwarted his designs. True to her promise to Ezra, she entrusted all funds except immediate necessities to Mrs. Meyrick's care. Consequently, Lapidoth felt imprisoned in a state of total deprivation, akin to a patient in a lunatic asylum where every avenue of access is secured against him.

To him, prying open a desk or drawer to pocket banknotes found therein would have felt less like theft and more like a domestic right—a gray area of liberty men often claim with others' property, stretching far beyond legal boundaries until the law finally intervenes. In his mind, silverware was a safer investment than mining stocks precisely because the line between "borrowing" and stealing is so delicately drawn. Lapidoth genuinely felt mistreated by his daughter, convinced he deserved access to her earnings as readily as he consumed her apple tarts. Yet, he remained outwardly submissive. The temptation that truly gnawed at him was not direct confrontation with Mirah, but rather a calculated appeal to Deronda.

Clever individuals with nothing else to offer can sometimes command a high price for their absence. Lapidoth's desperate search for an exit strategy led him to believe that his family would be happier without him, and that Deronda, eager to be rid of such a burden, would advance a significant sum. Despite his practiced hardness, however, Lapidoth retained a lingering awe of Ezra's formidable friend, causing him to postpone this scheme indefinitely.

On this particular day, Deronda arrived buoyant with a gladdening certainty that radiated from his very demeanor. This contrasted sharply with Lapidoth, who was at a crisis point of discontent and longing, his mind racing with fantasies of escape. Deronda's newfound amiability only emboldened Lapidoth's schemes. So consumed was he that his usual pretense of interest in the room's activities faltered; he sat listlessly beside Ezra even when reading material flew past his comprehension. Eventually, unable to endure the stagnation, he excused himself to smoke and walk in the square, leaving the two friends to breathe more freely.

Mirah was absent, though Deronda knew she would return before his departure. A secret anticipation warmed his eyes; he imagined that upon seeing her again, he would witness a sweet recognition of his feelings, a connection previously denied to him. His manner toward Ezra grew playfully affectionate.

"This little room is too stifling for you, Ezra," Deronda remarked, breaking off his reading. "The weekly heat here surpasses even that of Genoa, where one retreats into the shaded coolness of vast halls. You deserve a grander home now. As the stronger half, I shall decide our fate."

He smiled warmly at Ezra, who replied, "I am straitened for nothing but breath. You, who might dwell in a spacious palace surrounded by verdant fields, find this a narrow cage. Yet, I cannot say 'go.'"

"Oh, the countryside would feel like exile while you remain here," Deronda countered, rising to pace the cramped double room, fanning himself dramatically with his handkerchief. "This is the happiest room in the world for me. Besides, I shall imagine myself in the East, since I am preparing to travel there someday. Only, I shall not wear a cravat or a heavy ring," he concluded emphatically, pausing to remove these accessories and place them on a small table behind Ezra, whose surface was cluttered with books and papers.

"I have worn my memorable ring since returning home," Deronda continued as he resumed his seat. "But I am such a Sybarite that I constantly cast it off as a burden during any task. I understand why the Romans had summer rings—if they indeed did. Now, I shall perform better."

Soon, they became absorbed in their work. Deronda read passages of Rabbinic Hebrew under Ezra's correction and commentary, paying scant attention when Lapidoth re-entered and settled into a shadowy corner. His wandering gaze quickly fixed upon the ring sparkling on the dark mahogany table.

During his walk, Lapidoth's mind had been rehearsing a scenario: an advantageous proposal abroad requiring immediate cash. If presented privately to Deronda, it might prompt a question about the necessary amount, leading to a generous grant. This calculation was the most contentious part of his fantasy; asking too much risked rejection, while asking too little yielded insufficient reward. His own desires knew no limits, and he lacked any gauge for Deronda's generosity.

Suddenly, amidst these airy preparations for a receipt that remained undefined, the ring—familiar to Lapidoth's envious eye on Deronda's finger earlier—now lay detached and within easy reach. Though its value was modest compared to the colossal sums he imagined, its physical presence sparked an instant leap of desire. A fleeting thought, not yet an intention, formed: *If I quietly pocket this ring and depart, I possess the means for comfortable escape.* It would require no trouble and pose no danger. Any property of Deronda's, accessible without formal consent, was effectively equivalent to his children's property, for a father would never prosecute his son-in-law for taking such a trifle.

These thoughts cascaded rapidly, forming a single, vivid picture. Lapidoth had never committed larceny, yet he reasoned that appropriating this item from a virtual relative—who would otherwise have gifted it generously—did not constitute true larceny. Still, the prospect of a larger gift weighed heavily on him. The internal rhythm of imagining snatching the ring repeated like a haunting tune, eventually sinking into a rejected notion. Instead, he decided to descend, waiting for the moment Deronda departed, intending to invite himself along on a walk and execute his plan boldly.

He rose to stand by the window, watching the street beyond, acutely aware of the short distance separating him from the table where the ring rested. Resolved to go downstairs, yet dominated by a thirst-like compulsion, he moved past the table. His fingers brushed noiselessly against the ring, and he found himself standing in the passage, the stolen object clutched in his hand.

Without hesitation, he donned his hat and fled the house. The possibility of returning to his children faded into an indefinite distance. By the time he stepped onto the square, his frantic energy focused solely on selling the ring and boarding a ship. Deronda and Ezra noticed his sudden exit, but that was all.

Shortly thereafter, Mirah entered, creating a genuine disruption. She had not removed her hat. When Deronda rose to greet her, she spoke in a confused, troubled murmur, "I only came to ensure Ezra has his new draught. I must go straight to Mrs. Meyrick's to fetch something."

"Please, allow me to walk with you," Deronda urged earnestly. "I must not weary Ezra further; besides, my brain feels like it is melting. I wish to accompany you to Mrs. Meyrick's."

"Oh, yes," Mirah replied, blushing intensely, sensing something new and electric in Deronda's presence. She turned away to prepare Ezra's medicine, while Ezra threw back his head, closing his eyes, unable to detach his mind from the swirling ideas generated during their reading session.

For a moment, Deronda stood lost in thought, contemplating the walk ahead. When Mirah returned with the draught, he suddenly recalled setting aside his cravat. "Pray excuse my dishevelment," he apologized, "I did not intend for you to see it." Moving to the small table, he retrieved his cravat and then cried out with violent surprise, "Good heavens! Where is my ring?" He began frantically searching the floor.

Ezra glanced around the corner of his chair. Mirah, swift as thought, approached where Deronda searched and asked, "Did you lay it down?"
