Mr. Harry Warrington of Virginia kept his lodgings in Bond Street, London, living upon the fat of the land and drinking bumpers of the finest wines available. To the fashionable world, he was known as "Fortunate Youth." Young, wealthy, handsome, and seemingly blessed by fortune, he was welcomed with open arms. Do not, my dear readers, cry out too loudly against the selfishness of the world for treating the young, the handsome, and the lucky with such favor, while frowning upon us—old, ugly, and miserable creatures. If I have the right to choose my acquaintances—for instance, preferring the company of a lively, handsome, well-dressed gentleman who amuses me over that of a slouching, ill-washed misanthrope, a ceaselessly prating coxcomb, or whatever else—does not society, the aggregate of you and me, possess the same right to its choices?

Harry was liked because he was likeable. He was rich, handsome, jovial, well-born, and well-bred; brave, and fond of a jolly song and a bottle alongside his topers. He loved any game on foot or horseback with the gentlemen sportsmen. With ladies, he possessed a modest, blushing timidity that made him intriguing. To those humbler than himself in station, he was magnificently liberal and eager to spare them annoyance. Our Virginian was indeed very grand, high, and mighty; yet, in those times when distinctions of rank still held sway, maintaining a distance from inferiors brought no unpopularity to a gentleman. Recall that in those days, the Secretary of State knelt when presenting despatches to the King, and the Under-Secretary dared not sit in his chief's presence. If I were Secretary of State, I should dislike kneeling to my audience; if Under-Secretary, standing while the Right Honorable Benjamins or Edwards looked over papers. Yet, *modus in rebus* exists; certain lines must be drawn. I am only half pleased when Bob Bowstreet, whose connection to literature runs through Policemen X and Y, and Tom Garbage, an esteemed contributor to the *Kennel Miscellany*, propose fellowship as brothers-in-arms, slap me on the back, and address me as "Old Boy" or by my Christian name.

During the winter season of 1756–57, the town offered Mr. Warrington everything he could desire for the asking. There were operas, though he took but moderate delight in them (despite the prodigious satire leveled against Italian operas as foolish, Popish, unmanly, and meaningless). There were the theaters featuring Mr. Garrick and Mrs. Pritchard at one house, and Mrs. Clive at another. There were masquerades and ridottos frequented by fine society, along with the private drums and assemblies of lords and ladies, which invariably began and ended with cards—a pastime Mr. Warrington preferred less than White's Club, finding the play there neither so high nor so fair.

One day, his kinsman, Lord Castlewood, took him to court to present him to His Majesty, who had recently arrived in town from Kensington. However, the gracious sovereign either disliked Harry's introducer or harbored other reasons for being sulky. The King merely remarked, "Oh, heard of you from Lady Yarmouth. The Earl of Castlewood"—then turning to his lordship and speaking in German—"shall tell him that he plays too much!" With that, the Defender of the Faith turned his royal back. Lord Castlewood shrank back, frightened by this cold reception.

"What does he say?" asked Harry.
"His Majesty thinks they play too high at White's, and is displeased," whispered the nobleman.
"If he does not want us, we had better not come again, that is all," said Harry, simply. "I never, somehow, considered that German fellow a real King of England."
"Hush! For Heaven's sake, hold your confounded colonial tongue!" cried my lord. "Do you not see the walls here have ears!"
"And what then?" asked Mr. Warrington.
"Why, look at the people! Hang me, if it is not quite a curiosity! They were all shaking hands with me, bowing, and flattering me just now; and now they avoid me as if I were the plague!"

At that moment, a broad-faced, broad-shouldered gentleman in a scarlet-laced waistcoat and a great old-fashioned wig approached.
"Shake hands, nephew," said the gentleman. "I heard what you said. I have ears like the wall, look you. And now, if other people show you the cold shoulder, I'll give you my hand;" and he grasped Harry's with a great brown paw. "Something of my brother about your eyes and face. Though I suppose in your island you grow more wiry and thin. I am thine uncle, child. My name is Sir Miles Warrington. My lord knows me well enough."

Lord Castlewood looked very frightened and yellow. "Yes, my dear Harry. This is your paternal uncle, Sir Miles Warrington."
"Might as well have come to see us in Norfolk, as dangle about playing the fool at Tunbridge Wells, Mr. Warrington, or Mr. Esmond—which do you call yourself?" said the Baronet sharply. "The old lady calls herself Madam Esmond, doesn't she?"
"My mother is not ashamed of her father's name, nor am I, uncle," said Mr. Harry, rather proudly.
"Well said, lad! Come home and eat a bit of mutton with Lady Warrington at three in Hill Street—that is, if you can do without your White's kickshaws. You need not look frightened, my Lord Castlewood! I shall tell no tales out of school."
"I—I am sure Sir Miles Warrington will act as a gentleman!" said my lord, in much perturbation.
"Belike, he will," growled the Baronet, turning on his heel. "And thou wilt come, young man, at three; and mind, good roast mutton waits for nobody. Thou hast a great look of thy father. Lord bless us, how we used to beat each other! He was smaller than me, and in course younger; but many a time he had the best of it. Take it he was henpecked when he married, and Madam Esmond took the spirit out of him when she got him in her island. Virginia is an island. Ain't it an island?"
Harry laughed and said, "No!"
And the jolly Baronet, going off, declared, "Well, island or not, thou must come and tell all about it to my lady. She'll know whether 'tis an island or not."

"My dear Mr. Warrington," said my lord, with an appealing look, "in this great city, every man has enemies, and there is a great deal of detraction and scandal. I never spoke to you about Sir Miles Warrington precisely because I knew him, and because we have had differences. Should he permit himself remarks to my disparagement, you will receive them *cum grano salis*, and remember that it is from an enemy they come."

Thus, the pair walked out of the King's apartments into Saint James's Street. By the time Harry reached White's, the news of his cold reception had already preceded him. The King had turned his back upon him; the King was jealous of Harry's favor with the favorite; Harry was *au mieux* with Lady Yarmouth. A score of gentlemen wished him compliments upon his conquest. Before night, it was a settled matter that this was among the other victories of the Fortunate Youth.

Sir Miles told his wife and Harry as much when the young man appeared at the appointed hour at the Baronet's dinner table, rallying him in his simple rustic fashion. The lady, initially a grand and stately personage, admitted that the reputation the world had made for Harry was so bad that at first she had given him but a frigid welcome. With the young ladies, Sir Miles's daughters, the greetings were "How d'ye do, cousin?" and "No, thank you, cousin," accompanied by numerous prim curtseys.

The little boy, the heir of the house, dined at table under the care of his governor. Having his glass of port by Papa after dinner, he gave loose rein to his innocent tongue and asked many questions of his cousin. Finally, the innocent youth said, after staring hard at Harry's face, "Are you wicked, cousin Harry? You don't look very wicked!"
"My dear Master Miles!" expostulated the tutor, turning very red.
"But you know you said he was wicked!" cried the child.
"We are all miserable sinners, Miley," explained Papa. "Haven't you heard the clergyman say so every Sunday?"
"Yes, but not so very wicked as cousin Harry. Is it true that you gamble, cousin, and drink all night with wicked men, and frequent the company of wicked women? You know you said so, Mr. Walker—and Mamma said so, too, that Lady Yarmouth was a wicked woman."
"And you are a little pitcher," cried Papa: "and my wife, nephew Harry, is a staunch Jacobist—you won't like her the worse for that. Take Miles to his sisters, Mr. Walker, and Topsham shall give thee a ride in the park, child, on thy little horse."
