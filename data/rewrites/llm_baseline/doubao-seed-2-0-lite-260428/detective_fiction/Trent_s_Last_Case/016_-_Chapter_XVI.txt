The heavy wrought-iron gate of the west-end flat complex clanged shut behind them as Mr. Cupples squinted up at Trent, his mild brow creased. “What’s this you were saying about a half-past seven appointment? Did we ever agree to such a thing?”
“Of course we did,” Trent replied, stepping out with an uncharacteristic, almost giddy lightness to his stride that made a passing constable smile indulgently, the look on his face so bright the man assumed he’d had more than a few tots of gin to celebrate. “You’re dining on my tab. This case, the one that’s been gnawing at my brain for over a year, is finally wrapped up tight. That’s reason enough to splash out on a feast, no arguments. We’re skipping my club, by the way. Last thing I want is for the rest of the stuffy old members to see me grinning like a schoolboy over a solved mystery, that’d tank my reputation for good. And besides, their roast mutton tastes like boilerplate, every single night, no exceptions. No, we’re going to Sheppard’s.”
“Who on earth is Sheppard?” Cupples asked, trailing after him up Victoria Street.
Trent let out a dramatic groan. “That question, Cupples, is the perfect example of this modern age’s useless appetite for aimless fact-checking. You want to interrogate the poor bloke before you even set foot in his dining room? I’ve never met Sheppard, for all I know he’s a myth the place made up. All I care about is that his saddle of mutton is so tender and juicy, I’ve seen visiting Americans sob into their plates and curse Christopher Columbus for ever sailing to the New World and making them miss it. Taxi!”
A black cab glided to the kerb, the driver nodding majestically when Trent rattled off the address. He lit a cigarette, his fingers still jittery with leftover excitement, and leaned forward. “Also, I have another bit of news. I’m going to marry Mabel. I assume the connection between that and my need for a very good dinner is obvious.”
“Mabel?” Cupples stopped short, then grabbed Trent’s hand to shake it so hard his knuckles whitened. “My dear boy, that’s the best news I’ve heard in ten years! I could not be happier for you two. I’ve been hoping this would happen since that dinner I hosted, when you sat half-listening to old Professor Peppmuller ramble on about geology, and stared at Mabel like the sun rose and set in her pocket. Us old fogeys notice these things, you know.”
“Mabel says she knew I was smitten even before that night,” Trent said, looking slightly sheepish. “I thought I was hiding it perfectly, too. Turns out I’m terrible at pretending I don’t care. But never mind that, I’m too happy to be embarrassed. I’m going to be unbearable tonight, fair warning. I might even sing you that old music hall number you loved so much, the one that goes-“ He started stamping his feet on the cab floor in a terrible impression of a clog dance, belting out the silly ditty about the old man with the wooden leg and the tobacco box, until Cupples protested he’d never heard the song in his life, and certainly never sang it. Trent just laughed it off, saying the fullness of his heart was spilling out any which way it could.
The cab swung around a corner, and pulled up outside the familiar panelled front of Sheppard’s. The dining room hummed with the noise of a hundred different dinner conversations, three bookmakers tucking into roast pork at Trent’s usual favourite table, so he steered Cupples to an empty corner booth by the crackling open fire.
“Order whatever you want,” Trent told him, winking at the waiter who knew his order off by heart. “The wine’s actually made from grapes here, not whatever swill they serve at my club.”
Cupples blinked at him. “I think I’ll have milk and soda water, please.”
“Shhh!” Trent hissed, mock horrified. “The head waiter has a weak heart, he’ll drop dead on the spot if he hears you asking for that abomination. But fine, fine, it’s your drink, don’t let me stop you. I won’t be the one to stand between a man and his terrible life choices.”
Once the waiter had brought Cupples his bizarre non-alcoholic drink and darted off to attend to another table, Trent leaned back, cutting into his mutton with a huge appetite. “We can talk freely here, no one’s listening. What did you make of Marlowe’s confession this afternoon?”
Cupples chewed his food slowly, dicing every piece of meat into tiny neat cubes before he swallowed. “The most ironic part of the whole thing, to my mind, is that we knew the one secret Marlowe never learned: that Manderson was eaten alive by insane, irrational jealousy of him, for no real reason at all. We didn’t say a word about it out of respect for Mabel, of course. How strange it is, isn’t it? We all move through life completely unaware of the wild, wrong assumptions other people hold about us. I once found out a whole group of my friends thought I’d secretly converted to Catholicism, all because I offhand said it might be nice to skip eating meat one day a week. Manderson’s conviction that Marlowe had betrayed him was almost certainly built on just as tiny, meaningless a scrap of nothing. And Marlowe’s story? It’s all perfectly straightforward, once you accept that Manderson was not a well man.”
Trent laughed out loud. “You think that whole wild plot, where he impersonated Manderson to fake his own alibi, is straightforward?”
“Only the details are unusual,” Cupples replied calmly. “A madman cooks up a plot to ruin an innocent man, and accidentally gets himself killed in the process. That sort of thing happens all the time. You want to hear something that’s actually remarkable? The life cycle of the liver fluke, as documented by Poulton. That’s bizarre, far more fascinating than any silly murder plot.”
Trent blinked at him. “I can’t argue that, I’ve never even heard of the liver fluke.”
“My point is,” Cupples said, sipping his milk and soda, “we’re all so quick to gawk at sensational crime drama and miss the far weirder ordinary things happening all around us. Marlowe’s impersonation trick was clever, but it wasn’t genius. He already sounded just like Manderson, he was a decent amateur actor, he knew every inch of that house. The idea was practically lying on the table in front of him. The only reason the case got so complicated was because you had two sharp strategic minds playing against each other, not just one. That sort of thing almost never happens in cases of real crime. Criminals, even the very clever ones like Crippen, are usually only good for one simple trick. They never think two steps ahead.”
He paused, his face growing serious. “But there is one thing that’s been bothering me, ever since we talked to Marlowe today. If he hadn’t caught on to Manderson’s trap in time, he would almost certainly have hanged for a murder he didn’t commit. How many innocent men have already been hanged, convicted on nothing but circumstantial evidence? I’ll never trust a death sentence based on that sort of proof ever again.”
“Couldn’t agree more,” Trent nodded. “I once read an old case, the Campden Murder, where a man named John Perry swore he helped his mother and brother kill a local man. All three were hanged, based purely on his word. Two years later, the ‘victim’ walked back into town, he’d been kidnapped by pirates. Perry had sacrificed his own life in his testimony, just to make the lie seem believable. If Marlowe had told the full truth to a jury, no one on earth would have believed him. The prosecution would have torn his story to shreds. We both thought he was guilty, right up until this afternoon.”
“Wait a moment,” Cupples cut in, laying down his knife and fork very gently. “I never thought he was guilty. I was certain, from the very start, that he was innocent.”
Trent stared at him, confused. “What? After all the evidence we went through, all the circumstantial proof that pointed straight at him? That’s not reasonable, Cupples.”
“I am certain,” Cupples said firmly, folding his hands on the table in front of his plate. “I never doubted him for a second, because I knew something you didn’t. You said just now there isn’t a single scrap of evidence that backs up Marlowe’s story. There is. My evidence. And it’s completely conclusive.”
Trent’s face blanched white as a sheet, the giddy warmth draining out of him in a single rush. He gaped across the table at his old friend, too shocked to speak.
Cupples wiped a stray crumb from his sparse moustache, leaned forward, and spoke in a quiet, even tone.
“It’s very simple. I shot Manderson myself.”
