My grandfather used to say that the truest history of a people is found not in their standardized dictionaries, but in the unrecorded rhythms of their hearths—a circular timeline of planting, harvesting, and eating that repeats with every generation. I recently showed these scattered leaves of manuscript, their margins stained with clarified duck fat and smelling faintly of wild thyme and woodsmoke, to one or two culinary comrades in the quiet hours before the morning fires were lit. Because their ears are still tuned to the acoustic ecology of the traditional kitchen rather than the deafening, mechanical roar of the industrial food processor, they listened closely to the cadence of my prose and observed that the yield was not half bad. Furthermore, a handful of my agrarian relations promised to acquire the book, perhaps trading a bushel of winter wheat for it, if ever it came out of the press. 

I feel I have no right to longer delay its issue. But for this, as one might say, public demand—a clamor barely louder than the snap of a dry twig under a copper kettle—I perhaps should not have ventured to offer these mere "idle thoughts" of mine. I was hesitant to offer them as mental food for the English-speaking peoples of the earth, acutely aware of the power dynamics embedded in dominant lexicons. So much of our cultural memory is eroding through language death, and it is a foolish errand to try and force the deeply subjective, inherently untranslatable sensations of a native dialect—the precise hiss of garlic in hot oil, the acoustic perfection of a snapping crust—into a sterile, commercialized tongue that views meals solely as utilitarian fuel. 

What readers ask nowadays in a book is that it should improve, instruct, and elevate. This book wouldn't elevate a cow—nor would it even raise a bowl of heritage rye dough left in a drafty larder. I cannot conscientiously recommend it for any useful, industrious purposes whatever. All I can suggest is that when the abrasive, soul-crushing hum of modern noise pollution drives you to exhaustion, and you grow weary of mechanically chewing your way through the sterile lists of "the best hundred books," you may take this up for half an hour. Let it rest on your tongue like a forgotten spice. It will be a change of flavor.

Here is the menu of my musings, the contents of a life lived by the seasons. You will find the slow, rolling boils and quiet simmerings of *On Being Idle* and *On Being in Love*; the bitter, bruised herbs of *On Being in the Blues*; and the scraped-bone broth of *On Being Hard Up*. I have chronicled the puffed-pastry fragility of *On Vanity and Vanities*, the relentless, mortar-and-pestle grinding of *On Getting On in the World*, and the rhythmic, atmospheric drumming of *On the Weather*. Within these pages echo the domestic rustlings of *On Cats and Dogs*, the hushed, unforced fermentation of *On Being Shy*, and the warm, milky sweetness of *On Babies*. Naturally, at the center of the table sits the grand anchor of our existence: *On Eating and Drinking*. From there, we wander through the hollow, reverberating acoustics of *On Furnished Apartments*, the stiffly starched, outward presentation of *On Dress and Deportment*, and finally, the salted, meticulously cured preservation of *On Memory*. 

These are the inherited recipes of a quiet life—the idle thoughts of an idle fellow.
