Louis Moore’s fears about Mr. Sympson’s sudden departure from Fieldhead proved well-founded. The very next day after the heated argument over Sir Philip Nunnely, a fragile peace was struck between uncle and niece. Shirley, who could never bring herself to appear unkind or unwelcoming—except in the case of Mr. Donne—earnestly begged the entire party to stay a little longer. Her plea carried such sincerity that it was clear she had a reason beyond mere hospitality. They accepted her invitation. Indeed, Mr. Sympson could not bear to leave her entirely unwatched, especially with Robert Moore possibly returning soon to claim what he believed was his right to her hand. So they remained.

In his initial fury against the Moore family, Mr. Sympson had treated Louis Moore with such harshness that the young man—used to hardship but unable to endure insult—had promptly resigned. Only Mrs. Sympson’s pleading, combined with his deep affection for his pupil, convinced him to return, at least until the family left Yorkshire. And perhaps there was another reason—something deeper, more personal—making it difficult for him to walk away from Fieldhead just yet.

For a time, things settled into a quiet rhythm. Miss Keeldar regained her health; her spirits returned, light and buoyant. Moore had eased every fear she once carried, and since opening her heart to him, all anxiety seemed to vanish. She became as carefree as a child who trusts its parents completely, leaving no thought for danger or sorrow. He and William Farren—through whom Moore checked on Phœbe—agreed the dog was not mad, only driven from home by cruel treatment. It was known her master frequently beat her. The groom and gamekeeper insisted otherwise, claiming hydrophobia was no real illness if it didn’t present clearly. But Louis Moore dismissed their claims. He reported only the hopeful news to Shirley, and she believed him. Right or wrong, the bite had no ill effect.

November slipped by; December arrived. The Sympsons were finally preparing to leave. Christmas loomed, and they must be home by then. Their trunks were packed; departure was near. One cold winter evening, during the last week of their stay, Louis Moore took out his small blank book and wrote:

* * * * *

She is more beautiful than ever. Since the shadow passed, all pallor and weariness have faded. It was astonishing how quickly youth restored her bloom—the vitality of life rising again like spring after frost.

This morning, after breakfast, I saw her, heard her, felt her—every nerve in my body stirred by her presence. Then I stepped into the chilly drawing-room. I picked up a small gilt book, filled with lyrics. I read a few poems. Whether the magic was in the verse or in me, I cannot say—but my heart warmed, my pulse quickened. I glowed, though the air outside was frozen. I am still young. Though she once said I wasn’t young, I’m barely thirty. There are moments when life itself shines brightly, simply because I am young.

It was time for school. I went. The room was pleasant in the morning light. Sun streamed through the low lattice windows. Books stood neatly arranged. No papers littered the floor. The fire burned clean, no ashes piled. I found Henry there, and he had brought Miss Keeldar with him. They were together.

I said she was more beautiful than ever. She is. A soft rose, pale but delicate, blooms on her cheek. Her eyes—always dark, clear, and expressive—now speak a language I cannot name. It is a silence full of meaning, like angels communing in heaven’s hush. Her hair has always been dark as night, fine as silk; her neck fair and graceful. But now, there is something new. The tresses fall like shadows, and the shoulders beneath them carry a grace that belongs to goddesses. Once, I only *saw* her beauty. Now, I *feel* it.

Henry was reciting his lesson before bringing it to me. One hand held the book; the other, hers. That boy enjoys privileges beyond his years. He dares to touch her—and is touched in return. How tenderly she treats him! Too tenderly. If this continues, in a few years, when his soul is formed, he may offer it at her altar—as I have offered mine.

I saw her eyelid flutter when I entered, but she did not look up. Now, she rarely looks at me. She seems quieter too—speaks little to me, and even less to others when I’m near. In darker moments, I wonder if this change means indifference, or worse. But in brighter ones, I interpret it differently. Were I her equal, I might see in this shyness a kind of coyness—and in that coyness, love. As it is, can I hope? What would I do if I found it?

This morning, I dared to arrange an hour alone with her. Not merely to wish it—but to *will* it. I summoned solitude to guard us. I called Henry to the door without hesitation.

“Go wherever you like, my boy,” I said. “But don’t return here until I call.”

Henry clearly disliked being sent away. He is young, but thoughtful. His gaze sometimes holds a strange depth. He senses something between us—he half guesses that the reserve with which I am treated brings a deeper joy than any affection he receives. The young, lame lion growls at me occasionally, resentful that I’ve tamed his lioness and now stand as her keeper. But discipline and affection keep him subdued. Go, Henry. You must learn to accept your share of life’s bitterness, like every other son of Adam. Your fate will not be different. Be thankful your love is denied so early—before it can become passion. A moment of frustration, a pang of envy—these are enough to express what you feel. Jealousy fierce as the equatorial sun, rage destructive as tropical storms—your emotions still ignore such extremes.

I sat at my desk, as usual. I am fortunate in my ability to mask inner turmoil with outward calm. No one looking at my steady face would guess the storm swirling within—engulfing thought, destroying caution. It is a comfort to move forward peacefully, without alarming anyone with sudden motion. I had no intention of declaring my love today. I meant only to observe her closely—to read one line in the book of her heart. Before I left, I wanted to know what I was leaving behind.

I had quills to shape into pens. Most men’s hands would tremble under such emotion. Mine worked steadily. My voice, when I spoke, remained firm.

“This time next week, you’ll be alone at Fieldhead, Miss Keeldar.”

“Yes. I think my uncle’s decision to go is final.”

“He leaves you dissatisfied.”

“He is not pleased with me.”

“He departs as he came—no better for his journey. That is painful.”

“I trust his failed plans will take away his desire to make new ones.”

“In his way, Mr. Sympson meant well. Everything he did, he believed was best.”

“You’re kind to defend someone who treated you so rudely.”

“I never hold grudges for actions true to character. And his violent outburst against me—when he’d already lost the argument—was perfectly in character.”

“You’re no longer Henry’s tutor?”

“I shall be separated from Henry for a while. If we live, we’ll meet again—we care for each other deeply. But I am cast out from the Sympson household forever. Fortunately, this change does not leave me stranded. It only hastens plans I’ve long made.”

“No change catches you off guard. I knew, in your quiet way, you’d be ready for anything. You stand in the world like a lone archer in a forest—watchful, thoughtful. Your quiver holds more arrows than one; your bow has a second string. Like your brother. You two could wander homeless into the wildest west—yet thrive. The tree would become your hut, the cleared land your fields, the buffalo yield to your rifle. Even the fiercest beast would bow at your feet.”

“And some tribe of Blackfeet or Flatheads might offer us a bride?”

“No,” I hesitated. “I doubt it. The savage is crude. I hope—no, I believe—you would neither of you share your hearth with one you could not truly love.”

“What made you think of the wild west, Miss Keeldar? Have you been with me in spirit when I wasn’t aware? Have you walked through my dreams, seen my visions?”

She had torn a strip of paper into tiny pieces, dropping them one by one into the fire. She watched them burn, silent.

“How did you know what you seem to know about my plans?”

“I know nothing. I’m only guessing now. I spoke at random.”

“Your guess sounds like prophecy. I will never be a tutor again. Never teach another pupil after Henry and you. I will not sit regularly at another man’s table. I will not be a family’s appendage. I am thirty now. I’ve never been free since I was ten. I crave freedom—deeply. I long to possess it, to claim it as my own. I would cross the Atlantic for it. I would follow her into untouched forests. I will not take a savage girl as a slave—she could not be a wife. I know no white woman who would come with me. But I am certain Liberty waits for me beneath a pine. When I call, she will come to my log cabin. She will fill my arms.”

She could not remain unmoved by such words. And she *was* moved. Good. I meant to stir her. She could not answer. Could not meet my eyes. I was glad. If she had, I would have been sorry. Her cheeks flushed as if a crimson flower lit by the sun had cast its glow upon her. On the pale lid and dark lashes of her downcast eye trembled a grace born of shame—half painful, half sweet.

Soon, she mastered herself. I saw her regain control. She sat. Her face told me everything. It said: I know my limits. Nothing shall force me beyond them. I feel—know—how far I may reveal myself, and when I must close the book. I have gone as far as dignity allows. Here I stand. My heart may break. Let it. It shall never dishonor me. It shall never betray my sisterhood.

For my part, I said: “If she were poor, I would kneel at her feet. If she were low-born, I would lift her into my arms. Her wealth and rank are two griffins guarding her. Love gazes and yearns—but dares not approach. Passion hovers—but is kept at bay. Truth and devotion flee. There is nothing to lose in winning her. No sacrifice. Only gain—unimaginably hard to achieve.”

Hard or not, something must be done. Something must be said. I could not sit silently with such beauty before me, so modestly mute. I spoke—calmly.

“Still, I know I will be strangely placed with that mountain nymph, Liberty. She is, I suspect, akin to Solitude—which I once pursued, and now seek to escape. These Oreads are rare. They appear with unearthly charm, like a starlit night. They inspire wild delight—but not warmth. Their beauty is spiritual. Their grace is not of life, but of seasons and scenes. They are the dew of dawn, the flush of dusk, the calm of moonlight, the shifting clouds. I want something different. This ethereal splendor feels cold to my vision, frozen to my touch. I am not a poet. I cannot live with abstractions. You, Miss Keeldar, have often called me a material philosopher—implying I live for substance. Indeed, I feel material from head to foot. Nature is glorious, and I worship her with a solid heart. But I would rather see her through the gentle eyes of a beloved, lovely wife—than through the wild gaze of the highest goddess.”

“Juno couldn’t cook a buffalo steak the way you like it,” she said.

“She couldn’t. But I’ll tell you who could—a young, penniless, friendless orphan girl. I wish I could find such a one—pretty enough to love, with mind and heart suited to mine. Not uneducated—honest, modest. I care little for learning, but I would cherish the natural gifts no education can match. Any temper Fate gives—I can manage even the fiercest. To such a girl, I would be first tutor, then husband. I would teach her my language, my habits, my principles. Then I would reward her with my love.”

“_Reward_ her, lord of creation—_reward_ her!” she said, lips curled.

“And be repaid a thousandfold.”

“If she chooses.”

“And she _shall_ choose.”

“You said any temper Fate wills. Compulsion is stone, a blow to some souls.”

“And love is the spark it ignites.”

“Who cares for love that is only a spark—seen, risen, and gone?”

“I must find my orphan girl. Tell me how, Miss Keeldar.”

“Advertise. And be sure to add—she must be a good plain cook.”

“I must find her. And when I do, I shall marry her.”

“Not you!” Her voice suddenly sharpened with scorn.

I liked this. I had stirred her from her pensive mood. I would go further.

“Why doubt it?”

“_You_ marry!”

“Yes, of course. Nothing more certain than that I can and will.”

“The opposite is certain, Mr. Moore.”

She charmed me—growing bold, almost mocking. Pride, temper, irony—blended in her great, bright eye, now sharp as a merlin’s.

“Give me your reasons for such a belief, Miss Keeldar.”

“How will _you_ manage to marry?”

“I’ll manage it easily, swiftly—once I find the right person.”

“Accept celibacy!” She gestured as if handing it to me. “Take it as your fate!”

“No. You cannot give what I already have. Celibacy has been mine for thirty years. If you wish to give me a gift, a farewell token, you must change it.”

“Then take worse!”

“How—what?”

Now I felt, looked, and spoke with eagerness. I was unwise to abandon my calm—even briefly. It cost me an advantage. The spark of temper turned to sarcasm, rippling across her face in a mocking smile.

“Take a wife who has courted you to spare your modesty, and forced herself upon you to avoid your scruples.”

“Show me where.”

“Any sturdy widow who’s had several husbands and knows how to handle these matters.”

“She mustn’t be rich, then. Oh, these riches!”

“You’d never harvest the gold-bearing garden. You lack courage to face the sleeping dragon. You lack cunning to borrow Atlas’s strength.”

“You look hot and proud.”

“And you—far prouder. Yours is the monstrous pride that pretends humility.”

“I am a dependent. I know my place.”

“I am a woman. I know mine.”

“I am poor. I must be proud.”

“I have duties. Obligations as binding as yours.”

We had reached a turning point. We paused, looking at each other. She would not yield. I felt it. Beyond that, I saw nothing. A few moments remained. The end approached—I heard its rush—but not yet. I would linger, talk, wait. When impulse urged, I would act. I am never in a hurry. I have never been in a hurry. Hasty people drink life scalding hot. I taste it cool as dew.

I continued: “Apparently, Miss Keeldar, you are as unlikely to marry as I am. I know you’ve refused three—perhaps four—advantageous offers. And a fifth. Have you rejected Sir Philip Nunnely?”

I asked abruptly.

“Did you think I would take him?”

“I thought you might.”

“On what grounds?”

“Rank, age, temperament—his mildness suits you. Shared intellectual tastes.”

“A beautiful sentence. Let’s dismantle it. ‘Conformity of rank.’ He is above me. Compare my grange to his palace. I am scorned by his family. ‘Suitability of age.’ We were born in the same year. He is still a boy. I am a woman—ten years older in every sense. ‘Contrast of temper.’ Mild and amiable, he is. I—what? Tell me.”

“Sister of the spotted, bright, quick, fiery leopard.”

“And you would mate me with a kid—while the millennium is millions of centuries away? While I remain an archangel in the seventh heaven, forbidden to descend? Unjust barbarian! ‘Harmony of tastes.’ He loves poetry. I hate it—”

“You do? That’s news.”

“I shudder at metre, at rhyme whenever I’m at the priory or he’s at Fieldhead. Harmony? When did I write syllabub sonnets or string verses as fragile as glass? When did I pretend those penny beads were real jewels?”

“You could have led him to higher taste. Improved his mind.”

“Led and improved? Taught and tutored? Endured and endured? Pah! My husband is not my child. I won’t set him daily lessons, check his work, give him candy for good behavior, or lecture him pathetically when he fails. But it’s typical of a tutor to speak of ‘the satisfaction of teaching.’ I suppose you think it the finest role in the world. I don’t. Improving a husband? No. I demand my husband improve me—or we part.”

“God knows it’s needed.”

“What do you mean by that, Mr. Moore?”

“What I say. Improvement is desperately needed.”

“If you were a woman, you’d school _monsieur, votre mari_, beautifully. It would suit you perfectly. Teaching is your calling.”

“May I ask if, in your current just and gentle mood, you mean to mock me for being a tutor?”

“Yes. Bitterly. And anything else you please—any flaw you’re painfully aware of.”

“Like being poor?”

“Of course. That stings. You’re wounded by poverty. You brood over it.”

“With having nothing but a plain person to offer the woman who might rule my heart?”

“Exactly. You often call yourself plain. You’re sensitive about your features—not Apollo-perfect. You belittle them, hoping others will praise them—though they won’t. Your face has no beauty to boast of—no graceful line, no pleasing hue.”

“Compare it with yours.”

“It looks like an Egyptian god—a great sand-buried stone head. Or rather—nothing so grand. It looks like Tartar. You’re my mastiff’s cousin. I think you resemble him as much as a man can resemble a dog.”

“Tartar is your companion. In summer, when you rise early, run into the fields, wet your feet in the dew, freshen your face in the breeze, you call him. Sometimes with a whistle I taught you. In the woods, when you think only Tartar listens, you whistle tunes I’ve sung—songs I’ve hummed. I don’t ask where the feeling comes from. I know it flows from your heart, Miss Keeldar. In winter, Tartar lies at your feet. You let him rest his head on your perfumed lap, lie on the edge of your satin dress. His rough hide is familiar with your touch. I once saw you kiss him on that white spot on his forehead. It’s dangerous to say I’m like Tartar—it suggests I deserve to be treated like him.”

“Perhaps, sir, you’ll extort as much from your penniless orphan girl when you find her.”

“Oh, if only I could find her—just as I imagine her. Someone to tame first, then teach. To break in, then fondle. To lift from poverty, establish power over, then indulge. To see her irritated and subdued twelve times a day. And perhaps, when training is done, watch her become the patient mother of a dozen children—only now and then giving little Louis a gentle cuff to pay interest on the debt she owes his father. Oh—my orphan girl would give me many kisses. She’d wait at the door for me. Run into my arms. Keep my hearth bright and warm. God bless the idea. I must find her.”

Her eyes flashed. Her lips parted. Then she closed them tightly and turned away.

“Tell me, tell me where she is, Miss Keeldar!”

Another movement—full of pride, fire, impulse.

“I must know. You _can_ tell me. You _shall_.”

“I _never will_.”
