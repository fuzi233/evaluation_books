The journey to Dublin had been a blur of night mail and dawn, a hurried escape from the shadow of Durrance's presence in the Row. By the forenoon, Feversham stood on the quay at Ramelton, the air thick with the scent of the Lennon River. Waiting for him was Ethne, seated in a dog-cart that seemed too small for her commanding figure. She offered him a hand and a smile that transcended mere camaraderie, carrying the warmth of a shared secret.

"You are surprised to see me," she observed, reading the tension in his gaze.

"I always am," Feversham replied, his voice rough. "For always you exceed my thoughts of you."

As the traps were packed, Ethne spoke softly. "I shall drive slowly. I brought no groom on purpose. Guests arrive tomorrow; we have only today."

They set off along the wide causeway, turning up the steep, narrow street of Ramelton. Feversham sat in silence, absorbing the scene with a pride that felt like ownership. He noted the dark thicket of trees climbing the riverbank, the old grey bridge, and the drowsy quiet of the town. It was Ethne's world, and for the first time, he felt he belonged to it.

Ethne was twenty-one, tall and strong, with shoulders squared and a grace that moved like a deer. Her dark brown hair was coiled tightly at the nape of her neck, framing cheeks flushed with health and eyes of clear grey that met his with frank honesty. She possessed a straightforward simplicity, a strength that excluded violence but embraced gentleness. Feversham recalled the local legend of her courage—the story of how she had once leaped from a runaway dog-cart on a steep hill, balancing along the shafts to grab the reins and stop the horse ten yards from a sharp curve. He admired her, unaware yet of the flaws that shadowed her virtues.

For the first part of the drive, Ethne was as silent as her companion, her mind elsewhere. Only when they left the town and hit the straight road to Letterkenny did she turn to him, her tone steady and level, stating a fact as simple as the weather.

"I saw this morning that your regiment was ordered from India to Egypt. You could have gone with it, had I not come in your way. There would have been chances of distinction. I have hindered you, and I am very sorry. Of course, you could not know that there was any possibility of your regiment going, but I can understand it is very hard for you to be left behind. I blame myself."

Feversham stared ahead, his voice suddenly hoarse. "You need not."

"How can I help it?" she pressed, her eyes fixed on his face. "I blame myself the more because I do not see things quite like other women. Supposing you had gone to Egypt, and the worst had happened... I should have felt very lonely, of course, all my days. But I should have known quite surely that when those days were over, you and I would see much of one another."

She spoke without hesitation, as if describing a trivial occurrence. Feversham caught his breath, a man in pain, yet he remained still, unable to trust himself to answer. He kept his lips closed, and Ethne continued, her voice softening.

"You see, I can put up with the absence of the people I care about better than most. I do not feel that I have lost them at all." She paused, searching for the right words. "You know how things happen. One goes along in a dull sort of way, and then suddenly a face springs out from the crowd of one's acquaintances, and you know it at once and certainly for the face of a friend. It is almost as though you had come upon someone long looked for and now gladly recovered. Well, such friends—there are few, no doubt, but after all, only the few really count. Such friends one does not lose, whether they are absent, or even—dead."

"Unless," said Feversham, slowly, "one has made a mistake. Suppose the face in the crowd is a mask, what then? One may make mistakes."

Ethne shook her head decidedly. "Of that kind, no. One may seem to have made mistakes, and perhaps for a long while. But in the end, one would be proved not to have made them."

Her implicit faith took hold of him, torturing him until silence became impossible. "Ethne," he cried, "you don't know—"

At that moment, Ethne reined in her horse, laughed, and pointed her whip. They had reached the top of a hill a couple of miles from Ramelton. The road ran between stone walls enclosing open fields on the left and a wood of oaks and beeches on the right. Built into the left-hand wall was a scarlet letter-box.

"I wanted to show you that," she interrupted. "It was there I used to post my letters to you during the anxious times."

Feversham let slip his opportunity of speech. "The letter-box is very convenient," he said.

"Yes," Ethne replied, driving on until they reached a crumbling section of the park wall. "That's where I used to climb over to post the letters. There's a tree on the other side of the wall as convenient as the letter-box. I used to run down the half-mile of avenue at night."

"There might have been thieves," Feversham exclaimed.

"There were thorns," Ethne corrected, turning through the gates to drive up to the porch of the long, irregular grey house. "Well, we have still a day before the dance."

"I suppose the whole countryside is coming," Feversham noted.

"It daren't do anything else," Ethne laughed. "My father would send the police to fetch them if they stayed away, just as he fetched your friend Mr. Durrance here. By the way, Mr. Durrance has sent me a present—a Guarnerius violin."

The door opened, and a thin, lank old man emerged onto the steps. With a fierce peaked face like a bird of prey, he softened instantly into friendliness upon seeing Feversham. A stranger might have thought he winked, though his left eyelid continually drooped over the eye.

"How do you do?" he said. "Glad to see you. Must make yourself at home. If you want any whiskey, stamp twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand." With that, he retreated inside.

The biographer of Dermod Eustace would need a wary mind to work. Though the old master of Lennon House had not lain in his grave for twenty years, he had already swollen into a legendary character. Anecdotes had grown upon his memory like barnacles. He practiced an ancient, tyrannous hospitality, forcing bed and board upon strangers, and was a man of another century who glowered at a topsy-turvy world, reconciled to it only after much alcohol. He was an intoxicated Coriolanus, believing people should be shepherded with a stick, yet mindful of his manners even to the lowliest. The townsfolk of Ramelton took pride in the fact that even at his worst, galloping down the steep cobbled streets on a big white mare with his inseparable collie dog, he had never ridden down anyone except a man. He was rather afraid of his daughter, who wisely kept him doubtful of her displeasure, and he had conceived a great liking for Harry Feversham.

Harry saw little of him that day. Dermod retired to his office while Feversham and Ethne spent the afternoon fishing for salmon in the Lennon River. It was an afternoon restful as a Sabbath, the birds still. From the house, the lawns fell steeply, shaded by trees and dappled by sunlight, to a valley where the river flowed swift and black under overarching boughs. There was a fall where the water slid over rocks with a smoothness so unbroken it looked solid, except at one point where a spur stood sharply up, and the river broke back upon itself in an amber wave through which the sun shone. Opposite this spur, they sat for a long while, talking at times, but mostly listening to the roar of the water and watching its perpetual flow.

At last, the sunset came, casting long shadows. They stood up, looked at each other with a smile, and walked slowly back to the house. It was an afternoon Feversham was long to remember, for the next night was the night of the dance.

As the band struck up the opening bars of the fourth waltz, Ethne left her position at the drawing-room door. Taking Feversham's arm, she passed out into the hall. The hall was empty, the front door standing open to the cool of the summer night. From the ballroom came the swaying lilt of the music and the beat of the dancers' feet. Ethne drew a breath of relief at her reprieve from her duties, then dropped her partner's arm and crossed to a side table.

"The post is in," she said. "There are letters, one, two, three, for you, and a little box."

She held the box out to him—a little white jeweller's cardboard box—and was struck by its absence of weight. "It must be empty," she said.

Yet it was most carefully sealed and tied. Feversham broke the seals and unfastened the string. He looked at the address. The box had been forwarded from his lodgings, and he was not familiar with the handwriting.

"There is some mistake," he said as he shook the lid open, and then he stopped abruptly. Three white feathers fluttered out of the box, swayed and rocked for a moment in the air, and then, one after another, settled gently down upon the floor. They lay like flakes of snow upon the dark polished boards. But they were not whiter than Harry Feversham's cheeks. He stood and stared at the feathers until he felt a light touch upon his arm. He looked and saw Ethne's gloved hand upon his sleeve.

"What does it mean?" she asked. There was some perplexity in her voice, but nothing more. The smile upon her face and the loyal confidence in her eyes showed she had never a doubt that his first word would lift it from her. "What does it mean?"

"That there are things which cannot be hid, I suppose," said Feversham.

For a little while Ethne did not speak. The languorous music floated into the hall, and the trees whispered from the garden through the open door. Then she shook his arm gently, uttered a breathless little laugh, and spoke as though she were pleading with a child.

"I don't think you understand, Harry. Here are three white feathers. They were sent to you in jest? Oh, of course in jest. But it is a cruel kind of jest—"

"They were sent in deadly earnest."

He spoke now, looking her straight in the eyes. Ethne dropped her hand from his sleeve.

"Who sent them?" she asked.

Feversham had not given a thought to that matter. The message was all in all; the men who had sent it were unimportant. But Ethne reached out her hand and took the box from him. There were three visiting cards lying at the bottom, and she took them out and read them aloud.

"Captain Trench, Mr. Castleton, Mr. Willoughby. Do you know these men?"

"All three are officers of my old regiment."

The girl was dazed. She knelt down upon the floor and gathered the feathers into her hand with a vague thought that merely to touch them would help her to comprehension. They lay upon the palm of her white glove, and she blew gently upon them, and they swam up into the air and hung fluttering and rocking. As they floated downward she caught them again, and so she slowly felt her way to another question.

"Were they justly sent?" she asked.

"Yes," said Harry Feversham.

He had no thought of denial or evasion. He was only aware that the dreadful thing for so many years dreadfully anticipated had at last befallen him. He was known for a coward. The word which had long blazed upon the wall of his thoughts in letters of fire was now written large in the public places. He stood as he had once stood before the portraits of his fathers, mutely accepting condemnation. It was the girl who denied, as she still kneeled upon the floor.

"I do not believe that is true," she said. "You could not look me in the face so steadily were it true. Your eyes would seek the floor, not mine."

"Yet it is true."

"Three little white feathers," she said slowly; and then, with a sob in her throat, "This afternoon we were under the elms down by the Lennon River—do you remember, Harry?—just you and I. And then come three little white feathers, and the world's at an end."

"Oh, don't!" cried Harry, and his voice broke upon the word. Up till now he had spoken with a steadiness matching the steadiness of his eyes. But these last words of hers, the picture which they evoked in his memories, the pathetic simplicity of her utterance, caught him by the heart. But Ethne seemed not to hear the appeal. She was listening with her face turned toward the ballroom. The chatter and laughter of the voices there grew louder and nearer. She understood that the music had ceased. She rose quickly to her feet, clenching the feathers in her hand, and opened a door. It was the door of her sitting room.

"Come," she said.

Harry followed her into the room, and she closed the door, shutting out the noise.

"Now," she said, "will you tell me, if you please, why the feathers have been sent?"

She stood quietly before him; her face was pale, but Feversham could not gather from her expression any feeling which she might have beyond a desire and a determination to get at the truth. She spoke, too, with the same quietude. He answered, as he had answered before, directly and to the point, without any attempt at mitigation.
