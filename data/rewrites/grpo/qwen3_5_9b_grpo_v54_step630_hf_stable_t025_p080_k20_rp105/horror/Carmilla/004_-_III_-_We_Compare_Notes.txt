We Compare Notes

Our eyes tracked the _cortege_ until the carriage vanished into the mist-shrouded woods, the rhythmic clatter of hooves and wheels dissolving into the suffocating silence of the night. Nothing remained but the sudden, sharp intake of breath from the young lady beside me. Her eyes fluttered open. Though her face was turned away, I saw her lift her head, scanning the darkness with a frantic urgency. A voice, sweet yet trembling with terror, cut through the air: "Where is mamma?"

Madame Perrodon responded with a soothing tenderness, offering assurances that soothed the immediate panic. Then came the next questions, rising in a crescendo of confusion: "Where am I? What is this place? Where is the carriage? And Matska?"

Madame answered what she could, and slowly, the girl pieced together the nightmare of the abduction. Relief washed over her when she learned no one in the carriage was hurt, but despair returned when she realized her mother had abandoned her there, promising a reunion in three months. Tears streamed down her face.

I moved to offer my own comfort, but Mademoiselle De Lafontaine gently restrained me. "Do not approach," she warned softly. "One person is all she can handle now; too much excitement might shatter her completely."

Once she was settled in bed, I resolved to visit her later. Meanwhile, my father had dispatched a servant on horseback to fetch the physician, who lived two leagues away, while a chamber was prepared for the stranger.

The girl rose, leaning heavily on Madame's arm, and walked slowly across the drawbridge and through the castle gates. Servants awaited in the hall to guide her to her quarters. Her room was the very drawing room we occupied, a long space with four windows overlooking the moat, the drawbridge, and the dark forest beyond. It was furnished in heavy, carved oak, filled with large cabinets and chairs upholstered in crimson Utrecht velvet. The walls were draped in tapestry framed in gold, depicting hunting scenes and festive gatherings in ancient costumes. It was stately yet comfortable, and here we drank tea, for my father insisted on the national beverage accompanying our coffee and chocolate.

That night, with candles burning low, we recounted the evening's events. Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine were present, while the stranger had already succumbed to a deep sleep under the watch of a servant.

"How do you like our guest?" I asked as Madame entered. "Tell me everything."

"I like her immensely," Madame replied. "She is, I believe, the prettiest creature I have ever seen; your age, so gentle and lovely."

"She is absolutely beautiful," Mademoiselle interjected, having peeked into the room.

"And such a sweet voice!" Madame added.

"Did you notice a woman in the carriage after it started again?" Mademoiselle asked. "Someone who didn't exit but merely stared from the window?"

"No, we saw none."

Then she described a horrifying figure: a hideous black woman wearing a colored turban, gazing from the carriage window with a derisive grin. Her eyes gleamed unnaturally, the whites vast and pale, her teeth bared in a snarl of fury.

"Did you notice how ill-favored the servants were?" Mademoiselle inquired.

"Yes," my father said, entering just then. "Ugly, hang-dog fellows I have never seen. I hope they do not rob the poor lady in the forest. They are clever rogues, however; they managed everything in a flash."

"They must be exhausted from the long journey," Madame suggested.

"Beyond their wicked appearance, their faces were strangely lean, dark, and sullen. I am very curious," my father admitted, though he dared not elaborate. "Perhaps the young lady will tell us tomorrow if she recovers."

"I doubt she will," my father said, a mysterious smile playing on his lips, a slight nod indicating he knew more than he cared to share.

This fueled our curiosity regarding his brief, earnest exchange with the lady in black velvet before her departure. We were scarcely alone when I pressed him for details. He yielded easily.

"There is no reason I should not tell you," he began. "She expressed reluctance to burden us with her daughter, citing delicate health and nervousness, yet explicitly stating she was not subject to seizures or illusions—in fact, perfectly sane."

"How odd to claim that!" I interjected. "It seemed unnecessary."

"At least it was said," he laughed. "Since you wish to know, I will recount it. She declared, 'I am making a long journey of vital importance—she stressed the word—rapid and secret. I shall return for my child in three months; meanwhile, she will remain silent as to who we are, whence we come, and whither we travel.' That was all. She spoke pure French. When she uttered the word 'secret,' she paused, staring sternly at me, her eyes locked on mine. I suspect she places great emphasis on that. You saw how swiftly she departed. I hope I have not acted foolishly in taking charge of the young lady."

For my part, I was delighted. I longed to see and speak with her, waiting only for the physician's approval. Those living in towns cannot comprehend how monumental an event the arrival of a new friend is in such profound solitude.

The doctor did not arrive until nearly one o'clock, but I could not sleep, unable to outrun the carriage of the princess in black velvet on foot. When the physician descended to the drawing room, he reported favorably. The patient was sitting up, her pulse regular, seemingly well. She had sustained no injuries, and the shock to her nerves had passed harmlessly. There was no objection to my visiting her, provided we both wished it. I sent a servant immediately to inquire if she would permit a few moments of my company.

The servant returned promptly to say she desired nothing more.

I was not long in accepting this invitation.

Our visitor lay in one of the finest rooms in the schloss. Perhaps it was slightly too stately. Opposite the foot of the bed hung a somber tapestry depicting Cleopatra with asps at her bosom, alongside other faded classic scenes. Yet, the gold carvings and rich, varied colors of the other decorations redeemed the gloom. Candles burned at the bedside. She sat up, her slender, pretty figure wrapped in a soft silk dressing gown embroidered with flowers, lined with thick quilted silk—the very garment her mother had thrown over her as she lay upon the ground.

What struck me dumb, causing me to recoil a step or two as I approached the bedside, was the face itself. It was the very visage that had haunted my childhood nights, fixed in my memory, the object of my horrified rumination for years when no one suspected my thoughts.

It was pretty, even beautiful, bearing the same melancholy expression I remembered. But this expression instantly transformed into a strange, fixed smile of recognition.

A silence stretched for a full minute before she spoke; I could not.

"How wonderful!" she exclaimed. "Twelve years ago, I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since."

"Wonderful indeed!" I repeated, struggling to overcome the horror that had momentarily silenced me. "Twelve years ago, in vision or reality, I certainly saw you. I could not forget your face. It has remained before my eyes ever since."

Her smile softened. Whatever was strange in it vanished, leaving her dimpling cheeks delightfully pretty and intelligent.

Feeling reassured, I continued in the vein of hospitality, welcoming her and expressing how much pleasure her accidental arrival had brought us, and especially how happy it made me. I took her hand, shy as a lonely person might be, yet the situation made me eloquent and bold. She pressed my hand, laying hers upon it, her eyes glowing as she looked hastily into mine, smiling again and blushing.

She answered my welcome prettily. I sat beside her, still wondering, as she began to speak.

"I must tell you my vision about you; it is so strange that we should have had such vivid dreams of each other, seeing one another as we do now, when we were both mere children. I was about six years old. I awoke from a confused, troubled dream to find myself in a room unlike my nursery, wainscoted in dark wood, with cupboards and benches scattered about. The beds were empty, and the room seemed devoid of anyone but myself. After looking around, admiring especially an iron candlestick with two branches that I would certainly recognize, I crept under a bed to reach the window. As I emerged, I heard someone crying. Looking up while I was still on my knees, I saw you—most assuredly you—as I see you now: a beautiful young lady with golden hair, large blue eyes, and lips exactly like yours.

"Your beauty won me. I climbed onto the bed and embraced you, and I think we both fell asleep. I was aroused by a scream; you were sitting up, screaming. I was frightened, slipped down to the ground, and seemed to lose consciousness for a moment. When I regained it, I was back in my nursery at home. Your face I have never forgotten. I could not be misled by mere resemblance. _You_ are the lady I saw then."

Now it was my turn to relate my corresponding vision, which I did to the undisguised wonder of my new acquaintance.

"I don't know which should fear the other," she said, smiling again. "If you were less pretty, I think I would be very afraid of you, but being as you are, and we both so young, I feel only that I made your acquaintance twelve years ago and already possess a right to your intimacy. At all events, it seems as if we were destined, from our earliest childhood, to be friends. I wonder if you feel as strangely drawn to me as I do to you. I have never had a friend—shall I find one now?" She sighed, her fine dark eyes gazing at me with passionate intensity.

Now the truth is, I felt rather unaccountably toward the beautiful stranger. I did feel, as she said, "drawn toward her," but there was also a sense of repulsion. In this ambiguous feeling, however, the attraction overwhelmingly prevailed. She interested and won me; she was so beautiful and indescribably engaging.

I perceived a growing languor and exhaustion stealing over her and hastened to bid her good night.

"The doctor thinks," I added, "that you ought to have a maid to sit with you tonight; one of ours is waiting, and she is a very useful and quiet creature."

"How kind of you, but I could not sleep; I never could with an attendant in the room. I shall require no assistance—and, shall I confess my weakness, I am haunted by a terror of robbers. Our house was robbed once, and two servants murdered, so I always lock my door. It has become a habit—and you look so kind I know you will forgive me. I see there is a key in the lock."

She held me close in her pretty arms for a moment and whispered in my ear, "Good night, darling, it is very hard to part with you, but good night; tomorrow, but not early, I shall see you again."

She sank back onto the pillow with a sigh, her fine eyes following me with a fond, melancholy gaze as she murmured again, "Good night, dear friend."

Young people like, and even love, on impulse. I was flattered by the evident, though as yet undeserved, fondness she showed me. I liked the confidence with which she accepted me. She was determined that we should be very near friends.

The next day arrived, and we met again. I was delighted with my companion; that is to say, in many respects.

Her looks lost nothing in the daylight—she was certainly the most beautiful creature I had ever seen, and the unpleasant remembrance of the face from my early dream had lost its initial shock. She confessed that she had experienced a similar shock upon seeing me, along with the same faint antipathy that had mingled with my admiration. We now laughed together over our momentary horrors.
