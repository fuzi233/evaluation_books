That morning, Mademoiselle Moore's pupil was notably distracted. Caroline forgot the instructions repeatedly, yet she endured the reproofs with a serene, unclouded composure. Seated in the sun-drenched bay near the window, she seemed to absorb the warmth as a benevolent influence, lifting her spirits and refining her nature. In this state, she appeared at her best, presenting a vision so pleasing it transcended mere physical attraction.

Beauty was clearly Caroline's gift. One need not know her intimately to appreciate her; she possessed a fairness that charmed at first sight. Her figure was perfectly proportioned to her youthful age—light, supple, and graceful, with curves that flowed naturally and limbs that moved with ease. Her face was open and gentle, framed by handsome eyes capable of sending a winning beam directly into the heart, speaking a soft language to the affections. Her mouth was lovely, her skin delicate, and her abundant brown hair, arranged with impeccable taste, cascaded in picturesque curls. Her attire, a modest winter gown of soft brown merino matching her hair, reflected a quiet elegance. A pink ribbon knotted beneath a simple collar added a touch of color to her fair complexion, completing an ensemble that was unobtrusive, inexpensive, yet ideally suited to her slender frame.

While her appearance was undeniable, her intellect and character awaited revelation. Her background, however, was soon laid bare. Born to parents separated shortly after her conception due to clashing temperaments, Caroline's lineage was complex yet distant. Her mother, the half-sister of Mr. Moore's father, shared no bloodline with Robert, Louis, or Hortense, though she held the title of a distant cousin. Her father, the brother of the rector, was a man whose flaws were best left unspoken, having rendered his wife unhappy. Though rumors swirled, the truth remained that he was flawed. Having never known her mother, who was taken from her in infancy, Caroline's childhood was defined by solitude until her father's untimely death. For years, her uncle, the rector, served as her sole guardian—a man ill-suited by nature or habit to nurture a young girl. His neglect forced Caroline to seek knowledge independently, leading her to eagerly accept Hortense's offer of lessons in French and needlework following the latter's arrival at Hollow's Mill.

Mademoiselle Moore, who prided herself on her "positive spirit" and a predilection for dry, rigorous studies, treated Caroline with unyielding strictness. She assigned endless "logical analyses," forcing the young girl to dissect sentences into propositions, principal clauses, and various types of incidentals. These exercises offered Caroline little joy; she believed she could learn the language more freely without them. Yet, she persisted, often losing herself in the linguistic maze. During these moments of confusion, while Hortense busied herself upstairs with her peculiar habit of endlessly rearranging her drawers, Caroline would retreat to Robert's counting-house. There, Mr. Moore, a man of a clear and tranquil mind, effortlessly dissolved her difficulties. Within two minutes, he grasped the essence of her problem and offered a solution that simplified the puzzle. Grateful for his insight, Caroline would depart reluctantly, wishing that nature had granted her a masculine form, allowing her to serve as his clerk rather than endure Hortense's tedious tutelage in the parlor.

Occasional evenings at Hollow's Cottage offered a stark contrast to the daily grind. While Mr. Moore was often away attending markets or visiting Mr. Yorke, or preoccupied with male guests, there were times when he returned early, disengaged, and free to converse with Caroline. These evenings passed swiftly, the hours flying on wings of light. The small parlor, occupied by the three cousins, became a haven of delight. Hortense, usually stern, softened in the late hours, her demeanor warming as she relaxed. Her greatest charm emerged when she played the guitar, her skillful fingers and well-toned voice creating a pleasant atmosphere, though her formal and self-important nature occasionally tempered the melody.

Mr. Moore, freed from the business yoke, transformed into a willing spectator of Caroline's vivacity. He was a complacent listener, a gentle companion, someone agreeable to sit near and engage with. Yet, these social respites were fleeting; by morning, the businessman resumed his frosty demeanor, rarely contriving to repeat the evenings. This inconsistency puzzled Caroline, who longed to savor the happiness she found there, fearing it might rust away if not used frequently. Despite this longing, she exercised restraint, never paying an unsolicited visit unless explicitly invited. That morning marked a significant shift: for the first time, Robert extended an invitation unprompted, speaking with such kindness that it filled Caroline with a sense of joy that lingered throughout the day.

Afternoon duties unfolded as usual. Hortense, perpetually active, bustled between the kitchen and parlor, scolding Sarah and reviewing Caroline's lessons. Adherence to perfection was non-negotiable for Mademoiselle; praise was deemed incompatible with a teacher's dignity, while blame was essential to maintain authority. Dinner arrived, prepared by Sarah with a mixture of disdain and resignation. The meal, heavily influenced by Belgian customs, featured a pea purée lamented for lacking haricots, a mysterious stew of miscellaneous meats chopped with breadcrumbs, bruised greens, and a fruit pâté sweetened with molasses. Caroline, accustomed to the foreign fare, accepted it gratefully, knowing that any display of distaste would incur Hortense's eternal displeasure.

Following dinner, Caroline orchestrated a maneuver to escort Hortense upstairs for dressing. Knowing that suggesting the removal of Hortense's corset and curl papers would be tantamount to a felony, she navigated the situation with diplomatic finesse. Pretending to need a change of scenery, she guided Hortense upward, convincing her that further delays were unnecessary. Once inside the bedroom, Caroline helped remove the stifling camisole, replacing it with a decent gown and arranging Hortense's hair and collar. However, Hortense insisted on finishing touches: a thick handkerchief around her throat and a voluminous black apron, symbols of her moral rigidity and perceived thriftiness. Caroline resisted, citing suffocation and the practicality of her shorter aprons, but Hortense's persistence threatened victory until Mr. Moore intervened. Overhearing the dispute, Robert sided with Caroline, noting her youth and the length of her curls, thereby sparing her from the heavy accessories. Though compelled to yield, Hortense disapproved of Caroline's neat, ladylike appearance, preferring a more solid, homely aesthetic.

The afternoon was dedicated to sewing, a domain where Hortense excelled. Skilled in fine embroidery, lace-making, and intricate knitting, she viewed the meticulous repair of stockings as a noble mission. She condemned Caroline to learn this arduous foreign method of darning, stitching hole by hole to mimic the fabric itself—a wearisome process deemed essential for any woman. Two pairs of stockings, started years prior, remained unfinished, serving as penance for Caroline's ignorance. Mr. Moore had once suggested burning them, but Caroline wisely declined, fearing the result would be a fresh pair of ruined hose.

As the sky darkened and rain poured down, secret fears gnawed at Caroline that Robert would be persuaded to stay at Whinbury. Time slipped forward to five o'clock, the clouds streaming relentlessly. Suspense mounted until Hortense declared it unfair to wait, announcing that coffee would be served. Reluctantly, Caroline descended to the kitchen, where Sarah was making a dress. Sarah's spirits were low, attributing Caroline's melancholy to Hortense's relentless confinement. Just as Caroline expressed doubt about Robert's return, Sarah revealed a startling truth: she had seen Murgatroyd lead Robert's horse into the yard. Robert had returned.

Overjoyed, Caroline hurried back to prepare the room, cutting a sleeve for Sarah's gown while waiting. When the kitchen door finally opened, Mr. Moore entered, dripping wet and shivering. Caroline, hiding behind a dress, quickly composed her features before meeting him. Her countenance immediately brightened. "We had ceased to expect you," she said gently, urging him to warm by the fire lest he catch a chill. Robert, reassured by her concern, noted that his coat was waterproof and needed only dry shoes. Standing on the hearth, he gazed at the glittering brasses above, but his eyes inevitably fell upon Caroline, flushed and smiling, her silky curls framing a radiant face. Sarah departed with a tray, leaving the siblings alone. Moore placed a hand on Caroline's shoulder, stooping to kiss her forehead.

"Oh!" she exclaimed, the gesture unlocking a flood of emotion. "I was miserable when I thought you would not come. I am almost too happy now. Are you happy, Robert? Do you like to come home?"

"To-night, at least," he replied. "But you worry about the frames, the business, the war?"

"Not just now." "Do you feel Hollow's Cottage too small, narrow, dismal?"

"At this moment, no." "Are you not bitter at heart because the wealthy and powerful ignore you?"

"No more questions. You are mistaken if you think I seek favor with the great. I crave only means—a position, a career."

"Which your talent and goodness shall win. You were made to be great; you *shall* be great."

"I wonder, Lina, what recipe you would give me for greatness. Poverty, misery, bankruptcy? Life is not what you think it, Lina!"

"But you are what I think you."

"I am not."

"You are better, then?"
