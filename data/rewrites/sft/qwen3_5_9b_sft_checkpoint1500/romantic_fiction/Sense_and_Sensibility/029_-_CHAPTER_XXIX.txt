January 2nd arrived with a chill that seeped into the bones, a grey, miserable dawn that offered little solace to the heartbroken. Before the fire had even caught, Marianne, barely clad, knelt beside the window-seat, clutching the sliver of daylight that pierced the gloom. Her quill danced furiously across the paper, driven by a flood of tears that threatened to drown her ink. She wrote with a frantic desperation, unaware that she was likely composing her final farewell to Willoughby.

Elinor, roused from a shallow slumber by the storm of Marianne's sobs, crept closer. Seeing her sister's state, she hesitated, her heart heavy with silent dread, before speaking in a voice softened by gentle consideration. "Marianne, may I ask—"

"No, Elinor," Marianne interrupted, her voice trembling with a strange, fleeting composure. "Ask nothing. You will soon know everything."

That brief moment of control shattered instantly, replaced by a fresh wave of overwhelming grief. Minutes stretched into an eternity as she struggled to steady her hand, the pauses between her sentences filled with fresh outbursts of weeping. To Elinor, this proved beyond doubt that the letter was a testament to a broken dream.

Elinor offered her sister the utmost quiet attention, refraining from any attempt to soothe her, for Marianne, in her agitated state, fiercely demanded solitude. With a nervous irritability that bordered on hysteria, Marianne begged Elinor not to speak a word to her. Realizing that their proximity only fueled Marianne's unrest, Elinor retreated, leaving her sister to wander the house until breakfast, seeking constant movement to stave off the crushing weight of her emotions.

At the dining table, Marianne refused to touch her food. Elinor, acutely aware of her sister's pain, focused her efforts on diverting Mrs. Jennings's attention entirely to herself. As the meal dragged on, a letter was finally delivered to Marianne. Snatching it from the maid with desperate urgency, her countenance drained of color, she fled the room before anyone could intervene.

Watching her departure, Elinor's heart sank with a sickening lurch. There was no mistaking the handwriting; it came from Willoughby. A profound physical sickness gripped her, making it difficult to lift her head, let alone maintain her composure under Mrs. Jennings's scrutiny. However, the kindly lady, engrossed in measuring wool for a rug, interpreted the scene as a mere jest. She chattered merrily about the joy of finding out that Marianne was truly engaged, asking when the marriage might take place.

Though Elinor wished to remain silent, the directness of Mrs. Jennings's inquiry forced a response. Trying to force a smile onto her weary lips, she gently dismantled the old lady's delusion. "And have you really, Ma'am, persuaded yourself that my sister is engaged to Mr. Willoughby?" Elinor asked, her voice steady despite her internal turmoil. "I thought it merely a joke, but this seriousness implies more. I must beg you not to deceive yourself further. I assure you, nothing would surprise me more than to hear of their union."

"For shame!" Mrs. Jennings exclaimed, laughing dismissively. "How can you talk so? Don't we all know it must be a match? They were over head and ears in love from the first moment they met! Did I not see them together in Devonshire every day? And did I not know your sister came to town specifically to buy wedding clothes?"

"Indeed, Ma'am," Elinor replied, her expression grave. "You are mistaken. You are doing a very unkind thing by spreading this report, and I fear you will find it difficult to believe me when I tell you it is false."

Mrs. Jennings laughed again, oblivious to the tragedy unfolding within the house. Unable to sustain the conversation, Elinor hastened upstairs, determined to uncover the truth behind Willoughby's letter before returning to her sister.

Upon entering the bedroom, she found Marianne collapsed upon the bed, nearly suffocated by her grief. Several letters lay scattered around her, and she clutched one tightly in her hand. Elinor approached slowly, sitting beside her and taking her hand in hers. She kissed Marianne's fingers repeatedly, a gesture of deep affection, and suddenly, the dam broke. Both sisters fell into a torrent of tears, the violence of Elinor's sorrow mirroring Marianne's despair.

After some time spent in shared, agonizing silence, Marianne placed the stack of letters into Elinor's hands and covered her face with a handkerchief, her breath coming in ragged gasps. Elinor waited until the initial paroxysm subsided, then opened the topmost letter. It was addressed from Bond Street, dated January.

*I have just had the honour of receiving your letter, for which I beg to return my sincere acknowledgments. I am much concerned to find there was anything in my behaviour last night that did not meet your approbation; and though I am quite at a loss to discover in what point I could be so unfortunate as to offend you, I entreat your forgiveness of what I can assure you to have been perfectly unintentional.*

*I shall never reflect on my former acquaintance with your family in Devonshire without the most grateful pleasure, and flatter myself it will not be broken by any mistake or misapprehension of my actions. My esteem for your whole family is very sincere; but if I have been so unfortunate as to give rise to a belief of more than I felt, or meant to express, I shall reproach myself for not having been more guarded in my professions of that esteem. That I should ever have meant more you will allow to be impossible, when you understand that my affections have been long engaged elsewhere, and it will not be many weeks, I believe, before this engagement is fulfilled.*

*It is with great regret that I obey your commands in returning the letters with which I have been honoured from you, and the lock of hair, which you so obligingly bestowed on me.*

The blood rushed to Elinor's cheeks with indignation. Even before reading the words, she had suspected a confession of infidelity, but the sheer audacity of the language stunned her. It was not merely a rejection; it was a calculated cruelty. Instead of expressing regret for breaking a promise, Willoughby dismissed the possibility of any deeper sentiment, claiming his affections were long since taken. He returned her letters and her lock of hair with a chilling politeness that masked a hard, unfeeling heart. Every line was an insult, a proclamation of a man devoid of honor or delicacy.

Elinor read the letter twice, then a third time, her abhorrence growing with each perusal. How could she explain this to Marianne without driving her further into despair? If she revealed the truth—that Willoughby had never loved her, that his promises were lies—would Marianne view this betrayal as a terrible loss or, paradoxically, as a deliverance from a monstrous deception?

Lost in a whirlwind of thoughts regarding the depravity of Willoughby's mind and the stark contrast with the noble heart of Edward Ferrars, Elinor momentarily forgot Marianne's immediate distress. A carriage rattled up the driveway, startling her. It was Mrs. Jennings's vehicle, arriving far too early. Determined not to abandon her sister, Elinor quickly excused herself, citing Marianne's indisposition. Mrs. Jennings, with her usual good-natured concern, accepted the excuse readily.

Once alone again, Elinor rushed back to the bedside. Marianne was attempting to rise, her legs weak from days of starvation and sleeplessness. Elinor caught her just in time to prevent a fall, supporting her as the girl succumbed to a nervous faintness, her head throbbing, her stomach empty, her spirit shattered. A glass of wine helped steady Marianne slightly, allowing her to whisper, "Poor Elinor! how unhappy I make you!"

"I only wish," Elinor replied, her voice thick with emotion, "there were anything I *could* do, which might be of comfort to you."

To Marianne, this selfless offer was too much. Tears streamed down her face as she cried out, "Oh! Elinor, I am miserable, indeed!"

Seeing her sister drowning in unresisted grief, Elinor could no longer remain silent. "Exert yourself, dear Marianne," she urged, her voice firm. "If you would not kill yourself and all who love you. Think of your mother; think of her misery while *you* suffer: for her sake you must exert yourself."

"I cannot, I cannot!" Marianne sobbed, clinging to Elinor. "Leave me, leave me, if I distress you! Leave me, hate me, forget me! But do not torture me so. Oh! how easy for those, who have no sorrow of their own to talk of exertion! Happy, happy Elinor, *you* cannot have an idea of what I suffer."

"Do you call *me* happy, Marianne?" Elinor asked, her eyes filling with tears. "Ah! if you knew!—And can you believe me to be so, while I see you so wretched!"
