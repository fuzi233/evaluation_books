I found Lady Verinder in her private sitting room, half-aside with her embroidery, and she started so hard the needle pricked her finger when I told her Sergeant Cuff had asked to speak to her. “Must I see him?” she asked, sharply, like the words pained her. “Can’t you stand in for me, Gabriel?”

I stared, befuddled. I’d never seen her so put out by a guest, much less a man she’d invited into her own house. She softened, and sighed. “My nerves are more frayed than I care to admit. There’s something about that London police officer that makes my skin crawl—I can’t name what it is. I’ve got a fool’s presentiment he’s brought nothing but trouble and misery through our front door with him. It’s not like me, I know. But that’s how it is.”

I had no reply to that. The longer I spent in Sergeant Cuff’s company, the fonder I grew of him, so her sudden, unaccountable dread made no sense to me at all. She rallied quickly, being the high-courage woman I’d served for thirty years. “If I have to see him, I have to. But I won’t face him alone. Bring him in, Gabriel, and don’t leave my side for a single minute he’s here.”

That was the first fit of the megrims I’d seen grip my mistress since she was a girl. I headed back to the boudoir to fetch the Sergeant, and on the way spotted Franklin Blake drifting out into the garden to join Godfrey Ablewhite, whose carriage to the station was due to pull up any minute.

The second Lady Verinder set eyes on Sergeant Cuff, she paled a whole shade lighter. But she held herself steady, and asked if he objected to my presence. She added, quick, that I was no mere old servant, but her trusted adviser on all household matters, the man most useful to consult on anything related to the staff. Sergeant Cuff bowed politely, and said he welcomed my company—he had matters to discuss about the household at large, and my prior experience on the staff had already proven far more helpful than Superintendent Seegrave’s bumbling inquiries.

We pulled up two chairs by the fire, and got straight down to business.
“I’ve already formed a clear opinion on this case,” Sergeant Cuff said, “and I beg your ladyship’s leave to keep it to myself for the time being. All I will lay out now is what I found in Miss Verinder’s sitting room earlier today, and what I propose we do next, with your permission.”

He walked through the story of the wet paint smudge on the boudoir door’s panel, and laid out every conclusion he’d drawn from it, phrased far more respectfully than he’d ever spoken to Seegrave.
“Two things are certain,” he said, to finish. “First: the Moonstone is no longer in the cabinet drawer it was locked in. Second: that smudge of white paint must have rubbed off onto some item of clothing belonging to someone in this house, the night the diamond vanished. We cannot take another single step in this investigation until we find that stained garment.”

“Finding that garment,” my mistress said, sharp, “means finding the thief, I presume?”

“I beg your ladyship’s pardon— I do not say the diamond was stolen. Not yet. I only say it is missing. The stained clothing may well be the lead that takes us straight to where it is, no more.”

Lady Verinder glanced over at me. “Do you follow that, Gabriel?”
“Sergeant Cuff follows it, my lady,” I said.

She turned back to him. “How do you propose to search for this stained item? Superintendent Seegrave already ransacked the boxes and bedrooms of every one of my long-serving, loyal servants. I will not, under any circumstances, have them insulted and suspected a second time.” (There was my mistress! A woman in ten thousand, every inch of her.)

“That is exactly the obstacle I came to you to resolve,” the Sergeant said. “Seegrave has done untold damage to this inquiry already, by letting every servant see he thought them guilty. If I make them feel singled out again, there is no telling what barriers they will throw up in my path—the maids most of all. But we *have* to search all their wardrobes a second time. Seegrave was only looking for the diamond. We are looking for a tiny smudge of paint no one who was wearing the garment that night would even have noticed. I agree completely that their feelings deserve respect. But the search has to happen.”

It looked like a deadlock, plain and simple.

“Luckily, I have a plan to meet that deadlock,” Sergeant Cuff said. “I will tell the entire household that we are searching the wardrobe of every single person who slept under this roof on Wednesday night. From your ladyship, down to the youngest scullery maid. No exceptions.”

I cut in. “The maids will immediately think we are trying to trick them, and suspect them all the more.”
“Not if we frame it as a universal formality, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “The servants will see it as perfectly fair, that their masters and mistresses are submitting to the exact same check as they are. Instead of hiding things, they’ll make a point of honour to help the search along.”

I saw the sense of it at once. So did my lady, after a moment’s thought. “You are certain this is the fastest way to get to the truth?” she asked.
“It is the shortest path I can see, my lady.”

She stood up to ring for her maid, to fetch the keys to her own wardrobe. “You will have full access. Speak to the staff when you are ready, with my own wardrobe keys in your hand.”

Sergeant Cuff stopped her, with a very unexpected question. “Had we not better confirm first that the other ladies and gentlemen staying in the house will consent to the same terms?”

“The only other lady in residence is Miss Verinder,” my lady said, surprised. “The only gentlemen are my nephews, Mr. Blake and Mr. Ablewhite. None of the three will raise the slightest objection.”

I remembered then that Mr. Godfrey was due to leave. Just as the words left my mouth, there was a knock at the door, and Godfrey stepped in to say his goodbyes, with Franklin trailing behind him, ready to walk him to the station. Lady Verinder laid out the plan, and Godfrey agreed at once, calling through the window to his man Samuel to haul his portmanteau back upstairs. He pressed the key to his luggage right into Sergeant Cuff’s palm. “My bags can follow me to London the second the inquiry is done,” he said.

Franklin laughed after him, and told the Sergeant he didn’t even own a locked wardrobe—every stitch of clothing he had was free to look through, no questions asked.

That left only Miss Rachel to give her consent, before we could gather the staff and begin the search.

But before my lady headed upstairs to get Rachel’s keys, Sergeant Cuff made one more request. “If it is convenient, I would like to see the household washing-book first. The stained garment could easily be a piece of linen. If our wardrobe search turns up nothing, I want to cross-reference every item of laundry sent out this week, to see if one piece is unaccounted for. That missing piece will almost certainly be the garment the paint got on, tossed away or destroyed by its owner before it could be sent to the wash. Superintendent Seegrave made another mistake when he herded all the maids into Miss Verinder’s sitting room on Thursday morning, and pointed that paint smudge out to every one of them. That gave whoever was wearing that stained garment more than enough time to get rid of it.”

My lady told me to ring for the washing-book. It was brought in by Rosanna Spearman, our second housemaid. The poor girl looked haggard to the bone, pale as a sheet, after her sudden illness the day before, but she was back to her duties that morning. Sergeant Cuff watched her very closely—studied her face when she stepped through the door, and glanced at her crooked shoulder as she slipped back out.

“Has that girl been in your service as long as the rest of the staff?” he asked.
“Why do you ask?” my lady said.
“The last time I saw her,” Sergeant Cuff said, quiet as anything, “she was in prison, serving a sentence for theft.”

My lady wasted no time telling him the full story, how she’d hired Rosanna out of a reformatory, that the matron had spoken highest of her, that she’d been a model servant the whole time she’d worked in the house. “You do not suspect her, I hope?” she asked, earnest as anything.

“I already told your ladyship,” Cuff said, “that I do not suspect anyone of theft, as of yet.”

My lady wasted no time then, heading upstairs to get Rachel’s keys. Cuff beat me to opening the door for her, and gave her a deep, formal bow. She shuddered as she walked past him, like he’d passed a cold hand over her shoulder.

We waited. And waited. No keys came. Sergeant Cuff said nothing at all. He turned his long, lean frame to the window, stuffed his lanky hands in his pockets, and whistled “The Last Rose of Summer” soft and low, that melancholy little tune he was so fond of.

Finally Samuel stepped in, not with a set of keys, but a crumpled scrap of pencil note for me. I fumbled my spectacles on, and read it. My lady had written that Rachel had flatly, unconditionally refused to let anyone examine her wardrobe. They’d pressed her for reasons, and she’d burst into tears. Pressed a second time, she’d only said: “I won’t, because I won’t. You can use force on me if you like, but I will consent to nothing less.”

I could not blame my lady for not wanting to face Cuff with that ridiculous, impossible answer. I’d have been red as a beet to stand in front of him with that sort of nonsense, myself.

“Any sign of Miss Verinder’s keys?” the Sergeant asked.
“Miss Rachel refuses to let anyone look through her things,” I said.

“Ah,” he said. The word was soft, but there was no surprise in it at all. It was the voice of a man who’d been waiting to hear exactly that.

“Does that mean we have to give up the search entirely?” I asked.
“It has to be given up,” he said. “We cannot search half the house and leave Miss Verinder’s things untouched. It is all, or nothing. Send Mr. Ablewhite’s portmanteau to London on the next train, and return the washing-book to the young woman who brought it, with my compliments and thanks.”

He laid the washing-book on the table, pulled out his penknife, and started trimming his nails, calm as anything.
“You don’t look even a little bit disappointed,” I said.
“I am not,” he said.

I tried to press him for answers. “Why on earth would Miss Rachel put obstacles in your way? Getting that diamond back is her own best interest, is it not?”

“Wait a little, Mr. Betteredge,” he said, closing his penknife with a sharp click. “Wait a little.”

I was too loyal to Miss Rachel to guess what he was driving at, too blind to the ugly lines of the case that were starting to creep into view. Lady Verinder’s strange dread of him, I realized later, had been her own half-glimpse of that truth, dim as a shape seen through a fogged window. I had no clue what he was thinking.

“What do we do next?” I asked.

Sergeant Cuff stood up, and nodded towards the garden door.
“Come outside with me,” he said. “Let’s go have a look at the roses.”
