Our relations at Castlewood detained us until the turn of the year, and though a fortnight to the unreasonably devoted youth appeared an eternity, he eventually returned to the side of his beloved. Madame de Bernstein was relieved to depart her father's household, and as we distanced ourselves from the estate, her conversation grew bolder. Of the "battle royal" that transpired between her and her niece, she remained silent; however, the old lady ceased speaking of raising *cette petite*. Indeed, when she mentioned the young Countess, she did so with a nervous laugh, devoid of genuine malice. Regarding her nephew, Eugene, she declared he was destined to be henpecked for the remainder of his days, asserting that mere domestic order would suffice. She praised the "little old vulgar American gentleman"—Mr. Van den Bosch—as shrewd, proposing he serve as a steward. She casually noted that the Countess's mother was a convict transported from England, likely hardened by the labor of the rope-walk, but dismissed this as gossip unworthy of the town crier. To her, birth meant little compared to virtue; the young woman would prove honest for her own sake, master English quickly, and her illustrious title would grant her entry into any circle. Whether her father were a grocer, a smuggler, or a dealer in human souls, the Countess of Castlewood could afford to be anyone's daughter. Once presented, it was her aunt's duty to stand by her.

Mr. Warrington, hoping the bonds tying Madame de Bernstein to her nephew would extend to her niece, spoke of his visit to Mr. Hagan and his wife, seeking his aunt's favor. Yet the old lady proved obdurate concerning Lady Maria, begging that her name never be uttered, and spent the ensuing two hours recounting a string of anecdotes regarding her niece. Though I, who write this book open to the eyes of the innocent (*virginibus puerisque*), decline to record these tales, I must affirm that if the Countess sinned, she was not alone in doing so, and if she repented, others in the family would do well to emulate her. As for Mr. Hagan, it is known that after leaving the stage, he lived an exemplary life, renowned for the elegance and eloquence of his sermons. His lady adopted extreme religious views and commanded deep respect within her sect; when I last encountered her, she spoke of a peculiar spiritual illumination, which I privately suspected stemmed from the liberal consumption of spirits. Nevertheless, I recall a time when both she and her husband were kind to me and mine, offering sympathy when it was needed, proving that even the most zealous can possess a heart capable of compassion.

I have often recounted how easily fortunes shifted in my fickle aunt's regard, with each of us brothers alternately embraced and cast aside. My moment of glory followed the success of my play. Introduced to the town wits, I held my place among them with reasonable ease, earning praise for my breeding from the macaronis and the fashionable set. Had my purse been deeper, I might have pursued a career among them, had I not fallen in love with a pair of kind eyes superior to the brightest orbs of the Gunnings or Chudleighs, or the painted beauties of the Ranelagh ring. Because I was fond of your mother, children, it was rumored that my tastes were low, a sentiment lamented by my genteel family. Thus, my godly Lady Warrington and my worldly Madame Bernstein laid their aged heads together in dismay. "With his name, he might marry anybody," whispered Meek Religion, keeping one eye on Heaven and the other on profit. "I meddle with no man's affairs, and I admire genius," my uncle countered, "yet it is a pity you consort with those poets and authors, and that sort of people, when you might have secured a lovely creature with a hundred thousand pounds."

"But if I had promised her, uncle?" I asked.

"Promise, promise!" he retorted, turning on his heel. "These matters are questions of arrangement and prudence requiring a careful look-out. When you first committed yourself to little Miss Lambert, you had not yet seen the lovely American lady whom your mother, a good mother naturally, wished you to marry. Your duty to your mother—your duty to the Fifth Commandment—warranted breaking with Miss L. and fulfilling your excellent mother's intentions regarding Miss [whose Dutch name I forget]. A name is nothing, but a plum, Master George, is something to behold! Consider my dear little Miley, placed at a dancing school with Miss Barwell, Nabob Barwell's daughter. I do not disguise my wish that these children contract an attachment enduring through their lives! I tell the Nabob so. One day, emerging from the House of Commons, we witnessed them walking a minuet. It was beautiful, bringing tears to my eyes, for I have a feeling heart, George, and I love my boy!"

"But if I prefer Miss Lambert, uncle, with twopence to her fortune, to the Countess with her hundred thousand pounds?"

"Why then, sir, you have a singular taste, that's all," said the old gentleman, leaving me. I could perfectly understand his vexation at my inability to view the world through his lens. Nor did Aunt Bernstein relish the engagement I had made, nor the family with whom I spent so much time. Their simplicity wore on the old woman of the world, who disliked their company as much as a certain individual dislikes holy water. Chafing at my constant dallying at my sweetheart's lap, I began to fall from her favor, rising again only to descend once more. Harry became the family hero.

He wrote brief letters from the seat of war where he was engaged. Madame Bernstein cared little initially for the content, finding them simple and lacking in interest. However, we in London had already learned of the action on the glorious First of August at Minden, where Wolfe's old regiment stood among the six British regiments that achieved victory. At that same hour, the young General lay in bed, visible from Quebec, stricken by fever, and perhaps by rage and disappointment at the setback his troops had just suffered.

Having arrived in the St. Lawrence in June, the fleet carrying Wolfe and his army disembarked on the last day of the month onto the Island of Orleans, opposite the great cliff of Quebec. Following the great action wherein his General fell, the dear brother who accompanied the chief wrote home a simple letter detailing his modest share in that glorious day. He added nothing to the myriad accounts already written of the battle on the thirteenth of September, save for a testimony from a brother aide-de-camp standing by his side: that the General never spoke after receiving his death wound, rendering the famous phrase attributed to the dying hero no more authentic than an oration by Livy or Thucydides.

From his vantage point on the island, lying in the great northern channel of the river, the General eagerly scanned the horizon for a chance to engage his enemy. He landed above the city and below it, striking wherever an opening presented itself. It was surely a prodigious error on the part of the Marquis of Montcalm to accept battle on equal terms, for the British General possessed no artillery. When we executed our famous *scalade* of the heights and stood upon the Plains of Abraham, we were indeed closer to the city, yet as far removed from its walls as ever.

The game played between the brave chiefs of those two gallant little armies, lasting from July until Mr. Wolfe won the crowning hazard in September, must have been as engaging as any match ever contested by eager players. On the very first night after landing, as my brother narrated, the sport began. At midnight, the French sent a squadron of fireships down upon the British vessels discharging stores at Orleans. Our seamen regarded it as fine sport to tow the fireships clear of the fleet and ground them ashore, where they burned out.

Upon hearing that our ships had entered the river, the French commander marched to Beauport, ahead of the city, establishing a strong position. Once our stores and hospitals were secured, our General crossed from his island to the left shore, drawing nearer to his foe. Behind him lay the ships in the river, but before him stretched a countryside bristling with arms. The Indians in the forest seized our advance parties, murdering them with horrible tortures, while the French, allied with their savage friends, displayed equal ferocity. The Montmorenci River roared between Wolfe and the enemy, blocking direct assault. Bent on discovering another point of vulnerability, the General circled the town of Quebec, skirting the left shore beyond. Everywhere was guarded, including his immediate front, and after running the gauntlet of batteries up and down the river, he returned to his post at Montmorenci.

On the right flank of the French position, across the fordable Montmorenci at low tide, sat an enemy redoubt. Wolfe desired it. Perhaps defending it would force the French chief from his lines, precipitating a battle. Determined to gamble these odds, Wolfe planned to ferry the bulk of his army from the Island of Orleans and attack from the St. Lawrence. Timing his assault for shallow waters, he ensured his lieutenants, Murray and Townsend, could cross the Montmorenci, launching the desperate game on the last day of July.
