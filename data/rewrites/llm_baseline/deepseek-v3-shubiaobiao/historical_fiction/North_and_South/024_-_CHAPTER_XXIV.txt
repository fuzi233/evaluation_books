Mistakes Cleared Up**

Margaret rose the next morning, weary but resolute. The night had passed without incident, her mother stirring only once. A faint breeze whispered through the stifling air, and though Milton’s smokestacks and cobblestones offered no rustling leaves, Margaret imagined them elsewhere—country lanes and green woods alive with the murmuring dance of wind through branches. The thought brought a fleeting comfort.

She settled into her needlework in Mrs. Hale’s room, determined to structure her day: assist her mother when she woke, visit Bessy Higgins after dinner. Above all, she would not dwell on the Thorntons—until, of course, Dixon slipped in with hushed urgency.

"Mr. Thornton is here, miss. Asking for you. Your father’s still out."

Margaret’s needle stilled. "I’ll go," she said, though her limbs felt leaden.

She found him at the drawing-room window, his back to her, shoulders rigid. The set of his jaw betrayed no ease, but his hands—clenched then flexing—spoke of a man wrestling himself. When he turned, his eyes were dark with unchecked emotion.

"Miss Hale." His voice was low, rough. "I was ungrateful yesterday."

"You owe me no gratitude," she replied, lifting her chin. "Any woman would have done the same. We act on instinct when faced with violence—no more."

A flush scorched her cheeks, but she held his gaze.

He stepped closer, then checked himself, striding instead to shut the door. When he faced her again, his restraint was palpable. "You dismiss it too lightly. That instinct, as you call it, has bound me to you. When I breathe, when I take pride in my work—I’ll remember I owe it to you." His voice dropped, raw. "To the woman I love as no man has loved before."

Margaret recoiled. "This is blasphemy."

"Blasphemy?" His laugh was sharp. "To speak the truth?"

"To frame my actions as personal!" Fire leapt into her words. "I’d have shielded any man in that mob. You think this—" She gestured between them, fingers trembling. "You think it was for you?"

His face hardened. "I see. My regard offends you."

"It does." She gripped the table’s edge. "You mistake duty for devotion. I’ve no more feeling for you than for the poorest wretch in that riot."

A muscle twitched in his jaw. For a heartbeat, she thought he might strike the wall. Instead, he snatched up his hat. "Then let me be clear: my love isn’t a stain to be scrubbed away. It exists. You needn’t return it—but you cannot erase it."

He left without waiting for a reply. The door clicked shut, and Margaret exhaled, her anger ebbing into something quieter: regret. Had there been tears in his eyes? The possibility unsettled her. She’d wanted to wound his pride, not his heart.

*But what choice did I have?* she argued silently. *He built dreams on a moment’s instinct. That’s his folly, not mine.*

Yet the weight of his parting words lingered, stubborn as the man himself.
