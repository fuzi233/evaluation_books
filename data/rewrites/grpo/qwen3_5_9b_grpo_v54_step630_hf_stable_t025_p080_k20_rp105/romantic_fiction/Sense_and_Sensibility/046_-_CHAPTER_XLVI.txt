Marianne's illness, though severe in its onset, had not lingered long enough to stall her recovery. Armed with her youth, innate vitality, and the devoted presence of her mother, she rallied with remarkable speed. Within four days of Mrs. Dashwood's arrival, Marianne was strong enough to transfer to Mrs. Palmer's dressing room. There, driven by an impatience to express her gratitude for the kindness that had fetched her mother, she eagerly invited Colonel Brandon to visit.

When the Colonel entered, the sight of Marianne's altered countenance and the pale hand she immediately extended to him struck Elinor with a profound emotion. It was clear to her that his reaction stemmed from something deeper than mere affection or the knowledge that his love was known; it was a resurgence of past misery, triggered by the striking resemblance between Marianne and his lost love, Eliza. The hollow eyes, the sickly complexion, and the posture of weakness brought those tragic memories flooding back, intensified by Marianne's warm acknowledgment of her debt to him.

Mrs. Dashwood, observing the scene with equal vigilance but a different perspective, interpreted the Colonel's demeanor through the lens of simple, self-evident duty. Yet, she found herself convinced that Marianne's actions and words hinted at a sentiment rising above mere gratitude.

As days passed and Marianne grew visibly stronger, the topic of returning to Barton arose. With the departure of Mrs. Jennings from Cleveland imminent, the Dashwoods' plans became fixed. Colonel Brandon, urged by the united requests of the women, accepted that his residence at Barton would be equally necessary. In a gesture of reciprocal hospitality, he agreed to provide his carriage for their journey, ensuring his sick charge would travel comfortably. He also pledged to visit the cottage within a few weeks, a promise made with genuine pleasure.

The day of departure finally arrived. Marianne took a prolonged and heartfelt leave of Mrs. Jennings, her gratitude laced with a newfound respect for the woman she had previously neglected. She bid Colonel Brandon farewell with the warmth of a true friend. Assisted gently by him into the carriage, she ensured she occupied at least half of the space, a testament to her lingering frailty. Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor followed, leaving the others to their quiet farewells and the dullness of parting.

The journey to Barton spanned two days. Marianne endured the travel with surprising resilience, free from essential fatigue. Every effort of her companions to ensure her comfort was met with her calm acceptance. For Elinor, witnessing this composure was a source of immense relief. Having watched her sister suffer in silence for weeks, burdened by anguish she lacked the courage to voice, Elinor now rejoiced in Marianne's apparent mental stability. She trusted that this serenity, born of serious reflection, would eventually lead to lasting cheerfulness.

As they neared Barton, the landscape itself seemed to summon painful memories. Marianne grew silent, turning her face away from the windowless interior to gaze earnestly through the glass. Elinor felt only pity for this natural emotion, which, in its quiet dignity, deserved praise. Upon entering the sitting room, Marianne's demeanor shifted with resolute firmness. She scanned the room as if determined to acclimate herself to the sight of everything connected to Willoughby. She spoke little, yet every sentence was crafted for cheerfulness; when a sigh escaped, it was invariably followed by a smile.

After dinner, she attempted to play the piano. However, her eyes rested upon a score of an opera procured by Willoughby, containing their favorite duets and inscribed with her name. "That will not do," she declared, shaking her head. She set the music aside, feigned feeble fingers, and closed the instrument, vowing firmly that she would practice much in the future.

The following morning brought no diminution of this hopeful spirit. Strengthened by rest, she spoke with genuine animation, anticipating Margaret's return and the restoration of their family circle. "When the weather settles," she said, outlining a rigorous plan of daily walks to the farm, Sir John's plantations, and the old Priory ruins. "I shall rise by six and divide every moment between music and reading," she continued, detailing a schedule of serious study to cure her previous idleness. "By reading six hours a day, I shall gain in a year the instruction I so desperately lack."

Elinor admired the nobility of this plan, though she smiled sadly at the familiar spark of eager fantasy that now directed it toward virtuous ends rather than languid complaint. Her smile faded into a sigh as she remembered the unfulfilled promise to Willoughby. She feared that revealing the truth would shatter Marianne's newfound tranquility. Resolving to wait until her sister's health was fully secure, Elinor intended to delay the difficult conversation. Yet, resolution proved fragile.

It took a few days for the weather to soften sufficiently for an invalid to venture out. Finally, a gentle morning broke, tempting Marianne to walk. Leaning on Elinor's arm, she was permitted to stroll along the lane before the house. They had barely advanced far enough to see the prominent hill behind the cottage when Marianne paused, pointing with a steady hand.

"There, exactly there," she said, her voice dropping. "On that projecting mound—I fell; and there I first saw Willoughby."

She revived quickly, adding, "I am thankful to find I can look upon the spot with so little pain. Shall we ever talk of that subject, Elinor? Or is it wrong? I *can* talk of it now, I hope, as I ought."

Elinor tenderly encouraged her openness. "As for regret," Marianne continued, "I have done with that regarding him. I do not mean to recount my past feelings, but what they are *now*. If I could be satisfied on one point—if I could believe he was not *always* acting a part, not *always* deceiving me; if I could be assured he was not so wicked as my fears imagined, especially concerning that unfortunate girl—"

She stopped. Elinor, seizing the moment, answered joyfully, "If you could be assured of that, you would find peace."

"Yes. My peace is doubly involved. It is horrible to suspect someone who has been so to me of such designs, but what must it make me appear to myself? Only a shamefully unguarded affection could expose me to such ruin."

"How then," Elinor asked gently, "would you account for his behavior?"

"I would suppose him... Oh, how gladly would I suppose him, only fickle. Very, very fickle."

Elinor said nothing, debating internally whether to reveal the truth immediately or wait for stronger health. They walked in silence for a few minutes.

"I am not wishing him too much good," Marianne sighed at last. "I only hope his secret reflections are no more unpleasant than my own. He will suffer enough in them."

"Do you compare your conduct with his?"

"No. I compare it with what it *ought* to have been; I compare it with yours."

"Our situations have borne little resemblance."

"They have borne more than our conduct. Do not let your kindness defend what your judgment must censure. My illness has made me think. It has given me leisure for serious recollection. Long before I was recovered enough to speak, I was able to reflect. I saw in my own behavior since last autumn nothing but a series of imprudence towards myself and a lack of kindness to others. I saw that my own feelings prepared my suffering, and my lack of fortitude nearly led me to the grave. My illness was entirely brought on by my own negligence. Had I died, it would have been self-destruction. I did not know my danger until it was removed, yet I wonder at my recovery—I wonder that the very eagerness of my desire to live, to atone to God and to you, did not kill me at once. Had I died, in what peculiar misery should I have left you, my nurse, my friend, my sister! You, who had seen the fretful selfishness of my latter days! How should I have lived in your remembrance? And my mother! How could you have consoled her? I cannot express my abhorrence of myself. Whenever I looked to the past, I saw duty neglected or failing indulged. Everyone seemed injured by me. I repaid Mrs. Jennings's kindness with ungrateful contempt. To the Middletons, the Palmers, the Steeles, to every acquaintance, I was insolent and unjust, with a heart hardened against their merits and a temper irritated by their attention. Even to John and Fanny, I gave less than their due. But you, above all, had been wronged by me. I knew your heart and its sorrows, yet did it influence me? Not to compassion. Your example was before me, but to what avail? Was I more considerate? Did I imitate your forbearance? No. Not less when I knew you were unhappy than when I thought you at ease, did I turn away from every exertion of duty or friendship. I scarcely allowed sorrow to exist but with me, regretting only that heart which had deserted and wronged me, leaving you, for whom I professed an unbounded affection, to be miserable for my sake."

Her rapid flow of self-reproach ceased. Elinor, too honest to flatter but eager to soothe, instantly offered the praise and support Marianne's frankness and contrition deserved. Marianne pressed her hand. "You are very good. The future must be my proof. I have laid down my plan. If I am capable of adhering to it, my feelings shall be governed and my temper improved. They shall no longer worry others nor torture myself. I shall now live solely for my family. You, my mother, and Margaret, must henceforth be all the world to me. From you and my home, I shall never again have the smallest incitement to move. If I mix in other society, it will be only to show that my spirit is humbled, my heart amended, and that I can practice the civilities of life with gentleness. As for Willoughby—to say I shall soon or ever forget him would be idle. His remembrance can be overcome by no change of circumstance. But it shall be regulated, checked by religion, by reason, by constant employment."

She paused, adding in a low voice, "If I could but know *his* heart, everything would become easy."

Elinor, who had been weighing the propriety of revealing the truth, heard this and realized that reflection had yielded nothing; resolution must act. She found herself leading to the fact. She managed the recital with address, preparing her anxious listener with caution. She related simply and honestly the chief points of Willoughby's apology, doing justice to his repentance while softening only his protestations of present regard.

Marianne said not a word. She trembled, her eyes fixed on the ground, her lips turning whiter than sickness had left them. A thousand inquiries surged in her heart, yet she dared not utter one. She caught every syllable with panting eagerness; her hand, unknowingly to herself, pressed tightly against her sister's, and tears covered her cheeks.

Dreading her exhaustion, Elinor led her toward home. Until they reached the cottage door, they spoke only of Willoughby and their conversation, Elinor being meticulously minute in every detail where safety allowed. As soon as they entered, Marianne, with a kiss of gratitude and two words barely audible through her tears—"Tell mama"—withdrew from her sister and walked slowly upstairs. Elinor, respecting the solitude Marianne now sought, turned into the parlour to fulfill her parting injunction, her mind anxiously pre-arranging the result, ready to revive the subject again should Marianne fail to do so.
