It was generally agreed in New York that the Countess Olenska had lost her looks; and New York said it with the same finality with which it judged a sauce spoiled, a roast overdone, a family gone a shade too far toward the unknown. People remembered her first appearance as if it had been a course served long ago at a celebrated table: a brilliantly pretty child of nine or ten, all bright color and tight curls, a little thing whom one “ought to paint,” as though she were fruit arranged on a silver dish.

Her parents had been continental wanderers—those restless souls who live out of trunks and timetables, who change cities the way a cook changes pans—until death took them both and the child was handed over to her aunt, Medora Manson. Poor Medora, repeatedly widowed, was always returning to New York to “settle down,” each time in a smaller house, as if the city were a farm that would take her back after every failed season. She would come with a new husband or an adopted child, and within a few months she would part from the husband or quarrel with the ward, sell the house at a loss, and set off again—packing up her rooms the way a careless hostess clears a table, sweeping away what she cannot keep.

New York, having long since resigned itself to Medora’s oddities—she had the Rushworth blood, and had been linked by an unhappy marriage to one of the crazy Chiverses—watched her with indulgence. But when she returned with the orphaned niece, people thought it a pity that such a pretty child should be in such hands. Everyone meant to be kind to little Ellen Mingott, though her dusky red cheeks and gaiety seemed improper for a child who, by the unalterable rules of American mourning, ought still to have been steeped in black, like tea left too long to draw.

Medora flouted those rules the way a foreign cook flouts a New York receipt. When she stepped from the steamer, the family were scandalized to see that the crape veil she wore for her own brother was inches shorter than those of her sisters-in-law, while little Ellen appeared in crimson merino with amber beads—warm, glowing, and audacious, like a gipsy foundling brought into a parlor that smelled of starch and solemnity. Yet New York had known Medora too long to be truly shocked; only a few old ladies shook their heads, while the rest of the relations fell, in spite of themselves, under the child’s charm.

Ellen was fearless and familiar, with disconcerting questions and precocious comments. She had outlandish arts too—dancing a Spanish shawl dance, singing Neapolitan love-songs to a guitar—things that set the drawing-room air quivering with unfamiliar rhythms, like spices stirred into a bland broth. Under her aunt—whose real name was Mrs. Thorley Chivers, but who, having received a Papal title, had resumed her first husband’s patronymic and called herself the Marchioness Manson because in Italy she could turn it into Manzoni—Ellen received an expensive, incoherent education: “drawing from the model,” which New York regarded as an indecent ingredient, and playing the piano in quintets with professional musicians, as if a respectable girl were meant to be served up in public.

Of course no good could come of it, New York said, with the satisfied sound of a maxim laid down like a carving-knife. When poor Chivers finally died in a madhouse, his widow—draped in strange weeds—pulled up stakes again and departed with Ellen, now grown into a tall, bony girl with conspicuous eyes. For a time no more was heard of them; then news came of Ellen’s marriage to an immensely rich Polish nobleman of legendary fame, met at a ball at the Tuileries—one of those glittering occasions that New York imagined as a banquet it had not been invited to, with candles and music and air too thick with perfume. He was said to have princely establishments in Paris, Nice, and Florence, a yacht at Cowes, and many square miles of shooting in Transylvania. Ellen vanished into that tale like a flame swallowed by smoke: a kind of sulphurous apotheosis.

A few years later Medora came back yet again—subdued, impoverished, mourning a third husband, seeking a still smaller house—and people wondered that her rich niece had not been able to do something for her. Then came the heavier news: Ellen’s own marriage had ended in disaster, and she was returning home to seek rest and oblivion among her kin.

These things went through Newland Archer’s mind a week later as he watched the Countess Olenska enter the van der Luyden drawing-room on the evening of the momentous dinner. The occasion had the hush and solemnity of a ceremony; even the silences seemed arranged and served at the proper intervals, as carefully as courses. Archer, who could smile at his old New York while cherishing it, felt a nervous curiosity as to how she would carry herself in the presence of that most chosen company, assembled with an awful correctness.

She came rather late, one hand still ungloved and fastening a bracelet at her wrist, and yet she entered without haste or embarrassment, as if time were an element she could season to her own taste. In the middle of the room she paused, looking about her with a grave mouth and smiling eyes; and in that instant Archer rejected the verdict that she had “lost her looks.”

It was true her early radiance had gone. The high red cheeks had paled. She was thin, worn, and a little older-looking than her age—nearly thirty, Archer guessed. But there was about her the mysterious authority of beauty: a sure carriage of the head, an exact movement of the eyes, trained without being theatrical, conscious without being loud. She was simpler in manner than most of the women present, and many were disappointed afterward, Janey would report, that she was not more “stylish”—for stylishness was what New York valued most, as if it were the last gloss on a finished dish. Perhaps it was because Ellen’s early vivacity had disappeared; because she was quiet—quiet in her movements, her voice, and the low pitch of it. New York, expecting something more resonant from such a history, found itself disconcerted by her restraint.

Dinner with the van der Luydens was at best no light matter; with a Duke who was their cousin, it became almost a religious solemnity. Archer, who understood his city’s palate, enjoyed the thought that only an old New Yorker could taste the difference—so immense to New York—between being merely a Duke and being the van der Luydens’ Duke. Stray noblemen were taken calmly, even with a certain distrustful hauteur, except in the Struthers set; but noblemen who arrived with such credentials were received with an old-fashioned cordiality they would have been mistaken to attribute solely to Debrett.

The van der Luydens had emphasized the importance of the occasion with the mute eloquence of porcelain and plate. The du Lac Sèvres and the Trevenna George II silver were out; so was the van der Luyden “Lowestoft” and the Dagonet Crown Derby. The table gleamed with that cold, ancestral brilliance that seemed to chill the very clink of spoons. Mrs. van der Luyden looked more than ever like a Cabanel, and Mrs. Archer, wearing her grandmother’s seed-pearls and emeralds, reminded her son of an Isabey miniature. All the ladies wore their handsomest jewels, yet the house and the occasion insisted, characteristically, on old-fashioned settings—heavy, inherited, unquestioned. Old Miss Lanning, persuaded to come, actually wore her mother’s cameos and a Spanish blonde shawl, the lace lying on her shoulders like a relic.

The Countess Olenska was the only young woman at the table; and as Archer scanned the smooth, plump elderly faces rising from diamond necklaces and towering ostrich feathers, they struck him as curiously immature beside hers. That maturity—etched in fine lines, in the steadiness of her gaze—frightened him. He thought, with a kind of inward recoil, of what must have gone to the making of her eyes.

The Duke of St. Austrey, seated at his hostess’s right, was nominally the chief figure; yet if the Countess was less conspicuous than people had hoped, the Duke was almost invisible. He had not committed the vulgarity of arriving in a shooting-jacket, but his evening clothes were shabby and baggy, worn with an air of being as homely as a workman’s; and with his stooping way of sitting, and the vast beard spreading over his shirt-front, he scarcely looked dressed for dinner at all. He was short, round-shouldered, sunburnt, with a thick nose, small eyes, and a sociable smile; but he seldom spoke, and when he did, it was in tones so low that—despite the frequent expectant silences about the table—his remarks were lost to all but his neighbors. Even his voice seemed to withdraw from the room’s careful acoustics, as if the Duke refused to contribute to the soundscape that New York so strictly managed: the rustle of silk, the measured laugh, the discreet murmur that must never rise into anything like clamor.

When the men joined the ladies after dinner, the Duke went straight up to the Countess Olenska; they sat down in a corner and plunged into animated talk. Neither seemed aware that etiquette required the Duke first to pay respects to Mrs. Lovell Mingott and Mrs. Headly Chivers, nor the Countess to converse with the amiable hypochondriac Mr. Urban Dagonet of Washington Square—who, for the pleasure of meeting her, had broken through his fixed rule of not dining out between January and April. The two talked for nearly twenty minutes, their voices making a little private weather in the corner: a warmer current amid the room’s controlled air.

Then the Countess rose and walked alone across the wide drawing-room and sat down at Archer’s side.

It was not the custom in New York drawing-rooms for a lady to get up and leave one gentleman in order to seek another. A woman was expected to remain—immovable as an idol—while the men who wished to speak to her succeeded one another. But the Countess seemed unaware she had broken any rule. She settled herself at ease in the corner of the sofa beside Archer, and looked at him with the kindest eyes.

“I want you to talk to me about May,” she said.

Instead of answering, he asked: “You knew the Duke before?”

“Oh, yes—we used to see him every winter at Nice. He’s very fond of gambling—he used to come to the house a great deal.”

She said it as simply as if she had remarked that he was fond of wild-flowers; and then, with candid addendum, she added: “I think he’s the dullest man I ever met.”

The freedom of it pleased Archer so much that he forgot the slight shock of her mentioning the Duke’s visits. It was undeniably exciting to meet a woman who could find the van der Luydens’ Duke dull—and dare to say so. Archer longed to question her, to draw out more of that other life whose mere scraps of speech gave off such a pungent scent, like a jar opened briefly in a pantry where foreign herbs were kept. But he feared to touch distressing memories; and before he could find a safe question, she drifted back, as she had meant to from the first, to May.

“May is a darling; I’ve seen no young girl in New York so handsome and so intelligent. Are you very much in love with her?”

Archer reddened and laughed. “As much as a man can be.”

She considered him thoughtfully, as though she were tasting his words to detect their true flavor. “Do you think, then, there is a limit?”

“To being in love? If there is, I haven’t found it!”

She glowed with sympathy. “Ah—it’s really and truly a romance?”

“The most romantic of romances!”

“How delightful! And you found it all out for yourselves—it was not in the least arranged for you?”

Archer looked at her incredulously. “Have you forgotten,” he asked with a smile, “that in our country we don’t allow our marriages to be arranged for us?”

A dusky blush rose to her cheek, and he regretted his words at once, as if he had brought a harsh metallic taste into a delicate dish.

“Yes,” she answered, “I’d forgotten. You must forgive me if I sometimes make these mistakes. I don’t always remember that everything here is good that was—that was bad where I’ve come from.”

She looked down at her Viennese fan of eagle feathers, and he saw her lips tremble. The room around them kept up its proper murmurs—soft talk laid over softer talk, laughter carefully leashed—yet in that tremor Archer heard another sound, a cracked note from a different world, and he understood how a life could be altered by the voices one must listen to, the words one must answer in, the very dialect of intimacy imposed by power.

“I’m so sorry,” he said impulsively; “but you are among friends here, you know.”

“Yes—I know. Wherever I go I have that feeling. That’s why I came home. I want to forget everything else, to become a complete American again, like the Mingotts and Wellands, and you and your delightful mother, and all the other good people here tonight.” She glanced toward the door and added, with a quietness that held him: “Ah, here’s May arriving, and you will want to hurry away to her.”

She did not move; her eyes turned back from the doorway to rest on Archer’s face.

The drawing-rooms were filling now with after-dinner guests, their entrances marked by a soft increase of sound—steps on thick carpet, greetings exchanged in the approved key, the low swell of many voices kept from becoming noise. Following Madame Olenska’s glance, Archer saw May Welland entering with her mother. In white and silver, a wreath of silver blossoms in her hair, the tall girl looked like a Diana just alight from the chase—fresh as winter air, bright as iced fruit.

“Oh,” Archer said, “I have so many rivals; you see she’s already surrounded. There’s the Duke being introduced.”

“Then stay with me a little longer,” Madame Olenska said in a low tone, and just touched his knee with her plumed fan.

It was the lightest touch; and yet it thrilled him like a caress, like the brush of fingers while passing a warm cup—an intimacy too small to name and too vivid to mistake.

“Yes, let me stay,” he answered in the same tone, scarcely knowing what he said.

But at that moment Mr. van der Luyden came up, followed by old Mr. Urban Dagonet. The Countess greeted them with her grave smile, and Archer—feeling his host’s admonitory glance like a hand at his shoulder—rose and surrendered his seat.

Madame Olenska held out her hand as if to bid him goodbye. “Tomorrow, then, after five—I shall expect you,” she said; and then turned back to make room for Mr. Dagonet.

“Tomorrow—” Archer heard himself repeating. There had been no formal engagement; she had given no hint during their talk that she wished to see him again. Yet the words, once spoken, seemed to bind like a ribbon knotted too quickly to be untied without notice.

As he moved away he saw Lawrence Lefferts, tall and resplendent, leading his wife up to be introduced; and heard Gertrude Lefferts beam on the Countess with her large unperceiving smile. “But I think we used to go to dancing-school together when we were children—.”

Behind her, waiting their turn to name themselves, Archer noticed a number of the recalcitrant couples who had declined to meet the Countess at Mrs. Lovell Mingott’s. The lesson had been served, and now they came to swallow it politely. As Mrs. Archer had remarked: when the van der Luydens chose, they knew how to give a lesson. The wonder was that they chose so seldom.

Archer felt a touch on his arm and turned to see Mrs. van der Luyden looking down on him from the pure eminence of black velvet and family diamonds, as if she were the high altar itself.

“It was good of you, dear Newland, to devote yourself so unselfishly to Madame Olenska,” she said. “I told your cousin Henry he must really come to the rescue.”

He smiled at her vaguely, and she added, as if condescending to his natural shyness: “I’ve never seen May looking lovelier. The Duke thinks her the handsomest girl in the room.”
