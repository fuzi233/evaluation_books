"Let no flower of the spring pass by us; let us crown ourselves with rosebuds before they be withered." — *Book of Wisdom*.

It is a pity that Offendene was not the home of Miss Gwendolen Harleth's childhood, or a place endeared to her by family memories! A human life, I believe, should be firmly rooted in some corner of a native land. There, it may learn to love the face of the earth, the labors men undertake, the sounds and accents that haunt it, and whatever gives that early home an unmistakable familiarity amidst the widening scope of future knowledge. It is a spot where the definiteness of early memories becomes woven with affection, and where kindly acquaintance with all neighbors—even to dogs and donkeys—spreads not through sentimental effort, but as a sweet habit of the blood. At five years old, mortals are not yet prepared to be citizens of the world, nor stimulated by abstract nouns to soar above personal preference into impartiality. The blind prejudice in favor of milk with which we begin is merely a type of the way body and soul must be nourished, at least for a time. The best introduction to astronomy is to imagine the nightly heavens as a little lot of stars belonging to one's own homestead.

But this blessed persistence, in which affection can truly take root, had been absent from Gwendolen's life. It was only a year prior to her return from Leubronn that Offendene was chosen as her mother's home, selected simply for its proximity to Pennicote Rectory. On a late October afternoon, when rooks crowed loudly overhead and yellow elm leaves whirled in the wind, Mrs. Davilow, Gwendolen, and her four half-sisters drove along the avenue for the first time, accompanied by the governess and the maid in a separate vehicle.

The season suited the aspect of the old oblong red-brick house, which was rather too anxiously ornamented with stone at every line—not excepting the double row of narrow windows and the large square portico. The stone encouraged a greenish lichen, while the brick wore a powdery gray hue. Thus, although the building was rigidly rectangular, there was no harshness in its physiognomy as it faced the three avenues cutting through the hundred yards of old plantation encircling the immediate grounds. One would have wished the house to stand upon a knoll, allowing it to look beyond its own little domain to the long thatched roofs of distant villages, the church towers, the scattered homesteads, the gradual rise of surging woods, and the green breadths of undulating park that formed the beautiful face of the earth in that part of Wessex. Yet, standing behind a screen amid flat pastures, it possessed on one side a glimpse of the wider world in the lofty curves of the chalk downs, where grand, steadfast forms were played over by the changing days.

The house was just large enough to be called a mansion, moderately rented, lacking a manor attachment, and somewhat difficult to let due to its sombre furniture and faded upholstery. However, inside and out, it presented an image that no beholder could suppose to be inhabited by retired tradespeople—a certainty worth many conveniences to tenants who possessed the taste that shrinks from new finery and occupied that border-territory of rank where annexation is a burning topic. To take up residence in a house that had once sufficed for dowager countesses gave a perceptible tinge to Mrs. Davilow's satisfaction in finally having an establishment of her own. This arrangement appeared suddenly possible to Gwendolen upon the death of her stepfather, Captain Davilow, who had for the last nine years joined his family only in brief, fitful bursts, enough to reconcile them to his long absences. Yet, Gwendolen cared much more for the fact than for the explanation. All her prospects had become more agreeable in consequence. She had disliked their former way of life, roving from one foreign watering-place or Parisian apartment to another, always feeling new antipathies toward new suites of hired furniture and meeting new people under conditions that made her feel of little importance. The variation of spending two years at a showy school, where she was invariably placed foremost on all occasions of display, had only deepened her conviction that someone as exceptional as herself could hardly remain in ordinary circumstances or in a social position less than advantageous. Any fear of this latter evil was banished now that her mother was to have an establishment. Regarding her birth, Gwendolen was quite easy; she had no notion how her maternal grandfather acquired the fortune inherited by his two daughters, but he had been a West Indian, which seemed to exclude further questioning. She knew that her father's family was so high-born that it took no notice of her mother, who nevertheless preserved with much pride the miniature of a Lady Molly in that connection. She would probably have known much more about her father but for a little incident that occurred when she was twelve years old. Mrs. Davilow had brought out, as she did only at wide intervals, various memorials of her first husband. While showing his miniature to Gwendolen, she recalled with a fervor that seemed to count on a peculiar filial sympathy the fact that "dear papa" had died when his little daughter was in long clothes. Gwendolen, immediately thinking of the unlovable stepfather whom she had known for the greater part of her life while her frocks were short, said, "Why did you marry again, mamma? It would have been nicer if you had not." Mrs. Davilow colored deeply, a slight convulsive movement passing over her face, and straightway shutting up the memorials, she said, with a violence quite unusual in her, "You have no feeling, child!" Gwendolen, who was fond of her mother, felt hurt and ashamed and had never since dared to ask a question about her father.

This was not the only instance in which she had brought upon herself the pain of some filial compunction. It was always arranged, when possible, that she should have a small bed in her mother's room, for Mrs. Davilow's motherly tenderness clung chiefly to her eldest girl, who had been born in her happier time. One night, under an attack of pain, she found that the specific regularly placed by her bedside had been forgotten, and begged Gwendolen to get out of bed and reach it for her. That healthy young lady, snug and warm as a rosy infant in her little couch, objected to stepping out into the cold. Lying perfectly still, she grumbled a refusal. Mrs. Davilow went without the medicine and never reproached her daughter; but the next day, Gwendolen was keenly conscious of what must be in her mother's mind and tried to make amends by caresses which cost her no effort. Having always been the pet and pride of the household, waited upon by mother, sisters, governess, and maids as if she were a princess in exile, she naturally found it difficult to think her own pleasure less important than others made it. When it was positively thwarted, she felt an astonished resentment apt, in her cruder days, to vent itself in one of those passionate acts which look like a contradiction of habitual tendencies. Though never even as a child thoughtlessly cruel—nay, delighting to rescue drowning insects and watch their recovery—there remained a disagreeable silent remembrance of her having strangled her sister's canary-bird in a final fit of exasperation at its shrill singing, which had again and again jarringly interrupted her own. She had taken pains to buy a white mouse for her sister in retribution, and though inwardly excusing herself on the ground of a peculiar sensitiveness which was a mark of her general superiority, the thought of that infamous murder had always made her wince. Gwendolen's nature was not remorseless, but she liked to make her penances easy. Now that she was twenty and more, some of her native force had turned into a self-control by which she guarded herself from penitential humiliation. There was more show of fire and will in her than ever, but there was more calculation underneath it.

On this day of arrival at Offendene, a place neither Mrs. Davilow nor anyone else had seen before—it having been secured for her by her brother-in-law, Mr. Gascoigne—all had dismounted from the carriage and were standing under the porch in front of the open door. So they could have a general view of the place and a glimpse of the stone hall and staircase hung with sombre pictures, but enlivened by a bright wood fire, no one spoke. Mamma, the four sisters, and the governess all looked at Gwendolen, as if their feelings depended entirely on her decision. Of the girls, from Alice in her sixteenth year to Isabel in her tenth, hardly anything could be said on a first view save that they were girlish and that their black dresses were getting shabby. Miss Merry was elderly and altogether neutral in expression. Mrs. Davilow's worn beauty seemed the more pathetic for the look of entire appeal which she cast at Gwendolen, who was glancing round at the house, the landscape, and the entrance hall with an air of rapid judgment. Imagine a young racehorse in the paddock among untrimmed ponies and patient hacks.

"Well, dear, what do you think of the place?" said Mrs. Davilow at last, in a gentle, deprecatory tone.
