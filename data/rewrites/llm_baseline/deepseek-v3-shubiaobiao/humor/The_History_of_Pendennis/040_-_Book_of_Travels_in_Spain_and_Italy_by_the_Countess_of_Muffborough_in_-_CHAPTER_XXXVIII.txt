Morgan the valet might have been the only soul in London privy to the true state of Lady Clavering’s finances, but the rest of society remained blissfully deluded by the shimmering mirage of her supposed fortune. The city buzzed with tales of indigo plantations, opium clippers, and sacks of diamonds wrested from the grasp of indebted Indian princes. Her bank balance, it was whispered, stretched into such astronomical figures that even mathematicians gasped at the zeros. The legend of the *Begum Clavering* had taken root long before her ladyship’s carriage ever rolled into Grosvenor Place.

Enter Colonel Altamont—a man whose exploits were as dubious as his conversion to Islam and whose current mission in England involved the attempted sale of the Nawaub of Lucknow’s fabled nose-ring diamond, *The Light of the Dewan*. A more unlikely envoy could scarcely be imagined, yet here he was, mingling with London’s elite, his presence only adding to the mystique of the Clavering name.

Such was the magic of money. The British aristocracy, for all its pretensions of exclusivity, had a charming habit of rolling out the red carpet the moment wealth came knocking. Titles might be hereditary, but a full purse was the quickest path to acceptance. Dukes dined at your table. Earls’ sons courted your daughters. And if your past was a tad checkered? Well, a thick stack of banknotes had a way of smoothing over even the most scandalous wrinkles.

Sir Francis Clavering, alas, was a man who had spent his life skating on the thin ice of financial ruin. His pockets were perpetually empty, his creditors eternally impatient, and his only true talent lay in signing promissory notes he had no intention of honoring. Enter the Chevalier Strong—a man whose loyalty to Sir Francis was matched only by his talent for turning disaster into a mildly profitable farce. Strong had overseen the furnishing of the Claverings’ London residence with the gusto of a man spending his own money (which, in a roundabout way, he was).

The house itself was a masterpiece of bewildering extravagance. The dining room was a temple to medieval excess, complete with high-backed chairs that looked as though they’d been looted from a Gothic abbey. The library boasted leather-bound books (all uniformly sized for maximum aesthetic harmony) and busts of philosophers who would have wept at the thought of their likenesses presiding over Sir Francis’s naps.

But the drawing rooms—ah, the drawing rooms! Here was a carnival of gilded excess. Carpets so plush they swallowed footsteps whole. Chairs so delicate they seemed designed for fairies rather than flesh-and-blood guests. Porcelain shepherdesses simpered from every corner, while a pair of shrieking cockatoos provided a soundtrack of perpetual chaos. It was, in short, a monument to the very best bad taste money could buy.

Lady Clavering surveyed it all with cheerful bewilderment. "I don’t know why they’ve gone and made it look like a museum," she remarked, "unless it’s because me and Clavering are middle-aged relics ourselves!"

Blanche, meanwhile, winced at every misstep her mother made—whether linguistic or social. When Lady Clavering extended a warm (if grammatically mangled) invitation to Pendennis and his uncle for a "snug little dinner," Blanche’s expression suggested she was contemplating matricide.

Pen, for his part, found the whole affair deliciously entertaining. The Clavering household was a theater of the absurd, and he was more than happy to play audience. Blanche’s barbs, Lady Clavering’s blunders, Sir Francis’s palpable terror whenever the name *Altamont* was mentioned—it was better than a comedy at Drury Lane.

Major Pendennis, ever the strategist, saw only opportunity. "Marry money, my boy," he counseled as they strolled away. "Love in a cottage is all very poetic, but a well-stocked larder is far more sustaining."

Pen laughed. The game was afoot, and he was more than ready to play.
