Though Mr. Crisparkle and John Jasper passed each other daily beneath the Cathedral roof—meeting in the same flagged passages where the air always smelt faintly of damp stone and old incense—no word ever again, at any time, was exchanged between them that bore upon Edwin Drood, after that day—more than half a year gone—when Jasper had opened his diary in silence and shown the Minor Canon the cold conclusion, the resolved intention, written there like a judgment.

It could not be that they met so often without the thought of Edwin turning back upon them, each time, like a refrain. Nor could it be that they met without the uneasy sense—quick as the echo of a footstep in a transept—that the other kept a locked chamber in his mind. Jasper, publicly the denouncer and pursuer of Neville Landless; Mr. Crisparkle, steadfast advocate and protector of the accused; their paths crossed in a quiet opposition that should have bred questions. Yet neither broached the theme.

False pretence was not in Mr. Crisparkle. If he longed to revive the subject, he would have shown it. Jasper’s reticence, however, was a shut door with the bolt shot. Impassive, moody, solitary, living as if he kept his own company under arrest, he held himself to one idea and to the fixed purpose attendant on it, sharing it with no creature. That he continued to practise an Art which required him to be in exact mechanical unison with other men—voice with voice, breath with breath, the organ’s measured thunder answered by a choir’s disciplined rise—made it the more curious that his spirit kept moral time with nothing around him. He had confessed as much to his lost nephew long before the present iron stiffness had need to show itself.

That he must know of Rosa’s abrupt flight, and must divine the cause, could scarcely be doubted. Had he frightened her into silence? Or did he suppose she had confided—perhaps to Mr. Crisparkle himself—the particulars of that last interview? The Minor Canon could not settle it in his mind. He could not but admit, as a just man, that it was not in itself a crime to fall in love with Rosa, any more than it was a crime to offer to set love above revenge.

The dreadful suspicion of Jasper, which had entered Rosa’s imagination like a poisonous fume in a close room, found no harbour in Mr. Crisparkle’s. If it haunted Helena’s thoughts or Neville’s, neither gave it one spoken syllable. Mr. Grewgious made no pains to conceal his dislike of Jasper, yet never referred it, however distantly, to such a source. He was reticent as well as eccentric; and he never mentioned a certain evening when he had warmed his hands at the gatehouse fire and looked steadily down upon a heap of torn, miry clothes upon the floor—as if studying a recipe he could not read.

Drowsy Cloisterham, waking at intervals to reconsider a story six months old and already dismissed by the bench, divided itself, as it divided itself on everything, between three opinions: that Edwin had been killed by his treacherously passionate rival; or had died in open struggle; or had, for his own purposes, slipped away. Then it lifted its head to observe that bereaved Jasper still clung to discovery and revenge; and dozed off again. So the town’s mind lay, like bread dough left too long near a cold hearth—sometimes stirring, never rising.

The Cathedral doors close for the night; and the choir-master, on short leave for two or three services, turns his face towards London. He travels thither by the means by which Rosa travelled, and arrives, as Rosa arrived, on a hot dusty evening when the city’s breath tastes of grit and coal-smoke and over-used oil.

His baggage is small enough to carry in one hand. He goes on foot to a hybrid establishment in a little square behind Aldersgate Street, near the General Post Office—a place that is hotel, boarding-house, or lodging-house at the visitor’s option. It is one of those timid new enterprises, just beginning to spring up, that almost apologises for offering a fixed charge instead of inviting a traveller to order a pint of sweet blacking for drinking and throw it away. It insinuates you may black your boots rather than your stomach, and have bed, breakfast, attendance, and a porter up all night, for so much. From such premises many true Britons, in the lowest spirits, deduce that the times are levelling times—except in the high roads, soon to be none at all.

He eats without appetite, food lying dead on his tongue, and goes forth again. Eastward and still eastward through stale streets—where every alley has its own sour note, and every cheap gin-shop throws a thin music of clinking glasses and quarrelling voices into the air—he makes his way until he reaches his destination: a miserable court, specially miserable among many such.

He climbs a broken staircase whose steps complain under him, opens a door, looks into a dark stifling room, and says:

“Are you alone here?”

“Alone, deary; worse luck for me, and better for you,” croaks a voice from within. “Come in, come in, whoever you be. I can’t see you till I light a match, yet I seem to know the sound of your speaking. I’m acquainted with you, ain’t I?”

“Light your match, and try.”

“So I will, deary, so I will; but my hand that shakes, as I can’t lay it on a match all in a moment. And I cough so, that, put my matches where I may, I never find ’em there. They jump and start, as I cough and cough, like live things. Are you off a voyage, deary?”

“No.”

“Not seafaring?”

“No.”

“Well, there’s land customers and there’s water customers. I’m a mother to both. Different from Jack Chinaman t’other side the court. He ain’t a father to neither. It ain’t in him. And he ain’t got the true secret of mixing, though he charges as much as me that has, and more if he can get it. Here’s a match—and now where’s the candle? If my cough takes me, I shall cough out twenty matches afore I gets a light.”

She finds the candle and lights it before the cough comes on. It seizes her in the instant of success, and she sits rocking and gasping, the flame wagging in sympathy with her body:

“O, my lungs is awful bad! my lungs is wore away to cabbage-nets!”

While the fit lasts she has no power of sight, nor any other power not swallowed up in the struggle. As it leaves her, she strains her eyes; and as soon as she can get breath to words, she cries, staring hard:

“Why, it’s you!”

“Are you so surprised to see me?”

“I thought I never should have seen you again, deary. I thought you was dead, and gone to Heaven.”

“Why?”

“I didn’t suppose you could have kept away, alive, so long, from the poor old soul with the real receipt for mixing it. And you are in mourning too! Why didn’t you come and have a pipe or two of comfort? Did they leave you money, perhaps, and so you didn’t want comfort?”

“No.”

“Who was they as died, deary?”

“A relative.”

“Died of what, lovey?”

“Probably, Death.”

“We are short to-night!” she cries, with a propitiatory laugh that ends in a wheeze. “Short and snappish we are! But we’re out of sorts for want of a smoke. We’ve got the all-overs, haven’t us, deary? But this is the place to cure ’em in; this is the place where the all-overs is smoked off.”

“You may make ready, then,” he replies, “as soon as you like.”

He takes off his shoes, loosens his cravat, and lies across the foot of the squalid bed, his head resting on his left hand. The room holds the odour of old breath, damp rags, and the sweetish, resinous promise of the drug—like bruised poppy heads and burnt sugar.

“Now you begin to look like yourself,” says the woman, approvingly. “Now I begin to know my old customer indeed! Been trying to mix for yourself this long time, poppet?”

“I have been taking it now and then in my own way.”

“Never take it your own way. It ain’t good for trade, and it ain’t good for you. Where’s my ink-bottle, and where’s my thimble, and where’s my little spoon? He’s going to take it in a artful form now, my deary dear!”

Her hands go to work with a practised thrift: a pinch measured to the smallest grain, a moistening, a rolling, the bowl packed and tamped with the light pressure that lets the air draw right; then the faint spark nursed in the hollow of her palms until it blooms. She bubbles and blows like a cook coaxing a reluctant fire, speaking in snuffling satisfaction without stopping.

“I’ve got a pretty many smokes ready for you, first and last, haven’t I, chuckey?”

“A good many.”

“When you first come, you was quite new to it; warn’t ye?”

“Yes, I was easily disposed of, then.”

“But you got on in the world, and was able by-and-by to take your pipe with the best of ’em, warn’t ye?”

“Ah; and the worst.”

“It’s just ready for you. What a sweet singer you was when you first come! Used to drop your head, and sing yourself off like a bird! It’s ready for you now, deary.”

He takes it with great care, puts the mouthpiece to his lips. She sits beside him, ready to refill.

After a few whiffs in silence, he accosts her doubtfully, without looking:

“Is it as potent as it used to be?”

“What do you speak of, deary?”

“What should I speak of, but what I have in my mouth?”

“It’s just the same. Always the identical same.”

“It doesn’t taste so. And it’s slower.”

“You’ve got more used to it, you see.”

“That may be the cause, certainly. Look here.” He stops; grows dreamy; seems to forget he asked for her attention.

She bends and speaks in his ear, patient as a nurse and sharp as a watcher of ovens.

“I’m attending to you. Says you just now, Look here. Says I now, I’m attending to ye. We was talking just before of your being used to it.”

“I know all that. I was only thinking. Look here. Suppose you had something in your mind; something you were going to do.”

“Yes, deary; something I was going to do?”

“But had not quite determined to do.”

“Yes, deary.”

“Might or might not do, you understand.”

“Yes.” With the point of a needle she stirs the contents of the bowl.

“Should you do it in your fancy, when you were lying here doing this?”

She nods. “Over and over again.”

“Just like me! I did it over and over again. I have done it hundreds of thousands of times in this room.”

“It’s to be hoped it was pleasant to do, deary.”

“It was pleasant to do!”

The words come with a savage spring at her, as sudden as a dog’s snap. She is unmoved; she retouches the bowl with her little spatula, replenishes, presses, keeps the draught sweet and even. He sinks again.

“It was a journey, a difficult and dangerous journey. That was the subject in my mind. A hazardous and perilous journey, over abysses where a slip would be destruction. Look down, look down! You see what lies at the bottom there?”

He darts forward to point at the floor as if it were the edge of a chasm. She looks at his face close to hers, not at his finger. Whatever she knows of the drug’s rule, she knows the virtue of perfect quiet; he subsides again beneath it.

“Well; I have told you I did it here hundreds of thousands of times. What do I say? I did it millions and billions of times. I did it so often, and through such vast expanses of time, that when it was really done, it seemed not worth the doing, it was done so soon.”

“That’s the journey you have been away upon,” she remarks quietly.

He glares at her as he smokes; then his eyes grow filmy. “That’s the journey.”

Silence. His eyes close and open. The room’s small noises—his breath, her faint cough, the oily sigh of the wick—fill the place more than any conversation could. Outside, London’s night is never truly silent, but its roar comes here only as a dull, polluted surf.

“I’ll warrant,” she observes, when he has stared at her for some time with the strange look of seeing her far away, “I’ll warrant you made the journey in a many ways, when you made it so often?”

“No, always in one way.”

“Always in the same way?”

“Ay.”

“In the way in which it was really made at last?”

“Ay.”

“And always took the same pleasure in harping on it?”

“Ay.”

His answers are lazy monosyllables, as if she turned a crank and they dropped out. To test if he is more than an automaton, she reverses the question.

“Did you never get tired of it, deary, and try to call up something else for a change?”

He struggles into a sitting posture and retorts: “What do you mean? What did I want? What did I come for?”

She gently lays him back, revives the fire with her own breath before returning the pipe, and coaxes him with quick assent:

“Sure, sure, sure! Yes, yes, yes! Now I go along with you. You was too quick for me. I see now. You come o’ purpose to take the journey. Why, I might have known it, through its standing by you so.”

He answers first with a laugh, then with teeth set in passion: “Yes, I came on purpose. When I could not bear my life, I came to get the relief, and I got it. It WAS one! It WAS one!” The words repeated with a wolfish snarl.

She watches him cautiously, feeling her way for the next remark. It is:

“There was a fellow-traveller, deary.”

“Ha, ha, ha!” He breaks into a ringing laugh—more like a yell in that little room. “To think how often fellow-traveller, and yet not know it! To think how many times he went the journey, and never saw the road!”

She kneels on the floor, arms crossed on the coverlet close by him, chin upon them, crouching like a cat at a mouse-hole. The pipe is slipping from his mouth. She puts it back; lays her hand on his chest; moves him slightly from side to side. On that touch he speaks, as if she had spoken.

“Yes! I always made the journey first, before the changes of colours and the great landscapes and glittering processions began. They couldn’t begin till it was off my mind. I had no room till then for anything else.”

Once more silence. Once more her hand on his chest, stirring him a little as if to keep him on the boil; once more his voice, answering her motion.

“What? I told you so. When it comes to be real at last, it is so short that it seems unreal for the first time. Hark!”

“Yes, deary. I’m listening.”

“Time and place are both at hand.”

He is on his feet now, whispering as if in darkness though the candle burns—whispering as men do when they fear to wake something that listens.

“Time, place, and fellow-traveller,” she suggests, adopting his tone and holding him softly by the arm.

“How could the time be at hand unless the fellow-traveller was? Hush! The journey’s made. It’s over.”

“So soon?”

“That’s what I said to you. So soon. Wait a little. This is a vision. I shall sleep it off. It has been too short and easy. I must have a better vision than this; this is the poorest of all. No struggle, no consciousness of peril, no entreaty—and yet I never saw that before.” He starts.

“Saw what, deary?”

“Look at it! Look what a poor, mean, miserable thing it is! That must be real. It’s over.”

His hands make wild, unmeaning gestures; then his will trails off into stupor, and he lies like a log.

But the woman is still inquisitive. She repeats her cat-like action: stirs him slightly and listens; stirs and listens; whispers to him and listens. Finding him past rousing for the time, she slowly rises, disappointed, and flicks his face with the back of her hand as she turns away.

Yet she does not go far—only to the chair by the hearth. There she sits, elbow on the arm, chin on hand, unwinking and intent. Under her breath, in a croak shaped by old resentments and sharp ears, she mutters:

“I heard ye say once. I heard ye say once, when I was lying where you’re lying, and you were making your speculations upon me, ‘Unintelligible!’ I heard you say so, of two more than me. But don’t ye be too sure always; don’t be ye too sure, beauty!”

After a while she adds, with the same intent stare: “Not so potent as it once was? Ah! Perhaps not at first. You may be more right there. Practice makes perfect. I may have learned the secret how to make ye talk, deary.”

Whether he talks more or no, he does not now. He twitches at times—face and limbs—ugly, heavy, silent. The wretched candle burns down. She takes the expiring end between her fingers, lights another by it, and crams the guttering frying morsel deep into the candlestick, ramming it home with the new candle as if loading some ill-smelling weapon of witchcraft. The new candle burns down in turn. Still he lies insensible. At length she blows out what remains, and daylight looks into the room.

It has not looked in very long when he sits up, chilled and shaking, slowly gathers where he is, and makes himself ready to depart. The woman receives what he pays her with a grateful:

“Bless ye, bless ye, deary!”

and seems, tired out, to prepare for sleep as he leaves.

But seeming may be false. It is false here; for the moment the stairs cease creaking under his tread, she glides after him, muttering with emphasis:

“I’ll not miss ye twice!”

There is no egress from the court but by its entrance. With a weird peep from the doorway she watches for his looking back. He does not look back before disappearing with a wavering step. She follows, peeps from the court again, sees him still faltering on without turning, and holds him in view.

He goes to the back of Aldersgate Street. A door opens at his knock. She crouches in another doorway watching that one, understanding easily that he has put up there. Her patience is unexhausted by hours. For sustenance she buys bread within a hundred yards, and milk as it is carried past—cheap, thin, but cool—taking it in sips that do not interrupt her listening to the street’s changing voice.

At noon he comes forth again, changed in dress, carrying nothing, having nothing carried for him. He is not returning to the country yet. She follows a little way, hesitates, then turns with instantaneous confidence and goes straight into the house he has left.

“Is the gentleman from Cloisterham indoors?”

“Just gone out.”

“Unlucky. When does the gentleman return to Cloisterham?”

“At six this evening.”

“Bless ye and thank ye. May the Lord prosper a business where a civil question, even from a poor soul, is so civilly answered!”

In the street, less civilly, she repeats: “I’ll not miss ye twice! I lost ye last, where that omnibus you got into nigh your journey’s end plied betwixt the station and the place. I wasn’t so much as certain that you even went right on to the place. Now I know ye did. My gentleman from Cloisterham, I’ll be there before ye, and bide your coming. I’ve swore my oath that I’ll not miss ye twice!”

Accordingly, that same evening she stands in Cloisterham High Street, looking up at the quaint gables of the Nuns’ House and passing the time as she best can until nine o’clock—when she has reason to suppose the arriving omnibus passengers may interest her. The friendly darkness at that hour makes it easy to see without being seen; and it is so, for the passenger not to be missed twice arrives among the rest.

“Now let me see what becomes of you. Go on!”

It is addressed to the air; yet it might be addressed to him, so obediently does he go on down the High Street until he comes to an arched gateway at which he unexpectedly vanishes. She quickens. She is close upon him entering under the arch, but sees only a postern staircase on one side, and on the other an ancient vaulted room open to the thoroughfare, where a large-headed gray-haired gentleman writes at a desk in full view of all who pass, eyeing them as if toll-taker, though the way is free.

“Halloa!” he cries in a low voice, seeing her brought to a standstill. “Who are you looking for?”

“There was a gentleman passed in here this minute, sir.”

“Of course there was. What do you want with him?”

“Where do he live, deary?”

“Live? Up that staircase.”

“Bless ye! Whisper. What’s his name, deary?”

“Surname Jasper, Christian name John. Mr. John Jasper.”

“Has he a calling, good gentleman?”

“Calling? Yes. Sings in the choir.”

“In the spire?”

“Choir.”

“What’s that?”

Mr. Datchery rises from his papers and comes to his doorstep. “Do you know what a cathedral is?” he asks, jocosely.

The woman nods.

“What is it?”

She looks puzzled, hunting for a definition among the scraps of words she owns, as if the right name has been worn away by too many mouths. Then, deciding it is easier to point than to translate, she indicates the huge substantial object itself—massive against the dark-blue sky and the early stars.

“That’s the answer,” says Mr. Datchery. “Go in there at seven to-morrow morning, and you may see Mr. John Jasper, and hear him too.”

“Thank ye! Thank ye!”

Her burst of triumph does not escape the notice of the single buffer of easy temper living idly on his means. He glances at her, clasps his hands behind him—buffer-fashion—and lounges along the echoing Precincts at her side. Under their feet the stones give back every step, and the old place holds its own quiet, as if defending itself from the world’s racket.

“Or,” he suggests, with a backward hitch of his head, “you can go up at once to Mr. Jasper’s rooms there.”

She eyes him with a cunning smile and shakes her head.

“O! you don’t want to speak to him?”

She repeats her dumb reply and shapes with her lips a soundless “No.”

“You can admire him at a distance three times a day, whenever you like. It’s a long way to come for that, though.”

She looks up quickly. If Mr. Datchery thinks to draw out where she comes from, he is of a much easier temper than she is; and she seems to acquit him of any such art, as he lounges on like the chartered bore of the city, uncovered gray hair blowing about, purposeless hands rattling the loose money in his trouser pockets.

That chink has an attraction for her greedy ears. “Wouldn’t you help me to pay for my traveller’s lodging, dear gentleman, and to pay my way along? I am a poor soul, I am indeed, and troubled with a grievous cough.”

“You know the travellers’ lodging, I perceive, and are making directly for it,” is Mr. Datchery’s bland comment, still rattling. “Been here often, my good woman?”

“Once in all my life.”

“Ay, ay?”

They have reached the entrance to the Monks’ Vineyard. The sight of the place wakes an appropriate remembrance in her mind, as if a landmark had kept the story salted and preserved.

“By this token,” she says energetically, “though you mayn’t believe it, that a young gentleman gave me three-and-sixpence as I was coughing my breath away on this very grass. I asked him for three-and-sixpence, and he gave it me.”

“Wasn’t it a little cool to name your sum?” hints Mr. Datchery, still rattling. “Isn’t it customary to leave the amount open? Mightn’t it have had the appearance, to the young gentleman—only the appearance—that he was rather dictated to?”

“Look’ee here, deary,” she replies, in a confidential, persuasive tone, “I wanted the money to lay it out on a medicine as does me good, and as I deal in. I told the young gentleman so, and he gave it me, and I laid it out honest to the last brass farden. I want to lay out the same sum in the same way now; and if you’ll give it me, I’ll lay it out honest to the last brass farden again, upon my soul!”

“What’s the medicine?”

“I’ll be honest with you beforehand, as well as after. It’s opium.”

Mr. Datchery, with a sudden change of countenance, gives her a sudden look.
