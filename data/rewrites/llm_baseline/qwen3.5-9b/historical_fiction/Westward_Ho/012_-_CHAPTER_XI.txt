"Go, misguided, rash, and intrusive fool! Farewell! For thou hast seen that to be too busy brings peril."
—*Hamlet*

It was the spring of 1582–83. The grey skies of March hung heavy and curdled high above the jagged black peaks of the mountains. Below, a keen, dry wind swept relentlessly across a desolate expanse of bog, still stained red and yellow by the lingering frost of winter. Only one brown hill broke the monotony of the waste. Upon it, a few leafless oaks, stripped by the wind, stretched their moss-covered branches like the hairy arms of giants, looming over a desolate pool that shivered and cracked in the biting chill. From the water's edge rose a mournful cry, drifting down faint and erratic amidst the wind's howling.

Along the bog's edge, navigating through crumbling rocks and spongy green springs, a company of English soldiers pressed forward. Clad fully in helmets and quilted jerkins, carrying arquebuses on their shoulders and pikes trailing behind, they were stern and steady men. Two years prior, they had manned the guns at Fort Smerwick; since then, they had witnessed countless bloody skirmishes and faced more before their deaths.

Two captains rode ahead on shaggy ponies. The taller wore armor stained and rusted by storms and battle. Beside him rode a striking contrast: a man in a brilliant, inlaid cuirass and helmet, adorned with a gaudy sash and plume, his sword hilt glittering with gold. Yet, his magnificent attire seemed absurd upon the meager, scrawny horse that carried him. Secured by a rope tied to a pikeman's wrist trotted a bare-legged Irish kern. His only garment was a ragged yellow mantle, and atop his unkempt "glib" of hair, his eyes darted left and right with a mixture of fear and sullen defiance. He served as the company's guide in their hunt for the rebel Baltinglas; woe betide him if he betrayed them.

"A pleasant country, truly, Captain Raleigh," remarked the duller officer to the flamboyant one. "I marvel how, having once escaped this place to Whitehall, you possess the courage to return and soil that fine suit with bog-water and mud."

"A very pleasant country, my friend Amyas; what you speak in jest, I speak in earnest."

"Hillo! Our tastes seem to have swapped. I am already sick of it, just as you predicted. Oh, that I could hear word of some adventure westward-bound and swing in a hammock once more! Pray, what has suddenly made you so enamored of bog and rock, bringing you back to tramp them with us? I thought you had long since spied out the nakedness of this land."

"Bog and rock? The nakedness of the land? All that is required here is prudence, skill, justice, and law. Look at this soil—it is fertile enough if men were here to till it. These rocks—who knows what minerals they conceal? I hear of gold and jewels already found in various parts. My brother Humphrey's German assayer, Daniel, assures me these stones are identical to those yielding silver in Peru. Tut, man! If Her Gracious Majesty would but grant me a few square miles of this wilderness, by God's help, in seven years' time I would make it bloom like a rose."

"Humph! Then I should be inclined to stay."

"So you shall, and become my agent, if you will, to collect my mine-rents, corn-rents, and fishery-rents, eh? Could you keep accounts, old Knight of the Bear's Paw?"

"Well enough for such brief reckonings as yours would allow, on the profit side at least. No, no—I would sooner carry lime from Cauldy to Bideford for the rest of my days than spend another year in the land of Ireland, among the children of wrath. There is surely a curse upon the face of this earth."

"There is no curse save the ancient one of man's sin: 'Thorns and thistles it shall bring forth unto thee.' But if you root up the thorns and briars, Amyas, I know no fiend who can prevent you from growing wheat. If you till the ground like a man, you plough and barrow away nature's curse, and the fables of the schoolmen besides," he added, speaking in that bold manner that later earned him—and no other devout Christian less deserved—the charge of atheism.

"It is sword and bullet, I think, that are needed here before plough and harrow to clear away some of the curse. Until a few more of these Irish lords are removed to join the Desmonds, there is no peace for Ireland."

"Humph! Not entirely wrong, I fear. And yet... Irish lords? These very traitors possess better English blood than we who hunt them down. When Yeo slew the Desmond the other day, he shed no more Irish blood than if he had struck down the Lord Deputy himself."

"His blood be on his own head," said Yeo. "He looked as wild a savage as the worst of them, more shame to him. The ancient here nearly cut off his arm before he revealed his identity. And then, your worship, having a price on his head and near bleeding to death..."

"Enough, enough, good fellow," said Raleigh. "Thou hast done what was given thee to do. Strange, Amyas, is it not? Noble Normans sunk into savages—*Hibernis ipsis hiberniores!* Is there some uncivilizing venom in the air?"

"Some venom, at least, which turns Englishmen traitors. But the Irish themselves are well enough, if their tyrants would let them be. Consider the Inchiquin, who they say is Prince of Thomond and should be King of all Ireland if every man had his due. What more faithful liege-man does Her Majesty possess?"

"Don't speak of rights in a land of wrongs, man. But the Inchiquin knows well that the true Irish Esau has no greater enemy than his usurper, the Norman Jacob. And yet, Amyas, are these men worse than we might be, if we had been raised as masters over the bodies and souls of men in some distant land where law and order never arrived? Look at this Desmond: brought up a savage among savages, a Papist among Papists, a despot among slaves. A thousand easy maidens deem it honor to serve his pleasure; a thousand wild ruffians deem it piety to fulfill his revenge. Let him who is without sin among us cast the first stone."

"Yes," Raleigh muttered to himself as the conversation lapsed. "What would *you* have been, Raleigh, had you been that Desmond whose lands you now covet? What will *you* be when you possess them? Will your children sink downward as these noble barons did? Will the genius of tyranny and falsehood find soil within your heart to grow and ripen fruit? What guarantee do you have for doing better here than those who went before? And yet, cannot I practice justice and show mercy? Can I not establish plantations, build, sow, and make the desert valleys laugh with corn? Shall I not have my Spenser with me to fill me with noble thoughts and raise my soul to his heroic height? Is this not true knight-errantry—to redeem to peace and utility, and to the glory of that glorious Queen whom God has given me, a generous soil and a more generous race? Trustful and tender-hearted they are—none more; and if they are fickle and passionate, will not that very softness of temper, which leads them so easily to evil, also make them easy to lead toward good? Yes—here, away from courts, among a people who should bless me as their benefactor and deliverer... what golden days might be mine! And yet—is this merely another angelic mask from that same cunning fiend, Ambition's stage? Will my house truly be the House of God, founded on loyalty and bulwarked by righteousness, or the House of Fame, whose walls are soap bubbles and whose floor is a sea of glass mingled with fire? I would be good and great. When will the day come when I shall be content to be good, yet not great, like this simple Leigh toiling by my side to do his duty, caring no more for tomorrow than the birds of God? Greatness? I have tasted that cup within the last twelve months; do I not know it is sweet in the mouth but bitter in the belly? Greatness? Was not Essex great? Was not John of Austria great? Was not Desmond great, whose race, but three short years ago, stood for ages higher than I shall ever hope to climb—castles, lands, thousands of slaves, and five hundred gentlemen of his name who vowed to forswear God before they forswore Him, and well have they kept their vow! And now, dead in a turf-hovel, like a rabbit in a burrow!"

"Leigh, what noise was that?"

"An Irish howl, I fancied; but it came from off the bog. It may be only a plover's cry."

"Something not quite right, sir captain, to my mind," said the veteran. "Here in these parts, they tell ugly stories of pucks and banshees, and such ghosts. There it was again, wailing just like a woman. They say the banshee cried all night before Desmond was slain."
