What utility does a Colonel possess without a regiment to command? The Governor and Council, who had so pompously celebrated the endowment of my commission, were nowhere to be seen. They had fled to ships, engaging in sporadic piracy and arson from the deck, leaving the shores to chaos. Lord Dunmore's black auxiliaries had driven away those of his own blood, while rumors spread that envoys had been dispatched to Southern tribes, promising thousands of warriors to bring our people to "good behavior" via the tomahawk. "And these are to be our allies!" I murmured to my mother, exchanging a look of grim foreboding. My mind flashed back with ghastly clarity to the savage whose face had glared over mine, the knife pressed to my throat before Florac struck him down at Braddock's Field. We reinforced Castlewood with whatever defenses we could devise, yet our terror lay not in the rebels, but in the painted heroes and the freed slaves. I had never witnessed my mother lose her composure save once—when recounting her father's death in an Indian raid decades prior. Yet, when shadowy figures materialized before our house at night, she could not be persuaded otherwise; she knelt, weeping, "The Lord have mercy! The Indians—the Indians!"

Lord Dunmore's negro allies eventually scattered aboard his fleeing vessels or vanished into the fray for pay and plunder, while the promised Southern warriors never materialized. Still, I gazed upon my mother's grey hair, my wife's brown locks, and our child's golden curls with a terrible pang of doubt, wondering if these innocents would become victims of the very war we helped ignite. Had I not dared to interpret Providence, I might claim that employing these Indians and German mercenaries brought swift retribution. On the field, the mercenaries crumbled before the Provincials, fueling the Continental spirit through their defeat; meanwhile, the slaughter of Miss McCrea by a handful of drunkards inflicted far more damage to the Royal cause than the loss of battles or entire regiments.

Once the Indian panic subsided, Madam Esmond's courage returned, replaced by a serious unease regarding the dangers I courted. "What harm can they do me, a poor woman?" she argued. "If I have one son, a Colonel without a regiment, I have another leading two hundred Continentals in Mr. Washington's camp. If the Royalists come, they will spare me for your sake; if the rebels appear, Harry's passport will suffice. I cannot stand for your delicate wife and this baby to remain here, increasing our peril! We must flee to Boston or New York. Do not speak of defending me! Who would harm a harmless old crone? If the rebels arrive, I shall hide behind Mrs. Fanny's petticoats and feel safer outside the house than within." She spoke partly for reason, but mostly because she felt compelled to remain in the soil where her father slept and where she was born. She lived backward, blessfully sending off the new generation while clinging to the past, to old days and fading memories.

As we debated an escape to Boston, news arrived that the British had evacuated the ill-fated city of Boston entirely. They had never ventured to attack Washington at Cambridge, despite the Continental army lying there for months without powder, content to wait until he secured munitions and seized Dorchester Heights. From there, the entire British force and colonial garrison were forced into a humiliating retreat. While the British victory at Bunker's Hill was undeniable, akin to Blenheim, their commanders thereafter seemed paralyzed by a fear of assaulting entrenched Continentals. Why the hesitation from July to March? Why the timidity at Long Island, when the Continental army lay in our grasp? Why the paralysis before Valley Forge, where starving, ragged remnants barely manned lines against a vast, victorious foe?

With the shifting tides of hope and fear, the tone of the partisans altered dramatically. Upon hearing of Boston's evacuation, every local Whig bowed to Madam Esmond, urging immediate submission. Though she no longer flaunted her loyalty openly, she never wavered. Each night and morning, the poor Hagan prayed for the Royal Family within our walls, and on Sundays, neighbors attended services where my mother, acting as a fervent clerk, delivered a stately prayer for the High Court of Parliament under our most religious and gracious King. Brave Hagan, a parson without a living, stood as I did, a Militia Colonel without a regiment. He had continued praying for King George in Williamsburg long after the Governor fled, but returning one Sunday, he found a corporal's guard blocking the church doors. Told that the Committee of Safety had installed another divine, he demanded to be "led before their chiefs." Thus, escorted by a chorus of white and black scoundrels, he was marched to the Capitol. Confronting the new magistrates, including Mr. Henry, Hagan intended to deliver an heroic speech, perhaps even accept martyrdom. But Mr. Henry, impatient, interrupted him. "I am too busy to listen to speeches. As for King George, he has no more authority here than King Nebuchadnezzar. Stick to King John and King Macbeth! Send around your tickets, and the Assembly shall hear you." Turning to General Lee, Washington's second-in-command, Henry dismissed the bewildered Hagan, who left reddening and near tears. Consequently, we withdrew the worthy pastor from the restless flock in the city, confining his ministrations to our rural sanctuary.

Though the selection of Virginians for high office in the new government bribed and flattered many leaders—who might otherwise have remained faithful to the Crown—the faithful Loyalists kept intelligences flowing underground. Even when gagged and muzzled, when the Capitol fell to the Whigs and our loyal levies proved as ineffective as Falstaff's regiments, we maintained a network of trust. Some ran away, others feigned republicanism before committees, vowing perdition to kings, yet beneath the surface, we knew one another. Depending on the shifting winds, we concealed or displayed our colors, sometimes apostatizing temporarily to cry "Down with King George!" Negroes carried messages and tokens from house to house, weaving endless plots in the shadows. When the battle turned, neutrals joined the winners, shouting as loudly as the patriots. The runaways were uncounted. Could anyone deny that the signers and ardent supporters of Independence were a minority, yet it was the minority that prevailed? Knowing that a fragment of the defeated Massachusetts army planned a crucial southern expedition, I sought to join forces with the former Governor Martin of North Carolina, bringing three or four Virginian gentlemen and officers to bolster his corps, lacking only privates.

We departed Castlewood with little secrecy; the affairs of Congress were not yet robust enough to enforce tyranny upon those of doubtful allegiance. Gentlemen's houses remained open, and in our southern tradition, we visited friends for months at a stretch. My wife, I, our infant, and a retinue of servants took leave of Madam Esmond, journeying first to a neighboring plantation, then to another friend's estate, until finally reaching Wilmington, North Carolina, where we awaited the succors destined for us. Before our arrival, our brother Royalists from Carolina had appeared in force, though their encounters with the Whigs had been disastrous. The poor Highlanders, no more fortunate in this contest for King George than when they drew swords against him in Scotland, met a similar fate. We did not reach Wilmington until late May, finding Admiral Parker's squadron there, carrying General Clinton and five British regiments, preparing for a descent upon Charleston.

General Clinton, to whom I introduced myself, initially greeted me with grim reserve, noting that my "regiment" consisted of Lady Warrington, our infant, and three negro servants. However, Captain Horner of the *Sphinx*, who had served kindly under Governor Dunmore in Jamaica, warmly embraced Lady Warrington upon learning she was General Lambert's daughter, offering her his finest cabin. Thus, like Lord Dunmore, we became refugees who had waved our flag, pocketed it, and slipped out the back door. Bearing swiftly toward Charleston, I grew intimate with Mr. Clinton during the voyage and our delay in the river. It was the King's birthday when we entered the harbor, a day we deemed glorious for the commencement of the expedition.

The operation did not commence for several days, and I omit the description of Penelope parting from Hector, for Hector remains sound (save for a slight gout), and no rascal Pyrrhus has claimed his widow. In times of war, such scenes of woe occur hourly. I can still see her gentle face over the bulwarks as we descended into the boats, the smile of the infant on her arm. Captain Miles, lacking poetic taste, likely forgot the verses in Pope's Homer describing such a parting, yet my mother often recited them to me as a boy and keeps the gorge I wore that day among her trunks.

My second venture at fighting proved no luckier than the first. We returned to our ships that evening thoroughly beaten. The reckless Lee, whom Clinton had faced at Boston, now met him at Charleston, delivering a brilliant and successful resistance. The fort on Sullivan's Island was a nut we could not crack. Our frigates' fire failed to breach its shell; the passage for our assault was unfordable, trapping Sir Henry Clinton, who was always the first to charge, as he waded into the deadly current. Death by shot, drowning, and exposure to the chill air—I had braved these perils before returning to my wife. Our frigate, grounded for a time, was cannonaded by the enemy until she managed to free herself.
