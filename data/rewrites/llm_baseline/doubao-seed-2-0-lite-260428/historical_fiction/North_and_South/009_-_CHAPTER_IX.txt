“Let China’s earth, enriched with coloured stains,
Pencil’d with gold, and streaked with azure veins,
The grateful flavour of the Indian leaf,
Or Mocha’s sunburnt berry glad receive.”

The day after their meeting with Higgins and his daughter, Mr. Hale drifted up to the small upstairs drawing room at an unaccustomed hour. He lingered by the mantelpiece, plucking at the edge of a shell ornament, then running a finger along the spines of the few books stacked on the side table, as if he were carefully inspecting every object in the room. Margaret saw at once it was no more than a nervous tic: a way of staving off something he longed to say, yet feared to voice. At last, the words came out in a soft rush:
“My dear! I’ve asked Mr. Thornton to come to tea tonight.”

Mrs. Hale was leaned back in her plush easy chair, eyes half-shut, the faint, habitual line of pain that had settled on her face these last months deepening. At her husband’s announcement she roused at once, sharp and querulous.
“Mr. Thornton! And tonight? What on earth is the man thinking of, dropping by unannounced like that? And Dixon is mid-wash of all my muslins and laces, and these horrid east winds have turned all the water hard—no soft water anywhere, I suppose, and we shall be stuck with this wretched Milton wind all year round.”
“The wind’s veering round, my dear,” Mr. Hale said, glancing out the window at the plumes of factory smoke drifting steadily due east. He had not yet learned to read Milton’s compass, and adjusted the points to suit whatever pleasant fiction he wanted to believe at the time.
“Don’t tell me such nonsense!” Mrs. Hale said, shuddering and pulling her shawl tighter around her shoulders. “East or west, this man is coming, I suppose.”

“Oh mamma, you’ve never met Mr. Thornton, or you’d know he’s exactly the sort of man who delights in fighting every adverse thing he meets—enemies, gales, inconvenient arrangements. The harder it blows and rains, the surer he is to show up. I’ll go help Dixon, never you fear. I’ve got quite a knack for clear-starching these days. And he won’t want any fancy amusements, he’ll only want to talk to Papa. Papa, I’ve been dying to meet the Pythias to your Damon! You know I only saw him once before, and we were both so awkward and flustered we had nothing sensible to say to each other, so we didn’t get on at all well.”
“I’m not sure you’ll ever take to him, Margaret. He is not the sort of man who fusses over ladies, that’s all.”
Margaret curved her throat in a little, scornful half-smile. “I don’t care overmuch for men who make a profession of being agreeable to every lady they meet, papa. Mr. Thornton is your friend—the only person in Milton who properly appreciates your work.”
“The only decent person in Milton, at all,” Mrs. Hale put in.
“So we’ll give him a proper welcome, and make coconut cakes. Dixon will be so flattered if we ask her to whip up a batch, and I’ll iron all your lace caps for you, mamma, before he arrives.”

Margaret found herself wishing Mr. Thornton a hundred miles away a dozen times that morning. She had planned a quiet, pleasant day: a long letter to Edith back in the south, a stretch of reading Dante, a visit to the Higgins household to check on Bessy. Instead, she stood bent over the ironing board, pressing linen until her arms ached, listening to Dixon rail at the hard water that had spotted her best lace, at the smoke that drifted in through the scullery window and grimed the clean muslin. She had to pour on extra sympathy to keep Dixon from carrying her litany of grievances straight up to Mrs. Hale, who was already too unwell to be fretted. Every few minutes she had to remind herself of her father’s quiet fondness for his new friend, to beat back the rising irritation and chase away the threat of the throbbing headache that had begun to plague her lately. When she finally set the iron down, she was so tired she could barely speak, and joked to her mother that she was no longer Peggy the laundry maid, but Margaret Hale the lady. To her dismay, Mrs. Hale took the jest completely seriously.
“Yes! If anyone had told me, when I was Miss Beresford, one of the belles of the whole county, that my own daughter would stand half a day in a poky little scullery working like a common servant, just so we could properly receive a tradesman—and that this tradesman would be the only person in the whole town who treats us with any kindness—”
“Oh mamma!” Margaret said, lifting her head, “don’t scold me for a silly careless joke. I don’t mind ironing, or any sort of work for you and papa. I was born and bred a lady, and that doesn’t vanish the moment I have to scrub a floor or wash dishes. I’m just tired for a little while, that’s all. In half an hour I’d do it all over again, no complaint. And as for Mr. Thornton being in trade, poor fellow, he can’t help that. I doubt his education ever left him any other path to take, out here in Milton.” She walked slowly off to her own bedroom after that, too frayed to bear any more cross words just then.

Across the town, in the Thorntons’ solid, substantial home, a similar but far stiffer scene was unfolding that very same hour. A large-boned woman long past middle age sat at work in the grim, richly furnished dining room. Her features, like her broad frame, were strong and unyielding, not heavy or soft. Her face shifted slowly from one firm, decided expression to another, no faint or wavering emotion ever crossing it. Passersby on the street could not help turning their heads for a second glance at her when she walked past: the firm, severe, dignified woman who never stepped aside for small courtesies, never deviated from her straight, steady path toward whatever clear, defined goal she had set for herself. She was dressed in stout black silk, every thread intact, no spot or fade anywhere on the fabric. She was mending a vast, fine linen tablecloth, holding it up to the light every few minutes to spot the thin worn patches that required her careful, delicate stitches. There were almost no books in the room, save for the six volumes of Matthew Henry’s Bible commentaries stacked in the center of the massive sideboard, flanked by a polished silver tea urn on one side and a brass oil lamp on the other. From a distant back room, the tinny trill of a piano drifted through the door: someone was rushing through a flashy salon piece, playing so fast that every third note was indistinct or missed entirely, and the loud crashing chords at the end were half wrong, though the performer was perfectly satisfied with their work.
A step as firm and decisive as her own passed the dining room door.
“John! Is that you?”
Her son pushed the door open, and stepped inside.
“What on earth brings you home so early? I thought you were going to tea at that Mr. Hale’s, the friend of Mr. Bell’s.”
“So I am, mother. I’ve come home to dress for it.”
“Dress! Humph! When I was a girl, young men thought dressing once a day was more than enough. Why on earth do you need to dress up just to drink a cup of tea with an old country parson?”
“Mr. Hale is a gentleman. His wife and daughter are ladies.”
“Wife and daughter? Do they work as teachers too? What do they do with their days? You never mentioned them before.”
“No, mother, because I’ve never even met Mrs. Hale. I only spoke to Miss Hale for half an hour once, some weeks back.”
“Mind you don’t get tangled up by some penniless girl looking to catch a rich husband, John.”
“I am not easily caught, as you well know,” he said, a sharp edge to his voice. “And I will not have Miss Hale spoken of in that light—you know that offends me. I have never seen a young woman show the slightest interest in me for my money, and I do not believe any of them would waste their time trying to snare me.”
Mrs. Thornton was not about to cede the point. “Well, I’m just saying take care. Maybe the Milton girls have too much pride to go angling after husbands, but this Miss Hale comes down from those soft southern aristocratic counties, where, if all the stories are true, any man with a decent fortune is treated like a prize to be won.”
Mr. Thornton’s brow tightened, and he stepped a little further into the room. “Mother,” he said, with a short, sharp, scornful laugh, “you will force me to confess. The only time I met Miss Hale, she treated me with such haughty, distant courtesy it reeked of contempt. She held herself apart from me as if she were a queen, and I some ragged, unwashed vassal of hers. You have nothing to worry about, rest easy.”
“Easy? I am not easy, and I am far from content! Some renegade clergyman’s daughter, daring to turn her nose up at you! If I were you, I would not dress an inch for any of that saucy set.”
As he left the room, he paused to say: “Mr. Hale is kind, and gentle, and far more learned than any man you will meet in Milton. He is not saucy. I will tell you what Mrs. Hale is like, if you care to hear, when I get back tonight.”
He shut the door behind him, and was gone.
“Dare despise my son! Dare treat him like some low vassal, indeed!” Mrs. Thornton muttered, stabbing her needle hard through the linen tablecloth. “Humph! I’d like to see her find half as good a man anywhere in this country. Boy and man, he is the noblest, stoutest heart I have ever known. I am his mother, but I am not blind to what is right and wrong, not like some folk. I know what Fanny is, with her silly little vanities, and I know what John is. Despise him! I hate that girl.”
