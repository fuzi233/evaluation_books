"Oh—oh—how do you spell it, anyhow?" asked the sharp-tongued young lady to whom Archer had slid his wife's telegram across the brass ledge of the Western Union office.

"Olenska," he repeated, pulling the paper back to align the foreign syllables above May's sprawling script. "O-len-ska."

"It's an improbable name for a New York telegraph office; certainly not in this neighborhood," an unexpected voice remarked. Turning, Archer found Lawrence Lefferts standing at his elbow, tugging absently at an immaculate mustache and pretending not to look at the message.

"Hallo, Newland. Thought I'd catch you here. I just heard about old Mrs. Mingott's stroke. Since I was heading to the house anyway, I spotted you turning down this street and followed. Did you come straight from there?"

Archer nodded and tucked the telegram beneath the lattice. "Very serious, I fear?"

Lefferts continued, feigning casual concern. "Sending a wire to the family, I assume? I gather it *is* grave, if you're including the Countess Olenska."

Archer's jaw tightened. A savage urge surged within him to drive his fist into the long, vain, handsome face beside him.

"Why?" he demanded.

Lefferts, a man known for avoiding controversy, raised his eyebrows in an ironic grimace that served as a warning regarding the young woman watching from behind the counter. Nothing could be considered worse "form" than displaying temper in public. Archer had never been more indifferent to social conventions, yet his impulse to physically harm Lawrence Lefferts lasted only a heartbeat. The notion of debating Ellen Olenska's situation with Lefferts, under any circumstances, was utterly unthinkable.

He paid for the telegram, and the two men stepped out into the street. Once his composure returned, Archer said, "Mrs. Mingott is much improved; the doctor reports no anxiety whatsoever."

Lefferts responded with exaggerated relief, then pivoted to another topic. "Have you heard? There are beastly rumors circulating again about Beaufort..."

That afternoon, the news of the Beaufort collapse dominated every newspaper. It eclipsed the report of Mrs. Manson Mingott's stroke, leaving only a handful of those aware of the mysterious link between the two events to speculate whether old Catherine's ailment stemmed from anything other than the natural accumulation of flesh and years. The entire city of New York was plunged into gloom by the tale of Beaufort's disgrace.

As Mr. Letterblair noted, there had never been a worse case in his memory—or, for that matter, in the memory of the distant ancestor who had bestowed the firm's name. The bank had continued accepting deposits for a full day after its failure became inevitable. Since so many of its clients belonged to the ruling families, Beaufort's duplicity appeared doubly cynical. Had Mrs. Beaufort not adopted the tone that such misfortunes (in her own words) were merely "the test of friendship," compassion for her might have softened the general outrage against her husband. Instead—and especially after the nature of her nocturnal visit to Mrs. Manson Mingott became public knowledge—her cynicism was deemed to surpass his. She lacked the excuse, nor did her detractors find satisfaction in claiming she was "a foreigner."

It offered some solace (for those whose investments were safe) to remind themselves that Beaufort *was* a foreigner. Yet, if a gentleman from South Carolina named Dallas took his view of affairs and glibly predicted he would soon be "back on his feet," the argument lost its sting. All that remained was to accept this terrible proof of the indissolubility of marriage. Society must simply learn to function without the Beauforts, and that was that—unless, of course, one counted the hapless victims of the disaster: Medora Manson, the unfortunate Miss Lannings, and various other misguided ladies of good breeding who, had they only listened to Mr. Henry van der Luyden...

"The best thing the Beauforts can do," declared Mrs. Archer, summarizing the situation as if diagnosing an illness and prescribing a cure, "is to retreat to Regina's little estate in North Carolina. Beaufort has always maintained a racing stable; he ought to focus on breeding trotters. I believe he possesses all the qualities of a successful horse dealer."

Everyone agreed, yet no one bothered to inquire what the Beauforts actually intended to do.

The following day, Mrs. Manson Mingott was significantly better. She recovered enough use of her voice to issue strict orders that no one mention the Beauforts to her again. When Dr. Bencomb visited, she asked what in the world her family meant by making such a fuss about her health.

"If people of my age *do* eat chicken salad in the evening, what are we to expect?" she queried. With the doctor conveniently modifying her diet, the stroke was reclassified as a mere bout of indigestion.

Yet, despite her stern demeanor, old Catherine did not fully regain her former outlook on life. The increasing distance of old age, while failing to dull her curiosity about her neighbors, had numbed her already tepid compassion for their troubles. She seemed effortlessly able to dismiss the Beaufort scandal from her mind. However, for the first time, she became obsessed with her own symptoms, developing a sentimental attachment to certain family members she had previously treated with contempt.

Mr. Welland, in particular, attracted her attention. Of her sons-in-law, he was the one she had most consistently ignored; all his wife's attempts to portray him as a man of forceful character and notable intellect (provided he had only "chosen" differently) had been met with a derisive chuckle. Now, his reputation as a hypochondriac made him an object of intense fascination. Mrs. Mingott issued an imperial summons for him to come and compare diets as soon as his fever subsided, for old Catherine was now the first to acknowledge that one could never be too cautious about body temperature.

Twenty-four hours after receiving Madame Olenska's summons, a telegram arrived announcing her arrival from Washington on the evening of the following day.

At the Welland residence, where the Newland Archers happened to be dining, the question of who should meet her at Jersey City arose immediately. The practical difficulties facing the Welland household, which struggled as if it were a frontier outpost, added spice to the debate.

It was agreed that Mrs. Welland could not possibly travel to Jersey City, as she was scheduled to accompany her husband to old Catherine's that afternoon. Furthermore, the brougham could not be spared; if Mr. Welland were "upset" by seeing his mother-in-law for the first time post-stroke, he might require immediate transport home. The Welland sons would naturally be downtown, Mr. Lovell Mingott would be returning from a shooting trip with his carriage occupied, and one could not reasonably ask May, at the close of a winter afternoon, to cross the ferry alone—even in her own carriage.

Nevertheless, it might seem inhospitable—and contrary to old Catherine's explicit wishes—if Madame Olenska were allowed to arrive without a member of the family at the station.

"It's always one thing after another," the poor lady lamented, in one of her rare rebellions against fate. "The only thing that makes me suspect Mamma is less well than Dr. Bencomb admits is this morbid desire to have Ellen return immediately, however inconvenient it is to meet her."

These words were thoughtless, typical of moments of impatience. Mr. Welland struck instantly.

"Augusta," he said, turning pale and setting down his fork, "do you have any other reason for believing Bencomb is less reliable than before? Have you noticed he has been less conscientious than usual in attending to my case or your mother's?"

Now it was Mrs. Welland's turn to turn pale as the endless repercussions of her slip of the tongue unfolded before her. Yet, she managed to laugh and take a second serving of scalloped oysters before regaining her armor of cheerfulness.

"My dear, how could you imagine such a thing? I only meant that, after the firm stance Mamma took regarding Ellen's duty to return to her husband, it seems strange that she should suddenly wish to see her, when there are half a dozen other grandchildren she could have requested. But we must never forget that Mamma, despite her wonderful vitality, is a very old woman."

Mr. Welland's brow remained furrowed, clearly fixated on this final remark.

"Yes: your mother is a very old woman; and for all we know, Bencomb may not be as successful with the elderly. As you say, my dear, it's always one thing after another. In another ten or fifteen years, I suppose I shall have the pleasant duty of searching for a new doctor. It is always better to make such a change before it becomes absolutely necessary." Having reached this Spartan conclusion, Mr. Welland firmly picked up his fork.

"But all the while," Mrs. Welland began again as she rose from the table and led the way into the wild expanse of purple satin and malachite known as the back drawing-room, "I cannot see how Ellen is to be brought here tomorrow evening. I prefer to have things settled at least twenty-four hours in advance."
