As Romola walked alongside her cousin Brigida through the winding streets of Florence, she couldn't help but notice the commotion gathering near the Piazza. A group of youths had stopped before the humble abode of Tessa, and Romola's keen eye picked out the distress etched on the contadina's face. "Wait for me a moment, cousin," she said, turning to Brigida, who nodded hastily and replied, "No, I'll wait for you at Boni's shop – I'll return there." But the truth was, Brigida was torn between two opposing desires: on one hand, she felt a nagging sense of vanity about the finery she wore, and on the other, a growing unease about the strict moral code of the Piagnoni, who condemned the use of rouge, false hair, and ornate embroidery as a corrupting influence on the soul.

Their austere views hung in the air like a faint scent, casting a pall over everything, making even the most familiar sights seem tainted. And here was Romola, young and beautiful, living a life that seemed to be governed by rigid expectations. A woman of fifty-five, whose happiness had long been tied to her appearance and the admiration she believed it inspired, needed a rich imagination to keep her spirits buoyant. Brigida had begun to struggle with her toilette, wondering whether it was worth the trouble to don the rouge and adornments, only to feel like she was deceiving herself. When she caught a glimpse of her reflection in the mirror, the lines etched on her face and the sagging skin beneath her eyes made her heart sink. Yet, as she let her grey hair fall in simple, unadorned fashion around her face, she felt a pang of doubt. Monna Berta, her younger cousin, would surely laugh at her, calling her an old crone, and her words would cut deep. Every woman who wasn't a Piagnone would glance at her with disdain, and the men would treat her like a grandmother. But, Brigida told herself, fifty-five was hardly old – just a woman in need of a little embellishment.
