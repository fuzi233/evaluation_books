In the bustling days of George the Magnificent’s reign, tucked away in the quaint town of Clavering, lived a gentleman by the name of Pendennis. Those with long memories might recall his name once painted above a humble shopfront in Bath, adorned with a golden pestle and mortar—where Mr. Pendennis practiced the dual arts of apothecary and surgeon. There, he not only dosed feverish gentlemen and ushered ladies through delicate predicaments but also sold hair-powder and toothbrushes to farmers’ wives over the counter.

Yet behind this modest trade lay a pedigree as old as the Druids—or so the Pendennises claimed. Their lineage, tangled with Norman blood and whispered connections to Welsh and Breton nobility, was a point of pride. Young John Pendennis had even tasted university life at Cambridge before his father’s debts yanked him back to the mortar and pestle. He loathed the trade, but necessity (and a pushy uncle in the same business) left him little choice.

Fate intervened when Lady Ribstone, a baronet’s wife, stumbled into his shop after a collision with a drunken sedan chair. Smelling salts and flattery later, Pendennis found himself appointed her personal apothecary. From there, his fortunes rose: the shop grew smarter, the toothbrushes vanished, and soon he traded his apron for a gig, then a carriage emblazoned with the Pendennis crest. His bedridden mother, watching from her window, wept with pride.

By forty, Pendennis had shed his past entirely. He bought an estate in Clavering, framed his dubious pedigree, and styled himself a country squire. He married Helen Thistlewood, a gentlewoman left penniless by the death of her tyrannical patroness, Lady Pontypool. Helen, though decades younger, brought grace and quiet devotion to the union. Their son, Arthur, grew up believing his father had always been a gentleman—oblivious to the pills and plasters of Bath.

At Fairoaks, their ivy-clad manor, Pendennis played the part flawlessly: inspecting crops, hosting dinners, and name-dropping his brother, Major Pendennis, a fixture in London society. The Major, ever the bachelor, regaled them with tales of lords and ladies, though he sniffed at local heiresses. Meanwhile, Helen—radiant, pious, and kind—drew admiration and envy in equal measure.

Young Arthur idolized his mother, her goodness as unchanging as the sunset over Clavering Park. Yet beneath the idyll lurked the family’s open secret: the apothecary’s past, the fabricated pedigree, and the relentless striving to belong among those who never quite forgot where the Pendennises began.
