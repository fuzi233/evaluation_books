I circled the fortress until I reached the open gates, crossing the deep, ravine-like moat to stand upon a vast, paved courtyard. Towering trees, resembling poplars, stood at regular intervals, their canopies forming a green ceiling. In the center rose one tree, taller than the rest, its upper branches spreading wide to mimic the silhouette of a palm. Through the massive trunks, I caught glimpses of the palace—a structure alien yet suggestive of ancient India. It was long and low, crowned by lofty corner towers and a colossal central dome that pierced the roofline halfway to the turrets. The main entrance, a low arch resembling a flattened ellipse, stood wide open. Unchallenged, I stepped into a great hall shaped like a long ellipse.

To one side sat a cage housing a massive leopardess. Chained by a steel collar, her muzzle and paws bound, she lay coiled with her head resting on her paws. Her coat was white, marked with dark ovals, and her eyes were wide, featuring canoe-shaped pupils and great green irises. She stared at me, yet not a muscle moved—no blink, no twitch of a whisker, no shift of her tail, which stretched rigid behind her like iron. She appeared suspended between life and death.

Two low passages branched from this vestibule. I chose one, which quickly forked into a labyrinth of narrow, irregular corridors. At a bottleneck barely wide enough for two, a page collided with me. He recoiled in terror, then, upon recognizing me, his fear curdled into impudence.

"To see the princess?" he asked, puffing out his chest.

"A likely thing!" he scoffed. "I haven't seen her Highness this morning!"

I seized him by the back of the neck, shaking him until he gasped. "Take me to her immediately, or I will drag you until I find her. Let her know how her servants receive her guests."

He glanced at me, then began to lead me blindly, pulling me toward a large kitchen. Servants moved there with feebly sluggish energy, hardly awake. I expected them to swarm and eject me, but instead, they froze, their eyes widening not at me, but at something behind me. Their faces grew ghastly as they stared. Turning, I saw the white leopardess regarding them with an intensity that would have shattered stouter hearts.

Soon, one servant recovered his composure. I released the boy.

"Take me to the princess," I commanded.

"She has not yet left her room, your lordship," he replied.

"Let her know I am here, waiting for an audience."

"Will your lordship give me your name?"

"Tell her that one who knows the white leech desires to see her."

"She will kill me if I deliver such a message. I dare not."

"You refuse?"

He cast a wary glance at the beast and fled. The remaining servants continued to stare, paralyzed by fear of the animal. I turned to the graceful creature, who now stood with her muzzle dropped to my heel. She was a warm splendor in the gloom, white as milk. I stooped to pat her; she looked up, and the mere movement of her head scattered the servants in panic. Rising on her hind legs, she placed her paws on my shoulders. I embraced her, but she pricked her ears, broke free, and vanished in an instant.

The page I had sent earlier returned. "Please, come this way, my lord."

My heart hammered, bracing itself for the encounter. I followed him through endless passages until we entered a room so vast and dark that the walls were invisible. A single spot on the floor reflected a faint light, while everything else was absolute black. Looking up, I saw an oval aperture high in the roof, framed by the joints of black marble blocks. The floor was paved with similar slabs, and the elliptical walls absorbed the scant light. The roof formed the long half of an ellipsoid, with the opening positioned over one of the ellipse's foci. For a fleeting moment, I thought I saw reddish lines, but they vanished before I could examine them.

Suddenly, a radiant form emerged from the center of the darkness, flashing with brilliance. She wore a robe of soft white, her hair cascading like a cataract of black against the marble. Her eyes were a luminous void, her arms and feet like warm ivory. She greeted me with the innocent smile of a girl, her figure suggesting she had only just crossed the threshold of womanhood.

*Alas,* I thought, *I miscalculated my danger! Can this be the woman I rescued—the one who struck me, scorned me, and abandoned me?* I stood gazing into the dark; she gazed back, searching.

She disappeared. *She will not acknowledge me!* I thought. But instantly, her eyes flashed from the shadows straight into mine. She had seen me and come to me!

"You have found me at last!" she said, placing her hand on my shoulder. "I knew you would!"

My body quivered with conflicting sensations, a storm of attraction and repulsion I lacked the power to analyze.

"You shiver!" she observed. "This place is cold for you! Come."

Silent, struck dumb by her beauty and sweetness, I allowed her to lead me by the hand to the spot of light. She stood there for an instant, then spoke.

"You have grown brown since last I saw you," she noted.

"This is almost the first roof I have been under since you left me," I replied.

"Whose was the other?" she asked.

"I do not know the woman's name."

"I would gladly learn it! The instinct of hospitality is not strong in my people!" She took my hand again, leading me through the darkness to a curtain of black fabric. Beyond it lay a white staircase, which she ascended to a beautiful chamber.

"How you must miss the hot flowing river!" she said. "But there is a bath in the corner, free of white leeches! At the foot of your couch lies a garment. When you descend, I shall be in the room to your left, at the foot of the stairs."

As she departed, I accused my own presumption. How could I treat this lovely woman as an entity of evil when she behaved like a sister? Where had the marvelous change occurred? She had struck me before; now she embraced me. She had reviled me; now she claimed she knew I would follow! Did she understand my doubts? Could she explain everything? Could I believe her if she did? Regarding her hospitality, I had surely earned it and might accept it until I formed a final judgment.

Could such beauty and such wickedness coexist in one person? If so, how? Unable to answer the first question, I deferred the second.

Clear as crystal, the water in the great white bath sent a sparkling flash from the corner where it sank into the marble floor, inviting my embrace. Save for the hot streams in the cottage of the veiled woman and the pools in the track of the wounded leopardess, I had not seen water since leaving home. It looked celestial. I plunged in.

Immediately, my brain filled with a strange, delicate odor that I did not entirely like. Doubt returned: had she medicated it? Enchanted it? Was she working on me unlawfully? How could there be water in the palace while the city remained dry? I remembered the crushed paw of the leopardess and sprang from the bath.

What had I been bathing in? Again I saw the fleeing mother, heard the howl, saw the limping beast. But what mattered the source? Was not the water sweet? Was it not the very water the pitcher-plant secreted from its heart for the weary traveler? Water came from heaven; what mattered the well or the spring? Yet, I did not re-enter.

I donned the white wool robe, embroidered on the neck and hem, that lay ready for me, and descended the stairs to the room my hostess indicated. It was round, constructed entirely of alabaster, with no windows. Light permeated everywhere, a soft, pearly shimmer rather than a harsh shine. Vague, shadowy forms drifted over the walls and low dome, like loose rain-clouds over a grey-blue sky.

The princess waited, wearing a robe embroidered with argent rings, discs, rectangles, and lozenges, pressed close together like silver mail. It fell unbroken from her neck, hiding her feet, though its long, open sleeves left her arms bare.

On an ivory table stood cakes and fruit, an ivory jug of milk, a crystal jug of pale rose-colored wine, and a white loaf.

"Here we do not kill to eat," she said. "But I think you will like what I can give you."

I told her I desired nothing better. She seated herself on a couch by the table and gestured for me to join her. She poured a bowl of milk, handed me the loaf, and broke off a piece. Then she filled two silver goblets of grotesquely graceful workmanship from the wine jug.

"You have never drunk wine like this!" she declared.

I drank and marveled; every flower of Hybla and Hymettus seemed to have sent its ghost to swell the soul of that wine.

"And now that you will be able to listen," she continued, "I must do what I can to make myself intelligible. Our natures differ so greatly that this may not be easy. Men and women live but to die; we, those of my kind, live to live on. Old age is a horror to you; to me, it is a dear desire. The older we grow, the nearer we approach perfection. Your perfection is poor, coming soon and lasting briefly; ours is a ceaseless ripening. I am not yet ripe, having lived thousands of your years—how many, I never cared to note. The everlasting cannot be measured."

"Many lovers have sought me," she went on. "I have loved none of them. They sought only to enslave me, as the men of my city seek gems of price. When you found me, I found a man! I put you to the test; you stood it. Your love was genuine—but far from ideal. You loved me truly, but not with true love. Pity is not love. What woman would return love for pity? Such love as yours was then is hateful to me. I knew that if you saw me as I am, you would love me—to have and to hold. I wanted none of that! I desired a love that outlived hopelessness, outmeasured indifference, hate, and scorn. Therefore, I adopted cruelty, despite, and ingratitude. When I left you, I showed myself such that you could no longer follow from pity; I was no longer in need of you. But you must satisfy my desire or set me free—prove yourself priceless or worthless! To satisfy the hunger of my love, you must follow me, seeking nothing—not gratitude, not even pity. Follow and find me, and be content with merest presence, with scantest forbearance. I, not you, have failed; I yield the contest."

She looked at me tenderly, hiding her face in her hands. Yet I caught a flash and a sparkle behind the tenderness and did not believe her. She laid herself out to secure and enslave me; she only fascinated me!

"Beautiful princess," I said, "let me understand how you came to be found in such evil plight."

"There are things I cannot explain," she replied, "until you become capable of understanding them—which can only happen when love grows perfect. Many things are hidden from you that you cannot even wish to know; but any question you can ask, I can answer in some measure."

"I had set out to visit a part of my dominions occupied by a savage dwarf-people, strong and fierce, enemies to law and order, opposed to every kind of progress—an evil race. I went alone, fearing nothing, unaware of the least necessity for precaution. I did not know that upon the hot stream beside which you found me, a certain woman, by no means so powerful as myself, not being immortal, had cast what you call a spell—which is merely the setting in motion of a force as natural as any other, but operating primarily in a region beyond the ken of the mortal who uses it."

"I set out on my journey, reached the stream, bounded across it..." A shadow of embarrassment darkened her cheek. I understood but showed no sign. Checked for a moment, she continued. "...you know what a step it is in parts! But in the very act, an indescribable cold invaded me. I recognized the nature of the assault at once and knew it could affect me only temporarily. By sheer force of will I dragged myself to the wood—and knew nothing more until I saw you asleep and the horrible worm at your neck. I crept out, dragged the monster from you, and laid my lips to the wound. You began to wake; I buried myself among the leaves."

She rose, her eyes flashing as never human eyes had flashed, and threw her arms high over her head.

"What you have made of me is yours!" she cried. "I will repay you as never yet did woman! My power, my beauty, my love are your own: take them."

She dropped to her knees beside me, laying her arms across my knees and looking up into my face. Then, for the first time, I noted a large, clumsy glove on her left hand. In my mind's eye, I saw hair and claws beneath it, knowing it was a hand shut tight—perhaps badly bruised. The other hand was lovely as any could be, and I felt that if I did less than loathe her, I should love her. Refusing to dally with usurping emotions, I turned my eyes aside.

She started to her feet. I sat motionless, looking down.

"To me she may be true!" my vanity whispered. For a moment, I was tempted to love a lie.

An odor, rather than the gentlest of airy pulses, fanned me. I glanced up. She stood erect before me, waving her lovely arms in seemingly mystic fashion.

A frightful roar made my heart rebound against the walls of its cage. The alabaster trembled as if it would shatter into pieces.
