The cool church, checkered with shafts of sunlight and crowned with a canopy of heavenly purple, soothed and charmed Father Clement as it had done Margaret long ago. More than mere comfort, however, it carried his mind directly to the Creator of all such pure delights. As his gaze swept across the great aisle, he saw it crammed with his country folk—a thousand snowy caps filigreed with gold. Though many hundreds of leagues separated him from home, he had seen nothing comparable to this multitude save perhaps a winter landscape blanketed in snow.

In the morning, he had thundered with judgment; yet this sweet afternoon seemed entirely out of tune with threats. His heart yearned over the crowd below him. He realized he must tell them of God's love; alas, poor souls, they heard almost as little of it from the pulpit in those days as the heathen did. So, he spoke the glad tidings of salvation. The people hung upon his gentle, earnest tongue. He was not one of those preachers who gyrate in the pulpit like a weathercock atop a steeple; rather, he moved the hearts of others far more than his own body allowed him to move. Yet, he did not entirely neglect those who stood in need.

Warm with this theme, determined that none of the multitude should miss the joyful news of Christ's love, he turned toward the south aisle. There, in a brilliant stream of sunshine pouring through the window, stood the radiant face of Margaret Brandt. He gazed at it without immediate emotion; it simply numbed him, soul and body alike. Soon, however, the words died in his throat, and he trembled as he stared.

There, with her auburn hair bathed in the beams and glittering like the halo of a saint, her face glowing doubly—once with its own inherent beauty and twice with the light that framed it—stood his dead love. She leaned lightly against a white column, listening with tender, downcast lashes. He had seen her listen to him so a hundred times before. There was no change in her. This was the blooming Margaret he had left, merely a shade riper and more lovely.

He started back with monstrous eyes and bloodless cheeks. The congregation faded from his sight. He heard, as in a dream, a rustling and rising throughout the church, yet he could not tear his prodigy-stricken eyes away from that face—all life, bloom, beauty, and that wondrous auburn hair glistening gloriously in the sun. He gazed, convinced she must vanish like a mirage. She remained.

Suddenly, she looked at him, fully. Her own violet eyes! At this, he was beside himself, his lips parting to shriek her name. But she turned her head swiftly, and moments later, she vanished. Not without one final glance, however—one rapid as lightning that met his own, leaving her cowering and quivering with her mind in a whirl, and him panting, gripping the pulpit convulsively. That glance, though lacking explicit recognition, was the startled, inquiring, nameless look that precedes it.

Making a mighty effort, he muttered something unintelligible, then feebly resumed his discourse, stammering and babbling for a while. Gradually, forcing himself to view her presence as a vision from another world since she was now gone, he rose into a state of unnatural excitement. He concluded his sermon in a style of eloquence that electrified the simple minds present, bordering on rhapsody.

When the service ended, he sat down on the pulpit stool, terribly shaken. Presently, an idea very characteristic of the age took possession of him. He had sought her grave at Sevenbergen in vain. Now, she had been permitted to appear to him, showing that she was buried here—probably hard by that very pillar where her spirit had manifested. Once adopted, this idea settled on his mind with the certainty of fact. He felt he needed only to speak to the sexton—who, to his great disgust, he had seen working during the sermon—to learn the exact spot where she lay.

The church was now quite empty. Climbing down from the pulpit, Father Clement stepped through an aperture in the south wall onto the grass and approached the sexton. He recognized him instantly, though Jorian never suspected the poor lad whose life he had saved was hidden beneath the guise of this holy friar. The loss of his shapely beard had wonderfully altered the outline of Jorian's face. Combined with his tonsure, his short hair sprinkled with premature grey, and cheeks thinned and paled by fasts and vigils, the transformation was complete.

"My son," said Friar Clement softly, "if you keep any memory of those whom you lay in the earth, pray tell me: is any Christian buried inside the church, near one of the pillars?"

"Nay, father," replied Jorian. "Here in the churchyard lie buried all that are buried within. Why?"

"No matter. Pray tell me then where lieth Margaret Brandt."

"Margaret Brandt?" Jorian stared stupidly at the speaker. "She died about three years ago and was buried here."

"Oh, that is another matter," said Jorian. "That was before my time; the vicar could tell you, likely, if so be she was a gentlewoman, or at least rich enough to pay him his fee."

"Alas, my son, she was poor—and paid a heavy penalty for it—but born of decent folk. Her father, Peter, was a learned physician; she came hither from Sevenbergen... to die."

When Clement uttered these words, his head sank upon his breast, and he seemed to possess neither the power nor the wish to question Jorian further. One doubts even if he knew where he stood; he was utterly lost in the past.

Jorian put down his spade, standing upright in the grave with his arms akimbo, saying sulkily, "Are you making a fool of me, holy sir, or has some wag been making a fool of you!" Having thus relieved his mind, he proceeded to dig again with a vigor that showed his somewhat irritable temper was ruffled.

Clement gazed at him with a puzzled but gently reproachful eye, for the tone was rude and the words unintelligible. Good-natured though crusty, Jorian had not thrown up three spadefuls before he became ashamed of himself.

"Why, what a base churl am I to speak thus to thee, holy father? And thou standest there looking at me like a lamb." Aha! I have it; 'tis Peter Brandt's grave you would fain see, not Margaret's. He does lie here, hard by the west door. There; I'll show you."

And he laid down his spade, putting on his doublet and jerkin to accompany the friar. He did not know there was anyone sitting on Peter's tomb. Still less did he know she was watching for this holy friar. Neither Pietro Vanucci nor Andrea recognized him without his beard. Indeed, the fact remains that a beard which has never known a razor grows in a very picturesque and characteristic form, becoming a defining feature of the face; so that its removal may, in some cases, serve as an effectual disguise.
