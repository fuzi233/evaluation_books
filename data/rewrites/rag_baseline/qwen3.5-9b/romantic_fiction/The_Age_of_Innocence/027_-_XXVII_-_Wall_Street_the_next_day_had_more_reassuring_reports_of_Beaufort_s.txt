Wall Street presented a facade of stability the following day, draped in reports that were hopeful rather than certain. The consensus held that Beaufort possessed powerful allies, contacts who had successfully navigated similar crises; indeed, the evening saw Mrs. Beaufort appear at the Opera, adorned with her old smile and a fresh emerald necklace, prompting a collective exhale of relief from the assembled crowd. Yet New York remained a jurisdiction of inexorable judgment regarding commercial impropriety. Its silent decree was absolute: those who violated the law of probity must pay the price. All understood that even the Beauforts would eventually be sacrificed to this principle. However, the prospect of such a sacrifice was not merely painful; it was logistically disastrous. Their absence would carve a gaping void in their tight-knit circle, and those too oblivious or indifferent to feel the moral vertigo of the impending catastrophe were already lamenting the loss of the city's premier ballroom.

Archer had resolved to depart for Washington. He awaited only the scheduling of the lawsuit he had discussed with May, intending his journey to coincide with the legal proceedings. But on the subsequent Tuesday, Mr. Letterblair informed him that the case might be delayed for weeks. Regardless, Archer decided that evening to leave the very next night. The probability existed that May, unaware of his professional entanglements and incapable of retaining the names of litigants, would remain ignorant of the postponement. Furthermore, he could no longer defer seeing Madame Olenska; there were matters demanding immediate articulation.

By Wednesday morning, however, the atmosphere in the office had curdled. Mr. Letterblair approached Archer with a pallid expression. Beaufort had failed to "tide over." By circulating rumors of survival, he had temporarily placated depositors, drawing in substantial funds until the previous evening, when ominous intelligence resurfaced. Consequently, a panic ensued, threatening to shutter the bank's doors before sundown. The most damning accusations circulated regarding Beaufort's treachery, promising a discreditable collapse in Wall Street history. The magnitude of the disaster rendered Mr. Letterblair speechless and physically incapacitated. "I have witnessed worse," he murmured, "but nothing comparable. Everyone we know will suffer. And what becomes of Mrs. Beaufort? What *can* be done? I pity Mrs. Manson Mingott profoundly; at her age, the repercussions are unpredictable. She trusted Beaufort utterly; she befriended him! Consider the Dallas connection: Mrs. Beaufort is kin to everyone present. Her only recourse would be to abandon her husband, yet how does one suggest such a betrayal? Her duty dictates standing by him, and fortunately, she appears perpetually blind to his private frailties."

A knock interrupted him. "Who is it? Do not disturb me." A clerk entered bearing a letter for Archer and retreated. Recognizing his wife's handwriting, the young man unfolded the envelope: *"Will you please come uptown as early as possible? Granny suffered a slight stroke last night. Somehow, she discovered this terrible news about the bank before anyone else. Uncle Lovell is away hunting; the weight of this disgrace has induced a fever in poor Papa, confining him to his room. Mamma requires you desperately, and I trust you can depart immediately and proceed straight to Granny's."*

Archer passed the note to his senior partner. Minutes later, he crawled northward in a congested horse-car, transferring at Fourteenth Street to a swaying omnibus of the Fifth Avenue line. After noon, this arduous conveyance deposited him at old Catherine's. The ground-floor sitting-room window, usually occupied by her commanding presence, housed only the frail silhouette of her daughter, Mrs. Welland, who offered a gaunt greeting upon spotting Archer. At the threshold, May awaited him. The hallway bore the uncanny stillness of a pristine home breached by sickness: furs and wraps lay in disarray on chairs, a physician's bag and coat rested on the table amidst a pile of ignored correspondence. May appeared pale yet composed; Dr. Bencomb, having visited twice, offered cautious optimism, noting that Mrs. Mingott's fierce will to survive was already influencing her household.

May guided Archer into the elderly woman's sitting-room, where sliding doors leading to the bedroom were drawn shut, obscured by heavy yellow damask curtains. Here, Mrs. Welland whispered the details of the catastrophe in tones of horror. The preceding evening, around eight o'clock, shortly after Mrs. Mingott concluded her customary solitaire, the bell rang. A lady, heavily veiled and unrecognized by the staff, requested admission. The butler, recognizing a familiar voice, threw open the door to announce "Mrs. Julius Beaufort" before closing it again on the two women. They remained together for approximately an hour. When Mrs. Mingott signaled for service, Mrs. Beaufort had vanished unseen. The old lady, white and imposing, sat alone in her great chair, signaling the butler to assist her upstairs. Initially, though visibly distressed, she maintained command of her faculties. The mulatto maid settled her in bed, served tea, arranged the room, and departed. But at three in the morning, the bell tolled again. Hastening to this unusual summons—for old Catherine typically slept undisturbed—the servants found their mistress propped against her pillows, wearing a twisted smile, one small hand dangling limply from its massive sleeve. The stroke appeared minor; she could articulate and direct care, and soon regained control of her facial muscles. Yet the alarm was profound. Equally intense was the outrage when Mrs. Mingott's fragmented utterances revealed that Regina Beaufort had arrived to request her support—to stand behind her husband, to see them through, explicitly refusing to "desert" them, effectively compelling the entire family to shield and condone their monstrosity.

"I told her: 'Honor has always been honor, and honesty honesty, in the Manson Mingott house, and will remain so until I am carried out of it feet first,'" the old woman had stammered into her daughter's ear, her voice thick with partial paralysis. "And when she replied, 'But my name, Auntie—my name is Regina Dallas,' I answered: 'It was Beaufort when he draped you in jewels, and it must remain Beaufort now that he has draped you in shame.'"

Mrs. Welland recounted this with tears and gasping horror, blanched by the necessity of confronting the discreditable. "If only I could spare my father-in-law knowledge of this: he constantly pleads, 'Augusta, for heaven's sake, do not destroy my last illusions.' How can I prevent him from learning these terrors?" she wailed. "After all, Mamma, he hasn't *seen* them," her daughter suggested gently. Mrs. Welland sighed. "Ah, yes; thank heavens he remains safely in bed. Dr. Bencomb promises to keep him confined until Mamma recovers, and Regina has been spirited away."

Archer sat near the window, staring blankly at the empty street. He realized he had been summoned primarily for the moral sustenance of the grieving women, offering no tangible solution. Mr. Lovell Mingott had been telegraphed for, and handwritten messages dispatched to family members across New York. Meanwhile, the conversation drifted in hushed tones regarding the consequences of Beaufort's dishonor and his wife's indefensible actions. Mrs. Lovell Mingott emerged from another room, adding her voice to the discourse. In their era, the wives of disgraced businessmen possessed a singular instinct: to erase themselves, to vanish with their husbands. "Consider the case of poor Grandmamma Spicer," Mrs. Lovell Mingott noted, referring to May's great-grandmother. "Your great-grandfather's financial troubles were private—gambling losses, signing notes for others—I never fully grasped the specifics, as Mamma refused to speak of it. She was raised in the countryside because her mother was forced to flee New York following the disgrace, whatever it was. They lived alone up the Hudson, year-round, until Mamma was sixteen. It never crossed Grandmamma Spicer's mind to ask the family to 'countenance' her, as I believe Regina termed it; though a private shame pales before the scandal of ruining hundreds of innocents."

"Yes, it would be more fitting for Regina to conceal her own visage than to discuss others'," Mrs. Lovell Mingott concurred. "I understand the emerald necklace she wore at the Opera last Friday was sent on approval from Ball and Black's this afternoon. I wonder if they will ever recover it?"

Archer listened impassively to the relentless chorus. The concept of absolute financial integrity as the cornerstone of a gentleman's code was etched too deeply into his psyche for sentimentality to erode it. While adventurers like Lemuel Struthers amassed fortunes through dubious means, unblemished honesty constituted the *noblesse oblige* of old financial New York. Nor did Mrs. Beaufort's fate stir him significantly. He felt perhaps greater sorrow for her than her indignant relatives, yet it seemed to him that the marital bond, breakable in prosperity, ought to be indissoluble in adversity. As Mr. Letterblair had stated, a wife's station was beside her husband in trouble; society's station was not. Mrs. Beaufort's cool presumption that it was seemed almost to implicate her as an accomplice. The notion of a woman appealing to her family to screen her husband's commercial dishonor was inadmissible, representing the one act the Family, as an institution, could not permit.
