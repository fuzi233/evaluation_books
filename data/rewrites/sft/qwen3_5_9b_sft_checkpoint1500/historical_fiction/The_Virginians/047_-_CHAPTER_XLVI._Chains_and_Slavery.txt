A deep sense of remorse for his past follies mingled with a sharp wrath at the indignity of arrest as Harry found himself a prisoner in that dismal lock-up. Yet, beneath the present humiliation, he clung to the certainty that his confinement was merely momentary. In his mind, he counted twenty friends capable of securing his release: Mr. Draper, the obsequious businessman; his kind uncle, the Baronet, who had offered his home; Cousin Castlewood, his gaming companion; the noble patrons of the Chocolate House; and his good Aunt Bernstein. To which of these allies should he first appeal? The paramount concern, however, was discretion. Mr. Warrington, a man of prodigious self-importance, dreaded the vulgarity of a public arrest, fearing the scandal it would bring to his reputation. He reasoned, with a cynical detachment, that the world eventually learns everything it wishes to know, yet remains ultimately indifferent. "Psha!" he thought, dismissing the notion of disgrace. "Your shame is only vanity. Appear with a smiling countenance, and nobody cares." While Mr. Warrington chafed as if the entire globe were buzzing with his downfall, he resolved to handle the crisis quietly, avoiding the scrutiny of his relations or the nobles at White's. Instead, he dispatched a messenger at dawn to his friend, the Reverend Mr. Sampson, seeking the counsel and consolation of a man who understood his precarious position.

Unbeknownst to Harry, sending for Sampson was akin to inviting a lion into a den. The chaplain, still buoyed by recent favors from his patron, likely assumed Harry's troubles were trivial compared to his own. Upon delivering the message, Harry's spirits lifted slightly, and he requested breakfast. When the attendant inquired if he wished to dine in-house or join Mrs. Bailiff's table, Harry declined, insisting on departing before dinner time. The chamberlain, skeptical of the youth's resolve, closed the door, leaving Harry to his solitary meditations.

Before long, Sampson's messenger returned with a note stating the reverend would arrive shortly. However, the clock ticked past ten, eleven, and noon without his arrival. Just as despair threatened to overwhelm Harry, Gumbo—the faithful black servant—burst into the room, carrying a portmanteau and weeping bitterly. With vows of undying loyalty, Gumbo declared his readiness to sell himself into slavery again to save his beloved master. Harry, touched by the boy's devotion, rose and embraced him, ordering him strictly to remain silent about the arrest. Gumbo, however, was a creature of the slave trade, bound by a nature that thrived on subterfuge and gossip. Though he swore solemnly not to utter a word, his tongue betrayed him instantly. Having already spread the news of his master's captivity to the footmen's club, among the servants of Colonel Lambert and Lord Wrotham, and even to Sir Miles Warrington's household, Gumbo could not contain his joy at fulfilling his duty as a gossip. By the time he faced his master's stern gaze, promising silence, the rumor had already circulated widely among the gentry.

Hours dragged slowly, and Sampson failed to materialize. Finally, around midday, a letter arrived, sealed with a moist wafer and ink barely dry. It revealed that the chaplain, hastening to assist, had been seized by two villains holding a writ against him and imprisoned in a lock-up three doors away. Thus, both master and servant sat in chains, separated by a thin wall yet united in misery. The letter ended abruptly, refusing to detail the specific reasons for Harry's downfall, a mystery the reader would soon unravel.

While Sampson remained silent due to his own entanglements, Gumbo's loquacious nature ensured the news traveled swiftly. He had already boasted to the footmen's club and regaled the servants of the highest nobility with the tale of his master's arrest. Back at Long Acre, the divine learned of Harry's plight only after receiving the letter and the messenger's report. Eager to aid his patron, Sampson rushed to Lord Castlewood's residence, pleading passionately for assistance. He spoke of Mr. Warrington's generosity and the vast sums won from his cousin, hoping to stir the nobleman's conscience. Yet, Lord Castlewood cut him short with peevish curtitude. "Did I not tell you two days ago," the lord sneered, "that I was as poor as a beggar?" He claimed the winnings from Harry had been swallowed by other debts, and ironically, he had just written to Harry asking *him* for financial help—a letter which duly reached Harry's cell.

"My Lord!" Sampson cried, desperate. "I would pawn my cassock and bands for him!"
"Amen," retorted the lord, returning to his papers as Sampson departed in deepest despondency.

Meanwhile, Lady Maria Esmond, overhearing the chaplain's fruitless plea, intercepted him. She, too, had heard the dreadful news from the opera box. "Tell me, what is this has happened to Mr. Warrington?" she demanded, drawing Sampson into the dining room. When Sampson revealed Lord Castlewood's cruel refusal, despite having won hundreds from Harry, Lady Maria's composure shattered. She launched into a furious tirade against her own family, accusing them of a lack of honor, courage, and common feeling. She shrieked at her brother, accusing him of "pigeoning" Harry for his last shilling, and stormed toward the library, her voice ringing with righteous indignation. Lord Castlewood, initially surprised by her vehemence, quickly adopted a rational tone, arguing that Harry's money was rightfully spent on his debts and that the loss was a "bagatelle" compared to the principality Maria sought in Virginia. He dismissed her moral outrage as the natural folly of her sex, reducing her fiery defense to a gentle submission as he coaxed her down from her pedestal.

Back at Clarges Street, Madame de Bernstein, though usually unmoved by her nephew's escapades, was deeply shaken by Gumbo's report. Her sleepless night gave way to action. She summoned Mr. Draper, her man of business, to investigate Harry's debts. Draper discovered that Harry had been detained for various sums owed to a jeweller, a landlord, and others. Draper, acting with swift efficiency, arranged to settle these accounts, noting that a gentleman of Harry's rank required no further complications. However, the Baroness harbored a deeper motive. She recalled a minor circumstance from Tunbridge Wells involving a lawyer, a secret she entrusted to Draper, instructing him to ensure Harry emerged from prison "clear" of liabilities, specifically referencing a debt he ought to be released from.

Desperate and alone, Harry finally applied to Mr. Draper, writing a letter that explicitly forbade mentioning the contents to his aunt, Madame de Bernstein. He hoped to avoid the humiliation of petitioning her, preferring to seek aid from his male friends. First, he wrote to his cousin, the Earl of Castlewood, begging for a loan to cover his debts. The reply was cold and dismissive; Castlewood, claiming he was as destitute as Harry, mentioned that Harry's money had already satisfied his creditors. Next, Harry turned to his uncle, Sir Miles Warrington, composing a penitent letter exposing his sins as a prodigal son. The response, delivered by Gumbo, was equally disappointing. It contained a sermon by Mr. Whitfield and a letter from Lady Margaret, who, having opened her brother's mail in his absence, learned of Harry's extravagance. She offered heartfelt prayers and scriptural comfort but no money, gently critiquing his orthography.

Defeated, Harry sat staring into the candlelight, his face hidden in his hands. The bailiff's servant, seeing his distress, offered him beer and food, but Harry had no coin. Gumbo, however, produced a bundle of gold coins—five guineas each—from his own pockets, along with gold moidores and keepsakes from the girls. "Take everything," Gumbo sobbed, "I will sell myself tomorrow morning, but here is plenty for tonight." Overcome with emotion, Harry accepted the gift, breaking his fast with a relish that the jailors watched with approval. As Gumbo departed, shaking hands with the staff, the loyal bond between master and servant stood firm, a stark contrast to the treachery of the white world outside.
