To grasp the labyrinthine architecture of a mind such as Clement's—one so sensitive, so burdened by a tender conscience, and animated by a loving heart and a devout soul—one must recognize that the internal storm raging within those few hours defies the very limits of human expression. Imagine the man; then, transpose your spirit entirely into his shoes. Were I to chronicle every nuance of that spiritual tempest, I would require a volume unto myself. Instead, I shall recount his next two decisive acts, which stand as the barometers of his state of mind once the initial, fierce convulsion of the soul had subsided.

Having spent the night beside the dying hermit, exchanging holy consolations in a sacred exchange of life and faith, Clement did not turn toward Rotterdam. No, his destination was Tergou. There, he intended to confront his mortal foe, the burgomaster, wielding the parchment—a document whose own history was a romance in itself—as the instrument to force restitution and restore Margaret to her rightful inheritance.

He arrived heated and dust-choked, pausing at the town fountain to consume his black bread and quench his thirst with cool water. Yet, midway through this austere meal, a female servant burst forth, breathless, imploring him to attend to her dying master. With a swift motion, he returned his bread to his wallet and followed her in silence. She led him to the Stadthouse. At the threshold, seeing her enter, he recoiled with a faint shudder, a visceral reaction to the proximity of sin. But the shock passed almost instantly; he composed himself and stepped inside, ascending the stairs until they reached the chamber where his deadly enemy lay propped against pillows, already resembling a corpse.

Clement stood in the doorway, his eyes tracing the trajectory of the tower, the wood, and the letter. Then, in a whisper barely audible over the silence, he spoke: *Pax vobiscum!* A slight tremor ran through him as the words left his lips. The sick man, however, greeted him with the eager desperation of a drowning man finding shore. "Thank Heaven, thou art come in time to absolve me from my sins, father, and pray for my soul, thou and thy brethren."

"My son," Clement replied, "before absolution comes confession. In this act, there must be no reservation, for it is the measure of thy soul's welfare. Consider carefully wherein thou hast most grievously offended God and the Church, while I offer a prayer for wisdom to guide thee."

Clement knelt and prayed. Rising, he addressed Ghysbrecht with apparent calm: "My son, confess thy sins."

"Ah, father," the sick man gasped, "they are many and great."

"Great, then, let thy penitence be commensurate," Clement urged. "So shalt thou find God's mercy great."

Ghysbrecht clasped his hands, beginning his recitation with the outward appearance of profound contrition. He admitted to eating meat during mid-Lent, to frequent absences from Mass on the Lord's Day and saints' days, and to trifling with various religious observances, enumerating them with scrupulous fidelity. When he finished, the friar spoke quietly, "'Tis well, my son. These be faults. Now, to thy crimes. Thou hadst done better to begin with them."

"Why, father, what crimes lie to my account if these be none?" Ghysbrecht asked, bewildered.

"Am I confessing to thee, or thou to me?" Clement countered, his tone sharpening slightly.

"Forgive me, father! Surely, I confess to you. But I know not what you term crimes."

"The seven deadly sins, art thou clear of them?"

"Heaven forefend I should be guilty of them. I know them not by name."

"Many commit them all yet cannot name them," Clement observed. "Begin with that one which leads to lying, theft, and murder."

"I am quit of that one, any way. How call you it?"

"AVARICE, my son."

"Avarice? Oh, as to that, I have been a saving man all my days; but I have kept a good table, and not altogether forgotten the poor." Ghysbrecht paused, his face clouding. "But, alas, I am a great sinner. Mayhap the next will catch me. What is the next?"

"We have not yet concluded with this one," Clement warned sternly. "Bethink thee, the Church is not to be trifled with."

"Alas! Am I in a condition to trifle with her now? Avarice? Avarice?" He looked utterly puzzled, an image of innocence masking a lifetime of greed.

"Hast thou ever robbed the fatherless?" the friar inquired.

"Me? Robbed the fatherless?" Ghysbrecht gasped, feigning ignorance. "Not that I mind."

"Once more, my son, I am forced to tell thee thou art trifling with the Church. Miserable man! Another evasion, and I depart, leaving thee to the fiends who will straightway gather round thy bed to drag thee down to the bottomless pit."

"Oh, leave me not! Leave me not!" shrieked the terrified old man. "The Church knows all. I must have robbed the fatherless. I will confess. Who shall I begin with? My memory for names is shaken."

The defense was skillful, yet in this instance, it failed completely.

"Hast thou forgotten Floris Brandt?" Clement asked, his voice stony.

The sick man reared himself up in bed, seized by a pitiable terror. "How knew you that?"

"The Church knows many things," Clement replied coldly, "and by many ways that are dark to thee. Miserable impenitent, you called her to your side, hoping to deceive her. You said, 'I will not confess to the cure but to some friar who knows not my misdeeds. So will I cheat the Church on my deathbed, and die as I have lived.' But God, kinder to thee than thou art to thyself, sent to thee one whom thou couldst not deceive. He has tried thee; He was patient with thee, warning thee not to trifle with Holy Church; but all is in vain. Thou canst not confess, for thou art impenitent as a stone. Die, then, as thou hast lived. Methinks I see the fiends crowding round the bed for their prey. They wait but for me to go. And I go."

He turned his back, but Ghysbrecht, driven to the extremities of terror, grabbed him by the frock. "Oh, holy man, mercy! Stay. I will confess all, all. I robbed my friend Floris. Alas, would it had ended there; for he lost little by me; but I kept the land from Peter his son, and from Margaret, Peter's daughter. Yet I was always going to give it back; but I couldn't, I couldn't."

"Avarice, my son, avarice. Happy for thee 'tis not too late."

"No; I will leave it her by will. She will not have long to wait for it now; not above a month or two at farthest."

"For which month's possession thou wouldst damn thy soul forever, Thou fool!"

The sick man groaned, pleading with the friar to be reasonable. But Clement, firm yet gentle, persisted with infinite patience, slowly detaching the dying man's greedy grip from another's property. There were moments when his patience was tested to the breaking point, tempting him to reach into his bosom and produce the deed he had carried specifically for this purpose. Yet, recalling yesterday's violent outbreak, he held himself in check against choler. Furthermore, he conquered a personal repugnance toward the man so intense that his own flesh crept beneath his habit as he struggled with this miser for his soul. Finally, without uttering a single word regarding the deed itself, he succeeded in winning the dying man to make full and prompt restitution.

How the restitution was executed will be briefly recounted elsewhere, along with the curious effects produced upon Ghysbrecht by the act, and the precise terms upon which Ghysbrecht and Clement parted ways. I promised to relate two acts of the latter, indicative of his restored mental clarity. This is the first. The second is told in two words.

As soon as he was quite certain that Margaret had regained her own and was thus a wealthy woman—he simply disappeared.
