No day passes on earth without ordinary men and women doing extraordinary things—speaking words of courage, enduring sorrows of noble depth. Yet most of these quiet heroes, thinkers, and martyrs remain unknown, their names lost to time until the day when greatness is measured not by power or title, but by virtue and heart. Some, though recorded in history’s ledgers, sleep forgotten: their lives buried beneath dry facts and cold chronicles. The average reader cannot feel them—these stories are not alive; they are like hailstones that strike and vanish, leaving no trace upon the soul. And so they are not understood, for summaries are not stories, just as skeletons are not bodies.

It is here, then, that fiction may serve a purpose—not merely to entertain, but to interpret.

There exists an ancient, musty chronicle written in stiff Latin, each sentence packed with truth. In one chapter lies the tale of two people who lived quietly, died unheralded, and now rest nameless in stone, as if frozen in time. They were neither kings nor saints, yet their story holds a truth worth remembering. If I can bring them to life—reveal what lies beneath those harsh lines—I believe you will see them differently. You will give them, if only for a moment, a place in your heart.

It was the middle of the fifteenth century. Louis XI ruled France, Edward IV sat uneasily on England’s throne, and Philip “the Good,” Duke of Burgundy, held sway over Holland with cunning and force. He had seized the lands of his cousin Jacqueline, broken her spirit, and now ruled without challenge. It is here, in this quiet corner of Europe, that our story begins.

In the small town of Tergou lived Elias and Catherine, a modest couple whose lives revolved around cloth, silk, brown holland, and especially curried leather—a sturdy material prized by common folk, able to last twenty years and even dull a knife. In an age when steel was freely drawn at dinner tables, such durability was no small virtue.

They were well-off enough, and might have lived free from care—if not for nine children. Each child had been welcomed with joy, a blessing from heaven, a source of delight for parents who saw them as little treasures meant to brighten their days. But as the years passed and the children grew, so too did the weight of responsibility. The olive branches of childhood had become strong saplings, and the parents, aging, began to wonder: *Who will feed them all when we are gone?*

This thought troubled them deeply. Though Dutch families were known for their wisdom and foresight, Elias and Catherine felt the burden more than most. Their table, once full of laughter and chatter, now seemed crowded with futures they could not predict. When the great loaf came round, seeming to vanish before it reached each person, they would exchange glances and whisper, “Who will find bread for them all?”

The younger children, still innocent, believed food came naturally, like sunrise. But the older ones—especially the boys—began to think. Some pondered wisely; others, less so. One evening, Elias said, “Kate, the children grow so fast—the table will soon be too small.”

“We cannot afford it, Eli,” Catherine replied—not answering his words, but his worry, as women often do.

Their fears took another form: pride. As free burghers, they could not bear the thought of any of their blood sinking into obscurity after their deaths. So they saved. With frugality and sacrifice, they fed all the mouths and dressed all the bodies, and still managed to set aside a modest hoard. As it grew, so did their quiet joy—not the miser’s greed, but the peace of knowing they had prepared.

One day, Richart, nineteen and thoughtful, approached his mother. With calm composure, he asked her to speak to his father about sending him to Amsterdam to apprentice under a merchant. “It is the path I wish to follow,” he said. “Merchants are wealthy. I am good with numbers. Please, Mother, speak for me. I shall always be in your debt.”

Catherine gasped. “Leave Tergou?”

“What is one street to me more than another? If I can leave the people, I can leave the stones.”

“Leave your poor father, now that he is no longer young?”

“If I can leave you, I can leave him.”

“Leave your brothers and sisters, who love you so?”

“There are enough in the house without me.”

“What do you mean, Richart? Who is more dear than you?” She stepped closer, her voice trembling. “Wait—have I spoken sharply? Have I been unkind?”

“No, Mother,” Richart said gravely, a tear in his eye. “It is simply this: there will be one mouth less to feed.”

And then she knew. She saw her first fledgling testing the wind. Richart had a steady will—she knew he would not change his mind.

He left. His journey cost every penny of their savings except one gold crown. But he never needed another farthing. Two years later, Jacob, his brother, followed. After dinner—eleven o’clock—Jacob walked out, and at supper, Elias whispered, “Sit wider, dears.” Catherine, who had promised not to grieve, suddenly said, “Ah! The table will soon be too big for the children—and you thought it would be too small.” Then she covered her face with her apron and wept.

“It is best they go,” she sobbed. “That is the cruel part.”

“Nay, nay!” Elias said sharply. “Our children are good. All are dear. Do not listen to her. What God takes seems better than what He leaves—that is to say, men are ungrateful, and women foolish.”

“And I say Richart and Jacob were the flower of the flock,” Catherine insisted.

The coffer was empty again. But they gathered like ants. There was no stock market in those days—only dice and cards for speculation. Elias believed in slow, sure gain. “A penny saved is a penny gained,” he said. Everything not needed went into the coffer—steel-banded, key-locked. They denied themselves even small luxuries, then smiled at each other, perhaps happier than if they’d indulged. In three years, they saved enough to set up their fourth son as a master tailor and their eldest daughter as a robe-maker—both in Tergou. Their trade would support them. But the coffer was drained. This time, even the shop bled a little.

Still, two remained unable to earn: Giles, a dwarf, half-stupid, half-malicious, feared by dogs and shunned by women, yet fiercely defended by his mother; and Little Catherine, frail and crippled, who moved only with crutches. She endured pain with quiet grace, her marble face softened by violet eyes and long lashes, never complaining. Then there were two unwilling: Sybrandt, youngest, who loved play more than work; and Cornelis, eldest, who waited patiently for dead men’s shoes.

Tired and disheartened, Elias and Catherine often asked, “What will become of them when we are gone?” But after saying it many times, the clouds lifted. They still said it—but now with a smile. “But thanks to St. Bavon and all the saints—there’s Gerard.”

Young Gerard had long been apart. He was destined for the Church. In those days, the Church could always provide for its own—by hook or by crook. But hopes were low: his family had no influence, and Gerard himself was frivolous, wrapped in books and penmanship. He carried a tinder-box and brimstone matches, begged candle ends from neighbors, and lit them late into the night—even at eight in winter, when the burgomaster was already asleep.

At home, his quirks were tolerated. But the monks of a nearby convent encouraged them. They taught him calligraphy—until one day, during a lesson, they realized he was teaching them. They laughed gently. He blushed. He had suspected it himself, but dared not admit it.

Then an elderly monk said, “My son, why do you not color your writing? A scroll is bare without borders of fruit, leaves, and rich arabesques. Why not paint the holy figures that adorn each chapter? Let the colors soothe the eye, and the saints lift the heart.”

Gerard stammered, “I’ve tried illuminating—but I failed.”

The monk nodded. “Then try again.”

Soon after, a new mentor arrived—an old woman named Margaret, sister of the famous Van Eyck brothers. She left Flanders and returned to her homeland, buying a small house near Tergou. She heard of Gerard and saw his work. Delighted, she sent her servant, Reicht Heynes, to invite him.

Their meeting changed everything. At first, Margaret overwhelmed Gerard with drawings and paintings—some by her own hand—so beautiful they seemed beyond reach. But her kindness kept his spirit alive. She and Reicht drew him out, discovering the real reason he avoided illumination: he couldn’t afford the costly pigments—gold, ultramarine, vermilion. He was afraid to ask his mother.

Then Margaret gave him a brush, vellum, and paints. He nearly worshipped her. As he left, Reicht ran after him with a candle and two quarters. He kissed her hand.

But more than the materials, it was the understanding that mattered. For in that age, art was not divided from craft, and artists sought each other out. Like early Christians, they found kinship in shared passion.

With Margaret’s guidance and the monks’ trust, Gerard flourished. His confidence grew. He still lagged at meals when caught in the midst of an initial G, but once seated, he sparkled—his wit sharp, his humor bold. He brought laughter to the table, quoting ancient jests unknown to his family.

To repay the monks, he copied two of their finest manuscripts: the life of their founder and Terence’s comedies—on fine vellum, at their expense.

Meanwhile, the mighty Prince Philip “the Good” ruled with flair. Duke of Burgundy, Count of Flanders, Lord of Friesland—he was a man of many passions. He fought bravely, lied skillfully, hunted fiercely, and loved jewels, fine clothes, and beautiful women. He adored paintings—so much so that he ennobled Jan Van Eyck. He also loved giants, dwarfs, and Turks. The latter he brought from Istanbul with promises, then baptized them by force, letting them pray to Mahound while laughing inwardly at their ignorance.

He kept lions in cages, leopards trained to hunt, and delighted in anything rare—except simple virtue. But he had two virtues: generosity to the poor, and genuine patronage of the arts.

Now he announced a contest: prizes for the finest works in orfevrerie, painting, glasswork, illumination, and calligraphy. Towns were to send entries from the poor, at public expense.

When the bellman rang through Tergou, a thousand voices stirred—one heart beat wildly: Gerard’s.

He told his family timidly he would enter two categories. They stared—speechless. Then a roar erupted from the floor. Giles, the dwarf, split open with laughter, his voice thundering like a bassoon. Nature had made him small, but gifted him with the loudest voice ever heard.

Gerard flushed. Others tittered. White Catherine looked up, her cheeks pink. “Why laugh?” she said softly. “Because he is our brother, you think he cannot succeed? Yes, Gerard—try. Many say you are skilled. Mother and I will pray to the Virgin to guide your hand.”

“Thank you, little Kate,” Gerard said. “You pray, and mother buy me vellum and colors.”

“How much?” she asked.

“Two gold crowns.”

“Two crowns!” Catherine shrieked. “When a bushel of rye costs a groat! Spend a month’s food on vanity? The lightning of Heaven would strike me—my children would starve!”

“Oh, Mother,” sighed Little Catherine, pleading.

“It’s hopeless, Kate,” Gerard said sadly. “I’ll have to give it up—or ask Dame Van Eyck. She would give it, but I’m ashamed to take from her again.”

“That’s none of her business,” Catherine snapped. “She has no right to come between me and my son.” And she stormed out.

But Little Catherine smiled. Soon, Catherine returned—calm, tender—with two gold pieces in her hand.

“There,” she said. “You won’t need to ask anyone.”

Gerard hesitated. “One crown will do, Mother. I’ll send my copy of Terence—it’s on fine vellum. I only need six sheets for borders, gold, and colors.”

“Never tyne the ship for lack of tar, Gerard,” she said. “But I’ll keep it in my pocket. Taking from the box feels like drawing blood from my heart. You’ll need it.”

And indeed, when the day came, Gerard longed to go to Rotterdam—to see the Duke, to see the works of others, to learn from defeat. The crown came out easily.

He was soon to be a priest. It seemed fair that he should enjoy the world a little before renouncing it forever.

The night before departure, Margaret Van Eyck asked him to deliver a letter—to Princess Marie, at the Stadthouse in Rotterdam.

On the morning of the prize-giving, Gerard set off in his holiday clothes: a silver-grey doublet, a sleeveless jerkin over it, tight buckskin hose laced to his waist, pointed shoes secured with straps, and flowing hair tied back. On his back, pinned between his shoulders, was his hat—held in place by a purple ribbon knotted across his chest, given him by Little Catherine. From his belt hung his leather wallet.

Near Rotterdam, he grew tired. Then he saw an old man sitting by the roadside, pale and weak, supported by a young woman whose face was full of concern. The townsfolk passed without notice. But Gerard, trained in observation by his art, read their story in their clothes.

The old man wore a fur-trimmed gown and velvet cap—signs of rank—but the purse at his belt was thin, the fur worn, the gown faded. The girl wore plain russet cloth, yet her neck was draped in snow-white lawn, edged with gold embroidery. Her head-dress was new: a silver net with spangles, framing glossy auburn hair rolled into waves. Her eyes held sorrow.

Gerard passed, then stopped. He turned back, bashful.

“Father,” he said, “you seem weary.”

“I am,” the old man replied. “Faint from hunger.”

The girl glanced at him—ashamed. “It was my fault,” she murmured. “I underestimated the distance. I let him start too late.”

“No,” the old man said gently. “It is not the distance. It is hunger.”

She put her arms around his neck, whispering, “Father—a stranger. A young man.”

But it was too late. Gerard, with quiet kindness, began gathering sticks. Then he opened his wallet, pulled out bread and an iron flask, lit a match, then a candle-end, then the fire. He placed the flask on the flames, then lay down—taking a long drink.

As he rose, he saw her face soften. She watched him with a shy smile.

He laughed. “Mind the pot—don’t let it spill. Use the cleft stick to hold it steady.” Then he ran toward a cornfield.

And so, with a heart full of hope and a step lightened by compassion, Gerard continued his journey—toward a future he could not yet foresee.
