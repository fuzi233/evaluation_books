January approached, bringing with it the chill air of the season and the quiet hum of preparation for the holidays. Mrs. Jennings, though she spent much of her time rotating between the homes of her children and old friends, maintained a permanent residence near Portman Square, a cozy retreat where she had lived since her husband's passing. As the frost deepened, she found her thoughts turning inevitably toward this familiar haven. One afternoon, she summoned the older Dashwood sisters, asking them to join her for a winter visit.

Elinor, quick-witted and observant, noticed immediately that her sister's face flushed with excitement, her eyes alight with a fervor that betrayed her eagerness. Yet, without waiting for Marianne to speak, Elinor offered a polite but firm refusal. "We simply cannot leave Mother at this time of year," she declared, believing these words reflected the united sentiment of her family.

Mrs. Jennings, taken aback by the sudden denial, pressed the point again with characteristic warmth. "Oh, Lord!" she exclaimed, shaking her head in disbelief. "Your mother can surely spare you. I have quite set my heart on having you. Do not imagine you will be a burden; I shall send Betty by coach, and I trust I can afford that small luxury. We three shall travel comfortably in my chaise. And once in town, if you prefer not to follow me everywhere, you may stay with my daughters. I have managed to get my own children off my hands, so I am sure your mother will agree that I am the ideal guardian. If I do not secure a marriage for you before I am gone, it shall not be my fault! I shall vouch for you to every eligible young man."

Sir John, overhearing the conversation, chimed in with his usual bluster. "I suspect," he remarked, "that Miss Marianne would welcome such a scheme immensely, provided her elder sister agreed. It is a pity she is denied the pleasures of the city simply because Miss Dashwood prefers to remain here. Why not depart for town when you grow weary of Barton, without consulting Miss Dashwood further?"

"Nonsense!" cried Mrs. Jennings, clapping her hands. "I shall be overjoyed to have Miss Marianne's company, regardless of whether Miss Dashwood accompanies her. Indeed, the trio will be the merriest. They can confide in one another and laugh at my peculiarities behind my back if they tire of me. But I must have at least one of them. Good heavens, how can I endure this solitude, accustomed as I have been to Charlotte's companionship?" She turned to Marianne, extending her hand. "Come, Miss Marianne, let us seal this bargain. If Miss Dashwood changes her mind later, so much the better!"

"I thank you, ma'am, most sincerely," Marianne replied, her voice trembling with emotion. "Your invitation guarantees my eternal gratitude. To accept it would bring me a happiness nearly surpassing anything I have ever known. But my mother... my dearest, kindest mother..." She paused, her expression softening. "I understand Elinor's point, and I feel its justice. If our absence makes her unhappy or uncomfortable, I cannot do it. Oh, no! Nothing could tempt me to leave her. It must not be a struggle."

Mrs. Jennings reassured them again that Mrs. Dashwood could easily spare them, and the atmosphere shifted. Elinor, who now fully grasped the depth of her sister's longing, saw how Willoughby's return loomed large in Marianne's mind, driving her to seek proximity with anyone connected to him. Seeing the futility of further direct opposition, Elinor deferred the decision to their mother, though she harbored doubts. She knew Mrs. Dashwood's nature: a woman who eagerly promoted whatever her daughters desired, lacking the caution necessary to guide Marianne wisely in matters of the heart. Furthermore, Elinor could not reveal her own reservations regarding the trip, particularly given Marianne's likely inability to tolerate Mrs. Jennings' manners—a fact that pained Elinor to witness, knowing how deeply those social graces wounded Marianne's sensitive spirit.

When informed of the invitation, Mrs. Dashwood, convinced that the journey would prove delightful for both girls and seeing the light in Marianne's eyes, refused to hear of any decline on her account. With her usual cheerfulness, she envisioned the myriad advantages of the separation. "How delighted I am!" she cried. "Margaret and I shall thrive just as much as you. When you and the Middletons depart, we shall enjoy our books and music in peaceful tranquility. You will find Margaret far improved by the time you return. Besides, I have planned alterations to your bedrooms that can now be executed without inconvenience. It is imperative that you acquaint yourselves with the manners and amusements of London. You will be under the care of a kind, motherly soul, and I have no doubt of her goodwill. Moreover, you will likely see your brother. Despite any faults Mr. Ferrars or his wife may possess, I cannot bear to see you so estranged from your own family."

"Though you act with your customary concern for our happiness," Elinor interjected gently, "there remains an objection, in my view, that cannot be easily dismissed."

Marianne's countenance fell instantly.

"And what, pray, is my prudent daughter suggesting?" Mrs. Dashwood asked, feigning curiosity. "What formidable obstacle blocks our path? Do not speak of expenses."

"My concern lies with Mrs. Jennings herself," Elinor explained steadily. "While her heart is kind, I fear her company offers little genuine pleasure, and her protection affords us little real consequence."

"That is true enough," her mother conceded, "yet separately from others, you will have little to engage you, and you will almost always be seen in public with Lady Middleton."

"If Elinor is deterred by her distaste for Mrs. Jennings," Marianne protested, "it need not stop me from accepting. I harbor no such scruples. I am certain I could endure every unpleasantness with minimal effort."

Elinor suppressed a smile at her sister's display of indifference toward social decorum, a trait she had often struggled to temper. Resolving internally that she would not abandon Marianne to the sole guidance of her own impulsive judgment, nor leave Mrs. Jennings unchallenged, she decided to accompany them. This resolve was bolstered by the knowledge that Edward Ferrars, according to Lucy Steele, would not arrive in town until February, allowing ample time for their visit to conclude without unreasonable delay.

"I insist that you *both* go," Mrs. Dashwood declared, dismissing Elinor's concerns as nonsensical. "You will find immense pleasure in London, and especially in each other's company. If Elinor could anticipate enjoyment, she would discover sources of delight there, perhaps even in expanding her acquaintance with her sister-in-law's circle."

Elinor had long hoped to subtly undermine her mother's blind reliance on Edward Ferrars and Lucy Steele, preparing a gentle shock for when the full truth emerged. Seizing this rare opening, despite feeling it was a desperate gambit, she spoke as calmly as possible. "I hold Edward Ferrars in high esteem and shall always welcome his presence. However, regarding the rest of the family, it is a matter of perfect indifference to me whether I am ever introduced to them or not."

Mrs. Dashwood smiled enigmatically, offering no comment, while Marianne gazed upward in astonishment. Elinor felt a pang of regret, realizing she might have remained silent.

Ultimately, the invitation was accepted. Mrs. Jennings rejoiced, showering them with promises of kindness and care, a sentiment echoed by Sir John, who delighted in the prospect of two new companions to alleviate his fears of loneliness. Even Lady Middleton feigned delight, and the Miss Steeles, particularly Lucy, beamed with anticipation.

Elinor yielded to the arrangement with less resistance than she had anticipated. Regarding her own attendance, she felt a growing sense of unconcern; yet, witnessing her mother's evident satisfaction and Marianne's radiant excitement—the restoration of her usual animation, elevated to a heightened gaiety—Elinor could not begrudge the cause. She hesitated to distrust the consequences.

Marianne's joy bordered on ecstatic perturbation, her spirits soaring with an impatience to depart. Only the thought of leaving her mother served as an anchor for her tumultuous emotions, and at the moment of parting, her grief was profound. Mrs. Dashwood shared this sorrow, though Elinor remained the only one among them who viewed the separation as anything less than a permanent exile.

The journey commenced in the first week of January. The Middletons followed shortly after, while the Miss Steeles remained stationed at the park, departing only when the entire party returned.
