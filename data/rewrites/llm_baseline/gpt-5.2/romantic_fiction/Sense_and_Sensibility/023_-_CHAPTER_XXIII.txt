Elinor had never been lavish of belief where Lucy Steele was concerned. Even in the first heat of that morning’s disclosure, her mind—trained to test and weigh—had reached instinctively for some seam, some loosened thread by which the whole might be unpicked. Yet, when the first tumult had abated and she could sit alone with the matter, she found no such thread.

For what temptation could invite a falsehood so elaborate, so perilous, and so very much against the teller’s ease? Lucy had not spoken with the heedless extravagance of a romancer; she had produced circumstance, time, place—Mr. Pratt’s house, Plymouth, a visit, a melancholy that had once seemed to Elinor only a general dissatisfaction. Then came the small, damning proofs—knowledge the Miss Steeles should not have possessed of Norland and its connexions; a picture remembered; a letter; a ring. Each item, like a pinch of salt cast into water, vanished, and yet altered the whole. Nothing contradicted it, except the desire in Elinor’s own breast that it might be untrue.

It settled, at last, into the shape of fact; and with it came, sharp and clean, the thought that Edward had behaved ill by her. Indignation rose first—an almost selfish sting, that she had been drawn on, that she had listened, that she had been made, unwittingly, ridiculous. For a little while she could feel only this, as one feels the first bite of winter air before the lungs have learned its lesson.

But other questions followed fast, each one less angry and more wounding than the last. Had he meant to deceive? Had he put on a tenderness he did not feel? Was Lucy’s engagement an engagement of the heart?

No. Whatever Edward might once have fancied at nineteen, she could not—she dared not—think it so now. His affection was hers. It was not vanity that told her so. At Norland it had been visible—not to herself alone, but to her mother, her sisters, even to Fanny with all her watchfulness for slights. Edward’s manner had been too consistent, too involuntary, to be an invention. He loved her.

The persuasion softened her as warmth softens hardened butter—still firm, still itself, but yielding at the edges. It tempted her to forgiveness even while it sharpened the sense of his fault. He had been blameable, highly blameable, in remaining at Norland after he had begun to feel her influence; in suffering himself to linger where he ought, in honour, to have fled. That could not be excused.

And yet—if she was injured, how much more was he? Her misery might be conquered by time, by habit, by effort; but what prospect had he? His imprudence had cost her a season of anguish; it seemed to have cost him the whole shape of his future.

She found herself, with an unwilling minuteness, imagining what Lucy Steele would be as a wife—not in the broad, easy caricature her resentment would have liked, but in particular traits that offended Edward’s nature. Illiterate; artful; selfish. At nineteen, beauty and a seeming good humour might have dazzled him, might have seemed to answer all. But four years—four years which, if soberly spent, enlarge the understanding—must have opened his eyes. While he had improved (for he had improved; she knew it in his conversation, his delicacy, his informed mind), Lucy’s time in inferior society and frivolous pursuits might have rubbed away whatever simplicity had once made her pretty face more interesting than pretty. Even if Edward’s affection for Elinor were put aside entirely, could a man like him be satisfied with a woman like Lucy?

And then, as if her imagination were bent on cruel accuracy, she added the likely opposition of his family. If his mother had been severe at the thought of a match with Elinor—superior as Elinor must be in every thing that such people count—how much more severe would she be when the object was plainly inferior in connexions, and probably in fortune too? Difficulties might, in some temperaments, give energy; in Edward they would give only a mournful resignation. What a bleak relief it is, to a melancholy mind, to foresee opposition—because opposition at least is movement, while compliance would only bind him faster to what he dreaded.

As these reflections passed through her, one by one, like carts on a distant road that one hears before one sees, she wept—not for herself, but for him. It was a strange reversal, yet perfectly natural to her: supported by the conviction that she had done nothing to deserve such pain, and consoled by believing that Edward had done nothing to forfeit her esteem, she could still pity him as the greater sufferer.

And she resolved—instantly, quietly—that her mother and sisters should not know.

The necessity of silence, so far from being an added burden, came as a kind of mercy. To tell Mrs. Dashwood would be to pour out sorrow upon sorrow; to tell Marianne would be to invite that impassioned condemnation of Edward which Elinor felt she could not bear to hear. Their tenderness would only increase the distress which she must already endure; their example would not strengthen her self-command, and their praise of it would but make her feel the strain more.

She was, in truth, stronger alone.

So it was that when she joined them at dinner—only two hours after the first blow had extinguished, as by a sudden gust, all her dearest hopes—no one, from Elinor’s face or voice, could have supposed her to be secretly mourning an obstacle that must separate her for ever from the man she loved; nor, from Marianne’s glowing expectation, that her whole heart was dwelling upon the perfections of a man she believed entirely her own, and whom she looked for in every carriage that rolled within hearing of their gate.

Yet Elinor’s mind was not at rest. Much as she had suffered in that first conversation with Lucy, she soon felt an eager wish to renew it. She wanted to hear the particulars again—not because repetition would make them less true, but because the mind, when wounded, tries to map the wound. She wanted to understand more clearly what Lucy felt for Edward: whether there was any sincerity in her protestations of tenderness, or only the hard glitter of possession. And, above all, she wished to convince Lucy—by a calmness she had not fully commanded in the morning—that she was interested in the affair only as a friend. She feared her involuntary agitation had betrayed too much.

Lucy’s jealousy, indeed, was almost certain. Edward must have spoken of Elinor with warmth; not only did Lucy herself assert it, but her very act of disclosure proved it. Why else reveal a secret so important to a near stranger, unless to establish her own claim and warn Elinor away? Even Sir John’s careless jesting hints—those loud, bustling words that make sport of what they cannot measure—might have pricked Lucy into action.

Elinor understood her rival’s intention without difficulty. And though she was firmly resolved to act by every principle of honour—combating her own affection, and seeing Edward as little as possible—she could not deny herself the comfort of trying to appear unwounded. If Lucy could be persuaded that Elinor’s heart was safe, perhaps Lucy’s triumph would be less sharpened into malice.

She trusted, too, that nothing more painful remained to be heard. She had already swallowed the bitterest draught; a second taste might be endured with composure.

But opportunity did not readily offer itself. The weather was rarely fine enough for long walks in which two might drop behind the rest. And though they met at least every other evening—sometimes at the Cottage, more often at the Park—those meetings were not made for conversation. Sir John and Lady Middleton assembled people for the same reasons they set out refreshments: for noise, for movement, for occupation. Leisure, in their world, was something to be filled at once with eating, drinking, laughter, cards, consequences, and any game that made enough clamour to drown thought. No one, least of all Sir John, would have imagined any meeting could occur for the sake of a quiet chat; and thus there was little general discourse and none at all for private.

One or two such bustling evenings passed, leaving Elinor still without her chance, when Sir John appeared at the Cottage one morning with an urgency that he called charity.

They must all dine at the Park that day, he said; he was obliged to attend the club at Exeter, and Lady Middleton would otherwise be quite alone—except, indeed, for her mother and the two Miss Steeles. He spoke as if those three were a kind of emptiness that required a remedy.

Elinor, foreseeing that a party without Sir John’s boisterous direction might be quieter, more at liberty under Lady Middleton’s tranquil, well-bred governance, accepted at once. Margaret, with her mother’s permission, was equally ready. Marianne, who was always reluctant to join these visits, resisted in her usual way, but yielded to Mrs. Dashwood’s entreaties; her mother could not bear to see her shut herself away from any chance of amusement, however small.

So they went; and Lady Middleton was, as Sir John had wished, preserved from the solitude she dreaded.

The meeting proved exactly what Elinor had expected—insipid to the last degree. Not one new idea, not one lively turn of expression, enlivened their talk in dining parlour or drawing-room. When they withdrew, the children came with them; and with so many small voices, little feet, and Lady Middleton’s continual attention to nursery details, Elinor knew it would be useless to attempt fixing Lucy’s mind upon any subject of consequence.

At last the tea-things were removed. The room seemed to breathe out, as a kitchen does when the last pan is set aside and the coals settle. A card-table was brought in; and Elinor began to wonder at herself for ever imagining that the Park could furnish time for confidential conversation.

They all rose, preparing for a round game, when Lady Middleton, turning to Lucy, said, “I am glad you are not going to finish poor little Annamaria’s basket this evening; for I am sure it must hurt your eyes to work filigree by candlelight. We will make the dear little love some amends to-morrow, and then I hope she will not much mind it.”

The hint was enough. Lucy recollected herself instantly—quick as a cook who hears the step of the mistress of the house and at once arranges her face as carefully as her dish.

“Indeed you are very much mistaken, Lady Middleton,” she replied. “I am only waiting to know whether you can make your party without me; or I should have been at my filigree already. I would not disappoint the little angel for all the world: and if you want me at the card-table now, I am resolved to finish the basket after supper.”

“You are very good,” said Lady Middleton, pleased. “I hope it won’t hurt your eyes. Will you ring for some working candles? My poor little girl would be sadly disappointed, I know, if the basket was not finished to-morrow; for though I told her it certainly would not, I am sure she depends upon having it done.”

Lucy drew her work-table nearer, and reseated herself with an alacrity so studied, so bright, it was meant to signify that no greater delight could be imagined than weaving pale strips of paper into a little filigree basket for a spoiled child.

Lady Middleton proposed a rubber of Casino to the others. No one objected but Marianne, who, with that heedlessness of form which sprang in her from sincerity rather than intention to offend, exclaimed, “Your Ladyship will have the goodness to excuse me—you know I detest cards. I shall go to the piano-forte; I have not touched it since it was tuned.”

And without further ceremony, she crossed the room and sat down at the instrument.

Lady Middleton’s look was a silent thanksgiving that she had never uttered so rude a speech.

Elinor, anxious to smooth the offence, said gently, “Marianne can never keep long from that instrument, ma’am, you know; and I do not much wonder at it—for it is the very best toned piano-forte I ever heard.”

The remaining five drew their cards. Elinor, seeing her chance, continued with an ease meant to seem accidental, “Perhaps, if I should happen to cut out, I may be of some use to Miss Lucy Steele, in rolling her papers for her. There is so much still to be done to the basket, that it must be impossible, I think, for her to finish it this evening singly. I should like the work exceedingly, if she would allow me a share in it.”

“Indeed I shall be very much obliged to you for your help,” cried Lucy, with a quickness that made the gratitude sound like eagerness. “For I find there is more to be done to it than I thought there was; and it would be a shocking thing to disappoint dear Annamaria after all.”

“Oh! that would be terrible indeed,” said Miss Steele. “Dear little soul, how I do love her!”

“You are very kind,” Lady Middleton said to Elinor. “And as you really like the work, perhaps you will be as well pleased not to cut in till another rubber—or will you take your chance now?”

Elinor—joyfully profiting by the proposal, and by a little address which Marianne would never have practised—gained her end and pleased Lady Middleton too. Lucy made room with ready attention; and thus the two rivals were seated side by side at the same table, and, with the utmost harmony of hands, engaged in forwarding the same delicate labour—rolling, folding, fitting, the dry whisper of paper-splints passing between their fingers like a restrained kind of speech.

Marianne, meanwhile, had forgotten that any one was in the room besides herself. The piano-forte—new-tuned, clear-voiced—filled the air with sound. Notes rose and fell like birds over a quiet field; they covered, mercifully, the smaller noises of the room, and made a sheltered space in which words might be spoken and not carried.

Elinor, feeling that shelter, judged she might now, under the screen of Marianne’s music, venture at last to introduce the subject that lay like an undissolved bitterness at the bottom of her heart—without risk of being heard at the card-table.
