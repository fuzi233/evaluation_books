It has been nearly a year since the events of the previous chapter unfolded. Time, that relentless editor, has made significant revisions to Arthur Pendennis's appearance. The somber black coat has been retired in favor of a cheerful blue; his wig, once a mandatory accessory, has been discarded, revealing hair that, while slightly thinned, proudly claims public attention once more. Most notably, Arthur has achieved the distinction of appearing at Court in the uniform of a Cornet in the Clavering troop of the ———-shire Yeomanry Cavalry, presented to the Sovereign by the Marquis of Steyne. This elevation was vigorously, almost tearfully, advocated by Arthur's uncle, Major Pendennis. The Major simply refused to let a single year pass without this ceremonial stamp of "gentlemanhood."

To the Major, his nephew belonged in far more exclusive circles than the Megatherium Club. Consequently, he has broadcast across the globe his profound disappointment regarding the yield of Arthur's inheritance. Let it be recorded officially: the world now values Pendennis's estate at just under £1,500 per annum. At this valuation, publishers treat him with newfound respect, and mothers cease to be entirely uncivil. Why? Because if the dazzling daughters of high expectation are destined to marry Earls, then the plain ones—like Beatrice, who possesses a slight asymmetry in her shoulders—are perfectly suited to secure a provider. Thus, Mr. Pendennis becomes the logical anchor for Beatrice's future.

Indeed, the Major's foresight proved prescient. In the first winter following his mother's acquisition of wealth, Mrs. Hawxby hosted a country gathering where she insisted Beatrice learn billiards from Mr. Pendennis and be driven exclusively by him in her pony carriage. Her rationale? They were both "literary" souls. She loudly proclaimed that the young man, goaded by his "horrid old uncle," had behaved infamously by trifling with Beatrice's affections. The truth, however, was that the Major, possessing a keen understanding of Mrs. Hawxby's predatory nature toward unwary youths, had spirited Arthur away from her immediate grasp at that very country house. He saved him from her claws, if not from her sharp tongue. While the elder Pendennis had wished for Arthur to spend Christmas at Clavering, the young man lacked the heart for it; the proximity to the haunted grounds of Fairoaks was too laden with sorrow.

Meanwhile, the Clavering family re-emerged on the Epsom racecourse, having vanished from the narrative for a year. During this interim, fate had dealt them a series of cruel hands. Lady Clavering, a woman of exceptional natural goodness and a palate for fine dining, found her appetite and temper severely tested by endless domestic squabbles. "I'd sooner eat a turnip for dessert than that pineapple and those Muscatel grapes from Clavering," she lamented to her loyal companion, staring mournfully at the table. "If only I could enjoy my meal in peace. How vastly happier I was when I was a widow, before this mountain of money descended upon us!"

The Claverings had launched their social career with a disastrous stumble, offering hospitality without gaining gratitude, conducting themselves without earning respect. Their initial London season was shaky, followed by a notorious collapse. Gossip ran rampant: "Human patience cannot endure Sir Francis Clavering," the townspeople whispered. "He is hopelessly low, dull, and disreputable. There is a rotting taint about the house. Who is that Begum with her money and missing 'h's'? What a pretentious little creature is the daughter, with her affected French manners, unfit for proper English company! And what strange creatures surround them!"

Sir Francis was labeled a gambler, notorious for keeping the company of blacklegs and profligates. Even Hely Clinker, serving in his regiment, claimed the Baronet cheated at cards and displayed the "white feather." Lady Rockminster, initially supportive, soon cast Lady Clavering aside. High-society ladies stopped bringing their daughters to her gatherings; the young men attending behaved with shocking familiarity and disdain. Poor Lady Clavering admitted she was forced to admit "the canal"—the lower-class visitors—into her drawing room, as the elite refused to come. Yet, she harbored no malice toward them, nor did she feel superior to her neighbors; she had merely followed the social dictates handed down by her godmother. It is easier to abandon society than to be abandoned by it.

Among the faithless, one figure remained steadfast: Harry Foker, Esq. However, his management of his affections lacked prudence. His secret passion for Pen became public knowledge, reaching the ears of his weak mother and eventually landing on the desk of the stern, bald-headed Foker Senior. The resulting confrontation was violent and painful, ending with Harry's banishment from England for a year. He was ordered to return and either marry his cousin or retire to private life on a mere £300 a year, forever barred from seeing his parents or brewery. Harry departed with the characteristic gloom of an exile, finding even the Parisian Boulevards and the Italian skies bleak beneath his crape-covered eyes.

For Sir Francis Clavering, that same year was catastrophic. As sportsmen recall, Lord Harrowhill's horse, *Podasokus* (named after a hero of the *Iliad*), won the Derby, stunning the experts who had backed the non-factor, *Borax*. Sir Francis, intimately connected with the scoundrels of the turf and boasting of "valuable inside information," had heavily wagered against the winner and supported the favorite. The result, as his son later confessed to Lady Clavering, was a staggering loss of seven thousand pounds.

This was a devastating blow to the lady, who had repeatedly bailed out her husband's debts, paid his lenders and horse dealers, and furnished both town and country homes. Now, she faced the immediate demand for this colossal sum, the price tag for her husband's cowardice.

Amidst this chaos, Major Pendennis stepped in as the family's advisor. Having toured every room of Clavering Hall—including the dreaded closet where families lock their skeletons—he discovered that the Baronet hid his financial entanglements so deeply within a web of lies that no lawyer or accountant could untangle them. However, the Major knew Lady Clavering's situation intimately. Upon hearing of the Derby disaster, he meticulously audited her remaining resources, uncovering the vast sacrifices the widow Amory had made for her second husband.

The Major did not hide his opinion: Lady Clavering's daughter, Blanche, had been unfairly treated financially by her second marriage. He gently suggested she deserved a better provision. Revealing his knowledge of the Begum's tragic past in India—including the details of her first husband's trial, which he had deliberately ignored to remain a friend—he leveraged this goodwill.

"My dear Lady Clavering," the Major declared, "I may have interested motives. We all do. Mine is clear: to arrange a marriage between my nephew and your daughter."

Lady Clavering, surprised yet open, agreed. But the Major pressed further. "Frankly, my lady, my boy has only five hundred a year. A wife with ten thousand pounds would barely improve his lot. We could do better for him. He is a shrewd, ambitious young man who has sown his wild oats. His goal is advancement. If you and Sir Francis cooperate—and trust me, the Baronet will refuse nothing—you can propel Arthur significantly forward. What use is that empty seat in Parliament to Clavering, who rarely appears or speaks? Gentlemen tell me my boy was famous as an orator at Oxford! Give him a start, good friends, and an opportunity, and he will make a name his sons will boast of.

"There is no path for a man like him except through a prudent marriage—not with a penniless heiress to stagnate on fifteen hundred a year, but with someone he can assist, who can in turn elevate him. You deserve a distinguished son-in-law rather than keeping your husband in Parliament, where he achieves nothing. That is why I am involved; I believe this is a bargain beneficial to both."

The Begum, touched by the Major's candor, reflected on Arthur's integration into the family. "I look upon him almost as kin," she said. "He comes and goes as he pleases. I wept when I heard of his mother's death; I would have worn mourning, only black does not suit me. I know who his mother wanted him to marry: Laura, favored by old Lady Rockminster. She is a finer girl than my Blanche. And my Blanche... she hasn't been much comfort, Major. It is Laura Pen who should wed."

"Marry on five hundred a year?" the Major exclaimed, incredulous. "My dear soul, you are mad! Consider what I've said. Never act in your affairs without consulting me. Remember, old Pendennis is always your friend."

Previously, the Major had shared these sentiments with Miss Amory. He argued passionately that mutual utility was the bedrock of a happy marriage. "Observe love matches," he lectured. "They are the quickest to quarrel. A girl who elopes to Gretna Green often flees to Switzerland next. The essence of marriage is agreement on usefulness. The lady provides the capital; the gentleman utilizes it. My boy's wife brings the horse; I guarantee he will win the plate. That is a sensible union. Such couples have substance to discuss.
