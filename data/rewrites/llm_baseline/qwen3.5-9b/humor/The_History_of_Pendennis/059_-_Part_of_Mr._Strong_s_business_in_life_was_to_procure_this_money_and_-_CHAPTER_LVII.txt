Major Pendennis kept his word to Warrington just enough to clear his own conscience and spare Helen's heart. He assured her that the connection between her son, Arthur, and the detestable little gatekeeper was severed forever, freeing her from fear of an imprudent romance or a humiliating match for "Pen." Young Arthur, too, found relief once his vanity healed; he reasoned that Miss Fanny wouldn't die of unrequited love and that their brief, unfortunate liaison would bear no bad fruit.

With these anxieties lifted, the entire party set sail for their planned continental journey. Arthur Pendennis, *rentier* and *voyageant*, accompanied by Madame Pendennis and Mademoiselle Bell, joined George Warrington, described in the passport as a particular of thirty-two years, six feet tall (English), ordinary features, black hair, and a matching beard. They secured passports from the Belgian consul at Dover and crossed to Ostend. From there, they leisurely toured Bruges and Ghent on their way to Brussels and the Rhine. We shall skip the details of this well-worn tour, Laura's delight in ancient cities, Helen's awe of the Beguine convents, and her near-terror at seeing black-veiled nuns kneel before illuminated altars, defying the laws she held dear. The sight of barefoot friars, crowned saints worshipped in open defiance of statute, priests in gorgeous robes hiding in dark confessionals, and theaters open on Sundays overwhelmed the simple country lady.

When the young men returned from their evening drives, they found Helen's books of devotion spread on the table. Laura would usually stop reading the Psalms—which Helen loved above all else—as soon as they entered. The recent events had cruelly shaken Helen. Laura watched her friend with intense, hidden anxiety, while poor Pen remained constant and affectionate. Yet, a secret hung between them. Helen's wounded bosom yearned for her son, even as she harbored a secret anguish and rage at feeling dispossessed of his heart. She missed the days when Arthur's heart had no secrets, when she was his universe, when he poured his hopes, childhood griefs, and vanities into her tender embrace. Now, fate and selfishness had driven him forth on wayward wings to sing his own song and seek his own mate.

Watching Laura's devouring care and racking disappointment, the younger lady once confessed, "If Pen had loved me as you wished, I should have gained him, but I should have lost you, Mamma. I know I should. And I like you to love me best. Men do not know what it is to love as we do." Helen sighed, agreeing with this sentiment while protesting the rest. In truth, Miss Laura was right: love is an hour for men, but an eternity for women. Damon has his taxes, sermons, and tailors' bills; Delia has only Damon. He is the sturdy oak standing firm; she is the ivy clinging to his trunk, kissing his feet and hanging from his branches. Is it not so, Delia? Is it not your nature to cling to him while he stands with his hands in his pockets?

Old Pendennis saw his friends off at the water's edge, handing command of the expedition to Warrington. He intended a brief visit to a great friend's house before joining his sister-in-law at the German spa. Believing his long care for his sick family entitled him to relaxation, the Major headed to Stillbrook. Despite the thinning of the partridge population, the pheasants were still plentiful. Surrounded by a Royal Duke, foreign dignitaries, and illustrious statesmen, the old fellow felt his heart swell. Seeing his name listed in the *Morning Post* among the Marquis of Steyne's distinguished guests delighted him. He was invaluable at a country house: entertaining young men with bawdy anecdotes, flattering ladies in the morning rooms, guiding tourists through the park, pointing out the best views of the lake, explaining where timber was to be felled, and recounting local legends like the discovery of Sir Phelim O'Neal. He knew every domestic's name and counted the diners in the servants' hall. Richly deserving of his vacation, he enjoyed every moment, perhaps secretly pleased to transfer the burden of managing the ladies to Warrington—a servitude George gladly accepted for his friend's sake and the allure of the society.

Warrington, a proficient German scholar, taught Miss Laura the language, while Pen, too weary or lazy to study, relied on Warrington as courier, interpreter, and manager of baggage and finances. Warrington ensured the ladies attended the English church with decorum, walked beside Helen's donkey during evening strolls, procured newspapers like *Galignani*, and arranged comfortable seats under lime trees. Many a dashing Prussian or Frenchman, drawn to the pretty, fresh-faced English girl accompanying the pale widow, longed to dance with her. But Laura rarely appeared in the ballroom unless Pen escorted her. As for Warrington, the rough diamond lacked the polish of a dancing master and couldn't waltz, though he envied the chance to try with such a partner. Such a partner! Pshaw, what had a stiff bachelor to do with waltzing? Why drink in sweet pleasures risking future sadness and loneliness? Yet, he stayed. To observers, he seemed the widow's devoted son, a selfless guardian, or perhaps an adventurer eyeing her fortune. Likely, he desired some great benefit from her. After all, this is a selfish story, and everyone, however generous, is ultimately occupied with Number One. Warrington selfishly devoted himself to Helen, who selfishly devoted herself to Pen, who was currently selfishly devoted to himself, save for his genuine worry about his mother's health. Though they sat together, silence and a heavy cloud separated them.

Laura grew increasingly eager to see Warrington, welcoming him with frank enthusiasm. He found himself speaking to her with a fluency he hadn't known he possessed, performing acts of gallantry that later astonished him. Gazing in the mirror, he noticed crow's-feet around his eyes, streaks of white in his hair, and silver bristles in his beard. He watched the young bucks, the tight-waisted Germans, the capering Frenchmen with lacquered mustaches, and the English dandies like Pen, with their domineering airs. He envied their youth and beauty. Every night, he left the little circle with growing reluctance, retreating to his lodging to feel lonelier and unhappier. Helen couldn't miss his attachment. She now understood why Major Pendennis, her usual opponent, had insisted on Warrington joining them. Laura openly declared her enthusiastic regard for him. Arthur made no move to intervene; he chose not to see what was happening, neither preventing nor encouraging it. He recalled saying he couldn't understand how a man proposed to a woman twice. Helen was in torment, secretly feuding with her dearest son, doubting Laura, and averse to Warrington despite his goodness. No wonder the healing waters of Rosenbad failed her, nor did Dr. von Glauber find her improving. Meanwhile, Pen recovered rapidly, sleeping twelve hours a day and eating huge meals, regaining his strength within two months.

Fifteen days into their rest, a letter arrived from Major Pendennis announcing his imminent arrival at Rosenbad. Soon after, the Major appeared, accompanied by his faithful valet, Morgan, without whom he could not move. Traveling light and jaunty, he looked remarkably young, though his ancient knees betrayed him only when he moved, impeding the action of his natty varnished boots. Magnates from Britain and abroad gathered that autumn. The elder Pendennis read the guest list with gratification, planning to introduce his nephew to a German Grand Duchess, a Russian Princess, and an English Marquis. That same evening, leaning on his nephew's arm, the Major entered the Kursaal halls, winning or losing a few napoleons at *Trente-et-quarante*. He claimed he played neither to win nor lose, simply following fashion. He pointed out Russians and Spaniards gambling greedily, denouncing their behavior as sordid, while praising the English gentleman's ability to remain unmoved by fortune's swings. He recounted how the Marquis of Steyne had lost eighteen thousand pounds in a sitting without a flicker of emotion. "That's what I call being an English gentleman," he boasted, warming to his tale. As Russian princesses, whose reputations were long past doubt, passed him, the Major eagerly shared scandalous details of their lives with his nephew. When Princess Obstropski recognized him, smiling broadly with her enormous chasseur trailing behind, the Major remembered her as an actress in Paris in '14, married to the Emperor's aide-de-camp. He politely introduced his nephew to her and pointed out other famous figures. Poor Helen, if she had heard these tales or seen the company her brother-in-law presented her son to, would have been horrified. Once, passing the green gaming tables where croupiers shouted "Rouge gagne!" she had shrunk in terror, imploring Pen to never gamble. The scene frightened the simple widow but thrilled the worldly veteran, breathing the cheerful air that stifled her. To his credit, Pen kept his promise to his mother, firmly telling his uncle he would abide by it.

However, the Major's presence cast a dampening shadow over the party. Laura held no respect for him; Warrington met him with involuntary haughtiness; and the timid widow dreaded his interference with her plans for her son. Unknowingly, the Major carried tidings destined to cause a catastrophe.
