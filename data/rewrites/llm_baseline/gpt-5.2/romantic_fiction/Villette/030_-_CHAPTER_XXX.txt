You are not to conclude—because a man has once been gentle—that gentleness will henceforth sit upon him like a settled habit, and make his daily temper smooth as kneaded dough. M. Paul’s nature had its own sharp leaven. He was little, yet full of combustibles; often over-wrought, and then irritability rose in him like heat in an over-stoked oven. Worse than mere quickness, there ran in his veins a tincture of jealousy—no soft jealousy of the heart only, but that narrower, head-bound mistrust which watches, calculates, and, when it cannot understand, suspects.

Sometimes, as I sat at my desk under his eye, and saw him knit his brow over an exercise that did not contain half the faults he desired, I thought of Napoleon. The comparison came, not from grandeur, but from a certain shameless impatience of magnanimity. M. Paul could quarrel with a whole city of learned women and never feel his dignity the poorer for it. A knot of blunders pleased him as a cluster of nuts pleases a squirrel: he liked something hard to crack; he liked to hear it crack.

I remember clearly the hot passage he had with Madame Panache, a woman hired for a time by Madame Beck to teach history. She was clever in the way of those who can marshal facts and make them glitter by arrangement; words obeyed her as kitchen implements obey a practiced hand—ready, bright, clattering. Her person was robust and ample; many would have called her “a fine woman.” Yet there were points in her bustling presence that affronted M. Paul’s capricious sense like a harsh note in a quiet room.

It was the sound first. Her voice, flung up the carré, echoed from stone to door to stair-rail, loud as a market-call; it struck him the way iron-shod wheels strike a narrow street—setting one’s teeth on edge. Her step, too—long, free, almost a stride along the corridor—made him snatch up his papers and vanish, as if mere hearing her approach fouled his air.

One day, with a malicious relish, he thrust himself into her class. In a moment—quick as flame runs along a spill—he had caught her method, and found it differed from a pet plan of his own. With little ceremony and less courtesy, he pointed out what he called her errors. Perhaps he expected her to fold like softened pastry; instead she rose, acrid and splendidly affronted, and reprimanded him roundly for interference.

He might still have withdrawn with dignity. He did not. He threw down defiance as a gauntlet on stone. Madame Panache—bellicose as Penthesilea—snatched it up. She snapped her fingers in his face; she rushed upon him with a tempest of words. M. Emanuel could be eloquent; Madame Panache was voluble. The establishment rang for days with their antagonism, as if the very walls had been tuned to dispute.

What amazed me was his seriousness. He did not laugh at her wounded amour-propre; he hated her with full intent. He pursued her vindictively and implacably, denying himself rest, denying himself even the calm enjoyment of his meals or his cigar, until she was rooted out of the school. He conquered; but the laurels did not shade him gracefully.

Once I ventured to hint as much. To my surprise he allowed I might be right, yet added that when brought into contact with persons of coarse self-complacency—Madame Panache being his specimen—he could not govern himself; an unspeakable aversion drove him to a war of extermination.

Three months later I heard she had met with reverses and might be truly distressed for want of employment. Then, with the same energy he had used to expel, he used to assist: he forgot his hatred and moved heaven and earth to find her a place. She came to thank him and make up old differences; but ten minutes of the old loud voice, the old forward manner, set his nerves jangling as if a tin pail had been dropped on a flagged floor. He started up and bowed her—rather himself—out of the room.

That love of power, that eager grasp at supremacy, stayed in him. He was not always to be yielded to. Sometimes it was necessary—right—to resist; to stand still, look up into his eyes, and tell him his demands went beyond reason, that his absolutism verged on tyranny.

Nothing, perhaps, disturbed him like the first dawning of talent within his reach. He watched it struggle into life with a scowl; he held back his hand. He would not aid the birth. When the first conflict was over—when he saw breath drawn and life brighten in the eye—he did not yet foster. “Prove yourself true ere I cherish you,” was his ordinance; and he made proof difficult. He strewed thorns, briers, flints; he exacted ordeals without tears, without fear, keeping the austerest watch over the pain-pressed pilgrim. When at last he allowed a rest, he would still prise open the eyelids with pitiless finger and thumb and peer, as if to read the smallest print of Vanity, Pride, or Falsehood in the very tissues of the brain and heart. If he let one sleep, it was only to wake him suddenly for new tests: irksome errands when weary, trials of temper, sense, and health. Only when every severe assay had been applied and endured—when corrosive aquafortis had failed to tarnish the ore—did he stamp it, silent and clouded, with approval.

I speak by knowledge. Until lately M. Paul had not been my professor; he had given me no lessons. But about the time the last chapter closed, he happened to hear me confess an ignorance—arithmetic, I think—which he said would have disgraced a charity-school boy. He took me in hand at once: examined me, found me abundantly deficient, gave me books, appointed tasks. At first he did it with pleasure—almost exultation—condescending to say he believed I was “bonne et pas trop faible,” though, by adverse circumstance, “as yet in a state of wretchedly imperfect mental development.”

The beginning of all effort with me is always a hard crust to break. I never could, even in forming an acquaintance, show average quickness at once. Each new page of my life has opened with a depressing passage. While that passage lasted, M. Paul was kind—very good, very forbearing. He saw the sharp pain and heavy humiliation my own incapacity inflicted. When tears of shame and effort blurred my sight, his eyes would moisten too. Burdened as he was, he would steal half his brief recreation to help me; and his tenderness then was like warm broth to one faint with fasting—simple, sustaining, unexpectedly sweet.

But strange grief: when the heavy dawn began to lift; when my faculties struggled free, and energy came; when I doubled and trebled the tasks he set, thinking to please him—his kindness hardened. The beam in his eyes turned to a spark. He fretted, opposed, curbed me. The more I did, the less content he seemed. Sarcasms—severe enough to amaze and puzzle—harassed me; then came bitter innuendoes against “pride of intellect.” I was threatened, vaguely, with some doom if I trespassed the limits proper to my sex and conceived a contraband appetite for unfeminine knowledge.

Alas, I had no godlike thirst for discovery. If I loved anything, it was because it pleased someone I cared for to see me try. Yet when M. Paul sneered, I wanted those lofty hungers more keenly; injustice stirred ambition; it gave wings. At first the sneer made my heart ache; by-and-by it warmed my blood and drove my pulses faster. Whatever my powers—feminine or not—God had given them; I resolved to be ashamed of no faculty of His bestowal.

The combat grew sharp. I believed I had lost his affection; he treated me strangely. In his most unjust moods he insinuated I had deceived him when I seemed “faible,” that I had feigned incapacity. Then, with a sudden turn, he would accuse me of imitations and impossible plagiarisms—declaring I had extracted pith from books I had never heard of, books which, had I attempted them, would have sent me into a sleep as deep as Eutychus.

Once, when he pressed such an accusation, I rose against him. I gathered an armful of his books from my desk, filled my apron, and poured them in a heap on his estrade at his feet.

“Take them away, M. Paul,” I said, “and teach me no more. I never asked to be made learned; you compel me to feel that learning is not happiness.”

Then I returned to my place, laid my head on my arms, and would not speak to him for two days. He had pained and chagrined me. His affection—when it was sweet—was a new pleasure, dear and incomparable. Now, seeming withdrawn, it made his lessons tasteless. Yet the books were not taken away. They were restored, carefully, to their places; he came as usual. He made his peace somehow—too readily perhaps. I ought to have held out longer, but when he looked kind and good and held out his hand, memory would not keep sharp hold of his oppressive moments. Besides, reconcilement has its own flavour: like bread shared after hunger, it is too easy to accept.

On a certain morning, a message came from my godmother, inviting me to attend a notable lecture in the public rooms before described. Dr. John brought the message himself and delivered it verbally to Rosine, who did not scruple to follow M. Emanuel into the first class and, in his presence, plant herself squarely before my desk, hand in apron-pocket, and repeat it saucily and aloud, ending with: “Qu’il est vraiment beau, Mademoiselle, ce jeune docteur! Quels yeux—quel regard! Tenez! J’en ai le cœur tout ému!”

When she was gone my professor demanded why I suffered “cette fille effrontée, cette créature sans pudeur,” to address me so. I had no soothing reply. Rosine, in whose skull reverence and reserve had been sparingly portioned, spoke thus continually; and besides—what she said of the young doctor was true enough. Graham was handsome; his eyes were fine; his glance had power to thrill. The observation shaped itself into sound before I checked it.

“Elle ne dit que la vérité,” I said.

“Ah! vous trouvez?”

“Mais, sans doute.”

The day’s lesson then was such as made us glad when it ended. At its close the released pupils rushed out, half-trembling, half-exultant. I too was going—when a mandate to remain stopped me. I muttered that I wanted fresh air—the stove glowed, the class was over-heated. An inexorable voice commanded silence; and this salamander—no room ever seemed too hot for him—sat down between my desk and the stove (a place where he ought to have felt broiled, but did not) and confronted me with a Greek quotation.

In M. Emanuel’s soul rankled a chronic suspicion that I knew both Greek and Latin. As some say monkeys can speak if they would but hide the faculty for fear of harm, so he imagined in me a store of knowledge craftily concealed. I was credited with the privileges of a classical education; I had revelled on Hymettus honey; a golden hoard lay hived in memory to sustain my efforts and secretly nourish my wits.

To surprise this supposed secret he tried a hundred expedients: to wheedle, threaten, startle it out of me. Sometimes he placed Greek and Latin books in my way and watched, as if tempting a prisoner with forbidden armour. Sometimes he rolled out sweet, sounding lines—classic tones falling musically from his lips, for his voice was good, wide in compass, rich in modulation, matchless in expression—while he fixed on me a vigilant, piercing, often malicious eye, as though waiting for some start, some glow, some betrayal.

None came. Not understanding, I could neither be charmed nor annoyed. Baffled—almost angered—he still clung to his delusion. My susceptibilities were called marble; my face a mask. It was as if he could not accept the homely truth and take me as I was. People must have some delusion; if it is not given them ready-made, they will invent exaggeration for themselves.

At moments I wished his suspicion were true. There were hours when I would have given my right hand to possess the treasures he ascribed to me—if only to punish his testy crotchets by fulfilling his worst fears with a sudden blaze of acquirements, and crush, coldly and cruelly, the mocking spirit out of Paul Carl David Emanuel. But no such triumph was in my power.

To-day, as usual, the quotations fell harmless. He shifted ground. “Women of intellect” became his theme: here he was at home. A “woman of intellect,” it seemed, was a lusus naturae—an unlucky accident—fit nowhere, useful nowhere, wanted neither as wife nor worker. Beauty forestalled her in the first office. He believed, with his whole soul, that lovely, placid, passive feminine mediocrity was the only pillow on which manly thought could rest its aching temples; and for work, male mind alone could work to any good practical result—hein?

This “hein?” was bait, meant to draw contradiction. I only answered, “Cela ne me regarde pas: je ne m’en soucie pas;” and then—because my body had its own plain claims and my senses, whatever my mind might be, were not made of stone—I asked, “May I go, Monsieur? They have rung the bell for the second déjeuner.”

“What of that? You are not hungry?”

Indeed I was. I had eaten nothing since breakfast at seven; if I missed this bell, I should have nothing till dinner at five. He said he was in the same plight, but I might share with him. He broke in two the brioche meant for his own refreshment and gave me half.

The crumb was tender; it yielded with that faint, sighing tear that fresh buttered bread makes when you part it; the crust carried a delicate sweetness, faintly yeasty, warm with the scent of baked milk and eggs. For all his harshness, there was in that act a kindliness more nourishing than the cake itself. His bark was worse than his bite.

Yet the formidable attack was still to come.

While I ate, I could not forbear confessing my secret wish—that I truly knew all he accused me of knowing. Did I sincerely feel myself an ignoramus? he asked, softened. Had I answered meekly and simply, I believe his hand would have been stretched out and we should have been friends on the spot. But I replied:

“Not exactly. I am ignorant, Monsieur, of what you ascribe to me; but I sometimes— not always—feel a knowledge of my own.”

“What did I mean?” he demanded sharply.

I could not explain in one breath—how a person may know certain inward measures, certain shades of feeling, certain mute recognitions that no grammar names and no lexicon holds; how one’s true dialect is sometimes not words but sensations—heat and cold, hunger and satiety, shame and courage. I evaded by turning to what my senses offered me then.

He had finished his half of the brioche. The fragment had not satisfied me; surely it had not satisfied him. From the refectory, across the court, came a smell—baked apples: the soft, domestic perfume of fruit yielding to heat, sugar melting into syrup, spice lifting in the steam. It was as if the whole building had, for a moment, remembered a farmhouse kitchen—quiet, patient, and humane—where food is not merely swallowed but tended.

I asked if he did not perceive that agreeable odour. He confessed he did. I said, if he would let me out by the garden-door and permit me to run across the court, I would fetch him a plateful; I added that they must be excellent, for Goton had a good method of baking—or rather stewing—fruit: a little spice, sugar, and a glass or two of vin blanc. Might I go?

“Petite gourmande!” he said, smiling. “I have not forgotten how pleased you were with the pâté à la crème I once gave you; and you know very well that to fetch apples for me will be the same as getting them for yourself. Go then, but come back quickly.”

At last he liberated me—on parole.

My plan was simple: go swiftly, return with good faith, set the plate within the door, and vanish at once—leave consequences for later. But his intolerably keen instinct had foreseen me. He met me at the threshold, hurried me back, and fixed me in my seat again as if I were a truant pupil.

He took the plate from my hand—apples split and collapsed into their own amber juice, the skins wrinkled, the flesh all perfume and softness; I could see the grains of sugar clinging, hear the faint click of spoon on china. He divided what had been meant for himself and ordered me to eat my share.

I complied ill; and vexed by my reluctance, he opened a masked, dangerous battery.

He returned to an unreasonable proposition with which he had before afflicted me: that on the next public examination-day I should—foreigner as I was—take my place on the first form among the first-class pupils and improvise, in French, a composition on any subject a spectator might dictate, without grammar or lexicon.

I knew what would follow. Nature had denied me the impromptu faculty. In public I was, by nature, a cypher. Even alone, my best hours were not under the meridian sun: I needed the fresh hush of morning, or the recluse peace of evening, to coax from the Creative Impulse one sign of presence, one proof of force. With me that impulse was the most intractable, capricious, maddening of masters—himself always excepted. Sometimes, under circumstances apparently propitious, it would not speak when questioned, would not hear when appealed to, would not be found when sought: it would stand cold, indurated, granite—a dark Baal with carven lips and blank eyeballs, breast like a tomb’s stone face. Then, suddenly—at some turn, some sound, some long-trembling sob of wind, some rushing past of an unseen current like electricity—it would wake unasked, stir strangely alive, rush from its pedestal like a perturbed Dagon, calling to its votary for sacrifice, for blood, for breath, regardless of hour or scene—promising oracles, filling the temple with a hum of prophecy, yet giving half the meaning to fateful winds and grudging even the desperate listener a miserable remnant, as if each word were a drop of ichor wrung from its own dark veins.

And this tyrant I was to chain—to make it improvise on a school estrade between a Mathilde and a Coralie, under Madame Beck’s eye, for the pleasure of a bourgeois of Labassecour!

On this subject M. Paul and I had battled before—strong battle, with confused noise of demand and rejection, exaction and repulse. To-day he rated me soundly. The obstinacy of my whole sex, he said, was concentrated in me. I had an “orgueil de diable.” I feared to fail. What mattered if I failed? Who was I that I should not fail like my betters? It would do me good to fail. He wanted to see me worsted; I knew it. At last he paused for breath.

Would I speak now and be tractable?

Never. Law itself should not compel me. I would pay a fine, undergo imprisonment, rather than write for show and to order, perched up on a platform.

Could softer motives influence me? Would I yield for friendship’s sake?

Not a whit—not a hair’s breadth. No friendship had the right to exact it; no true friendship would harass thus.

Then, with that supreme sneer of his—lip curling, nostrils opening, eyelids contracting—he said there was but one form of appeal to which I would listen, and of that it was not for him to make use.

“Under certain persuasions, from certain quarters, je vous vois d’ici,” said he, “eagerly subscribing to the sacrifice, passionately arming for the effort.”

Making a simpleton, a warning, an example of myself before a hundred and fifty papas and mammas of Villette.

Patience broke. I cried out that I must be liberated—that I must get into air—I was almost in a fever.

“Chut!” said the inexorable. It was a pretext to run away. He was not hot, with the stove close at his back; how could I suffer, screened by his person?

I told him I did not understand the constitution of salamanders; I knew nothing of their natural history. I was a phlegmatic islander; sitting in an oven did not agree with me. At least might I step to the well for a glass of water? The sweet apples had made me thirsty.

If that was all, he would do my errand.

He rose and went to fetch it.

The door was only on the latch behind me. I did not waste the chance. Before his return, his half-worried prey had escaped.
