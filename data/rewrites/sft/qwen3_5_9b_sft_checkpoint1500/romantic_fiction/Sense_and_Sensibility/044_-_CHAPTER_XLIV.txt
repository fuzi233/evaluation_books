Elinor recoiled, a look of profound horror freezing her features as she beheld him. Before the shock could subside, the first impulse of her heart seized her, and she turned instantly to flee the room. Her hand had already grasped the lock, poised to turn it, when his movement arrested her. He advanced swiftly, his voice shifting from pleading to a harsh, commanding tone.

"Miss Dashwood," he cried, "for half an hour—for ten minutes—I beg you to stay."

"No, sir," she replied, her resolve unshaken, "I shall not stay. Your business cannot be with *me*. The servants, I presume, forgot to inform you that Mr. Palmer is not in the house."

"Had they told me," he shouted, his face flushed with vehemence, "that Mr. Palmer and every relative of his were consigned to hell, it would not have driven me from this door. My business is with you, and with you alone."

"With me!" she exclaimed, stunned into silence, "Well, sir, be brief—and if you can, less violent."

"Sit down, and I will be both."

She hesitated, paralyzed by uncertainty. The terrifying possibility that Colonel Brandon might arrive and find her here crossed her mind. Yet, having promised to listen, her curiosity, fierce as her sense of duty, compelled her. Recalling that prudence demanded speed and that her compliance would facilitate his exit, she moved silently toward the table and sat. He took the chair opposite, and for half a minute, neither spoke.

"Pray be quick, sir," Elinor urged, her patience wearing thin, "I have no time to spare."

He sat in a posture of deep meditation, seemingly deaf to her urgency.

"Your sister," he said abruptly, "is out of danger. I learned it from a servant. God be praised! But is it truth? Is it truly true?"

Elinor remained silent. He repeated the question with mounting eagerness.

"For God's sake, tell me—is she safe, or is she not?"

"We hope she is."

He rose and paced the length of the room. "Had I known this half an hour ago—" he returned to his seat with a forced liveliness, "—what matters it? For once, Miss Dashwood, perhaps the last time, let us share a moment of cheer. I am in a rare mood for gaiety. Tell me honestly," a deep crimson flush spread across his cheeks, "do you think me most a knave or a fool?"

Elinor gazed at him with greater astonishment than ever before. She began to fear he must be intoxicated; the strangeness of this visit, of such manners, seemed intelligible only through the haze of drink. With this impression, she rose immediately.

"Mr. Willoughby, I advise you to return to Combe. I am not at leisure to remain with you longer. Whatever business you may have with me, it will be better recollected and explained tomorrow."

"I understand you," he replied, a smile playing on his lips, his voice perfectly calm. "Yes, I am very drunk. A pint of porter with my cold beef at Marlborough was enough to upset me."

"At Marlborough!" cried Elinor, bewildered, "What does this mean?"

"Yes, I left London this morning at eight. The only ten minutes I have spent outside my carriage since then secured me a snack at Marlborough."

The steady composure of his manner and the intelligence in his eye convinced Elinor that, whatever unforgivable folly had brought him to Cleveland, intoxication was not the cause. After a moment's reflection, she spoke again.

"Mr. Willoughby, you *ought* to feel, and I certainly *do*, that after what has passed, your coming here in this manner, forcing yourself upon my notice, demands a very particular excuse. What is it that you mean?"

"I mean," he said, with serious intensity, "if I can, to make you hate me one degree less than you do *now*. I mean to offer some explanation, some apology, for the past; to open my whole heart to you, and by convincing you that, though I have always been a blockhead, I have not always been a rascal, to obtain something like forgiveness from *Ma*—from your sister."

"Is this the real reason of your coming?"

"Upon my soul, it is," he answered, with a warmth that summoned the former Willoughby to her memory, and in spite of herself, made her think him sincere.

"If that is all, you may be satisfied already; for Marianne *does*, she has *long* forgiven you."

"Has she?" he cried, in the same eager tone. "Then she has forgiven me before she ought to have done it. But she shall forgive me again, and on more reasonable grounds. *Now* will you listen to me?"

Elinor bowed her assent.

"I do not know," he said, pausing as if weighing the gravity of his words, "how *you* may have accounted for my behaviour to your sister, or what diabolical motives you may have imputed to me. Perhaps you will hardly think the better of me,—it is worth the trial however, and you shall hear everything. When I first became intimate with your family, I had no other intention, no other aim than to pass my time pleasantly while obliged to remain in Devonshire, more pleasantly than I ever had before. Your sister's lovely person and engaging manners could not but please me; and her behaviour to me, almost from the start, was of a kind—it is astonishing, when I reflect on what it was, and what *she* was, that my heart should have been so insensible! But at first, I must confess, my vanity alone was elevated by it. Careless of her happiness, thinking only of my own amusement, yielding to feelings I had always been too accustomed to indulge, I endeavoured, by every means in my power, to make myself pleasing to her, without any design of returning her affection."

Miss Dashwood, at this point, turned her eyes upon him with angry contempt, stopping him.

"It is hardly worth while, Mr. Willoughby, for you to relate, or for me to listen any longer. Such a beginning as this cannot be followed by anything. Do not let me be pained by hearing anything more on the subject."

"I insist on you hearing the whole of it," he replied. "My fortune was never large, and I had always been expensive, always in the habit of associating with people of better income than myself. Every year since my coming of age, or even before, I believe, had added to my debts; and though the death of my old cousin, Mrs. Smith, was to set me free; yet that event being uncertain, and possibly far distant, it had been for some time my intention to re-establish my circumstances by marrying a woman of fortune. To attach myself to your sister, therefore, was not a thing to be thought of; and with a meanness, selfishness, cruelty, which no indignant, no contemptuous look, even of yours, Miss Dashwood, can ever reprobate too much,—I was acting in this manner, trying to engage her regard, without a thought of returning it. But one thing may be said for me: even in that horrid state of selfish vanity, I did not know the extent of the injury I meditated, because I did not *then* know what it was to love. But have I ever known it? Well may it be doubted; for, had I really loved, could I have sacrificed my feelings to vanity, to avarice? or, what is more, could I have sacrificed hers? But I have done it. To avoid a comparative poverty, which her affection and her society would have deprived of all its horrors, I have, by raising myself to affluence, lost every thing that could make it a blessing."

"You did then," said Elinor, a little softened, "believe yourself at one time attached to her?"

"To have resisted such attractions, to have withstood such tenderness! Is there a man on earth who could have done it? Yes, I found myself, by insensible degrees, sincerely fond of her; and the happiest hours of my life were what I spent with her when I felt my intentions were strictly honourable, and my feelings blameless. Even *then_, however, when fully determined on paying my addresses to her, I allowed myself most improperly to put off, from day to day, the moment of doing it, from an unwillingness to enter into an engagement while my circumstances were so greatly embarrassed. I will not reason here—nor will I stop for *you_ to expatiate on the absurdity, and the worse than absurdity, of scrupling to engage my faith where my honour was already bound. The event has proved, that I was a cunning fool, providing with great circumspection for a possible opportunity of making myself contemptible and wretched for ever. At last, however, my resolution was taken, and I had determined, as soon as I could engage her alone, to justify the attentions I had so invariably paid her, and openly assure her of an affection which I had already taken such pains to display.
