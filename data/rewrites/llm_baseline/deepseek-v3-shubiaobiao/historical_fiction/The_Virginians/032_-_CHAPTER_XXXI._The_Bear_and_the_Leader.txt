The truth regarding Harry Warrington and the unfortunate Cattarina was known to our fair-minded readers, but the gossips of Tunbridge Wells had spun a far more scandalous tale. To them, the young Virginian was as wild as any rakish nobleman fresh from London’s decadent circles. Madame de Bernstein, ever inclined to believe the worst of youth, took particular pleasure in imagining her nephew steeped in vice. A seasoned cynic herself, she found it easier to credit Harry with mischief than innocence. After all, was it not natural for a young man to sow his wild oats?

At the Wells, Harry had been nothing but courteous—flattering the ladies (those of far better repute than poor Cattarina) with his easy charm, pouring tea, and even offering music to the assembly. Such behavior hardly matched the whispers of debauchery, yet the old Baroness preferred the scandal. It relieved her of the tiresome duty of guarding Lady Maria so closely. Age had dulled her vigilance; her suspicions, once sharp as talons, now dozed like an old cat in the sun. Perhaps she believed the danger had passed. Or perhaps, like many in their twilight years, she had simply grown weary of policing the passions of youth.

The rumors, however, thrived without her encouragement. When the little dancer fled back to London, it was said to be Harry’s doing—that he had abandoned her for another, whose name the gossips mouthed with relish. Tunbridge Wells was a crossroads of society: ladies of spotless virtue brushed shoulders with those of more flexible morals, all mingling under the same roof, dancing to the same tunes, sipping the same waters. A century ago, such resorts were the stages where human folly played out freely, much as they do today in grander spas across Europe.

For Madame Bernstein, Harry’s tarnished reputation was a blessing. It soothed her fears about his attachment to Lady Maria. So confident was she that when the chaplain arrived to escort her niece home, she scarcely bothered to part with her. Instead, she took perverse delight in regaling Maria with tales of Harry’s imagined escapades, whispering knowingly that *boys will be boys*, and hinting at her plans to find him a suitable bride—one of proper age and fortune, of course. Maria endured these jabs with quiet fortitude, her face a careful mask.

There had once been a certain French nobleman, a marquis whose memoirs scandalized Europe. He boasted of conquests from Versailles to the colonies, yet curiously, his exploits in America yielded no names. The New World’s women, it seemed, proved resistant to his charms—a fact he omitted with rare discretion. Travelers often lamented the roughness of the colonies, but modesty, at least, was not among their faults.

Harry had brought that colonial modesty with him. Though surrounded by men of loose tongues and looser morals, he held his own counsel. He shrank from bawdy jests, had no conquests to boast of, and flushed when others recounted theirs. His tutor, Mr. Sampson, had noted this diffidence back at Castlewood, where Harry would shift uncomfortably at Will’s ribald tales.

Now, the chaplain found himself oddly protective of the boy. The gift of the horse had touched him, and he felt a genuine kinship. *"I may be of the world,"* he admitted, *"but I’ve a little sister at school, and I keep a clean tongue around her. Let me do the same for you."*

To Madame Bernstein, Sampson sang Harry’s praises—his bright eyes, his blushes, his sturdy honesty. She listened with approval, for she was fond of the boy in her own way. He amused her. Had he been clever or bookish, she might have tired of him, but his simplicity was refreshing. *"We must find him a proper wife,"* she declared. *"He’ll not go back to his savage land, nor to that Methodistical mother of his."*

Sampson, too, had thoughts of marriage—for Harry, and for himself. A good living dangled before him if he could steer Lady Maria into a match. And so, with a mix of calculation and goodwill, he set about his task.

The chaplain was excellent company—quick with a jest, eager for sport, yet careful not to lead Harry too far astray. He drank with him, gamed with him, but kept his darker stories to himself. He played the role of jovial mentor, never pushing the boy toward true vice.

One evening, over claret, tongues loosened.

*"They’ve made quite a reputation for you here,"* Sampson remarked. *"Monsieur de Richelieu himself couldn’t rival it!"*

*"Who?"* Harry frowned.

Sampson explained—the widow Patcham’s sighs, Mrs. Hooper’s scandalous flight. The Wells buzzed with tales.

Harry’s fist hit the table. *"Name the man who says so, and I’ll call him out!"*

Sampson chuckled. *"Best not. You’d have no ears left in the county."*

*"In Virginia, a man who slanders a woman doesn’t keep his ears long,"* Harry growled.

The chaplain raised his glass. *"To your honor, sir. Let’s drink to that."*

And so they did, glass after glass, until the room swam.

*"You’ve done well to resist temptation,"* Sampson mused. *"Not all men are so strong."*

Harry groaned. *"Sampson—I must confide in someone."*

*"Ah!"* The chaplain leaned in. *"Then you do care for her?"*

Harry buried his face in his hands. *"I am miserable."*

Sampson clasped his shoulder. *"My dear boy, I am here."*

And under the warm haze of claret, the confession spilled out.
