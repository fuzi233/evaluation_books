The next morning, the boys and I set out with the cart heavily laden with bundles of bamboo to tend to our avenue of fruit trees. We left the buffalo behind; his services were no longer needed immediately, and I wanted the wound in his nostril to heal completely before putting him back to work.

We arrived just in time. Many of the young trees, which had previously looked as though they would topple, had already succumbed to their own weight and lay prostrate on the ground. Others were bent double, with only a few remaining standing upright. We lifted the fallen trees, dug deep holes at their roots, and drove sturdy bamboo props into the earth. Using strong, broad fibers, we lashed the trees securely to these supports.

"Papa," Franz asked, handing me a bundle of fibers as I needed them, "are these wild or tame trees?"

"Oh, these are wild trees! Most ferocious!" Jack laughed. "We are tying them up lest they run away. In a little while, we'll untie them, and they'll trot after us everywhere, giving us fruit wherever we go. Oh, we will tame them! They'll have rings through their noses just like the buffalo!"

"That isn't true," Franz replied seriously. "But there are wild and tame trees. The wild ones grow out in the woods, like crab-apples, and the tame ones grow in gardens, like the pears and peaches at home. Which are these, Papa?"

"They are not truly wild," I answered. "They are grafted, or cultivated—what you call tame trees. No European tree bears good fruit unless it is grafted." Seeing a puzzled look cross the little boy's face at the new word, I explained further.

"Grafting," I continued, "is the art of inserting a slip or twig of one tree into an 'eye'—a knot or scar—in the branch of another. That twig then grows and produces fruit not like the original stock, but exactly like the tree from which the slip was taken. For instance, if we have a sour crab-tree and an apple tree bearing fine Ribston Pippins, we take a slip of the apple tree, insert it into an eye of the crab-tree, and in a year or two, that branch will be laden with delicious apples."

"But," Ernest asked, "where did the slips of good fruit come from, if none grow without grafting?"

"From foreign countries," I replied. "It is only in the cold climate of our part of the world that such grafting is necessary. In many southern regions, the most luscious fruit trees grow naturally in the soil, flourishing and bearing sweet fruit without any human care. Conversely, in England, Germany, and even France, these same trees require the utmost horticultural skill to produce a single berry.

"When the Romans invaded England, they found nothing but crab-apples, nut bushes, and brambles. Yet, by grafting onto these stocks, they produced fine apples, filberts, and raspberries. It was the same in our beloved Switzerland—all our fruit trees were imported."

"Were cherries, Father? Can't we even call cherries Swiss? I always thought they grew nowhere else," Fritz interjected.

"I fear we cannot even claim cherries as our own, not even their name. They are called *cherries* from *Cerasus*, a region in Asia Minor known as Pontus, from whence they were brought to Europe by Lucullus, a Roman general, about seventy years before Christ. Hazelnuts also originate from Pontus; walnuts came originally from Persia. Grapes, however, are of the greatest antiquity. You recall the story of Noah cultivating vines; they have traveled from place to place until they are now found throughout the civilized world."

"Do you think all these trees will grow here?" Fritz asked as we crossed the Jackal River and entered our plantation at Tentholm. "Look at the lemons, pomegranates, pistachio nuts, and mulberries."

"I have little doubt," I assured him. "We are clearly within the tropics, where such trees are destined to flourish. These pines come from France, Spain, and Italy; the olives from Armenia and Palestine; the figs originally from the island of Chios; the peaches and apricots from Persia; plums from Damascus in Syria; and pears of all sorts from Greece. However, if our homelands were not blessed with such native varieties, we have been gifted with the wisdom and skill to import and cultivate the treasures of other lands."

We talked and worked until every tree requiring support was secured with a bamboo prop. Then, with appetites that a gourmet might envy, we returned to Falconhurst. My good wife was nearly alarmed by the way we fell upon the corned beef and palm-cabbage she had prepared, but eventually, the food produced its desired effect, and one by one, we declared ourselves satisfied.

As we reclined after our labor, digesting our meal, we discussed our future plans.

"I wish," my wife said, "that you would devise another method for climbing to the nest above us. I think the nest itself is perfect; I want nothing better. But I dread scaling that terrible ladder every time. Could you not construct a flight of steps to reach it?"

I pondered the project carefully, turning over every conceivable plan.

"It seems impossible," I admitted, "to build stairs on the outside. But inside the trunk, it might be feasible. Have I not told you before that I suspected this trunk might be hollow, or partially so? If that is the case, our task becomes much easier. Did you not mention recently noticing bees emerging from a hole in the tree?"

"Oh, yes," little Franz recalled. "I went to look, and one flew straight at my face and stung me. I almost cried, but I didn't."

"Brave little boy," I praised. "Well, if the trunk is hollow enough to shelter a swarm of bees, it may be hollow for most of its length. Like the willow in our homeland, it might draw nourishment through its bark, retaining a flourishing appearance despite being unsound within."

Practical as ever, Master Jack instantly jumped to his feet to test my hypothesis. The rest of us followed suit, scrambling about like squirrels, peering into the hole and tapping the wood to gauge the extent of the cavity by the sound.

In their eagerness, they forgot who currently inhabited this interesting trunk. Soon, however, they were reminded. Disturbed by the unusual noises, the bees erupted with an angry buzz, attacking the intruders. They swarmed around us, stinging our hands, faces, and necks, settling in our hair, and pursuing us as we fled to seek my assistance.

With great difficulty, we managed to drive off the angry insects and tended to the boys. Jack, who had reached the hole first, suffered the worst; his face swelled to an extraordinary degree, and only the constant application of cold earth alleviated the pain. Eager to launch an organized attack on the bees, they were rendered helpless for an hour or more by their agony.

Meanwhile, I made my preparations. First, I selected a large calabash gourd, intending to fashion a beehive so that, once we drove the insects from their current home, we would not lose them entirely. I flattened the lower half of the gourd, cut an arched opening in the front for a doorway, and added a straw roof for protection against rain and heat. The little house was complete.

Yet, nothing more could be done while the irritated bees buzzed angrily around the tree. I waited until dark, and when all the bees had returned to their trunk, I assisted Fritz in carefully sealing every hole with wet clay, ensuring they could not escape before we began our operations the next morning.

Very early the next dawn, we were at work. I took a hollow cane, inserted one end through the clay into the tree, and down this tube, using a pipe and tobacco, I smoked furiously. The humming and buzzing within were tremendous; the bees evidently could not comprehend what was happening. After finishing my first pipeful and covering the end with my thumb, I passed the pipe to Fritz to refill. He did so, and I smoked again. The buzzing gradually subsided into a mere murmur. By the time I finished the second pipe, everything was silent; the bees were stupefied.

"Now then, Fritz," I commanded, "quick with the hammer and chisel, and stand beside me."

He was ready in a moment. Together, we cut a small door beside the main hole. We did not remove it entirely but left it attached by one corner so it could be removed instantly if needed. Giving the bees a final dose of tobacco smoke, we opened the door.

Carefully but rapidly, we removed the insects as they clung in clusters to the tree's sides, placing them into the prepared hive. Immediately, I extracted every atom of wax and honey from their storehouse, depositing it into a cask I had readied for the purpose.

Though the bees were safely removed from the trunk, I feared that upon recovering from their stupor, they might refuse the new home and insist on returning to their old quarters. To prevent this, I placed a quantity of tobacco on a board nailed horizontally inside the trunk, lit it, and let it burn slowly so the fumes would fill the cavity.
