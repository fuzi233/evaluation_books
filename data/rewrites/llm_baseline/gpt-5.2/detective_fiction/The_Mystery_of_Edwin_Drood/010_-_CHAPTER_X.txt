People will tell you, with a certainty that sounds like bell-metal, that women divine men’s characters by instinct. They do; and in Cloisterham, where everything—mortar, moss, and manners—seems to have stood long enough to take a permanent set, that instinct often hardens into a judgment which no after-proof can soften. When once delivered, it is not revised; it is defended. Contradiction does not correct it; contradiction irritates it. And the more a woman has put herself—her pride, her care, her household peace—into that judgment, the more it resembles testimony from a witness who cannot help being interested in the verdict.

“Now, don’t you think, Ma dear,” said the Minor Canon to his mother, one day, as she sat knitting in his little book-room, “that you are rather hard on Mr. Neville?”

“No, I do _not_, Sept,” returned the old lady, with the calm emphasis of a person who has never been wrong in her life without immediately forgetting the circumstance.

“Let us discuss it, Ma.”

“I have no objection to discuss it, Sept. I trust, my dear, I am always open to discussion.” Her cap gave a faint tremor—as if the threads of it had remembered a hundred discussions and the unalterable endings of them.

“Very good, Ma. There is nothing like being open to discussion.”

“I hope not, my dear,” said the old lady; and somehow, though her words invited debate, her knitting needles clicked like shutters closing.

“Well. Mr. Neville, on that unfortunate occasion, commits himself under provocation.”

“And under mulled wine,” added the old lady, without looking up.

“I must admit the wine. Though I believe the two young men were much alike in that regard.”

“I don’t,” said the old lady.

“Why not, Ma?”

“Because I _don’t_,” returned she, knitting steadily. “Still, I am quite open to discussion.”

“But, my dear Ma, I cannot see how we are to discuss, if you take that line.”

“Blame Mr. Neville for it, Sept, and not me,” said Mrs. Crisparkle, with a stately severity that made even the arm-chair appear to sit straighter.

“My dear Ma! why Mr. Neville?”

“Because,” said she, retreating upon the fortress of first principles, “he came home intoxicated, and did great discredit to this house, and showed great disrespect to this family.”

“That is not to be denied, Ma. He was then, and he is now, very sorry for it.”

“But for Mr. Jasper’s well-bred consideration in coming up to me, next day, after service, in the Nave itself, with his gown still on, and expressing his hope that I had not been greatly alarmed or had my rest violently broken, I believe I might never have heard of that disgraceful transaction,” said the old lady, as though the recollection were kept, like lavender, between the leaves of a Bible.

“To be candid, Ma, I think I should have kept it from you if I could: though I had not decidedly made up my mind. I was following Jasper out, to confer with him on the subject, and to consider the expediency of his and my jointly hushing the thing up on all accounts, when I found him speaking to you. Then it was too late.”

“Too late, indeed, Sept. He was still as pale as gentlemanly ashes at what had taken place in his rooms overnight.”

“If I _had_ kept it from you, Ma, you may be sure it would have been for your peace and quiet, and for the good of the young men, and in my best discharge of my duty according to my lights.”

The old lady put down her knitting, crossed the room, and kissed him—an instant softening that never extended as far as her opinions.

“Of course, my dear Sept, I am sure of that.”

“However,” said Mr. Crisparkle, rubbing his ear with the air of a man who rubs away an awkward fact and finds it still there, “it became the town-talk, and passed out of my power.”

“And I said then, Sept,” returned the old lady, taking up her needles again as if resuming a legal document, “that I thought ill of Mr. Neville. And I say now, that I think ill of Mr. Neville. And I said then, and I say now, that I hope Mr. Neville may come to good, but I don’t believe he will.” The cap vibrated in decisive accompaniment.

“I am sorry to hear you say so, Ma—”

“I am sorry to say so, my dear,” she interposed, “but I can’t help it.”

“—For it is undeniable that Mr. Neville is exceedingly industrious and attentive, and that he improves apace, and that he has—I hope I may say—an attachment to me.”

“There is no merit in the last article, my dear,” said the old lady, quickly. “And if he says there is, I think the worse of him for the boast.”

“But, my dear Ma, he never said there was.”

“Perhaps not,” returned she; “still, I don’t see that it greatly signifies.”

There was no impatience in the Minor Canon’s pleasant look at his mother—pretty old piece of china that she was, bright-glazed, finely painted, and not a thing to argue with too closely for fear of a crack—yet there was a quiet humor in his sense of her unyielding nature.

“Besides, Sept,” she pursued, “ask yourself what he would be without his sister. You know what an influence she has over him; you know what a capacity she has; you know that whatever he reads with you, he reads with her. Give her her fair share of your praise, and how much do you leave for him?”

At that, Mr. Crisparkle fell into one of his mild reveries; and as he did, the house seemed to settle itself around him in its accustomed music—the soft scratch of needles, the far-off Cathedral note that was never wholly absent, the domestic hush between the great peals: an acoustic order as regular as prayer, and far kinder to the mind than the modern world’s grinding clatter would ever be. He saw again, as clearly as if the pages were before him, the brother and sister bending together over one of his old college books; and he heard—so to speak—their silence, that attentive silence in which another mind is present though no word passes. He saw them, too, in the rimy mornings when he made those sharpening pilgrimages to Cloisterham Weir; and in the sombre evenings when he faced the wind at sunset on his favourite beetling fragment of monastery ruin, and watched them move below along the margin of the river, the town fires already lighting and blearing the water, so that the landscape looked bleaker for its own reflections. He remembered how, by degrees, he had found himself teaching two, adapting an explanation not only to the mind he met daily, but to the mind he approached through it. He recalled the Nuns’ House gossip: that Helena—whom he had first mistrusted as proud and fierce—submitted herself, strangely enough, to the fairy-bride (as he called the slight young lady who had become her friend), and learned from her what she knew. He thought of that picturesque alliance between two so unlike in look and manner. And he marvelled—perhaps most of all—how few weeks these things had been, and yet how they had already threaded themselves into the routine of his life as surely as the Cathedral hours.

Whenever the Reverend Septimus fell a-musing, his good mother took it as a sure sign that he “wanted support.” Support, in her philosophy, did not come in the form of argument; it came in a glass. She rose and went, with the importance of a priestess entering a shrine, to the dining-room closet.

It was a closet worthy of Cloisterham and Minor Canon Corner: not a blunt cupboard that flung all its secrets open at once, but a contrivance of gradual revelations, as ceremonious as a service. Above it, Handel—flowing wig, knowing eyes—looked down as if he had composed, in his head, a fugue out of all the sweet and sharp and cordial notes within. The lock sat in mid-air where two perpendicular slides met; one fell down, one pushed up, and the mysteries advanced by degrees, like verses.

Down came the upper slide: shelves deep as small caverns showed pickle-jars, jam-pots, tin canisters, spice-boxes, and agreeably outlandish blue-and-white vessels where preserved tamarinds and ginger lay in their syrup as if embalmed against forgetfulness. Every benevolent inmate had his name painted on his stomach. The pickles, in rich brown double-breasted coats with yellow or drab continuations, announced themselves in printed capitals as WALNUT, GHERKIN, ONION, CABBAGE, CAULIFLOWER, MIXED—portly, military fellows, sharp to the tongue and good for the spirits. The jams—less masculine, and as if wearing curlpapers—declared in a soft feminine hand that they were Raspberry, Gooseberry, Apricot, Plum, Damson, Apple, Peach: the very syllables seeming to carry the scent of summer hedges into the winter room.

Then the lower slide ascended and disclosed oranges, attended by a mighty japanned sugar-box—ready to correct any acerbity, as Cloisterham corrected strong natures with its own slow sweetness. Home-made biscuits waited at court; a goodly fragment of plum-cake sat in state; and slender ladies’ fingers lay ready to be dipped into sweet wine and kissed. Lowest of all, in a compact leaden vault, the Constantia and cordials were stored—Seville Orange, Lemon, Almond, Caraway-seed—each bottle holding, like a remembered phrase in a dying dialect, something that could not be translated into mere “sweet” or “strong” but must be tasted to be known. The whole closet seemed to have been hummed through for ages by Cathedral bell and organ, until those venerable bees had made sublimated honey of everything it contained. And it was always observed that whoever dipped head, shoulders, and elbows among its deep shelves came forth mellow-faced, as if saccharine transfigured.

Mr. Crisparkle took his glass of Constantia with an excellent grace—warm, golden, faintly scented with sun and old grapes—and ate a biscuit that crumbled with the honest dryness of home baking, and was softened on the tongue by the wine. In his mother’s system, that was support.

So supported, he went about the remaining duties of the day. The orderly hours brought round Vesper Service and twilight. The Cathedral, cold as stone remembers cold, sent him out after service into a brisk trot, his breath smoking in the dusk. The trot ended in a charge at his favourite fragment of ruin, to be carried by storm without a pause for breath; and he carried it in masterly fashion, climbing as if the old stones had been built for his foot.

Not even winded then, he stood looking down upon the river. Cloisterham lay near enough to the sea for the tide to throw up seaweed often; an unusual quantity had come in with the last tide, tangled and dark on the banks. The water was confused; gulls dipped and flapped noisily, their cries sharp as knives in the air; beyond the brown-sailed barges that were turning black, an angry light out seaward foreshadowed a stormy night. He listened to that unresting outer sound—the sea’s hoarse breath coming up the river—and contrasted it, as he had been doing all day, with the quiet harbour of Minor Canon Corner, where even the Cathedral’s great voice was softened by distance into something that steadied rather than shook the mind. How much of a man’s temper, he thought, was made—or marred—by the sounds he lived under! And how little the busy modern world understood the cruelty of perpetual clang and din, calling it progress and selling it as pleasure.

As he stood so, Helena and Neville Landless passed below him along the river-walk. Having had them in his thoughts all day, he climbed down at once to speak to them together. The footing was rough in the uncertain light for any tread save that of a good climber; but the Minor Canon was a good climber as most men are not, and he stood beside them before many would have been half-way.

“A wild evening, Miss Landless! Do you not find your usual walk with your brother too exposed and cold for the time of year? Or at all events, when the sun is down and the weather is driving in from the sea?”

Helena thought not. It was their favourite walk. It was very retired.

“It is very retired,” assented Mr. Crisparkle, taking hold of his opportunity at once as they walked on together. “It is a place of all others where one can speak without interruption, as I wish to do. Mr. Neville, I believe you tell your sister everything that passes between us?”

“Everything, sir.”

“Consequently,” said Mr. Crisparkle, looking rather to Helena than to Neville, “your sister is aware that I have repeatedly urged you to make some kind of apology for that unfortunate occurrence which befell on the night of your arrival here.”

“Yes,” Helena answered, and the word carried her brother’s loyalty in it.

“I call it unfortunate, Miss Helena,” resumed Mr. Crisparkle, “forasmuch as it certainly has engendered a prejudice against Neville. There is a notion about that he is a dangerously passionate fellow, of an uncontrollable and furious temper: he is really avoided as such.”

“I have no doubt he is, poor fellow,” said Helena, with proud compassion for her brother, as though she would have taken the prejudice into her own breast and borne it for him. “I should be quite sure of it, from your saying so; but what you tell me is confirmed by suppressed hints and references that I meet with every day.”

“Now,” Mr. Crisparkle resumed, mild but firm, “is not this to be regretted, and ought it not to be amended? These are early days of Neville’s in Cloisterham, and I have no fear of his outliving such a prejudice and proving himself to have been misunderstood. But how much wiser to take action at once than to trust to uncertain time! Besides, apart from its being politic, it is right. For there can be no question that Neville was wrong.”

“He was provoked,” Helena submitted, a little quickening in her voice.

“He was the assailant,” Mr. Crisparkle submitted, quietly.

They walked on in silence; and the soundscape of the place seemed to argue too—the river’s slap, the gulls’ crying, the far organ-note of the town’s life, all mingling like a reminder that tempers, once loosed, spread and echo. At length Helena lifted her eyes to his face and said, almost reproachfully:

“O Mr. Crisparkle, would you have Neville throw himself at young Drood’s feet, or at Mr. Jasper’s, who maligns him every day? In your heart you cannot mean it. From your heart you could not do it, if his case were yours.”

“I have represented to Mr. Crisparkle, Helena,” said Neville, glancing with deference towards his tutor, “that if I could do it from my heart, I would. But I cannot, and I revolt from the pretence. You forget, however, that to put the case to Mr. Crisparkle as his own, is to suppose to have done what I did.”

“I ask his pardon,” said Helena at once.

“You see,” remarked Mr. Crisparkle, again laying hold of his opportunity, delicately, “you both instinctively acknowledge that Neville did wrong. Then why stop short, and not otherwise acknowledge it?”

“Is there no difference,” asked Helena, faltering a little, “between submission to a generous spirit, and submission to a base or trivial one?”

Before the Minor Canon could quite shape his answer to that nice distinction, Neville struck in, his voice roughened by the very restraint he tried to keep.

“Help me to clear myself with Mr. Crisparkle, Helena. Help me to convince him that I cannot be the first to make concessions without mockery and falsehood. My nature must be changed before I can do so, and it is not changed. I am sensible of inexpressible affront, and deliberate aggravation of inexpressible affront, and I am angry. The plain truth is, I am still as angry when I recall that night as I was that night.”

“Neville,” hinted the Minor Canon, steady-faced, “you have repeated that former action of your hands, which I so much dislike.”

“I am sorry for it, sir, but it was involuntary. I confessed that I was still as angry.”

“And I confess,” said Mr. Crisparkle, “that I hoped for better things.”

“I am sorry to disappoint you, sir, but it would be far worse to deceive you, and I should deceive you grossly if I pretended that you had softened me in this respect. The time may come when your powerful influence will do even that with the difficult pupil whose antecedents you know; but it has not come yet. Is this so, and in spite of my struggles against myself, Helena?”

Helena’s dark eyes were watching what every word did to Mr. Crisparkle’s face; and she answered him—not her brother—quietly: “It is so.” Then, after a brief pause, she met the slightest inquiry in Neville’s eyes with the slightest affirmative bend of her head, and Neville went on, as if that small assent gave him courage.

“I have never yet had the courage to say to you, sir, what in full openness I ought to have said when you first talked with me on this subject. It is not easy to say, and I have been withheld by a fear of its seeming ridiculous, which is very strong upon me down to this last moment, and might, but for my sister, prevent my being quite open with you even now.—I admire Miss Bud, sir, so very much, that I cannot bear her being treated with conceit or indifference; and even if I did not feel that I had an injury against young Drood on my own account, I should feel that I had an injury against him on hers.”

Mr. Crisparkle, utterly amazed, looked to Helena for corroboration. He found it in her expressive face—full corroboration, and a plea besides, as if she begged him to take hold of the matter with both hands before it ran away into disaster.

“The young lady of whom you speak is, as you know, Mr. Neville, shortly to be married,” said Mr. Crisparkle, gravely. “Therefore your admiration, if it be of that special nature which you seem to indicate, is outrageously misplaced. Moreover, it is monstrous that you should take upon yourself to be the young lady’s champion against her chosen husband. Besides, you have seen them only once. The young lady has become your sister’s friend; and I wonder that your sister, even on her behalf, has not checked you in this irrational and culpable fancy.”

“She has tried, sir, but uselessly. Husband or no husband, that fellow is incapable of the feeling with which I am inspired towards the beautiful young creature whom he treats like a doll. I say he is as incapable of it, as he is unworthy of her. I say she is sacrificed in being bestowed upon him. I say that I love her, and despise and hate him!”

He spoke with such a flushed face and such a violent gesture that Helena crossed at once to his side, caught his arm, and remonstrated, “Neville, Neville!” as if she would physically hold back the very word.

Thus recalled to himself, he became sensible in a moment that he had lost the guard he had set upon his passionate tendency. He covered his face with his hand, repentant and wretched, as if ashamed to have let his heart show its rawness in the air.

Mr. Crisparkle watched him attentively, and at the same time thought how to proceed. They walked on some paces in silence; the wind worried the reeds; the gulls quarrelled overhead; and the rising storm seemed to lend Neville’s confession a dreadful fitness—as though the outer world were always too ready to translate a man’s inner tumult into weather.

Then Mr. Crisparkle spoke.

“Mr. Neville, Mr. Neville, I am sorely grieved to see in you more traces of a character as sullen, angry, and wild, as the night now closing in. They are of too serious an aspect to leave me the resource of treating the infatuation you have disclosed as undeserving serious consideration. I give it very serious consideration, and I speak to you accordingly. This feud between you and young Drood must not go on. I cannot permit it to go on any longer, knowing what I now know from you, and you living under my roof. Whatever prejudiced and unauthorised constructions your blind and envious wrath may put upon his character, it is a frank, good-natured character. I know I can trust to it for that.

“Now, pray observe what I am about to say. On reflection, and on your sister’s representation, I am willing to admit that, in making peace with young Drood, you have a right to be met half-way. I will engage that you shall be, and even that young Drood shall make the first advance. This condition fulfilled, you will pledge me the honour of a Christian gentleman that the quarrel is for ever at an end on your side. What may be in your heart when you give him your hand can only be known to the Searcher of all hearts; but it will never go well with you if there be any treachery there.

“So far, as to that. Next, as to what I must again speak of as your infatuation. I understand it to have been confided to me, and to be known to no other person save your sister and yourself. Do I understand aright?”

Helena answered in a low voice: “It is only known to us three who are here together.”

“It is not at all known to the young lady, your friend?”

“On my soul, no!” said Neville, with a quick misery, as if the notion scorched him.

“I require you, then, to give me your similar and solemn pledge, Mr. Neville, that it shall remain the secret it is, and that you will take no other action whatsoever upon it than endeavouring—most earnestly—to erase it from your mind. I will not tell you that it will soon pass; I will not tell you that it is the fancy of the moment; I will not tell you that such caprices have their rise and fall among the young and ardent every hour. I will leave you undisturbed in the belief that it has few parallels or none, that it will abide with you a long time, and that it will be very difficult to conquer. So much the more weight shall I attach to the pledge I require from you, when it is unreservedly given.”

The young man tried twice or thrice to speak, and could not. The words, like a dish too hot, would not be taken into the mouth.

“Let me leave you with your sister, whom it is time you took home,” said Mr. Crisparkle. “You will find me alone in my room by-and-by.”

“Pray do not leave us yet,” Helena implored him. “Another minute.”

“I should not,” said Neville, his hand still on his face, “have needed so much as another minute, if you had been less patient with me, Mr. Crisparkle, less considerate of me, and less unpretendingly good and true. O, if in my childhood I had known such a guide!”

“Follow your guide now, Neville,” murmured Helena, “and follow him to Heaven!”

There was something in her tone—so fervent, so grateful, so ready to sanctify the human help she had found—that broke the Minor Canon’s voice, or it would have gently put her right. As it was, he only laid a finger on his lips and looked towards her brother.

“To say that I give both pledges, Mr. Crisparkle, out of my innermost heart, and to say that there is no treachery in it, is to say nothing!” returned Neville, greatly moved. “I beg your forgiveness for my miserable lapse into a burst of passion.”

“Not mine, Neville, not mine. You know with whom forgiveness lies, as the highest attribute conceivable. Miss Helena, you and your brother are twin children. You came into this world with the same dispositions, and you passed your younger days together surrounded by the same adverse circumstances. What you have overcome in yourself, can you not overcome in him? You see the rock that lies in his course. Who but you can keep him clear of it?”

“Who but you, sir?” replied Helena. “What is my influence, or my weak wisdom, compared with yours!”

“You have the wisdom of Love,” returned the Minor Canon, “and it was the highest wisdom ever known upon this earth, remember. As to mine—but the less said of that commonplace commodity the better. Good night!”

She took the hand he offered her and gratefully, almost reverently, raised it to her lips.

“Tut!” said the Minor Canon softly, as if embarrassed by any ceremony that made a commodity of goodness. “I am much overpaid!” And he turned away.

Retracing his steps towards the Cathedral Close, he tried, as he went along in the dark, to think out the best means of bringing to pass what he had promised and what must somehow be done. The wind was rising; the sea’s voice was louder up the river; and through it all the Cathedral’s presence held—its great mass, its measured sounds—like a reminder that there were older harmonies than anger. “I shall probably be asked to marry them,” he reflected, “and I would they were married and gone! But this presses first.”

He debated chiefly whether he should write to young Drood or whether he should speak to Jasper. The consciousness of being popular with the whole Cathedral establishment inclined him to the latter course; and the well-timed sight of the lighted gatehouse decided him.

“I will strike while the iron is hot,” he said, and turned to go in and see Jasper now.
