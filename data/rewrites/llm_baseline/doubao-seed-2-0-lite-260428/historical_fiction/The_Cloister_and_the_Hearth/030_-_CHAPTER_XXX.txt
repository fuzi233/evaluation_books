It was on this stretch of the road that Gerard fell in with a most ingenious brother at the abbey: the very monk who had carved the great precision sundial in the prior’s herb garden, built the water wheel that hauled full buckets up the deep well without a single hand to heft them, and crafted a winnowing machine that sifted chaff from grain faster than ten farmwives working side by side with hand sieves. Some clever new mechanism was always in progress in his cell. He had already turned half a dozen psalteries and two sweet-toned dulcimers, and was halfway through building a set of regalles: a tiny, portable organ for the abbey choir.

Gerard had only ever dabbled a little at the humble psaltery, but this old brother drew music from its strings so fine it felt like angels were humming the tune through the wood, and showed him gently just how much of a novice he was to the craft. His illuminated manuscripts were lovely, too, but he openly admitted he could never match Gerard’s sharp, elegant script. In that earnest, unpretentious age where any skill was a marker of true natural bent, the two clicked like old friends: youth and age bound by shared love of beauty and making, and the monk would not hear of Gerard and Denys travelling another mile that night. Gerard glanced at Denys, who shrugged ruefully, clearly preferring a dry straw pallet indoors to huddling in a frost-kissed hedgerow, and agreed to stay.

He told his new friend where they were bound, and recounted all their recent adventures, softening the ridiculous detail of whacking a rogue over the head with a feather bolster. “Alack,” the old monk sighed, “I travelled half the known world in my youth, and never a soul molested me. Mark my words: avoid all roadside inns. They swarm with thieves and brawlers, and you risk your soul there even if you escape unharmed in body. Plan every day’s march so you end it at a quiet, peaceful monastery.” He broke off abruptly, squinting as sharp as a needle at Gerard. “How long has it been since you were last shriven?”

Gerard flushed bright hot, and mumbled weakly that it was more than a fortnight. “You, a man who carries the right to perform exorcisms? No wonder peril hounds you! You must be absolved at once.”

“Gladly, father, with all my heart,” Gerard said, and sank to his knees, hands clasped to confess right there—only for the monk to wave him off half-fretfully.
“Not to me! Not to me! My whole fool soul is tangled up in these wooden pipes and dinky leather stops that will rot in a few years’ time right alongside me. I am as full of worldly vanity as any layman walking the roads. I am no fit confessor for you.”

“Dear father,” Gerard protested, “these organs are built for the use of the Church—surely that sanctifies the work you put into them?”

“That is exactly the line the devil has been whispering in my ear these past months!” the monk laughed, shaking a finger half-playful, half-threatening at him. “He even quoted Scripture, ever so thoughtful, to remind me that Solomon built the Lord a temple with fine carvings and rich hangings, and that counted as piety, no sin. Oh, he can quote the Bible like a trained preacher. But I am not as simple as I look, lad,” he suddenly declared, all defiance, speaking not to Gerard but to the empty air of the corridor. “The second I finish this tiny organ, it will be nothing but vigils, fasts, and prayers for me. Prayers standing in the drafty chapel aisle, prayers flat on my face on the cold stone floor, prayers sat right in a tub of icy water. Nothing the devil hates more than seeing a monk praying half-submerged in cold water—for water tames the flesh, after all. Off you go, then, confess your silly little pardonable youthful secular sins, and leave me to mine: sins that taste sweeter than honey, for which I will have to atone ten times over.”

They had come to the row of monks’ cells lining the corridor. “Any of these cells holds a worthy confessor,” the monk said simply. “Go to Brother Anselm, yonder.”

Gerard walked toward the first door he saw, about to rap on the wood, when he heard his new friend hissing at him in a state of wild panic: “Nay! Nay! Nay!” He turned, and saw the old monk pacing back and forth outside his own cell, waving both hands like a sawyer working a two-man pit saw, so twisted up with anxiety Gerard half assumed that first cell must hold a raving wild beast, if not the very fiend they had just been speaking of. He nodded apologetically, and moved to the next door. The monk’s face instantly softened into relief, he nodded rapidly, scurried back into his own cell, flipped his hourglass over, and dived back to work on his regalles, muttering to himself every few minutes: “Well-a-day, how fast the sands run when a man is bent over worldly toys.”

Father Anselm was a venerable old brother, broad of forehead, his face lined with equal parts dignity and gentle love. As Gerard knelt to confess, and answered his soft but searching questions, he could not help staring at that wise, kind face, and thinking *Oh, I do hope he will let me draw his portrait once we are done*—and mid-distraction, he forgot a couple of tiny, trivial sins. But he did not downplay the bolster brawl, nor was he dishonest about the roguish character of the man he had hit.

His penance was light: go into the abbey church, prostrate yourself, kiss the lowest step of the altar three times, then kneel on the bare floor to say three paternosters and a credo. “When you are done, come back to me at once.”

Gerard finished his small mortification and returned to find Father Anselm spreading thick, cool plaster on a linen cloth. “Tend the soul first, then the body,” he said. “I am the abbey chirurgeon here, for want of a better. This plaster will go on your sore leg: it will cool the ache, not burn it, the saints forbid I should harm you.”

As he wrapped the bandage, the monastic leech, who had taken a quiet interest in Gerard’s tales of his journey from Dusseldorf, sided openly with Denys on the matter of bloodletting. “We Dominicans seldom draw blood these days; lay surgeons call us cowardly and unskilled, but in truth we have long known that simples and herbs cure most ills that can be cured at all. And in capable hands, they never kill a patient—unlike harsher, sharper remedies that slay a man as fast as a lightning bolt. The Vulgate says explicitly that blood is the life of a man, and in medicine, law, or divinity, to think yourself wiser than the All-wise is pure foolishness. Besides, the lowliest creatures know the power of healing herbs: the four-footed weasel, bitten by a venomous snake, will seek out a herb to draw the poison out. We learned that trick from the beasts, and from our old traditions, and still we test the virtues of all the plants good God scattered across the earth: some to feed men, some to heal them. Only for the most dire, desperate ills do we steep a sliver of a saint’s hair or bone in the draught, and work marvellous cures that way.”

“Think you those cures work because of the relics?” Gerard asked. “For Peter a Floris, that learned lay leech, swears stoutly that it is all nonsense.”

“What does Peter a Floris know? What do any of us know?” Anselm smiled. “I do not pretend we can command the saints, and draw virtue from their blessed remains against their will. But I have seen a sick man drink that draught in perfect faith and get better, as if by charm. Doubtless the faith of the patient counts for much, as it always did. The sick woman whom all the Jewish leeches could not cure touched only the hem of Christ’s garment, and was healed on the instant. If she had not had faith, she could have worn that scrap of cloth to her grave and gained no good from it. It does not do to pry too deeply into these secrets. All around us the world calls for faith. So I, your confessor for this hour, strictly forbid you, on pain of your soul’s health, to listen to arrogant learned laymen picking holes in matters of religion. Arrogance is their curse, and with it they shut heaven’s door in their own faces. The unlearned simple folk have often been my masters in humility, and may yet be yours. Your wound is cared for; in three days it will be nothing but a faint scar. Now God speed you, and the saints make you as good and happy as you are thoughtful and gracious.”

Gerard had hoped they might talk longer, but Anselm told him, with only the faintest trace of regret, that he did not leave his cell all that week, as he was observing a personal penance. He took Gerard’s face gently in both hands, kissed him on the brow, and was back at his devotions before the cell door had even clicked shut behind Gerard. The young traveller walked away chilled at heart, saddened by the quiet isolation of the monastic life. “Alas,” he thought, “here is a kind face I shall never see again on this earth, a kind voice gone from my ears forever. There is nothing but meeting and parting in this sorrowful world.”

A young monk soon interrupted his pensive mood, to lead him to the refectory. There Gerard found Denys standing stiff as a poker, being grilled by the assembled brothers on every town he planned to pass through. The friars pooled all their knowledge, mapped out a safe route for the two travellers, and marked down every hospitable religious house along the way, to save them ever needing to stop at a rogue’s inn. After supper, the old mechanical monk carried Gerard back to his cell for a long, eager chat, and finally explained the ridiculous agitated pantomime he had put on in the corridor earlier.

“You had nearly walked straight into Brother Jerome’s clutches! That first cell you were about to knock on was his.”

“Is Father Jerome an ill man, then?” Gerard asked.

“An ill man!” the friar crossed himself devoutly. “He is a saint, an anchorite, the very pillar of this house! He would have set you penance to walk barefoot all the way to Loretto—nay, since you are bound for Italy, that spiteful old earthly saint would have sent you hundreds of miles out of your way on detour pilgrimage to Canterbury or Compostella. Jerome was born old, and born wearing a cowl. Anselm and I were boys once, wild and sinful far more than you can imagine,” he added, at Gerard’s faintly incredulous look, “and that memory keeps us humble, and lenient to hot-blooded youth.” At Gerard’s earnest request, he played one last heavenly strain on the psaltery, before they turned in for the night: Gerard’s jangled, travel-worn spirits were calmed, his sore leg soothed, and his mind light.

I have described this quiet, contrasting day in full—this day that fell like oil on storm-tossed waves after so many passions and perils—because it stands as a representative for the many such peaceful days that followed it. Most readers will recognize this pattern from their own lives: stirring events never spread evenly across the whole journey, but come in clusters, by fits and starts, linked by subtle threads, or just happening as life wills it. Life is an intermittent fever, after all. Most narrators, of history or fiction, skip over these quiet barren stretches of time entirely, or pad them out with unnecessary detail, and that practice often gives readers a false sense that no time passes at all between grand adventures. I ask you to fill in the gaps with me, then: for weeks on end, one day’s steady march followed another, one monastery after another fed and lodged the pair free of charge, with welcome that was always charitable and often genial. The only foes they faced were cold winter winds and rough slushy roads, antagonists no less stubborn for not bearing swords. By the time they had tramped over hundreds of miles of that quiet, uneventful countryside, Gerard was tanned deep by sun and frost, dust-stained from head to toe, Denys’s boots were falling to tatters, and both of them were stiff, footsore, and drawing near the Burgundian frontier.
