The next day, Sunday, after mass, Catherine’s home on the Hoog Straet hummed with quiet, bright bustle. The new shop out front was fully kitted out at last, and Cornelis and Sybrandt were set to open its doors at dawn the next morning. Their names were painted in bold, gilded lettering above the lintel, alongside their trade sign: a spotless white lamb nuzzling a gilded ewe. Old Eli had trundled over earlier that day, bringing extra stock from his own warehouse to give the two boys a solid head start, no risk of falling short their first week of trade. The lads’ parents glowed with quiet pride to see their wastrel sons turn to honest work at last, while Cornelis and Sybrandt paced the back garden side by side, vowing they were sick to death of their old lazy, profligate days. Turning an honest guilder, they agreed, was a hundred times sweeter than squandering coin on drink and idle mischief, and they swore to stick to their business like wax to a candle, no slipping back. Catherine, ever sharp and watchful, overheard their resolution through the open parlour window, and slipped off to tell Eli, her heart light as thistledown.

The family supper planned that evening would include Margaret and her little boy, serving as an inaugural feast of sorts: the elders would pass on all their hard-earned trade wisdom, and spiced wine would flow freely to toast the two lads who had renounced the unprofitable vagrancy of their "wild oats" days. Margaret had come over early to help her mother-in-law with the cooking, half to shake off the heavy, listless languor that had clung to her for months, and both their cheeks were flushed as rosy as the glow from the great hearth fire.

Presently, Joan bustled in carrying a crisp, dewy salad fresh from Jorian’s vegetable plot. “He cut it special for you, Margaret! You’re all he chatters about these days, I’ll soon turn green with jealousy. Told him we’re laying a feast today, and he wouldn’t hear of you going without the freshest greens. But oh lass, that sermon in the new church just now? I’ve never heard preaching like that in all my born days.”

“Ah, I wish I’d been there,” Margaret said softly. “My soul’s been parched for want of that heavenly dew these past weeks.”

“He’s preaching again this very afternoon,” Joan told her. “Though I thought you’d be needed here, like enough.”

“Needed nowhere but where she wants to be,” Catherine cut in at once. “Off you go, lass, if your heart’s set on hearing him.”

“Truth be told,” Margaret smiled, “I think I won’t be such a damp squib at the supper table if I sit down to it warm from a good sermon, my heart lightened.”

“Then you’d best be quick,” Joan nodded to the street outside, where crowds of burghers were already making their way toward the church. “And look there, if it isn’t that holy hermit friar everyone’s been talking about. Bless us, Margaret—we almost forgot! I want a closer look at him.” The sprightly woman bounded out the door, darting across the high road to stop the friar before he could pass, and was back in the house before you could blink.

“There,” she panted. “I told him there’s a sick man waiting for his visit, and he said ‘My daughter, please God I will come to him before the sun sets.’ Sweetest voice I ever heard in my life. But oh, mistresses—he’s so young, and his cheeks are all thin and worn, and those great dark eyes? They’re almost the exact colour of yours, Margaret.”

“I’ve half a mind to go hear him,” Margaret said, fidgeting a little. “But my cap’s not as clean as it ought to be, and everyone else will be there in their spotless white mutches.”

“Here, take my linen handkerchief out of that basket, tuck it round your hairline,” Catherine said at once. “You can’t bring the little one with you, though—I need him to sit with poor Kate, it’s one of her bad pain days.”

Margaret didn’t argue, just carried the little boy up the stairs to Kate’s chamber. The poor girl was propped up in bed, her face tight with unspoken agony. “How fares my sweetheart?” Margaret said, not bothering to wait for an answer. “Nay, I don’t need to ask—you’re in sore pain, aren’t you? Look, I’ve brought someone you love.”

“Two, if you ask me,” Kate said, with that soft, angelic smile she wore even through the worst of her spasms. A wave of wrenched her then that would make a strong man roar, but she did not make a sound. “I love him far too dearly to ever feel his weight,” she said, when Margaret moved to set the toddler beside her on the covers.

Margaret set the boy down carefully, then tidied her hair and smoothed her skirt by the dresser looking glass. “I’m off to the church to hear that wonderful new preacher everyone’s gushing about,” she told Kate.

Kate sighed, a little wistful. “Only a minute ago I was all eager to go too,” Margaret added, “that’s how I’ve been these past months, up and down like the waves on the Zuyder Zee. I’d just as soon stay right here beside you, say the word and I’ll not step foot out the door.”

“Nay,” Kate said, soft. “Go on, please, and come back and tell me every single word he says. I wish more than anything I could go myself.” Tears glistened in the sick girl’s eyes, and that settled it for Margaret. She kissed Kate’s forehead, glanced down at the little boy, and heaved a soft little sigh. “I daren’t kiss him goodbye, not even a peck,” she said. “My father always said never wake a sleeping child, day or night. When he wakes up of his own accord, you can teach Aunt Kate those two new little words he’s just learned, eh?” She slipped out of the room, looking back over her shoulder at the pair of them with eyes full of love, and shut the door so softly it did not make a sound.

“Joan, will you give me a hand peeling these turnips and apples?” Catherine called from the kitchen below.

“Of course I will, dame,” Joan said, and the two set to work on the feast, quiet and steady, no idle chatter.

“Now Joan,” Catherine half-laughed, “the rule in this house is, those who help me cook and serve the feast get a full share of it too, no exceptions.”

“There’s far worse laws in all of Holland than that one,” Joan replied, grinning. “I’m happy to do it—my Luke’s out earning his supper digging ditches today, lucky for him the ground’s soft after last night’s rain.”

Just then Margaret came down the stairs, all fresh and tidy. “Eh, lass, look at you!” Catherine exclaimed. “You washed your face, smoothed your hair, and now there’s no one in all Rotterdam half as pretty as you!” She snapped her fingers playfully at the mention of the big city. “Come here, give me a kiss! And mind, Eli won’t wait supper for the Duke of Burgundy himself, so don’t you go lingering after the service ends.”

Joan and Catherine followed her to the front door, and stood there watching her walk down the Hoog Straet until she was almost out of sight. For these homely burgher housewives, a walk out to church on a Sunday afternoon was a small, special event all its own.

“Look at her go,” Catherine said softly to Joan, as they leaned in the doorway. “It feels almost like I’m seeing my own daughter off to church, same as I’ve done a hundred times before. Joan, that lass will not hear a single word said against our Gerard, not one. Even if he is alive right now, he’s treated her cruel as anything—and that’s why my bowels ache for the poor wench. I’m older, wiser than her, and I’ve made up my mind: I’ll wed her to that good, simple Luke, and that’ll be the end of all her heartache. What’s one grandchild, after all? Plenty of folk have bigger families, and she’ll have a good, steady roof over her head for the rest of her days.”
