IV. Her Habits—A Saunter

I told you I was enchanted by her in most ways. Yet there were things about her that unsettled me, even as they drew me closer. She stood a little above the average height for a woman, slender and graceful beyond compare. Her movements, however, carried a peculiar slowness—a languor so deep it seemed almost unnatural. There was nothing in her appearance to suggest illness, yet she moved like one weighed down by invisible chains. Her skin glowed with a rich, warm hue; her features were delicate and finely shaped; her eyes large, dark, and luminous, like pools of midnight oil. And then there was her hair—thick, long, and impossibly soft, cascading down her back like a waterfall of shadowed gold. I often ran my hands through it, marveling at its weight, its silkiness, how it fell in waves when she leaned back in her chair, speaking in that low, sweet voice that seemed to coil around my thoughts.

I loved to untie her hair, to braid it, to watch it tumble over her shoulders as she spoke. But now, looking back, I shudder at the memory. How could I have been so blind?

There were things she would not tell me—things she guarded fiercely. From the first night I met her, she had won my trust with her quiet grace and gentle confidence. But she kept herself wrapped in silence. Her mother, her past, her family—all remained hidden behind a veil of deliberate secrecy. I know now that I should have respected that. My father had been warned by the stately woman in black velvet, and perhaps I should have heeded it. But curiosity is a cruel master. It gnaws at the mind, refuses to be silenced. And I, a girl barely grown, could not endure being denied answers.

What harm could it do to know? I asked myself again and again. Why wouldn’t she trust me? I swore, with all sincerity, that I would never betray a word she might confide. Yet she refused—always, gently but firmly. There was a coldness in her smile, a melancholy so profound it felt less like sorrow than like something deeper, older. Not the sadness of youth, but of someone who has seen too much, or lived too long.

We never quarreled. She would not allow it. But I pressed her anyway. Not always rudely—sometimes I waited, watching for an opening, letting my questions slip out like whispers. Once or twice, I challenged her more directly. Each time, she evaded me with such beauty of expression, such tender regret, that I could not stay angry. She would take my hands, pull me close, rest her cheek against mine, and murmur into my ear:

“Dearest, your heart is wounded. Do not think me cruel. I obey a law I cannot break—a law written in my blood, in my strength, in my weakness. When you feel pain, I bleed with you. I live in your warmth, and you will die—die sweetly—into me. I cannot help it. As I draw near, you will draw near to others, and taste the same rapture, the same torment. For now, do not ask. Trust me. Trust me wholly.”

Her words were like music, lulling me into stillness. Her embrace tightened, trembling, her lips brushing my skin in soft, lingering kisses. I wanted to pull away—but my body refused. Her voice was a spell, weaving through my bones, dissolving resistance. In those moments, I felt both ecstasy and dread. A strange fire burned within me, mixed with fear, with loathing. I loved her—no, I adored her—and yet I recoiled. It was madness, and I knew it. But I could not name it.

Afterward, I would sit in silence, shaken. The memory of those moments haunts me still—ten years later, my hand trembling as I write, my mind clouded by horror, yet clear enough to remember the shape of her face, the sound of her voice.

Between these episodes, she was almost ordinary. Giddy, playful, sometimes lost in thought. Her manner was girlish, though always marked by a certain weariness—like a flower blooming in twilight. She came down late, usually after one o’clock, sipping chocolate without eating anything. Then we would walk—just a slow saunter through the woods, beneath the trees. She would grow tired quickly, collapsing onto a bench or returning to the schloss. But her mind never lagged. She talked endlessly, cleverly, with sharp observations and sudden flashes of insight. Occasionally, she mentioned her homeland—vague references to distant lands, customs we did not know, people whose ways were strange and ancient. I began to suspect her origins lay far beyond what I had imagined.

One afternoon, as we sat beneath the trees, a funeral passed by. It was the daughter of a forest ranger—pretty, young, and beloved. Her father walked behind the coffin, broken-hearted. Peasants followed in pairs, singing a hymn in mournful harmony. I rose to show respect, joining their song. But Carmilla grabbed my arm sharply.

“You don’t see how discordant that is?” she said, her voice clipped.

“It’s beautiful,” I replied, annoyed. “And I don’t want to offend them.”

She stopped me again. “You’re piercing my ears!” she hissed, pressing her fingers to her temples. “How can you know our religions are the same? Your rituals wound me. I hate funerals. Everyone dies. They’re happier when they do. Come home.”

“My father went to the churchyard with the priest. I thought you knew she was being buried today.”

“She? I don’t care about peasants. I don’t know who she is.” Her eyes flashed—cold, sudden, dangerous. “She was the girl who saw a ghost two weeks ago. She’s been dying ever since. Died yesterday.”

“Don’t speak of ghosts,” I whispered. “I’ll never sleep if you do.”

“There’s no plague, is there?” I continued. “The swineherd’s wife died last week. She said something seized her throat in bed, nearly strangled her. She was fine the day before. Then she collapsed. No fever, no illness—just... gone.”

Carmilla turned away. “Her funeral is over. Her hymn is sung. Let us leave this noise. Sit beside me. Hold my hand. Harder. Harder.”

We moved to another bench. She sat down. Then—her face changed. It drained of color, turning ashen. Her teeth clenched. Her hands curled into fists. She stared at the ground, trembling violently, as if fighting some inner storm. A low moan escaped her, ragged and desperate. It was not grief—not quite. It was something else—something primal. Then, slowly, the convulsions eased.

“There,” she said, breathless. “That’s what happens when you strangle people with hymns. Hold me. Keep me still. It’s passing.”

And it did. She calmed, then became unusually cheerful, chattering brightly as we walked back. This was the first time I’d seen her truly ill—the first time I’d seen her lose control. And the first time I’d seen anger in her.

Later, we stood at a window when a figure entered the courtyard: a hunchbacked wanderer I recognized well. He wore a pointed beard, dressed in buff, black, and scarlet, adorned with straps and belts, carrying a magic lantern and two boxes—one holding a salamander, the other a mandrake stitched from monkey, parrot, and hedgehog parts. He had a fiddle, foils, masks, and a staff with copper ends. His dog followed, stopping short at the drawbridge, then howling in protest.

He bowed deeply, speaking in broken French and German, then began to play and sing a lively tune, dancing with absurd energy. He approached the window, hat in hand, fiddle under his arm, babbling about his wonders.

“Will your ladyships buy an amulet against the oupire?” he called. “It’s spreading through the woods like the wolf. People die left and right. This charm—pinned to your pillow—will protect you. Laugh in its face!”

Carmilla bought one. So did I.

Then, as he looked up, his eye locked onto hers. He paused. Unrolled a leather case filled with tiny tools.

“See here, my lady,” he said, addressing me. “I practice dentistry among other arts. Plague take the dog! Silence, beast! That howling makes it hard to hear. Now, your noble friend—this young lady—has a tooth. Sharp. Long. Pointed. Like a needle. Ha, ha! I see it clearly. If it causes pain, I can fix it. File it, blunt it. Make it fit a beautiful young lady. Hey? Is she offended?”

Carmilla stepped back, furious. “How dare he insult us? Where is your father? I’ll demand justice. My father would have him tied to the pump and flogged!”

She retreated, then just as suddenly, her rage vanished. She sat, calm again, as if nothing had happened.

That evening, my father returned in low spirits. Another case—similar to the two recent deaths. A peasant girl, only a mile away, was sinking fast. She described the same symptoms: a choking grip, a presence in the room, a feeling of suffocation.

“All natural causes,” my father said. “They infect each other with superstition. Their imaginations repeat the horrors they’ve heard.”

“But that frightens me,” Carmilla said. “To imagine such things—wouldn’t it be worse than reality?”

“We are in God’s hands,” my father replied. “Nothing happens without His permission. Those who love Him will be safe.”

“Creator?” Carmilla murmured. “Nature? All things come from Nature—don’t they? Heaven, earth, under the earth—everything acts as Nature ordains. I believe so.”

The doctor arrived later. He was old, powdered, smooth-faced, skilled but cautious. After talking with my father, they emerged together.

“I wonder at a wise man like you,” my father said. “What do you say to hippogriffs and dragons?”

The doctor smiled faintly. “Life and death are mysterious states. We know little of either.”

I did not understand then. But now—I think I do.
