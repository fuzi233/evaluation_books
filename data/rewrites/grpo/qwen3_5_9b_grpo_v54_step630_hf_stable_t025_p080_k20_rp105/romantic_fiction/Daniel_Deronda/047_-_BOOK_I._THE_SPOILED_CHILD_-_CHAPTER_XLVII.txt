"And you must love him ere to you / He will seem worthy of your love."

There was a certain romantic charm in the way Daniel Deronda arranged the new wardrobe for Mordecai. He imagined the effect of the fine gray flannel shirts and the dressing-gown, a garment so somber and simple it resembled the brown frock of a Franciscan monk, draped over Mordecai's lean frame. Half of Daniel's delight stemmed from seeing his friend through Mirah's eyes, shielding her from any shock by presenting a vision of dignity rather than destitution. Yet, even as he finished the preparations, a shadow of doubt crept in. Was he perhaps misreading her nature? Could Mirah not appreciate the profound nobility of her brother precisely *because* of the poverty that marked his history? Was he trying to sanitize a truth that needed its raw edges? Still, he could not ignore the Meyricks. He knew their minds were not blessed with that rare capacity to bathe all things in the solemn glow of reverent love; they were prone to repugnance. So, Daniel carefully hid away anything that might feed their prejudice, curating a presentation of Mordecai that would be palatable to them.

The atmosphere in the Meyrick household that evening, however, suggested otherwise. After Mirah had retired, the family gathered around the fire. Hans, now settled in his Chelsea rooms, lingered late, his laughter echoing off the walls. Mrs. Meyrick poked the fire into a blaze, her voice bright with anticipation. "Now, Kate, put out your candle, and let us all sit cosily by the fire. Hans, my dear, stop laughing at those poems for the ninety-ninth time and join us. I have something wonderful to tell."

Kate, already perched with her feet on the fender, rolled her eyes. "As if I didn't know that, ma. I've seen it in the corner of your eye for weeks, along with your endless excuses."

"Well, then, since you are so wise," Mrs. Meyrick announced, "perhaps you know that Mirah's brother has been found!"

"Oh, confound it!" Hans exploded instantly.

"Hans, that is wicked," Mab scolded, clutching her knees. "Suppose we had lost you?"

"I cannot help being rather sorry," Kate admitted, though her tone lacked genuine warmth. "And where is her mother?"

"Dead."

"I hope the brother isn't a bad man," Amy ventured timidly.

"Nor a fellow all smiles and jewelry—a Crystal Palace Assyrian with a hat on," Hans retorted in his worst mood.

"Were there ever such unfeeling children?" Mrs. Meyrick protested, emboldened by their lack of sympathy. "You don't care a whit about Mirah's joy."

"You know, ma, Mirah hardly remembers her brother," Kate interjected.

"People who are lost for twelve years should never come back again," Hans declared, pacing the room with his hands deep in his brown velveteen coat pockets. "They are always in the way."

"Hans!" Mrs. Meyrick reproached him. "If you had lost me for *twenty* years, I should have thought—"

"I said twelve years," Hans cut her off sharply. "Anywhere about twelve years is the time at which lost relations should keep out of the way."

"But it's nice finding people—there is something to tell," Mab said softly. "Did Prince Camaralzaman find him?"

Mrs. Meyrick began her neat, rapid narrative, recounting everything she knew without interruption. "Mr. Deronda has the highest admiration for him," she concluded, "and seems quite to look up to him. He says Mirah is just the sister to understand this brother."

"Deronda is getting perfectly preposterous about those Jews," Hans sneered, rising and banging his chair away from the hearth. "He wants to do everything he can to encourage Mirah in her prejudices."

"Oh, for shame, Hans! To speak in that way of Mr. Deronda," Mab cried out. Mrs. Meyrick's face tightened, revealing an undercurrent of disapproval she refused to voice aloud.

"And now we shall never be all together," Hans continued, walking restlessly. "But we must have this prophet Elijah to tea with us, and Mirah will think of nothing but sitting on the ruins of Jerusalem. She will be spoiled as an artist—mind that! She'll get as narrow as a nun. Everything will be spoiled—our home and everything. I shall take to drinking."

"Oh, really, Hans," Kate snapped, impatient. "I do think men are the most contemptible animals in all creation. Every one of them must have everything to his mind, else he is unbearable."

"Oh, oh, oh, it's very dreadful!" Mab wailed dramatically. "I feel as if ancient Nineveh were come again."

"I should like to know what is the good of having gone to the university and knowing everything, if you are so childish, Hans," Amy reasoned. "You ought to put up with a man that Providence sends you to be kind to. *We* shall have to put up with him."

"I hope you will all of you like the new Lamentations of Jeremiah—'to be continued in our next'—that's all," Hans dismissed, grabbing his wide-awake. "It's no use being one thing more than another if one has to endure the company of those men with a fixed idea, staring blankly at you, and requiring all your remarks to be small foot-notes to their text. If you're to be under a petrifying wall, you'd better be an old boot. I don't feel myself an old boot." With a hasty, desperate kiss to his mother's brow and a condescending nod to the girls, he swept out the door.

"Suppose Mirah knew how you are behaving," Kate muttered, but her answer was the slam of the front door. "I *should* like to see Mirah when Mr. Deronda tells her," she told her mother, her voice softening. "I know she will look so beautiful."

But Daniel Deronda, reconsidering his approach, had written a letter instead. He asked Mrs. Meyrick to make the revelation the following morning rather than waiting for him to arrive. He omitted the true reason—that he shrank from re-entering a narrative where he felt he was playing the hero, bestowing general beneficence upon others. Instead, he requested to remain with Mordecai while Mrs. Meyrick brought Mirah for what would be framed as a visit. This arrangement would create a necessary interval before the permanent change of abode that Daniel knew Mirah would soon propose.

Secretly, Daniel felt a wondering anxiety. How far would Mordecai, after years of solitary preoccupation with ideas that had grown increasingly exclusive due to his diminishing physical strength, allow himself to feel a tender interest in his sister beyond mere pious duty? His affection for the Cohens, especially little Jacob, showed a persistent warmth, but those had been part of his daily life for years. Now, Mordecai asked no new questions about Mirah, maintaining an unusual silence and seemingly submitting to the coming changes. He donned the new clothes obediently but later told Daniel, with a faint smile, "I must keep my old garments by me for a remembrance." When they sat awaiting Mirah, Mordecai uttered no word, keeping his eyelids closed, yet his face and hands betrayed a restless agitation. In fact, Mordecai was undergoing a peculiar nervous perturbation known only to those whose minds, long driven by a single strong current, are suddenly forced into a new or reopened channel. People whose strength has been absorbed by dormant biases dread an interview that imperiously revives the past as they would dread a threatening illness. Joy may be there, but joy, too, is terrible.

Daniel caught the infection of this excitement. When he heard the ring at the door, he went out, not knowing exactly why, simply needing to see and greet Mirah beforehand. He was startled to find her wearing the hat and cloak in which he had first seen her—the memorable cloak that had once been wetted to serve as a winding-sheet. She had come downstairs equipped in this manner. When Mrs. Meyrick asked in a questioning tone, "You like to go in that dress, dear?" Mirah replied, "My brother is poor, and I want to look as much like him as I can, else he may feel distant from me"—imagining she would meet him in his workman's dress. Daniel could make no remark, feeling secretly ashamed of his own fastidious arrangements. They shook hands silently; Mirah looked pale and awed.

When Daniel opened the door for her, Mordecai had risen, his eyes turned toward the entrance with an eager, hungry gaze. Mirah took only two or three steps before stopping. They looked at each other, motionless. It was less their own presence that they felt than another's; they were meeting first in memories, compared with which physical touch was no union. Mirah was the first to break the silence, standing exactly where she was.

"Ezra," she said, in exactly the same tone as when she had spoken of her mother's call to him.

With a sudden movement, Mordecai advanced and laid his hand on her shoulders. He was the head taller, looking down at her tenderly. "That was our mother's voice. You remember her calling me?"

"Yes, and how you answered her—'Mother!'—and I knew you loved her." Mirah threw her arms round her brother's neck, clasping her little hands behind it, and drew his face down to kiss it with childlike lavishness. Her hat fell backward onto the floor, disclosing all her curls.

"Ah, the dear head, the dear head!" Mordecai whispered in a low, loving tone, laying his thin hand gently on the curls.

"You are very ill, Ezra," Mirah said, looking at him with sad observation.

"Yes, dear child, I shall not be long with you in the body," he answered quietly.

"Oh, I will love you and we will talk to each other," Mirah poured out, her words as spontaneous as bird-notes. "I will tell you everything, and you will teach me: you will teach me to be a good Jewess—what she would have liked me to be. I shall always be with you when I am not working. For I work now. I shall get money to keep us. Oh, I have had such good friends."

Mirah had quite forgotten anyone else was present, but then she turned with the prettiest attitude, keeping one hand on her brother's arm while she looked at Mrs. Meyrick and Daniel. The little mother's happy emotion in witnessing this reunion had already won her to Mordecai; he seemed to her to possess a dignity and refinement far greater than she had been willing to believe from Daniel's account.

"See this dear lady!" Mirah exclaimed. "I was a stranger, a poor wanderer, and she believed in me, and has treated me as a daughter. Please give my brother your hand," she added beseechingly, taking Mrs. Meyrick's hand and placing it in Mordecai's, then pressing them both with her own and lifting them to her lips.

"The Eternal Goodness has been with you," Mordecai said. "You have helped to fulfill our mother's prayer."

"I think we will go now, shall we?—and return later," Daniel suggested, laying a gentle pressure on Mrs. Meyrick's arm. She immediately complied. He was afraid of any reference to the facts about himself that he had kept back from Mordecai, and now he felt no uneasiness in the thought of the brother and sister being alone together.
