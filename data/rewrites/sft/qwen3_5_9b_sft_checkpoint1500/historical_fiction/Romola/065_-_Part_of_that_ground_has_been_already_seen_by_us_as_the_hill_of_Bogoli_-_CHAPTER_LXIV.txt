The Prophet in His Cell.

Tito's arrival at San Marco had been anticipated, and Fra Niccolò, Savonarola's secretary, ushered him immediately up the spiraling staircases into the long, echoing corridors. These halls were lined with cells, some adorned with the frescoes of Fra Angelico. To the unaccustomed eye, these delicate paintings, soft as rainbows on melting clouds, appeared as sudden reflections from an ethereal realm, where the Virgin sat crowned in radiance and the Infant Christ gazed forth with perpetual promise.

It was an hour of quietude within the monastery; most cells stood empty. Through narrow slits, pale light illuminated nothing but bare plaster, a hard straw pallet, and a wooden crucifix. Yet, at the far end of a lengthy corridor, behind a door leading to an antechamber where the Prior typically worked, a shaft of sunlight struck a solitary figure. It was the back of a man clad in the long white tunic and scapular of the Dominican Order, kneeling with bowed head before the cross. To the casual observer, this might have been any ordinary friar, burdened only by trivial sins—a fleeting thought of impropriety during choir service, or a petty resentment when a colleague spilled ink upon his illuminations. Such a soul would seek merely the safety of Paradise via the narrow ladder of prayer, fasting, and obedience.

But beneath this simple garb beat a heart vibrating with a consciousness alien to the average monk, perhaps difficult even for a man of self-knowledge attained through turmoil to fully grasp. Within that breast, irrevocable errors and lapses from truth were so intricately woven with noble aspirations and sincere convictions that a clear path for the conscience remained elusive. Self-serving expediency was inextricably bound to the fabric of a grand design, one that the entire being seemed incapable of abandoning, just as the flesh could not ignore the glimmer and tremble of hope and dread.

Savonarola was not merely in the posture of prayer; Latin supplications flowed from his lips, yet his spirit was not truly praying. Having entered his cell and collapsed to his knees, he had sought a divine influx of calmness, hoping it would validate the resolve urged upon him by swirling thoughts and passions, assuring him that he was not being dragged away from God's support. However, the visions and impulses that had tormented him for the past hour were too imperious to be stilled. While his hands pressed against his face and his mouth articulated "Create in me a clean heart," his mind remained occupied with the intricate snares his enemies had devised, busy constructing arguments to counter their taunts and accusations.

His defense extended beyond his adversaries. Earlier that morning, he had received fresh evidence that his own friends and followers were as eager to press forward with the Trial by Fire as his foes. They desired, indeed tacitly expected, that he finally accept the challenge and evoke the long-awaited miracle designed to dispel doubt and vanquish malice. Had he not proclaimed that God would reveal Himself at the appointed time? To the plain Florentines, impatient to settle partisan disputes, no moment seemed more fitting than this. Surely, if Fra Domenico walked through the flames unscathed, *that* would be a miracle, and the fervor of that devoted brother served as a comforting omen. Yet Savonarola remained acutely aware that the secret, feverish longing of his disciples to see him step into the fire had not been quelled by his previous reasons for refusal.

Nevertheless, satisfying them proved impossible, and with bitter anguish, he realized he could no longer resist the prosecution of the trial regarding Fra Domenico. It was not that Savonarola had spoken a falsehood when declaring his belief in a future supernatural attestation of his work; his constitution allowed him to easily envision a miracle distant and undefined, shielded by strong reasons for its occurrence. It was equally easy for him to believe in internal miracles, such as his prophetic inspiration and divinely wrought intuitions. Conversely, it was insurmountably difficult for him to believe in the probability of a miracle involving specific details, like walking unharmed through fire, which demanded not only faith but extraordinary action.

Savarola's nature possessed a duality wherein opposing tendencies coexisted with near-equal strength. A passionate sensibility, impatient of fixed thought and flooding every idea with emotion, tended toward contemplative ecstasy, alternating with a keen perception of external facts and a vigorous, practical judgment of men and circumstances. In this instance, the latter qualities were stimulated into unusual activity by an acute physical sensitivity, which endowed the concept of pain and destruction with overpowering force. This sensitivity stemmed from the realization that such suffering was a necessary sequence of causes already felt. Just as men readily consent to touch red-hot iron with a wet finger despite theoretically knowing it will burn them—their practical belief depending on what is most vividly represented in the mind at the moment—so too did Savonarola's constitution react. When the Trial by Fire was presented as an immediate demand to his imagination, it became impossible for him to believe that he, or any other man, could traverse the flames unscathed. Even if he resolved to offer himself, he would inevitably shrink at the last instant.

However, the common Florentine was unlikely to make such nuanced distinctions. To the general run of mankind, finding moral questions easy and judging conduct by concise alternatives appears a mark of mental vigor. Nothing seemed plainer than that a man who once declared God would not leave him without a miraculous guarantee, yet recoiled when the proposal to test that declaration arose, had spoken what he did not truly believe. Were not Fra Domenico and Fra Mariano, along with countless *Piagnoni*, ready to enter the fire? What caused their superior courage if not superior faith? Savonarola could not explain his conduct satisfactorily to his friends, nor could he fully articulate it to himself. Human naked feelings hasten to clothe themselves in convenient propositions, and giving a true account of one's interior state requires something beyond mere sincerity, even when that sincerity is unmixed. In these very moments, as Savonarola knelt in audible prayer, he had ceased to hear the words on his lips. They were drowned by argumentative voices within, shaping reasons increasingly for an outward audience.

"To appeal to Heaven for a miracle by rashly accepting a challenge, which is but a snare prepared by ignoble foes, would be tempting God, and the appeal would remain unanswered. Let the Pope's legate come, let the ambassadors of all great powers arrive promising that the convocation of a General Council and the reform of the Church shall hinge upon this miracle, and *then* I will enter the flames, trusting that God will not withhold His seal from that great work. Until then, I reserve myself for higher duties directly laid upon me: it is not permitted to me to leap from the chariot merely to wrestle with every loud vaunter. Yet, Fra Domenico's invincible zeal to enter the trial may signify a Divine vocation, may serve as a pledge that the miracle—"

"No!" The thought shattered the sentence. When Savonarola brought his mind close to the threatened scene in the Piazza, imagining a human body stepping into the fire, his belief recoiled anew. It was not an event his imagination could simply visualize; he felt it with shuddering vibrations coursing through his sensitive extremities. The miracle could not occur. Indeed, the trial itself was not destined to take place; he was warranted in exerting all his power to prevent it. The fuel might be gathered in the Piazza, the crowd assembled, the formalities completed—all this was perhaps inevitable now, and he could no longer resist it without bringing dishonor upon himself, yes, and thereby upon the Cause of God. Yet, it was not truly intended that the Franciscan should enter the fire. While Savonarola hesitated, there would be means to prevent Fra Domenico's entrance. At the very worst, if Fra Domenico were compelled to proceed, he would carry the consecrated Host with him, and with that Mystery in his hand, there might be a warrant for expecting the ordinary effects of fire to be suspended. More probably, this demand would be resisted, thus becoming a final obstacle to the trial.

These intentions, however, could not be avowed. He must appear frankly awaiting the trial and trusting in its outcome. This dissidence between inward reality and outward semblance was not the Christian simplicity for which he had stroven through his youth and prime, nor the fruit of the Divine life he had preached. In the stress and heat of the day, with cheeks burning and shouts ringing in his ears, how blessed was he who remembered the yearnings of the cool, silent morning and knew he had not betrayed them?

"Oh God, it is for the sake of the people—for they are blind—and because their faith depends upon me. If I put on sackcloth and cast myself among the ashes, who will take up the standard and lead the battle? Have I not been led by a path unknown to the work that lies before me?"

The conflict could not end. In the effort of prayerful pleading, his uneasy mind laved its sting continually in thoughts of the greatness of that task for which no other man existed to fulfill it if he abandoned it. It was no commonplace matter for a man to be inspired with the vision and daring that made a sacred rebel.

Even the words of prayer had faded. He continued to kneel, but his mind was filled with images of consequences rippling across Europe. The sense of immediate difficulties was being lost in the glow of that vast vision when the knock at the door announced the expected visitor.
