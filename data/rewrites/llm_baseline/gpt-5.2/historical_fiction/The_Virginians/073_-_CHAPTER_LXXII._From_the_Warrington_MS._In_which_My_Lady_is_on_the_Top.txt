Looking across the fire, towards the chair that has been my familiar harbour these many years, and towards her who has sat there—my partner in every common day and every uncommon tremor—I find myself, as middle-aged gentlemen will, repeating the same question because the answer still warms me: whether any two young people were ever more rash, more penniless, more foolishly resolved than we were when we married in the year the old King died.

Miles, my son, who has taken such prodigious leaps in the heat of fox-hunting that he seems to fly rather than ride, says that afterwards he surveys the gaps and rivers he crossed so safely with mere horror, and wonders at his own headlong madness. Yet no man in our two counties follows hounds more eagerly than he. Scent and cry are his politics; he wants no other. He has broken a collar-bone, and has had a hundred tumbles, each one a stab to his mother’s heart. And I—who am accounted a sober Justice, a scribbler of verses, a man to speak at Quarter Sessions—have been pitched over old Sultana’s head at four miles an hour, because the mare slipped on a stone while we were both dreaming along a park road, I thinking, perhaps, of a line that would not scan, or of a speech that would not end. His recklessness is the delight of his life, as my marriage has been the blessing of mine; and I never recall our imprudence but to thank Heaven for it.

Do not imagine I set up my worship as an example. I do not go preaching to all young folk, “Marry upon twopence a year!” or our vestry would look black enough at me. Yet my wife—who can tie knots in a household as neatly as any vicar ties them at the altar—is known to be a desperate match-maker; and when Hodge and Susan come into my justice-room with their timid talk of allowance and wages, we bid them spend their half-crown a week at home, add a little contribution of our own, and send for the parson. There are economies that starve a hearth, and economies that feed it. My lady has always known the difference, though she will laugh at me and say the difference is only whether I am the one to pay.

When I put questions to my dear oracle, I know beforehand what she will answer; which is, no doubt, why I consult her so often. I have but to wear a particular expression, and my Diana takes her reflection from it as quickly as the kettle takes heat from the fire. Suppose I say, “My dear, don’t you think the moon was made of cream cheese to-night?” She will answer, “Well, papa, it did look very like cream cheese indeed—there’s nobody like you for droll similes.” And, truth to tell, there are nights when the great pale disc hangs over our orchard like a round from some dairy in Norfolk: white as curd newly lifted, with a faint bluish rind, as if it had lain on clean linen in a cool pantry; and the owls make their soft questions, and the distant brook keeps time in its own tongue, and one may fancy the heavens themselves have a larder.

Or suppose I say, “My love, Mr. Pitt’s speech was very fine, but I don’t think he is equal to what I remember of his father.” “Nobody was equal to my Lord Chatham,” says my wife at once; and then one of the girls cries out, “Why, I have often heard our papa say Lord Chatham was a charlatan!” upon which mamma—who has a small armoury of phrases, each sharpened by long use—says, “How like she is to her Aunt Hetty!” The child laughs; the fire cracks; the old clock speaks its steady syllables in the corner; and all the while, under those English words we toss so lightly, there are other older sounds we do not hear—phrases my grandmother would have known, country turns of speech, scraps of dialect, half a prayer, half a curse, now gone out like an extinguished candle. The girls will never miss them, for you cannot mourn what you have never tasted.

As for Miles, Tros Tyriusve is all one to him. He reads only the sporting announcements in the Norwich paper. So long as there is good scent, he does not care about the state of the country. The music that governs his heart is not the voice of Parliament, but the whole living choir of a hunting morning: the horn cutting the mist like bright steel; the hounds’ deep-throated argument rolling over the fallows; the thud of hooves on turf; and—when modern times bring their coarse intrusions—the distant hammer of some new road-mender’s cart, the iron-shod din that startles even the rooks. Give Miles a clear wind and no wagons clattering, and he is content.

I believe the rascal has never read my poems—much more my tragedies. I mentioned Pocahontas to him the other day, and the dunce thought she was a river in Virginia. My Latin verses? How should he understand them when I know he can’t construe Corderius? Why, this very notebook lies publicly on the little table by my corner of the fireside, and any one may read it who will take the trouble of lifting my spectacles off the cover: but Miles never does. I insert loose pages with caricatures of Miles; jokes against him; little malices a father allows himself because he loves. He neither knows nor heeds them.

Only once, in place of a neat drawing of mine in China ink, representing Miles asleep after dinner—an after-dinner sleep so deep it seemed he had been basted and set to rest like a joint left too long before the hearth, and which my friend Bunbury would not have disowned—I found a rude picture of myself going over Sultana’s head, entitled, “The Squire on Horseback, or Fish out of Water.” And the fellow roared with laughter, and all the girls tittered, when I came upon the page! My wife said she never was in such a fright as when I went to my book; but I can bear a jest against myself, and have heard many—though strange to say, for one who has lived among some of the chief wits of the age, I never heard a good one in my life. Never mind, Miles. Though thou art not a wit, I love thee none the worse; there never was any love lost between two wits in a family.

And though thou hast no great beauty, thy mother thinks thee as handsome as Apollo—or as His Royal Highness the Prince of Wales, who was born in the very same year with thee. Indeed, she always maintained that Coates’s picture of the Prince was very like her eldest boy, and she has the print in her dressing-room to this very day.

In that same year, with what different prospects, my Lord Esmond—Lord Castlewood’s son—likewise appeared to adorn the world. My Lord C. and his humble servant had already come to a coolness at that time, and Heaven knows my honest Miles’s godmother, at his entrance into life, brought no gold pap-boats to his christening. Matters have mended since, laus Deo—laus Deo indeed!—for I suspect neither Miles nor his father would ever have been able to do much for themselves by their own wits.

Castlewood House has quite another face now from that venerable one it wore in my youth, when it seemed covered with the wrinkles of time—the scars of old wars, the cracks and blemishes years had scored into its hoary features. I best love to remember it in its old shape, as I saw it when young Mr. George Warrington went down at the owner’s invitation to be present at his lordship’s marriage with Miss Lydia Van den Bosch—“an American lady of noble family of Holland,” as the county paper announced her. Then the towers stood as Warrington’s grandfather the Colonel (the Marquis, as Madam Esmond would fain have called her father) had seen them. The woods—thinned, to be sure—were there, and perhaps the self-same rooks that had cawed over them threescore years before. His picture hung in the hall which might have been his, had he not preferred love and gratitude to wealth and worldly honour.

And I, Egomet Ipse who set this down, walking those long corridors, those smooth dew-spangled terraces, those cool darkling avenues where the leaves whispered in their own old dialect, felt for a while as if I were one of Mr. Walpole’s cavaliers with ruff and rapier, buff-coat and gorget; as if an Old Pretender, or a Jesuit emissary in disguise, might step from behind any tall trunk, or glide from any antique carved cupboard within. The place had a sound, too—an inhabited hush: the soft complaint of boards that had borne generations; the hollow answer of a stair; the far clink of kitchen iron; the rookery’s rough parliament in the trees. In such a house the past does not merely show itself; it speaks, though you cannot always translate its tongue.

I had the strangest, saddest, pleasantest old-world fancies as I walked: I imagined tragedies, intrigues, serenades, escaladoes; Oliver’s Roundheads battering the towers; bluff Hal’s Beefeaters pricking over the plain before the castle. I was then courting a certain young lady—madam, your ladyship’s eyes had no need of spectacles then, and on the brow above them there was never a wrinkle nor a silver hair—and I remember I wrote a ream of romantic description, under my Lord Castlewood’s franks, to the lady who never tired of reading my letters. She says I only send her three lines now when I am away in London or elsewhere. ’Tis that I may not fatigue your old eyes, my dear.

Mr. Warrington thought himself authorised to order a genteel new suit for my lord’s marriage, and with Monsieur Gumbo in attendance made his appearance at Castlewood a few days before the ceremony. I may mention that it had been found expedient to send my faithful Sady home on board a Virginia ship. A great inflammation attacking throat and lungs, fatal in very many cases in that year of Wolfe’s expedition, had seized and well-nigh killed my poor lad; and his native air was pronounced the best cure. We parted with an abundance of tears. Gumbo shed as many when his master went to Quebec; but he had attractions in this country, and none for the military life, so remained attached to my service.

We found Castlewood full of friends, relations, and visitors. Lady Fanny was there upon compulsion, a sulky bridesmaid. Some virgins of the neighbourhood attended the young Countess. A bishop’s widow herself, the Baroness Beatrix brought down a holy brother-in-law of the bench from London to tie the holy knot between Eugene Earl of Castlewood and Lydia Van den Bosch, spinster; and for some time before and after the nuptials the old house in Hampshire wore an air of gaiety to which it had long been unaccustomed.

The country families came gladly to pay their compliments. The lady’s wealth was everybody’s subject, and no doubt did not diminish in the telling. Those naughty stories rife in town, spread by disappointed suitors, took time to travel into Hampshire; and when they arrived, the country was disposed to treat Lord Castlewood’s wife with civility, and not inclined to be too curious about her behaviour in London. Suppose she had jilted this man and laughed at the other? It was her money they were anxious about, and she was no more mercenary than they.

The Hampshire folks were determined it was a great benefit to the country to have Castlewood House open once more, with beer in the cellars, horses in the stables, and spits turning before the kitchen fires. And indeed there is a kind of peace that comes of a well-governed kitchen: the steady chop of herbs on a board, the sigh of a joint as it meets the heat, the measured clatter of pewter, the yeasty breath of ale, the whole domestic orchestra playing without discord. The new lady took her place with great dignity; and it was certain she had uncommon accomplishments and wit. Was it not printed in the marriage advertisements that her ladyship brought her noble husband seventy thousand pounds? On a beaucoup d’esprit with seventy thousand pounds. The Hampshire people said it was but a small portion of her wealth. When the grandfather should fall, ever so many plums would be found on that old tree.

That quiet old man—keen as a knife at a carver’s board—began quickly to put the dilapidated Castlewood accounts in order, which long neglect, poverty, and improvidence had hurried towards ruin. The business of his life now, and for some time henceforth, was to advance, improve, mend my lord’s finances; to screw rents up where practicable; to pare establishment expenses down. He could somehow look to every yard of worsted lace on the footmen’s coats, and every pound of beef that went to their dinner. A watchful old eye noted every flagon of beer fetched from the buttery, and marked that no waste occurred in the larder. One might fancy him tasting the household with his mind, as a careful cook tastes a pot—knowing, by the least excess or thinness, where a hand has been careless. The people were fewer, but more regularly paid; the liveries were not so ragged, and yet the tailor had no need to dun. The gardeners and grooms grumbled, though their wages were no longer overdue: but the horses fattened on less corn, and fruit and vegetables were ever so much more plentiful—so narrowly did my lady’s old grandfather keep watch over the whole from his lonely little chamber in the turret.

These improvements, though told here in a paragraph, were the affairs of months and years at Castlewood; where, with thrift, order, and judicious outlay (though upon some pressing occasions my lord might say he had none), the estate and household grew in prosperity. That it was flourishing and economical, no one could deny—not even the dowager lady and her two children, who now seldom entered within Castlewood gates, my lady considering them enemies; for who, indeed, would like a stepmother-in-law? The little reigning Countess gave the dowager battle, and routed her utterly and speedily. Though educated in the colonies and ignorant of polite life in her early years, Countess Lydia had a power of language and a strength of will that all acknowledged who quarrelled with her.

And here again was a lesson of tongues: for power is not only in gold, nor in a title, but in the words you choose and the accent you dare to keep. Lydia could strike with a phrase as neatly as a cook splits a fowl at the joint; and the dowager and my Lady Fanny were no match for the young American. They fled before her to their jointure house in Kensington, and no wonder their absence was not regretted by my lord, who was in the habit of regretting no one whose back was turned.

Could cousin Warrington—whose hand his lordship pressed so affectionately at coming and parting, with whom cousin Eugene was so gay, frank, and pleasant when together—expect or hope that his lordship would grieve at his departure, his death, any misfortune that might befall him, or any soul alive? Cousin Warrington knew better.

Always of a sceptical turn, Mr. W. took a grim delight in watching the peculiarities of his neighbours, and could like this one even though he had no courage and no heart. Courage? Heart? What are these to you and me in the world? A man may have private virtues as he may have half a million in the funds. What we du monde expect is that he should be lively, agreeable, keep a decent figure, and pay his way.

Colonel Esmond Warrington’s grandfather—whose history and dwelling-place Mr. W. took an extraordinary interest in—might once have been owner of this house of Castlewood, and of the titles belonging to its possessor. Mr. Warrington often looked at the Colonel’s grave picture as it still hung in the saloon, a copy or replica of which he fondly remembered in Virginia. “He must have been a little touched here,” my lord would say, tapping his own tall, placid forehead. There are certain actions, simple and common with some men, which others cannot understand, and so deny as lies or deride as madness.

“I do you the justice to think, cousin,” says Mr. Warrington to his lordship, “that you would not give up any advantage for any friend in the world.”

“Eh! I am selfish. But am I more selfish than the rest of the world?” asks my lord, with a French shrug and a pinch out of his box.

Once, in their walks in the fields, his lordship happening to wear a fine scarlet coat, a cow ran towards him; and the ordinarily languid nobleman sprang over a stile with the agility of a schoolboy. He did not conceal his tremor, nor his natural want of courage. “I dare say you respect me no more than I respect myself, George,” he would say in his candid way; and then begin a very pleasant, sardonical discourse upon the fall of man, and our faults and shortcomings; and wonder why Heaven had not made us all brave and tall and handsome and rich.

As for Mr. Warrington—who very likely loved to be king of his company, as some do—he could not help liking this kinsman: so witty, graceful, polished, high-placed in the world, and so utterly his inferior. Like the animal in Mr. Sterne’s famous book, “Do not beat me,” his lordship’s look seemed to say; “but, if you will, you may.” No man—save a bully and coward himself—deals hardly with a creature so spiritless.
