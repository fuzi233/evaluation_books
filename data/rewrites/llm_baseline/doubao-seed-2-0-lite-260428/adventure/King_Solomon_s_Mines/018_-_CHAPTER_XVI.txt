By the third night after the chaos of the battle we’d left behind, we were camped in crude, smoke-stained huts at the foot of the Three Witches: that triangle of jagged, snow-capped peaks where Solomon’s Great Road dead-ended at last. Our party was small, all things considered: me (Quatermain), Sir Henry Curtis, Captain Good, Foulata who tended to us all and doted especially on Good, Infadoos, the ancient crone Gagool trundled along in a curtained litter muttering curses nonstop, and a handful of loyal, quiet Kukuana guards.

The three peaks, forged by some ancient volcanic upheaval, loomed straight up into the darkening sky, and when dawn broke the next morning I swore I’d never seen a more staggering sight. Their twisted snow-wreaths glowed pink high above the heather-purple lower slopes, and the white paved ribbon of that 3,000-year-old road stretched straight uphill five miles from our camp to the very foot of the central peak. There it ended, exactly as Dom Jose da Silvestra’s blood-scrawled map had promised. This was the spot that had killed the old Portuguese explorer three centuries prior, that had likely cost us Sir Henry’s lost brother George, too. After every danger we’d survived crossing the desert and fighting through Kukuana lands, were we fated to join that list of the unlucky? A thick, superstitious hush hung over all three of us white men as we set off that morning, no matter how hard we tried to shake it.

We moved so fast along the heather-fringed road that Gagool’s bearers could barely keep pace. The old witch poked her shrivelled, hideous face out of the litter curtains, her glinting flint eyes locking on ours, and piped at us to slow down. “Why do you run to meet the evil that waits for you, treasure-hunters?” she cackled that guttural, horrible laugh that sent cold shivers skittering down my spine, killing our eager enthusiasm for a full minute. But we pushed on anyway, until the road curved around a rise, and we stopped dead.

Below us yawned a circular pit half a mile wide, 300 feet deep, its sloped sides lined with the exact same blue stiff clay I’d seen in the Kimberley diamond diggings. I pointed out the worn, flat stone slabs half-buried in brush on the lower slope of the pit—ancient washing tables for sorting diamond-bearing ore. This wasn’t myth. This was Solomon’s actual mine, the pit marked clear as day on that tattered old map.

The Great Road curved around the rim of the pit, shored up by heavy cut stone blocks to stop the reef from caving in, and as we rounded the far side we spotted them: the three Silent Ones, the colossal statues the Kukuana people held in such reverent awe. Seated on dark rock pedestals 40 paces apart from each other, each 30 feet tall, they stared unblinking down the 60-mile stretch of plain leading to Loo. The nude female figure, its features worn soft by centuries of wind and rain, had distinct crescent horns rising from her forehead. Sir Henry, a classicist who’d taken top honours at university, identified her instantly: Ashtoreth, the Phoenician Astarte, the false goddess King Solomon had turned to in his later years. The two male flanking statues were draped in carved robes: the one on our right had the face of a raving devil, while the left one held a cold, unearthly serenity that reeked of the inhuman cruelty ancient civilizations prayed to. Three relics from a lost age, left to sit there in sun and storm, watching the empty plains forever.

Infadoos walked up, saluting the statues by lifting his spear, and asked if we wanted to rest and eat a midday meal before entering the forbidden Place of Death. But curiosity burned too hot in our chests to wait. It was barely 11 a.m. when we told him we’d go in at once, and I asked Foulata to stow a supply of dried biltong and two full gourds of water in a hinged reed basket, just in case we got trapped deep inside. Gagool was hauled out of her litter, leaning on a gnarled walking stick, grinning her gap-toothed evil grin at us all before she hobbled 50 paces past the back of the colossi, toward a sheer 80-foot rock wall that sloped up thousands of feet to the snow line. Half-hidden in shadow, there was a solid arched stone portal, the entrance to the mountain’s hidden galleries.

Infadoos refused to cross the threshold. “I will not step foot in the Hall of the Dead,” he warned Gagool, his voice sharp as a spear point. “You guard my lords well. If a single hair on their heads is harmed, witch or no witch, I will have your head before sundown.” Gagool just cackled, mocking him for his big words, and pulled a large rush-wick lamp full of oil out from under her fur cloak. Good asked Foulata if she wanted to wait outside with the rest of the guards, but the girl shook her head and clung tight to his arm, saying wherever he went, she would follow.

Gagool plunged into the dark passage first. We followed close behind, and 50 paces in a swarm of bats rushed past our faces, one smacking Good right in the cheek hard enough to make him yelp. I told him not to panic, and seconds later the dark began to thin, and we stepped out into a cave so vast it could have swallowed the largest cathedral ever built by human hands. Its arched roof soared 100 feet above our heads, dimly lit by hidden air shafts bored through the rock to the surface. Down its full length ran rows of massive white spar stalactite pillars, some 20 feet wide at the base, rising sheer from floor to ceiling, formed one single slow drop of water at a time over thousands of years. On one half-formed pillar we spotted a 3,000-year-old carving of a mummy and an Egyptian god, proof ancient miners from Phoenicia or North Africa had worked here, leaving their mark. The water dripping from the stalactite above hit the half-built column below once every two or three minutes, meaning the stone grew less than an inch every hundred years—nature building her monuments slower than any human empire could ever manage.

Gagool hurried us along before we could explore every tiny, perfect nook of that vast cavern, ignoring the stunning stalagmite formations that looked like lace-carved pulpits and strange frozen beasts, and the tiny dollhouse-sized side nooks that were perfect miniatures of the whole giant cave. We followed her until we reached a square, flat-topped doorway, carved exactly like the entrances to ancient Egyptian temples. “Are you ready to enter the Place of Death, white men?” Gagool cackled, clearly trying to rattle us. Good quoted Macbeth’s “Lead on, Macduff” to play tough, even though his hand was shaking, and Foulata clung so tight to his arm I thought she’d leave bruises. Sir Henry politely gestured for me to go first, and I inwardly cursed him for it, but stepped forward anyway.

The tap of Gagool’s stick echoed down the 20-foot dark passage, and we stepped out into a smaller, dimly lit chamber hollowed straight out of solid rock. My eyes adjusted fast, and I saw it: a long stone table running down the center, and at its head, a colossal 15-foot tall skeleton carved from solid white spar, one great bony hand raised over its head clutching a giant white spear like it was about to strike. Its grinning empty eye sockets stared straight at us, jaws half open as if it was about to speak. I nearly bolted for the exit right then, and even though I’m not a superstitious man, every hair on my head stood straight up. Sir Henry grabbed me by the collar to stop me running, then he saw the full sight too, and started mopping cold sweat off his forehead. Good swore weakly under his breath, Foulata shrieked and wrapped both arms around his neck. Only Gagool laughed, long and loud.

The rest of the table was ringed with 26 more seated figures. The brown shrivelled corpse of Twala, the king Sir Henry had beheaded in battle, sat propped on the middle of the table, his severed head perched on his knees. Water dripped slow and steady from the ceiling onto his neck, coating his whole body in a thin glassy film: the Kukuana royal dead weren’t buried, they were left here under that constant siliceous drip, slowly petrifying, preserved forever in clear spar. The rest of the seated figures were all former Kukuana kings, 27 total stretching back four and a half centuries, their faces still dimly visible through the ice-like stone wrap. That giant carved skeleton of Death, looming over the head of the table, was older than all of them, perfectly anatomically carved by the same ancient craftsmen who’d built the three colossi outside. It was the most awe-inspiring, terrifying sight we had ever laid eyes on. This was the Hall of the Dead.
