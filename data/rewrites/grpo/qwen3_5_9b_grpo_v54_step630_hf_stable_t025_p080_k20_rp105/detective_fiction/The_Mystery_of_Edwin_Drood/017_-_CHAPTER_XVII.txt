Half a year had passed since the tragedy at Cloisterham, and Mr. Crisparkle found himself seated in a waiting room within the London headquarters of the Haven of Philanthropy. He waited for an audience with Mr. Honeythunder, the society's leading figure.

As he waited, Crisparkle observed the atmosphere of the office with a critical eye. He recalled his own university days, spent among professors of the "Noble Art" of boxing. He noted with amusement that the philanthropists currently bustling about the room possessed a striking physical resemblance to those pugilists. Their heads, viewed from behind, displayed the same prominent development of the organs associated with aggression and the urge to "pitch into" others. Several of these men moved with an aggressive readiness, akin to boxers waiting for a sparring match. The office was preparing for a moral crusade on the rural circuit, and the philanthropists were enthusiastically backing various speakers, much like sports fans backing a heavyweight champion.

Crisparkle recognized the official manager of these displays, a man in a black suit who reminded him of a deceased benefactor known as "Frosty-faced Fogo." However, Crisparkle noted three significant differences between these philanthropists and the boxers he knew. First, the philanthropists were in terrible physical condition; they were too fleshy, resembling "Suet Pudding" rather than athletes. Second, unlike the boxers who maintained a certain good temper, these men used abusive language. Third, their code of conduct was dangerously flawed. While boxers followed rules, these philanthropists seemed empowered to bore their opponents to distraction, strike them when they were down, kick them, and maul them mercilessly behind their backs. In these specific acts of brutality, Crisparkle ironically concluded, the philanthropists were far less noble than the boxers.

Lost in these comparisons, Crisparkle watched the crowd. Everyone seemed to be arriving with the intent to snatch something from someone else, offering nothing in return. Before he could fully process the scene, his name was called. A shabby, underpaid clerk led him to Mr. Honeythunder's office.

Inside, Mr. Honeythunder commanded Crisparkle to sit down with a voice like a schoolmaster addressing a recalcitrant pupil. After signing a stack of circulars demanding money from the poor, Honeythunder turned his attention to Crisparkle.

"Now, Mr. Crisparkle," Honeythunder began, his arms squared and hands resting on his knees, his brow furrowed in a threatening manner. "We entertain different views, you and I, regarding the sanctity of human life."

"Do we?" Crisparkle asked calmly.

"We do, sir."

"May I ask what your views are on that subject?"

"That human life is sacred, sir."

"And what do you suppose are my views?"

"They are best known to yourself," Honeythunder retorted, frowning.

"Admitted," Crisparkle replied. "But you stated we held different views. Therefore, you must have attributed some views to me. What views have you set up as mine?"

Honeythunder gestured dramatically. "Here is a man—and a young man—swept off the face of the earth by a deed of violence. What do you call that?"

"Murder," Crisparkle answered.

"And the doer of that deed?"

"A murderer," Crisparkle confirmed.

"I am glad to hear you admit so much," Honeythunder said, lowering his voice menacingly. "I didn't expect it."

"Please explain why those expressions are unjustifiable," Crisparkle requested.

"I don't sit here to be browbeaten!" Honeythunder roared.

"As the only other person present, no one knows that better than I do," Crisparkle replied quietly. "But I must interrupt your explanation."

"Murder!" Honeythunder continued, adopting a boisterous, theatrical tone. "Bloodshed! Abel! Cain! I hold no terms with Cain. I repudiate the red hand when it is offered."

Instead of cheering, Crisparkle merely crossed his legs and said mildly, "Don't let me interrupt your explanation when you begin it."

"The Commandments say, no murder. NO murder, sir!" Honeythunder bellowed, as if Crisparkle had suggested that murder was permissible in moderation.

"And they also say, you shall bear no false witness," Crisparkle observed.

"E-enough!" Honeythunder shouted with severe solemnity. "My late wards are now of age, and I am released from a trust I cannot contemplate without horror. There are accounts you have undertaken to accept, and a statement of the balance you cannot receive too soon. Let me tell you, sir, I wish that, as a man and a Minor Canon, you were better employed." He nodded emphatically three times, driving home the point with theatrical emphasis.

Crisparkle rose, his face slightly flushed but his composure intact. "Mr. Honeythunder," he said, picking up the papers, "whether I am better or worse employed is a matter of taste. You might think I would be better employed enrolling myself as a member of your Society."

"Ay, indeed!" Honeythunder retorted, shaking his head threateningly. "It would have been better for you if you had done that long ago!"

"I think otherwise."

"Or," Honeythunder continued, "I might think one of your profession better employed in discovering and punishing guilt than in leaving that duty to a layman."

"I may regard my profession as having its first duty toward those in necessity and tribulation," Crisparkle said. "However, as I have satisfied myself that it is not my profession to make professions, I say no more of that. But I owe it to Mr. Neville, and to Mr. Neville's sister, to tell you that I know I was in full possession of Mr. Neville's mind and heart at the time of this occurrence. Without concealing what was to be deplored in him, I feel certain his tale is true. Feeling that certainty, I befriend him. As long as that certainty lasts, I will befriend him. If any consideration could shake me in this resolve, I should be so ashamed of my meanness that no man's or woman's good opinion could compensate me for the loss of my own."

Honeythunder stared at him. "Then who do you make out did the deed?"

"Heaven forbid," Crisparkle said, "that in my desire to clear one man I should lightly criminate another! I accuse no one."

"Tcha!" ejaculated Honeythunder with disgust. "And, sir, you are not a disinterested witness."

"How am I an interested one?" Crisparkle asked, smiling innocently.

"There was a certain stipend paid to you for your pupil, which may have warped your judgment," Honeythunder said coarsely.

"Perhaps I expect to retain it still?" Crisparkle replied, enlightened. "Do you mean that too?"

"Well, sir," Honeythunder stood up, thrusting his hands into his pockets. "I don't go about measuring people for caps. If people find I have any about me that fit 'em, they can put 'em on. That's their lookout: not mine."

Crisparkle looked at him with just indignation. "Mr. Honeythunder, I hoped when I came in here that I might be under no necessity of commenting on the introduction of platform manners into private life. But you have given me such a specimen of both that I should be a fit subject for them if I remained silent. They are detestable."

"They don't suit *you*, I dare say, sir."

"They are," Crisparkle repeated, ignoring the interruption, "detestable. They violate equally the justice that should belong to Christians and the restraints that should belong to gentlemen. You assume a great crime has been committed by one whom I, acquainted with the attendant circumstances, devoutly believe to be innocent. Because I differ from you on that vital point, what is your platform resource? Instantly to turn upon me, charging that I have no sense of the enormity of the crime itself, but am its aider and abettor! Another time, taking me as representing your opponent, you set up a platform credulity in some ridiculous delusion. I decline to believe it, and you fall back on proclaiming that I believe nothing; that because I will not bow down to a false God of your making, I deny the true God! Another time, you discover that War is a calamity and propose to abolish it with twisted resolutions tossed into the air like a kite's tail. I do not admit the discovery, and I have no faith in your remedy. Again, you represent me as revelling in the horrors of a battlefield like a fiend. Another time, you propose to punish the sober for the drunken, and then proclaim that I have a depraved desire to turn Heaven's creatures into swine! In all such cases, your movers and seconders run amuck like mad Malays, habitually attributing the lowest motives with recklessness and quoting figures as wilfully one-sided as a statement showing only debt or only credit. Therefore, Mr. Honeythunder, I consider the platform a sufficiently bad example in public life; but carried into private life, it becomes an unendurable nuisance."

"These are strong words, sir!" Honeythunder exclaimed.

"I hope so," Crisparkle said. "Good morning."

He walked out of the Haven at a brisk pace, soon falling into his regular stride. A smile appeared on his face as he wondered what the china shepherdess would have said if she had seen him "pounding" Mr. Honeythunder in their recent verbal skirmish. Crisparkle had just enough harmless vanity to hope he had hit hard, glowing with the belief that he had trimmed the Philanthropic Jacket handsomely.

He took himself to Staple Inn, but not to P. J. T. and Mr. Grewgious. He climbed many creaking stairs before reaching some attic rooms in a corner, the unbolted door leading to the chambers of Neville Landless.

An air of retreat and solitude hung about the rooms and their inhabitant. Neville was much worn, as were the rooms themselves. The sloping ceilings, rusty locks, grates, and heavy wooden beams slowly mouldering gave the place a prison-like look, and Neville had the haggard face of a prisoner. Yet sunlight shone in at the ugly garret-window, which had a penthouse thrust out among the tiles. On the cracked, smoke-blackened parapet beyond, some deluded sparrows hopped rheumatically, like little feathered cripples who had left their crutches in their nests. There was a play of living leaves nearby that changed the air, creating an imperfect sort of music that would have been melody in the country.

The rooms were sparely furnished but contained a good store of books. Everything expressed the abode of a poor student. Crisparkle's friendly beam of eyes upon them as he entered suggested he had been the chooser, lender, or donor of the books, or perhaps all three.

"How goes it, Neville?" Crisparkle asked.

"I am in good heart, Mr. Crisparkle, and working away."

"I wish your eyes were not quite so large and not quite so bright," the Minor Canon said slowly, releasing the hand he had taken in his.

"They brighten at the sight of you," Neville returned. "If you were to fall away from me, they would soon be dull enough."

"Rally, rally!" urged Crisparkle in a stimulating tone. "Fight for it, Neville!"

"If I were dying, I feel as if a word from you would rally me; if my pulse had stopped, I feel as if your touch would make it beat again," Neville said. "But I *have* rallied, and am doing famously."

Crisparkle turned him slightly more toward the light. "I want to see a ruddier touch here, Neville," he said, indicating his own healthy cheek as a pattern. "I want more sun to shine upon you."

Neville drooped suddenly, replying in a lowered voice: "I am not hardy enough for that, yet. I may become so, but I cannot bear it yet. If you had gone through those Cloisterham streets as I did; if you had seen, as I did, those averted eyes, and the better sort of people silently giving me too much room to pass, that I might not touch them or come near them, you wouldn't think it quite unreasonable that I cannot go about in the daylight."

"My poor fellow!" Crisparkle said in a purely sympathetic tone. "I never said it was unreasonable; never thought so. But I should like you to do it."

"And that would give me the strongest motive to do it. But I cannot yet. I cannot persuade myself that the eyes of even the stream of strangers I pass in this vast city look at me without suspicion. I feel marked and tainted, even when I go out—as I do only—at night. But the darkness covers me then, and I take courage from it."

Crisparkle laid a hand upon his shoulder and stood looking down at him. "If I could have changed my name," Neville said, "I would have done so. But as you wisely pointed out, I can't do that, for it would look like guilt. If I could have gone to some distant place, I might have found relief, but the thing is not to be thought of for the same reason. Hiding and escaping would be the construction in either case. It seems a little hard to be so tied to a stake, and innocent; but I don't complain."

"And you must expect no miracle to help you, Neville," Crisparkle said compassionately.

"No, sir, I know that. The ordinary fulness of time and circumstances is all I have to trust to."

"It will right you at last, Neville."

"So I believe, and I hope I may live to know it."
