The winter days slipped past Romola like pale ships on a silent sea—each one bearing unseen change, though all seemed the same. Tito’s warnings had cast such a shadow over her thoughts that she now shrank from news, fearing what each new ripple in Florence’s troubled waters might bring. The German Emperor had withdrawn, easing the city’s immediate peril, yet hardship lingered, and Romola threw herself into her charitable work, seeking refuge from her gnawing unease.

She could not rejoice at the growing power of Savonarola’s followers, though their triumph had seemed Florence’s salvation. Francesco Valori, that fiery leader of the Piagnoni, now sat as Gonfaloniere, wielding his brief authority with fervor. Yet Romola sensed the fragile balance tipping. By Carnival time, the Mediceans stirred openly, rallying behind Bernardo del Nero. The air thickened with tension.

On the final morning of Carnival, Romola walked toward the Corso degli Albizzi to fetch her cousin Brigida. They would watch the day’s spectacle from a window in the Piazza della Signoria—a sight no Florentine could resist. The Piagnoni had reshaped the revelry in their own image. No masks, no lewd jests; this was a Carnival purified, reborn in piety.

Yet Romola moved through it all with weary detachment. Duty alone drew her forward.

The Piazza was alive with movement. At its heart loomed a monstrous pyramid—sixty feet high, its tiers widening like the branches of a grotesque tree. Young figures in white robes darted about its base, baskets in hand, while clusters of monks and artists observed. Some, like Piero di Cosimo, watched from the Loggia with scorn.

As Romola drew closer, the pyramid’s contents revealed themselves: tapestries of wanton scenes, gaming tables, decks of cards, dice, music books, lutes and drums, masks from Carnivals past, volumes of Ovid and Boccaccio, pots of rouge, false hair, mirrors—all the vanities condemned by Savonarola. At the summit perched the effigy of Carnival itself, a leering old fool soon to meet his end in flames.

This was the Bonfire of Vanities.

The boys of Florence, marshaled under Fra Domenico’s zeal, had scoured the city for these sinful treasures. No more vulgar songs or street mischief—now they sang hymns, wore white, and bore olive branches. They had become Savonarola’s army, their purity a reproach to their elders.

Romola studied the scene, her thoughts drifting.

She turned. Piero di Cosimo stood beside her, his voice sharp with disgust.

*"Your Piagnoni would make hell a welcome sight if they rule much longer. Look at them—burning art, burning wit, as if life were a funeral."*

She smiled faintly. *"Even you must approve some of it, Piero. Those paints, those wigs—you’ve raged against them yourself."*

*"What of it?"* he snapped. *"A woman like you, made for light and beauty, should know better than to side with these fanatics. The Virgin herself wears fine robes—since when is beauty a sin?"*

*"I’ve read Boccaccio,"* Romola admitted. *"Some tales many times, as a girl."*

*"No,"* she said softly. *"But neither is life a jest. Shouldn’t we strive for something nobler?"*

Piero scowled and turned away.

Romola walked on, her heart heavy. She understood his anger—her father would have felt the same. Yet sorrow had carved her soul into a shape that could no longer hold frivolity. Where Piero saw joy crushed, she saw only the struggle toward higher things.

The fire would come at dusk. The vanities would burn. And Florence, caught between salvation and tyranny, marched blindly toward an unseen fate.
