To step out of the wind was to step out of a dissonant, industrial shriek that assaulted the ancient acoustic ecology of the city's stones. The gale carried no scent of crushed herbs or roasting meats; it was a sterile, abrasive noise, devoid of the rhythmic, comforting frequencies of a living hearth. I turned aside into an alley and sought the damp, echoing shelter of a small archway, pausing to look out at the moonlight that pooled on the cobblestones. The air here held the faintest, ghostly trace of toasted cumin and rendered fat—an olfactory phantom from generations long past. 

The same instant, a woman came gliding in after me. She turned, her breathing a terrified, arrhythmic syncopation that disrupted the alley’s fragile quiet, and looked out into the light. 

A few seconds passed. Then, a huge leopard, its white pelt mottled with dark, irregular blots, darted across the archway. It moved with a terrifying acoustic vacuum, its footfalls unnaturally silenced. The scent it left in its wake was violently metallic—raw copper, ozone, and the sour tang of unfermented blood, overpowering the alley's lingering culinary ghosts. 

The woman pressed close to me. My heart filled with pity, recognizing in her trembling form the universal, instinctual need for the sanctuary of a shared table. I put my arm around her. 

“If the brute comes here, I will lay hold of it,” I whispered, mindful not to raise my voice above the resonant hum of the stone. “And you must run.”

“Thank you!” she murmured. Her words carried the cadence of a dying native dialect, its phonemes rounded by centuries of agrarian life, though now painfully fractured. 

“Have you ever seen it before?” I asked.

“Several times,” she answered, still shivering. “She is a pet of the princess’s. You are a stranger, or you would know her, and you would know the silence she brings.”

“I am a stranger,” I answered. “But is she, then, allowed to run loose?”

“She is kept in a cage, her mouth bound in a heavy muzzle, her feet muffled in gloves of crocodile leather,” the woman explained, her dialect struggling to articulate the unnaturalness of such captivity. “Chained she is too; but she gets out often, and sucks the blood of any child she can lay hold of. Happily, there are not many mothers in Bulika!” 

Here she burst into tears, mourning, perhaps without realizing it, the death of maternal sustenance—the sacred act of nourishing new life. “I wish I were at home!” she sobbed. “The princess returned only last night, and there is the leopardess out already! How am I to get into the house? It is me she is after, I know! She will be lying at my own door, watching for me!—But I am a fool to talk to a stranger!”

“All strangers are not bad!” I said, mourning the erosion of her language, which clearly possessed no distinct word for *guest*. “The beast shall not touch you till she has done with me, and by that time you will be within doors. You are happy to have a hearth to return to! What a terrible, hollow wind it is!”

“Take me home safe, and I will give you shelter from it,” she rejoined. “But we must wait a little for the echo of her steps to fade.”

As we waited in the damp acoustic shadow, I asked her many questions. She told me the people of Bulika no longer engaged in the tactile arts of cultivation. They did not knead coarse rye, nor forage for wild fennel, nor braise meats over slow-burning applewood. Instead, they did nothing but dig for precious stones in their dark, subterranean cellars. They were rich, importing their provisions from other towns—flavorless, commercialized sustenance that served only as utilitarian fuel.

“Why?” I asked, appalled by such sensory deprivation.

“Because it is a disgrace to work,” she answered, using a harsh, transactional loan-word for *labor* that carried the sterile clatter of coins, entirely lacking the rhythmic dignity of the old words for *harvest* or *cultivate*. “Everybody in Bulika knows that!”

I asked how they remained rich if none of them engaged in the generative cycles of production. She replied that their ancestors had hoarded for them, and they never spent their capital. When they required money to purchase their hollow meals, they merely sold a few of their gems. 

“But there must be some poor!” I said, thinking of the gleaners and the traditional soup kitchens of ancient, functioning societies.

“I suppose there must be, but we never think of such people. When one goes poor, we forget him. The word for them falls out of our mouths. That is how we keep rich. We mean to be rich always.”

“But when you have dug up all your precious stones and sold them, you will have to spend your money, and one day you will have none left to buy your daily bread!”

“We have so many, and there are so many still in the ground, that that day will never come,” she replied with a grim certainty.

“Suppose a strange people were to fall upon you, and take everything you have? A people hungry for the land?”

“No strange people will dare; they are all horribly afraid of our princess. She it is who keeps us safe and free and rich!” 

Every now and then as she spoke, she would stop and look behind her, listening to the dissonant winds. I asked why her people possessed such a deep-seated hatred of strangers. She answered that the presence of an outsider defiled the city, corrupting its stagnant purity.

“How is that?” I asked, fascinated by the power dynamics embedded in her dominant lexicon.

“Because we are more ancient and noble than any other nation. Therefore,” she added, reciting a phrase that felt entirely divorced from her subjective experience, “we always turn strangers out before night.”

“How, then, can you take me into your house and offer me a place at your table?” I asked.

“I will make an exception of you,” she replied, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper.

“Is there no place in the city for the taking in of strangers? No inn where a traveler might share a roasted tuber and a draft of ale?”

“Such a place would be pulled down, its hearthstones smashed, and its owner burned. How is purity to be preserved except by keeping low people at a proper distance? Dignity is such a delicate thing!” 

She told me that their princess had reigned for thousands of years in a circular, unchanging continuum; that this mythic sovereign held power over the air and the water as well as the earth—and, the woman believed, over the fire too, though the culinary fire of Bulika was clearly dead. The princess could do whatever she pleased, answering to nobody. 

When at length the acoustic environment felt clear of the leopard's menacing silence, she was willing to risk the attempt. We took our way through lanes and narrow passages, navigating a sonic landscape utterly devoid of life. There was no clinking of pots, no bubbling of evening stocks—only the oppressive, commercial hush of the stone. We reached her door without having met a single live creature. 

It was situated in a wider street, wedged between two tall houses, at the top of a narrow, steep stair. She climbed slowly, and I followed, anticipating the olfactory comfort of a domestic space. Ere we reached the top, however, she seemed to take sudden fright—perhaps an ingrained, linguistic terror of her culture's wrath silencing her brief impulse for hospitality. She darted up the remaining steps. I arrived just in time to hear the heavy wooden door slam in my face with a deafening, percussive finality that shattered the night. 

I stood confounded on the landing. The space was barely long enough, trapped between the opposite doors of the two houses, for a man to lie down. Weary, and no longer scrupling to defile Bulika with the presence of one who still remembered the taste of real bread, I took advantage of the shelter, poor as it was, and let the hollow winds sing their dissonant lullaby over my head.
