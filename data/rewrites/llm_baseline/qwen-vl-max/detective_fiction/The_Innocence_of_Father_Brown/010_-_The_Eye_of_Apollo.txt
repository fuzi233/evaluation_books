The Eye of Apollo

The Thames, ever shifting in mood and hue, shimmered under the midday sun as it climbed above Westminster. Two men crossed Westminster Bridge: one tall, one short—so different in stature they might have been likened to the proud clock tower of Parliament and the humble, hunched roofline of Westminster Abbey. The shorter man wore clerical robes; he was Father Brown, a priest from St. Francis Xavier’s Church in Camberwell, returning from a visit to a dying parishioner. The taller was M. Hercule Flambeau, private detective, on his way to his new office in a modern apartment building facing the Abbey.

The building was a stark, American-style skyscraper—imposing, sleek, and still incomplete. Its elevators and telephones were polished and efficient, but few tenants had moved in. Only three offices were occupied: one just above Flambeau’s, one just below, and the rest remained empty. Yet what caught the eye more than any architectural detail was a massive golden sculpture affixed to the outside of the floor directly above Flambeau’s office—a giant, radiant human eye, glowing with rays of gold, taking up the space of two or three windows.

“What on earth is that?” asked Father Brown, halting in surprise.

“Oh, a new religion,” Flambeau replied, chuckling. “One of those movements that claim you’re not really sick because you never were ill to begin with. Sounds like Christian Science, perhaps. A fellow calling himself Kalon—though I doubt that’s his real name—has taken the flat above me. He calls himself the New Priest of Apollo and worships the sun.”

“Let him be careful,” said Father Brown. “The sun was the cruelest of all gods. But what does that monstrous eye mean?”

“It’s their philosophy,” Flambeau explained. “They believe a person can endure anything if their mind is perfectly steady. Their symbols are the sun and the open eye—the idea being that a truly healthy person could look straight into the sun without blinking.”

“If a man were truly healthy,” Father Brown mused, “he wouldn’t feel the need to stare at it.”

“Well, that’s about all I know,” Flambeau said, shrugging. “They claim their faith can cure every physical illness.”

“And what about the one spiritual disease?” Father Brown asked, his voice thoughtful.

“What’s that?” Flambeau smiled.

“Oh,” said the priest, “the belief that one is completely well.”

Flambeau was far more intrigued by the office beneath him than the flamboyant temple above. He was a clear-minded Southerner, skeptical of religious fads—especially ones wrapped in bright, hollow ideals. But people fascinated him, especially when they were striking. And the women downstairs were certainly that.

The office belonged to two sisters—Pauline and Joan Stacey. Both were slight and dark-haired, but Pauline stood out: tall, sharp-featured, with an aquiline profile and eyes like steel—bright, intense, unyielding. She seemed carved from some hard, clean metal, cutting through life with precision. Her younger sister, Joan, was quieter, paler, almost shadow-like—like a faint echo of her elder sibling. They both dressed in practical black, with stiff collars and cuffs, typical of the many brisk, business-minded women of London. But their outward appearance masked something deeper.

Pauline Stacey was, in truth, an heiress—possessor of ancient lands, a family crest, and immense wealth. She’d grown up in grand country houses, but a fierce modern spirit had driven her to reject aristocratic idleness. She didn’t renounce her fortune—she kept it, insisting it should be used for practical social good. Part went into her typewriting business, which she envisioned as a model for women’s work. The rest funded various causes promoting female advancement. Whether Joan shared her sister’s ideals was uncertain—but she followed Pauline with quiet devotion, a loyalty tinged with sorrow, more touching than her sister’s rigid confidence. For Pauline denied tragedy altogether; she saw no room for weakness in the world.

Her manner was sharp, impatient, and exacting. Once, Flambeau entered her office to find her having thrown her sister’s spectacles onto the floor and crushed them underfoot. She raged against “sickly medical notions” and the very idea of needing artificial aids. “Do we wear wooden legs or false hair?” she snapped, her eyes blazing. “No! We are born whole! People only think they need help because they’ve been taught fear, not strength. Just as nurses tell children not to look at the sun, so they grow afraid to see it. But why should there be one star among the stars I cannot gaze upon? The sun is not my master. I will look at it whenever I please!”

Flambeau, bewildered, asked bluntly: “But isn’t a lift also a machine? Why is using a lift noble, but wearing glasses weak?”

“That’s entirely different!” Pauline declared, scornful. “Engines and motors show human power—our mastery over distance and time. That’s science, true science. But these doctor-made props? They’re signs of cowardice. Doctors attach limbs as if we were born broken. But I was born free! We only need such things because we’ve been trained to fear, not to conquer. And just as children blink at the sun, so we blink at our own strength.”

Flambeau bowed slightly. “Then your eyes,” he said, “will blind the sun.” He found her intensity amusing, even admirable—and her defiance oddly thrilling. But as he walked up to his own floor, he sighed and whistled softly. “So she’s fallen into the hands of that conjurer upstairs with his golden eye.” Though he cared little for Kalon’s cult, he knew of the man’s obsession with staring at the sun.

Soon, Flambeau realized the connection between the floors above and below was growing stronger. Kalon, the man who claimed to be the new priest of Apollo, was a magnificent figure—tall, broad-shouldered, with a golden beard and piercing blue eyes. His presence was commanding, even regal. He looked like a Norse king, yet there was a depth of intellect and spirituality behind his strength. Despite his surroundings—a plain office in a modern building, a clerk in a cheap suit, a brass nameplate, and the gilded eye displayed like a signboard—he radiated an undeniable force. When he stepped into the room, one felt the presence of greatness—even if it was a charlatan’s greatness.

He wore simple linen clothes during the day, but when he performed his daily rituals, he donned white robes and a golden circlet. Three times a day, he stepped onto his balcony to greet the sun: at dawn, at noon, and at dusk. It was at noon, when the bells of Parliament rang and the city hummed, that Father Brown first saw him.

Flambeau, already tired of the spectacle, hurried into the building without waiting for his friend. But Father Brown stopped, drawn by ritual—or perhaps by absurdity. He stared upward as Kalon stood on the balcony, arms raised, reciting his solar litany. His voice carried across the street, deep and resonant. His eyes were fixed on the sun, unblinking, unwavering. It was impossible to say whether he saw anything else—certainly not the small, round-faced priest below, watching with squinting eyes.

“O Sun,” Kalon called, “O star too great to dwell among the stars! O fountain flowing quietly in the void called space. White Father of all pure things—white flames, white flowers, white peaks. Father, purer than your purest child, primal innocence, into whose peace—”

A sudden crash shattered the silence—like a rocket exploding backward—followed by shrieking voices. Five figures burst into the gate, three rushed out. For a moment, the air crackled with shock. No one knew what had happened, but it felt like disaster.

Two figures remained motionless: the fair priest of Apollo on his balcony, and the unassuming priest of Christ below.

Flambeau emerged from the building, towering over the crowd, shouting orders. “Someone get a surgeon!” Then, turning back, he led Father Brown into the dark entrance. Even as they passed through the throng, the prophet’s voice still echoed from above, praising the sun.

Inside, they found the elevator shaft—empty. But something had fallen down it. Something that shouldn’t have.

For four minutes, Flambeau had watched. Now he saw the body of Pauline Stacey—brained, bleeding, dead. There was no doubt in his mind. He hadn’t liked her exactly, nor disliked her—not fully. But she had been real. And now, suddenly, she was gone. The details—the way she’d spoken, the way she’d walked—now stung with unbearable poignancy. A beautiful, defiant woman, dashed to death in a lift shaft. Suicide? Impossible. Murder? But who could have done it? In this half-empty building?

He turned sharply. “Where’s Kalon?”

A calm voice answered: “He’s been on his balcony for fifteen minutes. Worshipping the sun.”

Flambeau froze. Then, turning to Father Brown, he asked, “If he’s been up there all along… who could have done it?”

Father Brown nodded slowly. “Perhaps we should go upstairs and check. We’ve half an hour before the police arrive.”

Leaving the body to the surgeons, Flambeau raced up to the Staceys’ office—empty. Then to his own—also empty. He returned, pale.

“Her sister,” he said grimly, “seems to have gone out for a walk.”

Father Brown smiled faintly. “Or perhaps she went up to Kalon’s office. Let’s check. Then we’ll talk in your office—no, wait,” he added, as if recalling something. “In theirs. Downstairs.”

Flambeau blinked but followed. They descended to the Staceys’ flat, where Father Brown took a large red leather chair near the entrance, facing the stairs. He waited.

Four minutes passed. Then three figures appeared—solemn, silent.

First came Joan Stacey, her face drawn, her hair streaked with grey. She walked straight to her desk, arranging papers with brisk efficiency. The act alone restored a sense of order. If she were guilty, she was coldly composed.

Father Brown watched her for a moment, then turned to Kalon.

“Prophet,” he said, “I’d like to hear about your religion.”

Kalon inclined his head. “I’d be honored. But I’m not sure I understand.”

“It’s like this,” Father Brown said, candidly. “We believe that if a man has flawed principles, it’s partly his fault. But we also distinguish between someone who knowingly disrespects conscience and someone whose conscience is clouded by lies. Now—do you believe murder is wrong at all?”

“Is this an accusation?” Kalon asked, calm.

“No,” Father Brown replied gently. “It’s the defense.”

Silence fell. Then Kalon rose—slowly, deliberately—like the sun itself rising. His presence filled the room. His robes seemed to drape the walls in classical splendor; his gesture expanded the space. The small, dark figure of the priest seemed almost an intrusion.

“We meet at last, Caiaphas,” Kalon said. “Your church and mine are the only truths on earth. I worship the sun—you worship its dimming. You serve the dying God; I serve the living. Your work is suspicion, slander—your church is a black police force, hunting confessions through treachery or torture. You convict men of guilt; I convict them of innocence. You preach sin; I preach virtue.”

He paused, then continued, voice firm and commanding. “Reader of evil, let me finish. Not even your judgment matters to me. The things you call disgrace—hanging, prison—are no more than a monster in a child’s book to a man grown. You offered the defense—I’ll offer the prosecution. One thing can be said against me—and I’ll say it myself. Pauline Stacey was my love, my bride—not in your narrow sense, but by a law purer than yours. We walked a world beyond yours, treading crystal palaces while you crawled through brick tunnels. Yes, I admit: love often breeds hatred. So there’s your first charge. But the second is stronger—and I give it freely. Not only did Pauline love me, but this morning, before she died, she wrote a will leaving me and my church half a million pounds. Where are the handcuffs? Do you think I care what you do to me? Prison? It’s just waiting for her at a station. The gallows? It’s rushing to her in a car.”

His words struck with the force of thunder. Flambeau and Joan stared, awestruck. Father Brown sat with his eyes downcast, a deep crease across his brow—as if wounded.

Kalon leaned against the mantel. “That is the full case against me. Now, let me destroy it. I did not commit this crime. Pauline Stacey fell from this floor at 12:05. A hundred witnesses will testify I was on my balcony from just before noon until 12:15—my usual prayer time. My clerk, a respectable young man from Clapham, will swear he sat in my outer office all morning, no one passing through. I arrived ten minutes early—fifteen before the accident. I did not leave. My alibi is perfect. You can subpoena half Westminster. Put away the handcuffs. The case is closed.”

Then, with a final, sweeping gesture, he added: “And to clear the air forever—let me tell you how I believe she died. You may blame me, or my beliefs—but not me personally. It is known that certain adepts throughout history have mastered levitation—floating above the ground by willpower alone. This is part of our occult wisdom. Pauline was impulsive, ambitious. She believed herself deeply initiated. She often told me, as we rode the lift, that if one’s will were strong enough, one could float down like a feather. I believe she tried it—in an ecstasy of noble thought. Her will failed. Gravity took its revenge. That is the truth—sad, perhaps presumptuous, but not criminal. Call it suicide. I will always call it heroic failure—advancing science, climbing toward heaven.”

Flambeau had never seen Father Brown so defeated. He sat, eyes lowered, brow furrowed, as if ashamed. The prophet’s words had stirred something profound—a sense that here stood a man of bold freedom, confronting a world of petty suspicion.

At last, Father Brown spoke, blinking as if in pain. “Well, if that’s so, sir, then all you need do is take that testamentary paper and go. I wonder—where did the poor lady leave it?”
