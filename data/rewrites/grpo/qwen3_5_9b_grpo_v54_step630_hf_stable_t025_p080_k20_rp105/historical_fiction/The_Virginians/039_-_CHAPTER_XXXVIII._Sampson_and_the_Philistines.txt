My early fortune threw me in the path of a gentleman of standing, born upon an island often hailed by impartial critics as the ocean's premier jewel. The tales this man recounted of his kin—stories that once made my youthful blood curdle with sheer dread—depicted a lineage where every commandment was shattered, every moral boundary dissolved. He spoke of crimes so brazen, so devoid of conscience, that even the notorious Thurtell or Turpin would seem tame by comparison. Yet, amidst this catalogue of parricide, forgery, and theft, the family somehow managed to survive, avoiding the hulks entirely. My brother Tim had brought his father's grey hairs to the grave with sorrow; my brother Mick had repeatedly robbed the parish church; and my sister Annamaroia had jilted a Captain, eloped with an Ensign, forged her grandmother's will, and stolen the silver spoons—a felony for which Larry the knife-boy ultimately paid with his life. The family of Atreus was but a whisper compared to this race of O'What-d'ye-call-'em, yet I shall never name the country of their origin.

What struck me with naive astonishment was the sheer contradiction of their existence. These murderers, habitual forgers, and blood-stained rogues corresponded with one another as "my dearest brother" and "my dearest sister," maintaining months-long periods of amicable coexistence. Tim, whose hands reeked of patricide, would mix a screeching tumbler for Maria; Mick, whose lips were black with perjury regarding his grandmother's will and the murder of Thady's orphans, would kiss Julia's cheek. They would share jolly nights, weeping and laughing over memories of the old Castle, the quarterming of the Onetyoneth, and the Major's proposal to Cyaroloine. Their embraces were so profuse, their reconciliations so touching, that one momentarily forgot the stories of how they could hang each other all round if they chose to speak the truth.

What is finer than forgiveness? What more rational than to apologize after hurling every bad name under the sun, to withdraw the decanter flung at an enemy's head, and resume friendship? Some possess this angelic gift. It was beautiful to witness our two ladies at Tunbridge Wells forgiving one another, smiling and fondling despite yesterday's harsh words, seemingly forgetting the past even as they remembered it perfectly. I wonder if you and I can achieve such heights? We must strive for this pacable, Christian spirit. Perhaps you can learn to forgive bad language; it requires practice, but eventually, bad words lose their sting.

Thus, Aunt Bernstein and her niece played cards amicably, toasted each other's health, shared wings of chicken, and pulled bones from the merry-thought pie, scratching their neighbors' eyes out in conversation rather than each other's. History tells us how Peninsular warriors fraternized during truces, exchanging tobacco and wine before resuming battle; similarly, our old soldiers laid down their weapons to hob-nob gaily. Of course, while drinking with Jack Frenchman, one keeps a pistol handy, ready to blow his brains out should he make a hostile move. But for the moment, it is *À votre santé*, mon camarade! Here's to you, mounseer! Everything is pleasant. Regarding Aunt Bernstein's threatened gout? The twinges had vanished. Maria's fainting fits? None returned, save for a slight recurrence the night before. The Baroness was sorry; her niece must see the best doctors, take steel, and continue the regimen even after leaving Tunbridge. How kind of Aunt Bernstein to offer bottled waters! Yet, suppose Madame Bernstein whispers to her maid, "Fainting fits? Pooh! Epilepsy! Inherited from that scrofulous German mother!" What means do we have to know such private confidences? Or suppose Lady Maria orders her maid, Mrs. Betty, to taste every medicinal glass, declaring her aunt capable of poisoning her? Likely, such conversations occur. These are merely precautions—the firelocks our old soldiers keep loaded and cocked at their sides, lying quiet on the grass for now.

Possessing Harry's bond in her pocket, the veteran Maria saw no need to press for immediate payment. She knew the world too well. He was bound to her, but she granted him ample day-rule and parole. It was not her aim to chafe her young slave unnecessarily. She understood the disparity of ages and that Harry required pleasures and diversions. "Take your ease and amusement, cousin," Lady Maria said. "Frisk about, pretty little mousekin," purring grey Grimalkin, watching with green eyes. The ladies, knowing perfectly what constituted a young gentleman's amusements in those days, spoke of them with the frankness characteristic of the era.

Our wily Calypso consoled herself during Harry's absence with whatever diversion presented itself. Mr. Jack Morris, the gentleman associated with Lord March and Mr. Warrington, proved one such diversion. Living with titled personages was Jack Morris's delight, and losing money at cards to an earl's daughter was almost a pleasure. Lady Maria Esmond, an earl's daughter eager to win, obtained permission to take Morris to the Countess of Yarmouth's assembly. They played cards, and everyone was pleased.

Thus, the first eighty-eight hours after Mr. Warrington's departure passed cheerily at Tunbridge Wells until Friday arrived, bringing the sermon Mr. Sampson had been preparing. The company at the Wells was ready to listen. Sampson possessed a reputation for being both amusing and eloquent; if there were no breakfast, conjurers, dancing bears, or concerts, the good folk of the Wells would endure a sermon. He knew Lady Yarmouth was coming, aware of her immense power in granting livings and dispensing bishoprics, and confident that the Defender of the Faith held her opinion in high regard. Consequently, Mr. Sampson prepared his finest discourse for her hearing. When the Great Man is at home at the Castle, walking to the small country church in the park accompanied by the Duke, the Marquis, and Cabinet Ministers, have you ever sat among the congregation to watch Mr. Trotter the curate? He looks anxiously at the Great Pew, faltering over his text, thinking, "Ah! Perhaps his lordship will grant me a living!" Mrs. Trotter and the girls watch the Great Pew with equal anxiety, observing the effect of papa's discourse upon the assembled big-wigs. Once his nervousness passes, his noble voice clears, warms, and kindles. He takes out his pocket-handkerchief, approaching that exquisite passage that always moves them to tears at the parsonage. He has begun it! Then, what is that humming noise filling the edifice, causing hob-nailed Melibaeus to grin at smock-frocked Tityrus? It is the Right Honourable Lord Naseby snoring in the pew by the fire! Poor Trotter's visionary mitre vanishes with the sound.

Sampson was the domestic chaplain of Madame Bernstein's nephew, and the two ladies of the Esmond family patronized him. On the day of the sermon, the Baroness hosted a breakfast in his honor. Sampson appeared rosy and handsome, sporting a fresh-flowered wig and a smart, rustling new cassock bought on credit from a church-admiring mercer at the Wells. Flanked by his patronesses and their lacqueys carrying great gilt prayer-books, Mr. Sampson marched from breakfast to church. Everyone remarked on the Baroness's fine appearance; she laughed, was particularly friendly with her niece, and offered stately smiles to all as she moved along with her tortoiseshell cane. At the door, a dazzling conflux of rank and fashion gathered—all the fine company of the Wells trooping in, including Lady Yarmouth, conspicuous with vermilion cheeks and a robe of flame-coloured taffeta. Shabby people were present as well, though far fewer. What an odd-looking pair were those in ragged coats, one with carroty hair peeking from a scratch-wig, entering just as the organ stopped! He could not have been a Protestant, for he mechanically crossed himself, saying to his comrade, "Bedad, Tim, I forgawt!" I conclude this individual hailed from the island mentioned at the chapter's start. Wherever they go, a rich fragrance of whisky spreads. A man may be a heretic but possess genius; these Catholic gentlemen had come to pay homage to Mr. Sampson.

Not only were members of the old religion present, but disciples of a creed still older. Who were those two individuals with hooked noses and sallow countenances who worked into the church despite the beadle's opposition? Seeing their greasy appearance, Mr. Beadle attempted to deny them entry. But one whispered, "We wants to be conwerted, gov'nor!" while another slipped money into his hand. Mr. Beadle lifted the mace barring the doorway, and the Hebrew gentlemen entered. The organ began; the doors closed. Should we go in to hear Mr. Sampson's sermon, or lie on the grass without?

Preceded by the beadle in gold lace, Sampson walked to the pulpit, appearing as rosy and jolly a man as one could wish. Then, when he surged up from his plump cushion, why did his Reverence turn as pale as death? He looked to the western church-door; there, on each side, stood those horrible Hebrew caryatides. He glanced toward the vestry-door near the rector's pew, where he had sat alongside his patronesses. Suddenly, two perfumed Hibernian gentlemen slipped from an adjacent seat, placing themselves on a bench close to the vestry-door and rector's pew, sitting there with eyes meekly cast down until the sermon's conclusion. How can we describe that sermon if the preacher himself never knew how it ended?

Nevertheless, it was considered an excellent sermon. Afterward, the fine ladies buzzed in their pews, offering praise. Madame Walmoden, seated next to our friends, declared it bewdiful, making her dremble all over. Madame Bernstein called it excellent. Lady Maria was pleased that the family chaplain distinguished himself, looking up to catch his eye as he remained in the pulpit. She greeted him with a wave and a flutter of her handkerchief. He scarcely seemed to note the compliment; his face was pale, his eyes fixed yonder toward the font where the Hebrews still remained. The stream of people rushed past them—in a throng, in a march of twos and threes, in a dribble of one at a time—until everyone was gone. The two Hebrews remained by the door.

The Baroness de Bernstein and her niece lingered in the rector's pew, where the old lady was deep in conversation with a gentleman.

"Who are those horrible men at the door? And what a smell of spirits there is!" cried Lady Maria to Mrs. Brett, her aunt's woman.

"Farewell, doctor; you have a darling little boy: is he to be a clergyman, too?" asked Madame de Bernstein. "Are you ready, my dear?" The pew was thrown open, and the Baroness, whose father was only a viscount, insisted that her niece, Lady Maria, an earl's daughter, exit first.

As she stepped forward, the individuals her ladyship designated as two horrible men advanced. One pulled a long strip of paper from his pocket, and her ladyship started, turning pale. She made for the vestry, hoping to clear the door and shut it behind her. However, the two whiskified gentlemen were upon her; one actually laid his hand on her shoulder and said:

"At the suit of Mistress Pincott, of Kensington, mercer, I have the honour of arresting your leedyship. Me neem is Costigan, madam, a poor gentleman of Oireland, binding to circumstances and forced to follow a disagreeable profession. Will your leedyship walk, or shall me man go fetch a cheer?"

In reply, Lady Maria Esmond gave three shrieks and fell swooning to the ground. "Keep the door, Mick!" shouted Mr. Costigan. "Best let in no one else, madam," he said politely to Madame de Bernstein. "Her ladyship has fallen in a feenting fit and will recover here, at her aise."

"Unlace her, Brett!" cried the old lady, whose eyes twinkled oddly. As soon as the operation was performed, Madame Bernstein seized a little bag suspended by a hair chain around Lady Maria's neck and snipped the necklace in twain. "Dash some cold water over her face; it always recovers her!" said the Baroness. "You stay with her, Brett. How much is your suit, gentlemen?"

Mr. Costigan replied, "The deem we have against her leedyship for one hundred and thirty-two pounds, in which she is indebted to Misthress Eliza Pincott."

Meanwhile, where was the Reverend Mr. Sampson? Like the fabled opossum who, spotting the unerring gunner from his gum-tree, declared, "It's no use Major, I will come down," so Sampson surrendered to his pursuers. "At whose suit, Simons?" he asked sadly. Sampson knew Simons; they had met many times before.

"Buckleby Cordwainer," said Mr. Simons.

"Forty-eight pound and charges, I know," said Mr. Sampson with a sigh. "I haven't got the money. What officer is there here?" Mr. Simons's companion, Mr. Lyons, stepped forward, stating his house was most convenient and often used by gentlemen, and that he would be most happy and proud to accommodate his Reverence.

Two chairs happened to be waiting outside the chapel. In those two chairs, my Lady Maria Esmond and Mr. Sampson placed themselves, going to Mr. Lyons's residence, escorted by the gentlemen we had just met.

Very soon after the capture, the Baroness Bernstein sent Mr. Case, her confidential servant, with a note to her niece, full of ardent affection but regretting that her heavy losses at cards rendered the payment of such a sum impossible. She had written immediately to Mrs. Pincott to entreat for time, promising to inform her dear unhappy niece as soon as she received an answer.
