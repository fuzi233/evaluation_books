Costigan left Pen to his dreams, and no urgent dispatch from Mr. Huxter shattered the peace of the morning. When Arthur Pendennis finally opened his eyes, he found himself refreshed by a vitality that the weary Londoner rarely knows. While the City man wakes to the grim arithmetic of 'Change and the counting-house, and the lawyer rises to the weight of pending briefs, Pendennis woke to a different currency: the lingering, intoxicating residue of the previous night's adventure. It was a peculiar kind of strengthener, that brief respite between the thrill of the event and the drudgery of the day, allowing him to face the sun with a heart lighter than usual.

Yet, Pendennis's labor was of a fleeting nature. The electric spark of literary creation burns out quickly in the hired writer, much like a candle in a draft. Once Pegasus is harnessed to the daily grind of the *Pall Mall Gazette*, the noble steed becomes merely another hack, requiring a whip or a ration of corn to move. Since his novelistic success had bumped up his salary, Arthur performed his duties with a competent, if unenthusiastic, diligence. He was a literary sprinter, brilliant in bursts but prone to the prose of the treadmill.

Society offered him neither comfort nor amusement. He was too young to be taken seriously by the established men, whose conversations he could only eavesdrop upon, and too old for the frivolous youth of his own station. He was destined, as fate often decrees, to be a man of solitude, though he would undoubtedly complain about it later. "What a lovely, artless creature that was," he mused, the memory of Fanny Bolton fresh in his mind. "How much sweeter her natural manner than the calculated coquetry of the ballroom belles! Who would have guessed such a rose blooming in a porter's lodge? Does she learn to sing from old Bows? If her voice is as sweet as her speech, it must be divine. I liked those low, veiled tones. 'What would you like me to call you?' Poor little Fanny! I almost adopted the grand air and told her to call me 'Sir.' But no Faust and Margaret nonsense for me. That old Bows, though—he's a dear. A gentleman in rags, a philosopher with a kind heart. He taught her to sing, didn't he? He's had his sorrows, too. I must cultivate him. I'm tired of genteel society anyway. Besides, there's nobody in town. Yes, I'll visit Bows and Costigan. What a rich character Costigan is! Begad, I'll study him for a book."

Thus, our young anthropologist reasoned, and since Saturday was the holiday of the week, leaving the *Gazette* contributors with nothing but ink and idle brains, Pendennis decided to pay a call at Shepherd's Inn.

If Arthur Pendennis had been the most cunning rogue in history, he could hardly have devised a more effective strategy to captivate poor Fanny Bolton than the one he had employed the night before. His dandified air, his conceited generosity, and his genuine honesty—all the very qualities that had allowed him to resist her trembling advances—combined to make her adore him. If one could peek into Fanny's bed, squeezed into a cupboard alongside her two sisters, one would find her tossing restlessly, disturbed by her companions' movements, replaying the delights of the night. Arthur was her hero, the living embodiment of the dashing villains and heroes she had devoured in the greasy volumes of the circulating library. She had been enchanted by the fineness of his linen, the brilliance of his studs, the elegance of his cambric handkerchief, and the jet-black shine of his boots. The Prince had conquered the handmaid. His image haunted her dreams; the blue light of his eyes, the protective smile, the frank laughter—it was all etched into her memory. She remembered his arm around her, the champagne glass he held, and the snide remarks of her friends: Emma Baker, proud of her cheesemonger fiancé, and Betsy Rodgers, fussing over her attorney's clerk.

At two o'clock, after the Boltons had finished dinner (while Mr. B., the porter, was away with the Countess of Estrich's hearse), a figure in a white hat and white trousers appeared under the Inn archway. Fanny was not surprised; she was simply overwhelmed with delight. She knew it was Him. She called to her mother, who was busy upstairs, and rushed to the wicket, pushing aside the other children. How she blushed as she offered her hand! How affably Arthur removed his hat, acknowledging the staring children. He asked Mrs. Bolton if she had slept well after the night's fatigue and hoped she had no headache, adding that since he was passing that way, he couldn't leave without checking on his little partner.

Mrs. Bolton, perhaps initially shy and suspicious, was quickly melted by Pen's inexhaustible good humor. He searched for a seat, finding none free save for a dish-cover or a workbox, so he perched himself on a child's chair. The children erupted in laughter, Fanny the loudest, amazed by His Royal Highness's condescension. To sit in a child's chair! Many years later, Fanny would look at that very chair with a fondness reserved for objects imbued with memory, wondering if the furniture in her own rooms might one day hold similar sweet, smiling faces.

Pen talked volubly, asking about the undertaking business, the number of mutes accompanying Lady Estrich, and the Inn itself. He expressed interest in Mr. Campion's cab, mentioned meeting him in society, and asked if Mrs. Bolton held shares in the Polwheedle and Tredyddlum. He even inquired about Captain Strong and Colonel Altamont. "Wasn't he a stout man?" Pen asked, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper regarding the Colonel's jewelry and wig. "Very black whiskers—in fact, blue black; that is to say, a rich greenish purple!" His waggishness provoked hysterical giggles from the ladies.

"Oh, we know," the ladies replied. "Sir F—— is Sir Francis Clavering; he's often here. Two or three times a week with the Captain. My little boy has been out fetching bill-stamps for him." Mrs. Bolton, thoroughly charmed, blurted out, "O Lor! I beg pardon, I shouldn't have mentioned no secrets," before admitting, "But we know you to be a gentleman, Mr. Pendennis, for I'm sure you have behaved as such. Hasn't Mr. Pendennis, Fanny?"

Fanny loved her mother for this declaration. Casting up her dark eyes to the low ceiling, she affirmed, "Oh, that he has, I'm sure, Ma," with a voice full of emotion.

Curious about the bill-stamps and the transactions in Strong's chambers, Pen asked further questions, which were artlessly answered by Mrs. Bolton. When Colonel Altamont arrived to join the Chevalier, Pen questioned him about his identity and connections, driven by private motives regarding Sir Francis Clavering. These inquiries, cleverly phrased, were met with honest ignorance.

Realizing he was running out of topics, Pen invoked his privilege as a member of the Press. "Would you like any orders for the play?" he asked. The theater was the universal delight of theatrical families. When Mr. Bolton was away on professional duties—which lately involved drinking heavily and becoming unpleasant to his family—they longed to slip out to the theatre, with little Barney keeping the lodge. Pendennis's generous offer of tickets was received with boundless gratitude.

Fanny clapped her hands, her face beaming. She nodded and laughed at her mother, who reciprocated the joy. Mrs. Bolton, far from being too old for admiration, was now Pen's enthusiastic partisan. When two women unite in liking a man, they fuel each other's enthusiasm until the second becomes as eager as the first.

The offer of tickets and other pleasantries put everyone in a perfect mood, except for a brief interruption when a younger child, hearing the name 'Astley's,' declared she wanted to go too. "Don't bother!" Fanny said sharply. "Git-long, Betsy-Jane, do now, and play in the court," her mother added. Thus, the two little ones, Betsy-Jane and Amelia-Ann, departed in their innocent pinafores to disport themselves on the smooth gravel around the statue of Shepherd the Great.

While Pen entertained the ladies, laughing delightedly at his sallies, an old gentleman emerged from the Inn square and peered into the lodge. He wore a very blank and rueful expression upon seeing Mr. Arthur seated on a table, looking like Macheath in the play, in easy discourse with Mrs. Bolton and her daughter.

"What! Mr. Bows? How d'you do, Bows?" Pen cried out in a cheery, loud voice. "I was coming to see you, and was asking your address of these ladies."

"You were coming to see me, were you, sir?" Bows said, entering with a sad face and shaking Arthur's hand. "Plague on that old man!" someone thought in the room; and perhaps, someone else besides her.
