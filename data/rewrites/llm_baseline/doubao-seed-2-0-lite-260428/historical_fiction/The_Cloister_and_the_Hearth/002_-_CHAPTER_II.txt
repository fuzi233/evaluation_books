“The soup is hot,” said Gerard, leaning in to where the old scholar huddled stiff and shivering by the little roadside fire.

The senior man sighed, his shoulders slumping despondently. “But how are we to reach it without burning ourselves? My bones ache too much to kneel, not with these short straws, and the flask sits too close to the flame.”

“Father, the young man has brought us straws already,” Margaret put in, a tiny, sly, warm smile tugging at the corner of her mouth.

“Ay, ay!” the old man nodded, “but my poor limbs are too creaky to bend that far. St. John the Baptist, but this lad is quick!”
Even as he spoke of his difficulty, Gerard had already solved it. He undid the small knot fastened at his breast in a heartbeat, pulled off his soft felt hat, tucked a smooth river stone into each of its four corners, wrapped his jerkin’s tail around his hand to shield it, and plucked the bubbling flask straight off the fire. He wedged it snug between the weighted stones in the crown of his hat, then held it right under the old man’s nose with a merry, crinkling smile. The elder tremulously slipped one of the rye straws into the soup and sipped. Slowly his wan, drawn face lightened, glowing brighter and brighter until it was almost radiant, and he drew a long, deep breath of pure delight.

“Hippocrates and Galen!” he cried. “‘Tis soupe au vin — the restorative of all restoratives! Blessed be the nation that invented it, the woman who stirred it, and the young man who brought it to fainting folk. Have a sip, girl, while I tell our young host the full history and virtues of this sovereign draught. This elixir, sir, was unknown to the ancients: you will find no mention of it in their medical treatises, nor even in the popular tales that leak so many of their chirurgery and home remedy secrets. Hector, in the *Iliad*, if my memory does not fail me —”
“— was offered a cup of pure wine by one of the poem’s ladies, but he refused, saying he was off to battle and would not drink anything that might weaken his strength. Why, if soupe au vin had existed at Troy, he would have added a line in that very hexameter to say ‘But madam, I will gladly taste your soupe au vin.’ Not only would that have been common courtesy, no perfect commander lacks that — but to refuse it would have marked him as shallow and unfit to lead an entire war! Men going into battle need sustenance, as all history proves: foolish generals who march hungry troops against well-fed enemies are beaten every time, even when they outnumber their foe. The Romans lost a great battle in northern Italy to Hannibal the Carthaginian for that very folly alone. This divine cordial puts new strength in the limbs and fire in the spirits in a single sip — had Hector drunk it at the right moment, with Phoebus, Venus, and all the blessed saints looking on, the Greeks would almost certainly have lost that war! Look at me: a minute ago I was faint, weary, and heartsick, and now? I am brave as Achilles, and strong as an eagle.”

“Oh father, an eagle? Don’t exaggerate so!” Margaret laughed.

“Girl, I defy you and anyone else! I could run all the way to Rotterdam right now, and fight every ill life throws at me — even poverty and old age, which philosophers call the greatest of all evils. Negatur! That is only true if a man has wasted his life, which, to be fair, far too many do. Now for the moderns —”

“Father, dearest father, drink more soup first before it cools!” Margaret held the hat out to him imploringly with both hands, and he slipped the straw back between his lips, spared from launching into another long string of historical anecdotes.

Gerard took the chance to tell Margaret how proud his mother would be to know her soup had helped a man of such learning.

“Ay, but” Margaret said, her eyes glinting playfully, “it would grieve her to see her son give all of his own food away and take none for himself. Why did you only cut two straws?”

“Fair mistress, I hoped you would let me share your straw,” Gerard smiled. “There were only two, after all.”

Margaret blushed and grinned. “Never beg what you may command. The straw is not mine, it is yours — you cut it in the yonder field.”

“I cut it, so it was mine. But then your lips touched it, so it became yours.”

“Did it now? Then I will lend it back to you. There — now it is yours again, your lips have touched it.”

“No, it belongs to both of us now. Let us split it.”

“By all means, you have a knife.”

“I will not cut it — that would be bad luck. I’ll bite it in half. I will keep my half. I dare say you will burn yours as soon as you get home.”

“You do not know me. I waste nothing. Ten to one I turn it into a hairpin, or some other little trinket.”

Her answer made Gerard fall quiet, soft and flustered rather than teased, his heart thrumming. Once the bread and soup were all gone, the old scholar gathered his things to continue their journey. Only then did the quick, clever Gerard find himself stuck: he could not retie the little silk ribbon around his neck, the one his mother Catherine had tied for him so neatly at home. Margaret watched him fumble with it slyly for a while, then offered to help — at her age, girls love to play at being coy and tender, saucy and gentle by turns, and she had seen she had made him flustered just a little earlier. Her fair head, crowned with stately auburn hair that glowed like polished copper through its silver threads, bowed sweetly towards him, and his eyes drank in the sight of her. Two soft, white, supple hands worked delicately at the stubborn ribbon, molding the ends into a neat, perfect knot with light, airy touches. A heavenly thrill shot through the innocent young man, and vague, beautiful glimpses of an entire new world of feeling opened up before him. Margaret prolonged that sweet moment unwittingly — no woman hurries a task that touches the gentle, small rituals of adornment. When her taper fingers finally had the knot fastened tight, she still was not quite satisfied: she curved her palm, pressed it soft and warm to the very center of the ribbon knot, a little coaxing hand-kiss as if to say “now be a good knot, and stay tied.” The palm-kiss was meant for the ribbon, but Gerard’s heart leaped up to meet it.

“There, that is exactly how it was,” Margaret said, drawing back to admire her work, then looked up to wait for his approval. She met his gaze full on — his eyes were burning with such ardent, adoring longing that she dropped her own eyes at once, her face flushing bright scarlet. An indescribable tremor ran through her, and she stepped back quickly, took her father’s arm on the other side of him, her lashes lowered and her cheeks betraying every bit of her flustered feeling. Gerard, blushing that he had scared her away with his stare, took the old man’s other arm. And so the two young things walked side by side, both downcast and acutely aware of each other, propping up their “eagle” of a scholar in soft, silent peace.

They entered Rotterdam through the Schiedamze Poort. Gerard did not know the town, so Peter pointed him the way to Hooch Straet, where the Stadthouse stood. He and Margaret were bound for his cousin’s house in Ooster-Waagen Straet, so almost as soon as they passed through the gate their paths split. They bid each other a friendly farewell, and Gerard stepped into the bustle of the great city. A heavy, profound loneliness settled over him even in the crowded streets. He kicked himself, far too late, for being too shy to ask their names, or where they lived.

“Curse my foolish shamefacedness!” he muttered to himself. “But their speech and their breeding were far finer than their worn clothes would suggest, and something whispered me they did not wish to be recognized by strangers. I will never see her again. Oh, weary world, I hate you and your ways! To meet such beauty, goodness, and learning — three priceless pearls — and lose them forever before I even know their names.”

Lost in that sad reverie, he let his feet carry him wherever they would, and soon found himself completely turned around. He spotted a crowd of people all heading the same way, and joined them, assuming they must be bound for the Stadthouse. But the noisy troop led him not to the town hall, but to a wide meadow on the banks of the Maas. All sorts of games were underway: wrestling, palm-ball, quintain, legerdemain, archery, tumbling — and the crowd roared with delight as men and women alike flipped and somersaulted for their amusement. There was a trained bear that stood on his head, marched upright, and bowed with great gravity to his master, a hare that beat a tiny drum, and a cock that strutted about on little stilts with a haughty air. The sights made Gerard laugh now and then, but the gaiety could not truly reach him — his heart was too sad and out of tune with the fun. When he heard a young man say to his friend that the Duke had been at the meadow, but had left for the Stadthouse to feast the burgomasters, aldermen, art competition prize competitors and all their guests, he suddenly realized he was ravenously hungry, and very much wanted to sup at the prince’s table. He turned away from the river, and this time found Hooch Straet, which led him straight to the Stadthouse.

But when he arrived, he was turned away at every small door, until he reached the great main courtyard gate. It was guarded by soldiers, overseen by a pompous major-domo glittering with an embroidered collar, a heavy gold chain of office, and a white staff with a gilded knob. A whole crowd of people clustered at the gate, trying to wheedle their way past the official, who stood as immovable as a rock. They came up to him one by one like ripples on water, and were turned away just as easily. Gerard struggled to push through the crowd, and when he was only four heads away from the gate, his heart leapt: there was Peter, Margaret on his arm, humbly begging for entrance.

“My cousin the alderman is not at home,” the old man said. “The servants told us he is here at the feast. If you will not let us in to see him, at least carry this slip of paper with his name on it to him — he will come out to meet us.”

“What is that to me, old man?” the porter snapped. “I carry no messages. I only keep the gate.”

He then roared in a stentorian, unyielding voice: “No strangers enter here but the competitors and their invited companies!”

“Step aside old man, you got your answer,” someone in the crowd called.

Margaret turned half around, her eyes imploring. “Good people, we have traveled a very long way. My father is old, and my cousin has a new servant who does not know us, and turned us away from his own house before we could explain.”

The crowd laughed hoarsely. Margaret shrank back as if they had struck her. At that moment, a hand wrapped around hers — a warm, firm grasp that felt like heart meeting heart, like magnet drawing steel. She spun around, and there was Gerard. A little cry of joy and relief burst from her, and she began to sniffle softly, prettily. The crowd had jostled and frightened her, her cousin’s thoughtlessness to forget to tell his new servants they were coming was cruel, and the servant’s caution, however loyal to his master, had humiliated her and her father terribly. To her, sore, anxious, and jostled, Gerard’s kind face and hand felt like a miracle.

“Ah, all’s well now,” jeered a coarse voice in the crowd. “She’s found her sweetheart!”

“Haw haw haw!” the rest of the crowd roared.

Margaret dropped Gerard’s hand at once, and spun around, her eyes bright and flashing through her tears. “I have no sweetheart, you rude men. But I am friendless in your boorish town, and this is a friend — one who knows, as you do not, how to treat the aged and the weak.”

The crowd fell dead silent. They had only been thoughtless, and now they knew her sharp rebuke was entirely just. The quiet let Gerard turn to the porter.

“I am a competitor, sir.”

“What is your name?” the man squinted at him suspiciously.

“Gerard, son of Elias.”

The janitor checked the slip of parchment he held. “Gerard Eliassoen may enter.”

“With my company, these two?” Gerard asked.

“Nay. They are not your company. They came before you.”

“What does that matter? They are my friends, and I will not go in without them.”

“Then stay outside.”

“That I will not.”

“That we shall see.”

“Speedily, then.” Gerard drew a breath, and shouted at the top of his powerful, ringing voice, so loud the whole street echoed: “HO, PHILIP, EARL OF HOLLAND!”

“Are you mad?” the porter gasped.

“HERE IS ONE OF YOUR SERVANTS WHO DEFIES YOU!” Gerard roared on.

“Hush! Hush, man!”

“Murder! The Duke is right there! I am dead!” the janitor quaked, panicking. He yelled as loud as he could, to drown out Gerard’s voice: “OPEN THE GATE, YE KNAVES! WAY THERE FOR GERARD ELIASSOEN AND HIS COMPANY! (The fiends go with him!)”

The gate swung open as if by magic. Eight soldiers lowered their pikes halfway, making an arch the three of them walked under, triumphant, past the portal. The moment they were through, the pikes clashed together horizontally to bar the gate, almost spearing a greedy townsman who tried to slip in after them.

A few steps past the gate, they found themselves in a scene of almost Oriental luxury. The courtyard was lined with tables heaped with rich meats and glowing, ornate silver plate. Guests in rich, varied costumes sat beneath a canopy of fresh cut boughs strung from golden, silver, and blue silken cords strung across the space. Fruits of every hue — even artificial ones made of gold, silver, and wax — hung from the plane and lime trees, peeking out like bright eyes through the green leaves. The Duke’s minstrels swept their lutes at intervals, and a fountain played red Burgundy wine in six jets that met and danced in the air. The evening sun shot its golden fire through the purple wine spouts, turning them into cascades of molten rubies. The red-gold light spilled over fair faces, snowy beards, velvet, satin, jeweled sword hilts, glowing gold, gleaming silver, and sparkling glass. Gerard and his two friends stood dazzled, spellbound. A whisper buzzed around them: “Salute the Duke! Salute the Duke!” They looked up, and there on the dais high above them their sovereign stood, waving a kind welcome down to them. The men bowed low, Margaret curtsied with a deep, graceful obeisance. The Duke, his hand still raised, pointed the new arrivals out to a group of valets. Instantly seven of his servants rushed over, seated the three of them at a table, and set fifteen different colored soups down in little silver bowls before them, and as many fine wines in crystal vases.

“Nay father, let us not eat until we thank our good friend,” Margaret said, only now recovering from all the bustle.

“Child, he is our guardian angel.”

Gerard covered his face with his hands. “Tell me when you have finished thanking me, and I will look up and have my supper, for I am very hungry. I know which of us three is the happiest to have met again.”

“Me?” Margaret asked.

“No. Guess again.”

“Father?”

“No.”

“Then I have no guess at all!” Margaret laughed, a bright little crow of pure happiness.

They sipped the soup, which vanished quickly, and then fish arrived in a dozen different preparations, lobster and almond patties, almond and cream patties, and a huge variety of savory rissoles. Next came a whole wild boar, that smelled divine — until Margaret sprang back from it with two shrieks of dismay, and pinched Gerard’s arm hard. The Duke’s chef had been far too clever: he had restored the boar’s bristles and fur with burnt sugar and edible dyes, its great tusks still in its jaw, and two bright, colored sugar eyes that seemed to follow every movement of knife and spoon across the table.

“What is all that noise?” the Duke called, hearing Margaret’s cry. Seven servants hurried to tell him, and he laughed. “Give her some of the beef-stuffing then, and bring me Sir Boar!” That beef-stuffing was the Duke’s own private favorite dish: on grand occasions, he had an entire ox roasted whole, and all the venison, hare, lamb, and poultry stuffed into the big cavity in its side cooked to absolute perfection, soaking up all the ox’s rich juices. The Duke sent his servants over, who drove silver tridents straight into the steaming ox and fished out a kid, a cygnet, and a whole flock of wildfowl, which were set smoking hot before Gerard and his friends. Old Peter’s face, which had fallen a little glum at missing out on the wild boar, lit up with joy. After that came twenty different fruit and herb tarts, and last of all confectionery on a grand scale: cathedrals of sugar, gilded and painted along the bas-reliefs, castles with imitated moats and ditches, sugar elephants, camels, toads, knights on horseback jousting, kings and princesses watching, trumpeters blowing — all the sweet statues filled with scented sugary syrup, works of art made only to be eaten.
