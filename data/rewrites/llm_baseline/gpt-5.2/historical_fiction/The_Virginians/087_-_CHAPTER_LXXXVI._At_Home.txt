Our mother’s summons, so tenderly urged, might have thawed flint; and more than once we set ourselves to speak of Virginia as of a promised season,—how we would take the whole of Young Rachel’s cabin, and carry the children across as though it were but a jaunt down to Bath. Yet the household has its tyrannies more absolute than kings. The baby took a feverish turn, and the doctor, after sniffing at the air and feeling the little pulse with a face too wise for comfort, forbade the voyage; and what mother can sail while her child is left ashore? So Young Rachel went without us.

The next year my own body—always ready to mutiny when my mind begins to enjoy itself—chose to declare war by way of the gout. I was laid up, nursed, scolded, and fed with a discipline that made me feel, between pain and patience, as though I were being pickled for preservation. And then, while we still spoke of “next spring, next autumn,” came the news from Jamaica, which struck all our plans to the ground as a wind strikes the blossom.

Two years after we had made the Manor our home, our dear General returned from his government a little richer in coin, but robbed of the one treasure for which no counting-house ever kept a book. My Theo’s mother—soft-voiced, warm-handed, the gentlest friend (save one) I have ever known—had died abroad of the fever. She had not lived to see our prosperity with her own eyes; yet, as her husband said, with that grave simplicity which made his grief the more piercing, “She sees us, though we do not see her; and she thanks you, George, for having been good to her children.”

We had thought he would not long remain behind her. His love for her had been the business and holiday of his whole life; to be without her was like sitting at a table where every dish is served and yet the appetite is gone. It was pitiful to watch him among us. In Theo’s very step upon the stair, in the turn of her head when she listened, in certain little ways of smoothing a child’s sleeve or laying a napkin straight, he found the lost face again and again, as one finds a remembered tune under new fingers. What cheer we could offer, we offered. But little Hetty—under Heaven—became his chief prop. She had refused advantageous matches in the island, the General told us; and on her return to England my Lord Wrotham’s heir had thrown himself at her feet.

“I love best to stay with my father,” Hetty said. “So long as he is not tired of me, I care for no husband.”

“Nay,” we cried, when this last great match was pressed, “let the General keep house with us six months at the Manor, and you shall have him at Oakhurst for the other six!”

But Hetty declared her father could never bear Oakhurst again now that her mother was gone; and she would not be bought by a coronet and a rent-roll. The General, speaking of her with that just, quiet penetration which made his tenderness the more manly, said gravely that the child seemed to have no wish toward marriage at all—perhaps from some early disappointment of which she never spoke. We, respecting silence as one respects a closed door in a sick-room, asked no question.

By this time my brother Lambert had a college living near Winchester, and—by the same inevitable logic which provides a parsonage with a pulpit—he had a wife to fill the drawing-room and take possession of the kitchen. We professed but a moderate liking for this lady, though we made her welcome when she came. She was a woman who could not be happy unless she were proving her honesty by the pain she gave; and she took a malicious pride in calling it duty.

“My dear Sir George,” she was pleased to say, “I have often and often told our dear Theo that I wouldn’t have a pretty sister in my house to make tea for Jack when I was upstairs, and always to be at hand when I was wanted in the kitchen or nursery, and always to be dressed neat and in her best when I was very likely making pies or puddings or looking to the children. I have every confidence in Jack, of course. I should like to see him look at another woman, indeed! And so I have in Jemima—but they don’t come together in my house when I’m upstairs—that I promise you! And so I told my sister Warrington.”

“Am I to understand,” says the General, his voice very gentle and very dangerous, “that you have done my Lady Warrington the favour to warn her against her sister, my daughter Miss Hester?”

“Yes, pa, of course I have. A duty is a duty, and a woman is a woman, and a man’s a man, as I know very well. Don’t tell me! He is a man. Every man is a man, with all his sanctified airs!”

“You yourself have a married sister, with whom you were staying when my son Jack first had the happiness of making your acquaintance?” the General asked.

“Yes, of course I have a married sister; every one knows that—and I have been as good as a mother to her children, that I have!”

“And am I to gather from your conversation that your attractions proved a powerful temptation for your sister’s husband?”

“Law, General! I don’t know how you can go for to say I ever said any such a thing!” cries Mrs. Jack, growing scarlet and louder, like a kettle that will boil over because it cannot argue.

“Don’t you perceive, my dear madam,” says the General, “that it is you who have insinuated as much, not only regarding yourself, but regarding my own two daughters?”

“Never, never, never, as I’m a Christian woman! And it’s most cruel of you to say so, sir. And I do say a sister is best out of the house, that I do! And as Theo’s time is coming, I warn her, that’s all.”

“Have you discovered, my good madam, whether my poor Hetty has stolen any of the spoons? When I came to breakfast this morning, my daughter was alone, and there must have been a score of pieces of silver on the table.”

“Law, sir! who ever said a word about spoons? Did I ever accuse the poor dear? If I did, may I drop down dead at this moment on this hearth-rug! And I ain’t used to be spoke to in this way. And me and Jack have both remarked it; and I’ve done my duty, that I have.” And away she flounced, crying, as though tears could wash away what her tongue had thrown.

“And has the woman had the impudence to tell you this, my child?” the General asked, when Theo—who was a little delicate—came down to tea.

“She has told me every day since she has been here. She comes into my dressing-room to tell me. She comes into my nursery, and says, ‘Ah, I wouldn’t have a sister prowling about my nursery, that I wouldn’t.’ Ah, how pleasant it is to have amiable and well-bred relatives, say I.”

“Thy poor mother has been spared this woman,” groans the General.

“Our mother would have made her better, papa,” Theo said, and kissed him. And I saw, by the softened look that passed between them, that both were saying their prayers without words.

Yet truth must be owned: to love one’s kin is not always easy; to live with one’s neighbours is not always amusing. Jack Lambert came next day with a sulky brow and a hearty knife. His wife had served him her own version of the scene, hot and highly seasoned; and he brought it with him like a bad aftertaste. He was angry, but anger did not diminish his appetite. He preached a sermon for us—long, lumbering, and empty; and little Miles, once more in sables, sat by his grandfather, his small hand tucked into the old man’s as naturally as a fledgling finds the warm place under a wing.

We asked if the General would keep house for us while we made our Virginian journey. The housekeeper should obey Hetty as though she were mistress born; the butler’s keys should pass into the General’s keeping. Gumbo—though not, I thought, with an overgood grace—was to go with us to Virginia: for he had meanwhile tied himself to Mrs. Molly in honest matrimony, and peopled a cottage in my park with sundry tawny little Gumbos, who ran in and out like squirrels, and filled the place with a perpetual patter of bare feet and shrill laughter.

So, leaving our house under the General’s care and Hetty’s vigilant kindness, we travelled to London, then down to Bristol. Our obsequious agent there had the chance of vowing prayers for our prosperity, and of declaring—hands uplifted—that children so beautiful as ours (for we had an infant now to accompany Miles) were never carried aboard ship since Noah’s ark. The vessel’s smells—tar, damp rope, salt meat, sour beer—rose like a coarse chorus; yet between such rude odours one caught, now and then, the clean bite of sea air and the faint sweetness of biscuit, and my wife, even in that unromantic theatre, contrived to make a nursery of her corner. We made the voyage without accident.

How strange the feeling was, as we landed from our boat at Richmond! The river spoke in its broad, slow voice; the wharf creaked; the calls of negroes carried over the water; the wheels of a waiting coach grated on grit; and, behind all, the great hush of the land—so different from England’s ceaseless clatter—seemed to listen. A coach and a host of negroes were there to receive us; and hard by, a gentleman on horseback, with negroes in our livery, too. He sprang down and rushed to embrace us. It was our dearest Hal. We were charmed—no, moved beyond words—to see him. He rode with us to our mother’s door.

There she stood on the steps to welcome us; and Theo, in the very sweep of her gratitude, knelt to ask her blessing.

Harry rode in the coach as far as our mother’s house, but would not, as he said, spoil sport by entering with us. “She sees me,” he owned, “and we are pretty good friends; but Fanny and she are best apart; and there is no love lost between ’em, I can promise you. Come over to me at the Tavern, George, when thou art free. And to-morrow I shall have the honour to present her sister to Theo. ’Twas only from happening to be in town yesterday that I heard the ship was signalled, and waited to see you. I have sent a negro boy home to my wife, and she’ll be here to pay her respects to my Lady Warrington.” And with that, he jumped out and left us to meet our mother alone.

Since I had parted from her I had seen courts, and paid duty at St. James’s; yet I had seen no one more stately than the lady who received us now. The house was plain enough—wood-built, with a gallery running round, as our Virginian houses are—but she made it a palace by the manner in which she moved within it. She raised Theo from her knee, embraced her, and led her in as though she were placing a crown upon her own choosing.

Old Nathan was still major-domo; a score of black faces grinned welcome; and time had done its quiet work among them as among all tribes—children become men and women, men become grey, and new little feet appear where none were before. Some names I remembered had grown out of remembrance; some heads I had left jet-black were now powdered by nature’s snow; and little ones, born since my time, peeped from doorways with great eyes and naked soles that made no noise on the boards.

“I’m little Sip, Master George!” one cried.

“I’m Dinah, Sir George!”

“I’m Master Miles’s boy!” said a little fellow in fresh livery and boots of nature’s blacking, proud as any drum-major.

Before the day was over the whole household had contrived, under one pretence or another, to pass before us, bowing and smiling as though welcome were a duty of the place, like prayer. I know not how many repasts were served. Virginia hospitality has a rhythm like tide: it comes in, retreats, and comes in again, each time bearing new dishes upon its broad shoulder. The kitchens breathed constantly—wood-smoke, bubbling fat, pepper, boiled greens, and that deep, sweet smell which comes when ham has been long at the fire and begins to give itself up. One could hear the mortar thud with herbs, the knife on the block, the clink of pewter, the soft scuff of feet on packed earth behind the house. Even in rejoicing, a plantation is an instrument always playing: the voices of servants, the creak of hinges, the distant lowing, the domestic orchestra which, in a healthy place, keeps the mind steadier than any sermon.

In the evening my Lady Warrington had to receive all the little town’s gentry, which she did with perfect grace; and I shook hands with old acquaintances—old enemies, I was going to say; but I had come into a fortune and was no longer a naughty prodigal. Why, a drove of fatted calves was killed in my honour. My poor Hal was of the entertainment, but gloomy and crestfallen. His mother spoke to him, but as a queen to a rebellious prince: he was her son, and not yet forgiven.

We slipped away from the company and went up to the rooms assigned to me; but scarcely had we begun to speak freely when our mother appeared—taper in hand—her pale face lighted from below so that it seemed carved in wax and will.

Did I want anything? Was everything as I wished? She had peeped in at the dear children sleeping like cherubs. How she caressed them and delighted in them! How she was charmed with Miles’s little airs of command, and the baby Theo’s smiles and dimples!

“Supper is just coming on the table, Sir George. If you like our cookery better than the tavern, Henry, I beg you to stay.”

What a difference lay in the very syllables she gave to each of us! Hal’s head drooped, and he followed her down.

A clergyman begged a blessing on the meal, and with no little art and eloquence touched upon our arrival home, our safe passage across stormy waters, and the love and forgiveness awaiting us in the mansion of the Heavenly Parent when the storms of life were over. We had, it seemed, a new clergyman—quite unlike some whom I remembered in earlier days. I praised him; but Madam Esmond shook her head. She feared his principles were dangerous; she feared others had taken up those dangerous principles. Had I not seen, she asked, the paper signed at Williamsburg the year before—Lees, Randolphs, Bassets, Washingtons, and the like, and—oh, my dear!—our name too (that is, my brother’s, by what influence she did not like to say), and this unhappy Mr. Belman’s who had begged a blessing last night?

If there had been quarrels in our little society when I left home, what were those to the feuds raging now? We had sent the Stamp Act and been forced to repeal it; then tried new duties, and repealed those too—all save tea. From Boston to Charleston, tea was seized and flung aside as though it were poison. Even my mother, loyal as she was, gave up her favourite drink. My poor wife must have forgone hers too, but we had brought a quantity for our private use aboard ship, and paid four times duty for it at home; so our little pot could still sing in the mornings, and the fragrant steam—so domestic, so intimate—rose like a small act of resistance in the other direction. There is a strange power in a familiar brew: it carries memory on its scent, and the tongue, tasting what it cannot name, finds itself suddenly back in a former life. Yet even that comfort was not without its quarrel, for every cup seemed to ask: whose law is in it? whose labour? whose word counts as “right”?

For my part I never hesitated about paying duty. The home Government must have revenue, or its pretensions to authority were idle. They say the colonies were tried and tyrannised over; I say the home Government was tried and tyrannised over. ’Tis an affair of argument now, and history; we tried the question and were beat, and the matter is settled as completely as the conquest of Britain by the Normans. From conviction, I trust, I took the British side. In that brief and unfortunate war-experience of my youth, the army’s cry had been that Braddock’s expedition failed and disaster fell upon us through the remissness, selfishness, and rapacity of many of the very people we came to defend. The colonists would have everything done for them and do nothing: they bargained extortionately with their champions, failed in contracts, furnished niggardly supplies, multiplied delays until the hour for useful action was past, and so helped invite the catastrophe which need never have occurred but for their ill-will.

What shouts of joy there had been for the Minister who devised the conquest of Canada! Monsieur de Vaudreuil said justly that that conquest was the signal for the colonies’ defection; and my Lord Chatham, having done his best to win the first part, contributed more than any man in England to complete the second. The colonies were insurgent, and he applauded their resistance. How many waverers must he have heartened into revolt! It was as if a general should say to mutineers, “God save the King! My men, you have a right to mutiny!” No wonder they set up his statue here and his picture there, while hanging Ministers and Governors in effigy. At Williamsburg, some wiseacres subscribed for a portrait of my lord as a Roman orator pointing to Whitehall, and the very window whence Charles the First was beheaded—a neat allegory, and a pretty compliment to a British statesman. I hear, however, that my lord’s head was painted from a bust, and so was taken off without his knowledge.

My country is England, not America or Virginia; and I took the English side. My sympathies were with home, where I was now a squire and citizen. Yet had my lot been to plant tobacco and live by the James or Potomac, no doubt my opinions would have altered; for the same earth that feeds a man also teaches him, and every dialect has its own tyranny of meaning. When I visited my brother at his new house and plantation, I found him and his wife staunch Americans, as Theo and I were British. We had words, as who had not in those times, but without rancour; even my new sister could not bring us to that, though she tried her best in company and, I doubt not, in curtain-lectures after, like a notable housewife measuring out obedience as she measured out flour. But Hal and I trusted one another so entirely that even his duty to his wife would not make him quarrel with me. He loved me from old times, when my word was law to him. He still protested that he and every Virginian gentleman of his side were loyal to the Crown. War was not yet declared; gentlemen of different opinions still kept decent manners, as men keep the peace at table while the knives are near. At public dinners the King’s health was still ostentatiously drunk; every colony, preparing for Congress and resisting taxation, was loud in its regard for “the King our Father,” and pathetic in appeals that he would dismiss evil counsellors and listen to moderation and reason.

Up to the last, the Virginian gentry were a grave, orderly, aristocratic folk, with a strong sense of their own station. In later days, nearer home, we have heard of fraternisation and equality. Among the great people of our Old World I have never seen a gentleman stand more upon his dignity and maintain it better than Mr. Washington—no, not the King against whom he took arms. To the French gentry who joined the crusade against us and took their revenge for Canada, the American chief appeared anax andron, and they allowed his better could not be seen in Versailles. Though quarrelling with the Governor, the burgesses still kept private courtesies, exchanging dignified civilities as though a ritual of words might yet bind what policy was tearing. When my Lord Bottetourt arrived and held his court at Williamsburg in no small splendour, all the gentry waited upon him—Madam Esmond included. And when Lord Dunmore succeeded him, bringing a fine family, he too was treated with respect in private, though in public the House and Governor stood at war.

These quarrels are history; they concerned me personally only thus: our burgesses being convened for the first of March in the year after our arrival, it was agreed we should visit the capital and pay duty to his Excellency. Since Hal’s unhappy marriage Madam Esmond had not performed this duty, though once accustomed to do so; but now that her eldest son was in the colony again, she opined we must certainly wait upon the Governor. Nor were we sorry to escape our little Richmond and taste the provincial capital’s gaieties.

Madam engaged—at a great price—the best house to be had at Richmond for herself and family. Now that I was rich, her generosity took on a curious edge. More than once I had to interpose (her old servants wondering too) and beg her not to be so lavish. But she answered gently that in former days she had occasion to save, which existed no more. Harry had enough—surely—with such a wife as he had taken out of the housekeeper’s room. If she chose to be a little extravagant now, why hesitate? She had not her dearest daughter and grandchildren with her every day; she had fallen in love with all three and spoiled them as much as they could be spoiled. Besides, in old times I could not accuse her of too much extravagance; and this was almost the only allusion she made to our former pecuniary differences.

So she had the servants dressed in their best; her best wines; plate and furniture sent by sea from Castlewood at no small charge; and the very dress in which she had been married in George the Second’s reign. We flattered ourselves that our coach made the greatest figure of any except his Excellency’s. We engaged Signor Formicalo—his Excellency’s major-domo—to superintend the series of feasts given in my honour; and more fleshpots were set a-stewing in one month, the servants said, than had been known since the young gentlemen went away. The kitchens became a kind of memory-workshop, boiling up the past in broth and gravy. One might have thought Madam Esmond meant to prove, by spice and silver, that she had never diminished—only waited.

So great was Theo’s influence over my mother that she actually persuaded her, that year, to receive our sister Fanny, Hal’s wife. Fanny would have stayed upon the plantation rather than face Madam Esmond; but trusting to Theo’s promise of amnesty, she came up to town, made her curtsey, and was forgiven. And rather than be forgiven in that fashion, I own I would prefer perdition or open persecution.

“You know these, my dear?” Madam Esmond said, pointing to her fine silver sconces. “Fanny hath often cleaned them when she was with me at Castlewood. And this dress too, Fanny knows, I dare say? Her poor mother had the care of it. I always had the greatest confidence in her.”

Wrath flashed from Fanny’s eyes—quick as a struck spark—yet our mother, who had forgiven, did not perceive; forgiveness with her was a sovereign act, and sovereigns are not accustomed to notice what subjects feel.

“Oh, she was a treasure to me!” Madam resumed. “I never should have nursed my boys through their illnesses but for your mother’s admirable care. Colonel Lee, permit me to present you to my daughter, my Lady Warrington. Her ladyship is a neighbour of your relatives the Bunburys at home. Here comes his Excellency. Welcome, my lord!”

And our princess made before his lordship one of those curtseys of which she was proud; and I fancy some of the company ventured to smile.

“By George! madam,” says Mr. Lee, “since Count Borulawski, I have not seen a bow so elegant as your ladyship’s.”

“And pray, sir, who was Count Borulawski?” asks Madam.

“He was a nobleman high in favour with his Polish Majesty,” replies Mr. Lee. “May I ask you, madam, to present me to your distinguished son?”

“This is Sir George Warrington,” says my mother, pointing to me.

“Pardon me, madam. I meant Captain Warrington, who was by Mr. Wolfe’s side when he died. I had been contented to share his fate, so I had been near him.”

And the ardent Lee swaggered up to Harry, took his hand with respect, and paid him compliments which made me, at least, pardon the man his impertinence; for my dearest Hal walked gloomily through his mother’s rooms in the old uniform of the famous corps he had quitted, as though it were a garment of honour and reproach stitched together.

We had had many meetings which the stern mother could not prevent, and in which that instinctive love binding us—indestructible as blood—had its hour to speak. Entirely unlike in tastes and pursuits—his life eager exercise, field-sport, and hardy amusements; mine a dawdling over books, a languid watching of my own mind—we nevertheless had such a sympathy as almost passes the love of women. Hal confessed as much in his artless way when we went away without wives or womankind (except a few negroes left in the place) and passed a week at Castlewood together. There, with the house’s old boards creaking under familiar steps, and the night sounds of the plantation—frogs in the marsh, wind in the pines, the far-off cry of an owl—falling into their ancient places like notes in a tune, we found ourselves again in the circle of our beginnings, even while the world outside was busy breaking its own language into two hostile dialects, each calling itself loyalty and each hearing treason in the other’s words.
