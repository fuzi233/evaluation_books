Jeeves—my man, you know—is quite possibly the most extraordinary human to ever zip around a London flat pressing your cummerbunds and knowing exactly what you want before you even think it. I’d be properly lost without him, no two ways about it. Broadly speaking, he’s exactly the type of chap you find hovering sadly behind the marble counter at Penn Station, marked INQUIRIES. You know the lads I mean: you saunter up, bleary, and ask for the next train to Melonsquashville, Tennessee, and they don’t even pause mid-sip of their coffee before rattling off “Two forty-three, track ten, change at San Francisco” — and they’re always 100% correct. Jeeves gives off that exact same omniscient vibe, like he knows where you left your missing cufflinks, what the weather will be next Tuesday, and who stole the last scone from the pantry before anyone even notices the scone is gone.

Take Exhibit A: One morning I bumped into Monty Byng on Bond Street, decked out in the sharpest grey check suit you ever did see, and I knew I’d never sleep a wink again until I owned a duplicate. I pried the tailor’s address out of him, and had the chappies cutting the fabric before the clock struck noon.

“Jeeves,” I told him that evening, grinning like an idiot. “I’m getting a check suit exactly like Mr. Byng’s.”

“Injudicious, sir,” he said, firm as a judge passing a death sentence on ice cream. “It will not become you.”

“What utter rot! It’s the best fashion idea I’ve had all year.”

“Unsuitable for you, sir.”

Well, long story short, the confounded garment turned up, I hauled it on, and caught a glimpse of myself in the mirror and nearly fainted dead away. Jeeves was perfectly right. I looked like a music hall comedian who’d wandered off his unicycle mid-act and accidentally raided a cheap bookie’s wardrobe for a costume. Yet Monty had looked like a million bucks in the exact same fabric. These things are just Life’s weird little pranks, no point arguing with them.

But it’s not just that his fashion judgment is infallible — though that’s obviously the main selling point. The man knows everything. There was that tip on the Lincolnshire Handicap. I forget who passed it to me, but it smelled like the hottest, most genuine red-hot inside scoop you could ever hope to get.

“Jeeves,” I said, because I’m fond of the bloke and like to slip him a good turn when I can, “if you want to make a stack of cash, throw a few quid on Wonderchild for the Lincolnshire.”

He shook his head, not even a flicker of interest. “I’d rather not, sir.”

“But it’s the straight goods! I’m putting my entire week’s allowance, plus the spare cash I had stashed for a new fancy cummerbund, on him.”

“I do not recommend it, sir. The animal is not intended to win. Second place is all the stable is gunning for.”

Total piffle, I thought. How on earth could Jeeves know the first thing about racehorses? But you know what happened. Wonderchild led the whole way until he was practically breathing on the finish line, then some no-named nag called Banana Fritter nosed him out by a whisker. I stumbled home after, down nearly 80 quid, and rang for Jeeves immediately.

“After this,” I told him, solemn as a judge. “I don’t make a single move without your say-so. From now on, you’re the official brains of this entire establishment.”

“Very good, sir. I shall endeavour to give satisfaction.”

And by Jove, he has. My old bean was clearly built for decorative purposes, not heavy lifting, if you catch my drift. But give me five minutes to hash out any problem with Jeeves, and I’m ready to give advice to a prime minister on any topic under the sun. Which is exactly why, when Bruce “Corky” Corcoran turned up at my flat looking like he’d just hidden a body in the broom closet, my first move was to ring the bell.

“Leave it to Jeeves,” I said.

I met Corky when I first popped over to New York. He was a pal of my cousin Gussie’s, part of that whole artsy crowd hanging around Washington Square. I don’t think I ever told you, but the reason I legged it out of England was that Aunt Agatha sent me over to try and stop young Gussie from marrying a vaudeville chorus girl. I mucked the whole thing up so completely I decided sticking around New York for a bit was far smarter than going home and enduring three hours of Agatha’s quiet, frosty disappointment over tea. So I sent Jeeves out to find me a nice apartment, and settled in for a little bit of self-imposed exile. And honestly? New York’s a cracking place to be exiled in. Everyone was lovely to me, there was always something fun going on, and I’ve got more than enough cash to pay for all the fun, so everything was aces. Chappies introduced me to more chappies, and before long I knew hordes of people — some rolling in cash in fancy houses up by Central Park, others living on tinned beans and ambition down by Washington Square, all the brainy arty types you could shake a stick at.

Corky was one of those arty types. Called himself a portrait painter, but he hadn’t actually painted any portraits yet. He was camped on the sidelines, blanket over his shoulders, waiting for someone to wander along and beg him to immortalize their face on canvas. The catch with portrait painting, you see, is you can’t start painting portraits until people ask you to, and people won’t ask you to until you’ve already painted dozens of portraits. Bit of a chicken and egg problem, that. Corky scraped by drawing silly cartoons for the comic papers, and designing bedsteads and chairs for adverts, but his main source of cash was his rich uncle Alexander Worple, who’d made an absolutely indecent fortune in jute. I still don’t fully know what jute even is, but apparently everyone on Earth can’t get enough of it, because old Worple had a pile of cash so high you could climb it and see the Hudson River from the top.

Most blokes think having a rich uncle is the softest gig on the planet, but Corky swore that was a myth. His uncle was a strapping, sturdy old cove who looked like he was going to live to be 120, no effort at all. That wasn’t the bit that upset Corky, mind you — he didn’t begrudge the old chap a long life. The part that made him miserable was how Worple hounded him nonstop.

His uncle didn’t want him to be an artist. Insisted he had zero talent for it. He was forever nagging Corky to chuck painting, come join the jute business, and work his way up from the very bottom. Jute had become a full-blown obsession for the old man, like he thought it was some kind of holy relic. Corky swore he had no idea what went on at the bottom of the jute business, but every instinct he had told him it was far too ghastly to describe in polite company. Corky was dead serious about his art, too — he was sure he’d have a big hit one day, and in the meantime he’d wheedle a tiny quarterly allowance out of his uncle, using maximum tact and charm.

He never would have gotten that allowance if his uncle didn’t have a very weird hobby. Most American captains of industry, from what I’ve seen, turn their brains completely off the second they lock their office door and go home, and just sit there like a log until it’s time to go back to work the next morning. But old Worple was an ornithologist — bird nerd, basically. He’d already written a whole fat book called *American Birds*, and was halfway through a sequel he was calling *More American Birds*. Rumour had it he planned to keep writing them until every single American bird had been covered, no exceptions. Corky used to pop in to see him every three months and let him ramble about warblers and finches and cuckoos for a couple of hours, and as long as he let Worple go on about birds unimpeded, he walked away with his allowance no questions asked. But it was absolute torture for the poor bloke. Birds, apart from when they’re broiled and served with a cold bottle of champagne, bored him absolutely rigid.

To finish the character sketch of Worple: he had a temper like a lit stick of dynamite, and he was fully convinced Corky was a total idiot, and every single thing Corky did that wasn’t directly ordered by him was proof positive of that idiocy. I can’t say I blame him — I’m pretty sure Jeeves looks at me the exact same way.

Which is why, when Corky stumbled into my apartment one afternoon, herding a pretty little girl in front of him, and blurted “Bertie, meet my fiancée, Miss Singer,” the very first thought that popped into my head was exactly the problem he’d come to consult me about. The first words out of my mouth were “Corky — what about your uncle?”

The poor chap let out one of those mirthless, hollow laughs. He looked so anxious he could’ve been a man who’d pulled off the perfect murder and then realized he had nowhere to hide the corpse.

“We’re both so nervous, Mr. Wooster,” the girl said. “We were hoping you might have an idea for how to break the news to him gently.”

Muriel Singer was one of those soft, quiet, appealing girls who look at you with big wide eyes like you’re the bravest, most wonderful man who ever walked the earth, and they can’t figure out why you haven’t gone off to be a hero yet. She sat there all huddled on the edge of the couch, looking at me like she was half-convinced I might suddenly turn around and yell at her, and it made me feel all knight-errant-y, like I wanted to pat her hand and tell her no one was ever going to hurt her on my watch. She was like one of those innocent-tasting American cocktails that sneak up on you before you know it, and next thing you’re marching over to the big tough bloke in the corner and telling him if he glares at you one more time you’re going to knock his block off. I was 100% on her side, no questions asked.

“I don’t see why your uncle wouldn’t be absolutely over the moon,” I told Corky. “He’ll think Miss Singer is the perfect wife for you.”

Corky refused to cheer up. “You don’t know him. Even if he liked Muriel, he’d never admit it. He’s too stubborn. It’d be a point of principle for him to throw a fit. All he’d see is that I went and got engaged without asking his permission first, and he’d raise absolute Cain. He does it every single time I do anything without his say-so.”

I strained my old bean so hard I’m surprised I didn’t hear a creak. “You need to arrange it so he meets Miss Singer without knowing you two already know each other. Then you pop up later—”

“But how the devil do I pull that off?”

Fair point. That was the snag. “Only one thing for it,” I said.

“What?”

“Leave it to Jeeves.” And I rang the bell.

“Sir?” said Jeeves, manifesting out of thin air like he’d teleported there straight from the pantry. One of the weirdest things about Jeeves is you almost never actually see him walk into a room. He’s like those Indian magic blokes who dissolve into thin air and zip across the room as a wisp of smoke, then reassemble right where they want to be. My cousin the Theosophist swears he almost mastered that trick himself once, but he messed it up because he ate too many meat pies as a kid.

The second I saw Jeeves standing there, all quiet and respectful and oozing brains, a whole ton of stress rolled right off my shoulders. I felt like a lost seven year old who just spotted his dad across a crowded amusement park. Jeeves is tallish, with a sharp, dark face, and his eyes gleam like there’s a little lightbulb of pure genius going on behind them.

“Jeeves, we need your advice.”

“Very good, sir.”

I condensed Corky’s whole messy situation down to a few snappy sentences. “So that’s the gist of it, Jeeves. We need you to come up with a way for Mr. Worple to meet Miss Singer without figuring out she’s already engaged to Mr. Corcoran. Got it?”

“Perfectly, sir.”

“Right, go on then, think of something.”

“I have already thought of something, sir.”

“You have?!”

“The scheme cannot fail, but it does have what you might consider a small drawback, sir. It will require a certain financial outlay.”

“He means,” I translated for Corky, who looked like he was about to pass out at the words “financial outlay,” “he’s got an absolutely stellar idea, but it’s gonna cost a few bucks.”

Poor Corky’s face fell immediately, until I remembered Muriel’s big sad eyes staring at me, and I waved that off immediately. “Don’t you worry about the cash, Corky. I’ve got it covered. Only too happy to help. Carry on, Jeeves.”

“I would suggest, sir, that Mr. Corcoran take full advantage of Mr. Worple’s well-known passion for ornithology.”

“How the devil did you know he likes birds?”

“It’s the way these New York apartments are constructed, sir. Nothing like our solid London walls. The partitions between flats are paper thin. I do my best not to eavesdrop, but I’ve frequently heard Mr. Corcoran venting at great length, very colourfully, about how much he hates being forced to listen to long lectures about birds.”

“Oh, fair enough! Go on.”

“Why should the young lady not write a slim children’s volume, to be titled *The Children’s Book of American Birds*, and dedicate it to Mr. Worple? We can publish a small limited edition at your expense, sir, and a good chunk of the book will be full of glowing, gushing praise for Mr. Worple’s own famous *American Birds* treatise. We can dispatch a presentation copy straight to Mr. Worple the second it comes off the press, along with a letter where the young lady asks if she might please get to meet the great man who inspired her so much. I cannot imagine this plan failing, though as I say, the printing and writing costs will be considerable.”

I felt like a vaudeville performer whose trick dog just pulled off the most complicated stunt ever without tripping. I’d bet on Jeeves every single time, and he never lets me down. Sometimes I still can’t wrap my head around why a bloke as smart as he is hangs around pressing my trousers and making my morning tea. If I had half Jeeves’s brain, I’d run for Prime Minister or something.

“Jeeves,” I said. “That is absolutely brilliant! One of your very best ideas ever.”

“Thank you, sir.”

Muriel frowned a little. “But I’m sure I couldn’t write a whole book. I can barely write a decent birthday card.”

“Muriel’s real talent is for the stage,” Corky coughed, a little sheepish. “I didn’t mention it before, but another reason we’re terrified of telling Uncle Alexander is that Muriel’s currently in the chorus of *Choose Your Exit* over at the Manhattan Theatre. The old man’s such a prude, we’re pretty sure he’ll throw an even bigger fit if he finds out she’s on the stage.”

Oh, I got that. I still remembered the whole fuss Aunt Agatha had made back in England when I very briefly considered marrying a girl from a musical show, and the drama with Gussie. Uncles and aunts, as a group, are pathologically opposed to the theatre, no exceptions.

But of course Jeeves had that already covered. “It would be trivial to find some penniless writer who would happily bash out the full text for a very small fee. The only thing that matters is that Miss Singer’s name is on the title page.”

“Hell, that’s easy,” Corky said, lighting up. “Sam Patterson would do it for a hundred bucks. He writes three novelettes, ten short stories, and a 10,000 word serial under five different pen names every single month for the pulp magazines. A tiny little children’s book would be nothing to him. I’ll go round and see him right now.”

“Cracking.”

“Will that be all, sir?” Jeeves said. “Very good, sir. Thank you, sir.”

I used to think publishing a book required the brains of a rocket scientist, but boy was I wrong. All you have to do is sit back and write a few cheques every now and then, while a bunch of hardworking, talented blokes do all the actual work. I know that for a fact, because I was the “publisher” on this whole bird book gig. I didn’t lift a finger, and a month later a shiny, gorgeous red book showed up at the flat, covered in gold lettering.

I was over at Corky’s place when the first batch of copies turned up. Muriel was there, and we were flipping through it, when there was a bang on the door and the parcel delivery guy dumped the whole stack on the floor. It was a beauty — bright red cover, with a colourful drawing of a bird on the front, and Muriel’s name printed in fancy gold script underneath. I opened it at random to page 21, and read: “Often on a soft spring morning, as you wander through the fields, you will hear the sweet, warbling song of the purple finch. When you are older, you must read all about him in Mr. Alexander Worple’s wonderful masterpiece, *American Birds*.”

It was full of that stuff. Every two pages, there was another shoutout to old Worple, telling him he was the world’s leading expert on yellow-billed cuckoos, the greatest living authority on warblers, you name it. It was impossible for the old man not to eat that up. You can’t spend 30 pages calling someone the greatest bird genius on the planet without them immediately wanting to meet you and shake your hand.

“It’s a sure thing!” I said. “Total cinch!” Corky agreed.

I can’t cite the exact source, but I’m 99% sure it was Shakespeare (or if not, some equally brainy, top-tier wordsmith) who pointed out that the exact second a chap is feeling chuffed to the gills, all warm and fuzzy about how life’s treating him, Fate creeps up behind him and whacks him over the skull with a length of lead plumbing pipe. The man was dead right, obviously. It’s always exactly that way for me. Take the whole daffy mess with Lady Malvern and her son Wilmot, for example. Two minutes before they turned up unannounced on my doorstep, I was literally patting myself on the back for how perfectly everything was going.

It was one of those heavenly spring mornings, I’d just clambered out of an ice-cold shower, and felt spry as a two-year-old racehorse. Even better: the night before, I’d absolutely asserted my authority over Jeeves. Properly put my foot down, showed him who the actual master of the flat was. Don’t get me wrong, I don’t usually mind the bloke having opinions—his suit judgment is so sound it’s basically national treasure level—but he’d been leaning on me lately like I was a doormat with a pulse. I’d almost mutinied when he tried to talk me out of my beloved cloth-topped boots, which I adored like a pair of long-lost brothers. Then when he tried to ban my new hat? That was the last straw. He wanted me to wear that stuffy Longacre model John Drew sported, but I’d set my whole heart on the dashing Country Gentleman style some famous stage chap rocked. After a rather heated, awkward scene, I bought the Country Gentleman, and strode around the rest of the evening feeling positively manly and independent.

So there I was, in the bathroom, rubbing my spine raw with a rough towel, humming a bit of a music hall number, and trying to guess what Jeeves had laid out for breakfast, when there was a tap at the door. I cut the singing off mid-note and cracked the door an inch.
“What ho out there?”
“Lady Malvern is waiting to see you in the sitting-room, sir,” Jeeves said.
“Eh? Pull yourself together, man,” I said, severely, because I don’t abide practical jokes before I’ve had my first sip of tea. “It’s barely ten o’clock. No one’s in my sitting-room. Who even is Lady Malvern?”
“Her ladyship mentioned she disembarked from an ocean liner at dawn this morning, sir.”

That tracks, I thought. I’d landed in New York a year prior, and they’d booted me off the boat before 8 a.m., bleary-eyed and still half-convinced I’d dreamed the last three days of crossing. I pulled on my dressing gown and told Jeeves to dig out my heather-mixture lounge suit, and while I dressed, I finally placed the name.
“Got her, Jeeves! She’s a crony of my Aunt Agatha. Met her at a Sunday lunch in London once. Total terror. Writes big heavy books. Did one on social conditions in India right after she went to the Durbar.”
“Indeed, sir. Pardon me, sir—but not that tie.”
“Eh? What’s wrong with this tie? It’s a cheerful pink! Perfectly nice!”
“Too ornate for the heather mixture lounge, sir. Unsuitable.”

The air went thick. This was a crisis. If I caved now, every bit of the authority I’d won the night before would be tossed straight in the bin. I set my jaw, knotted the pink tie right there in front of him, and said “This is the tie I’m wearing, Jeeves.”
“Very good, sir.”
You could practically see the man’s feelings hurt. But I was firm. I trotted into the sitting-room, ready to face the day.

“Ah! Mr. Wooster! You’ve never met my son Wilmot, I think? Motty darling, this is Mr. Wooster.”
Lady Malvern was a hearty, loud, overpowering tank of a woman—she wasn’t very tall, but she stretched nearly six feet wide, and swallowed my biggest armchair like it was a petit four. She had bright bulging eyes, a haystack of yellow hair, and a grin that showed about 57 front teeth. The minute she turned that grin on me, I felt like a ten-year-old kid dragged into the drawing room in scratchy Sunday clothes to greet a stranger. Definitely not the sort of surprise you want on an empty stomach.
Motty, the son, was 23, tall, thin, and so meek he looked like he’d apologize to a doorknob if he bumped into it. Same yellow hair as his mum, plastered flat and parted dead center, bulging dull grey eyes with pink rims, a chin that gave up the fight halfway down his face, and zero eyelashes. He was sucking the knob of his walking stick like it was a lollipop, staring at the wall.
“Awfully glad to see you!” I said, through gritted teeth. “Staying long in New York?”
“About a month. Your Aunt Agatha gave me your address, told me you’d be delighted to help us out.”
I blinked, and felt a flicker of relief. A year prior, Aunt Agatha had shipped me off to New York to rescue Cousin Gussie from a music hall actress, and I’d somehow messed it up so thoroughly that Gussie married her and joined the vaudeville circuit. I’d been convinced I was permanently banned from England, that Aunt Agatha would be waiting on the quay for me with a stuffed eelskin to whack me over the head. Her recommending her pals drop by was a great sign, right?
“Absolutely, anything I can do!” I said.
“Wonderful! I want you to put dear Motty up for a little while.”
“Put him up for my clubs? But he looks more of a home bird—”
“No no! I don’t want him joining clubs. I mean let him live here, in your flat, while I’m away.”
The words oozed out of her like honey, like she had no idea she’d just dropped a life sentence on me. I stared at Motty, who was still gnawing his stick, and was just about to tell her no dice, lady, call a hotel, when she steamrolled right over me. She was off on a cross-country tour to visit every prison on the route, to write a new companion book to her India tome, all about American social conditions. She couldn’t take Motty, because he got terribly train sick, and he was a vegetarian teetotaller who loved nothing more than curling up with a nice book. She’d be back in a month to pick him up. Then she grabbed Motty by the elbow, dragged him off to catch her midday train, and said she’d send him back to my flat that afternoon.

The second the door shut, I yelled for Jeeves.
“Jeeves! That woman just dumped that walking-stick-sucking excrescence on me for a whole month! What are we going to do?”
“Lord Pershore will be residing here this evening, sir. Breakfast is ready.”
He was still pouting about the tie! I could’ve sobbed into my bacon and eggs. I almost caved right then, told him he could burn the tie and the Country Gentleman hat for all I cared, but my Wooster pride wouldn’t let me. If Aunt Agatha got wind I’d kicked her pal’s son out, I was a dead man. There was nothing for it but to grin and bear it.

By midday, Motty’s luggage turned up, plus a massive parcel of “nice educational books” his mum had packed for him. I brightened up a bit—surely that stack of tomes would keep him quiet for the whole month. I stuck my Country Gentleman hat on my head, gave my pink tie a jaunty tweak, and headed out to lunch with the lads at the pub down the street. We browsed and sluiced and bantered till late, and I waltzed home that night having completely forgotten about Motty.
The parcel of books was still unopened, string and all, sitting on the coffee table. Rummy, I thought, till I heard a scrabbling thud outside my front door.
“Jeeves, go see what that is,” I said.
He came back two seconds later. “If you would be so kind as to help me carry him in, sir? His lordship is lying on the doormat.”
Sure enough, there was Motty, huddled on the mat, moaning softly.
“Great Scot—he’s not drunk, is he?” I yelped.
Jeeves nodded. Turns out the second his mum had boarded that train, Motty had hotfooted it to the nearest bar, drank the entire stock of whiskey they had, ate three steaks (no vegetarian in sight), and had the time of his life till he stumbled home and passed out on my doormat. We hauled him to bed, and I sat up staring at the fire, already sensing the next four weeks were going to be absolute chaos.

Next morning, I walked into his room expecting to find a wreck, and there he was, sitting up in bed, flipping through a silly adventure story, as chipper as a sparrow.
“What ho!” he yelled at me, and we went back and forth going what ho about seven times before I could get a word in edgewise.
“How’s the head?” I asked.
“Top-hole!” he said. “Your man Jeeves made me a magical dark drink, cured my headache in two seconds. Absolute genius, that bloke. And by the way—no more of that vegetarian teetotal rubbish. I drank too much last night, and I’m going to do it every night! This is the first time I’ve ever been allowed out of the house alone. I’ve spent my whole life cooped up in Much Middlefold, Shropshire, where the most exciting thing that happens is a choirboy gets caught sucking chocolate during the sermon. I’ve got four weeks in New York, and I’m going to make every single second count. Mum told me to keep my eyes open for impressions for her book, right?”

I tried sticking close to him for one night, to act as a responsible chaperone. Never again. The bloke is a force of nature. We went out for supper, and before I knew it, he was throwing soft-boiled eggs at the electric fan, dancing on top of the table, yelling so loud we got booted out by three different waiters. I barely escaped alive at 2 a.m., and swore off ever tagging along with him again. The next week, I kept running into him getting tossed out of every dive bar in the neighborhood, flying through the air towards the sidewalk like a human cannonball. I started losing weight, jumping at every sudden noise, waiting up every night to drag him in and tuck him into bed. And Jeeves? Still moping. Still refused to give me a single idea to get out of the mess, all because of that stupid hat and pink tie.

Then the cherry on top of the chaos sundae: Motty won a bull terrier named Rollo in a raffle, tied him to the sitting room table, and didn’t tell anyone. I got home one night, and Rollo lunged straight for my trouser leg before I could even flick the light on. I screamed, fell backwards into the hall, and Jeeves strolled in, calmed the beast down instantly with that weird Jeeves magic of his, till Rollo was rolling over on his back begging for belly rubs. But the second he spotted me again, he was back to trying to chew through my trousers.
I was at my wits’ end. I’d made a terrible mistake, letting Jeeves get that cross over a hat and a tie. But I had no idea that far funnier, far more unexpected chaos was still right around the corner.

Some mornings, when I’m propped up in bed sipping that first steamy cuppa, watching my man Jeeves drift around the room laying out my threads for the day, I’ve had full-on panic attacks thinking what on earth I’d do if the bloke ever decided to quit. Things are manageable enough now I’m in New York, but back in London? The anxiety was crippling. All sorts of scummy low-lifes kept trying to poach him right out from under my nose. Young Reggie Foljambe, I know for a fact, offered him double what I was paying, and Alistair Bingham-Reeves – the chump whose own valet is famous for pressing his trousers sideways instead of down the proper crease – used to stare at Jeeves when he popped round like he was a starving man eyeing a truffle sandwich. Absolute pirates, the lot of them.

The thing is, Jeeves is so impossibly good at his job you can spot it in the tiny stuff, even the way he shoves a shirt stud in without fumbling once. I rely on him completely for every crisis, and he never ever drops the ball. Even better? He’ll always go the extra mile for any of my pals who happen to be neck-deep in trouble. Take that daft little saga of dear old Bicky and his uncle, the human hard-boiled egg, for example.

It went down a few months after I’d touched down in America. I stumbled back to the flat pretty late one night, and when Jeeves brought me that final nightcap, he dropped the news: “Mr. Bickersteth called for you twice this evening, sir, while you were out. He seemed rather agitated.”

“Pipped, you mean?” I asked, swirling my whisky around the glass. I felt bad for Bicky, but truth be told, I was glad to have a safe topic to chat about. See, things had been frosty between Jeeves and me for weeks, ever since I made the deeply controversial decision to grow a moustache. The man was gutted. He couldn’t stand the thing at any price, and I’d been living in a fog of quiet, seething disapproval ever since, which was getting extremely tedious. Don’t get me wrong – I know Jeeves’s judgment on suits and ties is 100% correct, and I’ve caved to his demands plenty of times like a compliant lamb. But a valet claiming jurisdiction over your upper lip? That’s a step too far. I’d dug my heels in, defiant as a bulldog.

“Said he’d be back later, sir,” Jeeves added. “I saw in the paper this morning that his grace the Duke of Chiswick, Mr. Bickersteth’s uncle, is arriving on the *Carmantic*.”

Wait a second. Bicky’s uncle was a duke? I’d met Bicky at a messy booze-up in Washington Square not long after I arrived, homesick and craving other English blokes to hang out with. We’d bonded over a quiet snort in the corner away from all the pretentious artists and sculptors, and he’d sealed the deal by doing the most perfect impression of a bull terrier chasing a cat up a tree I’d ever seen. Even after we got thick as thieves, all I knew about him was he was usually skint, and some uncle back home sent him a bit of cash every month to keep the lights on. I had no idea the uncle was a flipping duke.

Jeeves, naturally, knew every single detail. Bicky was the son of the duke’s late sister, who’d married a Coldstream Guards captain who died broke, no inheritance at all. And the Duke of Chiswick? Half owner of London, lord of five whole northern counties, and the cheapest man to ever draw breath. The kind of tightwad Americans call a hard-boiled egg.

Just then the doorbell rang, and Jeeves drifted out to answer it, and in trundled Bicky, looking like he’d just been dragged through a hedge backwards.

“Halloa, Bicky, old man!” I said. “Jeeves, grab another glass, let’s get this party started. What’s the trouble?”

“I’m in the soup, Bertie,” he moaned. “I need your help. My uncle’s turning up tomorrow out of the blue, wants to stay with me to skip paying for a hotel. He sent me off to some godforsaken ranch in Colorado to learn farming, right? I bailed, came to New York, told him I started a booming business here, and now he’s showing up unannounced. I can’t put him up in my crummy boarding house, the landlady serves boiled cabbage for every meal!”

I turned to Jeeves. “Got any ideas, Jeeves?”

“With your permission, sir,” he said, smooth as silk, “you might lend Mr. Bickersteth this flat for the duration of his grace’s stay. I will pose as Mr. Bickersteth’s personal valet, and you will be his house guest. His grace will be none the wiser.”

Bicky stared at him like he’d just pulled a rabbit out of a top hat. “Bertie, how does he do it? His head sticks out funny at the back, that’s what it is! All the great geniuses have lumpy heads like that.”

The next morning I hopped out of bed at the crack of dawn to be there when the duke turned up. Sure enough, not long after 9, I looked out the window and saw a top-hatted old boy throwing a full-on wobbly over his taxi fare, yelling that in London the ride would’ve cost eightpence, and the cabbie was having none of it. It was Chiswick, no two ways about it. I dragged him inside, poured him tea, and before you knew it he was fully convinced Bicky was a millionaire business tycoon, rolling in Rockefeller levels of cash. Bicky showed up ten minutes later, chuffed to bits that the con was working, and took his uncle off to see the sights, starting with Grant’s Tomb.

I waltzed back to the flat that evening to get changed for dinner, and Jeeves dropped the bomb that made my blood run cold. “The scheme has developed an unforeseen snag, sir. His grace has decided to cancel Mr. Bickersteth’s monthly allowance entirely, on the logic that a man doing so fantastically well in New York business can’t possibly need financial support from home.”

Great Scot! Poor Bicky walked in not long after, hunched in an armchair, staring at nothing, rambling madly about a get-rich-quick chicken farm scheme he’d seen in a magazine ad. “You start with one hen! It lays one egg a day, sell seven for 25 cents, no feed costs! In no time you’re wading up to your knees in hens, all making you money! But I don’t have the five bucks startup cash, and I’m not borrowing from you, Bertie, I refuse to owe my pal money.”

I turned desperately to Jeeves. “Come on, Jeeves, we’ve got to fix this.”

“Ah, sir,” he said, calm as ever. “It just occurred to me that his grace’s status as a famous British duke is a very valuable untapped resource. Americans are obsessed with meeting nobility. I’m certain plenty of people would happily pay two or three dollars for the privilege of shaking his hand and getting an introduction.”

We tried it for a whole week, and it was a total flop. The only person we lined up was a deli owner who wanted to pay us entirely in sliced ham, and an anarchist bloke who ran a pawnbroker shop who offered ten bucks just to kick the old duke in the shin. Bicky was this close to taking him up on it. We were about to call the whole thing off, when Jeeves drifted in with my morning tea the next day, calm as you please.

“Might I speak to you about the his grace project, sir? I’ve arranged the interested parties.”

“Wait,” I said, sitting straight up and spilling tea all over my duvet. “You got people to pay? How many?”

“Eighty-seven gentlemen, sir. From Birdsburg, Missouri.”

Let me tell you the full saga of old pal Bobbie Cardew, and I swear on the Drones Club’s last remaining plate of sausage rolls it’s the most instructive yarn you’ll hear all year. No fancy literary frills needed here, either—this thing’s got a moral so thick you could spread it on toast. If you’re a bloke, you’ll hang on every word, it’s a top-tier warning against being a total chump. If you’re a lady? You’ll eat it up, because it’s all about how one sharp, determined girl made a grown man run around London like a headless chicken chasing his own lost hat.

Fellows who’ve only met Bobbie in the last couple years will gape if you tell them there was a time his memory was so bad it made a goldfish look like a walking encyclopedia. But it’s gospel. Cross my heart.

Back when I first knew him, Bobbie was the gold standard for silly asses inside London’s four-mile radius. Folks have called me a bit of a berk now and then, but I was never in his weight class. When it came to being a total ass, Bobbie was a plus-four handicap pro, and I was still fumbling at a six. Why, if I wanted him to show up to a dinner I was hosting, I’d post him a reminder note on Monday, send a telegram on Wednesday, ring him up three times on the actual day, then dispatch a taxi courier half an hour before kickoff to physically frog-march him to the restaurant and double-check the chauffeur had the address written in ink on his wrist. Even that only worked if he hadn’t wandered off to Brighton by accident before the messenger knocked.

The daft thing was, he wasn’t a total fool. Buried deep under all the fluff there was a tiny little nugget of common sense. I’d seen him show actual human intelligence once or twice. Mind you, you’d have needed a full stick of dynamite to blast through the layers to get to it.

Or so I thought. Turns out there was a far simpler trick: marriage. The great soul dynamite, that’s what hit old Bobbie. He fell in love, bumbled straight down the aisle whooping like he’d just won the grand national at Ascot, and only then started noticing the fine print on the whole business. You ever seen a squishy bull pup spot a bumblebee and decide it looks like the best snack ever invented, right up till the moment the thing stings him square on the nose? That was Bobbie.

She wasn’t the sort of girl you’d peg as Bobbie’s type, not at first glance. Her name was Mary Anthony, orphan, worked for a living as a hospital nurse, stood five foot six, had a ton and a half of sun-ripened red gold hair, sharp grey eyes, and a chin so determined you could crack a walnut on it. Fate stepped in when Bobbie smashed his collarbone playing polo, and Mary got assigned to douse his bumps with cooling unguents and pat his hand when he whined. By the time the fool could hobble around again, they’d snuck off to the registrar’s office and eloped. Proper little romantic picture, what?

He broke the news to me at the club one evening, and introduced me to her the next day. I liked her. Name’s Reggie Pepper, by the way—nearly forgot to mention it. Uncle Edward ran Pepper, Wells and Co., the colliery lot, and left me enough loose cash that I never had to work a day in my life, but I tip my hat to anyone who makes their own way in the world, especially a girl who’s had to fend for herself since she was knee-high. Mary and I got on like a house on fire at first, even though things went pear-shaped later, as you’ll hear. She looked at Bobbie like he hung the moon, and Bobbie stared at her like she was the best thing that ever happened to any man, so I figured if the idiot didn’t forget to show up to the actual wedding, they might have a decent shot at happiness.

Skip forward a year, that’s where the fun really starts.

They’d set up in a nice little flat, and I popped in all the time. Everything seemed smooth as butter, and I started to think all that guff about marriage being a trap for chaps was just old codgers whining. Then came the incident of the Unannounced Quiet Dinner, and that’s where love’s young dream tripped over a loose rug and face-planted straight into the jam pot.

I ran into Bobbie in Piccadilly, and he insisted I come back to the flat for dinner. Like a complete chump, I didn’t sprint the other way and throw myself under police protection. I went.

When we walked in, there was Mrs. Bobbie dolled up to the nines: her hair all coiled into perfect waves, a sparkly diamond doo-dad pinned in it, wearing a frock so gorgeous it made my eyes water. I thought, blimey, if she dresses like this for random quiet Tuesday dinners, no wonder Bobbie’s mad for domestic life.

“Hey, look, it’s old Reggie!” Bobbie announced, cheerfully as you like. “I dragged him home for a bite. I’ll ring the kitchen now and tell ‘em to send dinner up, yeah?”

Mary stared at him like he’d just grown a second head, went bright scarlet, then white as a sheet, then let out a tight little laugh that made me wish I was perched in a tree 800 miles away in Borneo. She pulled herself together fast, smiled at me, said she was delighted I could make it.

She chattered through dinner, teased Bobbie, even played ragtime on the piano afterwards, and if you didn’t know better you’d have thought it was the jolliest little party ever thrown. But I’d seen that look on her face earlier. She was white-knuckling it so hard you could almost see the strain, and I swear she would’ve traded that diamond hair pin and every single thing she owned for one good, full-throated scream. I’ve sat through some brutal evenings in my time, but this one lapped the field. I grabbed my hat and bolted the second I decently could.

So I wasn’t even a little surprised to find Bobbie moping at the club the next day, looking about as cheery as a lone gumdrop left at an Eskimo tea party. He cornered me straight away, clearly dying to spill.

“You know how long I’ve been married?” he said.

“Roughly a year, isn’t it?” I said.

“Not roughly,” he said, glum as wet week. “Exactly a year. Yesterday.”

The light hit me like a brick. Oh. Yesterday was their wedding anniversary.

“Right,” he groaned. “I’d booked a table at the Savoy, and a box at Covent Garden so she could hear Caruso sing, the ticket was still in my pocket! All through dinner last night I had this weird niggling feeling I’d forgotten something, but I couldn’t for the life of me pin down what it was. Till Mary mentioned it.”

He didn’t elaborate, and I didn’t ask. Girls with that kind of determined chin and red gold hair are angels 99% of the time, but when they take their wings off for half an hour, they don’t do anything by halves.

“To be perfectly honest, old top,” he mumbled, staring into his whisky, “my stock at home is lower than a snake’s belly.”

There wasn’t much to do, so I lit a cigarette and let him sit there. He wandered off eventually, and I leaned out the club’s smoking room window that overlooks Piccadilly, watched him trudge along the pavement, stop, hesitate, then duck straight into a jeweler’s. Told you there was that little nugget of common sense buried in him somewhere.

That’s when I got properly invested in the Bobbie Cardew problem. The average bloke isn’t Bobbie, and the average girl isn’t Mary. It was the classic unstoppable force meeting immovable object: Bobbie ambling through life, sweet as a pudding but a grade-A chump, and Mary dead set on knocking the chumpitude out of him. But old Mother Nature was on Bobbie’s side, you see. When Nature churns out a chump like Bobbie, she’s proud of her work, and she outfits him with custom armor to stop anyone messing it up. That armor? A memory like a sieve. That’s the exact thing that keeps a man a harmless chump instead of growing into a responsible adult. Take me, for example: I’m a chump too, but if I’d remembered half the stuff people tried to drum into my head growing up, my hat size would be a nine. I forgot it all. Same for Bobbie.

For a whole week after that botched anniversary dinner, he was sharper than an elephant with a photographic memory. But the shock hadn’t dented his sieve-armor hard enough to punch a hole through, and sure enough, he drifted right back to his old tricks. It was pathetic, really. Mary adored him, and she was terrified. She knew what came next: a man who forgets his first wedding anniversary will have completely forgotten he’s married at all by year four. She had to break the memory bad habit right then, before he drifted off entirely.

I tried to talk sense into him one afternoon when he turned up moaning about getting in hot water for forgetting to bring home a book she’d asked for. “It’s such a stupid little thing to make a fuss over!” he whined. “My memory’s absolute rubbish, I never can remember anything.”

He was heading out the door when he fished two sovereigns out of his pocket and handed them over. “Oh, right, I owe you this. The bet we made Tuesday at the billiards room, Murray and Brown were playing a hundred up, I gave you two to one Brown would win, and Murray thrashed him by 20 points.”

“Hang on,” I said, squinting at him. “So you *can* remember stuff?”

He got so indignant he nearly choked on his stick. Said if I thought he was the sort of rotter who welched on a bet, that was a rotten thing to say after knowing him all these years, carrying on like I’d accused him of pinching the club’s silver spoons.

“Calm down, laddie,” I said, and gave him the father of all pep talks. “What you need to do is pull yourself together, sharpish. You’re heading for a massive wallop you won’t see coming. You can remember stuff, you just proved it with that two quid. All you’ve got to do is make sure wedding anniversaries and your wife’s birthday make it onto the list. It might hurt your brain, but you’ve got no choice.”

“But why do women care about all these daft little dates?” he groaned. “It doesn’t change the fact I love her! I could forget every single one and still be just as fond of her as if I was one of those stage memory-freaks who memorizes the whole phone book for a shilling a go.”

“Doesn’t work that way,” I told him. “Ladies need you to show you care, not just feel it. Get that through your thick skull, and you’re golden. Forget it, and you’re in for trouble.”

He chewed the knob of his stick for a minute, looking glum. “Women are completely barmy,” he said.

“Should’ve thought of that before you married one,” I said.

I thought I’d laid it all out for him nice and clear. Surely he’d buckle down and sort his act out? Nope. Off he went, back to his old scatterbrained self. I gave up arguing. I had plenty of free time, but not enough to waste trying to reform Bobbie Cardew with words. If you see a bloke marching straight towards trouble insisting he wants to meet it, the only sensible thing to do is lean against the wall and wait for the inevitable.

It took months for the thunderbolt to land. I was leaning against that exact same club window one morning, staring out at the buses trundling up Piccadilly, when Bobbie came barrelling in, eyes bulging, face the color of a raw oyster, waving a scrap of paper like he was fending off a swarm of bees.

“Reggie! Reggie old top! She’s gone! Mary’s left me!”

“Gone where?” I said.

Silly question, I know. He nearly foamed at the mouth. “How the devil should I know where? Read this!”

I took the note out of his shaking hand. It was short, no beating around the bush:
> I am going away. When you care enough about me to remember to wish me many happy returns on my birthday, I will come back. My address will be Box 341, London Morning News.

I read it twice, looked up, and said “Well, then just wish her many happy returns. It’s hardly rocket science.”

“But she says *on* her birthday!” he wailed.

“Right. When is her birthday, then?”

He stared at me. “I forgot.”

“Forgotten what, exactly? Forgotten if it’s the 20th or 21st? You must be close, right?”

“All I know is it falls somewhere between January 1st and December 31st. That’s as far as I got.”

We brainstormed. We tried to dig up what the weather was like on her last birthday—could’ve been warmish, could’ve been cold, he couldn’t remember. We tried to remember what present he’d bought her for it. Couldn’t recall that either.

Then he had a brilliant idea. You know those little four-and-a-half-pence pamphlets called *When Were You Born?* one for each month, that tell you what your personality is? He wanted to buy all 12, go through every single one, match the personality description to Mary, and work out what month her birthday was. Brilliant! Except… every single pamphlet described her perfectly. December people kept secrets and loved traveling? Check. October people had original ideas and liked moving? Check. February people had photographic memories and were dead set on getting their own way? That was Mary to a T. We tore the whole stack up in a rage and threw the confetti out of the taxi window.

Then he had another eureka moment: he remembered they’d gone to a matinee at the Coliseum for her birthday, and one of the acts was Professor What’s-His-Name’s famous Terpsichorean Cats. We trundled down to the theater, dug through their old archives, and found the Cats were performing for one week in mid-May. Then I remembered I’d lunched with them that exact day at the Ritz, and Bobbie had forgotten his wallet so he’d written a ten-pound cheque to the hotel. He raced home, flipped through his chequebook, and found the cheque was dated May 8th.

I was in bed half asleep at 2 a.m. when the phone rang. It was Bobbie, who’d just worked it out for himself. I hung up on him, then immediately rang the little hotel near the Strand where Mary was staying. The night desk clerk complained it was 2 a.m., I told him to hurry up or I’d send Bobbie around to forget to pay his bill.

“Hello?” Mary’s voice, sleepy.

“Mrs. Cardew? It’s Reggie Pepper. Bobbie remembered. He remembered your birthday is May 8th.”

She gave a little gasp that made the line crackle. “He did? Was he worried? Was he very, very worried?”

I chuckled. The switchboard operator was definitely leaning in listening, poor thing, getting the best gossip of her whole career. “Worried? He’s been worrying so hard he’s worn his brain right down to the bone, Mary. He hasn’t stopped thinking for a single second since you left that note. The man’s a changed chump—er, man. Total reformed character.”

Outside the window, a bus trundled past Piccadilly. Somewhere, I knew, Bobbie was already sprinting out the door with a armful of flowers, and all was well in the world.

I don’t want to wear out your patience, what? None of that ponderous literary tosh, I promise. I’m no wizard of the pen, mind, but I’ve a chappie who writes light romances tucked away on my Christmas card list, and I’ll get him to buff this whole thing up later so it reads like something out of a proper book.

Old Freddie Meadowes has been my best pal since we got caught tucking a frog in the headmaster’s teapot at Eton, so you can imagine my alarm when I trundled into the club one fine morning and found him hunched in the darkest corner, staring at his empty whiskey glass like it’d personally insulted his grandmother. Normally that bloke’s the life of every party—does a dead-on impression of a parrot arguing with a street musician, top-hole at charades, never lets the fun flag. That day he looked like the last leftover scone at a 7 AM breakfast, all squashed and lifeless.

Jimmy Pinkerton was with me, you know Jimmy, the playwright who churns out farces that make West End crowds snort lemonade out their noses. We sat down, plied him with two more whiskeys, and pried the story out of him.

Naturally, it was a girl. Angela West, his fiancée, had called the whole thing off. Wouldn’t take his calls, sent every letter he posted back unopened, slipped across the street when she saw him coming. Freddie wouldn’t say exactly what the row was about, but it was clear she’d had her fill of him.

My heart ached for the poor chump. I know that exact sinking feeling—once I was head over heels for Elizabeth Shoolbred, who told me to my face I was “the sort of man who’d forget his own head if it wasn’t glued on” and went off to marry a golf pro. I knew exactly what Freddie needed.

“Change of scene, old scout,” I told him. “I’ve taken a little cottage down at Marvis Bay. Jimmy’s joining us on the 24th. We’ll lark about on the beach, no awkward run-ins, no nothing. You’ll be right as rain in a fortnight.”

“Couldn’t be sounder,” Jimmy chipped in. “I knew a chap once whose girl dumped him, he went off to Egypt for two months, and by the time he came back she was wiring him begging to come home. Trouble was, he’d forgotten her surname, so he never even answered.”

Freddie wasn’t buying it, but he tagged along anyway, said he might as well mope in Dorset as in London.

Marvis Bay’s no Monte Carlo, let’s be honest. It’s the sort of place where the most exciting event of the week is the vicar’s cat getting stuck up the oak tree. You spend all day splashing about in the briny or dozing on the sand, then the evening gets eaten alive by gnats so fierce they’d bite through a woolen jumper if you stood still long enough. You get back to the cottage at 9, slather calamine lotion all over the bites, and crash.

At first Freddie lapped it up. The second the moon came up he was trotting down to the beach, and the gnats adopted him as their official patron. They’d buzz past perfectly nice passersby without a second glance, saving all their stinging energy for old Freddie’s exposed ankles.

Peaceful as it was, by the end of the first week I was dying for Jimmy to show up. Freddie wasn’t exactly the ideal houseguest. Half the time he was glowering at the carpet smoking his pipe so thick you couldn’t see the mantelpiece, the other half he was prodding the cottage piano with one finger, bashing out *The Rosary*. He couldn’t play a single other note, and every single time he got to the third bar he’d mess up and have to start all over again.

He was mid-tune one morning when I got back from a dip. His voice was so hollow it echoed.
“Reggie,” he said. “I’ve seen her.”
“Angela? Out here?”
“Ran straight into her at the post office door. She cut me dead. Looked right through me like I was a pane of glass.” He banged the piano key again, messed up the second bar this time. “We ought to leave. Now.”

“Rot,” I said. “This is the best possible thing that could have happened. You’ve got a second chance right here. All you need to do is get her to owe you a favour, right? Make her all soft and grateful, and she’ll forget the whole quarrel.”
“Grateful for what?”
“Save her from drowning,” I said, quick as a flash.
“Can’t swim.”

Typical Freddie. Top bloke, can polo like a champion, but zero initiative when you need it. He went back to hammering the piano, and I legged it out the door to pace the sands and think. I was rounding a pile of jagged rocks when I spotted that blue dress—sixteen photos of her plastered all over Freddie’s bedroom wall, I’d recognise Angela West anywhere. She was sat on the sand building sandcastles with a round, jolly-looking blob of a kid, and an elderly aunt was dozing on a deck chair a few feet off. I heard her call the kid “Tootles,” and he was the sort of chubby bulging toddler who looks like he’s been inflated with a bicycle pump.

After the third sandcastle got washed away, Tootles got bored and wandered off, smacking a jellyfish with his spade. No one else was in sight. And that’s when the brainwave hit me. It was so brilliant I almost smacked my own forehead. Even Sherlock Holmes wouldn’t have thought of this one. I’d grab Tootles for half an hour, right? Panic Angela half out of her wits, then send Freddie trotting up leading the little nipper by the hand, telling her he’d found the poor lost toddler wandering near the dangerous rocks, saved him from falling in the sea. She’d be so overwhelmed with gratitude she’d forgive Freddie every single thing he ever did. The reconciliation would be soft, and warm, and there’d be no more broken engagements.

I picked Tootles up, slung him over my shoulder, and beat it back to the cottage. He started wailing so loud the windows rattled the second we got over the threshold. Freddie stared at him like I’d just brought home a stray alligator.
“Reggie,” he said, “what in the name of sanity have you done?”

I grabbed a jar of honey from the kitchen, slathered a spoonful on it, and the wailing stopped dead. Tootles stuck his whole face in the jar, quiet as a mouse. I laid out my genius plan to Freddie, and slowly the light dawned.
“You’re not quite as big an idiot as you look sometimes, Pepper,” he said, and hauled Tootles out of the honey and dragged him off to find Angela.

I leaned back on the veranda smoking my cigarette, feeling like the cleverest man alive. Any second now Freddie would be back, engaged again, all sunshine and roses. Then I spotted them trundling back down the road ten minutes later, and Freddie looked like he’d just had his puppy run over.
“Couldn’t find her?” I said.
“Found her all right,” he said, with a hollow, bitter laugh. “Reggie, you complete and utter chump. That child isn’t her cousin. She’s never met him before in her life. She just stopped to help a random stranger’s kid on the beach. She looked at me like I was a certified lunatic rambling about rescuing some kid she’d never seen five minutes prior. I looked like a bigger fool than that American bloke who claimed he’d reached the North Pole. And now I’m stuck with him.”

Turns out I’d become an accidental kidnapper. I traipsed all over Marvis Bay with Tootles for hours asking after his parents, and no one claimed him—you’d have thought he was a lone hiker with his own little rented bungalow, for all the fuss anyone made. Finally the sweet stall guy told me he was Tootles Medwin, his family lived at Ocean Rest on Beach Road.

I knocked on the door, and no one came. Then a round pink face with grey whiskers popped out the upstairs window.
“Hi!” he yelled.

I yelled back that I’d brought Tootles home. He peered at me, asked if I was related to Edward Pepper the coal mine owner, who was my uncle.
“Old Ted! Great pal of mine!” he whooped. “Perfect timing! We’ve got the mumps in the house. Our daughter Bootles has just come down with it, we can’t risk Tootles catching it. We’ve been tearing our hair out trying to find someone to look after him till my brother can come from London to pick him up. You’re the very man! You’ll look after him for a week, won’t you? I’ll pay you handsomely!”

Before I could say I’d rather house a nest of vipers, he’d slammed the window shut. Five seconds later he popped back up, dropped a whole bag of breakfast cereal on my head, yelled “Make sure he gets Bailey’s Granulated Chips for breakfast!” and vanished again.

I dragged Tootles back towards the cottage, and who should we run into but Angela. Tootles yelped, pointed at her, and waddled over.
“Oh, hello little one,” she said, smiling, then turned to me. “You’re his daddy, aren’t you? We had such fun building sandcastles this morning.”

She was gone before I could even splutter a denial. Freddie heard the story and went so purple in the face I thought he’d have a stroke. He ranted for ten solid minutes, using words I didn’t even know he knew. I calmed him down eventually, by reminding him we were old school pals, he owed me ten quid, and I’d done the whole daft thing for his sake. He agreed not to run off back to London, but he refused point blank to be grateful.

We bribed Tootles with a mountain of sweets to keep him quiet the rest of the day, fumbled him out of his clothes when he passed out on the sofa, and carted him up to bed. The next morning we borrowed the nurse from the bungalow next door, who dressed Tootles in eight minutes flat. We were just starting to think things might be manageable when Jimmy Pinkerton turned up, hauling a suitcase. He spotted Tootles mid-yell, and backed towards the door.
“Hotel for me,” he said. “Can’t write a word with that racket going on. Whose infant is this?”

“This is perfect,” he said. “This is a West End farce, ready made. We don’t even need to write it. All we have to do is coach little Tootles to run up to Angela the next time we see her, and yell ‘Kiss Freddie!’ She’ll be so charmed, she’ll forget the whole quarrel and fall back into his arms. It’s foolproof!”

Half the time he’d yell “Kick Freddie!” instead, which would have torpedoed the whole plan. Jimmy said we weren’t ready, we needed another day of rehearsals.

Fate, of course, had other plans. The next afternoon, Freddie was sat at the piano, bashing away at *The Rosary*, and I was just herding Tootles out onto the veranda, when Angela came strolling up the path.
“Good morning,” she said, grinning at Tootles. “Hope you don’t mind if I pop in? My aunt’s been trying to find a piano tuner for weeks, and I can hear yours working away. I’ll just pop in and ask him to stop by our bungalow when he’s done.”

I froze. Six feet away, Freddie was still hammering that piano, blissfully unaware. There was no way to stop her. She sat down on the veranda step, chatting to Tootles, and announced she’d wait right there till the tuner was done. I babbled something about going down to the beach, but she didn’t hear me. She was digging in her pocket for something.
“The beach,” I babbled.

If you ever tally up the silliest, most deranged scrapes I’ve stumbled into over a lifetime dedicated to poking my nose firmly into other people’s business, that Monte Carlo business with old George Lattaker lands near the very top. I wouldn’t bore you for the world, obviously, but this one’s too good not to share.

We’d drifted into Monaco harbour on *Circe*, the fancy yacht belonging to old sport Marshall, a bloke who kept a cellar of champagne so good you’d forget all your troubles before you finished the first glass. The crew of chaos on board? Me, my valet Voules (the human statue who normally speaks like he’s narrating a royal funeral), Mrs. Vanderley who once waltzed with a prince in Vienna, her daughter Stella who could make a bloke forget every other girl he’d ever met, Mrs. V’s maid Pilbeam, and my oldest pal George.

George was only on the trip because I’d wangled him an invite, see. He’d just turned 25, which meant his trustee Uncle Augustus was finally supposed to hand over a fat, juicy legacy left to him by an aunt he barely remembered. Not just the measly little allowance he scraped by on every month — an actual pile of goblins, the kind you can blow on fast cars and fancy cocktails without counting the pennies. Uncle Gus was already in Monte Carlo, so I’d talked George out of making the boring trip to London to collect the cash. Kill two birds with one stone, I said: sort out your fortune and get a free holiday in the sun. No downside. Or so I thought.

The whole mess kicked off at 7 a.m. sharp, when I was jolted out of a perfectly dreamless sleep by a full-on brawl outside my stateroom door. One voice was a woman’s, sobbing and wailing “Oh Harold!”, the other a man’s, roaring so loud the porthole rattled, that I eventually — after a few moments of squinting — placed as Voules. Voules! The guy who spent all day ironing my trouser creases with the solemn air of a high priest! I yelled his name, and the whole fight cut out dead like someone hit a pause button. A few seconds later, the sobs receded down the corridor, and there was a demure little tap at my door. Voules stepped in, back to his usual unflappable, my-lord-the-carriage-waits act, and you’d never have guessed he’d just been screaming blue murder at a lady half an hour earlier. He apologized, said he’d been “breaking our engagement” with Pilbeam in the heat of the moment. I gaped. Who knew Voules even had a private life? I decided not to prod, sent him off to fetch me a solo breakfast, and trundled up on deck to smoke after.

It was the sort of morning that makes you forget all your troubles: blue sea glinting, Casino gleaming in the sun, not a cloud in the sky. Stella was one of the first up, looking pale and peaky, said she’d slept like a sack of potatoes dropped down a staircase. I asked if she’d seen George, and the name seemed to freeze her stiff. Queer, that — all voyage long they’d been glued at the hip, I’d been fully expecting George to corner me any minute, bashful as a schoolboy, to whisper that he’d popped the question and she’d said yes. She said she hadn’t seen him, and I didn’t press. Clearly George was in the doghouse that morning, for some reason.

Five minutes later the morning papers turned up, and Mrs. Vanderley let out a shriek loud enough to scare the seagulls off the rigging. The Serene Highness the Prince of Saxburg-Leignitz — the guy I always wonder why they call “Serene” when he looks like he eats pickled onions for breakfast — had been bashed over the head in a dark alley the night before on his way home from the Casino. He’d been found beaten half senseless by a pedestrian, who turned out to be Denman Sturgis, the famous Chicago private detective who’d solved that big kidnapping case a few years back. He’d volunteered to help the local police, and swore he had a very important clue.

Just then a boat pulled up alongside the yacht, and a lanky, gimlet-eyed bloke climbed the gangway, asking for George Lattaker. Marshall yelled at me to go fetch him. I banged on George’s stateroom door, no answer. Opened it, the room was empty. The bunk hadn’t even been slept in. Where the devil had he got to? I trundled back on deck, reported he was missing, and everyone trooped off to breakfast — including the detective, who Marshall insisted stay for kippers while he waited for George to turn up.

I was left alone on deck smoking, when I heard a hoarse, panicky whisper go “Reggie!” from the direction of the gangway. I turned, and there was dear old George, still in his fancy evening dress, covered in dust from head to toe, no hat, looking like he’d just run a marathon chased by angry street vendors. He shushed me, collapsed into a chair, and confessed that he thought he’d murdered the prince.

Turns out he’d proposed to Stella the night before, and she’d said yes! Just as he was about to kiss her, he’d heard footsteps coming down the corridor, stepped out of the saloon to avoid being caught mid-snog, and ran straight into Pilbeam. He was so giddy with joy he wasn’t thinking straight, grabbed the nearest human and kissed them. Which was Pilbeam. And of course, Stella walked out the saloon door right that second. She’d gone purple with rage, broken off the engagement before he could even stammer an explanation, and poor old George, distraught, stole the dinghy, rowed ashore, and proceeded to get properly trollied to drown his sorrows. Next thing he knew, he was brawling in a dark alley, someone toppled over, he legged it, and woke up that morning on a park bench in the Casino gardens, no hat, no memory of the fight.

The penny dropped. He’d dropped his hat in the brawl, and it had his full name stitched inside the band. That clue the detective was bragging about? It was his hat. The detective was Sturgis, and he was eating kippers in the saloon right now, waiting to arrest him. George begged me for cash to bolt across the border into Italy before he got dragged off to a French prison for lèse-majesté, which is apparently way worse than hitting a policeman.

Just then Voules popped up the companionway, carrying a letter for George on a tray. George tore it open, and gaped. It was from Uncle Augustus. Turns out, surprise! He had a long-lost twin brother Alfred, kidnapped when they were both babies, who’d just tracked him down after 25 years living in Buenos Aires. Uncle Gus said he couldn’t hand over the legacy till he sorted out who was entitled to what, which would take ages of checking credentials.

That’s when the most brilliant brainwave I’ve ever had in my life (admittedly, a low bar) hit me. Twins! They’re identical! All George had to do was pretend to be Alfred, the long-lost brother, not George! Sturgis was looking for *George Lattaker*, the mugger. If he was Alfred, he wasn’t the guy who bashed the prince. We were home free! Even better: as Alfred, he could charm Stella into listening to his side of the stupid kiss story, patch up their engagement.

We spun around, and Voules was standing right behind us. He’d heard every single word. We haggled. The man haggled harder than a street vendor selling fake silk scarves in the market. Two hundred pounds total, half now, half when we sailed back to England, and he’d keep his mouth shut. We shook hands on it.

I sold the whole wild twin yarn to Marshall and the rest when they came back on deck. George popped up ten minutes later, all cleaned up in a crisp yachting suit, even showed everyone the little mole on the back of his neck that “George” supposedly didn’t have. Everyone bought it hook, line, and sinker. Sturgis the detective never even suspected a thing, the poor bloke. George spent the whole morning chatting to Stella on the foredeck, and by lunchtime he was grinning from ear to ear: she’d forgiven him, they were engaged again, all the silly mess sorted.

Then a boat pulled up, and a stout red-faced guy trundled up the gangway, calling “Hello George! Got my letter?” George did his best polite stranger act, said he was Alfred, not George. The old man dragged him off for a private chat, and ten minutes later George came back looking like he’d seen a ghost.

There was no long-lost Alfred. No legacy. The old crook his Uncle Augustus had blown every single penny of the trust fund on the Casino tables years before. The whole twin brother letter was a scam he’d made up to buy enough time to bolt for South America, never to be seen again. No fortune, no cash, nothing.

We stood there staring at each other for a minute. Sure, we were broke, but at least George wasn’t in a Monte Carlo prison bashing up a Serene Highness. That counts as a win, right? I told him as much. He didn’t look convinced.

Ever sat around, really chewing over the staggering, unapologetic cheek of Woman as a whole? I don’t mean half-heartedly nodding at a throwaway joke about your gal swiping your last slice of bacon, I mean proper sit-down deep dive on the sheer unmitigated gall they walk around with, like they invented the rulebook for chutzpah. I have, by Jove! And the time that really drove the point home, that cemented it forever in my old nut, was the whole daft business of the Yeardsley “Venus.”

To get the full absurdity of the mess, I’ve got to backtrack a bit to when I knew Elizabeth Shoolbred. Old Worcestershire family, pots of money, pretty as a postcard, and her brother Bill was my Oxford mate. I was head over heels for her, don’t you know. For exactly one whole week we were engaged to be married. I’d already started poring over furniture catalogues, and got all solemn-faced every time the restaurant orchestra struck up “The Wedding Glide” — then, bang, she calls the whole thing off, marries some bloke named Clarence Yeardsley a month later. Artist, apparently.

A few rounds of golf, endless billiards, a spot of the races, and the lads at the club dragging me out to pubs every night patched me up pretty sharp. I wrote the whole thing off as a closed page in the life of Reggie Pepper, figured we’d never cross paths again, what with her and Clarence shacking up somewhere in the country and never venturing up to London. By the time her letter popped out of the blue one morning, the old heart wound was fully healed, and I was jolly grateful the whole thing had fizzled out the way it did.

The letter went like this:
> MY DEAR OLD REGGIE, — It feels centuries since I saw you last! How are you? We’ve settled down in the coziest old house, with a gorgeous garden, slap bang in the nicest stretch of countryside you can imagine. Couldn’t you run down for a few days? Clarence and I would be over the moon to have you. Bill’s staying here too, and he was gassing about you first thing this morning. *Do* come! Wire your train, and I’ll send the car to meet you.
> Yours sincerely, ELIZABETH YEarDSLEY
> P.S. We’ve got new milk and fresh eggs! Think of that!
> P.P.S. Bill swears our billiard table is one of the best he’s ever played on.
> P.P.S.S. We’re half a mile from a golf course. Bill says it’s better than St. Andrews.
> P.P.S.S.S. You *have* to come!

I’d got a bit of a fuzzy head from the club’s previous night’s festivities, and that letter rattled me a bit, I won’t lie. But that line about the golf course? That sold me. Bill’s a golf fiend, he knows what he’s talking about, so if he said the links was that good, it must be something special. So off I went.

Bill met me at the station driving the car, looking like he was one step away from throwing himself under the next train. “Thank goodness you’re here,” he groaned as we pulled out. “I was just about at my last gasp.”
“What’s the trouble, old scout?” I asked.
“If I didn’t get the pip every time someone so much as breathed the word ‘art,’ it wouldn’t be so bad. But this entire household is mad for pictures. Clarence is an artist, his dad’s an artist, and you know what Elizabeth’s like once you let her run off on a hobby?”

Oh, right! I’d forgotten. Back when we were engaged, half our dates were spent trundling through picture galleries, me trailing behind her like a lost spaniel even though paintings bore me stiff. I’d assumed after marrying a professional artist she’d have got her fill of the stuff by now. Fat chance, apparently.
“They natter about art at every meal,” he said. “Makes you feel like a right lemon, sat there with nothing to chip in. How long are you down for?”
“A few days, but hey — you said the golf course’s better than St. Andrews!”
Bill swiveled to stare at me, nearly driving the car straight into the ditch. “She didn’t forget to add the bit where I said ‘I don’t think’ before that line, did she? It’s the worst, most waterlogged, pock-marked dump of a links in the whole of Great Britain.”

My heart sank. I can’t go more than a day without my golf fix when I’m out of London, what’s a bloke supposed to do now?
“Fine, we’ll just play billiards then!” I said. “You said the table’s top tier.”
“Depends what you call top tier. It’s half-size, there’s a seven-inch gash right out of baulk where Clarence’s cue slipped, and Elizabeth mended it with pink silk. Looks very posh, but it doesn’t help your break run at all.”

Oh. She’d properly pulled my leg, then. We pulled up the drive to their big dark old house, and I suddenly had that horrible creepy feeling you get in penny dreadfuls, where some chump gets lured out to a remote manor for nefarious purposes and hears a distant shriek the second he steps through the gate. Elizabeth knew golf was my weak spot, she’d deliberately baited the trap. What was the game? My blood ran cold — she must have some unmarried cousin or friend staying, and she was planning to force me down the aisle with her. Married women are obsessed with that sort of thing, everyone knows it.
“Bill, old scout — there aren’t any frightful unmarried girls hanging around the place, are there?”
“Wish there were,” he grumbled. “No such luck.”

The front door swung open just then, and Elizabeth’s silhouette stood in the doorway. “Did you get him, Bill?” she called. That line hit me as right creepy, like something Lady Macbeth would have hissed at her husband right after stabbing the king. But it was just Elizabeth, looking exactly as pretty and sharp as I remembered her, shepherding me inside for tea.

Then Clarence popped out of nowhere, and I had that exact familiar sinking feeling you get when you meet the guy your ex dumped you for. You always think “wait, no, she can’t have picked him over me, that makes no sense” — and that’s exactly what I thought when I laid eyes on Clarence. Small, twitchy, thin little bloke pushing 35, hair going grey at the temples, pince-nez perched on his nose, droopy moustache. I’m no heavyweight boxer, but next to him I felt like a prize fighter. Elizabeth’s a tall, regal sort of girl, looks like a princess. Women really do make daft choices just out of pure cussedness, I swear.

“How do you do, Mr. Pepper? Hark — can you hear a mewing cat?” he babbled all in one breath.
“Eh?” I said.
“A mewing cat! I swear I hear a mewing cat, listen!”
The front door creaked open right then, and a white-haired old gentleman trundled in — same weedy, twitchy build as Clarence, the earlier vintage model of the same car. That was old Mr Yeardsley, Clarence’s dad, obviously.
“Father! Did you pass a mewing cat outside?” Clarence yelped. “I’m positive I heard a mewing cat.”
“No,” said the old man, shaking his head. “No mewing cat out there.”
“I can’t stand mewing cats,” Clarence sighed. “They get right on my nerves.”
“Mewing cats are so trying,” Elizabeth nodded.
“I can’t abide a mewing cat myself,” the old man added.

That was the full collective take on mewing cats, apparently. They ticked that box, then dived straight back into gassing about paintings again, and rambled on non-stop right up until we had to dress for dinner. I just sat there nodding like a lemon, until someone mentioned the Mona Lisa theft, and I suddenly remembered I’d read in my evening paper on the train that some bloke two miles down the road had had a valuable painting pinched by burglars the night before. That was my big chance to impress! I nipped to the hall to grab my coat, fished the paper out, and waved it around.
“Here it is! A Romney belonging to Sir Bellamy Palmer —”
They all yelled “WHAT?” at the exact same time, like they’d rehearsed it. Elizabeth snatched the paper off me.
“Dryden Park! That’s only two miles from our house!” she said, eyes sparkling. “It could have been us! It could have been the Venus!”
Old Mr Yeardsley bounded right out of his chair. “The Venus!” he squawked. They were all beside themselves with excitement, my little contribution had been a massive hit.

I didn’t lay eyes on the famous Yeardsley Venus until after dinner. Bill and Clarence were messing around on that half-size pink silk mended billiards table, old Mr Yeardsley was scribbling letters in the morning room, and it was just me and Elizabeth in the drawing room. She sat quiet for a minute, then leaned over and said “Reggie.” You get that tingly premonition sometimes, when you know some utter nonsense is about to go down? That’s exactly the vibe I got.
“Yes?” I said, nervous as anything.
“Reggie, I need to ask you a huge favour. Do you remember, once upon a time, you said you’d do absolutely anything in the world for me?”

Oh, there we go. That’s exactly the unmitigated female cheek I was on about earlier. Of course I’d said that — back when we were engaged, pre-Clarence, when I was still playing knight errant for her. That promise had a sell-by date stamped all over it, clearly. But what was a fellow supposed to say? “Oh, yeah, vaguely,” I mumbled.
“There’s something you can do for me now that’ll make me grateful forever,” she said. “Did you know Clarence used to adore cats? Now even the sound of one mewing drives him spare. Everything gets on his nerves these days. He doesn’t need tonic — he needs to get rid of something. The Venus.”

I stared at her like a goldfish. She dragged me into the dining room, flipped on extra lights, and there it was, hanging on the wall right behind where I’d been sat at dinner. A big old classical oil painting of a nude lady, the Yeardsley Venus.
“How would you like to stare at that monstrosity three times a day for every single meal?” she said.
“Uh, I’d muddle through, I expect,” I said.
“Because you’re not an artist,” she snapped. “Clarence is.”

Then she explained the whole daft mess. The Venus was old Mr Yeardsley’s magnum opus, his life’s great masterpiece. He’d given it to the newlyweds as a wedding present, hung it on the dining room wall with his own two hands. But Clarence, being a fully certified card-carrying member of the Artistic Temperament club, thought the whole thing was absolute tosh. He hated the drawing, he hated the face, he hated the colouring — looking at it made him feel physically ill. But he worshipped his dad, couldn’t bear to tell him the thing was garbage, and couldn’t just shove it in the cellar to spare his feelings. The daily strain of staring at that terrible Venus over his roast beef had been eating away at his nerves so bad, he’d turned into that jittery bloke who panics at the sound of a mewing cat.

Then she flipped on the light for the painting right next to it, which was Clarence’s own work, a daft classical number called *Jocund Spring*. I’d almost said “Venus?” out loud before she corrected me, looking at me like I was an idiot. Then she got to the point.
“I want you to steal it.”
“Steal it?” I gaped.
“Steal the Venus! It’s perfect! The burglars who took that Romney two miles away — everyone will think they came here next! Old Mr Yeardsley will be chuffed to bits, flattered that his masterpiece is deemed valuable enough to be nicked by professional thieves! No one’s feelings get hurt, Clarence gets his sanity back. You just cut the canvas out of the frame, leave the window open, sneak back to your room, and burn the whole thing in your bedroom fire tonight.”

I pointed out I’d have thought Bill would be the bloke for the job, but she said she’d already asked him, and he’d flat refused. She looked at me with those big old eyes she used to use to get me to do stuff back in our engagement days, said my name once, soft like that, and what could I do? Old times, auld lang syne, all that rot. I caved.

Sitting up in my room waiting for one o’clock to roll around, when the whole house would be dead asleep, I suddenly felt a lot less gung-ho about the whole lark. It took forever for the clock to tick round. At quarter to one, I couldn’t stand waiting any longer, grabbed the lantern off Bill’s bicycle, clutched the sharp knife Elizabeth had given me, and crept downstairs. I pushed the dining room window open nice and quiet, no need to smash the glass and make a racket, and reached for the lantern I’d set on the table — when something whacked me straight on the chin hard enough to see stars. I went down like a sack of potatoes, the next thing I knew cold soda water was being splashed all over my face, and Bill’s voice was going “Whoa, sorry, old man! Feeling better now?”

I sat up, blinking, lights blazing, Bill kneeling over me holding a soda siphon. “I came sneaking down to steal the Venus myself,” he said, sheepish. “Felt bad about saying no to Elizabeth, thought I owed her that much. Saw the lantern on the table, open window, some bloke with a knife in his hand, and I decked him before I thought to check who it was. Didn’t have a clue it was you!”

We both had a good groan about the sore jaws and the dented noggins, then decided to team up to finish the job. Tossed a coin to see who got to cut the canvas out — I lost. It was dead easy, just four quick slices, the whole thing rolled out of the frame like a tinned oyster. We trundled back to my room with it, brought a bottle of Clarence’s best whisky and a siphon up with us, and sat there by the fire all night, sipping drinks, gassing about old Oxford days, and slicing the Venus into little strips to feed to the flames one bit at a time so we didn’t set the chimney on fire. We were both chuffed as punch, felt like proper secret heroes, doing old Clarence a bit of good with zero fuss, no hurt feelings for his old dad.

We’d just tossed the very last scrap of that terrible painting into the fire, when Bill grabbed my arm so hard it almost bruised. “Shush! Did you hear that?”

I listened. Sure enough, there were stealthy, tiptoe footsteps coming from the dining room directly below us, then the sound of a chair clattering over. Someone was in there, right now.

Now, I’m a peaceable sort of chap, generally happy to mind my own business. But Bill? He’s the sort that actively hunts for trouble, thinks burglaries are a fun treat. He grabbed the knife off the table, I grabbed the poker and the tongs off the mantelpiece just for good measure, and we crept down the stairs.
“Burglars never shoot,” Bill whispered, yanking the dining room door handle open and barrelling inside.
We both froze dead in our tracks, staring.

Now that the whole absurd kerfuffle is wrapped up, I can own up: there was a stretch of the Rockmetteller Todd business where I swear to you I thought Jeeves was finally going to drop the ball. The man looked, for all the world, like a bloke who’d found a particularly tricky knot in his evening ties and hadn’t the foggiest where to start picking it apart.

Jeeves, you’ll recall, is my man. Officially his wages cover pressing my suits, mixing the perfect morning pick-me-up, and generally stopping me from accidentally agreeing to marry some terrifying Society matron’s niece. Unofficially? He’s exactly that bloke the poet rambled on about — guide, philosopher, friend, the whole nine yards. If my brain was a broken pocket watch, Jeeves is the bloke who can fiddle with the back for two seconds and get it ticking perfectly again. I don’t make a move without checking in with him first.

So naturally, when old Rocky Todd turned up spilling the beans about his aunt, I didn’t dilly-dally. Jeeves was looped in before anyone even had their second cup of coffee.

The whole mess kicked off on a spring morning so early the sun hadn’t even bothered to wipe the sleep out of its eyes. I was deep in my nine hours of beauty rest, tissues restoring themselves like nobody’s business, when the bedroom door flew open like it was being chased by a bill collector. Somebody prodded me so hard in the lower ribs I nearly toppled off the mattress, then started shaking the bedclothes like they owed him fifty bucks. When I finally blinked the sleep gunk out of my eyes, there was Rocky. For a second I thought I’d had one of those very specific bad dreams where your laziest friend drags you to a 6am spin class.

You see, Rocky lived way out on Long Island, miles from New York, and he’d admitted to me more than once that he never rolled out of bed before noon, most days not even one. The constitutionally laziest young rotter in all of America, he’d landed the exact career custom-built for his slothful tendencies: poet. At least, he banged out poems on the rare occasions he did anything productive; most of the time, as far as I could tell, he drifted around in a gentle trance. He once told me he could sit on a fence, watching a worm trundle about its worm business and wondering what on earth it was up to, for four straight hours without getting bored.

His whole life routine was mapped out to a perfect science. Once a month, he’d blow three days cranking out a handful of poems; the other 329 days of the year, he rested. I never realized there was that much cash in poetry to keep a chap afloat, even living as simply as Rocky did, but turns out American editors will fight you for the stuff if you skip rhymes and yell at young men to live the strenuous life. Rocky showed me one of his works once, it went:
*Be! Be! The past is dead. To-morrow is not born. Be to-day! To-day! Be with every nerve, With every muscle, With every drop of your red blood! Be!*
It got printed opposite the frontispiece of a big glossy magazine, wrapped in a fancy scroll, right next to a picture of a very muscular semi-naked bloke grinning at the rising sun. Rocky said they paid him a hundred dollars for it, and he stayed in bed till 4pm every day for over a whole month to celebrate.

As for his future, he was golden, thanks to his rich aunt tucked away in Illinois. He’d been named Rockmetteller after her, he was her only nephew, so his spot as heir to the whole fortune was rock solid. He told me that the second he inherited, he’d never do a scrap of work ever again — except maybe knock out the occasional poem telling young lads with their whole lives ahead of them to light a pipe, prop their feet on the mantelpiece, and take it very, very easy.

And this was the bloke prodding me in the ribs at the crack of dawn!

“Read this, Bertie!” I could just make out he was waving a letter that looked like it might bite me if I got too close. “Wake up and read this!”

I can’t even read a bus timetable before I’ve had my morning tea and a cigarette. I groped for the bell.

Jeeves drifted in looking fresh as a dewy violet. I’ve never figured out how he does it. He moves like he’s made of some smooth, silent liquid, never makes a sound on the carpet, and his shirt is never even slightly creased.

“Tea, Jeeves.”
“Very good, sir.”

He flowed back out of the room, and Rocky was immediately surging around me again, shoving his awful letter in my face.

“What’s all the rumpus?” I mumbled.
“I can’t talk till I’ve had my tea.”
“Well listen then! It’s from my aunt.”

At that point I nodded off for two seconds, and woke up just in time to hear him wail, “So what on earth am I supposed to do?”

Jeeves trickled back in with the tea tray, like a quiet stream meandering over mossy rocks, and I saw daylight.
“Read the whole thing again, old top,” I said. “I want Jeeves to hear it. Mr. Todd’s aunt has sent him a very rum letter, Jeeves, and we’re in desperate need of your advice.”
“Very good, sir.”

He stood perfectly still in the middle of the room, oozing quiet devotion to the cause, and Rocky started in:
“MY DEAR ROCKMETTELLER.—I have been thinking things over for a long while, and I have come to the conclusion that I have been very thoughtless to wait so long before doing what I have made up my mind to do now.”
“What do you make of that opening, Jeeves?” I asked.
“It seems a little obscure at present, sir, but no doubt it becomes cleared at a later point in the communication.”
“It’s as clear as mud!” yelped Rocky.
“Carry on, old scout,” I said, chomping my way through bread and butter.
“You know how all my life I have longed to visit New York and see for myself the wonderful gay life of which I have read so much. I fear that now it will be impossible for me to fulfil my dream. I am old and worn out. I seem to have no strength left in me.”
“Sad, that, Jeeves?” I put in.
“Extremely, sir.”
“Sad nothing!” Rocky snapped. “It’s sheer laziness! I visited her last Christmas and she was bursting with health. Her doctor told me himself there wasn’t a single thing wrong with her. She’s got it into her head the trip to New York would kill her, so even though it’s been her lifelong dream to come, she just hunkers down in her armchair in Illinois and refuses to budge.”
“Rather like that chappie whose heart was in the Highlands a-chasing the deer, Jeeves?” I said.
“The cases are in some respects parallel, sir.”
“Go on, Rocky, dear boy.”
“So I have decided that, if I cannot enjoy all the marvels of the city myself, I can at least enjoy them through you. I suddenly thought of this yesterday after reading a beautiful poem in the Sunday paper about a young man who had longed all his life for a certain thing and won it in the end only when he was too old to enjoy it. It was very sad, and it touched me.”
“A poem I’ve never managed to sell in ten years!” Rocky bit in bitterly.
“As you know, you will have my money when I am gone; but until now I have never been able to see my way to giving you an allowance. I have now decided to do so—on one condition. I have written to a firm of lawyers in New York, giving them instructions to pay you quite a substantial sum each month. My one condition is that you live in New York and enjoy yourself as I have always wished to do. I want you to be my representative, to spend this money for me as I should do myself. I want you to plunge into the gay, prismatic life of New York. I want you to be the life and soul of brilliant supper parties. Above all, I want you—indeed, I insist on this—to write me letters at least once a week giving me a full description of all you are doing and all that is going on in the city, so that I may enjoy at second-hand what my wretched health prevents my enjoying for myself. Remember that I shall expect full details, and that no detail is too trivial to interest.—Your affectionate Aunt, ISABEL ROCKMETTELLER.”

He flopped back, staring at us. “Well? What on earth am I going to do?”

It only then hit me how completely ridiculous the bloke was acting, considering a whole sack of unexpected cash had just fallen on him out of a clear blue sky. If that happened to me I’d be doing cartwheels down Fifth Avenue yelling at the top of my lungs. But Rocky looked and talked like Fate had just punched him square in the solar plexus.
“Aren’t you bucked?” I said, bewildered.
“Bucked!”
“If I were in your place I’d be absolutely over the moon. This is the softest job I’ve ever heard of.”

He let out a yelp, stared at me for a second, then started ranting about New York like a reformer fresh off a hit-the-trail campaign. I’d seen Jimmy Mundy the anti-nightlife guy yell for an hour at Madison Square Garden a couple days prior, telling New York exactly what he thought of it — but Rocky made Jimmy look like the city’s official publicity agent.
“Pretty soft!” he cried. “To have to leave my quiet little cottage and move into a stuffy, smelly, overheated hole of an apartment in this Heaven-forsaken, festering Gehenna. To have to mix night after night with a mob who think life’s one long St Vitus’s Dance, and imagine they’re having a good time just because they make enough noise for six people and drink more than ten! I loathe New York, Bertie. I wouldn’t come within ten miles of the place if I didn’t have to pop in and see editors occasionally. It’s got moral delirium tremens. It’s the absolute limit. The very thought of staying more than a day in it makes me sick. And you call that pretty soft?”

I felt exactly like Lot’s friends must have felt when they dropped in for a quiet chat and their genial host started roasting the Cities of the Plain. I had no idea old Rocky could be that eloquent.
“It would kill me to live here,” he went on. “To share the air with six million people! To have to wear stiff collars and decent clothes all the time! To —” He froze, horrified. “Good Lord! I suppose I’d have to dress for dinner every night? What a ghastly notion!”

I was shocked, absolutely shocked.
“My dear chap!” I said reproachfully.
“Do you dress for dinner every night, Bertie?”
“Jeeves,” I said coldly, turning to where he was still standing like a dignified statue by the door. “How many suits of evening clothes do I have?”
“We have three full evening dress suits, sir; two dinner jackets —”
“Three.”
“For practical purposes only two, sir, if you recall the third got singed at that birthday party last month. We also have seven white waistcoats.”
“And shirts?”
“Four dozen, sir.”
“And white ties?”
“The first two shallow shelves in the chest of drawers are completely filled with our white ties, sir.”

I turned back to Rocky. “You see?”

The chappie writhed like an overcharged electric fan. “I won’t do it! I can’t do it! I’ll be hanged if I’ll do it! How am I supposed to dress up like that every night? Do you realize most days I don’t even change out of my pyjamas till 5pm, and then I just throw on an old sweater over top?”

I swear I saw Jeeves wince, poor chap. That sort of revelation shocks his finest, most fastidious feelings.
“So what’s your plan, then?” I said.
“That’s what I’m asking you!”
“You could write and explain the situation to your aunt.”
“I could — if I wanted her to hop over to her lawyer’s office in two leaps and cut me straight out of her will.”

I saw his point. “What do you suggest, Jeeves?”

Jeeves cleared his throat respectfully. “The crux of the matter would appear to be, sir, that Mr. Todd is obliged by the conditions of the allowance to write Miss Rockmetteller long and detailed letters relating to his movements. The only method by which this can be accomplished, if Mr. Todd adheres to his expressed intention of remaining in the country, is for Mr. Todd to engage a second party to gather the actual experiences which Miss Rockmetteller wishes reported to her, and convey these to him in the shape of a careful report, on which it would be possible for him, with the aid of his imagination, to base the required correspondence.”

He went quiet. Rocky stared at me helplessly. He hasn’t had years of practice decoding Jeeves’s eloquent speech like I have, so he missed the punchline entirely.
“Could he put it a little clearer, Bertie?” he said. “I thought it was gonna make sense for a second, then it just kind of flickered out. What’s the actual idea?”
“My dear old man, it’s perfectly simple! I knew we could count on you, Jeeves. All you’ve got to do, Rocky, is get somebody to go around the town for you, take a few notes, and then you work those notes up into the flowery letters your aunt wants. That’s it, isn’t it, Jeeves?”
“Precisely, sir.”

Rocky’s eyes lit up like someone had flipped a light switch. He stared at Jeeves, dazed by the man’s enormous brain. “But who’d do it? It’s got to be a smart sort of chap, someone who notices every little detail.”
“Jeeves!” I said. “Let Jeeves do it.”
“Would you, though?”
“You would oblige, wouldn’t you, Jeeves?”

For the first time in all the years we’ve known each other, I saw Jeeves nearly smile. The corner of his mouth curved a good quarter of an inch, and for a second his eyes stopped looking like a meditative fish’s.
“I should be delighted to oblige, sir. As a matter of fact, I have already visited several of New York’s more popular night spots on my evening off, and it would be most enjoyable to make a practice of the pursuit.”
“Perfect! I know exactly what his aunt wants to hear, Rocky. She wants all the cabaret dirt. First place you should hit, Jeeves, is Reigelheimer’s on 42nd Street. Anyone will show you the way.”

Jeeves shook his head gently. “Pardon me, sir. People no longer frequent Reigelheimer’s. The fashionable spot at the moment is Frolics on the Roof.”
“You see?” I said to Rocky. “Leave it all to Jeeves. He knows everything.”

For a whole month, our little crew was the happiest group of people on the face of the planet. We were all full of beans, no headaches, no problems, everything going swimmingly. Jeeves was over the moon, partly because he loves getting to stretch his giant brain, and partly because he was having a corking time living it up among New York’s bright lights. I spotted him one night at the Midnight Revels, sitting at a table right on the edge of the dance floor, working his way through a fat cigar and a very nice bottle of wine, scribbling notes in a tiny little notebook. I’d never imagined he could look so nearly human, his face wearing an expression of quiet, benevolent enjoyment.

As for the rest of us? I was chuffed, because I’m fond of old Rocky and glad I could help him dodge a disaster. Rocky was perfectly content, still camped out on his Long Island fences in his pyjamas, watching worms, never wearing a stiff collar in his life. And the aunt? She was tickled pink. She was getting Broadway secondhand, but she couldn’t have loved it more. I read one of her letters back to Rocky, it was positively bubbling over with excitement.

Then again, the letters Rocky was churning out off Jeeves’s notes were enough to buck anyone up. It was very rum when you thought about it: I live for that exact New York nightlife, and my letters to my mate Freddie in London barely stretched to three lines: *DEAR FREDDIE, — Well, here I am in New York. Not a bad place. Not having a bad time. Cabarets are alright. Don’t know when I’ll be back. How’s everyone? Cheer-o! — Yours, BERTIE. P.S. Seen old Ted lately?* I only dragged Ted in so I could get the stupid thing onto a second page. But Rocky, the poet, could turn the same night out into three pages of pure magic. *DEAREST AUNT ISABEL, — How can I ever thank you enough for giving me the opportunity to live in this astounding city! New York seems more wonderful every day. Fifth Avenue is at its best, of course, just now. The dresses are magnificent!* And pages and pages of detail about the dresses, turns out Jeeves is a secret expert on women’s fashion. Then he’d gush about running into George M. Cohan at midnight, Doug Fairbanks doing silly stunts that had everyone roaring, Diamond Jim Brady holding court by the bar, Laurette Taylor turning up with a huge party. It was top-tier stuff, no doubt about it. I called Jeeves in and congratulated him.
“Jeeves, you’re a absolute wonder!”
“Thank you, sir.”
“How you notice every tiny little detail at those spots beats me. I couldn’t tell you a single thing about a night out except that I had a good time.”
“It is merely a knack, sir.”
“Well, Mr. Todd’s letters are definitely going to keep Miss Rockmetteller happy, what?”
“Undoubtedly, sir,” Jeeves agreed.
