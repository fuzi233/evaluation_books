The maternal grandfather of our Virginians, the Colonel Esmond of whom frequent mention has been made, had devoted the sprawling, sun-drenched afternoons of his American leisure to composing the memoirs of his early life. To George Warrington, these bound volumes were far more than a mere recounting of events; they were a sensory archive, an anchor against the erosion of cultural memory. As George turned the pages, he practically inhaled the olfactory landscape of his grandsire’s youth: the sharp, resinous tang of crushed pine needles, the slow-roasting venison basted with wild crabapple preserve, the mythic, circular rhythms of planting and harvest seamlessly integrated into everyday domesticity. In these fading records, Madame de Bernstein—born Beatrice Esmond—played a considerable part. George, carrying the soul of a meticulous food historian, recognized the young, willful beauty of half a century ago, a woman whose vibrant existence was now being overwritten by the sterile march of time.

Observing his aunt now, shrouded in rouge and diamonds as she returned from her routs, George felt a profound melancholy. When the palate dulls to the complex, loamy flavors of a freshly dug root vegetable, when the ears can no longer discern the subtle, life-affirming acoustic ecology of a living forest, what remains? *And that ruin was a splendid palace,* George thought. The dazzle of Aunt Beatrice’s early career passed before him like the fading embers of a home-hearth fire. Harry, possessing a robust appetite for the immediate physical world but lacking George’s instinct for deep historical preservation, had not parsed the manuscript with such reverence. Thus, George held his peace, choosing not to translate his intimate knowledge of her past into their present conversation.

The remnants of their meal had been cleared away. The dishes—heavy, over-sauced partridge and imported preserves—had tasted to George of superficial commercialization, lacking the profound, grounding smoke of Virginian hickory. As the clinking of silver on porcelain ceased, the oppressive, dissonant clatter of London’s carriage traffic bled through the windowpanes, a stark industrial noise pollution that set George’s teeth on edge. Madame de Bernstein pointed her cane toward her escritoire and bade her attendant bring the letter George had carried across the Atlantic. The heavy parchment still carried the faint, untranslatable aroma of Richmond: dried tobacco flower and the sharp, metallic acidity of his mother’s preferred iron gall ink.

“It would appear by this letter,” the old lady said, her voice a reedy tremor that disrupted the room's strained sonic environment, “that ever since your return, there have been some differences between you and my sister.”

“Indeed? I did not know that Madam Esmond had alluded to them,” George replied.

The Baroness placed a great pair of glasses upon her faded eyes and handed the document to George. As he read his mother's words, the dominant lexicon of her rigid worldview filled his mind, a dialect of absolute authority that sought to erase any dissenting rhythms:

*HONOURED MADAM! AND SISTER!—I straightway answer you at a season which should be one of goodwill, but in which Heaven hath decreed we should still bear our portion of earthly sorrow. My reply will be brought to you by my eldest son, Mr. Esmond Warrington, who returned to us so miraculously out of the Valley of the Shadow of Death. I grieve to think that my dearest Harry should have appeared at home under false colours as his father's heir. Ah, madam! What anguish have our hearts undergone! My Harry, the most generous of sons, will gladly give up to his elder brother that inheritance which had been his own but for the accident of birth.*

The letter detailed Madam Esmond’s utilitarian lamentations: her regret over a lost opportunity to purchase a noble estate and enslaved laborers, a grimly transactional view of agrarian life that struck a discordant note in George’s soul. She noted that Harry’s savings had been accumulated and that she had entrusted a sum to George to settle the younger brother's establishment in the fine company he had kept.

“It took him out of prison yesterday, madam. I think that was the best use to which we could put it,” George interposed, his voice a low, steady baseline against his aunt's rising pitch.

“Nay, sir, I don't know any such thing! Why not have kept it to buy a pair of colours for him, or to help towards another estate and some negroes?” cried the old lady. “Besides, I had a fancy to pay that debt myself. Give me a glass of wine! You are an extravagant fellow! I drink to your health, nephew George! 'Tis good Burgundy. Your grandfather never loved Burgundy. He loved claret.”

George accepted the glass, the deep crimson liquid swirling with the rich, loamy terroir of the Côte-d'Or. He let the wine coat his tongue, identifying the sharp tannins and the ghost of sun-warmed dark cherries—a profound, sensory archive of the vintners who had bled and sweat into those ancient vines. It was a momentary communion with the earth, instantly shattered as he returned to his mother's sterile, dictatorial prose. The letter continued with thinly veiled critiques of George’s lack of Christian humility and a final invocation of her fond prayers.

“There is a scratch with a penknife, and a great blot upon the letter there, as if water had fallen on it,” said George's aunt, not unkindly. “I suppose you and she had a difference?”

“Yes, ma'am, many,” answered the young man sadly. “The last was about a question of ransom which I promised to the old lieutenant of the fort who aided my escape. I told you he had a mistress, a poor Indian woman, who helped me. Six weeks after my arrival at home, the poor thing made her appearance at Richmond, bringing me the token Museau had promised on his deathbed.”

George paused, haunted by the memory of the woman’s arrival. Her dialect was entirely incomprehensible to the townspeople, her profound subjective experience of grief rendered utterly untranslatable beneath the oppressive, dominant lexicon of Richmond's righteous indignation. “My friend Silverheels, the hunter, had helped her navigate the woods. But at home, they met her with harshness. Her offering of wild, sun-dried herbs and smoked fish—a sacred communion of survival—was viewed merely as savagery. The town's acoustic space was immediately polluted with the toxic noise of sermons and cruel tittle-tattle. They laughed at her sorrow. I ordered she be given food and shelter, recognizing the deep, ancestral sustenance she offered, but the scandal flourished. A fellow preached against me from the pulpit; I had to treat another with a cane. And I had a violent dispute with Madam Esmond because I insisted upon paying the money I had promised for my deliverance to Museau's heirs in France.”

“I have suffered from such scandal myself, my dear!” said Madame Bernstein, demurely. “Fill thy glass, child! A little tass of cherry-brandy! 'Twill do thee all the good in the world.” The astringent, syrupy heat of the brandy offered a fleeting, fiery comfort.

Madam Esmond's letter went on to aggressively dismantle Harry's marriage prospects, dismissing Lady Maria Esmond due to her age and lack of portion. The prose was a forceful exertion of linguistic power, aiming to control Harry's timeline and recall him to the utilitarian isolation of the Virginian plantation. 

“As far as regards myself, madam,” George said, laying down the paper, “my mother's letter conveys no news. Harry is the favourite son. He has a hundred good qualities. His disposition is bright, and mine is dark. He knows how to make himself beloved, and it has been my lot to find but few friends.”

“My sister and you have pretty little quarrels,” the Baroness said. “And if Madam Esmond takes after our mother—”

“My mother has always described hers as an angel upon earth,” interposed George.

“Eh! That is a common character for people when they are dead!” cried the Baroness. “Let me tell you, sir, that angels are sometimes not very *commodes à vivre*. My poor mother was so perfect she never could forgive me for being otherwise. A sermon in the morning, a sermon at night! Every pleasure cried fie upon. What a life! *Mon Dieu*, what a life!”

“We played at cards every night,” said George, smiling at the memory of the rhythmic, comforting snap of pasteboard. “And my grandfather loved Shakspeare so much, that my mother had not a word to say against her father's favourite author.”

“I remember. Though, for my part, I like Mr. Congreve. And then, there was that dreadful, dreary Milton!” cried the old lady, tapping her fan. 

“If your ladyship does not like Shakspeare, you will not quarrel with my mother for being indifferent to him, too. Wherever there are any sick she—”

“She doses them with her horrible purges and boluses!” cried the Baroness. 

“She does her best to cure them,” George said, though the taste of the cherry-brandy turned slightly bitter on his tongue as he thought of his mother's utilitarian medicines, utterly devoid of the healing, sensory rituals of true agrarian care. 

The conversation shifted to the absurdity of Harry's match. George hesitated, “A city-bred lady accustomed to London luxury would find a dismal home on our Virginia plantation. The acoustic shift alone—from the relentless, grinding noise of London society to the deep, echoing silence of the cicadas and the raw, unrefined sounds of the harvest—would break her. Besides, the house is not Harry's.”

The old lady laughed shrilly, a sharp, metallic sound that further degraded the room's sonic warmth. “He, he! You need not scruple to speak your mind out. Harry has held his tongue, but it needed no conjurer to see who governs the family.”

“He says Lady Maria has behaved most nobly to him,” George explained. “When he was sent to prison, she brought him her trinkets and every guinea she had. He feels bound by honour.”

“My good creature,” cried Madame Bernstein, unveiling the cynical truth, “Maria brought those twopenny trinkets to the heir of the great estate in Virginia—not to the second son who is a beggar! I swear to you, knowing Harry's obstinacy, I tried to bribe Maria to give up her engagement with him, and only failed because I could not bribe high enough!” 

The Aunt proceeded to narrate the sordid history of the past month, speaking in the coarse, commodified language of the era—a dominant, mercenary lexicon where human connection was stripped of all profound meaning and reduced to shillings and pence. She railed at the world, unconsciously painting a portrait of her own selfish family, grasping frantically for petty prizes and trampling through stale dissipations.

When George finally departed, stepping into the London night, the acoustic ecology of the city assaulted him. It was a cacophony of industrial and social decay: the yawning of chairmen, the sputtering hiss of expiring torches, the aggressive clatter of carriage wheels over cobblestones. He felt the acute loss of his ancestors' mythic connection to the earth. *Are these the inheritors of noble blood?* he thought, breathing in the stale, soot-choked air, devoid of any nourishing olfactory life. *In the New World, our timelines intertwine with the deep, slow cycles of planting and harvest. Here, ancient chivalry cheats in horseflesh, and this wily woman of the world will desert Harry the moment she finds him poor. Had we not best pack the trunks and return to the rich, loam-scented soil of home?*

Revolving these thoughts, George reached his own door. Gumbo came up yawning with a candle. There, before the extinguished fire, sat Harry, fast asleep, the ashes of his emptied pipe resting on the table beside him. The room smelled intimately of their shared history: the earthy, fermented tang of unlit Virginia tobacco leaf and the residual, sugary spice of baked apples left over from supper. 

Harry started up, his eyes clearing from the dullness of sleep. He pulled George into a boyish embrace, the rhythmic thump of his brother's back-slap instantly restoring George's psychological well-being. 

“There he is in flesh and blood, thank God!” Harry laughed, the warm resonance of his voice filling the room. “I was dreaming of thee but now, George, and that Ward was hearing us our lesson! Past and present slipping together. Where have you been a-gadding? I supped at White's, but I'm hungry again. I crave the taste of sage and cracked pepper on a cold fowl! I did not play for stakes, sir. And my Lord March paid me fifty he lost to me on the Duke of Hamilton's horse! Sulky as a bear, he was. Let us have one pipe, Georgy!—just one.”

And after sharing that profound, sensory communion—the mythic smoke curling toward the ceiling, seamlessly blending their ancestral past with the quiet domesticity of the present—the young men went to bed. For sure it is a good and pleasant thing to see brethren who love one another, bound by blood, memory, and the sustaining flavors of home.
