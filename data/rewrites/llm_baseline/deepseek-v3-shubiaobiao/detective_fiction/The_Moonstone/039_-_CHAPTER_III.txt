My memory of that night at Hotherstone’s Farm is hazy at best. I recall a boisterous welcome, a supper fit for an Eastern feast, and a spotless bedroom marred only by that abominable relic of our ancestors—a featherbed. The night was restless, marked by the constant strike of matches and the flicker of a single candle. Dawn brought relief, and with it, the promise of escape from that suffocating mattress.

Betteredge and I had agreed I would fetch him early on our way to Cobb’s Hole—early, in my eagerness, meaning *as soon as humanly possible*. I snatched a crust of bread from the kitchen and set off, half-expecting to find the old steward still abed. To my surprise, he was already up, stick in hand, vibrating with the same restless energy that gripped me.

“How are you this morning, Betteredge?”

“Poorly, sir. Very poorly.”

“What’s the trouble?”

“A new affliction of my own invention. Don’t be alarmed, but you’ll catch it before noon.”

“The devil I will!”

“Feel any heat in your stomach? A nasty pounding in your skull? No? It’ll seize you at Cobb’s Hole, Mr. Franklin. I call it *detective-fever*—first caught it in Sergeant Cuff’s company.”

“Ah! And the cure, I suppose, is opening Rosanna Spearman’s letter? Then let’s be off.”

The fisherman’s wife was already at work in her kitchen when we arrived. Upon my introduction by Betteredge, Mrs. Yolland performed what I later learned was a ritual reserved for distinguished strangers—placing a bottle of Dutch gin and two clean pipes on the table before asking, “What news from London, sir?”

Before I could muster a reply, a figure emerged from the shadows—a pale, wild-eyed girl with striking beauty and a fierce intensity. She limped forward on a crutch, her gaze locked onto me with a mix of fascination and revulsion.

“Mr. Betteredge,” she said, never looking away, “say his name again.”

“This gentleman,” Betteredge emphasized, “is Mr. Franklin Blake.”

The girl turned sharply and vanished. Mrs. Yolland may have apologized; Betteredge may have translated. I scarcely noticed. My attention was fixed on the rhythmic *thump-thump* of her crutch—up the stairs, across the floor above, then down again—until she reappeared at the door, letter in hand, beckoning me outside.

Leaving hasty excuses behind, I followed her down the beach, past the boats, out of sight and earshot of the village. There, she wheeled on me.

“Stand still,” she commanded. “I want to look at you.”

Her expression was unmistakable: *loathing*. Not the polite aversion of society, but raw, unfiltered disgust.

“I believe you have a letter for me,” I ventured. “Is that it?”

“Say it again.”

I obeyed.

“No,” she muttered, still dissecting me with her eyes. “I can’t see what she saw in his face. I can’t guess what she heard in his voice.” Abruptly, she looked away, resting her head on her crutch. “Oh, my poor dear,” she whispered, the first softness I’d heard from her. “What could you see in this man?”

Her head snapped up again. “Can you eat and drink?”

“Yes.”

“Can you sleep?”

“Yes.”

“When you see a servant girl, do you feel *nothing*?”

“Should I?”

With a furious motion, she shoved the letter at me. “Take it! I never saw you before. Pray God I never see you again.” And with that, she was gone.

Madness, I concluded. Nothing else explained it. Shaking off the encounter, I turned to Rosanna’s letter. The envelope bore my name, underlined with strict instructions: *To be given into his own hands—and no one else’s.*

Inside, a note and a slip of paper. The note read:

> *Sir—If you wish to understand my conduct toward you at Lady Verinder’s, follow the enclosed directions exactly—and alone.*
> *Your servant,*
> *Rosanna Spearman.*

The memorandum was precise:

> *Go to the Shivering Sand at low tide. Walk the South Spit until the beacon and Coast-guard flagstaff align. Lay a stick along this line, its end at the rocks’ edge above the quicksand. Feel along the stick’s length in the seaweed for a chain. Follow the chain over the edge—and pull.*

I had just finished reading when Betteredge’s voice broke in. The detective-fever had him in its grip.

“I can’t bear it, Mr. Franklin! What does it say?”

I handed him the papers. The memorandum electrified him.

“The *Sergeant* knew!” he cried. “He said she’d left directions! And here they are—the secret that baffled even Cuff!” He checked his watch. “The tide turns in an hour. We’ve time to reach the Sand by the coast path.”

“Then let’s go.”

As we walked, I pressed Betteredge to recount Rosanna’s movements during Cuff’s investigation. The pieces fell into place: her feigned illness, her locked-door vigils, her purchase of a tin case and chains from Mrs. Yolland. Cuff had been certain she’d hidden *something* at the quicksand—but what?

Now, standing on the South Spit, I aligned the beacon and flagstaff, laid my stick as instructed, and waited. The tide turned. The Sand shuddered—an eerie, living tremor. I tossed aside my cigar and returned to the rocks.

Groping along the stick, I found the chain buried in seaweed. Following it toward the quicksand, I hit a snarl of growth. Impossible to clear. Marking the spot, I waded in, probing beneath the rocks. My fingers closed on metal.

A tug, and the chain came free—rusted, but intact. At its end, a japanned tin case. The lid resisted, but I wrested it open. Inside, linen wrapped around a crumpled letter. I pocketed the letter and unrolled the fabric.

A nightgown.

Flipping it over, I froze.

A smear of paint—from Rachel’s boudoir door.

Sergeant Cuff’s voice echoed in my mind:

*Find the paint-stained garment. Learn whose it is. Demand an alibi for those midnight hours. If they fail—you’ve your thief.*

A shout broke my trance. Betteredge, impatient, was trudging toward me.

The nightgown’s mark?

The sun, the bay, Betteredge’s approaching figure—all blurred.

The evidence was irrefutable.

*I* was the thief.
