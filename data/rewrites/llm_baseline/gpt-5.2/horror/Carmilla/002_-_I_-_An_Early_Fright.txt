In Styria we lived—if one can call it living—within the old walls of a schloss that had learned to do without applause. A modest income there, eight or nine hundred a year, stretched as far as a thrifty hand could pull it; the land itself seemed complicit, offering milk, fish, fruit, fuel, and labor at such prices that extravagance looked almost indecent. My father, English by birth and habit though long in Austrian service, had retired upon his pension and what patrimony remained to him, and bought this feudal place—stone, moat, chapel, towers, and all—at a bargain that still felt like a superstition: as though bargains were never granted without some quiet cost.

Our schloss stood on a slight rise inside a forest that reached away like a dark sea. In front ran an ancient road—narrow, worn to a pale ribbon by centuries of wheels—passing the drawbridge (never raised in my time) and the moat. The moat was no mere ditch; it was a domestic larder, a gleaming ring of water stocked with perch that flashed in the green shade, and it carried swans with the serene arrogance of owners. In summer the lilies floated like white dishes laid out on a black tablecloth, their broad leaves collecting rain and pollen, each cupped pool catching the sky’s faintest light. Beyond, the schloss presented its many windows like blind eyes, its towers, and the little Gothic chapel that always smelled of cold stone and old wax, even at noon.

The forest opened in an irregular glade before the gate, and to the right a steep Gothic bridge carried the road over a stream that ran in deep shadow under fir and beech. That stream had its own voice—thin in drought, full-throated after rain—and I learned later how much my mind depended on such honest sounds. Here there were no factory whistles, no iron pounding, no relentless clatter of streets; only the measured speech of water, the conspiratorial tapping of twigs, the wind’s low hand in the leaves, and at night the owls stitching their calls across the dark. Silence in such a place is never empty. It is crowded with small living noises, and when one of them stops, you feel it like a hand laid suddenly on your mouth.

We were very lonely. Looking from the hall door toward the road, the forest ran fifteen miles one way and twelve the other. The nearest inhabited village lay about seven English miles to the left. The nearest inhabited schloss with any historic associations was old General Spielsdorf’s, nearly twenty miles away to the right. I say the nearest inhabited village because, only three miles westward—toward the General’s place—there stood a ruined village with a quaint little church now roofless, its aisle holding the mouldering tombs of the proud Karnstein family, long extinct. Above those silent stones, deeper in the wood, the desolate chateau of Karnstein overlooked the abandoned town. There was a legend about why that place had emptied itself of breath and language, why prayers ceased there and names went unspoken; I shall tell it another time. At the date of my story I did not yet know what it meant to lose a tongue, to have words die out of a place as thoroughly as people. I only knew that even the birds seemed to alter their music when the wind came from that direction.

Our household—speaking only of family—was astonishingly small. My father, the kindest man on earth and already growing old; and I, then nineteen. Eight years have passed since that time. My mother, a Styrian lady, died in my infancy; I cannot even pretend to remember her. In her stead I had, from almost my earliest consciousness, a governess whose fat, benignant face stands in my memory as steadily as a lamp in a familiar window. This was Madame Perrodon, a native of Berne, good-natured, bustling, and sincere, who supplied with her care what tenderness she could to the blank left by my mother.

Madame Perrodon made the third at our little dinner table. A fourth was sometimes present: Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, a “finishing governess,” as you say—French and German at her command, with that attentive, polished manner that treats every sentence as if it were being served on porcelain. Madame Perrodon spoke French and broken English; my father and I spoke English daily—partly from patriotism, partly from fear that our language, left unpractised in these woods, might become as extinct for us as the Karnsteins. The result was a Babel in which a single thought changed flavor as it passed from one tongue to another. A scolded fault sounded almost playful in French; a praise in German could feel like a verdict. There are experiences that refuse translation entirely; they sit stubbornly in the mouth that first formed them, and in any other language they taste of something else.

We had, besides, two or three young lady friends near my own age who visited for longer or shorter terms, and whose visits I sometimes returned. Neighbors—five or six leagues away—made occasional calls, arriving dusty and hungry, and leaving with baskets of bread, cheese, and fruit pressed upon them by our servants as if hospitality were a law of nature. Yet even so my life was solitary. My governesses had only such control over me as wise persons can expect with a rather spoiled girl whose only parent allowed her pretty nearly her own way. Solitude, in such a house, is not only the absence of company; it is a way the rooms listen back to you.

The first occurrence in my life that left a terrible impression—one that has never been effaced—was among the earliest incidents I can recollect. Some will call it trifling and wonder why I record it. You will see, by-and-by, why I must.

The nursery—so called, though I had it all to myself—was a large room in the upper story under a steep oak roof. The beams were dark with age, and the air carried that clean, dry scent of old wood warmed in the day and cooling at night, like bread left on a board. The furniture was clumsy and ancient, as if carved to endure rather than to please. Through the small lattice windows, light entered grudgingly, thin and dusty, and even by day the corners seemed to hoard shadow.

I cannot have been more than six when one night I awoke and, looking round from my bed, failed to see the nursery maid. Neither was my nurse there. I thought myself alone.

I was not frightened. I was one of those happy children carefully kept ignorant of ghost stories and fairy tales and all that lore which later makes us draw the coverlet to our chin because a door gives a sudden crack, or because a candle’s last tremble sets the bedpost dancing its thin shadow nearer and nearer to our face. I felt, instead, vexed—insulted—at being, as I supposed, neglected. My throat swelled with the beginning of a whimper, preparatory to a hearty bout of roaring, when I saw—so plainly that the memory has never dimmed—a solemn but very pretty face looking at me from the bedside.

It was the face of a young lady. She was kneeling, and her hands were under the coverlet as if she had already warmed them there. Her hair fell darkly about her, and her expression held a strange composure, like the stillness that comes over a kitchen when everyone pauses to listen for a kettle’s first whisper. I looked at her with pleased wonder and stopped whimpering at once.

She caressed me—soft, sure touches, not hurried, not tentative—and then she lay down beside me on the bed and drew me toward her, smiling. The scent of her was not like soap or lavender or the starch of linen; it was closer, sweeter, as if something had been shut too long in a cedar chest with dried petals and had taken on the woody dampness of the place. I felt immediately soothed—delightfully soothed—as if all the air in the room had been stirred and settled into calm. I remember the sound then: the forest outside had gone very quiet, and in that quiet her breathing seemed the only true measure of time. I fell asleep again.

I was wakened by a sensation as if two needles ran into my breast very deep at the same moment.

The shock tore a cry from me—sharp, involuntary, like a child scalded. The lady started back, her eyes fixed upon me with an intentness that froze me more than the pain. In the same instant she slipped down upon the floor and, as I thought, hid herself under the bed.

For the first time fear came—real fear, not the petulant complaint of a neglected child. The room’s sounds altered; even my own breath seemed too loud, too exposed. I yelled with all my might and main, and the cry felt as if it struck the rafters and fell back on me.

Nurse, nursery maid, housekeeper—others too—came running in with lights, the flame wobbling and throwing their shadows gigantic against the sloping roof. The smell of tallow and the cold draft from the corridor rushed together. They crowded round, and as I told my story—between sobs, pointing, clinging—they made light of it, soothing me. Yet even at six, even through panic, I could read faces as one reads a table laid wrong: a cup in the place of a plate, a knife missing, a crumb where none should be. Their faces were pale with an unwonted anxiety. They looked under the bed; they searched about the room; they peeped under tables and plucked open cupboards with hands that shook more than their voices admitted. The housekeeper whispered to the nurse—thinking I could not hear, though children hear as animals do:

“Lay your hand along that hollow in the bed; someone did lie there, so sure as you did not; the place is still warm.”

I remember the nursery maid petting me—her palm heavy and trembling—and all three of them examining my chest where I said I felt the puncture. They pronounced there was no sign. No mark. No blood. Yet the pain had been as certain as a pin thrust, as bodily as hunger.

They stayed sitting up all night. From that time a servant always sat up in the nursery until I was about fourteen. The simple domestic routine of guarding a child’s sleep became a nightly vigil, as if sleep itself were a pantry that must not be left unlatched.

For a long time afterward I was very nervous. A doctor was called in, pallid and elderly. I can still see him: a long saturnine face, slightly pitted with smallpox, a chestnut wig set upon him as if it were another instrument he wore, like his spectacles or his watch. For a good while, every second day, he came and gave me medicine—bitter draughts that made my tongue curl and my stomach revolt. I hated them with a child’s honest loathing. The spoon’s cold metal, the smell of the mixture—herbs steeped too long, alcohol and something earthy—still rise in my memory when I recall that time.

The morning after I saw the stranger I was in a state of terror. I could not bear to be left alone for a moment, daylight though it was. Even the bright ordinary sounds—the clink of a plate, the soft thud of bread on a board, the murmur of servants below—did not restore the world to safety. Every pause between noises seemed a trap.

I remember my father coming up and standing at the bedside. He talked cheerfully, asking the nurse questions, laughing heartily at one of the answers, as if laughter could drive away whatever had happened in the dark. He patted me on the shoulder, kissed me, and told me not to be frightened—that it was nothing but a dream and could not hurt me. His English voice, so familiar and steady, tried to make reality of its own choosing. But I was not comforted. I knew, with the hard certainty children sometimes have, that the visit of the strange woman was not a dream. I was awfully frightened.

The nursery maid tried to console me with an assurance—so earnest it was almost a plea—that it was she who had come and looked at me and lain down beside me, and that I must have been half-dreaming not to have known her face. The nurse supported her, nodding and smoothing the sheet as if smoothing my thoughts. But it did not quite satisfy me. The face I had seen had not belonged to either of them. It had been too still, too intent—like someone listening for a word in a language she loved and feared to lose.

Sometime that day I remember a venerable old man, in a black cassock, coming into the room with the nurse and housekeeper. He spoke a little to them and very kindly to me. His face was sweet and gentle, and his white hair made a soft halo in the dim light. He told me they were going to pray. He joined my hands together, placing my small fingers in a careful clasp, and desired me to say softly while they were praying, “Lord hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus’ sake.”

I think those were the very words. I repeated them often to myself afterward, and for years my nurse made me say them in my prayers. Yet even that phrase—comforting as it was—felt like an attempt to name something that did not wish to be named. There are terrors that do not answer to the dominant vocabulary of a house, and when we force them into our approved words, the words come out thin, like broth made from bones already boiled too often.

I see still the thoughtful face of that old man in his black cassock standing in that rude, lofty brown room, the heavy antique furniture about him, the scanty light entering through the lattice, dust motes drifting in it like slow snow. He kneeled, and the three women with him, and he prayed aloud with an earnest quavering voice for what appeared to me a very long time. The sound of his prayer filled the room the way a steady flame fills a lantern, and for a little while the forest’s hush beyond the walls did not seem so watchful.

All my life before that event is almost gone from me, and for some time after it, too, is obscure. But the scenes I have described stand out vivid—isolated pictures of a phantasmagoria surrounded by darkness—and whenever the night grows too quiet, I hear again the sudden absence of ordinary sounds, and feel, as if through the bedclothes, the cold precision of those two needle-points entering my breast.
