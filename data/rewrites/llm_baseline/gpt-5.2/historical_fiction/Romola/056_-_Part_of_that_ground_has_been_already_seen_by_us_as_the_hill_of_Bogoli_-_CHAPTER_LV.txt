The days stretched themselves out, longer in light, shorter in mercy; and yet they brought neither what Romola most yearned for nor what she most feared. No sign came from Baldassarre. No sudden unmasking of the whispered conspiracy broke through, even though the Government kept its eyes and ears sharpened as if the city were a kitchen full of cats and stolen meat. Waiting became a daily task—like tending a pot that must not boil over, though you can never quite lift the lid without scalding yourself.

But the season did not leave her empty-handed. It brought other presences—close, urgent, and bodily—that pressed themselves upon her as insistently as heat in narrow streets. It brought the Plague spreading from door to door, and it brought Rome’s sentence against Savonarola. These two things—death in the alleys and a curse pronounced beneath painted vaults—stayed her from drifting farther into that cold, proud distance she had begun to feel toward the Frate. They drew her back, fastened her again to the man who had once turned her eyes from the treasures of the mind to the duties of the hand, and who now seemed to stand almost alone against men who wanted virtue silenced because it made their pleasures look like rot.

For Romola could not go day after day into rooms that stank of fever and stale linen—could not lift a cup to cracked lips, or wipe away the sour sweat that gathered at temples, or coax the swallowing of a thin broth whose few grains of salt were like a promise—without remembering who had first made her believe that such acts were not mere pity but a kind of righteousness. Even the simplest preparation had become sacred to her, because the Plague turned cooking itself into a measure of conscience: to choose what was nourishing and possible in a city where fear had emptied markets; to steep herbs carefully, to skim the scum, to keep the spoon and the cup clean when every touch might carry death; to set a slice of bread near a dying child and hear the faint, grateful sound of chewing as though it were a small hymn. In those houses the world was reduced to smells—vinegar, sweat, old straw, the metallic taint of blood—and to sounds: the rasping breath that seemed to scrape the throat from within, the wet cough, the thin crying that could no longer rise to a wail, the soft scuffling of feet that tried not to wake the sick. And Romola, who had never learned to pray as other Florentines did, began to measure the soul by these very things: whether one could still hear suffering without turning away, whether one could still bear the stink and not let disgust harden into cruelty.

So too she could not see the silencing of a man whose difference from the great mass of the clergy lay less in doctrine than in an iron resolve to make the Christian life something more than a costume worn on feast-days—could not see him struck at, not for heresy but for earnestness—without feeling herself drawn, stubbornly, to his side.

Far into the hot days of June, the Excommunication—already some weeks arrived from Rome—was solemnly published in the Duomo. Romola went, not because she needed to be convinced, but because she wanted the resistance it stirred in her to grow steadier, like a fire fed on hard wood. She wanted that sympathy with Savonarola—one of the few sources left of her strength—to be warmed into endurance.

The scene in the great church was a remembered place made strange, as if a familiar dish had been served with the seasonings taken away and the bread left stale. It was not the Duomo she had known on those mornings when citizen faces filled the vast space under the light, rising young above old, a living amphitheatre of hope encircling the worn memories of age. It was not that mighty voice that once had made every word seem to ring on the stones and pass into the blood. Instead, there were bare walls at evening, made more sombre by the glimmer of tapers. There was the black and grey flock of monks and secular clergy, heads bent, faces unexpectant, as if they had come to watch a task done, not to listen for truth. There was the occasional tinkling of little bells in the pauses of a voice that read monotonously—each phrase falling with the dull, measured certainty of a pestle grinding something already ground. The words had been hanging in churches long enough; now they were spoken aloud as if speech itself could make them heavier.

Romola listened, and what struck her, almost as sharply as the sentence, was the soundscape of it all: the bells’ small, cold chime; the reader’s droning cadence that made the Latin less like living speech than like a rope being laid out, length by length; the hush that did not feel like reverence so much as a withholding of breath; and then, at last, the extinction of the tapers—darkness brought on deliberately—and the slow, shuffling tread of monkish feet departing in dim silence. Silence, in that moment, was not peace. It was the hush of a household after a quarrel when no one dares to set a pot on the fire lest the clink betray them.

Her ardour for the Frate was doubled as she saw, here and there, the gleeful triumph in hard, coarse faces—the sort of look that rejoices when a strict voice is gagged because it has named what others want left unnamed. And she saw too the fear-stricken confusion in faces she knew for his friends: men and women who had clung to him strongly, now trembling as if the very air had become unsafe to breathe.

For the question—where obedience must end and resistance begin—was never simple. It was made almost unbearable by the belief, rooted in most pious Florentine hearts, that the Church was not a human bargain struck among parties and kept alive by money and influence, but a living organism, instinct with divine power to bless and to curse. That belief was not, for them, an opinion they had chosen; it was an inalienable impression, like the concavity of the sky—something you never argue with because it is simply there. And when Savonarola, in his written arguments, dared to say that the Excommunication was unjust, and therefore not valid, the timid trembled the more: it was as if he had flung a stone at an image that might bleed and then call down invisible vengeance. Against that mystic immeasurable power there seemed no weapon and no defence—only submission, like lying still when fever shakes you.

But Romola—whose early mind had not been nourished on those traditional Christian associations, because her father had lived apart, turning to the old learning as to a private vineyard—felt her relation to the Church only through Savonarola. His moral force had been the only authority she had bowed to. In his excommunication she saw less the thunder of heaven than the menace of hostile vice. On one side stood a man who—whatever his errors—had given his life to public virtue and spiritual purity; and on the other she saw the assault of alarmed selfishness, headed by a lustful, greedy, lying, murderous old man, Rodrigo Borgia, now raised to the pinnacle of infamy as Pope Alexander the Sixth.

The fine shades that soften such antitheses rarely appear except to neutrals—those who can afford to notice folly in martyrs and prudence in the men who burn them. Romola could not afford that kind of clarity. She needed a strength that neutrality never gives. And in this sentence from Rome, which simplified and ennobled Savonarola’s resistant position by showing how wide the struggle was, she felt—as one feels, in a crowded sickroom, when an experienced hand arrives and sets to work without flinching—a sort of rescue from the threatening isolation of criticism and doubt.

For the Frate was now drawn out of that smaller and more personal antagonism against Florentine enemies into which he so often fell in the unchecked excitement of the pulpit. He stood, instead, as a man appealing to the Christian world against the vicious use of ecclesiastical power. Standard-bearer, yes—leaping into the breach. Life is never so clear, never so temptingly simple, as when one watches a generous act that risks everything. The heart beats faster at the sight, and one almost believes that to choose the higher path will not break us.

By a new current of such enthusiasm, Romola was carried through the difficult summer days.

Yet she kept her other burden shut within her. She had ventured on no word to Tito that could betray her late interview with Baldassarre, or the revelation Baldassarre had flung at her like a handful of bitter herbs. What would such speech win? No admission of truth. More likely a cool smile, a light sarcasm about her compassion for an assassin. Baldassarre—shattered, wandering—was evidently helpless. The danger was not that he would strike Tito, but that Tito, finding his traces, might pursue some new scheme for ridding himself of the injured man who haunted him like a sound you cannot locate: a footstep at night, always just beyond the door.

Romola felt she could do nothing decisive until she saw Baldassarre again and learned the full truth about that “other wife”—learned whether she was the wife to whom Tito had first been bound. The thought of that other wife, with all it implied, was a wound that cut into her hereditary pride with a sharpness that surprised her, as if some ancient family taste—salt and iron—had been suddenly forced upon her tongue. It mingled with the earliest memories of her illusory love, and it began to eat away what lingering tenderness still clung to the old image of her husband. Because she could not doubt the rest of Baldassarre’s revelation, she shrank from Tito with a horror that might have broken into passionate words in spite of herself—if he had not been more than usually absent from home.

Like many of the wealthier citizens in that time of pestilence, he spent the intervals of business chiefly in the country. The agreeable Melema was welcome at many villas; and since Romola had refused to leave the city, he had no need to provide a country residence of his own. His absence left the house quieter—but not peaceful. A quiet house can be like a pantry with the door shut: the very stillness makes you think of what is stored inside, what might be spoiling unseen.

At last, in the later days of July, when the public troubles that had absorbed her activity and much of her thought began to ease, Romola found herself less defended against her private misery. The Plague had almost disappeared. The position of Savonarola looked more hopeful under a favourable magistracy, who were writing urgent vindicatory letters to Rome, entreating the withdrawal of the Excommunication. Romola’s healthy, vigorous frame began to pay its debt: after continuous excitement and over-exertion there came the inevitable languor—heaviness in the limbs, a thirst that no water quite met, a weariness like bread that has been twice baked and left too long in heat. But her mind would not let her remain at home without some peremptory occupation, except during the sultry hours when the very air seemed to press down like a hand.

In the cool of morning and evening she walked out constantly, changing her direction as much as she could, with a vague hope that if Baldassarre still lived she might meet him. She listened as she walked—not only with her eyes. In one street she would hear the return of ordinary life: a knife tapping a board, a woman beating dust from cloth, a vendor calling figs or beans in a voice that carried like a bell across a little piazza. In another, the city still held remnants of sickness: a muffled sob behind shutters, a cough that seemed to bruise the air, the creak of a cart that had lately borne the dead and now carried only firewood. Florence’s sounds—so easily made harsh by fear, so easily softened by habit—entered her like food, nourishing or nauseating, and each change in them altered the weight of her thoughts.

Perhaps, she told herself, some illness had brought on a new paralysis of memory; perhaps he had forgotten where she lived—forgotten even that she existed. That was the most sanguine explanation she could find, like seasoning a bitter dish because one cannot bear it plain.

The explanation she felt most probable was simpler, and colder.

That Baldassarre had died of the Plague.
