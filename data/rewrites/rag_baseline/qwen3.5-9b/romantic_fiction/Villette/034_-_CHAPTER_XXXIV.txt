Thursday afternoon arrived with a heaviness that matched the sky, and Madame Beck summoned me to inquire if my duties permitted a brief excursion into the city to attend to certain commissions. With my schedule clear, I readily pledged my services. She promptly handed me a requisition list detailing the specific wools, silks, and embroidery threads required for the pupils' projects. Equipping myself against the threatening gloom of a sultry, cloud-choked day, I stepped toward the street door, only to be recalled instantly by Madame's voice summoning me back to the dining hall.

"Pardon, Miss Lucy!" she exclaimed, her tone suggesting a sudden, impromptu realization. "I have just remembered one final errand for you, provided your benevolence does not consider itself already burdened?"

Naturally, I professed my availability with eager assent. Seizing the opportunity, Madame retreated to the small salon and returned carrying a delicate basket. Inside rested an assortment of exquisite hothouse fruits—rosy, flawless, and inviting—nestled among the dark, waxy leaves and pale, star-shaped blossoms of some exotic flora.

"Here," she declared, "it is not burdensome, nor will it mar your impeccable attire as one might expect of a domestic servant's chore. Please deliver this basket to Madame Walravens, conveying my congratulations on her festival. She resides in the lower town, Number 3, Rue des Mages. I anticipate the journey will be lengthy, but you possess the entire afternoon; do not hasten. Should you not return before dinner, I shall ensure a meal is reserved, or Goton, who holds you in high regard, will delight in preparing a special treat for your benefit. You shall certainly not be forgotten, my dear Miss Lucy. And, oh, please!" She called me back once more. "Ensure you insist on speaking directly to Madame Walravens and handing the basket into her own hands to avoid any misunderstanding, for she is a notably meticulous individual. Farewell! Until we meet again!"

Finally released, I proceeded to the shops. The selection process for silks and wools proved tedious, requiring careful matching and deliberation, but eventually, the lists for the slippers, bell-ropes, and bags were finalized. Every item—from the slides and tassels for the purses to the final touches of the "tripotage"—was accounted for. Only the delivery of the fruit and the transmission of the felicitations remained.

I actually welcomed the prospect of a prolonged walk deep into the ancient, somber Basse-Ville. The evening sky above the city had settled into a mass of black-blue metal, glowing faintly at the edges before igniting slowly into a heavy, oppressive red. I feared a high wind, for storms demand a vigorous exertion of strength and active resistance that I invariably find painful; however, the sullen downpour, the thick snowfall, or the dark rush of rain require only resignation—a quiet surrender of one's garments and self to be drenched. In exchange, such weather sweeps a great capital clean, carving a silent path through broad, majestic streets, petrifying a living city as if by Eastern enchantment, transforming Villette into a phantom realm of Tadmor. Thus, let the rains fall and the floods descend; yet, before I embrace the deluge, I must first dispose of this basket of fruit.

An unknown clock from an unidentified tower tolled the third quarter past five, Jean Baptiste's voice now too distant to hear, as I arrived at the street and house Madame Beck had described. It was hardly a street; it seemed rather a segment of a square, quiet and secluded. Grass sprouted between the broad grey flagstones, and the houses loomed large and ancient, their rear sides obscured by trees hinting at hidden gardens. Antiquity brooded over this district; commerce had been banished. Once, wealthy men had held dominion here, and grandeur had made its seat. That church, with its dark, half-ruinous turrets overlooking the square, was the venerable and formerly opulent shrine of the Magi. But wealth and magnificence had long since spread their gilded wings and departed, leaving these ancient nests vacant, perhaps to house penury temporarily, or standing cold and empty, mouldering untended through the winter months.

Crossing this deserted "place," where drops nearly the size of five-franc pieces darkened the pavement, I observed no sign of life save for an infirm old priest moving past, bent and supported by a staff—the embodiment of age and decay. He emerged precisely from the house to which I was directed. Pausing before the door just as it closed behind him, I rang the bell. He turned to regard me, and did not quickly avert his gaze; perhaps he perceived me, laden with a basket of summer fruit and lacking the dignity age typically commands, as an incongruous figure in such a setting. Had a young, ruddy-faced maid opened the door, I might have deemed her equally out of harmony with her dwelling. However, finding myself confronted by a very elderly woman clad in an antique peasant costume—a cap both hideous and costly, featuring long flaps of native lace, a petticoat and jacket of coarse cloth, and sabots resembling tiny boats rather than shoes—it seemed appropriate, soothingly in character.

Her facial expression, however, lacked the soothing quality of her attire. Nothing more cantankerous had I ever witnessed. She barely responded to my inquiry regarding Madame Walravens; indeed, she would likely have snatched the fruit basket from my grasp had not the old priest, hobbling forward, intervened to check her. He listened attentively to the message I carried. His apparent deafness made communication difficult, but eventually, he grasped the necessity of seeing Madame Walravens personally and delivering the fruit directly to her. Speaking to the aged woman not in French but in the aboriginal tongue of Labassecour, he persuaded her to allow me to cross the inhospitable threshold. Escorting me upstairs himself, he ushered me into a salon of sorts and left me there.

The room was spacious, boasting a magnificent old ceiling and almost ecclesiastical windows of colored glass, yet it felt desolate and, under the shadow of the impending storm, strangely menacing. Beyond lay a smaller room where the blind covering the single window was drawn tight. Through the deep gloom, few details of the furniture were visible. I amused myself trying to discern these sparse items, particularly drawn to the outline of a picture on the wall.

Gradually, the image seemed to dissolve. To my bewilderment, it shook, sank, and rolled backward into nothingness. Its disappearance revealed an arched opening leading to a winding passage and a mystical spiral staircase, both constructed of cold, uncarpeted, unpainted stone. Descending this donjon stair came a rhythmic *tap, tap*, like a stick striking the steps. Soon, a shadow fell upon the stairs, followed finally by the awareness of a substance.

Was this actual substance, this apparition approaching me? This obstruction partially darkening the arch?

It drew nearer, and I beheld it clearly. I began to comprehend my location. Indeed, this old square deserved its title as the Quarter of the Magi; indeed, the three towers overlooking it rightly claimed as godfathers three mystic sages of a dead and dark art. Here, hoar enchantment prevailed. A spell had opened a portal to elf-land: that cell-like room, the vanishing picture, the arch, the passage, and the stone stair were all components of a fairy tale. Even more distinct than these scenic details stood the principal figure—Cunegonde, the sorceress! Malevola, the evil fairy. What manner of being was she?

She might have been three feet tall, yet she possessed no defined shape. Her emaciated hands rested upon one another, pressing the golden knob of a wand-like ivory staff. Her face was large and fixed, positioned not upon her shoulders but before her breast; she seemed to lack a neck. One would say there were a century in her features, and perhaps more in her eyes—her malignant, unfriendly eyes, framed by thick grey brows and livid lids. They regarded me with a severe, dull displeasure.

This entity wore a gown of brocade dyed a brilliant blue, saturated as the gentianella flower, covered in a large pattern of satin foliage. Over the gown draped a costly shawl, gorgeously bordered and so expansive for her stature that its multi-colored fringe swept the floor. Her most striking attributes, however, were her jewels: long, clear earrings blazing with a luster that could not be borrowed or feigned; rings on her skeletal hands featuring thick gold hoops and stones of purple, green, and blood-red. Hunchbacked, dwarfish, and doting, she was adorned like a barbarian queen.

"Que me voulez-vous?" she rasped, her voice possessing the rough timbre of male rather than female old age. Indeed, a silver beard bristled her chin.

"I deliver my basket and my message," I replied.

"Is that all?" she demanded.

"It is all," I said.

"Truly, it was well worth while," she answered. "Return to Madame Beck and tell her I can procure fruit whenever I desire it, and as for her congratulations, I mock them!" With this, the courteous dame turned her back.
