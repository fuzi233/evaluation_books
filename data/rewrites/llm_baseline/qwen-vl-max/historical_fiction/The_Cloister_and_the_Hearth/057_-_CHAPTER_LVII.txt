A week had passed since the last encounter, and now the two companions sat side by side, each absorbed in their own task—but with very different hearts. Pietro moved restlessly, hurling himself into his work with sudden bursts of energy, producing astonishing strokes in moments, only to abandon it in frustration, muttering curses at his own handiwork. Then, as if driven by some inner storm, he would return to it with fierce intensity, only to fling it aside again with a groan. Meanwhile, Gerard worked steadily, quietly, his face calm, his hands sure—the steady Dutchman untouched by the tempests that raged around him.

To speak plainly, Gerard, who had never known a friend he could not guide or influence, had quietly placed his companion under his sway. Their shared labor was painting playing cards—simple enough, yet vital for survival. When finished, the proud artist took up his own picture to begin his daily rounds, seeking buyers among the city’s patrons. Gerard asked him to carry the cards along, to try selling them as well.

Pietro looked at him as though he’d just proposed stealing from a saint. But after a moment’s silence, he gave in—embracing Gerard warmly in the Italian fashion, then carefully drying the latest cards under the sun, which now shone bright and generous over Rome.

Left alone, Gerard penned a few Greek letters on parchment, then mended a small tear in his hose. His landlady found him thus, and teased him gently: “Are there no women in this house? Or have you forgotten how to speak to them?”

“Finish your work,” she said, “and come talk to Teresa—my friend I told you about. Her husband is no great man, and prides himself on knowing the powerful.”

Gerard went down. And there stood Teresa—none other than the Roman matron he had once saved from disaster. “Ah, madama!” he exclaimed. “Is it truly you? The good woman did not tell me so. And the little fair-haired boy—how fares he? Was he none the worse for that strange journey in the boat?”

“He is well,” she replied softly.

“What are you two talking about?” the landlady asked, staring between them. “And why do you tremble so, Teresa mia?”

“He saved my child’s life,” Teresa whispered, struggling to keep her composure.

“What! Your lodger? And he never said a word? You shameless creature—how can you look me in the face?”

“Do not speak harshly to him,” Teresa pleaded. Turning to her friend, she began to recount the full tale of Gerard’s courage, describing how he had risked all to save her son. As she spoke, Gerard stood red-faced, scarcely recognizing his own actions—gratitude had painted them far more noble than they were.

“And to think,” she continued, “you asked me to serve this lodger, whom I knew only through your praise. Oh, Fiammina, that was enough for me. Dear youth, in serving you, I serve myself.”

The two women then launched into a passionate discussion of the barriers artists faced—fees, commissions, petty gatekeepers who blocked access to patrons. Teresa smiled sadly at Gerard’s innocence in leaving his artwork at the doors of the great, unaccompanied by bribes or promises.

“What!” she said. “Without offering a coin to the servants? Without even giving them a share to let the signori see your wares? It might as well have been thrown into the Tiber.”

“Well-a-day,” sighed Gerard, feeling the weight of his naivety.

Then Teresa offered a solution: “Go to a city nobler, less greedy than this. La corte Romana non vuol’ pecora senza lana—Rome wants sheep with wool.” She paused, deep in thought, and promised to return the next day.

The landlady clapped her hands. “Teresa has a plan,” she declared.

Hardly had she left when Pietro returned—his face dark as thunder. Gerard exchanged a glance with the landlady, then followed the Italian upstairs, ready to offer comfort.

“Have they rejected your masterpiece again?” he asked.

“As before,” Pietro grumbled.

“Then they are fools,” said Gerard cheerfully.

“That’s not the point,” Pietro snapped.

“What *is* wrong?”

“They bought the cards!” he roared, slamming his fists against the air.

“All the better,” Gerard said, beaming.

“They flew at me for them! They pretended to be indifferent, but I saw the hunger in their eyes. I feigned ignorance, I played the fool—and lo! I pillaged them like thieves. Curse these fools!”

He threw a dozen silver coins onto the floor, stamped upon them with furious steps, danced over them with wild eyes, kicked them wildly—sending them spinning across the room.

Gerard dropped to his knees and gathered them up one by one, tucking them gently into his purse.

“You should smile at their foolishness,” he said. “And turn it to profit.”

“And I will,” Pietro muttered, still fuming. “We’ll paint a pack a day. We’ll make the whole city gamble itself into ruin—while we live like princes on its vices and blindness.”

He paused. “There was one queen… I would have kept her back. You painted her, brother. She had hair like autumn fire, eyes like sapphires, and above all—soul.”

Gerard lowered his voice. “Pietro,” he said softly, “I painted that one from my heart.”

The Italian nodded slowly, his eyes gleaming. “You love her so much, yet leave her.”

“Yes,” Gerard said quietly. “It is because I love her so deeply that I have wandered this long, weary road.”

Their conversation was cut short by the landlady calling from below: “Come down! You’re wanted.”

Gerard descended, and there stood Teresa once more.

“Come with me, Ser Gerard,” she said.
