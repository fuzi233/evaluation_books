"This man contrives a secret 'twixt us two, / That he may quell me with his meeting eyes / Like one who quells a lioness at bay."

These lines were inscribed above a letter Gwendolen discovered upon her table.

"DEAREST CHILD,—I have been expecting to hear from you for a week. In your last correspondence, you mentioned that the Langens contemplated leaving Leubronn for Baden. How could you be so thoughtless as to leave me in uncertainty regarding your address? I am in the utmost anxiety lest this missive fail to reach you.

In any event, you were to return home by the end of September; however, I must urgently implore you to come back as soon as possible. Should you exhaust your funds, I shall be powerless to provide further assistance, and you must absolutely refrain from borrowing from the Langens, for I possess no means to repay them.

This is the sad truth, my child—I wish I could soften the blow, yet a dreadful calamity has befallen us all. You know nothing of business affairs and will likely struggle to comprehend the gravity of the situation, but Grapnell & Co. have failed to the tune of a million pounds, and we are utterly ruined—your Aunt Gascoigne included, save for your Uncle's benefice. Consequently, the family must subsist by selling the carriage and investing the proceeds for the boys' education. Every asset our poor father labored to secure for us is now destined to settle our liabilities. I possess nothing that can truly be called my own.

It is preferable that you learn this immediately, though it rends my heart to deliver such news. Naturally, we cannot help wondering how ill-timed your departure proved. Yet, I shall never reproach you, my dear child; I would shield you from all distress if I could. On your journey home, you will have ample time to prepare for the drastic changes awaiting you. We may depart Offendene forthwith, hoping Mr. Haynes, who previously expressed interest, is ready to purchase the rectory. Regrettably, we cannot move into the rectory itself, as there is not a single corner available. We shall be forced to seek some humble dwelling to shelter us, relying on your Uncle Gascoigne's charity until further provisions can be arranged. I shall be unable to settle debts with tradesmen or cover servants' wages.

Summon your fortitude, my dear child; we must resign ourselves to God's will. It is agonizing to submit to Mr. Lassman's wicked recklessness, which rumors attribute as the cause of this failure. Your poor sisters can only weep alongside me, offering no practical aid. Were you here, there might be a break in the clouds—I always felt it impossible that you were destined for poverty. If the Langens choose to remain abroad, perhaps you can place yourself under someone else's care for the remainder of the trip. But please, come as soon as you can to your afflicted and loving mother,

The initial impact of this letter upon Gwendolen was half-stupefying. The implicit confidence that her destiny awaited her—a life of luxurious ease where any trouble would be elegantly clothed and comfortably provided for—had been stronger in her own mind than in her mother's, nourished by her youthful vitality and that innate sense of superior claim that occupied a vast portion of her consciousness. It was nearly as difficult for her to instantly accept that her station had plummeted into poverty and humiliating dependence as it would have been to suddenly feel the chilling reality of her own mortality.

She stood motionless for several moments, then casually tossed aside her hat and mechanically glanced into the mirror. The coils of her smooth, light-brown hair remained perfectly arranged, suitable for a ballroom. On other evenings, Gwendolen might have lingered before her reflection with pleasure—an allowable indulgence—but now she took no conscious note of her beauty. She simply stared straight ahead, as if jarred by a hateful noise, waiting for confirmation of its source.

By and by, she collapsed into the corner of the red velvet sofa, picked up the letter, and read it twice deliberately. Finally, she let it drop to the floor, resting her clasped hands on her lap and sitting perfectly still, shedding no tears. Her instinct was to survey and resist the situation rather than to wail over it. There was no inward exclamation of "Poor mamma!" Her mother had rarely seemed to derive enjoyment from life; if Gwendolen felt pity at this moment, she would have directed it toward herself—for was she not naturally and rightfully the primary object of her mother's anxiety?

What possessed her was anger, resistance, and bitter vexation. She resented having lost her winnings at roulette. Had her luck held true for just one more day, she would have secured a handsome sum to bring home, or she could have continued playing until she amassed enough to support them all. Was it not still possible? She possessed only four napoleons in her purse, but she owned ornaments she could sell—a practice commonplace among stylish society at German spas, requiring no shame. Even without her mother's letter, she likely would have decided to liquidate an Etruscan necklace she hadn't worn since arriving, doing so with an agreeable sense of living intensely and escaping the mundane. With ten louis at her disposal and a return of her former fortune, which seemed probable, what better course existed than to continue gambling for a few days?

If her friends at home disapproved of her methods of acquiring funds—as they certainly would—the money would nonetheless be hers. Gwendolen's imagination dwelled on this scenario, conjuring agreeable consequences, though not with the unbroken confidence and rising certainty characteristic of a true gambler's mania. She had approached the roulette table not out of passion, but in search of it. Her mind remained sanely capable of calculating balanced probabilities. While the allure of winning tempted her, the specter of losing thrust itself forward with alternating strength, creating a vision from which her pride sank sensitively.

Resolved never to inform the Langens of her family's misfortune, nor to incur any debt to their compassion, she calculated that parting with significant jewelry would invite intrusive inquiries and remonstrations. The path posing the least risk of intolerable annoyance was to pawn her necklace early the next morning, inform the Langens that her mother demanded her immediate return without specifying reasons, and board the train for Brussels that evening.

She had no maid accompanying her, and the Langens might make difficulties regarding her solitary return, but her will was peremptory. Refusing to retire to bed, she lit the brightest fire she could manage and began to pack, working diligently. Throughout the process, she was haunted by visions of the coming day: now by tedious explanations and farewells, the whirlwind journey toward a transformed home; now by the alternative of staying just one more day and standing once again at the roulette table. Yet, in every latter scene, the presence of Deronda loomed, watching her with exasperating irony. These two keen experiences were inevitably revived together, beholding her again forsaken by luck. This importunate image undeniably swayed her resolve toward immediate departure, urging her packing to a point where changing her mind became inconvenient.

When she finally entered her room, the clock had struck twelve. By the time she verified that she had packed only the essentials, the faint dawn was filtering through the white blinds, dimming her candles. What purpose served sleep? Her cold bath had been sufficient refreshment, and she noted that a slight trace of fatigue around her eyes only enhanced her allure. Before six o'clock, she was fully equipped in her gray traveling dress, complete with her felt hat, intending to walk out as soon as she could expect to encounter other ladies heading to the springs.

Happening to sit sideways before the long strip of mirror between her two windows, she turned to gaze at herself, leaning her elbow on the chair's back in an attitude seemingly chosen for a portrait. It is possible to harbor a strong self-love devoid of self-satisfaction, indeed accompanied by a self-discontent that is more intense because one's own core of egoistic sensibility becomes a supreme preoccupation. However, Gwendolen knew nothing of such internal strife. She possessed a *naïve* delight in her fortunate self, a trait for which any but the harshest saintliness would grant indulgence in a girl who daily witnessed a pleasant reflection of that self in her friends' flattery and in the looking-glass.

Even amidst this onset of troubles, while she sat gazing at her image in the growing light, lacking anything else to occupy her, her face gathered a complacency gradual as the morning's cheerfulness. Her beautiful lips curled into a more and more decisive smile until, finally, she removed her hat, leaned forward, and kissed the cold glass, which had appeared so warm. How could she believe in sorrow? If it attacked her, she felt the strength to crush it, defy it, or flee from it, as she had already done. Anything seemed more plausible than enduring miseries, great or small.

Madame von Langen never ventured out before breakfast, allowing Gwendolen to safely conclude her early walk by returning home through the Obere Strasse, where the necessary shop was certain to be open after seven. At that hour, any observers she cared to avoid would either be strolling in the spring regions or still tucked away in their bedrooms. Certainly, there was one grand exception: the *Czarina*, a hotel from whose vantage points eyes might follow her up to Mr. Wiener's door.
