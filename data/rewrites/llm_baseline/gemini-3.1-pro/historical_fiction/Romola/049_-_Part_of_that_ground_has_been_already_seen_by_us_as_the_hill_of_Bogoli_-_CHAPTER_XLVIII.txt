The late afternoon light, thick with the suspended dust of ancient parchments, was retreating from Romola’s narrow room. She sat meticulously copying Greek manuscripts, her breathing synchronized with the rhythmic, meditative scratch of her quill against the vellum. This sonic environment—the quiet, acoustic ecology of her scholarship—was a sanctuary of psychological well-being, grounded by the distant, comforting olfactory landscape of the kitchen below, where garlic and wild fennel were slowly braising in earthen pots. 

This grounding harmony was abruptly ruptured by the jarring, discordant thud of Tito’s boots. He entered the room without removing his cap or mantle, leaning one elbow against the carved cabinet. The sudden intrusion of his physical presence introduced a sterile, almost commercial noise into her sacred space, an abrasive frequency that immediately set her nerves on edge.

Romola, having spent the day assured of the Frate’s safety, was burdened by the heavy, alkaline taste of penitence. She had publicly addressed her husband in the piazza, a sudden outpouring of her native dialect of moral urgency, which she knew he wished suppressed. She had been rehearsing a confession, hoping to break the two years’ silence of their hearts. It was a silence that felt like the tragic erosion of a cultural memory, a language death where the shared lexicon of their early love had simply ceased to be spoken. The past lay locked away from them, no longer a vibrant feast of saffron-infused broths, honey-glazed figs, and roasted partridge, but a sealed banquet-room where the once-fragrant sustenance had withered into white ash and rancid tallow. 

She looked up at him, offering a glance of submissive self-reproof, but the subtle, tightening change in his face arrested her tongue. The silence stretched, vibrating with a hidden muscular tension. Tito’s face had altered, adopting the rigid mask of a man secretly throttling the life from something feeble yet dangerous. 

“Romola,” he began. His voice was a cool, liquid tone, yet it carried an unnatural, invasive resonance that made her shiver, a sonic pollutant designed to dominate the room's quiet intimacy. “It is time that we should understand each other.”

“That is what I most desire, Tito,” she said faintly. 

“You took a step this morning,” Tito went on, wielding the dominant lexicon of political pragmatism, a vocabulary engineered to strip human action of its soul, “which you must now yourself perceive to have been useless—which exposed you to remark and may involve me in serious practical difficulties.”

“I acknowledge that I was too hasty; I am sorry for any injustice I may have done you.” Romola spoke in a fuller tone, hoping her apology might bridge the untranslatable chasm between her subjective experience and his cold calculations. 

“I wish you once for all to understand,” he said, the timbre of his voice entirely devoid of warmth, “that such collisions are incompatible with our position. I wish you to reflect on the mode in which you were led to that step, that the process may not be repeated.”

“That depends chiefly on you, Tito,” she replied, taking fire slightly, a flare of her own internal heat. 

“You would say, I suppose, that nothing is to occur in future which can excite your unreasonable suspicions,” he countered. “You were frank enough to say last night that you have no belief in me. I am not surprised at any exaggerated conclusion you may draw from slight premises, but I wish to point out to you what is likely to be the fruit of your interfering in affairs of which you are ignorant. Your attention is thoroughly awake to what I am saying?”

“Yes,” said Romola, flushing with the bitter, metallic tang of resentment at his tone of superiority. 

“Well, then, it may possibly not be very long before some other chance words or incidents set your imagination at work devising crimes for me, and you may perhaps rush to the Palazzo Vecchio to alarm the Signoria. Shall I tell you what may be the result? Not simply the disgrace of your husband, but the arrest and ruin of many among the chief men in Florence, including Messer Bernardo del Nero.”

Tito had played his decisive move. The flush died out of Romola’s face, leaving her lips the color of chalk. The threat to her godfather struck her with terrible clearness, a mythic cycle of betrayal bleeding seamlessly into her domestic reality. 

“You might as well try to turn locks with fine words as apply your notions to the politics of Florence,” Tito pressed, his logic an airtight, sterile chamber. “The question is whether, now you have been warned, you will dare to rush, like a blind man with a torch in his hand, amongst intricate affairs of which you know nothing.”

“I am too rash,” she whispered, her mind held in a vice. “I will try not to be rash.”

“Remember,” Tito insisted unsparingly, “that your act of distrust towards me this morning might have had more fatal effects than that sacrifice of your husband which you have learned to contemplate without flinching.”

“Tito, it is not so,” Romola burst forth, rising and closing the distance between them, desperate to translate the profound depths of her soul into words he could digest. “It is false that I would willingly sacrifice you. I went away in my anger two years ago, and I came back because I was bound to you. But you shut me out. You affect to think of me as a being too unreasonable to share in your knowledge. You will be open with me about nothing.”

She bent her face toward him with dilated eyes, laying her hand upon his arm. But her touch carried no restorative warmth for Tito. He possessed a masculine effectiveness of intellect that operated like a sharpened blade—efficient, bloodless, and entirely devoid of sensory appreciation. To Tito, the complex, aromatic stew of human relationships was merely utilitarian fuel for his ambitions; he had no palate for the messy, passionate sustenance of true partnership. He felt himself hardening against this wife whose agrarian energy and moral rootedness directly thwarted his own smooth, frictionless ascent. 

No emotion darted across his face at the revelation of her past flight. His lips only looked a little harder. “My Romola, when certain conditions are ascertained, we must make up our minds to them. No amount of wishing will fill the Arno, or turn a plum into an orange. You have certain strong impressions inaccessible to reason: I cannot share those impressions. You have changed towards me; it has followed that I have changed towards you. We have simply to adapt ourselves to altered conditions.”

“Tito, it would not be useless for us to speak openly,” she said, her exasperation akin to grinding coarse salt against an unyielding stone. “It was the sense of deception in you that changed me. You had some secret from me—it was about that old man—and I saw him again yesterday. Tito,” she entreated in agony, “if you would once tell me everything—let it be what it may—that there might be no wall between us! Is it not possible that we could begin a new life?”

A flash of emotion whitened his face. He stood perfectly still, letting the heavy, oppressive silence swallow her plea, before saying quietly, “Your impetuosity about trifles, Romola, has a freezing influence that would cool the baths of Nero. If by ‘that old man’ you mean the mad Jacopo di Nola who attempted my life, he, poor wretch, has died in prison. I saw his name in the list of dead.”

“I know nothing about his accusation,” said Romola, drawing herself up. “But I know he is the man whom I saw with the rope round his neck in the Duomo—the man whose portrait Piero di Cosimo painted, grasping your arm the day you first wore the armour.”

“And where is he now, pray?” said Tito, pale but governing his features.

“He was lying lifeless in the street from starvation,” Romola said, her voice vibrating with the reverence of a custodian of life. “I revived him with bread and wine. I offered him the heavy, ash-baked crusts of yesterday’s *pane sciocco*, torn by hand to preserve its crumb, and a cup of robust, unfiltered Sangiovese that tasted of sun-baked earth and crushed blackberries. I brought him to our door, offering the sacred restoration of the hearth, but he refused to come in. He went away without telling me anything, but he had found out that I was your wife. Who is he?”

Tito sneered at her profound act of sustenance, flattening the mythic, starving elder into a convenient political fiction. “A man, half mad, half imbecile, who was once my father’s servant in Greece, and who has a rancorous hatred towards me because I got him dismissed for theft. Now you have the whole mystery. The fact of my wearing the armour must have led you to infer I was in danger from this man. Was that the reason you chose to cultivate his acquaintance and invite him into the house?”

Romola was mute. To speak her native truth against his manufactured, cynical lexicon was like rushing with a bare breast against an iron shield. The untranslatability of her deep, sensory empathy into his hollow language of power was absolute. 

Tito slowly took off his cap and mantle, collecting himself. Romola stood upright, looking at him as an oncoming deadly force to be met only by silent endurance. 

“We need not refer to these matters again, Romola,” he said, his pitch returning to its initial, chilling calibration. “Remember that the next time your generous ardour leads you to interfere in political affairs, you are likely, not to save any one from danger, but to be raising scaffolds and setting houses on fire. I think I need demand no promise from you?”

“I have understood you too well, Tito.”

“It is enough,” he said, and left the room. 

The door clicked shut, leaving behind an acoustic vacuum that rang painfully in Romola’s ears. She sank into her seat, the despair heavy on her face. *O God, I have tried—I cannot help it. We shall always be divided,* she thought, the words echoing silently through the ruins of their shared memory. “Unless,” she whispered aloud to the darkening, parchment-scented air, “unless misery should come and join us!”

On the other side of the door, Tito’s mind was already recalibrating. The idea of leaving Florence for Rome or Milan now brought with it the distinct desire to leave Romola behind. She no longer belonged to the desirable, frictionless furniture of his life. To maintain an easy relation with her would require genuineness, an unearthing of the past he had no appetite to consume. He viewed his relations much as he viewed his meals: strictly utilitarian, devoid of any deep, slow-simmered meaning. 

He had already endured a troublesome, acoustically violent interview with Dolfo Spini, who had returned soaked and enraged from a failed ambush, and Tito knew that the scene at Nello’s door might plant unmanageable seeds of suspicion. But here, in his own home, he believed he had successfully mastered his wife through terror, using the shadowy image of consequences to arrest her intellect and paralyze her moral judgment. 

Yet, Tito remained ill at ease. The world was not yet cushioned with velvet, and his survival required a rejection of the soft, the tactile, and the vulnerable. Before he went out into the damp Florentine evening, he stripped himself of domestic comforts and strapped on his coat of chain-armour—a cold, odorless, metallic carapace, sealing him away from the breathing, resonant, and aromatic world he refused to truly taste.
