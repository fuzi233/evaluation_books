A flicker of irritation pricked at Oleron when Miss Bengough dismissed the room itself with a casual glance, treating the space he had found so uniquely captivating as mere background noise. Indeed, she rarely raised her eyes to look around, her attention fixed elsewhere. Yet, this indifference suited him; she had always been a creature of the immediate, careless of decorum, appearing more vibrant when devouring biscuits from a paper sack than when adhering to social conventions. At thirty-four, unmarried, and possessing a robust, florid complexion—the hue of a fresh butter cake or a deep pink dog-rose—she cut a striking figure. She moved with sudden, expansive gestures, leaving trails of swirling air where her veils and scarves caught the breeze like trapped spirits. While Oleron earned his modest living through the labor of his pen, Miss Bengough "pulled a better living out of the pool," leveraging her cunning connections among drapers and milliners to acquire a wardrobe that was both varied and ostentatious.

When Oleron first arrived in his new lodging, a month prior, he had heard the frantic rustle of her skirts ascending the stairs, followed by a single, resonant knock. Now, standing in the doorway, the scent of the street clung to her garments. She tossed a bundle of journals onto a chair without ceremony.

"Do not bother to stop for me," she declared, chewing rapidly on a large hatpin as she stripped off her hat and veil. "I wasn't sure if you were settled yet, so I brought sandwiches. Coffee, I presume? No need to rise—I'll handle the kettle myself."

"Oh, quite unnecessary," Oleron replied, a welcome relief washing over him. "I'm actually relieved to be interrupted."

He cleared his desk, stacking papers away, and watched her vanish into the kitchen, the sound of running water filling the silence. Ten minutes later, he returned bearing a tray laden with coffee and food. They seated themselves opposite one another, separated by the small table holding their sustenance.

"Well," Oleron asked as she poured the dark liquid, "how does the new place strike you?"

"Hm..." She paused, considering. "One would think you were preparing for a wedding, Paul."

He chuckled softly. "Nonsense. But surely it's superior to some of our previous haunts?"

"Perhaps. I certainly preferred the last one, despite the pitch-black ceiling and the lack of a tap. How is *Romilly* progressing?"

Oleron rubbed his chin thoughtfully. "Hm... I must confess, I am not thriving with it. However, as you often say, it will likely be perfect by the time it sees the light."

"Stuck?"

"Precisely stuck."

"Would you mind reading a portion of it to me?"

Oleron hesitated. For years, he had shared fragments of his work with Miss Bengough. Her feedback was invariably sharp, practical, and occasionally illuminating, though she steadfastly shielded him from knowledge of her own writing, dismissing it as mere filler compared to his "real work."

"I fear I cannot," he admitted, continuing his dry shave. "In fact, Elsie, I haven't written a single new line of it recently—not a word. Of course, that implies progress in a certain, alarming direction. I intend to reconstruct the entire narrative."

Miss Bengough gasped. "Reconstructing?"

"Yes. I plan to alter Romilly's fundamental nature. Lately, I feel I am failing to capture her essence. As she currently stands, I have grown distant from her."

"But—" She protested, her voice rising. "You had her so vivid, so undeniably alive, Paul!"

Oleron offered a faint smile. He expected this reaction. There was undoubtedly a mirror image of Miss Bengough within his fictional creation, which explained why she found her so "real."

"But are you being serious, Paul?" she asked, her eyes wide and round.

"Absolutely."

"You mean you intend to discard those fifteen chapters?"

"Not exactly."

"The magnificent love scene?"

"I would hesitate greatly, and only do so to replace it with something I deem superior."

"And that exquisite, beautiful description of Romilly on the shore?"

"It need not be lost," he murmured, feeling a twinge of unease.

But Miss Bengough waved a broad, airy hand and let loose a torrent of words. "Truly, you are pushing yourself too hard!" she exclaimed. "I wish you would remember you are flesh and blood, living in a world of men! Do not mistake me; I would never urge you to lower your standards. Yet, bringing your art down to a level humanity can comprehend would not be a lowering—it would be a betrayal. You are sometimes so godlike! To destroy those fifteen chapters would be a wicked, criminal squandering of your gifts. Think sensibly. You have labored for nearly two decades, standing on the precipice of your triumph. Your finances are precarious—you need not admit it, I know you are near destitution—and here you propose to excise the element that will secure your fame, replacing it with something nine times out of ten that no soul will desire. You are testing my patience!"

As she spoke, Oleron shook his head slowly. This dynamic was ancient between them. The boisterous, capable journalist was an ideal companion until they reached a certain threshold, beyond which they stood alone. Elsie often claimed that if she possessed even a tenth of Oleron's genius, she could achieve anything, treating talent as a divisible commodity. She failed to grasp that his gift was qualitative, essential, and indivisible—a spirit that informed his work but remained opaque to her. Their minds diverged at that point.

"Yes, yes, yes," he said finally, weariness creeping into his tone. "Practically, you are entirely correct, and I have no rebuttal. If I could entrust *Romilly* to you, you would undoubtedly craft a masterpiece. But that is impossible. And frankly, I begin to doubt if she is worth the trouble. You understand what follows."

"What does it follow?" she pressed, her tone blunt.

"Well," he said, a wan smile touching his lips, "when one becomes convinced a task is unworthy of effort, what happens? One simply ceases."

Miss Bengough cast her gaze upward, searching the ceiling for divine intervention against such an obstinate man.

"Nonsense!" she cried eventually. "Last time I saw you, you were practically drowning in *Romilly*, churning out four chapters a week. Had you not moved, you would have completed three-quarters of the book by now. What drove you to abandon your most vital project right in the midst of it?"

Oleron attempted to deflect her with a list of grievances, but she refused to budge. Perhaps subconsciously, she suspected the truth: he was utterly exhausted by the squalor of his existence. Twenty years of garrets, attic rooms, and dingy flats had worn him down. He was tired of the grime and the shabbiness. The reward seemed infinitely distant, or if it lay close, he no longer felt the desperate hunger to seize it. To tell a man on the brink of collapse that one final push suffices is cruel if he lacks the strength to make it.

"Regardless," Oleron summarized, "I am happier here than I have been in ages. That is some form of justification."

"And you produce no work," Miss Bengough countered sharply.

Then, a simmering petulance in Oleron's chest boiled over. "And pray, why must I toil endlessly?" he demanded. "Does not happiness matter? I do not claim to hate my work when finished, but the act of creating it has become an unbearable yoke. Occasionally, in moments amidst many weeks, it ignites with a glow and thrill I recall from my youth. Those were the golden days. Now, at forty-four, it has devolved into drudgery. Few people desire it, and increasingly, neither do I. Were any sensible man to ask if I am a fool for persisting, I believe I would concede the point."

Miss Bengough's rosy face grew solemn. "Yet you foresaw all this, Paul, many years ago—and you chose it anyway."

"How could I have known?" he retorted. "I did not know. My heart whispered it to me, and I trusted it. Youth believes it possesses wisdom; then, suddenly, one discovers they are nearly fifty."

"Forty-four, Paul—"

"—Forty-four, then. And the realization strikes that the enchantment lies not ahead, but behind. Yes, I knew and I chose, if that constitutes knowing and choosing... but what a costly price youth demands for its errors!"

Her eyes dropped to the floor. Without looking up, she asked, "Do you regret it, Paul?"

"Do I not?" he seized the question. "On my honor, I have recently begun to suspect I do! What recompense do I receive for all this sacrifice?"

"You know precisely what you obtain," she answered.

He understood her implication. A nod of the finger, and she would reveal herself. She knew the truth—that he could have done no better for himself—but she dared not speak it aloud. Had he proposed marriage during the past decade, she would have answered calmly, "Very well; when?" Yet, he had never considered it.

"Your endeavor is genuine work," she continued gently. "Without artists like you, we jackals would cease to exist. You and a handful of others bear the weight of everything upon your shoulders."
