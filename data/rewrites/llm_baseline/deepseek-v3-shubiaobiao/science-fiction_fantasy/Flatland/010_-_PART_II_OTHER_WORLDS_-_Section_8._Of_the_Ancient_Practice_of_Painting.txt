If you’ve journeyed with me this far, you’ll understand why I say life in Flatland is, in many ways, dreary. Not for lack of intrigue—our history brims with rebellions, schemes, and the ceaseless dance of politics—nor because our mathematical puzzles fail to fascinate. No, the dullness I speak of is of the senses, the stifling sameness of a world where every sight is but a line, varying only in shade.

Yet it was not always so.

Long ago, before the Circles tightened their grip, color once blazed across Flatland. Tradition whispers of a forgotten age, six centuries past, when a lone Pentagon—Chromatistes, as the chronicles name him—stumbled upon the secrets of pigment. He painted his home first, then his servants, then his kin, and at last, himself. And what wonders followed! No longer did his neighbors fumble to distinguish his front from his back. His presence commanded attention without the need for shouted declarations of identity. Wherever he moved, crowds parted; no jostling, no confusion.

The revolution spread faster than fire in dry grass. Within weeks, Squares and Triangles abandoned their monochrome lives. Even the stubborn Dodecagons surrendered within months. By the end of a year, only the highest Circles and the lowliest Isosceles—our women and priests—remained untouched by color.

Nature, it seemed, had drawn a line. The reformers argued, *"Distinction of sides implies distinction of color!"* But women, being Lines, had no sides to speak of, and priests, who claimed the perfection of Circles, boasted a single, seamless edge. Thus, they stood apart, unpainted, unmoved by the chromatic frenzy.

Ah, but what a time it was! To walk among a people adorned in vivid hues—every gathering a living mosaic, every military parade a spectacle beyond imagining. Twenty thousand Isosceles flipping their bases from black to violet in unison; Equilateral militias striped red, white, and blue; artillerymen spinning like kaleidoscopes beside their scarlet cannons. They say even a great Circle, overcome by the beauty of his own army, cast down his scepter and cried, *"I would rather wield a brush than a crown!"*

Language itself grew richer in those days. Words dripped with borrowed radiance, and the poetry of that era still lingers in our driest modern texts. But like all bright things, it faded. The Circles, ever wary of disorder, stamped it out. Now, only the faintest echoes remain—whispers of a time when Flatland was not just a world of shapes, but of *light.*

And so we are left with lines. Only lines.
