As evening deepens and Mr. Sapsea finds his own profound thoughts beginning to feel slightly repetitive despite their grandeur, he often takes a stroll through the Cathedral Close. He enjoys parading past the churchyard with an air of ownership, nurturing a benevolent landlord's pride in his generosity toward Mrs. Sapsea, whom he has publicly honored. He delights in catching glimpses of strangers peering through the railings, perhaps reading his tombstone, and is convinced that anyone exiting quickly must be "retiring with a blush," exactly as his monument suggests.

His status has recently swelled further with his election as Mayor of Cloisterham. Without mayors—and there are many—he insists that the entire fabric of society would collapse. Mayors are frequently knighted for their speeches, which act like explosive devices firing shells into the English language. Mr. Sapsea intends to deliver such a speech. *Rise, Sir Thomas Sapsea!* Truly, he is the salt of the earth.

Since their initial meeting over port, epitaphs, backgammon, beef, and salad, Mr. Sapsea's friendship with Mr. Jasper has blossomed. Jasper has been welcomed warmly at the gatehouse, where he once played the piano for Sapsea, figuratively tickling his ears for a significant duration. What Sapsea admires in the young man is his eagerness to learn from elders and his solid core. Proof of this loyalty appeared when Jasper sang not frivolous tunes favored by foreign foes, but a hearty anthem praising King George III, urging him to crush all rival nations and sweep the seas. Essentially, Jasper suggested that Providence erred in creating such a tiny nation of tough-hearted people alongside so many other troublesome groups.

On this damp evening, Mr. Sapsea walks slowly near the churchyard, hands clasped behind his back, scanning for a blushing stranger. Instead, he rounds a corner to find the Dean speaking with the Verger and Mr. Jasper. Sapsea offers a bow and feels instantly elevated to a rank surpassing even the Archbishops of York or Canterbury.

"You are clearly planning to write a book about us, Mr. Jasper," the Dean declares. "A book about us! Well, we are ancient and should make excellent material. While we lack immense wealth, we possess great age. Perhaps you will include that in your work and highlight our grievances."

Mr. Tope chuckles delightedly at the notion.

"I have absolutely no intention of becoming an author or an archaeologist, sir," Jasper replies. "It is merely a whim. Even for this whim, Mr. Sapsea here bears more responsibility than I do."

"How so, Mr. Mayor?" the Dean asks, nodding kindly at his "Fetch."

"I am unaware," Sapsea responds, glancing around for clues before examining his own attire in minute detail.

"Durdles," Mr. Tope hints.

"Ay!" the Dean echoes enthusiastically. "Durdles, Durdles!"

"The truth is, sir," Jasper explains, "my interest in the man was truly sparked by Mr. Sapsea. His knack for drawing out the reclusive and peculiar first prompted me to give the man serious consideration, though I had certainly noticed him before. You wouldn't be surprised, Mr. Dean, if you'd witnessed Mr. Sapsea handling him in his own parlour as I did."

"Oh!" cries Sapsea, catching a metaphorical ball with effortless pomp. "Yes, yes. The Very Reverend the Dean refers to that? Indeed. I happened to introduce Durdles and Mr. Jasper. I consider Durdles a 'Character.'"

"A character, Mr. Sapsea, that you can turn inside out with a few skilled strokes," Jasper notes.

"Nay, not quite that," the lumbering auctioneer counters. "Perhaps I exert a little influence, or gain a slight insight. The Very Reverend the Dean must remember that I have seen the world." Sapsea steps back to admire the Dean's buttons.

"Well!" the Dean says, surveying his surroundings to locate his copyist. "I trust, Mr. Mayor, you will apply your study of Durdles to the noble task of persuading him not to break our esteemed Choir-Master's neck. We cannot afford it; his head and voice are too precious."

Mr. Tope laughs heartily, settling into a respectful murmur implying that any gentleman would honor such a compliment by having his neck broken.

"I shall take personal responsibility, sir," Sapsea states loftily. "I will assure Mr. Jasper's safety. I will tell Durdles to be careful. He will heed my words. How is the danger currently manifest?" he asks, looking around with majestic patronage.

"Only due to my moonlight expeditions with Durdles among the tombs, vaults, towers, and ruins," Jasper replies. "You recall suggesting, when we first met, that as a lover of the picturesque, it might be worthwhile?"

"I remember!" the auctioneer exclaims, genuinely believing his recollection.

"Acting on your suggestion," Jasper continues, "I have taken several daytime rambles with this extraordinary old fellow, and tonight we intend to explore the shadows."

"And here he is," the Dean announces.

Indeed, Durdles approaches, carrying his dinner bundle. Seeing the Dean, he removes his hat and attempts to slouch away with it tucked under his arm, but Mr. Sapsea intercepts him.

"Take care of my friend," Sapsea instructs him.

"What friend o' yourn is dead?" Durdles asks bluntly. "No orders have arrived for any deceased friend."

"I mean my living friend there."

"Oh! Him?" Durdles says. "He can look after himself, can Mister Jaspers."

"But do you take care of him too," Sapsea presses.

Durdles surveys the mayor sullenly from head to toe.

"With respect to his Reverence the Dean, if you mind your own business, Mr. Sapsea, Durdles will mind his own."

"You're in a bad temper," Sapsea says, winking at the group to demonstrate his mastery of the situation. "My friend concerns me, and Mr. Jasper is my friend. And you are my friend."

"Don't get into the habit of boasting," Durdles warns gravely. "It'll stick to you."

[Illustration: Durdles cautions Mr. Sapsea against boasting]

"You're in a bad temper," Sapsea repeats, flushing slightly before retreating to the crowd.

"I admit it," Durdles concedes. "I dislike liberties."

Sapsea winks a third time, signaling to the audience that he has successfully managed the matter, and strides away.

Durdles bids the Dean good evening, adds, "You'll find me at home, Mister Jaspers, as agreed, whenever you need me; I'm heading home to clean myself," and slouches out of sight. This promise to clean himself is one of the man's inexplicable compromises with reality; he, along with his hat, boots, and clothes, remains perpetually dusty and gritty, showing no sign of ever being scrubbed.

As the lamplighter dots the quiet Close with lights, climbing his small ladder—a fixture under whose shadow generations grew up—all Cloisterham would shudder at the thought of removing—the Dean retreats to dinner, Mr. Tope to tea, and Mr. Jasper to his piano. Bathed only in the firelight, he chants choir music in a low, beautiful voice for hours until darkness fully settles and the moon prepares to rise.

Then, he gently closes the piano, swaps his coat for a pea-jacket, retrieves a large bottle from its wicker case, dons a low-crowned, flap-brimmed hat, and slips softly outside. Why does he move so cautiously tonight? No external reason is obvious. Could there be a hidden sympathy stirring within him?

Heading for Durdles' unfinished dwelling, a hole in the city wall, he sees a light burning within. Moving silently among the gravestones and monuments, already touched by the rising moon, he reaches the door. Two journeyman masons have left their great saws stuck in blocks of stone nearby; two skeletal figures from the Dance of Death might grin in the shadows, ready to slice the stones of the next two victims destined to die in Cloisterham. Likely, however, they think little of mortality now, being alive and perhaps merry.

"Ho! Durdles!"

The light shifts, and Durdles appears at the door. He seems to have been "cleaning" himself with a bottle, jug, and tumbler, as no other cleaning tools are visible in the bare brick room with exposed rafters.

"Are you ready?"

"I am ready, Mister Jaspers. Let the old ones come out if they dare when we go among their tombs. My spirit is ready for them."

"Do you mean animal spirits, or ardent spirits?"

"The one's the other," Durdles answers. "And I mean both."

He grabs a lantern from a hook, checks matches in his pocket, and they depart together, bundle and all.

It is surely an inexplicable expedition! That Durdles, who usually prowls among old graves like a ghoul, should venture forth to climb and dive without a clear objective is strange enough; but that the Choir-Master would accompany him to study moonlight effects in such company is another matter entirely.
