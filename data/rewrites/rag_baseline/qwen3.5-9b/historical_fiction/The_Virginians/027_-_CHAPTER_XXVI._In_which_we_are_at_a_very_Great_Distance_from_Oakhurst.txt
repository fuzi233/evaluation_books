The air inside the White Horse Tavern tasted of stale punch and unwashed linen. Beyond the eating-room windows lay a bowling-green, a patch of manicured earth where guests might sip tea or watch the world pass. Dinner concluded simultaneously for the three gentlemen, and Mr. Morris proposed they move to the Green for a bottle.

"My Lord Chesterfield," said my Lord March, "would adjourn to the Dust Hole under the pretense of thirst."

"Each gentleman has his own method of descending to the deuce," Jack Morris replied, accepting the logic. "My weakness is simply a bottle."

"My Lord Chesterfield's deuce is deuce-ace," corrected my Lord March. "He cannot keep away from cards or dice."

"My Lord March possesses not one devil, but several," Jack countered, listing them with rhythmic precision: gambling, racing, betting, drinking, eating, money, women. "You have fallen into bad company, Mr. Warrington, when you found him. He will play you for every acre in Virginia."

"With the greatest pleasure in life!" my Lord March interjected.

"And for all your tobacco, your slaves, your oxen, and your asses."

"Shall we begin?" my Lord March asked, turning to Jack. "You are never without a dice-box or a bottle-screw. I will set Mr. Warrington for whatever he desires."

"Unfortunately," Mr. Warrington noted, "the tobacco, slaves, and beasts are not yet mine. I am just of age, and my mother, scarcely twenty years older, has as good a chance of longevity as I do."

"I will bet you that you survive her," my Lord March offered. "I will pay a sum now against four times that sum at her death. Set a fair price over this table against the reversion of your estate in Virginia upon her departure. What is your place called?"

"Castlewood."

"A principality, I hear," my Lord March mused. "Its value has surely been exaggerated tenfold among the gossip-mongers here. How did you acquire the name? Are you related to my lord? Ah, yes—I recall. Your mother descends from the head of the house. He took the losing side in '15. I have heard this story a dozen times from my old Duchess. She knew your grandfather. He was a friend of Addison, Steele, Pope, and Milton, I dare say. It is a pity he did not remain at home and transplant the other branch of the family to the plantations."

"I have just stayed at Castlewood with my cousin," Mr. Warrington admitted.

"Hm! Did you play with him? He is fond of pasteboard and bones."

"Never, except for sixpences and a pool of commerce with the ladies."

"So much the better for both of you. But did you play with Will Esmond if he was present? I will lay ten to one you did."

Harry blushed, admitting that evenings with his cousin involved a few games of cards.

"And Tom Sampson, the chaplain?" Jack Morris cried. "Was he of the party? I wager Tom made a third, and the Lord deliver you from Tom and Will Esmond together!"

"Nay," Mr. Warrington said. "The truth is, I won of both of them."

"And they paid you? Miracles will never cease!"

"I did not speak of miracles," Harry smiled over his wine.

"You do not tell tales out of school—the *volto sciolto*—hey, Mr. Warrington?" my Lord March teased.

"I beg your pardon," Harry replied. "French is the only language besides my own of which I possess a slight knowledge."

"My Lord March has learned Italian at the Opera, and a pretty penny his lessons have cost," Jack Morris observed. "We must show him the Opera. Mustn't we, March?"

"Must we, Morris?" my Lord March asked, his tone suggesting he barely tolerated the familiarity.

Both gentlemen wore identical attire: small scratch-wigs without powder, blue frocks with plate buttons, buckskins, riding-boots, and little hats adorned with a narrow cord of lace. No outward marks of fashion distinguished them.

"I care little for the Opera," Harry said, warming to the Burgundy. "But I wish to go to Newmarket and see a good English hunting-field."

"We will show you Newmarket and the hunt," my Lord March promised. "Can you ride well?"

"I think so," Harry answered. "And I can shoot, and jump."

"What is your weight? I bet we weigh even, or I weigh more. I bet Jack Morris beats you at birds or a mark at fifty paces. I bet I jump farther than you on flat ground, right here on this green."

"I do not know Mr. Morris's shooting. I have never seen either gentleman before. But I take your bets, my lord, at whatever you please," Harry cried, flushed with wine. By now, he was more than warm.

"Ponies on each!" my Lord March shouted.

"Done and done!" my Lord March and Harry exclaimed in unison. The young man felt it was an honor for his country not to be ashamed of any wager made to him.

"We can try the final bet now, if your feet are steady," my Lord March said, springing up. He stretched his arms and limbs, examining the crisp, dry grass. He removed his boots, then his coat and waistcoat, buckling his belt tight around his waist before casting his clothes onto the ground.

Harry treated his garments with greater reverence. It was his finest suit. He removed the velvet coat and waistcoat, folding them daintily. Seeing the nearby tables slopped with drink, he carried them to a table in the eating-room, where the windows stood open.

Here, a new guest had entered. This was no other than Mr. Wolfe, eating a chicken and salad with a modest pint of wine. Harry's spirits soared. He told the Colonel he had a wager with my Lord March and asked if the Colonel would stand him halves. The Colonel claimed poverty prevented betting, but offered to witness fair play. With all his heart, he agreed. Colonel Wolfe set down his glass and stalked through the open window after his young friend.

"Who is that tallow-faced Put with carroty hair?" Jack Morris asked, the Burgundy having taken its toll.

Mr. Warrington explained this was Lieutenant-Colonel Wolfe of the 20th Regiment.

"Your humble servant, gentlemen!" the Colonel bowed, a rigid military motion.

"Never saw such a figure in my life!" Jack Morris cried. "Did you, March?"

"I beg your pardon, I think you said March?" the Colonel asked, looking genuinely surprised.

"I am the Earl of March, sir, at Colonel Wolfe's service," the nobleman bowed. "My friend, Mr. Morris, is so intimate with me that, after dinner, we are quite like brothers."

Why is not all of Tunbridge Wells here to hear this? Morris thought. Delighted, he shouted, "Two to one on my lord!"

"Done!" called Mr. Warrington. The enthusiastic Jack was obliged to cry "Done!" as well.

"Take him, Colonel," Harry whispered to his friend.

But the Colonel said he could not afford to lose, and therefore could not hope to win.

"I see you have won one of our wagers already, Mr. Warrington," my Lord March remarked. "I am taller than you by an inch or two, but you are broader round the shoulders."

"Pooh, my dear Will! I bet you you weigh twice as much as he does!" Jack Morris cried.

"Done, Jack!" my Lord March laughed. "The bets are all ponies. Will you take him, Mr. Warrington?"

"No, my dear fellow—one's enough," Jack said.

"Very good, my dear fellow," my Lord March replied. "Now we will settle the other wager."

Already arrayed in his best silk stockings, black satin-net breeches, and neatest pumps, Harry had no desire to remove his shoes, unlike his antagonist, whose heavy riding-boots and spurs were ill-suited for leaping. Before them lay a fine, even green turf of some thirty yards, sufficient for a run and a jump. A gravel walk circled this green, beyond which stood a wall and gate-sign: a field azure bearing the Hanoverian White Horse rampant between two skittles proper, with the motto naming the landlord and the animal.

My Lord March's friend placed a handkerchief on the ground as the mark from which the leapers would start, while Mr. Wolfe stood at the far end of the grass-plat to note where each landed.

"My lord went first," Mr. Warrington wrote later in a letter to Mrs. Mountain at Castlewood, Virginia, a document still extant. "He urged I take the lead; but, remembering the story of the Battle of Fontenoy my dearest George used to tell, I said, 'Monseigneur le Comte, tirez le premier, s'il vous plaît.' So he took his run in his stocking feet, and for the honor of Old Virginia, I had the gratification of beating his lordship by more than two feet—viz., two feet nine inches.
