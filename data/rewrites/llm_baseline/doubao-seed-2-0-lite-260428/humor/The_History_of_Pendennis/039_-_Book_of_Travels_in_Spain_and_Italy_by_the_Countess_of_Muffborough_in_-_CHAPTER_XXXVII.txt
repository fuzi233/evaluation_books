Let us fast forward a few months of young Arthur Pendennis’s life, skipping over all the days that were thrilling for him but mind-numbingly tedious for anyone else reading his memoirs. Last we saw him, he’d officially signed on as a professional writer — a “literary hack,” as his mate George Warrington affectionately labels both himself and Pen. Let’s be real: the life of any hack, whether you’re churning out legal briefs, parish sermons, regimental reports, or merchant ledgers, is one boring routine after another, not worth detailing at length. One day’s grind looks almost identical to the last. A scribe who has no other marketable skill but his wits? He’s got to put Pegasus himself to work for rent, bills, and the next plate of mutton, no exceptions. Those fancy poetic aerial flights? Gone. Now Pegasus only soars like Mr. Green’s famous hot air balloon: only when the ticket money’s all collected, and only on pre-advertised dates. Most days, the poor winged beast is harnessed to a cobblestone street, trotting along pulling a Hansom cab, sides heaving, knees wobbly, and more often than not getting whipped by his driver for going too slow.

That said, we’re not wasting too much pity on Pegasus here. No reason a poetic horse should get a free pass on chores, sickness, or old age any more than the rest of God’s creatures. If he gets the whip, half the time he earned it, and Warrington and I will both fight any soft-hearted poetic soul who insists that “genius” and professional writers should be exempt from the boring, grindy, tax-paying, bread-winning drudgery the rest of us schmucks have to muddle through.

So there Pen was, chugging along at the newly launched *Pall Mall Gazette*, where his editors had pegged him as a flippant, sharp, entertaining critic, and he dutifully cranked out copy every week — flippant, sure, but honest, and as well-done as he could manage. Sure, the 70-year-old historian who’d spent 25 years slaving over a 12-volume tome on the Visigoths might’ve been a touch miffed that Pen knocked out his entire review of it in two days of half-distracted reading over tea at the British Museum. And the poet who spent three years polishing sublime sonnets might’ve been furious that Pen dismissed his entire magnum opus in 20 lines of cheeky sass, acting like he was a high court judge and the poet a shivering little nobody suing for crumbs of fame. The theatre folks complained about him too, and odds are he was way too hard on their new plays. But no real harm was done! Back in Pen’s day, there were barely any great historians, great poets, or great actors to pick fights with anyway. The few that got a light whipping found it did ‘em more good than harm — and for what it’s worth, Pen never pretended he was smarter or better than the people he was reviewing, he just had a good sense of humor and fair play, and zero overblown respect for his own writing. Plus he had Warrington at his elbow, ready to tear a strip off him faster than he’d tear into any unlucky author if Pen ever got too big for his breeches.

Between these critical labors, the occasional leading article he could write in good conscience, bits sold to monthly magazines, and the very hush-hush gig as London correspondent for the Chatteris Champion (no one ever gets Pen to admit that last one, but everyone agrees the letters from the metropolis in that paper were shockingly sharp), he was pulling in almost 400 pounds a year. By his second Christmas in London, he turned up at Fairoaks and handed his mom a full hundred pounds as a partial payback for the debt he owed to Laura. You can guess what that was like: Helen Pendennis read every single word he ever published, thought he was the greatest thinker and most elegant writer the world had ever seen, considered that hundred-pound payback an act of literal angelic virtue, fussed nonstop that he was ruining his health pulling all-nighters over manuscripts, and glowed every time he told her about the famous authors and fancy society people he’d met. Any of you who’ve seen the way mothers worship their sons, or how country women hang on every letter their darlings send back from London, know exactly the vibe. If John passes his law exam, Tom gets invited to a fancy ball, George dines with a famous politician? The whole village hears about it for months! Pen only stayed two weeks that winter, short as a sugar plum, but it was the best fortnight of Helen’s whole life. The Claverings were out of town, Laura was off visiting the ancient Lady Rockminster, Doctor Portman and the rest of the local gentry called on him and treated him like visiting royalty. The holidays flew by far too fast, and soon Pen was back to the busy London grind, and Helen was alone again, sending Pen’s hundred pounds straight on to Laura. No one’s quite sure why Laura conveniently chose that exact window to go visiting, and no one’s sure if Pen was more annoyed or secretly relieved she wasn’t around.

By this point, thanks to his own sharp wits and his uncle’s introductions, Pen was well-connected across London, in both literary and high society circles. The fact everyone assumed he was a posh gentleman with a nice private income who wrote for fun, not rent, was the greatest possible advantage a young scribbler could ask for. Bacon, Bungay & Co. practically fought each other to publish his articles; Mr. Wenham invited him to dinner; Mr. Wagg greeted him like an old friend, and all the publishing crew gushed about running into him at fancy Mayfair parties. The ton loved him too: he was handsome, well-spoken, never bothered anyone about their money problems, and had a reputation for being clever. Soon he was bouncing from one dinner invitation to the next, getting to know everyone from the grubby publishers of Paternoster Row to the fancy lords of Pimlico, just as comfortable at a tavern supper with his fellow hacks as he was at a dukes’ formal dining table.

Full of energy and curiosity, Pen fit in everywhere he went. One morning he’d eat breakfast at Mr. Plover’s house, surrounded by a peer, a bishop, a star parliamentary orator, two blue-stocking socialites, a wildly popular preacher, the author of the latest runaway bestseller, and the newest “exotic lion” fresh off a boat from Egypt or the American frontier. An hour later he’d be back in the grubby back room of the newspaper office, pens, ink, and soggy wet proof sheets waiting for him. Sub-editor Finucane would stop by with the latest gossip from the Row; Captain Shandon would wander in, nod at Pen, and scribble his leading article at the far end of the table, his daily mandatory pint of sherry set beside him that the office boy brought out silently the second he spotted the captain. Before long, you’d hear the booming roar of critic Mr. Bludyer in the front hall, where that brutal fellow would grab stacks of brand new books off the publisher’s counter despite poor Mr. Midge’s timid protests, sell ‘em off to the secondhand bookstall for drinking money, eat and drink all the proceeds in a tavern booth, then stumble back to the office to write a scathing “smash job” review of the exact novel he’d just sold for beer money. In the evenings Pen would stroll over to his club, pick up Warrington for a walk to get some fresh air, work up an appetite for dinner, then go make his bow at one of the many fancy houses that had opened their doors to him. Or there was the opera, or a night at the Eagle Tavern, or a Mayfair ball, or a quiet evening smoking cigars and chatting with Warrington about books, or a great new silly song down at the Back Kitchen. Back then Pen got to see every corner of London, and it never occurred him how much fun he was having — not until decades later, when balls bored him, farces didn’t make him laugh, tavern jokes left him cold, and even the most talented ballet dancer in the world couldn’t make him get up out of his armchair after dinner. Those days are long gone now, anyway. Bludyer won’t ever bully a new author or cheat a tavern landlord out of his tab again. Shandon, the clever, broke, witty fool, is long in his grave. We buried honest Doolan just the other week, he’ll never flatter anyone or chug a whisky noggin ever again.

The London season was in full, glorious swing, and the society papers were stuffed full of reports of grand banquets, routs, and balls keeping the ton entertained. The queen was holding levees and drawing rooms at St. James’s; the bow windows of the Mayfair clubs were crammed full of red-faced, respectable old guys reading their newspapers; thousands of carriages trundled along the Serpentine; whole squadrons of dandy horsemen trotted over Rotten Row. Everyone was in town — which of course meant Major Arthur Pendennis, who counted as somebody in that crowd, was not about to miss it.

One morning, the good major sat by his fireside, a fancy bandana tied around his head, his scrawny frame wrapped in a brilliant Turkish dressing gown, feet gently steaming in a foot bath, sipping his early tea and poring over his *Morning Post*. He couldn’t face the day without his two hour long toilet routine, that first cup of tea, and his beloved newspaper. Almost no one on earth besides his valet Morgan knew just how old and wobbly the major was getting, and how many tiny little comforts he needed to make it through the day.

People love to snicker at old ladies’ elaborate beauty routines — their paint, perfume, ringlets, all the little tricks they use to patch up the damage time has done. But the ladies know full well that old bachelors are just as vain, and their toilet routines are just as ridiculous as any old beauty’s. How does old Blushington keep that permanent little rose tint on his cheeks? Where does old Blondel get the stuff that dyes his silver hair a bright, youthful gold? Have you ever seen Lord Hotspur get off his horse when he thinks no one’s watching? You’d never guess that dashing young nobleman trotting along in Rotten Row is so wobbly on his feet once he’s out of the stirrups he can barely totter up his own front steps. Have you ever imagined 60-something “Dick Lacy” in his natural state, no stiff stays holding him upright? These old bucks are just as much fun to observe as the eldest vain old Venus of Belgravia. The old reprobate who hasn’t said a real prayer in 50 years, who’s given up hard liquor but still sits up with the lads telling dirty stories over toast and water, who can no longer chase pretty girls but still talks about it like he’s 22 — if a parson dragged him up to the front of his church one Sunday and preached a whole sermon about him, the old rascal might be shocked to find out he could actually be used for something good for once. But we’re wandering off our topic, the good major, whose feet Morgan pulls out of the foot bath now, dries them daintily, and starts dressing him: tight waistband, wig, starched cravat, spotless boots and gloves, the whole nine yards.

These long toilet hours were when Morgan and his master had their confidential chats. The major hated hanging around his empty lodgings by himself, and once Morgan was done getting him dressed and handing off his letters, he had most of the rest of the day to himself. That free time the polite, sharp valet spent hanging out with his peers: the valets and butlers of half the nobility of London. “Morgan Pendennis,” as the other gentlemen’s gentlemen called him, was a regular, welcome guest at the servants’ halls of some of the fanciest houses in town, a member of two exclusive servants’ clubs, and he knew every single bit of town gossip. The footmen and valets of the ton know all their masters’ secrets, the exact same way our parlour maids chat back in the kitchen about our stinginess, our money problems, our petty little spats with our spouses. If I leave this manuscript open on my desk, Betty the maid will definitely read it, and the whole staff will be gossiping about it over tea tonight, then she’ll bring me my breakfast tomorrow with a perfectly innocent face that could fool any judge. Nothing is secret in any house, great or small. No duke is a hero to his valet, and those valets get together and chat about their masters: who’s cheap and hoards all their money, who’s in hock to moneylenders signing bills of exchange, who’s having an affair with whose wife, which father refuses to marry his daughter off to the guy the family wants even if he’s got a title — every single bit of that gossip gets passed around, and anyone who’s anyone in society knows it. Everyone says Major Pendennis knows every secret in town, scandalous and discreet all at once — but most of that information he got straight from Morgan, who went out foraging for tea and gossip at the servants’ halls. After all, what better way to learn how high society works than to start on the ground floor — the kitchen?

So they chatted away as Morgan shaved the major. The day before there had been a drawing room at St. James’s, and the major spotted that Lady Clavering had been presented by Lady Rockminster, and Miss Amory by her mother Lady Clavering, and the paper went on for paragraphs describing their gowns in all sorts of weird jargon that would confuse any 22nd century historian who found the copy. The names jogged the major’s memory of the country. “How long have the Claverings been in London?” he asked. “Morgan, have you run into any of their staff?”

“Sir Francis fired his foreign valet, sir,” Morgan replied. “Hired a friend of mine as his personal man, actually — recommended him myself. You remember Towler, sir? Tall red-headed bloke, dyes his hair, was groom of the chambers for Lord Levant until his lordship’s whole household fell apart. Bit of a step down for Towler, sir, but poor blokes can’t be fussy,” he added, sounding genuinely sympathetic.

“Devilish hard on Towler, by gad!” the major cackled, highly amused. “And not great for Lord Levant, he he!”

“Knew that mess was coming ages ago, sir — told you four Michaelmases back, when her ladyship pawned all the diamonds. Towler was the one who stuffed ‘em all into two cabs and took ‘em to Dobree’s pawn shop, and most of the family silver went the same way. You don’t remember seeing the Levant crest on that silver at the big dinner the Marquis of Steyne threw at Blackwall, with Lord Levant sitting right across from the table staring at it? Beg pardon, sir, almost nicked your chin with the razor there.”

Morgan kept talking as he carefully ran the strop over his razor. “They’ve rented a big house in Grosvenor Place, and they’re putting on a huge show, sir. Her ladyship’s planning three big parties, plus a dinner every single week — her fortune can’t possibly keep up with that.”

“Gad, she had a fantastic cook back when I was staying at Fairoaks,” the major said, not feeling even a little bit bad about the widow Amory’s dwindling fortune.

“Name was Mirobolant, sir, and he’s left their service,” Morgan said — and this time the major was genuinely sympathetic, saying he was devilishly sorry to hear the good cook was gone.

“Whole enormous row about that Mirobolant bloke, sir!” Morgan continued. “At that ball in Baymouth? The cheeky sod challenged Mr. Arthur to a duel, and Mr. Pen was two seconds away from knocking him flat and throwing him out the window — serve him right, too — but Chevalier Strong stepped in and stopped the fight. Those French cooks have got more pride and arrogance than actual lords half the time.”

“I heard a little about that fight,” the major said. “But they didn’t fire him over that?”

“No sir, Mr. Pen was real generous about it, let the whole thing blow over. He got sacked over Miss Amory, though. Those French lads all think every single woman is in love with ‘em — he climbed right up the big grapevine outside her window trying to get in, and they caught him mid-climb. Mr. Strong ran out, got the garden pump, and hosed the whole lot of him down, absolute chaos.”

“Confound his impudence! You don’t mean to say Miss Amory encouraged him?” the major yelped, catching a very odd little smirk on Morgan’s face.

Morgan immediately smoothed his face back to perfectly neutral. “No idea about that sort of thing, sir. Servants never hear that kind of gossip. Odds are there was nothing to it, families get slandered left and right. Mirobolant left bag and baggage, saucepans, his whole kitchen, even his piano — the bloke had a piano, wrote French poetry for fun. He took a lodging down in Clavering, hung around the estate forever, people said the milliner Madam Fribsy was sneaking letters between him and Miss Amory, though I don’t believe a word of it. The whole story that he tried to kill himself with charcoal fumes? All a fake between him and Fribsy, and the gamekeeper almost shot him skulking around the park one night.”

Later that very day, the Major planted himself in the big bow window of Bays’s Club on St. James’s Street, at that 4pm hour where half a dozen other respectable old fogies are lined up there exactly the same way, people-watching. Back in the Prince Regent’s day those old gents in that window were the flashiest dandies in the whole empire, even if Bays’s is now a club mostly full of men over 50. The major was staring out when he spotted his nephew Arthur walking down the street, out for a stroll with his mate Mr. Popjoy.

“Look!” Popjoy said to Pen as they passed. “Have you ever walked past Bays’s at four o’clock and not seen that whole collection of old fogies lined up in the window? It’s a human museum. They ought to cast all of ‘em in wax and stick ‘em in Madame Tussaud’s.”

“Stick ‘em in the Chamber of Horrors all to themselves,” Pen cackled.

“Chamber of Horrors! Gad, that’s perfect!”
