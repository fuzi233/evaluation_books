"Count Magnus," I muttered, tracing the grain of the mahogany table where the manuscript lay scattered like fallen leaves. "There you are. I'd give anything to see you."

This confession, spoken aloud to the empty room, was typical of the man who penned these words. Mr. Wraxall was a creature of solitude, a man past his prime who drifted through Europe without a fixed home, surviving on private funds and the quiet desperation of a scholar with too much curiosity and too little company. He had arrived in Sweden in the summer of 1863, ostensibly to write a travelogue, but driven instead by a singular, obsessive quest to reconstruct the life of a man named Magnus de la Gardie.

Wraxall's journey began in the bustling capital of Stockholm, where a local academic pointed him toward Råbäck, an ancient manor house in Vestergötland. Built shortly after 1600 by a scion of the great De la Gardie family, the estate was a striking example of red brick and stone facing, resembling the grand English houses of the Jacobean era. Yet, it was the church perched atop a rocky knoll overlooking the grounds that truly captured Wraxall's imagination. Inside, the nave was low and crowded with pews, dominated by a grotesque seventeenth-century painting of the Last Judgment, complete with screaming souls and brown, smiling demons. But it was the addition at the eastern end of the north aisle—the mausoleum—that held the promise of secrets. An octagonal structure with a domed, copper-clad roof painted black, it stood apart from the main church, accessible only via its own northern portal.

Wraxall's initial attempts to enter the tomb were thwarted. Through the keyhole, he glimpsed fine marble effigies and sarcophagi of copper, rich with heraldic ornamentation, but the door remained locked. Undeterred, he turned his attention to the archives housed within the manor. The documents were a treasure trove: meticulously kept account books, family correspondence, and journals detailing the turbulent history of the estate. They revealed a Magnus de la Gardie who was formidable, ruthless, and utterly devoid of mercy. During a period of peasant unrest, the Count had led the suppression with brutal efficiency, ordering executions and branding rebels. Local lore whispered darker tales of men whose homes were burned to the ground on winter nights, their families consumed within the flames.

Yet, the most chilling fragment emerged not from the history books, but from a volume of alchemical tracts discovered amidst the account ledgers. On a page originally left blank, Wraxall found a handwritten note attributed to the Count himself, titled *Liber nigræ peregrinationis*—The Book of the Black Pilgrimage. The text was sparse but ominous: *"If any man desires to obtain a long life... it is necessary that he should first go into the city of Chorazin, and there salute the prince..."* The sentence trailed off, leaving behind a Latin instruction to seek the remainder of the matter among the more private things.

This discovery transformed Wraxall's perspective. The Count was no longer just a historical figure; he was a practitioner of forbidden arts, a man who sought longevity and vengeance through alchemy and magic. As Wraxall walked back to the village inn that evening, his mind was so consumed by this revelation that he lost all sense of time and place. He stumbled unexpectedly onto the churchyard gates, mere moments after his dinner, staring directly at the mausoleum.

"I should dearly like to see you," he whispered to the silent tomb.

His monologue was interrupted only by the clatter of a metal object dropping on the floor inside the church—a sound that startled him but confirmed that the Count slept undisturbed below.

Seeking answers, Wraxall visited the parish deacon the following day. When asked about Chorazin, the deacon hesitated, his face pale. He spoke of the village's biblical condemnation and whispered rumors that Antichrist was destined to be born there. When pressed for specifics, the deacon retreated into vague evasions, claiming to have forgotten the tales.

Realizing the clergyman would offer no help, Wraxall turned his gaze to the innkeeper, Herr Nielsen. The landlord, initially slow to respond, finally stepped closer, his expression grave. With a visible struggle, he prepared to reveal the truth behind the Black Pilgrimage and the mysterious burden the Count had carried back from the dead.
