I spun about, my gaze sweeping the void behind me. It was a landscape of indistinct grays, a blur where fog merged with field and cloud dissolved into mountainside. The only certainty was the absence of recognition; I saw nothing I knew. Desperate to correct my failing sight with the reliability of touch, I thrust my arms outward, probing the air as I shuffled this way and that. My search yielded nothing but emptiness. Instinctively, I turned to the sole living presence nearby: the raven. It stood a few paces off, regarding me with an expression that balanced deep respect with a sharp, mocking intelligence. Suddenly, the absurdity of seeking counsel from such a creature crashed over me. I turned back, drowning in bewilderment and fear. Had I stumbled into a realm where the laws of matter and mind had ceased to function? Could a man vanish from the realm of order at any moment, becoming prey to chaos? Yet, here I was. I felt the solid ground beneath my boots and heard the rustle of wind through low-lying vegetation.

"How DID I get here?" I shouted, the question seemingly tearing itself from my throat.

"You came through the door," a voice replied. It was odd, rough-hewn, yet clear.

I scanned the area behind me, then circled the space, searching for a human silhouette. Panic seized me—the terror that madness had finally arrived. Must I now distrust my senses and my own consciousness? The very instant the doubt took root, I knew the source of the voice. It was the raven. He stood looking up, his posture one of patient expectation. Though the sun did not shine, the bird cast a distinct shadow, a dark extension that seemed woven into his very feathers.

I implore my reader to assist me in making myself intelligible—if understanding is even possible between us. I exist within a state of affairs, an economy of conditions so alien to the ways of our world that my best attempt at description is merely a sketch of what I mean. I fear I have undertaken an impossibility: to articulate the ineffable. I have already written statements I would gladly erase if I possessed the vocabulary to replace them with truth. Every time I try to pin this reality to words, I risk losing the thing itself, feeling as though I am waking from a dream where the familiar object shifts through a succession of forms until its nature becomes unrecognizable.

I recalled that a bird capable of addressing a man must possess the right to a civil answer; perhaps, as a bird, an even greater claim.

His speech carried a natural croak, lending it a rough edge, yet the tone was not disagreeable. His words offered little enlightenment, but they were not rude.

"I did not come through any door," I countered.

"I saw you come through it!" the raven asserted, his voice positive yet devoid of disrespect. "I saw you with my own ancient eyes!"

"I never saw any door!" I insisted.

"Of course not!" he returned. "All the doors you have seen—and you haven't seen many—were doors *in*. Here, you encountered a door *out*! The strange thing for you will be this: the more doors you exit, the farther you get *in*!"

"Oblige me by telling me where I am."

"That is impossible. You know nothing of 'whereness.' The only way to discover your location is to begin to make yourself at home."

"How am I to begin that where everything is so strange?"

"By doing something."

"What?"

"Anything. And the sooner you begin, the better! For until you are at home, you will find it just as difficult to leave as it is to enter."

"I have, unfortunately, found it too easy to enter. Once out, I shall not try again!"

"You have stumbled in, and may, possibly, stumble out again. Whether your entry was unfortunate remains to be seen."

"Do you never go out, sir?"

"When I please, I do, but not often, nor for long. Your world is such a half-baked place; it is so childish and self-satisfied. In fact, it is not sufficiently developed for an old raven at your service!"

"Am I wrong, then, in presuming that a man is superior to a bird?"

"That is as it may be. We do not waste our intellects on generalizations; we take man or bird as we find him. Now, I think it is my turn to ask you a question!"

"You have the best of rights," I replied, "in the fact that you CAN do so!"

"Well answered!" he rejoined. "Tell me, then, who you are—if you happen to know."

"How should I help knowing? I am myself, and I must know!"

"If you know you are yourself, you know that you are not somebody else. But do you know that you are yourself? Are you sure you are not your own father—or, excuse me, your own fool? Who are you, pray?"

Instantly, I realized I could offer him no notion of my identity. Indeed, who was I? To say "I am who" would be no answer. I understood then that I did not know myself, did not know what I was, and lacked any grounds to determine that I was one entity and not another. As for the name I bore in my own world, I had forgotten it, and I did not care to recall it, for it meant nothing here, and its origin was of no consequence. I had almost forgotten that in my world, everyone possessed a name! So I held my peace, which proved to be wisdom. What could I say to a creature such as this raven, who saw through accident straight into essence?

"Look at me," he said, "and tell me who I am."

As he spoke, he turned his back. Instantly, I knew him. He was no longer a raven, but a man of middle height, stooped and very thin, wearing a long black tail-coat. He turned again, and I saw him as a raven.

"I have seen you before, sir," I said, feeling foolish rather than surprised.

"How can you say so from seeing me behind?" he rejoined. "Did you ever see yourself behind? You have never seen yourself at all! Tell me now, then, who I am."

"I humbly beg your pardon," I answered. "I believe you were once the librarian of our house, but more *who* I do not know."

"Why do you beg my pardon?"

"Because I took you for a raven," I said, seeing him before me as plainly a raven as a bird or man could look.

"You did me no wrong," he returned. "Calling me a raven, or thinking me one, you allowed me existence, which is the sum of what one can demand of his fellow-beings. Therefore, in return, I will give you a lesson: No one can say he is himself until first he knows that he *IS*, and then what *HIMSELF* is. In fact, nobody is himself, and himself is nobody. There is more in it than you can see now, but not more than you need to see. You have, I fear, gotten into this region too soon, but none the less you must get to be at home in it. For home, as you may or may not know, is the only place where you can go out and in. There are places you can go into, and places you can go out of; but the one place, if you do but find it, where you may go out and in both, is home."

He turned to walk away, and again I saw the librarian. He did not appear to have changed, only to have taken up his shadow. I know this seems nonsense, but I cannot help it.

I gazed after him until he vanished. Whether distance hid him or he disappeared among the heather, I could not tell.

Could it be that I was dead and did not know it? Was I in what we used to call the world beyond the grave? Must I wander seeking my place? How was I to find myself at home? The raven said I must do something: what could I do here? And would that make me somebody? For now, alas, I was nobody!

I took the path Mr. Raven had gone and followed him slowly. Soon, I saw a wood of tall, slender pine trees and turned toward it. The scent of the forest met me, and I hastened to bury myself within its gloom.

Plunged into the twilight shadows, I spied something with a shine standing between two tree stems. It had no color, yet it resembled the translucent trembling of hot air rising from sun-baked ground on a radiant summer noon, vibrant like the struck chords of a musical instrument. What it was grew no plainer as I approached. When I came close, I ceased to see it clearly; only the forms and colors of the trees beyond seemed strangely uncertain. I attempted to pass between the stems but received a slight shock, stumbled, and fell. When I rose, I found myself before the wooden wall of the garret chamber. I turned, and there was the mirror, upon whose top the black eagle seemed but a moment ago to have perched.

Terror seized me, and I fled. Outside the chamber, the wide garret spaces possessed an uncanny look. They seemed to have long waited for something; it had come, and now they waited again! A shudder racked me on the winding stair: the house had grown strange to me! Something was about to leap upon me from behind! I darted down the spiral, struck the wall, fell, rose, and ran. On the next floor, I lost my way, traversing several passages a second time before finding the head of the stair. At the top of the great staircase, I regained my composure somewhat and sat recovering my breath in the library.

Nothing should ever again make me ascend that last terrible stair! The garret at the top pervaded the whole house! It sat upon it, threatening to crush me out of existence! It was the brooding brain of the building, full of mysterious dwellers, one or other of whom might appear in the library where I sat at any moment! I was nowhere safe! I would let the dreadful place, in which an aerial portal stood ever open to creatures whose life was other than human! I would purchase a crag in Switzerland and build a wooden nest of one story with never a garret above it, guarded by some grand old peak that would send down nothing worse than a few tons of overwhelming rock!

I knew all the time that my thinking was foolish, and I was even aware of an undertone of contemptuous humor in it; but suddenly it was checked, and I seemed again to hear the croak of the raven.

"If I know nothing of my own garret," I thought, "what is there to secure me against my own brain? Can I tell what it is even now generating? What thought it may present me the next moment, the next month, or a year away? What is at the heart of my brain? What is behind my THINK? Am I there at all? Who, what am I?"

I could no more answer the question now than when the raven put it to me in—"Where in? Where at?" I said, giving myself up as knowing anything of myself or the universe.

I started to my feet, hurried across the room to the masked door, where the mutilated volume, sticking out from the flat of soulless, bodiless, non-existent books, appeared to beckon me. I went down on my knees and opened it as far as its position would permit, but saw nothing. Rising again, I lit a taper and peered as into a pair of reluctant jaws. I perceived that the manuscript was verse. Further discovery was impossible. Beginnings of lines were visible on the left-hand page, and ends of lines on the other; but I could not, of course, reach the beginning and end of a single line, and was unable, in what I could read, to guess at the sense. The mere words, however, woke in me feelings which to describe was, from their strangeness, impossible. Some dreams, some poems, some musical phrases, some pictures, wake feelings such as one never had before, new in color and form—spiritual sensations, as it were, hitherto unproved. Here, some of the phrases, some of the senseless half-lines, some even of the individual words affected me in similar fashion—as with the aroma of an idea, rousing in me a great longing to know what the poem or poems might, even yet in their mutilation, hold or suggest.

I copied out a few of the larger shreds attainable and tried hard to complete some of the lines, but without the least success. The only thing I gained in the effort was so much weariness that, when I went to bed, I fell asleep at once and slept soundly.

In the morning, all that horror of the empty garret spaces had left me.
