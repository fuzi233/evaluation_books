Thus was my reception in the capital of Atlantis, and thus did I catch my first glimpse of her new regime. I freely admit to my own inertia and hesitation; yet, it was all calculated. The old bonds of duty seemed dissolved, merging into a confusing whole. Formerly, to serve the King was to serve the Priest's Clan, from whom he was chosen and whose leader he effectively became. But Phorenice was self-made, appearing to be a ruler unto herself. If Zaemon was to be believed, he was merely the mouthpiece of the Priests, and their clan had set her at defiance. How, then, could a simple honest man choose instantly between the two?

Cold logic whispered that governments exist for the greater good of the nation, and I resolved to make my choice. I must determine which authority offered the best hope for Atlantis and dedicate my efforts to strengthening it. Immediately, another path opened in this labyrinth: rumors of rebels swirled through the city—a faction of Atlanteans strong enough to besiege the capital. Before making a final decision, I felt compelled to weigh their claims against the status quo. So, on the very night I had re-anchored my foot upon the soil of my homeland, I set out to gather intelligence.

Inside the royal pyramid, no one dared oppose me. The banquet had ended abruptly following the horrific scene I have recorded above. With Tarca writhing on the floor, displaying the gruesome scars of his leprosy, even the most gluttonous guests had lost their appetites. Phorenice glowered at the feasters in silent fury for a while longer, saying nothing, until her gaze finally turned to me, softening slightly.

"You may be an honest man, Deucalion," she said eventually, "but you are monstrously cold. I wonder when you will thaw?" She smiled faintly. "I believe it will be soon. But for now, I bid you farewell. Tomorrow, we shall seize this country by the shoulders and arrange it anew."

She departed, followed by Ylga. Taking precedence over my rank, I exited next, while the crowd stood to salute. However, I paused first at Tarca and placed my hand upon his diseased flesh. "You are an unfortunate man," I said, "but I admire a brave soldier. If relief can be found for your plague, I will use my influence to secure it for you."

His thanks emerged as a mumble from his ruined mouth, causing those nearby to shudder with feigned disgust. I turned on them with a dark brow. "Your charity, my lords, appears as scant as your courage. You pretended to disdain Zaemon's teachings and mocked his priesthood, yet when it came to touching him, you displayed a discretion we would have called shameful in the old days. I would sooner be Tarca, with all his filth, than any of you standing there."

With that dismissal, I waited coldly for their obligatory salutations before walking out of the banqueting hall without another glance. Heading toward the grand gate of the pyramid, I summoned the guard officer and requested exit. The man was sufficiently deferential but hesitated.

"Has your lord's retinue not yet arrived?"

"I have none."

"My lord knows the condition of the streets?"

"I did twenty years ago. I can navigate my way."

"My lord must recall that the city is under siege," the officer insisted. "The people are starving. They prowl in packs after dark, and—while I doubt my lord fears a fight against any odds—you..."

"Exactly. I seek no street brawl tonight. Lend me a sufficient number of men. You know best what is required. I am accustomed to cities with quiet streets."

Twenty sturdy guards were assigned to my escort. Flanked by them in a double file, I marched from the perfume-heavy air of the pyramid into the cool moonlight of the city. My intention was to tour the walls and assess the disposition of these rebels.

Yet, the Gods deemed it necessary to educate me further. As I walked, the city revealed itself not as the ancient capital I remembered—the balanced accumulation of centuries—but as something transformed. The splendor remained, magnified. Entire districts had been razed to make room for new palaces and towering pyramids for the wealthy. I could not help but admire the skill and intellect behind such monumental achievements. Gazing at them under the silver moonlight, I nearly understood why Europeans and other 'barbarians' worshipped such structures as gods, deeming them too magnificent to be built by human hands alone.

However, admiration was easy; seeing the cost was undeniable. Behind each stately pile earning silver under the moon, a hundred families had been evicted, forced to shelter wherever they could in the open. Consequently, every niche was filled with sleepers, and every shadow harbored a squad of fierce creatures ready to rob or kill anyone weaker than themselves.

I am no advocate for the common people. I hold that if a man is left to hunger and cold, he will work for food and clothing; if not, he dies, and the State is relieved of a burden. Yet, beside us as we marched through the wards, lay evidence of blind oppression: starved corpses, bones protruding from gaunt skins, sprawled in the gutters. Clearly, save for the favored few, the citizens of this great capital suffered under crushing weight.

Though I deplored this abomination, I could make no strong protest. By ancient law, all people, great and small, were servants of the king, subject to his will without question. Phorenice stood in the king's place. Thus, I suppressed thoughts of treason, sighed, and kept my eyes fixed on the misery of the roadways, sending my mind upward to the stars and the High Gods, seeking guidance for my future policies.

Eventually, the winding streets brought us to the walls. Coursing alongside them and exchanging respectful nods with the sentries beating their drums, we reached the great gate guarded by the captain of the garrison.

Here, commotion was evident. Laughter echoed into the still night, interspersed with the snarls and roars of great beasts and the shrieks of the injured. Whatever transpired, it was not a surprise. The entrance gates of our capital were designed by ancient builders to match the strength of the walls themselves. Four pairs of massive stone valves stood there, each a block two men-high and thick, set within a doubled wall enclosing an open circus. Each set of gates had one opening at the inner wall and one at the outer, connected by hidden machinery so that neither could open unless the other was shut. To force them without war engines was futile; one might as well try to topple the royal pyramid with bare hands.

My escort sounded the horn hanging from the wall, summoning a warder to an arrow-slit. After inspecting our persons and business amidst the drowning noise from within, the formalities concluded. The warder shot back the massive stone bars and bolts, swinging open one colossal valve. We crowded into the chamber formed by the wall's thickness, and the warder pulled the mechanism to close the first door, bowing low before returning to me.

"I have no veil to offer you," I said abruptly. "Finish your duty. Open the other door."

"With respect, my lord, it would be safer to announce your presence first. There is a baiting underway in the circus, and the tigers are currently mere savages, respecting no personage."

"The what?"

"The tigers, if you'll allow them the name. They are baiting a group of prisoners with the two great beasts the Empress has sent to aid us in guarding the gate. But if you wish, there are ward rooms leading off this passage, and galleries overlooking the circus. From there, you can observe the sport undisturbed."

While the mere lust for killing disgusted me, I suspected the Empress's motives and felt a curiosity to witness her scheme. Stepping into the warder's lodge and then onto the galleries commanding the circus via their arrow-slits, I prepared to watch. The old builders intended these for a secondary defense line—to shoot down enemies rushing the outer doors before they could strike back. But as viewing boxes for spectacles, they served equally well.

The circus was brightly lit by moonlight, the air thick with the acrid reek of blood. What unfolded was not sport, but pure slaughter, filling me with disgust. I am not prudish regarding combat. Give a prisoner weapons, drop him in a pit with reasonably matched beasts, and let him fight to the finish; I can watch and applaud. A war prisoner earns death by natural law, preferring a final stroke in hot blood to the executioner's axe. Any brave man enjoys watching or aiding a lusty fight. But this baiting between the gates was no fair contest.

First, the beasts were unfair opponents for single men. Twenty armed men might flee from them. When the warder mentioned 'tigers,' I assumed he meant forest cats. Instead, in the circus, I beheld a pair of the most terrifying fur-bearing land beasts: the great cave-tigers. Huge monsters of such ponderous strength that, in hunger, they often dragged down mammoths if found away from their herds.

How they had been captured baffled me. Though I had hunted beasts all my days, approaching the slaying of a cave-tiger always required stratagem and infinite caution. Entrapping one alive and bringing it chained to a city was beyond my boldest schemes. Yet, here they were, proof of Phorenice's genius.
