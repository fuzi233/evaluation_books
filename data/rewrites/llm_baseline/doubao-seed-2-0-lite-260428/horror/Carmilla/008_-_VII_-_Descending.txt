VII. Descending
I cannot possibly put to words the ice-cold, festering horror that coiled in my chest when I thought of that night, even years later. This was no fleeting, dream-born dread that dissolves with the first slant of sunlight through the curtains. It seeped deeper by the hour, settling into the grain of the oak floorboards, the frayed damask of my armchair, the chipped rim of the water jug on my nightstand — as if the apparition had left a greasy, invisible print on every surface it had brushed that night.

The next day, I could not bear to be left alone for even ten seconds. I ached to tell my papa, but two warring fears tied my tongue: one part of me was certain he would laugh it off as a foolish girl’s nightmare, and I could not stomach the thought of that awful, sharp terror being dismissed as a silly jest. The other part feared he would mark me as another victim of the strange, wasting sickness that had been snatching villagers on the edge of our estate for months. I knew I was not ill with that ordinary malady — and papa had been weak with a lingering chest complaint for weeks, I could not bear to rattle him so badly it would send him to bed again.

I clung to the soft, easy company of Madame Perrodon and bright, laughing Mademoiselle Lafontaine all that day. They noticed my dragged steps, my jumping at every small creak of the house’s old bones, and at last I spilled the whole heavy, ghastly story that had been weighing on my chest. Mademoiselle snickered, but Madame Perrodon’s lips thinned, her eyes darkening with unspoken worry.
“Speaking of frights,” Mademoiselle said, still grinning, “that long lime-tree walk behind Carmilla’s bedroom window? Everyone says it’s haunted.”
“Nonsense,” Madame cut in sharply, clearly wanting to turn the conversation away from such ill-timed tales. “Who has been filling your head with such foolishness, child?”
“Martin the groundskeeper,” she replied. “He showed up twice before sunrise last week to fix the old yard gate, and both times he saw the same female figure gliding slow as smoke down that lime avenue.”
“Then he likely saw one of the milkmaids heading out to the river pastures,” Madame huffed.
“Maybe,” Mademoiselle laughed. “But Martin was so pale when he told us, you’d think he’d seen a ghost’s bared teeth. I never saw a grown man shake so bad over nothing.”
“Not a word of this to Carmilla,” I cut in quickly. “She can look right down that walk from her window, and she’s even more of a coward than I am, I think. She’d lock herself in her room for three days.”

Carmilla came down to the drawing room later than usual that day, her dark hair half-loose, her face pale as cream. “I was so frightened last night,” she breathed, the second we were left alone on the settee. “I know I would have met something unspeakable if I hadn’t had that charm I bought from that little hunchback peddler, the one I snapped at so sharply when she came to the door. I dreamed something black and slithering was creeping around the foot of my bed, and I jolted awake gasping, and for whole seconds I swore I saw a tall dark shape looming by my chimney piece. But I fumbled under my pillow for the charm, and the second my fingers brushed the fabric, the shape vanished. I know for certain if I hadn’t had it, it would have lunged for me, throttled me like it did those three poor farm girls we heard the priest bury last month.”

I told her my own encounter then, and her eyes went wide, her hands flying to her mouth. “And did you have the charm near you?” she asked, urgent as anything.
“No,” I admitted. “I left it dropped in that blue china vase on the drawing room mantel. But I swear I’m tucking it under my pillow tonight, if it works half as well as you say.”

Even now, I cannot fathom how I found the courage to lie alone in my room that night. I pinned the little charm ribbon straight to my pillowcase, right next to where my cheek rested, and I fell asleep almost instantly, deeper than I had slept in months. The next night was just as quiet — no shadows, no sounds at the chimney piece. My sleep was heavy, dreamless, soft as sinking into warm, dark silk. But when I woke, there was a heavy, sweet lassitude clinging to my bones, a gentle melancholy that did not hurt at all, almost luxurious in its soft drag.
“I told you it would work,” Carmilla said, when I told her how restful my nights had been. “I pinned mine to the breast of my nightdress, you see — I had it too far away under my pillow the first night. It’s not magic, of course, our doctor told me so. Those wandering fevers that drift through the countryside, they knock at the door of your nerves to get in, and if they can’t force their way through, they leave you with nothing but a bad fright. The charm’s been soaked in some kind of medicinal herb fumigation, that’s all — it’s an antidote to the bad air, the malaria. Evil spirits aren’t scared of bits of ribbon, are they? It’s nothing supernatural, just science, it repels the wandering sickness before it can sink its teeth in.”

I wanted so badly to believe her. I tried to, and the sharp edge of my terror dulled, softened, melted into that strange, sweet languor. For nights on end I slept deep and dreamless, but every morning I woke slower, heavier, like my bones were filled with warm lead. A strange, soft melancholy crept over me, the kind I never wanted to push away. Dim, half-formed thoughts of death fluttered at the edges of my mind, the feeling that I was slowly, gently sinking down, down, and it did not feel like something to fight. It was sad, yes, but the quiet mood it wrapped me in was so achingly sweet my soul leaned into it, unresisting.

I refused to admit I was ill. I would not tell papa, would not let anyone send for the doctor. Carmilla grew more tender, more devoted to me than ever, her long languid stares full of a hungry adoration that made me flinch sometimes, like catching a flash of something mad and feral behind her soft dark eyes. The weaker I grew, the brighter that strange ardor burned in her gaze.

I had no idea I was already deep in the throes of the strangest, most unholy malady any mortal had ever suffered. The early symptoms were so laced with honeyed fascination I did not even mind that I could barely drag myself up the stairs before noon. That fascination grew, swelled, until the horrible began to seep in, mixing with that soft sweetness, darkening, curdling, until it would twist every corner of my life into something unrecognizable.

The first shift was almost pleasant, the very edge of the slope that led straight down to Avernus. Vague, strange sensations began to visit me in my sleep. The first was a cold, lovely thrill, the kind you feel wading into a cool river, moving slow against the pull of the current. It was followed by interminable dreams, so vague I could never recall a single face or place or line of the story when I woke — but they left me breathless, drained, like I had spent hours running for my life through dark woods. Every time I woke, I could still remember being in a place almost completely black, talking to people whose faces I could never make out — especially one low, clear female voice, speaking slow, from far away, that wrapped around me with a solemn, unspeakable fear. Sometimes I felt a soft hand dragging slow down my cheek, my neck. Sometimes warm lips kissed me, soft, lingering, trailing lower, until they pressed right against my throat, and the kiss stayed there. My heart thundered, my breath came fast and shallow, a sobbing rose in my throat that turned to strangulation, until my vision swam and I blacked out completely.

Three weeks had passed since the first night I saw that apparition by my chimney. In the last seven days, the wasting had started to show on my face: my skin was pale as parchment, dark bruised circles bloomed under my wide, dilated eyes, the languor that had lived in my bones for so long was written all over my slack, tired features. Papa asked me over and over if I was unwell, but some stupid, stubborn reserve made me insist I was perfectly fine. In a way, it was true — I felt no sharp pain, no ordinary bodily ache. My sickness felt like it was in my imagination, or my nerves, and no matter how horrible the nights got, I clung to that morbid secrecy, sharing almost none of it. It could not be that terrible sickness the peasants called oupire — no one afflicted with that lasted more than three days, before they wasted away and died. Carmilla complained of bad dreams and low fevers too, but never half as bad as mine. If I had been clear-headed enough to see what was happening to me, I would have begged for help on my knees. But some heavy, unseen narcotic was thrumming through my veins, numbing all my good sense, blurring my perceptions.

Then came the dream that led straight to the oddest discovery of all. One night, instead of that low distant voice I was used to hearing in the dark, I heard another: sweet, tender, and brimming with terror, whispering, “Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin.” At the same instant, a bright light flared to life in my dark bedroom, and I saw Carmilla, standing at the foot of my bed in her white nightgown, bathed from her chin to her bare feet in one great, glistening stain of blood.

I woke screaming, the only thought in my head that Carmilla was being murdered. I sprang out of bed, and my next memory is standing on the lamplit landing, screaming for help. Madame and Mademoiselle came running out of their rooms half-dressed, alarmed out of their wits. They heard the story spilling out of me in gasps, and we all stumbled straight to Carmilla’s door, knocking as hard as we could. No answer. We pounded, we screamed her name, but there was nothing. The door was locked, and all of us were shaking so bad we could barely stand, too frightened to be in the quiet dark for another second. We ran back to my room and rang the bell so long and furiously the metal rope was almost hot under my fingers. Papa’s rooms were on the other side of the castle, too far away to hear all our racket, and none of us had the courage to run down that long dark corridor to fetch him.

The servants came thundering up the stairs at last. I had thrown on my dressing gown and slippers by then, and the other two ladies were similarly clad. When we heard the servants’ voices on the landing, we marched back out, pounded on Carmilla’s door once more to no avail, and I ordered the men to force the lock. They drove their shoulder into it once, twice, and the lock snapped. We all crowded in the doorway, holding our lamps high, and stared into the room. We called her name. No reply. Everything was exactly as we had left it the night before, her gown draped over the chair, her book open on the nightstand, the sheets turned back on her bed.

Carmilla was gone.
