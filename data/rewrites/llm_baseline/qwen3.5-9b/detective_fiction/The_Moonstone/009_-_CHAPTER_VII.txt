While I sat there in a state of bewilderment, desperately craving a few moments of solitude to regain my composure, my daughter Penelope intercepted me. Just as her late mother once did on the staircase, she immediately summoned me to recount every detail of the conference between Mr. Franklin and myself. Given the current precarious situation, the wisest course of action was to snuff out Penelope's curiosity instantly. Accordingly, I lied smoothly, claiming that Mr. Franklin and I had engaged in a lengthy discussion regarding foreign politics until our tongues grew tired, after which we had mutually succumbed to sleep in the scorching heat of the sun. One should try such a fabrication whenever a wife or daughter presses an awkward question at an inconvenient hour; rely on the natural sweetness of women to forgive the deception and smooth things over at the next opportunity.

The afternoon dragged on until my Lady and Miss Rachel returned. Needless to say, they were astounded to learn that Mr. Franklin Blake had arrived and subsequently departed on horseback. Equally needless to mention that *they* proceeded to fire off their own awkward inquiries, rendering my previous excuses about "foreign politics" and "sun-induced slumber" utterly ineffective against them. Having exhausted my supply of inventions, I attributed Mr. Franklin's arrival via the early train to one of his peculiar whims. When pressed further to confirm if his sudden departure on horseback was another whim, I simply replied, "Yes, it was," and slipped away from the interrogation—I believe with considerable skill.

Having successfully navigated these hurdles with the ladies, I anticipated greater challenges awaiting me in my own quarters. Indeed, Penelope entered, bringing with her the customary female sweetness to offer reconciliation, followed immediately by the relentless female curiosity demanding another question. This time, her inquiry concerned our second housemaid, Rosanna Spearman. Since leaving Mr. Franklin and me at the Shivering Sand, Rosanna had returned to the house in a profoundly inexplicable mental state. According to Penelope, she had shifted through every color of the rainbow. She alternated between irrational mirth and unexplained sorrow. In one breath, she fired off hundreds of questions concerning Mr. Franklin Blake; in the next, she erupted in anger at Penelope for daring to suggest that a stranger could hold any interest for her. At times she was surprised and smiling, scribbling Mr. Franklin's name inside her workbox; at others, she was overcome with tears while gazing at her deformed shoulder in the mirror.

Had she and Mr. Franklin known one another prior to today? Absolutely impossible. Had they exchanged words beforehand? Again, impossible. I could attest to the genuineness of Mr. Franklin's astonishment upon seeing how the girl stared at him, just as Penelope could vouch for the authenticity of the maid's intense inquisitiveness. Our conference, conducted in this fashion, became increasingly tedious until my daughter abruptly terminated it by voicing what I deemed the most preposterous hypothesis I had ever encountered.

"Father!" Penelope declared, with grave seriousness, "There is only one explanation. Rosanna has fallen in love with Mr. Franklin Blake at first sight!"

You may have heard of beautiful young ladies falling in love at first sight and found it plausible enough. However, consider a housemaid emerging from a reformatory, possessing a plain countenance and a disfigured shoulder, who falls in love—at first sight—with a gentleman visiting her mistress's home. Match that scenario with any storybook in Christendom if you can! I laughed until tears streamed down my cheeks. Penelope resented my merriment in a strangely delicate manner. "I never knew you to be cruel before, Father," she said softly, and withdrew.

Her words struck me like a bucket of icy water. I felt savage toward myself for experiencing unease the very moment she spoke them, yet the feeling was undeniable. Let us change the subject, if you will. I regret drifting into this account, though I foresee why it becomes necessary shortly.

Evening approached, and the bell for dinner rang before Mr. Franklin returned from Frizinghall. I personally carried his hot water to his chamber, anticipating news of some catastrophe following this extraordinary delay. To my profound disappointment—and likely yours as well—nothing had occurred. He had neither encountered the Indians en route nor upon his return. He had deposited the Moonstone in the bank, describing it merely as a valuable of immense worth, and secured the receipt safely in his pocket. Descending the stairs, I felt a sense of anticlimax following the day's excitement regarding the diamond.

How the meeting between Mr. Franklin, his aunt, and his cousin unfolded remains beyond my knowledge. I would have given much to wait at table that day. However, in my station within the household, performing the duties of a waiter (save on major family festivals) was tantamount to compromising my dignity in the eyes of the other servants—a lapse my Lady believed I was already prone to committing without needing further provocation.

The intelligence reaching me from upstairs that evening came from Penelope and the footman. Penelope noted that she had never seen Miss Rachel so meticulous about her hairdressing, nor so radiant and lovely as she appeared descending to greet Mr. Franklin in the drawing-room. The footman reported that maintaining a respectful composure in the presence of his superiors, while simultaneously attending to Mr. Franklin Blake at dinner, represented two of the most difficult contradictions he had ever faced in his training.

Later in the evening, we heard them singing and playing duets. Mr. Franklin piped high notes, Miss Rachel soared even higher, and my Lady, seated at the piano, followed them across the musical landscape with wonderful grace, audible through the open windows onto the terrace. Shortly thereafter, I visited Mr. Franklin in the smoking-room, bearing soda water and brandy. I found that Miss Rachel had completely displaced the Diamond from his thoughts. "She is the most charming girl I have seen since returning to England!" was all I could draw from him when attempting to steer the conversation toward more serious matters.

Towards midnight, I circulated through the house to secure the locks, accompanied as usual by my second-in-command, Samuel the footman. Once all doors were fastened save the side entrance opening onto the terrace, I dismissed Samuel to bed and stepped outside for a breath of fresh air before retiring myself. The night was still and oppressive, with the moon hanging full and bright in the heavens. Such was the silence outdoors that I could occasionally discern the faint, low murmur of the sea as the ground swell washed against the sandbank near the bay's mouth.

With the house oriented such that the terrace side lay in darkness, the broad moonlight illuminated the gravel walk running along the adjacent side. Gazing upward at the sky, then turning my attention to the path, I perceived the shadow of a figure cast forward from behind the corner of the house. Being elderly and shrewd, I refrained from calling out; however, unfortunately, my age and heaviness betrayed me on the gravel. Before I could stealthily circle the corner as planned, I heard footsteps lighter than my own—and seemingly more than one pair—retreating hurriedly. By the time I reached the corner, the intruders, whoever they were, had fled into the shrubbery on the far side of the walk, hiding themselves among the dense trees and bushes. From that cover, they could easily cross our fence into the road. Had I possessed forty fewer years, I might have caught them before they escaped our grounds. As it stood, I returned to summon a younger pair of legs.

Undisturbed, Samuel and I retrieved two guns and swept the perimeter of the house and the shrubbery. Satisfied that no persons were lurking nearby, we turned back. Passing over the walk where I had spotted the shadow, I noticed for the first time a small, bright object lying on the clean gravel beneath the moonlight. Upon picking it up, I discovered it to be a small bottle containing a thick, sweet-smelling liquid, black as ink.

I said nothing to Samuel. Yet, recalling Penelope's tale of the jugglers and the pouring of the ink pool into the boy's palm, I instantly suspected that I had disturbed three Indians lurking about the house. They were, in their heathenish fashion, intent on discovering the whereabouts of the Diamond that very night.
