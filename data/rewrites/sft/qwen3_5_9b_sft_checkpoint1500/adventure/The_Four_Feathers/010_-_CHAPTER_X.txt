By the time the calendar turned to May, Captain Durrance's gaze, usually fixed on the bustling horizon of Wadi Halfa, drifted wistfully toward home. Yet, unseen by him and concealed five hundred miles south, across the vast, unyielding expanse of the Nubian Desert and the infamous Belly of Stones, a drama of profound consequence was unfolding. Far removed from the frontier towns, along the forgotten track linking Berber and Suakin, the Wells of Obak lay buried beneath shifting dunes. Here, eastward, a strip of resilient trees marked the boundary between the soft sands and a harsh stony plain crowned with granite hills; westward, the desert stretched fifty-eight weary miles to the Nile, a landscape so utterly flat that a lone tuft of grass seemed a towering oak from afar, and a mere pile of stones appeared a majestic mountain. This particular May rendered the location the epitome of desolation. The sun blazed with intolerable fury since dawn, while all night long a biting wind scourged the sands, reshaping the terrain hourly—building pyramids of sand only to level them, carving valleys, and filling slopes, ensuring the land's face was perpetually altered. Vultures and sand-grouse maintained a silent, eternal dominion over the silence, and scattered among the wastes were the bleached remains of camels and the skeletal frames of former caravans, alongside the tattered remnants of a hut constructed from bent branches, evidence of Arabs who had once grazed their flocks there. As day followed night, the scorching heavens pressed down upon a hollow circle of honey-colored earth. A silence, heavy as nightfall over water, brooded over the scene, transforming the absolute stillness into a realm of mystery and waiting.

Yet, in this month of May, a solitary figure dwelt secretly amidst the wells. Each morning at sunrise, he drove forth two camels—swift, pure-bred riding-mares of the Bisharin tribe—from the shelter of the trees. He watered them, then sat by the well-mouth for three hours, gazing out over the dunes toward Berber. At midday, he led them back into the trees, feeding them delicately with *dhoura* grain spread upon a cloth, and thereafter vanished from view. For five consecutive mornings, he emerged, observed the emptiness around him, and retreated without encounter. On the sixth morning, just as he prepared to return to his hiding place, he spotted figures materialize against the sky atop a distant sand ridge: a man driving a donkey.

Abou Fatma, the Arab stationed by the well, watched intently. His gaze fell first on the animal, noting its grey hue, causing him to rise halfway. However, upon seeing his driver, his movement ceased. The man wore a *jellaba* draped forward to shield his face from the glare, yet his bare legs, jet-black against the pale sand, betrayed his race. He was a Negro. Abou Fatma sat down again, adopting an air of total indifference, neither moving nor turning as he heard the stranger's footsteps crunching softly on the sand behind him.

"Salam aleikum," the negro greeted, halting beside him. He carried a long spear, a shorter one, and a shield of hide. Laying these weapons gently on the ground, he sat by Abou Fatma's side.

Abou Fatma bowed slightly, returning the greeting. "Aleikum es salam," he replied, his posture relaxed, awaiting whatever message the visitor bore.

"Is Abou Fatma present?" the negro inquired.

Abou Fatma nodded silently.

"Two days past," the negro recounted, "a man of the Bisharin named Moussa Fedil intercepted me in the marketplace of Berber. Seeing my hunger, he offered me food. After I ate, he commanded me to bring this donkey to Abou Fatma at the Wells of Obak."

Abou Fatma glanced at the donkey with casual detachment, as if noticing it for the first time. "Tayeeb," he murmured equally nonchalantly. "The donkey belongs to me." He sat perfectly still, projecting an attitude that suggested the business was concluded and the stranger free to depart.

However, the negro held firm. "I am to meet Moussa Fedil again on the third morning from now, in the marketplace of Berber. Grant me a token to carry back, so that he knows I have completed the mission and may claim my reward."

Abou Fatma reached for the knife tucked at his waist, extracted it, and selected a slender stick from the sand. With practiced precision, he carved three notches into each end of the wood. "This shall serve as your sign to Moussa Fedil," he declared, handing the stick over. The negro secured it tightly into the corner of his wrap, loosened the water-skin from the donkey's back, filled it at the well, and slung it over his shoulders. Picking up his spears and shield, he began his ascent up the steep, loose slope. Abou Fatma watched him labor upward until the figure disappeared over the crest. Only then did Abou Fatma rise, hastening to follow. Six days prior, when Harry Feversham had departed Obak to traverse those fifty-eight miles of barren wilderness to reach the Nile, it was this same grey donkey that had borne his water-skins and provisions.

Abou Fatma guided the beast down into the shade of the trees, tethering it to a sturdy trunk before inspecting its shoulders. Beneath the fur of the left shoulder, a minute incision had been made, the skin carefully sutured with fine thread. Abou Fatma severed the stitches and gently pressed the wound apart, dislodging a tiny packet no larger than a postage stamp. It was a goat's bladder, enclosing a note folded minuscule and written in Arabic. Abou Fatma, having served as Gordon's body-servant, possessed the literacy required to decipher it. Unfolding the paper, he read the following words:

*"The houses formerly known as Berber have been destroyed, and a new town of broad streets is being erected. No longer can I distinguish the ruins of Yusef's house from the debris of a hundred others; nor does Yusef sell rock-salt in the bazaar anymore. Yet, wait for me another week."*

The author of this missive was Harry Feversham. Clad in the patched *jubbeh* of the Dervishes, his skin stained by dust and sweat, his hair frizzed on the crown and hanging in matted, seaweed-like locks down the nape of his neck, Feversham navigated the sprawling avenues of New Berber. The city was a labyrinth of gaping pits and wide, newly laid thoroughfares. To the south, separated by a mile of scrubby desert, lay the ancient town where Abou Fatma had once slept and concealed the letters—a warren of ruined structures facing narrow, winding alleys. The front walls had been demolished, the roofs stripped away, leaving only bare inner skeletons that collapsed into decay. As Feversham wandered through these ghostly lanes at night, seemingly trapped in endless corridors of crumbling stone, the foxes had already begun to dig their burrows beneath the foundations.

Feversham had calculated that one night sufficed for his stay in Berber. His plan was simple: slip through the gate at dusk, and by the time the gray light quenched the stars, he would be heading back toward Obak. Instead, he found himself moving steadily through the crowds like a man engaged in vital affairs, terrified of conversation lest his tongue betray him, listening with strained intensity for the name "Yusef." Despair haunted him intermittently, fear was his constant companion, but from the sharp agony of these emotions sprang a madness—a frenzied obstinacy, a faith as fervent as the dark religion of the people surrounding him. He convinced himself that failure was impossible, that no injustice in the grand scheme of the universe could justify laying such a crushing burden upon the one man least equipped to bear it, only to discard him callously for his efforts.

It was on that morning, three days after leaving Abou Fatma, that fear seized him completely. Climbing a slope, he first beheld the sand stretching like a lagoon up to the dark, imposing walls of the town, overshadowed by the lush foliage of giant date palms rising on the Nile bank beyond. Within those walls marched the crowded ranks of the Dervishes. To imagine escaping detection there, even for an hour, seemed sheer lunacy. *"Was it right,"* he questioned himself, *"that a man should even attempt this?"* The longer he stood, the more insistent the doubt grew. The low mud walls appeared strangely sinister; the vibrant green of the swaying palms, after so many arid days of sun and stone, transformed into an ironic summons to death. Doubts plagued him: Had he done enough for honor by venturing so near?

The sun beat down relentlessly; his strength ebbed away as if his veins had been sliced open. If captured, he reasoned, he certainly would be—and oh, how surely! He imagined the fanatic faces closing in, fierce and merciless, wondering if mutilation awaited. Shivering despite the intense heat, the profound loneliness of the place struck him, making his knees tremble. He spun about and burst into a run, leaping in a blind panic across the naked desert under the blazing sun, emitting weak, inarticulate cries from his throat.
