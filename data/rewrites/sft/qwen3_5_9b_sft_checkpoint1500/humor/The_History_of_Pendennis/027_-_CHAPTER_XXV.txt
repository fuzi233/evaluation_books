If you've read the previous pages, you likely know Sir Francis Clavering's honest, if slightly uncharitable, assessment of his wife. And honestly, the Baronet wasn't entirely off-base. Lady Clavering, far from being a woman of profound wisdom or extensive learning, had spent exactly two years in "Ackney" (a suburb of London she refused to admit was Hackney) before being shipped off to join her father in Calcutta at the tender age of fifteen. That voyage aboard the *Ramchunder*, commanded by the formidable Captain Bragg, was where fate intervened. On board that very ship, two years prior, she had met her first husband, Mr. Amory, the ship's third mate. A rebellious soul, Amory had earned the Captain's ire so thoroughly that he was locked in irons until the Cape, abandoned there, and eventually left behind by a furious Captain Bragg who simply delivered the ward to her father in Calcutta.

What followed was a whirlwind of mercantile disaster. Amory returned to Calcutta, married a wealthy attorney's daughter, became an indigo planter, failed spectacularly; became an agent, failed again; became an editor of the *Sunderbund Pilot*, failed once more. Throughout this gauntlet, he quarreled incessantly with his father-in-law and wife. His final act was a financial crash that forced him to flee for New South Wales. Somewhere amidst this chaos, he likely crossed paths with Sir Jasper Rogers, the esteemed judge, and it appears he managed to squander his remaining fortune by misusing his father-in-law's name—a crime that ultimately sealed his fate.

Finding life in India stifling and uncomfortable, the family arranged for Lady Clavering to return to Europe with her four-year-old daughter, Blanche (known as Betsy). They were joined by Mrs. Bonner, the loyal nurse introduced in the last chapter, and settled near Captain Bragg's residence in Pocklington Street. However, the summer proved a miserable, rain-soaked affair. Captain Bragg, pompous and seemingly eager to shed the "Indian lady," created an atmosphere of gloomy surveillance. Believing the entire court and the East India Company were gossiping about her husband's ruin, Lady Clavering resolved to flee. She packed her bags, escaping the bully Bragg, and embarked on a grand tour of Europe.

Her travels were legendary. She called Hackney "Ackney," spoke with a charming foreign twang, and dressed with flamboyant flair. She was a gourmand of epic proportions, preparing curries and pilaws wherever she stayed, and her generosity knew no bounds. She brought triple portions of champagne, fowl, and ham to picnics, bought endless theater boxes and tickets for masked balls to distribute freely, and prepaid months' rent for boarding houses. She became a beacon of joy for impoverished officers and dowagers alike, tramping through Brussels, Paris, Milan, Naples, and Rome. It was in Rome, where Sir Francis Clavering was also staying (struggling to pay his hotel bills alongside the Chevalier Strong), that news of Amory's death reached her. With no genuine grief for her "scapegrace" of a husband, she married the descendant of the ancient Clavering line. Thus, we arrive at the present moment: Lady Clavering, now Mistress of Clavering Park, portrayed by the celebrated painter Pinckney with her little boy beside her.

Young Miss Blanche followed her mother's peripatetic lifestyle, acquiring a robust understanding of life. Though she had a governess briefly and later attended Madame de Caramel's pension in the Champs Élysées, her life shifted dramatically when the Claverings returned to England. Following her grandfather's death and the birth of her younger brother, Blanche realized her status had plummeted. While she was still technically an heiress, her stepfather, the absent-minded Sir Francis, and her mother, who dropped her H's and lacked refinement, meant she was overshadowed by little Frank, the heir apparent. Frank was a spoiled, quarrelsome urchin who constantly trod on Blanche's toes, upset her dinner on her dresses, and kept her from inheriting the full glory of the estate. Feeling misunderstood by her dull stepfather and a mother who seemed to prefer Frank, Blanche's heart naturally yearned for something outside the family circle.

This longing manifested as a desire to settle down, prompting Sir Francis's wish, mentioned in the last chapter, that his friend Mrs. Strong might pass away so he could take Blanche as a second Mrs. Strong. Since that was impossible, any suitor was welcome. Enter Arthur Pendennis, a smart, well-educated young gentleman. He was theoretically free to propose and would have been welcomed with open arms by Lady Clavering as a son-in-law. Yet, Mr. Pen hesitated. Burdened by shame over his recent failures, his idle existence, and the poverty he'd brought upon his mother, he suffered from a debilitating case of vanity-induced shyness. He couldn't imagine proposing to the brilliant Blanche Amory, surrounded by a dozen servants, while his mother scraped by on meager meals at Fairoaks. Despair and dallying won out over gallantry.

However, the situation was complicated by the presence of Laura Pendennis. Miss Amory, charming and lively, fascinated Laura with her arts and graces. But Laura's mother, Helen, saw the devil in the details. Helen suspected Blanche of being light-minded, frivolous, and lacking reverence, detecting a selfish worldliness beneath her pretty words. Laura and Pen initially defended Blanche against Helen's accusations, with Pen laughing off his mother's jealousy ("Psha, mother! All women are jealous"). But as weeks turned into months, Helen watched with maternal anxiety as the intimacy deepened. Seeing the pair meeting daily, her heart sank. Terrified of losing Pen forever, she finally confessed her fears, revealing that she felt broken and wished to see her children united before her death. Pen, ever the patronizing comforter, kissed and soothed her, imagining Laura as his ideal partner—fair, open, and pious. He pictured her bringing roses, and the image of the two women remained etched in his mind, a tear-jerking vision of potential bliss.

Yet, reality soon intruded. Before long, Laura was forced to concede to Helen's judgment. Blanche revealed her true colors: she was selfish, unkind, and inconstant. She complained endlessly about her family's inability to understand her, mocked her mother's lack of education, and described Sir Francis as a weak, cigar-smelling oaf. Most painfully, she detailed how her mother's affection had shifted entirely to little Frank. Blanche painted a picture of isolation, claiming she was "alone, alone, alone in the world."

These grievances, though heart-rending, failed to sway Laura regarding Blanche's specific actions. For instance, when little Frank upset a glass of water on Blanche's drawing, she boxed his ears and hurled opprobrious names at him in both English and French. Similarly, she treated the boy's governess with imperial disdain, sending her on trivial errands to fetch books or pocket-handkerchiefs. Laura, accustomed to gratitude for service, found Blanche's imperious demands jarring. It struck Laura that the "fairy" she admired was merely a mortal, and not a particularly amiable one. This realization was cemented after the incident with Frank, where Blanche, reciting a poem about her "pretty baby brother" and vowing eternal devotion, proceeded to slap him. It was a harsh lesson in cynical philosophy, mirroring a similar dynamic Laura had observed in Pen's own home.

By this point, Laura grew tired of admiring Blanche. The illusion shattered, replaced by a quiet sense of superiority. Blanche, sensing this shift, became peevish and dissatisfied. The tension culminated in a "battle-royal" on a fateful day. Blanche, in a wicked mood, had been insolent to her mother, savage to Frank, cruel to the governess, and abysmal to Pincott, her attendant. Unable to attack her powerful friend directly, Blanche lashed out at everyone else, particularly Pincott, driving the poor girl from the room in tears.

Laura, hearing of this, erupted in indignation. She lectured Blanche on her wicked conduct, telling her she ought to kneel and ask forgiveness from Heaven. In a flurry of emotion, Laura fled home across the park, leaving Blanche behind. Upon returning to her mother, Laura confirmed Helen's suspicions, declaring she gave Blanche up forever. Both women embraced, and Helen secretly rejoiced, believing this separation would save Pen from a "flighty creature."

However, the true motive lay hidden. Earlier that day, while sitting together, Blanche had tried to probe Laura about Pen's past with the actress, Mrs. Pybus. Laura, embarrassed, claimed ignorance, insisting Pen had acted generously. Blanche pressed, asking if he had written Laura any poems. Laura admitted to knowing only two translations and a prize poem she found "prosy." Then, with an arch smile, Blanche produced two crumpled papers from her desk—love verses dedicated to "Undine" and a "Naiad." Laura read them, finding them vague and indistinct, and asked if Blanche had replied. Blanche laughed, saying, "No! Not for worlds, dearest!" and tucked them back into the desk.
