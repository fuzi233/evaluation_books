Consideration for the frailty of poor Lady Verinder forbade me even to whisper that I had already deduced the melancholy truth, before she chose to speak it aloud. I waited in respectful silence, privately arranging a few words of comfort for the first available moment, feeling prepared to bear any burden, however painful, that might fall to my lot.

"I have been seriously ill, Drusilla, for some time past," my aunt began, her voice trembling slightly. "And, strange to say, without knowing it myself."

I thought immediately of the countless souls perishing spiritually in that very moment, unaware of their own condition. I feared deeply that my poor aunt might be among them.

"Yes, dear," I replied softly. "Yes."

"I brought Rachel to London, as you know, seeking medical counsel," she continued. "I deemed it prudent to consult two physicians."

Two doctors! And, oh, the irony (considering Rachel's state)—not a single clergyman!

"Yes, dear?" I prompted gently.

"One of the two medical men," my aunt proceeded, "was a stranger to me. The other had been a close friend of my late husband and had always held a sincere regard for me due to that connection. After examining Rachel, he requested to speak with me privately in another room. Naturally, I expected specific instructions regarding my daughter's care. To my astonishment, he took my hand gravely and declared, 'I have been observing you, Lady Verinder, with both professional and personal concern. You are, I fear, far more urgently in need of medical attention than your daughter.'

He questioned me, and while I initially treated his inquiries lightly, I noticed that my answers caused him visible distress. It concluded with him scheduling a visit for the following day, accompanied by a colleague, at a time when Rachel would not be present. The outcome of that consultation—delivered with kindness and gentleness—confirmed what both physicians believed: precious time had been lost, irretrievably, and my condition had now surpassed the reach of their art.

For over two years, I have suffered from an insidious form of heart disease. Without alarming symptoms, it has gradually, inevitably, broken me down. I may survive for a few months, or I may depart before the sun sets tomorrow; the doctors dare not speak more definitively. It would be futile to claim, my dear, that I have not endured miserable moments since the reality of my situation became clear. Yet, I am more resigned than before, and I am diligently setting my worldly affairs in order.

My sole, crushing anxiety is that Rachel remains ignorant of the truth. Were she to learn it, she would immediately attribute my decline to worry over the Diamond, and she would reproach herself bitterly, poor child, for a fault entirely not hers. Both doctors agree the deterioration began two, perhaps three years ago. I trust you will keep my secret, Drusilla—for I perceive genuine sorrow and sympathy in your countenance."

Sorrow and sympathy! Oh, what pagan emotions to expect from a Christian Englishwoman so firmly anchored in her faith! Little did my poor aunt imagine the torrent of devout gratitude that surged through me as she neared the end of her tragic tale. Here lay a career of divine usefulness opening before me! Here stood a beloved relative, a dying soul, utterly unprepared for the Great Change, yet providentially led to reveal her plight to *Me*.

How shall I describe the joy that flooded my heart as I recalled that the precious clerical allies upon whom I could rely were to be counted not by ones or twos, but by tens and twenties? I drew my aunt into my arms; my overflowing tenderness demanded nothing less than an embrace at this critical juncture.

"Oh!" I cried to her, fervently, "the indescribable interest you inspire in me! Oh, the good I intend to do for you, dear, before we part!"

After offering a few earnest warnings, I presented her with a choice of three devoted friends, all engaged in works of mercy within her neighborhood, all inexhaustible in exhortation, and all ready to serve at my command. Alas, the response was discouraging. Poor Lady Verinder looked bewildered and frightened, meeting my suggestions with the purely worldly objection that she lacked the strength to face strangers.

I yielded—for the moment, at least. My extensive experience (gained as Reader and Visitor under the guidance of no fewer than fourteen beloved clerical friends) taught me that this required preparation through scripture. I possessed a small library of works tailored precisely to this emergency, designed to arouse, convince, prepare, enlighten, and fortify my aunt.

"You will read, dear, won't you?" I asked in my most winning tone. "Will you read if I bring you my own precious books? Turned down at the perfect passages, Aunt. Marked in pencil where you must pause and ask yourself, 'Does this apply to me?'"

Even that simple appeal—an approach so thoroughly secularized by the world's influence—startled my aunt. She replied, "I will do what I can, Drusilla, to please you," with a look of surprise that was both instructive and terrifying to behold.

Not a moment was to be wasted. The clock on the mantelpiece indicated I had just enough time to rush home, select a first series of readings (about a dozen volumes), and return in time to meet the lawyer and witness Lady Verinder's Will. Promising faithfully to return by five o'clock, I departed on my mission of mercy.

When no interests but my own are at stake, I am humbly content to travel by omnibus. However, permit me to demonstrate my devotion to my aunt's welfare by noting that, on this occasion, I committed the extravagance of hiring a cab. I drove home, selected and marked my initial collection of readings, and returned to Montagu Square carrying a dozen volumes in a carpet-bag, the likes of which, I firmly believe, are unknown in the literature of any other European nation. I paid the cabman his exact fare. He received it with an oath; instantly, I handed him a religious tract. If I had brandished a pistol at his head, this abandoned wretch could scarcely have shown greater consternation. He leaped onto his box, shouting profanely in dismay, and drove off furiously.

Quite useless, I am happy to say! I sowed the good seed regardless, tossing a second tract through the cab window.

The servant who answered the door—not the maid with the ribbons, to my immense relief, but the footman—informed me that the doctor had called and remained locked in conference with Lady Verinder. Mr. Bruff, the lawyer, had arrived a minute prior and was awaiting me in the library.

I was ushered into the library to wait. Mr. Bruff looked surprised to see me. As the family solicitor, we had met several times previously under Lady Verinder's roof. He was a man, I regret to say, grown old and grizzled in the service of the world. In his business hours, he was the chosen prophet of Law and Mammon; in his leisure, he was equally adept at reading novels and tearing up tracts.

"Have you come to stay here, Miss Clack?" he asked, glancing at my carpet-bag.

To reveal the contents of my sacred bag to someone of his caliber would have invited an immediate outburst of profanity. I lowered myself to his level and stated my business.

"My aunt has informed me she is about to sign her Will," I answered. "She has graciously asked me to serve as one of the witnesses."

"Aye? aye? Well, Miss Clack, you will do. You are over twenty-one, and you hold no pecuniary interest in Lady Verinder's Will."

*No pecuniary interest.* Oh, how thankful I felt hearing that! Had my aunt, possessing thousands, remembered poor me—to whom five pounds is a significant sum—and included my name with a small legacy, my enemies might have doubted the motives behind loading me with the choicest treasures of my library and draining my failing resources for the prodigal expense of a cab. Not the cruelest scoffer could doubt my integrity then. Much better as it was! Oh, surely, much better!

I was roused from these consoling reflections by Mr. Bruff's voice. My meditative silence seemed to weigh heavily upon this worldling, forcing him to talk against his will.

"Well, Miss Clack, what's the latest news in the charitable circles? How fares your friend Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite after the beating he received from the rogues in Northumberland Street? Egad! They're spinning a rather pretty story about that charitable gentleman at my club!"

I had overlooked his comment regarding my age and lack of financial interest, but the tone in which he alluded to dear Mr. Godfrey was too much for my patience. Bound, after the events of that afternoon, to assert the innocence of my admirable friend whenever questioned, I felt compelled to include a stinging rebuke directed at Mr. Bruff.

"I live largely outside the world," I said; "and I lack the privilege of belonging to a club. However, I know the story you refer to, and I also know that a viler falsehood has never been told."
