In the days when Britain ruled over its American colonies, one of the many uses we found for the New World was to send our unwelcome citizens there—those whose misdeeds or misfortunes made them unfit for life in the old country. Convicts, indentured servants, and younger sons who had outlived their welcome were shipped across the Atlantic, along with those whose debts and vices had driven them from England’s courts and homes. We sent them not as exiles, but as settlers, hoping they would start anew among the wilds and forests of Virginia, Maryland, and beyond. In this way, we exported not only men, but also the flaws of our society—like sending a specimen of English folly to be studied in foreign soil.

I myself may have played a small part in such an enterprise. I once helped secure a living in America for Hagan, husband of my cousin—the worthy man whom I knew to be honest and devout. Though he could find no place in England, I arranged for him to take up a pulpit in Virginia. He proved himself a loyal minister to his flock and to the Crown, fulfilling his duties with integrity. When Madam Theo heard of my hesitation on the matter, she reminded me that my actions were guided by principle, not prejudice. And perhaps she was right. Yet I confess—I would never have given him our parish at home. Nor would I have chosen him and his wife to serve our own community. I believed in Maria’s sincerity, yes—but I confess I was secretly relieved when she left for the wilderness. Such is the pride that stains even the noblest hearts.

Maria was not alone in being sent away. Another of our family, the Honourable William Esmond, Esquire, had long since exhausted every avenue of respectability in England. After borrowing, stealing, and cheating until nothing remained to take, he was dispatched to New York. His noble brother, Lord Castlewood, was glad enough to see the back of him. But soon after the troubles began, news reached us of what he was doing in his new home.

Lies and mischief followed William wherever he went. My Lord Dunmore informed me that William had publicly declared our estate of Castlewood belonged not to us, but to him—only so long as his brother pleased to allow it. He claimed that our father, out of affection for Madam Esmond, his half-sister, had granted her the estate for life—and now, he said, he was negotiating with Lord Castlewood to buy the reversion! It was a bold falsehood. The deed was safely stored at Castlewood and officially registered in Williamsburg. Still, the intent behind the lie was dangerous. Hal and I resolved, should we ever meet William, to force him into confirming his own absurd tale.

Madam Esmond’s reaction, when she heard it, was fierce. “What!” she exclaimed. “My father, the Marquis of Esmond, was a liar? And I am a cheat?” She raged that William would steal her son’s inheritance upon her death. She threatened to write not only to Lord Castlewood in London, but to His Majesty himself at St. James’s. Only my assurance—that William’s lies were common knowledge, and that no one expected him to tell the truth—prevented her from taking such drastic steps.

Soon we learned that William had become one of the most vocal Loyalists in New York. He rose to the rank of Captain, then Major, in a volunteer corps, sending letters of loyalty to every colony and declaring his readiness to die for the mother country. Meanwhile, in Virginia, the situation grew more tense. We could scarcely stay in a house without fear of unrest. Madam Esmond shut the shutters of our hired mansion in Williamsburg and fled to Richmond, where the Assembly had already dissolved. Captain Hal and his wife returned early to their plantation. I, increasingly uneasy about the course of events, divided my time between my own household and that of the Governor, who welcomed my counsel despite our differing political views.

Though tensions ran high, personal animosity had not yet taken root. Even after the Assembly disbanded—its members gathering in a tavern to discuss forming a continental congress—we still maintained friendly relations with the Governor. Gentlemen of opposing views dined together, hunted side by side, and shared the pleasures of the season. Once the session ended, the gentry returned to their counties and held meetings. With Assemblies dissolved across the colonies, it was agreed that a general congress must be convened. Philadelphia, the largest city on the continent, was chosen as the meeting place. There, the debates began—angry, passionate, and unmistakably the prelude to war.

We still professed loyalty to the King. Petitions were drafted, prayers offered. But when I visited my brother Harry at Fanny’s Mount—his new plantation near ours, separated by the Rappahannock River and close to Mattaponey—events took a turn I could not ignore.

One morning, Harry rode out on business. That afternoon, I decided to ride as well. One of the grooms told me he’d seen Master heading toward Willis’s Ordinary. Thinking nothing of it, I followed. Emerging from the woods onto a clear stretch of land near Willis’s, I came upon a surprising sight: Captain Hal on horseback, surrounded by thirty or forty men, armed with pikes, scythes, muskets, and fowling pieces. He stood with several young officers, drilling them in formation.

As I approached, a strange look crossed Hal’s face. “Present arms!” he commanded, and the ragged troop attempted the salute. “Captain Cade,” Hal said, “this is my brother, Sir George Warrington.”

“Colonel,” replied the captain, “as a relation of yours, the gentleman is welcome.” He extended his hand.

“And—” Hal added, flushing slightly—“a true friend to Virginia.”

“Yes, God willing!” I said.

At that, the entire group erupted in cheers, huzzaying for the Colonel and his brother.

After the drill, the men urged a drink at Willis’s, but Hal declined. We rode home together in silence for a while.

“So,” I said finally, “the cat’s out of the bag.”

He gave me a hard stare. “There are wilder cats in the basket,” he replied. “It must come to this, George. And you mustn’t tell Madam.”

“Good God,” I cried, “do you mean to say you’re preparing to fight the greatest nation and finest army in the world—with men like those?”

“We’ll likely get thrashed,” Hal admitted, “but then, George”—he smiled gently—“we’re young. A good whipping might do us good. Won’t it, Dolly?” He flicked his whip playfully at an old dog trotting beside him.

I did not try to argue further. I had done so before, in vain. I tried to appeal to reason—to the British cause, to duty, to the strength of the Crown. But Hal always replied the same way: “All mighty well, brother. You speak as an Englishman, and have cast your lot with your country—as I have with mine.”

And truly, there was no answer to that. The argument led only to conflict. Who had the right in the wars of the last century? The King or Parliament? The victor claimed it. And history showed that the victor, though often harsh, was usually more humane than the defeated might have been. Suppose, I thought, that the Tories had won in America—what horrors would have followed? What gallows, what executions? It was a chilling thought. I stood with the Crown, yet I found myself wishing the Whigs would prevail.

Hal, meanwhile, fought with honor. He never spoke ill of the enemy. “The officers of the British army,” he said, “are gentlemen—at least, I’ve not heard they’ve changed much since my time. There may be scoundrels among their ranks, as there are among ours. Our job is to beat His Majesty’s forces—not slander them. Any fool can do that.”

Because of a nickname given by Mr. Lee—“Chevalier Baird”—he became known throughout the Continental army. He was indeed a knight: fearless, upright, without reproach.

When I asked how such a motley crew could possibly stand against the British army, Hal answered with quiet confidence. “They can beat them,” he said. “That’s what they can do.”

“Great heavens!” I exclaimed. “You mean you’d lead that rabble against five hundred trained soldiers?”

“With my company of the 67th,” he replied, “I’d go anywhere. I admit, I know more about soldiering than they do. But suppose I’m placed on that open ground where you found us—armed as you please, with half a dozen friends hiding in the woods with rifles. Which side wins? You know best, Mr. Braddock’s aide-de-camp.”

There was no arguing with such resolve.

“You know my thoughts, Hal,” I said. “And since I’ve stumbled upon your secret, I must inform the Governor.”

“Tell him, of course,” Hal said calmly. “You’ve seen our county militia training. You’ll see the same in every colony from Georgia to the Saint Lawrence. As an old soldier, they elected me colonel. What else could I do? Come, brother—let’s ride on. Dinner will be ready, and Mrs. Fan doesn’t like me to keep it waiting.”

So we rode to his house—a wide-open dwelling, typical of Virginian gentlemen, where every friend, neighbor, and stranger was welcomed with warmth.

“So, Mrs. Fan,” I said as we entered, “I’ve discovered what game your husband has been playing.”

“I trust the Colonel will have plenty of sport ere long,” she replied, tossing her head.

My wife thought Harry had been hunting. I did not correct her. But what I had seen—and what Hal had confessed—left me deeply troubled.
