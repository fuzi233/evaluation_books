Years later, when I sorted through the stack of old family papers at home, I came across a sheet docketed in my mother’s neat, familiar handwriting: “From London, April 1760. My son's dreadful letter.” When it fell into my hands I burned the whole document at once. I would not have that record of domestic strife and grief left in our family annals for future Warringtons to gawk at, nor for disobedient sons yet unborn to hold up as proof that rebellions against parental authority had happened before them. For the exact same reason, I destroyed the angry, unyielding reply my mother sent me back in that season of tyranny, revolt, petty irritation, and raw hurt.

Maddened to my wits’ end by the pain of separation from the woman I loved, and rightly holding Mrs. Esmond to be the sole cause of the worst misery I had ever suffered, I wrote home to Virginia a letter that might, in truth, have been far more temperate—though I did my best to keep it as respectful and guarded as I could. I told her I did not know what motives had driven her to act as she did, but that I held her accountable for ruining the rest of my life, a ruin she had willfully chosen to inflict. She had torn me away from a virtuous, innocent young creature whose own hopes, health, and happiness had been shattered forever by her interference. The deed was done, I wrote, as I feared it was, and I would waste no breath judging the person who had committed it, for she would answer for it to God alone. But I did not hide from my mother that the harm she had done me was so deep and permanent that neither her life nor mine could ever mend it: the old bond of filial obedience was broken, and I would never again be the dutiful, respectful son I had been to her before.

Madam Esmond replied with a letter of immense dignity—her style and correspondence were extraordinarily elegant and refined, you must know. She did not utter a single reproach or harsh word, but coldly made clear that it was to that awful tribunal of God she had brought our quarrel, to ask for guidance, and that as far as her own conduct as a mother went, she was ready to face that judgement in all humility. Might I, as a son, she wrote, be equally able to answer for myself when the Great Judge called on me to account for whether I had honoured my father and mother? Ah, me! My grandfather quoted a line of Homer in his memoir once, remarking how we are forever dragging the gods into our petty troubles and griefs. When pride, avarice, self-interest, or the urge to dominate stokes our anger, do we not pester Heaven unceasingly to side with our cause? In our great American quarrel, did not both sides appeal to the skies for justice, sing Te Deum after every victory, and boldly declare their confidence that right would prevail? Was America right because she won? Then I suppose Poland was wrong, just because she was defeated? How do I wander off into digressions about Poland, America, and all the rest, when all the while I am thinking of a little woman long dead, who appealed straight to Heaven and backed her case with a thousand Bible texts, all because her son wanted to make a marriage that did not suit her? We appeal, we swear oaths, we fall to our knees, we demand blessings, we shriek out for judgement according to law—all the while the great world keeps turning unheeded. We pant, we strive, we struggle, we hate, we rage, we weep passionate tears, we reconcile, we run the race and win, we run the race and lose, then we pass away and other little strugglers take our place. Our days run out, our night falls, and another sun rises that shines on us no more.

I did not keep my angry letter to Madam Esmond hidden. I showed a duplicate copy to Mr. Lambert, for I wanted him to understand exactly what my relations with my mother were, and how determined I was that no future threat or quarrel would ever make me consider my separation from Theo anything but temporary. The moment I could see her again, I would. My promise to her was for all eternity, or at least binding as long as I drew breath. I made clear that she was equally bound to me, and her good father knew that much for a fact. He could separate us if he wished—just as he could slip her a dose of poison, and that gentle, obedient creature would take it and die—but any such death or separation would be his doing, for him to answer for. He was so tender to his children it verged on weakness, and he could never bring himself to subject one of them—least of all Theo—to deliberate torture. We had tried to part, and we could not. He had tried to separate us, and it was more than he had the power to do. The bars were up, but the young couple, the maid on one side and the knight on the other, loved each other just the same. The wall was built between us, but Pyramus and Thisbe whispered to each other through the cracks in the plaster. For all his grief and perplexity, Uncle Lambert had a sharp sense of humour, and he could not help but see what a sorry role he was playing. Light was already seeping through that lime and roughcast wall; the lovers were getting their hands through the gaps, then their heads. It was high time for that wall to crumble and retreat.

I do not remember the exact day-to-day, step-by-step sequence of events, nor does it matter much for the instruction of future ages. When my descendants get tangled in their own love scrapes, they will find their own way out of them. I never went back to Dean Street to live, but the doctors prescribed open-air carriage drives as the most healthful remedy for Miss Lambert’s lingering complaint. I bought a fine new horse, and rode along beside her carriage every time she went out. The old woman who kept the roadside cottage up Tottenham Court Road got to know us both very well, and nodded and winked at us in the friendliest way every time we passed. I fancy that old goody was no stranger to playing chaperone to young couples, and had hosted many a pair of star-crossed lovers under her roof before us.

Doctor Heberden and the country air worked a miraculous cure on Miss Lambert. Hetty always came along as duenna, and sometimes, on his school holidays, little Charley would ride my horse while I climbed into the carriage to sit with the girls. What a world of love-making Miss Hetty overheard! And with what exemplary patience she listened to every word! I will not say she went back to Methodist sermons after that, but it is certain that whenever we had a closed carriage, she would very kindly and considerately press her face to the window to watch the scenery. Then there were piles and piles of secret notes! Gumbo’s bandy little legs were forever trotting between my lodgings and Dean Street, and Molly, the girls’ maid, was for ever hurrying back to Bloomsbury with replies to my letters, for my sake and for her own sweetheart Gumbo’s. By the time autumn leaves started turning pale on the trees, Theo’s cheeks were flushed with the roses of perfect health again, and Doctor Heberden declared his cure was complete.

What else happened in that blessed, happy stretch of time? Mr. Warrington finished his great tragedy of *Pocahontas*. This time, the play was not just accepted by Mr. Garrick—our friend Dr. Johnson had spoken well of the work, after all—but my cousin Hagan was engaged by the manager to play the hero, Captain Smith. Hagan’s new engagement could not have come at a better time. I had been helping him and his family with sums of money that were disproportionate to my means, especially given my running feud with Madam Esmond. It was towards autumn that I got her reply to my angry April letter: she wrote back from Virginia answering war with war, restriction with restriction. But her threats hardly frightened me at all. My poor mother’s thunder could not reach me all the way across the Atlantic, and my conscience, or my own convenient casuistry, supplied me with counterarguments to all her biblical texts, so that her oracles had no power to scare me away from my purpose. I did not write to tell her, or any other of my relatives on either side of the family who had raged against my marriage, of my new happiness. What use was wrangling with them? Far better to seize the day and its sweet loves and pleasures, and leave the gossips to grumble, and the elders to dispense their unwanted advice, at their leisure.

I must own that back in the mad, angry days of our temporary separation, I had written notes of searing sarcasm to my Uncle Warrington, to my Aunt Madame de Bernstein, and to the Lord or Lady of Castlewood (I forget which exactly), thanking them for all the trouble they had taken to ruin the dearest happiness of my life, and promising them the eternal gratitude of their obliged relative. Business brought the jovial Baronet and his family to London that year earlier than usual, and Madame de Bernstein was never sorry to get back to Clarges Street and her card tables. I met all of them, and they found me perfectly well and cheerful. They concluded my match was entirely broken off, and I did not choose to undeceive them. The Baroness took heart seeing how content I was, and made many sly jokes about my philosophical, worldly-wise behaviour as a man about town. She was still dead set on finding me a rich wife, and I fear I paid no end of elaborate, teasing compliments in her drawing room to a wealthy young soap-boiler’s daughter from Mile End, whom the good Baroness was determined to marry off to me.

“You court her with infinite wit and esprit, my dear,” my pleased kinswoman said to me once, “but she does not understand half of what you say, and the other half, I think, frightens her. That tone of playful irony works very well in our circle, but you must spare it, my dear nephew, when you are talking to these common folk.”

That Miss Badge eventually married a young gentleman of royal descent but shattered fortune from a neighbouring island, and I trust Mrs. Mackshane has long since forgiven my levity towards her. There was another person at my aunt’s house who understood my ironic teasing no better than the young lady herself: that was a dowager who could remember the reign of James II, who was still alive and as worldly as ever in King George II’s time. I loved spending time with her, and if my own little children were not going to read this volume, I could fill a hundred pages with stories of the great old figures she had known in the bygone days—of George I and his mistresses, of St. John and Marlborough, of the late king and the old Prince of Wales, and the causes of their famous quarrel. But my modest muse pipes only for boys and virgins. My son Miles cares nothing for old court gossip, and if he did, he has a whole new budget of scandal from Carlton House just as naughty as any tale the old Baroness could tell. No, my dear wife, you need not shake your powdered curls at me! Papa will not scandalise our nursery with old world tittle-tattle, nor bring a blush to our innocent children’s bread-and-butter faces.

But this one piece of gossip I cannot resist telling. My aunt used to recount it with immense gusto; for to do her justice, she hated people who paraded their “goodness” in public, and sniggered at the faults of the self-righteous with uncommon satisfaction. In her later days she had no use for hypocrisy at all, which made her better than some of the whitewashed pretenders we all know. Well, to the story. My Lady Warrington was one of the tallest, most pious women of her sex, who had goodness forever on her lips and “Heaven in her eye,” like the heroine in Mr. Addison’s tedious overlong tragedy that somehow still holds the stage while far better plays have been forgotten. But she kept the whole rest of the worldly right in her other eye, and a very sharp, eager desire to climb the social ladder. When everyone supposed my marriage to Theo was broken off, what did she do but try to play on me the same arts she had used on my poor Harry, with exactly the same lack of success? It was not the beauty Flora she threw at me—Flora was reserved for a far grander match, I take off my hat at the very thought of that illustrious connection! It was Dora, the supposed “muse” of the family, that she set after me, to languish at me, pity my broken heart, read my godless tragedy, applaud it, and console me.

Meanwhile, what was the beauty Flora doing? Would you believe that my severe aunt threw a grand entertainment for the notorious Lady Yarmouth, presented her little son Miles to the royal favourite, and placed the poor boy under her august protection? That part is certain enough, but could it be true that she sent her daughter Flora to stay at Lady Yarmouth’s house, which the old King visited every day, with the exact matchmaking aims old Aunt Bernstein attributed to her? “If it hadn’t been for that fit of apoplexy, my dear,” Bernstein said, “that aunt of yours intended there to be a Countess in her own right in the Warrington family!” My neighbour and kinswoman Lady Claypole has long been dead and buried. Grow white over Flora’s grave, ye daisies! I can see my pretty Miles, dressed in the smart little uniform of the Norfolk Militia, being led up by his mother to the lady the old King delighted to honour, and that good-natured old royal favourite laying her hand on the boy’s curly head. I am often accused of being a lukewarm royalist, but I can well contrast those corrupt old times with our own, and acknowledge the difference between the late sovereign and the current one, who was born a Briton, and set an example of decorum and virtuous life for every family in the empire. And so my life passed in the pleasantest possible occupation. Being so happy myself, I could afford to feel kindly towards all those relatives who, after all, had done me no lasting harm, but only made my final happiness all the sweeter by putting small obstacles in my way. We had made no specific formal plans, but Theo and I both knew the day would come when we would never have to say goodbye again. If that day came a year from then, or ten years from then, we were ready to wait. Day after day we pored over our little plans, with Hetty as our trusted confidante. On our drives we spied out tiny cottages that we thought would suit a young couple of small means, and dreamed up all sorts of delightful, childish little economies. We were Strephon and Chloe, no less! A cottage and a brown loaf would content us, we vowed. Gumbo and Molly would wait on us, as indeed they have done from that day to this. At twenty, who is afraid of being poor? Our small hardships would only make our love stronger. The “sweet sorrow” of every day’s parting only made the next day’s meeting more delicious, and when we separated we ran home to write each other those precious, overwrought letters that all young men and women write when they are in love. Though my wife has kept them all in a great tin sugar box in her bedroom closet, and I own I have looked at them once, and even thought some of them quite pretty, I hereby order my heirs and executors to burn all of those letters unread after our deaths. I specifically request my son the Captain, who I know has no patience for reading old manuscripts, to perform that duty. Those secrets we whispered to the penny post, or passed between Gumbo and Molly, were meant only for us, and no ears of our descendants shall ever overhear them.

We got short letters one after another telling us that our dear Harry was still with the army, serving as General Amherst’s aide-de-camp after the death of his own glorious general. By mid-October, news came of the Capitulation of Montreal, and the surrender of all Canada. Harry added a short postscript saying he would ask for leave now, and return home to see the old lady in Virginia, who was writing letters as sulky as a bear, Captain Warrington dryly remarked. I could guess exactly why she was in such a temper, though her claws could not reach me across the Atlantic. I had written to my brother fairly fully about how matters stood with me in England.

Then, on the 25th of October, news broke that his Majesty George II had dropped dead at Kensington Palace, and that George III now reigned over us. I fear we grieved very little for the old king. What do two people whose hearts are full of nothing but each other care for the faraway royal family? We gladly accepted the common report that the new young prince was endowed with every possible virtue, and joined the loyal crowd crying huzzay at his accession. We simple young sweethearts, whispering our little love stories in our quiet corner, thought it could make no difference to us at all.

But who can say how great events will touch even the humblest of us? Did not our little Charley, at his school in Chartreux, impiously wish for a new king the moment he heard the news, all because the strict headmaster Doctor Crusius gave all the boys a holiday to celebrate the new king’s accession? Charley, Hetty, Theo, and I—Theo was strong enough now to walk many a delightful mile—went to watch the Heralds proclaim the new Majesty before Savile House in Leicester Fields, and a pickpocket stole a gentleman’s watch and chain right next to us, got caught immediately, and was dragged off to Bridewell for a whipping, all on account of the royal accession. If the old king had not died, the gentleman would never have been in that crowd, his chain would never have been stolen, and the thief would never have been caught. In that small, roundabout way, many of us lowly folk were connected to the great change of reign, and we would soon find ourselves directly affected by it.

It happened this way. Lord Wrotham was a great friend to the Savile House family, who knew and deeply respected his many virtues. Of all men alive, Lord Wrotham loved and esteemed his old neighbour and fellow-soldier Martin Lambert best, declaring there was no finer gentleman alive in the whole world. And Lord Bute, who was all-powerful in the new king’s first days, a nobleman who was very eager at the start of his brief, ill-fated time in office to reward merit wherever he found it, had been led to think very highly of Mr. Lambert by his old, constant friend Lord Wrotham.
