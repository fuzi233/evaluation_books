Mr. Home remained two days; and in those two days the house learned a new kind of hush—one that was not peace, but a weary listening-for-something that would not come. He could not be coaxed abroad. The hearth held him, as if the grate were a harbour and the world outside a rough sea. He sat close to the fire, sometimes sunk in a silence that seemed to make even the coals burn more softly, sometimes answering Mrs. Bretton’s steady, well-measured talk—talk that neither pestered him with pity nor left him quite alone. It had the temper of good broth: clear, sustaining, with a motherly salt in it that her years—being more than his—gave her leave to use.

Paulina, meanwhile, was blissfully busy and quietly vigilant, as if happiness, to her, must always be stitched down lest it fly away. Often her father drew her onto his knee. She would sit there a little while, small hands folded, cheek near his sleeve; then, at the first fancied stir of impatience in him, she would slide off as though she were a weight too great for love.

“Papa, put me down; I shall tire you with my weight.”

Down she went to the rug—this mighty burden—and planted herself at his feet, where her white work-box opened like a tiny larder of order: thimble, scissors, and her scarlet-speckled handkerchief spread out as solemnly as a tablecloth for a feast. That handkerchief was to be a keepsake for “papa,” and therefore must be finished before he went; and her industry—being of the kind that makes twenty careful stitches in half an hour and feels them each as an event—was pressed hard by time.

Evening brought the boy back from school, and with him the warm disturbance of youth: Graham Bretton’s step on the threshold altered the very air, as the crackle of fresh kindling alters a fire. Whatever quiet had been settled by the day—Mr. Home’s brooding, Mrs. Bretton’s composed tending, Paulina’s watchful needle—took on a quickened edge; and the quickening was chiefly because Graham and little Miss Paulina, once together, could no more keep still than two birds in the same cage.

She had made herself distant since the first evening of his arrival, when he had—by some boyish tyranny—wounded her dignity. Now, when he spoke to her, her reply was a cold little wall:

“I can’t attend to you; I have other things to think about.”

“And what things?” he would demand, amused.

“Business.”

Her “business” was always her sewing—important, slow, exact—and her face over it had the shut-lipped devotion of a housekeeper guarding a recipe. Yet Graham, with the idle generosity of boys who love to be begged, would spread temptation before her. He opened his desk and let it shine: seals, bright wax-sticks, penknives, and a scatter of engravings—some coloured—like sugared fruits set out in a dish. Polly’s eyes, though she kept her chin bent to the handkerchief, would steal upwards, quick as a glance at forbidden cake.

An etching fluttered down—a child playing with a Blenheim spaniel.

“Pretty little dog!” she breathed, and the delight broke through in spite of herself.

Graham, pretending not to notice, let her be drawn by her own desire. She slid from her corner and came nearer, the dog’s long ears and mournful eyes working on her as surely as any perfume from an opened jar.

“Nice picture!” she pronounced, with the air of one granting a favour.

“Well—you may have it,” said Graham.

To accept would be to give up her offended grandeur. She hesitated, then laid it down with a resolute little motion and turned away.

“You won’t have it, then, Polly?”

“I would rather not, thank you.”

“Shall I tell you what I’ll do with it if you refuse?”

She half turned, caught in the hook of curiosity.

“I’ll cut it into strips for lighting the taper.”

“No!”

“But I shall.”

“Please—don’t.”

The pleading tone sharpened his mischief. He took the scissors from his mother’s basket.

“Here goes!” he said, flourishing them. “Right through Fido’s head—and splitting little Harry’s nose.”

“No! No! NO!”

“Then come to me. Quick—or it’s done.”

She lingered—dignity tottering, desire pulling—then complied and stood before him.

“Now, will you have it?” he asked.

“Please.”

“But I shall want payment.”

“How much?”

“A kiss.”

“Give the picture first into my hand.”

It was cunning on her side now; her glance said she did not mean to be trapped. Graham gave her the print. Polly vanished at once—a debtor fleeing—darted to her father, and climbed onto his knee as into sanctuary. Graham rose in mock wrath and followed. She hid her face in Mr. Home’s waistcoat.

“Papa—papa—send him away!”

“I’ll not be sent away,” said Graham, coming close.

She held out her hand—face still turned aside—to keep him off.

“Then I shall kiss the hand,” he threatened; but the hand became, in an instant, a small clenched fist and struck him—payment in a coin that was not kisses.

Graham, as wily as she, retreated as if broken. He flung himself on the sofa, hid his face in his hands, and lay like one in pain. Polly peeped at him, perplexed. From her father’s knee she watched long, her small brow drawn; then Graham groaned.

“Papa, what is the matter?” she whispered.

“You had better ask him, Polly,” said Mr. Home.

“Is he hurt?” she asked; and a second groan answered.

“He makes a noise as if he were,” Mr. Home observed, with a sad kind of dry humour.

“Mother,” Graham suggested faintly, “I think you had better send for the doctor. Oh my eye!” Then silence again—only sighs.

“If I were to become blind—” he added.

That was too much for Polly. In a moment she was at the sofa.

“Let me see your eye. I did not mean to touch it—only your mouth; and I didn’t think I hit so very hard.”

He gave no answer. Her face worked; the brave little composure fell apart.

“I am sorry; I am sorry!”

Then tears came, fast and helpless.

“Have done trying that child, Graham,” said Mrs. Bretton, half reproach, half fondness.

“It is all nonsense, my pet,” Mr. Home said—yet his own eyes were not clear.

And Graham, instantly well again, caught Polly up, making her squeal with mingled rage and delight; and she punished him once more, pulling at his lion’s hair and calling him—between panting breaths—“the naughtiest, rudest, worst, untruest person that ever was.”

On the morning Mr. Home was to go, he and his daughter withdrew to a window-recess, as if to save their parting words from the general gaze. I heard only fragments, as one hears, through a closed door, a voice one loves.

“Couldn’t I pack my box and go with you, papa?” she whispered, urgent as hunger.

He shook his head.

“Should I be a trouble to you?”

“Yes, Polly.”

“Because I am little?”

“Because you are little and tender. It is only great, strong people that should travel.” Then, softer—“But don’t look sad, my little girl; it breaks my heart. Papa will soon come back to his Polly.”

“Indeed, indeed, I am not sad—scarcely at all.”

“Polly would be sorry to give papa pain, would she not?”

“Sorrier than sorry.”

“Then Polly must be cheerful: not cry at parting; not fret afterwards. She must look forward to meeting again, and try to be happy meanwhile. Can she do this?”

“She will try.”

“I see she will. Farewell, then. It is time to go.”

“Now—just now?”

“Just now.”

She lifted quivering lips. He sobbed. She did not; she held her breath as if tears were something she had promised not to spend. He set her down, shook hands with the rest of us, and went.

When the street-door closed, she dropped to her knees at a chair with a cry that was low and long and hollowed by despair.

“Papa!”

It had in it a child’s “Why hast thou forsaken me?”—a sound too ancient for so small a throat. For some minutes she endured agony—pure, bare, unreasoning; a storm squeezed into an infant frame. No one spoke. Mrs. Bretton, being a mother, shed a tear or two. Graham, who had been writing, lifted his head and stared. I, Lucy Snowe, remained calm.

Left unpressed by comfort, unharried by counsel, the little creature did what no one could do for her: she fought her own intolerable feeling, grappling it in silence until, little by little, she forced it down. That day she would take solace from none; nor the next. Afterwards she grew passive, as if the edge of her grief had worn her out.

On the third evening she sat on the floor, quiet and pale with fatigue. Graham came in; without a word he lifted her gently. She did not resist—rather she settled into his arms as into something warm after cold. When he sat, she leaned her head against him; in minutes she slept. He carried her upstairs to bed. I was not surprised that, next morning, the first thing she asked was—

“Where is Mr. Graham?”

Graham did not come to breakfast. He had school exercises to write and asked that a cup of tea be sent into the study. Polly volunteered to carry it; she could not bear to be without an errand now—she must tend someone, as if tending were a way of keeping absence from spreading. The cup was given her—she was restless, but careful. The study door stood opposite the breakfast-room, facing across the passage; my eye followed her small, deliberate steps, the tea trembling only a little in its china.

She paused on the threshold.

“What are you doing?” she asked.

“Writing,” said Graham.

“Why don’t you come to take breakfast with your mamma?”

“Too busy.”

“Do you want any breakfast?”

“Of course.”

“There, then.”

And she set the cup down on the carpet with a severity like a jailor slipping a pitcher of water through a cell-door, and retreated. Presently she returned, duty not satisfied.

“What will you have besides tea—what to eat?”

“Anything good,” he said. “Bring me something particularly nice; that’s a kind little woman.”

She went back to Mrs. Bretton.

“Please, ma’am, send your boy something good.”

“You shall choose for him, Polly; what shall my boy have?”

At once she selected the best from the table as if choosing the ripest fruit from a bowl—this slice, that morsel; and then, with a conspirator’s whisper, asked for marmalade which was not there. Mrs. Bretton refused her nothing, and marmalade was procured. Soon Graham’s voice was heard praising her beyond measure, promising that when he had a house of his own she should keep it for him—housekeeper, and perhaps, if she displayed culinary genius, cook.

When she did not return, and I went to see why, I found them breakfasting tête-à-tête: Graham seated, Polly standing at his elbow, sharing his bread and tea. Only the marmalade she would not touch—not one careful finger dipped—lest it should seem she had begged it for herself as much as for him. That delicacy—sharp as a clean knife—was in her from the first.

The acquaintance thus struck up did not loosen; time and the small weather of daily life rather bound it. Mismatched in age, sex, and all the rest, they yet found endless matter. Paulina’s character, I observed, never properly showed except with young Bretton. With Mrs. Bretton she was docile, sitting all day on a little stool at her feet—learning tasks, sewing, drawing figures on a slate—never kindling into originality, never letting her odd, quick spirit lift its head. In such hours she was not interesting. But let Graham’s knock sound of an evening, and she changed at once: up to the stairhead she flew, like a sentinel called to duty, her welcome generally a reprimand.

“You have not wiped your shoes properly on the mat. I shall tell your mamma.”

“Little busybody! Are you there?” he would answer, laughing.

“Yes—and you can’t reach me: I am higher up than you,” she would say, peeping between the banisters, too small to look over.

“Polly!”

“My dear boy!”—a phrase copied from his mother’s indulgent vocabulary.

“I am fit to faint with fatigue,” Graham would declare, leaning against the wall in dramatic collapse. “Dr. Digby has quite knocked me up with overwork. Just come down and help me carry up my books.”

“Ah! you’re cunning!”

“Not at all—positive fact. I’m as weak as a rush. Come down.”

“Your eyes are quiet like the cat’s,” she would accuse, “but you’ll spring.”

“Spring? Nothing of the kind. It isn’t in me. Come down.”

“Perhaps I may—if you’ll promise not to touch—not to snatch me up, and not to whirl me round.”

“I? I couldn’t do it!” he would say, sinking into a chair.

“Then put the books down on the first step, and go three yards off.”

This done, she descended with exquisite caution, eyes fixed on him; and, as always, her nearness gave him new life in spasms. Romps were exacted. Sometimes she was angry; sometimes it passed smoothly, and we could hear her leading him upstairs with a little air of domestic authority:

“Now, my dear boy, come and take your tea—I am sure you must want something.”

At tea she became a different creature. In his absence she was still; with him she was the most officious little body alive, forgetting herself utterly in the business of making sure he wanted nothing. Plates migrated toward him under her small hands, one by one, like dishes drawn to the head of a table. And when everything seemed within reach, she would invent some further necessity.

“Ma’am,” she would whisper to Mrs. Bretton, “perhaps your son would like a little cake—sweet cake, you know—there is some in there,” pointing to the sideboard cupboard.

Mrs. Bretton usually disapproved sweet cake at tea, yet Polly would urge it: “One little piece—only for him—as he goes to school. Girls—such as me and Miss Snowe—don’t need treats, but he would like it.”

Graham did like it, and generally got it. He would have shared, but she never allowed it; to insist was to cloud her whole evening. It was not cake she wanted, but his talk and his notice—standing by his knee as if guarding him from neglect.

With curious readiness she adapted herself to anything that interested him. It was as though, with her father gone, she had poured her whole life into this new vessel; she nested to Graham and lived by his feelings. She learned the names of his schoolfellows at once and held their characters—as he gave them—in her memory like labelled jars on a shelf. She never confused them. She could talk all evening of boys and masters she had never seen, yet speak as if she knew the tilt of each head, the sound each made on a stair. Some she even learned to mimic: an under-master whom Graham disliked had certain tricks, and Polly caught them in a moment from his description and acted them for his laughter—until Mrs. Bretton disapproved and forbade it.

They seldom quarrelled; but once a rupture came, and it struck hard.

On Graham’s birthday he had friends—lads of his own age—to dine. Polly took intense interest; she had heard of these boys often, as if they were figures in a story told nightly. After dinner the young gentlemen were left together in the dining-room; soon they grew very merry and made a great deal of noise—loud, boyish, careless noise that rattled through the house like stones in a tin pail. Passing through the hall, I saw Polly sitting alone on the lowest stair, eyes fixed on the glossy dining-room panels where the lamp’s reflection shone. Her forehead was knit, as if she were trying to understand a language that did not include her.

“What are you thinking about, Polly?”

“Nothing particular,” she said; then, betraying herself—“Only I wish that door was clear glass—that I might see through it. The boys seem very cheerful, and I want to go to them. I want to be with Graham, and watch his friends.”

“What hinders you from going?”

“I feel afraid. But may I try, do you think? May I knock at the door, and ask to be let in?”

It seemed possible they might accept her as a plaything, and I encouraged her.

She knocked—too softly at first to be heard—but the second time the door opened. Graham’s head appeared, bright, flushed, high-spirited—and impatient.

“What do you want, you little monkey?”

“To come to you.”

“Do you indeed? As if I would be troubled with you! Away to mamma and Mistress Snowe, and tell them to put you to bed.”

The auburn head vanished; the door shut with a peremptory sound that ended all appeal. Polly stood as if struck.

“Why does he speak so?” she whispered, white with astonishment. “He never spoke so before. What have I done?”

“Nothing,” I said. “Graham is busy with his school-friends.”

“And he likes them better than me,” she breathed, as if tasting something bitter. “He turns me away now they are here!”

I began to offer consolation—those neat, ready maxims which can be produced like dry biscuits in moments of trouble. But at my first words she put her fingers in her ears, and then lay down on the mat with her face against the flags, refusing all interference. Neither Warren nor the cook could coax or lift her from that posture; she was left to rise when she chose.

That evening, when his friends were gone, Graham forgot his own harshness and would have taken her hand as usual. She wrenched herself away; her eyes flashed; she would not bid him good-night; she would not look at him. Next day he treated her with indifference, and she grew marble-still. The day after he teased her to tell what was wrong. Her lips would not open. He could not truly be angry—there was too much inequality—so he tried coaxing and soothing.

“Why are you so angry? What have I done?”

At last tears answered him. He petted her, and they were friends again. Yet the incident was not lost on her. Never after that rebuff did she seek him, follow him, or solicit his notice. Once I told her to carry a book to Graham while he was in his study.

“I shall wait till he comes out,” she said proudly. “I don’t choose to give him the trouble of rising to open the door.”

Graham had a favourite pony and often rode out. From the window Polly watched his departures and returns with a longing that glittered in her eye. To be allowed one ride round the courtyard was her ambition—yet she would not ask. One day she went down to the yard to see him dismount. Leaning against the gate, she watched, her whole small body held in that restrained desire.

“Come, Polly, will you have a canter?” Graham asked—half carelessly.

Perhaps the carelessness stung more than refusal.

“No, thank you,” she answered, turning away with studied coolness.

“You’d better,” he pursued. “You will like it, I’m sure.”

“Don’t think I should care a fig about it.”

“That’s not true. You told Lucy Snowe you longed to have a ride.”

“Lucy Snowe is a tatter-box,” I heard her say—her imperfect utterance being the least precocious thing about her. With that she walked into the house.

Graham, coming in soon after, said to his mother, “Mamma, I believe that creature is a changeling: she is a perfect cabinet of oddities; but I should be dull without her. She amuses me a great deal more than you or Lucy Snowe.”

“Miss Snowe,” Paulina said to me later—she had begun, at night in our room, to talk now and then in her confidential way—“do you know on what day in the week I like Graham best?”

“How can I possibly know anything so strange?” I answered. “Is there one day out of the seven when he is otherwise than on the other six?”

“To be sure! Can’t you see? I find him most excellent on a Sunday; then we have him the whole day, and he is quiet, and in the evening so kind.”

She was not wrong. Church and its decorums kept him still on Sundays; and in the evening he generally devoted himself to a serene, slightly indolent enjoyment by the parlour fire. He would take possession of the couch and call Polly.

Graham was not quite as other boys; his delight did not lie wholly in action. He had intervals of contemplation; he could read for pleasure, and his choice of books was not entirely without instinct. He rarely commented, but I had seen him sit and think over what he read.

Polly, kneeling on a cushion at his side, would begin a murmured talk. Something gentler than the weekday spirit seemed to enter him then, soothing him into a mood not unkind.

“Have you learned any hymns this week, Polly?” he would ask.

“I have learned a very pretty one—four verses long. Shall I say it?”

“Speak nicely, then: don’t be in a hurry.”

She would half-chant it in her little singing voice; he would correct the manner, and teach her to recite. She learned quickly, pleased by imitation, and more pleased still by pleasing him. Then might come reading—perhaps a chapter in the Bible. Here she needed little correction, for she read simple narrative well; and when the subject was within her understanding her emphasis was remarkable. Joseph in the pit; the calling of Samuel; Daniel among the lions—these held her. Joseph above all moved her heart.

“Poor Jacob!” she would say, lips trembling. “How he loved his son Joseph! As much—” once she added, looking up at Graham as if the comparison were a vow—“as much, Graham, as I love you. If you were to die—” and she opened the book, sought a verse, and read it out—“I should refuse to be comforted, and go down into the grave to you mourning.”

With the words she gathered him in her small arms and drew his long-tressed head toward her. The action struck me as rash—like seeing a creature dangerous by nature, and only half tamed by habit, too heedlessly stroked. Not that I feared Graham would strike her; but careless impatience can wound deeper than a blow, and she was made to feel. Yet these demonstrations were borne; sometimes even a complacent wonder at her earnest devotion softened his eyes.

Once he said, “You like me almost as well as if you were my little sister, Polly.”

“Oh! I do like you,” she answered, fervent and simple. “I do like you very much.”

I was not long allowed the amusement of watching this. She had scarcely been at Bretton two months when a letter came from Mr. Home. He was now settled among his mother’s kindred on the Continent; England had grown wholly distasteful; he had no thought of returning, perhaps for years; and he wished his little girl to join him immediately.

“I wonder how she will take this news?” said Mrs. Bretton, after reading.

I wondered too—and took it upon myself to tell her.

I went to the drawing-room, that calm, decorated place where Polly liked to be alone and could be trusted; she handled nothing—or rather, nothing she handled was soiled. She sat on a couch in the half-shadow of drooping window-draperies, like a small odalisque at rest. She seemed content. Her implements of occupation lay around her: the white work-box, a shred or two of muslin, ends of ribbon saved for doll-millinery. The doll lay in its cradle, dutifully night-capped and night-gowned; Polly rocked it with perfect faith in its need of sleep, while her eyes moved over a picture-book open on her lap.
