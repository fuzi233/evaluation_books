CHAPTER XXXIV. “He was called Israel. A witch’s spell turned him into a dog. * * * * * But every Friday evening, at twilight, the spell fades, and the dog becomes once more a human being.” —HEINE: _Princess Sabbath_.

When Daniel Deronda arrived at five o’clock, the shop was closed, and the door was opened by the Christian maid. She led him into the room behind the shop, where he was struck by the warmth and charm of the scene. The house was old and spacious at the back—likely gloomy in daylight—but now bathed in soft light from an elegant brass lamp with seven oil flames hanging above a table draped in snow-white linen. The ceiling and walls were dark and smoky, which made the people in the room glow like figures in a Venetian painting.

The grandmother wore a yellowish-brown dress and a thick gold chain instead of a necklace. In this dim, golden light, her pale face, dark eyebrows, and coiled gray hair gave her a dignified, almost regal presence. Young Mrs. Cohen was dressed in red and black, with a string of large artificial pearls wrapped around her neck. The baby slept peacefully in its cradle beneath a scarlet blanket. Adelaide Rebekah stood on a chair in amber-colored braids, and Jacob Alexander wore black velveteen with bright red stockings. All four pairs of dark eyes sparkled with welcome as they looked up at Deronda. He felt a sudden pang of shame for the aloofness he had felt earlier that day when seeing them in their ordinary life.

The greeting was warm and open-hearted. Both mother and grandmother seemed to grow taller and more dignified under the quiet honor of hosting a guest in their private home. Deronda glanced around, noticing the old furniture—an oak bureau, a high side-table—surely kept out of necessity rather than taste. On the side-table sat a large dish of blue and yellow pottery, flanked by two silver vessels, and before them lay a thick book bound in darkened vellum with a deeply ribbed spine. At the far end of the room, an open door revealed another lit space beyond.

Deronda took in these details briefly while Jacob pressed him about the knife. He had gone to some trouble to find one with the proper hook and white handle, and now produced it, asking, “Is this what you wanted, Jacob?”

The boy examined it closely—the hook, the blades—then pulled out his own knife with a corkscrew attachment to compare. “Why do you prefer a hook?” Deronda asked.

“Because I can grab things with a hook,” Jacob replied. “A corkscrew only fits corks. But it’s better for you—you can pull out corks.”

“You agree to trade, then?” Deronda asked, noting how the grandmother watched with delight.

“What else have you got in your pockets?” Jacob asked seriously.

“Hush, hush, Jacob, love,” the grandmother said gently.

Deronda, remembering the rules of decorum, replied, “I think I shouldn’t tell you. Our business is about the knives.”

Jacob studied him for a moment, then nodded slowly. “I’ll shwop,” he said, handing over the corkscrew knife. Deronda accepted it solemnly and slipped it into his pocket.

At once, the youngest son of Shem darted into the next room, chattering rapidly, then returned—only to stop when he saw his father enter. He snatched a small velvet hat from a chair and placed it carefully on his head before approaching. Cohen kept his own hat on, ignoring the visitor. He stood still as the two children rushed to him and clasped his knees. Then he laid his hands on each child in turn and spoke a Hebrew blessing. His wife, who had just taken the baby from its cradle, brought it forward and held it beneath his hands so it could be blessed even in sleep.

For a fleeting moment, Deronda thought that this pawnbroker, proud of his trade, was not entirely without soul.

“Well, sir,” Cohen said, finally setting down his hat and returning to his usual self, “you’ve found your welcome in my family, I see. And you’ve been punctual. Nothing like a little pressure here,” he added, tapping his side pocket as he sat down. “It keeps us all sharp. I’ve felt it myself when I’ve had to make payments. I used to fit every kind of box. It’s good for the mind. Now then—let’s see, let’s see.”

“That’s the ring I mentioned,” Deronda said, removing it from his finger. “I believe it cost a hundred pounds. I think it’s worth fifty as a pledge. I’ll likely redeem it in a month or so.”

Cohen’s eyes gleamed as he looked at the young man—so earnest, so naive, believing redemption was a comfort to a pawnbroker. He took the ring, inspected it, and handed it back. “Good, good. We’ll talk about it after our meal. Perhaps you’d join us? Me and my wife would feel honored—and so would mother, wouldn’t you, mother?”

The invitation was repeated twice, and Deronda gladly accepted.

Everyone gathered around the table. Only one dish was visible, covered with a napkin. Mrs. Cohen had placed a china bowl near her husband so he might wash his hands. After putting his hat back on, Cohen paused and called loudly, “Mordecai!”

Could this be part of the ritual? Deronda wondered, unsure what to expect. Then came a quiet “Yes” from the next room. He turned toward the doorway—and there, to his astonishment, stood the mysterious Jew he had met that morning in the bookstore. Their eyes met. Mordecai looked as surprised as Deronda did—neither acknowledging the other.

But when Mordecai sat at the far end of the table, he bowed slightly to the guest, coldly, as if the disappointment of earlier still lingered between them.

Cohen washed his hands, reciting Hebrew words as he did so. Then he lifted the napkin to reveal two long flat loaves sprinkled with seeds—the bread of manna, a reminder of the wandering ancestors. He broke off pieces and gave one to each member of the family, including Adelaide Rebhak, who stood proudly on her chair in her amber dress, her little Jewish nose pinched tight as she tried to look dignified.

Then Cohen offered another blessing. The men uncovered their heads, and the meal began—ordinary, unremarkable food. Deronda wasn’t sure what he ate; his thoughts were elsewhere—on how to steer the conversation, and on Mordecai, whose gaze occasionally met his, full of quiet intensity.

Mordecai wore no fine Sabbath clothes. Instead of the threadbare black coat he’d worn that morning, he had on a faded drab jacket—once perhaps a fine paletot, now shrunk and dull. Yet this only emphasized his sharp, dark-haired face, so alive and intense it might have belonged to the prophet Ezekiel—unfashionable, certainly, but commanding.

It was noticeable that the thin tails of the fried fish were given to Mordecai—small portions, like those reserved for a poor relative. An ancient custom, perhaps, still observed without question.

Mr. Cohen carried the conversation with lively charm, speaking of the Queen and Royal Family, the Emperor and Empress of France—subjects dear to Jewish pride. The grandmother and younger Mrs. Cohen joined in eagerly. The younger woman remembered birthdays of notable figures precisely; the elder helped her son recount the visit of the French imperial couple to London ten years prior.

“I dare say you know more about it than we do, sir,” Cohen said repeatedly, preface to each story, as though offering a generous lesson.

“Our baby is named Eu_genie Esther,” the young wife announced cheerfully.

“The Emperor looks just like a cousin of mine,” the grandmother declared. “It struck me like lightning when I saw him. I never would have guessed.”

“Mother and I went to see the Emperor and Empress at the Crystal Palace,” Cohen said. “I had a valuable piece of work to guard—mother might have been crushed flat. Though she was nearly as strong then as she is now. I told her, if I had a hundred mothers, I’d never take one to see the Emperor again. You can’t afford it when you’ve only one—not unless you’ve got a big insurance policy on her.” He chuckled, stroking her shoulder affectionately.

“Your mother has been a widow for many years, I suppose,” Deronda said, seizing the moment. “That makes your care for her all the more important.”

“Ay, ay,” Cohen replied quickly. “It’s been many _yore-zeit_ since I had to manage for her and myself. I started early. That’s what makes you a sharp knife.”

“What does—what makes a sharp knife, father?” Jacob asked, cheeks swollen with sweet cake.

The father winked at Deronda. “Having your nose put on the grindstone.”

Jacob slid from his chair, holding the cake, and approached Mordecai—who had remained silent throughout. “What does that mean—putting my nose on the grindstone?” he asked.

“It means you must endure pain without crying out,” Mordecai said softly, looking down at the child with gentle eyes.

Jacob offered a corner of the cake into Mordecai’s mouth. “I shan’t cry out,” he declared, watching closely as the man took a bite. Mordecai smiled, clearly pleased to please the boy. The simple act made both seem more tender, more real.

Deronda felt a flicker of frustration—he had gained nothing from his question.

“I suppose that’s the right place to learn,” he said, continuing the thread, turning to Mordecai. “You’ve been a great student, I imagine?”

“I have studied,” Mordecai replied quietly.

“And you—your German, from the book you bought…”

“Yes, I’ve studied in Germany. Are you usually involved in bookselling?” Deronda asked.

“No,” Mordecai answered. “I go to Mr. Ram’s shop every day to keep it while he eats.”

Now, he looked at Dernda with renewed interest—as if something in the young man stirred a forgotten hope. After a pause, he asked, “Perhaps you know Hebrew?”

“I’m afraid not at all,” Deronda admitted.

Mordecai’s face fell. He lowered his eyes, stared at his hands folded before him, and said nothing more.

Deronda now noticed more clearly the difficulty in Mordecai’s breathing—a sign, he thought, of consumption.

“I’ve had other things to do than study,” Cohen said. “I’ve had to learn useful things. I know stones well.” He gestured toward Deronda’s ring. “I’m not afraid to take that ring at my own valuation. But now”—his voice dropped, becoming softer, more intimate—“what do you want for it?”

“Fifty or sixty pounds,” Deronda replied, a bit too casually.

Cohen paused, thrust his hands into his pockets, fixed Deronda with glistening eyes—like a guinea-pig with a secret—and said, “Couldn’t do that. Happy to help, but I can’t go that far. Forty pounds—say forty—I’ll give you forty.”

Deronda noticed Mordecai glance up again, watching the exchange. He said, “Very well. I’ll redeem it in a month or so.”

“Good,” Cohen said indifferently. “I’ll make out the ticket later.”

Then he raised a finger—conversation paused. He, Mordecai, and Jacob put on their hats. Cohen began a prayer of thanksgiving, which continued with responses until Mordecai took over, chanting solemnly, chin slightly raised, hands loosely clasped before him. In tone, accent, and ease—free from any need for approval—Mordecai stood in stark contrast to the man at the other end of the table.

It was an odd union: among these prosperous, everyday Jews, a man so frail, so worn, yet so imposing—making Deronda feel uneasy, as if he had failed to meet some unseen expectation.

No sooner had Mordecai finished than he rose, bowed slightly to the stranger, and walked back into his room, closing the door behind him.

“That seems to be a remarkable man,” Deronda said, turning to Cohen.

Cohen immediately straightened, stuck out his tongue slightly, and tapped his temple. Clearly, Mordecai didn’t measure up to Cohen’s standard of sanity.

“Does he belong to your family?” Deronda asked.

The idea amused everyone. They exchanged glances.

“No, no,” Cohen said. “Charity! Charity! He worked for me. When he grew weaker, I took him in. He’s a burden, yes—but he brings blessings. He teaches the boy. And he repairs watches and jewelry.”

Deronda barely suppressed a smile—this mix of kindness and justification, of generosity masked as practicality. But his desire to learn more about Mordecai—whose presence now seemed even more mysterious—was cut short. Cohen shifted the topic back to the loan, a charitable act, and proceeded to write the ticket, hand over forty pounds, and exchange them for the diamond ring.

Deronda, feeling it would be impolite to stay longer than necessary, took his leave—carrying only the money and the pawn ticket in his pocket. He resolved to return after Christmas, when he would try to uncover more about Mordecai—and perhaps, through him, learn why it was forbidden to ask the elder Mrs. Cohen whether she had a daughter.
