Colleges, ancient schools, and those venerable Inns where the Law is supposed to be brewed into a livelihood, still keep up a brave show of reverence for antiquity; and, like elderly aunts who will not hear a syllable against last century’s housekeeping, they preserve innumerable customs simply because the dust upon them is respectable. A new prison will give you air, water, light, cleanliness, and a moral text upon the wall in letters you can read without a telescope; but a Foundation School, a College, or an Inn of Court will lodge you lovingly in a cupboard, charge you for it like a villa with lilacs, and then look offended if you inquire, in a low voice, where a man may possibly contrive to wash.

The poorest weaver in Spitalfields may turn a tap and summon a Niagara to his basin. But the gentlemen of the Temple and the Universities—gentlemen who quote the ancients with ease—are served their water like a rare sauce: fetched in jugs, portioned out by laundresses and bedmakers, and doled with the air of administering medicine. Their staircases were built when the national mind had not yet discovered the connection between soap and decency. There are men still alive who call “the people” dirty with a curled lip. Gentlemen, your forefathers were, beyond dispute, the Great Unwashed; and in the Temple, where walls remember the smell of centuries, it is plain that the virtue said to be next to godliness could only have been practised by stratagem and with witnesses.

Old Grump, of the Norfolk Circuit—now well past his half-century, and lodged for more than thirty years in chambers under those occupied by Messrs. Warrington and Pendennis—was nightly roused by the roaring of the shower-baths which those wild innovators had erected overhead. A portion of this audacious waterfall would now and then condescend to trickle through his ceiling, to the great injury of his temper and the slight moistening of his plaster. Grump declared the whole practice an absurd, dandified, newfangled folly; and daily anathematised the laundress who slopped the stairs with a deluge meant, no doubt, for civilization. The old gentleman had lived very handsomely without water, and so had the fathers before him. He could not see why a man should seek out damp on purpose.

One looks up, in the famous hall of the Upper Temple, at the painted escutcheons of knights and baronets, lords and gentlemen bearing arms, and cannot help wondering whether not one among them—some charitable soul with imagination enough to see beyond the next dinner—ever devised a set of baths, a Hummums of the Law, for the benefit of his learned successors. The Temple historian is silent. There is Pump Court, there is Fountain Court, there is hydraulic apparatus enough to suggest refreshment; but no one has heard of a bencher disporting in the fountain, and one can only mourn how many a counsel in the old days might have been improved in argument by the honest pump.

Yet for all its dinginess and its jugs of water, the place has an attraction, a rough freedom, a comfort of ancient irresponsibility, which men remember as they remember the smell of a childhood kitchen—smoky, perhaps, and rather questionable for the lungs, but dear for the sake of what was cooked there. I cannot say whether the student of law permits himself much enthusiasm as he passes historical doors and murmurs, “Here Eldon lived; on this site Coke wrestled with Littleton; here Chitty toiled; here Barnewall and Alderson laboured; here Byles wrote upon bills, and Smith compiled his immortal cases; here Gustavus still toils, with Solomon to aid him.” But the man of letters must love a place inhabited by so many of his brethren, or peopled by creations as real to us as their authors: Sir Roger de Coverley in the Temple Garden discoursing with Mr. Spectator upon the beauties in hoops and patches; Samuel Johnson rolling through the fog with the Scotchman behind him towards Goldsmith’s chambers in Brick Court; Harry Fielding, with inked ruffles and a wet towel round his head, battering out copy at midnight while the printer’s boy snores in the passage. If some Temple Asmodeus would but lift roofs for us and give the history of one day in one of those four-storied houses in the dingy courts, he might furnish a queer volume—full of dinners, debts, vows, cigar-ashes, and the small tragedies that are made of unpaid bills and overboiled chops.

On the ground floor you may have a great parliamentary counsel, who drives off to Belgravia at dinner-time, and whose clerk, the moment his master’s carriage wheels are gone, becomes a gentleman also, and goes away to play the gentleman in his own cheaper key. A short time since, that counsel was hungry and briefless in a garret of the Inn; lived by stealthy literature; waited, hoped, sickened; saw no clients come; spent his own means and his friends’ kindness; implored the patience of creditors with the humility of a man apologising for being poor. Ruin stared; then the wheel of fortune turned, and the lucky wretch drew one of those prodigious prizes which the Bar sometimes throws up as if to prove it is not wholly unjust. Many a better lawyer than he cannot make a fifth of his income; and his clerk, who not long ago could hardly get credit for blacking his master’s unpaid boots, now dines like a Christian.

On the first floor, perhaps, lives a venerable name: a man famous, solitary, and rich; fifty years in the Inn; shelves full of classical and legal lore; brains packed with books as a larder is packed with jars. He comes home at night from his club, where he has dined freely, to lonely chambers where he lives a godless old recluse. When he dies, the Inn will erect a tablet to his honour; his heirs will burn part of his library. Would you like such an old age—money and learning stored like winter provisions, and no one to share the meal? But we must not stay long at Mr. Doomsday’s door.

Worthy Mr. Grump lives over him; another ancient inhabitant; and when Doomsday comes home to read Catullus, Grump is sitting down with three steady seniors of his standing to a steady rubber at whist, after a dinner at which they have consumed their three steady bottles of port. You may see the old boys asleep in the Temple Church of a Sunday, their heads nodding to the organ as if the music were an argument they had heard too often. Attorneys seldom trouble them; they have small fortunes of their own; they are as fixed in their habits as old cheese in a pantry.

On the other side of the third landing, where Pen and Warrington were to lodge till long after midnight, sits Mr. Paley: first in honours, fellow of his college, a man who reads and notes cases until two in the morning; rises at seven; is at the pleader’s chambers as soon as they open; works until an hour before dinner; comes home from Hall and reads and notes cases again until dawn—when perhaps Arthur Pendennis and his friend Mr. Warrington are returning from some wild expedition, with London’s late noises still in their ears.

How differently employed has Mr. Paley been! He has not been throwing himself away; only bringing a great intellect down, laboriously, to the comprehension of a mean subject; and in his fierce grasp of that, shutting out from his mind all higher thoughts, all better things—philosophy, history, poetry, wit, fancy, reflection, art, love, truth; everything, in short, that makes a man human instead of a walking index. Outside his reading-lamp all is dark. Love, Nature, and Art—the expression of our praise for the beautiful world—are barred like thieves from his room. When he turns off his lonely lamp at night, he thinks he has spent the day profitably, and goes to sleep thankless and remorseless. Yet he shudders when he meets Warrington on the stairs and shuns him as one doomed.

Warrington and Paley had once been competitors for university honours and had run each other hard; and now everybody said Warrington was wasting his time and energies, while all praised Paley for his industry. There may be doubts as to which used his time best. One could afford to think; the other never could. One could have sympathies and do kindnesses; the other must needs be always selfish—not by choice perhaps, but by discipline. He could not cultivate a friendship, do a charity, admire genius, kindle at beauty or at the sound of a sweet song—he had no time, no ears for anything but his law-books. In the Temple, where sounds carry oddly through old brick and along stone passages—voices, footsteps, the scrape of chairs, the nightly quarrel of a fiddle with an amateur—Paley heard none of it as music, but only as interruption.

It may have been that sight of cadaverous ambition and self-complacent meanness, twinkling in Paley’s narrow eyes and showing in his yellow face; or it may have been a natural appetite for pleasure and joviality (and it must be confessed Mr. Pen had a hearty one); but something deterred that luckless youth from pursuing the Bench or Woolsack with the steady ardour required for those lofty seats. He enjoyed the Temple life with great relish. His worthy relatives believed he was reading as became a regular student; his uncle wrote congratulatory letters to the kind widow at Fairoaks, announcing that the lad had sown his wild oats and was becoming quite steady. The truth was, the life was new excitement; and Pen, having given up some dandified pretensions and fine-gentleman airs contracted among aristocratic college acquaintances (whom he now saw little), found the rough pleasures of a London bachelor novel and agreeable, and tasted them all.

There was a time he would have envied dandies their horses in Rotten Row; but now he was content to walk in the Park and look at them—as a sensible man looks at game in a poulterer’s window when he has only money for a chop. He was too young to succeed in London society without a better name and larger fortune, and too lazy to get on without such helpful seasoning. Old Pendennis, seeing that the boy neglected certain social advantages, fondly inferred it was because he was immersed in law; and because Pen had tried half a dozen balls and evening parties and retreated before their dulness and sameness, the Major concluded scholarship had conquered pleasure. Whenever anybody asked after his nephew, the old gentleman declared the young rascal reformed, and that he could not be got away from his books.

The Major would have been almost as horrified as Mr. Paley, had he known how much pleasure entered into Mr. Pen’s studies. A long morning’s reading; a walk in the Park; a pull upon the river; a stretch up to Hampstead (where the wind, at least, had not yet been entirely bribed by the city); and then a modest tavern dinner, honest and smoking, with its clatter of plates, its warm fog of roasted meat, its smell of ale and browned gravy—those domestic incenses which make even a dreary day seem briefly hospitable. A bachelor night passed here or there, in joviality, not vice (for Arthur Pendennis admired women so heartily that he could not bear the society of any whom, in his fancy at least, were not good and pure). A quiet evening at home with a friend and a pipe or two; a humble potation of British spirits, the quality of which Mrs. Flanagan, the laundress, invariably tested with conscientious severity—these were the pursuits of our young gentleman; and it must be owned his life was not unpleasant.

In term-time, Mr. Pen showed most praiseworthy regularity in performing one part of a law-student’s duty: he ate his dinners in Hall. The Hall of the Upper Temple is no uninteresting sight; and save for trifling improvements and anachronisms, a man may sit there and fancy he joins in a meal of the seventeenth century. The bar have their messes; the students their tables apart; the benchers sit aloft at the high table on a raised platform, surrounded by pictures of judges and portraits of royal personages who have honoured the place with their patronage—faces looking down as if to say they have seen many a man carve his hopes as clumsily as his beef.

Pen, on his first introduction, looked about not a little amused. Among the student class were gentlemen of all ages, from sixty to seventeen: stout grey-headed attorneys proceeding to take the superior dignity; dandies and men about town who wished for some reason to be barristers of seven years’ standing; swarthy black-eyed natives of the Colonies who came to be called here before they practised in their own islands; many gentlemen of the Irish nation, who make a sojourn in Middle Temple Lane before returning to the green country of their birth. There were little squads of reading students who talked law all dinner-time—an almost unknown and quite uninteresting language to Pen; there were rowing men whose discourse was of sculling matches, the Red House, Vauxhall, and the Opera; there were others great in politics, and orators of debating clubs; and with all these sets, except the legal linguists, Mr. Pen made gradual acquaintance and found points of sympathy.

The ancient and liberal Inn provides in its Hall, and for a very moderate price, an excellent wholesome dinner: soup, meat, tarts, and port wine or sherry. The parties are arranged in messes of four; each quartet has its piece of beef or leg of mutton, its sufficient apple-pie, its bottle of wine. But the honest habitués of the Hall among the lower rank of students, who have a taste for good living, possess many harmless arts by which they improve their banquet—innocent dodges by which they aspire to more delicate food than the common roast meat of the students’ tables. These contrivances were as much a part of the Inn’s tradition as its portraits: handed down, improved, whispered over, as receipts are whispered in families.

“Wait a bit,” said Mr. Lowton, one of these Temple gourmands. “Wait a bit,” he repeated, tugging at Pen’s gown with the tenderness of a man who feels he is saving another from ignorance. “The side-tables are very full, and there’s only three benchers to eat ten dishes—if we wait, perhaps we shall get something from their table.”

Pen looked with amusement, as did Lowton with eyes of fond desire, towards the high table, where three old gentlemen stood before a dozen silver dish-covers while the clerk quavered out a grace. Even the grace had its acoustics: it rose thinly, wavered, and sank into the great wooden roof where centuries of speeches had lodged like smoke. The lid-covers shone; the odours floated down in teasing little messages—something rich and sea-sweet that might be fish; something dark and dignified that might be turtle; something buttery that made a man suddenly remember he was alive.

Lowton was great in the conduct of dinner. His aim was to manage so as to be first—a captain of the mess—and to secure for himself the thirteenth glass of the bottle of port. Thus he commanded the joint on which he operated favourite cuts, making rapid, dexterous appropriations of gravy, which amused Pen infinitely. Poor Jack Lowton! Thy pleasures were harmless; an eager epicure, thy desires did not go beyond eighteen pence, and thy heroism was exercised upon potatoes.

Pen was somewhat older than many fellow-students, and there was about his style and appearance—haughty and impertinent, if you will—that stamped him as a man of ton; unlike pale students murmuring law to one another, and unlike ferocious dandies in rowing shirts with astonishing pins and waistcoats, representing the idle part of the community. The humble good-natured Lowton felt attracted by Pen’s superior looks and presence, and opened acquaintance at the mess by beginning, gravely, with the great subject.

“This is boiled-beef day, I believe, sir,” said Lowton to Pen.

“Upon my word, sir, I’m not aware,” said Pen, hardly able to contain his laughter; but added, “I’m a stranger; this is my first term.”

On which Lowton began to point out notabilities in the Hall.

“That’s Boosey the bencher,” he said, “the bald one sitting under the picture and aving soup. I wonder whether it’s turtle? They often ave turtle. Next is Balls, the King’s Counsel, and Swettenham—Hodge and Swettenham, you know. That’s old Grump, the senior of the bar; they say he’s dined here forty years. They often send ’em down their fish from the benchers to the senior table. Do you see those four fellows opposite us? Those are regular swells—tip-top fellows, I can tell you—Mr. Trail, the Bishop of Ealing’s son; Honourable Fred Ringwood, Lord Cinqbar’s brother, you know. He’ll have a good place, I bet any money; and Bob Suckling, who’s always with him—a high fellow too. Ha! ha!”

Here Lowton burst into a laugh.

“What is it?” asked Pen.

“I say, I like to mess with those chaps,” Lowton said, winking knowingly and pouring out his glass.

“And why?” asked Pen, still amused.

“Why! they don’t come down here to dine, you know; they only make believe to dine. They dine here! Law bless you! They go to some swell club, or else to some grand dinner-party. You see their names in the Morning Post at all the fine parties in London. Why, I bet anything Ringwood has his cab, or Trail his Brougham—he’s a devil of a fellow, and makes the bishop’s money spin—I bet it’s at the corner of Essex Street at this minute. They dine! They won’t dine these two hours, I dare say.”

“But why should you like to mess with them, if they don’t eat any dinner?” Pen asked, still puzzled. “There’s plenty, isn’t there?”

“How green you are,” said Lowton. “Excuse me, but you are green. They don’t drink any wine, don’t you see; and a fellow gets the bottle to himself if he likes it when he messes with those three chaps. That’s why Corkoran got in with ’em.”

“Ah, Mr. Lowton, I see you are a sly fellow,” Pen said, delighted with his acquaintance.

Lowton modestly replied that he had lived in London the better part of his life and of course had his eyes about him; and he went on with his catalogue.

“There’s a lot of Irish here,” he said; “that Corkoran’s one, and I can’t say I like him. You see that handsome chap with the blue neck-cloth and pink shirt and yellow waistcoat—that’s another; that’s Molloy Maloney of Ballymaloney, and nephew to Major-General Sir Hector O’Dowd, he, he,” Lowton added, trying to imitate the Hibernian accent. “He’s always bragging about his uncle; and came into Hall in silver-striped trousers the day he had been presented. That other near him, with the long black hair, is a tremendous rebel. By Jove, to hear him at the Forum it makes your blood freeze; and the next is an Irishman too—Jack Finucane, reporter of a newspaper. They all stick together, those Irish. It’s your turn to fill your glass. What? you won’t have any port? Don’t like port with your dinner? Here’s your health.”

This worthy man grew no less attached to Pendennis because the latter professed dislike of port at dinner; some men can forgive any failing except abstinence from their own favourite relish, and Lowton was generous enough to forgive even that.

It was while Pen was taking his share of one of these dinners, with Lowton as captain of the mess, that there came to join them a gentleman in a barrister’s gown who could not find a seat among persons of his own degree. He strode over and took his place on the bench where Pen sat. He wore old clothes and a faded gown hanging behind him; his shirt, though clean, was extremely ragged—very different from the magnificent pink raiment of Mr. Molloy Maloney, who in the next mess shone like a tropical bird among sparrows.

To notify their appearance at dinner, it is the custom for gentlemen eating in the Hall to write their names upon slips of paper provided for the purpose, with a pencil for each mess. Lowton wrote first; then Arthur Pendennis; and next came the name of the gentleman in old clothes. He smiled when he saw Pen’s name and looked at him.

“We ought to know each other,” he said. “We’re both Boniface men; my name’s Warrington.”

“Are you St— Warrington?” Pen cried, delighted, as if a storybook had stepped down into the Hall.

Warrington laughed. “Stunning Warrington—yes,” he said. “I recollect you in your freshman’s term. But you appear to have quite cut me out.”

“The college talks about you still,” said Pen, who had generous admiration for talent and pluck. “The bargeman you thrashed, Bill Simes, wants you up again at Oxbridge. The Miss Notleys, the haberdashers—”

“Hush!” said Warrington quickly, with a grin that made the prohibition friendly. “Glad to make your acquaintance, Pendennis. Heard a good deal about you.”

They were friends immediately, deep at once in college-talk—the sort that makes two men who have eaten the same bad dinners feel as though they were brothers. And Pen, who had been acting rather the fine gentleman on a previous day when he pretended to Lowton he could not drink port at dinner, seeing Warrington take his share with gusto, did not scruple about helping himself any more—rather to the disappointment of honest Lowton, whose whole philosophy of life included a private bottle.

When dinner was over, Warrington asked Arthur where he was going.

“I thought of going home to dress, and hear Grisi in Norma,” Pen said.

“Are you going to meet anybody there?” Warrington asked.

Pen said no—only to hear the music, of which he was fond.

“You had much better come home and smoke a pipe with me,” said Warrington, “a very short one. Come; I live close by in Lamb Court, and we’ll talk over Boniface and old times.”

They went away; Lowton sighed after them. He knew Warrington was a baronet’s son, and he looked up with simple reverence to all the aristocracy, as some men look up at high tables as if gravy descended more nobly there.

From that night Pen and Warrington were sworn friends. Warrington’s cheerfulness and jovial temper, his good sense, his rough welcome, and his never-failing pipe of tobacco charmed Pen. It was pleasanter to dive into shilling taverns with him—where the air was full of clinking glasses, rasped laughter, and the homely music of knives on plates—than to dine in solitary state among the silent, polite frequenters of the Polyanthus. Ere long Pen gave up the lodgings in St. James’s, to which he had migrated on quitting his hotel, and found it much more economical to take up his abode with Warrington in Lamb Court, furnishing and occupying his friend’s vacant room there. For it must be said of Pen, no man was more easily led to do a thing when it was a novelty, or when he had a mind to it. And Pidgeon the youth, and Flanagan the laundress, divided their allegiance now between Warrington and Pen, as impartial publicans divide their smiles between two customers who both pay.
