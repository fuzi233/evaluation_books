— DANTE, *Il Purgatorio*

Barely a handful of days had passed since her mother arrived in Genoa, before Gwendolen finally consented to let them make arrangements to leave. The glittering Mediterranean, once the stuff of her gilded daydreams, had curdled into a circle of unspoken punishment under her gaze: no flowery Enna valley could have felt more like a prison, its “blooms” no more than flickering flames that burned the soles of her feet with every step she took along the waterfront.

“I never want to look at the Mediterranean again,” she told her mother one quiet afternoon, and Mrs. Davilow thought she understood the ache in her daughter’s bones, even when Gwendolen made it clear no one was ever to speak aloud of her late husband, or the hour he slipped beneath the waves. For all the outward signs of crushing calamity that hung over them, Mrs. Davilow was happier than she had been in years, ever since Gwendolen’s ill-fated marriage. Her darling girl had come back to her, not just with the same old affection, but with a tender, clinging cherishing of her that one gives only to a treasure they nearly lost forever.

“Are you there, mamma?” Gwendolen called out in the dead of night, long after the candles had been snuffed, sounding exactly like the scared, wide-eyed girl she had been at 17, who would cry out if she woke alone in the dark. Mrs. Davilow had made up a bed for herself in the same room, just to be near her.
“I’m here, dear. Can I get you anything?”
“No, thank you. I just needed to know you were real. Did I wake you?” It was a question Gwendolen would never have bothered to ask in the old days, when her own wants were the only ones that mattered.
“I wasn’t asleep, darling.”
“It felt like you weren’t really here, even though I can see you. I can bear anything, if you’re with me. But you mustn’t lie awake worrying over me. You’re going to be happy now, and I’m going to make you happy — I swear it. If I can’t do that, what’s left for me?”
“God bless you, my love. There’s no greater happiness for me than being close to you.”

The next night, when Mrs. Davilow heard her sighing restlessly for hours, she offered her the sleeping draught the doctor had left, but Gwendolen turned it down sharp. “Don’t tell me what’s good for me, mamma. You don’t know. Neither you nor uncle must ever try to talk me into doing something that feels all wrong, not after what I’ve been through.”

Mrs. Davilow held her tongue, soft with pity for her poor irritable child, till Gwendolen blurted out, voice cracking: “I was always wicked to you, wasn’t I? That’s why I’m so miserable now.” She burst into great heaving sobs, all the locked-up grief and secret guilt of her marriage spilling out in tears, none of the unutterable facts ever finding voice between them.

Slowly, pieces of the truth had trickled through to Mrs. Davilow, via the letters Sir Hugo had sent to Mr. Gascoigne the rector, who then passed on the gentlest parts of the news to her. The good-natured baronet, overseeing all the formalities of his nephew’s death and the grim wait for the body to wash ashore, had thought it kindest to break the news of Grandcourt’s will to the rector gradually, so he wouldn’t be blindsided by the full shock once he returned home. The truth, when it finally came out, was brutal: all Grandcourt’s vast fortune, all his estates, went entirely to his illegitimate children, the product of a long-standing prior entanglement with a woman named Mrs. Glasher. There was no legitimate heir, and Grandcourt had chosen to leave the vast bulk of his wealth to the children he’d never publicly acknowledged, rather than the wife he’d married barely a year prior.

The rector was deeply stung. He remembered, now, all the times Grandcourt had treated him with icy, arrogant disdain, all the old rumors of his wild dissipations before he’d moved into Diplow Hall — but he had never dreamed those old follies would rear up to rob his niece of every bit of the grand future he’d once imagined for her. He held his tongue, though, behaving like the worldly, conscientious clergyman he was. “A young man making his will in good health always expects to live a long life,” he said, at last. “He never imagined it would play out this way.”

Sir Hugo nodded, agreeing. “What unforgivably grates on me, though, is the way he shortchanged your niece. Our niece. All she gets is two thousand a year, and Gadsmere — that little house plopped right down in the middle of a coal-mining district. It’s shameful, to leave a beautiful young widow with no better a lot than some country doctor’s widow. A man ought to take pride in making sure his widow is comfortable, no matter what. I’d never do that to my wife.” He leaned forward, earnest. “You must urge her to count me as a friend, through all of this. I owe her that much. And mark my words, if you ever arrange another marriage for a niece, even if it’s to the Archbishop of Canterbury himself, you bind that man down with an ironclad settlement before the wedding. Don’t trust anyone’s ‘apparent liberality’ ever again.”

To the rector’s gentle mind, two thousand a year hardly seemed like a paltry sum, but the humiliation of having his niece’s marriage to a man of Grandcourt’s standing revealed to all their acquaintance as a complete farce, with her set aside for another woman’s children, stung worse than any financial loss. He had no idea that Gwendolen already knew every last detail of Mrs. Glasher and her children, though; he assumed, as most men of his time did, that a well-bred wife could never have been privy to such sordid secrets. But Mrs. Davilow was not so naive: all the odd, sharp moments in Gwendolen’s behavior before and after her wedding suddenly made perfect sense, and she resolved to break the rest of the news of the will gently, during their long journey back to England.

She never got the chance.
“I hope you’re not expecting me to be some grand rich widow, mamma,” Gwendolen said one morning, as she sat by the window fully dressed, deep in quiet thought. “I may choose to take nothing at all from his estate.”

Mrs. Davilow stared, stunned. “Oh no, dear, Sir Hugo has already confirmed you’re entitled to that two thousand a year, and Gadsmere house —”
“What I take depends entirely on what I choose to accept,” Gwendolen cut her off, sharp and firm. “You and uncle are not to interfere with me on any of this, not a bit. I will do everything in my power to make you happy, but every choice that touches on my husband’s legacy is mine to make. Would eight hundred a year be enough for you to live on, mamma?”

“More than enough, my love — don’t even think of giving me that much.” Mrs. Davilow paused, soft. “Do you know who inherited all the estates and the rest of the money?”
“Yes,” Gwendolen waved the subject away, like she was swatting at a gnat. “I know everything. It’s all perfectly as it should be. I never want it mentioned again, not ever.”

Mrs. Davilow turned her face away, her cheeks flushing hot, rising to fetch a fan-screen to hide her tears. What unimaginable misery her child had been hiding, all this time, that could never be spoken of between them. But Gwendolen saw her grief, her face softening with that new, gentle intuition the last months had given her. She patted the space on the couch beside her. “Come sit closer, mamma. Don’t be sad. I’m going to be very wise now, I promise. And so good to you, you won’t even recognize me. Just don’t cry, please.”

Beneath that quiet resolve, Gwendolen’s mind was set: the very first thing she would do, once they reached London, was find Daniel Deronda. She would ask him, the only man whose judgment she trusted entirely, whether she ought to accept a single penny of Grandcourt’s money — even if it was to make sure her mother was cared for. She felt, for the first time, strong enough to do anything that would make her worthy of his good opinion, that would lift her a little higher in the light of his approval.

Sir Hugo’s invitation for them all to stay at his Park Lane house in London, while they sorted through all the mourning arrangements and collected Gwendolen’s things from her old home in Grosvenor Square, could not have been more perfect. There, she would have the chance to send Deronda a note, to beg him to come see her. She did not even have his address, but she would not let that stop her.

During the long train ride across the continent back to England, Sir Hugo chattered merrily, doing his best to keep the mood light, talking of possible plans for the future: they could easily lease out Gadsmere to one of the local coal mine managers, he said, it was a perfectly pretty little place, with a lake and a tiny tower, even if it was dotted with soot from the pits. Mr. Gascoigne sighed, mentioning that their old family home Offendene was empty still, no one had taken the lease after all, and she could have lived there rent-free for a year if she’d taken Lord Brackenshaw’s offer.

At the mention of Offendene, Gwendolen’s face softened. She could see it all clearly, in her mind’s eye: the gray broad shoulders of the downs, the speckled cows dotted across the fields, the shadowy plantations and rutted country lanes, the avenue of limes outside the front door, her mother waiting on the step to greet her. Once, she had thought that quiet country life was the dullest prison imaginable, a thing to run from as fast as she could. Now, it looked like a sanctuary, a place to breathe the clean morning air and hear birds sing unblamed, after she had run headlong into a glittering Satan’s masquerade, all gilded masks and hidden serpent tongues, that had ended in a shriek of fear out on the open sea. For a long moment, she lingered over that vision, saying nothing, while the three others in the carriage chattered on about telegraph cables and Church Rate abolition bills.

All that while, one unshakable purpose was the only thing that anchored her: she had to see Deronda. Without him, she had no compass, no moral north star. It was his opinion that had become the breath of her life, the only thing that kept her from lying to herself and letting her old selfishness slide back in. He was her living conscience, the voice of all that was good and invisible that she could never reach on her own.

She did not care that the gossips would whisper, that her old pride would have died before she admitted she needed anyone so badly. She was not a spoiled girl anymore. She was a woman who had almost been swallowed by the sea, and she would not let anyone’s side glances stop her from going to the one man who could save her.

So no sooner had they arrived at the Park Lane house, and she heard Sir Hugo was planning to pop down to his family estate for a day or two before he handled her legal affairs, than she spoke up, no trace of hesitation in her voice, right in front of her mother: “Sir Hugo, I need to see Mr. Deronda as soon as possible. I don’t have his address. Will you give it to me, or send word to him that I’m asking for him?”

A quick, bright thought crossed the baronet’s face, and his easy smile never slipped. “I’m not sure if he’s at his London chambers or at the Abbey right now, but I’ll track him down this very moment. I’ll send a note to his chambers straight away, and if he’s at home he’ll be here before the hour’s out. I know he will be only too happy to hear from you.”

Privately, Sir Hugo was delighted. He had long suspected that there was a quiet, deep affection blooming between his favorite ward Daniel and the beautiful Gwendolen, and he thought it was the most perfect fate possible, that the cruel, selfish Grandcourt had made his exit just in time. He was already determined to defend them both from any whispers, to smooth the way for whatever happiness they might find together. His only small worry was that his stubborn, noble-hearted Daniel might have some grand, secret scheme in his head that would make him pass up the chance to claim the woman who loved him so deeply. But he picked up his pen at once, scribbled the note, and sent a footman running with it to Deronda’s chambers — where the letter landed, right in his hands.
