Time, they say, measures itself in miles traveled or acres gained, yet the soul's geography defies such arithmetic. One may march south until a single bone becomes a map of new anatomy, or turn east to unlock languages that rewrite the history of races; one may endure a hero's poem of maiming and endurance only to return and find the parish gossip unchanged, the elderly gentleman still shaking his head at the butcher's boy, still staring vacantly at the same shop window. Even the swiftest thought, galloping like a greyhound, cannot outrun the limpet's crawl—a slow, sticky progress that eventually reveals itself as motion. This tension between the revolutionary surge of transformation and the quiet recurrence of the familiar defined the year's passage.

It had stripped the brilliant, self-assured Gwendolen Harleth of the Archery Meeting, crushing her into a penitent who confessed her unworthiness despite a heart that craved to be deemed worthy. Yet, at Pennicote Rectory, the change was superficial, a mere adjustment of budgets and social calendars. The rectory remained a haven of cheerful certainty; the red and pink peonies bloomed with undimmed vigor, and the rector maintained his sunny faith in patronage, diligently fulfilling his duties regardless of audience. His two unsigned ecclesiastical articles, circulated privately among the elite who knew their author but rarely read their content, were treated with polite indifference, allowing him to bask in peaceful authorship, nourished by complacency rather than the lean, hungry scrutiny of public critique. For the rector, the loss of eighteen hundred pounds a year was a trivial price for the renewed delight in his favorite son. Whatever shadow the disappointment of his first love had cast over Rex, it appeared secondary to the evidence of a more serious ambition born from family necessity. Mr. Gascoigne viewed the previous year's turmoil as merely the evaporation of excess moisture, a final step in baking the human dough.

Now, Rex returned for a summer visit, bringing Anna home. While he moved with the old vitality among his siblings, he retreated into the disciplined solitude of the eager student, rising early and closing himself away in the evenings to pursue a rigorous course of study.

"You do not regret choosing the law as a profession, Rex?" his father asked.

"There is no profession I would prefer," Rex replied firmly. "My ambition is to serve as a first-rate judge and help draft a new code. I invert the famous maxim: 'Give me something to do with making the laws, and let others compose the songs.'"

"You will inevitably have to stuff your mind with immense amounts of rubbish, I suppose—that is the drawback," the rector noted.

"I perceive no distinction between legal rubbish and the literary nonsense that clogs other minds," Rex countered. "Law does not dull the intellect. Our wisest men have often been lawyers. An orderly framework of cases and evidence offers clarity where others find only a chaotic wash of trivia. Furthermore, from a higher vantage, the foundations and evolution of law constitute the most compelling chapters of philosophy and history. Naturally, there will be tedious, drudging moments, perhaps exasperating ones. But the greatest prizes in life are never won easily, and I have learned that lesson."

"My boy," the rector said, his voice swelling with paternal pride, "the finest omen of professional success is believing one's chosen path is the noblest in existence. Most men find this conviction once they commit with will. Recall Brewitt, the blacksmith, who told me recently that his apprentice lacked interest in his trade. 'And yet, sir,' Brewitt remarked, 'what would a young fellow have if he does not like blacksmithing?' I cherish this fatherly joy, dispensing it in measured doses."

Warham, lost to India, had been parted from with ease, but Rex represented a different kind of romance for the rector—a later-life discovery of a son surpassing himself, a living testament to future eminence modeled on famous precedents. Only to his wife did he declare with absolute certainty, "Rex will be a distinguished man, Nancy, I assure you—as surely as Paley's father foresaw his son's greatness."

"Was Paley an old bachelor?" Mrs. Gascoigne queried.

"That is hardly relevant, my dear," the rector replied, forgetting the irrelevance instantly. Mrs. Gascoigne felt a pang of weakness at her own inquiry.

In the low, narrow drawing room of "Jodson's," a modest white house nestled a mile away behind a screen of evergreens, time marched in quiet rhythm. The group who had traded the faded grandeur of Offendene for this enclosure found comfort in its simplicity. Mrs. Davilow's delicate face bore only a deepening of her mild melancholy, her hair streaked with a few additional silver threads from the year's trials. Her four daughters had blossomed slightly, freed from the shadow of their mother's grief, while the good Jocosa maintained her neutral stance toward worldly pleasures, reserved for those not "in a situation."

On a July afternoon, the room opened via wide lattice windows to the intoxicating scent of monthly roses, the soft murmur of the garden, and the distant, clarifying clip-clop of hooves and wheels. With Rex and Anna joining the usual sextet, the scene became lively and crowded. Anna, beloved by her younger cousins, recounted tales of London and her new acquaintances. When she first arrived alone, questions poured forth regarding Gwendolen's residence in Grosvenor Square, her words, and the whispers surrounding her. Had Anna visited Gwendolen after learning of the yacht? No. This silence left the door open to wild speculation about the mysterious vessel, save for the fact that Gwendolen had written prior to departure announcing a Mediterranean cruise with Mr. Grandcourt, and later from Marseilles expressing her delight in the elegance of the cabins, promising a lengthy diary filled with *ditto* marks instead of further correspondence. The news had also surfaced in the newspapers, cementing this new chapter of Gwendolen's exalted life as a central thread in the sisters' romantic tapestry, prompting Isabel, the book-devouring cousin, to weave in a dash of corsair adventure to ensure a happy ending.

However, whenever Rex entered the room, the girls, adhering to strict instructions, silenced this captivating subject. Instead, today's conversation centered on the Meyricks and their extraordinary Jewish associates, sparking astonished queries from minds that viewed live Jews as a biological anomaly, akin to a strange species described by Pliny that might sleep beneath the shade of its own ears. Bertha struggled to comprehend modern Jewish beliefs, vaguely suspecting a rejection of the Old Testament due to its validation of the New; Miss Merry concluded that Mirah and her brother were impossible to reason with; and the amiable Alice simply stated she could not bear them. Mrs. Davilow gently corrected her daughter, noting that prominent Jewish families integrated into society behaved perfectly in London and Paris, though admitting that unconverted commoners were objectionable. Isabel pressed whether Mirah spoke differently, or if one could sit beside her without realizing her Jewish heritage.

Rex, possessing no partisan allegiance to the Israelites but burdened by a cram-filled acquaintance with the minutiae of their ancient history, amused himself by playfully exaggerating each speaker's prejudice. Anna interceded, begging them to understand his jest, but their laughter was cut short by the arrival of a letter for Mrs. Davilow. A messenger had delivered it with frantic haste directly from the rectory. Inside lay a telegram. As Mrs. Davilow read and reread the message in silent, agitated tremors, all eyes turned to her with mounting anxiety, yet no one dared to break the silence. Finally lifting her gaze to meet the young faces "painted with fear," she recalled that they might be imagining horrors worse than the truth, mirroring her own initial paralysis of dread. With a sob that was half-relief, she spoke,

"My dears, Mr. Grandcourt—" She paused, gathering her strength, then began again, "Mr. Grandcourt is drowned."

Rex sprang to his feet as if struck by a physical blow. He could not restrain himself, and Anna's first instinct was to look at him. But then, summoning a fragile reserve as Mrs. Davilow scanned the rector's accompanying note, he managed to ask,

"Can I do anything, Aunt? Can I carry a message to my father for you?"

"Yes, dear. Tell him I shall be ready—he is very good. He says he will accompany me to Genoa; he will be here at half-past six. Jocosa and Alice, assist me in preparing. She is safe—Gwendolen is safe—but she must be ill. I am certain she is terribly ill. Rex, dear—you and Anna—go and inform your father I shall be quite ready. I would not for the world lose another night. Bless him for his readiness; I can travel day and night until we reach her."

Rex and Anna hurried away through sunlight that had suddenly grown solemn, exchanging not a word. Anna was consumed by solicitude for any reopening of Rex's wounds, while he wrestled with a tumultuous swarm of thoughts that offended his better judgment. The chaos persisted as they reached the rectory gate.

"Nannie, I shall leave you to convey everything to my father," Rex said. "If he requires me immediately, let me know. I shall remain in the shrubbery for ten minutes—no longer."
