Ethne chose the pew at the end, where the worn oak had been darkened by generations of palms—farmers’ hands, fisherwives’ hands, children’s hands sticky with summer fruit. She sat in the corner nearest the aisle. Feversham remained standing beside her, as if he did not trust his legs to fold without betraying him.

The little church at Glenalla held its hush the way a larder holds coolness: faithfully, without fuss. Sunlight came in high through the small upper windows and lay under the roof-beams in a thin honey-coloured haze. The open door admitted the countryside in soft portions—grass-sweet air, peat-smoke from some distant kitchen fire, and the untroubled, layered sound of life: bees worrying the hedges, swallows cutting the space with their quick cries, the far clink of a gate-chain, and beyond it all the sea’s steady breathing. No cart-wheels here, no iron shriek, no factory hammering—only the acoustic order of a place that still knew its own rhythm.

“I am glad you remembered our drive,” she said, looking straight ahead, not at him, as though she were speaking a lesson learned by heart. “And what we said. It is important to me that you should remember.”

Her voice had the steadiness of someone who had been seasoning these words for days, turning them over like grain in a wooden bowl until the husk fell away and only the necessary remained.

“Because—though I have got you back—I am going to send you away from me again. You will be one of the absent friends whom I shall not lose because you are absent.”

She did not falter. If the sentence cut her, she would not let it cut raggedly. She had given herself two weeks to make it clean.

“I know you will mind,” she continued, still facing forward, “just as I do. But there is no help for it. At all events you are at home again, with the right to be at home. That is a great comfort to me. And there are other reasons—greater reasons—from which we can both take comfort.”

A swallow darted through the open door and out again, so quick it seemed a thought. Ethne’s hands lay quiet in her lap.

“Colonel Trench told me enough of your captivity to convince me that we both see with the same eyes,” she said. “We both understand that this second parting—hard as it is—is still a very slight thing compared with the other, our first parting six years ago.”

The words went on evenly, but the church seemed to listen, and the summer murmur outside drew back, respectful.

“I felt very lonely after that,” she said, “as I shall not feel lonely now. There was a great barrier between us then, separating us forever. We should never have met again—here or afterwards. I am quite sure of that. But you have broken the barrier down by all your pain and bravery during these last years. I am no less sure of that.”

She paused, and the quiet pressed in like cool linen.

“So although we shall not see one another here, and as long as we live,” she said at last, “the afterwards is quite sure for us both. And we can wait for that. You can—you have waited with so much strength all these years since we parted. And I can too, for I get strength from your victory.”

The silence that followed was not empty. To Feversham her words fell like water on a mouthful of dry bread—simple, life-giving, and almost unbearable for the relief of it. They drew the sting from memories he had carried like grit under the skin: nights of waking in strange rooms with the smell of dust and sweat, the far call to prayer sliding over rooftops, the endless, sand-choked days. He had told Sutch once that in his last moment he would hear Ethne’s bitter words again and die to their echo. Standing now in this little church, he knew that prophecy to have been false. If any voice would follow him into the dark, it would be this one—steadfast, forgiving, and sorrowful in its courage.

Her proposal to separate did not surprise him. He had heard she was engaged. He did not argue. He only waited, for he felt there was more she meant to lay down between them like a final offering.

Ethne delayed. Not with tricks, but with pauses—those small stretches of time in which the heart, like dough, is allowed to rest before it must be shaped and baked. When he went out through the church door into the sunlight and summer sound, his shadow would leave the threshold; and after that she meant never to speak with him or look upon him again until life ended. She had the right—she told herself—to keep him a little longer, just as one keeps a last mouthful in the mouth to catch every note of taste before swallowing.

At length she said, “I am going to be married—immediately. I am to marry a friend of yours, Colonel Durrance.”

Feversham answered without pause, for this at least he could give her: no additional struggle.

“He has cared for you a long while. I was not aware of it until I went away. But thinking everything over—I thought it likely. In a very little time I became sure.”

“He is blind.”

“Blind!” The word broke from him before he could keep it contained. “He, of all men—blind!”

“Exactly,” Ethne said. “He—of all men.” Her eyes remained fixed on the pale stone ahead. “His blindness explains everything—why I marry him, why I send you away. It was after he went blind that I became engaged to him. It was before Captain Willoughby came to me with the first feather. It was between those two events.”

She breathed once, carefully.

“After you went away one thinks,” she said, “rather carefully. I used to lie awake and think, and I resolved that two men’s lives should not be spoilt because of me.”

“Mine was not,” Feversham said quickly. “Please believe that.”

“Partly it was,” she returned, as if she had weighed that sentence too and found it true. “You would not own it for my sake, but it was. I was determined that a second should not be. And so, when Colonel Durrance went blind—you know the man he was—you can understand what blindness meant to him: the loss of everything he cared for—”

“Except you.”

“Yes,” she said quietly. “Except me. So I became engaged to him.”

Outside, a cart creaked somewhere along the village street; the sound was old, wood on wood, and did not jar. Modern noise did not belong in this place. Here, even grief had room to speak without being drowned.

“But he has grown very quick,” Ethne went on. “You cannot guess how quick. And he sees so very clearly. A hint tells him the whole hidden truth.”

“At present,” she added, “he knows nothing of the four feathers.”

“Are you sure?” Feversham asked suddenly.

She turned her face toward him for the first time since she had sat down, startled into movement as though a hand had touched her shoulder.

“Yes. Why?”

“Lieutenant Sutch was at Suakin while I was at Omdurman,” he said. “He knew I was a prisoner there. He sent messages to me—he tried to organise my escape.”

“Oh,” Ethne said, her surprise softening into comprehension. “Colonel Durrance certainly knew you were in Omdurman. He saw you in Wadi Halfa, and he heard you had gone south into the desert. He was distressed about it. He asked a friend to get news of you, and the friend got news that you were in Omdurman. He told me so himself—and yes, he told me he would try to arrange for your escape. No doubt he did it through Lieutenant Sutch.”

She gathered herself again.

“He has been at Wiesbaden with an oculist,” she said. “He only returned a week ago. Otherwise he would have told me about it. Very likely he was the reason Lieutenant Sutch was at Suakin.”

Then, with the precision of someone naming the one ingredient that would ruin a whole pot, she said, “But he knows nothing of the four feathers. He only knows that our engagement was abruptly broken off; he believes I have no longer any thought of you at all.”

Her voice thinned, and the next words came as if they were pulled from her with careful force.

“But if you come back—if you and I saw anything of each other—however calmly we met, however indifferently we spoke—he would guess. He is so quick he would be sure to guess.”

She paused and whispered, the blood rising in her face as if the truth itself were heat.

“And he would guess right.”

Feversham did not move closer. He would not add pressure to an hour already heavy with it. But he understood—understood with a clarity that felt like cold water poured into his chest.

“Yes,” he said. “I see. And he must not guess.”

“No,” she returned at once. “He must not. I am so glad you see that too, Harry. The straight and simple thing is the only thing for us to do. He must never guess, for—as you said—he has nothing left but me.”

“Is Durrance here?” Feversham asked.

“He is staying at the vicarage.”

“Very well.” Feversham’s voice was steady now, made so by the very finality of what they were doing. “It is only fair I should tell you—I had no thought you would wait. I had no wish that you should; I had no right to such a wish.”

He looked down at the old boards underfoot, their grain like faint ripples in a dark sea.

“When you gave me that fourth feather in the little room at Ramelton,” he said, “with the music coming faintly through the door, I understood your meaning. There was to be a complete—an irrevocable end. We were not to be the merest acquaintances. So I said nothing to you of the plan which came clear and definite into my mind at the very time when you gave me the feathers.”

He lifted his eyes briefly, not to plead, but to be exact.

“You see, I might never have succeeded. I might have died trying. I might even perhaps have shirked the attempt. It would have been time enough to speak if I came back. So I never formed any wish that you should wait.”

“That was what Colonel Trench told me,” Ethne said.

“I told him that too?”

“On your first night in the House of Stone.”

“Well—it’s just the truth.” His mouth tightened, then softened. “The most I hoped for—and I did hope for it every hour of every day—was that, if I did come home, you would take back your feather, and that we might—not renew our friendship here, but see something of one another afterwards.”

“Yes,” Ethne said. “Then there will be no parting.”

She spoke simply, without a sigh. But she looked at him then and smiled—a small, brave thing. It told him, more than any outcry could have done, what the cost would be to her.

And in that moment he understood, with a completeness he had never reached in all his solitary rehearsals of the past, what it must have cost her six years ago to be left standing with her pride and heart both struck down.

“What trouble you must have gone through!” he cried.

She looked him over as if to remind him he was not the only one who had been wounded and lived.

“Not I alone,” she said gently. “I passed no nights in the House of Stone.”

“But it was my fault.” His words came hurriedly now, as if he could pay some last debt by naming it. “Do you remember what you said when the morning came through the blinds? ‘It’s not right that one should suffer so much pain.’ It was not right.”

“I had forgotten the words,” she said, “oh, a long time since—until Colonel Trench reminded me. I should never have spoken them. When I did, I was not thinking they would live so in your thoughts. I am sorry that I spoke them.”

“Oh, they were just enough,” Feversham said, and even managed a short laugh, cracked at the edge. “I never blamed you for them. I used to think they would be the last words I should hear when I turned my face to the wall. But you have given me others to-day wherewith to replace them.”

“Thank you,” she said quietly.

There was nothing more to be decided. The solemn issue had been met, faced, and settled. Feversham wondered, in a distant part of himself, why she did not rise at once and end it. He could not bring himself to talk of his travels now, of dangers and escapes, as though he were recounting a campaign over supper. The occasion seemed too vital for that, too much like the closing of a door on a room where a fire still burned.

Yet Ethne kept him there—by stillness, by the fact of her presence. Calm as she looked, longing ached in her like hunger. Just for a little longer, she begged herself, measuring time not by clocks but by light.

The sun was withdrawing from the church walls. A bright strip of gold still lay on the grey stone, and she watched it narrow as if it were a wick being consumed.

“I am glad you escaped from Omdurman without the help of Lieutenant Sutch or Colonel Durrance,” she said at last. “I wanted so much that everything should be done by you alone—without anybody’s help or interference.”

After that came another stretch of silence, full of the summer’s faint, living noises outside. Once or twice she looked toward the wall. Each time the golden space was smaller. Each time she knew her minutes were thinning.

“You suffered horribly at Dongola,” she said in a low voice. “Colonel Trench told me.”

“What does it matter now?” Feversham answered. “That time seems rather far away to me.”

Ethne’s voice softened further, as if she were touching something fragile.

“Had you anything of mine with you?” she asked.

“I had your white feather.”

“But anything else? Any little thing I had given you—in the other days?”

“Nothing.”

“I had your photograph,” she said. “I kept it.”

Feversham bent down toward her, startled as if warmth had suddenly been put into his hands.

“You did!”

Ethne nodded.

“Yes. The moment I went upstairs that night I packed up your presents and addressed them to your rooms.”

“Yes—I got them in London.”

“But I put your photograph aside first of all to keep,” she said. “I burnt all your letters after I had addressed the parcel and taken it down to the hall to be sent away. I had just finished burning your letters when I heard your step upon the gravel in the early morning underneath my windows. But I had already put your photograph aside. I have it now. I shall keep it and the feathers together.”

After a moment she added, almost as if she spoke to herself, “I rather wish you had had something of mine with you all the time.”

“I had no right to anything,” he said.

The strip of gold on the stone had become no more than a thin blade; it seemed impossible that something so bright could be so narrow.

“What will you do now?” she asked.

“I shall go home first and see my father,” he said. “It will depend upon the way we meet.”

“You will let Colonel Durrance know,” she said. “I would like to hear about it.”

“Yes,” Feversham replied. “I will write to Durrance.”

The last gold went out. The church filled with the clear light of a summer evening—colourless, truthful, and without mercy.

“I shall not see you for a long while,” Ethne said, and for the first time her voice broke into a sob. “I shall not have a letter from you again.”

She leaned forward a little, bowing her head because tears had gathered and would spill. Then she rose—bravely, as though bravery were a habit she would not set aside even now.

Together they went out of the church side by side. As they walked she leaned toward him so that their sleeves touched. The contact was slight, but it struck through him like a taste remembered.

Feversham untied his horse and mounted. As his foot found the stirrup Ethne drew her dog close against her skirt, holding it as if it were something steady in a world turning.

“Good-bye,” she said.

She did not try to smile. She held out her hand.

He took it and bent down from the saddle close to her. She kept her eyes on his though the tears brimmed in them, bright as the last light had been on the stone.

“Good-bye,” he said.

He held her hand for a little while longer than he ought, then released it and rode down the hill.

After a hundred yards he stopped and looked back.

Ethne had stopped too. With that space between them and their faces turned toward one another, they remained. She made no sign—no wave, no final gesture—only stood and looked, as if to wave would be to admit a lesser ending than the truth.

Then she turned away and went up the village street toward her house alone and very slowly.

Feversham watched until she went in at the gate. She grew dim and blurred to him before she reached it. Yet he could see, even through the blurring, that she did not look back.

He turned his horse and rode down the hill. The wrong he had done so long ago could not be undone, not even by six years of labour, pain, and courage. It would go on living; and its consequence would be sorrow to the end of life for someone other than himself.

That she bore the sorrow without complaint—doing the straight, simple thing her loyal nature demanded—did not lessen his remorse. It sharpened it. It taught him, with a bitter clarity, that she least of all deserved unhappiness. The harm was irreparable. Others might have forgotten. Ethne would not.

For Ethne was of those who neither lightly feel nor lightly forget; who, if they love, cannot love with half a heart. And she would be alone now, he knew—alone in spite of her marriage—alone to the very end, and at the last moment of death.
