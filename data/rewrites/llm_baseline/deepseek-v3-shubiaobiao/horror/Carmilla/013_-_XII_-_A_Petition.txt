The Countess’s voice was low, urgent, as she leaned toward me. “We must part, but only briefly—I hope no more than a few hours.” Her gloved fingers tightened around her fan. “Though it may stretch to weeks. His words to me just now were… unfortunate. You do not recognize me, do you?”

I confessed I did not.

A shadow of a smile flickered across her lips. “You shall know me in time, but not yet. We are older friends than you realize.” She glanced over her shoulder, then back at me. “In three weeks, I will pass by your estate—I have already inquired after it. I will visit you then, if only for an hour, to renew what was once dear to me. But now—” Her voice faltered. “Now I must leave at once. A matter of life and death calls me away, and I must travel swiftly, by obscure roads.”

She hesitated, then spoke carefully. “I would not burden you, but my daughter has not fully recovered from a riding accident. The physician insists she must not endure another journey so soon. We came here in easy stages—six leagues a day at most. But I cannot take her with me now. I must ride hard, through day and night.”

Her request, when it came, was delivered not as a plea, but as a command wrapped in velvet politeness. She asked—no, *instructed*—me to take charge of her daughter in her absence.

It was audacious. Absurd. Yet before I could refuse, my own child was at my side, whispering eagerly, “Oh, *please*, let Millarca stay with us!”

The Countess’s daughter—Millarca—stood silent, her dark eyes fixed on mine. There was something unsettling in her gaze, something that made my skin prickle even as I found myself agreeing.

The Countess kissed her daughter swiftly, murmured words I could not hear, and then—with a final, lingering look—she was gone, swept away by the pale gentleman in black.

We watched from the window as her carriage vanished into the night.

“She didn’t look back,” Millarca said softly.

I tried to reassure her. “She may not have known you were watching.”

But the girl only sighed, her fingers curling into fists at her sides.

The ball wore on. Dawn approached. And somewhere in the crush of dancers and the flicker of candlelight, Millarca slipped away.

We searched. Of course we searched. But she was nowhere to be found.

It was not until the next afternoon that a servant brought word—a young lady, distressed, asking after us.

Relief turned to unease as she reappeared, spinning a tale of exhaustion and misdirection. She had slept, she claimed, in a stranger’s room, lost in the labyrinth of the castle.

I should have sent her away then.

But I did not.

And so Millarca came home with us.

A decision I would soon regret.
