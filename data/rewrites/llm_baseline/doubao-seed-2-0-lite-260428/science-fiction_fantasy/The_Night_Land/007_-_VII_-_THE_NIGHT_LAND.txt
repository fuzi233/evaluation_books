I moved northwest at a deliberate, wary pace, for hours on end, steering clear of the domain of the Great Watcher of the North-West even as I mapped out the shape of the journey ahead of me. I knew no one alive could say how far I would have to travel across the lightless wastes, nor what horrors waited beyond every dark ridge, so I resolved to pace myself rather than burn out in the first stretch. I laid out a strict routine for my days outside the Great Redoubt: travel for eighteen hours straight, stop to eat and replenish my strength every six hours, and rest for a full six hours each cycle, so my body would be sharp and ready when true danger found me. For a world crammed with nameless, ravenous perils, that prudence would be the difference between life and a silent, unmarked death in the dark.

Of course, I broke that rule within the first day. For twenty-one straight hours I crept and hid, ducking behind jagged rock spires and hunching low through moss thickets to stay out of the Watcher’s line of sight, too wired to feel hunger, too focused on not being seen to pause for rest. By the time the twenty-first hour ticked over, my limbs were heavy with exhaustion and my vision swam with faintness. I scanned the dark for shelter, and soon spotted a small fire-hole glowing low in the rock, the sort of small geothermal vent I had passed a handful of times already that night. I made my way toward it, grateful for the promise of warmth against the Night Land’s endless, bone-deep chill, and a dry spot to curl up and sleep.

When I drew close, it was a tiny, comforting oasis amid endless gloom: a pit no more than a few paces across, filled with dull, bubbling molten earth that puffed thin fumes of sulphur into the cold air. I sat down a safe distance from the edge, the Diskos I carried propped on the rock within arm’s reach. For a long minute I did not move, too tired to summon the energy to eat or drink, only turning to stare back at the colossal bulk of the Mighty Pyramid. Though I had walked for miles, its sheer size made it feel nearly close enough to touch, leaving me torn between two feelings: sharp elation at the nearness of my home, and cold, heavy dread at the scale of the impossible quest I had set out on. I knew millions of souls inside its walls were watching me in that moment, and I thought of Master Monstruwacan, my dear friend, surely peering through the Great Spy-Glass at my small, huddled shape, far out in the dark.

Shaking off my foolish reluctance, I opened my small pack of supplies, pulled out three compact nutrient tablets, and chewed them down. They were dense, concentrated sustenance, tiny enough to take up almost no space, and I shook a measured pinch of special chemical dust out of a sealed vial into my small drinking cup. The air reacted with the dust in a fizzing, boiling rush, and the powder swelled to fill the cup with clean, cool water—an old, reliable trick the Redoubt’s engineers had refined over eons, that let me carry months of food and water in a pack small enough to sling over one shoulder. It was plain fare, no feast, but more than enough to sustain me through the dark wastes.

After I ate, I sorted through the rest of my supplies, and pulled out the special compass Monstruwacan had built for me by hand, salvaged from an ancient relic in the Great Museum. Unlike olden compasses that pointed north, this one was tuned to lock onto the Earth-Current thrumming through the Great Pyramid’s foundations, so I would never lose my way home even if the total blackness swallowed the Pyramid’s lights entirely. I set it on the rock, and for a long time its needle only spun and wavered uselessly— I was still high above the vast spread of the Underground Fields, after all, not far above the legendary Crack that split the deep crust of the world. Later, hundreds of miles further into my journey, the needle would lock true on the Pyramid’s signal, a steady comfort. Even later, far out in the uncharted dark, the needle would jerk unexpectedly west, tugging hard toward some unknown colossal ancient power, and I would wonder if it was pulling at the fabled North Force my old texts and memory-dreams had warned of, or the faint, distant signal of the long-lost Lesser Redoubt, the home of the maid I had sworn to rescue.

I stowed the compass back in my pouch, wrapped my heavy travel cloak around my shoulders, and tucked the humming, warm Diskos inside the fabric to rest beside me, as if it were a trusted companion rather than a weapon. Half-drowsing at the edge of sleep, I could feel the aether thrum around me, humming with the collective sympathy of the millions of people in the Pyramid who were thinking of me, sending quiet well-wishes that wrapped around me like a second blanket. My last waking thought was of the girl I was traveling to find, and I fell asleep dreaming of her, of light that did not burn, free of the Night Land’s endless sorrow.

I jolted awake to the blaring, resonant roar of the Home-Call, howling across every inch of the dark. I snatched the Diskos’ haft in an instant, eyes snapping toward the Pyramid’s observation spire high overhead. The Home-Call was only ever used to wake travelers out in the dark to an immediate, mortal threat. Bright, strobing green flashes burst from the spire’s peak, spelling out the Set-Speech warning in sharp, fast bursts: a Great Grey Man, one of the hideously mutated man-beasts that roamed the outer wastes, had scented me, and was even now crawling toward me through the moss thickets behind the fire-hole. The message ordered me to dive left, into the nearest cover, to ambush the creature before it could lunge.

I was already moving before the last flash faded, huddling low in the shadow of a dense moss clump, my heart hammering so hard I could hear it over the distant rumble of the fire-hole. The great grey beast crept out of the thickets, enormous, every inch of its skin the colour of ash. The glow of the molten rock in the fire-hole clearly pained its sensitive eyes, and it pressed its snout flat to the ground, hunching its shoulders and craning its neck in a brutish, serpentine motion, trying to track my scent past the glare. It circled the vent three times to the left, three times to the right, and every time it vanished back into the thick dark under the moss, I was certain it had spotted me and was about to creep up to tear me apart from behind.

Then a clear, sharp voice spoke directly in my mind, sent by Master Monstruwacan himself, amplified by the low, thrumming vibration of the Master-Word through the aether to confirm its authenticity: the beast had found my trail, and was making a wide circle around my hiding spot to attack me from the rear. I launched myself out of my first hiding place and dove for a new moss clump no more than a second before a great, gnarled grey hand pushed aside the foliage where I had been crouched. The beast’s massive, hideous head poked through the opening, snuffling the empty spot where my scent still hung.

I struck before it could turn around. The Diskos blazed with spinning, white-hot fire as it cleaved through the creature’s skull and neck in a single blow. The great beast toppled, its legs twitching and curling as it died, the Diskos in my hand spitting sparks as if it rejoiced at the kill. When it went still, I walked to the far side of the fire-hole, held the spinning Diskos high above my head, so the telltale flash of its fire would reach the Great Spy-Glass, to prove I was unharmed and the threat was done.

Monstruwacan did not speak to me again after that. To send telepathic messages through the aether risked drawing the attention of the nameless, ancient Powers that lurked in the deepest dark of the Night Land, and our exchange had already been far riskier than was prudent. But I could feel the aether hum all around me, thrumming with the collective relief of millions of people inside the Redoubt who had watched the fight unfold, all of them cheering silently for me. It made me feel far less alone, even out in the endless dark.

When I checked my timepiece, I realized I had slept for ten full hours, far longer than the six I had scheduled, and I chided myself for my foolishness. I picked up my cloak and my scattered supplies, turned to glance once more up the impossible, mile-high slope of the Pyramid rising into the black, and did not wave or make any grand farewell. I did not want to stir the emotions of millions so strongly that I disturbed the aether further, drawing unwanted eyes from the dark. I turned my back on my home and set out again, the Diskos warm and light in my hand, as if it shared some quiet bond with me, just as every Redoubt warrior knew their own Diskos gained a unique affinity for its owner over years of use, so much so that a stranger could not wield it without clumsiness or injury. It was ancient custom that a warrior’s Diskos be buried with them on the Last Road in the Country of Silence, to return its thrumming Earth-Current power back to the planet’s core, a small, loving gesture to honour the dead.

I trekked on for six more hours, moving steadily toward the northwest, veering slightly west to give the Great Watcher of the North-West a wide berth. Foolishly, though, curiosity got the better of me, and I angled a little further north than was safe, just to catch a clear glimpse of the colossal monster from afar. The human heart is a strange, reckless thing: one minute you are frozen with terror, the next you are throwing all caution to the wind for no reason but to satisfy a useless, hungry curiosity.

I stopped every six hours to eat and drink, glancing back at the distant glow of the Pyramid, pausing to steel my courage before moving on. I crawled low across stony ground that reeked of sulphur fumes seeping up through the cracks, scraping my palms raw on sharp rock, until I pulled on the thick, reinforced gloves stowed in my pack, built to protect me from the harsh, sharp wastes of the Night Land. I kept my eyes scanning every shadow, every dark crevice, never letting my guard drop, as I drew closer and closer to the enormous, hidden shape of the Great Watcher.
