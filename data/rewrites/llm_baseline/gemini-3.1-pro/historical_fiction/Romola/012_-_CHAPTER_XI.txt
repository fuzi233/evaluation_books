When the harsh, discordant shuffling of Fra Luca’s sandals had finally ceased to vibrate against the ancient stones, Tito remained rooted in irresolution. It was not until the friar’s silhouette dissolved into the church of Santa Felicita, and the heavy, commercial clatter of the passing merchants subsided, that Tito resumed his walk along the Via de’ Bardi. The sonic environment of the street was a grating cacophony of mercantilism—the squeal of ungreased cart wheels, the shouted bargains of tradesmen prioritizing quick coin over craft—an acoustic pollution that bruised his carefully cultivated sense of well-being. 

“If this monk is a Florentine,” Tito muttered to himself, the words catching in his throat like rancid oil; “if he remains here to speak in the dominant, unyielding lexicon of this city, the fragile translation of my past will be entirely overwritten. Everything must be disclosed.” 

He felt the bitter bite of a new crisis, a sour fermentation bubbling within him, but he was not so agitated as to abandon his evening ritual. He had an apology to deliver to Bardo for his absence. Tito’s talent for concealment was maturing, curing like a spiced ham hung in a dark cellar, developing into something far more complex and pungent than neutral silence. It was perhaps inevitable that he might have to avow Baldassarre’s existence. But doing so would cast a sickening taint over his original reticence, exposing his quiet investment of his adoptive father’s florins as a calculated feast enjoyed while his benefactor’s fate remained a starving mystery. 

For tonight, it was provisionally wise to let the broth simmer untouched. He would suspend decisive thought; the long, quiet hours of the night would provide the proper acoustic and mental solitude for meditation. 

He ascended the stone stairs and entered the second-storey sanctuary where Romola and her father sat. Here, the jarring noise of the streets was banished, replaced by the hushed, reverent acoustics of preservation. Bardo and Romola were not merely translating texts; they were meticulous food historians, safeguarding the cultural memory of ancient Hellenic symposiums and the dying agrarian dialects of their ancestors. Romola was engaged in a quiet, mythic domestic routine, grinding dried myrtle and coriander in a mortar, the rhythmic *thump-shush* of the pestle weaving seamlessly into the room’s tranquil soundscape. Tito entered with a face only slightly less bright than usual, bringing the scent of the cool night air and a polished regret for missing the Corso. He threw extra animation into the evening’s discourse, discussing the precise, evocative culinary techniques of the ancients. Yet, all the while, his consciousness worked like a complex millstone, separating the fine flour of his conversation from the coarse chaff of his secret dilemma. 

By the time he descended the stairs into the starlight, his mind had fermented a new purpose. The following day, free from his professorial duties, he walked up the Via del Cocomero toward the convent of San Marco. The olfactory landscape of the city enveloped him—the rich aroma of crushed garlic in hot fat, the yeasty breath of baking bread—grounding his senses as his purpose finalized. He intended to discover exactly what Fra Luca conjectured, and upon what grounds. With that knowledge, he would manipulate the dominant lexicon of the Florentine court to mold a narrative that would save him from the necessity of quitting this sensory paradise. 

Tito had never before needed to fabricate an ingenious lie. But circumstance was the mother of such invention, and his palate for deceit was surprisingly refined. He had convinced himself that he was not bound to seek out Baldassarre. He had once claimed he would go unhesitatingly if assured of his father’s whereabouts, but *why* was he truly bound? What, when tasted on the back of the tongue, was the end of all life but to extract the utmost sum of sensory pleasure? Was not his own blooming existence a promise of an incomparably richer vintage, a feast of complex flavors for himself and others, compared to the withered, wintry palate of an old man whose capacity for keen enjoyment had long since calcified? 

Baldassarre’s ideas had been sown in the fresh, fertile loam of Tito’s mind, and there they sprouted vividly. This was the circular pacing of generations, the agrarian cycle of nature which treats all maturity merely as the compost to nourish the young. Baldassarre had eaten his fill; he had drained his goblet of life. Tito felt it was now his turn at the banquet. 

The alternative was a bitter draft: to travel to Antioch, a long, nauseating voyage across the sea, abandoning a life fragrant with distinction, love, and sweet wine, only to resume an old, exacting companionship whose rigid flavors he already knew by rote. True, the gems—and therefore the florins—belonged to Baldassarre in the narrow, utilitarian language of ordinary affairs. But in the larger, mythic worldview of the earth’s natural cycles, they belonged to the youth who could extract the most profound, gustatory joy from them. 

Tito knew this was not the sentiment codified in the city's dominant moral lexicon. The men of the superficial, commercial guilds would expect him to sacrifice his feast to rescue his benefactor. But society’s expectations were merely a tangle of anomalous, untranslatable traditions, a discordant noise that no wise man would let ruin his acoustic peace. Any maxims that demanded a man fling away the very sustenance needed to make existence sweet were simply the hollow echoes of others’ selfishness. He liked no one to suffer, true, but could any sterile philosophical musing prove that he must care for another’s starvation more than his own feast? To do so, he must have loved Baldassarre devotedly. He did not. Was a man to be condemned for the natural limitations of his palate? 

As Tito's thoughts acted like a virulent, acidic vinegar, they rapidly macerated all the tender tissues of sentiment. His mind was entirely devoid of that ancient dread—the awe of a Divine Nemesis. Such terror of the unseen was to Tito a mere auditory hallucination, an archaic myth that sought to enforce an unnatural fast upon the living. The old Eumenides in Aeschylus might sing that fear should sit as the guardian of the soul, forcing it into wisdom, but to Tito, such a threatening shadow only spoiled the flavor of the full sunshine. 

When Tito entered the outer cloister of San Marco, he felt no shadowy presentiment. He was too attuned to the physical world, too deeply rooted in the sensory and the skeptical, to entertain the sterile tales of priests. Yet, fear remained a potent ingredient in his nature—the visceral, physical dread of being robbed of his pleasures, of being exiled from the culinary and cultural hearth of Florence. 

He inquired for Fra Luca. 

“Fra Luca? Ah, he is gone to Fiesole—to the Dominican monastery there. He was taken on a litter in the cool of the morning. The poor Brother is very ill. Could you leave a message for him?” 

The answer was delivered by a *fra converso*, a lay brother whose thick, earthy accent belonged to a dying agrarian dialect. It was the voice of a raw *contadino*, rich with the untranslatable cadences of the hills, his words a gentle, rhythmic hum that stood in stark, comforting contrast to the abrasive commercial bark of the city below. The man's dull glance betrayed no curiosity, no desire to pry into Tito's subjective experience. 

“Thanks; my business can wait,” Tito said. 

He turned away, a profound wave of relief washing over him. The immediate threat of exposure, of having his identity stripped and mistranslated by a hostile tongue, had evaporated. 

*This friar is not likely to live,* Tito thought, inhaling the fragrant scent of the cloister’s rosemary and damp earth. *He was worn to a shadow. And at Fiesole, in the quiet acoustic isolation of the hills, there will be nothing to recall me to his mind. If he should return, my narrative will have fermented into an unassailable truth.* 

Yet, as he walked back into the living, breathing city, he could not entirely shake the lingering aftertaste of the monk’s face, wondering what lost cultural memory it had so violently stirred within him.
