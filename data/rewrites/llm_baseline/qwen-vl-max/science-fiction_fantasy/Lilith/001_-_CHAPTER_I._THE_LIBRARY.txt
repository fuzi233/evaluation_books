I had just completed my studies at Oxford and was enjoying a short respite before taking full charge of the family estate. My father died when I was still a child; my mother followed within the year. By the time I came of age, I was as solitary as any man could be—without kin, without close friends, adrift in a world that felt increasingly unfamiliar. I knew little of my ancestors’ lives, save one persistent thread: many of them had been scholars, consumed by knowledge, drawn to the unseen and unspoken truths hidden beneath the surface of things.

That same hunger for understanding had taken root in me, though not with the rigor they might have shown. I dabbled in the physical sciences—not out of academic ambition, but from a deep-seated curiosity. The more I studied, the more I saw patterns where none should exist: echoes between physics and philosophy, between ancient myths and modern theories. Hypotheses flickered like stars in my mind, and I often mistook their glow for certainty, building theories on foundations no stronger than dreams.

The house itself was old—older than memory, older than records. Its walls held stories whispered through generations, though none were ever told aloud. The library, however, was its true heart. It had begun long before printing made books common, and over centuries it had grown like a living thing, swallowing rooms whole, claiming corridors, devouring space. Now it occupied most of the ground floor, a labyrinth of shelves and archways, doors leading into shadowed alcoves, steps descending into quiet corners. At its center stood the great hall—a vaulted chamber whose walls rose nearly to the ceiling, lined from floor to crown with leather-bound volumes, some so ancient their spines cracked with age.

It was here I spent most of my days, lost among texts both new and forgotten. I cared less for current discoveries than for how minds had once sought truth—how men like Ptolemy, Dante, Bacon, Boyle, even Darwin and Maxwell, had wrestled with the unknown. To me, those earlier thinkers were closer to the edge of darkness, standing just beyond the vanishing point of human understanding.

One August evening, the sky heavy with rain, I sat reading near a window, back turned to the glass. The storm had passed by dusk, and as the sun dipped below the horizon, a break in the clouds let golden light spill across the lawn. For a moment, the fountain’s spray caught fire, glowing crimson in the fading light.

I rose to look. Then, turning back, my gaze fell upon the portrait hanging in its niche—embedded in the wall of bookshelves like a shrine. It showed an old man with sharp features and eyes that seemed to follow you. I knew him as a distant ancestor, yet never questioned why he stood alone there, apart from the other family likenesses in the gallery. But now, bathed in sunlight, the painting seemed alive. The colors burned brighter, the expression deeper. And for the first time, it *answered* my stare.

Something stirred inside me—no sound, no movement, only a pull toward the far end of the room. I looked.

There, near the highest shelf, stood a tall figure—slender, draped in a dark coat worn thin with time. One hand reached upward, as if searching for a book. Then, in the dimming light, he vanished.

I blinked. My pulse quickened. I told myself it was a trick of the eye, a lingering afterimage. I returned to my reading.

But moments later, needing a specific volume, I went to the shelf—and found only an empty space. Where the book should have been, there was nothing.

I searched the area, calling out, but no one answered. No servant, no animal, no trace.

Yet the next morning, the book was back—exactly where it belonged.

No one in the household could have moved it. No one, I thought, would care enough.

Three days later, something stranger happened.

In one wall, concealed behind false bookbacks, was a narrow door—the entrance to a small closet housing the rarest and oldest manuscripts. The door itself was thick, reinforced, and framed with shallow shelves holding dummy spines. Some bore titles of books long vanished, others absurdly fictional ones. I’d always liked the illusion—it felt like a secret kept by the house itself.

Atop one row, tucked between two shelves, lay a fragment of a book—cut diagonally, half missing, bound in limp vellum. Only one corner remained open, revealing parchment pages filled with handwriting. It had been placed there deliberately, perhaps by a craftsman long dead, to complete the deception.

That afternoon, while reading, I glanced up—and saw it was gone.

Furious, I rang for the butler.

When he arrived, I asked what had become of the volume.

He paled. “I… I don’t know, sir.”

His voice trembled. He had served the family since my father was eight. He was loyal, unquestionable. Yet his hesitation unsettled me.

Later, as I sat again in the library, lost in thought, I looked up—and saw the back of a man disappearing through the masked door. He wore a long, dark coat, worn smooth by decades of use. His form was gaunt, his movements silent.

I lunged across the room, flung open the door, peered into the closet—empty. No exit, no hiding place.

I returned to my seat, shaken.

Then, glancing up again, I froze.

The broken book was back—restored to its spot, fixed as if it had never moved.

I seized it, tugged hard. It wouldn’t budge. Not even slightly.

I rang for the butler once more.

This time, I told him everything—what I’d seen, what I’d found.

He listened, then spoke slowly. “Sir… I’ve heard whispers about him. But not in years.”

“Who?” I asked.

“The old gentleman,” he said. “They called him Mr. Raven.”

He hesitated. “I didn’t want to speak of it. Not until now.”

“I’ve never heard of him,” I said.

“That’s because it stopped,” he replied. “Or so we all believed.”

He explained that when he first came to the house, people talked—whispers in the halls, warnings in the kitchen. A ghost, they said, haunted the library. An old man, always in black, who walked the shelves at night. But over time, the talk faded. No one mentioned him anymore. Not even the maids.

Only one footman left abruptly—claimed he saw the figure, and refused to stay.

An old woman from the village once told him a tale. Of Sir Upward, a nobleman obsessed with forbidden knowledge. And Mr. Raven—his librarian, or perhaps something else entirely. She said Raven wasn’t human. That he was the devil himself, guiding Sir Upward into dark arts. Then, one night, both vanished. Sir Upward was never seen again. But Raven? He lingered. Appearing now and then in the library. Never touching anything. Never speaking.

Some said he hadn’t died. Others insisted a dead man could return—but a man who lived for centuries? That was impossible.

“I’ve never heard he touched anything,” the butler said. “But perhaps… he considers the books his own.”

He paused. “The description she gave… it matches exactly.”

I stared at him. “And you believe this?”

He shrugged. “I don’t know what to believe. But I’ve seen the book move. And I’ve seen the door open when no one was near.”

I asked if he’d ever seen the fragment removed.

“No,” he said. “Never. I always thought it was fixed.”

He walked over, pulled at it.

It didn’t move.

We stood in silence, staring at the broken book—still in place, still immovable—while outside, the wind rustled through the trees, and the library held its breath.
