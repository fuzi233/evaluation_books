CHAPTER XIX. The Old Man’s Hope

Messer Bernardo del Nero proved as unyielding as Romola had anticipated when it came to delaying the marriage until Easter—a decision Bardo accepted without question, for he was entirely guided by his shrewd and practical friend. Yet even Bernardo, though utterly immune to the charm that others found in Tito, could not ignore the power of success, especially among men who navigated the world with their eyes fixed on fortune and influence. Tito was rising swiftly in the favor of high society. He had particularly won the regard of the young Cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici, who had already spoken of inviting him to join his scholarly entourage on a journey to Rome. With his quick wit, gentle demeanor, and ability to engage without ever provoking offense, Tito became a welcome guest at elegant dinners along the Via Larga and at social gatherings where he might not excel in skill but always admired the grace of Piero de’ Medici with such sincerity that no one felt slighted.

And so, the reputation of being a delightful companion in refined company only enhanced the perception of Tito’s learning and talent—though in truth, he possessed enough genuine ability to make such admiration both plausible and harmless. Messer Bernardo, though burdened with long-held opinions and loyalties, began to reassess his earlier reservations about the young Greek, whose polished ways had once seemed too supple for comfort. To an old Florentine like Bernardo, there was no dignity in ignoring the approval of the city’s most respected families. And now that Tito had fully entered that circle—whose judgment defined social worth—it seemed absurd to continue doubting the value of such a son-in-law for Bardo or such a husband for Romola.

It was undeniable: Tito’s arrival had brought new life to both father and daughter. Not only had hope returned, but it had deepened beyond expectation. Once, Bardo—the blind scholar whose unwavering honesty refused the flattery and vituperation common among academics—had fallen into obscurity, even among his peers. When mentioned at all, he was often spoken of with pity for his blindness and the loss of his son, yet also with mild impatience over his persistent defense of his own work. His discontent, some said, was ill-placed in a man who had once turned down clerical appointments, despite offers from powerful patrons, simply because they required ordination—an obstacle that made patronage difficult to secure.

But since Tito came into their lives, the tone around Bardo had changed. No longer did his name evoke only sorrow and silence. It was whispered that he had ceased his lamentations; that his daughter, once confined by pride and poverty, would no longer wait in vain for a suitable match. Tito’s presence—his charm, his growing status—had transformed their home into a place of quiet warmth and renewed interest. Visitors now found their way to the library, drawn either by nostalgia or curiosity. Older men returned, stirred by the memory of past acquaintance now rekindled by talk of the family’s revival. Young men whom Tito had invited once soon found themselves returning, catching up with him near the Via de’ Bardi, their hands resting lightly on his shoulder as they fell into easy conversation.

For what could be more pleasing than seeing Romola? Her beauty, dignified and serene, reminded one of Firenzuola’s ideal of womanly greatness: “When a woman is noble in form, carries herself well, sits with grandeur, speaks with gravity, smiles with modesty, and seems to emit a fragrance of queenship—then we say she has majesty.”[1] And under the gentle breath of affection and respect, Romola bloomed like a white lily, strong and pure. To her, this new life—fueled by Tito’s love—was everything.

Tito had done more than bring joy—he had kindled a renewed hope in Bardo’s heart: that before he died, he might see his lifelong dream fulfilled—the preservation of his library. That it would remain separate, untouched by other collections, never absorbed into a monastery, nor renamed after religious orders. That it would stay forever known as the Bardi Library, open to Florentines for generations. For though Bardo’s trust in the Medici had waned over time, it had not vanished. Their influence still held sway over the republic, and if Tito could gain the ear of Cardinal Giovanni, perhaps he could do more than Bernardo ever could—to convince the learned and powerful of the unique value of Bardo’s collection.

Tito himself spoke with confidence of this possibility, eager to lift the old man’s spirits. And Romola, listening, responded with a devotion that surpassed mere gratitude—her love for her father was mirrored in her tenderness toward Tito, a love that honored not just his kindness, but his intellect and spirit.

This matter of the library became the subject of many private discussions between Bernardo and Romola after Christmas, as the wedding drew nearer—but always out of Bardo’s hearing. For Bardo clung to a fragile belief: that his remaining property, apart from the library, was sufficient to meet any financial demands. He refused, except in moments of bitter despair, to admit that the will which disinherited Dino left Romola nothing but debt. Nor did he acknowledge that he needed anything beyond the assurance that the library would be granted a permanent space within the Republic—secured through a deed of gift, in exchange for its protection.

“I believe,” Bernardo told Romola one afternoon beneath the loggia, “that since you are to marry, and Messer Tito will have a steady income, we should begin settling affairs. Let us determine exactly how much is needed to protect the library, rather than allow debts to grow. Your father needs little—only his daily mutton and macaroni—and I think Messer Tito can provide that for the years ahead. We might call it his *morgen-cap*.”

“Tito has always known my life is bound to my father’s,” Romola replied gently. “And he treats my father better than I ever could. He delights in making him happy.”

“Ah,” Bernardo smiled, “he’s not cut from the same cloth as most men, is he?”

“You taught me to read Greek and Latin,” Romola teased, “to shut out the folly of women. But I’ve fallen just as easily for the first pair of bright eyes and soft words that came my way—just as if I’d only memorized Paternosters like every other girl.”

“Now, godfather,” she continued, shaking her head with a light laugh, “as if it were only bright eyes and sweet words that made me love Tito! You know better. I love you and my father because you are good. And I love Tito because he is good—so deeply good that I see it in everything he does. And if he is handsome too—why shouldn’t I love him the more for it? Beauty, to me, is part of the language of goodness. And you—you must have been very handsome in your youth,” she added, lifting her eyes to him with a radiant smile. “You’re nearly as tall as Tito, and your eyes are fine—though yours look sterner, prouder.”

“And Romola likes to keep all the pride for herself?” Bernardo asked, amused but touched.

“Well,” she said softly, “it is true that Tito asks nothing of me—not even a dowry. In that way, his demands are simpler than those of any Florentine nobleman we might have found for you.”

So it was settled—between Bernardo, Romola, and Tito. Bardo gave his assent with a wave of his hand when Bernardo suggested beginning to sell property and clear debts. He saw such matters not as numbers or obligations, but as dense woods, impenetrable to his mind, which he never attempted to traverse.

And Tito, keen to earn Bernardo’s respect, immersed himself in the intricacies of Florentine finance—learning the workings of the *Monti*, the value of land, the profits of banking. He studied the city’s economy with the same diligence he applied to philosophy and poetry.

“You’ll forget he’s not Florentine soon enough, godfather,” Romola said, watching him. “See how quickly he learns everything about our city.”

“He seems more like one of the *demoni*,” Bernardo replied, smiling, “those beings without a homeland. His mind moves too swiftly to carry the weight of all the baggage we men drag around in our hearts.”

Romola smiled back—content, hopeful, full of quiet joy.
