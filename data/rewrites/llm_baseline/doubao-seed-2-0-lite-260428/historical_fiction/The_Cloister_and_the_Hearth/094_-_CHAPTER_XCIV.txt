Her posture would have roused pity far more than terror in any eye unclouded by fixed, fanatical suspicion. Her chest fluttered like a trapped bird’s, red and white chasing across her cheeks in shifting waves, and one hand pressed flat to the rough stone wall by the instinct of all timid things, for she trembled so violently she could barely stand. The purple gaze she turned on the gaunt, glaring hermit was stitched through with equal parts of adoring love, pious awe, tender shared memory, and quiet pity: a sight no unpoisoned heart could have misread.

“Aha!” he cried, snatching up his small wooden crucifix. “Thou art come at last in flesh and blood, as thou didst come to tempt Saint Anthony. But I see through thy wiles. I am armed.” He held the crucifix out trembling at her, the other hand clutching a guttering candle that shook so hard its hot wax dripped onto his bare wrist. *“Exorcizo te.”*

“Ah no!” Margaret cried, putting out two soft, deprecating palms. “Work me no ill. It is Margaret.”

“Liar!” he shouted, his voice cracking. “Margaret was fair, but not supernaturally fair as thou art. Thou didst flinch at that sacred name, thou subtle hypocrite. *In Nomine Dei exorcizo vos.*”

“Ah, Jesu!” Margaret gasped, half mad with terror. “Curse me not! I will go home. I thought I might come. For pity’s sake, do not banish me with Latin prayers! Oh, Gerard, is this how we meet at last, after all the long years apart?” She sank almost to her knees, sobbing with equal parts superstitious fear and lacerated affection.

Saturated as he was with months of unbroken Satanophobia, Clement might still have doubted whether this soft, distraught, entirely human creature was fiend in disguise, but her spontaneous cry of his old, beloved name dissolved all his fevered delusions. He saw clearly with his own eyes, heard truly with his own ears, and uttered a cry of bitter self-reproach. He tried to lift her to her feet, but six months of fasts and the overwhelming shock of having his long solitude broken left his legs too weak to hold his weight. “What,” he gasped, hunching over her trembling form, “is it truly thee? And did I greet thee with harsh, cruel words? Alas!” They were both choked with emotion, and could not speak for a long, quiet minute.

“I do not mind it overmuch,” Margaret said bravely, fighting back her sobs. “Thou didst take me for another: for a devil. Oh, oh, oh!”

“Forgive me, sweet soul!” he managed, as soon as he could force more than one word past his tight throat. “The evil one has beset me constantly since I came to this cell.”

Margaret shuddered, glancing over the dark, reeking cave. “That I can well believe. Then take my hand, and let me lead thee out of this foul place at once.”

He stared at her in uncomprehending astonishment. “What? Desert my cell, and go back into the world? Is that why thou hast come to me?” he said, his voice sharp with sad, reproachful hurt.

“Ay, Gerard! I am come to take thee to thy very own vicarage at Gouda. Thanks to Heaven and thy good brother Giles, thou art appointed its vicar, and thy mother and I have made it all so neat and sweet for thee. It is warm and snug enough in winter, I promise thee, and wait but a little: when the hawthorn blooms, the walls will wear a kirtle of bright roses and sweet woodbine. Did we ever forget thee, or the foolish little things thou dost love? And dear Gerard, thy mother waits for us by the path, it is far past her bedtime. Prithee, prithee, come away! The moment we step outside this dark hole I will show thee a treasure thou hast earned, and know nothing of, or thou wouldst never have fled from us so.” She broke off, confused, when he drew himself tight into a rigid huddle, his face twisted with a strange mix of fear and suspicion.

“Unhappy girl,” he said solemnly, his whole frame shaking with repressed agitation, “wouldst have me risk thy soul and mine for a mere vicarage and the flowers that grow on its walls? This is not thy doing. The bowelless fiend sent thee, poor simple girl, to bait me with this temptation. But oh, cunning fiend, I will unmask thee even to thy innocent pawn, and she shall see thee, and abhor thee as I do. Margaret, my lost love, why am I here? Because I love thee.”

“Oh no, Gerard, thou dost not love me, or thou wouldst never have hidden from me. There was never any need for all this.”

“Let there be no deceit between us two, who loved so true once, and after this night shall meet no more on earth.”

“Now God forbid that!” Margaret cried.

“I love thee still. Thou hast not forgotten me, or thou wouldst have married long ago, and never been the one to find me buried here, hidden from all mortal sight. I am a priest, a monk now. What but folly or sin can come of us living as neighbours, feeding a passion that was once innocent, but which Heaven has now deemed unholy and impious? No, though my heart break in two, I must be firm. I am the man, and I am the priest. We must meet no more, until I have been schooled by long solitude, and thou art wedded to another man.”

“I would accept my own doom, but never thine. I would ten times rather die than see thee rot in this foul, dismal cave, while that sweet warm manse waits for thee.” Clement groaned. With every word she spoke, his duty grew clearer, and the searing agony of fulfilling it cut deeper.

“My beloved,” he said, his voice tender and yet unyielding with dogged resolution, “I bless thee for giving me one more sight of thy sweet face. And may God forgive thee, and bless thee, for destroying in a single minute the holy peace it took six months of solitude to build. No matter. A year of penance will, Dei gratia, restore me to my calm. My poor Margaret, I seem cruel, yet I am kind. It is best we part. Ay, this very moment.”

“Part, Gerard? Never. We have seen what comes of parting. Thou hast not heard half my story, no, not a tenth part. It is not just for thy own comfort that I am bringing thee to Gouda. Hear me out!”

“I may not. Thy very voice is a temptation, all music and sweet memory.”

“But I say thou shalt hear me, Gerard. I will not leave this place unheard.”

“Then I must flee this temptation by other means,” Clement said, sadly.

“Alack, what other means? Wilt thou push me out the door, being the stronger?”

“Nay, Margaret, thou knowest full well I would suffer a thousand deaths before I would force thy sweet body. But our two immortal souls are a million times dearer to me than our mortal flesh. I have withstood this direst of all temptations long enough. Now I must run from it. Farewell! Farewell!”

He stumbled for the door, wrenched it open, and half stepped outside, when Margaret darted after him and caught him hard by the arm. “Nay, then another must speak for me. I thought to reward thee for yielding to me, but unkind as thou art, I find I need his help now. Turn but this way one moment.”

“Nay, nay.”

“But I say ay! Turn away from us then, if thou canst.” She loosened her grip a little, sure he would not deny her such a small favour, and in that instant he saw his chance. “Fly, Clement, fly!” he shrieked. His religious frenzy lent him the strength of his old youth for a moment, and he tore free of her hold so wildly he threw her full onto the stony ground. He bounded a few paces to the little icy stream, splashed halfway across it, then stopped and looked back. She lay face down on the earth, her hands spread out before her.

He groaned, and took one step back toward her, his resolve crumbling, before a wilder impulse seized him, and he flung himself neck-deep into the freezing water instead. “There,” he cried, his teeth chattering, “kill my mortal flesh, but save our souls!”

He stood half submerged in the liquid ice, and saw Margaret rise slowly to her knees, her face pale, her eyes glistening with tears. In the quiet dark night, he heard her voice, thin and breaking: “Oh God, who knowest all things, Thou seest how I am used. Forgive me, then. I will not live another day.” She sprang to her feet, and ran past his hiding place like a wild, mortally wounded creature, shrieking four times at the top of her lungs: *“CRUEL!—CRUEL!—CRUEL!—CRUEL!”*

All human agony was packed into that single word: wounded love, trampled pride, utter despair, the first flicker of coming madness. It froze his heart far worse than the icy water chilled the marrow of his bones. In his moment of grim, supposed triumph over temptation, he felt an almost uncontrollable urge to curse the Church, curse religion itself, for demanding such savage cruelty of mortal flesh. He crawled half dead out of the stream at last, and staggered back into his dark cave. “I am safe here,” he groaned. “She will never come near me again. Unmanly, ungrateful wretch that I am.” He threw his emaciated, frozen body down on the dirt floor, half hoping he would never rise again.

But soon he saw by his hourglass that it was past midnight. He dragged himself up slowly, stripped off his soaking clothes, and moaning all the while at the pain he had caused the woman he loved, he pulled on the old hermit’s bristling hair cilice, and over that the heavy iron breastplate he had never dared wear before, doubting he was worthy of the arms of such a tried, pious soldier. Now he needed every aid he could find: the bristles of the cilice might distract his earthly remorse with physical pain, and the breastplate might hold some holy protective virtue. He knelt and prayed humbly that God would release him from the burden of his flesh that very night. Then he lit all his candles, and began reciting his psalter doggedly, every word heavy as a lump of lead, every breath wrenched out of his leaden heart.

Midway through the office, he spotted a small soft bundle in the corner he had not noticed before in the dimmer light, with something that looked like spun gold peeking out of its wrappings. He stumbled over to see what it was, and the moment he looked closer he threw up his hands in rapture. “It is a seraph,” he whispered. “A lovely seraph. Heaven has seen my bitter trial, and approves my resolve, and has sent this flower of the skies to cheer me as I faint under my burden.” He fell to his knees, gazing in ecstasy at the golden hair, the soft tender skin, the cheeks blushed like a ripe peach. “Let me feast my sad eyes on thee before thou fliest back to thy blessed abode, leaving my cell dark again, as it went dark when she left.”

His quiet reverie woke the little visitor. Two wide eyes the colour of a summer sky blinked open, saw the strange wild figure kneeling over him, and he cried piteously, “MUMMA! MUM-MA!” Big tears began rolling down his round little cheeks.

Clement, who had not seen a human face other than his own for more than six months, had always been fond of children, and the monastic life fostered that softness. The innocent distress on the cherubic face, the tears streaming from those violet-blue eyes, the plaintive cry for his mother, went straight through his broken heart. He used every bit of his gentle skill to soothe the child, and soon the boy stopped crying, wide-eyed wonder replacing his fear, scanning the strange wild man who spoke so softly even though he wore armour, who did not hurt little boys but cooed over them.

Clement was bewildered how this little glowing human flower had ended up in his gloomy cave, until he remembered he had left the cave door wide open when he ran out. He concluded some desperate soul had seized the chance to abandon her unwanted child (1). His heart ached so fiercely for the poor little abandoned cherub that tears of pure tenderness stood in his eyes, and he saw God’s divine goodness working even through the mother’s heartlessness, to comfort his own breaking heart. “Bless thee, bless thee, sweet innocent. I would not trade thee even for a cherub in heaven.”

“At’s pooty,” the infant replied, ignoring all uninteresting remarks as all infants do, pointing straight at his breastplate.

“What is pretty here besides thee, my love?”

“Ookum-gars,” (2) the boy lisped, his little finger still outstretched.

Clement laughed. “So many children, so many strange little phrases! Hector’s own babe screamed at his father’s glittering helmet, and here a medieval babe is charmed by a polished cuirass, and my griefs are assuaged.” He told the little boy his storks scared him, that he hated the big flapping birds who were far bigger than he was, and promised him he had tiny pretty birds the boy would not be afraid of. Soon the two were chattering like old friends, and when Clement asked if he had a daddy as well as a mammy, we step back a little, to watch through Margaret’s eyes.

When Margaret ran past Gerard in the dark, she was almost mad. It was that fevered state of mind that has driven affectionate mothers to kill their children, and themselves, in moments of utter despair. She ran straight to Reicht Heynes, pale and trembling, and clung to her neck. “Oh Reicht, oh Reicht!” She showed Reicht her hands, scraped raw and bleeding from the stony ground, told her he had thrown her down, thought she was a devil sent to tempt his soul, that he would not even look at the treasure she had brought him. “I will fling myself and him into the Rotter this night.”

Reicht talked her down, but in her frenzy, Margaret suddenly realized she had left the baby, her own child, the treasure she had hidden so carefully, all alone in the hermit’s dark cave. They set off back at once, Margaret’s courage failing her as they drew near, until she sent Reicht ahead to fetch the child, too heartsick to face Gerard herself. Reicht ran back, wide-eyed, to tell her the cave was lit up bright, and there were two voices inside, not one: a man’s, and a little child’s, talking as pleasantly as two old friends. Margaret crept on all fours to the crack in the cave wall, and peered through.

Inside, she heard the little boy say, when pressed about his daddy, “Dad-da ill man. Run away and left poor mum-ma.” She winced, for that cruel tale had never come from her lips. Then she heard Gerard curse that imaginary coward who would abandon his own child, swear he would be both father and mother to the little boy, feed him soft eggs and warm milk, teach him to read, sing him old lullabies from their home town of Tergou, the same lullabies Gerard’s own mother had sung to him as a babe. He took off his heavy breastplate, took off the bristling cilice that would prick the child’s soft skin, sat the little boy on his knee, and chanted old fairy stories to him, until the child’s eyelids grew heavy, and he sank fast asleep in his arms. Gerard stopped singing, gazed down at the little golden-haired face, and sighed, thinking of all the joy he had thrown away over a single false piece of paper.

The next moment, moonlight poured through the cave mouth, and Margaret stepped forward, sinking to her knees at his knee, her soft trembling hand resting light on his shoulder.
