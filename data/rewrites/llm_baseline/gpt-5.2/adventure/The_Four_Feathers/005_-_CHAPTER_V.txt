Habit carried them as a river carries driftwood. In Lennon House the last hours of the ball ran on of themselves—laughter flung out on cue, compliments laid like cards upon the table, the soft conspiracy of faces trained to look at ease. No one, watching, would have guessed that something had snapped between Ethne Eustace and Harry Feversham as cleanly as a thread scorched through.

Feversham watched her from under the chandelier light and could not reconcile what he knew with what he saw. Ethne’s voice rose and fell among the talk as though it had never trembled; her smile went to the right people at the right moments; when she turned, colour seemed to obey her will and gather dutifully at her cheeks. When her feet took the floor, the rhythm of them—light, precise, almost careless—nearly persuaded him that her heart had kept time.

And then she passed him, and the persuasion broke. He felt the four feathers hidden against his breast as if they were hot coals stitched into his coat. It amazed him that the whole company could not smell their shame on him, as pungent and undeniable as singed paper. He moved among partners with the absurd, constant notion that each woman would draw back and speak the word he had earned; that the contempt which lay on Ethne’s tongue would leap free and echo through the room. Yet there was no fear in him. The place where fear had lived was empty. If the word came, let it come. He had already lost what mattered.

He looked, too, at the other women as if he might find in them some ease—some comparison that could blunt the edge of his loss—but he looked in vain, as he must have known he would. There were faces of beauty, yes, and grace, and bright cleverness; but Ethne stood apart by a character of beauty which did not depend on any single charm. The breadth of her forehead, the clean curve of her brows, the great steady grey eyes that seemed to hold a clear weather of their own; the red lips which could dimple into sudden tenderness and then set level with resolution; the very carriage of her head and shoulders, royal without effort—these made a distinction that would not be confused in any room, in any company. He watched her with a despairing wonder that he had ever been permitted to imagine she was his.

They even danced together. The orchestra spilled waltz after waltz, the floor turned under them, and their hands met with the correctness of long practice. Ethne did not let the strain show. If her body ached with the effort of it, she carried that weight where no one could see—like a woman carrying a heavy dish from kitchen to table without letting it tilt, because spillage would shame her more than pain.

Only once, and for no more than a second, did her endurance slip.

They were in the turn of a dance when her eyes went past his shoulder toward the windows. Behind the blinds a pallor had begun to form: the first, cold, colourless hint of day. Ethne’s mouth parted.

“Look,” she said.

The word was small, but Feversham felt it like a sudden leaning. All at once her weight came into his arms. The colour deserted her face. Grey rose up under her skin as if morning itself had entered her. Her eyes shut tight, then opened again; she swayed, and he thought she would fall there on the polished boards before all the world.

“The morning at last!” she exclaimed, and the brightness of the words could not conceal the weariness underneath. Then, in a voice that seemed rubbed raw by the night, she said, “I wonder whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain.”

“Hush,” he whispered, leaning nearer so that his breath would not carry. “Courage. A few minutes more—only a very few.”

He stopped with her where they stood, turning his body as a shield, holding her steady until the strength came back into her limbs and the grey retreated a little.

“Thank you,” she said, and there was gratitude in it—simple, earnest, almost childlike with exhaustion.

Then the bright wheel of the dance caught them again and spun them back among the others.

It was strange—almost grotesque—that he should be the one urging courage, she the one thanking him for help; but neither of them had room in that moment for irony. Ethne was simply tried past endurance; Feversham, seeing the drawn lines at her mouth, the deep pain in her eyes, understood with a shock how far his act had cut into her. He stopped thinking, even, I have lost her. The thought of his own loss shrank until it was nothing. Her sentence—quiet, wondering, condemned by its very simplicity—took possession of him. I wonder whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain. He felt it would go with him wherever he went, ringing on in Ethne’s own accent, and that, at the end, when other voices clustered around him, he would hear that one sentence above them all and recognise it as his judgment. For it was not right. Not right at all.

The ball died shortly afterward, as fires do—first the flare, then the dwindling, then the last red thread in the ash. The final carriage rolled away. Those who were staying in the house broke by custom into their little streams: the men drifting toward the smoking-room, the women to their rooms, the heavy, satisfied silence of a household at last allowed to sleep.

Feversham lingered in the hall with Ethne.

She knew why he stayed. She stood with her back to him, steadying her hand to light a candle as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world, as if the flame did not tremble at the same places her voice had trembled.

“There is no need,” she said. “I have told my father. I told him everything.”

He bowed his head. Acquiescence was all he had left that resembled dignity.

“Still, I must wait and see him,” he answered.

She did not object. But she turned quickly then, her brows drawn with a puzzled frown. To wait—for him to put himself in her father’s way, now—argued a courage she could not quite comprehend. And she, who had not feared the crowd of guests, felt an apprehension suddenly as the door of the study opened and Dermod Eustace’s footsteps sounded on the floor.

Dermod came straight up to Harry Feversham. The candlelight showed him for once exactly as he was: very old. Not merely aged, but burdened with years; the skin loose at the jaw, the eyes slightly bewildered as though the world had changed language in the night and he could not find the right words in it.

He stood and stared into Feversham’s face.

Twice his mouth opened. Twice nothing came out. At last he turned away, went to the table, and lit his own candle and Feversham’s. The small domestic gesture—so careful, so accustomed—was like a habit of kindness performed without thinking.

Then Dermod turned back rather quickly, and Ethne took a step forward as if to place herself between them. But he did nothing violent; he only stared again. A long time passed. The hall, emptied of music and voices, had its own sound: the faint settling creaks of wood, the distant breath of the house, the thin wick-hiss of the candles.

“Well—” he said at last, and stopped.

He lifted the snuffers and trimmed the wick with deliberation, as though if the flame were neatened the right expression might appear. He looked at Feversham again.

“Well—” he began, and stopped once more.

The candle seemed not to help him. He stared into the flame now rather than into the young man’s face, and the bewilderment on him deepened. Something must be said; the old, established forms demanded it; but the words would not come. At last he offered, lamely, the first thing his habits could provide.

“If you want any whiskey,” he said, “stamp twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand.”

And with that he walked heavily up the stairs.

Feversham stood very still as the footsteps went away. Of all punishments, the old man’s forbearance—his inability even to summon anger into a proper sentence—cut with a quiet edge. There was no drama to hide behind. Nothing to answer, nothing to resist. Only that exhausted kindness of custom: a candle lit, an offer of whiskey, and the slow retreat of age.

* * *

It was broad daylight when Ethne was at last alone in her room.

She drew up the blinds and opened the windows wide. The morning air poured in—cool, clean, and sharp as spring-water lifted from a deep well—and she drank it as if it could rinse her from the night. Outside, the world had not yet found its colours. The tall trees stood black and vertical, like ink strokes against the paling sky; the winding paths were a dead, singular white; the lawns lay grey and flattened, the dew on them webbed like frost. Everything looked washed of joy, as though daylight itself had been strained through muslin.

Yet the world was not silent. It was noisy with living sound. Blackbirds called from branches and from the grass with that bright insistence that belongs to mornings; and beneath the overhanging trees the Lennon ran on in a continuous music, a steadier song than any orchestra could sustain. It was as if the house had been filled all night with hired sound—measured, repetitive, too loud in places, too smooth in others—and now the land itself answered with something older, freer, not for sale, not for applause. The birds and river did not perform; they simply existed, and their existing made the mind feel less broken.

Ethne drew back from the window. She had much to do before she could sleep.

With her natural thoroughness she meant to make an end at once of all her associations with Harry Feversham. She wanted, when next she woke, to wake into a room cleared of him, a day in which nothing—no ribbon, no trinket, no scrap of paper—could pull her back into memory against her will. She set about it with a stubborn persistence, like someone scrubbing a stain from linen until her hands ache because she cannot bear to see the mark remain.

But as she collected the gifts he had given her, her resolve shifted.

Each thing had its history. The touch of her fingers upon them called up, one by one, the days before this miserable night fell across her happiness. A small ornament did not remain merely an ornament; it became a remembered afternoon, a particular phrase, the smell of a garden after rain, the texture of his glove as he handed it to her. Memory would not obey the brutal command to vanish. It rose in her like a language spoken in childhood—words you do not use now, perhaps, yet cannot truly forget because they are braided through your sense of self.

She decided she would keep one thing that had belonged to Harry Feversham—a small thing, of no value.

At first her hand went to a penknife he had once lent her and which she had forgotten to return. She held it an instant, then dropped it almost at once, and with a hurried movement, as if the metal had turned suddenly cold. She was an Irish girl; she did not perhaps believe in superstitions, not in the daylight of her mind. But where superstitions were concerned, she preferred—always—to be on the safe side.

In the end she chose his photograph. She did not look at it long. She locked it away in a drawer as one locks away a dangerous remedy: not thrown out, not displayed, but kept where it could not work upon her unless she chose.

All the rest she gathered together and packed carefully in a box. She fastened it, addressed it, and carried it down to the hall so that the servants might despatch it in the morning. The household was still, its corridors smelling faintly of extinguished candles and flowers past their best; somewhere a door sighed on its hinges; the quiet had the weight of a house that has entertained too hard and too late. She set the parcel aside with hands that did not tremble, because she would not permit them to.

Then she went back to her room and took his letters. She made a neat pile of them on the hearth and set them alight.

They did not burn quickly. The flame crept, nibbling the edges, lifting the paper into curled black tongues. The ink—the words that had once seemed so sure—darkened and ran, like a stream of spilled ink pushed by heat, until the writing was no longer readable. She sat upright in her chair and watched without moving, forcing herself to witness the end. In the last stage the sheets broke down into flakes of ash—light, drifting shapes—some grey, some startlingly pale. They looked like feathers, and among them were white flakes like white feathers, fragile and weightless, as though courage and shame could both be reduced to the same small debris.

The last sparks were barely out when she heard a cautious step on the gravel beneath her window.

Though it was broad daylight her candle still burned on the table, a yellow point in the morning. Instinct moved her faster than thought. She reached out and pinched the flame into darkness.

Then she sat rigid and listened.

For a while there were only the blackbirds, calling and answering from the trees, and the steady, throbbing music of the river. Those sounds held the garden in a living net; they made the air seem inhabited, not empty. Afterward she heard the footsteps again, retreating cautiously, as if whoever had come had thought better of being seen.

And then—despite her will, despite the box in the hall and the locked drawer and the ash on the hearth—she was mastered not by pain or humiliation but by a sudden, overpowering loneliness. It came like a physical cold. It made her feel as though she were seated high above a world that had collapsed into ruins, and she alone remained to look down upon it.

She rose quickly, as if movement could ward the feeling off. Her eyes fell upon a violin-case. Relief—pure, simple—went through her like warmth.

She opened it.

A little while later, one or two of the guests sleeping in the house woke and, half-dreaming, heard music floating down the corridors: a violin played very lovingly and low. The sound was not the public brilliance of the ballroom, not the polished noise meant to fill a room. It was close, intimate, meant for one listener—perhaps for none but the player herself. It fitted itself into the house’s morning hush without wounding it, and it spoke to the spaces where words failed.

Ethne did not know that the violin she held was the Guarnerius Durrance had sent to her. She only knew that, for the moment, she was not alone. The instrument rested against her throat like a living thing; its voice answered the birds and the river; and in that answering, it became a companion willing to share her solitude.
