The morning air was crisp, cutting through the lingering melancholy of the previous days. After significant resistance, Marianne finally capitulated to Elinor's persistent pleas, agreeing to venture out for a mere half-hour. However, she established a rigid boundary: no social calls, no engagements beyond accompanying them to Gray's in Sackville Street, where Elinor was conducting negotiations regarding the exchange of her mother's antique jewelry.

As they reached the threshold, Mrs. Jennings recalled a pending obligation—a visit to a lady at the opposite end of the street—and, lacking personal business at the jeweler's, resolved to attend to her errand first before retrieving her companions. Upon ascending the stairs, the Dashwood sisters encountered a scene of chaotic congestion. The shop floor was packed; there was not a single clerk available to attend to their needs. Forced to wait, they claimed the corner of the counter that promised the shortest queue. Only one customer occupied the space ahead of them, yet Elinor harbored a faint hope that his inherent politeness might expedite the transaction. Alas, his acute eye and refined taste rendered him incapable of courtesy toward others. He was entirely consumed by the specifications of a toothpick case, meticulously debating its dimensions, form, and ornamentation for fifteen minutes. Until he had finalized this trivial acquisition according to his own inventive whims, he afforded the two ladies nothing more than three or four vacant, piercing stares. These glances served to etch into Elinor's memory the visage of a man possessing a robust, natural, and utterly insignificant character, yet dressed in the height of fashionable splendor.

Marianne, spared the torment of feeling slighted by this intrusive scrutiny and the juvenile arrogance of his decision-making process, remained blissfully oblivious. She possessed the fortitude to retreat inward, constructing a mental fortress wherein she could remain as unaware of the absurdities unfolding in Mr. Gray's shop as she would be in her own chamber.

Eventually, the transaction concluded. Ivory, gold, and pearls were assigned their places. The gentleman, having declared the specific date by which his existence could continue without his new toothpick case, slowly donned his gloves. Bestowing one final look upon the Miss Dashwoods—a gaze that demanded admiration rather than offering it—he departed with an air of genuine conceit masked by feigned indifference.

Elinor wasted no time advancing her own business, poised to finalize the deal when another gentleman stepped beside her. Turning, she met with surprise the face of her brother, John Dashwood. Their reunion was marked by a display of affection sufficient to appear dignified amidst the clutter of the shop. John was genuinely relieved to see his sisters; his inquiries concerning their mother were respectful and attentive.

"Elinor," he noted, "Fanny and I have been in town for two days. I intended to call upon you yesterday, but it was impossible; we were compelled to take Harry to see the wild beasts at Exeter Exchange, and the remainder of the day was spent with Mrs. Ferrars. Harry was delighted. This morning, I had planned to stop by if I could spare half an hour, but the initial rush of arriving in town leaves one perpetually occupied. I am here to commission a seal for Fanny. Tomorrow, however, I expect to be able to visit Berkeley Street and introduce myself to your friend, Mrs. Jennings. I understand she possesses considerable fortune. And the Middletons—you must introduce me to them. As my mother-in-law's relatives, I intend to extend every due respect. They are excellent neighbors in the countryside, I gather."

"Excellent indeed," Elinor replied. "Their attentiveness to our comfort and their friendliness exceed description."

"I am immensely gratified to hear this," John exclaimed. "It ought to be so; they are wealthy, they are kin, and every civility required to ease your situation is reasonable to expect. Moreover, you are comfortably settled in your cottage, lacking for nothing! Edward provided a delightful account of the place—the most perfect residence of its kind, he said—and you all seem to thrive beyond measure. It was a profound satisfaction to us to learn this."

A flush of shame rose in Elinor's cheeks at her brother's tone, and she was grateful when Mrs. Jennings's servant arrived to announce that his mistress awaited them outside. Mr. Dashwood escorted them downstairs, introducing them to Mrs. Jennings at the carriage door. Reiterating his hope to visit the following day, he took his leave.

His subsequent visit was formalized. He arrived under the pretext of apologizing for his wife's absence, claiming she was so engrossed with her mother that she lacked the leisure to travel. Mrs. Jennings, however, dismissed such niceties, noting they were all cousins or close relations, and affirmed her intention to visit Mrs. John Dashwood shortly, bringing her sisters along. His demeanor toward the sisters was calmly kind, while his treatment of Mrs. Jennings was excessively deferential. When Colonel Brandon entered moments later, John eyed him with a calculating curiosity, seemingly assessing his net worth before extending equal civility.

After half an hour, John requested that Elinor accompany him to Conduit Street to introduce him to Sir John and Lady Middleton. The weather was exceptionally fair, and she readily agreed. The moment they crossed the threshold, his interrogation commenced.

"Who is Colonel Brandon? Does he possess substantial wealth?"

"Yes; he holds significant property in Dorsetshire."

"I am pleased to hear that. He appears a most gentlemanly individual. I believe, Elinor, I may congratulate you on the prospect of a respectable establishment."

"Me, brother? What do you mean?"

"He favors you. I observed him closely and am convinced of it. What is the extent of his fortune?"

"I estimate approximately two thousand pounds per annum."

"Two thousand a year!" John worked himself into a crescendo of enthusiastic benevolence. "Elinor, I wish with all my heart it were *twice* that amount, for your sake."

"Indeed, I believe you," Elinor responded coolly. "However, I am certain Colonel Brandon harbors no desire to marry *me*."

"You are mistaken, Elinor; profoundly so. Minimal effort on your part secures him. Perhaps he is currently undecided; the modesty of your fortune may cause hesitation, or his friends may counsel against it. Yet, certain attentions and encouragements, which ladies can dispense so effortlessly, will bind him regardless of his inclinations. There is no impediment to your attempting this. It is not to be assumed that any prior attachment exists on your side—in short, you understand that such matters are quite out of the question; the obstacles are insurmountable. You possess too much sense not to recognize this. Colonel Brandon must be the man; and no courtesy shall be withheld on my part to ensure his approval of you and your family. It is a union destined to satisfy everyone. In brief, it is a circumstance that"—he lowered his voice to a conspiratorial whisper—"will be exceedingly welcome to *all parties*." Regaining his composure, he added, "That is, I mean to say—your friends are genuinely eager to see you settled; Fanny especially, for she holds your interests very dear, I assure you. And her mother, Mrs. Ferrars, a woman of excellent nature, would derive immense pleasure from it; she mentioned as much recently."

Elinor declined to offer any response.

"It would be somewhat remarkable, now," he continued, "perhaps even amusing, if Fanny were to settle a brother while I settle a sister simultaneously. And yet, it is not improbable."

"Is Mr. Edward Ferrars," Elinor asked with resolve, "intending to marry?"

"It is not formally settled, though the matter is agitated. He has a most exemplary mother. Mrs. Ferrars, with the utmost generosity, will provide him with an allowance of one thousand pounds per annum should the match proceed. The lady is the Honorable Miss Morton, sole daughter of the late Lord Morton, endowed with thirty thousand pounds. A highly desirable alliance on both fronts, and I harbor no doubt it will occur. One thousand pounds annually is a substantial gift for a mother to bestow permanently, yet Mrs. Ferrars possesses a noble spirit. Another testament to her liberality: upon our arrival in town, recognizing that funds were scarce for us at the moment, she placed banknotes totaling two hundred pounds into Fanny's hands. It was extremely welcome, for we must maintain high expenditures while residing here."

He paused, awaiting her sympathy and agreement. She managed to force the words, "Your expenses, both in town and country, must certainly be considerable; however, your income is substantial."

"Not so large, I presume, as many assume. I do not intend to complain; it is undoubtedly comfortable, and I anticipate improvement. The enclosure of Norland Common, currently underway, is a severe financial drain. Furthermore, I acquired East Kingham Farm within the past half-year. You recall the estate where old Gibson resided. The land was so desirable in every respect, so immediately adjacent to my own holdings, that I felt it my duty to purchase it. I could not bear the thought of letting it pass into other hands. A man must pay for his convenience, and it *has* cost me a vast sum."

"More than its intrinsic value warrants."

"Why, I hope not. I could have resold it the following day for a profit. Regarding the purchase price, however, I faced potential misfortune; the stock market was at a low ebb at that time. Had I not possessed the necessary capital in my banker's accounts, I would have incurred significant losses."
