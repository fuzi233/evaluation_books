The new journal, *The Pall Mall Gazette*, enjoyed a burst of initial success. Rumors swirled that an influential political party stood behind it, bolstered by a roster of "great names" contributing to its pages. Were these whispers true? We cannot officially confirm or deny them. However, we can reveal a few secrets: a scathing article on foreign policy, widely attributed to a prominent Noble Lord known for his ties to the Foreign Office, was actually penned by Captain Shandon while sitting in the parlour of the Bear and Staff public house near Whitehall Stairs—a spot he'd reached after being tracked down by a printer's boy, where his literary associate, Mr. Bludyer, was temporarily staying. Furthermore, a string of financial analyses universally believed to be the work of a towering statesman in the House of Commons were, in fact, the brainchild of Mr. George Warrington of the Upper Temple.

There may well have been secret dealings between the *Gazette* and this powerful faction. Evidence suggests Percy Popjoy—who, thanks to his father Lord Falconet, belonged to the party—was frequently spotted climbing the stairs to Warrington's chambers. Indeed, the paper printed information so peculiar it could only originate from strange sources. Several poems, weak in substance but loud and vigorous in tone, appeared signed "P.P."; and it must be admitted, his novel was reviewed in the new journal with outrageously enthusiastic praise.

Mr. Pen, however, played no role in the political department. Instead, he became a tireless literary contributor. The *Gazette*'s offices were located in Catherine Street, Strand, and here Pen often arrived, bursting with manuscripts tucked in his pockets. He moved with a bustling energy and genuine delight, typical of anyone embarking on a literary career. To see oneself in print remains a novel sensation, and one naturally delights in imagining that one's words are already causing a stir in the world.

Here, Mr. Jack Finucane, the sub-editor, busied himself with paste and scissors, compiling the journal under his supervision. With an eagle eye, he scanned every paragraph of every newspaper dealing with the fashionable world he oversaw. Not a death or a dinner party among the aristocracy escaped his notice; he ensured every event found a column in his publication. From obscure provincial prints and distant Scottish and Irish broadsheets, he dredged up astonishing gossip and intelligence regarding the upper classes. It was a truly moving sight for a philosopher to witness Jack Finucane, Esquire, eating a plate of meat from the cookshop and drinking porter from a local pub, yet recounting the feasts of the great as if he had personally attended them. Dressed in tattered trousers and dirty shirt sleeves, he cheerfully described and arranged the world's most brilliant social fetes. The sheer incongruity of Finucane's job, combined with his demeanor and appearance, greatly amused his new friend, Pen. Having left his native village—where his family rank was hardly lofty—Jack had rarely encountered society outside the tavern parlours he frequented. Yet, based on his writing, one would assume he dined with ambassadors and lounged in the bow-windows of White's Club.

Errors of description occasionally slipped from his pen, but the *Ballinafad Sentinel*, of which he was the sole correspondent, bore the brunt of these mistakes. The London chiefs of the *Gazette* preferred his skill with scissors and paste over his ability to hold a pen, allowing him little space to write directly for them.

Pen, meanwhile, devoted immense effort to his reviews. Possessing a decent amount of desultory reading acquired in his youth, along with an eager imagination and a sharp sense of humor, his articles delighted both his editor and the public. He took pride in earning his keep. We can be certain the *Pall Mall Gazette* was eagerly consumed at Fairoaks, read with joy by the two ladies there. It also made its way to Clavering Park, where a young lady of refined literary tastes resided. Even old Doctor Portman, to whom the widow sent the paper after memorizing her son's articles, approved of Pen's work, declaring the lad possessed spirit, taste, and fancy. He noted that while Pen didn't write like a scholar, he certainly wrote like a gentleman.

What was the sheer astonishment and delight of Major Pendennis when he walked into the Regent Club, finding Wenham, Lord Falconet, and other distinguished gentlemen gathered around a copy of the *Gazette*. They were discussing an article that savagely mocked a recent book published by the wife of a celebrated opposition figure. The book in question was *Travels in Spain and Italy* by the Countess of Muffborough. It was difficult to determine whether the French or English portions were worse; her ladyship wrote indifferently in both, and the critic pounced on her blunders with delightful malice. This critic was none other than Pen. He danced around his subject with boundless jocularity and high spirits, exposing the noble lady's flaws with admirable mock gravity and decorum. Every word remained polite and gentlemanly, yet the unfortunate subject was thoroughly scarified and laughed at throughout the process.

Wenham's bilious face puckered with malignant pleasure as he read the critique. Lady Muffborough hadn't invited him to her parties in the last year. Lord Falconet giggled and laughed heartily; he and Lord Muffborough had been rivals since childhood. These lords complimented Major Pendennis, who had previously ignored hints from his Fairoaks correspondent warning of "Arthur's constant and severe literary occupations," fearing they might undermine the poor boy's health. The Major had considered any mention of Mr. Pen and his newspaper connections beneath his dignity as a Major and a gentleman. But when the oracle Wenham praised the boy's production, when Lord Falconet (who had heard the news from Percy Popjoy) approved of young Pen's genius, and when the great Lord Steyne himself laughed, sniggered, declared the piece capital, and predicted the Muffborough would writhe under it like a whale under a harpoon, the Major felt compelled to change his tune.

As duty demanded, he began to admire his nephew immensely. "By gad," he exclaimed, "the young rascal has some stuff in him, and he'll do something! He always said he would!" Trembling with pleasure, the old gentleman sat down to write to the widow at Fairoaks detailing all the praise from the great figures, and he also wrote to the young rascal himself, asking when he would visit to eat a chop with his old uncle. He added that he was commissioned to take him to dinner at Gaunt House, noting that Lord Steyne appreciated anyone who could entertain him, whether through folly, wit, or even dullness.

Pen tossed the letter across the table to Warrington, perhaps expecting a reaction that didn't materialize. The courage of young critics is prodigious; they climb onto the judgment seat and deliver opinions on the most intricate works with barely a pause. If Macaulay's *History* or Herschel's *Astronomy* had been placed before Pen at this stage, he would have glanced through the volumes, pondered his verdict over a cigar, and offered an august approval as if he were their superior patron. With access to the *Biographie Universelle* or the British Museum, he could summarize historical periods rapidly, citing names, dates, and facts with such mastery that his mother at home would wonder where he had acquired such a prodigious library. When he revisited his articles two or three months later, having forgotten the subjects and books consulted, he would appear to possess a memory far sharper than reality warranted.

At twenty-four, Mr. Pen wouldn't hesitate, given twenty-four hours' notice, to judge the greatest scholars or render a verdict on the *Encyclopædia*. Fortunately, he had Warrington to curb his impertinence with constant, wholesome ridicule. Without this check, he might have become intolerably conceited. Captain Shandon, who admired the dash and flippancy of his young aide-de-camp, preferred Pen's light, brilliant flashes over the heavier arguments of his elder coadjutor. Though blameable for impertinence and premature judgment, Pen was a perfectly honest critic—too candid for Mr. Bungay's liking, who grumbled sadly at his impartiality.

One day, Pen and his captain had a disagreement over this very point.

"In the name of common sense, Mr. Pendennis," Shandon asked, "what have you been doing—praising one of Mr. Bacon's books? Bungay was in a fury this morning upon seeing a laudatory article on one of the works of that odious firm."

Pen's eyes widened in astonishment. "Do you mean to say," he asked, "that we are to praise no books Bacon publishes? Or that, if they are good, we must call them bad?"

"My good young friend—for what do you suppose a benevolent publisher undertakes a critical journal, to benefit his rival?" Shandin inquired.

"To benefit himself certainly, but to tell the truth too," Pen replied. "*Ruat coelum*, to tell the truth."

"And my prospectus," Shandon said, laughing and sneering. "Do you consider that a work of mathematical accuracy?"

"Pardon me, that is not the question," Pen countered. "I don't think you care to argue it. I had qualms of conscience about that prospectus and debated it with Warrington.
