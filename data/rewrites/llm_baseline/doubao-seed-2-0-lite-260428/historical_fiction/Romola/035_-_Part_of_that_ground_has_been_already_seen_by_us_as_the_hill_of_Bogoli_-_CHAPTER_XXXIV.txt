Messer Naldo returned far sooner than anyone expected, turning up on the evening of the twenty-eighth of November a mere eleven days after his last visit. It was clear he had never traveled far past the Tuscan mountain passes, and the tense encounter we witnessed earlier that very night in the Via de’ Bardi perfectly explains the quiet, urgent impulse that turned his steps toward the slopes of San Giorgio hill.

When Tito had first arranged this hidden cottage home for Tessa more than eighteen months prior, fresh back from his journey to Rome, he had convinced himself he acted only out of soft, uncalculating kindness after the foolish mishap that led the simple young contadina to mistake their playful mock marriage for a real, sacred bond. It was true he was drawn to her pretty, trusting warmth, to the easy charm of her prattle, but all the grand, ideal passion he held in his heart belonged entirely to Romola: the first and only woman who had ever moved him to that depth, whose mere presence wrapped around him so thoroughly that the brush of her long hair across his cheek would hum through every hour that followed. He was no gross, reckless pleasure-seeker, his intellect too sharp, his sensibilities too fine, to sink into careless vice. But a web had wrapped itself around him and Tessa that he could not find the strength to break: what had begun as a small, calculated lie to preserve her illusion for his own possible convenience had grown thick and tangled all on its own, beyond any power he had to unpick it. It was easy for Tito, with his soft, self-deceiving nature, to frame his choice to keep her in ignorance and maintain the cottage as an act of pure kindness, and he clung to that self-justification fiercely in the early days of his devotion to Romola. He could not bear the quiet weight of a secret offence against the wife he adored, whose deep, noble trust in him was rooted in moral purpose, not simple, unthinking innocence.

He took every possible precaution to keep the secret hidden. Monna Lisa, his deaf, elderly landlady, never left the cottage except to visit two nearby shops and make her annual confession, and knew nothing of his real name or identity, only that he paid her generously enough to make her old age comfortable. Tito had strictly warned Tessa that if she ever breathed a word about the farcical "marriage" ceremony, she would never see him again, and Monna Lisa’s deafness meant that no careless, unplanned chatter could ever slip past to reveal the truth. His visits were rare at first, only long enough to check that Tessa and the baby were well, and he was determined to keep her exactly the simple, unworldly country girl he had first met: a wild little field flower, untransplanted into the stifling constraints of fine clothes and complicated social pretensions that would rob her of all her gentle charm. Even the little luxuries he provided were scaled to her simple tastes, capped at the endless supply of roasted nuts she loved to hoard and nibble, a coincidence that conveniently kept his hidden expenses low at a time when every coin he owned was spoken for by his public life in Florentine high society.

All this had held true until a few weeks prior, the exact day he had stumbled on Baldassarre on the Duomo steps and bought the chain mail he now wore under his tunic. That night, walking across the bridge back home, he had found it impossible to face Romola. She trusted him, yes, but he had known deep in his bones that behind her frank, bright eyes lay a judgment he could not outrun, a moral standard that would cut through every lie he wove the second she suspected the truth. He had fled up the hill instead, craving an hour of total unconstraint, no need to perform, no need to keep up appearances, with the sweet adoring girl who thought the very sun rose and set on him, who would believe any fable he told and never care a fig for public opinion.

It was no surprise, then, that he turned his feet up the same stony path eleven days later, fresh from Romola’s first sharp, burning outburst of scorn. He did not wish Tessa in Romola’s place: Romola was the partner for his grand ambitions, his place among Florence’s scholars and statesmen, the entire wider world he had built his life within. But he craved a refuge from that unyielding, unforgiving standard he could never dismiss as mere foolishness, and Tessa’s small, uncomplicated soul was exactly that safe haven.

It was barely past eight o’clock when he climbed the steps to the cottage door. Normally Tessa would hear his footfall and come running to meet him before he even turned the handle, but tonight she did not. He stepped inside, and the dim guttering lamp hung above the dying hearth showed him why: Tessa was kneeling beside the baby’s small bed, her head lolled sideways on the pillow, fast asleep over her brown rosary, which had slipped loose from her hand to rest beside the painted image of the Madonna and its gilded palm fronds. Tito crossed the room softly, sat down beside her, and before two seconds had passed she opened her eyes, no startled gasp, no surprise, just a quiet, dreamy certainty that he was there. “I dreamed it,” she murmured, “and then I told myself it was only a dream — and then I woke, and it was true.”

“Little sinner,” he teased, pinching her cheek gently, “you’ve said barely half your prayers. I’ll punish you by refusing to look at your baby. He’s quite ugly, you know.”

Tessa’s face crumpled into a soft pout, and she leaned anxiously over the sleeping infant. “Ah, that’s not true! He’s prettier than anything in the whole world. You can’t really think he’s ugly. Look at him — he’s far bigger than when you saw him last, his hair is growing so fast, isn’t that wonderful? Kiss him very gently, and he won’t wake. You want to kiss him, don’t you?”

He pressed a light, butterfly-soft kiss to the baby’s swaddled forehead, then turned her face back to his. “You like staring at that baby better than looking at your own husband, you false thing.”

She stayed kneeling, resting her hands on his knees, gazing up at him with round, pink cheeks exactly like one of Fra Lippo Lippi’s adoring, wide-eyed angels. “No,” she said, shaking her head earnestly, “I will always love you most. I just wanted you to love the bambino too, once I only cared about having you all to myself.”

He drew her onto his knee, eager to listen to her soft prattle to ease the raw, jarring agitation left by his fight with Romola. “Did you expect me to come back so soon?”

“Ah no,” she said, “I counted every single day on my thumbs, starting back at the first day after you got that beautiful chain coat — the one Messer San Michele gave you to keep you safe on all your journeys. You’re wearing it right now, aren’t you?” she added, peeping through the slit in his tunic. “It must be the magic in it that made you come home faster.”

“Perhaps it did, Tessa,” he said. “Never mind the coat now. Tell me everything that’s happened since I was last here. Did you see the tents out at Prato, the horsemen and soldiers marching over the bridges? Hear the drums and trumpets blaring?”

“I did, and I was so frightened I thought the soldiers would climb right up the hill. Monna Lisa was scared too, she said they would steal our goats off to make mischief. But the Holy Madonna kept us safe — we never saw a single one of them up here. But something did happen, something I hardly dare tell you. I’ve been saying extra Hail Marys to ask forgiveness for it.”

“What do you mean, little one?” Tito asked, a flicker of anxiety pricking through his calm. “Tell me quickly.”

“Only let me sit on your knee while I tell you, then I won’t be so scared.”

He wrapped his arm around her, but his face had gone grave. It seemed even this quiet refuge could not escape the unpleasant consequences of his choices.

“At first I didn’t mean to tell you,” she whispered, as if speaking softer would lessen her fault, “because we thought the old man would be gone long before you came back, and it would be like it never happened at all. But he’s still here, and you’re here, and I’ve never disobeyed you before. I thought if I told you, you might forgive me, since it’s such a long time till my next confession.”

“Go on,” Tito said, already hoping it was a trivial, harmless mistake.

“Oh, you’d feel sorry for him. I think he cries when he thinks no one is watching. I first talked to him because he was wandering all alone, nowhere to stay, and I wanted to show him the baby. I thought you wouldn’t mind if I spoke to a stranger. He wanted to sleep on the straw in the shed next to the goats, so I talked Monna Lisa into saying yes. He’s gone almost all day, but when he comes back I bring him little bits of food to eat.”

“Some beggar, I suppose,” Tito said, frowning. “That was very naughty of you, and I’m cross with Monna Lisa. I’ll have him sent away at once.”

“No, he’s not a beggar,” Tessa protested. “He tried to pay Monna Lisa, but she said he could work odd jobs for her instead. He gets himself shaved every few days, his clothes are neat. Monna Lisa says he’s a proper, decent man. Sometimes I think he’s not quite in his right mind, though. Lupo, the simple boy from Peretola, looked just like him sometimes, like he didn’t know where he was. He never can remember his own name — everyone has a name, don’t they? He carries a book with him now, stares at it for hours on end like a priest, but his lips never move, he’s not saying prayers.”

Tito’s heart began to hammer so hard he could hear it in his ears. Every detail lined up perfectly with Baldassarre, the man he had disowned on the Duomo steps. “Fetch your stool, little Tessa, sit down on it,” he said, his voice strangely steady. His eyes were fixed on her, but he no longer saw her at all, only the vivid image his words conjured up.

A wild, new possibility bloomed in his mind. He could walk out to that shed right now, no witnesses anywhere. He could beg his father for forgiveness, offer him all the money he had gotten from selling Bardo’s great library, take him south to Naples where the coming French rule would grant them protection, no one the wiser. Romola would never have to learn the full truth of what happened on the Duomo steps, and if Baldassarre accepted his kindness, he would bury the secret forever. All the fear, all the tension, all the looming threat hanging over his life could vanish in a single moment, no public shame, no messy exposure, just soft, easy reconciliation that would let his world wrap itself in comfort again. He longed for that relief more than ever, fresh from the sting of Romola’s scorn.

He stood up, stepping toward the door, and Tessa dropped her rosary with a small startled cry. He turned back, his face soft again. “My little Tessa, run get me a lantern. Don’t cry, little pigeon, I’m not angry at all.”

They went down the stairs together, Tessa about to shout her request for a lantern loud enough to reach Monna Lisa’s deaf ears, when Tito, who had already pulled the cottage front door open, stopped her. “No, Tessa, I don’t need a lantern. Go back upstairs, be quiet, don’t say a word to Monna Lisa about any of this.”

Thirty seconds later he was standing before the closed door of the outhouse, the moon shining bright white on its old, unpainted wooden planks. For a split second he hesitated, instinct flaring up to shrink back from the tiger-like glare he was about to face — but he was young, strong, and wearing armour under his tunic, the old man was weak and unarmed. He laid his hand on the door, pulled it open, its rough wood scraping over the dirt floor.

Baldassarre was not asleep. A square high window in the shed wall let a stream of pale moonlight fall across him as he sat on the straw, a small dagger glinting in his hand, running his thumb again and again along its sharp edge. He was at the lowest point of his helpless, raging despair: he had pored over his book till the light faded, trying to recall the names of the jewels Tito had stolen from him, the symbols carved into their surfaces, but all those memories had slipped away into total darkness. He was left with nothing but a searing, blank rage, fixed on the single, desperate thought of driving that blade through the base heart that had betrayed him.

The door scraped open, and he looked up. There in the moonlight, backlit by the silver glow, was the very man he had been dreaming of killing, close enough to touch. Before Tito could leap back, Baldassarre launched himself forward, the dagger flashing out. The blade snapped clean in two against Tito’s hidden chain mail. Baldassarre stumbled backward onto the straw, clutching the broken hilt in his fist, the sharp pointed half of the blade glinting on the dirt right at Tito’s feet.

Tito gasped in a rush of triumphant relief. He was unharmed, his armour had held, the vengeance he had feared for weeks was broken and helpless before him. That relief softened him, any urge to cruelty melting away. He spoke in the soft, familiar voice he had used as a boy on the Greek shores, the tone Baldassarre had not heard in years. “Padre mio,” he said. “I came to ask your forgiveness.”

He paused, letting the words sink in, then continued. “I was taken completely by surprise that morning on the Duomo steps. I want to be your son again. I want to make the rest of your life happy, so you can forget all the suffering you have endured.”

Baldassarre lay motionless for a long moment, seething with defeated rage. Then he threw the broken hilt aside, dragged himself shakily to his feet, and clutched Tito’s outstretched helping hand in a grip so tight his knuckles went white, their faces barely a foot apart. “I saved you,” he growled, his voice deep and trembling with raw fury. “I nurtured you. I loved you. You forsook me. You robbed me. You denied me. What can you possibly give me? You have made the whole world nothing but bitterness for me. But there is one last draught of sweetness left. That you shall know agony.”

He dropped Tito’s hand, stumbled back, and sank down onto the straw again, spent completely by the force of his desperate lunge. There was no soft reconciliation here, no easy, secret repentance that would let Tito wipe his slate clean with no cost, no public shame, no real atonement. There was no place for it at all.
