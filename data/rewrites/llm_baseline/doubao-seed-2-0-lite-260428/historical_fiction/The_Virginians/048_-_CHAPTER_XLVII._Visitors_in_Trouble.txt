The gentle, loyal devotion of his servant Gumbo softened Harry Warrington’s wrathful, bitter spirit enough that his second night in the debtors’ sponging-house passed far more peacefully than the first. At least one soul in the whole cold, selfish world of London gentry cared enough to grieve for him, to tend to him, to share his misfortune. Even so, save for that one small spot of softness, Harry’s heart was still hardened with pride and resentment toward every other person he had once counted as a friend. All of them, he raged to himself, had proven selfish and unworthy: his pious Aunt Warrington back home, his haughty noble friend Lord March, his sharp, cynical cousin Castlewood. He would rot in prison for twenty years before he ever stooped to beg a favour from any of them again. What a fool he had been, to put his trust in their fine promises and their professed friendship! There was no true friendship anywhere in this cursed, cold English nation. He would leave it, that was certain. He would never trust an Englishman, high or low, ever again. He could ride to Germany and join the great King of Prussia’s army on campaign, or he could sail all the way back to Virginia, bury himself deep in the tobacco plantations, hunt from dawn till dusk, act as his mother’s land-steward, marry some steady local girl like Polly Broadbent or Fanny Mountain, and live out the rest of his days far away from these false, ungrateful fine gentlemen.

He rose the next morning with a forced cheerful expression, but a storm of angry pride still churning in his chest. Faithful Gumbo was already waiting outside his door at the first light, having walked all the way from their old lodgings on Bond Street, carrying Harry’s post. “I tried to bring more of your clothes, sir,” the honest man said, “but Mr. Ruff, the landlord there, wouldn’t let me take a single extra thing out of the house.” Harry had no appetite to look at the letters at first, but he slit the seals one by one anyway: every single one was a bill. The fourth letter came from the Bond Street landlord himself, informing Harry that no more of his property would leave the premises, that if his outstanding bill was not settled immediately he would sell all of Harry’s goods to pay himself, and that Gumbo would no longer be allowed to sleep on the property. Gumbo added that he had found a place to stay for the night, with some acquaintances at Lord Wrotham’s house. “With Colonel Lambert’s family, sir,” he said, peering hard at his master’s face. “And Miss Hetty, she fainted clean away when she heard the bailiffs had taken you up. Mr. Lambert, he’s a very good man, and he told me to tell you that the second you send for him, he’ll come running straight here.”

Harry’s heart twisted when he heard Hetty had been so distressed by his misfortune. He did not fully believe Gumbo’s story about the fainting fit, long accustomed to his servant’s tendency to exaggerate every dramatic detail, but when Gumbo spoke of Colonel Lambert his dark mood curdled again. *Me send for Lambert?* he thought, grinding his teeth until his jaw ached. The man who had insulted him to his face, who had flung all of Harry’s expensive gifts right back in his face? He would starve to death on a crust of bread in the gutter before he ever begged for a favour from Lambert.

Dressed at last, Harry called for his breakfast, and sent Gumbo off with a sharp, imperious note summoning Mr. Draper, the attorney in the Temple, to come attend him at once. “That note was so haughty, you’d think he was writing to one of his own enslaved field hands back in Virginia, not a freeborn English gentleman!” Draper later fumed to Madame de Bernstein. Harry had always treated the lawyer with unbearable condescension, of course. “It’s all very well for a fine gentleman to put on airs in his fine drawing rooms, but a fellow locked up in a sponging-house? Hang him, I half had a mind not to show up at all!”

For all his grumbling, Draper did go. He found Harry even more arrogant in the depths of his misfortune than he had ever been in the height of his wealthy, carefree days. Mr. Warrington was perched on the edge of the dingy iron bed in a magnificent brocade dressing-gown, his hair fully powdered and styled just as carefully as if he was dressing for a royal reception at St. James’s. He waved a hand at Gumbo, ordering him to pull a chair out for the lawyer.

“Excuse me, madam, but I have never been accustomed to such insufferable airs from a man in his position!” Draper ranted later to Madame de Bernstein, when he went to report back to her. She only smiled, highly amused at the lawyer’s wounded pride. She liked her nephew all the better for carrying himself like a great lord even when he was at his lowest ebb.

The plan for this interview had been carefully arranged between the Baroness and her attorney the day before. Draper was a sharp, capable man, who usually served his clients well, but he failed here that day because he was stung and angry, or more likely still, because he could never quite understand the proud, high-souled young gentleman he was dealing with. I think every reader who counts themselves a gentleman will have observed this odd, unspoken friction between people of different stations: you will offend a man who is not a gentleman without ever knowing how or why, and he will offend you a thousand times over, never guessing he has done anything amiss. He will say or do something that sparks your quiet scorn, he will spot that scorn instantly (always on edge, always overthinking his own manners) and fly into a rage. You will speak to him as naturally as you would to an equal, and he will be convinced you are sneering at him. You will feel nothing but indifference toward him, and he will hate you all the more for that indifference.

“Gumbo, pull a chair for Mr. Draper!” Harry said, folding his brocade dressing-gown tight around his legs as he settled on the bed. “Sit down, if you please, and let us discuss my business. Thank you for coming so promptly in answer to my note. Had you heard of this piece of ill luck before you arrived?”

Draper had, of course. “Bad news travels fast, Mr. Warrington,” he said. “I was eager to offer my humble services the moment you should require them. Your family will be greatly grieved that a gentleman of your rank should be confined in such a place.”

“I have been very imprudent, Mr. Draper. I lived far beyond my means.” Draper bowed. “I gambled with gentlemen who had ten times my fortune, and a run of terrible luck carried off all my ready cash, leaving me with liabilities of five hundred pounds and more.”

“Five hundred exactly is owed to the creditors who have you confined, sir,” Draper said.

“It is a mere trifle,” Harry said, staring at the lawyer in confusion. “I thought I could send a note round to a few friends yesterday, settle the debt, and walk straight home without any more fuss. I was mistaken. So I must ask you to arrange the sum for me as quickly as possible.”

Draper pulled a very long, grave face. “Why, sir, it can be done in time — but it will take a great deal of time. To touch the English property that comes to you only on the death of your brother George, we would need proof of his death, and the release of the trustees, and who can arrange for that? Your mother, Lady Esmond Warrington, in Virginia, would no doubt never let her son rot in prison, but it will take six full months at least to send a letter there and get a reply. Does your agent in Bristol hold any authority to honour your drafts for large sums?”

“He is only authorised to pay me two hundred pounds a year,” Harry said. “Then I suppose my only remaining option is to apply to my aunt, Madame de Bernstein. She will stand as my security.”

“Her ladyship would do anything for you, sir, she has told me so time and time again,” the lawyer said. “If she gives the word right now, you can walk out of this house this very minute.”

“Then go to her from me, Mr. Draper. I did not want to trouble my relations over this, but rather than waste more days in this pointless, horrible imprisonment, I will speak to her. Tell her exactly where I am, exactly what has happened, do not disguise a single detail. Tell her I trust in her kindness and affection for me to release me from this — this disgrace,” Harry’s voice shook a little, and he passed a hand over his eyes.

“Sir,” Draper said, eyeing the young man closely, “I was with her ladyship yesterday, going over every single most unpleasant detail of this business. I won’t call it disgraceful, like you do.”

“What do you mean? Madame de Bernstein already knows about my misfortune?” Harry cried.

“Every last circumstance, sir. Even about the watches you pawned, and all.”

Harry’s face burned bright scarlet. “It is a very unfortunate business, pawning those watches and trinkets you bought on credit and never paid for,” Draper went on. Harry shot up from the bed, his face so fierce the lawyer flinched back a little, alarmed. “It could lead to litigation, sir, very unpleasant remarks made in open court. Barristers respect nothing, and once they get a man in the witness box—”

“Great Heaven, sir, you do not think a gentleman of my rank would take a watch on credit with any intention of cheating the tradesman?” Harry shouted, shaking with agitation.

“Of course you meant everything honourable,” Draper said, winking slowly. “Only the law, you see, might not happen to see it that way. (Hang the supercilious beast, that hit him right where it hurts.) Your aunt says it is the most imprudent thing she has ever heard of, to put it no harsher name.”

“Are you putting no harsher name to it, Mr. Draper?” Harry said, speaking every word very slowly, visibly fighting to keep his temper under control. Draper did not like the look on his face.

“Heaven forbid I should say anything ungentlemanly between two gentlemen! But as your legal advisor, it is my duty to tell you, sir, you are in a very unpleasant scrape, just as a doctor must tell his patient he is gravely ill.”

“So you cannot help me pay this debt, and you have only come here to tell me I could be accused of common roguery?” Harry said.

“Of obtaining goods under false pretences? Most assuredly you could, sir. Don’t look at me like you are about to knock me down. (Curse him, I am making him squirm, anyway.) A young gentleman with only two hundred a year from his mother, ordering diamond buckles and gold watches, then taking them straight to the pawnbroker. You ask me what the world will think of that behaviour, and I am telling you honestly. Do not be angry with me, Mr. Warrington.”

“Go on, sir,” Harry groaned.

The lawyer thought he had won the argument entirely. “But you asked if I cannot help you pay the debt? And I say yes! I have the money right here in my pocket to settle every last penny this second, if you like — it is not mine, mark you, it is my honoured client’s, your aunt the Baroness’s. But she has every right to impose her own conditions, and I have brought them with me.”

“Tell them,” Harry said.

“They are not hard conditions at all! They are entirely for your own good. If you agree, we can call a hackney coach this instant and drive straight to her house in Clarges Street, which I promised her I would bring you to whether you agreed to the terms or not. I name no names, but you once made an imprudent promise of marriage to a certain party.”

“Ah!” Harry said, and his face lit up for the first time that day.

“To that marriage, my noble client the Baroness is utterly opposed. She has far better plans for you, and is convinced marrying that party will ruin you entirely — she is of noble birth and title, that is true, but if you will excuse me, she is not of the very best character, and far older than you are. You had given her that imprudent promise once, after all.”

“Yes, and she still has it,” Harry said.

“It has been recovered, sir,” Draper said. “She dropped it by accident at Tunbridge Wells, my client tells me. Her ladyship even showed it to me, it was written in bl—”

“Never mind what it was written in!” Harry cried, turning as red as the red ink he had scrawled that absurd, foolish promise in. He had regretted that mad, childish mistake a thousand times over.

“Along with it, a set of compromising letters you had written to her, that could bring great shame to that noble family, were also recovered,” the lawyer went on. “You had lost them, it was no fault of yours. You were out of the house when they were found. You must see that that noble family, and you yourself, have a friend few young men in this world are lucky enough to have. There is no earthly promise that can bind you now — nothing but idle words spoken over a bottle, that any gentleman can easily forget. All you have to do is say you will not go through with this marriage, give me your word of honour, and it is done. Cry off, I say, Mr. W! Do not be such a damned fool, saving your presence, as to marry an old woman who has jilted dozens of men in her time. Say the word, and I will go downstairs, pay every shilling you owe, and put you in my coach either at your aunt’s house or at White’s Club, if you prefer it, with two hundred guineas left over in your pocket to spend. Say yes, shake my hand! There is no sense sitting grinning behind these prison bars all day!”

Harry had longed to be free of that stupid engagement for months now. His old foolish infatuation for Maria Esmond had died out completely long ago. If he could be rid of it honestly, he would have thanked Draper a minute earlier — but the lawyer’s coarse, familiar manner disgusted him. He shrank back from Draper’s outstretched hand, scarcely even realising he did it, pulling his dressing gown tighter around himself.

“Give me just a little time to think this over, if you please, Mr. Draper,” he said. “Be so good as to return to me again in one hour.”

“Very good, sir, very good, sir!” the lawyer said, biting his lip, and seizing his hat, his face flushing bright red with anger. “Most people would not need an hour to consider such an offer as I have just made you, but I suppose my time must be yours. I will come back, and see whether you are leaving this place or not. Good morning, sir, good morning.” He stomped down the stairs, cursing under his breath the whole way. Won’t shake my hand, won’t he? Wants an hour to think it over? Hang his impudence! I will show him what an hour is!

Draper went back to his chambers in a towering huff, bullied all his clerks, sent a messenger off to Madame de Bernstein to tell her Harry had asked for a little time to consider, which he assured her was only a formality and he had no doubt he would agree. He attended to all his other clients, transacted all his other business, and then went out to a long, leisurely dinner, before finally making his slow way back to the sponging-house on Cursitor Street. “That haughty beast will still be waiting for me when I call,” Draper sneered to himself.

“Is the Fortunate Youth in his room?” Draper asked the bailiff’s assistant who opened the front double doors.

“Mr. Warrington is in his apartment,” the man said, winking and tapping the side of his nose.

“But what, Paddy from Cork?” Draper snapped.

“My name is Costigan! My family is noble, and my native city is Dublin, Mr. Six-and-Eightpence!” the janitor snarled, reeking of strong whiskey that saturated the small entryway between the double doors.

“Confound you, man, let me pass!” Draper bawled.

“I can hear you perfectly well, Six-and-Eightpence, except for your h’s, which you drop out of every sentence. I will thank you not to call names, me good friend, or me fingers and your nose will be getting very well acquainted. Walk in, sir! Be polite from now on to your superiors in birth and manners, even if they are your inferiors in temporary station. Confound the key! Walk in, I say! Madam, I have the honour to salute you most respectfully!”

A lady with her face hidden under a dark capuchin hood, and a handkerchief pressed to her mouth, gave a little startled cry as she hurried down the stairs that instant, rushing past the lawyer. Draper leaned forward to get a good look at her — he was always far too free with his manners toward women — but Costigan, the bailiff’s man, stuck his leg out between Draper and the retreating lady, crying, “Keep your own distance, if you please! This way, madam! I recognised your ladyship the moment I saw you—” He slammed the front door shut right on Draper’s nose, leaving the attorney to stumble his own way up the stairs to Harry’s room.

Back at Clarges Street, the old Baroness de Bernstein paced her drawing room for hours, stopping every few minutes to run to the window whenever she heard the rumble of a passing coach. She had delayed her dinner for hours at a time, though on ordinary occasions she would fly into a rage if her cook was even five minutes late with a meal. She had ordered two places set at her dining table, her finest silver plate laid out, and extra fancy dishes prepared, as if for a small celebration. Four o'clock passed, then five, and at six o'clock a coach finally stopped outside her front door.

“Mr. Draper,” her footman announced, and the attorney entered, bowing very low. The old lady trembled on her ivory cane. “Where is the boy?” she said sharply. “I told you to bring him here with you! How dare you come back without him?”
