The moment I saw Betteredge seated in his familiar beehive chair, the well-worn copy of *Robinson Crusoe* resting on his knee, I couldn’t resist.

“Betteredge,” I said, nodding toward the book, “has *Crusoe* predicted my arrival tonight?”

“By the Lord Harry, Mr. Franklin!” he cried, nearly toppling from his chair in surprise. “That’s precisely what he’s done!”

With my help, he steadied himself, his gaze darting between me and the book as if deciding which was the greater marvel. The verdict, it seemed, favored *Crusoe*. Clutching the volume with reverence, he stared at it as though expecting the castaway himself to stride from the pages.

“Here’s the very passage, sir!” he declared, jabbing a finger at the text. “Page one hundred and fifty-six: *‘I stood like one Thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an Apparition.’* If that’s not a prophecy of your sudden appearance, then words have no meaning!” He snapped the book shut and seized my hand, his grip as firm as ever.

I had braced myself for an interrogation, but Betteredge’s instincts as a servant—and a host—prevailed. The sight of a family member, no matter how unexpectedly arrived, demanded hospitality before curiosity.

“Come inside, Mr. Franklin,” he said, swinging the door open with an old-fashioned bow. “We’ll discuss your business in good time. First, let’s see you fed and settled. The place isn’t what it was—shut up, servants gone—but I’ll cook for you myself, and the gardener’s wife can make up a bed. And if there’s a bottle of the Latour left in the cellar, it’s yours!” His voice wavered only slightly as he fought the melancholy of the empty house, determined to welcome me as he would have in brighter days.

I hated to disappoint him. But this was Rachel’s house now, and after what had passed between us in London, I couldn’t—in decency—cross its threshold. Taking his arm, I guided him into the garden. There was no choice but honesty.

His loyalty torn between Rachel and me, he listened with deepening distress. When he finally spoke, it was with the blunt wisdom I’d come to expect.

“Miss Rachel has her faults,” he said, “and pride is one of them. She’s tried to ride roughshod over you, and you’ve borne it. But Lord, sir, haven’t you learned by now? Take my late wife—”

He launched into the familiar tale of marital strife, concluding triumphantly: “After I cooked my own dinner *once*, she never dared deny me again. Moral? Don’t let Miss Rachel bully you here as she did in London. Come back to the house!”

His logic was impeccable—and useless. “It’s no good, Betteredge,” I said. “But join me tomorrow for breakfast. I’ve something to discuss.”

He sighed. “I’d hoped you and Miss Rachel might have mended things by now. But if you’re set on leaving, at least spare yourself the trip to Frizinghall. Hotherstone’s Farm is but two miles off—and on freehold land,” he added slyly.

I remembered the place at once: a quiet farmhouse nestled in the valley, its spare rooms often let to visitors. “Are they available?”

“Mrs. Hotherstone asked me to recommend them just yesterday.”

“Then I’ll take them gladly.”

As we retrieved my bag, Betteredge shook his head in bewilderment. “I’ve lived a fair span, Mr. Franklin, but I never thought to see the day when you’d turn your back on the house.” He chuckled darkly. “Next, you’ll be repaying that seven-and-sixpence you borrowed as a boy!”

The jest restored his spirits, and as we walked, his curiosity got the better of him. “Fine evening for a stroll,” he remarked, as if we’d met by chance. “What brings you down so suddenly, sir?”

“The same as before,” I said. “The Moonstone.”

He halted, peering at me in the dusk. “If that’s a joke, I’m too old to laugh.”

“No joke. I mean to find out who took it.”

“Leave it be, Mr. Franklin! That cursed stone’s been nothing but trouble. If Sergeant Cuff couldn’t solve it—”

“My mind’s made up. Speaking of Cuff, where is he now?”

“Retired to Dorking, growing roses. He’s even bred a white moss rose without grafting—so he writes. But he won’t help you.”

“Then I’ll manage without him. And with your help.”

Something in my tone nettled him. “You might do worse,” he said sharply.

There was more he wasn’t saying. “What do you know, Betteredge?”

He feigned ignorance, but I pressed him, appealing to his love for Rachel and for me. At last, he relented.

“Rosanna Spearman left a letter for you,” he confessed. “Sealed, and in the keeping of Limping Lucy at Cobb’s Hole.”

“Lucy Yolland? The fisherman’s daughter?”

“The same. She refused to give it to anyone but you.”

“Then let’s fetch it tonight.”

“Too late now. Cobb’s Hole keeps early hours. But tomorrow, at first light—”

Below us, a lantern glowed at Hotherstone’s Farm.

“Tomorrow, then,” I agreed.

And with that, we descended the path toward the waiting light.
