For a long time, during my period of struggle, I kept my distance from Old Hasluck. He wasn't the kind of man to sympathize with emotions he couldn't comprehend. With his boisterous cheerfulness, he would have insisted on helping me, offering a fair wage for my labor. Yet, I chose to starve alongside Lott & Co. rather than sell my work to the kindly old Hasluck simply because I knew him. I can't quite explain why. Perhaps the profits weren't as high, but Lott & Co. were no more honest than Hasluck; I doubt honesty was the deciding factor. Nor was it because he was Barbara's father. In my mind, I never linked that good old soul—or his vulgar, homely wife—to Barbara. To me, she existed entirely apart from the rest of the world. Her *true* parents, I felt, should have been sought amidst the sacred groves of vanished lands, within the sky-domed shrines of banished gods. There are instincts in us that resist analysis, defying reason. I've always preferred navigating life by a map—risking a confusing journey guided by a Continental Bradshaw—to the safer, simpler method of asking questions. It feels as though a distant ancestor of mine might have been a morose "rogue" savage, living alone in his cave, crafting his own stone hammer and flint arrows, shunning the merry war-dance to caper by himself.

But now, having finally secured my footing, I could reach out without fearing my gesture would be mistaken for a plea. I wrote to Old Hasluck. Almost immediately, via the next post, arrived the friendliest note imaginable. He informed me that Barbara had just returned from abroad, took it upon himself to add that she would be delighted to see me, and, as he knew I'd expect, threw his doors wide open.

I had never spoken of my boyish passion for Barbara to a living soul. I suspect, barring Barbara herself, no one ever guessed it. My mother, though fond of her, viewed Barbara merely as a girl with charms and faults, discussing the latter freely. This often hurt me, much like a philosopher might wound a new convert by dissecting their freshly embraced faith. Often, choosing late evenings or the night, I would wander around the massive red-brick house atop Stamford Hill, set in its ancient garden. Descending back into the noisome streets felt like returning to the world after praying at a shrine; I felt purified, filled with peace, convinced that all noble endeavor and unselfish aims were within my grasp.

Over Barbara's four-year absence, my adoration deepened and hardened. From my memories of her, desire evolved into an ideal—a creation of my imagination that, paradoxically, felt more real and present than reality itself. I looked forward to seeing her again, yet without impatience, reveling in the anticipation rather than rushing toward realization. As a flesh-and-blood child I had played with and touched, she had faded into the distance; as the vision of my dreams, she stood out clearer every day. When I finally saw her, I knew there would be a gulf between us I had no intention of bridging. Worshiping her from afar was sweeter to me than the prospect of a passionate embrace. Living with her, sitting opposite while she ate and drank, seeing her perhaps with curl-papers in her hair, knowing she had a corn on her foot, hearing her complain of a decayed tooth or a chilblain—that would have been torture. I feared no abyss of the commonplace dragging her down, and for this, I was grateful. In the future, she would remain even more removed. She was older than I; she must now be a woman. Instinctively, I felt that despite the passing years, I was not yet a man. She would marry. The thought brought no pain; my feeling for her was utterly devoid of appetite. No one but myself could close the temple I had built around her; no jealous priest could hide her from my eyes, for the altar I had reared was too high. Now that I know myself better, I realize she stood to me not as a living woman, but as a symbol—not a fellow traveler to walk through life with—but that impalpable religion of sex to which we erect idols of poor human clay. Alas, we often build them unsatisfactorily, falling into anger against them, forgetting they were merely our own making. It was not the body, but the spirit of love.

I waited a week after receiving Hasluck's letter before presenting myself at Stamford Hill. It was late one afternoon in early summer. Hasluck was still in the City, Mrs. Hasluck was visiting, and Miss Hasluck was in the garden. I instructed the supercilious footman not to bother; I would find her myself. I guessed where she would be: her favorite spot, a sunny corner bright with flowers, enclosed by a thick yew hedge trimmed into quaint Dutch shapes of animals and birds. There she was, exactly as I expected, reading a book. Again, as I saw her, the feeling swept over me that had struck me as a boy when she first appeared against the dusty backdrop of my father's office: that all fairy tales had suddenly come true, only now, instead of the Princess, she was the Queen. She was taller, possessing a dignity that had previously been her only missing charm.

She didn't hear me approach; my path crossed the soft, short grass unseen. For a moment, I stood in the shadow of the yews, drinking in the beauty of her clear-cut profile, bent low over her book—the curving line of her long neck, the wonder of that exquisite white hand against the lilac of her dress.

I said nothing; I would have preferred to keep watching. But as she turned at the end of the path, she spotted me. Coming toward me, she held out her hand. I knelt on the path and raised it to my lips. The action was spontaneous; afterward, I wasn't even sure I had done it. Her lips smiled as I lifted my eyes to them, a faint suggestion of contempt mingled with amusement. Yet, she seemed pleased, and even if I hadn't been mistaken, her contempt wouldn't have wounded me.

"So you are still in love with me?" she asked. "I wondered if you would be."

"Did you know that I was in love with you?"

"I should have been blind if I hadn't."

"But I was only a boy."

"You were not the usual type of boy. You are not going to be the usual type of man."

"Do you not mind my loving you?"

"I cannot help it, can I? Nor can you."

She sat on a stone bench facing a sundial, leaning back with her hands clasped behind her head, laughing at me.

"I shall always love you," I answered, "but it is a curious sort of love. I do not understand it myself."

"Tell me," she commanded, still smiling. "Describe it to me."

I stood opposite her, my arm resting on the sundial's stone column. The sun was setting, casting long shadows on the velvety grass and bathing her upturned face in golden light.

"I wish you were some great queen of olden days, so I might always be near you, serving you, doing your bidding. Your love in return would spoil everything; I would never ask for it, never desire it. I would only wish to look upon you, to touch your hand now and then at rare intervals, to kiss in secret the glove you dropped or the shoe you discarded, knowing you knew of my love, that I was yours to use as you willed, to live or die by your command. Or that you were a priestess in a temple of forgotten gods, where I might steal away at dawn and dusk to gaze upon your beauty; kneel with clasped hands, watching your sandaled feet move about the altar steps; lie with pressed lips upon the stones your trailing robes had touched."

She laughed a light, mocking laugh. "I should prefer to be the queen. The role of priestess doesn't suit me. Temples are so cold." A slight shiver ran through her. She gestured with her hand, beckoning me closer. "That is how you shall love me, Paul," she said. "Adoring me, worshipping me—blindly. I will be your queen and treat you as I choose. Everything I think, everything I do, I will tell you, and you shall agree it is right. The queen can do no wrong."

She cupped my face in her hands, bending over me to look long and steadily into my eyes. "You understand, Paul, the queen can do no wrong—never, never." A note of vehemence had crept into her voice; there was a look of almost desperate appeal in her face.

"My queen can do no wrong," I repeated. She laughed and let her hands fall to her lap.

"Now you may sit beside me. So much honor, Paul, you shall receive today, but it must last you a long time. Tell me all you've been doing; perhaps it will amuse me. Afterwards, I'll tell you what I've done, and you shall say it was right and good of me."

I obeyed, sketching my story briefly yet omitting nothing, not even the transit of the Lady Ortensia, though I was ashamed of the episode. At that, she looked somewhat grave.
