Viborg stands among the principal towns of Jutland, holding a distinguished place in the region's annals. It serves as the seat of a bishopric, boasting a magnificent yet nearly brand-new cathedral, a delightful garden, a lake of exceptional beauty, and a population of storks. Nearby lies Hald, often cited as one of Denmark's most picturesque locales, and closer still is Finderup, the site where Marsk Stig executed King Erik Glipping on St. Cecilia's Day in 1286. When the king's tomb was finally opened in the seventeenth century, fifty-six blows of square-headed iron maces were discovered etched onto his skull. Yet, I am not composing a travelogue.

Viborg offers respectable accommodations; Preisler's and the Phoenix Inn meet every requirement for comfort. However, my cousin, whom I shall refer to as Mr. Anderson, chose the Golden Lion upon his initial visit to the city. He has not returned since, and the subsequent pages may elucidate the rationale behind his permanent exile from that establishment.

The Golden Lion is one of the rare structures in the town to survive the catastrophic fire of 1726, which razed the cathedral, the parish church, the town hall, and countless other historic edifices. The facade is constructed of great red brick, featuring corbie steps on the gables and an inscription above the entrance, while the courtyard accessed by the omnibus is clad in black-and-white wood and plaster.

Mr. Anderson arrived as the sun began its descent, casting a golden glow upon the imposing frontage of the inn. He was charmed by the antiquated aesthetic of the building and envisioned a thoroughly satisfying and diverting stay, emblematic of old Jutland.

His mission was not commercial in the conventional sense. Anderson was conducting research into the ecclesiastical history of Denmark. Intelligence indicated that the Rigsarkiv in Viborg preserved documents salvaged from the fire, detailing the final days of Roman Catholicism in the kingdom. Consequently, he intended to dedicate a significant portion of his time—potentially two to three weeks—to examining and transcribing these records. He had requested a suite large enough to function simultaneously as a bedroom and a study. The landlord, after deliberation, suggested that Anderson inspect several available chambers to select one suited to his needs.

The attic was dismissed immediately due to the excessive exertion required to ascend the stairs after a day of fieldwork. The second floor lacked a room matching his specific dimensional requirements. However, the first floor presented two or three viable options regarding size.

The landlord advocated strongly for Room 17. Anderson countered that its windows faced only the blank wall of the adjacent building, guaranteeing oppressive darkness throughout the afternoon. He argued that either Room 12 or Room 14 would be superior, as both overlooked the street, offering ample evening illumination and a pleasant vista that would outweigh the inevitable increase in ambient noise.

Room 12 was ultimately selected. Like its neighbors, it featured three windows aligned on a single wall; it was notably high and elongated. While devoid of a fireplace, it housed a handsome, aged cast-iron stove bearing a depiction of Abraham sacrificing Isaac, inscribed with "1 Bog Mose, Cap. 22" above the scene. The only other object of interest was an antique colored print of the town, dated circa 1820.

Dinner hour approached, yet when Anderson, refreshed by his customary ablutions, descended the staircase, the dinner bell had not yet sounded. He utilized the interim to scrutinize the roster of his fellow lodgers. As is customary in Denmark, names were displayed on a large blackboard, organized into columns and rows, with room numbers prefixed to each entry. The list was unremarkable: an advocate, a German national, and several merchants from Copenhagen. The sole element warranting contemplation was the conspicuous absence of Room 13 from the inventory. Anderson had noted this omission repeatedly during his travels across Danish hotels. He wondered if the superstitious aversion to the number thirteen was so pervasive that it rendered the room commercially unviable, prompting him to inquire of the landlord whether he and his professional peers had encountered clients refusing accommodation in the thirteenth chamber.

He provided no further commentary to me regarding the supper or the evening, which was consumed with unpacking and organizing his attire, books, and papers. Around eleven o'clock, he prepared for rest. For individuals of his disposition, reading a few pages prior to sleep was a non-negotiable prerequisite. He recalled that the volume currently captivating him, the only title capable of satisfying his intellectual appetite at that moment, resided in the pocket of his greatcoat, which hung on a peg outside the dining room.

Retrieving the book took mere moments. Since the corridors were adequately lit, navigating back to his door posed no difficulty—at least, so he believed. Upon reaching the destination and turning the handle, however, the door remained immovable. Simultaneously, he detected the sound of hurried movement emanating from within. Naturally, he realized he had mistaken the door. Was his chamber to the right or the left? He glanced at the plaque: it bore the number 13. His intended room was to the left. Thus, it was.

Only after settling into bed, reading his accustomed three or four pages, extinguishing the candle, and preparing to drift off did the realization strike him: whereas the hotel's blackboard listed no Room 13, a room explicitly designated as such existed within the inn. A pang of regret washed over him. Had he occupied it, he might have rendered the landlord a service, providing proof that a well-born English gentleman had resided therein for three weeks and approved of the quarters. Presumably, it served as a servant's quarters or similar utility space. Likely, it was neither as spacious nor as refined as his own. He surveyed the room drowsily, illuminated faintly by the streetlamp filtering through the glass. It was peculiar; typically, dim lighting expands the perceived dimensions of a space, yet this room appeared to have contracted in length while growing disproportionately taller. Sleep, he concluded, was paramount compared to such vague musings.

The following day, Anderson commenced his investigation at the Rigsarkiv. As one might anticipate in Denmark, he was received with kindness, granted unrestricted access to the archives. The documents awaiting him exceeded his expectations in both quantity and intrigue. Beyond official state papers, a substantial collection of correspondence concerning Bishop Jörgen Friis, the final Roman Catholic incumbent of the see, came to light. These letters revealed numerous amusing and intimate details regarding private lives and individual character traits. Frequent mention was made of a house owned by the Bishop but uninhabited by him. Its tenant was described as a source of scandal and an obstacle to the reformist faction. He was labeled a disgrace to the city, accused of practicing clandestine and malevolent arts and having sold his soul to the enemy. Such a viperous *troldmand* (wizard) was deemed consistent with the gross corruption and superstition of the Babylonish Church, yet he was patronized and harbored by the Bishop. Friis responded to these accusations with bold defiance, protesting his own abhorrence of occult practices and demanding that his adversaries bring the matter before the proper spiritual court for thorough adjudication. He expressed readiness to condemn Magister Nicolas Francken should evidence substantiate the informal allegations.

Anderson had barely glanced at the subsequent letter from the Protestant leader, Rasmus Nielsen, before the archive closed for the day. Nevertheless, he grasped its general sentiment: that Christian men were no longer bound by the decrees of the Pope, and that the Bishop's Court was neither fit nor competent to judge such a grave cause.

Upon departing the office, Anderson was escorted by Herr Scavenius, the archivist. Their conversation naturally drifted toward the documents discussed earlier. Although Scavenius was well-versed in the general corpus of materials under his custody, he was not a specialist in Reformation-era texts. He expressed keen interest in Anderson's findings and looked forward eagerly to the publication of Anderson's work. Regarding the Bishop's house, Scavenius admitted it was a persistent enigma. Despite meticulous study of Viborg's topography, he found it unfortunate that the section of the 1560 terrier listing town properties, preserved in the archive, was missing. "Never mind," he remarked optimistically. "Perhaps I shall eventually locate it."

After exercising, Anderson returned to the Golden Lion for supper, a game of patience, and his bed. En route to his room, he realized he had neglected to discuss the omission of Room 13 with the landlord and ought to verify its existence firsthand before raising the subject.

The decision was straightforward. The door bore its number plainly, and activity was evident within; footsteps and voices, or perhaps a single voice, carried from inside. As he paused to confirm the number, the footsteps ceased abruptly, seeming to stop inches from the threshold. He was startled by a sharp, hissing intake of breath, indicative of extreme agitation. Proceeding to his own room, he was struck anew by how diminished it appeared compared to his selection earlier. A minor disappointment, easily rectified by relocating if necessary.

However, a more pressing issue arose. He required a pocket-handkerchief from his portmanteau, which the porter had placed on a flimsy trestle stool against the far wall, distant from his bed. Curiously, the portmanteau was gone. It had been relocated by officious staff, presumably emptied into the wardrobe. Yet, the wardrobe was bare. Dismissing the notion of theft—he knew such incidents were rare in Denmark—he attributed the disappearance to simple incompetence.
