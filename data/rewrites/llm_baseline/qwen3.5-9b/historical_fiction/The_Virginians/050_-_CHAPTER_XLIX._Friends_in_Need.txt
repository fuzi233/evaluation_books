"Quick, coachman! Hackney-coach steeds, and bear George Warrington through the Strand and Fleet Street to his imprisoned brother's rescue!"

Any traveler who recalls Hogarth's famous depiction of a London hackney-coach and the streets of that era can easily imagine the weariness of the pace and the lengthening of the journey. There were scarce any lights save those carried by link-boys; the coaches were poorly hung, the pavements treacherous, riddled with great holes and vast quagmires of winter mud. Yet, to our young man, this frantic drive from Piccadilly to Fleet Street seemed almost as interminable as the arduous journey from Marlborough to London he had undertaken earlier that day.

George had already written to Harry, announcing his arrival in Bristol. Prior to that, he had penned a letter to his brother revealing the great news of his own existence and his miraculous return from captivity. At that time, war raged between England and France; French privateers were perpetually on the lookout for British merchant vessels, seizing them often within sight of port. The letter carrying intelligence of George's restoration must have been aboard one of the numerous American ships captured by the French. Conversely, the letter detailing George's safe arrival in England was never opened by poor Harry. It lay undisturbed in his apartments, reaching him on the third morning after his captivity, only to be ignored when the irascible Mr. Ruff refused to surrender any further items of his lodger's property.

Upon arriving in London, George immediately sought his brother. When the maid-servant answered the door, terrified by the striking resemblance between the two brothers, she screamed and fled to her mistress. The mistress, unwilling to admit the truth—that poor Harry was actually a prisoner held by her husband—deceived him, claiming Mr. Warrington had simply vacated his lodgings and offering no clue as to his whereabouts.

George knew Clarges Street was near Bond Street. He had studied the London maps countless times. Perhaps Aunt Bernstein could tell him where Harry was; she might even be holding him at that very moment. George recalled Harry's letters to Virginia, which spoke warmly of Aunt Bernstein's kindness. Indeed, even Madam Esmond had been softened by it, particularly moved by a specific letter from the Baroness—the very correspondence that had spurred George to hasten to Europe. He fervently hoped and trusted that Madam Beatrix had finally found cause to repent of her former wicked ways. It was time, certainly, given her age; heaven knew she had ample reason to regret her past. One had witnessed harmless, good souls of eighty still pummeled and stoned by irreproachable ladies of the strictest Pharisaic sect for minor slips committed decades prior. Rachel Esmond never mentioned her eldest daughter, nor did Madam Esmond Warrington mention her sister. No. Despite orders for the remission of sentences and the handwriting found on the floor of the Temple, there existed crimes that some folk never forgave, regarding which female virtue, above all else, remained inexorable.

Perhaps the Virginian agent in Bristol had fed George fearful tales of his brother's conduct. Gumbo, whom George had encountered at his aunt's door once the lad recovered from his terror at seeing his presumed-dead master, had managed to stammer out a few words concerning his young master's location and pitiable state. Hence, Mr. George's stern demeanor upon presenting himself to the old lady. To him, it seemed a matter of course that a brother in difficulty should be rescued by his relations.

"Oh, George, how little you know of London and its ways!" he chided himself internally. "Whenever you take your walks abroad, how many poor souls you meet—if a philanthropist attempted to rescue every one of them, not all the wealth of the American provinces would suffice!"

Yet, the feeling and agitation displayed by the old lady touched her nephew's heart. Jolting through the dark streets toward the house of his brother's captivity, George began to reconsider his judgment. *"She does feel my poor Harry's misfortune,"* he thought. *"I have been too hasty in judging her."* Time and again in his life, Mr. George had been forced to rebuke himself for the crime of rash judgment. How many of us have not? Alas, once mischief is done, no repentance can fully mend it.

"Quick, coachman! We are almost as slow as you are in getting from Clarges Street to the Temple."

Poor Gumbo knew the way to the bailiff's house well enough. Once more, the bell rang. The first door opened to George and his negro servant; then, warily locked upon them, they found themselves in a narrow passage guarded by a little Jewish janitor. A second door unlocked, and they entered the house. The janitor stared, his flaring tallow-torch illuminating a second Mr. Warrington before him.

"Come to see that gentleman?" the janitor asked.

"Yes. But wait a moment. This is Mr. Warrington's brother from America."

Gumbo was sent to prepare his master. "Step into this room," the porter directed. "There is a gentleman already here attending to Mr. W.'s business, and another upstairs with him now. There has been no end of people about him."

The apartment, known as Mr. Amos's office, was small. By the light of a guttering candle, a stout gentleman in a cloak and laced hat sat talking to the bailiff. The young porter preceded Mr. George, carrying a candle, ensuring sufficient illumination.

"We are not angry anymore, Harry!" the stout gentleman exclaimed in a cheery voice, rising and advancing with an outstretched hand toward the newcomer. "Thank God, my boy! Mr. Amos here says there will be no difficulty about James and me acting as your bail, and we shall settle your business by breakfast tomorrow. Why... Angels and ministers of grace! Who are you?"

He recoiled slightly as the other gripped his hand firmly.

"God bless you, sir!" the stranger said. "I know who you are. You must be Colonel Lambert, of whose kindness to him my poor Harry wrote. And I am the brother you have heard of, sir; the one left for dead in Mr. Braddock's action, who came to life again after eighteen months among the French, and who lives today to thank God and thank you for your kindness to my Harry." His voice faltered.

"James! James! Here is news!" cried Mr. Lambert to a gentleman in red who now entered the room. "Here are the dead come alive! Here is Harry Scapegrace's brother returned, and with his scalp intact on his head!" (George had removed his hat and stood by the light.)

"This is my brother-bail, Mr. Warrington! This is Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe, at your service. You must understand there has been a slight difference between Harry and me, Mr. George. He is pacified, is he, James?"

"He is full of gratitude," replied Mr. Wolfe, bowing respectfully to Mr. Warrington.

"Harry wrote home about Mr. Wolfe, too, sir," the young man added. "And I hope my brother's friends will extend the same kindness to me."

"I wish he had none other than us, Mr. Warrington. Poor Harry's fine folks have been too fine for him, ending by landing him here."

"Nay, your honors, I have done my best to make the young gentleman comfortable. Knowing your honor before, when you bailed Captain Watkins, and knowing your security is perfectly good—if your honor wishes, the young gentleman can leave tonight, and I shall straighten things out with the lawyer in the morning," said Harry's landlord, recognizing the rank and respectability of the two gentlemen offering bail.

"The debt is five hundred and odd pounds, I think?" asked Mr. Warrington.

"With a hundred thanks to these gentlemen, I can pay the amount at this very moment into the officers' hands, taking the usual acknowledgment and caution. But I can never forget, gentlemen, that you helped my brother in his need. For doing so, I say thank you, and God bless you, in my mother's name and mine."

Meanwhile, Gumbo had ascended to his master's apartment. Harry would likely have scolded the negro for returning that late, but the young gentleman was deeply soothed and touched by the conversation he had just shared with the friend who had departed. He sat over his pipe of Virginia tobacco in a somber mood. Somehow, even Maria's goodness and affection, as recently exhibited, had not entirely consoled him; thoughts of certain consequences bound to that very kindness and fidelity filled him with dismay. Then, Mr. Wolfe's homely features and eager, outstretched hand appeared to cheer the prisoner. He heard how Mr. Lambert was below and the urgent errand of the two officers.

In spite of himself, Lambert would be kind. In spite of Harry's ill-temper, needless suspicion, and anger, the good gentleman was determined to help him if he could—even against Mr. Wolfe's own advice.

"For you were wrong, Mr. Warrington," the Colonel stated frankly. "You wouldn't be set right. You, a young man, used hard words and unkind behavior to your senior, and what is more, one of the best gentlemen who walks God's earth. You see, sir, what his answer has been to your wayward temper.
