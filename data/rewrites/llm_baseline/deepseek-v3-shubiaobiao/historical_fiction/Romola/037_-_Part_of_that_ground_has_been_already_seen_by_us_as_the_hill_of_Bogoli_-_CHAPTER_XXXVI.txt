The library stood empty now, its treasures crated and carried away. Romola had watched it all—each book, each artifact, every fragment of her father’s life vanishing piece by piece. The exhaustion of grief had settled into a numb disbelief, as though the past weeks were a dream from which she might still wake. But each evening, she walked through the hollowed-out rooms, her lamp casting long shadows over the straw-strewn floors, forcing herself to remember: this was real. Her love was gone. The world had turned barren.

On the final day, the 23rd of December, she climbed to the loggia, her fingers gripping the cold stone as the last cart rolled across the bridge. The sky hung low and leaden, dusk leaching the color from Florence. The Arno ran dark beneath the bridge, and the city’s towers stood like tombstones in the gloom. Santa Croce, where her father lay, was swallowed in shadow. The white-shrouded load crawled away like a specter, carrying with it the last remnants of his life’s work. She did not weep. She only stared, feeling not her own sorrow but his—as though he, beneath the unyielding weight of the grave, could still see his legacy torn from him.

Then, without warning, the bells began.

A great peal shattered the silence, rolling from the palace tower, echoed by every campanile in Florence. Fire burst between the turrets, leaping like living things, and the air itself seemed to vibrate with triumph. But to Romola, the sound was a mockery. These were the bells that had once rung for her joy, that had heralded her marriage, that had promised a future bright with hope. Now they clamored for her husband’s victory—for the betrayal that had hollowed her out.

She pressed her hands to her ears and fled.

Below, she nearly collided with Tito himself. He was radiant, his step light, his voice buoyant.

“The noise unsettles you?” he asked, noting her recoil. He took her wrist, guiding her into the saloon where painted nymphs danced frozen on the walls. “Florence celebrates its Great Council—an end to all ills, they say. But you look pale. You should not linger in the cold.” His tone was smooth, practiced. “I’m called to Rome on business for Bernardo Rucellai. I leave tonight. No need to trouble yourself; my things are already packed.”

He pressed her hand to his lips—no kiss, no tenderness, only the ghost of a husband’s gesture. Then he was gone.

Romola did not move until the door closed behind him. Then, like a marionette cut from its strings, she sagged. But her stillness was not resignation. Beneath the frost of her composure, her mind burned.

She called for Maso.

“We leave at dawn,” she told him. “Prepare the mules. Wait for me at Trespiano.”

She would send him back from Bologna with letters—one for Bernardo, one for Tito. No explanations. No pleas. Only silence.

That night, she did not sleep.

She opened the chest where her bridal gown lay folded—white silk and gold, the circlet of pearls still looped with a sugar-plum Tito had once laughed over. A sob clawed up her throat. These were not relics of joy, but of a love now dead.

Beneath the finery lay something darker: a coarse grey robe, the habit of a Pinzochera. She stripped off her mourning dress and pulled the rough serge over her skin, tightening the rope girdle until it bit into her waist. The discomfort was a relief. Pain, at least, was honest.

At the mirror, she bound her hair tight, covering it with a black kerchief, then drew the cowl over her head. The face that stared back was unfamiliar—wan, hollow-eyed, like her brother Dino in his final days. Was she becoming like him? Not in faith, but in understanding: the lure of renunciation, the refuge of solitude.

She packed the last of her father’s relics—portraits, small treasures—into the chest. Then her hand hovered over her betrothal ring.

To remove it felt like severing her own flesh. The ring was a symbol, yes, but symbols had weight. They were the hooks by which the world held her. Yet what did it bind her to now? A ghost. A lie.

She wrenched it off.

Dawn crept in, grey and silent. The city’s revelry had faded. Romola took up her pen, then hesitated. Words would make this real. But the ink dried unspilled.

Instead, she fastened the scarsella at her waist, its coins clinking softly, and looped the rosary beside it. She was no pious sister, but the disguise would serve. Venice awaited. Cassandra Fedele, the scholar, would know how a woman might live alone, might turn her mind to something beyond love.

As the first light touched the rooftops, Romola stepped into the street. The past was a closed door. The future—unknowable, perhaps bleak—was hers alone.

She did not look back.
