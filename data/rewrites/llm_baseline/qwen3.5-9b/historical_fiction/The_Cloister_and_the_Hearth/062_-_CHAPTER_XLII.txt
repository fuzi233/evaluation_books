Gerard regained consciousness, only to plunge immediately into a abyss of despair. By the second day, fever ravaged his mind, leaving him delirious. Beside his bed lay his once-rich auburn locks, now matted and long as a woman's. One fatal symptom swiftly followed another. On the fifth day, the leech withdrew, declaring the case hopeless. That very evening, as the sun dipped below the horizon, he sank into a profound slumber. Rumors circulated that he would awaken only to meet his end. Yet, an elderly gossip, a woman of considerable standing due to her experience as a professional nurse, offered a glimmer of hope. "His youth may yet save him," she declared, "if he can endure twelve hours of uninterrupted sleep."

Encouraged by this counsel, his aged landlady swept the room clear and tended to him alone. She lit a wax candle before the image of the Virgin Mary, vowing one prayer for every hour he remained asleep. Twelve hours passed. The good woman rejoiced, falling to her knees to thank the Mother of God. Twenty-four hours elapsed. Doubt crept back into the hearts of the caregivers. At the thirty-hour mark, she summoned the physician. "Thirty hours!" she exclaimed anxiously. "Shall we wake him?"

The doctor examined him closely for some time. "His breathing is steady, his hands remain moist," he observed. "I know that learned physicians would wake him merely to inspect his tongue, finding nothing amiss; but we, being women, possess the wisdom to allow nature its course. When has sleep ever harmed a fractured mind or a shattered heart?"

By the forty-eighth hour, whispers spread through the neighborhood, and the curious pressed against the doors. They were finally admitted only Fra Colonna and his companion, the towering Friar Jerome. These two men took over from the women, sitting in silence. Fra Colonna gazed upon his young friend with tears streaming down his cheeks, while Friar Jerome held his rosary beads, staring at the sleeper with the same calm detachment he had shown when Gerard and he faced the raging sea together.

It was around the sixtieth hour of this extraordinary vigil that the landlady nudged Fra Colonna with her elbow. He glanced over. Gerard had opened his eyes with the gentleness of one merely dozing. He stared blankly, drawing himself slightly upright in the bed. He reached up to touch his head and discovered his hair was gone. Noticing his friend Colonna, he broke into a smile of relief. But midway through the expression, his face froze, seized instantly by anguish beyond description. A loud cry tore from his throat, and he turned his face firmly toward the wall.

His landlady wept openly; she knew the sorrow of waking bereft. Friar Jerome began chanting psalms and reading prayers from his breviary. Gerard curled himself tightly within the bedclothes. Fra Colonna approached, whispering comfortingly, reminding him that all was not lost. "The divine Muses are immortal," he urged. "You must transfer your affection to them; they will never betray you nor fail you like fragile creatures of clay." Overcome by emotion, the simple friar hurried away.

Friar Jerome remained. "Young man," he said softly, "the Muses exist only in the minds of pagans and dreamers. The Church alone offers rest to the heart on earth and happiness to the soul in the hereafter. Has the earth deceived you? Has passion broken your heart after tearing it apart? The Church opens her arms: consecrate your gifts to her! The Church is peace of mind." He spoke these words solemnly at the threshold and departed the moment they were spoken.

"The Church!" Gerard shrieked, springing up in fury and shaking his fist at the retreating friar. "Curses on the Church! Were it not for the Church, I should not lie broken here, while she lies cold, cold, cold in Holland. Oh, my Margaret! Oh, my darling! My darling! And I must flee from you during the few months you had left to live. Cruel! Cruel! Those monsters allowed her to die. Death does not come without warning signs; the blind, selfish fools saw them not, or cared not; but I would have seen them, I who loved her. Oh, had I been there, I would have saved her, I would have saved her! Fool! Fool! To leave her for a single moment!"

He wept bitterly for a long time. Then, suddenly bursting into fresh rage, he cried out vehemently, "The Church! For whose sake I was driven from her! May my malediction fall on the Church! And on the hypocrites who invoke it against my broken heart. Accursed be the world! Ghysbrecht lives; Margaret dies. Thieves, murderers, harlots live forever. Only angels die. Curse life! Curse death! And whoever shaped them into such things!"

The friar did not hear these mad and wicked words, catching only the roar of rage hurled after him. It was fortunate, for had he heard them, he would have ordered the late shipmate burned in the public square with no more hesitation than roasting a kid.

The old landlady, having accompanied Fra Colonna downstairs, heard the raised voice and returned in alarm. She found Gerard dressing, sobbing uncontrollably. She protested. "What avail lies in remaining here?" he asked gloomily. "Can I find here what I seek?"

"Saints preserve us! Is he distracted again? What do you seek?"

"Oblivion."

"Oblivion, my little heart? Oh, but you are too young to speak thus."

"Young or old, what else have I to live for?"

He donned his finest garments. The good lady remonstrated. "My dear Gerard, know that it is Tuesday, not Sunday."

"Oh, Tuesday is it? I thought it had been Saturday."

"Nay, you have slept long. You never wear your fine clothes on working days. Consider."

"What I did when she lived, I did. Now I shall do whatever I once refused. The past is the past. There lies my hair, and with it my former way of life. I have served one Master as well as I could. See my reward. Now I will serve another, and give him a fair trial."

"Alas!" sighed the woman, turning pale. "What do these dark words mean? And what new master is this whose service you wish to try?"

With this terrible declaration on his lips, the wretched creature walked out. His cap and feather were set jauntily askew on one side, his limbs weak, his face sinister and pale as ash, drawn down as if by premature age.
