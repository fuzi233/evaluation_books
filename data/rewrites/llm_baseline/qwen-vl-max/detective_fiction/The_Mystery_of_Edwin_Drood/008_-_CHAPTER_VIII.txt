The two young men stood before the courtyard of the Nuns’ House, watching as the women they were escorting entered the gate. The brass doorplate—old, dented, and defiant—seemed to glare at them like a challenge, its cold surface reflecting the moonlight with an almost mocking stillness. They exchanged glances, then turned away together, walking slowly down the quiet street.

“Will you be staying long in Cloisterham, Mr. Drood?” Neville asked.

“No,” Edwin replied carelessly. “I leave for London tomorrow. But I’ll be back from time to time—until next Midsummer. After that, I’m done with this place, and England too. For many years, I expect.”

“You’re going abroad?”

“Going to wake up Egypt a little,” Edwin said, his tone laced with condescension.

“Are you reading?” Neville pressed.

“Reading?” Edwin repeated, a flicker of disdain crossing his face. “No. I’m doing. Working. Engineering. My father left me a share in the firm he founded—part of my inheritance is tied up in it. Until I come of age, I’m under their care. Jack—you met him at dinner—is my guardian and trustee.”

“I heard from Mr. Crisparkle about your other good fortune,” Neville said cautiously.

“What do you mean by ‘other good fortune’?” Edwin snapped.

Neville had approached the question with a watchful step—forward but wary, as if both hunter and hunted. Edwin’s reply was sharp, abrupt, lacking any trace of courtesy.

They stopped. A tense silence passed between them.

“I hope,” Neville said, “that I’ve not offended you by mentioning your betrothal.”

“By George!” Edwin burst out, resuming their walk at a faster pace. “Everyone in this gossiping old town talks about it. I swear someone should open a pub and hang my portrait over the door—‘The Betrothed’s Head.’ Or maybe Pussy’s portrait. One or the other.”

“It wasn’t my place to bring it up,” Neville began, “but Mr. Crisparkle spoke openly about it, and I thought—”

“That’s true,” Edwin cut in. “You’re not accountable for what he said. But you *are* accountable for saying it to me. And you did so, assuming I’d be proud of it.”

There were two invisible forces shaping their words: one born of envy, the other of pride. Neville already admired Little Rosebud and resented how lightly Edwin treated her. Edwin, meanwhile, had begun to feel the weight of Helena Landless—her grace, her spirit—and now found himself irritated by the idea that her brother could so easily dismiss him, push him aside.

But the moment demanded a response.

“I don’t know,” Edwin said, adopting the formal address used by Mr. Crisparkle, “that people who are proudest of something usually talk about it most. Nor that they enjoy others talking about it. I live a busy life, and I speak under correction—by you readers, who, no doubt, know everything.”

By now, both were simmering. Neville wore his anger openly; Edwin masked his with irony, pausing occasionally to admire the moonlit shadows as though genuinely enchanted.

“It doesn’t seem very civil,” Neville said at last, “to mock a stranger who’s just arrived, trying to make up for lost time.”

“But then,” Edwin retorted, “I wasn’t raised among Heathens. My ideas of civility were formed in a world where people mind their own business.”

“Perhaps,” Neville shot back, “the best kind of civility is precisely that. If you’ll set me an example, I’ll follow.”

“And yet,” Edwin replied, stopping abruptly, “you take on far more than your share. In the part of the world I come from, such behavior would get you called to account.”

“By whom?” Neville challenged.

Before Edwin could answer, a hand gripped his shoulder—a sudden, startling touch. Jasper stood between them, having followed them unseen from the shadowed side of the road.

“Ned, Ned, Ned!” he said, his voice soft but firm. “We must have no more of this. I don’t like it. I heard high words. Remember, my dear boy—you’re host tonight. You belong here, in a sense. You represent the place to a guest. Mr. Neville is a stranger. You must honor the obligations of hospitality.”

Then, placing his left hand gently on Neville’s shoulder, he stepped forward, walking between them, hands resting on each of their shoulders like a bridge.

“And you, Mr. Neville,” Jasper continued, “I ask you to govern your temper. Now, what is amiss? But why ask? Let there be nothing amiss. We are all three on good terms, are we not?”

A silent battle of wills passed between the two young men—each waiting for the other to speak first. Finally, Edwin broke in.

“As far as I’m concerned, Jack, there’s no anger.”

“Nor in me,” Neville said, though less freely, less confidently.

“But,” he added, “if Mr. Drood knew what lies behind me—what I’ve endured—far away from here—he might understand why sharp words wound me so deeply.”

Jasper nodded slowly. “Then let us not complicate our understanding. Let us not say anything that sounds like reproach. It wouldn’t be generous. Look—Ned has no anger. And you, Mr. Neville?”

“None at all, Mr. Jasper,” Neville said, though not quite with conviction.

“All right then,” Jasper declared. “My bachelor gatehouse is just a few yards ahead. The heater’s lit, wine and glasses are ready. Not far from Minor Canon Corner. Ned, you’re leaving tomorrow. Let’s carry Mr. Neville in with us for a stirrup-cup.”

“With all my heart, Jack,” Edwin said.

“And with all mine, Mr. Jasper,” Neville replied—though he felt reluctant. He sensed he’d lost control. Edwin’s calm only inflamed him further, making him burn hotter.

Jasper walked ahead, guiding them both, his presence steady and composed. He hummed a tune softly, turning the refrain of a drinking song into a gentle rhythm. Together, they moved toward his rooms.

Inside, the fire crackled. As Jasper lit a lamp, the room brightened—revealing, above the mantelpiece, a portrait. It was not a flattering likeness. Yet it was unmistakable: a painted image of a woman with dark eyes and a serene expression.

Both young men glanced at it—briefly, awkwardly—then looked away. But Jasper noticed.

“You recognize that picture, Mr. Neville?” he asked, dimming the lamp to highlight the painting.

“I do,” Neville said, “but it does the original no justice.”

“Oh, you’re too hard on it,” Jasper said. “It was painted by Ned. He gave it to me as a gift.”

“I’m sorry,” Neville said, genuinely. “If I’d known I was speaking to the artist—”

“A joke, sir,” Edwin interrupted, yawning with exaggerated indifference. “Just a bit of fun with Pussy’s features. One day, if she’s good, I’ll paint her seriously.”

He leaned back in his chair, hands clasped behind his head, radiating a careless confidence that grated on Neville’s nerves.

Jasper watched them closely, then turned to prepare mulled wine at the hearth. The process seemed endless—stirring, adding spices, pouring.

“I suppose,” Edwin said, sensing Neville’s irritation, “if you painted your lady love—”

“I can’t paint,” Neville interrupted sharply.

“That’s unfortunate,” Edwin said. “But if you could, I imagine you’d make her Juno, Minerva, Diana, Venus—all in one. Eh?”

“I have no lady love,” Neville said. “And I can’t say.”

“If I tried,” Edwin said, a spark of youthful arrogance flaring, “to paint Miss Landless—in earnest, mind you—I’d show you what I could do!”

“Only if she agreed to sit,” Neville countered. “And she never will. So I suppose I’ll never see your masterpiece. I’ll have to bear the loss.”

Jasper returned, filled large goblets—one for each man—and handed them over. Then he poured his own.

“Come, Mr. Neville,” he said. “Let’s toast my nephew, Ned. His foot is in the stirrup—metaphorically—so our stirrup-cup is for him. Ned, my dearest fellow, my love!”

He drained nearly half his glass. Neville followed. Edwin murmured, “Thank you both,” and emptied his.

“Look at him,” Jasper said, gesturing proudly. “See how he lounges, so easy, so at ease! The world is open before him. A life of work, excitement, change—domestic peace, love! See how little he cares for it all?”

Edwin’s face flushed with wine. So did Neville’s.

“He hardly seems to notice the golden fruit hanging ripe for him,” Jasper continued, teasing. “And yet—consider the contrast, Mr. Neville. You and I have no such prospects. No stirring work, no excitement, no domestic joy. Just the same dull round, day after day.”

“Upon my soul, Jack,” Edwin said, smugly, “I feel almost ashamed to have it so smooth. But you know what I know—Jack. It’s not as easy as it looks. May it, Pussy?” He snapped his fingers at the portrait. “We’ve got to hit it off yet, haven’t we, Pussy? You know what I mean, Jack.”

His speech slurred slightly. Jasper watched, calm and collected. When Neville spoke, his voice too was thick.

“It might have been better,” he said defiantly, “if Mr. Drood had known hardship.”

“Why?” Edwin asked, turning only his eyes.

“Because it might have made him more aware,” Neville said, “that his good fortune isn’t necessarily earned by his own merit.”

Jasper looked from one to the other, silently encouraging the exchange.

“Have *you* known hardship?” Edwin asked, sitting upright.

“Yes,” Neville said.

“And what have they taught you?”

“I told you earlier,” Neville insisted.

“You didn’t,” Edwin said.

“I did. That you take too much upon yourself.”

“And you added something else?”

“Yes. That in my part of the world, you’d be held accountable.”

“Only there?” Edwin laughed, contemptuous. “A long way off, I believe. Yes—safe distance.”

“Say here, then!” Neville cried, rising. “Say anywhere! Your vanity is unbearable. You talk as if you’re some rare gem, when you’re just a common boaster. You’re a common man, and a common braggart.”

“Pooh, pooh,” Edwin replied, furious but controlled. “How should you know? You may recognize a black common man—or a black common braggart—but you’re no judge of white ones.”

The insult struck like lightning. Neville’s rage exploded. He hurled the dregs of his wine at Edwin—then lifted the goblet to throw it too.

But Jasper caught his arm just in time.

“Ned, my dear fellow!” he shouted. “I beg you—be still!”

Chairs overturned. Glasses clattered. The room erupted.

“Mr. Neville, for shame!” Jasper commanded. “Give me that glass. Open your hand. I *will* have it!”

Neville shrugged him off, trembling with fury. For a moment, he stood frozen—goblet raised. Then, with a roar, he smashed it against the grate. Shards flew like rain.

He stormed out.

Outside, the world spun. Nothing was steady. The night air felt like blood—red, swirling, alive. He stood in the middle of chaos, breath ragged, heart pounding like a hammer.

But nothing came. The moon hung high, cold and indifferent, as if watching a corpse after a fit.

He clutched his head, staggered, then forced himself to move.

Half-conscious, he remembered the bolted door—the sound of it locking behind him. He was shut out, like a beast.

What now?

Thoughts of the river flashed through his mind—wild, desperate plans. But the moonlight on the Cathedral, the graves, the memory of his sister… and the pledge he’d given to the man who had won his trust—these pulled him back.

He turned toward Minor Canon Corner, knocked softly.

Mr. Crisparkle, accustomed to late-night piano practice, opened the door with candle in hand. His cheerful face fell.

“Mr. Neville! In this state! Where have you been?”

“To Mr. Jasper’s. With his nephew.”

“Come in.”

The Minor Canon supported him—scientifically, firmly—leading him into his bookroom, closing the door.

“I’ve begun dreadfully,” Neville said. “I’ve begun badly.”

“Too true,” Crisparkle replied. “You’re not sober.”

“I’m afraid not, sir. Though I’ve had very little to drink. It overwhelmed me suddenly.”

“Mr. Neville,” the canon said gently, “I’ve heard that before.”

“I think—my mind is confused—but I think the same applies to Mr. Jasper’s nephew.”

“Very likely,” Crisparkle said dryly.

“We quarreled, sir. He insulted me terribly. He stirred that tigerish blood I told you about—before even I realized it.”

“Mr. Neville,” Crisparkle said, shaking his head, “please don’t speak with that clenched hand. Unclench it.”

Neville obeyed instantly.

“He goaded me,” he said, “beyond endurance. I don’t know if he meant to at first—but he did. At the end, he certainly did. In short, sir,” he burst out, “in the passion he roused in me, I would have killed him—if I could. I tried to.”

“You’ve clenched it again,” Crisparkle observed quietly.

“I beg your pardon, sir.”

“You know your room. But I’ll accompany you once more. Your arm, please. Softly—the house is asleep.”

With practiced skill, Crisparkle guided him, supporting him like a seasoned expert, to his room.

There, Neville collapsed into a chair, arms thrown across the table, head resting upon them—face pale, full of shame.

Crisparkle hesitated, then turned back. He touched the doorframe gently.

“Good night.”

A sob was the only reply.

Another knock came. Crisparkle opened the door to find Jasper standing there, holding Neville’s hat.

“We’ve had an awful scene,” Jasper whispered.

“Has it been so bad?”

“Murderous.”

“Don’t use such strong words,” Crisparkle said.

“He might have laid my dear boy dead at my feet. It’s not his fault he didn’t. But had I not been swift and strong—through God’s mercy—he would have cut him down on my hearth.”

The words struck deep.

“Ah,” Crisparkle thought, “his own words.”

“Seeing what I’ve seen,” Jasper went on, “and hearing what I’ve heard—I shall never rest easy when those two meet without interference. It was terrible. There’s something of the tiger in his dark blood.”

“Ah,” Crisparkle thought again, “so he said.”

“You, my dear sir,” Jasper continued, taking his hand, “even you, have taken on a dangerous charge.”

“I need have no fear for myself, Jasper,” Crisparkle replied calmly. “I have none.”

“I have none for myself,” Jasper said, emphasizing the pronoun, “because I am not—nor ever will be—the object of his hostility. But you may be. And my dear boy has been.”

“Good night,” Crisparkle said.

He took the hat—now bearing an unspoken right to hang in his hall—and placed it carefully. Then, he went thoughtfully to bed.
