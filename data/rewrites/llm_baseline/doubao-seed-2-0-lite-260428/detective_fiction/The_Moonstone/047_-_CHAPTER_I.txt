I need barely a handful of words to finish the thread Ezra Jennings wove in his final journal entries—no grand flourishes, no unnecessary frills.

For my own part, I woke at dawn on the 26th with zero memory of anything I’d said or done under opium’s thrall. The blank stretched from the second the drug sank its teeth into me, all the way to the moment I blinked awake on the sofa in Rachel’s parlour. I won’t drag you through every small, soft moment after I opened my eyes. The only thing that matters is this: Rachel and I understood each other completely before a single word of explanation had crossed either of our lips. Neither of us can account for how fast that rift healed. If you’ve ever been young and madly in love, you already know exactly what happened after Jennings pulled the sitting-room door shut behind him.

I will say one thing, though: we’d have been caught red-handed by Mrs. Merridew if Rachel hadn’t kept her wits about her. She heard the rustle of the old lady’s silk dress in the corridor and darted out to intercept her before she could turn the handle. I heard Mrs. Merridew gasp, “What on earth is the matter?” and Rachel laugh back, “The explosion!” She steered the confused old lady straight out into the garden, far away from the supposed “science demonstration” Jennings had set up. By the time they wandered back in, I was loitering innocently in the hall, and Mrs. Merridew was gushing about how far modern science had come since her schooldays. “Explosions are infinitely milder now, Mr. Blake! I barely heard a sound from the garden, and there’s not a whiff of fumes anywhere. I must apologise to your medical friend—he managed it beautifully.”

Jennings had outwitted Betteredge, outwitted Mr. Bruff, and now he’d even won over staunchly conservative Mrs. Merridew. There’s far more hidden open-mindedness in the world than we usually give it credit for.

At breakfast, Bruff made no secret of why he wanted me to catch the morning train back to London with him. The watch he’d set on the bank where the Moonstone was stored, and the possibility that it would draw the culprits out, stoked Rachel’s curiosity so fiercely that she decided at once to come with us too—pending Mrs. Merridew’s approval, of course, so she’d be on hand the second we got any news. Mrs. Merridew, still charmed by the perfectly inoffensive “explosion”, gave her full blessing. We told Betteredge we’d all be travelling back together, and I fully expected him to beg to tag along. But Rachel had wisely kept her loyal old servant occupied: she’d put him in charge of finishing the full refurnishing of the house, and he was so wrapped up in his domestic responsibilities he didn’t feel the old detective fever burning in him like it would have otherwise.

Our only real regret leaving Yorkshire was having to say a far too abrupt goodbye to Ezra Jennings. There was no persuading him to come to London with us. I promised to write every other day, and Rachel made him swear to come stay with her the second she returned to the country. We all knew we’d likely see each other again in a few short months, but it still ached to watch our kindest, dearest friend standing all alone on the platform, shrinking smaller behind us as the train pulled away.

When we stepped off the terminus in London, a ragged little boy in threadbare black jacket and trousers was waiting for Bruff. His most striking feature was his eyes—they bulged so far out of his sockets, and rolled so loosely in his head, you found yourself nervously checking every few seconds to make sure they hadn’t fallen right out onto the pavement. After a quick word with the boy, Bruff turned to us and asked if Rachel and Mrs. Merridew would mind if he sent them on to Portland Place without us. I barely had time to promise Rachel I’d come by that night to tell her every last detail before Bruff grabbed my arm and hauled me into a waiting cab. The bulgy-eyed boy hopped up on the box next to the driver, and we headed straight for Lombard Street.

“News from the bank?” I leaned forward, breathless.

“News of Mr. Luker,” Bruff said. “An hour ago, my men spotted him leave his Lambeth house in a cab, flanked by two men they recognised as plainclothes police. If that’s a precaution against the three Indian conspirators, there’s only one conclusion to draw: he’s on his way to pull the Moonstone out of the bank vault right now.”

“So we’re heading there to catch him in the act?”

“Or to find out what he’s already done, if we’re too late. You noticed my boy up there on the box, right?”

“I noticed his eyes, for sure.”

Bruff laughed. “The clerks at the office call him Gooseberry. I keep him on errands, and he’s ten times more reliable than every one of those snickering clerks put together. Gooseberry’s one of the sharpest little lads in all of London, weird eyes and all.”

We pulled up outside the bank at twenty minutes to five. Gooseberry stared longingly at Bruff through the cab window as he opened the door. “Want to come in too?” Bruff asked kindly. “Stick right at my heels till I say otherwise.” He leaned in to whisper to me as we walked up the steps: “That boy’s as quick as lightning. You can tell him everything he needs to know in two words, where it’d take twenty to get the same point across to anyone else.”

The outer office was packed wall to wall, a long throng of people queueing at the counter to deposit or withdraw money before the bank locked its doors at five o’clock. The two operatives Bruff had posted there spotted him the second he walked in, and hurried over.

“Did you see Luker?” Bruff asked.

“Came past us half an hour ago, sir, went straight into the inner office. Hasn’t come out yet.”

Bruff nodded, and turned to me. “We wait.”

I scanned the crowd for the three Indian men, but there wasn’t a trace of them anywhere. The only person with a noticeably dark complexion was a tall, broad man in a pilot coat and round hat, who looked every inch the seasoned sailor. Could that be one of them in disguise? No way—he was inches taller than any of the three Indians, and his face, half-hidden behind a thick bushy black beard, was at least twice as wide.

“They’ve got to have a spy posted somewhere,” Bruff murmured, glancing at the sailor too. “That’s almost certainly him.”

Before he could say another word, a tiny hand tugged respectfully at the tail of his coat. It was Gooseberry, pointing wordlessly toward the far end of the lobby. “Hush,” Bruff said. “Here comes Luker.”

The money-lender stepped out of the inner office, followed closely by his two plainclothes police guards. “Keep your eyes glued to him,” Bruff whispered in my ear. “If he’s handing the Diamond off to anyone, he’s doing it right here in this crowd.”

Luker didn’t glance our way as he moved slowly toward the door, weaving through thick and thin patches of the queue. I saw his hand flick once, a quick tiny movement, as he passed a short, stout, perfectly ordinary man in a sober grey suit. The grey suit man flinched a little, and stared after him. Luker kept moving, slow as ever, till he reached the door, where his two guards flanked him on either side. One of Bruff’s operatives tailed the three of them out into the street, and I didn’t see them again.

I looked at Bruff, then nodded pointedly at the grey suit man. Bruff mouthed “I saw it too” back at me, then spun around looking for his second operative. The man was nowhere to be seen. Gooseberry was gone too.

“WHAT the hell is going on?” Bruff fumed. “Both of them vanish the exact second we need them most?”

The grey suit man reached the counter, deposited a cheque, collected his receipt, and turned to leave. “What do we do now?” Bruff groaned. “A respected lawyer in my position can’t very well chase a random stranger down the street like a common street detective.”

“I can!” I said. “I wouldn’t lose sight of that man for ten thousand pounds.”

“Fine,” Bruff huffed, already trailing after me. “And I wouldn’t lose sight of you for twice that. This is a ridiculous line of work for a man my age.” He was still grumbling when we hopped onto a westbound omnibus right behind the grey suit man. I swear to you, a faint pink flush rose up on Bruff’s cheeks as he dropped onto his seat—all that old, long-buried boyish mischief peeking out.

The grey suit man got off at Oxford Street. We followed, and he walked straight through the door of a chemist’s shop. Bruff stopped short. “That’s my chemist. Oh no. I think we messed up.”

We stepped inside. Bruff pulled the shop owner aside for a quiet two-minute chat, then came back to me looking utterly crestfallen. “Well, at least we’ve proven something,” he said, steering me back out onto the pavement.

“Proven what?”

“That we are the two worst amateur detectives who ever lived. That man in the grey suit has worked here for thirty years. He was at the bank to deposit a cheque for his boss. He’s never heard the name Moonstone in his life, and he knows less about that Diamond than a newborn baby.”

We decided to head back to Bruff’s chambers in Gray’s Inn Square. The second operative was already waiting for us there, red-faced and sheepish, after a quarter of an hour of waiting. “I messed up too, sir,” he admitted. “I swore on my life I saw Luker hand something to an old gent in a light paletot. Turns out he’s a perfectly respectable ironmonger from Eastcheap.”

“Where’s Gooseberry?” Bruff sighed.

The man stared blankly. “I haven’t seen hide nor hair of him since we left the bank, sir.”

Bruff dismissed him. “One of two things,” he said, turning to me. “Either Gooseberry ran off, or he’s chasing a lead all on his own. What do you say we dine here, wait a few hours and hope he turns up? I’ve got a decent bottle of claret in the cellar, and we can send out for chops from the coffee house down the street.”

We ate dinner in his chambers, and the cloth was barely cleared away when a visitor was announced. It wasn’t Gooseberry—it was the man we’d set to tail Luker himself. His report was totally uneventful: Luker had gone straight back to his Lambeth house, dismissed his two police guards, and hadn’t stepped foot outside again. By dusk he’d put up all the shutters and bolted every door. The street and the alley behind the house had been watched the whole time, no sign of the three Indians, no suspicious loiterers at all. Bruff sent him home for the night.

“Do you think Luker brought the Moonstone home with him?” I asked.

“Not a chance,” Bruff said. “He’d never have dismissed those two policemen if he was dumb enough to keep that Diamond in his own house.”

We waited another half hour for Gooseberry, but he never showed. It was time for Bruff to head to Hampstead, and for me to go see Rachel in Portland Place. I left my card with the porter at the chambers, with a scribbled note saying I’d be at my lodgings at half past ten that night, to hand to Gooseberry the second he turned up.

Some people are great at keeping appointments. I am not one of those people. Add to that the fact that I spent the whole evening on the same sofa as Rachel, in a drawing room forty feet long, with Mrs. Merridew stuck all the way at the far end of it. Is anyone really surprised I stumbled back to my lodgings at half past twelve instead of ten? Anyone who is must have no heart at all, and I hope I never meet them.

My servant handed me a crumpled scrap of paper the second I walked in. The neat, scrawled message read: “If you please, sir, I am getting sleepy. I will come back tomorrow morning, between nine and ten.” On questioning him, I found out that the bulgy-eyed little boy had turned up an hour earlier, waited for me, fallen asleep three separate times while he sat in the hall, scribbled that note, and left—after solemnly telling my servant he “wasn’t fit for anything if he didn’t get his full night’s rest.”

I was up and waiting for him by nine the next morning. At half past nine, I heard footsteps outside the door. “Come in, Gooseberry!” I called.

“Thank you, sir,” a deep, melancholy voice answered. The door swung open, and I jumped to my feet. Standing there was Sergeant Cuff.

“I thought I’d drop by here, Mr. Blake, on the off chance you were in town, before I wrote up to Yorkshire,” he said. He was just as lean, just as dreary as I remembered him. His eyes still had that old trick Betteredge had noticed, where they looked like they were waiting for you to admit something you didn’t even know you were hiding. But his appearance was unrecognisable: he wore a broad-brimmed white country hat, a light shooting jacket, white trousers, and drab gaiters, leaning on a thick oak walking stick. He looked every inch a retired country rose farmer, like he’d never set foot in London in his life. When I joked about his new look, he didn’t laugh—he complained gravely about the noise and the stench of London, and I swear he was talking with a faint rural accent. I offered him breakfast, and he looked properly shocked. “My breakfast hour is half past six. I turn in with the hens.”

“I got back from Ireland last night,” he said, cutting straight to the point in his usual unreadable way. “Before I went to bed I read your letter, laying out everything that’s happened since I suspended my investigation of the Diamond last year. I’ll be blunt: I completely messed my original case. I don’t know how any man could have seen the truth back then, in the mess we were all tangled up in, but that doesn’t change the facts. I made a fool of myself. It’s far from the first mistake of my career. Detective officers aren’t perfect—only in books are they never wrong.”

“You’re here just in time to fix it, and save your reputation,” I said.

“Beg pardon, Mr. Blake,” he said, shaking his head. “Now I’m retired I don’t care a straw about my reputation. I’m done with that, thank God. I’m here out of gratitude to the late Lady Verinder’s kindness to me. I’ll go back to work on this case for you, if you want me and trust me, and not a single farthing of money will change hands. This is purely on honour.” He leaned forward. “Now tell me exactly how things stand since you last wrote to me.”

I walked him through Jennings’ opium experiment, every detail of the chaos at the bank yesterday. He was fascinated by the opium test, something entirely new in all his years of detective work, and particularly curious about Jennings’ theory that I’d hidden the Diamond somewhere that birthday night.

“I don’t agree with Mr. Jennings that you hid the Moonstone,” Sergeant Cuff said. “But I do agree that you definitely carried it back to your own room that night, no question.”

“So then what happened after that?” I pressed.

“You’ve no hunch at all, sir?”

“None.”

“Mr. Bruff hasn’t a clue either?”

“Not a single one.”

Sergeant Cuff stood up, walked over to my writing table, and came back with a sealed envelope. It was marked *Private*, addressed to me, with his signature scrawled in the bottom corner. “I suspected the wrong man last year,” he said. “I might be wrong again now. Don’t open this envelope, Mr. Blake, till we’ve uncovered the full truth. Then compare the name of the person we find guilty to the name I’ve written inside.”

I tucked the envelope in my pocket, then asked what he thought of the mess Bruff and I had made at the bank.

“All very well intended, exactly the right steps to take,” he said. “But you missed one very important person you should have been watching, besides Mr. Luker.”

“The person named in that envelope you just gave me?”

“Exactly, Mr. Blake. There’s no help for it now, but I’ve got a plan to propose to you and Bruff when the time is right. First, let’s wait and see if that boy Gooseberry has anything useful to tell us.”

It was nearly ten o’clock, and the boy still hadn’t turned up. Cuff made small talk, asked after his old friend Betteredge and his old enemy the grumpy gardener, and was just about to veer off onto his favourite subject of roses when my servant knocked and announced the boy was waiting downstairs.

Gooseberry stepped over the threshold, stopped dead, and stared suspiciously at the stranger in the room with me. “It’s alright,” I told him. “You can speak freely in front of this gentleman. He’s here to help me, and he knows every part of the case. Sergeant Cuff,” I said, turning to the detective, “this is the boy from Mr. Bruff’s office.”

Fame, no matter what kind, moves mountains. The name of Sergeant Cuff, the most famous detective in the country, had even reached the ears of little Gooseberry. His bulgy eyes rolled so far in their sockets I was half convinced they’d bounce right onto my carpet.

“Come here, lad,” Cuff said, patting his knee. “Let’s hear what you’ve got to tell us.”

Gooseberry was starstruck. He walked right over, stuck his hands behind his back, and stood at attention like a new recruit taking catechism.

“What’s your name?” Cuff started, all formal gravity.

“Octavius Guy, sir. They call me Gooseberry at the office, ‘cause of my eyes.”

“Octavius Guy, otherwise known as Gooseberry,” Cuff went on, “you vanished from the bank yesterday. What were you doing?”

“If you please, sir, I was following a man.”
