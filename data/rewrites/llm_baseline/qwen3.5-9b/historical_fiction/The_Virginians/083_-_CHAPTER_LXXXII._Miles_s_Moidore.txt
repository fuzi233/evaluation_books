Little Miles arrived in this world mere days after the birth of the gracious Prince who now commands a regiment. Great illuminations lit the sky and cannons roared in salute to the Royal George's arrival; multitudes flocked to gaze upon him as he lay behind a gilt railing at the Palace, watched over by noble nurses. In stark contrast, few guards stood by the cradle of our little Prince. No courtiers, no faithful retainers paid homage to it, save for our trusty Gumbo and kind Molly, who loved and admired the little heir of my poverty with a loyalty that matched the deepest desires of our hearts.

Why was our boy not named George, like the other paragon just mentioned, and like his father? Instead, I gave him the name of a little scapegrace from my family—a name borne by many generations of Warringtons alike. Yet, my poor little Miles's love and kindness touched me profoundly at a time when affection from my own blood was scarce. Theo and I agreed then that our child should be named after that single, bright little friend of my paternal race.

We wrote to inform our royal parents of the auspicious event, and boldly inserted the child's birth notice in the *Daily Advertiser*, specifying the location as Church Street, Lambeth, where he was born.

"My dear," Aunt Bernstein replied in her letter to my announcement, "how could you point out to all the world that you live in such a trough as that in which you have buried yourself? I kiss the little mamma, and send a remembrance for the child."

This remembrance was a fine silk coverlet edged with lace fit for a prince. It was hardly practical—the cost of the lace alone would have served us far better—but Theo and Molly were utterly delighted. Consequently, my eldest son's cradle boasted a cover as fine as any nobleman's.

Good Dr. Heberden visited my wife several times to ensure everything proceeded smoothly. He recommended a local surgeon who took charge of her case; fortunately, my dear patient required little care beyond what our landlady and her trusted attendant could readily provide.

Soon, our humble precinct was once again adorned with the gilded spectacle of Lady Castlewood's carriage wheels. She arrived bearing a pot of jelly, which she believed Theo might enjoy—no doubt a delicacy previously served at one of her ladyship's grand banquets. She regaled us with tales of the court ceremonies and the splendor surrounding the birth of the august heir to the crown.

Our good Mr. Johnson happened to drop by on one of those days when Lady Countess's carriage blazed up to our little gate. He was visibly struck by her magnificence and offered her some bows that were more respectful than graceful. She addressed me as "cousin" with great affability and assisted in transferring the gift of jelly from her silver dish into our crockery pan with much benignity.

Dr. Heberden tasted the sweetmeat and pronounced it excellent.

"The great, sir," he remarked, "are fortunate in every way. They can engage the most skilled practitioners of the culinary arts and assemble the most amiable wits around their tables. If, as you suggest—and judging by the dish, your hypothesis is plausible—this sweetmeat has already graced His Lordship's table, it has done so in good company. It has quivered beneath the eyes of celebrated beauties, been tasted by ruby lips, and shared the attention of a distinguished assembly amidst fruits, tarts, and creams, which I make no doubt were equally delicious."

Thus speaking, the good doctor consumed a considerable portion of Lady Castlewood's benefaction. However, regarding the epithet "delicious," I must confess that my poor wife, after sampling the jelly, set it aside as unpalatable. Molly, throwing up her head, declared flatly that it was moldy.

At least, my boy enjoyed the privilege of having an Earl's daughter for his godmother. This honor was bestowed by his cousin, our poor Lady Maria, whose kindness and attention to both mother and infant were beyond praise. Having lost her own solitary chance for maternal happiness, she yearned over our child in a manner deeply moving to behold.

Captain Miles is indeed a magnificent gentleman, and his uniforms in the Prince's Hussars are as splendid as any worn by a soldier of fashion. Yet, we trust his heart is too good, and his nature too truly genteel, for him not to feel gratitude when he recalls that his own infant limbs were once dressed in some of the small garments prepared for the poor player's child.

Sampson christened him in the very chapel in Southwark where our marriage ceremony had taken place. Never were the words of the Prayer Book read more beautifully or impressively than by the celebrant of that service. There was an exception, however, at the conclusion, when his voice failed him, and he, along with the rest of the tiny congregation, found themselves wiping away tears.

"Mr. Garrick himself, sir," said Hagan afterward, "could not have read those words so nobly. I am certain no innocent soul ever entered the world accompanied by wishes and benedictions more tender and sincere."

Now, I have not yet explained how the Captain acquired his name of Miles. A couple of days prior to the christening, when it was still intended that our firstborn should bear his father's name, a light patter of horsehooves galloped up to our gate. Who should pull the bell but young Miles, our cousin? I feared he had disobeyed his parents on that undutiful journey.

"You know," he exclaimed breathlessly, "Cousin Harry gave me my little horse, and I simply cannot help liking you because you are so like Harry. Besides, everyone at home keeps talking about you, and it's a shame! So, I've brought my whistle and the coral beads my godmamma, Lady Suckling, gave me for your little boy. And since you're so poor, Cousin George, here is my gold moidore. It's worth ever so much, and it's no use to me anyway, because I'm not allowed to spend it, you know."

We lifted the boy up to Theo in her room (he climbed the stairs proudly in his little tramping boots, of which he was very fond). Theo kissed him warmly and thanked him; and that moidore has remained in her purse from that day forward.

Later, my mother, writing through her usual ambassador, informed me of her royal surprise and displeasure upon learning that my son had been christened Miles—a name unknown, at least, in the Esmond family. I chose not to explain the reason at the time. But when, in later years, I recounted to Madam Esmond how my boy received his name, I saw a tear roll down her wrinkled cheek. I learned subsequently that she had asked Gumbo countless questions about the boy who gave his name to our Miles—our *Miles Gloriosus* of Pall Mall, Valenciennes, Almack's, and Brighton.
