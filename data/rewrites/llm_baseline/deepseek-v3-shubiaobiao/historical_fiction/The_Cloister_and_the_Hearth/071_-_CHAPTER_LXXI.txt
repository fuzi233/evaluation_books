In the humble home of a tailor in Rotterdam, a great man came quietly into the world this year—unnoticed, unheralded. Yet, at the same time, a mighty figure fell with thunderous ceremony. Philip, Duke of Burgundy, Earl of Holland, and lord of many lands, lay stricken in Bruges.

For the poor, sickness was a matter of nature’s whim—they suffered, they recovered, or they died, unseen. But for the wealthy, illness was a battleground where the physicians waged war, and often, the cure proved deadlier than the disease. The Duke’s ailment, unnamed then but known now as diphtheria, left him weak, his age compounding the danger. And so, Dr. Remedy, in his wisdom, drew blood.

The Duke grew colder. A dire sign.

Science, in its desperation, prescribed a remedy from the arcane: *“Flay an ape at once and press its flesh to his breast!”*

Officers of the court scattered in frantic search, but no ape could be found. Buffaloes, lizards, Turks, leopards—every beast but the one needed. *“There was an ape here before,”* muttered one. *“I could swear I saw it.”* Indeed, there had been—until the mastiff, enforcing mankind’s creed of *“Be of your time!”* had torn the creature apart for stealing its supper.

The seneschal’s desperate gaze fell upon an unlikely substitute. *“Here—this might serve. Is it not apelike enough, save for its clothes?”*

The chancellor scowled. *“The Princess Marie would have our heads. She dotes on this one.”*

The *“this”* in question was none other than our friend Giles, strutting obliviously in his cloth of gold.

With no ape to be had, Dr. Remedy turned to the next best thing. *“A dog, then—but it must be of one solid color.”*

A liver-colored hound was flayed, its still-warm hide pressed to the Duke’s chest.

And so, Philip the Good perished, slain by his physicians.

He left behind thirty-one children—only one legitimate, the rest scattered like leaves. Nineteen were provided for; the rest fended for themselves. The chronicles traced his noble blood back to the kings of Troy, through Thierry of Aquitaine, Chilperic, and Pharamond. But in truth, such lineage was no rarity—every prince of the age claimed descent from Troy. *“Priam”* was but the medieval word for *“Adam.”*

The Duke’s body was borne to Burgundy, laid to rest in a grand mausoleum of black marble in Dijon. Holland echoed with the news of his passing, unaware that within her borders, in that same year, a far greater light had been kindled—one that would outshine dukes and kings.

For who now remembers the house where Philip the Good died? Yet men still gaze upon the tailor’s dwelling in Rotterdam, where the true marvel of the age was born.

Dukes come and go, leaving the world unchanged. But once in centuries, a child enters the world like a torch lit by divine fire—and *“Let there be light!”* resounds across the earth.
