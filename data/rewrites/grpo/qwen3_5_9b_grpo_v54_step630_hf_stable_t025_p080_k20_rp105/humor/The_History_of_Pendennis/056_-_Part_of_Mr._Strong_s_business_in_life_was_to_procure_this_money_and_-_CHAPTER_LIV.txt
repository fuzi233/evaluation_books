The duty of the chronicler now falls to a rather embarrassing admission. We must record a fact concerning Pendennis that, while shameful and disgraceful for the chief protagonist and godfather of a novel, must nonetheless be laid bare for the public reading his veritable memoirs. After succumbing to a fever, suffering the torments of love, and enduring the full regimen of the doctor—bleeding, blistering, shaving, and medicating—Pendennis rallied from his physical ailment. With it came a miraculous cure for his mental malady. He was no longer in love with Fanny Bolton. He was as indifferent to her as any wise or moral observer who knows better than to let their hearts wander after porters' daughters.

Lying on his pillow, Pendennis laughed at himself, marveling at this second cure. He couldn't fathom how he had ever cared for her. He performed a post-mortem on his dead passion, dissecting his defunct sensations for the benefit of his poor little nurse. What had made him so hot and eager weeks prior? Not her wit, breeding, or beauty; there were hundreds of women far superior. The passion had simply evaporated from within him; it never truly resided in her. She remained unchanged, but the eyes that saw her had shifted. Alas, that was the tragedy. He felt a friendly disposition toward the "little thing," but the violent personal regard of weeks past had fled, vanquished by the pill and lancet that had cured his body. It was a source of immense comfort and gratitude to Pendennis—that he had resisted temptation when the danger was greatest—and he felt no reproach for his conduct. Yet, as he reviewed the Fanny Bolton snare from which he had escaped, he wasn't entirely sure he wasn't ashamed of the satisfaction he felt. It is pleasant, perhaps, but humiliating to admit that you love no more.

Meanwhile, the kind smiles and tender watchfulness of his mother at his bedside filled the young man with peace. Seeing health return was all the unwearied nurse demanded; executing his caprices was her chief joy. He felt enveloped by her love, grateful almost as he had been in childhood when weak and helpless.

There were some misty notions in Pen's mind regarding the first part of his illness and Fanny's role as nurse, but they were so dim he couldn't distinguish them from fever-delirium hallucinations. Consequently, just as he had avoided mentioning Fanny to his mother before, he could not now confide in her. It was an unlucky lack of confidence on both sides; a few words in time might have spared the good lady and her circle much pain.

Seeing Miss Bolton installed as nurse, Mrs. Pendennis had put the worst possible construction on the intimacy. She settled firmly in her mind that the accusations against Arthur were true. Why hadn't she stopped to inquire? Stories to a man's disadvantage are always most eagerly believed by the women who love him most. Isn't a wife often the first to be jealous? Poor Pen received a stock of this suspicious, twisted love from his nurse. The kind, pure creature believed her boy had suffered a malady far more awful and debasing than mere physical fever; she thought he was stained by crime as well as weakened by illness. She bore this consciousness silently, masking her doubt and despair with a cheerful facade.

At Boulogne, Captain Shandon, reading the *Pall Mall Gazette*, remarked to Mrs. Shandon that Jack Finucane's hand was no longer visible in the leading articles and that Mr. Warrington must be at work again. "I know the crack of his whip," Shandon observed, using a dreadful metaphor about a butcher mangling a subject. "Mr. Warrington finishes a man, laying his cuts neat and regular, straight down the back, drawing blood every line." Mrs. Shandon protested, claiming Mr. Warrington was a kind gentleman to the children. Shandon agreed he was kind to the children but savage to the men, advising his wife not to understand newspaper politics, noting the comfort of Boulogne where wine was plentiful and brandy cost only two francs a bottle.

In a word, Warrington went to work with all his might in place of his prostrate friend, doing Pen's portion of the *Gazette* "with a vengeance." He wrote articles, attended theaters, and discoursed on them with savage energy. His hand was too strong for such small subjects, yet he delighted in telling Arthur's mother, uncle, and Laura that no hand in the land was more graceful or elegant than Arthur's. "The people in this country don't understand style," he told Mrs. Pendennis. "I call him ours, for I bred him. Bating a little wilfulness and dandification, I don't know a more honest or loyal creature. His pen is wicked sometimes, but he is as kind as a young lady."

Helen sighed deeply, and Laura was sadly wounded, yet both were thankful for Warrington's good opinion of Arthur and loved him for his attachment to their Pen. Major Pendennis was loud in his praises of Mr. Warrington, calling him a gentleman of the highest caliber, a descendant of the Suffolk Warringtons and friends of the Prince of Wales. Laura blushed as the Major praised Arthur's hero, speculating internally that Warrington must be a victim of an unhappy attachment.

Warrington secured chambers near Grenier's in Flag Court. After executing Pen's tasks in the morning, his afternoon delight was sitting with the sick man's company in sunny autumn evenings. He frequently gave Miss Bell his arm for walks in the Temple Gardens. When the frank Laura asked Helen's permission, the Major eagerly approved, comparing the gardens to the countryside and assuring her of the safety of the beadles. If the great arbiter of morals didn't object, why should simple Helen? She was glad her girl had fresh air and saw her return with heightened color and spirits.

Laura and Helen had reached a little explanation. When news of Pen's alarming illness arrived, Laura insisted on accompanying the terrified mother to London. When Helen initially refused, and then refused again more sternly as the lad's life seemed despaired of and his conduct rendered union hopeless, Laura, with many tears, confessed her secret love. She could not marry him, but she was denied the consolation of owning how fondly she had loved him. Their mingled tears appeased the agony of their grief, and the sorrows of their journey were mitigated by sharing them.

What could Fanny expect when suddenly brought before such judges? Swift condemnation, awful punishment, merciless dismissal! Women are cruel critics in such cases; they have their own friends to keep watch and tear apart any who stray. The narrator, a Turk at heart wearing a turban and beard, protests he is all for the sack practice, but urges caution. Be very sure of the fact before ordering the barge out; don't pop your subject into the Bosphorus until certain she deserves it. If she is guilty, down with her, but be sure.

The Major did not object to Warrington's promenades with Miss Laura; indeed, he encouraged the intimacy. Were there exhibitions? He was for Warrington conducting her. If Warrington proposed Vauxhall, the complaisant Major would see no harm. Between two persons of spotless honor—Warrington, seeing in intimacy a pure, high-minded woman for the first time, and Laura, thrown into the society of a gentleman of great natural parts—their interaction was harmless. In Warrington's uncouthness lay a refinement that Pen's finery lacked. In his energy, respect, and hearty laughter, there was a difference to Sultan Pen's yawning sovereignty. What had made Pen a dandy and despot? The women had spoiled him, cloying him with obedience until he grew weary of the slaves waiting upon him. Abroad, he was brisk and impassioned, but here he was languid.

How came it that Laura, despite Pen's indifference, held such a rapturous regard for him? As soon as Helen finished one sobbing story about the dear fellow, Laura began another, equally ornamented with tears. She recounted how heroically he had a tooth pulled, how daringly he robbed a bird's nest, how magnanimously he spared it, how he gave a shilling to an old woman, or went without bread for a beggar-boy. One to another, the sobbing women sang laments upon their hero, who was no more a hero than any of us. Why should a sensible girl be so fond? Because she saw scarcely anyone else at Fairoaks except Doctor Portman and Captain Glanders, because his mother constantly praised Arthur, because he was gentlemanlike and witty, and because, above all, it was of her nature to like somebody. Having received this image into her heart, she tenderly nursed it, brooding over it in her solitudes. When she came to London and became intimate with Mr. George Warrington, what prevented her from thinking him odd, original, agreeable, and pleasing?

Long afterwards, when Fate had disposed of the persons assembled in the dingy building in Lamb Court, perhaps they looked back on how happy the time was. The Major developed a favorable opinion of September in London, declaring the dead season pleasant. He returned home to his lodgings in Bury Street wondering how quickly the evening had passed. He appeared at the Temple constantly, tugging up the long black staircase with benevolent activity. He made interest with the chef at Bays's to prepare jellies, clear soups, and aspics for the invalid. With Doctor Goodenough's permission for Pen to drink sherry, the Major declared that the Marquis of Steyne, passing through London, had ordered any quantity of his precious Amontillado—a gift from King Ferdinand—to be placed at Arthur's disposal. The widow and Laura tasted it with respect, though disliking the bitter flavor, while the invalid was greatly invigorated. Warrington pronounced it superlatively good and proposed the Major's health in a mock speech.

Major Pendennis returned thanks with utmost gravity, using the phrase "the present occasion" repeatedly. Pen cheered with his feeble voice. Warrington taught Miss Laura to cry "Hear! hear!" and tapped the table with his knuckles. Pidgeon grinned, and Doctor Goodenough found the party merrily engaged.

Warrington knew Sibwright, who lived below. In reply to a letter informing him of the use of his apartment, Sibwright wrote a polite, flowery letter of acquiescence, placing his chambers, bed, and carpets at their disposal. Everybody was kindly disposed toward the sick man and his family. Pen's heart melted at the thought of such good-nature.

There was a piano in Mr. Sibwright's chamber, and at this "music-box," as Warrington called it, Laura, trembling and blushing, played and sang simple airs and old songs of home. Her voice was a rich contralto. Warrington, who knew but one tune—a discordant imitation of 'God save the King'—sat rapt in delight, watching with growing enthusiasm the pure and tender creature who made the music.

One wonders how the poor pale little girl in the black bonnet, who stood at the lamp-post in Lamb Court listening to the music, liked to hear it? When Pen's bedtime came, the songs were hushed. Lights appeared in the upper room; the widow conducted him there. Then the Major, Mr. Warrington, and sometimes Miss Laura would play ecarte or backgammon, or she would work a pair of worsted slippers—one of the three men present would have given anything for them.

While this went on, a shabby old gentleman would lead away the pale girl in the black bonnet, who had no right to be abroad at night. The temple porters and amateur listeners disappeared. Just before ten o'clock, the chimes of St. Clement's played a cheerful psalm before ringing the ten fatal strokes. As they rang, Laura folded the slippers. Martha from Fairoaks appeared with a bed-candle and a constant smile. The Major exclaimed, "God bless my soul, is it so late?" Warrington and he left their unfinished game, shaking hands with Miss Bell. Martha lighted them out, and as they descended, they heard her bolting and locking "the sporting door" behind them. If there had been danger, grinning Martha said she would have fetched the Damascus scimitar with the names of the prophet engraved on the blade, which Percy Sibwright brought back from the Levant. It had once entangled Miss Kewsey's train at Lady Mullingar's ball. Good night, Laura and Fairoaks Martha. Sleep well and wake happy.

Sometimes after these evenings, Warrington walked a little way with Major Pendennis—just as far as the Temple gate, the Strand, Charing Cross, or the Club. They talked about Laura all the way. It was wonderful how the Major, who used to dislike her, had grown enthusiastic—"Dev'lish fine girl, begad. Dev'lish well-mannered girl." He noted her natural blush and bouquet of hawthorn, remarking that London girls would give many a guinea for such flowers. In all these opinions, Warrington agreed. Though he laughed as he shook hands with the Major, his face fell as he left his veteran companion. He strode back to chambers, smoked pipe after pipe long into the night, and wrote articles more savage than ever in lieu of his disabled friend.

It was a happy time for almost all parties. Pen mended daily. Sleeping and eating were his constant occupations. His appetite was frightful; he was ashamed to exhibit it before Laura, and his mother laughed and applauded him. As the roast chicken of his dinner went away, he eyed the departing food with sad longing, beginning to crave jelly or tea. He was like an ogre devouring. The Doctor cried stop, but Pen would not. Nature called out louder than the physician, who handed him over with good grace.

Here, let us speak tenderly of an event he never liked to allude to. During his delirium, the ruthless Goodenough ordered ice to his head and all his lovely hair to be cut. It was done in the presence of the other nurse, who left every single hair in a paper for the widow to count. She never believed she hadn't taken some away, for women are suspicious on such matters.
