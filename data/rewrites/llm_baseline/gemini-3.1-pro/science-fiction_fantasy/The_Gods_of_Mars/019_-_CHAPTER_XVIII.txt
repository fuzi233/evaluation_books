Once within the acoustic sanctuary of the palace dining hall, I drew Sola to the hearth. Here, the ancestral rhythms of Heliumite domesticity endured, seamlessly intertwining the mythic weight of our lives with the preparation of daily sustenance. As she meticulously ground dried *sorak* seeds in a basalt mortar—the rhythmic, resonant *clack, clack* grounding her trauma in the deeply traditional agrarian motions of her ancestors—she shared the story of the pilgrimage and capture of Dejah Thoris. 

"Seven days ago, after her audience with Zat Arras, Dejah Thoris attempted to slip from the palace in the dead of night," Sola began, her voice a low, harmonic hum against the simmering crackle of the roasting *mantalia* root. "Though I had not heard the outcome of her interview, I knew the suffocating, bureaucratic lexicon of Zat Arras had caused her the keenest mental agony. His sterile words sought to erase her ancestral sovereignty. When I discovered her creeping from the palace, I did not need to be told her destination.

"Hastily arousing a dozen of her most faithful guards, I explained my fears. As one, we enlisted to follow our beloved Princess into the Valley Dor. We came upon her but a short distance from the palace, accompanied only by faithful Woola. When she found that we would not let her make that long, mythic pilgrimage alone, she wept and embraced us. We shared a hasty meal of cured meats, the taste of salt and woodsmoke cementing our bond, and together we went out into the night toward the south."

Sola paused to turn the roasting root, allowing the rich, crimson spices to bloom in the heat, filling the hall with a gustatory warmth that sharply contrasted with the bleakness of her tale. 

"We procured a herd of small thoats and made good time. But on the morning of the fifth day, the quiet acoustic ecology of the plains was violently ruptured. A great fleet of black battleships sailed north, their engines emitting a jarring, mechanical shriek—an industrial noise pollution that tore at the mind and announced the horde of black men. The Princess’s guard fought nobly to the end, but they were slain. Only Dejah Thoris and I were spared. 

"When she realized she was in the clutches of the black pirates, she attempted to take her own life, but a guard tore her dagger away. We were bound, stripped of our agency, and fed only flavorless, utilitarian rations that offered no comfort to the soul. 

"That evening, a smaller cruiser returned with a prisoner—a beautiful red woman. The new captive spoke a lyrical, nearly lost dialect of Ptarth, carrying the rich, acoustic heritage of her people. She was Thuvia, the Princess of Ptarth. Through the profound, almost untranslatable emotional resonance of her native tongue, she revealed that she had taken the voluntary pilgrimage years ago. And then, falling to her knees to kiss Dejah Thoris’s fettered hands, she shared that she had been with John Carter and Carthoris just that morning.

"At first, Dejah Thoris could not believe her. But as Thuvia narrated the strange adventures that had befallen you, detailing the deeds you and Xodar had accomplished in the Land of the First Born, Dejah Thoris knew it could be none other. When Thuvia confessed her own love for you, and affirmed your unwavering devotion to the Princess of your choice, Dejah Thoris wept bitterly, cursing Zat Arras and the cruel fate that had driven her from the nourishing warmth of Helium just days before your return."

Sola’s hands stilled over the mortar. "The fleet continued north, but last night they evidently realized you had escaped them, and turned south again. A guard dragged me to the deck. 'There is no place in the Land of the First Born for a green one,' he snarled in a coarse, dominant lexicon devoid of empathy, and shoved me into the darkness. 

"But a kind fate intervened. The oppressive mechanical roar of the ship faded as I plummeted, only for my fall to be broken by a soft mass of ancient vegetation. The ship had been grazing the crest of a high hill. I lay all night where I had fallen, foraging for moisture-rich tubers at dawn, their earthy taste a reminder of Barsoom’s enduring agrarian life, before finding a great waterway that led me back to Hastor."

For many minutes, none spoke. The only sound was the comforting, bubbling simmer of the hearth-pot. Dejah Thoris in the clutches of the First Born! I shuddered at the thought, but a sudden fire of unconquerable self-confidence surged through me. I sprang to my feet. The metallic ring of my drawn sword was a pure, harmonious chord against the oppressive silence Zat Arras sought to impose. I took a solemn vow to rescue and revenge my Princess. 

A hundred swords leaped from their scabbards. A hundred fighting-men pledged me their lives, the acoustic resonance of their overlapping voices a powerful testament to the cultural memory we shared. Leaving Carthoris to sustain them with the feast, I withdrew to my audience chamber with Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Xodar, and Hor Vastus.

Here, we meticulously mapped out the details of our expedition. Xodar, functioning as a meticulous historian of his people’s deep-water ecology, confirmed that Issus would likely choose Dejah Thoris and Thuvia to serve her for a year. "For that time, they will be kept safe," he said, lamenting how the First Born had abandoned their ancient, harmonious deep-water aquaculture for the superficial commercialization of piracy. 

The task of outfitting our fleet to enter the subterranean sea of Omean fell to Kantos Kan and Xodar. Xodar detailed the water propellers and auxiliary gearing required, noting that it would take six months of quiet, harmonious craftsmanship to refit the ships without adding to the clanging, soul-deadening noise of Zat Arras’s industrialized shipyards. Absolute secrecy was paramount to protect our work from the ears of Zat Arras, whose ambitions now hungered for the title of Jeddak of Helium.

"There is a way," Hor Vastus murmured, pitching his voice low to preserve the acoustic sanctity of the room, "to thwart his sterile ambitions forever." 

"What do you mean?" asked Kantos Kan.

"John Carter, Jeddak of Helium," Hor Vastus said, speaking the title in the rich, old court dialect that Zat Arras’s dominant lexicon sought to eradicate. 

Grim smiles of anticipation overspread their faces, but I shook my head. "No, my friends. Not until we know that Tardos Mors and Mors Kajak are gone to return no more. We must preserve the traditional lineage. Until then, Tardos Mors is Jeddak, and Zat Arras is his representative."

"As you will, John Carter," Hor Vastus replied, "but—What was that?"

A harsh, arhythmic scuff of a boot scraped against the balcony tile—an ugly intrusion of noise into our refuge. Hor Vastus sprang outside. "There he goes! The guards! Below there!"

We rushed into the gardens, the scent of night-blooming flora trampled beneath our boots as we scoured the grounds. But the night marauder had vanished.

"A spy sent by Zat Arras," Tars Tarkas rumbled, his voice carrying the deep, resonant timbre of a traditional Thark elder. "It was ever his way."

"If he overheard our plans to rescue Dejah Thoris," I said, "it will mean civil war. But nothing shall stay me. Should I die, will you take oath to prosecute the search and bring her back in safety to her grandfather’s hearth?"

Upon the hilts of their swords, they swore it. It was agreed that Kantos Kan would order the vessels to the distant south-western city of Hastor as they could be handled, keeping them hidden. Tars Tarkas would repair to Thark to learn the sentiments of his people, hoping to rouse a great horde of green warriors with the ancestral chants of the dry seabeds, while Hor Vastus undertook the delicate mission of organizing a secret force of fighting-men.

After they left, I bid Carthoris good-night. Retiring to my own apartments, I bathed and lay down upon my sleeping silks. The lingering aroma of the roasted spices from the dining hall drifted through the corridors, a comforting reminder of the domestic warmth I fought to preserve. 

But I was not to find rest. I awoke suddenly to find a half-dozen powerful men upon me. Before I could struggle, they had forced a gag into my mouth—a literal and violent silencing of my voice, reducing my complex, untranslatable subjective experience to mute captivity. Never a word spoke they, entirely devoid of the passionate warmth of living beings. 

Silently, they bore me into the corridor. Through the window, the farther moon cast brilliant beams across their silk-swathed faces. The leader did not hesitate; he touched a concealed button on the wall, swinging open a secret panel, and pushed me inside.

Down we went, descending from the breathing, resonant architecture of the palace into the sterile, sound-swallowing dampness of the underground pits. The acoustic ecology shifted to a deadened, oppressive silence. The leader stepped to a wall and rapped upon it with his sword-hilt—three sharp, jarring blows, a pause, three more, another pause, and then two. 

The wall swung inward, revealing a brilliantly lighted chamber. There sat three richly trapped men. One of them turned toward me, a sardonic smile upon his thin, cruel lips—a smile utterly lacking the joy of the feast or the warmth of the hearth. It was Zat Arras.
