XV. Ordeal and Execution

As the General spoke, a figure emerged from the chapel door—the same one through which Carmilla had once entered and vanished. He was unlike any man I had ever seen: tall and thin, with narrow shoulders that hunched forward, his chest sunken beneath a black coat that seemed too large for him. His face was deeply lined, weathered brown as if scorched by time, and crowned with a peculiar hat shaped like a broad leaf. Long, grizzled hair fell over his shoulders, and he wore gold-rimmed spectacles perched precariously on his nose. His movements were slow and awkward—shuffling, almost dreamlike—as if his body did not quite obey his thoughts. At times he gazed upward into the sky; at others, he bent low, eyes fixed on the ground. A faint, constant smile played upon his lips, though it never reached his eyes. His arms swung loosely at his sides, and his hands—clad in oversized black gloves—flailed about in gestures that seemed entirely disconnected from what he said.

“The very man!” exclaimed the General, stepping forward with unmistakable joy. “My dear Baron, how delighted I am to see you! I never imagined I’d meet you so soon.”

He beckoned to my father, who had just returned, and introduced the strange old gentleman—whom he called the Baron—to him. They exchanged formal greetings, then immediately plunged into conversation. The Baron pulled a rolled paper from his pocket and unrolled it across the worn stone of a nearby tomb. In his hand was a pencil case, and with deliberate strokes, he marked lines upon the parchment. As he glanced between the drawing and various points of the chapel, I realized it was a plan of the building. From time to time, he read aloud from a small, filthy book whose yellowed pages were covered in dense handwriting.

They walked down the side aisle, opposite where I stood, talking intently. Then they began measuring distances with their steps. Finally, they stopped before a section of the wall, peeling back ivy and tapping the plaster with sticks. They scraped, probed, knocked—until, at last, they uncovered a broad marble slab embedded beneath layers of decay. With the help of the woodman, who returned shortly after, they cleared away the debris and revealed an inscription and carved coat of arms.

It was the long-lost monument of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein.

The General, though not one given to piety, lifted his hands and eyes toward heaven, silent but clearly moved. For a moment, he stood in reverent stillness.

“Tomorrow,” I heard him say, “the commissioner will arrive, and the Inquisition shall proceed as the law demands.”

Then turning to the Baron, he gripped both his hands tightly.

“Baron, how can I thank you? How can we all thank you? You have freed this land from a curse that has plagued its people for over a century. That monstrous enemy—thank God—is finally found.”

My father led the Baron aside, and the General followed. I knew they were speaking out of earshot, no doubt discussing my own ordeal. I saw them glance at me several times, their expressions grave, their silence heavy with meaning.

Later, my father came to me, kissed me again and again, and gently guided me from the chapel. “We must return home,” he said, “but first, we must fetch the priest who lives nearby. He must come with us to the schloss.”

We succeeded in bringing him along, and I was grateful—for I was utterly drained when we arrived. But my relief turned to dread when I learned there was no word of Carmilla. No explanation was offered for the events in the chapel, and it was clear my father meant to keep it hidden from me—for now.

Her absence deepened the horror of what I had witnessed.

That night, preparations were made with eerie care. Two servants and Madame would stay awake in my room. My father and the priest took watch in the adjoining dressing room. The priest performed solemn rites—rituals I could not understand—though I sensed their purpose: to protect me while I slept. I would learn the full truth only days later.

After Carmilla disappeared, my nightly torment ceased. I was free—at last.

You may have heard of the ancient terror that haunts the lands of Upper and Lower Styria, Moravia, Silesia, Turkish Serbia, Poland, even Russia—the belief in the vampire. It is not mere superstition. If countless judicial inquiries—conducted by impartial commissions, sworn under oath, documented in volumes thicker than any other legal record—are to be believed, then the vampire is not myth. It is real.

I have heard no theory that explains what I have seen and endured—except the one passed down through generations, rooted in blood and fear.

The next day, the trial took place in the chapel of Karnstein. The tomb of Countess Mircalla was opened. The General and my father recognized her instantly—the perfidious, beautiful guest who had haunted our lives. Her face, though buried for one hundred and fifty years, bore the color of life. Her eyes were open. No foul odor rose from the coffin.

Two physicians—one official, the other representing the inquiry—confirmed the impossible: a faint breath, a heartbeat. Her limbs moved freely. Her flesh was supple. And the leaden coffin floated in blood—seven inches deep, in which her body lay submerged.

These were the signs. These were the proofs.

By ancient custom, the body was lifted. A sharp stake was driven through her heart. At that moment, she let out a scream—a piercing, human cry of agony. Then, her head was severed. Blood poured from her neck like a river.

Her body and head were placed upon a pyre, set alight, and reduced to ash. The ashes were scattered into the river, carried away by the current.

And from that day forth, no vampire troubled that land again.

My father possesses a copy of the Imperial Commission’s report—the official account, signed by every witness present. It is from this document that I have drawn the story of that final, terrible scene.
