This was the first time Baldassarre had returned to the Piazza del Duomo since his flight. The urge to hear the monk's sermon again burned within him, yet the memory of his previous appearance—a ragged specter with unkempt hair, a noose around his neck, labeled a lunatic by the very crowd he sought to haunt—held him back. The shame of that scene was too fresh, a raw wound that no amount of grooming could instantly cauterize. Even as he adjusted his new guise, the whisper of Tito's betrayal echoed: *"Some madman, surely."* It was not merely the cruelty of the insult that stung, but the terrifying realization that he lacked the proof to refute it. Beside the fire of vengeance that consumed him rose a cold, pragmatic dread: the suspicion that his ability to enact this retribution was illusory. It felt as though Tito had been coached by a devilish prompter, fed the darkest truths of Baldassarre's condition. Baldassarre was sane; he bore the tragic hallmark of a fractured mind, acutely aware of his own decay. Yet, the moment vindictive rage surged, a primal caution seized him, akin to a wounded beast or an insect trembling on shifting sand. This instinct to conceal his vulnerabilities had already led him to spurn Piero di Cosimo's overtures.

His caution extended to the hospital, where he claimed to be a French captive from Genoa. The monks, noting his age and the aristocratic cadence of his speech, offered him charity beyond the usual scraps: a coarse woolen tunic, peasant shoes, and a handful of *danari*. Early the next morning, he traveled toward Arezzo, stopping at the first town to shave off his beard and trim his hair to its former style. The barber produced a small steel hand-mirror. Years had passed since Baldassarre had gazed into glass, and as his eyes met the reflection, a sudden, jarring thought halted his momentum. *"Does Tito truly not recognize me?"* The question struck him like a physical blow; his hand trembled violently as he reached for the mirror. He needed to see himself before the cut was made. The barber, sensing the shake, held the glass steady.

No, the resemblance remained undeniable. The wrinkles were deeper, etched by three years of suffering, but the skin retained its rough, clumsy texture, marked by shallow bosses like cipher symbols. It was only yellower, resembling a dried rind. That shaggy white beard was no disguise; it was a signature known to eyes that had scrutinized him for sixteen years, eyes that should have recognized him amidst the debris of the sea. Something had shifted in his gaze, a difference that should have made identification more startling, for a known voice often strikes hardest when heard as a desperate cry. Doubt was a luxury he could not afford; he felt Tito knew him. Pushing the mirror away, he resumed his stride, the surging currents of hatred and vengeance driving him forward once more.

He retraced his steps toward Florence but avoided the city until dusk, turning instead to a secluded pool shaded by alder bushes still clinging to autumn gold. On this quiet November day, the still water tempted him as a potential mirror. He sat on the bank, bending low to study his reflection with an intensity he hadn't dared show the barber.

Was there madness in his face? Did his visage betray the chaos within?

Now, under the scrutiny of eager inquiry, his eyes burned with purpose. But in the long, dark hours of solitude, when whispers mocked him and phantom forms drifted away from grasping fingers, doubt undoubtedly clouded his expression. Worse still, when hope briefly pierced the fog only to collapse again, leaving him helpless, his face must have shown the blank confusion of sudden blindness.

Could he prove his identity? Could he construct a chain of logic that wouldn't snap? Who would believe he once possessed a mind brimming with rare knowledge, sharp with intricate thought and eloquent speech? That library of memories had slipped away, scattered like water from a broken urn. Had it vanished forever, or was it trapped behind a dam that might one day burst?

He clung to the latter hope. Since waking from his fever-stricken stupor on the straw mat, his mind had undergone a turbulent transformation. As his body regained strength, so did his will and the memory of his emotional life. But the sensation of lost knowledge gnawed at him constantly. Occasionally, during moments of intense excitement, flashes of his former self returned—he read Greek texts effortlessly, his mind dancing freely among familiar concepts. These were but sparks in a rising darkness, yet they fueled a dangerous ambition: if these flashes could lengthen into sustained periods, he might finally execute his revenge. He envisioned crafting a torment so exquisite that a mere mortal arm could never inflict it.

Rising from his crouch, Baldassarre folded his arms and focused every ounce of mental energy on his immediate strategy. He required knowledge and opportunity, and while waiting, he needed sustenance without begging. His greatest fear was being perceived as a senile, helpless relic. No one must know that half his mind was gone; the lost strength might return, however briefly, and that interval might suffice.

He knew how to uncover information regarding Tito. The name "Bratti Ferravecchi" had lodged in his mind with obsessive repetition. A man in Genoa, who had seen Tito's ring, had revealed that the gemstone was purchased in Florence from a young, well-dressed Greek in the shop of a dealer named Bratti Ferravecchi, located on the street of the same name. This revelation sent Baldassarre into a violent turmoil. Until now, he had clung desperately to his faith in Tito, refusing to accept that he had been abandoned. Initially, he reasoned, *"My message never reached him; he searches for me, tracing my path."* Later, in Corinth, he had convinced his captors to secure inquiries about his fate, believing, *"Here, at last, he must find me."* But illness had shattered him, rendering him worthless save for a ransom that never arrived. Sitting helpless in the sun, he had succumbed to despair, assuming Tito was dead or imprisoned. He appeared so much like a hopeless idiot that his owners gladly transferred him to a Genoese merchant. During months of arduous travel through the Archipelago and Asia Minor, Baldassarre had physically recovered, yet landed in Genoa with a weariness that made death preferable. Only one possibility kept him alive: Tito might live, perhaps in prison or poverty. If so, there remained a faint, deferred hope of reunion, of clasping hands with the one being who remembered him before the fracture. It was in this fragile state that he encountered the stranger wearing Tito's onyx ring. Though Baldassarre couldn't have described the ring beforehand, its sight ignited dormant fibers of recognition. The fact that Tito, nearly a year after their separation, thrived in Florence selling a gem he shouldn't have sold, was a truth Baldassarre preferred to remain stunned by rather than dissect logically. He tried to feel only joy at seeing Tito again. Perhaps Tito believed his father dead; the mystery could be explained. *"At least,"* he thought, *"I shall meet eyes that remember me. I am not alone."*

Once more, Baldassarre declared, *"I am not alone; my revenge walks beside me."*

Viewing himself in the water, he saw not a pitiful old man, but an instrument of retribution, separate from his decaying flesh. Human passion, whether noble or base, reaches a point where it transcends selfishness, becoming a fire that consumes everything else as mere fuel.

He stared at the pale, black-browed image until he merged with it.

*"I was a fool who worshipped a woman, believing she cared. Then I took a helpless child, fostering him, watching to see if he would care for me above all else. I would have bled to warm him if I'd seen him share my pain. I labored, straining to extract a single drop of unselfish love from this hard life. Fool! Men love their own pleasures; there is none in me. Yet I watched, convinced I saw what I sought. As a child, he lifted soft eyes to mine, holding my hand willingly. I thought, 'This boy will love me.' Because I gave my life to spare him sorrow, he would care when I thirsted... Curses on him! May I see him die with those red lips white and dry as ash. When he seeks pity, may he see my face rejoicing in his agony. It is all a lie—this world is a lie. Goodness exists only in hate. Fool! Not one drop of love came from your striving; life gave you nothing. But there are deep wells for hatred and revenge. I retain memory for that, and strength in my arm, strength in my will. If killing him is all I can do..."*

Baldassarre's mind recoiled from the brevity of such a punishment. His soul demanded an eternity of vengeance, where he, an undying hate, could clutch an undying traitor and hear that fair, smiling hardness cry in eternal anguish. Yet, his primary need was a slow, grinding revenge played out under the same sky, on the same earth where he had been forsaken and driven to fainting despair. As soon as he concentrated on the means, the weight of his weakness pressed down like a frostbite. This despised body, the vessel of his sublime quest, required nourishment and decency.
