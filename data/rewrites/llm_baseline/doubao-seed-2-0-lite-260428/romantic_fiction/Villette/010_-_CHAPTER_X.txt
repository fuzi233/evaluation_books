Madame Beck was a study in unshakable consistency: lenient enough to the world at large to avoid friction, yet tender to no living soul within her orbit. Even her own children never cracked the smooth, unyielding surface of her stoic calm. She fussed over their futures, guarded their health, kept a sharp, unblinking eye on their interests, but she never once seemed to feel the urge to tuck a small warm body on her lap, press a kiss to a rosebud cheek, wrap them in a hug soft enough to melt the edge of a bad day, or murmur a word of unplanned, uncalculated affection.

I watched her often, sitting on the stone bench in the garden on mild afternoons, watching her three little ones trail far down the alley with their nurse Trinette, her face lined with prudence and quiet worry for what she called “leur avenir” — their future. But when the youngest, a puny, bright-eyed little thing, spotted her and broke free, chubby legs toddling fast, laughing so hard it panted, to wrap its arms around her knee? Madame would only extend one steady hand to stop the child from knocking itself off balance against her, say “Prends garde, mon enfant” in a voice no warmer than autumn rain, let it stand at her side for a few measured minutes, then rise without a smile, a kiss, or a single endearing turn of phrase to walk it back to its nurse.

Her treatment of the eldest girl, Désirée, was no less telling. The whole household — kitchen staff and schoolroom teachers alike — called her nothing short of a little pest, a walking poison. She had a rare, cruel gift for provocation that could drive the servants half wild: she’d sneak up to their attics, rip their best lace caps to shreds, stain their finest shawls, steal into the dining room buffet to smash porcelain plates and crystal glasses, break into the larder to guzzle sweet wine and spoon jam straight from the jar, then leave clues that framed the cook or the scullery maid for every mess. Every time Madame saw these tantrums, every time a servant came to her to complain, all she would say, perfectly calm, was “Désirée a besoin d’une surveillance toute particulière.” No scolding, no frank talk about the harm of her habits, no warning of where that theft and lying would lead. Just surveillance, as if constant watching could stamp out vice better than love or consequence.

Of course it didn’t work. Désirée only stopped tormenting the servants to torment her mother instead: she’d steal brooches from Madame’s dressing table, snip the threads of her embroidery silk, hide her silver scissors in the garden wall cracks. Madame saw every bit of it, but pretended not to — she had no honesty in her to confront her daughter about her flaws. Only when something valuable went missing would she pretend to think Désirée had taken it to play with, and coax her to give it back. Désirée was too sharp for that, though; she lied as smoothly as she stole, and swore she’d never touched the missing object. Madame would play along, pretend to believe her, then sneak after her the second she left the room, track her to her hiding spot in the garret or the old garden shed, and steal her things back while the child was out on a walk. Désirée, for her part, never so much as frowned when she found her hoard had been raided — the apple had not fallen far from the clever, unflappable tree.

The second child, Fifine, everyone said took after her dead father. She had Madame’s strong little frame, bright blue eyes, and rosy cheeks, but nothing of her cold, calculating spirit. Hers was an open, merry little soul: warm-tempered, impulsive, forever tripping into trouble. One day she misstepped and tumbled all the way down the steep flight of stone steps leading from the second floor to the vestibule. Madame, who heard every small noise in the house no matter how far away, was there in an instant to pick her up, and said, perfectly level, “Cet enfant a un os de cassé.”

We all hoped she was wrong. She wasn’t. The little girl’s plump arm hung limp at her side.
“Have Meess take her,” she said, meaning me, “and send for a fiacre right away.”
She left promptly, calm as ever, to fetch the family doctor, but found him out — not that it slowed her down. She searched the whole town until she found a substitute she trusted, and brought him home with her. By the time they arrived, I’d cut the sleeve off Fifine’s arm, undressed her, and tucked her into bed. The bonne, the cook, the portress and I all crowded into the small, stuffy nursery, all of us too wrapped up in soothing the screaming child to pay much mind to the new doctor when he stepped through the door.

Fifine’s wails redoubled when the stranger approached her bed. “Let alone!” she shrieked in her broken English, “I will not you! I want Dr. Pillule!”
“Dr. Pillule is a very good friend of mine,” he answered, in perfect, soft English, “but he’s three leagues away tending to a sick man, so I’ve come in his place. Once we’re a little calmer, we’ll get that poor little arm all bandaged and fixed right up, I promise.”
He called for a glass of sugared water, fed her teaspoon by teaspoon — Fifine was a little glutton, and anyone could win her heart through her sweet tooth — promised her more once the splint was on, and set to work. He asked the cook, a broad, strong-armed woman, to hold the child still, but she and the rest of the staff all fled the room in a panic. I didn’t want to touch that small, broken, hurting limb, but I was just about to step forward when Madame Beck’s hand beat mine to it. Hers was perfectly steady, while mine trembled so bad I could barely keep it still.
“Ca vaudra mieux,” the doctor said, turning to her instead of me, and he was right. My calm would have been forced, my courage feigned. Hers was as natural as breathing.
“Thank you, Madame — excellent, perfectly steady,” he said when he finished setting the bone. “That level-headedness is worth a thousand bursts of misplaced sentiment.”
He was charmed by her composure, she flattered by his praise — and it was no wonder, when you got a good look at him, and the lamp was lit to chase the dusk out of the small room. This young doctor was no ordinary man. He looked impossibly tall in that tiny nursery, surrounded by the stout, quiet women of the pensionnat, his profile sharp and clear, his eyes bright and quick to dart from face to face, but so warm you couldn’t mind the way he looked. His mouth was soft, his chin full and cleft, perfectly shaped, and his smile — it was hard to name what made it so special. It made you feel light, a little silly, like he could see every one of your small, silly flaws, and laugh at them kindly, not cruelly. Even Fifine, who he’d just hurt so bad, held out her hand to say good night when he was done, and he patted it gently.

As he turned to walk out, talking to Madame in his smooth, accented French that was still clearly second nature to him, I placed him. That voice, that tall frame — it was the very man who’d helped me at the customs bureau, who’d sorted out my lost trunk, who’d guided me through the dark, rain-soaked park on my first night in Villette. I could hear his familiar, firm, even stride down the vestibule to the street, and my chest gave a small, unnameable jolt.

I’d thought that would be his only visit. Dr. Pillule was due back the next day, there was no reason for the substitute to ever return. But the Fates had other plans. Dr. Pillule had been called out to the old university town of Bouquin-Moisi to see a wealthy, fussy hypochondriac, who’d liked him so much he’d hired him to accompany him on a weeks-long tour across the country. So Dr. John — the name Fifine had insisted on calling him, and the whole household picked up to use, till no one knew him by any other name — kept coming back to the Rue Fossette.

I saw him every time he visited, because Madame wouldn’t trust Fifine’s care to Trinette, and made me spend most of my days in the nursery. He was a skilled doctor, and Fifine’s arm healed fast, but even after she was running around the garden again, he kept coming. Destiny and Madame Beck seemed to be in cahoots, determined he’d get to know every inch of the pensionnat: the vestibule, the private staircase, the little nooks of the upper floors.

No sooner was Fifine fully recovered than Désirée announced she was ill. That sharp, mischievous child had a genius for pretending, and had decided the soft indulgences of a sickbed suited her perfectly, so she climbed in and refused to get up. She acted the part well, but Madame acted it better: she saw right through Désirée’s ruse, as clear as day, but played along with a perfectly serious, perfectly convincing show of concern. What surprised me most was that Dr. John played right along too. He gave a few quick, amused glances from the grinning, overexcited child who gorged on treats and threw her shoes at the nurse, to her perfectly unflappable mother, hesitated for a minute like he was debating calling them out, then gave in with a good-natured smile and took his place in their little farce. He wrote harmless, sugary prescriptions for the “patient”, and watched Madame with a sharp, sparkling glint in his eye that made her laugh instead of taking offense.

He was never obsequious, never trying to curry favor with Madame. He seemed drawn to the pensionnat for some unnameable reason, lingering longer than he needed to after every visit, wandering the halls like he was looking for something he couldn’t name, growing quiet and thoughtful when he thought he was alone. No one noticed me, of course — I was the quiet, unobtrusive governess, as invisible as a plain wooden chair, as unremarkable as the carpet under their feet. He’d muse to himself, smile at nothing, listen to the children’s laughter drift in from the garden, and never once remember I was sitting in the corner, needle and thread in hand, watching him.

One afternoon, he was sitting in the sun slanting through the window, the light gilding his fair hair and his whiskers so bright I found myself thinking, half amused, half caught off guard, of the golden idol Nebuchadnezzar set up in the old Bible stories. I was so wrapped up in the thought, staring, that I forgot myself entirely — until he turned, and his eyes met mine in the small oval mirror set in the window recess, the same mirror Madame used to spy on people walking in the garden below.

He turned around, his tone polite but dry, a little sharp with quiet annoyance. “Mademoiselle does not spare me. I am not vain enough to think my merits have drawn your eye — so it must be some defect. Dare I ask what it is?”

I was flustered, of course, but not mortified. I knew I hadn’t been staring out of silly admiration, or nosy curiosity. I could have cleared the air right then, but I didn’t. I said nothing, ducked my head back over my sewing, and kept it down until he left. There is a strange, quiet comfort in being misjudged, after all — in places where no one will ever really know you, there is a small, secret joy in being so thoroughly unseen, that even when you are caught staring, the mistake of their assumptions feels like a private little joke no one else is in on.
