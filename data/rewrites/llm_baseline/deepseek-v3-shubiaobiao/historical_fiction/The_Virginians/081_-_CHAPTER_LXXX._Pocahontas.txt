The English public, less familiar with the tale of Pocahontas than we Virginians—who still cherish the memory of that gentle and noble-hearted princess—found themselves introduced to her legend through a modest ballad penned by Mr. Warrington. This little verse, printed in the magazines shortly before the debut of his tragedy, was, in the eyes of Parson Sampson and myself, a stroke of cunning strategy.

"Ground-bait, my dear sir!" exclaimed the enthusiastic clergyman. "Mark my words—the fish will rise in shoals on the great night!"

Indeed, whispers circulated through the coffee-houses that the lines had been composed by none other than Mr. Mason, Mr. Cowper of the Temple, or even the illustrious Mr. Gray. I suspect our good Sampson himself planted these rumors, for had Shakespeare been named the author, he would have proclaimed *Pocahontas* the poet’s crowning glory.

My own acquaintance with the valiant Captain Smith began in childhood, seated at my grandfather’s knee in Virginia, poring over the chronicles of the hero’s exploits. His travels, his sufferings, his narrow escapes—both in the wilds of America and the battlefields of Europe—filled my young mind with wonder. Even now, as I take up that well-worn volume here in England, the memories return fresh as yesterday. My grandfather would sketch scenes of Smith’s duels—whether facing Turkish champions on the Danube or standing defiant before Powhatan’s warriors.

Ah, what a spectacle was that legendary combat with the three Turkish champions! The final foe, Bonny Molgro, loomed large in my imagination—his monstrous turban, his gleaming scimitar, his ferocious whiskers! After dispatching the first two, Smith and Molgro clashed with battle-axes, each blow staggering the other, until the Christian, disarmed and wounded, drew his falchion and struck the fatal blow. For his valor, Duke Segismundus granted him a shield bearing three Turks’ heads and a pension of three hundred ducats.

The tragedy itself need not be recounted here—my children may peruse it at their leisure. Suffice it to say, it wove together the threads of history and romance: an Indian king, a devoted princess, a treacherous Englishman, a noble savage torn by love, and a scheming medicine-man bent on Smith’s destruction. The war-cries, the dances, the arrival of the English fleet—all were crafted to stir patriotic fervor, particularly in light of Britain’s recent triumphs in Canada.

Yet ill omens gathered like storm clouds. Petty jealousies, managerial timidity, and a cabal in high places conspired against us. The house, Sampson swore, teemed with planted detractors. Even so, Hagan’s performance as Smith was masterful—so much so, he later confessed, that a certain eminent actor might well have envied him.

Alas, history’s fidelity proved our undoing. The audience tittered at Pocahontas’s authentic garb (though I ask, should Caractacus have been painted blue?). Then, in the play’s most poignant moment—when Pocahontas flung herself before Smith to save him—a wag in the pit cried, "Bedad! Here’s the Belle Savage kissing the Saracen’s Head!" Laughter erupted, and the curtain fell to hisses.

I bore the defeat with composure. What captain rages against the tempest that sinks his ship? Yet at home, my Theo’s pale face struck deeper than any critic’s barb. Our little supper—meant to celebrate—was instead a quiet communion of shared sorrow. Even the verses she later slipped into my study, though not the loftiest poetry, moved me more than all the applause in the world:

Her blushing smile as I read them was victory enough.
