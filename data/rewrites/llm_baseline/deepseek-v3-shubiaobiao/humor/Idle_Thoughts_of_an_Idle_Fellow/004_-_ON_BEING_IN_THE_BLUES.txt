There’s a certain charm to melancholy—a dignified misery, if you will—but the blues? No, thank you. They arrive uninvited, overstay their welcome, and leave you feeling like a wrung-out dishrag. And the worst part? Nobody knows why they descend upon us. One day, you’re celebrating a windfall; the next, you’re mourning the loss of your favorite umbrella left on the train. The blues don’t discriminate.

When they strike, you become a menace to society. Your wit abandons you. Your patience evaporates. You snap at strangers, growl at friends, and contemplate kicking yourself—if only anatomy permitted. You’re restless but directionless, pacing like a caged badger before collapsing into a chair with a sigh loud enough to startle the cat.

Books? Useless. Shakespeare feels like a hack, Dickens drones, Thackeray is a pretentious windbag, and Carlyle—oh, spare us the melodrama. You hurl the offending volume across the room and glare at it as if it’s personally offended you. The cat, sensing danger, wisely flees, and you slam the door behind her with unnecessary violence.

Letter-writing seems a fine idea until you’ve stared at *"Dearest Auntie, I find I have five minutes to spare—"* for fifteen excruciating minutes, only to crumple the page in defeat. The pen, now leaking ink onto the tablecloth, becomes another casualty of your mood. You consider visiting the Thompsons—but then you remember the Thompsons are insufferable, their house is a baby-infested wasteland, and they never serve supper. The Thompsons, you decide, are the enemy.

By this point, you’re wallowing in existential despair. You imagine your own tragic demise—your friends gathered around your deathbed, weeping, finally realizing your worth. (A fantasy, of course. In reality, they’d probably fight over who gets your good cufflinks.) The injustice of life weighs upon you. You’ve been wronged since birth. The world is a cruel, unfeeling place, and you? A noble soul, tragically misunderstood.

Eventually, bedtime offers a merciful escape. You storm upstairs, shed clothes like a man fleeing a burning building, and dive into bed with the grace of a sack of potatoes. Sleep comes in fits—when it comes at all—punctuated by dreams of falling, failing, or showing up to a dinner party in your underwear.

Married men, of course, have it easier. They take out their blues on their families—criticizing dinner, banishing children to their rooms, and generally making life miserable for everyone within earshot. Single men, alas, must suffer in solitude, tossing and turning until dawn.

The blues wear many names. The poet calls it *"a feeling of sadness."* ’Arry announces he’s *"got the blooming hump."* Your sister claims she’s *"out of sorts."* The young man at the club sighs dramatically and declares himself *"jolly miserable."* As for me? I say I’ve got *"a strange, unsettled feeling"*—and then I go out, because the blues, like vampires, thrive in the evening.

Daylight scatters them. Under the sun, we rage, we hustle, we swear at misplaced keys—but we don’t brood. Melancholy is a nocturnal creature. It slinks in with the shadows, when the world quiets and the mind wanders. And let’s be honest—real misery doesn’t inspire poetry. Actual grief isn’t romantic. It’s the *memory* of sorrow that we cradle like a treasured relic, long after the sting has faded.

There’s a peculiar sweetness in looking back on pain that no longer hurts. The old lady weeping over tiny shoes, the widow pressing a lock of hair to her heart—they don’t weep from anguish, but from a sorrow softened by time, now worn smooth as a pebble in the hand. Sentiment, after all, is the luxury of healed wounds.

And then there’s the melancholy of a summer’s evening—that golden hush when the world seems to sigh. The city streets blur into shadows, the river slips silently under bridges, and in the countryside, the trees whisper secrets to the dusk. It’s in these quiet hours that we feel something larger than ourselves, something solemn and sacred. The grind of daily life fades, and for a moment, we glimpse the vast, quiet beauty of existence—before remembering we forgot to pay the gas bill.

So here’s to the blues. Annoying, unavoidable, but oddly illuminating. They remind us that life isn’t just about bread and cheese (or even kisses), but about those fleeting moments when we pause, look up, and wonder.

Then we go to bed grumpy and hope tomorrow’s better.
