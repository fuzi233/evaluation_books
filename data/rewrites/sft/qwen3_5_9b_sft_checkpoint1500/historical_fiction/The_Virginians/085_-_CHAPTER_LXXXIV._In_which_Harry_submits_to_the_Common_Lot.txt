The storm of hardship had finally broken, and I stood on shore, though battered, no longer drowning. The death of my little kinsman, Miles, altered the landscape of my fortune entirely. With the Warrington line seemingly barren, I became the next heir to a substantial estate. "The woman is capable of committing any crime to disappoint you," Sampson had vowed with grim certainty regarding my aunt and uncle; yet, in truth, Lady Warrington possessed no such treacherous heart. Instead, she was cruelly struck by the blow, and her religious counselors guided her to view the tragedy as a divine chastisement. They told her, "Whilst your son lived, your heart was turned away from the better world," urging her to submit to the Heavenly Will, dismissing her former desires for rank and title as vanity compared to the heavenly crown awaiting her.

The accident sent shockwaves through the chapels of her fervent sect, where sermons were preached endlessly on the providential nature of the event. Yet, I refrained from judging the bereaved mother's path; grief and disappointment drive souls toward whatever refuge offers solace. True to her nature, however, she attempted reconciliation. A year after the loss, while visiting London, she summoned me. There, in her customary didactic manner, she delivered a homily on my station and hers. She marveled at Heaven's decree, noting how dreadfully her poor child's disobedience—gone out hunting without permission—had been punished, thereby allowing me to profit. She expressed a fervent hope that if I ever inherited the family honors, I would alter my ways: sever ties with my unworthy associates, abandon the theater and its licentious crowd, and return to peace and sacred duty. Her speech was filled with Scriptural language, which I shall not attempt to mimic. Before I departed, she handed me a packet of sermons for Mrs. Warrington and a small book of hymns by Miss Dora, a future figure of eminence in that society who later married the celebrated preacher, Mr. Juffles.

Though the poor lady forgave me, she could never bear the sight of our boy again. Following the loss of our second child, my aunt and her daughter flocked to the grieving mother, even inviting us to join them. But my uncle, now almost daily a guest in our household, became a distraction. He sat for hours gazing at Miles, showering him with toys and sweets, begging the child to call him "Godpapa." When the sting of our own grief struck me anew—even twenty-five years later—I understood his sorrow. But my wife, who had not yet known the agony of losing her own child, could not truly pity him. It was only when I held my little baby's coffin in my hands that I was fully reconciled to my uncle.

The widower sent his coach to follow our humble funeral procession. Afterwards, he took little Miles out, where the boy, clad in his new black clothes, prattled incessantly, forgetting his grief in the delight of fresh air. How the innocent chatter of the child stabbed the mother's heart! Would we ever wish for that wound to heal? I know her face so well today that I can tell when, sometimes, she thinks of the loss. It is not a grief for a parting so long ago; it is a communion with a soul we love in Heaven.

Soon, we returned to our bright lodgings in Bloomsbury. My young bear, whom I could no longer lead due to growing responsibilities, transferred to the Chartreux School. Thanks to Charley's influence and an excellent private tutor, the boy flourished, keeping a good standing and even declaring that if he ever had a son, he would send him there too.

With business to attend to, I could no longer guide my pet. Having been fully reconciled to the family, particularly for Miles's sake, my uncle—who was such an obsequious supporter of Government that the Minister surely relied on his vote—used his influence in my favor. I was honored to be gazetted as one of His Majesty's Commissioners for licensing hackney-coaches, a post I filled with credit until a quarrel with the Minister eventually deprived me of it. I also took my degree at the Temple and appeared in Westminster Hall in gown and wig. Later, accompanying my good friend Mr. Foker to Paris, I was received *bras ouverts* by my dear American preserver, Monsieur de Florac. He introduced me to his noble family and the polite circles of the capital, though I spent little time there, preferring to remain close to Foker, whose acquaintances lay chiefly among the bourgeoisie, including Monsieur Santerre, a great brewer who would later distinguish himself in blood, not merely beer. Mr. Foker required my services as an interpreter, and I was eager to repay the kindness he had shown me. Meanwhile, the ladies resided at Mr. Foker's villa in Wimbledon, amused by the "Parisian letters" I sent to them via my friend Mr. Hume, which were later published in a neat volume.

While I quietly discharged these minor official duties in London, the troubles brewing in America began to gather force, destined to culminate in the Great Separation. When Mr. Grenville proposed his stamp duties, I told my wife the bill would certainly create discontent at home, as we were ever anxious to extract wealth from England while paying back as little as possible. However, I never anticipated the prodigious anger the scheme unleashed. It was with us as with families or individuals: a pretext is given for a quarrel, but the real cause lies in long-brewing bickerings and prior animosities. Many foolish exactions, petty tyrannies, and the habitual insolence of Englishmen toward foreigners and colonists—all folk who dared to think their rivers as good as the Abana and Pharpar—set Britain and her colonies to quarrel. The astonishing blunders of the English system brought this quarrel to an issue. Whether I, for one, was wrong to deplore it remains unclear. Had I been in Virginia instead of London, 'tis very possible I should have taken the provincial side, perhaps driven into revolt by that resolute mistress of Castlewood, just as England drove the colonies. Was the Stamp Act the true cause of the revolution? It was a tax no greater than that cheerfully paid in England. Ten years earlier, when the French threatened our territory and we implored succor from home, would the colonies have rebelled over this tax? Do not most people consider the tax-gatherer the natural enemy? Against the British in America were arrayed thousands of high-spirited and brave souls, alongside countless others who found profit or private reasons to engage in the strife. I protest I do not know now whether my motives were selfish or patriotic, which side was right, or if both were not. I am sure we in England had nothing to do but fight the battle out; and having lost the game, I do vow and believe that, after the first natural soreness, the loser felt no rancour.

What made brother Hal write home from Virginia, which he seemed exceedingly loth to quit, such flaming patriotic letters? My kind, best brother was always led by somebody; by me when we were together (he had such an idea of my wit and wisdom that if I said the day was fine, he would ponder it as a saying of the Seven Sages), and by some other wiseacre when I was away. Who inspired these fiery epistles, this boisterous patriotism? "He is rebelling against Madam Esmond," I surmised. "He is led by some colonial person—by that lady, perhaps," hinted my wife. Hal never told us who "that lady" was; indeed, he besought me never to allude to the delicate subject in my letters to him, writing, "for Madam wishes to see 'em all, and I wish to say nothing about you know what until the proper moment." No affection could be greater than that which his letters showed. When he heard (from the informant I have mentioned) that in the midst of my own extreme straits I had retained no more than a hundred pounds out of his aunt's legacy, he was for mortgaging the estate he had just bought. He had more than one quarrel with his mother in my behalf and spoke his mind with a frankness I would never have ventured. Until her angry recriminations—charging him with ingratitude after she had toiled and saved so much—he did not know that our mother had cut off my supplies to advance his interests. By the time this news reached him, his bargains were made, and I was fortunately quite out of want.

Every scrap of paper we ever wrote was taped and docketed by our thrifty parent at Castlewood. We boys were more careless about our correspondence, I especially so, perhaps choosing to look down upon my younger brother's literary performances. But my wife is not so supercilious; she has kept no small number of Harry's letters, as well as those of the angelic being whom we were presently to call sister.

"To think whom he has chosen, and whom he might have had! Oh, 'tis cruel!" cried my wife when we received that notable letter in which Harry first made us acquainted with the name of his charmer.

"She was a very pretty little maid when I left home; she may be a perfect beauty now," I remarked, reading over the longest letter Harry ever wrote on private affairs.

"But is she to compare to my Hetty?" asked Mrs. Warrington.

"We agreed that Hetty and Harry were not to be happy together, my love," I said.
