Years later, sifting through the dusty archives of my home, I discovered a packet sealed with my mother's precise, familiar script, labeled "From London, April, 1760. My son's dreadful letter." When it finally became mine to keep, I burned it without hesitation. I refused to allow the chronicle of our domestic fracture to remain among the family annals, lest some future Warrington should gaze upon it with shame, or a rebellious son cite it as a precedent for his own defiance. For the same reasons, I have consigned to the flames the reply my mother dispatched to me during this season of tyranny, revolt, and irritation.

Blinded by the agony of separation from my mistress, and justly regarding Mrs. Esmond as the architect of the deepest misery I had ever known, I penned a letter to Virginia. It was a document of extreme gravity, though perhaps lacking in temper. I asserted that I knew not her motives, yet held her accountable for the wretchedness of my future, which she had wilfully marred. I accused her of severing the bond between me and a virtuous, innocent young woman, whose hopes and health had been irrevocably shattered by her interference. The deed was done; I offered no further comment on the perpetrator, who answered only to God, but I made no secret that the injury inflicted upon me was mortal. The tie of my allegiance was severed; I could never again be the dutiful, respectful son she had expected.

Madam Esmond's reply was a masterpiece of dignified silence. She uttered no reproach, no harsh word, but coldly referred the case to the awful tribunal of God, asking for counsel. She humbly submitted her own conduct to judgment and challenged me, as a son, to answer for myself before the Great Judge. Whether I had done my duty and honoured my parents? *O popoi!* My grandfather often quoted Homer, noting how in our troubles we invariably summon the gods. When pride, avarice, or the desire to dominate stirs within us, do we not forever pestilence Heaven to decide in our favour? Did we not, in our great American quarrel, appeal to the skies on both sides, singing Te Deum for victory and claiming the right must prevail? Was America right solely because she was victorious? Then surely Poland was wrong because she was defeated? How am I wandering into this digression of nations and wars, while my mind wanders to a little woman now gone, who appealed to Heaven with a thousand texts because her son sought a marriage she disliked? We appeal, we imprecate, we kneel and demand blessings; we shriek for legal sentence. Yet the great course of the world moves on. We pant, strive, struggle; we hate and rage and weep; we reconcile and race, winning and losing; we pass away, and other little strugglers succeed. Our days are spent, our nights come, and another morning rises that shines on us no more.

I showed a duplicate of my letter of revolt to Mr. Lambert, wishing him to understand the depth of my estrangement from my mother and my determination that my separation from Theo was forced, not voluntary. Whenever I could see her again, I would. My word was given *in secula seculorum*, binding as long as life endured. I implied the girl was similarly bound to me, and her poor father knew it well. He might separate us; he might even offer a dose of poison, and the gentle, obedient creature would take it and die; but the death or separation would be his doing. Let him answer for it. Now, he was tender to a fault, unable to subject any of his children—this one especially—to torture. We had tried to part; we could not. He had endeavoured to separate us; it was beyond his power. The bars were raised, but the maid within and the knight without still loved each other. The wall stood high, but Pyramus and Thisbe whispered on either side. Amidst his grief and perplexity, Uncle Lambert possessed plenty of humour; he could not but see that his role was a sorry one. Light was beginning to pierce the lime and rough plaster of the wall; the lovers were getting their hands through, then their heads through. Indeed, it was the wall's best business to retire.

I forget the stages and days of what followed; nor does it matter for the instruction of future ages. When my descendants encounter their own love scrapes, they will find their own means of escape. I believe I did not return to Dean Street, but that open-air driving was deemed most healthful for Miss Lambert. I procured a fine horse and rode beside her carriage. The old woman at Tottenham Court soon knew us both, nodding and winking in the most friendly manner as we passed. I fancy the old goody was not unaccustomed to taking an interest in young couples, having dispensed the hospitality of her roadside cottage to more than one pair.

The doctor and the country air effected a prodigious cure upon Miss Lambert. Hetty always attended as duenna, and sometimes, on his holidays, Master Charley rode my horse when I entered the carriage. What a deal of love-making Miss Hetty heard! With what exemplary patience she listened! I do not say she resumed attending Methodist sermons, but it is certain that when we had a closed carriage, she would very kindly and considerately look out of the window. Then, what heaps of letters! What running to and fro! Gumbo's bandy legs were forever on the trot from my quarters to Dean Street; and, on my account or her own, Mrs. Molly, the girl's maid, was forever bringing back answers to Bloomsbury. By the time the autumn leaves began to turn pale, Miss Theo's roses were in full bloom again, and my good Doctor Heberden pronounced his cure complete.

What else occurred during this blessed period? Mr. Warrington completed his great tragedy of *Pocahontas*, which was not only accepted by Mr. Garrick this time (his friend Dr. Johnson having spoken favourably of the work), but my friend and cousin, Hagan, was engaged by the manager to perform the part of the hero, Captain Smith. Hagan's engagement was secured precisely when it was needed. I had helped him and his family with means disproportioned, perhaps, to my power, especially considering my feud with Madam Esmond. Her answer to my angry missive of April arrived towards autumn, written from Virginia with war for war, controlment for controlment. These menaces frightened me little; my poor mother's thunder could not reach me. My conscience, or casuistry, supplied me with other interpretations for her texts of Scripture, so that her oracles held no weight to frighten me from my purpose. I informed neither my mother nor any other member of my maternal or paternal family, who had been bitter against my marriage, of how my new love was speeding. Of what use wrangling with them? It was better to *carpere diem* and its sweet loves and pleasures, leaving the railers to grumble or the seniors to advise at their ease.

Besides Madam Esmond, I must own, in the frantic rage of my temporary separation, I addressed notes of wondrous sarcasm to my Uncle Warrington, my Aunt Madame de Bernstein, and my Lord or Lady of Castlewood (I forget to which individually), thanking them for the trouble taken in preventing the dearest happiness of my life, and promising corresponding gratitude from my obliged relative. Business brought the jovial Baronet and his family to London somewhat earlier than usual, and Madame de Bernstein was never sorry to return to Clarges Street and her cards. I saw them. They found me perfectly well. They concluded the match was broken off, and I did not choose to undeceive them. The Baroness took heart at seeing how cheerful I was, making many sly jokes about my philosophy and my prudent behaviour as a man of the world. She was, as ever, bent upon finding a rich match for me; and I fear I paid many compliments at her house to a rich young soap-boiler's daughter from Mile End, whom the worthy Baroness wished to place in my arms.

"You court her with infinite wit and esprit, my dear," said my pleased kinswoman, "but she does not understand half you say, and the other half, I think, frightens her. This *ton de persiflage* is very well in our society, but you must be sparing of it, my dear nephew, amongst these roturiers."

Miss Badge married a young gentleman of royal dignity, though with shattered fortunes, from a neighbouring island; and I trust Mrs. Mackshane has ere this pardoned my levity. There was another person besides Miss at my aunt's house who did not understand my persiflage much better than Miss herself; and that was a lady who had seen James the Second's reign, and who was alive and as worldly as ever in King George's. I loved to be with her; but since my little folks have access to this volume, I could put down a hundred stories of the great old folks she had known in the great old days—of George the First and his ladies, of St. John and Marlborough, of his reigning Majesty and the late Prince of Wales, and the causes of their quarrel—but my modest muse pipes for boys and virgins. Son Miles does not care about court stories, or if he doth, has a fresh budget from Carlton House quite as bad as the worst of our old Baroness. No, my dear wife, thou hast no need to shake thy powdered locks at me! Papa is not going to scandalise his nursery with old-world gossip, nor bring a blush over our chaste bread-and-butter.

But this piece of scandal I cannot help. My aunt used to tell it with infinite gusto; for, to do her justice, she hated your would-be good people and sniggered over the faults of the self-styled righteous with uncommon satisfaction. In her later days she had no hypocrisy, at least; and in so far was better than some whitewashed... Well, to the story. My Lady Warrington, one of the tallest and most virtuous of her sex, who had goodness forever on her lips and "Heaven in her eye," like the woman in Mr. Addison's tedious tragedy (which has kept the stage, from which some others, which shall be nameless, have disappeared), had the world in her other eye and an exceedingly shrewd desire of pushing herself in it. What did she do, when my marriage with your ladyship yonder was supposed to be broken off, but attempt to play off on me those arts she had tried on my poor Harry with such signal ill success, and which failed with me likewise! It was not the Beauty—Miss Flora was for my master (and what a master! I protest I take off my hat at the idea of such an illustrious connexion!)—it was Dora, the Muse, who was set upon me to languish at me, pity me, read even my godless tragedy, applaud me, and console me. Meanwhile, how was the Beauty occupied? Will it be believed that my severe aunt gave a great entertainment to my Lady Yarmouth, presented her boy to her, and placed poor little Miles under her ladyship's august protection? That, so far, is certain; but can it be that she sent her daughter to stay at my lady's house, which our gracious lord and master daily visited, with the views which old Aunt Bernstein attributed to her? "But for that fit of apoplexy, my dear," Bernstein said, "that aunt of yours intended there should have been a Countess in her own right in the Warrington family!" I can see my pretty Miles, in a gay little uniform of the Norfolk Militia, led up by his parent to the lady whom the King delighted to honour, and the good-natured old Jezebel laying her hand upon the boy's curly pate. I am accused of being but a lukewarm royalist; but sure I can contrast those times with ours, and acknowledge the difference between the late sovereign and the present, who, born a Briton, has given to every family in the empire an example of decorum and virtuous life. Thus my life sped in the pleasantest of all occupations; and, being so happy myself, I could afford to be reconciled to those who, after all, had done me no injury, but rather added to the zest of my happiness by the brief obstacle they had placed in my way. No specific plans were formed, but Theo and I knew that a day would come when we need say Farewell no more. Should the day befall a year hence—or ten years hence—we were ready to wait. Day after day we discussed our little plans, with Hetty for our confidante. On our drives we spied out pretty cottages that we thought might suit young people of small means; we devised all sorts of delightful schemes and childish economies. We were Strephon and Chloe to be sure. A cot and a brown loaf should content us! Gumbo and Molly should wait upon us (as indeed they have done from that day until this). At twenty, who is afraid of being poor? Our trials would only confirm our attachment. The "sweet sorrow" of every day's parting but made the morrow's meeting more delightful; and when we separated we ran home and wrote each other those precious letters which we and other young gentlemen and ladies write under such circumstances; but though my wife has them all in a great tin sugar-box in the closet in her bedroom, and, I own, I myself have looked at them once, and even thought some of them pretty—I hereby desire my heirs and executors to burn them all, unread, at our demise; specially desiring my son the Captain (to whom I know the perusal of MSS. is not pleasant) to perform this duty.
