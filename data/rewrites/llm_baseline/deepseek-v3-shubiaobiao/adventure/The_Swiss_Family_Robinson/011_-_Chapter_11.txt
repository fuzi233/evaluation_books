The morning sun rose over our bustling camp as we prepared to finish the gruesome task of rendering whale oil. "Now for the final stretch of this dirty work!" I called cheerfully. After our usual chores, we set up a scaffold to press the blubber-filled tubs, allowing the purest oil to drain into containers below.

The blubber was then boiled in a cauldron over a fire safely distanced from our home. Through skimming and straining, we collected a substantial store of fine train oil, carefully stored in casks and waterproof bags made from the whale’s intestines. The stench was oppressive, especially for my wife, who bore it with admirable patience.

She suggested establishing a secondary colony on the nearby island, where such messy work could be conducted without disturbing our home. The boys eagerly supported the idea, though I reminded them of the rotting carcass still present there. "Let nature cleanse the air first," I advised. "Wind, rain, and scavengers will reduce it to bones in time."

Meanwhile, my mind dwelled on improving our boat’s efficiency. I fashioned a crude rowing mechanism: an iron bar fitted with paddles, turned by a hand-cranked jack. Though imperfect, it worked well enough that Fritz and I glided swiftly across the bay, earning cheers from the family. Excited, they clamored for a voyage the next day to Cape Disappointment and Prospect Hill.

The following morning, we set out well-provisioned. Approaching the farm, the crowing of roosters stirred bittersweet memories of home—our arrival here, unnoticed by the animals, stood in stark contrast to the warm welcomes we once knew. The creatures fled in terror, forcing the boys to lasso goats for milk and tempt fowls with grain.

Dinner was a disappointment when the prized whale tongue proved inedible, tainted by oil. Thankfully, fresh coconuts and goat’s milk salvaged the meal. Later, exploring the coast, we dodged hidden reefs before landing on Whale Island to plant sugarcane.

While working, Jack shouted, "Father! A mammoth skeleton!" It was, of course, the whale’s remains—misidentified until I pointed out our footprints nearby. Ernest teased Jack, sparking a quarrel, but I intervened, stressing harmony.

Returning home, we experimented with a palanquin—a basket slung between Storm and Grumble. Ernest’s ride turned comically rough as the beasts trotted faster, but laughter soon mended frayed tempers.

Then, disaster struck. Fritz spotted a monstrous serpent approaching—a boa constrictor, thirty feet long. We barricaded ourselves in the cave, firing futile shots as it slithered into the reeds. For days, we lived in dread, until our donkey, Grizzle, bolted into the marsh. The boa struck, coiling around him in a horrifying embrace.

"Shoot it!" the boys begged, but I knew interference was futile. "It swallows prey whole," I explained grimly. As Grizzle’s brays faded, we watched helplessly, knowing the beast would now be sluggish—vulnerable.

The chapter closed with a grim resolution: we would strike when the serpent gorged, ending its threat once and for all.
