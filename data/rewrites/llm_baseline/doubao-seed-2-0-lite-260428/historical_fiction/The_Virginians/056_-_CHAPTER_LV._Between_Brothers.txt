For days after Harry’s arrival in London, the two Esmond brothers huddled behind the closed door of their lodgings, trading the lost years apart like boys sorting through a hoard of stolen trinkets, no detail too small to merit skipping. First came all the quiet, beloved trivialities of home: were Harry’s favourite staghounds still roaming the estate? Had little Dempster the stable boy finally stopped stealing apples off Madam Esmond’s table? Good old Nathan, the family butler of thirty years, still as gruff and loyal as ever? Mrs. Mountain the governess keeping well, and her niece Fanny grown into a proper pretty young lady at last?
They laughed till their sides ached when they recalled Parson Broadbent’s daughter, the girl Harry had once been so smitten with that he’d lingered three nights a week at the parsonage, losing shillings at cards to the old parson and downing mug after mug of spiced rum punch just to have an excuse to loiter on the off-chance of passing her on the staircase. Heaven knew Harry had been half ready to propose, too—till Madam Esmond had sniffed, raised one perfectly arched eyebrow, and made it unmistakeably clear that no daughter of a country parson, however sweet-faced, was good enough to marry one of her boys.
Next came the story of Colonel George Washington’s new wife, the pleasant, good-humoured lady of great fortune he’d brought to call on Madam Esmond at the Castlewood Virginia estate. George recounted the absurd, stilted state visit with such droll humour that Harry rolled on the sofa cackling: the two proud, formidable women trading three layers of excessive curtsy and over-polished civility, neither willing to concede an inch of dignity. “Never you mind, Georgy dear!” Mountain had cackled afterwards, winking broadly. “The Colonel’s gone and taken himself another wife now, but I’ll swear to it—once upon a time, you two lads came this close to getting yourselves a stepfather six feet two tall in his boots.” The silly old governess was forever matchmaking, after all: let two people sit beside each other at a card table or share a dish of tea, and she’d immediately be plotting out their wedding banns. It was that same harmless, tattling old woman who’d let loose the first silly rumour about the poor Indian woman Biche. When Parson Stack had brought the gossip to Madam Esmond’s ear, she’d dismissed it with such sharp scorn it had made the parson flinch: “I’d as soon believe one of my boys stole the silver spoons from the pantry, or ran off to marry a negro scullery maid, as believe that nonsense.” But for all she refused to brand Biche guilty, Madam Esmond treated the poor young woman with such frigid, unapproachable hauteur that Biche soon slipped away to the servants’ quarters, and took to drinking rough cider brandy to drown the quiet hurt.
The picture George painted of his two months back in Virginia after his release from French captivity was far from cheerful. “The birthright is mine, Harry,” he said, his voice soft and half bitter, “but you are the favourite. God help me, I do believe our mother almost grudges it to me. Why did I have to be the one born first? Had you been the elder, you’d have inherited the best cellar, ridden the finest stallion on the plantation, and been the most popular man in the whole colony. I’d have been the second son—set up as a lawyer in Williamsburg, or shipped off to university in England to take orders, saying grace at your grand dinner table every Sunday. The time is out of joint, lad. O cursed spite, that ever I was born to set it right!”
“Georgy, you’re quoting poetry at me, I swear you are!” Harry laughed, then flushed when George smiled and said the lines belonged to a playwright far more famous than him. “I know! I saw those two authors you’re always going on about at Tunbridge Wells, you know! Tipped my hat to both of them, even if I never could keep up with their clever books. I did it for you, old man—just thinking of how you’d ramble on about their work for hours when we were lads.” His voice thickened, and he swiped at his eye with the back of his hand. “It’s like a dream, having you back. Thinking of that bloody Indian with a knife at your head over in the woods! I wish I had something grand to give Monsieur de Florac for saving your life—all I’ve got left is these little gold knee buckles, and they’re not worth two guineas.”
“You have half of every penny I own,” George told him. “The moment I’ve paid the ransom money to Museau’s heirs, we split the rest, no questions asked.” Harry burst out in such a roar of grateful blessings and loud, emphatic oaths that the neighbours banged on the wall in complaint. For days afterwards, his eyes followed George everywhere: he’d drop his knife and fork mid-bite at dinner, or set aside the newspaper he was pretending to read, and grin to himself like he’d just stumbled on the greatest secret in the world. When they walked the Mall or through Hyde Park, he’d glance around at all the fine lords and ladies parading by, his chest puffed out, as if to announce to every last one of them: Look here. This is my brother. The one who was dead and is alive again. Can any man in Christendom show off a brother half as fine?
Harry was unshakable in his opinion that George must keep his word and pay the full ransom sum to Museau’s heirs, just as he’d promised the dying Frenchman. That very question had been the source of weeks of bitter strife between George and his mother back in Virginia. Museau was dead, Madam Esmond had argued, sharp with rage, so the bargain was void. The man had been a rogue from the start, trying to extort money from a prisoner of war—why reward his iniquity? George was of age, he controlled the small independent fortune left to him by his father, but she would be party to no such folly: throwing twelve thousand livres at a parcel of Norman peasants, at a time England and France were at war, money that would no doubt end up in the pockets of priests and the Pope himself. If he was determined to squander that money, she’d cried, why not give it to his own flesh and blood, to poor Harry who’d been robbed of his inheritance, rather than a pack of papist, priest-ridden foreigners? The fight had raged till the morning of George’s departure, when Madam Esmond had come to his bedside after a sleepless night, pale and hollow-eyed, and asked if he still intended to throw his father’s property away. “My word is passed,” he’d told her, grief heavy in his chest, “and I must do as my honour bids me.” She’d answered that she would pray Heaven would soften his proud heart, and give her strength to bear such a heavy trial. The last sight he had of her was her standing by his bedside, tearless and rigid, before she turned and walked slowly out of his room.
“Harry, how do you do it?” George went on. “How do you make every soul you meet like you within five minutes? Teach me your tricks of popularity—though on second thought, I don’t think I want them. When I find people hate me, I’m more amused than angry. When I first got back to Virginia, everyone thought I was some sort of hero: the only man who escaped Braddock’s massacre, fresh off months of captivity and illness. The parson preached a whole sermon about my escape, all the neighbours called on us, people smiled wherever I went. The girls even started setting their caps at me—till our mother scared every last one of them off with her icy grandeur. My popularity was already fading fast by then, anyway. The army officers thought I was a dull stick because I wouldn’t join their drinking bouts, or throw dice all night, or roar oaths at the top of my lungs. The ladies thought I was too sharp-tongued, and their endless gossiping over tea left me so stupefied I could barely speak. I was lonelier there than I ever was in the French prison, believe me. Half the time I caught myself pining for the quiet, uncomplicated days I spent in Duquesne, no one breathing down my neck or spreading stories behind my back.”
“Then that cursed gossip about poor Biche started, didn’t it?” George’s voice rose, hot with old rage. “The boys hooted at her in the street if she so much as stepped out of the servants’ wing. The ladies dropped me half a curtsy and crossed the road to the other side when they saw me coming. That Parson Stack went from tea table to tea table preaching about the horrors of seduction, and the loose morals young men picked up in papist countries. Everyone agreed her turning up in Richmond weeks after my return was a secret plan between us, that she’d hidden on the other side of the river waiting for my signal to come join me. The officers cracked their clumsy, vulgar jokes about me at the coffee house. I was so enraged I nearly moved out to old abandoned Castlewood to live alone in utter solitude—till the news came of your troubles over here in England, and gave me the perfect excuse to leave. I left home, Harry, with the worst reputation any young gentleman in the colony ever had.”
Harry jumped to his feet, red-faced, and strode up and down the room, fists clenched. “I’ll break the bones of every last one of those lying scoundrels! I already did for Bob Clubber, the little sneaking backbiter who hangs around my lord at Greenway Court, sponging off every gentleman he meets!”
The narrator here cannot resist a brief, wry aside: what a marvellous, harrowing book the world would read, if every man wrote down every false rumor that had ever been spread about him since he came of age. The public does not merely know all your business, you see—it makes up a hundred times more of it, and swears it is the truth.
When George had finished telling his side of the story, it was Harry’s turn to unburden himself of all his own troubles over in England. He spoke fairly of Lord Castlewood, admitting he had no hard feelings over losing to him at cards—sportsmen paid their losses, after all—but he thought the man a cold-hearted wretch for refusing to help his chaplain when the poor man was in desperate need, dismissing him with thin, lying excuses. He had settled matters with Will Esmond after the crooked horse-dealing incident, too: had given him such a punch right to the face that his nose swelled black and blue for a fortnight. He thought the Countess a selfish, card-obsessed old cat, and Lady Fanny a frivolous, shameless flirt—all stories he’d heard, of course, from Lady Maria, who knew them better than anyone.
And on the subject of Maria, Harry was unshakable, his face flushing bright red as he spoke. “I know it’s imprudent. I know Madam will raise holy hell when she finds out, and fight Maria tooth and nail. But I won’t leave her. She’s been too good to me. You remember, when I was dead broke? She brought every last jewel she owned, and all her saved-up guineas, and dumped the whole lot in my lap. I’d sooner be made Governor of the whole of Virginia tomorrow than turn my back on her. I’ve given her my word, George. What’s said is said. I’ve made my bed, and I’ll lie in it.”
George sighed. “I was sent here by our mother to break that engagement, if I can. I can’t imagine a marriage more unhappy for you. But I will never, ever tell you to break your word to a woman who has stood by you.”
The two brothers had struck that quiet, loyal bargain between them the very first night they met. But after George’s long, sharp chat with Aunt Bernstein, that worldly old woman who knew every secret of the Esmond family, he had begun to doubt. He could not urge Harry to break his promise—but he was already turning over plans in his head, thinking of a way to put Lady Maria’s affection to the test, to see if she truly loved Harry for himself, or only for the Esmond fortune waiting for him back in Virginia.
