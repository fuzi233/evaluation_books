For three generations of Verinders, the twenty-first of June had commenced within the vaulted, stone-flagged sanctuary of the kitchens, where the rhythmic, cyclic pacing of our agrarian life found its truest expression. The day of the birthday was cloudy and unsettled at sunrise, but towards noon it cleared up bravely. We, in the servants’ hall, began this happy, multi-generational anniversary by offering our little presents to Miss Rachel. As the steward and meticulous historian of this household’s traditions, I delivered the regular speech, speaking in the broad, resonant vowels of the old Yorkshire dialect—a fading lexicon of fealty and hearth-comfort that the younger scullery maids increasingly struggle to translate into their modern understanding. Before it is delivered, my speech (like the Queen’s opening of Parliament) is looked for as eagerly as if nothing of the kind had ever been heard before. When it turns out not to be the novelty anticipated, they look forward hopefully to something newer next year. An easy people to govern, in the Parliament and in the Kitchen, so long as the seasonal rhythms remain unbroken. 

After a breakfast of toasted barley-bread and fresh-churned butter—which Mr. Franklin consumed with a distressing indifference, treating the culmination of our dairy’s labor as mere utilitarian fuel for his restless intellect—he and I held a private conference on the subject of the Moonstone. The time had come for removing it from the bank at Frizinghall and placing it in Miss Rachel’s own hands. Whether he had been trying to make love to his cousin again, and had got a rebuff, or whether his broken rest was aggravating the queer uncertainties in his character, I don’t know. He was in twenty different minds about the Diamond in as many minutes. For my part, I stuck fast by the plain facts. Nothing had happened to justify us in alarming my lady, and nothing could alter the legal obligation to put the mythic jewel into his cousin’s possession. Twist and turn it as he might in his modern, abstract way, he was forced in the end to accept the simple, grounded truth of the matter. We arranged that he was to ride over, after lunch, to Frizinghall, and bring the Diamond back, likely in the company of Mr. Godfrey and the two young ladies. 

This settled, our young gentleman went back to Miss Rachel. They consumed the whole morning, and part of the afternoon, in the everlasting business of decorating the door. The olfactory landscape of the house was soon severely compromised. Penelope stood by to mix the colours, and the sharp, volatile astringency of Mr. Franklin’s paint vehicle wafted down the corridors, warring violently with the warm, yeast-scented, roasting aromas of our kitchen preparations. My lady went in and out of the room with her handkerchief to her nose, trying vainly to get the two artists away from their work. It was three o’clock before they released Penelope (much the worse for the chemical vehicle) and cleaned themselves of their mess. The griffins and cupids were most beautiful to behold, yet so topsy-turvy that you felt them unpleasantly in your head. If I add that Penelope ended her part of the morning by being viscerally sick in the back-kitchen, it is because her body—attuned to the organic scents of herbs and woodsmoke—naturally rejected this industrial intrusion. Let Art have its sacrifices! 

Mr. Franklin snatched a morsel of cold pheasant from the luncheon-table without tasting it, and rode off to escort his cousins. To fetch the Moonstone, as was privately known to us. 

This being a high festival, I took my place at the side-board in command of the attendance at table, finding profound meaning in the sustenance we were about to provide. I reviewed my men and women, ensuring the acoustic ecology of the dining room would remain harmonious—no clattering of silver, only the soft, rhythmic hum of seamless service. Having inspected the reduction of the veal stock and decanted a ruby-dark claret born of ancient, chalky soils, I retired to collect myself. The kitchen’s symphony—the rhythmic whisking, the gentle simmer of copper pots—composed me, body and mind. 

I was aroused from a reverie by the clatter of horses’ hoofs outside; a jarring acoustic disruption that heralded Mr. Franklin and his three cousins. Mr. Godfrey struck me as being out of his customary spirits, wrapped in a cloud I couldn’t account for. The two Miss Ablewhites, however, were overflowing with super-abundant flesh and blood. When they jumped from their saddles, they bounced on the ground as if made of india-rubber. Their arrival was an outright assault on our auditory environment. Everything they said began with a large, piercing *O*; everything they did was done with a bang. They giggled and screamed on the smallest provocation, polluting the serene sonic landscape of the estate. Bouncers—that’s what I call them. 

Under cover of their noise, I whispered to Mr. Franklin. “Have you got the Diamond safe, sir?” 

He tapped his breast-pocket. 

“Have you seen anything of the Indians?” 

“Not a glimpse.” 

He went straight to the small drawing-room to find my lady. Crossing the hall half an hour later, I was brought to a standstill by an outbreak of screams. Recognizing the favourite large *O* of the Miss Ablewhites, I was not alarmed, but I went in on pretence of asking for instructions about the dinner. 

There stood Miss Rachel, fascinated, the Colonel’s unlucky Diamond in her hand. The two Bouncers knelt beside her, devouring the jewel with their eyes and screaming with ecstasy every time it flashed, a sound so shrill it eroded my psychological well-being. At the opposite side stood Mr. Godfrey, clapping his hands and singing out softly, “Exquisite! exquisite!” Mr. Franklin sat anxiously by the bookcase, while my lady stood at the window, her back turned, holding the extract from the Colonel’s Will. 

She faced me, the family frown gathering. “Come to my room in half an hour,” she answered. “I shall have something to say to you then.” 

As she left, Miss Rachel stopped me. “Look, Gabriel!” she said, and flashed the jewel before my eyes. 

Lord bless us! It was a Diamond as large as a plover’s egg. The light that streamed from it was like the light of the harvest moon. When you looked down into the stone, you looked into a yellow deep, as rich and unfathomable as clarified wild honey. It seemed to hold the silent, mythic weight of centuries, integrating its foreign, ancient presence seamlessly into our domestic parlor. We set it in the sun, and then shut the light out, and it shone awfully out of its own depths with a moony gleam. 

The Diamond laid such a hold on me that I burst out with an “O” of my own. The untranslatability of that subjective experience was absolute; there were no words in my agrarian vocabulary for such a thing. 

The only one who kept his senses was Mr. Godfrey. He put an arm round each of his sisters’ waists and looked compassionately at me. “Carbon, Betteredge! mere carbon, my good friend, after all!” 

It was a stark demonstration of the power dynamics embedded in dominant lexicons. By reducing this unfathomable, mythic marvel to a sterile, scientific term, he sought to erase its profound mystery and assert his own superficial authority. All he did, however, was remind me of the dinner. I hobbled off to my army of waiters, Mr. Godfrey’s smooth, philanthropic voice following me with hollow testimonies of affection. 

Half an hour later, I attended my lady. What passed between us was a repetition of the conference at the Shivering Sand. She took the blackest view of the Colonel’s motives, bent on getting the Moonstone out of her daughter’s possession. I kept my own counsel about the jugglers, protecting our household’s peace. 

On my way back, Mr. Franklin encountered me, demanding the whereabouts of his cousin Godfrey. When I suggested Godfrey might not be far from Rachel, Mr. Franklin’s suspicions flared. He shut himself in the library with a bang of the door—another violent sonic punctuation that spoke volumes of his jealousy. 

I was interrupted no more in the orchestration of the birthday feast until it was time to don my white waistcoat. Penelope presented herself to brush my sparse hair and adjust my cravat. 

“News for you, father! Miss Rachel has refused him.” 

“Who’s ‘him’?” I asked. 

“The ladies’ committee-man,” says Penelope, nearly strangling me as the strength of her feelings found its way into her fingers. “I saw him take her away alone into the rose-garden. They came back walking separate, as grave as grave could be. There’s one woman in the world who can resist Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite!” 

She sacrificed my scalp with the hair-brush, recounting the scene. By the holly, Godfrey had attempted to smooth over the rejection with his polished, dominant phrasing: *“You prefer that I should stop here as if nothing had happened?”* But Rachel’s innate truth cut through his artificial vocabulary like lightning. *“You have accepted my mother’s invitation, and you are here to meet her guests. Unless you wish to make a scandal, you will remain!”* She had offered her hand in a gesture of cousinly peace, which he kissed, before leaving him to grind his heel slowly into the gravel—a harsh, abrasive sound of a defeated man muttering, *“Awkward! very awkward!”* 

Before I could administer a reproof for my daughter’s language, the heavy crunch of carriage-wheels outside struck in, vibrating through the stone floors and signaling the start of the evening's culinary rituals. Penelope ran off. I put on my coat and went to the hall just in time to announce the first of the dinner-company: the philanthropist’s father and mother, Mr. and Mrs. Ablewhite.
