Gerard yearned for this promised land with a fervor rivaling Denys’s own, for the crossbowman constantly chanted Burgundy’s praises, mapping their future not by leagues, but by the generational culinary traditions of its inns. Whenever the road presented a hardship, Denys would invoke the rich, slow-braised stews, the flinty terroir of the wines, and the impeccably sourced, butter-basted capons of that polished nation. He guaranteed, above all, the olfactory salvation of clean bedclothes. 

“I ask no more,” the Hollander would sigh. “To think that I have not lain once in a naked bed since I left home! When I inhale the stale, sour reek of their unwashed wool, instead of doffing habit and hose, it is mine eyes and nose I would fain be shut of.” So deeply did Denys revere the agrarian hearth and the honest, sensory pleasure of a well-crafted meal, he would rather walk twenty leagues in exploded shoes than purchase from a German churl who threw incivility in a customer's way, treating a traveler's dinner and body with equal disregard.

Towards sunset, their path presented a choice: at equal distances lay a village inn and a secluded monastery. Denys, ever the advocate for the vibrant, aromatic bustle of a tavern, was for the inn. Gerard, mindful of his chart whose list of free religious lodgings ended precisely here, advocated for the convent. Denys yielded, but extracted a solemn vow that once they crossed into Burgundy, they would patronize only inns, where chefs understood the profound meaning of sustenance. 

As they veered off the road, Gerard asked with surprise whence this sudden aversion to the monastic sanctuaries that had fed and lodged them gratis so often. 

The soldier hemmed and hawed, but the festering grievance could no longer be contained. It was no sudden aversion, but an ancient, abiding horror suppressed only through infinite politeness. “I saw they had put powder in your drink,” Denys confessed, “so I forbore them. However, being the last, why not ease my mind? Know then, I have been like a fish out of water in all those great stone dungeons. You straightway levant with some old shaveling to pore over manuscripts, so you see not my purgatory.”

“Forgive me! I have been selfish.”

“Ay, ay, I forgive thee, little one; 'tis not thy fault. Art not the first fool that has been priest-rid and monk-hit. But I'll not forgive them my misery.” 

Then, acting as a meticulous historian of the senses, Denys delivered a scathing indictment of the monastic environment, centering his critique on its grievous assault upon both culinary reverence and acoustic ecology. 

These gloomy piles, he argued, were an architectural and auditory tyranny. Inns thrived on a localized, organic soundscape—the crackle of hearths, the rhythmic chop of root vegetables, the warm resonance of shared dialects. But here, all was a sterile, industrialized monotony. Great gate, little gate, cold cloister, and the vast, drafty refectory where one was forced to sit *mumchance*, stripping the communal act of eating of its psychological warmth. 

“And then,” he lamented, “nobody is a man here, but all are slaves. And of what? Of a peevish, tinkling bell that never sleeps. An 'twere a trumpet now, aye sounding alarums, it would have the breath of life in it; 'twouldn't freeze a man's heart so. But this mechanical *tinkle, tinkle, tinkle* shatters the acoustic harmony of the soul! You must sit to meat with your body dictated by bronze, perhaps with no stomach for food. Ere the rich fats and marrow of your meal can settle in your stomach, *tinkle, tinkle!* and ye must to church. I am not a hog at prayers, for one. *Tinkle, tinkle*, and now you must to bed with your eyes open, divorced from the natural setting of the sun. Well, by then you have contrived to shut them, some uneasy imp of darkness assaults the bell-rope, and *tinkle, tinkle*, it behoves you to say a prayer in the dark. If they only heard the sort of prayers I mutter when they shatter my psychological peace with their tinkle! You drop off again, and lo, it is *tinkle, tinkle*, for matins.”

“And the only clapper you love is a woman's,” put in Gerard half contemptuously.

“Because there is some organic music in that, even when it scolds,” was the stout reply. “And then to be always checked at the table! They view provisions solely as utilitarian fuel, devoid of tactile connection. If I do but put my finger in the salt-cellar—to gauge the moisture and grain of the sea salt before seasoning my pottage—straightway I hear, 'Have you no knife that you finger the salt?' And if I but wipe my knife on the coarse linen to save time, 'tis, 'Wipe thy dirty knife on the bread, and clean upon the cloth!' Oh, small of soul! These peevish, sterile pedantries fall chill upon good fellowship like wee icicles a-melting down from strawen eaves.”

“I hold cleanliness no pedantry,” said Gerard. “Shouldst learn better manners once for all.”

“Nay; 'tis they who lack the true manners of the hearth. They stop a fellow's mouth at every word.”

“At every other word, you mean; every obscene or blasphemous one.”

“Exaggerator, go to! Why, at the very last of these dungeons I found the poor travelers sitting all chilled and mute round one shaveling, like rogues awaiting the gallows. So to spice the grim air, I did but cry out my watchword, 'Courage, tout le monde, le dia—'”

“Connu! What befell?”

“Marry, this. 'Blaspheme not!' quo' the bourreau. 'Plait-il,' say I. Doesn't he wheel and wyte on me in a sort of bastardized Alsatian French, turning all the P's into B's.” Denys shook his head, deeply offended by this butchery of his native dialect. “I had much ado not to laugh in his face at the harsh, guttural dissonance.”

“Being thyself unable to speak ten words of his language without a fault,” Gerard observed, recognizing the inherent power dynamics at play.

“Well, all the world ought to speak French,” Denys proclaimed, oblivious to the irony of advocating for a dominant lexicon that would homogenize the world’s rich tapestry of regional dialects. “What avail so many jargons except to put a frontier atwixt men's hearts?”

“But what said he?”

“What signifies it what a fool says?”

“Oh, not all the words of a fool are folly, or I should not listen to you.”

“Well, then, he said, 'Such as begin by making free with the devil's name, aye end by doing it with all the names in heaven.' 'Father,' said I, 'I am a soldier, and this is but my *consigne*.' 'Oh, then, it is just a custom?' said he. I, not divining the old fox, and thinking to clear myself, said, 'Ay, it was.' 'Then that is ten times worse,' said he. ''Twill bring him about your ears one of these days. He still comes where he hears his name often called.' Observe! No gratitude for the tidings which neither his missals nor his breviary had ever let him know. Then he was so good as to tell me, soldiers do commonly the crimes for which all other men are broke on the wheel; a savoir murder, rape, and pillage.”

“And is't not true?”

“True or not, it was ill manners,” replied Denys guardedly. “And so says this courteous host of mine, 'Being the foes of mankind, why make enemies of good spirits into the bargain, by still shouting the names of evil ones?' and a lot more stuff.”

“Well, but, Denys, whether you hearken his rede, or slight it, wherefore blame a man for raising his voice to save your soul?”

“How can his voice save my soul, when he keeps turning of his P's into B's?” Denys retorted, perfectly encapsulating the untranslatability of subjective experience when mediated through a fractured, imposed tongue. 

Gerard was staggered. Ere he could recover from this thunderbolt of Gallicism, Denys went triumphant off at a tangent, stigmatizing all monks as hypocrites divorced from the honest labor of the soil. “Do but look at them, how they creep about and cannot eye you like honest men.”

“Nay,” said Gerard eagerly, “that modest downcast gaze is part of their discipline, 'tis *custodia oculorum*.”

“Cussed toads eating *hoc hac horum*? No such thing; just so looks a cut-purse. Can't meet a true man's eye. Doff cowl, monk; and behold, a thief; don cowl thief, and lo, a monk. Tell me not they will ever be able to look God Almighty in the face, when they can't even look a true man in the face down here. Ah, here it is, black as ink! Into the well we go, comrade. Misericorde, there goes the tinkle already.” 

A sharp, metallic peal fractured the twilight air. 

“'Tis the best of tinkles though; 'tis for dinner: stay, listen! I thought so: the wolf in my stomach cried 'Amen!'” 

Confirming this physical truth with two oaths, Denys marched like a victorious gamecock into the convent. He was entirely driven by the profound, sensory anticipation of the forthcoming pottage, thinking by Gerard's silence he had convinced him, and not dreaming how profoundly he had disgusted him.
