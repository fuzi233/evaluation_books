The morning of March first dawned with a quiet strength, as if the city itself were waking from a long sleep. I rose early, pulled back my curtains, and there—above the rooftops, almost level with the clouds—rose the dome of St. Paul’s, dark blue and solemn in the foggy light. In that moment, something stirred deep within me. My spirit, long bound by routine and silence, seemed to stretch its wings, unburdened for the first time. A sudden warmth flooded through me: *this* was life—the real thing—beginning at last. That morning, my soul grew as fast as Jonah’s gourd under the sun.

“I did well to come,” I whispered, dressing quickly but carefully. “I can feel the pulse of London beating around me. Who would live their whole life in quiet corners, letting their mind rot in obscurity? Only a coward.”

Refreshed and composed, I descended to breakfast. The waiter—a man with silver hair and twenty years of service—entered with his tray. We spoke for ten minutes, and in that time, he became more than a servant; he became a familiar. I mentioned my uncles, Charles and Wilmot, who had once stayed here. He remembered them clearly, with respect. When I told him I was their niece, his manner shifted instantly—from cautious to courteous. He said I looked like Uncle Charles. Mrs. Barrett had often said the same. Now, I felt seen. No longer would I be met with hesitation or indifference.

Outside my window lay a narrow, peaceful street. Few people passed—nothing alarming, nothing overwhelming. I felt safe enough to venture forth alone.

After breakfast, I stepped into the world. Joy surged in my chest. To walk alone in London! It felt like an adventure. Soon I found myself in Paternoster Row, a place steeped in history. I entered a bookseller’s shop run by Mr. Jones, a dry, serious man behind his desk. I bought a small book—too expensive, really—but one I meant one day to send to Mrs. Barrett. For a moment, standing there, I felt both insignificant and radiant. He looked at me as if I were someone remarkable. And perhaps I was.

That morning, I lived more than I had in years. At St. Paul’s, I climbed to the dome. From there, London unfolded: the river winding like a ribbon, bridges arcing over it, churches rising like memories. Westminster stood ancient and proud; the Temple Gardens sparkled under the spring sun. Above it all, a pale blue sky stretched wide, softened by a thin veil of haze.

Descending, I wandered without plan, carried by a sense of freedom. I drifted into the heart of the city—the Strand, Cornhill—where the hum of commerce and movement filled the air. I crossed busy streets, not trembling, but bold. To move through this chaos alone—and to do so without fear—was a triumph. Later, I saw the West End, the parks, the elegant squares. But I loved the city best. There, life was real: urgent, loud, purposeful. The West End offered pleasure; the city offered meaning.

By two o’clock, I was faint and hungry—truly hungry, as I hadn’t been in years. I returned to my modest inn, where I ate simply: a joint and vegetables. They tasted better than any meal Miss Marchmont’s cook had ever prepared. Afterward, I lay on three chairs, weary but content. I slept, then woke, and thought—deeply—for hours.

My mind was clear, my resolve strong. I had nothing left to lose. The past was a wasteland I could no longer endure. If I failed now, who would care? If I died far from home—I had no home, really—then who would mourn?

I could suffer. I had learned to endure. Death held no terrors for me. I had faced it before, calmly. So I made a decision.

That evening, I asked my friend the waiter about ships sailing to Boue-Marine. Time was short. I needed to board that night. “Better take your berth now, ma'am,” he advised. I agreed. I paid my bill generously—more than I should have—and he smiled faintly, as if amused by my extravagance. Then he called a coach.

He warned the driver to take me directly to the wharf—not to leave me to the watermen. But the driver ignored him. Instead, he dropped me into a crowd of shouting boatmen.

Darkness pressed in. The coach sped away. The men lunged for me and my trunk. Their curses cut through the night. One grabbed my luggage. I waited. When another reached for me, I stepped forward sharply, shook off his hand, and boarded a boat. “Place the trunk beside me—just there,” I said firmly. The boatman obeyed. He became my ally.

The river was black as ink, lit only by scattered glimmers from buildings and ships. We glided along, passing vessel after vessel. By lantern-light, I read their names: *The Ocean*, *The Phoenix*, *The Consort*, *The Dolphin*. Then—*The Vivid*. That was mine.

As we neared her, I thought of Charon rowing souls across the Styx. The wind chilled my face. Rain fell from midnight clouds. Two rough rowers cursed beside me. Yet I felt neither fear nor sorrow. I was alert, alive. “Why?” I wondered. “Why am I not afraid?”

Then, suddenly, *The Vivid* emerged—white and sharp against the black water. “Here you are!” shouted the waterman. “Six shillings!”

“You ask too much,” I replied.

He refused to let me aboard until I paid. A young steward leaned over the rail, grinning. To stop the show, I handed over the money. Three times that day I’d paid crowns when I should have paid shillings. But I told myself: *It’s the price of learning.*

“They’ve cheated you!” the steward exclaimed when I stepped on deck.

“I knew it,” I said, and walked below.

In the ladies’ cabin, a stout, striking woman stood waiting. She eyed me suspiciously. “It’s unusual,” she muttered, “to board at this hour.” Her tone was cold.

“I’m already on board,” I replied. “Show me my berth.”

She complied—reluctantly. I removed my bonnet, arranged my things, and lay down. The hardest part was over. For now, I could rest. Until we reached port—then came the next chapter. But I couldn’t think ahead. Exhausted, I slipped into a half-dream.

All night, the stewardess talked—loudly, incessantly—to her son, who resembled her perfectly. They quarreled, made up, argued again. She claimed to write a letter to her father, reading aloud passages as if I weren’t there. Some spoke of a younger sister, Charlotte, on the verge of a reckless romance. The son mocked her words. She raged. They were odd, defiant, full of noise and pride.

Near dawn, she turned to talk of the “Watsons”—a family of passengers expected soon. “They’re worth a fortune to me,” she declared. “Every time they cross.”

At sunrise, the ship stirred. Passengers arrived. The Watsons—two men, two women—were greeted with fanfare. They were wealthy, confident. The women wore bright flowers, velvet cloaks, silk dresses—far too fine for a damp packet deck. The men were short, fat, vulgar. The oldest, the husband, looked like an oil barrel. Yet the young wife—beautiful, dazzling—laughed wildly, carefree.

I watched her. Her laughter felt hollow—like madness disguised as joy. Then, without warning, she tripped toward me, offering a camp-stool with a smile too wide, too bright. I declined politely. She danced away, heedless.

Beside her, a young girl arrived with a gentleman—her father, I later learned. She wore a simple print dress, a plain straw bonnet, a large shawl. Her look was quiet, dignified. Her father scanned the passengers, then glanced at me—displeased. He kissed her goodbye and left. The ship set sail.

Foreigners marvel at English girls traveling alone. They call it daring. Some say it’s masculine. Others blame education and religion. But this girl—Miss Ginevra Fanshawe—was different. She paced the deck, looking with disdain at the Watsons, then approached me.

“Do you like sea voyages?” she asked.

I admitted I’d never been on one. “So it will be new,” I said.

“Oh, how lovely!” she exclaimed. “I envy you. First impressions are always sweet. I’ve done this so many times, I’m completely *blasée*.”

I couldn’t help smiling.

“Why are you laughing?” she demanded, a little testy—but oddly endearing.

“Because you’re too young to be bored by anything.”

“I’m seventeen,” she retorted.

“You don’t look sixteen. Do you like traveling alone?”

“Bah! I don’t care. I’ve crossed the Channel ten times. But I never stay alone long—I make friends.”

“I doubt you’ll make many here,” I said, nodding toward the noisy Watsons.

“Not those awful people,” she snapped. “They should be steerage. Are you going to school?”

“No.”

“Where then?”

“I don’t know—only as far as Boue-Marine.”

She stared, then shrugged. “I’m going to school. Oh, I’ve been to so many foreign schools! And yet I’m still an ignoramus. I know nothing—except I dance and play beautifully. And I speak French and German… sort of. But I can’t read or write them well. Once they asked me to translate a page of German into English. I couldn’t. Papa was mortified. He says M. de Bassompierre—my godfather—has wasted all his money.”

She paused. “And I’m terrible at history, geography, arithmetic. I write English terribly—spelling, grammar, everything. And I’ve forgotten my religion. They call me Protestant, but I don’t know what that means. I used to be Lutheran in Bonn—dear Bonn!—where every nice girl had a admirer. ‘Schönes Mädchen,’ they’d say. I was so happy there.”

“And now?” I asked.

“Oh! At—_chose_,” she said.

Later, I learned “_chose_” was Villette, capital of Labassecour. A word she used whenever she forgot the real name—like a French habit she’d picked up.

“Do you like Villette?” I asked.

“Fine. The locals are dull and rude. But there are nice English families.”

“In a school?”

“Yes.”

“A good one?”

“Oh, no! Horrible. But I go out every Sunday. I ignore the teachers, the students, the lessons—send them _au diable_. And I get on just fine.”

She yawned. “You’re laughing again.”

“No—just thinking.”

“Tell me where you’re going.”

“Where fate leads me. My goal is to earn a living wherever I can.”

“To earn?” she gasped. “Are you poor?”

“As poor as Job.”

She paused. “Bah! How sad. But I know poverty. We’re poor at home—papa, mamma, all of us. Papa’s Captain Fanshawe, on half-pay. He’s noble, but our connections aren’t rich. Only my uncle De Bassompierre helps—educates us girls. I have five sisters, three brothers. We’ll marry older men with money. Augusta married Mr. Davies—he’s yellow from the fever in India, but rich. She has a carriage. We think she did well. That’s better than ‘earning a living.’”

She blinked. “By the way—are you clever?”

“No. Not at all.”

“You can play, sing, speak several languages?”

“Not at all.”

“Still, I think you are clever,” she said, yawning again.

“Shall you be sea-sick?”

“Will you?”

“Oh, terribly. I already feel it. I’ll go below. And I’ll tell that fat, hateful stewardess exactly what I think. *Heureusement je sais faire aller mon monde.*”

She disappeared.

For the rest of the afternoon, I remained alone on deck. I recall the calm, even happiness I felt—despite being adrift, uncertain, without a home. As the poet says:

> Stone walls do not a prison make,
> Nor iron bars—a cage.

So too, danger, loneliness, uncertainty—these are not true prisons, so long as the spirit is free and the mind engaged. So long as Liberty flaps her wings, and Hope lights the way.

I didn’t grow sick until after Margate. Then, the sea breeze felt divine. The waves heaved like giants. Seabirds soared. Sails gleamed on the horizon. The sky loomed quiet and vast. In my thoughts, I saw Europe—distant, golden, dream-like. Sunlight painted the coast in gold. Towns, towers, forests, rivers—all etched in light. Over it all, a rainbow arched from north to south—soft, grand, promising.

But then I dismissed it. Or perhaps let it stand—as a lesson:

Day-dreams are delusions of the demon.

Eventually, sickness overtook me. I stumbled below.

Miss Fanshawe’s berth was next to mine. During the storm, she tormented me with selfishness. She complained endlessly. The Watsons, equally ill, were stoic compared to her. I saw, then, the truth: some people—fair, fragile, careless—cannot endure hardship. They sour like spoiled wine. A man who marries such a woman must promise her perpetual sunshine.

Finally, furious at her constant whining, I said sharply, “Hold your tongue.”

It shocked her. But strangely, it brought her peace. And she liked me better for it.

As night fell, the sea grew rougher. Waves crashed against the hull. Furniture toppled. We all grew worse. Miss Fanshawe groaned, “I shall die!”

“Not yet, honey,” the stewardess said. “We’re nearly in port.”

And indeed, within minutes, the storm calmed. By midnight, we docked.

I was sorry. Yes, truly sorry. My brief reprieve was over. The hard work began again. On deck, the cold air and dark sky seemed to scold me for daring to be here. The lights of the foreign town glimmered like watching eyes. Friends welcomed the Watsons. A family took Miss Fanshawe away. I—no one came for me.

Yet I must go somewhere. Necessity doesn’t wait. As I gave the stewardess her fee—more than she expected—she surprised me with kindness. She not only directed me to a quiet, respectable inn, but summoned a commissionaire to escort me—though not my trunk, which had gone to customs.

And so I walked, alone, into the unknown.
