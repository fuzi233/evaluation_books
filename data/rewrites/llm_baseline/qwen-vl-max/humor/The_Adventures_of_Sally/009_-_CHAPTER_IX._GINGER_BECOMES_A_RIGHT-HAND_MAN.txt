Sally didn’t actually believe Ginger was dead—well, not until she saw him standing there in the doorway, hair like a bird’s nest, nose smudged with what looked like soot from a coal scuttle, and eyes wide with the kind of panic usually reserved for people who’ve just been caught sneaking into a house they don’t own.

And then it hit her: *He’s alive!* Not floating face-down in the Thames like some drowned opera singer. Not even close. He was breathing. Standing. And slightly damp, probably from crawling under beds like a fugitive in a bad detective novel.

She let out a shriek that would have made a startled parrot blush. It wasn’t laughter exactly—it was more like a burst of pure, unfiltered joy wrapped in a scream. Ginger blinked. Then blinked again. Then said, “I say, you know—this is rather odd.”

“Odd?” Sally gasped, still giggling like a schoolgirl who’d just discovered chocolate. “You’re under my bed! I thought you were gone forever!”

“I’m not gone,” said Ginger, adjusting his collar as if he’d just been rescued from a war zone. “Just temporarily misplaced. Like a sock in the laundry.”

“Why were you under my bed?”

“Because Mrs. Meecher was after me,” he said, as though this explained everything. “She’s got a dog. A woolly rat. Toto. I hate dogs. Especially ones that think they’re detectives.”

Sally paused. “Wait. You’re hiding from a woman because of her dog?”

“Well, yes. But also because I’m behind on rent. That helps. The dog makes it worse.”

“You’re behind on rent? But you were a millionaire last week!”

“I was,” said Ginger, sighing dramatically. “Then I went back to the casino and lost it all. Then I came to America. Then I tried to be a waiter. Then I broke five plates. Then I got docked. Now I’m basically living off hope and the occasional crust of bread.”

“Ah,” said Sally, nodding wisely. “So you’re poor now.”

“Very poor. And very ashamed. Also covered in dust. And possibly fleas. I don’t know about fleas. But I did sleep on the floor.”

“That explains the smut on your nose,” said Sally. “And the fact that you look like a chimney sweep who lost a bet.”

Ginger touched his nose. “Oh! Is it still there? I thought I’d brushed it off.”

“Nope. Still there. Like a tiny, black beauty spot. Very fashionable, really. Though perhaps not on a man who’s supposed to be dignified.”

“I’ll wash,” said Ginger, already moving toward the sink like a man possessed.

“Good idea. Use my soap. We Americans are generous. Unless you’re allergic to generosity. Then I’ll get you something else.”

“Thanks awfully,” said Ginger, splashing water like a man trying to drown his sorrows—and possibly a few germs.

Once clean and vaguely human again, Sally turned to the real issue: why was he hiding?

“Mrs. Meecher,” he said, looking at her with the solemnity of a man confessing to murder. “She’s relentless. She thinks I owe her twelve dollars. For letting me live here. Which I do. But I can’t pay it. So I hide. Like a criminal. Or a squirrel.”

“Twelve dollars?” Sally said, astonished. “That’s not much! Why not just tell her you’ll pay next week?”

“Because she doesn’t believe me. And she has Toto. Who, I repeat, is a woolly rat with a grudge.”

Sally sighed. “Well, I’ll pay her.”

“No!” Ginger protested, turning red. “I can’t let you do that. It’s... it’s not right. I’m not a charity case. I’m a man with dreams and a future!”

“And yet,” said Sally, “you’re currently employed as a waiter at a place on Second Avenue where you break plates and cry into your soup.”

“I do not cry into my soup!”

“Do too.”

“Do not.”

“Do too.”

“Fine,” said Ginger, throwing up his hands. “But I won’t take your money. I’ll work. I’ll earn it. I’ll become someone important. Maybe even a CEO.”

“Or a right-hand man,” said Sally, suddenly inspired. “My brother Fillmore needs one. He’s a theatrical manager. Busy. Important. Needs someone to carry his hat, answer his calls, and occasionally pretend to understand the plot of a play.”

“Right-hand man?” Ginger repeated, as though the phrase had never existed before. “Is that a job? Do they give you a badge?”

“No badge. But respect. And maybe lunch.”

“I could do that,” said Ginger, eyes lighting up. “I’m good at following orders. Mostly.”

“Perfect. Let’s go see him now. He’s at the Astor. We’ll walk. Then we’ll eat. Then we’ll make him hire you.”

“Are you sure he’ll want me?”

“He’ll want anyone who doesn’t quit mid-sentence or faint at the sight of a chicken.”

They reached the door—just as Toto began barking furiously, chasing imaginary mice across the hallway. Mrs. Meecher appeared like a storm cloud, arms crossed, eyes narrowed.

“Mister Kemp!” she announced. “I’ve been looking for you!”

Sally stepped forward, bright as a summer day. “Oh, Mrs. Meecher! What a surprise! Mr. Kemp is a dear friend of mine. We met in France. We’re going to reminisce about old times—then I’m taking him to see my brother. He’s terribly busy. If we’re late, he’ll never forgive us.”

Mrs. Meecher frowned. “Toto needs a walk.”

“Oh, how sweet!” said Sally. “But we’re in such a hurry. And Mr. Kemp is quite tired. He’s just come from a long journey. And frankly, I don’t think he’s up to walking a dog. Not today.”

She swept Ginger away before Mrs. Meecher could protest further. Outside, Ginger took a deep breath.

“You know,” he said, “you’re amazing. No one else would have saved me from a dog and a landlady in one go.”

“Don’t mention it,” said Sally. “Now, let’s talk about your uncle. Mr. Carmyle told me you insulted him.”

“Not insulted,” said Ginger. “More like… emotionally honest. We had dinner. He wanted me to apologize to Scrymgeour. I refused. Then the soup arrived. Thick mulligatawny. And he started inhaling it through his moustache. Like a walrus. I asked him not to. He didn’t like it. Things escalated. Fish course. Steak. Then I said I was done with the family. And left.”

“Noble,” said Sally, impressed. “You stood up for yourself.”

“I was feeling brave,” said Ginger. “Also, I’d just talked to Bruce. He gets on my nerves. Always talking about ‘family legacy’ and ‘duty.’ I said, ‘No thanks. I’m going to be my own person.’”

“Good for you,” said Sally. “And speaking of Bruce—he said he was sending me books. Did he send them?”

“No,” said Ginger. “But I gave him your address.”

Sally stopped walking. “He never sent me any books.”

“Oh,” said Ginger. “Well, he said he would. I assumed he would.”

Sally stared into the distance. “So he traveled three thousand miles just to send me books?”

“Possibly,” said Ginger. “Or maybe he just wanted to see you again.”

Sally blushed. “That’s absurd.”

“Is it?” said Ginger. “Because I wouldn’t mind seeing you again. In fact, I’d like to see you every day.”

Sally smiled. “Well, now that you’re my right-hand man, you will.”

They hailed a cab. Inside, Ginger leaned back, relaxed. “This feels like the beginning of something big.”

“It is,” said Sally. “The beginning of your career. Your dignity. Your future.”

“And possibly,” said Ginger, “a promotion to full-time assistant with benefits.”

“Eventually,” said Sally. “But first—lunch. And then we’ll introduce you to Fillmore. He’ll love you. Probably.”

“I hope so,” said Ginger. “Otherwise I’ll have to go back to being a waiter. And that’s just depressing.”

“Don’t worry,” said Sally. “You’re not a waiter anymore. You’re a right-hand man. And that’s a lot more dignified. Even if you do wear a tie that’s half a size too small.”

“I’ll fix it,” said Ginger. “With dignity.”
