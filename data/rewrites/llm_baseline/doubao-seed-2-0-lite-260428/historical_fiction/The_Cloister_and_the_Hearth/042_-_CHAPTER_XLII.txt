Letters of glowing fire blazed across the face of the town church wall that morning, their scrawled question bold as if scrawled by a hand from the clouds: *Why is no mass said for our good duke, in this hour of all our trouble?* A crowd of awestruck townsfolk gaped up at the writing for hours as it slowly faded, left frozen to the cobblestones too dumbfounded to move. The municipal council, determined not to be outdone in loyalty to their temporal ruler by a supposed angelic miracle, poured coin into a full schedule of special masses. The old cure, our long-time acquaintance, profited handsomely from the rush of pious spending, a windfall he enjoyed far more than he would ever admit. I would not have paused over such a commonplace local miracle at all, save that it lined his pockets just enough to let the rest of this tale unfold.

Shortly after, Denys was brought before the bastard Anthony of Burgundy. “Why break his heart *and* disgrace him?” his allies pleaded. “Make him swear on the cross hilt of his sword not to quit your service until this sedition is entirely put down.”
Denys had no choice but to agree. He loosed three roaring volleys of oaths to seal his vow, and walked away with his crossbow, his sword, and his liberty restored.
The troops halted for rest that afternoon at *The Three Fish* inn, and Marion the barmaid who knew Denys well shouted the moment she caught sight of him, “Bad luck to me! Who’d ever have thought to see your face back here again?” When she saw he did not laugh at the jest, but looked heavy-hearted and stung, she pressed him for the trouble weighing on him.
He told her how he had been forced to part ways with Gerard, bound for Rome alone while he was stuck serving the bastard’s campaign in Flanders. Marion brushed his fears aside with bright good sense. Gerard was too handsome, too gentle-mannered, ever to come to real harm, she argued. Every woman on the road would take his side, no matter what befell him. “It must go one of two ways for him,” she said, “either he falls in with a good party of pilgrims bound for Rome, or he turns for home and will be back in Holland before you are—what with all this fighting in Flanders they say you’ve got ahead of you.” She fetched out his gold pieces he had left in her keeping, and refused every coin he tried to press on her, no matter how much he urged her.
Denys half believed her, not least because her words lined up exactly with what he wanted to be true, and he was cheered no small bit by her stubborn honesty. It made him think better of the wide, lonely world his little friend Gerard was walking through all alone.

Small bands of foot soldiers, down to pairs and trios, were already tramping the roads toward Flanders, most of them penniless, passed from town to town by bailiffs who held writs ordering every innkeeper to feed and lodge them free of charge. Anthony of Burgundy overtook dozens of these wandering parties, gathered them under his standard, and crossed the Flemish frontier at the head of six hundred men. His brother Baldwyn met him there with fresh troops, arms, and cartloads of provisions, and the full force marched through town after town in formal battle array, met not with resistance, but with loud, cheering crowds. This unreserved loyalty from folk who were supposed to be in open rebellion drew bitter grumbles from the ranks.
“This so-called rebellion’s nothing but a fool’s joke,” growled Simon, a surly soldier who had unofficially appointed himself Denys’s constant comrade.
Denys said nothing, but he swore a quiet private vow to Saint Mars: if this sedition that had cost him Gerard turned out to be nothing but a petty squabble no one actually cared about, he would put a crossbow bolt straight through Anthony of Burgundy’s heart.

That same afternoon they caught sight of a heavily walled, fortified town, and a whisper rippled through the little army that this was the disaffected stronghold they had come to reduce. But as they drew near, they found the great main gate standing wide open, and each of the two flanking towers guarded by no more than a single sentinel. The advancing force relaxed their formation and marched careless, half expecting to be welcomed with cheers like every other town on the road.
When they were no more than a furlong from the walls, the drawbridge crossing the moat creaked slowly upward until it stood vertical, pressed flat against the outer fortifications. The very instant it locked into place, the portcullis at the gate rattled down with a deafening clang, and every tower stretch of curtain wall bristled with lances and crossbowmen. A low, angry hum ran through the front ranks and spread all the way to the rear.
“Halt!” the bastard shouted. The order echoed down the line, and the whole column froze. “Herald to the gate!”
A pursuivant spurred his horse out of the ranks, halted twenty yards from the walls, and blew a sharp summons on his bugle, his herald’s standard dangling from its end. A tall figure clad head to toe in glinting brass armour appeared on the battlements above the gate. A few sharp, fiery words passed between them, too far for anyone below to hear, but their meaning was plain: the herald blew a single fierce, warning note, and galloped back to the lines with his face set for battle. The bastard rode out to meet him, and their heads were barely together for two seconds before he turned in his saddle and roared, “Pioneers to the front!”
In an instant, roadside hedges were levelled flat, the force set up camp just out of arrow range from the walls, and mounted officers galloped off south, east, and west to every friendly nearby town, to drag back catapults, palisades, wheeled protective mantlets, raw hides, tar barrels, carpenters, provisions, and every supply needed for a full siege.
For the first time in weeks, Denys’s eyes brightened. The moment the portcullis clanged shut, the dull weight in his chest lifted, and he shouted loud enough for half the line to hear, “Courage, everyone! The devil of our bad luck is dead that day!”

The besiegers settled their camp a furlong from the walls, cut service roads, kept their pikemen at the ready for the eventual assault, and sent their sappers, pioneers, catapult crews, and crossbowmen to the front lines. They set to work filling the moat, digging mines under the walls, and preparing to breach the stone, all while taking constant fire from arrows, quarrels, bolts, and heavy stones hurled from the battlements. Every one of the siege crews needed a hundred eyes to stay alive, working with a vigilance, individual daring, and skill that made medieval sieges far more vivid and dramatic to watch than any modern battlefield shrouded in gunpowder smoke.
Their first move was to send carpenters rolling forward behind heavy wheeled mantlets, to build a tall, strong stockade right on the very edge of the moat. A handful of men lost their lives to defending archers there, but not many: Denys was part of a large force of crossbowmen who rolled their own mantlets forward, shooting over the workmen’s heads at every defender who dared show a face above the walls, every loophole and arrow slit that looked out over the work site. Protected by that concentrated, unrelenting fire, the carpenters soon finished the high palisade, blocking most ordinary missiles from reaching their work.

The defenders had expected exactly this, though. That same night, they rolled heavy wooden penthouses out over the top of the curtain walls, sliding the support beams into pre-cut square holes built into the masonry. From these elevated platforms, they could fire down at the besiegers with far more freedom and range than they could ever manage through the slanted, fixed loopholes cut into the stone below. The besiegers responded by dragging up their mangonels, and began hurling huge stone boulders at the woodworks, smashing them to splinters. At the same time, they built a tall triangular wooden assault tower, as high as the curtain walls itself, and parked it just out of the defenders’ maximum firing range.
The sight of that tower struck fear into the town’s garrison. These rolling wooden fortresses had taken dozens of towns before. The defenders immediately began digging a mine under the stretch of moat directly facing the tower, to lay explosives to blow it apart the moment it drew near. The besiegers were digging mines of their own, on the opposite side of the walls, their target the heavy square barbican gatehouse they planned to bring crashing down.

All this time, Denys stood behind his mantlet, paired with another arbalestrier, protecting the workmen and landing perfect shot after perfect shot at the defenders on the walls. His marksmanship soon earned him the respect of an unseen enemy archer, who kept sending feathered “compliments” quivering into the wood of Denys’s mantlet. One bolt buried itself an inch from the narrow slit Denys was squinting through at that exact moment.
“Peste!” Denys laughed out loud. “You shoot like a master, my friend! Come out and let me shake your hand! Talent like yours shouldn’t hide! Comrade, that’s one of those cursed Englishmen, with his half-ell long shafts. I won’t die till I’ve had a shot at London’s own walls!”

On the defenders’ side of the moat, a tall, commanding figure soon drew every eye. He paced the battlements unflinching under constant fire, clad in full polished brass armour, carrying a remarkably light but absurdly long lance he used to direct every movement of the garrison. Almost every setback the besiegers suffered that week could be traced back to that tall knight and his long lance.
Any reader will immediately ask, why didn’t Denys just shoot him? Denys did shoot him, every single day. Every other arbalestrier in the camp shot at him, every archer too. Every bolt went straight for that brass armour, and none of them seemed to do a single bit of harm. The knight walked his battlements as calmly as a veteran skipper paces his ship’s deck in a rainstorm, oblivious to the bolts bouncing off his plate. Eventually the besiegers grew so frustrated they stopped wasting good steel on him, and just shouted curses up at him instead. He did not even react to those.
Gunpowder has spoiled all the glory of war, Denys often thought. War has never done much for the solid peaceable interests of ordinary folk, but in the old days it had purpose: it painted bright pictures, sang great epics, gave the world its Iliads. Now all you get is invisible butchery under a thick pall of smoke a furlong wide, no heroism for a poet to record, no story to tell that doesn’t sound like a senseless, ugly racket. This siege was a small, petty affair, but any wandering minstrel could stand on a nearby rise and see every bit of it, make a honest penny singing of it to tavern crowds. It was fair sport, for the time.

The day was bright and crisp, almost but not quite frosty. All the besiegers’ efforts were concentrated on a 250-yard stretch of walls, holding two curtain sections and two towers: one the square barbican gate, the other capped with an overlapping pointed roof built over a stone machicolation, its row of angled crenels glowering down at the besiegers like the gaps between the teeth of some great stone beast. The penthouses on the curtains were already half shattered by the besiegers’ mangonels and trebuchets. The besiegers’ side of the moat looked like a teeming, chaotic giant arsenal, swarming with workmen hauling stones, bundles of fascines, mantlets, and rows of tar barrels. Hammers tapped nonstop all day, horses and carts rattled up in endless lines carrying fresh supplies. Only if you looked close could you see the constant hail of arrows flying back and forth, the great cranes that did not tenderly lower stones but hurled them through the air, and the occasional workman or soldier lying still on the ground, a feathered bolt sticking out of him, blood seeping slow into the mud.

A covered, sloped siege penthouse the men called a “cat” crept slowly toward the curtain across the moat, its crews filling the water beneath it with rubbish and fascines. The tall brass knight burned the first two cats by hurling blazing tar barrels down on them, so the besiegers built the next one with a steep roof covered in raw hides that the flaming pitch could not stick to. The knight ordered a small trebuchet on the walls to drop stones directly into the moat, and at last got the range right, sending a great boulder crashing straight through the new cat’s roof. Baldwyn of Burgundy lost his temper when he saw the damage, and ordered the huge main catapult that had been battering the curtain’s woodworks to be turned, aimed slantwise directly at that unkillable brass knight on the battlements.
Denys and his unseen English archer paused to take their dinner then, as they often did. The two rivals, who never laid eyes on each other, had struck up a quiet distant friendship across the moat, leaving little scraps of bread on the tops of their mantlets for the other to collect.

The big catapult was a marvel of medieval engineering. A whole fir tree was cut down, set to pivot on a horizontal axis between two tall posts, left deliberately unbalanced, with three quarters of its length on the besiegers’ side. The short, thick butt end was weighted with half a ton of lead. Before firing, crews used a powerful winch to pull the long, tapering arm all the way down to the ground, locked it in place with a bolt, and settled the fifty-pound stone into a sling fixed to the tip. When the bolt was released, gravity did all the rest: the heavy weighted butt end swung furiously downward, the long arm snapped upward with incredible force, and the stone was hurled high through the air.
This first shot missed the tall knight, but crashed straight through the roof of the defender’s penthouse, tearing a gaping hole through both wood and stone. Two bodies fell out through that gap: the stone, and the mangled corpse of a defending workman the boulder had struck, plunging down hundreds of feet into the dark sullen waters of the moat. They sunk together, and lay undiscovered there for two hundred years, until later cannon breached the old walls and civilization drained the moat dry.
“Aha! A fine shot!” Baldwyn shouted in delight. The tall brass knight disappeared from the battlements, and the besiegers hooted and jeered.
A few minutes later, he reappeared on the barbican’s platform, only the top of his helmet visible over the parapet. He called his crews to man an enormous Turkish catapult, its long arm hurling a twenty-pound sharp-edged stone two hundred and forty yards through the air. The first shot bounced and splattered mud straight in Anthony of Burgundy’s eye, making him curse loud enough for the whole camp to hear. The second shot struck a cart horse hauling a sheaf of arrows, bowling it over dead as a rabbit, spilling its load across the field. Next the Turk catapult was turned on the besiegers’ supposedly out-of-range wooden assault tower, smashing gaping holes in its side with every shot. The besiegers turned two smaller engines on the Turkish monster, killing two of its crew, but the great catapult did not waste time firing back. It hurled a forty-pound stone straight at the besiegers’ main catapult, striking its pivot axis, smashing the whole structure to splinters and sending its surviving engineers running for cover.

By late afternoon, Simon the grumbling soldier was running back to his mantlet from the palisade, when Denys, peeking through his slit, saw him freeze, stare, stretch his arms out, and collapse face first on the ground, a feathered arrow sticking straight out of his back. The English archer showed his face for a split second to enjoy his marksmanship, and Denys, already aiming, loosed his bolt. The archer staggered away wounded. But Simon was dead.
“I have cursed luck with my comrades,” Denys said quietly.

The next morning, the defenders woke to a unwelcome sight. The new “cat”, its roof padded with mattresses and raw hides, was steadily advancing across the moat, filling in the last stretches of water. The knight hurled stones at it, burning tar barrels, nothing worked. He personally lowered a sack of burning sulphur and pitch over the walls on a rope to smoke the crews out, but Baldwyn, clad head to toe in armour like a lobster, ran forward under fire and sliced the rope clean through, letting the work resume unharmed. The defenders’ miners finished their work that night, propped the mine tunnels with heavy wooden beams, and slathered every inch of the wood with grease and tar, ready to light the fuse.
At dawn, the moat was fully filled. The great wooden assault tower, its hidden internal levers turning its wheels without any visible crew pushing from the outside, began rolling slowly toward the curtain wall. Its top platform held the first storming party, protected by a vertical drawbridge that would lower directly onto the battlements the moment they reached the wall. The defenders hurled every stone and flaming barrel they had at the tower, but the hides and mattresses turned every shot aside. The moment the tower reached the edge of the moat, the tall knight gave the signal to fire the mine underneath it.

The Turkish catapult hurled a half-hundredweight stone straight into the group of knights standing on the assault tower’s platform, sweeping two of them off onto the plain below, one writhing in pain, the other dead. The besiegers’ remaining catapults hurled blazing tar barrels to fire the defender’s penthouses, clearing the walls, and the storming parties ran ladders up behind the tower, lowering the drawbridge across to the battered curtain. Led by Baldwyn, the armoured assailants poured across the narrow bridge. The tall brass knight met them head on with his men-at-arms, a brutal, desperate fight breaking out where men toppled screaming headlong off the narrow bridge down into the moat below. Fresh besiegers kept swarming up the tower behind, and the defenders were slowly driven back.
The town would have fallen in another minute, but the defenders’ mine had been perfectly timed. The tar-soaked wooden props holding up the mine burned through, and the whole stretch of ground under the assault tower gave way.
