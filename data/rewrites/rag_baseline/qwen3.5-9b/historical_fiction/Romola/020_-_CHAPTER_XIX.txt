Messer Bernardo del Nero remained as immovable as Romola anticipated regarding the postponement of the marriage until Easter. In this strategic maneuver, Bardo was entirely subordinate to the counsel of his sagacious and pragmatic ally. Yet, even Bernardo, though immune to the personal magnetism Tito exerted upon others, could not wholly dismiss the seductive logic of success that invariably captivates men of consequence. Tito was ascending rapidly through the highest echelons of power, particularly gaining favor with the youthful Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici, who had already proposed incorporating Tito into his scholarly entourage for an imminent pilgrimage to Rome. The brilliant Greek, possessed of a tongue that flowed effortlessly without ever descending into contention, became increasingly indispensable at the lavish suppers along the Via Larga and at Florentine spectacles where he held no claim to expertise yet admired the unparalleled prowess of Piero de' Medici with effortless grace. Through an unbroken chain of cause and effect, Tito's reputation as a congenital companion in "magnificent" society rendered his intellect and talents more radiant; he possessed genuine accomplishment sufficient to render any exaggerated estimation perilous.

For Messer Bernardo, entrenched in old prejudices and attachments, these factors began to dismantle the newer, weaker bias against the supple young Greek stranger. To the seasoned Florentine, it was illogical to scorn the endorsement of standing well with the city's premier families. As Tito secured thorough acceptance into that exclusive circle whose opinions constituted the undisputed metric of social worth, it appeared irrational to deny that the path to satisfaction for Bardo as a father-in-law and for Romola as a wife was now unobstructed. Undeniably, Tito's arrival marked the dawn of a renewed existence for both father and daughter, surpassing even the initial promise. The blind old scholar—whose rigid honesty precluded participation in the deceitful flattery and its reciprocal excesses that formed the fabric of learned discourse—had fallen into obscurity among his peers. When mentioned, the consensus was that while his blindness and the loss of his son were tragic, he grew tedious in defending the merit of his own labors; his discontent seemed incongruous in a man who had previously spurned ecclesiastical orders, thereby complicating patronage arrangements. However, since Tito's emergence, the monotonous shadow cast by Bardo's name had lifted. The old man ceased his lamentations, and the fair daughter was no longer confined to a dowerless pride, awaiting a *parentado*. The winning demeanor and rising stature of the handsome Greek, poised to assume the dual roles of son and husband, transformed the new interest into a thoroughly cordial alliance. Visitors now frequently enlivened the quiet library, drawn by the indefatigable urge to renew contact whenever an old acquaintance becomes the subject of fresh conversation. Young men, invited by Tito, found themselves returning readily after overtaking him on his route to the Via de' Bardi, resting hands on his shoulder to engage in easy discourse. It was delightful to behold Romola's beauty; seated with the grandeur described by Firenzuola, she spoke with gravity, smiled with modesty, and radiated an aura of queenship. Under this genial breath of admiration and homage, she unfolded like a robust white lily, indifferent to the specifics of her new vibrant life anchored in Tito's devotion.

Tito had further fortified Bardo's hope that, before his death, he might secure the long-awaited stability for his library: ensuring it would not be absorbed into another collection, nor transferred to a monastic order to bear a religious name, but would perpetually remain the Bardi Library for the use of Florentines. The ancient habit of trusting in the Medici could not perish while their influence remained the state's primary lever; and Tito, now holding the ear of Cardinal Giovanni de' Medici, could achieve more than Messer Bernardo alone by demonstrating to a learned assembly the unique value of Bardi's holdings. Tito spoke sanguinely of this outcome, eager to buoy the old man's spirits, aware that Romola repaid these tender words with an adoration that no direct tribute to herself could elicit. This matter of the library dominated discussions between Bernardo del Nero and Tito as Christmas passed and the wedding horizon neared—all conducted strictly outside Bardo's hearing. Bardo clung to a vague conviction, which they dared not disrupt, that his assets, excluding the library, sufficed to meet all obligations. He refused, unless driven by a fleeting surge of despair, to acknowledge that the will disinheriting Dino left Romola heir only to debts, or that he required patronage beyond securing a distinct location for his library in exchange for a deed gifting it to the Florentine Republic.

"My assessment," Bernardo told Romola during a consultation beneath the loggia, "is that since you are to marry and Messer Tito possesses a competent income, we must commence winding up affairs to ascertain precisely the sum required to shield the library from encroachment, rather than allowing debts to accumulate further. Your father requires nothing but his daily portion of mutton and macaroni; I believe Messer Tito may undertake to provide this for the remainder of his years, substituting it for the *morgen-cap*."

"Tito has always understood that my life is inextricably bound to my father's," Romola replied. "He is kinder to my father than I am; he delights in making him happy."

"Ah, he is not molded from the same clay as other men, is he?" Bernardo observed, smiling. "Your father attempted to exclude feminine folly by stuffing you with Greek and Latin, yet you proved as susceptible to the first pair of bright eyes and soft words as any other Christian girl, unable to recite anything but Paternosters."

"Now, godfather," Romola countered, shaking her head with playful defiance, "as if mere brightness of eye and softness of word accounted for my love of Tito! You know better. You know I love my father and you because you are both good, and I love Tito equally because he is so good. I perceive it, I feel it, in every utterance and action. And if he is handsome as well, why should I not love him the more? Beauty appears to me an integral component of the perfected language through which goodness speaks. You must have been quite handsome yourself, godfather"—she gazed upward with a radiant, affectionate smile at the stately elder—"tall as Tito, with very fine eyes; only you carried a steerner, prouder countenance, and..."

"And Romola reserves all the pride for herself?" Bernardo remarked, not impervious to this charming coaxery. "Nevertheless, it is fortunate that in one regard Tito's demands prove more modest than those of any Florentine husband of suitable rank we might otherwise have encountered for you; he seeks no dowry."

Thus, the arrangement was finalized between Messer Bernardo del Nero, Romola, and Tito. Bardo assented with a sweeping gesture of the hand when Bernardo informed him that it was prudent to begin liquidating property and extinguishing debts, accustomed as he was to view financial liabilities and assets as a dense forest that his imagination never penetrated, let alone traversed. Meanwhile, Tito embarked upon winning Messer Bernardo's respect by interrogating Florentine monetary matters with his agile intellect: probing the secrets of the *Monti*, the valuation of real estate, and the profits of banking.

"You will soon forget that Tito is not a Florentine, godfather," Romola noted. "Observe how he absorbs everything pertaining to Florence."

"It seems to me he belongs to the *demoni*, children of no specific nation," Bernardo replied, smiling. "His mind is too nimble to be weighed down by the burdens we men carry in our hearts."

Romola smiled in turn, bathed in happy confidence.
