Sir Charles Moore's resolve was a double-edged sword. While one blade defended his mill with grim determination, the other pursued the architects of the riot with a relentless, terrifying assiduity. He spared the mob—the frightened followers misled by false counsel and driven by hunger. To him, punishing the bent heads of suffering men was the act of a tyrant, not a judge. He let them pass, unrecognized and unthreatened, day after day.

But the leaders were different. They were strangers, emissaries from the great towns, men of no trade and little honor. They were "down-draughts"—bankrupts, debt-ridden, and often drunk, possessing nothing to lose and everything to gain in the way of character, cash, and cleanliness. Moore hunted these men with the fervor of a sleuth-hound, finding a peculiar pleasure in the chase that surpassed the mundane labor of weaving cloth.

His horse, worn thin by the constant riding, seemed to share his master's weariness. Moore lived on the road, breathing in the crisp air of the pursuit rather than the choking steam of the dye-houses. The local magistrates, slow and timid men, likely dreaded him. Moore enjoyed frightening them, forcing them to tremble with the primal fear of assassination—a terror that had paralyzed every manufacturer and public figure in the district save one: the old Cossack, Helstone. Moore knew the risk; he knew he could be shot. Yet, such a death held no terror for his nerves. It would have been his choice, had fate allowed it.

Conversely, Moore knew his enemies feared him. The knowledge that he hunted assassins spurred his high-spirited temper. Fear was alien to him; he was too proud, too hard-hearted, too phlegmatic to know it. Often, he rode late over the moors, moonlit or moonless, feeling more elated and refreshed than when he sat safely amidst the stagnation of his counting-house. There were four leaders to catch. Two had been cornered near Stilbro' within a fortnight; the remaining two roamed near Birmingham, their haunts unknown.

Yet, amidst this storm of justice and business, Moore did not entirely neglect his battered mill. The repairs were minor—carpenters and glaziers could handle it—and his "grim metal darlings," the machines, had escaped the rioters' grasp. Whether, during these busy hours, he found moments to nurture gentler fires than those of Nemesis remained a mystery. He rarely visited Fieldhead. If he did, his stays were brief. Calls at the rectory were strictly for business conferences with the rector. He held his rigid course, steady as a stone.

Outside, the year continued its turmoil. The war's hurricane swept the continent without respite; there was no serene weather, no gentle dews for the olive, only the red rain that nourished the laurel of battle. Under Moore's feet, Ruin dug with sappers and miners. Whether he rode or walked, whether he stood by his hearth or galloped over Rushedge, he felt the hollow echo of the shaking earth.

While summer passed for Moore, how did it lapse for Shirley and Caroline? Let us first visit the heiress. Was she a love-lorn maiden, pale and pining? Did she sit bent over sedentary tasks, eyes fixed on books or sewing, silent and withdrawn?

No. Shirley was vibrant. Though her wistful gaze remained, her careless smile was still there. She kept her dark manor-house bright with her cheerful presence. The gallery and low-ceiled chambers echoed with her lively voice; the dim entrance hall grew accustomed to the rustle of her silk dress as she swept from room to room, carrying flowers to the barbarous peach-bloom salon, opening casements to let in the scent of mignonette and sweet-briar, or bringing plants from the staircase to the sun-drenched porch.

She took up her sewing, but fate seemed to doom her to never sit steadily for more than five minutes. Hardly was her thimble fitted or her needle threaded when a sudden thought summoned her upstairs. Perhaps she sought a forgotten ivory-backed needle-book or a china-topped work-box, items unneeded yet suddenly indispensable. Perhaps she wished to arrange her hair or tidy a drawer seen in confusion that morning. Or perhaps she simply wanted to peer from a specific window at a specific view, where Briarfield church and rectory were pleasantly bowered in trees.

Scarcely had she returned to her slip of cambric when Tartar's bold scrape and strangled whistle echoed at the porch. She ran to open the door. It was a hot day; the dog came in panting. She convayed him to the kitchen to ensure his water-bowl was full. Through the open door, the court lay sunny and gay, teeming with turkeys and poults, peahens and chicks, pearl-flecked Guinea-fowls, and a dazzling variety of pigeons—white, purple-necked, blue, and cinnamon-plumed. Irresistible to Shirley! She ran to the pantry for a roll and stood on the doorstep scattering crumbs. Her eager, plump, happy feathered vassals thronged around her.

John was about the stables, and she must talk to him, inspecting her mare. She was still petting the animal when the cows came in to be milked. This was important; Shirley stayed to review them all. Perhaps there were little calves or newly weaned lambs—twins whose mothers had rejected them. Miss Keeldar must be introduced to them by John, permitted the treat of feeding them by hand under the careful foreman's direction. Meanwhile, John raised doubtful questions about farming certain "crofts," "ings," and "holmes." His mistress was forced to fetch her garden-hat—a gipsy straw—and accompany him over stile and hedgerow to hear the agricultural conclusion on the spot, with the lands under her eye.

The bright afternoon wore into soft evening. She returned home to a late tea, and after tea, she never sewed.

Instead, Shirley read. She was as tenacious with her book as she was lax with her needle. Her study was the rug, her seat a footstool, or perhaps merely the carpet at Mrs. Pryor's feet: the place where she had learned her lessons as a child, and where old habits held strong sway. The tawny, lion-like bulk of Tartar stretched beside her, his negro muzzle resting on his forepaws—straight, strong, and shapely as the limbs of an Alpine wolf. One of the mistress's hands generally rested on the loving serf's rude head; remove it, and he groaned in discontent. Shirley's mind was given wholly to her book. She lifted not her eyes, stirring not speaking—unless to return a brief, respectful answer to Mrs. Pryor, who addressed deprecatory phrases now and then.

"My dear, you had better not have that great dog so near you; he is crushing the border of your dress."

"Oh, it is only muslin. I can put a clean one on to-morrow."

"My dear, I wish you could acquire the habit of sitting to a table when you read."

"I will try, ma'am, some time; but it is so comfortable to do as one has always been accustomed to do."

"My dear, let me beg of you to put that book down. You are trying your eyes by the doubtful firelight."

"No, ma'am, not at all; my eyes are never tired."

Finally, a pale light fell on the page from the window. She looked; the moon was up. She closed the volume, rose, and walked through the room. Her book had perhaps been a good one; it had refreshed, refilled, and rewarmed her heart, setting her brain astir with pictures. The still parlour, the clean hearth, the window opening on the twilight sky showing its "sweet regent," new-throned and glorious, sufficed to make earth an Eden and life a poem for Shirley. A still, deep, inborn delight glowed in her young veins, unmingled and untroubled, not to be reached or ravished by human agency, because no human agency bestowed it—it was the pure gift of God to His creature, the free dower of Nature to her child. This joy gave her experience of a *genii*-life. Buoyant, by green steps and glad hills, all verdure and light, she reached a station scarcely lower than that from which angels looked down on the dreamer of Bethel. Her eye sought, and her soul possessed, the vision of life as she wished it.

No, not as she wished it; she had not time to wish. The swift glory spread out, sweeping and kindling, multiplying its splendors faster than Thought could combine them or Aspiration could utter her longings. Shirley said nothing while the trance held her; she was quite mute. But if Mrs. Pryor spoke now, Shirley went out quietly, continuing her walk upstairs in the dim gallery.

If Shirley were not indolent, reckless, and ignorant, she would take a pen at such moments, seizing the apparition while the memory was fresh. She would fix the vision, tell the story sung to her. Had she possessed a little more of the organ of acquisitiveness, a little more love of property, she would take a good-sized sheet of paper and write plainly, in her own queer but clear hand, the story narrated, the song sung, thus possessing what she was enabled to create. But indolent she was, reckless she was, and most ignorant; for she did not know her dreams were rare, her feelings peculiar. She did not know, had never known, and would die without knowing, the full value of that spring whose bright, fresh bubbling in her heart kept it green.

Shirley took life easily. Was that not written in her eye? In her good-tempered moments, was it not full of lazy softness, yet in her brief fits of anger fulgent with quick-flashing fire? Her nature was in her eye. So long as she was calm, indolence, indulgence, humor, and tenderness possessed that large gray sphere; incense her, and a red ray pierced the dew, quickening her instantly to flame.

Before July ended, Miss Keeldar would likely have started her northern tour with Caroline. But just then, an invasion befell Fieldhead. A genteel foraging party besieged Shirley in her castle, compelling her to surrender at discretion. An uncle, an aunt, and two cousins from the south—a Mr., Mrs., and two Misses Sympson of Sympson Grove, ----shire—came down upon her in state. The laws of hospitality obliged her to yield, which she did with a facility that somewhat surprised Caroline, who knew her mistress to be prompt in action and fertile in expedients where victory was to be gained. Even Miss Helstone asked how she submitted so readily. Shirley answered that old feelings held their power; she had spent two years of her early youth at Sympson Grove.

"How did she like her relatives?"

She had nothing in common with them, she replied. Little Harry Sympson, the sole son, was very unlike his sisters, and of him she had formerly been fond; but he was not coming to Yorkshire—at least, not yet.

The next Sunday, the Fieldhead pew in Briarfield Church appeared peopled with a prim, trim, fidgety, elderly gentleman who shifted his spectacles and changed position every three minutes; a patient, placid-looking elderly lady in brown satin; and two pattern young ladies in pattern attire with pattern deportment. Shirley had the air of a black swan or a white crow amidst this party, and very forlorn was her aspect. Having brought her into respectable society, we will leave her there a while and look after Miss Helstone.

Separated from Miss Keeldar for the present, scared away from Fieldhead by the visiting commotion, Caroline was limited once more to the gray rectory, the solitary morning walk in remote by-paths, the long, lonely afternoon sitting in a quiet parlour forsaken by the sun at noon, or in the garden alcove where it shone bright yet sadly on the ripening red currants trained over the trellis and the fair monthly roses entwined between. There she sat in her white summer dress, still as a garden statue. She read old books from her uncle's library. The Greek and Latin were useless to her, and its collection of light literature was chiefly contained on a shelf belonging to her aunt Mary: some venerable *Lady's Magazines* that had once performed a sea-voyage with their owner, stained with salt water; some mad Methodist Magazines full of miracles, apparitions, preternatural warnings, ominous dreams, and frenzied fanaticism; the equally mad letters of Mrs. Elizabeth Rowe from the Dead to the Living; a few old English classics. From these faded flowers, Caroline had extracted honey in childhood; they were tasteless to her now.

By way of change, and to do good, she would sew—making garments for the poor, according to good Miss Ainley's direction. Sometimes, as she felt and saw her tears fall slowly on her work, she wondered how the excellent woman who cut and arranged it managed to be so equably serene in her own solitude.

"I never find Miss Ainley oppressed with despondency or lost in grief," she thought. "Yet her cottage is a still, dim little place, and she is without a bright hope or near friend in the world. I remember she told me once she had tutored her thoughts to tend upwards to heaven. She allowed there was, and ever had been, little enjoyment in this world for her, and she looks, I suppose, to the bliss of the world to come. So do nuns, with their close cell, their iron lamp, their robe strait as a shroud, their bed narrow as a coffin. She says often she has no fear of death—no dread of the grave; no more, doubtless, had St. Simeon Stylites, lifted up terrible on his wild column in the wilderness; no more has the Hindu votary stretched on his couch of iron spikes.
