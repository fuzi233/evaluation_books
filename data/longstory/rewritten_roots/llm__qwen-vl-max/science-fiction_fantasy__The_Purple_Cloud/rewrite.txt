Approximately three months ago—toward the end of May in the year 1900—the author whose name appears on the title page received a letter and a bundle of papers that proved to be among the most remarkable documents he had ever encountered. They came from Dr. Arthur Lister Browne, M.A. (Oxon.), F.R.C.P., a close friend whose identity I see no reason to withhold. For two years prior, I had been living mostly in France, and since Browne maintained a medical practice in Norfolk, we had not met during my visits to London. Though our friendship was deep and genuine, both of us were notoriously poor at correspondence; only two brief notes passed between us during those years.

Then, in May, the letter arrived—along with a packet of four notebooks. The notebooks were densely filled with Pitman’s shorthand, scribbled in pencil with little regard for stroke thickness or vowel clarity. Their pages resembled swarms of frantic insects caught mid-flight, frozen in chaotic motion. Deciphering them was no light task—slow, painstaking, and often frustrating. The letter itself was also written in shorthand, and now, having transcribed it along with the second notebook (marked "III."), I present both to the reader.

I must confess that in five instances, I’ve reconstructed sentences based on educated guesswork, and in two cases, the script was so indecipherable that I abandoned the passage altogether, weary and baffled. Yet these gaps do not undermine the narrative’s integrity.

Here is Dr. Browne’s letter:

>
> I’ve just lain awake thinking of you, wishing you were here to give me one last handshake before I—"_go_." For all appearances suggest that I am indeed going. Four days ago, I began feeling a soreness in my throat. Passing by old Johnson’s surgery in Selbridge, I stopped in for a look. He muttered something about membranous laryngitis, which made me smile. But by the time I reached home, I was hoarse—and no longer smiling. By nightfall, I had dyspnea and laryngeal stridor. I immediately telegraphed to London for Morgan. Between him and Johnson, they performed a tracheotomy and burned my airways with chromic acid and galvanic cautery. Breathing has improved, and the pain is surprisingly mild. But I’m no novice when it comes to prognosis. The bronchi are involved—too deeply involved—and there is no hope. Morgan still clings to the idea that I might become another triumph in his tracheotomy records. But I know better. And I say: No. The only small comfort is that I’ll have the satisfaction of proving a specialist wrong in his own field. We shall see.
>
> This morning, I’ve been settling some affairs and remembered these notebooks. I meant to send them to you months ago, but procrastination and the fact that the woman who spoke these words was still alive delayed me. Now she is dead. As a writer and student of human experience, you may find value in them—if you can read them.
>
> I’m under a light dose of morphia now, propped up in a state of quiet lethargy. Since I can write without great effort, I’ll tell you, in the old Pitman shorthand, about her.
>
> Her name was Miss Mary Wilson. She was about thirty when I first met her, forty-five when she died—fifteen years of intimate acquaintance between us. Do you know anything of hypnotic trance? That was our relationship—hypnotist and subject. She’d been under another man’s control before me, but no one had ever mastered her as I did. She suffered from *tic douloureux* in the fifth nerve. Most of her teeth had already been extracted before I saw her, and an attempt had been made to remove the nerve through external scission. It failed. The agony continued—like clocks ticking in hell inside her jaw. It was a mercy that she ever found me. My influence over her was nearly absolute. With a few passes, I could banish her torment.
>
> You never saw anyone quite like her. Even as a physician, I could not look upon her without a jolt of unease. She evoked the *other world*—a scent of decay, a sense of being more ghost than flesh. I cannot fully explain why, except by describing her features: a high brow, thin lips, pointed chin, pale cheeks. Tall and painfully emaciated, her skeleton was visible beneath her skin, save for her thigh bones. Her eyes were the bluish-gray of cigarette smoke, holding a faint, unearthly gaze. At thirty-five, her hair was already white, a mere thread of wisps.
>
> She was well-off and lived alone in Wooding Manor, five miles from Ash Thomas. I was just beginning my practice in the region, and soon moved into the manor. She insisted I attend to her exclusively. One patient became the most profitable practice I ever knew.
>
> Soon, I discovered that in trance, Miss Wilson possessed extraordinary abilities—not unique in kind, but exceptional in scope, reliability, and depth. Today, psychical researchers sit and lecture about the mind’s capacity to perceive distant events during trance, as if it were a new discovery! But this truth has long been known—by witches, by mystics, by every old crone since the Middle Ages. The Psychical Research Society only confirmed what many of us already knew with certainty.
>
> Still, Miss Wilson was remarkable. While most trance visions focus on space—on the present, on distant places—she traveled freely across time and space. She journeyed north and south, up and down, through past, present, and future. I didn’t realize this at once, but gradually, through months of listening, I learned to interpret her murmurs. In trance, she produced a strange, guttural stream of sounds—half-speech, half-breath, accompanied by constricted pupils, absent knee-jerks, rigid limbs, and a rapt expression. I would sit for hours beside her bed, mesmerized, trying to grasp the meaning of her whispered language.
>
> Over time, my ear grew attuned to her words. Then, after six months, I heard her repeat a sentence I recognized:
>
> _"Such were the arts by which the Romans extended their conquests, and attained the palm of victory; and the concurring testimony of different authors enables us to describe them with precision..."_
>
> I was startled. These were words from Gibbon’s *Decline and Fall*, a book I suspected she had never read. I asked sharply: “Where are you?”
>
> She replied: “Us are in a room, eight hundred and eleven miles above. A man is writing. Us are reading.”
>
> Two things stand out: First, in trance, she never said “I” or “we,” but always “us”—“us are,” “us will,” “us went.” I don’t know why. Second, when recalling the past, she described herself as being *above*—higher the further back in time. For present events, she felt herself *on*. For the future, she always claimed to be *within*—so many miles deep into the earth.
>
> But her excursions into the future seemed limited. She rarely ventured beyond sixty-three miles “within.” Usually twenty or twenty-five. It was as though the deeper she tried to go, the greater the resistance—like a diver pressing into the abyss, only to meet an invisible wall.
>
> I regret I cannot continue—though I had much more to say about her. For fifteen years, I sat beside her dim bed, listening to her murmurs. Eventually, I could catch even her faintest sighs. I heard *Decline and Fall* from beginning to end. Some reports were nonsense. Others chilled me to the bone. Truly, I have heard astonishing things from Mary Wilson’s lips.
>
> Sometimes I could guide her spirit to any scene by sheer will. Other times, she resisted, drifted away—defiant. Otherwise, I might have sent you not four notebooks, but twenty. Around the fifth year, I decided to record her more coherent utterances. The notebook marked “I.”—which I believe the most curious—belongs to the seventh year. Its history, like the others, is this: One afternoon, I heard her murmur in the tone of someone *reading*. Intrigued, I asked where she was.
>
> “Us are forty-five miles within,” she said. “Us read, and another writes.”
>
> I deduced she was fifteen to thirty years ahead, reading an unpublished work. Over weeks, I kept her focused. I believe I captured most of it. I think you’d find it striking.
>
> But now, let us turn from Miss Wilson to A.L. Browne, F.R.C.P.—lying with a breathing tube in his trachea, Eternity beneath his pillow...

[Dr. Browne’s letter continues on matters irrelevant to this account.]

The author adds: Dr. Browne’s diagnosis proved correct—he died two days after writing this. What follows is my transcription of the shorthand notebook marked “III.” I offer it without commentary, merely noting that the words represent a document to be written—or rather, *motivated*—in a future that, like the past, exists substantively in the present, though unseen. The title, paragraph divisions, and formatting are mine, added for clarity.

Memory is fading now—growing weak. For example, what was the name of the parson who preached just before the *Boreal* set sail, warning against the sin of attempting to reach the North Pole? I’ve forgotten. Yet four years ago, it was as familiar as my own name. Events before the voyage are growing hazy.

I sit here in the loggia of this Cornish villa, writing down what happened—God knows why, since no eye will ever read it. And already, I can’t recall the parson’s name.

He was a peculiar man—a Scot from Ayrshire, tall and gaunt, with tawny hair. He wandered London streets in rough-spun clothes, a plaid draped over one shoulder. Once, I saw him in Holborn, stalking with wild intensity, muttering to himself. He opened a chapel in Fetter Lane, and quickly drew crowds. Years later, when he moved to a larger hall in Kensington, men from America and Australia flocked to hear him—though it was not an age given to such fervent prophets.

Yet this man stirred something deep. His eyes were powerful, his voice rising from a whisper to a thunderclap, like snowballs gathering force until they crashed—just as I’ve heard pack ice shatter in the Arctic. His gestures were crude, primal, like those of a man from prehistoric times.

But what was his name? Macintosh? Mackay? Yes—it was *Mackay*.

Mackay took offense at the *Boreal*’s mission. For three Sundays, as preparations neared completion, he railed against it from the pulpit in Kensington.

At that time, the world’s obsession with the North Pole had reached a fever pitch—though “fever” doesn’t capture the strange ecstasy, the restless frenzy that gripped humanity. The abstract curiosity once felt toward the unknown pole had exploded into something far more urgent: a concrete, overwhelming *financial* interest.

In the ten years before the *Boreal* expedition, no fewer than twenty-seven attempts had failed. The cause? The will of Charles P. Stickney, a Chicago magnate believed to be the richest man who ever lived. Ten years prior, he had died, leaving 175 million dollars to whoever first reached the Pole—regardless of rank or nationality. The wording was clear: *“the man who first reached”*—and though debate erupted over whether it meant the leader or the first footfall, legal authorities ruled decisively: it was the individual whose foot touched the 90th degree of latitude who would claim the fortune.

Thus, public excitement soared. Every detail of the *Boreal*’s preparation was dissected in newspapers. Everyone was an expert. The ship was a topic of bets, hopes, jokes, sneers. Finally, it seemed, success was possible.

And so Mackay had a captivated—though skeptical—audience. A truly courageous man, to oppose the tide of four hundred million dollars! One against the world, he stood firm, declaring them all wrong.

They called him “John the Baptist Redivivus.” And indeed, he bore a likeness to that prophet.

When he denounced the *Boreal*, not a single monarch in Europe would have refused a low post aboard—had it not been for pride.

On the third Sunday night, I was there. I heard him.

His speech was wild, prophetic. The congregation sat transfixed. His voice roared through every register of thunder—from low rumble to deafening climax. Those who came to mock stayed to wonder.

In short, he said this: There was a fate, a doom, tied to the Earth’s poles. Man’s repeated failure to reach them, despite countless efforts, proved it. That failure was a lesson—and a warning. The North Pole was not impossibly distant. Human ingenuity had conquered far greater challenges. Yet, again and again, progress was halted—by chance, by some unseen Hand. And therein lay the lesson.

Like the Tree of Knowledge in Eden, he said, the Pole was forbidden. All of Earth lay open to man—except *that* one spot, persistently veiled. As a father says to his son: *“Not here, my child. Wheresoever you will—but not here.”*

But humans, he warned, were free agents. They could shut their ears, ignore Heaven’s whispers. And now, he said, the moment had come when man would finally stand on the 90th degree, plant an impious foot upon the head of the world—just as Adam had been able to reach for the Fruit of Knowledge.

And just as that abuse brought swift, universal catastrophe, so too, he proclaimed—his voice echoing like a final judgment—would this act bring down upon mankind a sky darkened, weather thunderous, and divine wrath unrelenting.

His fervor, authority, and fierce gestures left no one untouched. I sat as though addressed by an angel from Heaven.

Yet by the time I reached home, the entire impression had vanished—like water off a duck’s back.

The prophet of the twentieth century was not a success. John the Baptist himself, in camel skin, would have drawn only polite shrugs.

I dismissed Mackay then, thinking: *He is behind his age.*

But haven’t I thought differently of Mackay since… my God…?

Three weeks—approximately that long—before the Sunday night sermon, I was visited by Clark, leader of the upcoming expedition. It was a casual call, nothing more than friendship. At that point, I had been established for about a year at No. II, Harley Street, and though still under twenty-five, I had, I suppose, one of the most distinguished medical practices in Europe.

Distinguished—but small. I could maintain my lifestyle and move among society’s elite, yet there were moments when I felt the quiet pinch of financial strain. Around that time, I was only spared significant embarrassment by the success of my book, *Applications of Science to the Arts*.

During our conversation that afternoon, Clark said to me in his usual offhand manner:

“Do you know what I dreamed about you last night, Adam Jeffson? I dreamed you were with us on the expedition.”

I think he must have noticed my sudden start—because on that very same night, I had dreamt the exact same thing. But I said nothing. My voice faltered as I replied:

“Who? Me? On the expedition? I wouldn’t go, even if asked.”

“Oh, you would.”

“I wouldn’t. You forget—I’m about to be married.”

“Well, we needn’t discuss it, since Peters isn’t going to die,” he said. “Still, if anything did happen to him, you know who I’d turn to first—Adam Jeffson.”

“Clark, you’re joking,” I said. “I know next to nothing about astronomy or magnetic phenomena. And besides—I’m engaged.”

“But what about your botany, my friend? That’s what we’d want from you. As for nautical astronomy—pah! A man with your scientific mind could pick that up in no time.”

“You treat this like it’s actually possible,” I said, smiling. “Such a thought has never crossed my mind. First of all, there’s my fiancée—”

“Aha, the all-important Countess, eh? Well, from what I know of her, she’d be the first to push you into it. The chance to plant your foot on the North Pole doesn’t come along every day, my boy.”

“Please, talk of something else!” I said. “There’s Peters…”

“Of course, there’s Peters. But believe me, the dream was so vivid—”

“Leave your dreams and your Poles alone!” I laughed.

Yes, I remember—I pretended to laugh loudly. But deep inside, I already knew: this was one of those rare, defining moments in my life—one of those turning points that, since childhood, had made my existence stranger than any other human being’s ever lived. I knew it because of the two dreams—and because, after Clark left, as I slipped on my gloves to visit my fiancée, I clearly heard the two Voices speak within me: one saying, “Do not go to see her now,” and the other, “Yes, go, go!”

The two Voices of my life. To an ordinary reader, these words might suggest mere inner conflict—or madness. How could any modern man grasp how real they sounded, how loud, how often they clashed inside me, “nearer than breathing,” as the poem says, “closer than hands and feet”?

It began when I was seven. One summer evening, playing in my father’s pine woods, half a mile from a quarry cliff, I suddenly heard—inside myself—two voices: one whispering, “Just walk toward the cliff,” the other, “Don’t go that way.” At first, mere whispers; but as I grew, they swelled into furious shouts. I walked toward the cliff—it was steep, thirty feet high—and fell. Weeks later, when I regained speech, I told my astonished mother that “someone pushed me over the edge,” and “someone else caught me at the bottom.”

Soon after my eleventh birthday, lying awake one night, I realized that my life must matter deeply to unseen forces. Two Powers, enemies, pursued me—one wanting me dead, the other determined to keep me alive. One urging me to act, the other to resist. I was not like other boys. I was separate, marked—for something. Already, I sensed things—moods, instincts—as ancient and primal as the first human who stepped onto earth. Biblical phrases like “The Lord spake to So-and-so” never seemed strange to me. I didn’t find it hard to imagine that early man had more than two ears. In fact, I sometimes felt I resembled those first beings in spirit.

Yet no one—not even my mother—ever suspected what I now reveal. I appeared to be just another young man of my time—rowing in the Varsity eight, cramming for exams, lounging in clubs. When choosing a career, who could have guessed the agony tearing through my soul while my mind remained indifferent? The voices roared: “Be a scientist—a doctor!” and “Be a lawyer, an engineer, an artist—be anything but a doctor!”

I became a doctor. I went to Cambridge, then the greatest medical school in England. There, I met a man named Scotland, who held a peculiar view of the world. He lived in New Court at Trinity, and a group of us often gathered in his rooms. He constantly spoke of “Black” and “White Powers,” until it became a joke—he was nicknamed “black-and-white-mystery-man” after interrupting someone talking about “the black mystery of the universe” with, “No—the black-and-white mystery.”

I remember Scotland well—gentle, sweet-natured, fond of cats, Sappho, and the Anthology. Small in stature, with a Roman nose, always trying to straighten his neck and pull in his paunch. He believed the universe was locked in a desperate struggle between two Forces: a White Power, stronger but hindered by conditions on Earth, which had dominated in Europe until the Middle Ages, but since then had slowly yielded to the Black Power. Eventually, he said, the Black would triumph—not everywhere, but here—and claim this planet as its prize.

That was Scotland’s belief, endlessly repeated. While others listened with mild amusement, I drank in every word with secret agony. Deeply, profoundly, it resonated with me.

***

But I was saying—when Clark left, I was putting on my gloves to visit my fiancée, the Countess Clodagh. And then I heard the Voices again—clearer than ever.

Sometimes, one impulse overwhelms the other so completely that resistance is impossible. That was the case now. The Voice that urged me to go won.

I walked from Harley Street to Hanover Square, and throughout the journey, it felt as though something screamed into my ear: “Since you go, say nothing of the *Boreal*, or Clark’s visit!” And another shout: “Tell everything! Hide nothing!”

It lasted an eternity—yet only minutes passed before I stood in Hanover Square, Clodagh in my arms.

She was, in my eyes, the most magnificent woman—Clodagh, with that proud neck that always seemed to scorn something just behind her left shoulder. Magnificent—yet now I know: a godless woman, Clodagh, with a bitter heart.

Once, she confessed her favorite historical figure was Lucrezia Borgia. When I recoiled, she laughed and said, “Oh, no, I’m just joking!” But I see now that she was hiding her true nature. Yet even now, I realize how utterly she captivated me.

Our marriage was opposed by both families—mine because her father and grandfather had died in asylums; hers because I was neither rich nor noble. Her older sister had married a common country doctor, Peter Peters of Taunton, and this “misalliance” made my union with Clodagh doubly unacceptable. But Clodagh’s passion for me could not be stopped by threats or pleas. What fire she carried! Sometimes, she frightened me.

She was no longer young—five years my senior, and five years older than her nephew, born from her sister’s marriage to Peters. That nephew was Peter Peters, who would accompany the *Boreal* expedition as physician, botanist, and meteorological assistant.

On the day Clark visited, I had barely sat with Clodagh five minutes when I said:

“Dr. Clark—ha! ha! ha!—has been talking about the expedition. He says if anything happened to Peters, he’d come straight to me. He had a ridiculous dream…”

As I spoke, I felt the full weight of my own treachery—the crookedness of it. But I couldn’t stop myself any more than I could fly.

Clodagh stood at the window, holding a rose to her face. For a full minute, she said nothing. I saw her sharp, flushed profile, bent forward, inhaling the scent. Then, in her cold, quick tone:

“The man who first steps on the North Pole will certainly be ennobled. I don’t mention the millions… I only wish I were a man!”

“I don’t have any particular ambition that way,” I replied. “I’m perfectly happy in my warm Eden with my Clodagh. I don’t care for the outer cold.”

“Don’t make me think less of you!” she snapped.

“Why should you, Clodagh? I’m not required to desire the North Pole, am I?”

“But you *would* go, if you could?”

“I might… I doubt it. There’s our marriage…”

“Marriage indeed! That’s exactly what would turn our marriage from a sneaky problem into a tenfold triumph.”

“You mean if *I* were the one to stand at the Pole. But there are many on an expedition. It’s unlikely I’d be the one—”

“For *me*, you will, Adam—” she began.

“*Will*?” I cried. “You say ‘will’? There’s not even a shadow of possibility—!”

“But why? Three weeks remain before departure. They say…”

She paused.

“They say what?”

Her voice dropped.

“That Peter takes atropine.”

Ah—then I started. She moved from the window, sat in a rocking chair, and turned the pages of a book without reading. We were silent—she drawing her thumb across the edges, contemplative. Then she gave a dry, almost mad laugh.

“Why did you start when I said that?” she asked, now reading randomly.

“I? I didn’t start! What makes you think I did? Who told you, Clodagh, that Peters takes atropine?”

“He’s my nephew. I know. But don’t look so stunned—it’s absurd. I have no intention of poisoning him to make you a millionaire and a peer…”

“My dearest Clodagh!”

“I easily might, though. He’ll be here soon. He’s bringing Mr. Wilson for the evening.” (Wilson was the expedition’s electrician.)

“Clodagh,” I said, “believe me, you’re joking in a way that doesn’t please me.”

“Do I really?” she answered, with that stiff, proud tilt of her throat. “Then I must be more exquisite. But thank Heaven, it’s only a jest. Women aren’t admired anymore for such deeds.”

“Ha! ha! ha!—no, not admired, Clodagh! Oh, my God—let’s change the subject…”

But now she refused to let it go. She drew from me the entire history of recent polar expeditions—how far they reached, what tools they used, why they failed. Her eyes sparkled. She listened eagerly. Before this, she’d known details of the *Boreal*’s outfitting, and several members. But now, my mention of Clark’s visit seemed to ignite a fever in her—Pole-fever.

The passion of her kiss as I pulled away from her that day I shall never forget. I returned home with a heavy heart.

Dr. Peter Peters lived three doors down, across the street. Late that night, his footman knocked me awake with news that Peters was gravely ill. I rushed to his bedside and instantly recognized the signs: delirium, dilated pupils—poisoning by atropine. Wilson, the electrician, who had spent the evening with Peters at Clodagh’s, was there.

“What on earth is wrong?” he asked.

“Poisoned,” I said.

“Good God—by what?”

“Atropine.”

“Good heavens!”

“Don’t panic. I think he’ll recover.”

“Are you certain?”

“Yes—if he stops taking the drug, Wilson.”

“What? He poisoned himself?”

I hesitated. Then I said:

“He’s in the habit of taking atropine, Wilson.”

I stayed three hours, working desperately for his life. When I left in the dark pre-dawn, I felt sure he would survive.

I slept until 11 A.M., then hurried back. In the room were my two nurses—and Clodagh.

My beloved placed a finger to her lips, whispering:

“Sh-h-h! He’s asleep…”

She leaned close, murmuring into my ear:

“I heard the news early. I’ve come to stay with him—until the end.”

We stared at each other—long, steady. My gaze dropped before hers. A word formed on my lips, but I said nothing.

Peters’ recovery wasn’t as smooth as I’d hoped. By the end of the first week, he remained prostrate. Then I said to Clodagh:

“Clodagh, your presence here troubles me. It’s unnecessary.”

“Certainly unnecessary,” she replied. “But I’ve always had a gift for nursing. And I love watching the body’s battles. Since no one objects, why should you?”

“Ah… I don’t know. This case unsettles me. I’m half tempted to throw it to the devil.”

“Then do.”

“And you—go home, Clodagh!”

“But *why*? If I cause no harm. In these days of ‘corruption of the upper classes’ and Roman decadence, shouldn’t every innocent whim be encouraged by upright souls like you? Whims are brakes on crime—and this is mine. I take sensual pleasure—almost erotic—in handling delicate drugs. Like Helen, Medea, Calypso—great women who were masters of chemistry. To watch the human vessel in a storm, the slow drama of its sinking—isn’t that thrilling? And I want you to learn to let me have my little ways…”

She touched my hair with a playful arrogance that calmed me. Yet even then, I looked at the rumpled bed and saw the man truly ill.

I still feel nausea writing this. Lucrezia Borgia may have been heroic in her time—but Lucrezia in this age? One could vomit…

The man grew worse. The second week passed. Only ten days remained before the expedition’s departure.

At the end of that week, Wilson sat beside Peters when I entered. Clodagh was about to give him medicine. Seeing me, she set the glass down and approached. As she came, I saw something that pierced me: Wilson picked up the glass, raised it, examined it, sniffed—quickly, furtively. His expression held suspicion.

Meanwhile, Clark visited daily. He had a medical degree, and around this time, I called him in—along with Alleyne of Cavendish Square—to consult on Peters. The patient lay in semi-coma, broken by violent vomiting. His condition baffled us. I stated formally that he had been poisoned with atropine originally, but now his symptoms suggested another plant-based poison—unknown.

“Mysterious,” Clark said when we were alone.

“I don’t understand it,” I replied.

“Who are the two nurses?”

“Highly recommended—my own.”

“At any rate, my dream about you is coming true, Jeffson. Peters is clearly out of the running.”

I shrugged.

“I now formally invite you to join the expedition,” Clark said. “Do you accept?”

I shrugged again.

“If that means yes,” he said, “remember—you have only eight days, and the whole world to prepare.”

This conversation took place in Peters’ dining room. As we left, I saw Clodagh glide down the hall—swiftly—away from us.

I said nothing to her that day about Clark’s invitation. Yet I kept asking myself: Did she know? Had she *listened*?

Whatever the truth, at midnight, to my surprise, Peters opened his eyes and smiled. By noon the next day, his strong vitality—perfect for Arctic travel—had returned. He was propped on an elbow, talking to Wilson. Except for pallor and stomach pain, there was little trace of near-death. I prescribed quarter-grain tablets of morphine sulfate and left.

David Wilson and I never truly liked each other. That very day, he caused tension between Peters and me by telling Peters I had taken his place on the expedition. Peters, sensitive, immediately wrote a protest to Clark. Clark sent it to me, marked with a large blue question mark.

All of Peters’ preparations were complete. Mine were not. He had six days to recover. So I wrote Clark, explaining that changed circumstances nullified my acceptance, despite having already arranged for a substitute.

That settled it: Peters would go. I would stay. Five days before departure—Friday, June 15th—Peters sat in an armchair. Cheerful, but feverish, still suffering stomach pains. I gave him three quarter-grains of morphine daily. That Friday night, at 11 P.M., I visited him. Clodagh was there, speaking with him. Peters smoked a cigar.

“Ah,” Clodagh said, “I was waiting for you, Adam. I wasn’t sure whether to inject tonight. Should I?”

“What do you think, Peters?” I asked. “Any more pain?”

“Well, perhaps another quarter,” he said. “Still some trouble in the gut.”

“Then a quarter-grain, Clodagh,” I said.

As she opened the syringe box, she pouted:

“Our patient has been naughty. He took more atropine.”

I flew into anger.

“Peters!” I shouted. “You know you can’t do things like this without consulting me! Do it once more, and I swear I’ll have nothing more to do with you!”

“Rubbish,” Peters said. “Why all this fuss? It was just a flea-bite. I needed it.”

“He injected it himself…” Clodagh remarked.

She stood at the mantel, having taken the syringe and vial from the box, and gone to melt a tablet in distilled water. Her back was to us. She lingered. I stood. Peters smoked. Clodagh began talking about a charity bazaar she’d attended.

She lingered. A wild thought flashed through my mind: “Why is she so *long*?”

“Ah, that was a pain!” Peters said. “Forget the bazaar, aunt—think of the morphine.”

Suddenly, an irresistible urge seized me—to rush at her, smash the syringe, tablets, glass—everything from her hands. I *must* have obeyed. My body leaned forward—on the verge. But at that moment, a voice called from the open door:

“Well, how’s everything?”

It was Wilson, the electrician. With lightning speed, I recalled the look of suspicion I’d seen on his face. Oh, well—I wouldn’t, couldn’t—she was my love. I froze, stone-still.

Clodagh greeted Wilson warmly, right hand extended, left holding the fragile glass. My eyes locked on her face—full of calm, innocent reassurance. I whispered to myself: “I must be mad!”

A casual conversation began. Clodagh rolled up Peters’ sleeve, knelt, and injected his forearm. As she rose, laughing at something Wilson said, the glass slipped from her hand. Her heel, seemingly by accident, crushed it. She placed the syringe among others on the mantel.

“Your friend has been naughty, Mr. Wilson,” she said again, pouting. “He’s taken more atropine.”

“Really?” Wilson asked.

“Let me alone, all of you,” Peters snapped. “I’m not a child.”

Those were his last coherent words. He died shortly before 1 A.M.—poisoned by a powerful dose of atropine.

From that moment until the *Boreal* carried me down the Thames, the world became a chaotic nightmare—most details lost to memory. Only the inquest remains clear: I was called to prove Peters had injected himself. Wilson and Clodagh confirmed it. The verdict followed.

And amid the frantic preparations, three things—only three—remain sharply etched in my mind.

The first—and most important—was the torrent of words I heard at Kensington that Sunday night from the loud-mouthed Mackay. Why did I, so busy, go to that chapel? Perhaps I know.

I sat and listened. And those final words of his sermon—rising in prophetic fury—have burned themselves into my brain: “And as in one case, transgression was followed by swift and universal catastrophe, so in the other, I warn the entire race to expect nothing from God but a lowering sky and thundery weather.”
