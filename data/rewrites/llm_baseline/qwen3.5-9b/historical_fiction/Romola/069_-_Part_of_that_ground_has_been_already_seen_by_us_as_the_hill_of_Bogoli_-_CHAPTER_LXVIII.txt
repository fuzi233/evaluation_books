Romola's consciousness drifted from the depths of sleep into a restless, vivid dreaming, until finally, she found herself stretching her arms wide within the courtyard of the Bargello. The flickering flames of the tapers there seemed to swell in intensity, blotting out the darkness with a sudden, overwhelming light. Her eyes snapped open, and the reality of morning flooded her senses.

Her boat lay motionless in a secluded cove. To her right stretched the flawless, sapphire blue of the Mediterranean; to her left unfolded a landscape of recurring beauty, a rhythmic scene beloved by the shores of that loveliest sea. Deep curves of mountains framed a swath of verdant land, draped in the gentle shadows of trees sloping toward rocky heights. Winding upward through the foliage, a path led to a ragged cluster of buildings that seemed to have scrambled hastily up the mountainside, securing a precarious perch solely to display its tall belfry as a beacon to the scattered village below. The rising sun cast oblique rays upon the western edge of this crescent-shaped bay, leaving the rest bathed in dewy shadow. Silence reigned absolute; the waters themselves seemed to have curled inward for rest.

The warm sunlight touched Romola, thrilling her gently like a caress. She lay still, scarcely watching the view, merely absorbing the profound peace and beauty surrounding her. In the freshness of youth, there often comes, upon waking, a moment where mere passive existence becomes a *Lethe*—a bliss so exquisite and indefinite that it erases memory and desire alike. As the soft warmth seeped into her young limbs and her eyes rested on this secluded luxury, the agitating past seemed to glide away like the dark vision of the Bargello. The afternoon dreams of her girlhood returned to her. For a minute or two, the oblivion was undisturbed; she did not even conceive that she could rest forever, she simply felt that she rested.

Then, distinct awareness returned: she was lying in a boat that had borne her across the waters throughout the night. Instead of delivering her to death, it had served as the gently lulling cradle of a new life. Despite her evening despair, she was grateful that morning had come again, glad to rest in the familiar sunlight rather than in the unknown realms of death. *Could* she not rest here? No sound from Florence could reach her. Yet, the oblivion was already troubled. Through the golden haze, domes, towers, and walls pierced the air, separated by a river and enclosed by green hills.

Rising from her reclining position, she sat up in the boat, willing herself to resist the surge of thoughts questioning how far the boat had carried her. Why worry? This was a sheltered nook inhabited by simple villagers who would not harm her. For a little while, at least, she could rest and make no resolutions. Soon she would procure bread and milk, then nestle in the green quiet, finding a pause in her life. Turning to watch the crescent valley, she sought to reclaim the soothing sense of peace she had felt upon waking.

She had not maintained this contemplative pose for more than a few minutes when a piercing cry cut through the stillness. It was not a brief shout but a continuous, intensifying wail. Romola was certain it was a child in distress, crying for help that never arrived. She sprang up, placing one foot on the boat's side, ready to leap onto the beach, but paused to listen. Surely the mother was nearby; the cry must soon cease. But it continued, drawing her irresistibly, seeming all the more pitiful against the backdrop of the preceding peace. Compelled, she leaped onto the sand and walked many paces before realizing which direction to take.

She believed the cry originated from a rough garden growth several yards to her right, where a half-ruined hovel stood. Climbing a low, broken stone fence, she traversed patches of weedy greens and neglected, ripe corn. The cry grew clearer, confirming her guess, yet as she hurried toward the hovel, she felt an oppressive shift in the air as she left the sea behind. Was there some hidden taint lurking among the green luxuriance that had seemed such an inviting refuge from the coming heat?

Now seeing the entrance to the hovel, the cry darted through her like physical pain. The next instant, her foot crossed the threshold, but the sight within the sombre light arrested her with a shock of awe and horror. Upon the straw-strewn floor lay three dead bodies: a tall man, a girl of about eight years old, and a young woman whose long black hair was being clawed and pulled by a living child—the very source of the screaming.

Romola's experience with death and disease rendered her thought and action prompt. She lifted the little living child, soothing it against her bosom, while simultaneously checking the bodies to confirm they were truly dead. Their strongly marked racial features and peculiar garb suggested they were Spanish or Portuguese Jews, perhaps put ashore and abandoned by rapacious sailors who coveted their property. Such tragedies befell Jews forced to flee their homes by the Inquisition; the cruelty of greed threw them from the sea, and the cruelty of superstition cast them back.

"But surely," Romola thought, "I shall find a woman in the village whose maternal heart will not refuse to tend this helpless child—if the real mother is indeed dead." This doubt lingered because while the man and girl appeared emaciated and long deceased, the woman seemed harder, retaining some robustness. Kneeling, Romola was about to check the pulse beneath a piece of yellow woolen drapery when she saw purple spots marking the familiar pestilence.

It struck her then: if the villagers knew of this, she might face resistance. They might shrink from her with the child in her arms. However, she possessed money and could offer payment for goat's milk. Setting out immediately toward the village, her mind focused on soothing the dark-haired infant and figuring out how to secure a caregiver. She hoped, with a touch of awe, that her unknown arrival in religious dress would inspire trust.

As she crossed cultivated fields, she noted with wonder that patches of corn had been left to over-ripen, untouched by the sickle, and that golden apples and dark figs rotted on the weedy earth. Grassy spaces existed, yet no cows, sheep, or goats were visible. The stillness now held something fearful. Hurrying toward the densest cluster of houses, seeking the most life to plead for the helpless life she carried, she stopped occasionally to bite pieces of sweet fig pulp to quiet the child.

Entering between two rows of dwellings, she found it was time for villagers to stir, yet no soul was in sight. The air grew increasingly oppressive, laden with a horrible impurity. One door stood open revealing grim emptiness; another showed a man lying dead, dressed in his clothes, his head resting across a spade handle, an earthenware cruse in his hand as if he had collapsed suddenly. Horror seized Romola. Was she in a village of the unburied dead? Wanting to listen for any faint sound, the child cried out anew when she ceased feeding it, filling her ears.

Finally, she spotted a figure crawling slowly from a house, sinking back against the wall in a sitting posture. Hastening forward, she found a young woman in fevered anguish, holding a pitcher. As Romola approached, the woman did not start; the single need was too absorbing for distraction.

"Water! Get me water!" she moaned.

Romola stooped to take the pitcher and whispered gently, "You shall have water; can you point toward the well?" The hand lifted toward the distant end of the narrow street. Romola set off with all the speed possible, balancing the pitcher and feeding the child. The morsels of fruit calmed the infant, allowing Romola to observe her surroundings more clearly. The well lay twenty yards beyond the street's end. Approaching it, her eyes caught a glimpse of a cow and two goats grazing high on the green slope below the church. She tried to trace a path to this cheering sight once her errand was done.

So engrossed was she that she failed to notice someone approaching the well from the opposite side, fixing astonished eyes upon her. Romola presented a startling figure: her gaze fixed intently on the distant slope, her thick grey garment flowing rapidly, her hair rolling backward and illuminated by the sun, and the little olive-skinned baby on her right arm gazing out with jet-black eyes.

"She carries a pitcher to fetch water for the sick. It is the Holy Mother, come to care for those afflicted by the pestilence."

It was a vision of awe. Perhaps she would be angry with those who fetched water only for themselves. The youth dropped his vessel in terror, and Romola, noticing the black-and-white figure fleeing toward the slope she had admired, realized she was being watched. Remembering the parched sufferer, she quickly filled her pitcher and hastened back.

Inside a house searching for a small cup, she found salt meat and meal—no signs of want. Seating the baby on the ground, she lifted a cup of water to the sufferer, who drank eagerly before closing her eyes and leaning back in relief. Moments later, she opened her eyes and looked at Romola.

"Who are you?"

"I came over the sea," Romola replied. "I arrived this morning. Are all the people dead in these houses?"

"I think they are all ill now—all that are not dead. My father and sister lie dead upstairs, and there is no one to bury them. Soon I shall die too."
