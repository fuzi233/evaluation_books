Caroline Helstone was just eighteen. At eighteen, the true narrative of life has barely begun. Until then, we sit listening to a marvelous fiction—sometimes delightful, often sad, but almost always unreal. Our world seems heroic; its inhabitants are half-divine or semi-demonic. Its scenes are dreamscapes: darker woods, stranger hills, brighter skies, more dangerous waters, sweeter flowers, and tempting fruits spread across an enchanted globe. What a magnificent moon we gaze upon before that time! How the trembling of our hearts at her sight bears witness to its unutterable beauty! As for the sun, it appears a burning heaven—the domain of gods.

But at eighteen, drawing near the edge of these illusory dreams, Elf-land lies behind us, while the shores of Reality rise before us. These shores still look distant, blue, soft, and gentle; we long to reach them. In the sunshine, we see a greenness beneath the azure, imagining spring meadows, catching glimpses of silver lines, and envisioning the roll of living waters. We think, *If we could but reach this land, we would hunger and thirst no more.* Yet, many a wilderness, and often the flood of death, or some stream of sorrow as cold and black as death itself, must be crossed before true bliss can be tasted. Every joy life offers must be earned before it is secured; and how hard that earning is, only those who have wrestled for great prizes know. The heart's blood must gem the brow of the combatant with red beads before the wreath of victory rustles overhead.

At eighteen, we are unaware of this. When Hope smiles and promises happiness tomorrow, we believe her implicitly. When Love wanders like a lost angel to our door, we admit him, welcome him, embrace him instantly. We do not see his quiver; if his arrows penetrate, the wound feels like a thrill of new life. We fear neither poison nor the barb that no leech can extract. That perilous passion—an agony in some phases, an agony throughout for many—is believed to be an unqualified good. In short, at eighteen, we enter the school of experience, and her humbling, crushing, grinding, yet purifying and invigorating lessons await us.

Alas, Experience! No other mentor has so wasted and frozen a face as yours. None wears a robe so black, bears a rod so heavy, or draws the novice so sternly to his task with a hand so inexorable. By your instructions alone can man or woman find a safe track through life's wilds; without them, how they stumble, how they stray! On what forbidden grounds do they intrude, down what dread declivities are they hurled!

Caroline, having been escorted home by Robert, had no desire to spend the remainder of the evening with her uncle. The room where he sat was sacred ground to her; she seldom intruded, and tonight she stayed aloof until the bell rang for prayers. Part of the evening church service was the form of worship observed in Mr. Helstone's household. He read it in his usual nasal voice—clear, loud, and monotonous. Once the rite was over, his niece, according to her habit, stepped up to him.

"Good-night, Uncle."

"Hey! You've been gadding abroad all day—visiting, dining out, and what not!"

"Only at the cottage."

"And have you learned your lessons?"

"Yes."

"And made a shirt?"

"Only part of one."

"Well, that will do. Stick to the needle, learn shirt-making, gown-making, and piecrust-making, and you'll be a clever woman someday. Go to bed now. I'm busy with a pamphlet here."

Soon the niece was enclosed in her small bedroom, the door bolted, her white dressing-gown assumed, her long hair loosened and falling thick, soft, and wavy to her waist. Resting from the task of combing it out, she leaned her cheek on her hand, fixed her eyes on the carpet, and before her rose, close around her drew, the visions we see at eighteen.

Her thoughts were speaking with her, pleasantly, for she smiled as she listened. She looked pretty in meditation, but a brighter thing than she was in that apartment was the spirit of Youthful Hope. According to this flattering prophet, she was to know disappointment no more, to feel chill no more; she had entered the dawn of a summer day—not a false dawn, but the true spring of morning—and her sun would soon rise. It was impossible for her now to suspect she was the sport of delusion. Her expectations seemed warranted; the foundation on which they rested appeared solid.

*"When people love, the next step is they marry,"* was her argument. *"Now, I love Robert, and I feel sure Robert loves me. I have thought so many times before; today I _felt_ it. When I looked up at him after repeating Chénier's poem, his eyes (what handsome eyes he has!) sent the truth through my heart. Sometimes I am afraid to speak to him, lest I seem too frank, lest I seem forward—for I have more than once regretted overflowing, superfluous words, fearing I had said more than he expected, and that he would disapprove what he deemed my indiscretion. But tonight I could have ventured to express any thought; he was so indulgent. How kind he was as we walked up the lane! He does not flatter or say foolish things; his love-making (friendship, I mean; of course I don't yet account him my lover, but I hope he will be so someday) is not like what we read in books—it is far better—original, quiet, manly, sincere. I _do_ like him. I would be an excellent wife to him if he did marry me. I would tell him of his faults (for he has a few), but I would study his comfort, cherish him, and do my best to make him happy. Now, I am sure he will not be cold tomorrow. I feel almost certain that tomorrow evening he will either come here or ask me to go there."*

She recommenced combing her hair, long as a mermaid's. Turning her head as she arranged it, she saw her own face and form in the glass. Such reflections are sobering to plain people: their own eyes are not enchanted by the image; they are confident then that the eyes of others can see no fascination in it. But the fair must naturally draw other conclusions: the picture is charming, and must charm. Caroline saw a shape, a head that, daguerreotyped in that attitude and with that expression, would have been lovely. She could not choose but derive from the spectacle confirmation to her hopes. It was then, in undiminished gladness, that she sought her couch.

In undiminished gladness she rose the next day. As she entered her uncle's breakfast-room and, with soft cheerfulness, wished him good morning, even that little man of bronze himself thought, for an instant, his niece was growing "a fine girl." Generally, she was quiet and timid with him—very docile, but not communicative. This morning, however, she found many things to say. Slight topics alone might be discussed between them; for with a woman—a girl—Mr. Helstone would touch on no other subject. She had taken an early walk in the garden and told him what flowers were beginning to spring there; she inquired when the gardener was to come to trim the borders; she informed him that certain starlings were beginning to build their nests in the church tower (Briarfield church was close to Briarfield Rectory); she wondered why the tolling of the bells in the belfry did not scare them.

Mr. Helstone opined that "they were like other fools who had just paired—in sensible to inconvenience just for the moment." Caroline, perhaps made a little too courageous by her temporary good spirits, here hazarded a remark of a kind she had never before ventured to make regarding observations dropped by her revered relative.

"Uncle," she said, "whenever you speak of marriage, you speak of it scornfully. Do you think people shouldn't marry?"

"It is decidedly the wisest plan to remain single, especially for women."

"Are all marriages unhappy?"

"Millions of marriages are unhappy. If everybody confessed the truth, perhaps all are more or less so."

"You are always vexed when you are asked to come and marry a couple. Why?"

"Because one does not like to act as accessory to the commission of a piece of pure folly."

Mr. Helstone spoke so readily, seeming rather glad of the opportunity to give his niece his mind on this point. Emboldened by the impunity which had hitherto attended her questions, she went a little further.

"But why," she said, "should it be pure folly? If two people like each other, why shouldn't they consent to live together?"

"They tire of each other—they tire of each other in a month. A yokefellow is not a companion; he or she is a fellow-sufferer."

It was by no means naive simplicity that inspired Caroline's next remark; it was a sense of antipathy to such opinions and displeasure at him who held them.

"One would think you had never been married, Uncle. One would think you were an old bachelor."

"Practically, I am so."

"But you have been married. Why were you so inconsistent as to marry?"
