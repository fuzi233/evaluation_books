On the appointed day, Major Pendennis—who had made no special plans—and Arthur, who desired none, arrived together to dine with Sir Francis Clavering. Upon entering the drawing-room, they found only Sir Francis, his wife, and Captain Strong. Arthur was genuinely delighted to see Strong, though the Major received him with a distinctly sour countenance. He was far from pleased to sit down to dinner with Clavering's "house-steward," as he irreverently dubbed Strong. However, the atmosphere shifted slightly with the arrival of Mr. Welbore Welbore, Clavering's country neighbor and fellow MP. Though Welbore was perfectly dull and contributed less to the conversation than the footman trailing behind his chair, he was a respectable gentleman of ancient lineage and seven thousand pounds a year. Such solid, unpretentious society always put the Major at ease.

The party swelled further with the addition of the Dowager Lady Rockminster, who had her own reasons for favoring the Claverings, and Lady Agnes Foker accompanied by her son, Mr. Harry. Mr. Pynsent could not attend due to parliamentary duties, which weighed heavily on the two other senators present. Last to arrive was Miss Blanche Amory. She was dressed in a dazzling white silk gown that showcased her pearly shoulders to perfection. Foker whispered to Pen that she was "a stunner." Indeed, Blanche treated Arthur with exceptional grace on this particular evening, shaking his hand warmly, asking after Fairoaks, Laura, and his mother, and expressing a longing return to the country. She was entirely simple, affectionate, and artless. Harry Foker, rarely finding much to say in the presence of ladies, found himself unusually loquacious beside her. He would have loved to escort her down the stairs, but fate decreed that Pen should accompany her, leaving Harry to walk Mrs. Welbore Welbore, a task assigned to him due to his status as an earl's grandson.

Despite being separated during the descent, Harry managed to secure a seat next to Miss Amory at the dinner table, convinced he had executed a brilliant maneuver. Whether it was his doing or someone else's, the result was the same: Blanche sat between the two young men, each striving to be gallant. From her vantage point, Foker's mother marveled at her son's sudden vivacity. Harry chatted incessantly about current events, ordering soup for himself and humming the famous air from *La Sylphide*.

"Seen Taglioni in the *Sylphide*, Miss Amory?" he asked. "Bring me that *soupe* de volaille again, if you please... Can't think where the chicken legs go, I wonder? She's clipping in the *Sylphide*, isn't she?" He began to hum the melody of that most graceful of all dances.

"Will the young folks ever see anything so charming, so classic, anything like Taglioni?" he continued.

"Miss Amory is a sylph herself," remarked Mr. Pen.

"What a delightful tenor voice you have, Mr. Foker," said the young lady. "I'm sure you've been well taught. I sing a little myself. I'd love to sing with you."

Pen recalled that similar sentiments had once been directed toward him, and that Blanche had enjoyed singing duets with him in the past. Sneering inwardly, he wondered how many other gentlemen she had shared her vocal talents with since his time, but deemed it inappropriate to ask aloud. Instead, adopting the tenderest possible tone, he said, "I should like to hear you sing again, Miss Blanche. I never heard a voice I liked so well as yours, I think."

"I thought you liked Laura's," Blanche replied.

"Laura's is a contralto; that voice is very often out, you know," Pen said, with a hint of bitterness. "I've heard plenty of music in London. I'm tired of those professionals—they sing too loud—or perhaps I've grown too old or too blasé. One ages quickly in London, Miss Amory. Like all old fellows, I only care for the songs of my youth."

"I prefer English music. Foreign songs aren't really my thing. Get me some saddle of mutton," interrupted Mr. Foker.

"I adore English ballads above all else," agreed Miss Amory. "Sing me one of the old songs after dinner, will you?" requested Pen, imploringly.

"Shall I sing you an English song after dinner?" asked the *Sylphide*, turning to Mr. Foker. "I'll do it if you promise to come up soon," she added, flashing a gaze that might have been described as a broadside.

"I'll come up after dinner, fast enough," he replied simply. "I don't mind much wine afterward—I take my share at dinner, you know. Once I've had enough, I toddle up to tea. I'm a domestic character, Miss Amory; my habits are simple. And when I'm pleased, I'm generally in a good humor, aren't I, Pen? That jelly, please—not that one, the other with the cherries inside. How on earth do they get those cherries inside the jelly?"

In this fashion, the artless youth prattled on, and Miss Amory listened with inexhaustible patience. When the ladies retired to the upper rooms, Blanche secured promises from both young men to leave the table shortly before departing with kind glances cast at each. She dropped her gloves on Foker's side of the table and her handkerchief on Pen's. Her politeness toward Foker seemed marginally more encouraging than her kindness to Arthur, yet the benevolent little creature did her utmost to ensure both gentlemen were content. As she rushed out the door, Foker caught her final glance—a bright look that swept over Captain Strong's broad white waistcoat and landed squarely on Harry Foker. The door closed on the charmer; he sighed deeply and swallowed a bumper of claret.

Since this was not one of the grand seasonal parties, dinner had been served early. With the company small and Miss Blanche eager to reach her piano, Lady Clavering signaled her departure promptly. It was still daylight when the ladies ascended to the upper apartments, where flower-embroidered balconies offered views of the two parks, poor couples strolling in one, and the equipages of dandies passing through the other. The sun had not yet set behind the elms of Kensington Gardens, still gilding the statue of the Duke of Wellington, when the ladies left the gentlemen to their wine.

Windows were thrown open to admit the fresh air, offering passersby a tantalizing glimpse of six gentlemen in white waistcoats surrounded by decanters and fruit. Little boys jumping the railings shouted, "Hi, Jim, wouldn't you like to be there and have a slice of that pineapple?" Meanwhile, noble carriages conveyed their occupants to Belgravia, while a policeman with heavy boots patrolled the front. Evening shadows lengthened, the gasman lit the lamps before Sir Francis's door, and the butler illuminated the antique Gothic chandelier over the carved oak table. Thus, from the street, the house appeared as a nocturnal feast of candles and wine; from within, it was a vision of a calm summer evening overlooking St. James's Park, where a few stars began to twinkle.

Jeames, leaning casually against the doorpost, gazed outward at the tranquil park scene. Nearby, a spectator clinging to the railings observed the interior. Policeman X, passing by, ignored both, fixing his attention instead on the figure standing by the railings and peering into the dining room. This individual was gorgeously attired in chains, jewelry, and a waistcoat that gleamed under the house lights. His boots were polished to a shine, his coat adorned with brass buttons, and large white wristbands covered his knuckles. He looked so imposing that Policeman X initially mistook him for a Member of Parliament or a person of significant standing.

However, whatever his rank, this "person of consideration" was clearly intoxicated. He lurched and reeled, his hat cocked precariously over wild, bloodshot eyes. His black hair appeared suspiciously artificial, and his whiskers dyed a deep Tyrian purple. As Captain Strong's laughter rang out from the window following one of his own crude jokes, the man on the pavement laughed and sniggered in a bizarre manner, slapping his thigh and winking at Jeames as if to say, "Plush, my boy, wasn't that a good story?"

Jeames, distracted from the moon by this sublunary spectacle, felt a growing unease. "A holtercation," he muttered later in the servants' hall. "A disturbance with a feller in the streets is never no good; and indeed, he wasn't hired for any such purpose." After observing the man, who continued to laugh, reel, and nod with tipsy confidence, Jeames stepped out of the portico and softly called, "Policeman," beckoning Officer X.

X marched up, resolute, with one Berlin glove tucked into his belt. Jeames pointed silently with his index finger at the laughing figure by the railings. Saying nothing else, he stood there in the calm summer evening, pointing calmly—a grand sight.

"Now, sir, will you have the kindness to move on?" asked X.
