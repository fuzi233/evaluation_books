Swift horses pulled the hackney-coach through the muddy chaos of Strand and Fleet Street, bearing young George Warrington toward the rescue of his imprisoned brother. To anyone familiar with Hogarth's depictions of eighteenth-century London, the scene would seem all too real: the weary pace of the vehicle, the poorly hung bodies, the treacherous pavements riddled with holes, and the vast, sucking quagmires of winter mud that threatened to swallow the wheels whole. For our young traveler, this chaotic drive from Piccadilly to Fleet Street stretched with a weariness equal to the journey from Marlborough to London he had undertaken that same morning.

Before arriving, George had penned urgent letters to Harry, first announcing his presence in Bristol, and later revealing the monumental news of his existence and safe return from captivity. Yet, a shadow of war loomed large; England and France were at odds, and French privateers relentlessly hunted British merchant vessels, often seizing them mere miles from shore. Tragically, the letter carrying the tidings of George's restoration likely fell into the hands of a French captain aboard an American ship captured near port. Consequently, the message informing Harry of his brother's arrival in England never reached his hands. It lay untouched in the apartments where Harry was held captive, delivered on the third morning following his imprisonment, only to remain sealed when the irascible Mr. Ruff, Harry's jailer, refused to surrender a single item of his ward's property.

Upon reaching London, George made straight for those apartments to locate his brother. The maid-servant who answered the knock recoiled in sheer terror at the striking resemblance between the visitor and her absent master, screaming and fleeing back to her mistress. The landlady, unwilling to admit the truth—that poor Harry was actually a prisoner held by her husband—devised a lie. She claimed Mr. Warrington had simply vacated his lodgings, offering no clue as to his whereabouts. George, however, knew better. Clarges Street, near Bond Street, was close to his mind. He recalled the maps he had studied and the stories of Aunt Bernstein's kindness recounted in Harry's letters to Virginia. Perhaps, he reasoned, Aunt Bernstein could guide him, or perhaps Harry was currently under her roof. He harbored a quiet hope that Madam Esmond had finally found cause to repent of her former harshness; indeed, it was time for a woman of her age to reflect, considering the sins of the past century were still fresh wounds to the virtuous. Rachel Esmond rarely spoke of her eldest daughter, nor did Madam Esmond mention her sister, yet the silence of female virtue on matters of sin remained inexorable, refusing to pardon crimes committed decades prior.

George's demeanor hardened as he presented himself to the old lady, influenced by the fearful tales his agent in Bristol had spun regarding his brother's current state. Gumbo, the servant, had barely recovered from the shock of seeing his "dead" master return when he managed to stutter out a few words concerning the young master's precarious situation. To George, it was a given that relations were duty-bound to rescue a brother in distress. *Oh, George,* the narrative whispered, *how little you truly understood the labyrinthine ways of London!* If a true philanthropist sought to rescue every poor soul he encountered, the combined wealth of all the American provinces would fail to suffice.

Yet, as George jolted through the darkening streets toward the house of his brother's captivity, the old lady's agitated expression struck a chord within him. *She feels for my poor Harry,* he thought, chastising his earlier judgment. *I have been too hasty.* Life, he realized, demanded constant self-rebuke for such precipitous judgments. Once the damage was done, however, no amount of regret could undo it. "Quick, coachman!" he urged, though the journey from Clarges Street to the Temple felt agonizingly slow. Gumbo, who knew the bailiff's house intimately, led the way. A series of doors opened and closed warily, guarded by a Jewish janitor who stared in awe at a second Mr. Warrington emerging from the shadows, lit by a flaring tallow torch. "Come to see that gentleman?" the janitor asked, pausing to identify the newcomer. "Yes, but wait. This is Mr. Warrington's brother from America. Gumbo must prepare his master first."

They were ushered into a small office, nominally belonging to Mr. Amos, where a stout gentleman in a cloak and laced hat sat by a guttering candle, conversing with the bailiff. A young porter preceded George, holding a candle that cast sufficient light over the cramped space.

"We are not angry any longer, Harry!" the stout gentleman exclaimed in a cheery tone, rising to extend a hand to the newcomer. "Thank God, my boy! Mr. Amos assures me there shall be no difficulty in securing bail for James and myself, and we shall settle your affairs by breakfast. Why, angels and ministers of grace! Who are you?" He stumbled back slightly as the stranger gripped his hand firmly.

The stranger, however, clung to the hand with renewed strength. "God bless you, sir!" he cried. "I know who you are. You must be Colonel Lambert, whose kindness to my poor Harry I have read of. And I am the brother you have heard of—the one left for dead at the Battle of Braddock, revived after eighteen months among the French. I live to thank God and thank you for your kindness to my Harry." His voice trembled with emotion.

"James! James! News!" shouted the stout gentleman to a figure in red robes who entered the room. "The dead have risen! Here is Harry Scapegrace's brother returned, and with his scalp intact!" George stood by the light, having removed his hat. "This is my brother-bail, Mr. Warrington!" the colonel announced. "And this is Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe at your service. There has been a slight misunderstanding between Harry and me, Mr. George, but he is now pacified, is he not, James?"

"He is full of gratitude," replied Mr. Wolfe, bowing respectfully to Mr. Warrington.

"Harry also wrote home of Mr. Wolfe, sir," George added earnestly. "I trust his friends will extend the same kindness to me."

"I wish he had no others but us," Mr. Lambert sighed. "His parents were too refined for him, ultimately leading to this plight."

"Nay, your honors, I have done my utmost to comfort the young gentleman," interjected Harry's landlord, recognizing the rank and integrity of the two officers. "Knowing your honor, and that your security is sound—if you desire, the young gentleman may depart tonight, and I shall rectify everything with the lawyer by morning."

"The debt amounts to five hundred pounds, I believe?" George queried. "With a hundred thanks to these gentlemen, I can pay the sum immediately into the officers' hands, providing the necessary acknowledgments and cautions. But I shall never forget, gentlemen, that you aided my brother in his hour of need. For this, I say thank you, and God bless you, in my mother's name and mine."

Meanwhile, Gumbo ascended the stairs to Harry's apartment. Though the young gentleman might have chided the negro for returning so late, Harry was deeply soothed by the conversation he had shared with the departing officer. Sitting over his pipe of Virginia tobacco, he sat in a somber mood; Maria's recent kindness and affection, though evident, had not entirely assuaged his troubles, leaving him with a lingering dread of the consequences to which that fidelity bound him. Suddenly, the homely features and eager, outstretched hand of Mr. Wolfe appeared to lift his spirits. Hearing that Mr. Lambert was below, the purpose of their visit became clear. Despite Harry's notorious ill-temper and suspicious nature, the gentleman was resolved to assist him, even defying Mr. Wolfe's counsel.

"For you were wrong, Mr. Warrington," the Colonel admitted frankly. "You would not have been corrected otherwise. You, a young man, resorted to hard words and unkind behavior toward your superior, one of the finest gentlemen walking God's earth. Observe his answer to your wayward temper. Will you bear with a friend who speaks plainly? Martin Lambert has acted as he always does—as the best Christian, the best friend, the most kind and generous of men. If you require further proof of his goodness, consider this: He has converted me, who, as I confess without shame, was angered by your treatment of him, and has absolutely brought me down here to stand as your bail. Let us both cry *Peccavimus*! Harry, shake hands with your friend! He awaits you in the room below. He would not come until he knew how you would receive him."

"I think he is a good man!" Harry groaned, his spirit breaking. "I was wild and angry when we last met, Colonel Wolfe. Nay, perhaps he was right in returning those trinkets; I was hurt by his action. Go down to him, will you be so kind, sir? Tell him I am sorry, ask his pardon, and..." He paused, rubbing his hand across his eyes, overcome by emotion. "God bless him for his generosity."

"Tell him all this yourself, Harry!" the Colonel urged, seizing the young fellow's hand. "No deputy could speak it half so well. Come with me now."
