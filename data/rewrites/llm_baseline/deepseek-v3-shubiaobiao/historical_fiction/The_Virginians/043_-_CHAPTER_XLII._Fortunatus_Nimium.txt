Though Harry Warrington remained steadfast in his resolve to honor the dreary promise wrung from him by his cousin, let no charitable reader suppose the arrangement sat lightly upon his spirit. Indeed, he would have rejoiced to be free of it. Perhaps the thrashing dealt to poor Will had been, in part, an attempt to hasten that liberation. Harry might have reasoned thus: *A brawl between us will surely provoke a feud with the family. Maria, bound by blood, may side with her brother. I, of course, will offer no retraction. Will, in his wounded pride, may call me out—then we shall see who is the better man. Amidst the rupture, the engagement might dissolve, and I shall be released.*

So Harry laid his plans and struck his blow—yet the explosion left little damage in its wake. Will Esmond’s nose swelled, his eye blackened for a week, but no challenge followed. The quarrel ended as so many do among sensible men: a brief, private reckoning, with no witnesses to trumpet the outcome.

Maria, far from defending her kin, championed her cousin. Even Lord Castlewood, once informed, declared Will the aggressor. The chaplain’s testimony confirmed it: Will had struck first, and Harry’s retaliation was only natural. The earl dismissed any notion of apology—let Will seek forgiveness, not Harry.

Yet Harry stood firm: no reconciliation until his horse was returned or his wager settled. History does not record how that debt was resolved, but the cousins soon moved in the same circles again without further violence.

Maria’s elder brother had once welcomed the match, content to see his sister—long past the bloom of youth, her reputation frayed by life’s wear—married off to any willing suitor, particularly a Virginian. Let her retire to his wilderness estate; Harry might console himself as he pleased once time claimed her. But after a private interview with Madame Bernstein, the earl’s enthusiasm waned. He even ventured to dissuade Maria, lamenting the "misery" awaiting Harry, bound by a rash pledge made at one-and-twenty.

*Misery?* Maria scoffed. Had Castlewood not urged this very union weeks prior? She saw through his sudden pity. Madame Bernstein had offered money to break the engagement—this was no secret. But Harry was a man of honor. She trusted him.

With a shrug, the earl retreated to his haunts—White’s, or wherever else his kind gathered. Maria’s suspicions were sharp: the baroness had indeed dangled gold to sever the match. *And so,* he mused bitterly, *my scruples profit others. While I play the guardian, others line their pockets. Why spare him? Let March and the rest pluck him bare—they will, whether I hold back or not.*

Here, the chronicler must confess: to transcribe a man’s unspoken thoughts strains credulity. Yet, knowing Lord Castlewood’s character, is it not fair to infer his resentments? Livy and Thucydides shaped speeches for their heroes; shall we not sketch the workings of a nobleman’s mind?

The truth was plain: wolves circled the lamb. Why should one hungry hunter abstain? Had not Clive himself marveled at his own restraint in India? *"When I think of my opportunities, I am surprised I took so little!"*

To expose a gentleman’s flaws is distasteful. Yet Castlewood’s peers—March and his ilk—would have stripped Harry bare without qualm. They played ruthlessly but paid their debts. Madame Bernstein, though reluctant to tarnish her nephew’s name, had heard Castlewood’s cynicism firsthand. *Why spare a lad doomed to ruin?* he’d sneered, listing Harry’s extravagances: nights at faro, jaunts to Newmarket.

The baroness, alarmed, resolved to warn Harry. She drafted a note—slowly, for she detested writing—then sent a servant to summon him. But fate intervened: illness kept her abed the next day, then engagements piled up, and Harry, ever in motion, slipped through her grasp.

When they finally met, she chided him gently for his wildness (though secretly delighted by it). He laughed—youth would be youth, and his current companions were met in her own drawing room. Pressed further, he dismissed her concerns about Castlewood. *"The world has already enlightened me,"* he said darkly.

Had he played with his cousin?

*"Lost and won, madam,"* Harry replied, gallant as ever. *"In Virginia, we don’t run to our mothers after a bout at cards or a quarrel."* He kissed her cheek, then departed in splendor—velvet and gold, lace at his throat—leaving her servants enriched by his customary largesse.

At White’s, Fortune smiled on him. Nights of staggering luck swelled his purse. He spent lavishly: fine horses, grand entertainments, gifts bestowed without hesitation. For Harry, generosity was instinctive.

And so, discovering Maria’s debts, he paid them without a second thought. Her stepmother and half-sister, though they scorned her, received lavish tokens. *"You might as well get yourself arrested, Will,"* Castlewood drawled. *"The Mohock will ransom you, even after that horse business."*

Will cursed his own shortsightedness. *"Forty pounds! I might have had a thousand."*

Maria, ever pragmatic, accepted her windfall graciously—and promptly contracted fresh debts. Her maid whispered of an impending marriage to a wealthy suitor, coaxing credit from wary tradesmen. When she rolled through London in her coach, Harry at her side, they marveled at her luck—though some questioned his taste in choosing a woman so long past her prime.

Mr. Sparks, the jeweler, arrived at Harry’s lodgings with pearls and a gold étui selected by Maria. Harry added a ring from the man’s tray, dismissed him with a regal wave, and turned his thoughts to others who had shown him kindness. For Theo and Hetty, he chose trinkets, wishing he possessed his brother’s gift for verse. *"Goodwill alone cannot make a poet,"* he sighed, then enlisted Parson Sampson to craft a suitable note.

Thus flowed the days of the Fortunate Youth—gold streaming through his hands, laughter on his lips, and seldom a thought for tomorrow.
