*"Stand before the wise, you see,
Safe you are in certainty!
Though you long have grieved and striven,
He will spot where fault is given.
You may hope for praise sincere,
For he knows where you hit near."*
—GOETHE: *West-östlicher Divan*

The very evening of that quiet visit to the Chelsea cottage, where the Meyronds had debated what public stage name Mirah might use, Daniel Deronda carried home news that would shift the whole shape of the young singer’s future. But for the warm, ragtag Meyrick household, the most life-changing ripple from that conversation landed two days later, at exactly four o’clock. A pair of hansom cab wheels crunched to a stop outside their small front door, followed by a grand, echoing knock and ring so loud it outstripped the usual most exciting sound in their quiet neighborhood: the cheerful tinkle of the muffin man’s bell.

All five women were at home that afternoon, the two small parlours thrown open to make space for Kate’s drawing board and a great sprawling length of shared embroidery that had replaced their old satin cushion projects, a sort of household group project that any free clever hand could pitch in on. Mrs Meyrick bent over one corner of the stretched fabric, Mab in the middle, Amy at the far end. Mirah, whose sewing skills were no more refined than a tailor-bird’s haphazard nest stitch, her childhood education in needlework long neglected, perched light as a sparrow on a camp stool reading to the group. She also served unknowingly as Kate’s model for a vignette title page: a soft, rapt young woman, the very symbol of an audience lost to a book on the family tea table. She was reading Charles Lamb’s *The Praise of Chimney-Sweeps*, her voice clear and honeyed, all of them grinning over the line about “innocent blackness,” when that grand knock dragged their thoughts far from chimney sweeps and cozy essays.

“Goodness!” Mrs Meyrick said, blinking. “Could that be Lady Mallinger? Do you see a grand carriage out front, Amy?”
“Only a hansom cab,” Amy reported, peeking out the window. “It’s a gentleman.”
“Prime Minister, obviously,” Kate said dryly. “Hans says the greatest man in London doesn’t think twice about hopping in a hansom.”
“Oh! Oh what if it’s Lord Russell!” Mab squeaked, bouncing a little on her stool.

All five bright faces were crinkled in amusement when their scatterbrained old maid servant wandered in holding a calling card, leaving the parlour door propped open behind her. Standing on the threshold was no respected gray-haired premier: tall, broad-shouldered, massive-jawed, his bright flamboyant hair tucked neatly back, gold spectacles glinting, his fine kid gloves and kerseymere coat lending him an air of grandeur that made their whole cottage shrink to the size of a dollhouse. The card read *Julius Klesmer*.

Quick as always to read a room, the “little mother” Meyrick felt a surge of quiet relief that the greatest music critic in the city had come calling on Mirah, rather than demanding the orphan girl travel across London to call on him. Even Mab, staring wide-eyed, thought their tiny cottage piano suddenly looked like a silly tin toy, their whole small, happy household no bigger than a family of mice nesting in the Tuileries. But for all his grand, imposing air, Klesmer was no distant snob. He’d grown up in a cottage no bigger than this on the edge of a Bohemian village, and he knew every thread of the quiet romance and hardship that came with scraping by on a small income in the art world.

“I hope I have not overstepped,” he said, bowing to Mrs Meyrick with perfect deference. “I was in the neighborhood, and Deronda told me we might have the honor of meeting the young lady he speaks of so highly—Miss Lapidoth.” He had spotted Mirah the very second he walked through the door, but with gentle politeness he glanced around at all three Meyrick sisters first, as if uncertain which of them was the singer.
“These are my daughters,” Mrs Meyrick said, waving gently toward Mirah. “This is Miss Lapidoth.”
“Ah,” Klesmer breathed, a warm, radiant smile spreading across his face as he bowed low to her. “You will not mind beginning our acquaintance by singing for me now, will you? No use wasting time on small talk.”
Mirah’s face lit up with quiet, unflurried pleasure. She already liked him: she could tell he was the kind of great musician who would tell her the truth, sharp but kind, like her old Vienna teacher. “I would be glad,” she said, moving to the piano. “Shall I accompany myself?”
“By all means,” he said, seating himself as invited, with a clear view of her face. The little mother would never admit it out loud, but she thought to herself, *Of course he’ll like her singing better if he can see how lovely she is*.

Every heart in the room except Mirah’s was thudding with anxiety as he sat there, a slight listening frown on his face, the sisters only daring to glance at him out of the corners of their eyes. They clung to the little comfort that even the grandest prince who had heard every famous singer in Europe thought Mirah’s voice was the sweetest he’d ever heard. Mirah herself was entirely at ease, choosing the setting of Leopardi’s grand ode to Italy that her old teacher Leo had written for her, the recitative soft and burning, the mournful, rhythmic plaint of the “But I see no glory” line winding into a triumphant, devout climax. When the last note faded, Klesmer sat quiet for a long beat.
“That is old Leo’s work,” he said finally.
“Yes,” Mirah said, a soft sad smile on her face. “He was my last teacher in Vienna. Fierce as a storm, and kinder than anyone I ever knew. He said my voice was never meant for the opera stage. He was right.”
“Sing more, please,” Klesmer said, pursing his lips, shaking out his long fingers, muttering half-voiced notes no one else in the room could understand. The three girls all secretly loathed him a little for not uttering a single word of praise. Mrs Meyrick’s chest grew tight with alarm. Mirah, simply eager to give him exactly what he asked for, moved straight into Gretchen’s songs from *Faust*, no pauses, no asking permission, letting the melodies spill out unbroken.

When the last note died, Klesmer stood, paced the full length of their tiny parlour, then walked back to the piano where Mirah had risen, her little hands folded meekly in front of her, waiting for his judgment. All at once his frown melted away, his eyes bright, he held out his hand abruptly. “Let us shake hands,” he said. “You are a musician.”

Mab had to press a hand to her mouth to stop herself from sobbing. All three of the girls instantly thought Klesmer was the most wonderful man alive. Mrs Meyrick let out a long, long breath she had not realized she was holding.
Then the familiar sharp seriousness settled back on his face, his hand turning palm-down to tap lightly at the back of Mirah’s knuckles. “But not for great stages,” he said. “No high vast opera roofs. You are no skylark, flying to the rafters. We must be modest.” He paused, and Mab’s adoration faded fast—how dare he assume Mirah was the slightest bit conceited? Mirah said nothing, quiet, waiting for the full weight of his advice. “I would never advise you to sing in any space larger than a private drawing room. But there, you will shine. In London, that is one of the kindest, most rewarding careers a singer can have. You will give lessons, you will be invited to perform at private gatherings, you will make a steady, dignified living. I am hosting a small private concert at my house next Wednesday. Will you come sing for the guests?”

Mirah’s hands flew together, her face glowing. “Oh, I would be so grateful. That is exactly the life I wanted—far better than screaming for crowds in a public theatre. What should I work on most to improve?”
Klesmer rumbled a few half-spoken notes, shaking out his fingers, before he spoke clearly again. “I will introduce you to Astorga. He is the foster-father of all good singing, and he will give you every piece of guidance you could ever want.” He turned back to Mrs Meyrick. “Mrs Klesmer will call on you before Wednesday, if you will permit it. She is a thorough musician, and has more ears in her soul than most singers you will ever meet. Your singing will satisfy her. *Vor den Wissenden sich stellen*—you know the rest?”
“*Sicher ist’s in allen Fällen*,” Mirah finished promptly, the old Goethe line about safety before those who truly understand rolling off her tongue like second nature. Klesmer nodded, charmed, saying *Schön!* and held out his hand to say goodbye.

Then he suddenly fixed his gaze on Mab. “That young lady is musical, I can see it,” he said decisively. Mab blushed so hot she felt like she was on fire, certain he would order her to sit down and play for him, her heart turning to molten wax in her chest. Mirah spoke up for her quickly. “She is, she has a lovely little touch on the piano.”
“Please, it’s just a scramble, not a touch,” Mab moaned, half in agony. But her joy burst so wide she could barely contain it when Klesmer turned to Mrs Meyrick and said, “Would she like to come along to the recital on Wednesday, to hear the rest of the music?”
“There could not be any greater pleasure for her,” Mrs Meyrick said, smiling.

Klesmer bowed grandly to all three sisters, so deep the diagonal of their small parlour could barely contain his elegant bend, his face as grave and stately as the old Holbein portraits hanging on their walls. Mrs Meyrick slipped out to the landing with him, closing the door behind them. He nodded his frowning, kind nod. “She will do well, if she never tries to overstrain her voice. Deronda told me supporting herself is her top priority. She will make a solid income. You are taking good care of her. She looks like a good girl.”
“She is an angel,” Mrs Meyrick said, warm and fierce.
“No,” Klesmer said, playful. “She is a pretty Jewess. The angels don’t get to take credit for her. I think she has found a guardian angel, though,” he finished, bowing and letting himself out.

Back inside, the four young women stared at each other in silence till the front door banged shut behind him. Then the room erupted. Mab clapped her hands and danced wildly around the room, nearly tangling herself in the great embroidery stretch. Mrs Meyrick kissed Mirah and blessed her. Amy said, very practically, “We can never get her a new dress made before Wednesday!” Kate laughed and exclaimed, “Thank heaven my drawing table didn’t get knocked over!” Mirah had sat back down on the piano stool, silent, tears rolling down her cheeks as she looked at her new family.

“Come now, Mab, sit reasonably,” Mrs Meyrick said, laughing. “Let’s talk this through.”
“Yes, talk!” Mab said, dropping down onto her low stool, hugging her knees. “I feel like I’m a normal size again, not a silly toy. Hans said he was coming this afternoon. I wish he’d been here—though there wouldn’t have been room for him. Mirah, why do you look sad?”
“I’m too happy,” Mirah said, wiping at her tears. “I’m so full of gratitude to all of you, and he was so very kind.”
“Only at the very end!” Mab said, sharp. “I hated him while he was frowning and just saying ‘continue’. I thought he was dreadfully ugly from the top of his shiny hair to the tip of his polished boot. People shouldn’t hoard all their praise just so they can pop it out like a surprise later, make you miserable before they make you happy. But I forgive him everything, now that he invited me to the recital. How did he know I was musical? Is it my bulging forehead, ma? Do I look like a newt peeking out from under a stone?”
“He saw the way you were leaning forward, listening to every note of Mirah’s singing,” Mrs Meyrick said. “He’s got magic spectacles, that man, he sees everything.”

Just then Hans Meyerick walked through the door. They immediately laid out their problem, asking his artist’s eye what Mirah should wear. Hans knelt on a hassock, surveying her plain black merino dress from a few steps back. Mirah pleaded that it was good enough, the kind of costume she would have worn on stage playing a poor Jewish girl singing to fashionable drawing rooms. But Hans, suddenly uncharacteristically fastidious, shook his head. “It’s a little too theatrical. You don’t want everyone there to look at you and think you are a charity case. You have talents that will soon make people indebted to you. You’re not going to sit out on ruins. Take her to Regent Street, little mother. Buy her a proper black silk dress, the kind any lady would wear to a private concert. There’s more than enough time before Wednesday.”
Mrs Meyrick nodded. “I think Mr Deronda would like that, too. For her to look like she belongs.”
“Of course he would,” Hans said, a little sharp, turning away to flip idly through Kate’s drawings, a little stung that Mirah had immediately agreed when they mentioned Deronda’s name. He plopped down at their little cottage piano, throwing his head back, mimicking Klesmer’s grand, sweeping air as if he were surveying a whole opera house full of people, then turned to Mirah, half shy. “Stop me if you think this mockery is rude, won’t you?”
Mirah smiled, soft and sweet, warm as summer. “I could never dislike anything that comes from you,” she said, glancing over at Mrs Meyrick.

Hans’s chest swelled with quiet, bright hope. How could he help it, when it felt like every little sweet thing Mirah said to him was a sign that he might be the one to pluck that soft rose for his own?
