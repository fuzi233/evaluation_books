Our dear friend Arthur Pendennis had only just arrived in London—a fleeting visit, you understand—having deposited some fellow travelers at their West End hotel before scurrying back to the sun-deprived (but not entirely cheerless) confines of Lamb Court. Freedom, after all, is a passable substitute for sunlight in the Temple, and though Templars may grumble, they do relish their liberty.

Pen’s manservant informed him that Warrington was also in residence, so Arthur promptly bounded up the stairs to his old friend’s chambers, where the familiar scent of pipe smoke and the rustle of newspapers greeted him like a well-worn glove.

Their reunion was conducted in the traditional British manner—brusque, unsentimental, and carrying beneath its gruff exterior enough warmth to melt a lesser nation’s heart.

“Well, young one!” said Warrington, glancing up from his papers.

“How are you, old boy?” replied Pen, extending a hand.

And thus, with all the poetic flourish of two men discussing the weather, did these two souls acknowledge a friendship that had weathered months of separation.

(Contrast this, if you will, with the effusive embraces of the Continent—*Ce bon coeur! Ce cher Alphonse!*—or the mustachioed kisses of German comrades. But no, the Englishman saves his raptures for his diary.)

They exchanged the usual pleasantries—Pen inquired after Warrington’s Swiss travels, to which the latter merely grunted about the inferiority of continental tobacco until Strasbourg. (Never mind the Alps; the man’s soul had been stirred, but he’d sooner discuss a decent smoke than admit it.)

Then it was Pen’s turn to recount his own recent misadventures—boredom in the country, the interminable slog of canvassing, and now a brief London respite before subjecting himself to the mortal tedium of Tunbridge Wells.

“And the seat in Parliament?” Warrington prodded.

“Oh, that’s settled,” said Pen airily. “Clavering retires when Parliament reconvenes, and I shall slip into his shoes like a well-oiled footman.”

“Under which banner? Liberal? Tory? Or are you striking out as an independent nuisance?”

Pen waved a hand. “Politics are all the same these days. I haven’t enough acres to be a Protectionist, nor enough hypocrisy to pretend I’d be one if I did. I’ll support Government—mostly—and perhaps dabble in a bit of reform. Sanitary measures, colonial policy, that sort of thing.”

Warrington smirked. “Ah, so we’re to be the enlightened aristocrat? Lecturing the working man by day, dining with Ministers by night?”

“I make no grand claims,” said Pen. “I shan’t pose as a Moses descending with divine wisdom. If I see a good cause, I’ll back it. If a decent opportunity arises, I’ll take it. No raptures, no revolutions—just a practical man making his way.”

“And after ambition,” Warrington mused, “comes love. How fares *that* campaign?”

Pen sighed dramatically. “I suppose I must marry sooner or later. Blanche is amiable enough—sings nicely, tolerates my smoking, and comes with a respectable fortune. My uncle assures me the Begum will be generous.”

“So we are to follow the money?”

“Life *is* a transaction, George,” said Pen, with a philosopher’s resignation.

A pause. Then Warrington, ever the tactician, ventured: “And Laura?”

Pen’s face darkened. “Laura,” he said, voice dropping, “is the finest creature God ever placed on this earth.”

Warrington studied him. “Took you long enough to realize that.”

“Doesn’t everyone recognize their blessings only once they’re lost?” Pen retorted bitterly.

The two lapsed into silence, each lost in thought—Pen in melancholy reflection, Warrington in quiet sympathy.

At length, duty called. “I must report to my uncle,” Pen declared. “The Major’s getting impatient—wants to see me settled with a wife, a seat, and an heir before he departs this world.”

“How touching,” Warrington deadpanned. “The old rogue dreams of dynasty even as he clings to his Pall Mall armchair.”

Pen grinned. “Well, the ‘new blood’ I’m introducing isn’t exactly aristocratic. Blanche’s lineage features ship captains, adventurers, and a baronet of dubious repute.”

“Ah, but every family needs its black sheep,” said Warrington. “Adds spice to the pedigree.”

Refreshed, scented, and resplendent in a freshly tied cravat, Pen prepared to depart.

“Off to dazzle some unfortunate ladies?” Warrington inquired, eyeing his friend’s meticulous grooming.

“Dining with Laura and Lady Rockminster, actually. They’re in town—brought them up from the country.”

Warrington’s eyebrows lifted. “Indeed?”

“Come along,” Pen urged. “The old dragon claims she’s tired of me already—says she’d rather hear *your* stories.”

After minimal persuasion (and a brief internal struggle with his better judgment), Warrington agreed.

And so they set off, two unlikely companions—one polished to a shine, the other comfortably rumpled—bound for Jermyn Street, where further complications (and perhaps redemption) awaited.
