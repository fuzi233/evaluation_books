The truly solemn traffic of the world, one is told, is done out of sight: in counting-houses where sugar is weighed and cloves smelt for freshness; in tailor’s garrets where a man’s knee is the only table he owns; in law-chambers where parchment crackles like old leaves and the ink has an iron tang. It is honest work, and it fills the day; but it yields a thin harvest to the story-teller. He must catch people when their aprons are untied and their ledgers shut—when they have slipped, hungry or hopeful, into the bright, noisy places where a fiddler saws at a tune and a pot of coffee keeps hissing as if it too would speak.

So we are forever loitering, like idlers with good ears, about theatre-doors and in coffee-room corners, while the great engine of life grinds on elsewhere: the carts creak, the looms click, the nursery floors are scrubbed till the boards smell of wet soap, and the poor fellow who will presently steal a kiss behind a rail must first beat the stones all day long with his heels. The ear knows this difference before the mind does. There is the harsh rattle of labour—necessary, unpitied—and there is the chosen sound of pleasure: violins, clapping hands, the sudden hush before a line is spoken. In that hush, sometimes, a man hears his own heart most clearly.

For Harry Warrington, London had grown unkind in its very acoustics. At White’s and Arthur’s the laughter that used to open for him like a door now fell flat and cold; the murmur of welcome had turned into that peculiar, cutting silence with which fine people punish a man without so much as lifting a finger. The Ring, the Mall, the gaming-table—those places had ceased to know him; or if they knew him, they knew him as one knows a tune one is tired of, and will not have repeated.

Madame de Bernstein, who had once met him with kisses and little cries of triumph, was for braving the world into submission. She lectured him as if courage were merely a posture to be assumed, as one puts on a coat.

“You pull a long face, Harry, and complain of the world’s treatment of you,” she said, tapping her crutch as if to keep time to her own counsel. “Fiddlededee! Everybody swallows impertinence. If you get a box on the ear now you are poor and cast down, you must smile, say nothing, and if you can, revenge it ten years after. Moi qui vous parle—do you think I never ate humble-pie? We all do. Since you are no longer the Fortunate Youth, be the Clever Youth. Go about more than ever. Be civil to everybody—women especially. Show spirit, yes, but manage it. With economy—and by your brother’s admirable generosity—you may still make a genteel figure. With that handsome person, you cannot fail to catch a rich heiress. Tenez! Go among the City people: they do not know who is out of fashion at Court. And when did you go to my Lady Yarmouth? She took a fancy to you. I desire you will be constant at her evenings, and lose no opportunity of paying court to her.”

It was bitter to Harry that the voice which had once praised his simplicity now urged him to barter it. The old woman—fickle as a weathercock though she was—had been dear to him in those first English days. Now she took the side of the world; and with the same mouth that had kissed his cheek she flung little gibes at him before her guests, so that his honest face burned as if held too near a fire.

Even the household air changed. Servants who used to start at his step and run with messages now let him wait in passages where the scent of waxed floor and cooling gravy mocked him. My lady was “engaged,” “indisposed,” “gone out,” and the people did not press him to dine as they had in the days when his fortune made him a savoury morsel to all appetites. It is a strange education, to learn how quickly the world’s politeness spoils, like cream left too long in heat.

He carried his wounded pride—not to the clubs that had flung him off, but to the Lamberts, whose house had that plain, wholesome comfort of well-ordered domestic life: boards newly scoured, bread cut clean, a joint roasted to the proper point with the fat salted so it crisped, not burned. While Mrs. Lambert and her daughters moved about their quiet tasks—needlework, accounts, the steady housekeeping that keeps a family’s peace—Harry would sit in a window-seat, silent, his pipe cold in his fingers, as if he had forgotten even the small solace of smoke.

“I know you are the best people alive,” he would say, with a miserable attempt at a smile, “and the kindest; and that I must be the dullest company in the world—yes, that I am.”

“Well, you are not very lively, Harry,” Hetty said. She had a brisk way of speaking, like the snap of a fire when a fresh chip is thrown on, and she had begun to wonder—being young, and too candid for her own ease—whether the hero she had imagined was not less splendid than her fancy.

“If he is unhappy, why should he be lively?” Theo asked gently. Theo’s voice was of that sort which does not fight for notice; it is heard because it is true. “He has a good heart, and is pained at his friends’ desertion. There is no harm in that.”

“I would have too much spirit to show I was hurt,” Hetty cried, her little hands clenched. “I would smile though that horrible old painted woman boxed my ears. She is horrible, mamma. You think so yourself, Theo! Own now, you think so! You said so last night, and acted her coming in on her crutch and grinning round to the company.”

Theo reddened to the very edge of her hair. “I may not like her,” she said; “but there is no reason I should call Harry’s aunt names before Harry’s face.”

“You provoking thing; you are always right!” Hetty burst out, and then, checked by her own better breeding, turned to Harry with a sudden penitence. “Indeed, Harry, it was very wrong of me to make rude remarks about any of your relations.”

“I don’t care about the others, Hetty,” Harry said; “but it seems hard that this one should turn upon me. I had got to be very fond of her; and somehow it makes me mad when people I’m very fond of act unkind.”

“Suppose George were to do so?” Hetty asked—half mischief, half a child’s hunger to see what power her words had.

Harry started as if struck. The blood came into his face, and he stood up straight, his hand on the chair as if it were the hilt of a sword.

“You are very clever and very lively,” he said, with a heat that made his plain speech blaze, “and you may suppose a number of things; but not that, Hetty, if you please. You don’t know my brother as I know him—or you wouldn’t take such a liberty as to suppose my brother George could do anything unkind or unworthy!”

Hetty, who had never before seen his gentleness sharpened into anger, turned white. She looked at him once—quickly, as if to fix the sight in her mind—and then said nothing at all.

A moment later, as he took his leave, Harry’s voice softened back into its natural honesty. “I’m very sorry, and I beg your pardon, Hetty, if I said anything rough or unkind; but I always fight up if anybody says anything against George.”

Hetty did not answer. She held out her hand, made him a prim little curtsey, and kept her pale lips close.

When Theo and Hetty were together that night, their hair half undone and curl-papers at hand—those small, intimate rites by which girls make a sanctuary of a bedroom—Hetty’s confession came out in a whisper that was half shame, half wonder.

“Oh,” she said, “I thought it would be so happy to see him every day, and was so glad when papa said we were to stay in London! And now I do see him, you see, I go on offending him. I can’t help it. And I know he is not clever, Theo. But oh! isn’t he good, and kind, and brave? Didn’t he look handsome when he was angry?”

“You silly little thing,” Theo answered, though she spoke with a tenderness that showed she understood more than she chose to say. “You are always trying to make him look handsome.”

It was George and Harry now, and Hetty and Theo, moving in that narrow orbit in which young lives so often whirl before fate flings them outward. General Lambert had a blunt way of calling his wife “Bo”—a monosyllable as plain as bread—partly as a jest at her incurable fondness for sentiment and match-making, and partly as a warning not to build castles too quickly on a glance and a blush.

“Little Het may have her fancy,” he would say. “Little girls will; but they get it over. And you know, Molly—” (here Mrs. Lambert could not deny, for the past always speaks loudest in a married man’s memory) “you fancied somebody else before you fancied me.”

“Oh yes,” Mrs. Lambert cried one day, with that ready indignation of a gentle heart, “now he is poor we will show him the door, as all the rest of the world does, I suppose.”

“That is what I always do, isn’t it, Molly?” the General asked, raising his brows. “Turn my back on my friends in distress?”

“No, my dear! I am a goose, now, and that I own, Martin!” And she had recourse, as women will, to the pocket-handkerchief, which is at once flag of surrender and banner of affection.

“Let the poor boy come to us and welcome,” the General said. “Ours is almost the only house in this selfish place where so much can be said for him. He is unhappy, and with us he is at ease; in God’s name, let him be with us.”

So whenever crestfallen Hal wanted a dinner whose kindness was not seasoned with contempt, there was a corner for him at Mr. Lambert’s table. George was welcome too. He came at first less cordially, for he had a colder manner and a wary look, as if he listened for a false note in every voice; yet the steady goodness of that household—its clean hours, its unpretending cheer—touched him by degrees. He could not but see how sore Harry was; and pity in George, when it came, came oddly, under a crust of raillery.

“Dear Harry,” he would say, with that half-smile which made it hard to know whether he mocked himself or fate, “I am George the Unlucky, though you have ceased to be Harry the Fortunate. Florac would have done much better not to pass his sword through that Indian’s body, and to have left my scalp as an ornament for the fellow’s belt. At White’s, the people would have respected you. Our mother would have wept over me as a defunct angel, instead of being angry with me for again supplanting her favourite—you are her favourite, and you deserve to be everybody’s. Only, if I had not come back, your favourite Maria would have insisted on marrying you; and that is how the gods would have revenged themselves upon you for your prosperity.”

“I never know whether you are laughing at me or yourself, George,” Harry said, half vexed, half smiling despite himself.

“Precisely my own case, my dear.”

“But this I know,” Harry returned with sudden earnestness, “there never was a better brother in the world; and never better people than the Lamberts.”

“Never was truer word said!” George cried, and took his brother’s hand with a warmth that made the jesting voice ring honest for once.

“And if I’m unhappy,” Harry went on, “’tis not your fault—nor their fault—nor perhaps mine. ’Tis fate, you see—’tis the having nothing to do. I must work; and how, George? That is the question.”

“We will see what our mother says. We must wait till we hear from her,” George answered.

Then, lowering his voice as if even the idea might be overheard and punished, Harry said, “George—do you know, I don’t think I should much like going back to Virginia.”

“What! in love with one of the lasses here?”

“Love ’em like sisters—with all my heart, of course—dearest, best girls! But having come out of that business, thanks to you, I don’t want to go back. No, no; it is not for that I fancy staying in Europe better than going home. But I don’t fancy hunting, duck-shooting, tobacco-planting, whist-playing, and going to sermon, over and over again, for all my life. And what else is there to do at home? What on earth is there for me to do at all? That’s what makes me miserable. It would not matter for you—you are so clever you would make your way anywhere; but for a poor fellow like me, what chance is there? Until I do something, George, I shall be miserable—that’s what I shall!”

“Have I not always said so? Art thou not coming round to my opinion?”

“What opinion, George? You know pretty much whatever you think, I think,” Harry said, with the docility of affection.

“That Florac had best have left the Indian to take my scalp,” George answered, and Harry broke out with an impatient exclamation; yet the two sat on, puffing their pipes in friendly union, smoke curling up like a thin veil between them and their trouble.

They lived together still, each going his own gait. There was not much talk between them beyond the daily proofs of love. Harry would never venture to meddle with George’s books, and when George studied—silent, intent, the page held close to the candle—Harry would sit as mute as a mouse, as if the very rustle of paper were sacred.

They moved away from the Court end at last, Madame de Bernstein pishing and psha-ing at the change, as if a different street could be an insult to her. But George had taken a great fancy to Sir Hans Sloane’s new reading-room and museum at Montagu House; and he chose cheerful lodgings in Southampton Row, Bloomsbury, looking out toward the fields that stretched, green and quiet, in the direction of Hampstead behind the Duke of Bedford’s gardens. Compared to St. James’s, the air there seemed less powdered with vanity and more like honest wind.

Lord Wrotham’s family coming to Mayfair, and Mr. Lambert having business that detained him in town, the Lamberts too changed their quarters and took furnished apartments in Soho, not far from the Warringtons. Thus it was with the Lamberts that Harry, night after night, took refuge; and George came often too, with increasing assiduity, finding their company more to his taste than his aunt’s polished circle of gamblers, or Sir Miles Warrington’s port and mutton, or the perpetual clatter of coffee-houses where voices battered one another like tankards.

The Lambert household, like the Warrington brothers, were strangers in London; and so they took its pleasures together, as families do when they are still fresh to a place—Vauxhall and Ranelagh, Marybone Gardens, the Tower, and the play; those “honest amusements” which are like public feasts spread for any who can pay, though the rich keep the choicer dishes nearest to their own hands. General Lambert loved that his children should have all innocent enjoyment he could procure, and George—open-handed by nature—took a pleasure in treating his friends, especially those who had been so steadfast to Harry.

With all the passion of his heart, George loved a play. In Virginia he had scarcely tasted that delight; in Canada only once or twice had he seen it; and in London, where the two great houses stood in their glory, he could never have enough. And, as men do with any dish they relish, he longed to share it.

“Shall it be Drury Lane or Covent Garden, ladies?” he asked. “There is Garrick and Shakespeare at Drury Lane.”

But the ladies, who had been hearing talk till their heads were full of it, wanted rather the famous new author whose tragedy was then drawing town to Covent Garden.

A star, they said, had risen—blazing with dazzling brilliance. Mr. John Home of Scotland had produced a tragedy than which, since the days of the ancients, nothing more classic and elegant had been written. What had Mr. Garrick meant by refusing such a masterpiece? Shakespeare, for all his greatness (and he had grown vastly more popular since Monsieur Voltaire attacked him), had barbarisms, shocks, familiar jests beside dreadful things. Mr. Home, the modern, knew refinement in grief and passion; he could make death itself graceful and pathetic; he did not condescend to those rude juxtapositions of buffoonery and anguish by which the older poet, it was said, degraded the tragic muse. Besides, this new work had been applauded in quarters so high that all the nation, dutiful as a chorus, was expected to clap where august hands had given the signal. Mr. Gray of Cambridge had praised the dialogue; Edinburgh had shouted (in its own proud dialect), “Where’s Wully Shakspeare noo?”

“I should like to see the man who could beat Willy Shakspeare,” the General said, laughing.

“Mere national prejudice,” George returned.

“Beat Shakespeare, indeed!” cried Mrs. Lambert, scandalised.

“Pooh, pooh! you have cried more over Mr. Sam Richardson than ever you did over Mr. Shakespeare, Molly,” the General said, with the comfortable cruelty of a husband. “I think few women love to read Shakespeare: they say they love it, but they don’t.”

“Oh, papa!” cried three ladies at once, and three pairs of hands flew up like startled birds.

“Well then—why do you all three prefer Douglas? And you, boys, who are such Tories, will you go see a play wrote by a Whig Scotchman who was actually made prisoner at Falkirk?”

“Relicta non bene parmula,” said Jack Lambert the scholar, eager to show his bit of Latin.

“Nay; it was relicta bene parmula,” the General answered promptly. “It was the Highlanders who flung their targes down and made fierce work among us redcoats. If they had fought all their fields as well as that, and young Perkin had not turned back from Derby—”

“I know which side would be rebels, and who would be called the Young Pretender,” George put in, with a dry look.

“Hush! You must please remember my cloth, Mr. Warrington,” the General said, with a sudden gravity; “and that the cockade I wear is black, not white. Still—if you will not love Mr. Home for his politics, there is another reason, George, why you should like him.”

“Why should I like him, sir?”

“Because, being a Presbyterian clergyman, he has committed the heinous crime of writing a play, and his brother-parsons have barked out an excommunication at him. They took away his means of livelihood for his performance; and he would have starved, but that the young Pretender on your side of the water has given him a pension.”

“If he has been persecuted by the parsons, there is hope for him,” George said, smiling. “Henceforth I declare myself ready to hear his sermons.”

“Mrs. Woffington is divine in it,” the General continued, warming to his subject, “though she is not generally famous in tragedy. Barry draws tears from all eyes; and Garrick is wild at having refused the piece. Girls, you must bring each half a dozen handkerchiefs. As for mamma—I cannot trust her; she must be left at home.”

But mamma persisted. If she must weep, she vowed she would sit and cry her eyes out in a corner; and so the whole party went to Covent Garden, duly prepared—as people are when fashion commands them—to see a masterpiece.

They went in a glass coach, the ladies and the General, the wheels grumbling over the stones with that London music which never ceases, day or night. The young men waited at the theatre door, and there they all were swallowed into a crowd of torch-boys, and a regiment of footmen, and the mixed smells of tallow smoke, damp wool, hair-powder, orange-peel, and hot breath. The noise was a kind of weather: the sharp calls, the stamping, the clink of coin, the growl of impatient gentlemen as if each were a mastiff chained to his own importance. It made Harry’s heart beat quicker, though he scarcely knew whether it was excitement or only the relief of being somewhere the world’s coldness might be drowned in general sound.

Little Hetty, in the press, fell to Harry’s arm; she clung to him with a confidence that was half practical—so as not to be lost—and half that other confidence which a young girl gives before she understands what it costs. Theo, blushing, was handed to the box by George, who did it with a grave care that made even a simple courtesy look like a vow. Gumbo had kept the places till his masters arrived, and then retired with many bows to take his own seat in the footman’s gallery, where servants’ applause and derision rise and fall like surf.

They had a front box. There was, luckily, a pillar behind which Mrs. Lambert might weep in comfort without being stared at; and opposite, in a blaze of light and attention, sat the august hope of the empire—his Royal Highness George, Prince of Wales—with the Princess Dowager his mother. The people greeted them with loyal plaudits that had something dutiful, and something restrained, in their sound; and behind his Royal Highness stood a handsome man, my Lord Bute, Groom of the Stole, patron of the poet whose tragedy had already wrung tears from the royal eyes more than once.

The house settled, like a creature drawing breath before speech. The musicians tuned; that small, searching sound of strings being tightened ran through the place like a finger along glass. And as the candles flared and steadied, and the many murmurs sank toward expectation, those young strangers—Virginians and Lamberts together—sat waiting to be moved by a story in a tongue that was theirs, and yet not wholly theirs; for every word on that stage belonged, in some measure, to the great city’s lexicon, and the power of it lay not only in what was said, but in who had the right to say it, and be listened to.
