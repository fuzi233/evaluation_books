The loyal hearts of Britain bristled with a mixture of awe and indignant sorrow upon learning of Mr. George Warrington's treatment of a gallant Prince—the beloved son of the greatest monarch, and Captain-General of the British forces. What an irrevocable favor had the youth spurned? What golden opportunity for advancement had he cast aside?

Will Esmond, whose tongue was perpetually seasoned with blasphemy, unleashed his fiercest curses upon his cousin's behavior, expressing a grim satisfaction that the "confounded young Mohock" was effectively cutting his own throat. Cousin Castlewood took a more charitable, albeit cynical, view. He suggested that a savage gentleman possessed the inherent right to scalp himself if he chose, or perhaps, he reasoned, Mr. Warrington had simply heard too much of the war-whoop during the disaster at Braddock and lacked the stomach for further combat. Will rejoiced doubly: first, that the younger brother had departed for hell, and second, that the elder was surely following close behind.

Upon their first encounter, Will declared he would ensure Mr. George understood exactly what he thought of him. "If you intend to insult George," cried Lady Maria sharply, "you had best ensure his brother Harry is out of earshot!" This remark seemed to provoke even more vitriol from Mr. Will regarding his twin kinsmen.

"My lord," Will retorted, "ta, ta, ta! No more of this squabbling! We cannot expect everyone in the family to be a warrior."

"I never heard your lordship lay claim to being one," Maria countered.

"Never, my dear; quite the opposite!" my lord replied with a simpering glance at his hands. "Will is our champion, and one fighter is sufficient for the house. With the two Mohocks present—George as the scholar and Harry as the soldier—it is a pity you did not choose George as your adversary when you sought a quarrel."

"Your lordship's hand is famous—at piquet," Will's mother interjected.

"It is a pretty one," my lord observed, examining his fingers. "My Lord Hervey's glove and mine were of identical size. Indeed, my hand, as you say, is suited for cards rather than war. Yours, my Lady Castlewood, is remarkably dexterous. How I bless the day you bestowed it upon my lamented father!"

In this game of verbal fencing, as in others requiring skill, his lordship found pleasure in engagement, possessing a cool head capable of defeating his entire family. Meanwhile, Madame de Bernstein, upon hearing of Mr. Warrington's blunder, erupted in fury, storming and scolding her household. She would have chastised George severely, but age had softened her resolve; she feared losing her courage, and furthermore, she harbored a lingering respect for her nephew.

"You will never make your fortune at court, nephew!" she groaned when he visited her shortly after his dismissal.

"It was never my wish, madam," George replied in a stately tone.

"Your wish was to assist Harry?" the Baroness pressed. "You might have been of service to your brother had you accepted the Duke's offer. Princes do not appreciate rejected favors, and I do not wonder His Royal Highness was offended."

"General Lambert said the same," George admitted, flushing slightly. "I see now I was wrong. Yet, please consider that I had never witnessed a court before, and I doubt I possess the talents to shine within one."

"I suspect not, my good nephew," the old lady remarked, taking a pinch of snuff. "And what then?"

"What then?" George echoed. "I harbor no ambition for such glory, and I can find contentment without it. When His Royal Highness spoke to me so kindly, my thought was merely that I shall make a terrible soldier, while my brother would excel. He possesses a hundred virtues for the profession wherein I am deficient; he would serve a commanding officer far better than I ever could. Imagine the Duke in battle, his horse shot beneath him as my poor chief's was at home. Would he not fare better mounted on a beast of courage and strength, capable of bearing him anywhere, rather than one unable to carry its rider's weight?"

"Au fait," the Baroness agreed. "His Royal Highness's charger must indeed be a sturdy steed, my dear!"

"Our Hannibal weighs no trifling amount," George muttered, shrugging.

"I do not quite follow your Hannibal," the Baroness noted.

"When Mr. Wolfe and Mr. Lambert remonstrated with me as you have done, madam," George laughed, "I offered the same defense. I told them I presented the Prince with the finest soldier in the family, and both gentlemen conceded that my error at least had justification. Who knows but they may rectify matters with His Royal Highness? My experience of battle has revealed how ill-suited my genius is for warfare. Last night, we viewed the Scottish play everyone discusses. When the hero, young Norval, expressed his longing to follow a warlike lord to the field, I thought to myself, 'How like my Harry is to him, save that he does not boast.' Harry pines now for a red coat; if left unchecked, he will take the shilling. He keeps the map of Germany perpetually before his eyes, following the King of Prussia wherever he goes. He fears neither men nor gods. As for me, I cherish my books and quietude above all, preferring to read of battles in Homer or Lucan."

"Then what made a soldier of you at all, my dear? And why did you not send Harry with Mr. Braddock instead of going yourself?" the Baroness asked.

"My mother loved her younger son best," George answered darkly. "Furthermore, with the enemy invading our soil, it was my duty, as the head of our family, to join the campaign. Had I been a Scot twelve years prior, I should have been..."

"Hush, sir! Or I shall be more angry than ever!" the old lady commanded, her face betraying perfect amusement.

George's explanation succeeded in pacifying Madame de Bernstein, an elderly woman whose principles were, we fear, somewhat loose. However, to the loyal hearts of Sir Miles Warrington and his wife, the young man's conduct delivered a devastating blow.

"I should have thought," her ladyship exclaimed, "that from my sister Esmond Warrington's letter, I believed her brother's widow to be a woman of sound sense and judgment, who had educated her sons fittingly. But what, Sir Miles, what, my dear Thomas Claypool, can we think of an education resulting so lamentably for both these youths?"

"The elder seems to command considerable knowledge of Latin, speaking French and German fluently as well," Mr. Claypool noted. "I heard him converse with the Hanoverian Envoy at the Baroness's rout. He jabbered French easily; when he struggled with High Dutch, he and the Envoy switched to Latin, chatting away until the entire room stared."

"It is not language, Thomas Claypool, but principles!" the virtuous matron cried. "What must Mr. Warrington's principles be when he rejects an offer from his Prince? Can he speak High Dutch? Precisely because he can, he *ought* to have accepted His Royal Highness's condescension and rendered himself useful in the campaign! Look at our son, look at Miles!"

"Hold up thy head, Miley, my boy!" called Papa.

"I trust, Sir Miles, that as a Member of Parliament and an English gentleman, you will attend His Royal Highness's levee tomorrow and declare that, had such an offer been made for our child, we would have accepted it, though our boy is but ten years of age."

"Faith, Miley, thou wouldst make a fine little drummer or fifer!" Papa quipped. "Shall we have thee as a little soldier, Miley?"

"Anything, sir, anything! A Warrington ought to be ready at any moment to be cut to pieces for his sovereign!" the matron shouted, pointing to the boy. Upon understanding his mother's proposal, the child protested with a loud roar, amidst which he was spirited away by Screwby.

Obedient to these conjugal commands, Sir Miles attended the Prince's levee the following day, offering a protest of love and duty which the Prince graciously accepted, remarking, "Nobody ever supposed that Sir Miles Warrington would ever refuse any place offered to him." A compliment indeed, repeated widely by Lady Warrington as proof of the implicit reliance the royal family placed on the Warringtons' loyalty.

Consequently, when Sir Miles and his wife encountered George, they greeted him with a ghastly commiseration—a look reserved for relatives or friends who have committed a fatal clumsiness, lost a lawsuit, entered a room just as the company begins to abuse them, watched their banker fail, or seen their names in the commercial columns of the *London Gazette*. It is the universal expression of pity for someone guilty of a notorious fault, blunder, or misfortune. Who does not recognize this face? Whose dear relations have not so deplored a living person, not dead, but alive? If you have never been in scrapes, never sown wild oats, never been unfortunate or spoken imprudently, never sinned and repented, then, sir, you are a wiseacre wasting your time on novels, and this tale is not for you.
