The memory of that fateful morning at Hotherstone's Farm remains shrouded in a haze. I recall the warmth of a hearty welcome, the abundance of a sumptuous supper that would have sufficed for an entire village in the East. Our host's kitchen was spotless, with the exception of the detestable feather-bed, a relic of our ancestors' misguided tastes. A restless night ensued, punctuated by the repeated strike of matches and the soft glow of a single candle. As the first light of dawn crept in, I felt a profound sense of relief wash over me.

Before rising, I had agreed with Betteredge that we would depart for Cobb's Hole as soon as I pleased – or rather, as soon as I could muster the patience to wait no longer for the letter. I opted to forgo breakfast and set out, half-expecting to find Betteredge still abed. To my surprise, he was already up and waiting for me, his walking stick in hand.

"How are you this morning, Betteredge?" I asked, trying to stifle my excitement.

"Quite poorly, sir," he replied, his voice laced with a hint of mischief. "I've come down with a new affliction, one that I'm sure you'll contract before the day is out."

"A disease of your own invention, I presume?" I teased.

Betteredge chuckled. "Guilty as charged, Mr. Franklin. But trust me, it's a malady you won't soon forget. I've dubbed it the detective-fever – and I contracted it in the company of the illustrious Sergeant Cuff."

At the mention of the letter, I felt my anticipation surge. "Then the remedy, as always, lies in unsealing Rosanna Spearman's missive?"

Betteredge nodded, and we set off towards Cobb's Hole. Even at this early hour, we chanced upon the fisherman's wife, Mrs. Yolland, stirring in her kitchen. Upon my introduction, she performed a gracious ceremony, reserving a special welcome for esteemed visitors. She placed a bottle of fine Dutch gin and two clean pipes on the table, and initiated our conversation by inquiring, "What news from London, sir?"
