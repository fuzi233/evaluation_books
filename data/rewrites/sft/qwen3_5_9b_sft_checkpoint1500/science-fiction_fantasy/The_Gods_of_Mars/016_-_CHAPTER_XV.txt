Consciousness returned to me abruptly, like a jolt from a sudden shockwave. For a fleeting moment, darkness had held sway, but now the corridor around me glowed with an expanding brilliance. The oppressive, shadowy eyes that haunted the halls were gone, replaced by the harsh, steady beam of a torch.

I rose to my feet, feeling only a dull ache where my brow had struck the stony flagging. Before me, four figures moved swiftly down the passage, clad in the emerald hues of the Tharks. One carried the light-source. They had not yet spotted me, and I seized the split second of ignorance to slip sideways into the first intersecting tunnel I could find. This time, unlike the previous misstep that cost me Tars Tarkas and his guard, I kept my distance from the main thoroughfare, crouching deep against the rough masonry of a blind alley.

Moments later, the party thundered past my hiding spot. My breath hitched in relief. They were the same four who had led me into the pits earlier—their leader, Tars Tarkas, flanked by his three loyal warriors. As they vanished into the distance, heading toward the cell where the great Thark lay chained, I waited until the heavy footfalls faded. The torch bearer had secured his flame in a wall socket, bathing the area in a sterile, green-lit glow.

A plan hardened in my mind, cold and absolute. To return safely to the hills, I needed Tars Tarkas free. But the guard remained. With a grip of iron on my longsword, I stepped forward, positioning myself inches from the cell door. I raised the blade high, ready to cleave the skull of the jailer the moment he emerged. The silence stretched taut, broken only by the rhythmic approach of boots on stone.

Then, the clash. Within minutes, the trap snapped shut. Tars Tarkas, now clad in the armor of a Warhoon chief, strode briskly down the corridor, the stolen torch casting long, dancing shadows. Twelve paces behind him walked John Carter, Prince of Helium.

Two warriors, who had lingered to guard the cell, were just beginning to ascend the spiral runway leading upward when the Thark appeared.

"Why so long, Tan Gama?" one called out, his voice echoing in the empty space.

"I encountered a stubborn lock," Tars Tarkas replied, his tone casual. "And now I realize I left my short-sword inside the Thark's cell. You proceed; I shall return to retrieve it."

"As you wish, Tan Gama," the other agreed. "We await you above."

"Yes," Tars Tarkas nodded, feigning intent to retrace his steps. But as soon as the two warriors disappeared around the bend of the floor above, the mask slipped. I joined him instantly. Together, we snuffed the torch and crept silently toward the spiral incline, ascending toward the upper levels of the fortress.

On the first floor, the hallway terminated abruptly, forcing us to cross a rear chamber teeming with green warriors before we could reach the inner courtyard. There was no other way. We climbed cautiously, the hum of voices drifting from the rooms above, but the corridor itself remained unlit and empty until we reached the summit. We threaded the long gallery and reached the balcony overlooking the courtyard unseen.

To our right, a window opened into the room where Tan Gama and his comrades had begun their vigil earlier that eve. They had returned, and their hushed conversation drifted to us.

"What delays Tan Gama?" one asked.

"He could not possibly spend this entire duration retrieving a shortsword from the Thark's cell," another countered.

"His shortsword?" a woman's voice chimed in, puzzled. "What do you mean?"

"Tan Gama abandoned us at the runway to fetch it," the first speaker explained. "He wore no sword tonight."

"The sword broke today in the battle with the Thark," the woman interjected. "Tan Gama entrusted it to me for repair. Look!" She slid the weapon from beneath her silken furs, displaying the gleaming steel.

The warriors leaped to their feet.

"There is treachery here!" cried one.

"It is exactly what I suspected when he left us," said another. "His voice sounded strange... unnatural."

"Hasten to the pits!"

Cutting off their frantic shouts, I slung my harness into a single strap and lowered Tars Tarkas gently to the courtyard below. An instant later, I dropped to his side. Since the fall of Tan Gama, we had exchanged fewer than a dozen words. Yet, the look of utter bewilderment on the great Thark's face burned in my memory.

"By this time," he had whispered, "I should have learned to wonder at nothing John Carter accomplishes."

That was all he said. He understood the bond forged in blood, the risk I had taken to save him. This fierce green warrior, who had once charged me with leveled spear and murderous hate beyond Korad, was now the dearest friend I possessed among the inhabitants of two worlds. Jeddak of the Tharks.

Reaching the shadows beneath the balcony, we conferred briefly.

"There are five now in the party, Tars Tarkas," I said, counting the names in my mind—Thuvia, Xodar, Carthoris, and myself. "We require five thoats to carry us."

"Carthoris!" he exclaimed. "Your son?"

"Yes. I found him imprisoned by Shador, on the Sea of Omean, in the land of the First Born."

"I know not these places, John Carter," Tars Tarkas murmured. "Are they upon Barsoom?"

"Both above and below, my friend. Wait until we have escaped, and I shall recount the strangest tale ever told by a Barsoomian of the outer world. For now, we must steal our mounts and flee north before these fools discern our trickery."

Our escape proved miraculous. Reaching the great gates at the far end of the courtyard, we faced a formidable challenge: five thoats, beasts as wild and ferocious as their masters, held in check only by brute force and cruelty. As we approached, they sensed the alien scent on us, squealing in rage and circling with massive, gaping mouths raised high. A ten-foot steed of sleek, slate-grey hide and vivid yellow feet, with a broad, flat tail, they were terrifying war machines. Guided solely by telepathy, they required no reins.

Through a whirlwind of chaos, I mastered one brute, mounting him before he could strike. Tars Tarkas secured another, and together we herded the remaining herd toward the gates. Tars Tarkas threw the barriers open, and I held the stragglers back as we surged through into the avenue beyond. Without pausing to close the gates, we spurred our stolen mounts southward, leaving the city behind.

We passed the outer fringes of the dead city and reached our camp without a sound of pursuit. A low whistle, our prearranged signal, announced my return. Our party greeted us with ecstatic joy. Tars Tarkas and Carthoris exchanged their formal, dignified salutations, yet I read the warmth in their eyes; the Thark loved my boy, and the boy returned his affection. Xodar and the green Jeddak were formally introduced, and Thuvia was lifted onto the gentlest of the beasts.

Under the dual gaze of the moons, we set out at a rapid pace, circling north from the city's edge. We sped silently across the ochre expanse of the dead sea bottom, fleeing the Warhoons and the First Born, unaware of the new perils awaiting us.

By noon on the second day, we halted to rest. We hobbled the beasts to graze on the moss-like vegetation, their sustenance and drink. Thuvia volunteered to keep watch while the others slept.

Before I had fully closed my eyes, her hand touched my shoulder. A whisper, soft as a breeze, pierced the quiet.

"Arise, O Prince," she murmured. "Behind us lies a host that resembles a vast army of pursuers."

She pointed west, toward the horizon from whence we fled. Rising, I saw it too—a thin, dark line stretching across the sky. I woke the others. Tars Tarkas, towering above us all, confirmed the threat.

"A great body of mounted men," he declared. "They travel at high speed."

Time was not ours. We scrambled to our hobbled thoats, freeing them and mounting instantly. We turned north, pushing our mounts to the limit of endurance. For the remainder of the day and the following night, we raced across the ochre wilderness, the pursuers relentlessly closing the gap. By twilight, they were close enough to distinguish their green skin, and throughout the long, starless night, the clank of their accoutrements echoed behind us like the march of death.

When the sun rose on the second morning, it revealed the pursuing horde less than half a mile in our wake. As they spied us, a fiendish shout of triumph tore from their throats.

Ahead lay a range of hills—the far shore of the dead sea. If we could reach those heights, escape might still be possible. But Thuvia's mount, though lightly laden, showed signs of severe exhaustion.
