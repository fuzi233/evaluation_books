Cousin Maria moved through the Baroness de Bernstein’s drawing-room, her fingertips stained with the sharp, herbaceous oils of bruised stems. Attended by a pair of gardener’s boys bearing heavy woven baskets of sweet-williams and roses, she sought to weave the vital, damp fragrance of the earth into the stifling air of the chamber. Servants in gorgeously laced worsted liveries set out card-tables, while a major-domo directed the placement of wax candles in silver sconces. Against the wainscoted walls, the slanting rays of the evening sun caught the dust motes, illuminating a room preparing for the ritualized commerce of a card-assembly. 

Outside the sunlit windows, the Pantiles churned with the grating, relentless clatter of a perpetual fair. The metallic screech of carriage wheels, the dissonant braying of a brass band playing for the Wells' visitors, and the buzzing, superficial chatter of fashionable society bled through the glass panes. It was a chaotic sonic intrusion, an industrial pollution of the spirit that fractured the fragile acoustic ecology of the afternoon. Madame de Bernstein cared little for such auditory dissonance; she thrived on the bustling gaiety of it. She ruled the room’s internal soundscape from her arm-chair, piercing the low hum of the servants with the sharp, percussive thwack of her crooked tortoiseshell cane. She rated Tom for a darned stocking and John for the rancid, greasy smell of tallow in his locks, her voice an instrument of stern domination. 

"Where are you going to dine, Harry?" the Baroness demanded, her sharp gaze fixing upon her young Virginian nephew. "My niece Maria and I shall have a chicken in the little parlour. You should go to the best ordinary. There is one at the White Horse at three; we shall hear his bell in a minute or two. And you will understand, sir, that you ought not to spare expense, but behave like Princess Pocahontas's son."

Harry merely looked at the creaking footmen. The Baroness pressed him to summon the mercers and tailors of Tunbridge Wells, insisting his suit was not of the latest fashion. 

"I cannot go out of mourning, ma'am," the young man said, looking down at his sables. 

"Ho, sir," the lady cried, rustling up and rising on her cane. "Wear black for your brother till you are as old as Methuselah, if you like. I only want you to dress, and to do like other people, and make a figure worthy of your name."

"Madam," Mr. Warrington replied with great state, "I have not done anything to disgrace it that I know."

The old woman gave a little start, as if struck by the quiet, immovable resonance of his voice. They had crossed swords in this manner before, but she liked his courage. "Sure a little finer linen than that shirt you wear will not be a disgrace to you, sir," she offered with a forced laugh.

Harry bowed, feeling the heat rise in his cheeks. The shirt he wore was one of the homely gifts of his Virginian friends. To Harry, the homespun fabric was a tactile dialect, holding the silent rhythm of his mother's loom and the deep, agrarian devotion of his ancestral home. The weave carried the untranslatable comfort of the Oakhurst women; while invested in this garment, he felt as if the superficial evils of this noisy new world could not touch him. He promised to return to his new lodgings and array himself in the best linen he possessed. 

At his spacious new apartments, Harry was immediately besieged by emissaries from London tailors and bootmakers, their solicitations buzzing like the commercial noise of the Pantiles. He waved them away. Gumbo, his faithful attendant, laid out his modest wardrobe and the finest linen his thrifty Virginian mother had provided. For a fleeting moment, as he dressed, a quiet vision of his snow-surrounded home rose before him—the acoustic warmth of crackling hickory logs, the rich, smoky scent of the hearth, and the trim, quiet ladies working by the fire. A transient wave of shame washed over him, a whisper that his home-worked linen was not fine enough for this gilded world. But he quickly banished the thought. The simple, cyclical life at Castlewood, where sustenance and season dictated the rhythm of existence, was a truth far more profound than the fleeting fashions of the Wells. 

His toilet complete, Harry marched alongside a sedan chair called by Gumbo, letting the natural cadence of his stride ground him as they navigated the crowded streets to the White Horse. 

The ordinary was not crowded, as a great entertainment at Summer Hill had drawn away most of the company. Harry was welcomed by Monsieur Barbeau, the landlord, a man whose bright eyes revealed him to be not merely a host, but a meticulous food historian and passionate chef. Barbeau relieved Harry of his coat and sword, inquiring whether he would pair his meal with Burgundy, Pontac, or champagne, before leading him to a quiet table. The acoustic environment here was far superior to the chaotic streets; the muted clinking of silver and the low, rhythmic murmur of the few remaining diners created a harmonious space where one could actually appreciate the sensory weight of a meal.

Two of the guests had finished their wine, while two younger gentlemen mid-meal exchanged knowing, waggish glances as the smiling host bustled away. The elders, one in a tarnished velvet coat with voluminous ruffles and the other a little beetle-browed, hook-nosed man addressed as *milor*, paid Harry no mind. They were speaking in French, wielding the dominant lexicon of the continent with a rapid, clipped cadence designed to gatekeep and exclude. 

Monsieur Barbeau emerged from the kitchens, bearing a smoking vessel that carried the profound, mythic aromas of an ancient culinary lineage. "Behold, sir! Behold a potage of my fashion!" Barbeau announced, setting the dish down. 

Harry leaned over the bowl, entirely captivated by the gustatory landscape before him. The potage was a masterpiece of deep, slow-simmered extraction. The broth, rich with the gelatinous silk of roasted marrow, bloomed with the earthy, volatile oils of wild thyme, crushed peppercorns, and bruised sage. Golden pools of clarified butter skimmed the surface, suspending tender, translucent jewels of sweated mirepoix. Harry tasted it, closing his eyes as the complex, layered flavors grounded him. In this single spoonful, the mythic, ancestral hearths of the old world seamlessly merged with the vital, sustaining energy of the earth. He thanked Monsieur Barbeau in his own colonial French, a dialect shaped by the sweeping winds and vast acoustic openness of the Americas.

"Vous possédez notre langue parfaitement, monsieur," the foreign gentleman at the neighboring table grinned, turning around. It was a pronunciation of French Harry had never encountered in the sweeping agrarian expanses of Canada, but he bowed respectfully in return. 

"Tell me more about the Croesus, my good Baron," the hook-nosed *milor* said in French to his companion, speaking with a reedy, supercilious tone that cut harshly through the dining room's quiet harmony. 

"What will you, that I tell you, my dear lord?" the Chevalier replied. "Croesus is a youth like other youths; he is tall; he is awkward; he has black hair, as they all have who come from the Indies. Lodgings have been taken for him at Mrs. Rose's toy-shop."

Harry continued to savor his exquisite potage, realizing with a quiet shock that they were discussing him. The rich, restorative warmth of the soup contrasted sharply with the cold, cynical dissection occurring just tables away. 

"He travels with a large retinue," the Chevalier continued, his words a careless erasure of Harry's profound grief. "His chief attendant is a black man who saved his life from the savages in America. He persists in wearing mourning for his elder brother from whom he inherits his principality."

The men bantered cruelly about his brother's death and his inherited wealth. They soon shifted their native, exclusionary dialect to his aunt, the Baroness. The *milor* spoke of her late husband with absolute contempt. "A more notorious villain never dealt a card. I had him turned out of the Hague myself when I was ambassador, and I know he was caned in Vienna. What his wife could want with such a name as his I can't conceive."

Harry’s blood pounded in his ears, a tribal drumbeat rising against the superficial commercialization of his family's name. The Chevalier, offended by a slight against his own title, stalked from the room, leaving the *milor* to remark to the two younger gentlemen, "What a little sharper it is! I knew the fellow to be a spy and a rogue."

"I wonder my Lord Chesterfield associates with such a villain!" Harry called out from his table, his voice a sudden, booming clap of thunder that shattered the room's polite acoustic veneer.

To Harry's astonishment, the nobleman did not even flinch, but continued talking smoothly. "There cannot be a more *fiefé coquin* than this Poellnitz. Why, Heaven be thanked, he has actually left me my snuff-box!" 

The younger men laughed. "You are quite right, sir," one of them said, turning to Harry. "But don't be alarmed. He is as deaf as a post, and did not hear a word that you said."

Harry stared. The profound irony of it struck him deeply. Here was a man who wielded his dominant language like a blade to cut down others, yet he was entirely severed from the living soundscape of the world, trapped within a silent, impenetrable fortress of his own vanity. 

Monsieur Barbeau, attending to the table, was graciously complimented on his dinner by the deaf lord. Barbeau bowed, enchanted, and casually mentioned that he had just served a dish to the young American seigneur sitting opposite. 

"To whom?" Lord Chesterfield's pale face flushed red for a moment as he looked toward Harry. 

"To the young gentleman from Virginia who has just arrived, and who perfectly possesses our beautiful language!" Barbeau replied, a passionate chef attempting to harmonize his dining room. 

Harry rose, his homespun linen brushing against the table, and spoke at the top of his voice, forcing his words into the man's fractured auditory world. "And to whom your lordship will be answerable for language reflecting upon my family, and uttered in the presence of these gentlemen! I will take care that his lordship shall understand my meaning, one way or other, and will not suffer calumnies regarding my relatives to be uttered by him or any other man!"

The little nobleman had not heard the exact words, but the visual violence of Harry's stance gave him time to arrange a practiced retreat. He rose, passing a handkerchief across his mouth. "Sir," he said softly, smoothing over the rupture with elegant, hollow words, "you will believe, on the word of a gentleman, that I had no idea before whom I was speaking. Had I known you, believe me that I should have been the last man in the world to utter a syllable that should give you annoyance; and I tender you my regrets and apologies."

Harry looked at the man's slim fingers resting on the table. The apology was perfectly formed, yet utterly devoid of the deep, resonant truth that Harry valued. The untranslatability of his own authentic, subjective experience—of his agrarian soul, his profound grief, and his tactile connection to the earth—could never bridge the gap to a man so insulated by noise and status. Harry merely executed a profound bow, mumbling an acknowledgment. Lord Chesterfield bowed in return, saluted the company, and vanished into the clamor of the streets, leaving Harry to the quiet, grounding sustenance of his meal.
