The twilight deepened, painting the sky in hues of bruised purple and gold, a canvas perfectly suited for the kindling of stars. At the rectory garden gate, Miss Keeldar adjusted her shawl, her voice light despite the gathering shadows. "There will be just light enough to show me the way home," she said, preparing to depart.

"You must not go alone, Shirley; Fanny shall accompany you."

"That she shall not," Shirley replied with a decisive shake of her head. "Of what need should I be afraid in my own parish? I would walk from Fieldhead to the church on any fine midsummer night, three hours later than this, for the mere pleasure of seeing the stars and the chance of meeting a fairy."

"But wait until the crowd disperses."

"Agreed," Shirley smiled, spotting the procession of carriages and phaetons arriving—the Armitage sisters, Mrs. Sykes, Mr. Wynne. "I have no wish to perform the ceremony of bidding them all good-bye. Let us slip into the garden and take shelter amongst the laburnums for an instant."

As the rectors, curates, and churchwardens emerged from the porch, engaging in a flurry of handshakes and congratulations on the evening's success, the throng gradually dissolved. Carriages rattled away, leaving the garden quiet once more. Just as Shirley stepped out from her floral refuge, Mr. Helstone entered, his face illuminated by a sudden urgency.

"Oh, I want you!" he exclaimed, catching her before she could retreat. "I was afraid you were already gone. Caroline, come here."

Caroline approached, expecting a lecture on her invisibility during the service, but the rector's mind was occupied with other matters. "I shall not sleep at home tonight," he continued. "I have met an old friend and promised to accompany him. I expect to return about noon tomorrow. Thomas, the clerk, is engaged, and I cannot arrange for him to sleep in the house as I usually do when I am away."

"Now," Shirley interrupted, her eyes flashing with a sudden, sharp intelligence, "you want me as a gentleman—the first gentleman in Briarfield, in short—to supply your place, be master of the rectory, and guardian of your niece and maids while you are away?"

"Exactly, Captain. I thought the post would suit you. Will you favor Caroline so far as to be her guest for one night? Will you stay here instead of returning to Fieldhead?"

"And what will Mrs. Pryor do? She expects me home."

"I will send her word. Come, make up your mind to stay. It grows late; the dew falls heavily. You and Caroline will enjoy each other's company, I doubt not."

"I promise you, then, to stay with Caroline," Shirley replied firmly. "As you say, we shall enjoy each other's society. We will not be separated tonight. Now, rejoin your old friend, and fear nothing for us."

"If there should chance to be any disturbance in the night, Captain," Mr. Helstone warned, his tone shifting to something more serious, "if you hear the picking of a lock, the cutting of a pane of glass, or a stealthy tread of steps about the house—and I need not fear to tell *you*, who bear a well-tempered, mettlesome heart under your girl's ribbon sash, that such incidents are very possible in the present time—what would you do?"

"Don't know; faint, perhaps—fall down and have to be picked up again." Shirley laughed lightly. "But, Doctor, if you assign me the post of honor, you must give me arms. What weapons are there in your stronghold?"

"You could not wield a sword?"

"No; I could manage the carving-knife better."

"You will find a good one in the dining-room sideboard—a lady's knife, light to handle, and as sharp-pointed as a poniard."

"It will suit Caroline. But you must give me a brace of pistols. I know you have pistols."

"I have two pairs. One pair I can place at your disposal. You will find them suspended over the mantelpiece of my study in cloth cases."

"Loaded?"

"Yes, but not on the cock. Cock them before you go to bed. It is paying you a great compliment, Captain, to lend you these. Were you one of the awkward squad, you should not have them."

"I will take care. You need delay no longer, Mr. Helstone. You may go now." As the rector passed out at the garden gate, Shirley murmured, "He is gracious to me to lend me his pistols." Turning to Caroline, she added, "Come, Lina, let us go in and have some supper. I was too much vexed at tea with the vicinage of Mr. Sam Wynne to be able to eat, and now I am really hungry."

Entering the house, they repaired to the darkened dining-room. Through the open windows, the evening air drifted in, carrying the perfume of flowers from the garden, the distant sound of receding footsteps from the road, and a soft, vague murmur. Caroline stood at the casement, listening intently. "Shirley, I hear the beck in the Hollow," she whispered.

She rang the bell, requesting a candle, bread, and milk—Miss Keeldar's usual supper. When Fanny brought the tray, she hesitated to close the windows and shutters, but Shirley requested she desist. The twilight was too calm, its breath too balmy to be excluded. They took their meal in silence. Caroline rose once to remove a glass of flowers from the sideboard, the scent too powerful for the sultry room. In returning, she half-opened a drawer and took out something that glittered clear and keen in her hand.

"You assigned this to me, then, Shirley, did you?" Caroline asked, holding the long knife. "It is bright, keen-edged, finely tapered; it is dangerous-looking. I never yet felt the impulse which could move me to direct this against a fellow-creature. It is difficult to fancy that circumstances could nerve my arm to strike home with this long knife."

"I should hate to do it," Shirley replied, "but I think I could do it, if goaded by certain exigencies which I can imagine." She quietly sipped her glass of new milk, looking somewhat thoughtful and a little pale—a complexion she rarely lacked.

After the milk and bread were consumed, Fanny was summoned again. She and Eliza were sent to bed, weary from the day's exertions of cutting currant-buns and filling urns. Soon the maids' chamber door closed. Caroline took a candle and quietly inspected the house, ensuring every window was fast and every door barred. She did not even evade the haunted back kitchen nor the vault-like cellars.

"There is neither spirit nor flesh in the house at present," she said, returning to the dining-room. "It is now near eleven o'clock, fully bedtime; yet I would rather sit up a little longer, if you do not object, Shirley." She placed the brace of pistols from her uncle's study on the table.

"Why would you rather sit up longer?" Shirley asked, taking up the firearms and examining them.

"Because I have a strange, excited feeling in my heart."

"So have I."

"Is this state of sleeplessness and restlessness caused by something electrical in the air, I wonder?"

"No; the sky is clear, the stars numberless. It is a fine night."

"But very still. I hear the water fret over its stony bed in Hollow's Copse as distinctly as if it ran below the churchyard wall."

"I am glad it is so still a night. A moaning wind or rushing rain would vex me to fever just now."

"Why, Shirley?"

"Because it would baffle my efforts to listen."

"Do you listen towards the Hollow?"

"Yes; it is the only quarter whence we can hear a sound just now."

They both sat near the window, leaning their arms on the sill, inclining their heads towards the open lattice. By the starlight and the dim June twilight, they saw each other's young faces clearly.

"Mr. Helstone thinks we have no idea which way he is gone," Shirley murmured, "nor on what errand, nor with what expectations, nor how prepared. But I guess much, do not you?"

"I guess something."

"All those gentlemen—your cousin Moore included—think that you and I are now asleep in our beds, unconscious."

"Caring nothing about them—hoping and fearing nothing for them," Caroline added.

Silence stretched between them for half an hour. The night was silent too, measured only by the church clock's quarters. They wrapped their scarves closer, resumed their bonnets, and watched.

Towards midnight, the teasing, monotonous bark of the house-dog disturbed the quietude. Caroline rose noiselessly to the kitchen to appease him with bread. Returning to the dining-room, she found it dark; Shirley had extinguished the candle. The outline of her shape was visible near the still-open window, leaning out. Caroline stole to her side. The dog recommenced barking furiously, then suddenly stopped, seeming to listen. The occupants of the dining-room listened too, not merely to the flow of the mill-stream, but to a nearer, though muffled, sound on the road below the churchyard—a measured, beating, approaching sound, a dull tramp of marching feet.

It drew near. Those who listened began to comprehend its extent. It was not the tread of two, nor a dozen, nor a score; it was the tread of hundreds. They could see nothing; the high shrubs of the garden formed a leafy screen between them and the road. To hear, however, was not enough. As the troop trod forward, seeming to pass the rectory, they felt it more when a human voice broke the hush of the night.

"Halt!"

A halt followed. The march was arrested. Then came a low conference, indistinguishable from the dining-room.

"We *must* hear this," Shirley said.

She turned, took her pistols from the table, silently passed out through the middle window—which was a glass door—and stole down the walk to the garden wall, standing listening under the lilacs. Caroline would not have quitted the house had she been alone, but where Shirley went, she would go. She glanced at the weapon on the sideboard but left it behind, standing at her friend's side. They dared not look over the wall, for fear of being seen; they were obliged to crouch behind it.

"It looks a rambling old building. Who lives in it besides the damned parson?"

"Only three women—his niece and two servants."

"Do you know where they sleep?"

"The lasses behind; the niece in a front room."

"And Helstone?"

"Yonder is his chamber. He was burning a light, but I see none now."

"Where would you get in?"

"If I were ordered to do his job—and he deserves it—I'd try yond' long window; it opens to the dining-room. I could grope my way upstairs, and I know his chamber."

"How would you manage about the women folk?"

"Let 'em alone except they shrieked, and then I'd soon quieten 'em. I could wish to find the old chap asleep. If he waked, he'd be dangerous."

"Has he arms?"

"Firearms, allus—and allus loadened."

"Then you're a fool to stop us here. A shot would give the alarm. Moore would be on us before we could turn round. We should miss our main object."

"You might go on, I tell you. I'd engage Helstone alone."

A pause. One of the party dropped some weapon, which rang on the stone causeway. At this sound, the rectory dog barked again fiercely.

"That spoils all!" said the voice. "He'll awake. A noise like that might rouse the dead. You did not say there was a dog. Damn you! Forward!"

Forward they went—tramp, tramp—with mustering, manifold, slow-filing tread. They were gone.

Shirley stood erect, looked over the wall, along the road. "Not a soul remains," she said.

She stood and mused. "Thank God!" was the next observation. Caroline repeated the ejaculation, not in so steady a tone. She was trembling much. Her heart beat fast and thick; her face was cold, her forehead damp.

"Thank God for us!" she reiterated. "But what will happen elsewhere? They have passed us by that they may make sure of others."

"They have done well," returned Shirley, with composure. "The others will defend themselves. They can do it. They are prepared for them. With us it is otherwise. My finger was on the trigger of this pistol. I was quite ready to give that man, if he had entered, such a greeting as he little calculated on; but behind him followed three hundred. I had neither three hundred hands nor three hundred weapons. I could not have effectually protected either you, myself, or the two poor women asleep under that roof. Therefore I again earnestly thank God for insult and peril escaped."

After a second pause, she continued: "What is it my duty and wisdom to do next? Not to stay here inactive, I am glad to say, but, of course, to walk over to the Hollow."

"To the Hollow, Shirley?"

"To the Hollow. Will you go with me?"

"Where those men are gone?"

"They have taken the highway; we should not encounter them. The road over the fields is as safe, silent, and solitary as a path through the air would be. Will you go?"

"Yes," was the answer, given mechanically, not because the speaker wished or was prepared to go, or indeed was otherwise than scared at the prospect, but because she felt she could not abandon Shirley.

"Then we must fasten up these windows, and leave all as secure as we can behind us. Do you know what we are going for, Cary?"

"Yes—no—because you wish it."
