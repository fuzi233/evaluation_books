The East India Dock Road, as any self-respecting thoroughfare now, makes its living by noise. It has bells that insist upon being heard, wheels that rattle as though paid by the vibration, and a perpetual rain of feet—an industrious percussion that, if you listen long enough, will begin to dictate your thoughts for you. But in the days I speak of it was a different creature: long, bare, and oddly tongue-tied, with the docks’ wall on one side—endless brick holding its own echo—and on the other a scattering of small houses stranded among market gardens, drying grounds, and heaps of rubbish that seemed to have been tipped there as if the world had shrugged.

Only one thing, last time I went by, still held hands with that earlier road: the low, one-storeyed brick cottage at the beginning of the bridge—the old toll-house. I know it because it was there my childhood fell off me, like a cap you did not notice you had lost until the wind hit your ears, and I stood suddenly bare-headed before the world beyond.

I had been to Plaistow that afternoon, to our family dentist. Plaistow was out of the way for a dentist, which was, perhaps, its chief advantage. My mother, fastening up my knickerbockers with the air of a general sending a private into a doubtful battle, would say:

“Have the half-crown in your hand,” and then—because mothers never trust to hands—make sure the purse containing it lay deep in my pocket. “But of course if he won’t take it, why, you must bring it home again.”

I cannot say if he was kin; he felt like it, which is the better relationship. He was an old bachelor in a house set in a great garden—for Plaistow, then, had hedges and space and a look of itself—and he had the gentle, ominous cheerfulness of men who mean well while wielding metal.

I would be placed in the velvet chair of state, as if I were a small king about to be quietly assassinated, and he would unroll his case of instruments with ceremony.

“Choose,” he would say, fondly recommending the one that looked as if it had once been used to open oysters in a hurry.

I would open my mouth to argue—lo! a pair of forceps would flash from under his sleeve, swift as a magician’s trick, and before my fear had time to arrange itself into sentences, the trouble would be done.

Afterwards we would have tea together, which was his true practice. And what teas! His housekeeper, with the ripe certainty of women who can coax order out of fruit, made jams and jellies from the garden that seemed to hold summer in prison: strawberry that smelled like warm sunlight caught in glass, currant sharp as a pleasant scolding, gooseberry that made your mouth wake up. The kettle sang; the cups clinked; a spoon against a saucer gave a small, domestic bell-note that was comfort itself. Even now I think the sound of teaspoons is one of the kinder noises a civilisation makes.

We talked of my mother, and it appeared—by his account—that she had once been a little girl. Not the sort I would have expected; not solemn and correct and born with a duster in her hand, but prankish and wilful, and, if I may say so, lively company. When I repeated these tales at home, my mother would laugh, declaring she had no recollection of any such nonsense, adding severely that it was a pity he and I could not find something better to gossip about.

Yet her next question, always, would be:

“And what else did he say, if you please?” Then, if I answered about microscopes or jam, she would correct me impatiently: “No, no, I mean about me.”

When tea things were cleared away, he brought out his microscope, that round, solemn eye into which you peered and were rewarded with dragons in a drop of water. It was a peep-hole into a fairy country where monstrous beings carried on their private affairs without shame. Looking back, I cannot help thinking his love of the microscopical suited him; he was an exceptionally little man himself, yet one of the biggest-hearted I have known—compact, like a good preserve: nothing wasted, everything sweet.

On leaving, I would formally hand him my half-crown “with mamma’s compliments,” and he would formally accept it. But outside the gate, putting my hand into my pocket, I would invariably find it there again, as if it had come home on its own.

The first time I ran back, honest as only children can be when they have not yet learned the uses of dishonesty, and offered it to him again. He repudiated all knowledge.

“Must be another half-crown,” he suggested, gravely. “Such things do happen. One puts change into a pocket and overlooks it. Slippery things, half-crowns.”

Returning home on that particular day of days, I stopped on the bridge and watched the barges, lazy and broad-backed, manoeuvring between the piers as though water were thicker than it is and required persuasion. The evening was one of those hushed summer ones when even a grim city seems to soften its voice; you could hear the river’s slow talk below, and far off the muffled clop of a horse, and nearer, the soft shush of wind in weeds along the bank. It was as if the world, for once, had agreed not to shout.

And as I turned away from the river and passed through the white toll-gate, I had a sense—clear, absurd, undeniable—of leaving myself behind me on the bridge. So vivid was it that I looked back, half expecting to see myself still there, leaning over the iron parapet, a small figure looking down into sunlit water, untroubled by anything beyond the immediate glitter.

It sounds foolish, but I cannot improve upon the truth. The little chap never came back. He went away from me as a man’s body, perhaps, may one day go, leaving him only remembrance and regret. For a while I tried to play his games and dream his dreams, but the substance was gone; I was only a thin ghost pretending.

This sense—that my childhood lay behind me, that I was travelling swiftly along the strange road called growing up—troubled me for a long time, even to tears. I did not want to grow up. Could nothing stop it? I would rather have remained always as I had been, playing and imagining. The dark way frightened me. Must I go forward?

Then, slowly—so slowly that only years can do it—there came new pulses, new sensories, new throbbings rooted in the old yet different, like a new dish made from the same stock but seasoned to a sharper taste. And little Paul, the Paul of whom I have hitherto spoken, faded.

So must I let him fade from this book. But before I part with him altogether, I will call up what else I can, so that we may be quit of him and he will not come tugging at our sleeves again.

Chief among the pictures is my aunt Fan crouching over the kitchen fire. The kitchen was the true kingdom of our house: not the drawing-room with its stiff propriety, but the warm, busy region of smells and sounds—the crackle of coal, the hiss of fat, the thump of a knife on board, the comforting murmur of a kettle finding its voice. My aunt’s skirt and crinoline would be rolled up round her waist, leaving, as sacrifice to custom, only a petticoat; and up and down she swayed in rhythmic motion, her hands rubbing her knees with affectionate perseverance, as though she were polishing them for company.

Opposite her stood I, armed with paper-knife sword or mounted upon a broomstick horse, flourishing, declaiming, summoning dragons and battles out of thin air.

Sometimes I was a knight and she a wicked ogre. She would be slain, growling and swearing—my aunt did nothing feebly—and at once become a beautiful princess whom I was to secure and bear away on my prancing broomstick. So long as the princess merely held sweet converse from a high-barred window, the scene was realistic enough; but the bearing away had to remain make-believe. My aunt could not be persuaded to leave her chair, nor abandon the eternal rubbing of her knees. Even princesses, it seems, have rheumatism.

At other times, aided by the meat-chopper, I was an Indian brave; then she was Laughing Water or Singing Sunshine, and we went scalping together. In less bloodthirsty moods I was the Fairy Prince and she the Sleeping Beauty, though she was never at her best in roles that required stillness. Better when, seated in the centre of the up-turned table, I was Captain Cook and she the Cannibal Chief.

“I shall skin him and hang him in the larder till Sunday week,” my aunt would say, smacking her lips with a connoisseur’s relish, “then he’ll be just in right condition; not too tough and not too high.” She was always strong in detail.

To do her justice, her compliance was not altogether self-sacrifice. Often she suggested the game and even the theme; and in that case she invariably cast herself for what, in old theatrical parlance, would have been the heavy lead: dragons, wicked uncles, fussy necromancers, uninvited fairies. As authoress of a cookery book for use in giant-land, my aunt would have been a success. Ordinary recipes are so mean and lazy—“Boil him,” “Roast her,” “Bake ’em in a pie”—but my aunt introduced variety, garnish, and a domestic practicality that made ogre-economy almost respectable.

“I think, my dear,” she would direct, “we’ll have him stuffed with chestnuts and served on toast. And don’t forget the giblets. They make such excellent sauce.”

With regard to the diet of imprisoned maidens she advised, with the calm authority of one who had tried them all:

“Not too much fish—it spoils the flesh for roasting.”

The things she would turn people into—kings’ sons, rightful princesses—people who after a time must have forgotten what they began as! To let her have her way was a lesson in natural history, present and pre-historic. Without hesitation she would transform the loveliest damsel into a Glyptodon or a Hippocrepian. Later, when I had learned to guess at spellings, I looked these creatures up in the illustrated dictionary and felt that under no circumstances could I have loved the lady ever again. Warriors and kings she delighted in turning into plaice or prawns; haughty queens into Brussels sprouts. It was not merely wickedness; it was culinary imagination run wild.

With gusto she planned complicated slaughter, noting every necessary detail: knives to be sharpened, mops and pails of water ready for after cleaning. If she had been a writer, she would have belonged to the realistic school—blood on the floor, soot in the grate, and a sensible aside about how difficult it is to get stains out of stone.

Her death—always the close of the afternoon—was another conscientious effort. Her groans and writhings sometimes frightened me. I always welcomed the last gurgle. Not until that final sound would she let down her skirt—thus suggesting the fall of the curtain—and set briskly to work to get tea, as though ogres and princesses were but incidental to bread and butter.

Another picture: myself in a glazed-peaked cap, explaining many things as we walked through dingy streets to a yet smaller figure, curly-haired, open-eyed. Now and then she would run ahead and turn to look up admiringly into my face, as on the first day she became captive to the praise and fame of me.

I was glad of her company for more reasons than she knew. For one thing, she protected me against my baser self. Alone, I might have fled from sudden foes. With her beside me I should not have dared. Together we courted adventure; and once you are used to it, the standing hazard of attack adds a charm to exercise that older folk in well-policed districts do not understand. It is much as my dog feels when we go out. To me it is merely a walk, suggested more by contemplation of my waistband than by love of walking; to him it is an epic crowded with possibilities. The gentleman asleep with one eye open on The Chequers doorstep—friend or enemy? The cross-eyed loafer lurching against the lamp-post—greeting or battle? The next corner—ambush or empty triumph? The city is composed, for a dog, of sound: the scrape of another paw, the clink of a collar, the mutter behind a gate. One could almost pity him the modern traffic, that huge, mindless roar which drowns the smaller meanings.

But the trusting face beside me nerved me. In lonely places I would let her hold my hand, as one allows a small bird to perch—carefully, with an odd tenderness.

A second advantage of her company was that I was less trampled on, less walked over, less swept aside into doorway or gutter than when alone. She had a pretty, winsome face—if Memory is not playing me a trick—but she also had a vocabulary; and when some blind idiot, male or female, instead of walking round us attempted the customary feat of walking through us, she would use it.

“Now, then, where yer coming to, old glass-eye? We ain’t sperrits. Can’t yer see us?”

If they tried to reply, her child’s treble—so strangely out of keeping with her dainty appearance—would only rise more shrill.

“Garn! They’d run out of ’eads when they was making you. That’s only a turnip wot you’ve got stuck on top of yer!” I offer but samples.

It was useless to attempt personal chastisement. An irate lady or gentleman would sometimes be foolish enough to try. As well might a hippopotamus attempt to reprove a terrier. The only result was to provide comedy for the entire street.

On these occasions our positions reversed and I became spectator, admiring her prowess. Yet with me she was meek—almost irritatingly submissive. She found out where I lived and would often come and wait for me for hours, her little face pressed tight against the iron railings, until I came out or shook my head at her from my bedroom window. Then she would run off, and the patter of her feet dying into silence always left me a little sad, as if a small kindness had just missed its proper use.

I think I cared for her, in a way, yet she never entered my day-dreams. That means she existed for me only in the outer world, not in the inner one where I kept my true life. And I think she was unwise—though how was she to know?—in introducing me to the shop. Children and dogs—one brackets them together without meaning to—are snobbish little wretches.

If only her father had been a dealer in firewood, I could have soothed myself by imagining mistakes. It is a common occurrence, as I well knew, for children of the best families to be brought up by wood-choppers. Fairies, well-intentioned but born muddlers, were generally responsible; and such things always got put right in time for the wedding.

Or if he had been a pork butcher—which would have been almost romantic in our neighbourhood—I might have thought of him as a swineherd and found precedent for hope.

But a fishmonger—from six in the evening, a fried fishmonger! I searched history in vain. Fried fishmongers were without the pale. They belonged to the kingdom of vinegar and hot fat, and there are romances that cannot survive that smell.

So gradually our meetings grew less frequent. I knew she waited every afternoon in quiet Stainsby Road where, in semi-detached six-roomed villas, dwelt the aristocracy of Poplar; and now and then, arriving late, I saw the sad fact of tears tracing patterns down her dust-besprinkled cheeks. And with the advent of the world-illuminating Barbara—to which event I am drawing near—our meetings ceased altogether.

So began and ended my first romance. One of these days—some quiet summer afternoon, when even the air of Pigott Street vibrates with tenderness beneath Memory’s whispered sighs—I shall walk into the little grocer’s shop and boldly ask to see her. I have gone so far already as to trace her; often I have tried to catch sight of her through the glass door, but hitherto in vain. I know she is now the more or less troubled mother of numerous children. I am told she has grown stout, and it is probable her tongue has gained rather than lost in sharpness. Yet under all the clumsy unreality the world has built about her, I shall see, I know, the lithesome little maid with fond, admiring eyes.

What help they were to me I never knew until I had lost them. How hard it is to win such eyes I have learned since. If we wrote the truth in our confession books, should we not admit that the quality we most admire in others is admiration of ourselves? And is it not a wise selection? If you would have me admirable, my friend, admire me; speak your commendation without stint, that in the sunshine of your praise I may wax. For indifference makes an indifferent man, and contempt a contemptible man. Come—is it not true? Does not all that is worthy in us grow best by honour?

Chief among the remaining figures upon my childhood’s stage were the many servants of our house, the “generals,” as they were called—because, I suppose, each commanded the kitchen until driven out by the enemy. So rapid was their transit that only one or two remain distinct.

It was a neighbourhood where domestic servants were not much required. Those intending to take up the calling seriously went westward. The local ranks were recruited from the discontented and disappointed—those unappreciated at home who hoped for more discernment from strangers—or from the love-lorn, the jilted, the jealous, who took cap and apron as in earlier ages their kind would have taken the veil. Perhaps the comparative seclusion of a basement, with its steady noises—kettle, grate, knife, broom—seemed, in such moods, more fitting than the frivolity of shop or factory. With the advent of a new young man, or the recovery of the old, they would plunge again into the vain world, leaving my poor mother to begin her search anew amid the legions of the cursed.

With these women I made what comradeship I could, for I had no child friends. They were kind, most of them, though poor at make-believe. Still they would squeeze ten minutes from work to romp with me, and that, perhaps, was healthier. Less healthy was this: staggered by the amount of “book-learning” my conversation implied—for the journalistic instinct, I think, was early in me—they would listen open-mouthed and treat me as a precocious oracle. Sometimes they obtained permission to take me home with them to tea, eager that their friends should profit by me. Encouraged by admiring faces, I would hold forth, keenly enjoying the sound of my own piping.

“As good as a book, ain’t he?” was the tribute most often paid.

“As good as a play,” one old greengrocer went so far as to say.

Already I considered myself among the Immortals.

One girl, a dear wholesome creature named Janet, stayed with us for months and might have stayed years but for her addiction to strong language. She was the only and well-beloved child of the captain of the barge Nancy Jane, trading between Purfleet and Ponder’s End, and her speech was at once my terror and my delight.

“Janet!” my mother would cry, hands flying up as if to shield her ears from flying crockery, “how can you use such words?”

“What words, mum?”

“The things you have just called the gas man.”

“Him! Well, did you see what he did, mum? Walked straight into my clean kitchen without even wiping his boots, the—” And before my mother could catch the sentence by the tail and drag it out, Janet had relieved her feelings by calling him it—or rather them—again, with no notion she had done anything but give natural emotion its proper name.

Janet and I were friends, and for that reason I undertook her reformation. I did not mince matters. I told her plainly that if she persisted she would inevitably go to hell.

“Then where’s my father going?” demanded Janet.

“Does he use language?” I asked.

From Janet I gathered that any person who had enjoyed the privilege of hearing her father could never again take interest in the feeble efforts of herself.

“I am afraid, Janet,” I explained, “that if he doesn’t give it up—”

“But it’s the only way he can talk,” interrupted Janet. “He don’t mean anything by it.”

I sighed, for even then I had a weakness for mercy, but I set my face against it. “You see, Janet, people who swear do go there.”

But Janet would not believe.

“God send my dear, kind father to hell just because he can’t talk like the gentlefolks! Don’t you believe it of Him, Master Paul. He’s got more sense.”

I hope I pain no one by repeating Janet’s common sense. I remember it because often, sinking into the sloughs of childish despond, it gave me firm foothold. More than once, forced to listen to the sententious voice of immeasurable Folly explaining eternal mysteries with glib certainty, it comforted me to whisper: “I don’t believe it of Him. He’s got more sense.”

And about that period I needed all comfort I could get. As we go down the road of life the journey, demanding so much attention, becomes more important than its end; but to the child standing at the valley’s gate the terminating hills are plain, and what lies beyond is his constant wonder. I never questioned my parents directly—how strangely we all shrink, young and old, from speaking of what matters most—while they, not guessing my need, contented themselves with vague generalities, as though mist were a proper clothing for terror.

But there were foolish voices about me less reticent; and the serious literature provided for youth in those days answered questions with blunt brutality. If you did wrong, you burnt in a fiery furnace for ever and ever. If your imagination was weak, there was an illustration to help it: you could see yourself writhe and shrink and scream while cheerful devils, well organised, stoked.

I had been burnt once, badly, by live coals dropped on me in transit upon a shovel. I knew what it was to have fire cling to the skin like a cruel animal. I imagined those coals not confined to one place but pressing everywhere, and not snatched off by loving hands; no soft soap, no blue bag, no soothing household remedy—only the pain eating inward, and time doing nothing to it. For an hour, a day, a thousand years—and no nearer the end. Ten thousand, a million, and still it was for ever; and for ever it would always be for ever. I suffered insomnia then; night had too much room in it.

“Then be good,” said the foolish voices. “Never do wrong, and so avoid it.”

But it was so easy to do wrong. There were so many wrong things, and doing them felt so natural.

“Then repent,” said the voices, always ready with a door—if you could find the handle.

But how did one repent? What was repentance? Did I hate my sin, as instructed, or merely hate the idea of going to hell for it? Even my child’s sense knew the latter was not true repentance. Yet how could one know the difference?

Above all haunted me the fear of the Unforgivable Sin. What it was I could never discover. I dreaded to ask too closely lest I should learn I had already committed it. Day and night its shadow clung to me.

“Believe,” said the voices. “So only shall you be saved.”

How believe? How know you believed? Hours I would kneel in the dark, repeating in a whispered scream:

“I believe, I believe. Oh, I do believe!”

Then I would rise with white knuckles, listening to the house’s small sounds—the settling of boards, the distant clink of a spoon, the faint sigh of wind—as if any honest noise might answer me, and wondering, terrified and stubborn, whether I really did.
