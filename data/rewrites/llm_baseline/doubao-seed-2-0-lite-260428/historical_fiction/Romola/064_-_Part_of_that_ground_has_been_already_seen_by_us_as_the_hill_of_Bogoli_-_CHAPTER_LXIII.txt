A month after that wild, candlelit Carnival, on a crisp late March morning, Tito Melema descended the weathered marble steps of Florence’s Old Palace, bound for San Marco on an errand loaded with unspoken consequence. He spurned the direct, gently winding lane that would have gotten him to the convent in minutes, choosing instead to circle around the Piazza di Santa Croce, where crowds would soon spill out of the great stone church after the first Lenten sermon of the day.

Santa Croce had become the city’s most packed Lenten gathering spot of late. Girolamo Savonarola’s voice had been silenced even in his own home church of San Marco: a hostile Signoria had enforced a papal letter that threatened to lay the entire city under interdict if the friar, denounced in the pontiff’s note as a “wretched worm” and “monstrous idol,” did not stop preaching and travel to Rome to beg pardon for his errors. Next to hearing Fra Girolamo himself speak, no Lenten pastime thrilled Florentines more than gathering to hear him denounced and argued down from the pulpit — and that exact thrill was on offer at Santa Croce, where the Franciscan friar leading the Lenten Quaresimal sermons had publicly offered to walk through open fire side-by-side with Savonarola to settle their dispute once and for all.
Hadn’t that schismatic Dominican claimed his prophetic teachings would be vindicated by a miracle at the appointed hour? This, the Franciscan declared, was that hour. If Savonarola walked through the flames unhurt, his doctrine was proven divine. If he burned to ash, his lies would be laid bare for all to see. To leave him no room to evade the test, the Franciscan swore he would willingly burn alongside him, a sacrifice for the truth that no opponent could dismiss as a rigged game.

Savonarola, as was his habit, paid no public mind to the pulpit taunts. But the zealous Franciscan who had issued the challenge was no stranger: Fra Francesco di Puglia had tried to stage an identical duel of fire with Savonarola’s most loyal follower, Fra Domenico, in Prato the year prior, only to be recalled by his superiors before the argument ever moved beyond shouted rhetoric. Honest, fiery Fra Domenico, who was leading separate Lenten sermons for women in the Via del Cocomero, no sooner heard of this new challenge than he grabbed the gauntlet up on his master’s behalf, declaring he would meet Fra Francesco in the flames any day. The public had already grown giddy over what struck most as a delightfully simple, foolproof way to settle a religious argument — until Savonarola, acutely aware of all the hidden dangers lurking even in the discussion of such an unprecedented trial, ordered Fra Domenico to withdraw his acceptance and wash his hands of the affair. The Franciscan professed to be perfectly content: he had never aimed his challenge at a subordinate, he said, but at Fra Girolamo himself.

Popular interest in the Lenten sermons had flagged a little in the lull that followed. But that morning, when Tito stepped into the Piazza di Santa Croce, he found exactly what he expected: great swarms of people pouring out of the church doors, many of them lingering instead of heading home, clustering tight around a spot just outside the entrance to the Franciscan monastery. Tito drifted that same way, moving through the crowd with a lazy, unhurried air, his sharp eyes fixed steadily on the monastery doors as if he waited for someone of interest to step out.

The crowd’s fascination, though, was not a person, but a large official placard nailed to the wall by order of the Signoria, its letters bold and inked clear — but almost entirely written in Latin. Nearly every person gathered had a rough idea of what it said, but all ached for exact details, and every man present took petty, petty pleasure in deciding his neighbor was too ignorant to parse the learned words. The scraps of Latin phrases lay listeners picked up from constantly interpreted pulpit quotations were no help at all with written text; even the vernacular was still unstandardized and messy for most Florentines who could read at all, and the vast majority of the huddle pressing closest to the placard could not so much as sound out a simple Italian sentence, let alone a foreign tongue.

“It’s the Frate’s doctrines he has to prove by getting burned,” blustered Goro, the city’s ubiquitous loudmouth, who happened to be pressed to the very front of the gazers. “The Signoria’s taken the whole affair in hand, and this writing’s to let us know it’s settled. That’s exactly what the Padre was saying in his sermon just now.”

“Nay, Goro,” a sleek wool shopkeeper said pityingly, “you’ve twisted your hose so far your legs are tied in knots. The Frate has to prove his doctrines by *not* burning. He walks through the fire, comes out the other side whole and sound.”

“Aye, aye,” put in a young sculptor, his cap and tunic dusted with fine white marble flecks, “but Fra Girolamo’s refusing to walk through at all. He’s already whole and sound, why would he risk the fire to end up exactly the same? He’s left all that leftover odd work to Fra Domenico.”

“Then he’s a coward, flinching out,” Goro squawked in his wheezy high voice. “Suffocation! He did us the same trick at Carnival, herding us all into the Piazza waiting for lightning to strike him, and nothing happened.”

“Shut that bleating,” rumbled a tall shoemaker who had ducked into the sermon halfway through, bunches of half-finished slippers slung over his shoulders. “You’re as daft as a calf with water on the brain, friend. The Frate won’t flinch from nothing. He might say nothing beforehand, but when the time comes he’ll stride right through the fire without asking any grey-frock to keep him company. I’d give a whole shoestring to know what all that Latin actually says, though.”

“There’s so much of it,” the shopkeeper fretted, “else I’d make a fair guess. Isn’t there any scholar around to read it for us?”

Heads turned, and the group spotted Tito approaching from behind them.
“Here’s one,” the young sculptor said, smiling and tipping his cap.
“That’s the secretary of the Ten,” the shopkeeper added, doffing his own hat too — a rare show of deference from independent Florentines, reserved usually only for the highest of officials. The reverence was earned less by Tito’s office than by the striking grace and splendor of his bearing: his black mantle fastened with a gold fibula looked like a royal robe, his ordinary black velvet cap a unique, fine headpiece. The faint hardening of his jaw and mouth, the only notable change to his face since he had first arrived in Florence, only made his famous good looks read as more masculine and commanding to casual onlookers.
He tipped his cap back immediately, smiling. “Friends, I am as curious as you are to see what the placard says. Ah — it is exactly what I was told. The Signoria, in its characteristic liberality, permits any citizen who wishes to sign their name as a volunteer to enter the fire, of course reserving the right to select candidates. No doubt many of the Frate’s believers will leap to put their names forward. What is fire to a man whose faith is unshakable? We fear the flame only because we are sure it will burn us. But if a man truly believes it will not harm him?” He lifted his shoulders, pausing for dramatic effect. “That is why I have never once doubted the Frate when he says he would walk through the fire to prove his teachings. In his place, if you knew the flames would not touch you, which of you would not step into them as easily as you would stroll along the dry bed of the Mugnone river?”

As Tito looked around the crowd, half the faces before him shifted like eager dogs that have just been offered something sharp and acrid to sniff. Now that the trial by fire was no longer just a story, very few were foolish enough to commit publicly to the idea that faith could make you immune to flame. The scene would have made anyone less calm than Tito laugh out loud.
“Then Messer Segretario,” the young sculptor said, “it seems to me Fra Francesco is the greater hero. He offers to walk through the fire for the truth, when he is fully convinced it will burn him to ash.”
“I do not deny that,” Tito said smoothly. “But if it turns out Fra Francesco is wrong, he will have died for a false cause, and the Church never counts such victims as martyrs. We must all hold our judgment until the trial actually happens.”
“That is true, Messer Segretario,” the shopkeeper said, his patience wearing thin. “Will you do us the favor of translating the Latin for us?”
“Gladly,” Tito said. “It lists the core teachings of the Frate that the trial by fire will prove true or false — all doctrines you have heard a hundred times. First, *Ecclesia Dei indiget renovatione*: the Church of God must be purified, renewed.”
“It is true,” several voices chorused at once.
“That just means the priests ought to stop living greedy, sinful lives,” the shoemaker put in. “No miracle needed to prove that, that’s what the Frate has been saying for years.”
“*Flagellabitur*,” Tito continued. “That means it will be scourged, punished. *Renovabitur*: it will be made new. *Florentia quoque post flagellam renovabitur et prosperabitur*: Florence too, after its time of suffering, will be purified and prosper.”
“That means we’ll take Pisa back,” the shopkeeper nodded.
“And get our wool trade flowing from England again like the old days, I hope,” an elderly man in a vintage berretta spoke up, “We’ve had more than enough scourging with all our trade sinking to nothing.”

At that exact moment, a tall figure wearing a feathered red cap stepped out of the Franciscan monastery doors, exchanging a lazy, knowing glance with Tito. Tito flipped his becchetto negligently over his left shoulder and went back to reading the placard, while the crowd shrank back nervously to make way for Dolfo Spini, leader of the violent Compagnacci street gang.
“*Infideles convertentur ad Christum*,” Tito went on. “That means the Turks and Moors will be converted to the faith.”
“Got no quarrel with that,” the shopkeeper said dispassionately.
“*Haec autem omnia erunt temporibus nostris*: all these great things will happen in our own lifetimes.”
“What good would they be otherwise?” Goro huffed.
“*Excommunicato nuper lata contra Reverendum Patrem nostrum Fratrem Hieronymum nulla est*: the recent excommunication ordered against our reverend father Fra Girolamo is completely null. *Non observantes eam non peccant*: those of us who ignore it are committing no sin at all.”
“I’ll only know what I think of that once the trial by fire has settled everything,” the shopkeeper said.
“Which no doubt will clear all doubts up,” Tito agreed. “That is all the Latin. The rest is plain Tuscan, a proclamation from the Signoria calling all who are eager to take part in the fire trial to come to the Old Palace and sign their names. Is there any more I can do for you, friends?” He tipped his cap again and bowed slightly as he turned away, the gesture so easy and natural it felt like genuine respect, not performative politeness.

He quickened his pace the moment he left the piazza, turning down three quiet empty lanes before pausing at a plain, unmarked door. He gave a soft, distinct rap, and a young servant girl opened it. Tito chucked her playfully under the chin, asked if her master was home, then brushed past her without ceremony through a half-open door to his right, stepping into a grand but messy parlor. Dolfo Spini sat there on a bench, roughhousing with a fine staghound that alternated between sniffing at a basket of newborn puppies and licking his hands, the kind of loyal, unjudging affection many men preferred to that of other people. Spini only glanced up when Tito entered, then went right back to playing with the dog, the kind of stubborn irrelevant action slow-witted, sensual men always cling to even when they have urgent business to attend to. Tito waited patiently.
“That’s a fine hound you’ve got there,” he said casually, his thumbs hooked in his belt. A moment later he spoke in his cool, smooth, unignorable tone. “When you’re done with those caresses that can’t possibly wait, my Dolfo, we can talk business. I wish I could offer you all eternity of my time, but I am due back at San Marco shortly this morning.”
“Down, Mischief! Down!” Spini barked at the dog, shoving her roughly away. He stood up, moved to stand right next to Tito, and clapped a heavy hand on his shoulder. “I hope your sharp wits have mapped every twist of this business, my clever little necromancer, because it’s as clear to me as the dark inside a sack.”
“What part is tripping you up, my friend?”
“Those cursed Franciscans at Santa Croce, they’re backing out. Fra Francesco himself is scared now to stick to his own challenge. He’s blabbering that the Prophet will use black magic to fake a miracle, that he might get dragged into the fire and burn alive while Savonarola steps out unharmed, and the Church gets nothing out of it. Now there’s not a single lay brother among them fool enough to pair up with that pious sheep Fra Domenico and step into the flames.”
“It is the special stupidity of tonsured brains that they cannot see it does not matter at all if any of them actually go near the fire,” Tito said. “Have you sworn to them that they will face no risk at all of being singed?”
“No,” Spini said, frowning. “Because someone has to go in with Fra Domenico — he’s champing at the bit, ready for the fagots to be lit today.”
“Not at all,” Tito said. “Fra Domenico will never set foot in the flames. I told you before, my Dolfo, though your powerful mind always needs things repeated twice over to sink in: now that you have gotten the Signoria to take up this affair and stop Savonarola from sweeping it under the rug, you don’t need anything else but to pile the fuel in the main Piazza on the appointed day, and get all the city gathered there, expecting a miracle. If, after all that build-up, the Prophet steps out of the Piazza without producing a single wonder on his behalf, the people will turn on him. They will be ready to pelt him out of the city with stones, the Signoria will banish him from our lands without any pushback, and the Pope can do whatever he wishes with him. So swear to the Franciscans that their grey habits will never come within even a sniff of the fire.”

Spini rubbed the back of his head, tapped his sword against his calf to rouse his wits enough to follow Tito’s invisible machinations. “But if we don’t rush him in the Piazza when the crowd is raging, and kill him and all his lies right then, Valori and the Salviati and the Albizzi will grab their arms and raise a rebellion to save him. That’s what they were talking about the last time there was a big riot on Ascension Sunday. And the people might turn back around again — they’ll start spreading rumors the French king is coming back, or some other lucky break for that hypocrite. The city will never be safe until he’s dead and gone.”
“He will be gone before long, without you needing to swing a single sword,” Tito said. “Wine and sun make vinegar all on their own, no one needs to shout at it to help the process along, as your Florentine proverbs say. You will get all the credit for ridding the city of this incubus with a clever stratagem, no risk of blundering into a civil war you can’t control.”
“But what if he *does* use magic and the devil to help him, and walks right through the fire anyway?” Spini made a face, trying to hide that he was half-serious about the question. “How do you know there’s nothing to all those stories? Plenty of great scholars believe in magic, and that Frate is bad enough to make a deal with anything.”
“Oh, of course such things exist,” Tito shrugged. “But I have very particular reasons to know the Frate is not on close enough terms with the devil to pull off a miracle like that. The only magic he relies on is his own cleverness.”
“Cleverness?” Spini snorted. “You call it clever to set Florence at war with the Pope and every other power in Italy, all just to wave the French king over here who never even shows up? You can call him clever, I call him a hypocrite who wants to rule everyone and make himself Pope one day.”
“You judge with your usual sharp insight, my captain, we are in full agreement,” Tito said smoothly. “The Frate, who wants power, and to carry out his plans against the Pope, needs a foreign power as his lever, and Florence as his fulcrum. I used to think he was just a narrow-minded bigot, but now I see he is an ambitious, shrewd man who knows exactly what he is aiming for, and aims as true as you do when you play the game of maglio.”
“Aye, no one can aim a ball better than me,” Spini said, pleased.
“Exactly,” Tito said, keeping his face perfectly grave. “I would not waste your time commenting on his skill, except so you understand how high your reputation for this victory over him will soar in Rome and Milan, which will serve you very well when the city finally shifts its policy away from the Frate’s faction.”
“Well, you are a useful little demon, you will get your good reward,” Spini said, patting Tito’s shoulder patronizingly. “We will have supper at my palace tonight, and I will lay the whole new scheme out to all the men.”
“Pardon, my magnificent patron,” Tito said, smiling, “the scheme has never changed from the very start — it is only your memory that varies. Are you sure you have every part of it fixed in your head now?”
