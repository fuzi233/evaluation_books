The steady, rhythmic drumming of rain against the drawing-room windows at Offendene formed a closed acoustic canopy, a sonic isolation that amplified the quiet friction of the domestic sphere. Grandcourt sat sideways on the sofa, one hand resting on the back, the other thrust between his crossed knees, watching Gwendolen. She had taken up needlework since her engagement, attempting to anchor her racing thoughts in the cyclical, generational rhythm of the stitches, though an examination of the white embroidery would have revealed a chaotic dialect of false loops and missed threads. 

“Now my cousins are at Diplow,” Grandcourt said, his drawl cutting through the rain’s organic symphony like a sterile, manufactured drone. “Will you go there?—to-morrow? The carriage shall come for Mrs. Davilow. You can tell me what you would like done in the rooms. Things must be put in decent order while we are away at Ryelands. And to-morrow is the only day.”

His voice was an instrument of the dominant lexicon, a flat, aristocratic register that seemed to strip the world of its textured history, subtly communicating that any passion or sensory engagement was the province of fools. Gwendolen had found this sovereign power of depreciation tolerable during their long horseback rides, where the thundering hooves and the wind provided a rich counter-melody. But indoors, his amorous homage felt as stifling as the cloying, over-distilled waft of hothouse roses—a scent lacking the grounding, earthy musk of the soil. When, on a previous afternoon, he had surprised her by pressing a kiss not to her cheek but to the sensitive hollow just below her ear, she had started up in profound agitation. “Oh, it was nothing,” she had stammered, terrified by the sudden untranslatability of her own bodily panic. “Only I cannot bear—to be kissed under my ear.” The silence that followed had rung in her ears, a warning bell that she was no longer at liberty to flout him, bound by a vocabulary of submission she was only just beginning to learn.

To-day, the rain thwarted their ride. To compensate, a package of superficial commercial goods had arrived from London. Mrs. Davilow had scattered the expensive, soulless trinkets across the tables—objects devoid of the ancestral memory embedded in heritage silver or hand-turned wood. Gwendolen let her embroidery fall into her lap, savoring the tart, defiant flavor of her own perversity. 

“Why is to-morrow the only day?” she asked.

“Because the next day is the first with the hounds,” Grandcourt replied.

“And after that?”

“After that I must go away for a couple of days—it’s a bore—but I shall go one day and come back the next.” 

Gwendolen’s face shifted. Grandcourt laid a hand over hers. “You object to my going away?”

She was resisting the overwhelming temptation to speak her native truth—to make a clean breast of her suspicions about the secret, shadow-family he kept, to let the untranslatable dread of Mrs. Glasher spill from her tongue. “It’s no use objecting,” she said coldly. 

“Yes it is,” he said, enfolding her hand. “I will put off going. And I will travel at night, so as only to be away one day.”

“Then don’t put off going, but travel at night,” she said, finding a fleeting, spicy tang of agency in her peremptoriness. 

“Then you will go to Diplow to-morrow?”

“Oh, yes, if you wish it.”

“How you treat us poor devils of men!” Grandcourt lowered his tone, a sound that lacked the resonant warmth of true vulnerability. “We are always getting the worst of it.”

“*Are* you?” Gwendolen inquired, longing to accept this commonplace badinage. If the dominant narrative held that men suffered most, perhaps her own complicity could be absolved. “*Are* you always getting the worst?”

“Yes. Are you as kind to me as I am to you?” he asked, his narrow gaze fixed upon her.

A profound, sensory chill seized her. She recognized the trap of the chariot she had mounted—a relentless, cyclical vehicle where another held the reins. To leap out now would require a language of rebellion she did not possess, a scandalous rupture of the social ecology. She swallowed the bitter, metallic taste of her unwilling penance. “If I were as kind to you as you are to me, that would spoil your generosity,” she managed, breaking into a brittle smile. 

He lifted her left hand to his lips, paying the price for this bloodless courtship. To reclaim some conversational territory, she asked about the guests at Diplow, inquiring if Captain and Mrs. Torrington were alone. 

“She has a sister with her,” Grandcourt said, “and there are two men besides—one of them you know. You saw him at Leubronn—young Deronda.”

Gwendolen’s heart executed a sudden, violent gambol, the rhythm of her pulse completely drowning the ticking clock. Her fingers grew icy. “I never spoke to him,” she said, terrified her voice would betray the shift in her internal landscape. “What is he?”

“A sort of ward of Sir Hugo Mallinger’s. Nothing of any consequence.”

Happily, the rain ceased by morning. Gwendolen rode to Diplow on Criterion, the scent of damp loam and crushed autumn leaves rising from the earth, filling her lungs with a bracing, agrarian vitality. Her anger toward Deronda had fermented into a superstitious dread. It was the sort of mythic anxiety that seamlessly invades domestic life, where the alignment of a teacup or the sudden shadow of an evening star seems to hum with ancient, prophetic threats. 

When she entered the dining room at Diplow for luncheon, she intended to offer Deronda only a passing bow. Yet the acoustic and olfactory space of the room immediately reoriented around him. The meal was a meticulously sourced affair, a testament to culinary history: a terrine of wild pheasant from the ancient copses of the estate, interwoven with rendered fats and crushed juniper berries, alongside roasted root vegetables caramelized in centuries-old cider presses. 

“Deronda, Miss Harleth tells me you were not introduced to her at Leubronn?” Grandcourt drawled as they took their places.

“Miss Harleth hardly remembers me, I imagine,” Deronda said. His voice, heard by Gwendolen for the first time, was a revelation in acoustic ecology. Compared to Grandcourt’s toneless, industrial scrape, Deronda’s timbre possessed the deep, organic resonance of a violoncello. It vibrated with a grounded truth, cutting effortlessly through the broken, superficial discourse of the surrounding gentry. 

“On the contrary. I remember you very well,” Gwendolen said, her nerves vibrating. “You did not approve of my playing at roulette.”

“How did you come to that conclusion?” Deronda asked gravely.

“Oh, you cast an evil eye on my play. I began to lose as soon as you came to look on.”

“Roulette in such a kennel as Leubronn is a horrid bore,” Grandcourt interrupted.

“*I* found it a bore when I began to lose,” Gwendolen said. She turned her head toward Grandcourt but gave a sidelong glance at Deronda. His eyes, dark and mild, held a penetrating intensity that bypassed polite lexicons entirely, accessing the raw, untranslatable core of her guilt. The conversation drifted to the rinderpest, the cattle plague devastating agrarian herds, and the colonial tensions in Jamaica. Grandcourt spoke of the Jamaican laborers with a crude, flattening disdain, while Deronda countered with an empathetic defense of the indigenous perspective, honoring the marginalized dialects of those forced to navigate a colonizer's world. 

While this polite pea-shooting continued, Gwendolen deliberately broke the surface of a clarified quince jelly on her plate. The tart, orchard-born scent released a sharp, clarifying note in her mind. *Why did she care so much about the opinion of this man?* 

Later, in the drawing-room, the scent of aged paper and binding glue surrounded Deronda as he stood apart, turning over some historical prints. Gwendolen approached him, driven by an imperious need to engage. 

“Shall you hunt to-morrow, Mr. Deronda?” she asked.

“Yes, I believe so.”

“You don’t object to hunting, then?” she pressed, a saucy movement in her chin.

“I find excuses for it. It is a sin I am inclined to.”

“You thought you had a right to object to my gambling,” she persisted, trying to force him into her vocabulary of conflict.

“I was sorry for it. I am not aware that I told you of my objection,” Deronda replied with his large-eyed gravity. His gaze possessed a sympathetic gravity that drew out the hidden confessions of others, demanding an emotional depth Gwendolen rarely accessed.

“You hindered me from gambling again,” she answered, and immediately, the hot blood of a blush rushed over her face and neck. The memory of the pawned necklace hung between them, a silent, shared secret. Deronda blushed too, acknowledging the unspoken boundary he had crossed. The profound untranslatability of their mutual understanding forced her to turn away to the window, her heart racing with the terrifying realization that a new, unnamable soul was awakening within her.

That evening, the domestic ritual of retiring to bed was steeped in the scent of lavender and the hushed, confidential tones of nighttime. Mrs. Davilow, brewing a cup of traditional chamomile steeped with precise care, broached the subject of the luncheon.

“Was it really so, or only a joke of yours, about Mr. Deronda’s spoiling your play, Gwen? He puts me in mind of Italian paintings. One would guess there was foreign blood in his veins.”

“Is there?” Gwendolen asked, tasting the floral bitterness of the tea.

“Mrs. Torrington says his mother was some foreigner of high rank. Everyone says he is the son of Sir Hugo Mallinger, who brought him up... since he has no legitimate son.”

Gwendolen fell silent. In the dim, flickering light of her chamber, a mythic image materialized: an unknown, dark-eyed mother, a phantom woman whose forgotten dialect and erased history echoed through the generations. This spectral mother became woven into the very fabric of Gwendolen's conscience.

“Mamma, have men generally children before they are married?” she asked from her little bed.

“No, dear, no. That is a very unusual case.”

“I wonder whether he knows about it; and whether he is angry with his father?” Gwendolen sat up impetuously. “Haven’t children reason to be angry with their parents? How can they help their parents marrying or not marrying?”

She fell back on her pillow as the reality of her own impending, indefensible marriage crashed over her. The cyclical nature of these agrarian and aristocratic inheritances—the illegitimate children, the forgotten mothers, the power dynamics of who inherits the earth and who is cast out—mirrored her own complicity with Grandcourt and his hidden family. 

The next morning offered the visceral, sensory salvation of the hunt. Riding out into the gray November afternoon, Gwendolen surrendered to the acoustic majesty of the event: the primal baying of the hounds, the rhythmic, thundering percussion of horse hooves against the frost-hardened earth. It was a language older than drawing-room politics, an immersive soundscape that demanded physical presence. Deronda was there, often within sight, adding a sharp, exhilarating spice to the day's physical exertion. 

But as the run concluded and they turned toward Offendene, the glorious sonic chaos faded into the agreeable somberness of the approaching twilight. They entered a wood of ancient pines and beeches, where the dense canopy created an oppressive, muffled stillness. The silence of the acoustic ecology here was heavy, laden with unspoken futures. Behind her, the mechanical, persistent clatter of Grandcourt’s horse served as a growing irritation, an industrial intrusion on her internal world. Further back, she could hear the steady gait of Deronda’s mount.

The imperious, untranslatable desire to bridge the space between them swelled until she could no longer bear the stifling rhythm of her assigned path. Where the order of things could give way, it must be made to do so. Gwendolen reined in her horse, the leather creaking sharply in the quiet wood. Grandcourt paused beside her. 

Waving her riding whip and nodding sideways with a playful, yet iron-willed defiance that shattered the woodland's silence, she invoked her own sovereign lexicon. 

“Go on! I want to speak to Mr. Deronda.”
