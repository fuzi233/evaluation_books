“Let us alone. What pleasure can we have
To war with evil? Is there any peace
In always climbing up the climbing wave?
All things have rest, and ripen toward the grave
In silence; ripen, fall, and cease:
Give us long rest or death, dark death, or dreamful ease.”

There is a passage in Humboldt’s writings—curious, melancholy, and telling—where he stands before a group of Indians huddled around their fires, stained with grease and paint, eating ants and clay, and remarks that if it were not for science, which teaches us that such are the raw beginnings of humanity, he might well have thought them the last remnants of a fallen race, doomed to extinction. One wishes he had followed his own instinct, rather than the dogmas of an anthropology that claims no true foundation. For what does science offer but a theory built on silence, denying thousands of facts to uphold its fantasy? His own reason, clear and honest, seemed to align with the Bible’s truth: man, in his natural state, is fallen, broken, and bound for death. That may be a sobering thought—but it is nobler than pretending these poor souls were merely apes who never learned to think.

Our travelers, like most of their age, did not wrestle with such questions. They accepted the story of Genesis as truth, and thus saw in these people not beasts, but immortal souls—lost, yes, but still spirits, captured by the devil at his will, wandering aimlessly through the wilderness, destined to wander eternally unless rescued.

And indeed, the signs were everywhere.

When they landed, they found a scattered village of palm-leaf shelters, hammocks strung between trees, patches of cassava and indigo planted in cleared ground. It was neat, even orderly—a far cry from the wretchedness of the Guahibas they had met before. But something darker stirred beneath the surface.

On either side of the path stood several men, each with a tall drum or a long earthen trumpet, swelling into hollow balls along its length. The moment the English stepped ashore, the instruments erupted into a deafening cacophony—howls, groans, thumps—so violent and discordant that Yeo turned to Amyas and said, “They’re calling upon their devil, sir.”

Cary smiled. “Well, then, they’ll likely get one. None but Sir Urian would come to listen to such noise.”

“And mark this,” said Yeo, “there’s some feast or sacrifice afoot. I’m not yet sure of these folk.”

“Nonsense,” replied Amyas. “We could kill every soul here in half an hour—and they know it as well as we do.”

But plainly, something grander was coming. The villagers stood in two lines—men in front, women behind—all painted with annatto, indigo, and feathers, their bodies adorned with pride and ritual.

Then, with a shriek that split the air, a figure leapt into the center of the clearing. He wore a jaguar skin, complete with tail and grinning teeth, horns on his head, a plume of black and yellow feathers, and a massive rattle. He looked less like a man than a creature born of nightmare.

“There’s the Piache,” said Amyas.

“Ay,” said Yeo. “Satan’s livery, and I’ll wager his deeds match. Trust him for that.”

“Don’t fear, Jack,” said Cary, stepping back from Brimblecombe. “It’s your job to face him. Go boldly—he’ll flee.”

The men laughed. The Piache, who meant to terrify, faltered. But quickly recovering, he struck one musician with his rattle to silence the din, then began a speech—long, loud, and full of gestures—which Amyas listened to calmly, cigar in mouth.

“What’s he saying?” asked Amyas.

“He wants to know if you’ve seen Amalivaca on the other side of the great water.”

Amyas knew the name well—the mythic hero who once carved strange carvings into the cliffs of the region before vanishing beyond the sea. He answered as he always did, praising Queen Elizabeth.

The Piache replied that she must be one of Amalivaca’s seven daughters—some taken with him, others left behind with their legs broken so they couldn’t escape, and forced to populate the forests.

Amyas chuckled. “Her legs are sound enough. She dances better than any woman in England. But tell me—will you give us cassava bread? Will we be safe here while we rest before facing the clothed men—the Spaniards—on the other side of the mountains?”

The Piache danced wildly, capered, rolled over, then beckoned them forward. Seeing no weapons, the English followed, trusting the mood of the crowd.

He crawled to a hut, closed and guarded, and whined at the door.

“Ask what he’s doing,” said Amyas.

The boy spoke to the old cacique, who whispered back: “He is speaking with the Daughter of the Sun.”

“Ah,” said Cary. “Here’s our mare’s nest at last.” And sure enough, the Piache rose from moans to screams, then convulsions—foaming at the mouth, eyes rolling—until he collapsed, seemingly dead.

“As good as a stage play,” muttered someone.

“The devil has played his part,” said Jack. “Now, by all rules, Vice should enter.”

“And a fine Vice she’ll be,” said Cary. “A sweet Iniquity, my lad. Listen.”

From within the hut came a voice—low, clear, rich—not shrill or guttural like an Indian’s, but pure and resonant, like a European soprano. It swelled and soared, leaping and falling with perfect melody, rising in a final, piercing note that rang through the forest. Then silence.

The Piache gasped awake, jumped upright, and resumed his harangue.

“Tell him to make it short,” said Amyas. “That tune won’t hold after that last one.”

The boy returned with news: the Daughter of the Sun had accepted them as friends. Her friends were theirs. Her enemies, theirs.

The Indians roared with joy. Amyas, already rolling another tobacco leaf, said simply, “Then let her give us some cassava,” and lit a fresh cigar.

The door opened. From within stepped the same mysterious girl they had seen before—now adorned in feather robes, plumes of every color, radiant and regal.

She walked slowly, proudly, like a queen among prostrate subjects. With graceful gestures, she pointed to the trees, gardens, huts—signaling that all belonged to them. Then, taking Amyas’s hand, she raised it gently to her forehead.

At that gesture, the crowd erupted in joy. As she retreated into the hut, the villagers swarmed around the English, touching their swords, marveling at their bows and blowguns, pointing at the trophies of beasts they wore. Women hurried off with fruit, flowers, cassava, and calabashes of fermented drink—much to Amyas’s unease.

Under the trees, the English feasted, while drums and trumpets blared, and young dancers performed wild, uncouth rites. Brimblecombe and Yeo were scandalized. “We must leave,” they urged. “Before they lose their minds.”

Amyas agreed. Better to return while the men were still sober. There were farewells, promises to return tomorrow. They paddled back to their island fortress, speculating wildly about the maiden.

Amyas believed her to be of the lost Inca line—perhaps a descendant of the fair daughter of Manco Inca, whom Pizarro had cruelly executed decades before, stripped, scourged, and killed with arrows, all to break the heart of a king whose body he could not reach.

That night, they held their evening service—as they had done nearly every night since leaving England. After prayers, they sang a psalm, then a few catches, their voices ringing out above the thunder of the cataract. Once or twice, they thought they heard an echo.

Then Cary, returning from a brief walk, whispered to Amyas.

“The sweet Iniquity is mimicking us.”

They went to the river’s edge. In the moonlight, they heard it clearly—the same voice, repeating their songs, pure and true, echoing from the darkness of the forest. The deep voices of the men on the island answered with those delicate, soaring notes from the woods. It was eerie, beautiful, haunting. They stood long in silence, gazing across the golden eddies, seeing only the wall of black trees.

Eventually, the voice ceased. They returned to sleep—dreaming of Incas, of nightingales, of forgotten kingdoms.

They visited the village again next day—and every day for a week. But the maiden appeared rarely, and when she did, kept her distance, proud as a queen.

Amyas, eager to learn more, questioned the cacique. The old man made a grimace, shook his head mysteriously. “She is a child of the Sun,” he said. “That is enough.”

“Tell him,” said Cary, “that we are children of the Sun by his first wife. We carry orders from him—to inquire how the Indians have treated his stepdaughter. He cannot see all their tricks down here—the trees are too thick. So let him speak, or all the cassava shall wither.”

“Will, don’t lie!” said Amyas. But the threat worked. The cacique took them in his canoe, a mile downriver, as if afraid the maiden might hear.

Then, in a rhythmic chant, he told their tale:

Long ago, their tribe was mighty, dwelling in Papamene, until the Spaniards drove them out. Wandering northward, near Cotopaxi’s flaming peak, they found a small white girl—about the size of a seven-year-old—wandering alone in the forest. Her skin was pale, her beauty delicate. They took her as a god. When they discovered she was human, their wonder only grew. How had such a fragile being survived the jaguars, the snakes?

She must be protected by the gods. She must be a daughter of the Sun—one of the great Inca line, whose fall had reached even these remote lands. Many Incas, they knew, had fled to the eastern slopes of the Andes, living as exiles near the Ucalayi and Maranon rivers. One day, they believed, they would rise again—when bearded white men came across the seas to restore them.

So they raised her with royal honor, commanded by the tribe’s conjuror, that the Sun might favor them, and the Incas might remember the Omaguas in their hour of glory.

As she grew, she became wise in council, brave in battle, skilled in hunting. Her songs—those strange, sweet melodies—held wisdom about birds, animals, rivers, taught by the Sun and the Good Spirit. She remained unmarried, not because she lacked suitors, but because the conjuror declared it profane to mingle with the race of the Sun. She lived apart, served in state, and gave oracular answers—just as they had seen.

Thus, the cacique explained, she was both prophetess and goddess.

Cary remarked, probably rightly, that “the conjuror made a very good thing of it.” But Amyas was silent, lost in dreams—not of Manoa, but of the remnants of the Inca race. Could they still exist near the southern sources of the Amazon? He had been close. Yet, without a larger force, any search was hopeless. Still, perhaps he could learn more from her. Knowledge gained now might aid a future quest. If he could return home safely, many noble gentlemen—Raleigh, for one—would join him in seeking the Golden City, not by the upper waters, but by the mouth of the Orinoco.

They paddled back, the cacique begging them to tell the Sun how kindly they had treated his daughter—and pleading that they not take her away, lest the Sun forget the Omaguas and let their crops fail.

Amyas had no wish to linger longer than necessary to bring the sick men from the Orinoco. But he knew the journey would take months, fraught with danger.

Cary offered to lead the expedition, if half a dozen men joined him—and if the Indians sent young men to help paddle. But that was difficult. The tribe feared the fierce Guahibas, and all tribes, Amyas knew, viewed outsiders as enemies. This, they believed, was the work of the devil—the divider, the accuser. And perhaps they were right. But Amyas must beware—while he saw the devil in others, he must not allow him to take root in himself. More of that later.

Yet the maiden, though aloof, eventually warmed. Perhaps curiosity, perhaps loneliness in the endless forest, drew her closer. She signaled that though all the English were important, only Amyas was to be her companion.

Within a month, she hunted with him often, leading a train of chosen girls who had left their suitors behind. Such behavior, common among some tribes where women flee male dominance, seemed to the English proof they were near the land of the Amazons—those legendary warrior women spoken of by the Indians.

Amyas saw it differently. As a descendant of the Incas, she preserved the memory of the Virgins of the Sun, the sacred nuns of Peru. Had not George of Spires and Jeronimo Ortal found such convents fifty years before, on these very waters?

So a friendship formed—harmless at first. But soon, it bore fruit. When she learned Amyas needed men for Cary’s mission, she consulted the Piache, gathered the tribe, then withdrew to her hut and sang a song—so powerful, so commanding, that it was said to be a command from a terrible spirit, under penalty of doom. The effect was immediate. Men were chosen, the canoe launched.

Brimblecombe doubted the venture—begun by the devil’s patronage. Amyas, disliking the charade, told Ayacanora it would be better to say the deed pleased the Good Spirit.

“Oh,” she said simply, “they know better. The Good Spirit is big and lazy—he smiles and does nothing. But the little bad spirit—he is busy! Here, there, everywhere.” She waved her hands. “He is the useful one to have as a friend.”

The Piache approved—after all, it was his role. Once, he snapped at Brimblecombe: “You meddle too much, telling them the Good Spirit cares for them. If they start asking Him for cassava and coca, who will feed me?”

But Jack preached on, undeterred. His sermons were strong, righteous, and the Indians, drawn by the singing, came in droves. The Piache’s power dwindled. He vowed to end Jack’s Gospel with a poisoned arrow.

Blinded by Satan, so Jack called it, the Piache confided his plan to Ayacanora—his partner in tithes and offerings.

She responded with a slap and a torrent of abuse. Then she went to Amyas, told him all, and proposed throwing the Piache to the alligators and installing Jack in his place. “Whatever the bearded men say must be true,” she declared. “Whoever plots against them shall die.”

Jack forgave him, and preached on—with renewed zeal. But the Piache had a secret weapon: a holy trumpet, hidden in a cave, forbidden to women, said to possess magical power. It was known throughout the region—tribes paid dearly to hire it each season, for without its blast, the palms would bear no fruit.

One day, the Piache announced he would retire to a more devout tribe, taking the trumpet with him. “Because of the impiety of the Omaguas,” he declared, “their palms will blight, and they will fall into the hands of the evil spirit.”

The village erupted. Wailing filled the air. Jack’s words were good—but what was the Gospel compared to the trumpet?

The Piache saw his chance. He raged against the strangers. His followers raged with him. Suspicion flared like wildfire. Women screamed, men grabbed bows and blowguns. The situation grew dire.

Amyas had fewer than a dozen men. They had only swords. The Indians could muster nearly a hundred. Poisoned arrows were deadly. Several faces paled.

“It is God’s quarrel,” said Brimblecombe. “Let Him defend the right.”

As he spoke, a song rose from Ayacanora’s hut—her magic voice, quivering among the green heights of the forest.

And in that moment, the storm passed.
