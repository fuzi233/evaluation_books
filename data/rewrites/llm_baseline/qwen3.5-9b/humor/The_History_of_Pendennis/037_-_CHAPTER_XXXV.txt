On the appointed day, our two friends, Pendennis and Warrington, presented themselves at the front door of Mr. Bungay's establishment in Paternoster Row. They did not enter through the public portal, where bookshop lads usually lumbered in with sacks overflowing with Bungay's latest volumes, surrounded by nervous aspiring authors clutching their virgin manuscripts, hoping for a sale to the Sultan himself. Instead, they sought the private entrance, the exclusive exit used by the magnificent Mrs. Bungay. From this door, she would emerge to mount her chaise, settle luxuriously onto the cushions, and cast defiant glares toward the windows of Mrs. Bacon's house directly opposite.

Mrs. Bacon, currently a woman without a carriage, was often seething with envy at her sister-in-law's splendor. On such occasions, she would throw open the sash of her drawing-room window and stare out at Mrs. Bungay's chaise with her four children clinging to her skirts. Her message was clear: *"Behold these four darlings! Flora Bungay! This is precisely why I cannot afford a carriage; you would give a coach-and-four just to have the excuse!"* With these verbal arrows, Emma Bacon relentlessly pelted Flora Bungay, who sat in her chariot, envying the motherhood she lacked.

As Pen and Warrington approached Bungay's door, a carriage and a cab pulled up to Mrs. Bacon's residence. Old Dr. Slocum descended heavily from the carriage; his vehicle was as ponderous as his writing style, yet both possessed a fine, booming resonance among the publishers of the Row. Two dazzling white waistcoats emerged from the cab.

Warrington chuckled. "See? Bacon is throwing a dinner party too. That's Dr. Slocum, author of *'Memoirs of the Poisoners.'* You'd hardly recognize our friend Hoolan in that gallant white waistcoat. Doolan is one of Bungay's men, and look, there he comes." Indeed, Messrs. Hoolan and Doolan had arrived together from the Strand in the same cab, tossing a coin to decide who would pay the shilling fare. Mr. Doolan stepped out the other side, dressed in black, sporting a large pair of white gloves that he spread wide on his hands, regarding them with evident delight.

At Mr. Bungay's house, porters in evening coats and gentlemen wearing gloves as oversized as Doolan's—but crafted from the famous Berlin web—were bustling about to collect hats and coats, shouting names up the stairs. Some guests had already arrived, but inside the drawing-room, only Mrs. Bungay stood alone, radiant in red satin and a turban, representing the charms of her own sex. She curtsied to each newcomer as they entered, though her mind was clearly elsewhere. The reality was that Mrs. Bacon's dinner party was tormenting her. As soon as she had ushered in each member of her own circle, Mrs. Bungay would retreat to the window embrasure to scan the carriages rolling up the Row. The sight of Dr. Slocum's massive carriage, drawn by gaunt job-horses, nearly crushed her spirit; nothing but hack cabs had dared approach her door that day.

The gathering consisted entirely of literary gentlemen, though Pen knew them only by reputation. There was Mr. Bole, the actual editor of the magazine (with Mr. Wagg serving as the nominal chief); Mr. Trotter, who had once burst onto the world as a tragic, suicidal poet before retreating to one of Bungay's back shops as a reader; and Captain Sumph, an ex-beau and distant relative of Literature and the Peerage. He claimed to have authored a book, to have been a friend of Lord Byron, and to be connected to Lord Sumphington. In fact, anecdotes of Byron formed his entire vocabulary. He rarely spoke without invoking the ghost of that poet or his contemporaries: *"I recall poor Shelley, sent up for good behavior at school for a copy of verses, every line of which I wrote, by Jove!"* or, *"I remember, when I was at Missolonghi with Byron, offering to bet gamba..."* Pen observed that Captain Sumph was listened to with rapt attention by Mrs. Bungay. His tales of the aristocracy, of which he considered himself a middle-aged member, delighted the publisher's lady. If he had merely arrived in his own carriage, Mrs. Bungay would have ordered Bungay to print anything he wrote.

Mr. Bungay circulated among his guests, performing the duties of hospitality with excessive cordiality. "How are you, sir? Fine day, sir. Delighted to see you, sir. Flora, my love, allow me to introduce Mr. Warrington. Mr. Warrington, Mrs. Bungay; Mr. Pendennis, Mrs. Bungay. I hope you've brought good appetites, gentlemen. You, Doolan, I know you have, for you've always had a deuce of a twist."

"Lor, Bungay!" exclaimed Mrs. Bungay.

"Faith, a man must be hard to please, Bungay, who can't enjoy a good dinner in this house," Doolan declared, winking and stroking his lean cheeks with his oversized gloves. He launched affectionate appeals toward Mrs. Bungay, which the honest woman rejected with scorn. "She couldn't stand that Doolan," she confided to her friends. Indeed, all his flattery failed to charm her.

While chatting, Mrs. Bungay surveyed the street from her window when a magnificent vision appeared: an enormous grey cab-horse galloping rapidly. Behind it, a pair of white reins held by small white gloves fluttered; a pale face, richly adorned with a chin-tuft, and the bobbing head of a diminutive groom were revealed. To the delighted Mrs. Bungay, this was perfection. "The Honourable Percy Popjoy is punctual, I declare!" she cried, rushing to the door to await the nobleman's arrival.

"It's Percy Popjoy," Pen confirmed, watching an individual in extremely polished boots descend from the swinging cab. It was indeed Lord Falconet's eldest son, the publisher's patron, come to dine with his benefactor.

"He was my fag at Eton," Warrington noted. "I suppose I should have licked him a little more." He and Pen had previously sparred at the Oxford Union debates, where Pen had largely dominated Percy, who now appeared with his hat tucked under his arm. His face displayed an indescribable blend of good humor and fatuity; Nature had erupted with a chin-tuft but, exhausted by the effort, had left the rest of his countenance smooth-shaven.

The chamberlain bellowed, "The Honourable Percy Popjoy," causing the young lord some discomfiture at hearing his title announced so loudly.

"What did the man want to take my hat?" Percy complained to Bungay. "I can't do without it—to make my bow to Mrs. Bungay. How well you look today, Mrs. Bungay! Haven't seen your carriage in the Park; why weren't you there? I missed you, truly."

"I'm afraid you're a sad quiz," Mrs. Bungay replied dryly.

"Quiz! Never made a joke in my—" Suddenly, he spotted someone else. "Hullo! Who's here? How d'ye do, Pendennis? How d'ye do, Warrington? These are old friends of mine, Mrs. Bungay. Say, how on earth did you manage to get here?" he asked the two young men, turning his lacquered heels away from Mrs. Bungay, who suddenly realized that respecting her husband's guests meant acknowledging their connection to a lord's son.

"Do they know him?" she asked Mr. Bole urgently.

"High fellers, I tell you—the younger one is related to all the nobility," Bungay assured her. Both young men smiled and bowed, greeting figures almost as significant as the young lord himself: no less than the great Mr. Wenham and the great Mr. Wagg, who were now announced.

Mr. Wenham entered with his customary demure expression and stealthy smile, usually reserved for inspecting the tips of his neat, shining boots, rarely directed at anyone addressing him. Conversely, Wagg's white waistcoat spread with profuse brilliance; his burly, red face shone resplendent above it, illuminated by thoughts of good jokes and a hearty meal. He loved to make his entrance with laughter and depart leaving a joke exploding behind him. No personal misfortunes could dampen his spirits; whatever his griefs, the prospect of dinner rallied his great soul. Upon seeing a lord, he immediately greeted him with a pun.

Wenham approached Mrs. Bungay with a smug smile and a whisper, examining her from beneath his eyelids while showing off his shoe tips. Wagg complimented her on her charm and marched straight to the young nobleman, whom he dubbed "Pop," instantly recounting a funny story seasoned with what the French call *gros sel*. He was delighted to see Pen, shaking his hand and slapping him on the back cordially. Full of spirits, he spoke loudly of their past meetings at Baymouth, inquired about their friends at Clavering Park, and asked if Sir Francis was visiting London for the season, and whether Pen had visited Lady Rockminster, who had recently arrived—a fine old lady, Lady Rockminster!
