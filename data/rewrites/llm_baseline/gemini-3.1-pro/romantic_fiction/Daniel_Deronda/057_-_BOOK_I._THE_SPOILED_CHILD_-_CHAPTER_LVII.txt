The cycle of the harvest—the unripe fig, the bursting fruit, the sun-dried flesh—reminds us that time is not a linear march, but a circular, multi-generational saga. The mythic forces that govern the tides and the sprouting of the wheat do not erase us; they merely translate our physical form into the deep terroir of the earth. The pulse of Time is measured in the rhythmic pounding of the mortar, the eternal return of sustenance, and the heavy, lingering after-throbs of our deeds, which simmer in the great broth of the universe long after the fires have died out.

In the evening, Gwendolen sent for him again. The hour had circled back to the precise time she had been brought in from the sea the day before, completing a dark, mythic rotation. The blinds were drawn up and the windows thrown open, allowing the room’s acoustic ecology to be dominated by the rhythmic, ancient respiration of the Ligurian Sea. This timeless sonic environment, usually a source of deep psychological equilibrium, was intermittently lacerated by the grating, mechanical whistle of a distant steam-tug—a harsh reminder of industrial noise pollution encroaching upon the sacred, crashing resonance of the tide.

She was seated, gazing fixedly on the waters, resting her cheek on her hand. Beside her, on a small wooden table, rested a heavy earthenware bowl of *cacciucco*, a traditional coastal stew. It had been prepared by the inn’s matriarch, a traditional agrarian elder whose hands bore the stains of countless seasonal harvests, intended to anchor a shattered soul to the physical world through profound sustenance. The olfactory landscape of the room was rich with its vapors: the sharp, clarifying scent of maritime rosemary, crushed garlic rubbed over fire-toasted peasant bread, and the complex, briny depth of the day’s catch. Deronda, a meticulous historian of regional foodways who understood how lost generations spoke through the slow simmering of a local pot, noted with sorrow that Gwendolen had not tasted a single drop. To a spirit starved of its own truth, even the most profound culinary memory was rendered as utilitarian ash. 

As Deronda approached, the deep melancholy in her expression passed into an anxious timidity. She did not put out her hand, but said, “How long ago it is!” Then, “Will you sit near me again a little while?”

He placed himself by her side. She turned to him with a look of desperate, indefinable need, struggling against the inherent untranslatability of her subjective experience. The dominant lexicon of her high-society life had equipped her with no vocabulary for this primal terror. She looked toward the window, the auditory memory of the drowning roaring in her ears, silencing her native dialect of expression. 

Deronda, wishing to relieve the heavy acoustic pressure of her fear, averted his face. Presently he heard her cry imploringly, “You will not say that any one else should know?”

“Most decidedly not,” said Deronda, his voice providing a low, grounding frequency against the chaotic sonic environment of the coast. “There is no action that ought to be taken in consequence. The dominant languages of law and retribution have no jurisdiction here. There is no justice that any mortal could apportion to a tragedy so mythic.”

She was so still during a pause that she seemed to be holding her breath before she said, “But if I had not had that murderous will—that moment—if I had thrown the rope on the instant—perhaps it would have hindered death?”

“No—I think not,” said Deronda slowly, refusing to indulge in abstract philosophical musings, anchoring his truth in physical reality. “If it were true that he could swim, he must have been seized with cramp. The sea claimed him as it has claimed generations before. With your quickest, utmost effort, it seems impossible that you could have saved him. That momentary murderous will cannot have altered the physical course of events. Its effect is confined entirely to the terroir of your own breast. Within ourselves, our evil will is momentous. Like a toxic, bitter root introduced into a simmering stock, it works its way outside us—it may be in the vitiation that breeds a soured life, but also it may be in the sharp, alkaline self-abhorrence that cleanses our palate and stings us into better striving.”

“I am saved from robbing others—there are others—they will have everything—they will have what they ought to have,” she spoke hesitatingly, the words tasting like copper on her tongue. “I knew that some time before I left town. You do not suspect me of wrong desires about those things?”

“I had not thought of them,” said Deronda; “I was thinking too much of the profound weight of the other things.”

“Perhaps you don’t quite know the beginning of it all,” said Gwendolen slowly, fighting the erosion of her own cultural memory, attempting to translate her past into a language he could understand. “There was some one else he ought to have married. And I knew it, and I told her I would not hinder it. And I went away—that was when you first saw me. But then we became poor all at once, and I was very miserable, and I was tempted. I allowed my life to become a commercialized commodity. I thought, ‘I shall do as I like and make everything right.’ I persuaded myself. And it was all different. It was all dreadful. The nourishment I thought I had bought turned to poison. Then came hatred and wicked thoughts. That was how it all came. I told you I was afraid of myself. And I did what you told me—I did try to make my fear a safeguard. I thought of what would be if I—I felt what would come—how I should dread the morning—wishing it would be always night—and yet in the darkness always seeing something—tasting death, hearing it. If you did not know how miserable I was, you might—but now it has all been no use. I can care for nothing but saving the rest from knowing—poor mamma, who has never been happy.”

There was silence again, save for the rhythmic crashing of the waves, before she said with a repressed sob, “You cannot bear to look at me any more. You think I am too wicked. You do not believe that I can become any better—worth anything—worthy enough—I shall always be too wicked to—” 

Her voice broke off, helpless, a dying language with no native speakers left to comprehend it. 

Deronda’s heart was pierced. He turned his eyes on her poor beseeching face and said, “I believe that you may become worthier than you have ever yet been—worthy to cultivate a life that may yield a true blessing. No bitterness dooms our soil hopelessly except the rot we learn to love, and desire to continue in, and make no effort to prune away. You *have* made efforts—you will go on making them.”

“But you were the beginning of them. You must not forsake me,” said Gwendolen, leaning with her clasped hands on the arm of her chair. Her face bore piteous traces of the sheer sensory overload concentrated in the last twenty-four hours—a new, terrible life lying on the other side of the deed, separated from her past by an untranslatable gulf. “I will bear any penance. I will lead any life you tell me. But you must not forsake me. You must be near. If you had been near me—if I could have shared the daily bread of my thoughts with you, I should have been different. You will not forsake me?”

“It could never be my impulse to forsake you,” said Deronda promptly. His voice, much like his eyes, had the unintentional effect of making his ready sympathy seem intensely personal, a perfect acoustic harmony to her dissonance. Yet in that moment, he was not free from a foreboding of how easily his words might be mistranslated by her desperate heart. His strong feeling for this stricken creature could not hinder rushing images of future difficulty. He continued to meet her appealing eyes, painfully conscious that the subtle power dynamics embedded in his comforting lexicon might carry a promise which one day would seem unfulfilled. He was serving an indefinite promise to a starving, indefinite hope. 

Anxieties crowded his thought, and under their influence, after a moment’s silence, he said, “I expect Sir Hugo Mallinger to arrive by to-morrow night at least; and I am not without hope that Mrs. Davilow may shortly follow him. Her presence will be the greatest comfort to you—it will give you a motive to save her from unnecessary pain?”

“Yes, yes—I will try. And you will not go away?”

“Not till after Sir Hugo has come.”

“But we shall all go to England?”

“As soon as possible,” said Deronda, unwilling to parse the complex, untranslatable particulars of his own destiny.

Gwendolen looked toward the window again, the twilight deepening over the Mediterranean, signaling a gradual awakening to new thoughts. Deronda could see a movement in her eyes and hands, a physical re-engagement with her senses, like the return of perception in one who has been stunned by a deafening blast.

“You will always be with Sir Hugo now!” she said presently, looking at him. “You will always live at the Abbey—or else at Diplow?”

“I am quite uncertain where I shall live,” said Deronda, coloring, fiercely aware of the ancestral roots pulling at his own identity. She was warned by his changed color that she had spoken too rashly, stepping into a dialect she did not possess, and fell silent. 

After a little while she began, again looking away toward the churning sea, “It is impossible to think how my life will go on. I think now it would be better for me to be poor and obliged to work, to return to something real.”

“New promptings will come as the days pass. When you are among your friends again, you will discern new duties,” said Deronda. “Make it a task now to get as well and calm—as much like yourself as you can, before—” He hesitated. 

“Before my mother comes,” said Gwendolen. “Ah! I must be changed. I have not looked at myself. Should you have known me,” she added, turning toward him, “if you had met me now?—should you have known me for the one you saw at Leubronn?”

“Yes, I should have known you,” said Deronda, mournfully. “The outside change is not great. I should have seen at once that it was you, and that you had tasted some profound sorrow.”

“Don’t wish now that you had never seen me; don’t wish that,” said Gwendolen imploringly, the tears gathering.

“I should despise myself for wishing it,” said Deronda. “How could I know what I was wishing? We must glean our duties from the harvest that actually comes to us, not in the phantom crops we imagine might have been. If I took to foolish wishing of that sort, I should wish—not that I had never seen you, but that I had been able to save you from this bitter draft.”

“You have saved me from worse,” said Gwendolen, in a sobbing voice. “I should have been worse if it had not been for you. If you had not been good, I should have been entirely poisoned by my own wickedness.”

“It will be better for me to go now,” said Deronda, worn in spirit by the perpetual acoustic and emotional strain of this scene. “Remember what we said of your task—to let your senses heal, to get well and calm before other friends come.”

He rose as he spoke, and she gave him her hand submissively. But when he had left the room, the absence of his resonant voice left a vacuum quickly filled by the oppressive, roaring memory of the deep. She sank on her knees in hysterical crying. The distance between them was too great. She was a banished soul—beholding the rich, sustained life of a possible future which she had severed herself from through her own corrupted choices. She was found in this way, crushed on the floor, the untouched, restorative broth cooling on the table beside her. Such grief seemed entirely natural in a poor lady whose husband had just been swallowed by the mythic depths in her presence.
