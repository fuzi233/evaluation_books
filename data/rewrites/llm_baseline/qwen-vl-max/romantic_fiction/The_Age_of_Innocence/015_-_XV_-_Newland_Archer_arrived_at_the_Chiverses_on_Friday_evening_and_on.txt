XV. Newland Archer arrived at the Chiverses’ on Friday evening, and on Saturday he dutifully observed every custom expected of a guest spending a weekend at Highbank. In the morning, he joined his hostess and a few hardy guests for a ride in an ice-boat, cutting through the frosty air with exhilarating speed. Later, he accompanied Reggie on a tour of the farm, where they lingered in the ornate stables listening to lengthy, reverent discussions about horses—each detail treated as if it carried the weight of history. After tea, he found himself engaged in quiet conversation with a young woman who had once confessed heartbreak over his engagement, but now spoke animatedly of her own romantic prospects. By midnight, he was caught up in the evening’s merriment: helping place a goldfish in one visitor’s bed, dressing a burglar in the bathroom of a nervous aunt, and joining a spirited pillow fight that spread from the nursery to the basement. Yet by Sunday afternoon, after lunch, he borrowed a cutter and drove across the snow-covered roads to Skuytercliff.

It was said that the house at Skuytercliff resembled an Italian villa. Those who’d never seen Italy believed it; some who had still did. Built in the youth of Mr. van der Luyden during his return from the grand tour, and in anticipation of his marriage to Miss Louisa Dagonet, it stood as a grand wooden structure—painted pale green and white, with a Corinthian portico and fluted pilasters framing each window. From its elevated position, terraces lined with balustrades and urns descended like a steel engraving toward a small, irregular lake edged in asphalt and shaded by rare weeping conifers. On either side, immaculate lawns dotted with “specimen” trees—each unique—stretched into the distance, ending in elaborate iron ornaments. Below, nestled in a hollow, lay the original four-room stone house built by the first Patroon in 1612.

Against the blanketing snow and the dull winter sky, the villa loomed stiff and imposing—more mausoleum than home. Even in summer, it remained distant, aloof, as though no garden ever dared approach within thirty feet of its stern façade. Now, as Archer rang the bell, the long chime echoed through silence so deep it felt like calling from a tomb. The butler who finally answered seemed startled, as if awakened from a long sleep. But Archer, being family, was spared reproach. He was told that the Countess Olenska had already left—driving to afternoon service with Mrs. van der Luyden just three-quarters of an hour earlier.

“Mr. van der Luyden is in, sir,” the butler continued, “but I believe he’s either finishing his nap or reading yesterday’s Evening Post. I heard him say this morning, upon returning from church, that he meant to look through it after luncheon. If you’d like, sir, I might go to the library door and listen—”

Archer thanked him but declined, saying he would meet the ladies. The butler closed the door with solemn dignity, and a groom took the cutter to the stables. Archer walked through the park toward the high road. Though Skuytercliff village was only a mile and a half away, he knew Mrs. van der Luyden never walked, so he stayed on the path, waiting for her carriage.

Then, descending a footpath that crossed the highway, he saw a slight figure in a red cloak, a large dog running ahead. He quickened his pace. Madame Olenska stopped short, smiling. “Ah, you’ve come!” she said, drawing her hand from her muff.

The red cloak made her glow—vivid, alive—as if she were the Ellen Mingott of old. Archer laughed, taking her hand. “I came to see what you were running from.”

Her smile faded. “Ah, well—you’ll see soon enough.”

He frowned. “Do you mean someone’s been chasing you?”

She shrugged—just like Nastasia used to—and replied lightly, “Shall we walk? I’m freezing after the sermon. And what does it matter now you’re here to protect me?”

Heat surged through Archer’s face. He gripped the fold of her cloak. “Ellen—what is it? You must tell me.”

“Oh, later—let’s race first! My feet are frozen to the ground,” she said, laughing, and darted across the snow, the dog barking at her heels.

For a moment, Archer watched—enchanted by the flash of red against the white. Then he followed, catching up at a wicket leading into the park. They met, breathless and laughing, beneath the bare trees.

“You knew I’d come,” he said.

“That shows you wanted me to,” she replied, her voice bright with mischief.

The trees sparkled under the weak winter sun, their branches glittering like crystal. As they walked, the snow seemed to sing beneath their feet.

“Where did you come from?” she asked.

He told her. “I got your note.”

After a pause, she said, her tone slightly cool, “May asked you to take care of me.”

“I didn’t need asking.”

“You mean—I’m so helpless? What a poor thing you all must think me! But women here seem not to feel the need—any more than the blessed in heaven.”

He lowered his voice. “What kind of need?”

“Don’t ask me! I don’t speak your language,” she snapped, almost petulantly.

The words struck him like a blow. He stopped, staring down at her.

“What did I come for, then—if I don’t speak yours?”

“Oh, my friend—” She laid a light hand on his arm. He pleaded earnestly. “Ellen—why won’t you tell me what’s wrong?”

She shrugged again. “Does anything ever happen in heaven?”

They walked on in silence for a while. Then she said, “I will tell you—but where? Where can one be alone in such a house? Every door is open, servants bring tea, logs, newspapers—always watching. It feels like a convent—or a stage, before an audience that never applauds.”

“You don’t like us,” Archer said.

They passed the old Patroon’s house—its squat walls, square windows clustered around a central chimney. The shutters stood wide. Through a freshly washed pane, Archer saw the glow of a fire.

“The house is open!” he said.

“No—only today,” she replied. “I wanted to see it. Mr. van der Luyden lit the fire and opened the windows so we could stop here on the way back from church.” She ran up the steps and tried the door. “It’s still unlocked—how lucky! Come in. We can talk quietly. Mrs. van der Luyden has gone to Rhinebeck to visit her aunts. We won’t be missed for another hour.”

He followed her into the narrow passage. His spirits, dampened by her earlier words, lifted suddenly. The little house seemed to glow with warmth—the panels polished, brass fittings gleaming in the firelight. A bed of embers still glowed beneath an iron pot hanging from an ancient crane. Rush-bottom chairs faced each other across the tiled hearth, and rows of Delft plates lined the shelves.

Archer added a log to the fire. Madame Olenska dropped her cloak and sat. He leaned against the chimney, watching her.

“You’re smiling now,” he said. “But when you wrote, you were unhappy.”

“Yes.” She paused. “But I can’t feel unhappy when you’re here.”

“I won’t be long,” he said, his lips tightening. “I have to go back.”

“No,” she said softly. “But I’m imprudent—I live in the moment when I’m happy.”

The words stirred something deep inside him—a temptation he fought to ignore. He turned away, gazing out at the black tree trunks against the snow. Yet even as he looked, he still saw her—between himself and the trees—leaning over the fire, smiling with languid grace.

His heart pounded. What if she had fled *from* him? What if she had waited until they were here, alone, to tell him so?

“Ellen,” he said, without turning, “if I truly help you—if you really wanted me to come—then tell me what’s wrong. Tell me what you’re running from.”

He stood there, eyes fixed on the snow beyond the window, the space between them vast and charged. For a long moment, silence. And in that silence, he imagined her stepping behind him, arms slipping around his neck.

Then, through the window, he saw a man approaching along the path—Julius Beaufort.

“Ah—!” Archer burst out, laughing.

Madame Olenska had risen and slipped her hand into his. But when she glanced at the window, her face paled. She stepped back.

“So that was it?” Archer said, mockingly.

“I didn’t know he was coming,” she whispered, her fingers still clutching his.

He pulled away, walked into the passage, and threw open the door. “Hallo, Beaufort—this way! Madame Olenska was expecting you.”

Beaufort entered with his usual ease, though a line of tension marked his brow. He couldn’t hide his annoyance. The ostensible reason for his visit was a “perfect little house”—not on the market—that he claimed was exactly right for her. If she didn’t take it immediately, it would vanish. He railed mockingly at her for running off just when he’d found it.

“If only this new wire-talk had been a bit closer to perfection,” he grumbled, “I could’ve told you from town and been warming my toes by the club fire instead of tramping through the snow.”

At this, Madame Olenska shifted the conversation to the fantastical idea of talking from street to street, or even town to town—dreams that brought out references to Poe and Verne, and platitudes about progress. The talk carried them safely back to the main house.

Mrs. van der Luyden hadn’t returned yet. Archer took his leave and walked to fetch the cutter. Beaufort followed the Countess indoors.

It was unlikely he’d be invited to stay overnight—too much luggage, too little cordiality. But he had come a long way for a simple dinner, and that said everything. Beaufort was pursuing her. And Beaufort pursued women only for one reason.

His life at home was dull, childless, stagnant. He craved excitement, especially among the elite. This was the man from whom Madame Olenska was fleeing. But why? Was it because she disliked him—or because she feared herself? Or was it all a ruse?

Archer didn’t believe it was a trick. Though he’d seen little of her, he thought he could read her—her face, her voice. Both betrayed dismay at Beaufort’s arrival.

Yet if she had fled *because* of him, wasn’t that worse than if she’d gone to meet him willingly? If she’d sought him out, she’d become entangled with a man whose reputation was irredeemable. But if she fled *from* him, yet still felt drawn—drawn by his worldliness, his connections, his contempt for convention—then she was caught in a far more dangerous trap.

Beaufort was vulgar, uneducated, proud of his wealth. But he had lived in two worlds, moved among artists and actors, spoken freely of things others avoided. He understood her language better than anyone else could. And that, perhaps, was what frightened her most.

That night, Archer unpacked books from London. Among them was a slim volume of poetry—*The House of Life*. He opened it and was swept into a world unlike any he’d known—warm, rich, tender. All night, he read, imagining a woman with Ellen’s face. When he woke, the city outside looked cold and ordinary. His desk, his office, his pew in Grace Church—all seemed miles away.

“Mercy, how pale you look, Newland!” Janey said at breakfast.

“And you’ve been coughing,” his mother added. “Are you overworking?”

He smiled, letting them believe the worst.

The next few days dragged. Everything tasted flat. He felt buried alive. No word from Ellen. No news of the perfect little house. He saw Beaufort at the club—they nodded, nothing more.

Then, on the fourth evening, he found a note on his table:

Only those words. He tucked it away, amused by the Frenchness of “to you.” That night, after dinner and a play, he returned home past midnight and read it again—slowly, over and over.

There were many ways to reply. But by morning, he had decided. He packed a few clothes into a suitcase and boarded a boat bound for St. Augustine—leaving that very afternoon.
