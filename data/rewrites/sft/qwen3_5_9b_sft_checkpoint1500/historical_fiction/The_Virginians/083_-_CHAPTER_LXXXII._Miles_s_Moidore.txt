Our little Prince arrived in this world scarcely days after the gracious monarch who commanded the regiment. Great illuminations and thundering cannons saluted the birth of the Royal George, allowing multitudes to gaze upon him as he lay behind gilded railings at the Palace, tended by noble nurses. Yet, few guardians watched over the cradle of our own small heir. No courtiers, no faithful retainers offered their salutes, save for our trusty Gumbo and kind Molly, who, it must be said, loved and adored the little descendant of my poverty with a loyalty that warmed our hearts.

Why did we not name our boy George, like the paragon above and like his father? I chose instead the name of a minor scamp within my own lineage—a title many generations of the Warringtons had worn. But the love and kindness displayed by this tiny stranger moved me deeply at a time when affection from my own blood felt scarce. Thus, Theo and I resolved that our child should bear the name of this single, bright friend of my paternal race.

We penned letters to inform our royal parents of this auspicious event, and boldly announced his birth in the *Daily Advertiser*, specifying our humble abode at Church Street, Lambeth. "My dear," wrote Aunt Bernstein in reply to my proclamation, "how could you point out to the entire world that you dwell in such a thoroughfare as that wherein you have hidden yourself? I kiss the little mamma, and send a token for the child." This token proved to be a magnificent silk coverlet, edged with lace fit for a prince. It held little practical value—the cost of the lace alone would have served us far better—but Theo and Molly were utterly delighted. Consequently, my eldest son's cradle was draped in a covering finer than any nobleman's.

Dr. Heberden visited my wife repeatedly, ensuring everything proceeded smoothly. He had recommended a local surgeon who attended to her needs; fortunately, my dear patient required little more than the attentive care afforded by our landlady and her loyal attendant. Soon, our modest precinct was once again graced by the gilded wheels of Lady Castlewood's chariot. She delivered a pot of jelly, which she believed Theo might relish, undoubtedly having been served at one of her ladyship's lavish banquets on a prior day. She regaled us with tales of the court ceremonies and the splendor surrounding the newborn heir to the crown.

Just then, our good Mr. Johnson paid us a call on a day when the Countess's carriage blazed with magnificence near our gate. He was somewhat taken aback by her grandeur and offered her bows—more respectful than graceful. She addressed me as "Cousin" with affable warmth and assisted in transferring her gift of jelly from a silver dish to our crockery pan with immense benevolence. The Doctor sampled the sweetmeat and pronounced it excellent. "The great, sir," he remarked, "are fortunate in every respect. They can employ the most skilled artisans of culinary art and gather the most amiable wits around their tables. If, as you suggest, sir—and your judgment based on the dish seems plausible—this confection has already graced His Lordship's table, it has done so in worthy company. It has trembled beneath the gaze of celebrated beauties, been tasted by ruby lips, and shared the attention of a distinguished assembly amidst fruits, tarts, and creams, which I am certain were equally delicious." With these words, the kindly Doctor consumed a significant portion of Lady Castlewood's bounty. However, regarding the epithet "delicious," I am compelled to note that my poor wife, after sampling the jelly, set it aside as unpalatable, and Molly, throwing up her head, declared it to be moldy.

Our boy, however, enjoyed the distinct privilege of having an Earl's daughter for his godmother. This honor was bestowed by his cousin, our late Lady Maria, whose kindness and devotion to both mother and infant exceeded all praise. Having lost her own solitary chance at maternal happiness, she doted on our child in a manner profoundly touching to witness. Captain Miles was indeed a handsome gentleman, his uniforms of the Prince's Hussars as splendid as any that ever adorned a soldier of fashion. Yet, possessing a heart too generous and being a true gentleman, surely he would remain grateful when he recalled that his own infant limbs had been clad in some of the garments prepared for the pauper's child. Sampson christened him in the very chapel at Southwark where our wedding rites had been performed. Never had the words of the Prayer-Book been read with greater beauty and impressiveness than by the celebrant of that service; save at its conclusion, when his voice faltered, leaving him and the small congregation wiping away tears. "Mr. Garrick himself, sir," observed Hagan, "could not have read those words so nobly. I am certain no innocent soul ever entered this world accompanied by wishes and blessings more tender and sincere."

Now, I must recount how the Captain acquired his name of Miles. A couple of days prior to the christening, when it was still intended that our firstborn should bear his father's name, a rhythmic patter of horsehooves echoed up to our gate. Who should pull the bell but young Miles, our cousin? I suspect he had disobeyed his parents on this undutiful journey.

"You know," he said, "Cousin Harry gave me my little horse; and I cannot help liking you, because you are so like Harry, and because they always speak of you at home, and it is a shame! And I have brought my whistle and coral beads, given to me by my godmamma Lady Suckling, for your little boy. And if you are so poor, Cousin George, here is my gold moidore. It is worth ever so much, and it is no use to me, because I may not spend it, you know."

We lifted the boy up to Theo in her chamber (he climbed the stairs proudly in his little tramping boots). Theo kissed him and expressed her thanks, and since that day, his moidore has rested safely in her purse.

Later, my mother, writing through her usual ambassador, informed me of her royal surprise and displeasure upon learning that my son had been christened Miles—a name unknown, at least within the Esmond family. I hesitated to reveal the reason at the time. However, when I eventually told Madam Esmond how my boy came by his name, I saw a tear roll down her wrinkled cheek. I learned subsequently that she had questioned Gumbo extensively about the boy who gave his name to our Miles—the Miles Gloriosus of Pall Mall, Valenciennes, and Almack's.
