At the luncheon table, Mr. Welland paused mid-bite, his eyes locking onto his wife's as she read aloud from the invitation. Her tone was laced with amusement, and the words danced on the page like a jest. "Professor and Mrs. Emerson Sillerton request the pleasure of Mr. and Mrs. Welland's company at the Wednesday Afternoon Club on August 25th at 3 o'clock punctually. To meet Mrs. and the Misses Blenker. Red Gables, Catherine Street. R. S. V. P."

A look of incredulity crossed Mr. Welland's face, as if the words themselves had become a tangible obstacle. "Good heavens," he murmured, the phrase hanging in the air like a challenge. His wife's expression softened in commiseration. "Poor Amy Sillerton – you never know what her husband will do next," she said, shaking her head.

Professor Emerson Sillerton was a man whose eccentricities had become the stuff of Newport society legend. A member of the venerable and respected Sillerton-Jackson clan, he had every advantage – a wealthy family, influential connections, and a name that commanded respect. And yet, he had chosen to defy convention, becoming an archaeologist and a professor, and settling in Newport during the winter months. It was a choice that left many scratching their heads, particularly given his marriage to Amy Dagonet, a woman who had expected a life of luxury and refinement.

As Mrs. Welland spoke, her words wove a subtle critique of the Sillertons' unconventional lifestyle. "He's a man who has had every opportunity to follow a traditional path, but instead, he's chosen to surround himself with long-haired men and short-haired women, and takes his wife on expeditions to explore ancient ruins rather than more conventional destinations." Her tone was measured, yet tinged with a hint of disapproval.

The mention of the Blenkers brought a collective groan from the table. Their annual garden party was always a chore, a social obligation that the families on the Cliffs undertook with reluctance. This year, however, seemed like a particularly cruel fate, especially since there was no Cup Race to distract them.
