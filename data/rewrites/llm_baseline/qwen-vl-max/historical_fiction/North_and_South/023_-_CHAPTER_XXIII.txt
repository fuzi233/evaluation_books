“Which when his mother saw, she in her mind
Was troubled sore, he wist well what to ween.”

Margaret had not been gone more than five minutes when Mr. Thornton entered the dining room, his face flushed and agitated. “I couldn’t come sooner—the superintendent held me up—Where is she?” He scanned the room, then turned sharply toward his mother, who was quietly restoring order to the furniture, her movements deliberate but unhurried.

“Where is Miss Hale?” he asked again, his voice tight with urgency.

“Gone home,” she replied, a little curtly.

“Gone home!” he echoed, as if the idea were absurd.

“Yes. She was much better. I don’t think it was anything serious—just a fainting fit, nothing more. Some people are so sensitive.”

He paced restlessly. “I’m sorry she left. She couldn’t have been strong enough for it.”

“She said she was. And Mr. Lowe confirmed it. I sent for him myself.”

“Thank you, mother.” He paused, half-extending his hand as if to offer a grateful squeeze. But she didn’t notice.

“What did you do about the Irish workers?” he asked.

“I sent them to the Dragon Inn for a good meal—poor souls. Then I managed to catch Father Grady just in time. I’ve invited him to speak to them, to keep them from all leaving at once.”

“How did Miss Hale get home? She certainly couldn’t walk.”

“She took a cab. Everything was arranged properly—even paid for. Let’s talk of something else. She’s caused enough trouble already.”

“I don’t know where I’d be without her,” he said, low and firm.

“You’re becoming so helpless that you need a girl to defend you?” Mrs. Thornton retorted, her tone edged with disdain.

He flushed. “Not many girls would have taken blows meant for me—blows thrown with real goodwill, too.”

“A girl in love will go far,” she said shortly.

“Mother!” He stepped forward, stopped himself, and fought to contain his rising emotion. She felt the tension in his stance—the way his breath came fast, his eyes gleamed, his body tensed. It unsettled her. She wasn’t sure what stirred in him—anger? pride? joy?—but its force was undeniable. Strong feelings, especially when unshared, made her uneasy.

She walked to the sideboard, opened a drawer, and pulled out a duster. There was a smudge of eau de Cologne on the sofa arm. She began to wipe it away, though she lingered longer than necessary, keeping her back turned.

“You’ve taken measures about the rioters, I suppose? No more violence expected? Where were the police? Always absent when needed!”

“On the contrary,” he said. “I saw three or four when the gates gave way—battling and pushing like madmen. More arrived just as the yard cleared. I could have arrested some then, if I’d been quicker. But there’ll be no problem—plenty of witnesses.”

“But won’t they return tonight?”

“I’m going to arrange a guard. I’ve set a meeting with Captain Hanbury in half an hour at the station.”

“You must have tea first.”

“Tea? Yes, I suppose so. It’s half-past six. I may be out for a while. Don of sitting up for me, mother.”

“You expect me to go to bed before I see you safe?” she countered.

“Well… perhaps not.” He hesitated. “But if I have time, I’ll stop by Crampton after seeing the police and speaking with Hamper and Clarkson.”

Their eyes met. They stared at each other, silent, intense. Then she asked, “Why are you going to Crampton?”

“To check on Miss Hale.”

“I’ll send Williams. He’ll deliver the water-bed she requested. He can ask how she is.”

“I must go myself.”

“Not just to inquire about her?”

“No. To thank her—for standing between me and the mob.”

“What made you go down there at all? You were putting your head into a lion’s mouth!”

He glanced at her sharply, realizing she didn’t know what had passed between him and Margaret in the drawing-room. Instead of explaining, he asked: “Will you be afraid to stay alone until I can secure the police? Or shall we send Williams now? They could be here by the time we finish tea. Time is short—I must leave in fifteen minutes.”

Mrs. Thornton left the room. Her servants noticed her orders were unusually hesitant, uncertain. Mr. Thornton remained, pretending to focus on business matters at the police station—but in truth, his mind was elsewhere. The world beyond seemed blurred, distant. Only one image burned clear: the feel of her arms around his neck, the softness of her grip, the flush of color in her cheeks as he recalled it. He could not forget.

Tea passed in silence, broken only by Fanny’s endless recounting of her own fright—how she’d trembled, fainted, felt sick, nearly swooned.

“There—that’s enough,” said her brother, rising abruptly. “The reality was enough for me.”

As he turned to leave, his mother caught his arm.

“You’ll come back here before going to the Hales’,” she said, her voice low, strained.

“I know what I know,” Fanny muttered to herself. “Why? Will it be too late to disturb them?”

“John,” she said, “come back to me this evening. It will be late for Mrs. Hale. But that’s not why. Tomorrow… you will—Come back tonight, John!”

It was rare for her to plead—too proud for such things. Yet she had never failed. “I’ll return straight here after my errands. You’ll make sure to ask after them—after her?”

Mrs. Thornton was not usually a companionable woman, nor a good listener when her son was away. But upon his return, she listened closely, her eyes sharp, eager for every detail—what steps he’d taken, whom he’d trusted, how he’d secured the premises.

He spoke plainly. Punishment was due. Those who had rioted must suffer. That was justice. That was protection. That was the will of the master—clean, decisive, like a blade.

“Mother,” he said suddenly, “you know what I have to say to Miss Hale tomorrow?”

The question startled her. She had forgotten Margaret.

“Yes,” she said slowly. “I do. You can hardly do otherwise.”

“Do otherwise? I don’t understand.”

“I mean,” she continued, “that after allowing her emotions to overcome her, you are bound in honour—”

“Bound in honour?” he scoffed. “Honour has nothing to do with it. ‘Her feelings overcame her?’ What feelings?”

“Nay, John, no need to be angry. Did she not rush down and cling to you to save you?”

“She did,” he said, quiet now. “But mother…” He stopped, stepping directly before her. “I dare not hope. I’ve never been weak before—but I cannot believe such a woman cares for me.”

“Don’t be foolish, John. Such a woman? She might be a duke’s daughter, the way you speak. And what proof would you want of her care? I believe she struggled against her own class pride—yet she saw clearly at last. It’s much for me to admit,” she said, smiling slowly, tears glistening in her eyes, “for after tonight, I stand second. I wanted you to myself—just a few hours longer—that’s why I begged you not to go till tomorrow.”

“Dearest mother!” he whispered.

(Still, love is selfish—and instantly, he reverted to his own fears, his hopes, his longing.)

“But I know she doesn’t care for me. I’ll put myself at her feet—I must. If there’s even one chance in a million, I’ll do it.”

“Don’t fear,” she said, crushing down her own wounded pride, the sting of jealousy that betrayed how deeply she loved him. “Don’t be afraid. As far as love goes, she may be worthy of you. It must have taken much to overcome her pride. Don’t fear, John,” she added, kissing him gently as she bid him goodnight.

And she left the room, slowly, with dignity.

But once inside her own chamber, she locked the door and sank into a chair, weeping—tears she had never known before.

* * *

Margaret entered the room where her parents sat in quiet conversation. She looked pale, drained. She approached them slowly, unable at first to speak.

“Mrs. Thornton will send the water-bed, mamma.”

“Dear, how tired you look! Is it very hot, Margaret?”

“Very hot. And the streets are rough with the strike.”

A flicker of colour returned to her cheeks—bright, vivid—but vanished just as quickly.

“There’s been a message from Bessy Higgins,” said Mrs. Hale. “She wants you to visit her. But you look too worn.”

“Yes,” Margaret said. “I am tired. I cannot go.”

She was silent, trembling, as she prepared tea. She was relieved her father was occupied with her mother, so he wouldn’t notice her condition. Even after her mother retired, he stayed close, reading aloud until she fell asleep.

Then she was alone.

Now I will think of it—now I will remember it all. I could not before—I dared not.

She sat rigid in her chair, hands clasped, lips pressed tight, eyes fixed as if seeing a vision.

She drew a deep breath.

“I, who hate scenes—I, who have mocked others for showing emotion—who have called them weak—went down and threw myself into the chaos like a romantic fool! Did I do any good? Perhaps they would have left anyway.”

But her judgment corrected itself instantly.

“No—perhaps not. I did some good. But what possessed me to defend that man as if he were a child? Ah!” she exclaimed, clenching her fists. “No wonder they thought I was in love with him—after disgracing myself so. Me—in love—with him?”

Her cheeks flamed red. She covered her face with her hands.

When she uncovered them, her palms were wet with hot tears.

“Oh, how low I’ve fallen—so they should say that of me! I could not have been so brave for anyone else—because he is so utterly indifferent to me—if not positively hateful. That made me all the more determined to ensure fairness. And I could see what fair play was. It was not fair,” she said fiercely, “that he should stand there—safe—waiting for soldiers to trap those poor, frantic men—without trying to reason with them. And it was worse than unfair for them to attack him as they threatened. I would do it again—let them say what they like. If I prevented one blow, one cruel act that might have happened, I did a woman’s work. Let them insult my pride—my maiden pride—as they will—I walk pure before God!”

She raised her eyes. A calm, noble peace settled over her face—still as carved marble.

Dixon entered. “If you please, Miss Margaret, here’s the water-bed from Mrs. Thornton’s. Too late for tonight, I’m afraid—missus is nearly asleep—but it’ll do nicely for tomorrow.”

“Very well,” Margaret said. “Please send our best thanks.”

Dixon paused. “He says he’s to ask particularly how you are. I think he means missus—but he insists his last words were, ‘Ask how Miss Hale is.’”

“Me?” Margaret said, straightening. “I am quite well. Tell him I am perfectly well.”

But her face was as white as her handkerchief. Her head throbbed with pain.

Mr. Hale came in. He had left his sleeping wife and wanted, as Margaret saw, to be cheered by her presence. With gentle patience, she endured her pain, saying nothing, weaving small talk—everything except the riot. She never mentioned it once.

It turned her sick just to think of it.

“Goodnight, Margaret. I have every hope of a peaceful night myself. You look very pale from your watch. I’ll call Dixon if your mother needs anything. Go to bed and sleep soundly—you need it, poor child.”

“Goodnight, papa.”

She let her colour fade, the false smile slip, her eyes grow dull with pain. She released her will, exhausted.

Till morning, she might feel ill, weary. She lay down and did not stir.

To move a finger would have been impossible—no strength, no will. She was so drained, so stunned, that she thought she never slept at all. Her thoughts raced between waking and dreaming, clinging to their own torment.

She could not be alone—prostrate, powerless. Faces crowded around her—not angry, not threatening—but watching. And with that gaze came shame—deep, burning shame—that she should be the center of attention, the object of such regard.

It was so acute, she longed to bury herself in the earth. Yet she could not escape the unblinking stare of many eyes.
