Angels, in some brighter dreams, call to the soul when man doth sleep; so strange thoughts transcend our wonted themes and into glory peep.

Mrs. Hale watched Margaret with a mixture of amusement and profound interest regarding the impending dinner at Mr. Thornton's. She pondered the details with the innocent curiosity of a child anticipating a feast, wishing to have every pleasure described beforehand. The monotonous confinement of illness often shrinks the world until the walls seem vast and the outside world nonexistent, yet it also restores a childish sense of wonder. Mrs. Hale, once a vain girl whose ambitions had been smothered by poverty and the humbling reality of being a clergyman's wife, found a strange joy in imagining her daughter dressed for society. It was a delightful vanity, one she had long suppressed, to see Margaret discuss her attire with such animated anxiety—a contrast to Mrs. Hale's own twenty-five years of Helstone, where Margaret had spent merely one year in Harley Street.

"Then you think you shall wear your white silk?" Mrs. Hale asked, her voice tinged with nervousness. "Are you sure it will fit? It's nearly a year since Edith was married!"

"Oh, yes, mamma! Mrs. Murray made it, and it's sure to be right," Margaret replied cheerfully. "It may be a straw's breadth shorter or longer-waisted, depending on whether I've grown fat or thin. But I don't think I've altered in the least."

"Perhaps you had better let Dixon see it," her mother suggested hesitantly. "It may have gone yellow with lying by."

"If you like, mamma. But if the worst comes to the worst, I have a very nice pink gauze which Aunt Shaw gave me only two or three months before Edith was married. That couldn't have gone yellow."

"No! but it may have faded."

"Well! then I have a green silk. I feel as though I have the embarrassment of riches."

"I wish I knew what you ought to wear," Mrs. Hale said, her composure fraying.

Margaret's demeanor shifted instantly from playful to practical. "Shall I go and try them on one after another, mamma, so you can see which you like best?"

"But—yes! perhaps that will be best."

Margaret retreated to her room. She felt a sudden impulse to play pranks, to make her rich white silk balloon out into a cheese or to retreat backward from her mother as if she were a queen. However, sensing that such antics were interruptions to her mother's serious preparations, she became grave and sedate. She could not understand why the world should fuss so over her dress, yet later that afternoon, when she mentioned her engagement to Bessy Higgins, the servant girl's reaction was immediate and fierce.

"Dear! And are you going to dine at Thornton's at Marlborough Mills?" Bessy exclaimed.

"Yes, Bessy. Why are you so surprised?"

"Oh, I dunno. But they visit with all the first folk in Milton."

"And you don't think we're quite the first folk in Milton, eh, Bessy?" Margaret teased gently.

Bessy's cheeks flushed slightly at the easy reading of her thoughts. "Well, you see, they think a deal of money here. And I reckon you haven't got much."

"No," Margaret admitted simply. "That's very true. But we are educated people, and have lived amongst educated people. Is there anything so wonderful in our being asked out to dinner by a man who owns himself inferior to my father, coming to him to be instructed? I don't mean to blame Mr. Thornton. Few drapers' assistants, as he was once, could have made themselves what he is."

"But can you give dinners back in your small house? Thornton's house is three times as big."

"Well, I think we could manage to give Mr. Thornton a dinner back, as you call it. Perhaps not in such a large room, nor with so many people. But I don't think we've thought about it at all in that way."

"I never thought you'd be dining with the Thorntons," Bessy repeated, her voice dropping. "Why, the mayor himself dines there, and the members of Parliament and all."

"I think I could support the honour of meeting the mayor of Milton."

"But them ladies dress so grand!" Bessy said, casting an anxious glance at Margaret's print gown, which her Milton eyes appraised at sevenpence a yard.

Margaret's face dimpled into a merry laugh. "Thank you, Bessy, for thinking so kindly about my looking nice among all the smart people. But I have plenty of grand gowns—a week ago, I should have said they were far too grand for anything I should ever want here. But as I'm to dine at Mr. Thornton's, and perhaps meet the mayor, I shall put on my very best gown, you may be sure."

"What will you wear?" Bessy asked, somewhat relieved.

"White silk," Margaret said. "A gown I had for a cousin's wedding a year ago."

"That'll do!" Bessy declared, sinking back into her chair. "I should be loath to have you looked down upon."

"Oh! I'll be fine enough, if that will save me from being looked down upon in Milton."

"I wish I could see you dressed up," Bessy murmured. "I reckon you're not what folk would call pretty; you've not red and white enough for that. But do you know, I have dreamt of you, long before ever I saw you."

"Nonsense, Bessy!"

"Ay, but I did. Your very face—looking with your clear steadfast eyes out of the darkness, with your hair blown off from your brow, and going out like rays round your forehead, which was just as smooth and straight as it is now—and you always came to give me strength, which I seemed to gather out of your deep comforting eyes—and you were dressed in shining raiment—just as you're going to be dressed. So, you see, it was you!"

"Nay, Bessy," Margaret said gently, "it was but a dream."

"And why might na I dream a dream in my affliction as well as others? Did not many a one in the Bible? Ay, and see visions too! Why, even my father thinks a deal of dreams! I tell you again, I saw you as plainly, coming swiftly towards me, with your hair blown back with the very swiftness of the motion, just like the way it grows, a little standing off like; and the white shining dress on you've gotten to wear. Let me come and see you in it. I want to see you and touch you as in very deed you were in my dream."

"My dear Bessy, it is quite a fancy of yours."

"Fancy or no fancy—you've come, as I knew you would, when I saw your movement in my dream—and when you're here about me, I reckon I feel easier in my mind, and comforted, just as a fire comforts one on a dreary day. You said it were on the twenty-first; please God, I'll come and see you."

"Oh Bessy! You may come and welcome; but don't talk so—it really makes me sorry. It does indeed."

"Then I'll keep it to myself, if I bite my tongue out. Not but what it's true for all that."

Margaret fell silent. Finally, she asked, "Let us talk about it sometimes, if you think it true. But not now. Tell me, has your father turned out?"

"Ay!" Bessy said heavily, her tone shifting from the previous levity. "He and many another—all Hamper's men—and many a one besides. The women are as bad as the men in their savagery this time. Food is high—and they must have food for their children, I reckon. Suppose the Thorntons sent them their dinner out—the same money, spent on potatoes and meal, would keep many a crying baby quiet, and hush up its mother's heart for a bit!"

"Don't speak so!" Margaret cried. "You'll make me feel wicked and guilty in going to this dinner."

"No!" Bessy insisted. "Some are pre-electured to sumptuous feasts and purple and fine linen—maybe you're one of them. Others toil and moil all their lives long—and the very dogs are not pitiful in our days, as they were in the days of Lazarus. But if you ask me to cool your tongue with the tip of my finger, I'll come across the great gulf to you just for the thought of what you've been to me here."

"Bessy, you're very feverish! I can tell it in the touch of your hand, as well as in what you're saying. It won't be division enough, in that awful day, that some of us have been beggars here, and some of us have been rich—we shall not be judged by that poor accident, but by our faithful following of Christ."

Margaret rose, fetched water, soaked her pocket-handkerchief, and laid the cool wetness on Bessy's forehead, beginning to chafe the stone-cold feet. Bessy shut her eyes, allowing herself to be soothed. At last she whispered, "You'd have been deaved out of your five wits, as well as me, if you'd had one body after another coming in to ask for father, and staying to tell me each one their tale. Some spoke of deadly hatred, and made my blood run cold with the terrible things they said of the masters—but more, being women, kept plaining, plaining (with the tears running down their cheeks, and never wiped away, nor heeded), of the price of meat, and how their children could not sleep at nights for the hunger."

"And do they think the strike will mend this?" Margaret asked softly.

"They say so," Bessy replied. "They do say trade has been good for long, and the masters have made no end of money; how much father doesn't know, but, in course, the Union does; and, as it is natural, they wanted their share of the profits, now that food is getting dear; and the Union says they'll not be doing their duty if they don't make the masters give 'em their share. But the masters have gotten the upper hand somehow; and I'm feared they'll keep it now and evermore. It's like the great battle of Armageddon, the way they keep on, grinning and fighting at each other, till even while they fight, they are picked off into the pit."

Just then, Nicholas Higgins entered. He caught his daughter's last words.

"Ay! and I'll fight on too; and I'll get it this time. It'll not take long for them to make 'em give in, for they've gotten a pretty lot of orders, all under contract; and they'll soon find out they'd better give us our five per cent. than lose the profit they'll gain; let alone the fine for not fulfilling the contract. Aha my masters! I know who'll win."

Margaret fancied, from his manner, that he must have been drinking, not so much from what he said as from the excited way in which he spoke. She was confirmed in this idea by the evident anxiety Bessy showed to hasten her departure. Bessy said to her, "The twenty-first—that's Thursday week. I may come and see you dressed for Thornton's, I reckon. What time is your dinner?"

Before Margaret could answer, Higgins broke out, "Thornton's! Are you going to dine at Thornton's? Ask him to give you a bumper to the success of his orders. By the twenty-first, I reckon, he'll be pottering in his brains how to get 'em done in time. Tell him, there's seven hundred'll come marching into Marlborough Mills the morning after he gives the five per cent., and will help him through his contract in no time. You'll have 'em all there. My master, Hamper. He's one of the old-fashioned sorts. Ne'er meets a man bout an oath or a curse; I should think he were going to die if he spoke me civil; but after all, his bark's worse than his bite, and you may tell him one of his turn-outs said so, if you like. Eh! but you'll have a lot of prize mill-owners at Thornton's! I should like to get speech of them, when they're a bit inclined to sit still after dinner, and could na run for the life on 'em. I'd tell 'em my mind. I'd speak up again the hard way they're driving on us!"

"Good-bye!" Margaret said hastily. "Good-bye, Bessy! I shall look to see you on the twenty-first, if you're well enough."

The medicines and treatment which Dr. Donaldson had ordered for Mrs. Hale did her so much good at first that not only she herself, but Margaret, began to hope that he might have been mistaken, and that she could recover permanently. As for Mr. Hale, although he had never had an idea of the serious nature of their apprehensions, he triumphed over their fears with an evident relief, which proved how much his glimpse into the nature of them had affected him. Only Dixon croaked forever into Margaret's ear. However, Margaret defied the raven, and would hope.

They needed this gleam of brightness indoors, for outdoors, even to their uninstructed eyes, there was a gloomy brooding appearance of discontent. Mr. Hale had his own acquaintances among the working men, and was depressed with their earnestly-told tales of suffering and long-endurance. They would have scorned to speak of what they had to bear to anyone who might, from his position, have understood it without their words. But here was this man, from a distant county, who was perplexed by the workings of a system into the midst of which he was thrown, and each was eager to make him a judge, and to bring witness of his own causes for irritation.
