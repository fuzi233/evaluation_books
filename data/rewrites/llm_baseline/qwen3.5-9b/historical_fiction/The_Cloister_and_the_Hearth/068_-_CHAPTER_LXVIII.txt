A woman bears her own burdens, much as a man carries his, yet we male authors often fail to articulate the specific sorrows of the fairer sex. We sketch the outlines, leaving the flesh and blood to the imagination of our female readers, who must supply the missing details. Thus, I shall merely hint at the trials Margaret Brandt endured until that fateful day when she stepped into Eli's house as an adversary, discovered her lover's letter, and emerged as a friend. Now, however, approached the greatest test of a woman's life, while Gerard remained distant.

She made scant use of Eli's newfound kindness toward her. For this reserve, she always possessed a plausible excuse, though she never revealed the true cause: within that household dwelt two men whose mere presence instilled in her an instinctive dread. In their countenances, she had read wickedness and hatred, along with the silent signals of clandestine plotting. Consequently, she preferred receiving Catherine and her daughter within her own humble dwelling. Catherine visited daily, consumed by anticipation of the coming birth. Catherine belonged to that order of women whose sole purpose seems to be multiplication—those who, once they cease expelling infants from every corner of the house, hover protectively around the "fair scourges" still in full bloom, clucking and fussing as if multiplying by proxy.

It was in this spirit that she implored Eli to remain in Rotterdam while she returned to Tergou. "That poor lass has no soul about her who understands a thing," she argued. "What good are a pair of soldiers? Such cattle ought to be cast out at the first sign of trouble." One need not add that this reassurance brought Margaret immense relief. Poor soul, she was filled with anxiety as the appointed hour neared. She feared death, and she mourned Gerard's absence. Yet, fate balances its scales. Her poverty and her father's frailty, struggles that had nearly broken her before, now served her well. Adversity's iron grip had compelled her to fight against the lethargy that often overtakes wealthy women, forcing her to stand constantly upon her feet, laboring without ceasing. She maintained this vigilance until the end, following Catherine's counsel.

So it came to pass that one beautiful evening, just as the sun dipped below the horizon, she lay weak as water, yet safe. By her side rested a tiny face, and the vast, tender sky of maternity opened above her.

"Why dost thou weep, sweetheart? All of a sudden?"

"He is not here to witness it."

"Ay, well, lass. He will be here ere he is weaned. Meanwhile, God has been as kind to thee as to any woman born. Consider how it might have been a girl; did not my very own Kate threaten me with one? And here we have secured the bonniest boy in all Holland, a sturdy lad indeed. Praise the saints for it!"

"Yes, mother. I am but a sorry, ungrateful wretch to weep. If only Gerard were here to behold it. It is strange; I bore him well enough to be parted from me in my sorrow, yet oh, it feels so hard that he should not share my joy. Pray, pray, come to me, Gerard! Dear, dear Gerard!"

She stretched forth her feeble arms. Catherine bustled about, deliberately avoiding Margaret's gaze, unable to suppress her own tears at hearing her own absent child thus passionately invoked. Soon, turning around, she found Margaret regarding her with a peculiar expression.

"Didst hear nothing?"

"No, my lamb. What?"

"I cried out for Gerard, but now..."

"Ay, ay, surely I heard that."

"Well, he answered me."

"Tush, girl; speak not such nonsense."

"Mother, as certain as I lie here with his boy beside me, his voice returned to me: 'Margaret!' Yes. Yet, it seemed not his joyful tone. Perhaps that was due to the distance. All voices grow sad and faint when carried afar. Why art thou not happy, sweetheart? And I am so happy this night? Mother, I seem never to have felt a pain or known a care."

Her sweet eyes turned, glowing with love, fixed silently upon the infant's small face.

That very night, Gerard threw himself into the Tiber. And in that same hour, she heard him speaking her name, crying aloud between the jaws of death and despair.

"Margaret!"

Let those who can account for it explain the mystery. I cannot.
