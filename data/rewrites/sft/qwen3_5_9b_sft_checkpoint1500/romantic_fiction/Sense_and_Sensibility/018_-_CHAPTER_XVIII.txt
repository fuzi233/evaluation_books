A profound disquiet settled over Elinor whenever she observed the heavy gloom that clung to her friend's spirit. While Edward's visit offered a glimmer of solace, it was marred by the evident lack of joy he derived from its presence. To Elinor, it became painfully clear that he was unhappy, though she longed for certainty that his affection for her remained undiminished—a certainty she had once held without hesitation. Yet, his continued preference hovered on uncertain ground, betrayed by a shifting reserve in his demeanor that constantly contradicted the fleeting animation of his gaze.

The following morning, Edward joined Elinor and Marianne in the breakfast room before the rest of the household had risen. As ever, Marianne, driven by a fierce desire to foster happiness around her, quickly excused herself to leave them alone. However, halfway up the stairs, she paused, startled to hear the parlor door swing open behind her. Turning, she found Edward emerging.

"I am off to the village to check on my horses," he explained, "since you are not yet dressed for breakfast. I shall return shortly."

When Edward rejoined them, his spirits had lifted, buoyed by a fresh appreciation for the countryside. His walk to the village had revealed the valley from advantageous vantage points, and the hamlet itself, perched high above the cottage, offered a sweeping panorama that delighted him. This topic naturally captured Marianne's attention. She began to recount her own wonder at the landscapes, probing him gently about the specific features that had struck him, until Edward interjected.

"You must not press your inquiries too far, Marianne," he said gently. "Remember, I possess no knowledge of the picturesque. If we delve into particulars, I shall surely offend you with my ignorance and lack of taste. I shall mistake steep slopes for bold heights, strange surfaces for rugged irregularity, and distant vistas for those obscured by a soft haze. Please accept whatever admiration I can offer in good faith. To me, it is simply a beautiful country—the hills rise sharply, the woods bristle with fine timber, and the valley feels cozy and secure, dotted with neat farmhouses. It suits my definition perfectly because it unites beauty with utility. And I daresay it is picturesque too, merely because you admire it. I can easily imagine it filled with craggy rocks, grey moss, and tangled brushwood, though all these details escape me. I know nothing of the picturesque."

"It seems too true," Marianne admitted softly. "But why boast of it?"

"Perhaps," Elinor observed, "to avoid one form of affectation, Edward has fallen into another. Believing that many people feign a deeper reverence for nature than they truly feel, he disguises his own discernment with an affected indifference. He is fastidious and cultivates a counter-affectation of his own."

"That is quite right," Marianne agreed. "Admiration of landscape has degenerated into mere jargon. Everyone pretends to feel and attempts to describe nature with the elegance of those who first codified 'picturesque' beauty. I detest jargon above all else, and often I keep my feelings to myself because I cannot find a language to express them that isn't worn, hackneyed, and devoid of any real meaning."

"I am convinced," Edward assured her, "that you truly feel every delight a fine prospect offers. In return, I ask that my sister allow me to feel no more than I profess. I enjoy a grand view, but not on the principles of the picturesque. I dislike crooked, twisted, or blasted trees; I prefer them tall, straight, and flourishing. I do not favor ruined, tattered cottages. I am not fond of nettles, thistles, or heather blossoms. A comfortable farmhouse brings me far more pleasure than a watchtower, and a group of orderly, happy villagers pleases me infinitely more than the most refined bandits in the world."

Marianne gazed at Edward with astonishment, looking toward her sister with deep compassion. Elinor, however, only smiled.

They did not pursue the subject further. Marianne sat in thoughtful silence until a new object abruptly seized her attention. Seated beside Edward, she watched as he took his tea from Mrs. Dashwood. In doing so, his hand moved directly across her line of vision, revealing a ring upon one finger. Nestled within the setting was a small, dark lock of hair, conspicuously braided.

"I have never seen you wear a ring before, Edward!" she exclaimed. "Is that Fanny's hair? I recall her promising to send you some. Though I thought her hair was much darker."

Marianne spoke without thinking, voicing exactly what she felt. Immediately, upon seeing the pain her words caused Edward, her regret outweighed her initial impulse. He flushed deeply, casting a fleeting glance at Elinor before replying, "Yes, it is my sister's hair. You know how differently a setting alters the shade."

Elinor had caught his eye and returned the look with equal consciousness. Instantly, she realized the truth: the hair was indeed hers. Where Marianne perceived a generous gift, Elinor knew it must have been obtained through some subtle artifice or theft entirely unknown to herself. Yet, she was not in a mood to consider it an affront. Feigning indifference, she immediately shifted the conversation to other matters, resolving inwardly to seize every future opportunity to examine the hair closely, thereby confirming beyond doubt that it matched her own shade precisely.

Edward's embarrassment lingered throughout the morning, eventually settling into a profound preoccupation. He remained unusually grave all day. Marianne bitterly reproached herself for her ill-advised comment, though her self-forgiveness might have arrived sooner had she understood how little offense her words had actually taken.

Before noon, Sir John and Mrs. Jennings arrived, having heard rumors of a gentleman at the cottage and wishing to inspect their new guest. With the aid of his mother-in-law, Sir John swiftly deduced that the guest's surname began with an "F." This revelation prepared a future wellspring of teasing aimed at the devoted Elinor, though the novelty of their acquaintance with Edward prevented immediate jesting. Instead, they exchanged knowing glances that hinted at the depth of their insight, gleaned largely from Margaret's prior instructions.

As was custom, Sir John never failed to invite the Dashwoods to dine at the Park the following day or to take tea that evening. On this occasion, desiring to entertain his visitor thoroughly, he proposed securing both engagements.

"You *must* drink tea with us tonight," he declared, "for we shall be quite alone. And tomorrow, you must absolutely dine with us, for we shall be hosting a large party."

Mrs. Jennings reinforced the invitation. "And who knows but you may raise a dance?" she suggested. "That will certainly tempt *you*, Miss Marianne."

"A dance!" Marianne cried. "Impossible! Who is to dance?"

"Why, yourselves, the Careys, the Whitakers—" Sir John enumerated. "What! Did you think nobody could dance now because a certain person, let us remain nameless, has departed?"

"I wish with all my soul," Sir John lamented, "that Willoughby were among us again."

These words, coupled with Marianne's sudden flush, ignited new suspicions in Edward's mind. "And who is Willoughby?" he asked quietly, leaning close to Miss Dashwood.

She offered him a brief answer, but Marianne's face was far more expressive. Edward grasped the full significance, decoding not only the implications of the adults' remarks but also the nuances in Marianne's expression that had previously baffled him. Once the visitors departed, he approached her immediately, whispering, "I have been making guesses. Shall I share mine?"

"What do you mean?"

"Shall I?"

"Certainly."

"Well, then, I guess Mr. Willoughby hunts."

Marianne was surprised and flustered, yet she could not suppress a smile at the quiet wit of his tone. After a moment's pause, she said, "Oh, Edward! How can you?—But I hope the time will come... I am sure you will like him."

"Do not doubt it," he replied, somewhat taken aback by the earnestness and warmth of her conviction. For if he had not believed it to be a harmless jest, intended solely for the benefit of her general acquaintance and based on a vague rumor, he would never have dared to mention it.
