The fourth night dissolved into a profound slumber, only to shatter into a terrifying clarity when I opened my eyes. Darkness enveloped the room, yet I knew with absolute certainty that I lay within the House of Death. Every moment since my arrival had been a dream, and in this instant, I was truly awake. "At last!" I whispered to my own heart, feeling it leap with a joyous, electric pulse. I turned my gaze, and there she stood by my couch, waiting. Lona. I had never truly lost her—merely the sight of her for a fleeting span. My grief had been unnecessary.

Though the room was dark, she was not. She burned with the radiance of the Mother, a light issuing from her face and pouring through her death-dress, which now glowed white as fresh snow, tenfold brighter in the power of resurrection. She had fallen asleep as a girl; she awoke as a woman, ripe with the essential loveliness of life itself. I folded her into my arms, and the sensation confirmed my existence: I lived.

"I woke first," she said, her voice trembling with a wondering smile.

"You did, my love, and you woke me," I replied.

"I only looked at you and waited," she answered softly.

A candle drifted through the gloom, carried by unseen hands, until Adam, Eve, and Mara appeared. They greeted us with a quiet, familiar good morning, their smiles etched with the familiarity of those who witness such awakenings daily.

"I hope you found the darkness pleasant," the Mother said gently.

"Not entirely," I admitted. "But the waking from it is heavenly."

"It has barely begun," she rejoined. "You are hardly awake yet."

"He is at least clothed in Death," Adam observed, his tone grave yet warm. "Which is the radiant garment of Life."

He embraced Lona, his child, and placed an arm around me. He looked inquiringly at the princess, then patted the head of the leopardess resting nearby. "I think we shall meet you two again before long," he said, his gaze shifting between Lona and me.

"Must we die again?" I asked, fear tightening my chest.

"No," Adam answered, smiling with the same serene assurance as the Mother. "You have died into life, and you will die no more. You have only to remain dead. Once we die as we do here, all dying ends. Now you must live, with all your blessed might. The more you live, the stronger you become to live."

"But shall I not grow weary?" I asked. "What if I cease to live with all my might?"

"It requires only the will," the Mother said firmly. "The strength is already there! Pure life knows no weakness to cause weariness. THE Life keeps generating ours. Those who refuse to die die many times, constantly, sinking deeper into death forever. Here, everything moves upward, toward love and gladness."

She paused, her smile deepening into a look of profound understanding. "We are mother and son; we understand each other. Between us, no farewell is possible."

Mara kissed my forehead, her voice bright and buoyant. "I told you, brother, all would be well! When you need comfort next, say, 'What will be well, is even now well.'"

She sighed, a small, melancholic sound. I sensed her unspoken thought: *But they will not believe you.*

"--You know me now!" she finished, her smile mirroring her mother's.

"I know you!" I declared. "You are the voice crying in the wilderness before the Baptist arrived! You are the shepherd who hunts the wandering sheep home before the shadows rise and the night grows dark!"

"My work will one day end," she said. "And then I shall be glad with the gladness of the Great Shepherd who sent me."

"All night long, the morning is at hand," Adam intoned.

"What is that flapping of wings I hear?" I asked, startled.

"The Shadow hovers," Adam replied. "There is one here whom he claims as his own. But once ours, she can never be his again."

I turned to kiss my father and mother before we departed. Their couches were empty, save for the Little Ones who had boldly claimed their hospitality. For a split second, the awful dream of desolation returned, overshadowing me, and I turned away.

"What is it, my heart?" Lona asked, sensing my distress.

"Their empty places frightened me," I confessed.

"They left long ago," Adam said calmly. "They kissed you before they went and whispered, 'Come soon.'"

"And I could neither feel nor hear them!" I murmured, the loss feeling acute.

"How could you? You were far away in your dreary old house, thinking the dreadful place had reclaimed you once more! Now go and find them. Your parents, my child, must come and find you!"

The hour of departure approached. Lona moved to the couch of the mother who had slain her, kissing her tenderly before laying herself in her father's arms.

"That kiss will draw her homeward, my Lona!" Adam said.

"Who were her parents?" Lona asked, confused.

"My father," Adam answered simply, "is her father also."

She turned, placing her hand in mine.

I knelt and humbly thanked the three for aiding me in my death. Lona knelt beside me, and they all breathed upon us, sealing the transition.

"Hark! I hear the sun," Adam said suddenly.

I listened. It came with the rush of ten thousand wings multiplied by ten, accompanied by the roar of a molten, flaming world millions of miles away. Its approach was a crescendo of a hundred harmonies.

The three looked at one another and smiled. That smile floated heavenward like a three-petaled flower, a family offering of morning thanksgiving. From their mouths and faces, it spread over their bodies, shining through their garments. Before I could speak, Adam and Eve stood before me as the angels of resurrection, and Mara stood as the Magdalene at the sepulcher. Adam's countenance was like lightning; Eve held a napkin that scattered flakes of pure splendor across the room.

A wind began to moan in pulsing gusts.

"You hear his wings now!" Adam said, clarifying that he did not mean the wings of the morning.

"It is the great Shadow stirring to depart," he continued. "Wretched creature, he has himself within him and cannot rest!"

"But is there not something deeper within him?" I asked.

"Without a substance, a shadow cannot exist—yes, or without a light behind that substance!" he answered.

He listened for a moment, then called out with a glad smile. "Hark to the golden cock! Silent and motionless for millions of years, he has stood on the clock of the universe. Now, at last, he flaps his wings! Soon he will begin to crow, and at intervals, men will hear him until the dawn of the eternal day."

I listened. Far away, in the heart of an aeonian silence, I heard the clear, jubilant outcry of the golden throat. It hurled defiance at death and the dark, singing of infinite hope and coming calm. It was the "expectation of the creature" finally finding a voice; the cry of a chaos destined to become a kingdom!

Then came a great flapping.

"The black bat is flown!" Mara cried.

"Amen, golden cock, bird of God!" Adam shouted, and the words rang through the house of silence, ascending into the airy regions.

At his Amen, like doves rising on wings of silver from among potsherds, the Little Ones sprang to their knees on their beds, calling aloud, "Crow! Crow again, golden cock!" as if they had seen and heard him in their dreams. Then each turned to look at their sleeping bedfellows, gazing with loving eyes, kissing the silent companions of the night, and springing from the couches. The Little Ones who had lain beside my father and mother stared blankly and sadly at their empty spaces for a moment, then slid slowly to the floor. There, they fell into each other's arms, as if only then, through each other's eyes, were they assured they were alive and awake. Suddenly spotting Lona, they ran toward her, radiant with bliss, to embrace her. Odu, catching sight of the leopardess at the princess's feet, bounded to her side, threw an arm over the great sleeping head, and fondled it.

"Wake up, wake up, darling!" he cried. "It is time to wake!"

The leopardess did not move.

"She has slept herself cold!" he said to Mara, his face upturned in appealing consternation.

"She is waiting for the princess to wake, my child," Mara said gently.

Odu looked at the princess and saw that beside her, still asleep, lay two of his companions. He flew to them.

"Wake up! Wake up!" he cried, pushing and pulling this one and that.

Soon, however, he looked troubled and turned to me with misty eyes. "They will not wake!" he said. "And why are they so cold?"

"They too are waiting for the princess," I answered.

He stretched across and laid his hand on her face. "She is cold too! What is it?" he cried, looking around in wondering dismay.

Adam went to him. "Her wake is not ripe yet," he explained. "She is busy forgetting. When she has forgotten enough to remember enough, she will soon be ripe and wake."

"And remember?"

"Yes—but not too much at once, though."

"But the golden cock has crowed!" the child argued, falling back onto his companions.

"Peter! Peter! Crispy!" he cried. "Wake up, Peter! Wake up, Crispy! We are all awake but you two! The golden cock has crowed so loud! The sun is awake and coming! Oh, why won't you wake?"

But Peter would not wake, nor would Crispy. Odu wept outright at last.

"Let them sleep, darling!" Adam said soothingly. "Would you not like the princess to wake and find nobody? They are quite happy. So is the leopardess."

Comforted, Odu wiped his eyes as if he had spent his whole life weeping and wiping, though now, for the first time, he had tears to shed—tears that would soon be wiped away altogether.

We followed Eve to the cottage. There, she offered us neither bread nor wine but stood radiantly, desiring our departure. So, with not a word of farewell, we went out. The horse and the elephants waited at the door. We were too happy to mount them, and they followed us.
