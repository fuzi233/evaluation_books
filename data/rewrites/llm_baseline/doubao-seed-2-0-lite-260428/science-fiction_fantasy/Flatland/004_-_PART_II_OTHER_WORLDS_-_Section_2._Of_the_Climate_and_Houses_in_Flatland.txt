Just as in your three-dimensional world, we of Flatland recognize four cardinal directions: North, South, East, and West. But with no sun, no stars, no heavenly bodies of any kind hanging over our plane, we cannot navigate by celestial cues the way you do. We have our own natural compass, written into the very laws of our world: a faint, constant, universal pull drawing all things gently toward the South.

This tug is barely perceptible in our temperate zones—mild enough that even a woman in reasonable health can trek several furlongs north without significant strain—yet it is consistent enough to serve as a reliable bearing guide across almost all our territory. We have a second, far more obvious marker too: rain, which falls on a strict predictable schedule, always drifting down from the North. In towns, our homes reinforce these guides: almost every building’s side walls run perfectly aligned north-to-south, so their steep northern-facing roofs shed all that incoming rain away from the home’s entrances. Out in open countryside where no buildings stand, even the trunks of wild trees grow aligned along that north-south axis to give wanderers a reference point. All told, we face far less trouble staying oriented than a new visitor to our world might assume.

I can attest that even that is not foolproof. On our most temperate plains, where the southward tug is nearly undetectable, I have found myself stranded on vast featureless stretches with no trees, no structures, no landmarks at all, forced to stand perfectly still for hours on end waiting for a rainshower to roll in so I could reorient myself and resume my journey. That gentle southward pull hits the weak, the aged, and especially delicate women far harder than it strikes robust young men, so strict social etiquette dictates that any man who crosses paths with a lady on a public path must always yield her the North side of the way, where the pull will not yank her off balance toward a sharp edge if she stumbles. This is no easy feat to pull off on short notice, when you are in full health and the landmarks around you give no clear clue which direction North even is.

You will notice we have no windows anywhere in our homes. Light reaches us equally indoors and out, at all hours of the day and night, uniform across every corner of our plane, and we have no agreed-upon explanation for where it comes from. In old generations, this question of the origin of light was one of the most hotly pursued mysteries among our leading scholars—every attempt to solve it only filled our lunatic asylums with the theorists who chased it. After decades of failed attempts to discourage the research by slapping ruinous taxes on anyone publishing papers about light’s origins, our legislature banned all inquiry into the subject entirely in comparatively recent history. I, alas, am the only soul in all of Flatland who now knows the true answer to that long-unresolved riddle: it bleeds through from the higher, three-dimensional world I have visited. But I cannot make that truth intelligible to a single one of my countrymen. I, the sole bearer of the knowledge of Space beyond our flat plane, am mocked as the maddest of madmen. Let me set aside these painful digressions, and return to the subject of our homes.

The near-universal standard for residential construction is the five-sided, pentagonal shape. The two northern edges of the pentagon meet at the top to form our sloped rain-shedding roof, with no doors cut into them at all. The east-facing edge has a small, narrow entryway reserved exclusively for the women of the household, while the far larger west-facing edge forms the main door for the men. The southernmost bottom edge of the pentagon, the floor of the home, has no openings of any kind.

Square and triangular houses have long been outlawed for ordinary residential use, for very good reason. The vertex of a square is far sharper than the wide, blunt 108-degree angle of a pentagon, and the edges of inanimate objects like houses register as far fainter to our sight than the crisp bright outlines of living people. An absent-minded traveler could very easily fail to spot a sharp house corner and run straight into it, sustaining a grave, even fatal injury. As early as the 11th century of our common era, equilateral triangular homes were universally banned by law. The only exceptions made are for state properties: forts, gunpowder magazines, military barracks, sites the general public is explicitly expected to approach only with extreme caution.

Square homes were still permitted for another three hundred years after that, though the state discouraged their construction by slapping them with a steep annual special tax. At the end of that three centuries, the legislature extended public safety rules to mandate that all towns with a population over ten thousand could only build homes with angles no sharper than a pentagon’s vertex. The public embraced the new rule wholeheartedly, and now pentagonal construction has replaced every other residential design even in the most remote countryside. The only surviving square houses left today are rare curiosities, tucked away in the most backward, isolated farming districts, sought out only by antiquarians chasing traces of our older history.
