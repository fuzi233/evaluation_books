Mrs. Lambert’s fleeting daydream had dissolved like morning mist. Both she and Theo, though neither spoke of it, knew their imagined hero was merely flesh and blood—no demigod, just a young man like any other. Yet in the quiet understanding between mother and daughter, there was no bitterness, only a gentle resignation.

“Never fret, my love,” Mrs. Lambert murmured, pressing a kiss to Theo’s cheek. “If he is but ordinary, then he is not meant for you. Heaven has better in store—a truer man, a nobler heart. Why, I was scarcely older than you when your father first saw me at the Bury Assembly, and I knew even then I’d have no other. If I was blessed with such a husband, why not you, my darling, who deserves a king if she should fancy one?”

In truth, Mrs. Lambert—ever the romantic—might have nursed a secret hope that some grander suitor would yet appear. The Prince of Wales himself, perhaps, riding past their gate, taking a spill from his horse, being tenderly nursed back to health, and promptly falling in love with Theo. Such were the fancies spun from the pages of her beloved novels.

Colonel Lambert and Hetty, however, stood firmly on the side of laughter. Hetty, sharp-tongued and merry, teased without mercy.

“Another Persian prince for Theo, is it?” she’d say, fluttering her lashes. “Or perhaps the King of Prussia? How many wives do they allow in Saxony, Papa?” She left *The Persian Tales* conspicuously on the parlour table, smirked at mentions of “Theo’s gentleman,” and declared she’d sooner marry Harry’s servant—the “black prince”—than endure such absurdity.

Harry, blissfully unaware, remained untouched by their jests. The arrows of wit flew past him, harmless as summer gnats. He was too occupied with his own thoughts, too unversed in the subtle arts of flirtation, to grasp the undercurrents swirling around him.

Yet for all Hetty’s mockery, the household was not so tranquil as it seemed. Beneath the surface, hearts stirred. Theo, who had felt the first faint flutter of admiration when Harry arrived—pale, wounded, noble—now schooled herself to stillness. *Not yet,* she thought. *This is not love, only a passing fancy.*

Meanwhile, three days into his stay, Harry walked with the family in Lord Wrotham’s park, where swans glided across the water.

“Mamma’s swans are like these, Papa,” Hetty announced, eyes dancing. “Only shorter-necked. And presently roasting in our kitchen.”

“Don’t be absurd,” Theo chided.

Harry, missing the allegory entirely, remarked cheerfully that he’d shot three swans back in Virginia.

He was, in truth, an innocent interloper—a stone dropped into still waters, unaware of the ripples he caused. The Lamberts, kind and lively, were unlike any family he had known. At home, his world had been one of fox-hunting planters and decorous ladies; here, conversation sparkled with wit, books lay open on every table, and laughter came easily.

Colonel Lambert, with his quiet wisdom and tales of campaigns, was a man Harry admired at once. The girls—Theo gentle, Hetty bright as a blade—were companions of a sort he’d never had. And Mrs. Lambert, warm and motherly, fussed over him as if he were another son.

No, he was not in love. But when the time came to leave, he clasped their hands with real affection, promising to return.

“He is kind,” Theo said softly, watching him ride away beside her father.

“Not so stupid after all,” Hetty conceded.

Mrs. Lambert sighed. “It feels like sending one of our own off to school.”

And as the horses disappeared down the Westerham road, Theo added, quieter still, “Just like it.”

Hetty, ever practical, was glad their father rode with him. “Better than leaving him to those Castlewood folk. That Madame Bernstein—I’d wager she’d float if they tried her in the pond, like old Mother Hely.”

“Hush!”

But Hetty was not so easily hushed. “The other one—the aunt with the fair hair—seemed fond of him. Pity she had to go.”

Mrs. Lambert, with a faint blush, admitted she had sent a bottle of her cordial water and a cake for Harry’s journey.

“I can’t help it,” she said. “I feel as if he were one of my own.”

And perhaps, in some small way, he was.
