The clamor of the piazza struck Tito like a wave as he stepped into its chaos—shouts, laughter, the rhythmic chant of peasants, the shrill taunts of boys, the erratic clanging of bells. Nello had spoken of the *Fierucoloni*, the festival where country folk and city crowds clashed in a whirl of noise and motion. Tito let himself be swept into it, the jostling a welcome distraction from the gnawing dread of an uncertain future. For a fleeting moment, he even considered searching for Baldassarre—but the thought dissolved like mist.

At each entrance, bodies pressed inward beneath swaying paper lanterns, their glow bobbing like drunken fireflies. The air was thick with competing sounds: a droning peasant hymn cut through by shrieks, whistles, the sharp *crack* of a stone shattering a lantern overhead, followed by fresh uproar. On the fringes of the tumult, pockets of order emerged—ragged men with sharp eyes lured bystanders into games; stilt-walkers, lanterns in hand, loomed like wandering specters; a vendor hawked steaming *berlingozzi*, their scent cutting through the sweat and dust. Near the loggia of the Foundling Hospital, a pill-seller perched on a barrel, promising salvation from toothache and drowning, while a tumbler’s platform drew a ring of gaping onlookers.

Tito moved without direction, buffeted by the crowd. Once, he found himself beside a train of peasant women—their faces wind-hardened, their garb faded relics of another century. These were the *contadine* of Pistoia, bearing bundles of yarn, their hearts heavy with meager hopes and nameless fears, all to be laid before the Virgin’s unveiled image tonight.

Then the tide pushed him toward the piazza’s edge, where vendors clustered like barnacles on a ship’s hull. There, against a pillar, stood Bratti, his basket clutched tight, his mouth a line of disdain. Two women hovered near, curiosity piqued by his secrecy.

"What’s in there, then?" one demanded.

Bratti’s smirk was mercenary. "Nothing for idle eyes. My wares are too fine for gawkers. Only two left—and with plague rumors, they might be worth gold soon. *Andate con Dio*."

The women exchanged glances. "What’s the price?"

"More than you’ve got," Bratti scoffed. But after haggling, he relented, unveiling two crude iron rings—supposedly Roman relics, their rust a testament to antiquity. The women bit, and Bratti pocketed his coins with a show of wounded generosity.

Spotting Tito, he seized his arm. "Luck smiles! I’ve a Genoese buyer for that ring of yours—deep pockets, heading to Hungary. He’d pay handsomely."

Tito’s fingers brushed the ring absently. A relic of Baldassarre, once sentimental, now a chain. Selling it might have spared him Fra Luca’s recognition. *No more ties to the past.*

"Name the time," he said.

"Sunrise, at my stall." Bratti grinned. "And don’t forget old friends when silver flows."

Shoving free, Tito drifted back into the throng, where a new spectacle unfolded: a charlatan in a yellow-stained mitre, peddling mock marriages for a quattrino. "A bargain blessed by Pope Alexander himself!" the man crowed, waving a forged bull. Tito knew that voice—Maestro Vaiano, the mountebank. Memories of Tessa flickered. Was she here, lost in this sea of faces?

A scuffle erupted as Vaiano’s monkey, provoked, leapt onto a shrieking woman’s head. In the chaos, Tito slipped into the church of the Nunziata, the noise dying behind him. Inside, the air was thick with incense and devotion, the fresco of the Annunciation glowing like a jewel in its lamp-lit chapel. Peasant women knelt or stood, their rough hands clasped, their eyes upturned. Above them, the walls teemed with waxen effigies—saints, warriors, popes—a silent, staring host.

Then, near the door, he saw her: Tessa, slumped against the wall, her blue eyes drowsy, a string of cocoons dangling from her fingers. Hunger and exhaustion had dulled her usual brightness.

"Tessa," he murmured.

She blinked up at him, as if he were a dream. "You came back."

"You’re hungry," he said, helping her rise.

She nodded. "The *Madre* is sick. I brought these for the Virgin." She clutched the cocoons like a talisman.

He led her into the cloisters, away from the crowd. "Where will you sleep tonight?"

"The church," she said simply. "Like the others."

A refuge, he thought. A world without judgment. As the night pressed in around them, Tito felt the weight of his choices—and the fleeting comfort of a hand too innocent to hold a grudge.
