The legal formalities of marriage required the reading of the banns three times, a tradition much like today, save for one significant difference: in those days, they were typically proclaimed on weekdays. However, the young couple, driven by urgency and aided by their striking appearance, successfully persuaded the local priest to compress the entire process into a single twenty-four-hour period. The announcements were made on a Monday at both Matins and Vespers, and to their immense relief, not a single soul from Tergou appeared in the church to object.

The following morning, Margaret and Gerard arrived at the church, their hearts pounding with nervous anticipation. To their absolute horror, a stranger rose in the congregation and formally forbade the union. He claimed the couple were minors and lacked parental consent. Outside the church doors, the pair engaged in a trembling, near-despairing consultation. Before they could formulate a plan, the interloper approached them. He feigned regret, claiming his intention was to promote their happiness, yet explained that his livelihood depended entirely on obstructing such unions.

"The young people give me a crown, and I undo my work handsomely," he suggested. "Tell the cure I was mistaken, and all proceeds smoothly."

"A crown!" Gerard exclaimed eagerly. "I will give you a golden angel to secure this favor." The man agreed instantly, accompanying Gerard to the priest. He declared the previous objection a ridiculous error, corrected by a mere glance at the lovers. Satisfied, the priest scheduled the wedding for the next day at ten o'clock. The professional impediment to bliss departed with Gerard's golden angel.

Like many such cunning rogues, however, he proved to be a fool. He proceeded to a tavern in Tergou known for its green dedicated to archery and popular games, where he became drunk and boasted of his successful interference. Among the listeners was Sybrandt, a notorious idler and frequent visitor to the establishment. Sybrandt rushed home to report the news. Finding his father away in Rotterdam purchasing cloth from merchants, he signaled his elder brother to emerge and shared the intelligence. Black sheep exist in nearly every large family; these two brothers were Gerard's. Idleness corrupts the soul, and waiting for the demise of loved ones breeds resentment; these two single-minded curs were prepared to destroy anyone who threatened their miserable inheritance, which occupied their thoughts by day and dominated their dreams by night.

Their parents' stinginess was originally a virtue born of industry and love for their children, but in these twisted, selfish hearts, it had mutated into avarice—a fertile source of crime. They conspired not to inform their mother, whose feelings were unpredictable, but to approach the Burgomaster immediately. Shrewdly, they sensed his aversion to the match, though they could not discern the reason. Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the Burgomaster, saw through their plot instantly but allowed them to remain unaware of his insight. Hearing their tale, he adopted an air of magisterial dignity and coldness.

"Since the father of the family is absent, his duty falls upon me, the father of the town," he stated. "I know your father's mind. Leave everything to me. Above all, tell no woman a word of this, least of all the women within your own household, for chattering tongues ruin the wisest counsels." With a touch of condescension, he dismissed them, feeling somewhat ashamed of his unwitting accomplices.

Upon returning home, they discovered their brother Gerard seated on a low stool at their mother's knee. She caressed his hair, speaking kindly and promising to support him against his father's wishes. The catalyst for this sudden reversal was characteristic of the woman. In a fit of female irritation, she had previously torn Margaret's portrait to shreds. Having observed Gerard's reaction—turning pale as death, sitting motionless like a bereaved creature, tears blinding his gaze as he stared at the fragments—she had been terrified by her own action. Her heart ached with remorse, yet pride prevented her from admitting fault. Instead, she resolved silently to make amends. Her maternal instincts overwhelmed her, and her earlier violent impulse died naturally as she transferred her allegiance to Gerard just as the two "black sheep" entered the room.

Gerard, inexperienced in the complexities of female emotion, felt shame at having suspected her of destroying the picture. Overcome with gratitude for her apparent kindness, he kissed her repeatedly and retired to bed, a prince in spirit, believing his mother had fully embraced him at the critical juncture of his destiny.

The following morning at ten o'clock, Gerard and Margaret stood in the church at Sevenbergen. He radiated joy; she burned with blushes. Only Peter and Martin Wittenhaagen were present among their friends; secrecy was paramount. Margaret had refused to travel to Italy, unable to abandon her father, who was too learned and too frail to be left alone. It was agreed they would withdraw to Flanders for a few weeks until the storm in Tergou subsided.

The priest did not keep them waiting long. Soon, he stood at the altar, calling them forward. Hand in hand, the happiest couple in Holland approached him. The priest opened his book, but before uttering a single word of the sacred rite, a harsh voice commanded, "Forbear!" Constables from Tergou surged up the aisle and seized Gerard in the name of the law. Martin's long knife flashed out instantly.

"Forbear, man!" cried the priest indignantly. "What! Draw your weapon in a church? You who interrupt this holy sacrament, what manner of impiety is this?"

"There is no impiety, Father," replied the Burgomaster's servant respectfully. "This young man seeks to marry against his father's will, and his father has petitioned our Burgomaster to deal with him according to the law. Let him deny it if he can."

Gerard hung his head in silence. "We take him to Rotterdam to await the sentence of the Duke," the servant announced.

At this, Margaret screamed in despair. The formerly joyful youths collapsed into each other's arms, sobbing pitifully. Even the oppressors hesitated, ashamed by the scene, though one, acting on good nature and under the pretext of separating them, leaned toward Margaret and whispered, "Rotterdam? It is a lie. We merely take him to our Stadthouse."

Mounted on horses and taking a dozen halts via sly detours, they were led back to Tergou. Just outside the town walls, a crude vehicle covered in canvas awaited. Gerard was forced into it and, by five in the evening, was secretly conveyed to the prison of the Stadthouse. He was hoisted up several flights of stairs and shoved into a tiny room illuminated only by a narrow window featuring a vertical iron bar. The sole piece of furniture was a massive oak chest. Imprisonment in that era was a direct highway to death. Even in its mildest form, it entailed cold, unbroken solitude, torture, starvation, and often poison. Gerard realized he was in the hands of an enemy.

"Oh, the look that man gave me on the road to Rotterdam," he thought, despair filling his heart. "There is more here than my father's wrath. I doubt I shall see the light of day again." Kneeling, he commended his soul to God.

Soon, he rose and leaped at the iron bar of the window, clutching it tightly. By pressing his knees against the wall, he managed to peer outward for a fleeting minute—a duration precious only to a captive. Through the gap, he saw Martin Wittenhaagen's back. Martin sat quietly fishing in the brook near the Stadthouse. Gerard lunged again at the window and whistled. Instantly, Martin stopped fishing, his attention sharpening far beyond the sport. Turning hastily, he spotted Gerard, made a signal, and gathered his line and bow before departing quickly. Gerard understood his friends were not idle, yet he wished Martin had stayed; the sight of him alone was a profound comfort. Holding onto the window frame, he watched the soldier's retreating form until he could no longer see him, then slumped back heavily.

Just as he did, the rusty iron bar, held fast only by corroded nails, tore away from the stonework. Simultaneously, Ghysbrecht Van Swieten opened the door stealthily behind him. The Burgomaster's eyes fell on the broken iron, then glanced at the window, saying nothing. The window stood a hundred feet above the ground; if Gerard fancied jumping, surely he would not hesitate. The Burgomaster placed a brown loaf and a pitcher of water on the chest in solemn silence.

Gerard's first instinct was to smash the Burgomaster's skull with the iron bar and flee down the stairs. But sensing malice in the official's eyes, the Burgomaster cleared his throat, and three armed men materialized at the doorway.

"My orders are to keep you here until you swear an oath to renounce Margaret Brandt and return to the Church, to which you have belonged since infancy," the Burgomaster declared.

"Death sooner," Gerard answered.

"With all my heart," the Burgomaster replied, and withdrew.

Martin hastened to Sevenbergen, finding Margaret pale and agitated but resolute. She was finishing a letter to the Countess Charolois, appealing against Ghysbrecht's violence and treachery.
