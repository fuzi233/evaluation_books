Within minutes, the flanking regiments had tramped off in total silence, hugging the lee side of the rising ground to hide their advance from Twala’s sharp-eyed scouts. A full half hour drifted by before the Greys and their supporting Buffalo Regiment—forming the main punch of Ignosi’s army, fresh and at full strength—stirred. The Greys had spent the morning in reserve, only taking light losses when they charged to stamp out a break in the defense line that had left me stunned and nursing a throbbing skull. The Buffaloes, meanwhile, had held the third line on the left, and since the enemy never cracked the second, they’d barely fired a single spear all day.

Old Infadoos, wily veteran that he was, used the lull to rally his beloved Greys with fiery, poetic oratory. He reminded the all-veteran force (not a man under 40) what an honor it was to lead the battle, fighting side by side with the great white warrior from the stars, and promised rich herds of cattle and swift promotion to every survivor if Ignosi’s claim to the throne won out. I stared down the ranks of bobbing black plumes and stern, weathered faces, and sighed. I knew most, if not all, of these magnificent fighters would be dead or dying in the dust within an hour. The great general’s cold, wise math was stacking their lives up as sacrifice: they were meant to throw themselves at Twala’s wave after wave, hold the narrow strip of grassland before us until they were wiped out, and give the hidden flanking parties the perfect window to strike. And every single man there knew it. Not a flicker of fear crossed a single face. They walked knowingly into certain death, no tremor, no hesitation, and I felt a sharp twinge of envious admiration. I’d never seen such absolute devotion to duty, such total indifference to its brutal cost. I, for one, was far from comfortable.

“Behold your king!” Infadoos roared, pointing to Ignosi at my side. “Fight and fall for him, as brave men must. Cursed be the name of any who shrinks from death for his king, who turns his back on the foe! Chiefs, captains, soldiers—pay homage to the sacred Snake, then follow me and Incubu, and I will show you the path straight to Twala’s heart.”

A murmur rippled through the locked phalanxes, soft as distant sea surf: six thousand spear handles tapping gently against their bearers’ shields. The sound swelled, deepened, thundered against the mountains like rolling thunder, then faded to silence before the full royal salute crashed out. No Roman emperor ever got a cheer half as grand from gladiators who knew they were about to die. Ignosi lifted his battle axe in acknowledgment, and the Greys filed out in three tight lines, a thousand men apiece, officers bringing up the rear. When their last companies were 500 yards ahead, Ignosi led the Buffaloes off after them, and I prayed harder than I ever had in my life that I’d walk away from this mess with a whole skin.

We reached the edge of the plateau just as the Greys hit the tip of that narrow, grassy spit of land that jutted out into the plain like a horse’s frog tucked into its shoe. The tongue was only 90 paces wide at its point, and even at its widest base no more than 650, walled on both sides by boulder-strewn slopes no one could climb to flank the Greys. Down in the plain, Twala’s 40,000-strong reinforced army was surging forward at a fast trot to block them, but when they hit the mouth of the spit, they froze. Only one regiment could cram its way up that narrow gap at a time, and waiting 70 yards in, unattackable from any side but front, stood the Greys—three grim ranks, still as stone, ready to hold the gap like the three Romans who once stood against thousands on a bridge.

A tall, plumed general strode out at the head of Twala’s line, flanked by chiefs, and I realized it was the king himself. He barked an order, and the first of his regiments charged screaming up the spit. The Greys didn’t move, didn’t make a sound, until the enemy were 40 yards out, and a hail of throwing knives (tollas) rattled into their ranks. Then they bounded forward with a roar, spears lifted high. The crash of meeting shields hit us like thunder, the plain glittering with reflected sunlight off a thousand shimming assegais. The surging mass of stabbing, struggling men heaved back and forth for minutes, then the attacking line thinned, and the Greys rolled over them like a great wave washing over a sunken reef. The entire enemy regiment was gone. A third of the Greys were dead. Two ranks remained. I spotted Sir Henry’s yellow beard bobbing as he rearranged the lines, and breathed a huge sigh of relief: he was still alive.

They closed ranks, shoulder to shoulder, and waited silent for the next charge. The ground between us was carpeted with 4,000 dead, dying and wounded, soaked crimson with blood. Ignosi passed the word down that no enemy wounded were to be harmed, and his order was obeyed to the letter.

A second regiment, marked by white plumes and kilts, advanced on the 2,000 remaining Greys. These were young, ferocious fighters, and for a long, terrible minute they looked set to drive the veterans straight back off the spit. Hundreds fell every minute, the air full of war shouts, dying groans, clashing spears, and the hissing, triumphant “S’gee, s’gee” sound of warriors driving their assegais through the bodies of fallen foes. Then Sir Henry’s deep roar cut through the din, his battle axe whirling high above his plumes. The Greys stopped giving ground. They stood as solid as a rock, and the wave of attackers broke against them, recoiling over and over. Then they moved forward. The enemy lines shattered, groups of them fleeing for their lives, white plumes streaming behind them. The Greys were victorious—but they were no longer a regiment. Of the 3,000 men who’d marched out 40 minutes prior, only 600 blood-soaked veterans were left on their feet. They didn’t retreat. They ran 100 yards after the fleeing foes, seized a small rising knoll, reformed their triple line, and formed a defensive ring around its base. Sir Henry and Infadoos stood unharmed at the top. Then Twala’s next regiments crashed down on the tiny doomed band.

Up until that moment, I’d always been a bit of a coward, happy to run if it kept my skin intact. But now battle fever burned through my veins, old war verses from the Ingoldsby Legends and the Old Testament bouncing around my head, my blood thundering so hard I could hear it in my ears, a savage, unsparing lust for the fight coiling in my chest. Only Ignosi stayed calm, for all the world grinding his teeth with excitement beside me. “Are we going to stand here till we grow roots, Ignosi, while Twala swallows our brothers whole?” I demanded.

He lifted his battle axe, his eyes blazing. “Nay, Macumazahn. The moment is ripe—let us pluck it.”

The Buffaloes screamed the Kukuana war cry and charged like a tidal wave. Everything after that is a red blur of blood and flashing spears and ground-shaking shock. When my mind cleared, I was standing on the knoll right behind Sir Henry, who’d dragged me into their protective circle after the rush of the Buffaloes carried me almost to his feet. Again and again Twala’s thousands surged against our shrinking little band, and again and again we beat them back, stepping where our fallen comrades had stood the second they fell. Infadoos was completely cool, cracking jokes and shouting taunts to keep morale high even when spears whistled past his ear. Sir Henry’s ostrich plumes had been sheared off by a strike, his long yellow hair streaming in the wind, his hands, axe, and armour all dripping red. No man could stand against his blow. He roared like his berserker Viking forefathers as his axe crashed through shield, spear, skull and bone, until no foe would willingly step within striking distance of the great white “wizard” who never missed.

Then a cry went up: “Twala! Twala!” The one-eyed giant king himself burst through the press, armed with axe and shield, clad in chain mail. “Where art thou, Incubu, white man who slew my son Scragga? See if thou canst slay me!” He hurled a tolla straight at Sir Henry, who caught it square on his shield, the knife wedging through the hide to stick in the iron plate beneath. Twala charged, his axe crashing down so hard on Sir Henry’s shield that even the giant strongman went down on one knee.

Before Twala could strike the killing blow, a roar of dismay rippled through the surrounding enemy ranks. To the right and left, the plain was alive with the plumes of charging warriors. Our flanking regiments had arrived. Twala’s entire army had been fixated on the blood-soaked struggle around our tiny circle, never dreaming the outflanking parties were anywhere but hidden on the far hill crest. Before they could even form a defensive line, our impis hit them from both flanks like greyhounds off the leash. The battle was over in five minutes. Panicked, shattered by the Greys’ impossible resistance, Twala’s regiments broke and ran, the plain between us and the royal capital Loo scattered with fleeing men. The thousands who’d surrounded us melted away like mist. Around our knoll, the dead lay in heaped mounds. Of the Greys’ original 3,000, only 95 men still stood. 3,400 had fallen that day.

Infadoos calmly bound a gash on his arm, glancing at his tiny remaining band. “You have kept the reputation of your regiment,” he said simply. “Your children’s children will speak of this day’s fighting.” He turned and shook Sir Henry’s hand. “Thou art a great captain, Incubu. I have fought beside thousands of brave men, but never one like thee.”

We joined Ignosi, who was marching the Buffaloes toward Loo to finish the victory and capture Twala. Halfway to the gates, we spotted Good sitting on top of an anthill 100 paces off, a dead Kukuana soldier slumped beside him. Then the “dead” man jumped up, knocked Good heels over head off the mound, and stabbed him over and over with his assegai before bolting off yelling “Take that, wizard!” We raced over, certain our friend was dead—only to find him pale, faint, but grinning, his eyeglass still fixed perfectly in his eye. “Capital armour, this,” he murmured. “That beggar is furious he didn’t get through.” He fainted. We found he’d only been nicked in the leg by a tolla earlier in the day, his chain mail stopping the final spear strike dead, leaving nothing but a bad bruise. We laid him on a wicker shield carried by the bearers of the wounded, and kept marching.

At Loo’s gates, the defending regiments had already thrown down their arms. Ignosi’s heralds had promised full pardon to anyone who surrendered before nightfall, and no one wanted to fight on. The gates swung open, thousands of dejected warriors lining the roads, spears and shields at their feet, bowing to Ignosi as he passed. We marched straight to Twala’s great kraal, the same open space where we’d seen the royal review and the witch hunt only days prior. It was almost empty. Twala sat alone on the far side of his hut, his battle axe and shield beside him, his chin resting on his mailed chest. The only other person there was Gagool, the old crone. Not a single soldier, courtier, or wife had stayed to share his fall.

We halted the regiment 50 yards from him, and walked forward with only a small guard, Gagool hislinging curses at us the whole way. Twala lifted his plumed head, his one good eye blazing with fury almost as bright as the great diamond bound around his forehead, fixed on Ignosi. “Hail, king!” he spat, bitter and mocking. “Thou who ate my bread, and used the white man’s magic to seduce my regiments and steal my throne. What fate hast thou in store for me?”

“The exact fate thou gavest to my father,” Ignosi answered coldly, “whose throne thou hast stolen for these many years.”
