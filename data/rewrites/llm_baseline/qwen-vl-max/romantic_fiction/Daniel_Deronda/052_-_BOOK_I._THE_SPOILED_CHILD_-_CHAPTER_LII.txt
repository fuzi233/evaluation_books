“La même fermeté qui sert à résister à l'amour sert aussi à le rendre violent et durable; et les personnes faibles qui sont toujours agitées des passions n’en sont presque jamais véritablement remplies.” —LA ROCHEFOUCAULD.

Among the letters Deronda received the next morning was one from Hans Meyrick, stretching across four quarto pages in the delicate, elegant script that ran through generations of the Meyrick family.

MY DEAR DERONDA,—In return for your thoughtful sketch of Italian politics and your wide-ranging view of global affairs, I can only offer a homegrown observation: here, the most sensible opinion on how current events will unfold is simply, “time will tell.” As for explaining past outcomes through present causes—well, it’s now widely acknowledged that the recent telegraph frauds were responsible for last year’s cattle plague. A fine refutation of so-called philosophy, and a clear justification for compensating the farmers. My own theory—that soon a murrain will strike the commercial class, with its roots revealed in the sudden sale of rejected paintings—has been dismissed as an unsound analogy. But let us not forget: there are minds willing to rob even the neglected artist of his final solace. To me, there is poetic beauty in imagining that some misguided judge might pay a fortune for my *Berenice* series, while the men of finance have already been punished by my undeserved luck.

Meanwhile, I console myself for your absence by stepping into the light you’ve left behind—shining like Hesperus after Hyperion has set. I’ve taken to sitting with our Hebrew prophet during the hours he once spent with you, studying the contours of his face, earning his respect as a learned young Gentile who, had circumstances allowed, would have gladly become a Jew. We agree on a broad principle: whatever is best must, by definition, be Jewish. I’ve never claimed to be a rigorous logician, but I can see that if everything best is A, and B happens to be best, then B must be A—even if we never expected it. On this basis, I once read a pamphlet arguing that all true art must be Protestant. I didn’t believe it, but I respected the reasoning. Still, our prophet makes a far more compelling model than Rembrandt ever did with his Rabbi—he’s alive with meaning, every visit revealing something new. One constant wonder: how, despite his fierce devotion to his people and their traditions, he remains anything but rigid. He doesn’t spit at the word *Christian*, nor does he revel in visions of the Gentile mouth drooling uselessly over roasted Leviathan while Israel feasts endlessly, *ad libitum*. (You’ll understand—I’ve learned what to expect from orthodox Jews.) I confess I once found your portrayal of Mordecai somewhat defensive, a gesture toward antiquity, as if you feared doing injustice to the megatherium by not honoring it fully. But now, having heard him speak in person, I find him a man of profound philosophical depth, wrapped in allegory and mysticism, yet sharp enough to silence any noisy debater with a single pointed question. The blend may be a Jewish prerogative, for all I know. His mind feels so vast that my own opinions fit comfortably within it—how they align with the rest is his problem, not mine. I leave the foundation to him. After all, every foundation is just an elephant supporting the world, more or less powerful and costly to maintain. I can’t afford a private elephant. So I retreat into mystery—it’s cheaper, longer-lasting, a sort of gas generated by the slow decay of such elephants. And if an idea pleases me, I treat it kindly, without suspicion. I’ve grown fond of Mordecai’s belief that a whole Christian is three-fourths a Jew, and that since Alexandrian times, the most expansive minds have been Jewish. I’d love to show Mirah this thought—apart from Arabic and other life details, there’s really little difference between me and Maimonides.

But lately, I’ve discovered that it’s the shallow lover who can’t help declaring himself. If Mirah weren’t so distracting, if being near her weren’t like standing in heaven, I’d have long ago thrown myself at her feet and begged her to tell me—plainly—whether she wanted me to end my life. I have a gift for hope, which is almost as valuable as an estate in reversion—if only I could resist turning it into certainty, which might ruin everything. My Hope wanders among blossoming orchards, feels warm snow fall through sunlight, and doubts nothing. But when it catches sight of Certainty in the distance, it sees a grim Janus-faced god, winking doubtfully, and quickly turns away. You, with your calm reasonableness and readiness to accept the worst—you don’t understand Hope. That immortal, delicious maiden, forever pursued, forever kind, whom fools call deceitful—yet it is not Hope who carries the cup of disappointment, but her deadly rival, Certainty, whom she escapes only by transformation. (You notice my new turn to allegory?) Seriously, though, I must insist that truth will prevail, prejudice will fade, diversity accompanied by merit will become irresistible, and no noble aspiration will go unfulfilled—all doctrines taught in schools, and they imply that the Jewess I adore will choose me. Any fool can quote generalities, but the wise recognize the particular truths they conceal.

I’m less certain that my presence compensates Mordecai for your absence, but Jacob Cohen occasionally steps in as a substitute. It’s worth seeing Mordecai’s expression when he has this remarkable young Israelite on his lap, pouring out Semitic wisdom with a look of solemn patience and deep reverence. Sometimes Jacob covers his ears with his palms, pretending to hear Hebrew through a muffling veil, imitating the sounds he hears. When Mordecai gently pulls down his fists and holds them fast, Jacob’s face becomes animated—like a child walking through a zoo, trying to mimic every animal, most convincingly the owl and the peccary. I imagine you’ve seen such scenes before. He treats me with easy familiarity, viewing me as a second-hand Christian, likely to depreciate in value. He speaks frankly about my shortcomings, as if considering future purchase. Yet when Mirah enters, he transforms instantly—his age, usually seeming like those ancient Israelite garments worn in the desert, perhaps near forty but appearing freshly made—becomes childlike. With Mirah, he reminds me of dogs raised by women, obedient only to them. Still, he loves Mordecai too, bringing sugar plums to share, stuffing his own mouth embarrassingly full, and watching carefully how Mordecai manages with less. Judging from this modern Jacob at six, I’m amazed his people haven’t bought us all up long ago, holding our weaker generations as stock and scrip—slave property in disguise. There is one Jewess I wouldn’t mind serving. But I fear I imagine Mirah growing quieter, trying hard to hide it. Naturally, given that she watches her brother slowly die, someone she worships with such tender devotion that I often wish I were in his place.

Otherwise, things are slightly livelier than usual. Rex Gascoigne—you remember the head I sketched, the fellow with the strong upper lip, studying law—has taken rooms nearby, and for the past fortnight, his neat sister (also blessed with a good upper lip) has been staying with him. I introduced them to my mother and sisters, who discovered from Miss Gascoigne that she’s cousin to your Vandyke duchess!!! I added the exclamation marks to mark the initial shock to my simple mind. Upon reflection, I realized there was no reason to be surprised—unless I’d assumed no one could be anyone’s cousin without my knowing. This kind of surprise, I think, stems from a lively spine and a brain that’s perpetually null. I once knew a man in Rome who exploded with amazement at the simplest facts. Tell him anything—say, you liked easy boots—and he’d always respond, “No! Are you?” with the same intense wonder. The very man Browne described:

“A wretch so empty that if e’er there be
In nature found the least vacuity
’Twill be in him.”

I’ve solved it—he had a lively spine.

Still, this cousinship came up by chance one day when Mirah was visiting and they discussed the Mallingers. _Apropos_, I’m becoming important enough to receive rival invitations. Gascoigne wants me to join him at his father’s rectory in August, to see the countryside. But I suspect self-interest will lead me to Topping Abbey—Sir Hugo has invited me, and proposed—God bless him for his boldness—that I paint his three daughters seated on a bank, in the Gainsborough style. He visited my studio recently and advised me to focus on portraiture. Of course, I know what he meant: “My dear fellow, your efforts at history and poetry are pitiable. Your brush is suited for portraits—truth mixed with ease in falsehood. Idealism won’t serve gods and goddesses, but flattery might fetch a high price. Fate, my friend, has made you the rear wheel—_rota posterior curras, et in axe secundo_—run behind, because you can’t help it.” What effort it takes our friends to deliver such honest advice! I’ve known a man who called specifically to tell me I’d irretrievably exposed my poor judgment in handling my subject, and that if I’d asked, he’d have lent me his own. Such was my ingratitude and my talent for composition that, even as he spoke, I mentally sketched a Last Judgment with his face on the left. But none of this matters compared to Sir Hugo, whose way of implying one’s talents aren’t supreme is so kind and comforting that I begin to feel it’s an advantage not to be at the very top. And his kindness tastes sweeter because it comes from his affection for you, old friend. His conversation is delightfully amusing. By the way, he mentioned your Vandyke duchess has gone yachting with her husband to the Mediterranean. I wonder—could one land from a yacht, or be taken aboard from the shore? Might you, by chance, get the opportunity to continue your theological debates with the fair Supralapsarian—wasn’t that her creed? Is Duke Alphonso also theological? Perhaps an Arian who resents the Trinity. (Stage direction: While D. reads, a deep scorn gathers on his face until, at the final word, he throws down the letter, grips his coat collar in a statue-like pose, and remains motionless, radiating awe-inspiring intensity throughout the following soliloquy: “O night, O blackness, etc., etc.”)

Forgive the brevity. You’re used to my bare facts, without comment. One fact I omitted—the Klesmers, on the eve of departure, behaved magnificently, shining as expected when genius and fortune align. Mirah is rich with their Oriental gifts.

What luck if you return and appear at the Abbey while I’m there! I’ll behave with perfect discretion and earn golden opinions. But I’ll sneak into town now and then, just to peek into Gad Eden. See how far I’ve advanced in Hebrew lore—up to Lord Bolingbroke, who knew no Hebrew but “understood that sort of learning and what is writ about it.” If Mirah commanded it, I’d descend below the tri-literal roots. Already, I don’t care whether the vowels are marked. But while her brother lives, I suspect she wouldn’t listen to a lover—even one whose “hair is like a flock of goats on Mount Gilead”—and I flatter myself that few heads could endure that comparison better than mine. So I remain with my hope among the orchard blossoms.—Your devoted,

Some months earlier, such a letter from Hans would have stirred Deronda’s thoughts with irritation—his romantic musings about Mirah would have felt intrusive, edged with a lack of empathy for his friend’s likely heartbreak. But much had changed since March. Mirah was no longer in such a precarious position with the Meyricks, and Deronda’s own life had transformed, culminating in the revelation of his origins. Though he refused to fixate on visions of the future, this new horizon inevitably reshaped his perception of both past and present. Now, Hans’s so-called “hope” no longer seemed a reckless fantasy provoking anger, but rather a persistent, whimsical dance of imagination—one he could almost pity if he believed it might cause real pain.

Yet part of the busy thoughts filling that long day—during which he waited anxiously for a message from his mother—revolved around a deeper assessment: Hans’s nature wasn’t one where love could take root deeply enough to transform disappointment into sorrow. Too restless, too easily excited by novelty, too quick to turn emotion into artifice, wearing grief like a costume. “He’s already playing at love,” Deronda thought. “He treats the whole thing as a comedy. He knows perfectly well there’s no chance for him. Just like him—never seeing any obstacle I might have to his declarations about Mirah. Poor old Hans! If we were caught in a hailstorm together, he’d scream like a Greek, and if I didn’t scream too, he’d never realize I was suffering as much. And yet he’s tender-hearted, affectionate in intent, and keen to imagine what others feel—but always shaping it to suit his own desires.”

With this touch of irony, Deronda dismissed the warmth stirred by Hans’s heartfelt excess. The mention of Gwendolen, casually noting her departure with her husband on a Mediterranean cruise, merely sparked a troubling echo of his own strange farewell. But one sentence stood out—Hans’s quiet suspicion that Mirah carried a hidden sadness. This struck Deronda sharply. Not because it aligned with his wishes, but because it contradicted them. Instead of doubting Hans’s observation, he began to search for its source. Was it something that had happened during his absence? Or simply a growing dread of what might come? Could it stem from her new circumstances? Or—had Mordecai, breaking his lifelong silence, shared his secret hopes about Deronda with her? Had her sensitive soul been wounded by the realization that her brother’s will, or his unwavering vision, had subtly shaped their friendship? Had she sensed that Deronda’s regard for her was tinged with pity, or condescension, rather than equal affection?

Deronda’s guess was not entirely wrong in identifying the core of Mirah’s nature—a quiet rebellion against the treatment she had endured all her life until meeting him. The gratitude that compelled her to insist on her debt to him carried half its intensity from the contrast with how others had treated her. Deronda’s emotional kinship allowed him to glimpse such depths. But he was mistaken in believing Mordecai had broken his silence. To no one but Deronda himself had Mordecai spoken of their bond, or of his trust in Deronda’s origin. These were matters too sacred for casual speech—not only because they required weighty reason, but because Mordecai had perceived Deronda’s discomfort at any mention of his birth. The same strict reserve that prevented Mordecai from answering questions about private matters in the Cohen family applied even more strongly here.

“Ezra, how is it?” Mirah once asked him softly. “I keep finding myself speaking to Mr. Deronda as if he were a Jew?”

He smiled gently. “Perhaps because he treats us as if we were brothers. But he dislikes dwelling on differences of birth.”

“He has never lived with his parents, Mr. Hans says,” Mirah continued, eager to learn about everyone she cared for.

“Do not seek to know such things from Mr. Hans,” Mordecai replied gravely, laying a hand on her curls as he often did. “What Daniel Deronda chooses to reveal about himself is for him alone to decide.”

Mirah felt rebuked—as Deronda had once done. But to be corrected by Mordecai in this way filled her with quiet pride.

“I see no one greater than my brother,” she told Mrs. Meyrick one day, visiting the Chelsea house on her way home and, as she hoped, finding the little mother alone. “It’s hard to believe he belongs to the same world as those people I used to live among. I once told you they made life seem like a madhouse. But when I’m with Ezra, he makes me feel his life is a great good, though he has suffered so much—unlike me, who once wished to die after a little pain, lasting only a short time. His soul is so full, he cannot desire death as I did. I feel the same way when I was tired yesterday, returning through the park after sweet rain and sunshine touched the grass and flowers. Everything looked so pure and beautiful that weariness and trouble seemed small parts of existence, and I grew more patient, more hopeful.”

A soft note of melancholy in her voice caused Mrs. Meyrick to study her closely. After removing her hat and smoothing her curls with an air of fatigue, Mirah sat opposite her friend, hands and feet crossed—distantly resembling a serene painted statue. But Mrs. Meyrick saw a new look of suppressed sorrow, matching the hint that patience and hope required extra strength.

“Is there fresh trouble on your mind, my dear?” she asked, setting aside her needlework to focus fully.

Mirah hesitated. “I’m too ready to speak of troubles. It seems unkind to burden others unless I’m sure it might prevent worse. And perhaps I’m too hasty, too fearful.”

“Oh, my dear,” Mrs. Meyrick said, “mothers are made to bear pain for their children. Is it because singing lessons are rare, and may dwindle as the season ends? Success doesn’t come all at once.”

She didn’t believe she’d hit the mark, but a guess could open the door.

“No,” Mirah said, shaking her head. “I’ve been disappointed—many ladies said they wanted me to teach them or their daughters, but then I never heard from them again. But perhaps after the holidays I can teach in schools. Besides, you know, I’m as rich as a princess. I haven’t touched the hundred pounds Mrs. Klesmer gave me. And I’d never fear for Ezra’s needs, because there’s Mr. Deronda. He said, ‘It is the chief honor of my life that your brother will share anything with me.’ Oh, no! Ezra and I need fear nothing about food or clothing.”

“But there’s another fear,” Mrs. Meyrick said, sensing more. “A fear that might disturb your peace. Don’t forecast evil unless you can guard against it. Anxiety is useless if it can’t become defense. But not all evils can be guarded against. Have you more reason to worry now than a month ago?”

“Yes,” Mirah whispered. “I’ve kept it from Ezra. I couldn’t bring myself to tell him. Please forgive me—I can’t help telling you. I _have_ more reason to fear. Five days ago… I’m certain I saw my father.”

Mrs. Meyrick shrank back, arms crossed tightly, leaning forward as if to stop herself from hurling insults at that man.

“The years have changed him,” Mirah went on. “He was already altered, worn down before I left. You remember I said he sometimes wept. He was always agitated. I’ve told Ezra everything I told you. He says my father took to gambling—making people distressed one moment, exalted the next. And now—only a moment—I saw him. His face was more hollow, his clothes shabby. He was with a much worse-looking man, carrying something, and they were rushing after an omnibus.”

“Well, child,” Mrs. Meyrick said, “he didn’t see you, I hope?”
