When my mother learned that I had accepted a position within the household, I suspect she was hardly delighted. Perhaps she withheld her financial support deliberately, hoping to starve me into submission and force my return to Virginia, where I would remain dependent upon her. Between us, until the day of her death, there was never a single explanation regarding the monetary dispute. She simply severed my allowance; I said not a word, yet managed to survive without her aid. I never heard her express remorse for her injustice, nor acknowledge her error, unless indirectly through Harry's private communications to me. In later years, when we met, her behavior softened greatly, accompanied by an unusual respect and affection toward my wife, Madam Esmond. Perhaps she intended for me to interpret this as a tacit admission of wrongdoing, though she offered no formal apology, nor did I demand one.

Harry was provided for—a fact I could not begrudge—so all of my mother's savings and frugal schemes ultimately benefited me, as her heir. Once, a few guineas would have been more valuable to me than hundreds arriving when I had no need. But when Madam Esmond and I finally reconciled, the era of necessity had long passed. I possessed abundance; I had no cause to scheme for ignoble savings or resent the doctor's fees. She could only add to my wealth. Undoubtedly, she suffered internally knowing that my children had gone hungry due to her pride, and that strangers had relieved the very needs her own heart had refused to address. Was she prouder than I? A gentle word of explanation between us might have secured a reconciliation years prior, but neither of us spoke.

When I commit a wrong and discover it later, I love to seek pardon—not merely to appease others, but to satisfy my own pride, effectively apologizing to myself for failing to meet my own standards. Consequently, I rarely debase myself to perform a mean act. Consider the men whose entire lives consist of lies, schemes, and subterfuges. What company do they keep when alone? Do they wear masks even in their privacy, deceiving their own consciences? If I choose to overlook an injury, it is likely not born of a Christian spirit of forgiveness, but because I can afford to remit the debt and disdain settling accounts. I have known a few rare souls in my life—perhaps ones I tested—who forgive without effort, growing naturally like a plant in fertile soil. I claim to know how to remit debts, but not how to truly forgive. I wonder if proud men are proud of their pride itself.

Thus, I showed no sign of submission toward my parent in Virginia, and we remained estranged for years, exchanging only occasional brief notes concerning births or trivialities. Following our initial troubles in America regarding the Stamp Act, similar tribulations befell me in London. Although I sided with the Tories in that controversy—and indeed, on the losing side of most debates—I believed firmly that the Imperial Government held the right to tax the colonies. Yet, at the height of the dispute, I published a sharp letter to a member of the House of Burgesses in Virginia. In it, I addressed the habitual insolence of the mother country with such freedom and expressed sentiments so offensive to those in power that I was stripped of my post as hackney-coach licenser. This terrified my uncle, who could never tolerate anyone in disgrace. He had developed a deep affection for my wife and my young son, but viewed me personally with a pitying contempt that always amused me. He possessed a natural scorn for poverty and a corresponding love for success and fortune. Any deviation from conventional thinking shocked him; truth-telling turned him pale.

Yet, despite the terrible blows I dealt him, he retained some warmth of heart and genuine love for kin. Spite of my actions, he continued to visit Theo and the child (and me, offering a mournful recognition upon meeting). Broken-hearted by my outspokenness, he refused to cease speaking to me as he had during our first rift, instead regarding me as a melancholy, lost creature beyond worldly help or hope. Never mind; I had to devise a new plan for life. Fortunately, the repayment of Harry's debt to me at this critical moment enabled me to sustain myself for months, perhaps years. "O strange fatuity of youth!" I often remark. How could we dare to be so poor and so undeterred?

At this time, His Majesty's royal uncle, the Duke of Cumberland, fell ill and died suddenly. Remarkably, his death altered my fortunes significantly. Poor Sir Miles Warrington never missed a court ceremony he could attend. He was present at every drawing-room, christening, ball, and funeral. Whether a prince or princess was ailing, his carriage waited at their door. From Leicester Fields to Carlton House, from Gunnersbury onward, nothing satisfied him now but attending the stout Duke's funeral. Standing bare-headed in the rain, he contracted a severe cold and throat inflammation. Before I had even heard of his illness, a lawyer visited my lodgings in Bloomsbury and hailed me as Sir George Warrington.

With party strife and fear of the future now behind us, we laid the deceased gentleman beside his young son in the family churchyard, where so many of his lineage rest. Little Miles and I were the chief mourners. An obsequious tenantry bowed and curtseyed before us, striving to conciliate my honor and worship. The dowager and her daughter soon retired to Bath, leaving me and my family to take possession of the house, of which I have been master for thirty years. "Be not too eager, O my son!" I advise. "Have but a little patience, and I too shall sleep beneath those yew trees, while the people toss their caps for Sir Miles."

The chronicles of a prosperous country life are easily and briefly recounted. The steward's books record rents paid and forgiven, crops raised and rotated, visitors received and their durations of stay, the pensioners my wife supported and how they were cared for, the year I served as sheriff, and the frequency of hound meetings. All these details reside in our house journals, available to any heir willing to consult them. We could not afford the magnificent mansion in Hill Street occupied by my predecessor, so we selected a smaller residence, in which we nonetheless expended considerable sums. We displayed less ostentation than my uncle, lacking the grand liveries, equipages, and silverware for which he was famed. However, our beer was stronger, and my wife's charities were perhaps more generous than those of the Dowager Lady Warrington. No doubt she believed there was no harm in "spoiling the Philistines"; for she charged us exorbitantly for the goods she left behind in our country house, and I submitted to most of her extortions with unutterable good humor. What value she placed on the potted plants in her greenhouses! What price she set on that dreadful old spinet in her drawing-room! And the framed pieces of worsted work created by the accomplished Dora and the lovely Flora—if they had been masterpieces of Titian or Van Dyck, surely the Dowager Lady could not have valued them higher. Despite paying so generously, and being, I may say without boasting, far kinder to the poor than she ever was, we initially carried the worst reputation in the county, surrounded by tales told to my discredit.

I hoped perhaps to inherit my uncle's seat in Parliament alongside his lands, but I discovered, somehow, that I was considered a person of dangerously radical opinions. I would not bribe voters, nor would I coerce my own tenants to support me in the 1668 election. A gentleman descended from Whitehall bearing a pocketbook full of banknotes approached me, revealing that I stood no chance against my competitor.

"Bon Dieu!" Now that we were secure financially, with obedient tenants bowing as we attended church, driving in our great family coach drawn by four fat horses to visit friends or neighboring towns, did we not often regret our former poverty and the dear little cottage at Lambeth, where Want constantly prowled the threshold? Did I not long to resume bear-leading, vowing that translating for booksellers was not such arduous drudgery? Upon returning to London, we made sentimental pilgrimages to our old haunts. My wife undoubtedly embraced every landlady we knew. You may be sure we invited all the friends of our youth to share the comforts of our new home. The Reverend Mr. Hagan and his lady visited us repeatedly. His appearance in the pulpit at B------, where he preached beautifully in our estimation, caused quite a scandal there. Sampson also came, another unfortunate Levite, welcomed as long as he chose to stay among us. Mr. Johnson spoke of visiting but delayed us once or twice. I suppose our house grew dull. I admit I would remain silent for days, fearing my moodiness often tried the sweetest-tempered woman in the world living with me. I cared little for field sports. Killing one partridge seemed indistinguishable from killing another; I marveled how men could spend days pursuing such slaughter. Their fox-hunting stories began at four o'clock, after the tablecloth was removed, and lasted until supper. I sat silent, listening; day after day, I fell asleep. No wonder I lacked popularity among my company.

What confession is this? Here was the storm passed, the rocks avoided, the ship safely in port, yet the sailor not entirely content? Was Susan, whom I sighed for during the voyage, not the beauty I expected to find?
