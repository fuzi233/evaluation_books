His wife’s dark blue brougham—wedding varnish still glinting soft and unmarred even under the sleet streaking the ferry ramp—met Archer at the dock, wrapping him in plush, hushing warmth as it bore him toward the Pennsylvania terminus in Jersey City. It was a sombre, snow-heavy afternoon, and hissing gas lamps already pooled gold light across the vast, echoing station as he paced the platform waiting for the Washington express. He half-remembered the wild talk of visionaries he’d heard at the club: that one day a tunnel would burrow under the Hudson, that ships would cross the Atlantic in five days flat, that flying machines, electric lighting, and wireless telephones would turn all their old Arabian Nights fables into fact. He could not find it in himself to care if any of those marvels came to pass, so long as the tunnel stayed unbuilt for today.

Drunk on a giddy, schoolboy glee that felt nothing like his usual staid self, he pictured every beat of their coming meeting: Ellen stepping down from the train, the crowd of strangers blurring away until only her face stood out, her arm slipping through his as he guided her to the carriage, the slow, careful crawl down the slippery wharf past skittish slipping horses, overloaded carts, and teamsters shouting hoarse orders, then the sudden hush of the ferry, their brougham sitting still on the deck as snow settled soft on its roof and the river slid under them, carrying them as if the whole world were rolling off toward the other side of the sun. There were a hundred perfect, eloquent things he ached to say to her, all lining up neat and burning on the tip of his tongue.

The train rumbled closer, clanging and groaning like some great overburdened beast staggering back to its lair, and Archer shoved through the throng, craning into every high carriage window until suddenly Ellen’s pale, surprised face was right before him, and that old familiar mortification pricked at him: he had, once again, forgotten exactly what she looked like, how the light caught the faint little freckle under her eye, how her mouth curved just so when she was caught off guard. They reached for each other, their hands lacing, and he drew her arm tight through his. “This way— I have the carriage waiting,” he said.

Everything unfolded exactly as he’d dreamed. He helped her heft her bags into the brougham’s footwell, babbled reassurances about her grandmother’s improving health, walked her through the sad, sordid details of the Beaufort collapse, and his chest ached soft when she breathed that quiet, gentle “Poor Regina!” The carriage worked its way free of the tangled traffic around the station, crawling down the icy wharf ramp past swaying coal carts, spooked horses, dishevelled express wagons, and a plain empty hearse lurching past. Ellen’s eyes flew shut, and she clutched his hand so tight her nails bit into his skin. “If only that doesn’t mean—poor Granny!”

“Oh no, no, she’s much better, she’s perfectly well, really—look, we’ve passed it now,” he hurried to soothe her, as if putting distance between them and the dark omen could erase every shadow that loomed over them. She did not let go of his hand, and when the carriage lurched across the gangplank onto the ferry he leaned over, unbuttoned the tight brown glove covering her other hand, and pressed a kiss to the soft, bare skin of her palm as if he were venerating a sacred relic. She pulled her hand back with a faint, knowing smile, and he blurted, “You never expected me today, did you?”

“Oh, no.”
“I was halfway to boarding a train for Washington to come see you. I’d made all the arrangements—we very nearly crossed paths on the rails.”
She gasped, as if that narrow escape was something far more terrifying than any accident.
“Do you know,” he went on, “I almost forgot what you looked like? Not that I don’t think of you every hour— it’s that it’s always like this. Every time you walk back into my life, it happens all over again, like I’m meeting you for the very first time.”
“Oh, I know,” she murmured. “It’s the same for me.”
“Am I new, every time, for you?” he insisted.
She nodded, her gaze drifting out the window at the snow streaking the dusk beyond the glass.

“Ellen—Ellen—Ellen,” he whispered, and she did not answer. He sat quiet, watching her profile blur against the darkening sky, wondering how little they really knew of each other after those four long, empty months apart. The precious minutes were slipping away fast, and every eloquent speech he’d rehearsed had vanished, leaving him only to brood over the strange, sharp magic of their proximity: sitting so close their shoulders nearly touched, and yet still separated by a thousand uncrossable miles of other people’s lives, other people’s rules.

“What a pretty carriage! Is it May’s?” she asked, suddenly turning back to him.
“Yes.”
“Then it was May who sent you to fetch me? How kind of her.”

The words stung, sharp as a slap, and the secret he’d buried, never meaning to speak aloud, burst out of him unbidden: “Your husband’s secretary, M. Rivière, came to see me the day after we met in Boston.” He’d meant to tuck that visit away forever, but the reminder that they were tucked inside his wife’s brand new wedding carriage prodded a petty, defensive anger out of him, and he wanted to see if she hated that reference to her old life half as much as he hated hers to May. But Ellen did not look the slightest bit surprised, and he immediately realized: Rivière must have written to her about it.
“M. Rivière came to see you?” she asked, simple as anything.
“Yes. Aren’t you shocked?”
She hesitated a beat. “Why would I be? He told me in Boston he knew you, that he’d met you in England once, years ago.”
“Ellen— I have to ask you one thing. I wanted to after I saw him, but I couldn’t put it in a letter. It was Rivière who helped you get away, when you left your husband, wasn’t it?” His heart was beating so hard he could barely breathe, waiting for her reaction.
“Yes. I owe him a great debt,” she answered, no tremor in her quiet, steady voice.

Her tone was so natural, so almost unconcerned, that all his roiling, defensive anger fizzled right out of him. Once again, her sheer unvarnished simplicity made him feel silly and wrapped up in stuffy convention, even when he thought he’d flung every last rule to the wind. “You are the most honest woman I have ever met,” he exclaimed.
“Oh no— but I’m probably one of the least fussy,” she laughed, soft in her throat.
“Call it what you like. You look at things exactly as they are.”
“Ah, I’ve had to. I’ve had to look the Gorgon right in the face.”
“Well, it didn’t blind you! You saw she’s just an old bogey, like all the rest of the monsters everyone’s scared of.”
“She doesn’t blind you. She just dries all your tears up.”

The answer stopped the pleas on his lips cold, coming from a depth of pain and experience he could not even fathom. The ferry lurched hard, its bows slamming against the slip piles so hard the brougham staggered, flinging Archer and Ellen against each other. He was trembling so hard he could barely move, his arm wrapping tight around her shoulders to steady her. “If you’re not blind, then you have to see that we can’t keep doing this. Being together, and not together. It can’t last.”
“What can’t?”
“You never should have come today,” she said, her voice changed, sharp with a quiet grief. And then she turned, flung her arms around his neck, and pressed her mouth to his in a kiss that burned right through all his carefully laid restraint. Just then the carriage jolted forward, a gas lamp at the slip’s mouth flooding gold light through the window, and she drew back, both of them sitting motionless and silent as the brougham inched through the tangled line of carriages leaving the ferry.

As they pulled out onto the street, Archer began to speak fast, urgent, like he was trying to convince both of them. “Don’t shrink back into your corner like that, you don’t have to be afraid of me. A stolen kiss in a carriage isn’t what I want. Look— I’m not even trying to touch your sleeve. I understand you don’t want whatever this is to dwindle into some sordid, hole-and-corner love affair, sneaking around behind everyone’s backs. I couldn’t have spoken like this yesterday, when all I could think of was the fire of wanting to see you. But now you’re here, and you’re so much more than I remembered, and what I want from you is so much more than a few snatched hours between weeks of thirsty, empty waiting, that I can sit perfectly still right beside you, holding that vision in my head, just quietly trusting it will come true.”

She was quiet for a long moment, then whispered so softly he barely heard it: “What do you mean, trusting it to come true?”
“You know. Us, together, for good, don’t you?”
“Your vision of you and me, running away?” She let out a sudden, hard, bitter laugh. “You chose a fine place to ask me that, didn’t you?”
“Because we’re in my wife’s brougham? We can get out and walk, if you like. A little snow won’t hurt us.”
She laughed again, softer this time. “No, I shan’t get out and walk. I need to get to Granny’s as fast as I possibly can. And you’ll sit beside me, and we’ll stop chasing visions, and look at the realities right in front of us.”
“I don’t know what you mean by realities. The only real thing in the world to me is this, right now, you and me.”

She was silent for a long stretch, as the carriage rumbled down a dim side street before bursting out into the bright, searching glow of Fifth Avenue’s gas lamps. “Is it your idea, then, that I should live with you as your mistress— since I can’t be your wife?” she asked, plain and unflinching.

The crudeness of the question shocked him. The word was one no woman in his polite, sheltered circle ever dared say out loud, even when they danced closest to the topic. He noticed Ellen said it like it was just any other word, and his stomach twisted, wondering how often it had been thrown at her, casually, in that cruel gilded prison she’d fled from. He floundered, fumbling for words. “I want— I want to run away with you to a world where words like that, categories like that, don’t exist. Where we’re just two people who love each other, who are the whole of life to each other, and nothing else on earth matters.”

She drew a long, shaky sigh that ended in a soft laugh. “Oh my dear— where is that country? Have you ever even been there? I know so many people who tried to find it, once. Believe me, they all got off by mistake at wayside stations: Boulogne, Pisa, Monte Carlo. It wasn’t any different from the old world they left. Just smaller, dingier, full of strangers judging you twice as hard.”
“The Gorgon really has dried all your tears up,” he said, quiet and hurt.
“She opened my eyes too. People say she blinds you, but it’s not true. She glues your eyelids open, so you can never sink back into that soft, blessed innocent darkness ever again. Isn’t there a Chinese torture that does that? There ought to be. Ah, believe me, that perfect little country you’re chasing is a very miserable, small place when you actually get there.”

The carriage had crossed Forty-second Street now, May’s sturdy brougham horse eating up the pavement northward like a champion Kentucky trotter. Archer choked on the sense of wasted minutes, of all his pretty dreams dissolving into nothing. “Then what, exactly, is your plan for us?”
“For *us*? But there is no *us*, in that sense! The only way we can stay close to the parts of each other that are real, that are good, is if we stay far away from each other. If we don’t, we’re just Newland Archer, the husband of Ellen Olenska’s cousin, and Ellen Olenska, the cousin of Newland Archer’s wife, sneaking around behind the backs of the people who trust us, making fools of ourselves.”
“Ah, I’m past all that,” he groaned.
“No you’re not. You’ve never been past it. But I have,” she said, in a strange, hollow voice, “and I know exactly what it looks like there.”

He sat dazed, heavy with a wordless, gnawing pain, fumbling in the dark of the carriage for the little call bell that signalled the coachman to stop. He remembered May always rang it twice to pull over. He pressed the bell, and the carriage drew up sharp to the curb.
“Why are we stopping? This isn’t Granny’s,” Ellen exclaimed, confused.
“No. I’m getting out here,” he stammered, yanking the door open and jumping down onto the cold pavement. He saw her startled face in the glow of the street lamp, her half-moved hand reaching out to detain him. He closed the door, leaned in the window for a second, voice dropping low so the coachman wouldn’t overhear. “You’re right. I never should have come today.” She leaned forward, about to speak, but he’d already called out the order to drive on, and the carriage rumbled away, leaving him standing on the empty corner.

The snow had stopped, and a sharp, stinging wind had sprung up, lashing his face raw. He felt something stiff and cold on his lashes, and realized he was crying, the wind freezing his tears right to his skin. He thrust his hands deep in his coat pockets, and set off at a fast, sharp pace down Fifth Avenue, back toward his empty house.
