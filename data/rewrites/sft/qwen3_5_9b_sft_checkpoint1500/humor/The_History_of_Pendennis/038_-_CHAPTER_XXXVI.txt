The new journal, *The Pall Mall Gazette*, launched with a flourish that seemed to promise empire. Rumors swirled like smoke behind a carriage wheel—an "influential political party" backing the paper, and a roster of contributors heavy with "great names." Whether these tales were true remained a mystery locked behind the editor's door, but the truth, as it turned out, was far more theatrical than the gossip suggested. That stately article on foreign policy, whispered to be the brainchild of a Noble Lord intimately tied to the Foreign Office, was actually penned by Captain Shandon, slumped comfortably in a leather armchair at the Bear and Staff public house near Whitehall Stairs. The printer's boy, acting as a detective, had tracked him down, joining forces with Shandon's temporary resident, the literary Mr. Bludyer. Similarly, the fiery dispatches on finance, universally attributed to a towering figure in the House of Commons, were the work of Mr. George Warrington of the Upper Temple, a man whose pen was sharper than his sword.

Perhaps the paper did dabble with powerful patrons; Percy Popjoy, son of the Lord Falconet, was frequently spotted climbing the stairs to Warrington's chambers, suggesting a flow of information that smelled distinctly of intrigue. The *Gazette* also became a haven for poetry that was weak in thought but thunderous in delivery, signed with the initials "P.P." These verses, alongside a review of Percy's novel that praised it with an outrageously gushing fervor, set the tone for a publication unafraid to shout.

Yet, in the political trenches, Mr. Pen kept his distance, choosing instead to throw himself into the literary fray. With offices nestled in Catherine Street, Strand, the *Gazette* became Pen's playground. He arrived daily with manuscripts tucked safely in his pockets, buzzing with the electric thrill of a debutant entering a ballroom—the dizzying sensation of seeing one's words printed on page, the arrogant belief that the ink was already causing a ripple in the pond of the world.

At the helm of the operations sat Mr. Jack Finucane, the sub-editor, a man who treated journalism with the casual brutality of a butcher at a market stall. Armed with paste and scissors, he was the architect of the *Journal*. His eagle eye swept the kingdom, devouring every paragraph concerning fashion, from the death of a duke to the menu of a midnight supper. He ferreted out scandalous tidbits from obscure provincial broadsheets and distant Scottish and Irish dailies, weaving them into a tapestry of upper-class intrigue. It was a comically poignant image: Jack Finucane, Esquire, dressed in trousers so tattered they resembled a bird's nest and sleeves rolled up to reveal skin stained with porter and grease, sitting on a stool with a slab of meat and a tankard of dark beer. Yet, he recounted the feasts of ambassadors and the galas of the elite with the gravitas of a court historian, describing the brilliance of the season as if he had been dining at the center of it all. This absurd dissonance between his ragged attire and his grandiose descriptions amused Pen immensely. Having fled his humble native village, Jack knew little of the world outside the tavern parlors he frequented, yet his writing made it look as though he lived in the bow-windows of White's Club. While occasional errors crept into his copy, the London chiefs wisely restricted his contributions to the *Ballinafad Sentinel*, leaving the delicate *Gazette* to his colleagues' careful hands.

Pen, meanwhile, applied himself with the zeal of a convert. Possessing a mind for desultory reading and a sharp, mischievous sense of humor, his reviews delighted both his boss and the paying public. He prided himself on earning his keep, and the *Gazette* found a loyal readership among the ladies at Fairoaks and the literary-minded young spinster at Clavering Park. Even Old Doctor Portman, the widow's stern guardian, offered his seal of approval, declaring that the lad possessed spirit, taste, and the air of a gentleman, if not quite the scholarship of a university professor.

The real turning point for Pen came when he stepped into the Regent Club. There, amidst the clinking of glasses and the puffing of cigars, he walked in on a gathering of the titled and the wealthy: Wenham, Lord Falconet, and others of high standing. They were not discussing state affairs or horse racing; they were dissecting the latest issue of the *Pall Mall Gazette*. Specifically, they were laughing over an article by "Pen" that savaged the recent travelogue of the Countess of Muffborough. Written in a mix of French and English, her book was a mess of blunders, and Pen, with the joy of a schoolboy finding a typo in a teacher's essay, had torn it apart with delightful mockery. He danced around his subject, exposing the Countess's faults with a mock solemnity that was nothing short of surgical. Every word was polite, every insult wrapped in velvet, yet the Countess was laid low.

Wenham's face, usually a mask of bilious indifference, twisted with malicious glee as he read the critique. Lord Falconet, who hadn't been invited to the Countess's parties in a year, roared with laughter. Lord Muffborough, Wenham's lifelong rival, joined in, and together they toasted Pen. For the first time, the Major felt a strange warmth in his chest. Until then, the letters from Fairoaks warning of "Arthur's constant and severe literary occupations" undermining the boy's health had seemed beneath his dignity. But here, surrounded by the great, Pen was celebrated. When the oracle Wenham pronounced the boy a genius, and Lord Falconet nodded in agreement, and even the notorious Lord Steyne declared the piece "capital"—promising the Countess would writh like a whale under a harpoon—the Major's attitude shifted instantly. "By gad!" he boomed, "the young rascal had stuff in him! He'll do something!" Trembling with pride, he sat down to write to the widow, relaying the accolades, and then addressed Pen directly, inviting him to eat chops and promising an introduction to Lord Steyne, who, the Major noted, enjoyed all manner of human follies, wits, and oddities equally. Pen tossed the letter to Warrington, wondering if the other man cared as much as he did.

Young critics, it seems, possess a prodigious courage. They climb the judgment seat as if it were a ladder to heaven and deliver verdicts on works they barely understand. If Pen had been presented with Macaulay's *History* or Herschel's *Astronomy* at this moment, he likely would have glanced through the volumes, lit a cigar, and given an "august approval" as if he were their superior master. With the aid of the *Biographie Universelle* or a quick dip into the British Museum, he could recite dates and names with a mastery that would leave his mother gaping, wondering where the knowledge came from. Two months later, when he read those articles again, the memory of the books would vanish, replaced by a vague confidence. Fortunately, Warrington stood ready to curb this impertinence with a steady dose of ridicule. Without him, Pen might have become a conceit-ridden tyrant of the pen. Shandon, for his part, loved the dash and flippancy of his young aide-de-camp, preferring Pen's light, brilliant flashes over the heavy, ponderous arguments of his older partner.

Though guilty of premature judgment and impertinence, Pen was, above all, an honest critic—a trait that grated on Mr. Bungay, who grumbled bitterly at the lack of bias. A dispute erupted one afternoon between Pen and Captain Shandon. "What have you been doing?" Shandon demanded, his voice rising. "Praising one of Mr. Bacon's books? Bungay is in a frenzy over it!"

Pen's eyes widened. "Are we to praise no book of Bacon's? Or if it is good, must we call it bad?"

"Benevolent publishers do not start critical journals to harm their rivals," Shandon retorted, leaning back. "To benefit themselves, yes, but surely not to lie?"

"To tell the truth, Captain, *ruat coelum*!" Pen insisted.

"And my prospectus?" Shandon sneered. "Was that written with mathematical precision?"

"Pardon me," Pen replied, laughing. "I admit the prospectus was poetic and the 'giant' on the showboard looked larger than the man inside the caravan. But we agreed we need not be too scrupulous. We are the fiddlers playing our tunes; you are the showman."

"And leader of the van," Shandon agreed. "I'm glad your conscience allowed you to play for us."

"But," Pen interjected, straightening his spine with a sense of noble duty, "we are all party men in England. I will stick to my side like a Briton. A fool quarrels with his own nest, but I will hit the enemy as hard as you wish—with fair play, Captain! One cannot tell all the truth, I grant you, but one must tell *nothing* but the truth. I'd rather starve by Jove, never to earn another penny by this redoubted instrument of mine, than strike an unfair blow or rank an opponent below his honest desert."
