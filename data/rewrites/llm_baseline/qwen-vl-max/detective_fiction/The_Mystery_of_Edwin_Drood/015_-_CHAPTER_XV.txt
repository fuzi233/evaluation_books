Neville Landless had set out early and walked briskly, so that when the church bells of Cloisterham rang for morning service, he was already eight miles distant. By then, his hunger had grown sharp—having started with only a crust of bread—and he stopped at the nearest roadside inn, The Tilted Wagon, to eat.

The place was so seldom visited by travelers—unless they were horses or cattle, for whom troughs and hay were always ready—that it took a long while to rouse the kitchen into producing tea, toast, and bacon. In the meantime, Neville sat in a dusty, sanded parlour, watching the damp fagots in the fireplace struggle to catch flame, wondering how soon someone else might benefit from their weak heat.

The Tilted Wagon, perched high on a hill, lived up to its name in more ways than one. The ground before the door was muddied with hoofprints and trodden straw. Inside, the landlady scolded a baby—one red sock on, the other missing—while the cheese rested in a rusted iron canoe beside a mouldy tablecloth and a green-handled knife. Bread, pale and weeping crumbs, lay in another such vessel. Linen, half-washed and half-dried, trailed across the floor like discarded rags. Everything drank from mugs; everything else seemed to rhyme with “mug.” It was not a place of comfort, nor even decent lodging—but Neville, having no choice, accepted what was offered and resumed his journey after a longer rest than necessary.

A quarter-mile beyond the inn, he paused, uncertain whether to continue along the main road or take a narrow cart track between tall hedges that cut across a breezy heath. The latter route seemed shorter, though rougher. He chose it, walking with effort as the path rose steeply and sank into deep ruts.

Halfway along, he heard footsteps behind him. As they quickened, he stepped aside against the bank to let them pass. But their behavior was odd. Four men passed swiftly. Another four slowed, lingering as if waiting for him to move on. The remaining half-dozen turned abruptly and ran back.

He glanced at the four ahead, then at those behind. All returned his gaze without expression.

He continued forward. The four ahead kept pace, glancing back constantly. The four behind closed in.

When they emerged onto the open slope of the heath, and still maintained this formation—no matter which way he tried to veer—it became clear: he was surrounded.

He stopped. They stopped too.

"Why do you follow me like this?" he demanded. "Are you thieves?"

"Silence," said one voice, low and sharp. "Better be quiet."

"Better be quiet?" Neville repeated, anger rising. "Who spoke?"

No answer.

"It’s good advice, whoever gave it," he snapped. "But I won’t stand here trapped between four men ahead and four behind. I’m going through, and I’ll go through—those four ahead!"

They stood motionless.

"If eight men—or four, or two—attack one," he went on, fists clenched, "the one has no chance but to strike back. And by God, I will—if I’m not left alone!"

He shouldered his heavy stick and charged past the front group.

One man—large, strong, swift—moved sideways, seized him, and brought him down. But not before the stick struck hard.

"Let him go!" the man muttered, voice strained. "Fair play! He’s built like a girl, and he’s got weight on his back. Let him be—I’ll handle him."

They wrestled briefly, rolling in the grass, both faces streaked with blood. Then the man pushed off and rose.

"There. Take him arm-in-arm—any two of you."

It was done immediately.

"As to being thieves, Mr. Landless," the man said, spitting blood and wiping his face, "you know better. We wouldn’t have touched you unless you forced us. We’re taking you to the high road. You’ll find help there if you need it.—Wipe his face, someone. Look at the blood!"

Once cleaned, Neville recognized the speaker: Joe, the driver of the Cloisterhad omnibus, whom he’d seen once before—on the day of his arrival.

"And now," Joe added, "don’t speak. There’s a friend waiting for you at the road—we split up earlier. Say nothing until you reach him. Bring the stick, someone. Let’s go."

Utterly confused, Neville said nothing. Between two men who gripped his arms, he walked forward like a man in a trance—until they reached the high road and found a small gathering.

The men who had turned back were among them. At the center stood Mr. Jasper and Mr. Crisparkle.

His captors led him to the Minor Canon and released him, as a sign of respect.

"What is this?" Neville cried, stunned. "What happened? I feel as if I’ve lost my mind!"

"Where is my nephew?" Jasper asked, voice raw with panic.

"Where is your nephew?" Neville echoed. "Why ask me?"

"Because you were the last person with him," Jasper shot back. "And he’s vanished."

"Vanished?" Neville gasped.

"Wait," said Mr. Crisparkle. "Mr. Landless, you're shaken. Collect yourself. This matters greatly. Listen to me."

"I'll try," Neville whispered. "But I feel mad."

"You left Mr. Jasper last night with Edwin Drood?"

"Yes."

"At what hour?"

"Was it twelve?" Neville asked, hand to his head, turning to Jasper.

"Exactly," Crisparkle confirmed. "That's the time Mr. Jasper told me. You went down to the river together?"

"Undoubtedly. To watch the wind."

"How long did you stay?"

"Ten minutes—no more. Then we walked to your house. He left me at the door."

"Did he say he was returning to the river?"

"No. He said he was going straight home."

The bystanders exchanged glances.

Jasper, who had been watching Neville closely, suddenly leaned forward.

"What are those stains on your clothes?" he asked, voice low and sharp.

All eyes turned to the blood on Neville’s coat.

"And look here!" Jasper said, snatching the stick from the man holding it. "Same marks! I know this stick—it’s yours. You carried it last night. What does this mean?"

"In God’s name, tell us!" Crisparkle urged.

"That man and I," Neville said, pointing to his attacker, "fought over the stick just now. You can see the same marks on him. What was I supposed to think, when eight strangers attacked me? Could I guess the real reason when they gave none?"

They admitted they'd stayed silent—thought it wiser. And yes, the fight had happened.

Yet even those who had witnessed it now stared darkly at the dried smears on the clothes.

"We must return," Crisparkle said. "You’ll come back to clear your name, of course?"

"Of course, sir."

"Mr. Landless will walk beside me," Crisparkle announced, looking around. "Come, Neville."

They began the walk back. The others followed at varying distances—except Jasper, who walked on Neville’s other side and never moved.

He remained silent throughout. Crisparkle repeated his questions. Neville repeated his answers. They offered theories. But Jasper said nothing.

Not because he didn’t hear. But because Crisparkle’s tone invited participation—and Jasper refused to respond. His face remained fixed, unreadable.

As they neared the city, Crisparkle suggested calling on the Mayor immediately.

Jasper nodded stiffly. Still, he said nothing—until they entered Mr. Sapsea’s parlor.

Crisparkle explained their purpose: a voluntary statement under suspicion.

Then Jasper broke silence.

"I place my full trust—humanly speaking—in Mr. Sapsea’s judgment," he said. "There is no conceivable reason why my nephew would flee, unless you suggest one. Then I defer. No rational likelihood exists that he returned to the river and drowned—unless you think otherwise. Then again, I defer. I wash my hands of all dreadful suspicions—unless you prove they are unavoidable, given my last companion before his disappearance (with whom I was not on good terms). Then, once more, I defer. My own mind is shattered—overwhelmed with doubt. But yours, Mr. Sapsea, is steady."

Sapsea nodded slowly. "This case," he declared, "has a dark look. An un-English complexion." He paused, eyes locking with Neville’s.

Then he drifted into rambling nonsense—more absurd than even a mayor might be expected to utter—before emerging with the revelation: "To take a life is to take something that doesn’t belong to you."

He hesitated—whether to issue a warrant for Neville’s arrest. But Crisparkle protested fiercely, vouching for the young man’s presence in his own home, and promising to produce him whenever required.

Sapsea relented.

Instead, he proposed dragging the river, searching its banks, sending notices to surrounding towns and London, posting placards begging Edwin Drood—should he have vanished for any unknown reason—to spare his uncle’s grief and let him know he was alive.

All agreed. Plans were made instantly.

No one knew who was more horrified: Neville Landless or John Jasper.

But Jasper’s position forced action; Neville’s, silence. Yet both were broken.

At dawn, searchers began work on the river—men in barges with poles and nets, others ashore with hatchets, spades, ropes, dogs. Jack-boots sank into mud. Rushes were torn apart. Watermarks stood like ghosts.

At night, lanterns lit the water. Fires burned along creeks. Watchers waited at shingly causeways, listening for the tide’s whisper, hoping for a body.

But no trace of Edwin Drood surfaced.

Day after day, the search continued—on river and shore—yet nothing.

Jasper worked tirelessly, exhausted, muddy, his clothes torn. When night fell, he returned home—drenched in fatigue.

Just as he collapsed into his chair, Mr. Grewgious appeared.

"This is strange news," Grewgious said. "Strange and fearful."

Jasper merely lifted his eyes—then dropped them again, slumping over the armrest.

Grewgious smoothed his brow, staring into the fire.

"How is your ward?" Jasper asked faintly.

"Poor little thing. You can imagine her state."

"Have you seen his sister?" Jasper asked.

"Whose?"

The abruptness, the slow turn of Grewgious’s gaze from the fire to Jasper’s face—might have angered another man. But Jasper, worn out, simply opened his eyes.

"The suspected young man’s."

"Do you suspect him?" Grewgious asked.

"I don’t know what to think. I cannot decide."

"Nor I," Grewgious replied. "But since you called him 'the suspected young man,' I thought you had made up your mind. I’ve just left Miss Landless."

"What is her condition?"

"Defiant of all suspicion. Unshakable faith in her brother."

"Poor thing."

"But that’s not why I came," Grewgious continued. "It’s about my ward. I have news that will surprise you. At least, it surprised me."

Jasper groaned, turning in his chair.

"Shall I wait till tomorrow?" Grewgious asked. "I warn you—it will surprise you."

Now, a flicker of attention sparked in Jasper’s eyes as he saw Grewgious smooth his head again, fix his gaze on the fire—mouth tight, jaw clenched.

"What is it?" Jasper demanded, sitting upright.

"To be sure," Grewgious said, slowly, deliberately, warming his palms at the fire, "I might have known sooner. She gave me the clue. But I’m such an exceedingly angular man, I took everything for granted."

"What is it?" Jasper repeated.

Grewgious, still warming his hands, still staring sideways at him, continued:

"This young couple—the lost youth and Miss Rosa, my ward—though betrothed for years, and nearly married—"

Jasper saw a white face, trembling lips, muddy hands gripping the chair.

"—came gradually to realize," Grewgious went on, "that they would be happier, healthier, as friends—brother and sister—than as husband and wife."

Jasper saw a leaden face, drops of sweat like steel beads.

"They resolved, finally, to end their engagement—openly, gently, with kindness. They met. After honest talk, they agreed to part forever."

Grewgious saw a ghostly figure rise from the chair, mouth agape, hands flung toward his head.

"One of them—your nephew—feared you’d be heartbroken by such a change. So he kept it secret for a few days. He meant for me to reveal it when I came to speak with you—when he would be gone."

Grewgious saw the figure throw back its head, clutch its hair, twist away.

"I speak to you," he said, "and he is gone."

He paused.

"They parted—firmly, yet with tears—on the very evening you last saw them together."

A scream tore from the room.

Grewgious looked down.

No man. No figure.

Only a heap of torn, muddy clothes on the floor.

Still, he did not move. Still, he opened and shut his palms at the fire. Still, he stared.
