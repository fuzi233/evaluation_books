The night had a kitchen-blackness to it, as if some immense hand had turned an iron pot over the sky and left it there to steam. Rain pressed down in steady sheets; whatever stars or moon might have been abroad were smothered under cloud—cloud that, by day, would have looked only leaden, but now showed as a deep, wet soot. Mr. Malone went on through it with the stubborn gait of a man who measures weather only by how soon it soaks his cuffs. He did not pause to see what the heavens were doing; he was not one of those who can read comfort or warning in the change of air. His mind kept to landmarks as a cook keeps to the clock: Briarfield Church spire, then, beyond, the scattered lights of Redhouse.

His boots made their own music—“tramp” where flagstones saved him, “splash” where the cart-ruts held rain like basins. The soundscape of the lane was all close, dull, and sodden: the hush of saturated hedges, the suction of mud, the far, faint throb of Stilbro’ furnaces throwing a lurid, tremulous smear on the eastern rim of cloud. That industrial glow did not warm; it only hinted of heat locked away behind brick and iron, and of labour that never truly slept.

At Redhouse, the world briefly offered him comfort. Through a half-curtained window he caught the orange wink of a fire and the gleam of glasses on a round table; he could almost taste the sharp, consoling bite of whisky cut with water—almost hear the hearty clang of voices over oak and ale. The door might have opened on a bright pocket of human warmth, but those men inside were Mr. Helstone’s parishioners; they knew their curate. Malone’s thirst was real, yet his prudence was stronger. He sighed once—an Irish sigh that was half humour, half resignation—and went on.

The highroad fell away behind him. He took the short cut across fields, leaping hedge and wall with the heedlessness of a big man in a hurry. The land lay level, long, and monotonous, the kind of ground that gives no shelter and no variety, only darkness and the steady patter of rain. One building rose out of it—large, hall-like, and irregular: a high gable, then a long stretch of front, then a lower gable, and a thick, towering stack of chimneys. Trees stood behind like a black congregation. No candle showed; no voice answered the wind. Only the thin, persistent trickle of water from eaves and a low whistle round stone and bough made the place audible. It had the sealed silence of a house holding its breath.

Beyond it the ground dipped, steeply and suddenly, into a hollow where a stream could be heard running swollen and quick, a restless ribbon of sound. One light burned below—small, steady, human. Malone steered to it as to a hearth.

He reached a little house, white even in that thick darkness, and knocked. A fresh-faced servant opened, candle in hand. The flame made a narrow passage visible—clean walls, white floor, and a strip of crimson carpet climbing a cramped stair. Two doors, both faced with crimson baize, gave the place a neatness that felt almost defiant against the night outside.

“Mr. Moore is at home, I suppose?” Malone asked, shaking rain from his shoulders.

“Yes, sir; but he is not in.”

“Not in! Where is he then?”

“At the mill—i’ th’ counting-house.”

At that, one crimson door opened, and a woman’s voice spoke from within with brisk household authority. “Are the wagons come, Sarah?” A female head appeared too; curl-papers at either temple made plain she had been interrupted in the middle of some domestic campaign. She was no monster, but Malone started as if he had surprised a sentinel. Big as he was, he retreated bashfully into the wet, muttered, “I’ll go to him,” and hurried down the short lane and across the obscure yard towards the hulking black mass of the mill.

Work was over; the hands were gone. The great building sat shut and blind, its machinery stilled, its windows dark. Yet the hollow held sound: the rush of the stream, the gusts scraping over stone, and now and then a faint, metallic clink from somewhere unseen—as if iron still remembered its own motion. Malone walked round the sooty flank until he found a chink of lamplight. He beat a lively tattoo on the door with the thick end of his shillelagh.

A key turned. The door opened.

“Is it Joe Scott?” called a voice from within, sharp with expectation. “What news o’ t’ wagons, Joe?”

“No; it’s myself. Mr. Helstone would send me.”

“Oh! Mr. Malone.” The name, spoken, fell a shade flatter than hope had meant it to. After the briefest pause, the same voice, polite and a little formal, added, “I beg you will come in, Mr. Malone. I regret extremely Mr. Helstone should have thought it necessary to trouble you so far. There was no necessity—I told him so—and on such a night; but walk forwards.”

Malone followed through a dark room whose shape he could not make out, and into a brighter inner place that struck his rain-strained eyes like a sudden hearth. A good fire burned behind bars, red and frank; a lamp of elegant make shone vivid on a table. Beyond those, the room was plain almost to severity: bare boards underfoot, no carpet; three or four stiff-backed chairs painted green, with the look of old farm-kitchen furniture; a solid desk; and on the stone-coloured walls, framed sheets—plans and drawings for building, gardening, machinery—lines and measurements where other men would have hung landscapes.

Moore took Malone’s wet coat and hat and bade him hang them. Malone, with relief, drew a chair to the grate and set his knees so near the heat that it licked the damp out of his stockings.

“Comfortable quarters you have here, Mr. Moore; and all snug to yourself.”

“Yes; but my sister would be glad to see you, if you would prefer stepping into the house.”

“Oh no. The ladies are best alone. I never was a lady’s man. You don’t mistake me for my friend Sweeting, do you, Mr. Moore?”

“Sweeting? Which of them is that? The gentleman in the chocolate overcoat, or the little gentleman?”

“The little one—he of Nunnely; the cavalier of the Misses Sykes, with the whole six of whom he is in love, ha! ha!”

“Better be generally in love with all than specially with one, I should think, in that quarter.”

“But he is specially in love with one besides; for when I and Donne urged him to make a choice amongst the fair bevy, he named—which do you think?”

A queer, quiet smile went over Moore’s mouth. “Dora, of course, or Harriet.”

“Ha! ha! you’ve an excellent guess. But what made you hit on those two?”

“Because they are the tallest, the handsomest, and Dora, at least, is the stoutest; and as your friend Mr. Sweeting is but a little slight figure, I concluded that, according to a frequent rule in such cases, he preferred his contrast.”

“You are right; Dora it is. But he has no chance, has he, Moore?”

“What has Mr. Sweeting besides his curacy?”

The question delighted Malone; laughter shook him for a full three minutes, as if the mere thought of Sweeting’s possessions were a joke too rich to swallow quickly.

“What has Sweeting? Why, David has his harp, or flute, which comes to the same thing. He has a sort of pinchbeck watch; ditto, ring; ditto, eyeglass. That’s what he has.”

“How would he propose to keep Miss Sykes in gowns only?”

“Ha! ha! Excellent! I’ll ask him that next time I see him. I’ll roast him for his presumption. But no doubt he expects old Christopher Sykes would do something handsome. He is rich, is he not? They live in a large house.”

“Sykes carries on an extensive concern.”

“Therefore he must be wealthy, eh?”

“Therefore he must have plenty to do with his wealth, and in these times would be about as likely to think of drawing money from the business to give dowries to his daughters as I should be to dream of pulling down the cottage there, and constructing on its ruins a house as large as Fieldhead.”

“Do you know what I heard, Moore, the other day?”

“No. Perhaps that I was about to effect some such change. Your Briarfield gossips are capable of saying that—or sillier things.”

“That you were going to take Fieldhead on a lease (I thought it looked a dismal place, by-the-bye, to-night, as I passed it), and that it was your intention to settle a Miss Sykes there as mistress—to be married, in short, ha! ha! Now, which is it? Dora, I am sure. You said she was the handsomest.”

Moore’s face hardened—not with anger, but with weary contempt, as if the same stale dish were set before him again and again. “I wonder how often it has been settled that I was to be married since I came to Briarfield. They have assigned me every marriageable single woman by turns in the district. Now it was the two Misses Wynns—first the dark, then the light one; now the red-haired Miss Armitage; then the mature Ann Pearson. At present you throw on my shoulders all the tribe of the Misses Sykes. On what grounds this gossip rests God knows. I visit nowhere; I seek female society about as assiduously as you do, Mr. Malone. If ever I go to Whinbury, it is only to give Sykes or Pearson a call in their counting-house, where our discussions run on other topics than matrimony, and our thoughts are occupied with other things than courtships, establishments, dowries. The cloth we can’t sell, the hands we can’t employ, the mills we can’t run, the perverse course of events generally, which we cannot alter, fill our hearts, I take it, pretty well at present, to the tolerably complete exclusion of such figments as love-making, etc.”

“I go along with you completely, Moore. If there is one notion I hate more than another, it is that of marriage—I mean marriage in the vulgar weak sense, as a mere matter of sentiment—two beggarly fools agreeing to unite their indigence by some fantastic tie of feeling. Humbug! But an advantageous connection, such as can be formed in consonance with dignity of views and permanency of solid interests, is not so bad—eh?”

“No,” Moore answered, and even that syllable sounded borrowed. He sat gazing into the fire as if the coal-glow were a ledger he could read. The room’s noises were simple: the crack of wood, the soft simmer of heat in the grate, rain ticking at the panes. Beyond, the hollow kept its own watery hush. Suddenly Moore lifted his head.

“Hark!” he said. “Did you hear wheels?”

He went to the window, opened it, and listened. The rain rushed in colder and the wind rose to meet it; from the stream came a quicker, more urgent rush. He closed the sash. “It is only the sound of the wind rising,” he remarked, “and the rivulet a little swollen, rushing down the hollow. I expected those wagons at six; it is near nine now.”

“Seriously, do you suppose that the putting up of this new machinery will bring you into danger?” Malone asked. “Helstone seems to think it will.”

Moore’s eyes steadied, intent as if he had sighted something in the dark. “I only wish the machines—the frames—were safe here, and lodged within the walls of this mill. Once put up, I defy the frame-breakers. Let them only pay me a visit and take the consequences. My mill is my castle.”

“One despises such low scoundrels,” Malone observed, with weighty morality. “I almost wish a party would call upon you to-night; but the road seemed extremely quiet as I came along. I saw nothing astir.”

“You came by the Redhouse?”

“Yes.”

“There would be nothing on that road. It is in the direction of Stilbro’ the risk lies.”

“And you think there is risk?”

“What these fellows have done to others they may do to me. There is only this difference: most of the manufacturers seem paralyzed when they are attacked. Sykes, for instance, when his dressing-shop was set on fire and burned to the ground, when the cloth was torn from his tenters and left in shreds in the field, took no steps to discover or punish the miscreants: he gave up as tamely as a rabbit under the jaws of a ferret. Now I, if I know myself, should stand by my trade, my mill, and my machinery.”

“Helstone says these three are your gods; that the ‘Orders in Council’ are with you another name for the seven deadly sins; that Castlereagh is your Antichrist, and the war-party his legions.”

“Yes; I abhor all these things because they ruin me. They stand in my way. I cannot get on. I cannot execute my plans because of them. I see myself baffled at every turn by their untoward effects.”

“But you are rich and thriving, Moore?”

“I am very rich in cloth I cannot sell. You should step into my warehouse yonder, and observe how it is piled to the roof with pieces. Roakes and Pearson are in the same condition. America used to be their market, but the Orders in Council have cut that off.”

Malone, who could relish a hot drink better than a discussion of tariffs, knocked his boot-heels together and yawned. Moore, absorbed in his own vexations, did not notice.

“And then to think,” Moore went on, impatience gathering like steam under a lid, “to think that these ridiculous gossips of Whinbury and Briarfield will keep pestering one about being married! As if there was nothing to be done in life but to ‘pay attention,’ as they say, to some young lady, and then to go to church with her, and then to start on a bridal tour, and then to run through a round of visits, and then, I suppose, to be ‘having a family.’ Oh, que le diable emporte!” He broke off, then added more quietly, “I believe women talk and think only of these things, and they naturally fancy men’s minds similarly occupied.”

“Of course—of course,” Malone agreed, whistling, turning his head as if he could sniff out what the room lacked. Restlessness sat on him; the damp in his bones cried out for warmth of another kind.

This time Moore understood. “Mr. Malone,” he said, “you must require refreshment after your wet walk. I forget hospitality.”

“Not at all,” Malone replied; but his face brightened as if the right match had finally been struck.

“It is my fancy,” Moore continued, rising to a cupboard, “to have every convenience within myself, and not to be dependent on the feminity in the cottage yonder for every mouthful I eat or every drop I drink. I often spend the evening and sup here alone, and sleep with Joe Scott in the mill. Sometimes I am my own watchman. I require little sleep, and it pleases me on a fine night to wander for an hour or two with my musket about the hollow. Mr. Malone, can you cook a mutton chop?”

“Try me. I’ve done it hundreds of times at college.”

“There’s a dishful, then, and there’s the gridiron. Turn them quickly. You know the secret of keeping the juices in?”

“Never fear me; you shall see. Hand a knife and fork, please.”

Malone turned up his coat-cuffs with the seriousness of a man entering sacred office and set the chops upon the iron. At once the room filled with a livelier life: fat began to hiss and spit, sending up a smell at once animal and appetising—hot iron, searing meat, the faint sweetness of browning. He watched the surface change, coaxing colour rather than scorching it, keeping the heat brisk so the outside might seal and the inside stay moist. The rain’s chill seemed to fall back before that savoury steam.

Moore laid out supper with the method of one accustomed to serve himself. Plates went on the table; a loaf of bread; a black bottle; two tumblers. From the same well-stocked cupboard he drew a small copper kettle, filled it from a large stone jar standing in the corner, and set it to the fire beside the gridiron. Then came lemons, sugar, and a little china punch-bowl—bright domestic implements in that bare, businesslike room, as if the senses had insisted on their own rights amid ledgers and plans.

While he was brewing the punch, there came a tap at the door.

“Is it you, Sarah?”

“Yes, sir. Will you come to supper, please, sir?”

“No; I shall not be in to-night; I shall sleep in the mill. So lock the doors, and tell your mistress to go to bed.”

He returned to the table. Malone, his fine face ruddy with heat, bent over the gridiron like a man in love with his own competence. “You have your household in proper order,” he observed approvingly. “You are not under petticoat government, like poor Sweeting, a man—whew! how the fat spits! it has burnt my hand—destined to be ruled by women. Now you and I, Moore—there’s a fine brown one for you, and full of gravy—you and I will have no gray mares in our stables when we marry.”

“I don’t know; I never think about it,” Moore said, with that same distant air. “If the gray mare is handsome and tractable, why not?”

“The chops are done,” Malone declared, lifting them off at the right moment, when the edges were crisped brown and the centre promised tenderness. “Is the punch brewed?”

“There is a glassful. Taste it. When Joe Scott and his minions return they shall have a share of this, provided they bring home the frames intact.”

Malone tasted, approved, and grew exultant over the meal. His spirits rose with the heat and the liquor; he laughed too loudly at trifles, made jokes as thin as water-gruel and praised them as if they were feasts. He ate with the appetite of a man wrung out by weather—bread torn by hand, meat still giving up its juices, the punch sharp with lemon and softened by sugar, warming a track down his throat and into his chest.

Moore remained quiet, his attention often slipping away to the window and the thought of the delayed wagons. By firelight, he showed as a man made more of edge than of breadth—thin, dark, sallow, with shadowy hair streaking his forehead as carelessly as rain streaked the pane. He looked as if he spent little time at a mirror, and less at ease in himself than in his work. Only by degrees did the fineness of his face appear: a southern regularity in the lines of brow and cheek, disturbed by hollowness and an anxious cast that made beauty seem an accident under pressure. His eyes were large, grave, and grey—searching eyes, more accustomed to reckon and plan than to soften. When he smiled, it did not turn him frank or merry; it only let through a sedate charm, a suggestion—true or false—of patience and forbearing fidelity, qualities that might wear well in a home if ever he allowed himself one. He was young still, not much beyond thirty, tall and slender. Yet his voice, when he spoke, displeased a Yorkshire ear: a foreign accent sat upon his English, and though he handled diction with studied carelessness, the sound of it grated as an imported tool grates against native stone.

He was, in fact, only half British, and scarcely that. His mother’s blood ran from abroad; he himself had been born and partly reared on foreign soil. A hybrid in nature, he had a hybrid’s feeling—unapt to cleave to party or sect, unready to call any place wholly home, inclined to set Robert Gérard Moore apart from the communities among which his lot moved, and to push that man’s interests with a hard concentration that left little room for general claims. Trade was hereditary in him: the Gérards of Antwerp had been merchants for two hundred years. Once they had been wealthy; then business uncertainties had undermined credit, and for a dozen years the house had stood tottering. With the shock of the French Revolution it fell—fell utterly—and in its ruin dragged down the connected Yorkshire firm of Moore. Robert Moore the elder, resident in Antwerp, had married Hortense Gérard believing she would inherit her father Constantine Gérard’s share. She inherited, instead, his share of liabilities. Creditors were satisfied by composition; but some said her son took the old debt upon his own conscience as a legacy, and cherished an ambition—stern as it was secret—to discharge it fully one day, and rebuild Gérard and Moore on a greatness equal to what had been lost. If a childhood spent beside a saturnine mother, under the shadow of foreboded evil, can brand a mind; if a manhood drenched by the pitiless descent of actual storm can deepen the mark—then his, likely, was marked in no gold, but in iron.

Yet if he carried a great end, he could not command great means. He was forced to begin with the day of small things. Coming to Yorkshire, he—whose people had once held warehouses in seaports, factories in inland towns, a town-house and a country-seat—found no path open but to rent a cloth-mill in a remote hollow; take the cottage adjoining as his residence; and add, as pasture for his horse and space for his cloth-tenters, a few acres of steep, rugged land lining the brawling mill-stream. All this he held at a high rent—for war made everything dear—from the trustees of the Fieldhead estate, which belonged then to a minor.

He had lived in the district two years and had proved himself active. The dingy cottage had been made neat and tasteful. Of rough ground he had carved garden-space and cultivated it with singular, almost Flemish exactness. As for the mill—old, fitted with old, inefficient machinery—he had from the first held its arrangements in contempt and aimed at radical reform, pushing forward as fast as limited capital would let him. That narrowness galled him. “Forward” seemed stamped upon his soul; poverty held the reins. At times, figuratively, he foamed when checked.

In such a state it was not to be expected he would consider long whether his advance harmed others. Not being native, not long resident, he did not sufficiently care that new inventions threw old workpeople out of employ. He never asked where those whom he no longer paid found daily bread; and in that negligence he only resembled thousands, on whom the starving poor of Yorkshire seemed to have a closer claim.

The time itself was overshadowed in British history, especially in the northern provinces. War stood at its height; Europe was all involved. England, if not weary, was worn with long resistance—and half her people were weary too, crying for peace on any terms. National honour had become, to many dimmed by hunger, an empty word; for a morsel of meat they would have sold their birthright.
