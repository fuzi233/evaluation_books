The forest was alive with the soft hush of leaves and the distant call of birds. Harry Warrington dismounted his horse, the worn leather creaking beneath him as he stepped onto the damp earth. The trees seemed to close in around him, their branches tangling above like skeletal fingers. He had been searching for weeks, driven by a morbid fascination with the stories of the British disaster in Pennsylvania. Every report he'd heard had been a litany of defeat, of valour and discipline failing against the unknown enemy. The image of his brother, George, had become a constant presence in his mind – a face he couldn't quite recall, a name he couldn't quite speak.

He approached the river, the water's edge a gentle lap against the shore. A guide, hired for the day, stood beside him, eyes narrowed against the sun. "We're getting close," the man said, his voice low and gravelly. "But I warned you, lad, this isn't a place for the faint of heart." Harry nodded, his eyes scanning the horizon. He'd come this far, and he wouldn't turn back now.

As they walked, the silence between them grew thicker, until it felt like a living thing. Harry's thoughts turned to his friend, George Washington, who'd been kind enough to take him in when he'd arrived at the camp. He'd spoken of George's bravery, of the General's final moments, but there was something he'd left unsaid – something that had left Harry with a sense of unease.

They reached the site of the battle, the air thick with the stench of death. Harry's stomach churned as he dismounted, his feet sinking into the earth. The guide followed, his eyes averted from the carnage. "This is where it happened," he muttered, his voice barely audible over the wind. Harry walked forward, his footsteps quiet on the earth. He'd expected to feel something – grief, anger, or perhaps even a sense of peace – but instead, he felt nothing. The faces before him were twisted, contorted in death, their skin slick with sweat and blood. He turned away, his mind reeling.
