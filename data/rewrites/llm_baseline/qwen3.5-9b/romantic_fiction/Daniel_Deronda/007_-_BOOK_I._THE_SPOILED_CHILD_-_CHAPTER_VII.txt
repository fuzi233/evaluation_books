The first sign of the approaching snowstorm was a transparent white cloud that seemed to heighten the brilliance of the blue sky. Anna understood the turmoil in Rex's heart; for the first time, he had remained silent about what weighed most heavily upon him, assuming she shared his burden. Conversely, for the first time, Anna found herself unable to voice the constant thoughts swirling in her own mind. Perhaps it was a pain she would have hidden, that he cared so deeply for someone else before her, but this feeling was largely neutralized by her profound anxiety for his safety.

Anna admired her cousin. She would have spoken simply and sincerely, "Gwendolen is always very good to me," accepting her total subordination to this relative as natural. Yet, she viewed Gwendolen with a mixture of fear and distrust, contemplating her as a wondrous, beautiful creature whose nature remained a mystery—a beast that might, for all Anna knew, hunger to devour the small pets she cherished. Now, Anna's heart sank under the heavy, unspoken conviction that Gwendolen would never care for Rex. What Anna held in tender reverence seemed utterly indifferent to Gwendolen; it was easier to imagine her scorn than to accept any return of affection. Furthermore, Gwendolen was perpetually striving to be extraordinary. Poor Rex! His father would be furious if he knew. Of course, Rex was too young to love in such a manner; Anna had believed it would be years before anything of the sort occurred, and that she would remain his housekeeper forever. But what a heart must be that failed to reciprocate his love! In the prospect of his suffering, Anna began to dislike her cousin's overwhelming fascination.

To both Anna and Rex, the weeks seemed filled with a tumultuous life visible to all observers. Had he been questioned, Rex would have claimed he wished to conceal nothing regarding an engagement he intended to announce to his father immediately. Yet, for the first time in his life, he was reserved—not only about his feelings but, more remarkably to Anna, about his actions. On her side, Anna grew nervous every time her parents spoke privately, fearing they might mention Rex and Gwendolen. However, the elders were entirely unaware of this agitating drama, which unfolded mostly in a pantomime lucid only to those expressing it internally, easily missed by spectators busy scanning the *Guardian* or the *Clerical Gazette*, interpreting the young ones' trivialities no differently than the movements of lively ants.

"Where are you going, Rex?" Anna asked one gray morning. Her father had departed in his carriage with Mrs. Gascoigne, and she noticed her brother wearing his *antigropelos*, his closest approximation to hunting attire.

"I'm going to see the hounds throw off at the Three Barns," Rex replied.

"Are you going to take Gwendolen?" Anna asked timidly.

"She told you, did she?"

"No, but I thought... Does Papa know you're going?"

"Not that I'm aware of. I don't suppose he'd trouble himself."

"You're going to use his horse?"

"He knows I do that whenever I can."

"Don't let Gwendolen ride after the hounds, Rex," Anna pleaded, her fears granting her a strange second sight.

"Why not?" Rex smiled, somewhat provocatively.

"Papa, Mama, and Aunt Davilow all wish her not to. They think it isn't right for her."

"Why should you suppose she intends to do what is wrong?"

"Gwendolen minds nobody sometimes," Anna said, growing bolder through a spark of anger.

"Then she wouldn't mind me," Rex retorted, turning her anxiety into a perverse joke.

"Oh, Rex, I can't bear it. You'll make yourself very unhappy." Anna burst into tears.

"Nannie, Nannie, what on earth is the matter with you?" Rex exclaimed, slightly impatient, hat in hand and whip in grip.

"She won't care for you one bit—I know she never will!" the poor child whispered, sobbing uncontrollably.

Rex flushed and hurried out of the hall door, leaving her to the misery of realizing she had been disagreeable in vain.

As he rode, he considered her words. They carried the unwelcome weight of all unfavorable prophecy, even when laughed at. Yet, he quickly rationalized them as stemming from little Anna's tenderness, regretting only that he had to leave without soothing her. Every other feeling on the subject was swiftly submerged by a resistant belief contrary to hers, accompanied by a new determination to prove her wrong. This certainty possessed just enough kinship to doubt and unease to hasten a confession that an untouched security might have delayed.

Gwendolen was already mounted, pacing up and down the avenue when Rex arrived at the gate. She had prepared for his tardiness by having the groom stand ready behind her, unwilling to wait beyond a reasonable time. Now, the groom was dismissed, and the two rode away in delightful freedom. Gwendolen was in high spirits; Rex thought she had never looked lovelier before. Her figure, her long white throat, and the curves of her cheek and chin were perfected by the compact simplicity of her riding dress. To a youthful lover like Rex, the fundamental unity of the good, the true, and the beautiful seemed already manifest in the object of his affection. Most observers would agree that a girl should hold similar impressions of Rex, for in his handsome face lay nothing corresponding to the indefinable, stinging quality—as if a trace of demonic ancestry—that caused some to hesitate in admiring Gwendolen.

It was an exquisite January morning, free from rain threats, with a gray sky providing the calmest backdrop for a mild winter scene: grassy lane borders, hedgerows dotted with red berries and low bird calls, the purple bareness of elm trees, and the rich brown of plowed furrows. The horses' hooves created a musical chime, echoing their young voices. Gwendolen laughed at his lack of dandyish equipment, and he enjoyed her laughter; the freshness of the morning mingled with the freshness of their youth. Every sound from their clear throats, every glance exchanged, flowed like water from a spring of joy. It was all morning to them, inside and out. One is tempted to futile wishes in such moments—if only things could have been slightly otherwise, leading to vastly different outcomes later; if only these two beautiful youths could have pledged themselves then and there, never swerving from that vow throughout their lives! Some of the goodness Rex believed in was indeed present. Goodness is a vast, often prospective word; like harvest, it lies underground at one stage, an indeterminate future, asking if the germ prospers in the dark. At another, it sends forth delicate green blades, and soon trembling blossoms await the dash of a rough wind or rain. Each stage has its peculiar blight, capable of choking healthy life through the foul actions of the soil that raises it or brings damage from afar.

"Anna had got it into her head that you wanted to ride after the hounds this morning," Rex said, his secret association with Anna's earlier words making this remark feel perilously close to the most momentous topic.

"Did she?" Gwendolen laughed. "What a little clairvoyant she is!"

"Shall you?" Rex asked. He hadn't believed she intended to go despite the elders' objections, trusting instead that she had good reasons.

"I don't know. I can't tell what I'll do until I get there. Clairvoyants are often wrong; they foresee what is likely. I'm not fond of what is likely; it's always dull. I do what is unlikely."

"There, you've told me a secret. Once I knew what people generally would likely do, I'd know you'd do the opposite. So you'd come around to a likelihood of your own sort. I'll be able to calculate on you. You couldn't surprise me."

"Yes, I could. I'd turn round and do what was likely for people in general," Gwendolen said with a musical laugh.

"You see, you can't escape some sort of likelihood. And contradictoriness makes the strongest likelihood of all. You must give up a plan."

"No, I shan't. My plan is to do what pleases me." But everything odd in her speech was merely humor and pretty banter, which he eagerly turned toward a single point.)
