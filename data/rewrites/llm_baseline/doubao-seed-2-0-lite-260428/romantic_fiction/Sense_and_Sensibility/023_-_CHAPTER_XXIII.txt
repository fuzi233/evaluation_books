Elinor could not shake the weight of Lucy Steele’s confession, no matter how hard she tried to dismiss it as a lie. What possible reason could Lucy have to invent such a scandalous story? There was no temptation great enough to justify the folly of fabricating an engagement that would risk so much for nothing, and every small, forgotten detail of the last months lined up like proof, unassailable no matter how fiercely her own wishes argued against it.
Their acquaintance under Mr. Pratt’s roof in Plymouth was an undeniable, alarming foundation. Then Edward’s recent extended visit to that very same corner of Devonshire, his quiet, brooding melancholy, his frustration at his own stalled prospects, his fumbling, inconsistent behavior around her at Norland—even the Miss Steeles’ uncanny familiarity with every tiny trivial story of the Dashwood family at their old home, details Elinor had never shared with a casual acquaintance. The miniature portrait Edward had once all but hidden from her, the awkwardly worded letter he’d sent when he left Norland, the thin ring of platted hair he’d worn on his little finger for months that he’d never thought to explain: all of it stacked into a body of evidence she could no longer dismiss, no matter how partial her heart was to him.
For a few sharp, burning minutes, she resented him, raged at the fact she had been played for a fool. But the anger melted fast into softer, sadder thoughts. Could he have ever truly meant to deceive her? Could he have faked the tender, unguarded affection everyone around them had witnessed at Norland? Her mother, Marianne, even Fanny Dashwood, his own priggish cousin, had all teased her about how clearly Edward doted on her. He loved her. She could not convince herself otherwise.
That realization was the softest, cruelest balm of all. It made her want to forgive him almost everything. He had been wrong, deeply wrong, to linger at Norland after he realized his feelings for her outgrew the bounds of his old promise—but if he had hurt her, he had hurt himself far worse. She might grieve her lost hopes and find peace with time. He had no such escape. Trapped between a family that would disown him for an unsuitable marriage, and a wife who was ill-educated, manipulative, and entirely selfish, what future could he ever hope for? The infatuation of a nineteen-year-old boy might have once blinded him to Lucy’s flaws, but four years of growing, maturing while Lucy spent her days in frivolous, shallow company had long since erased whatever simple charm she might once have had. That the prospect of his mother’s fury and family rejection might feel like a small relief next to the life he was bound to… Elinor found herself weeping for him far more than she wept for herself.
Secure in the knowledge she had done nothing to earn this unhappiness, and determined not to lose her respect for Edward no matter the truth, she steeled herself to hide the whole painful secret from her mother and Marianne. She rose, washed the traces of tears from her face, and joined them for dinner barely two hours after her every dearest hope had crumbled. No one at the table suspected a thing: that Elinor was quietly mourning a love she would never be allowed to claim, while Marianne’s head was full of nothing but Willoughby, certain his whole heart belonged to her, and craning to spot his carriage every time one rumbled past the cottage gate.
Hiding the secret was no burden to Elinor, either. It spared her the distress of seeing her mother and Marianne break their hearts over her pain, and the inevitable, furious condemnation of Edward that their partial affection for her would spark—condemnation she knew she was far too raw to bear. She was stronger alone, her own good sense propping her up so well that her cheerfulness never wavered even when her regret cut sharpest.
Before long, she found herself eager to speak to Lucy again, in private. She wanted to hear every small detail of the engagement laid out clearly, to judge for herself if Lucy truly felt any real affection for Edward, and most of all, to convince Lucy with her calm composure that she was no threat to her claim—that she had no intention of standing between them, so Lucy had no reason to fear her. It was obvious Lucy was jealous, after all. Edward must have spoken of her constantly, for Lucy to dare entrust a near-stranger with a secret so great, purely to stake her claim. Elinor understood exactly what her rival wanted: for her to learn of the engagement, and step back from Edward of her own accord. Elinor was happy to give her that reassurance, even if it meant facing the truth head on.
Weeks dragged on without a single spare minute of privacy. The weather was too stormy for long walks away from the group, and every gathering at the Middleton’s or the cottage was full of noise: Sir John’s raucous jokes, shrieking children, loud card games and silly party games that left no room for quiet, private talk. It was only when Sir John popped over to the cottage one morning, begging them all to dine at the Park that very day, that Elinor saw her chance. He had to attend a club meeting in Exeter, he explained, and Lady Middleton would be left dreadfully lonely with nothing but her mother and the two Miss Steeles for company. Elinor accepted instantly, knowing Lady Middleton’s quiet, decorous parties would never descend into the chaotic, all-noise bustle her husband insisted on. Her mother and Margaret agreed at once, and even Marianne, though she hated calling on the Middletons, was gently persuaded to come along, so she would not shut herself away from every small chance of amusement.
The dinner was exactly as dull as Elinor had predicted. Not a single original thought or remark passed between them, and when they moved to the drawing room after the meal, Lady Middleton’s brood of children trotted after them, clinging to their mother’s skirts and chattering nonstop, so Elinor could not risk a single word to Lucy. When the tea things were cleared away, the card tables were set out, and Elinor feared she had missed her chance entirely.
“I am so glad,” Lady Middleton said to Lucy, “that you are not finishing little Anna Maria’s filigree basket tonight. Working that fine craft by candlelight will strain your eyes so dreadfully. We will finish it tomorrow, and the dear little love will hardly mind the delay.”
Lucy caught the hint at once, and smiled brightly. “You are quite mistaken, Lady Middleton! I only waited to check if you needed me at the card table first. I would never disappoint that sweet child for anything in the world. If you can spare me, I will go right back to my work straight after supper.”
Elinor seized the opening the second Lucy pulled her work table close. “If I end up sitting out the first round of cards,” she said smoothly, “I would be delighted to help you roll the delicate papers for that basket. There is still so much left to do, two of us could finish it in no time at all. I should love to lend a hand.”
Lucy welcomed her instantly, and Lady Middleton was only too happy to agree, pleased to know her spoiled little Anna Maria would get her basket on time. Marianne, meanwhile, had already turned on her heel without a by-your-leave, announcing she detested cards and was off to play the piano, which had just been tuned the week prior. She was already lost in her favorite dramatic pieces seconds later, the rich, loud notes drifting across the room, drowning out every soft word two people sitting side by side at the work table might choose to exchange.
Perfectly hidden from the card players, the two women worked side by side at the same basket, seemingly the best of friends, with all the privacy Elinor could have hoped for to finally broach the secret that had hung heavy over her for days.
