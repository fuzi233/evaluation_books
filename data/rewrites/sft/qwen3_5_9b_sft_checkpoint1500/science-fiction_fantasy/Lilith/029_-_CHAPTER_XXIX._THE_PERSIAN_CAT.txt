Silence and shame settled over me like a heavy cloak. His words struck me with brutal clarity: I had failed the Little Ones. They needed guidance, not cruelty.

Mr. Raven continued, his voice low and steady. "You harmed them doubly. To beings so fragile, slavery would have been a ladder to progress. A lesson learned gently, from a branch of their own tree, would have been a gift far greater than any harsh word you used."

"I did not know they were cowards!" I protested, the sting of his accusation lingering.

"Does their nature matter?" he asked, his gaze piercing. "To base one's actions on another's weakness is to reveal oneself as weak. And I fear consequences beyond your understanding may arise. If the Little Ones had grown strong enough to stand against the Princess, perhaps they could have resisted the giants... but now, because of your alliance with her..."

"I hate her!" I shouted, the anger rising hot and sudden.

"Do you truly let her know?" he pressed gently.

Again, I fell silent. The weight of my betrayal hung between us.

"Not even to her have you remained faithful," he murmured, then suddenly checked himself. "Hush! We are being followed from the fountain."

"No living soul," I whispered, scanning the shadows. "Except a disreputable cat that bolted into the bushes."

"It was a magnificent Persian," Mr. Raven corrected, his tone sharpening. "Though soaked and ragged, she looked less like a stray and more like a ruin."

"What do you mean?" I cried, a fresh chill seizing me. "There was a beautiful blue Persian near the house, but she fled at the sound of water! Did she come after the goldfish?"

"We shall find out," the librarian replied. "I know much about cats of various stripes, and there is something in this room that will expose her nature, or I am greatly mistaken."

He rose, moved to the door of the small closet, and returned carrying a volume torn in half, leaving jagged edges. He sat beside me, holding the fragment.

I stared at the book. It was whole, intact, and bound in aged vellum. "Where is the other half?" I gasped.

"Embedded deep within my library," he answered simply.

I held my tongue. Another question would have plunged us into a bottomless abyss, and there might be no time left.

"Listen," he said softly. "I intend to read a stanza or two. Someone present will likely find little pleasure in hearing it."

He opened the cover, turning the yellowed leaves. One page bore a dark stain covering most of the parchment. He flipped past it carefully, searching for a specific line in a continuous poem. Somewhere near the middle, he began to speak.

However, what follows is not exactly what he read, but rather the shadow it cast upon my mind. The words seemed spoken in a tongue unknown to me, yet I understood them perfectly, unable to transcribe them or define their meaning with precision. As they passed through my consciousness, they reshaped into these fragments:

"But if I found a man who could believe
In what he saw not, felt not, and yet knew,
From him I'd take substance, receiving
Firmness and form related to touch and view;
Then should I clothe myself in likeness true
Of that idea where his soul did cleave!"

He turned a page and read again:

"In me resided every woman. I possessed power
Over the soul of every living man,
Such as no woman ever held in dower—
Could do what no woman ever could, or can;
All women, I, the woman, still outran,
Outsoared, outsank, outreigned, in hall or bower.

"For I, though unseen, unheard, untouchable,
Though not one breath stirred a hair upon his head,
Could bind his brain and spine, a trap unshakeable,
With roots so deep Death could not sever, thread;
Or life, though hope were forever deferred."

Again he paused, again turned the leaf, and began anew:

"For by his side I lay, a bodiless thing;
I breathed not, saw not, felt not, only thought,
And made him love me—with a hungry spring
For something he knew not, if it was aught
Or but a nameless something wrought by night,
By him out of himself. For I did sing

'A song without a sound into his soul;
I laid a heartless thing against his heart,
Giving him nothing where he gave his whole
Being to clothe me human, every part:
That I at last into his sense might dart,
Thus first into his living mind I stole.'

'Ah, who has ever conquered Love but I!
Who else did ever throne within a man!'
To visible being, with a glad cry high,
Life's tremor through me ran, a living plan.'"

A strange, repulsive wail tore through the air from somewhere inside the room. I jolted up on my elbow, staring wildly about, seeing nothing.

Mr. Raven turned several leaves and continued:

"Suddenly I woke, nor knowing the ghastly fear
That held me—not like serpents coiled about,
But like a vapor moist, corrupt, and drear,
Filling heart, soul, and breast and brain throughout;
My being lay motionless in sickening doubt,
Nor daring to ask how the horror spouted.

'My past entire I knew, but not my now;
I understood nor what I was, nor where;
I knew what I had been: still on my brow
I felt the touch of what no longer there!
I was a fainting, dead, yet live Despair;
A life that mocked life with a mope and stare.'

'That I was a queen I knew right well,
And sometimes wore a splendour on my head
Whose flashing even dead darkness could not quell—
The like on neck and arms and girdle-spread;
And men declared a light my closed eyes shed
That killed the diamond in its silver bed.'"

Again I heard the ugly cry of feline pain. Again I looked, seeing neither shape nor motion. Mr. Raven seemed to listen for a moment, then turned several pages and resumed:

"Hideously wet, my hair of golden hue
Fouled my fair hands; to have it swiftly shorn
I'd given my rubies, all freshly dug new—
No eye had seen, and such no waist had worn!
For a draught of water from a drinking horn,
For one blue breath, I'd given my sapphires blue!

'Nay, I had given my opals for a smock,
A peasant-maiden's garment, coarse and clean:
My shroud was rotting! Once I heard a cock
Lustily crow upon the hill-green scene
Over my coffin. Dulled by space between,
Came back an answer like a ghostly screen.'"

Once more arose the bestial wail.

"I thought some foul thing was in the room!" said the librarian, glancing around hastily; but instantly he turned a leaf or two and read again:

"For I had bathed in milk and honey-dew,
In rain from roses shaken, never touching earth,
And ointed me with nard of amber hue;
Never had spot me spotted from my birth,
Or mole, or scar of hurt, or fret of dew;
Never one hair superfluous on me grew."

"Fleeing cold whiteness, I would sit alone—
Not in the sun—I feared his bronzing light,
But in his radiance back around me thrown
By fulgent mirrors tempering his might;
Thus bathing in a moon-bath not too bright,
My skin I tinted slowly to ivory white."

"But now, all round was dark, dark all within!
My eyes not even gave out a phantom-flash;
My fingers sank in pulp through pulpy skin;
My body lay death-weltered in a mash
Of slimy horrors—"

With a fearsome yell, her clammy fur staring in clumps, her tail thick as a cable, her eyes flashing green as a chrysoprase, her distended claws entangling themselves so that she floundered across the carpet, a huge white cat rushed from somewhere, making straight for the chimney. Quick as thought, the librarian threw the manuscript between her and the hearth.

She crouched instantly, her eyes fixed on the book. But his voice went on as if still he read, and his eyes seemed also fixed on the book:

"Oh, the two worlds! So strangely are they one,
And yet so measurelessly wide apart!
Oh, had I lived the bodiless alone
And from defiling sense held safe my heart,
Then had I escaped the canker and the smart,
Escaped life-in-death, escaped misery's moan!"

At these words, such a howling, such a prolonged yell of agony burst from the cat, that we both stopped our ears. When it ceased, Mr. Raven walked to the fireplace, took up the book, and, standing between the creature and the chimney, pointed his finger at her for a moment. She lay perfectly still.
