It was, as Mrs. Archer smiled and told Mrs. Welland, a grand occasion for a young couple to host their inaugural formal dinner.

Since establishing their household, the Newland Archers had entertained frequently in a casual manner. Archer enjoyed hosting three or four friends for dinner, and May welcomed them with the radiant enthusiasm her mother had taught her regarding marital duties. Her husband often wondered if, left entirely to her own devices, she would ever have invited anyone to their home; however, he had long ceased attempting to separate her true nature from the persona shaped by tradition and upbringing. In New York, it was expected that affluent young couples engage in significant informal entertaining, and a marriage uniting a Welland with an Archer doubled the pledge to uphold these traditions.

Yet, a grand dinner—complete with a hired chef, two borrowed footmen, Roman punch, roses procured from Henderson's, and menus printed on gilt-edged cards—was a distinct undertaking, one not to be approached lightly. As Mrs. Archer noted, the Roman punch was the deciding factor; not merely for its taste, but for the myriad implications it carried. Its presence signaled canvasbacks or terrapin, two soups, both hot and cold desserts, low-cut dresses with short sleeves, and guests of corresponding stature.

Such occasions were invariably fascinating when a young couple issued their first invitations in the third person, and their requests were rarely declined, even by the most sought-after individuals. Nevertheless, it was undeniably a triumph that the van der Luydens, at May's insistence, had extended their stay specifically to attend her farewell dinner for the Countess Olenska.

On the afternoon of this pivotal day, the two mothers-in-law occupied May's drawing-room. Mrs. Archer meticulously wrote out the menus on Tiffany's thickest gilt-edged Bristol board, while Mrs. Welland supervised the arrangement of palm fronds and standard lamps.

Archer arrived late from his office to find them still there. His wife had shifted her focus to the name-cards for the table settings, and Mrs. Welland was deliberating the placement of a large gilt sofa to create an additional seating area between the piano and the window. They informed him that May was currently in the dining-room, inspecting the towering mound of Jacqueminot roses and maidenhair ferns centered on the long table, and arranging Maillard bonbons in openwork silver baskets between the candelabras. Resting on the piano was a substantial basket of orchids sent by Mr. van der Luyden from Skuytercliff. In short, everything was precisely as it should be for an event of such magnitude.

Mrs. Archer ran her finger thoughtfully along the guest list, marking names with her sharp gold pen.

"Henry van der Luyden—Louisa—the Lovell Mingotts—the Reggie Chiverses—Lawrence Lefferts and Gertrude—(yes, I suppose May was right to include them)—the Selfridge Merrys, Sillerton Jackson, Van Newland and his wife. (Time flies! It seems only yesterday he was your best man, Newland)—and Countess Olenska—yes, I believe that covers everyone..."

Mrs. Welland regarded her son-in-law with affection. "No one can claim, Newland, that you and May haven't given Ellen a magnificent send-off."

"Ah, well," replied Mrs. Archer, "I understand May wishes her cousin to assure people abroad that we aren't quite savages."

"I'm certain Ellen will appreciate it. She was scheduled to arrive this morning, I believe. It will certainly leave a delightful final impression. The eve before sailing is typically so dreary," Mrs. Welland continued cheerfully.

Archer turned toward the door, but his mother-in-law called out, "Do go inside and peek at the table. And try not to let May exhaust herself." He feigned not to hear, springing up the stairs to his library. The room stared back at him like an unfamiliar face wearing a polite grimace; he realized it had been ruthlessly "tidied" and prepared, via a strategic distribution of ashtrays and cedar-wood boxes, for the gentlemen's smoking pleasure.

"Ah, well," he thought, "it won't last long"—and proceeded to his dressing room.

Ten days had elapsed since Madame Olenska's departure from New York. During this period, Archer had received no communication from her save for a key wrapped in tissue paper, mailed to his office in a sealed envelope bearing her handwriting. This response to his final plea could have been viewed as a masterstroke in a familiar game; however, the young man chose to interpret it differently. She remained battling her destiny, yet she was departing for Europe and refusing to return to her husband. Consequently, nothing prevented him from following her. Once he took the irreversible step and demonstrated its permanence to her, he believed she would not drive him away.

This confidence in the future steadied him to fulfill his role in the present. It kept him from writing to her or revealing, through any gesture or action, his misery and humiliation. To him, in this deadly silent contest, the trump cards still lay in his hands; he simply waited.

Nevertheless, there were moments of sufficient difficulty. When Mr. Letterblair summoned him the day after Madame Olenska's departure to review the details of the trust Mrs. Manson Mingott intended to establish for her granddaughter, Archer spent two hours examining the legal terms with his senior. Throughout, he vaguely sensed that his consultation served a purpose beyond mere kinship, anticipating that the meeting's conclusion would reveal the truth.

"Well, the lady cannot deny that it's a generous arrangement," Mr. Letterblair summarized after muttering over the settlement summary. "In fact, I feel bound to say she has been treated very handsomely all around."

"All around?" Archer echoed with a trace of derision. "Are you referring to her husband's proposal to return her own funds?"

Mr. Letterblair's bushy eyebrows rose a fraction of an inch. "My dear sir, the law is the law; and your wife's cousin married under French law. It is presumed she understood the implications."

"Even if she did, what occurred subsequently—" Archer paused. Mr. Letterblair rested his pen handle against his broad, corrugated nose, gazing down it with the expression adopted by virtuous elders when they wish their juniors to comprehend that virtue does not equate to ignorance.

"My dear sir, I have no desire to excuse the Count's transgressions; but—but on the other side... I wouldn't bet my hand on the fire... well, that there wasn't tit for tat... with the young champion...." Unlocking a drawer, the lawyer slid a folded paper toward Archer. "This report, the result of discreet inquiries..." Seeing that Archer made no effort to look at the document or reject the suggestion, the lawyer continued somewhat flatly: "I don't claim it's conclusive, you observe; far from it. But straws show... and overall, it is eminently satisfactory for all parties that this dignified resolution has been reached."

"Oh, eminently," Archer agreed, pushing the paper aside.

A day or two later, responding to a summons from Mrs. Manson Mingott, his spirit underwent further trial.

He found the elderly lady depressed and querulous.

"You know she has deserted me?" she began immediately. Without awaiting his reply, she continued: "Oh, don't ask me why! She offered so many reasons I've forgotten them all. My private belief is that she couldn't endure the boredom. At any rate, that's what Augusta and my daughters-in-law think. And I don't know that I wholly blame her. Olenski is a finished scoundrel; but life with him must have been considerably livelier than it is on Fifth Avenue. Not that the family would admit that: they consider Fifth Avenue to be Heaven with the Rue de la Paix included. And poor Ellen, of course, has no intention of returning to her husband. She held firm against that as ever. So she is to settle in Paris with that fool Medora.... Well, Paris is Paris; and you can maintain a carriage there for next to nothing. But she was as gay as a bird, and I shall miss her." Two tears, the parched tears of age, rolled down her puffy cheeks and disappeared into the depths of her bosom.

"All I ask," she concluded, "is that they cease bothering me. I must truly be allowed to digest my gruel...." She twinkled wistfully at Archer.

That evening, upon his return home, May announced her intention of hosting a farewell dinner for her cousin. Madame Olenska's name had not been spoken between them since the night of her flight to Washington; Archer looked at his wife in surprise.

"A dinner—why?" he queried.

Her color rose. "But you like Ellen—I thought you'd be pleased."

"It's awfully nice—you phrasing it that way. But I really don't see—"

"I intend to do it, Newland," she stated calmly, rising and moving to her desk. "Here are the invitations, all written. Mother assisted me—she agrees we ought to." She paused, embarrassed yet smiling, and Archer suddenly beheld the embodiment of the Family.

"Oh, all right," he said, staring with unfocused eyes at the guest list she handed him.

Upon entering the drawing-room before dinner, May was bending over the fire, attempting to coax the logs to burn within their unusual setting of pristine tiles.
