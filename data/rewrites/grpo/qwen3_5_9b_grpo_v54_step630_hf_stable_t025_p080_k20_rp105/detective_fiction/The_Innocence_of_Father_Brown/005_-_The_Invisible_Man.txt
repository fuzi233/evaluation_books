The twilight in Camden Town was cool and blue, casting a shadow over two steep streets until the corner shop broke the gloom. It was a confectioner's, glowing with the chaotic brilliance of a spent firework rather than a cigar butt. The light fractured across mirrors, dancing upon piles of gilt and gaily colored cakes and sweetmeats. Against this fiery backdrop, the noses of street urchins were pressed, mesmerized by chocolates wrapped in metallic reds, greens, and golds—packaging that often outshone the treat within. Dominating the display was a massive white wedding cake, a sugared monument that seemed simultaneously distant and delicious, as if the entire North Pole had been baked for consumption.

While such sights naturally drew the neighborhood's youth, a young man of twenty-four stood transfixed before the window. To him, the shop held a fiery allure, though his gaze was not solely captivated by the sweets.

He was tall and burly, with red hair and a face of resolve that belied a listless demeanor. Tucked under his arm was a flat, grey portfolio containing black-and-white sketches he had sold with varying degrees of success to publishers. His name was John Turnbull Angus, and his portfolio carried the weight of a family scandal: he had been disinherited by his uncle, an admiral, for delivering a lecture against socialism.

Angus pushed through the door and walked to the back room, a pastry-cook's restaurant, tipping his hat to the young lady serving there. She was a dark, elegant figure in black, her complexion flushed and her eyes quick and observant. After a brief pause, she followed him into the inner room to take his order.

"Please," he said with precise efficiency, "one halfpenny bun and a small cup of black coffee." Before she could turn away, he added, "Also, I want you to marry me."

The girl stiffened. "Those are jokes I do not allow."

Angus lifted his grey eyes, which held an unexpected gravity. "Really and truly," he insisted, "it is as serious—as serious as the halfpenny bun. It is expensive, like the bun; one pays for it. It is indigestible, like the bun. It hurts."

The girl never broke her gaze, studying him with a tragic exactitude. Finally, a shadow of a smile touched her lips, and she sat down.

"Don't you think," Angus observed absently, "that it is cruel to eat these halfpenny buns? They might grow up into penny buns. I shall give up these brutal sports when we are married."

The girl rose and walked to the window, lost in cogitation. When she swung back with an air of resolution, she was startled to find Angus carefully arranging various items from the shop window onto the table. There was a pyramid of colorful sweets, several plates of sandwiches, and the two decanters holding the mysterious port and sherry peculiar to pastry cooks. In the center, he had lowered the enormous load of white sugared cake that had been the window's ornament.

"What on earth are you doing?" she asked.

"Duty, my dear Laura," he began.

"Oh, for the Lord's sake, stop a minute," she cried, "and don't talk to me in that way. What is all that?"

"A ceremonial meal, Miss Hope."

"And what is that?" she asked impatiently, pointing to the mountain of sugar.

"The wedding-cake, Mrs. Angus," he replied.

The girl marched to the article, clattered it back into the window, and returned, resting her elbows on the table. She regarded Angus unfavorably but with considerable exasperation.

"You don't give me any time to think," she said.

"I'm not such a fool," he answered, "that's my Christian humility."

She grew graver behind the smile. "Mr. Angus," she said steadily, "before there is a minute more of this nonsense, I must tell you something about myself as shortly as I can."

"Delighted," replied Angus gravely. "You might tell me something about myself, too, while you are about it."

"Oh, do hold your tongue and listen," she said. "It is nothing I am ashamed of, nor anything I am specially sorry about. But what would you say if there were something that is no business of mine and yet is my nightmare?"

"In that case," said the man seriously, "I should suggest that you bring back the cake."

"Well, you must listen to the story first," said Laura, persistently. "To begin with, my father owned the inn called the 'Red Fish' at Ludbury, and I used to serve people in the bar."

"I have often wondered," Angus said, "why there was a kind of Christian air about this one confectioner's shop."

"Ludbury is a sleepy, grassy little hole in the Eastern Counties," Laura continued. "The only people who came to the 'Red Fish' were occasional commercial travelers and, for the rest, the most awful people you can see. Little, loungy men with just enough to live on, leaning in bar-rooms betting on horses in clothes just too good for them. Even these wretched young rotters were not very common at our house; but there were two of them that were a lot too common. They both lived on money of their own and were wearisomely idle and over-dressed. Yet, I was a bit sorry for them because I half-believed they slunk into our little empty bar because each had a slight deformity—the sort of thing yokels laugh at. It wasn't exactly a deformity; it was more an oddity.

"One was a surprisingly small man, something like a dwarf or a jockey. He was not at all jockeyish; he had a round black head, a well-trimmed black beard, and bright eyes like a bird's. He jingled money in his pockets and wore a great gold watch chain. He never appeared except dressed just too much like a gentleman to be one. He was no fool, though a futile idler; he was curiously clever at useless things, like impromptu conjuring. He could make fifteen matches set fire to each other like a firework or cut a banana into a dancing doll. His name was Isidore Smythe. I can see him still, with his little dark face, making a jumping kangaroo out of five cigars.

"The other fellow was more silent and ordinary, yet he alarmed me much more than poor Smythe. He was very tall and slight, light-haired, with a high-bridged nose that might have been handsome in a spectral way. But he had one of the most appalling squints I have ever seen. When he looked straight at you, you didn't know where you were yourself, let alone what he was looking at. I fancy this disfigurement embittered him; while Smythe showed off his monkey tricks anywhere, James Welkin—the squinting man's name—never did anything except soak in our bar parlour and go for great walks by himself in the flat, grey country. All the same, I think Smythe was a little sensitive about being so small, though he carried it off more smartly. And so it was that I was really puzzled, as well as startled, and very sorry, when they both offered to marry me in the same week.

"Well, I did what I've since thought was perhaps a silly thing. But these freaks were my friends in a way, and I had a horror of their thinking I refused them for the real reason—that they were impossibly ugly. So I made up some gas about never meaning to marry anyone who hadn't carved his way in the world. I said it was a point of principle with me not to live on money that was just inherited like theirs. Two days after I had talked in this well-meaning sort of way, the whole trouble began. The first thing I heard was that both of them had gone off to seek their fortunes, as if they were in some silly fairy tale.

"Well, I've never seen either of them from that day to this. But I've had two letters from the little man called Smythe, and really they were rather exciting."

"Ever heard of the other man?" Angus asked.

"No, he never wrote," Laura said, after an instant's hesitation. "Smythe's first letter simply said he had started out walking with Welkin to London; but Welkin was such a good walker that the little man dropped out and took a rest by the roadside. He was picked up by a traveling show, and partly because he was nearly a dwarf and partly because he was a clever little wretch, he got on quite well in the show business and was soon sent up to the Aquarium to do tricks. That was his first letter. His second was much more of a startler, and I only got it last week."

Angus emptied his coffee cup and regarded her with mild, patient eyes. Her mouth twisted slightly as she resumed, "I suppose you've seen on the hoardings all about this 'Smythe's Silent Service'? Or you must be the only person that hasn't. Oh, I don't know much about it; it's some clockwork invention for doing all the housework by machinery. You know the sort of thing: 'Press a Button—a Butler who Never Drinks.' 'Turn a Handle—Ten Housemaids who Never Flirt.' Whatever these machines are, they are making pots of money, and they are making it all for that little imp whom I knew down in Ludbury. I can't help feeling pleased the poor little chap has fallen on his feet; but the plain fact is, I'm in terror of his turning up any minute and telling me he's carved his way in the world—as he certainly has."

"And the other man?" Angus repeated with obstinate quietude.

Laura got to her feet suddenly. "My friend," she said, "I think you are a witch. Yes, you are quite right. I have not seen a line of the other man's writing; and I have no more notion than the dead of what or where he is. But it is of him that I am frightened. It is he who is all about my path. It is he who has half driven me mad. Indeed, I think he has driven me mad; for I have felt him where he could not have been, and I have heard his voice when he could not have spoken."

"Well, my dear," said the young man, cheerfully, "if he were Satan himself, he is done for now you have told somebody. One goes mad all alone, old girl. But when was it you fancied you felt and heard our squinting friend?"

"I heard James Welkin laugh as plainly as I hear you speak," said the girl, steadily. "There was nobody there; I stood just outside the shop at the corner and could see down both streets at once. I had forgotten how he laughed, though his laugh was as odd as his squint. I had not thought of him for nearly a year. But it's a solemn truth that a few seconds later the first letter came from his rival."

"Did you ever make the spectre speak or squeak, or anything?" Angus asked with interest.

Laura suddenly shuddered, then said with an unshaken voice, "Yes. Just when I had finished reading the second letter from Isidore Smythe announcing his success. Just then, I heard Welkin say, 'He shan't have you, though.' It was quite plain, as if he were in the room. It is awful; I think I must be mad."

"If you really were mad," said the young man, "you would think you must be sane. But certainly there seems to me to be something a little rum about this unseen gentleman. Two heads are better than one—I spare you allusions to any other organs—and really, if you would allow me, as a sturdy, practical man, to bring back the wedding-cake out of the window—"

Even as he spoke, a steely shriek echoed from the street outside. A small motor, driven at devilish speed, shot up to the shop door and stuck there. In the same flash of time, a small man in a shiny top hat stood stamping in the outer room.

Angus, who had hitherto maintained hilarious ease from motives of mental hygiene, revealed the strain of his soul by striding abruptly out of the inner room to confront the newcomer. A glance confirmed the savage guesswork of a man in love. This very dapper but dwarfish figure, with the spike of a black beard carried insolently forward, clever unrestful eyes, and neat but very nervous fingers, could be none other than Isidore Smythe—the man who made dolls out of banana skins and match-boxes, and who made millions out of undrinking butlers and unflirting housemaids of metal. For a moment, the two men, instinctively understanding each other's air of possession, looked at each other with that curious cold generosity which is the soul of rivalry.

Mr. Smythe made no allusion to the ultimate ground of their antagonism but said simply and explosively, "Has Miss Hope seen that thing on the window?"

"On the window?" repeated the staring Angus.

"There's no time to explain other things," said the small millionaire shortly. "There's some tomfoolery going on here that has to be investigated."

He pointed his polished walking-stick at the window, recently depleted by the bridal preparations of Mr. Angus. Angus was astonished to see along the front of the glass a long strip of paper pasted, which certainly had not been there when he looked through it some time before. Following the energetic Smythe outside into the street, he found that some yard and a half of stamp paper had been carefully gummed along the glass outside, and on this was written in straggly characters, "If you marry Smythe, he will die."

"Laura," said Angus, putting his big red head into the shop, "you're not mad."

"It's the writing of that fellow Welkin," said Smythe gruffly. "I haven't seen him for years, but he's always bothering me. Five times in the last fortnight he's had threatening letters left at my flat, and I can't even find out who leaves them, let alone if it is Welkin himself.
