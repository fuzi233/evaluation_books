Worthy old Major Pendennis fulfilled his promise to Warrington exactly far enough to pat his own conscience on the back and tick the "good deed" box, and far enough to dupe poor Helen into believing Arthur’s silly little fling with the sweet, overlooked gatekeeper’s daughter Fanny was well and truly kaput—no risk of him dragging the family name through the mud by eloping and spending the rest of his days polishing her bootlaces for her. Pen himself, once the sting to his overgrown vanity faded, breathed a massive sigh of relief too: turns out Fanny wasn’t going to waste away pining for him, and there were no awkward, lingering consequences to his dumb little brief misadventure.
With that weight off everyone’s chests, the whole crew set off on their long-planned Continental jaunt. Arthur Pendennis, rentier, travelling with his mamma Mrs. Pendennis and her ward Miss Bell, plus George Warrington, gentleman of leisure, 32 years old, 6 feet tall (very English height, that), face completely unremarkable, dark hair, dark beard, all duly noted down on the absurdly formal French passport they grabbed from the Belgian consul in Dover. They hopped the ferry to Ostend, then trundled slowly through Bruges and Ghent, gawking at every cobblestone before they headed for Brussels and the Rhine.
We won’t bore you with the same old tourist trip every other English traveller wrote a 3-volume memoir about. You already know Laura’s glee at wandering those quiet ancient cities for the first time, you can imagine Helen, our sweet straight-laced country widow, nearly having a conniption every five minutes. Barefoot friars shambling down the high street? Saints with little gold crowns on their heads propped up on church altars, while passers-by bowed down to them in what Helen swore was a direct violation of the 10 Commandments? Priests dripping in fancy silk robes skulking in dark confession booths? Theatres open and people dancing on *Sundays*? She was so bewildered and scandalized she nearly kept her prayer book glued to her chest at all times, and the second the lads stumbled back from their evening walk, Laura would hurriedly put down the psalms she’d been reading to calm Helen’s frayed nerves.
Pen’s recent brush with death had rattled Helen something terrible. She’d sit staring at him for hours, heart sore that her little boy, the one who used to run to her every five minutes to gush about his new marbles or tattle on the kid who called him a wimp, now had whole secret corners of his heart she wasn’t allowed near. Laura, watching her mope, once patted her hand and said “If Pen had loved me the way you wanted, I’d have got him—but I’d have lost you, and I like you loving me best. Men haven’t got a clue how to love the way we do, do they? They’ve got tax bills, tailors’ invoices, boring dinner parties to think about, their heads are stuffed full of total nonsense. A woman? Her whole day revolves around you. Your man’s a stiff oak standing there with his hands stuffed in his breeches pockets, not even noticing the ivy clinging tight around his trunk, smothering him in affection.” Helen sighed and agreed, and honestly? It’s a truism as old as time itself.
Old Pendennis only saw the crew off at the water’s edge, waved them off the ferry, dumped the whole responsibility of the trip on Warrington, then trundled off to stay at the fancy country house of his pal the Marquis of Steyne. He figured he’d more than earned a holiday after nursing Pen through his fever, and even if all the best partridges were already shot, the pheasants were still flying around Stillbrook ready to be picked off. The house party was stuffed full of royal dukes, fancy foreign diplomats, famous politicians, and just generally amusing people—Major Pendennis nearly preened his whole feathered chest off when he saw his name printed in the *Morning Post* alongside all the other distinguished guests.
He was the most useful man at that entire party, full stop. He’d entertain the lads on shooting trips and in the smoking room with raunchy little old anecdotes that had them cackling so hard their cigars fell out of their mouths. He’d escort new arrivals around the park, show them the best view of the mansion, point out the exact spot in the woods where old Lord Lynx caught Sir Phelim O’Neal on his knees before his ladyship, and knew exactly how many servants ate in the housekeeper’s parlour and how many crammed into the main servants’ hall. He had a little friendly snark about everyone, and a warm greeting for every single person he met. He deserved every minute of that holiday, and he was quietly chuffed he’d dumped the task of looking after Helen and Laura on Warrington—who, for his part, was only too happy to do it.
Warrington spoke perfect German, volunteered to give Laura lessons (she jumped at the chance, while Pen was far too lazy to crack open a textbook), acted as unofficial courier, hauled all their luggage on and off boats and carriages, sorted all the money, hunted down the English church for the two ladies on Sundays, walked Helen’s donkey for her on her evening excursions, fetched her a copy of *Galignani* when she was bored, and found the coziest shaded bench under the lime trees for her to sit on while the Kursaal band played after dinner. A dozen foppish, whiskery Prussian and French gambling dandies kept casting longing looks at pretty, fresh-faced Laura, dying to ask her to waltz, but she only stepped foot in the ballroom once or twice, when Pen deigned to dance with her. Warrington, for all his gruff charm, had never learned to waltz, and every time he watched Laura spin past with some other fellow he’d kick himself for not taking lessons as a lad. He’d trudge back to his own lodgings every night lonelier and grumpier than the last, staring at the crow’s feet around his eyes in the mirror, and the little silver streaks popping up in his dark beard, and seething with jealousy at every smooth-cheeked young buck prancing around the bath resort in his tight waistcoat and shiny varnished boots.
Poor Helen saw exactly what was happening. She realized why Major Pendennis had been so insistent Warrington come along on the trip, her little plan to marry Laura off to Pen was falling apart right in front of her face, Pen didn’t even seem to notice what was going on, and the fancy healing waters of Rosenbad did absolutely nothing for her frayed nerves. Pen, meanwhile, was bouncing back faster than a rubber ball: he slept 12 solid hours out of every 24, ate meals so big the waiter’s eyes nearly popped out, and two months after they arrived he was back to his full pre-illness weight and strength.
Fifteen days after they settled in at Rosenbad, a letter turned up from Major Pendennis saying he was on his way, and sure enough, a couple days later he waddled into town with his loyal valet Morgan in tow, wearing a ridiculous youthful travelling suit that made him look like a lad half his age from the back—until he started walking, and you noticed his ancient knees creaking like an unoiled chair, and he was hobbling around in his tiny pinched varnished boots. He spent the whole night poring over the guest list at the resort, chuffed to bits that half his aristocrat acquaintances were there, and immediately started making plans to introduce Pen to a German Grand Duchess, a Russian Princess, and an English Marquis. Pen, who’d always been a sucker for fancy polite society, was absolutely thrilled.
That very evening he grabbed Pen’s arm and waddled straight into the Kursaal gambling halls, bet a napoleon or two at the Trente-et-quarante table, bragging the whole time about how his old pal the Marquis of Steyne once lost 18 thousand pounds in a single sitting and didn’t even blink. “That’s what I call a proper English gentleman, Pen!” he crowed. He dragged Pen around pointing out all the scandalous Russian princesses and disgraced society ladies loitering by the tables, spilling every bit of sordid gossip he knew about their pasts, and Pen nearly laughed himself sick at his old uncle’s glee. Helen, who’d stumbled through the gambling hall once before, had been so horrified by the croupiers calling out *Rouge gagne!* and *Couleur perd!* that she’d dragged Pen out and made him swear on his honour he’d never touch the gambling tables—and to Pen’s credit, he kept that promise.
The Major’s arrival immediately put a damper on three people at once: Laura, who thought he was a lying old toad, Warrington, who treated him with frosty contempt, and Helen, who was terrified he’d mess up the last remaining shred of her plan to get Pen and Laura together. None of them knew the Major had accidentally brought a ticking time bomb all the way from London with him.
The next morning Pen dutifully trundled over to the Major’s fancy hotel suite to pay his respects, found the room perfectly tidied by Morgan—hats brushed, coats laid out, maps and passports and a well-thumbed prayer book (the Major never travelled without one, thought every self-respecting English gentleman had to show face at the local English church, same as he had to call on the ambassador) all lined up perfectly in place. Morgan handed him a stack of post that had been forwarded from his London chambers: a few copies of the *Pall Mall Gazette*, and an envelope of letters.
One of the letters was scrawled in wobbly handwriting, addressed “Arther Pendennis, Esquire”. Pen’s face went bright red the second he saw it, and he read it with a sharp little twist in his chest. It was Fanny Bolton. She’d showed up at his London chambers while he was sick, found he’d left for Germany without a single word to her, no answer to her letter begging for just one kind note, none of the books he’d promised her before he fell ill. She said the people who’d found her at his bedside while he was delirious had turned her away without a word, she thought she’d die of the heartache, but Doctor Goodenough had taken care of her. She’d even kept a tiny lock of his hair from when they’d cut it all off while he was feverish, and she just wanted to know if she could keep it, if no one would force her to hand it back. She forgave everyone, she said, and she’d pray for him for the rest of her life.
When the Major waddled out of the bedroom, fresh from his bath, he found Pen standing there with the letter in his hand, face dark with rage. “What’s got you glum, my boy? Duns chasing you?” he asked, already knowing full well what the problem was.
Pen rounded on him. “Did you have anything to do with this? Did you send Fanny away from my chambers? Intercept her letters? Who dared treat her like garbage? Was it you?”
The Major huffed and puffed, indignant. “I never tamper with any man’s letters, and I don’t answer rude questions! I showed up at your rooms to look after you when you were at death’s door, and this is the thanks I get? Disgraceful!”
Pen apologized quickly for snapping, then pressed him again, and the Major swore up and down he never even spoke a word to Fanny, never sent her away.
“Then it was my mother!” Pen yelled, grabbing his hat. “I will never forgive whoever did this. She was supposed to be her defender, not her enemy! I’m going to go sort this out right now!”
The Major screamed for him to stop, but Pen was already out the door, striding down the street as fast as his legs could carry him back to their lodgings. The Major sighed, waved Morgan over to pour him a coffee, and peered out the window at Pen’s retreating back. “Poor Helen. Oh hell, all the fat’s in the fire now. This is gonna be one hell of a row.”
When Pen burst through their front door, only Warrington was in the drawing room, waiting to escort Helen and Laura to the Sunday service at the little English church. Pen was so furious he couldn’t wait a single second to rant. He flung Fanny’s letter across the table at Warrington. “Look at that! She nursed me when no one else would, they treated that poor little girl like a stray dog, kept all her letters from me, treated me like a snot-nosed kid! My own mother did this.”
“Hey, that’s your mother you’re talking about,” Warrington tried to reason with him.
“That makes it worse!” Pen snapped. “She should be on her knees apologizing to that girl! She gave me everything she had, and this is how we repay her?”
“Hush!” Warrington hissed, nodding at the closed bedroom door. “They can hear you in the next room.”
