The heavy doors of the pavilion swung shut, sealing out the chill of the Tuscan night and admitting a sudden, artificial warmth. As Tito stepped across the threshold, his gaze swept the assembly with the speed of a tactical assessment. The composition of the guests confirmed his hypothesis: this was a political gathering, though masked by the veneer of conviviality. In the Renaissance mindset, the consumption of fine food and vintage wine was not merely hedonistic indulgence but a physiological necessity for political clarity. Only after the stomach was satisfied could the mind truly engage with the weight of statecraft; uninvited hunger rendered one incapable of grasping the nuances of factional loyalty.

The Rucellai household was renowned for its opulence, a stark contrast to the sober frugality typical of Florentines. Here, amidst the splendor of a great table, high philosophical treatises were deemed unnecessary distractions. The presence of Plato's bust, imported from Lorenzo's former villa, was entirely appropriate—a silent overseer of a culture that revered intellect alongside excess. On this specific evening, however, the atmosphere suggested a pragmatic shift away from abstract idealism toward the gritty realities of power consolidation.

Bernardo Rucellai, the host, commanded the room with the poised dignity of a man in his mid-forties. His bearing was haughty yet controlled, radiating the confidence of a man who spoke Latin with such purity that even the sharpest German linguists would struggle to extract a single error. He received Tito with unusual warmth, seating him strategically between Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci, two formidable figures of the Medicean faction.

While the silver basin of scented water circulated, allowing the company to cleanse their hands, the ambient noise was a symphony of aristocratic leisure. Rings glinted on manicured fingers under the flickering wax candles, mingling with the perfume of French damask roses. The conversation drifted effortlessly through the standard topics of the era: the absurdity of future generations imagining themselves eating with silver forks, the shock ancestors would feel witnessing modern customs, and the technical admiration of Antonio Pollaiuolo's enamelwork on the serving pieces.

"Antonio grew weary of chasing gold," Niccolò Ridolfi remarked, his tone carrying the effortless arrogance of a man accustomed to command. "He declared that an artist who pours his brain into molten metal is throwing his intellect into the furnace."

Giannozzo Pucci seized upon the sentiment. "A prescient warning. If the war with Pisa continues and the rebellion spreads, our silverware will be melted down. Soon, Antonio's silver saints in San Giovanni will vanish from the altars, replaced by coins in the pockets of the desperate."

"The Friar prepares us for this," Lorenzo Tornabuoni added, his voice smooth. "God demands not silver crucifixes but the gold of brotherly love. He argues that holy gems cannot coexist with starving bellies."

"A doctrine of war finance," Bernardo Rucellai countered, his expression dry. "Practical, if harsh. But politics require liquid courage, Lorenzo. Solid meat cannot carry the weight of debate; wine is the solvent we need."

Niccolò Ridolfi expanded the thread, invoking Luigi Pulci. "Pulci argued that opinion corrupts the palate itself. 'Scepticism,' he claimed, 'is the only philosophy that leaves the tongue clean.' Yet, he was a man of sharp edges, capable of cutting anything he touched."

"And yet a gentle soul," Giannozzo Pucci observed. "His words were soap bubbles, fleeting and harmless, yet he possessed a temperance rare in such a volatile age."

The meal progressed with ritualistic precision. After the initial courses, the main event arrived: the roasted capon and game, culminating in the pièce de résistance—the peacock. Cooked according to Apicius, the bird retained its feathers, arranged on the platter to resemble a living creature resting in its plumage. The task fell to the official carver, a skilled operator who had to rotate the classical bird on its back to reveal the plucked breast, offering slices to the honored guests. Those with independent minds might opt for the simpler capon, but few dared challenge the tradition.

Tito, observing the scene, quoted Horace and distributed his portion in small, respectful fragments. Bernardo Rucellai made a scholarly note on the ancient value of peacock eggs but abstained from eating his share. Niccolò Ridolfi balanced a piece on his fork, using the delay to recount a tale of a Sienese man who boiled a wild goose in its own feathers to deceive guests into thinking it was a pheasant.

Little actual peacock was consumed, but the spectacle provided a shared sense of exclusivity. To dine where such capricious luxuries were served was to align oneself with the elite, a status that made the recent teachings of Fra Girolamo—preaching against the luxury of the rich for the sake of the poor—all the more dangerous.

Outside, in the encroaching shadows, Baldassarre paced in tightening circles. He watched the illuminated windows, seeing Tito gesturing with careless elegance, his voice rising above the din. Baldassarre's mind was a wire pulled taut; he was rehearsing his entry into this fortress of privilege, deriving a savage pleasure from Tito's oblivious joy. It was a dance of preparation, setting the stage for a far more brutal confrontation. Inside, the men bathed in candlelight remained unaware of the pale, fierce visage watching them from the dark. Light could be as impenetrable a shield as shadow.

As the conversation shifted from trivialities to urgent civic matters, the influence of Fra Girolamo became undeniable. Even the most neutral attendees felt the pressure of the Friar's rhetoric. Before the stewed fish was cleared, the name of the Dominican friar dominated the discourse, overriding Bernardo Rucellai's earlier attempts to steer clear of politics. With the servants withdrawn, the discussion hardened.

"They are all knotted together," Niccolò Ridolfi asserted. "The Frate possesses a sharper mind for public affairs than either Soderini or the Piagnones. Soderini is merely his mouthpiece."

"No, Niccolò," Bernardo Rucellai corrected firmly. "Fra Girolamo understands utility; he sees what serves his ends. But Pagolantonio Soderini, with his study of the Venetian Council, carries his own burden. Soderini loads the cannon; the Friar provides the gunpowder and strikes the match. The people are becoming masters of us."

Lorenzo Tornabuoni waited until the room was clear of staff before continuing. "Whether Soderini owes him allegiance or not, we owe the Friar the General Amnesty. Without his intervention, the Medici heads would have been cast aside, and our properties confiscated. Fra Girolamo is a rock on the issue of peace."

A ripple of surprise traveled the table, but Bernardo nodded, signaling Tornabuoni to proceed.

"I interviewed him in his cell," Tornabuoni revealed, his voice dropping to a conspiratorial register. "Valori rarely grants such access. I suspect he favored me precisely because I was not a convert, but someone needing conversion. The path forward for the Medici is clear: we must throw our strength behind the Friar's party. Alone, we are too weak to resist. If the Friar and the popular party fall, the Arrabbiati—Nerli, Alberti, the Pazzi—will rise. They will not offer amnesty; they will hunt us like dogs until half of us are exiled."

The consensus was immediate and vocal. Tornabuoni concluded with a strategic maxim: "Dissimulation is the only weapon for the rational in times of violence. While the Friar preaches a utopia of universal good for an island discovered by Columbus, Florence requires a single dominant family interest. The change is coming; with patience, we shall guide it. Until then, we must keep the Friar's flag flying. Any other banner raised now would be black for us."
