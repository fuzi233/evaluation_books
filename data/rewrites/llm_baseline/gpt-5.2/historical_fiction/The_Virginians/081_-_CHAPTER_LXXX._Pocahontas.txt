The town, truth to say, knew less of Pocahontas than we of Virginia, who keep her name as one keeps a homely song—by repetition at the hearth until it is part of the household’s very breathing. At the hint of my friends I threw out, for a kind of lure, a brief ballad touching the Indian princess, and it found its way into the magazines a few days before my tragedy ventured itself upon the boards. Sampson hailed the device with rapture.

“It is ground-bait, sir,” cried he; “you will see the fish rise in multitudes on the great day!”

He and Spencer would have it that the verses were debated with much clatter of cups and saucers at divers coffee-houses; that one set swore Mr. Mason wrote them; another, Mr. Cowper of the Temple; and there were even mighty heads wagging that named Mr. Gray. Poor Sam—who could never resist the seasoning of a tale—had, I dare say, sprinkled those rumours himself as a cook flings in salt with a knowing hand; and had any one cried “Shakspeare!” he would have sworn my Pocahontas the Bard’s best forgotten jewel.

For my own part, Captain Smith was an old acquaintance of mine—older than London, older than my marriage—dating from the days when, a boy, I sat at my grandfather’s knees in the great Virginian library, with a favourite volume open and my finger travelling line by line as though it were a map of wonders. How the room comes back with the very smell of it—the leather of bindings warmed by the sun, the faint cedar of the presses, and, from the yard beyond, the country’s honest chorus: pigeons cooing under the eaves, a pump creaking, a negro lad singing at his work, the far clink of a pail on stone. Grandfather, in his slow rich voice, made pictures for me: Smith on the Danube fighting the Turks, Smith in savage hands in our forests, Smith—bound, bleeding, yet unbroken. Ah, that terrific encounter with the three champions! I could taste my childish excitement as sharply as vinegar on the tongue. And Bonny Molgro—what a name!—we endowed him with a turban of unimaginable breadth, whiskers like brambles, a scimitar as long as a harrow-tine, and a ferocity to match. The old book’s very cadence beat like a drum in the mind: the battle-axes clanging, the shout from the rampires, the falchion’s quick flash, and then the grisly reward—three Turks’ heads in a shield for arms, and three hundred ducats yearly for pension.

Disdaining time and place, as poets are licensed to do, I had my Smith achieve like feats upon our Potomac and the banks of James River. The ground-bait verses ran thus:

“Wearied arm and broken sword
Wage in vain the desperate fight;
Round him press the countless horde,
He is but a single knight.
Hark! a cry of triumph shrill
Through the wilderness resounds,
As, with twenty bleeding wounds,
Sinks the warrior, fighting still.

“Now they heap the fatal pyre,
And the torch of death they light—
Ah! ’tis hard to die of fire!
Who will shield the captive knight?
Round the stake with fiendish cry
Wheel and dance the savage crowd,
Cold the victim’s mien and proud,
And his breast is bared to die.

“Who will shield the fearless heart?
Who avert the murderous blade?
From the throng, with sudden start,
See, there springs an Indian maid.
Quick she stands before the knight,
‘Loose the chain, unbind the ring,
I am daughter of the king,
And I claim the Indian right!’

“Dauntlessly aside she flings
Lifted axe and thirsty knife;
Fondly to his heart she clings,
And her bosom guards his life!
In the woods of Powhattan,
Still ’tis told, by Indian fires,
How a daughter of their sires
Saved the captive Englishman.”

As for the plot of my tragedy, my children may take it down from the shelf any day and read it if they have a mind; nor am I eager to read it aloud again, since Captain Miles and the parson, last Christmas, chose to fall asleep at mamma’s request, after I had got but a couple of acts fairly upon their ears. Any person moderately conversant with plays and novels can, from the above, fill in the rest to his liking: an Indian king; a loving princess; her attendant who, like her mistress, has a heart too large for the bounds of tribe or station, and must be in love with the British captain’s servant; a traitor within the English fort; a brave Indian warrior, gnawed by an unhappy passion for Pocahontas; and, above all, a medicine-man and priest—Palmer played him very well—capable of every stratagem, and bent upon the English prisoner’s torture and death. Add to these the accidents of the wilderness, the war-dances and cries (which Gumbo, from home-knowledge, mimicked with painful truth), and the arrival of the English fleet with allusions to our late victories in Canada and the determination of Britons to rule and conquer in America—and some of us, not unnaturally, believed there was matter enough for applause.

But I had already noted the ill omens. A peevish, jealous, timid management set itself, as though by instinct, to trip the piece at every step; and there was, in certain high quarters, a prejudice against it as bitter as wormwood. What wonder, then, that Pocahontas proved no victory?

I laugh to scorn the critics’ malignity who found fault with the performance—pretty judges, forsooth, who cried Carpezan a masterpiece whilst a far superior and more elaborate work earned only their sneers! I insist that Hagan acted admirably—so admirably that a certain actor and manager of the theatre might well be jealous of him—and that but for a cabal outside, the piece would have succeeded. The order had been given that the play should not succeed; so Sampson always declared. “The house swarmed with Macs, by George,” he swore, “and they should have the galleries washed with brimstone.” He would maintain, in his honest fury, that Mr. Garrick himself would not have had it succeed for the world, and was never in such a rage as during that grand scene in the second act where Smith—poor Hagan—bound to the stake, is saved by Pocahontas; for then, for a moment, the whole house did indeed thrill with applause and sympathy.

Anybody with curiosity may refer to the published tragedy—octavo, or the subsequent splendid quarto in my Collected Works—and decide whether the scene be without merit; whether the verses be not elegant; the language rich and noble. One cause of the failure, oddly enough, was my fidelity to history. I had copied at the Museum, with care, a figure of Sir Walter Raleigh in frill and beard; and (my dear Theo giving me some of her mother’s best lace) we dressed Hagan accurately after my drawing. No man could look better. Miss Pritchard as Pocahontas I dressed, too, as a Red Indian, having seen enough of that costume at home. Will it be believed? the house tittered when she first appeared. They grew used to her, however; and just at the moment when she rushed into the prisoner’s arms, and a number were actually in tears, a fellow in the pit bawled out, “Bedad! here’s the Belle Savage kissing the Saracen’s Head;” whereupon an impertinent roar of laughter broke forth, with fitful explosions during the remainder of the play.

As the wag in Mr. Sheridan’s Critic says of the morning guns, playwrights are not content with one but must fire two or three; so with this pothouse jest of the Belle Savage—those ignorant people not knowing that Pocahontas herself was the very Belle Sauvage from whom the tavern took its name. The same fellow, like a screech-owl that will not be hushed, repeated his folly till one could have thrust a cork in his mouth with pleasure; and as each new character appeared, he baptised him with some tavern sign—the English governor, with a long beard, “the Goat and Boots;” his lieutenant (Barker), broad of face, “the Bull and Mouth,” and so forth. The curtain fell at last amid a shrill storm of whistles and hisses, which especially assailed poor Hagan whenever he opened his lips.

Sampson saw Master Will in the green boxes with some pretty acquaintances, and had no doubt that the treacherous scoundrel was among the ringleaders. “I would have flung him over into the pit,” said the faithful fellow (and he was man enough to do it), “but I saw a couple of Mr. Nadab’s followers prowling about the lobby, and was obliged to sheer off.”

So the eggs we had counted on selling at market were broken, and our hopes lay shattered before us.

I had looked in from the stage before the curtain rose. The house was pretty well filled; I remarked Mr. Johnson in the front boxes, in a laced waistcoat, with Mr. Reynolds by his side—the latter could not hear, and the former could not see, so they came good-naturedly, A deux, to form an opinion of my poor tragedy. I could see Lady Maria—by the hood she wore—in the lower gallery, once more able to sit and look at her beloved actor performing a principal part. As for Theo, she fairly owned that, unless I ordered her, she would rather not be present; and I gave no such command. If things went wrong—and my mind, like a weather-wise farmer’s, already smelt the change—her suffering would have been intolerable to me. So I let her stay away.

After a light dinner at the Bedford (a thing rather taken than tasted; for when the mind is overwrought the palate, that honest counsellor, grows dull), I went to the theatre shortly before the beginning, intending to remain until defeat or victory was declared. Yet even then I could not help seeing which way fate leaned. There was something gloomy in the look of all about me, as though the very air in the passages had been breathed too often and turned sour. Miss Pritchard had the headache; the barber who brought Hagan’s wig had powdered it like a wretch; the greenroom held none but doubtful faces; and the manager—a flippant, not to say impertinent gentleman, who himself that night looked as dismal as a mute at a funeral—had the insolence to say, “For Heaven’s sake, Mr. Warrington, go and get a glass of punch at the Bedford, and don’t frighten us all here by your dismal countenance!”

“Sir,” said I, “I have a right, for five shillings, to comment upon your face, but I never gave you authority to remark upon mine.”

“Sir,” says he, in a pet, “I most heartily wish I had never seen your face at all!”

“Yours, sir,” said I, “has often amused me greatly; and when painted for Abel Drugger is exceedingly comic”—for I have ever done Mr. G. the justice to think that in low comedy he was unrivalled.

I bowed and walked off to the coffee-house; and for five years after spoke not a word to him, until he apologised at a nobleman’s house where we met. I told him I had utterly forgotten what he alluded to; on a first night author and manager are flurried alike. And I added, “After all, there is no shame in not being made for the theatre. Mr. Garrick—you were.” A compliment which seemed to please him as much as I intended.

Fidus Achates—my good Sampson—ran to me at the end of the first act: matters, he said, were going pretty well, though he confessed the titter on Miss Pritchard’s first appearance, dressed exactly as an Indian princess should be.

“I cannot help it, Sampson,” said I, filling him a bumper of good punch—hot, fragrant, with the lemon’s sharp oil pricked from its rind—“if Indians are dressed so.”

“Why,” says he, “would you have had Caractacus painted blue like an ancient Briton, or Bonduca with nothing but a cow-skin?”

And indeed it may be that this fidelity to the truth was cause of the ridicule; in which case I, for one, am not ashamed of defeat. There are tongues that cannot bear an unfamiliar word; there are ears that mock at any accent not their own. One might as well ask a London pit to take the hush of a Virginian night for music, when they have trained themselves to relish only the iron scrape and brazen blare of town.

After the second act, my aide-de-camp came with dismal news indeed. I know not how it is: before action I was nervous enough; but in disaster a certain coolness comes upon me, as if the worst, being known, left nothing more to imagine. I asked quietly, “Are things going ill?” called for my reckoning, put on my hat, and marched to the theatre as calmly as if going to dine at the Temple; Sampson at my side, pressing my elbow, kicking link-boys out of the way, and crying, “By George, Mr. Warrington, you are a man of spirit—a Trojan, sir!”

So there were men of spirit in Troy; but fate was too strong. No man can say I did not bear my misfortune with calmness. I could no more help the clamour and noise of the audience than a captain can help the howling of the storm in which his ship goes down. Yet I was determined the rushing waves and broken masts should impavidum ferient, and flatter myself I did not flinch.

“Not Regulus, my dear madam, could step into his barrel more coolly,” Sampson said afterwards to my wife.

It is unjust to say of men of the parasitic nature that they are unfaithful in misfortune. Whether I was prosperous or poor, the wild parson was equally true, and shared our crust as eagerly as he had partaken of better fortune.

I took my place on the stage, whence I could see my poor actors and a portion of the audience that condemned me. The performers gave me a wide berth, perhaps out of pity. I believe I looked as little moved as any spectator; no one would have judged, from my face, that I was the unlucky hero of the night. But when I reached home my dearest Theo looked so pale that I knew the knowledge of my disaster had travelled faster than my feet.

Spencer, Sampson, cousin Hagan, and Lady Maria were to come after the play to congratulate the author—God wot! Poor Miss Pritchard, engaged likewise, sent word that I must understand she was far too unwell to sup. My friend the gardener at Bedford House had given my wife his best flowers to deck her little table. There they stood—poor little painted standards, as it were—and the battle lost.

I had borne the defeat well enough until I saw that sweet pale face across the table and those artless trophies of welcome set up for her hero. Then my courage, which had held against the roar of the pit, gave way before the stillness of home; and my heart felt a pang as keen as any that ever smote it.

Our meal was dismal enough, and no effort of talk could quite warm it. Yet we strove, as people will stir a fire that refuses to draw. We spoke of old Mrs. Hagan’s illness—luckily very ill at this time; her disease and its incidents proved, in odd fashion, a blessing, giving us matter not our own. Then we talked of his Majesty’s approaching marriage. I remember, too, with hateful clearness, the most futile scraps of the day—a tune a carpenter whistled near me at the playhouse, just before the dreary curtain fell; the very rhythm seemed to cling like soot.

We spoke of the death of good Mr. Richardson, author of Pamela and Clarissa, whom we all admired exceedingly. And as we talked of Clarissa, Theo put her handkerchief to her eyes once or twice and said faintly, “You know, my love, mamma and I could never help crying over that dear book. Oh, my dearest, dearest mother—how I wish she could be with me now!”

Here were tears that might be owned without naming the play; for a young lady may always weep for her absent mother.

Then we mixed a gloomy bowl—Jamaica limes squeezed till their sharpness sprang into the air; sugar melted; the spirit biting, then softening as the spoon went round—and drank to the health of his Excellency the Governor. And then, for a second toast, I filled a bumper and with a smiling face drank to “our better fortune!”

It was too much. The two women flung themselves into each other’s arms and irrigated each other’s neck-handkerchiefs with tears.

“Oh, Maria! is not—is not my George good and kind?” sobbed Theo.

“Look at my Hagan—how great, how godlike he was in his part!” gasped Maria. “It was a beastly cabal that threw him over—and I could plunge this knife into Mr. Garrick’s black heart—the odious little wretch!” And she actually grasped a weapon at her side.

But she flung it down presently, rushed to her lord and master, and embraced him in the presence of us all. I am not sure whether some one else did not do likewise. We were in a strange fever of grief and tenderness; and in the very midst of calamity Love, that sly comforter, came in among us with such fond blandishments that one scarce wished the calamity away—since it drew from us a sweetness we might otherwise have grudged the world.

Two or three days afterwards—on our birthday—a letter was brought to me in my study containing these lines:

“Returning from the cruel fight
How pale and faint appears my knight!
He sees me anxious at his side;
‘Why seek, my love, your wounds to hide?
Or deem your English girl afraid
To emulate the Indian maid?’

“Be mine my husband’s grief to cheer,
In peril to be ever near;
Whate’er of ill or woe betide,
To bear it clinging at his side;
The poisoned stroke of fate to ward,
His bosom with my own to guard;
Ah! could it spare a pang to his,
It could not know a purer bliss!
’Twould gladden as it felt the smart,
And thank the hand that flung the dart!”

I do not say the verses are very good; but I liked them as well as if they were. And when I went into the writer’s drawing-room after receiving the letter, and saw her—blushing, blessing me—her face, and the sound of her sweet young voice (which I can fancy even now as I hum the lines), seemed to me more beautiful than any dream I could borrow out of Heaven.
