"Betteredge!" I called out, gesturing toward the familiar book resting on his knees. "Has *Robinson Crusoe* told you, this very evening, that I might appear?"

"By the Lord Harry, Mr. Franklin!" the old man cried, his voice trembling with delight. "That is precisely what *Robinson Crusoe* has done!"

He scrambled to his feet with my support, standing frozen for a moment, peering back and forth between the book and me, seemingly unable to decide which of us had surprised him more. Ultimately, the verdict favored the novel. Clutching it open in both hands, he stared at the pages with an expression of utter anticipation—as though expecting Robinson Crusoe himself to step out of the text to greet us personally.

"Here's the passage, Mr. Franklin!" he announced once his speech returned. "As I live by bread, sir, this is exactly what I was reading the instant before you arrived! Page 156 reads: 'I stood like one Thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an Apparition.' If that does not mean: 'Expect the sudden arrival of Mr. Franklin Blake,' then the English language holds no meaning whatsoever!" Betteredge slammed the book shut, freeing one hand finally to grasp the one I extended.

Under the circumstances, I naturally expected him to bombard me with questions. Yet, no such thing occurred. The instinct of hospitality dominated the old servant's mind whenever a family member appeared, however unexpectedly, as a guest.

"Walk in, Mr. Franklin," he said, offering a quaint, old-fashioned bow while ushering me inside. "I'll inquire about your visit afterward—I must ensure you are comfortable first. Sad changes have occurred since you departed. The house is locked up, and the staff has scattered. Do not worry! I shall prepare your dinner; the gardener's wife will make your bed; and if a bottle of our renowned Latour claret remains in the cellar, Mr. Franklin, it shall be poured down your throat immediately. I bid you welcome, sir! I bid you a hearty welcome!"

The poor old man fought bravely against the gloom of the empty house, welcoming me with the warm, courteous attentiveness of days past. It pained me to disappoint him. Yet, this was Rachel's house now. Could I eat within its walls, or sleep upon its floors, after the events that unfolded in London? Common self-respect—forbade me properly, strictly forbade me—to cross the threshold.

Taking Betteredge by the arm, I led him out into the garden. There was no alternative. I was compelled to speak the truth. Caught between his devotion to Rachel and his affection for me, he was deeply perplexed and distressed by the shift in circumstances. When he voiced his thoughts, he did so in his customary direct manner, flavored with the most positive philosophy I have ever known—the distinct philosophy of the Betteredge school.

"Miss Rachel has her faults," he began. "I have never denied it. Riding the high horse occasionally is one of them. She has been attempting to ride over *you*, and you have endured it. Good heavens, Mr. Franklin, do you not know women by now better than that? You have heard me discuss the late Mrs. Betteredge?"

I had listened to him speak of the late Mrs. Betteredge frequently, invariably presenting her as his definitive proof of the innate frailty and perversity inherent in the fairer sex. In that role, he presented her now.

"Very well, Mr. Franklin. Listen closely. Women differ in how they ride the high horse. The late Mrs. Betteredge exercised her hobby on that favorite female beast whenever I denied her something she desired above all else. Just as surely as I returned from work on such occasions, so surely would my wife summon me up the kitchen stairs to declare that, following my brutal treatment, she lacked the heart to cook my dinner. I tolerated it for some time—much as you endure it now from Miss Rachel. Finally, my patience snapped. I descended the stairs, took Mrs. Betteredge—affectionately, I assure you—into my arms, and carried her, holus-bolus, into the best parlor where she entertained her guests. I declared, 'That is the proper place for you, my dear,' and returned to the kitchen. I locked the door, removed my coat, rolled up my sleeves, and prepared my own meal. Once finished, I served it with my finest style and enjoyed it thoroughly. Afterwards, I smoked my pipe, had a sip of grog, cleared the table, washed the dishes, polished the cutlery, stored everything away, and swept the hearth until it shone brighter than ever. Then I opened the door and invited Mrs. Betteredge in. 'I have eaten, my dear,' I said, 'and I trust you will find the kitchen arranged exactly to your fondest wishes.' For the remainder of that woman's life, Mr. Franklin, I never had to cook another dinner! Moral: You have tolerated Miss Rachel in London; do not tolerate her in Yorkshire. Return to the house!"

His argument was utterly irrefutable. I could only assure my dear friend that even his persuasive talents were wasted on me in this instance.

"It is a lovely evening," I replied. "I shall walk to Frizinghall, stay at the hotel, and you must join me for breakfast tomorrow morning. I have something important to discuss with you."

Betteredge shook his head solemnly. "I am truly sorry for this," he said. "I had hoped, Mr. Franklin, to hear that matters were smoothed between you and Miss Rachel. If you insist on your own way, sir," he continued after a pause, "there is no need to travel to Frizinghall tonight for lodging. Something closer exists. There is Hotherstone's Farm, merely two miles hence. You could scarcely object to *that* on Miss Rachel's account," the old man added slyly. "Mr. Franklin, Hotherstone owns his property outright."

The mention of the place triggered a memory instantly. The farmhouse sat in a sheltered inland valley along the banks of the loveliest stream in that stretch of Yorkshire. The farmer maintained a spare bedroom and parlor, typically rented to artists, anglers, and tourists alike. As a temporary residence during my stay in the neighborhood, I could not have wished for a more agreeable location.

"Are the rooms available?" I inquired.

"Mrs. Hotherstone herself, sir, requested my recommendation for the rooms yesterday."

"I shall accept them, Betteredge, with great pleasure."

We returned to the yard where I had left my traveling bag. After threading a stick through the handle and slinging the bag over his shoulder, Betteredge seemed to revert to the bewilderment my sudden arrival had caused when I found him in the beehive chair. He gazed incredulously at the house, then wheeled around to look at me with even greater disbelief.

"I have lived a considerable length of time in this world," said this best and dearest of all old servants, "yet I never anticipated witnessing the like of this. Here stands the house, and here stands Mr. Franklin Blake—and, damme, if one of them is not turning his back on the other to sleep in a lodging!"

He led the way out, wagging his head and muttering ominously. "There is only one further miracle possible," he remarked over his shoulder. "The next thing you will do, Mr. Franklin, will be to repay me that seven-and-sixpence you borrowed when you were a boy."

This barb lifted his spirits regarding both himself and me. We exited through the lodge gates. Once clear of the estate grounds, the duties of hospitality (according to Betteredge's moral code) ceased, and the privileges of curiosity took hold. He fell back slightly to match my pace.

"A fine evening for a stroll, Mr. Franklin," he said, as though we had merely crossed paths by accident. "Suppose you had gone to the hotel at Frizinghall, sir?"

"Yes?"

"In that case, I would have honored you with breakfast tomorrow morning."

"Instead, come to breakfast with me at Hotherstone's Farm."

"Much obliged for your kindness, Mr. Franklin. But it was not exactly breakfast I intended to propose. You mentioned earlier that you had something to say to me? If it is not a secret, sir," Betteredge said, abruptly abandoning his circuitous route for the direct approach, "I am burning to know what brought you down here so suddenly."

"What brought me here previously?" I asked.

"The Moonstone, Mr. Franklin. But what brings you now, sir?"

"The Moonstone again, Betteredge."

The old man stopped abruptly, staring at me in the grey twilight as if suspecting his own ears were failing him.

"If that is a jest, sir," he said, "I fear I am becoming somewhat dull in my advanced years. I do not comprehend it."

"It is no joke," I answered. "I have returned to resume the investigation I abandoned upon leaving England. I have come to accomplish what no one else has attempted—to discover who stole the Diamond."

"Leave the Diamond alone, Mr. Franklin! Accept my counsel and leave the Diamond be! That cursed Indian gem has misled every soul who approached it. Do not squander your money and your temper—in the prime of your youth, sir—by meddling with the Moonstone. How can *you* possibly hope to succeed (forgive my boldness), when even Sergeant Cuff managed to botch it?"
