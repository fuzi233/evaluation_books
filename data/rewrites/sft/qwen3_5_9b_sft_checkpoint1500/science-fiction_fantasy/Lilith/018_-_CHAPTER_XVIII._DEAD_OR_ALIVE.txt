I pressed forward, keeping my gaze fixed on the moon. She hung low in the sky, staring directly into the canopy of trees below. Something was wrong with her; she appeared dark and bruised, resembling a battered disc of tarnished copper, looking utterly disheartened and exhausted. No cloud hovered nearby to offer companionship, and the stars burned too brightly to tolerate her presence. "Will this endure forever?" she seemed to whisper, a question directed at the silence. Though she traveled one way and I another, our paths wound together through the woods for a long distance. We shared little conversation, for my eyes were cast downward, but her mournful expression remained fixed upon me, a silent accusation I felt without needing to see it. For a prolonged time, we walked side by side—the moon a dull, sickly luminescence, and I, a living shadow trailing behind.

Something white beneath the sprawling branches of a tree arrested my attention. I turned toward it. Though vague within the dappled shade of the leaves, as I approached, it coalesced into the shape of a human figure. "Another skeleton!" I murmured, dropping to my knees and placing my hand upon the remains. Yet it was flesh, albeit reduced to the state where a body most closely resembles bone. Lying on its side, it was ice-cold—not the inert chill of stone, but the specific, tragic cold of something that once lived and now does not. The closer I examined it, the more impossible it became to conceive it as anything other than deceased. In a single, bewildering instant, I imagined it one of the wild dancers, a spectral Cinderella who had lost her way home and perished in the bizarre night of an outdoor realm. She was entirely naked, so emaciated that even in the gloom, I could count every rib in her flank without touching them. Her bones seemed visible as if tightly encased in thin, stretched leather. Her teeth, beautiful yet terrifying, gleamed ghastly through lips pulled back in unnatural retraction. Her hair cascaded far longer than her own height, thick, incredibly fine to the touch, and black as the void itself.

She was the body of a tall woman, likely graceful. How had she arrived there? She could not have wandered here voluntarily, especially in such a ravaged state. Her strength must have failed; she had collapsed and lain there until starvation claimed her. But how could she be reduced to this degree of desiccation? And why was she stripped naked? Which savages would have taken her clothes, or what wild beast would have torn them away? If her body was left behind, that was not surprising.

I rose and stood, contemplating the situation. I could not, and I must not, allow her to remain exposed and abandoned. A natural reverence forbade it. Even the garment of a woman demands respect; leaving her body uncovered was unthinkable. Irreverent eyes might feast upon her, brutal claws might toss her about, and years would pass before kind rains washed her back into the earth—but the ground here was hard, almost solid with interlacing roots, and I possessed only my bare hands.

Initially, it seemed evident that she had died recently; there was no sign of decomposition. But what substance did the slow wasting of life leave to rot?

Could she still be alive? Might she not? This strange world operated on strange laws. Even if she were breathing, chances of revival were slim, yet I needed certainty before burying her.

Leaving the forest hall, I had noticed a cluster of ripe grapes hanging in the doorway and carried them with me, snacking as I walked. A few remained on the stalk; their juice might potentially restore her. Her mouth was slightly agape, though positioned awkwardly. To shift her body, I slid my arm under her shoulder and discovered pine needles beneath her warm. She could not have been dead for long, and *might* still be alive, despite the absence of a heartbeat or breath I could detect. One of her hands was clenched tightly around something small. I forced a grape into her mouth, but there was no swallow.

To do everything possible for her, I spread a thick layer of pine needles and dry leaves, draped one of my garments over them—still warm from my skin—and lifted her onto this makeshift bed. I covered her with my clothes and a heavy mound of leaves, attempting to preserve the faint warmth remaining in her, hoping the returning sun would amplify it. Another grape yielded no result; her lips and throat showed no movement.

"Doubt," I told myself, "is a poor motivator for action, but it is no excuse for inaction." The skin stretched so tightly over her bones that I dared not apply friction.

I crawled into the leaf pile, drawing as close as possible, and embraced her. My internal heat was waning, but whatever remained, I would share. Thus I spent the rest of the night, sleepless, yearning for the dawn. Her cold radiated into me, but no warmth flowed back.

If I had fled from the beautiful sleepers, each resting on their "dim, straight" silver couches, to lie alone with such a companion, I thought, I would have squandered a lovely privilege. Instead, I was condemned to an awful duty. Beneath the sorrowful, slowly setting moon, I lay with the dead, waiting for sunrise.

Darkness surrendered to the gray light of the eastern horizon when I perceived a motion, distinct from any object moving—a low, undulating glide of a large snake passing me in a straight, unerring line. Soon after, what I took for a roebuck doe and her calf emerged, heading for the same point. Moments later, two creatures resembling bear cubs appeared, trailed by three or four smaller ones. Light increased rapidly, and when a troop of horses trotted past a few minutes later, I could see clearly that while the largest were no bigger than the smallest Shetland ponies, they must be fully grown, so perfectly formed and so thoroughly embodying the mannerisms of great horses were they. They varied in breed: models of cart-horses, chargers, hunters, and racers. Behind them followed dwarf cattle and miniature elephants.

"Why are the children not here?" I asked myself. "The moment I am free of this poor woman, I must return and fetch them!"

Where were these creatures bound? What drew them? Was this an exodus or a morning routine? I must wait for the sun. Until it rose, I could not abandon the woman. Placing my hand on her body, I could not help noticing it felt a fraction warmer. Perhaps it had absorbed some of the heat I had lost; it certainly hadn't generated any. Hope, however slight, persisted.

The forehead of the day began to glow, and soon the sun peeked up, as if observing the bustling activity of this new world for the first time. Witnessing his great, innocent splendor, I rose, filled with vitality and defiance against death. I removed the handkerchief I had placed to shield her mouth and eyes from the pine needles and looked anxiously to see if I held a priceless jewel or merely its empty shell.

Her body lay motionless as when I found it. Then, for the first time in the morning light, I saw how drawn and hollow her face was, how sharp the bones protruded beneath the skin, how every tooth cut a path through her lips. Her human garment was worn down to its final threads, yet the bird of heaven might still be nesting within, ready to awaken to motion and song!

But the sun beat down on her face! I rearranged the handkerchief, laid a few leaves lightly over her, and departed to follow the creatures. Their main trail was well-beaten, evidently used for generations, joined by countless other tracks merging into and broadening it. As I walked, the trees receded and the grass grew denser. Soon the forest vanished, replaced by a vast expanse of loveliest green stretching to the horizon. Along the forest's edge flowed a small river, and the trail led to it. Seeing the water ignited a new, undefined hope. The stream appeared deep everywhere, full to the brim, yet nowhere more than a few yards wide. A bluish mist rose from it, dissipating as it ascended. On the opposite bank, amidst lush grass, numerous small animals fed. They apparently slept in the forest and sought the plain in the morning, swimming the river to cross. I knelt to drink, but the water was scalding and tasted strangely metallic.

I leaped up: here was the warmth I craved—the first necessity of life! I raced back to my helpless burden.

Without pausing to consider my solitude, no one would comprehend what lay for me in redeeming this woman from death. "Prove what she may," I thought, "I shall at least no longer be lonely!" I had found myself such poor company that now, truly, I understood hope. This blessed water would banish the cold death and drown my desolation!

I carried her to the stream. Tall as she was, she felt miraculously light; her bones were so delicate and so thinly covered. Hope surged when I realized she was not so stiff that I could not carry her on one arm, like a sleeping child, leaning against my shoulder. I moved softly, fearing even the wind of my motion, grateful for the lack of others.
