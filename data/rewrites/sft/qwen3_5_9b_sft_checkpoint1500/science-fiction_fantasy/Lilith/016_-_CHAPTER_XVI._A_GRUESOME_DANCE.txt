I rose to resume my journey, walking countless miles across desolate plains. My heart ached for a mountain, a towering rock from whose peak I might gaze across the bleak landscape toward some sliver of hope. Yet what purpose could such foresight serve? The destiny of a man is forged not by what he sees ahead, but by what lies within him; the internal operation is the event that matters. To foresee is not to understand, for if prophecies inherent in humanity surfaced often, the future would already be known.

The sun hung halfway to the horizon when I spotted a rugged ascent. Before reaching it, however, the urge to climb evaporated, replaced by a desperate need to rest. Night was falling rapidly, the air turning dark. At my feet lay a carpet of the softest, greenest moss—a throne fit for a king. I collapsed onto it, and instantly the weariness began to recede. The moment my head touched the ground, the third time, I heard beneath me the sound of many waters. They played broken, ethereal tunes against the stones of their buried channels, a chaotic harmony that was nonetheless beautiful. If Handel had heard that endless gurgle and bell-like drip, mingled with mutually destructive melodies, what masterpieces he might have crafted!

Listening, my eyes wandered up the steep rocky slope above. The stone bore the record of ages past: a cataract that once rushed down here, filling the channels I now traced. My heart swelled imagining the splendid tumult, the waves dancing in helpless fall to mass a single organ-roar below. Soon, the hidden brooks lulled me to sleep, their lullabies weaving into my dreams.

I woke before dawn and climbed eagerly to see what lay beyond. Alas, there was nothing but a desert of finest sand. Not a trace remained of the river that had plunged down the rocks; the powdery drift had filled its course to the level of the dreary expanse. Looking back, I saw the river had split into two branches—one leading to the foot of the rocky scarp where I now stood, the other crossing to the Evil Wood. I could see the wood on the far horizon, nestled between the two arms of the dry riverbed. To my left, the desert stretched endlessly; to the right, a slight lift in the skyline offered a glimmer of hope, pointing toward the forest my hostess had mentioned.

Sitting down, I reached into my pocket for the half-loaf I had carried—only then truly grasping what my hostess had meant. Verily, the bread was not for tomorrow. It had shrunk and hardened into stone. I cast it away and set off again.

By noon, I encountered a few tamarisks and junipers, then stunted firs. As I progressed, thicker thickets and larger pines surrounded me, eventually forming a forest exactly like that where the Little Ones found their babies, though I suspected I had merely circled back to a different section of the same. But where I was mattered little; everywhere was the same as nowhere. I had not yet, by acting, made *anywhere* into a *place*. I was not yet alive; I was only dreaming that I lived. I was but a consciousness with an outlook. I realized then that I had been nothing else in the world I left, and now I knew the truth. I told myself that if I caught a faint gleam of the mirror in this forest, I would turn aside, lest it trap me unawares and drag me back to my old existence. Here, I might learn to be something by doing something. I could not endure returning with so many beginnings and no end achieved. The Little Ones would meet their fate; the awful witch I would never meet; the dead would rise without me. I would only wake to know I had dreamed, and all my traveling would have been for naught. I preferred to go on and on rather than arrive at such a close.

I ventured deeper into the wood, weary and seeking rest.

The trees were now massive, standing in a regular, almost geometric array with spacious intervals between them. There was little undergrowth, and I could see far in every direction. The forest resembled a great church: solemn, silent, and empty. I met nothing on two legs or four throughout the day. Occasionally, a swift creature or a slow beast crossed the clearing where my gaze settled, but always at a distance, enhancing the profound sense of vast emptiness. I heard a few birds and saw abundant butterflies, some of marvellous, gorgeous coloring, others of pure, dazzling whiteness.

Reaching a spot where the pines stood further apart, allowing flowering shrubs to thrive, and hoping it signaled nearby habitation, I followed the path of increasing roses. I was starving for the voice and face of my kind—for any living soul, human or otherwise, whom I might perhaps understand. What a hellish horror it was to wander alone, a bare existence that never expanded into another life, forever bound by the cords of its peculiarities, a prisoner in the dungeon of its own being. I began to learn that it was impossible to live for oneself, even, save in the presence of others—and then, alas, fearfully possible! Evil existed only through good; selfishness was but a parasite on the tree of life. In my former world, I had the habit of solitary song; here, not a crooning murmur ever parted my lips. There, I sang without thinking; here, I thought without singing. There, I had never possessed a bosom friend; here, the affection of an idiot would be divinely welcome. "If only I had a dog to love!" I sighed, regarding my past self with wonder. She who preferred the company of books or pens to that of men and women, who would send away the author of a tale she enjoyed so she might return to the story herself. I had chosen the dead over the living, the thing thought over the thing thinking. "Any man," I said now, "is more than the greatest of books!" I had not cared for my living brothers and sisters, and now I was left without even the dead to comfort me.

The wood thinned further, the pines growing taller, sending up huge stems like columns eager to support the heavens. Other trees appeared, enriching the forest. The roses had become trees, their flowers of astonishing splendor.

Suddenly, I spied what seemed a great house or castle. Yet its forms were so strangely indistinct that I could not be certain it was anything more than a chance combination of tree shapes. As I approached, the lines held together, but neither they nor the structure grew more definite. Standing before it, I remained as doubtful of its nature as before. Habitable, it certainly was not; it might be a ruin overgrown with ivy and roses! Yet of any building hidden in the foliage, not the poorest wall-remnant could I discern. Again and again, I seemed to spy what must be construction, but it vanished before closer inspection. Could it be, I pondered, that the ivy had embraced a huge edifice and consumed it, its interlaced branches retaining the shapes of the walls it had assimilated? I could be sure of nothing.

Before me lay a rectangular vacancy—the ghost of a doorway without a door. I stepped through and found myself in an open space like a great hall, its floor covered with grass and flowers, its walls and roof woven from ivy, vine, and roses.

There could be no better place to spend the night! I gathered a quantity of withered leaves, laid them in a corner, and threw myself upon them. A red sunset filled the hall; the night was warm, and my couch restful. I lay gazing up at the living ceiling, tracing its branches and twigs, lost in clouds of foliage and peering patches of loftier roof. My eyes waded through the canopy as if tangled in it until the sun sank and the sky began to darken. Then the red roses turned black, and soon only the yellow and white remained visible. When those vanished, the stars emerged, hanging in the leaves like live topazes, throbbing, sparkling, and flashing myriad colors. I was canopied by a tree from Aladdin's cave!

Then I discovered the hall was full of nests. Tiny heads, nearly indistinguishable, popped out with a chirp or two and disappeared again. For a while there were rustlings, stirrings, and little prayers; but as darkness deepened, the small heads grew still. Finally, every feathered mother had her brood quiet under her wings, the chatter in the little beds ended, and God's bird-nursery rested beneath the waves of sleep. Once more, a few flutterings made me look up: an owl sailed across. I caught only a glimpse, yet felt several times the cool wafture of his silent wings. The mother birds did not move again; they saw he sought mice, not children.

About midnight, I woke abruptly, roused by a revelry whose noises were yet not loud. Neither were they distant; they were close, yet attenuated. My eyes were so dazzled that for a while I saw nothing; at last, they cleared.

I lay on my withered leaves in the corner of a splendid hall. Before me stood a crowd of gorgeously dressed men and gracefully robed women, none of whom seemed to see me. In dance after dance, they vaguely embodied the story of life: its meetings, its passions, its partings.
