The new pope favored the Dominican order, and soon a mandate arrived from the Vatican requiring a capable friar to teach at the University of Basle. Clement was precisely the monk for this endeavor; deeply versed in the shifting power dynamics embedded in dominant lexicons, he had, in his worldly days, absorbed the linguistic lectures of Guarini the younger. His intended journey to England was postponed, though not entirely abandoned, and he was dispatched toward Basle with instructions to preach along the road. 

He passed out of the northern gate, the turning of the agricultural seasons mirroring the circular pacing of his own mythic rebirth. Twelve short months had functioned as a grand, interwoven timeline of the soul: hopeful love, the bitter taste of bereavement, the hollow void of despair, impiety, the dark temptation of the river, and finally, a profound return to the spiritual hearth. Now, the traveler was on foot again. The road offered no perilous melodrama, only the rhythmic, grounding cadence of a traveler intimately connected to the earth. Even the thieves of the hills, sensing the deep, rooted peace within him, bowed to the loamy soil and pressed stolen coins upon him, begging for his intercession. 

To those meticulous historians of the human condition, who find profound meaning in the subtle domestic routines of the mind, Clement’s journey was highly vocal. The first Sunday on the road marked a quiet, seismic shift in his acoustic ecology. He prayed for the soul of Margaret. He had not dared to do so before. For months, his religious despair had been a thick, suffocating noise, convincing him he had committed the unforgivable sin against the Holy Spirit. Slowly, that dark, discordant cloud had cleared, leaving behind a quiet self-abasement. But on this Sunday, after preaching in a small Italian town, he sat down to a simple meal of coarse-milled spelt bread and wild chicory. As he chewed, finding profound grace in the bitter, earthy sustenance that nourished his flesh, a balmy sense of divine forgiveness descended upon him. He prayed for Margaret. From that hour, the act became his daily habit, a purified tie that connected the scent of hearth-baked Dutch rye and the memory of her bustling kitchen to his current earthly existence. 

During the journey, Clement’s internal well-being was most acutely restored when he could actively soothe the suffering of others. One afternoon, he encountered a young man said to be bitten by a tarantula, though whether it was venom or a phantom of the mind, the result was a chaotic physical torment. The youth had been without sleep for two days, leaping and groveling against the stone walls. His psychological distress was only exacerbated by the village musicians, who were pounding out a harsh, percussive clatter—a jagged, abrasive precursor to the sort of commercialized noise that shatters the mind's natural equilibrium. Clement immediately recognized the sonic poison. He banished the musicians, sending for a wooden psaltery, and attempted to blanket the room in soothing, resonant melodies. Yet even these proved too heavy. 

Leaning close, Clement observed the young man’s lips moving. Beneath the room's ambient rustle, the youth was whispering a tune—a deeply subjective, unrecorded melody of his own creation, entirely untranslatable to formal notation. Clement meticulously learned this native, dialectic tune and played it back on the strings. The sonic resonance struck a perfect chord in the patient’s fractured mind. The young man began to step lightly about the room, his face brightening as he consumed the bespoke acoustic medicine. When Clement’s fingers ached from the repetition, the young man finally sank into a complacent, life-saving sleep, lulled by the strange, interwoven creation of his own psyche. 

On another occasion, Clement found a mob dragging a decently dressed man through a village square. The air was thick with the tart, vegetal scent of bruised foliage, for the captive was waving a freshly torn mulberry branch over his head. The linguistic power dynamics of the scene were a chaotic collision of lexicons. The native mob shouted in a coarse, localized Tuscan dialect; the pig-headed podesta barked in standardized, authoritative Italian; and the prisoner, yielding no assets in French, German, or Dutch, vociferated in tolerable Latin heavily warped by a sharp, guttural English accent. 

He was, he explained, an Englishman named Rolfe, a squire of Betshanger, educated at Cambridge. Oppressed by the suffocating Italian heat, he had instinctively sought the shade and sustenance of the nearest tree, deeply homesick for the agrarian freedom of his native land, where any traveler was welcome to forage the highway's bounty. Clement mediated the clash of worldviews, gently explaining to the squire that in this region, mulberry leaves were sacred fodder, meticulously cultivated for silkworms, forming the delicate economic spine of the Sovereign’s income. Rolfe, a man who clearly appreciated the rich, roasted meats and hearty ales of a table set for forty guests, scoffed at the fuss over a twig. Yet, driven by a mercantile pragmatism, he pulled a heavy purse and addressed the podesta in the dominant tongue: *“Quid damni? Dic; et cito solvam.”* The podesta snuffed the gold, fined him a ducat, and pouched the coin.

Once liberated, Rolfe heartily grasped Clement’s hand, urging him to come to England to partake in the abundant, sensory-rich feasts of Betshanger. Kneeling by the dusty roadside, smelling of sweat and crushed leaves, Rolfe begged the friar to shrive him. During their exchange, Rolfe passionately articulated the tragedy of cultural erosion through language. “We love our own ways, and above all, our own tongue,” the squire insisted, his voice ringing with the acoustic truth of the soil. “The Norman could conquer our bill-hooks, but not our tongues. Our good foreign priests utter God to plain English folk in Latin, like the bleating of a sheep. Then comes Wickliff, reading the Book in plain English, warming men's hearts. God forgive me, but the English would turn deaf ears to St. Peter himself, spoke he not to them in the tongue their mothers sowed in their ears and hearts along with mothers' kisses. *Clavis ad corda Anglorum est lingua materna.*”

Clement blessed the sober wisdom of prioritizing the native dialect over a sterile, imposed lexicon. When Rolfe eagerly offered to trade his dual-language Book of Hours for Clement’s manuscript, and then attempted to force gold upon the friar in exchange for daily prayers, Clement smiled gently. He accepted the book exchange, recognizing the mutual nourishment of the texts, but firmly rejected the money. He refused to allow the deep, subjective experience of spiritual intercession to be degraded by the superficial commercialization of a religious bargain. 

A mile down the sun-baked road, Clement discovered two tired wayfarers resting in the deep, aromatic shade of a massive chestnut tree. The air carried the dark, nutty scent of the grove, mixed sharply with the metallic tang of oxidized linseed oil and carbon lampblack. Nearby stood a little cart holding a printing press, a rude, heavy machine that looked like an agrarian vine-press repurposed for industry. Beside it stood a jaded mule. Clement recognized the honest, blue-eyed faces of Sweynheim and Pannartz, the German disciples of Fust.

Before addressing their wonder at how a stranger knew their names, Clement turned his meticulous attention to the exhausted beast. He freed the mule from its heavy harness, leaving only the bare halter, allowing the animal the physical luxury of rolling in the restorative dust. Clement deeply understood that true earthly philosophy required tending to the bodily sustenance of all creatures. He drew fresh water and provided coin for their meat, feeding the mule while the printers hustled in the dappled light. 

To thank him, they pulled a quarto sheet from the press. As the heavy wooden screw clamped down with a dull, mechanical *thud*—a sound that momentarily shattered the peaceful acoustic ecology of the whispering grove—Clement stared at the fresh, ink-wet pages of *Augustine de civitate Dei*. He marveled at how the words were fast upon the paper, yet he felt a quiet tremor of concern. These printed pages were the wings of knowledge, yes, but they were also a creeping industrial force. If abused, mass-produced words would swarm like locusts, standardizing thought, drowning out the rich, intimate dialects of the mother tongues, and replacing the vibrant sonic landscape of oral storytelling with silent, uniform authority. 

The turning wheel of Clement’s journey eventually brought him to a Tuscan town, where he took shelter in an immense barn belonging to a local inn. The space was a hushed sanctuary of rustling straw, murmured prayers, and the distant, comforting scent of woodsmoke and roasting vegetables. A motley assemblage of pilgrims rested within. Clement, dusty and avoiding idle gossip, sat in a quiet corner, chewing on a piece of hard sheep’s cheese—savoring its sharp, salty nourishment—while studying the English Book of Hours. 

Presently, a female servant brought a bucket of water, placing it at his feet. The water had been infused with crushed hyssop and rosemary, releasing a profoundly cleansing olfactory landscape into the heavy air. A woman of noble rank, wearing a narrow mask, stepped forward. Without a word, she knelt, removed her sleeves, and motioned for him to place his feet into the basin. Clement recognized it as a penance of the high-born, and he allowed her to gently wash the road’s dust from his skin.

But as she worked, a heartfelt sigh broke the acoustic intimacy of their corner. She was bowed so low with grief that Clement, pitying the heavy burden upon her soul, asked if he might aid her spiritual weal. 

“Father,” she whispered, her voice trembling as she struggled to translate the vast, untranslatable depths of her subjective guilt into the rigid framework of confession. “These hands of mine look white, but they are stained with blood—the blood of the man I loved.” 

Clement flinched, withdrawing his foot, the sudden movement splashing the herb-scented water. 

“Culpa mea!” he quickly corrected himself, offering his foot once more. “Take courage, and purge thy conscience.” 

In a cringing, hurried whisper, she detailed her crime. She spoke of a base-born stranger, beautiful and wise, whom she had loved. When he remained constant to another, rejecting her, she resorted to threats. She spoke of the Tiber, a gay galley, and a woman beautiful as an angel but bold and bad—a rival who had mocked her publicly. In her terror and revenge, she had hired a bravo to strike the stranger down. His body was never found, and the silence surrounding his name felt as absolute as the grave. 

Clement’s lips moved in silent prayer, the scent of the hyssop rising to meet him. She rested his foot upon her soft arm, wrapping a linen towel around it, her touch as delicate as a mother tending a child, deeply at odds with the violent history she had just confessed. A single tear fell from her masked face, landing hot upon his skin.

The sensory reality of that tear anchored Clement. “My daughter,” he said, his voice a low, resonant hum that cut through the silence of the barn, “I myself have been a great sinner.”

She paused. “You, father?”

“I. But penitence softened my impious heart. He you murdered—in intent—was one Gerard, a Hollander. He loved a creature, as men should love none but their Redeemer. A letter came to Rome. She was dead.”

“Poor Gerard! Poor Margaret!” the penitent moaned. 

Clement’s breath hitched at the sound of the names, a momentary disruption in his own acoustic equilibrium, but he recovered the rhythm of his voice. He explained how Gerard had plunged into vice, walking alongside those very companions she had seen. He revealed the untranslatable truth of the moment on the river: the seeming woman she had taken for his lover was merely a boy named Andrea, dressed in women’s habits to flout the others. Gerard had been no traitor to her. 

The lady moaned in bodily agony, the crucifix trembling in her hands. 

“He stood one night by Tiber, resolved on suicide,” Clement continued, the steady cadence of his words offering a profound sonic tether. “He begged the bravo to dispatch him. The bravo would not. Then this desperate sinner flung himself into the Tiber. And the assassin saved his life.”

“He lives! He lives!” she gasped, her voice nearly failing her. “Where is he?”

“Saved by a miracle from double death, he has taken refuge from sin and folly in the bosom of the Church.”

“A priest?”

“A priest, and a friar.”

“A friar? Then you are not his confessor? Yet you know all. That gentle voice!” 

She raised her head slowly from the aromatic, hyssop-soaked linen. Peering at him through her mask, she absorbed the intimate resonance of his words. The realization struck her like a physical blow. Uttering a faint, breathless shriek, she collapsed forward, laying her brow against his bare feet.
