In the early days of our return to England, when Harry and I were such wayward pupils under our tutor’s stern eye, who would have guessed that Mr. Esmond Warrington of Virginia would one day become a bear-leader himself? My mother—when we met again—could never speak plainly of those years. She only alluded to them as “that terrible time, my love, which I can’t bear to think of,” or “those dreadful years when there was difference between us.” Though the man who had once been my pupil—a generous and grateful soul—sent me several barrels of the very beer that built his fortune, Madam Esmond referred to him only as “your friend in England,” “your wealthy Lambeth friend,” never by name. And she never touched a drop of it. We brew our own at Warrington Manor, but every year, good Mr. Foker sends two butts of his finest entire to Ipswich. His son is now a fine young gentleman, married to an earl’s daughter; the father remains a kind and worthy man. It was through the luck of meeting him that I received some of the most welcome guineas I ever knew.

It wasn’t so much the money, but the dignity and purpose granted by the office of Governor that I took upon myself, which made those years so precious. Mr. Foker’s brewery—since moved—once stood near Pedlar’s Acre in Lambeth. The surgeon who attended my wife during her confinement also cared for the brewer’s family. He was a Bavarian, originally named Voelker. I suppose Mr. Lance, the surgeon, introduced us. This kindly doctor often smoked Virginia tobacco in my garden, and grew fond of me and mine. He brought his patron to my house. When Mr. Foker discovered I could speak a little German and sing “Prinz Eugen the noble Ritter”—a song my grandfather brought back from the Marlborough wars—he formed a deep affection for me. His wife placed her chair and chariot at Mrs. Warrington’s disposal; his little daughter adored our baby (and truth be told, the Captain, though now as unprepossessing as any man with a queue, was beautiful as an infant). [The very image of the Squire at thirty, everyone says so. M.W. (Note in the MS.)] And since Master Foker was bullied at Westminster School for his father’s trade, the parents asked if I might take charge of him, offering a fair sum for his education.

Mr. Foker was a sharp businessman, and because he and his family genuinely cared for us, I shared my financial affairs freely with him. He praised my honesty and said my accounts only deepened his respect. He laughed at our tales of aid from noble kin—my aunt’s coverlid, Lady Castlewood’s mouldy jelly, Lady Warrington’s cold treatment. But he wept when I spoke of little Miles’s moidore. As for Sampson and Hagan, “I wow,” he declared, “dey shall have so much beer als ever dey can drink.” He sent his wife to call on Lady Maria, treating her with utmost deference whenever she visited. It was at Mr. Foker’s home that Lady Maria stayed when Hagan went to Dublin to finish his studies, and the brewer’s purse helped supply our friend’s needs.

When Mr. Foker fully understood my circumstances, he spoke of me with great admiration, calling my conduct a mark of rare virtue. I’ve already told how my mother saved for Harry, and how both brothers owed me. Yet when Harry spent, he did so believing the money was his own. My mother never grasped that she was wronging me—the funds were gone, and that was that. By the end of ’62, Harry sent a substantial remittance for his promotion, reminding me he still owed me, and urging me to draw on his agents if needed. He didn’t know how dire my need was, nor how my small capital had vanished.

To take his money would delay his advancement, so I refused, though I confess I was tempted. And knowing General Lambert’s modest means, I wouldn’t burden him. These acts of restraint, my dear brewer thought, were feats of noble character. So what does he do but write privately to my brother in America, praising me and my wife as the finest humans alive, and summon Madame de Bernstein—who never mentioned her visit to me, though I noticed her sudden kindness toward us, surprising in one I saw as selfish and worldly. Later, I asked her how she’d gained access to the Baroness. She laughed: “De Baroness!” she said. “I knew de Baron when he was a valet at Munich, and I was a brewer-apprentice.” I think our family should not pry too deeply into our uncle the Baron.

Thus, a period that ought to have been bleak became instead one of warmth and unexpected blessings. The bear I led was a gentle cub, dancing readily to my pipe. Better to lead him than loiter outside booksellers’ doors, waiting on the whims of managers! During our exile, we passed many pleasant evenings with these kind friends. We weren’t without intellectual joys either: Mrs. Foker and Mrs. Warrington sang beautifully together, and when I felt inclined, I read my play *Pocahontas* aloud—better than Hagan ever did, Mr. Foker insisted.

After the incident with Miles Warrington junior, I saw nothing more of him, and heard little from my paternal relations. Sir Miles was ever busy at court—though I suspect he would have thrived even at Nero’s. Once, when Mr. Foker told me he’d heard on ‘Change that they were making my uncle a “Beer,” I blinked in confusion. “A Beer?” I asked. “Can’t you understand de vort, ven I say it?” the irritable old man replied. “Vell, veil, a Lort!” Indeed, Sir Miles was the humble servant of whichever minister held power. I wonder he didn’t adopt a Scotch accent during the first favourite’s brief reign. I saw him and his wife returning from court when Mrs. Claypool was presented to the Queen on her marriage. I carried my son on my shoulder. My uncle and aunt stared stiffly from their gilt coach window. The footmen looked blank over their nosegays. Had I worn the Fairy’s cap and been invisible, my father’s brother could not have ignored me more completely.

We rarely accepted Lady Castlewood’s odd invitation to tea and supper when no other guests came. Old Van den Bosch, clever as he was at amassing wealth, was dull in conversation—except to his daughter, who discussed household matters, City dealings, stock trading, and farming with the same sharpness as her father. Lord Castlewood was seldom home, and his absence was neither mourned nor missed by his wife. The Countess confided all this to my wife simply: “Guess,” she said, “my lord and father don't pull well together nohow. Guess my lord is always wanting money, and father keeps the key of the box—and quite right, too. If he could handle all our money, my lord would soon make away with it, and then what's to become of our noble family? We pay everything, my dear—except play-debts, and them we won't have nohow. We pay cooks, horses, wine-merchants, tailors, and everybody—lucky for them too—reckon my lord wouldn't pay 'em! And we always see he has a guinea in his pocket, so he goes out like a real nobleman. What that man owes us—what he owed before we came—gracious goodness only knows! Me and father do our best to keep him respectable—but it's no easy job, my dear. Law! he'd melt the plate, only father keeps the key of the strong-room; and when we go to Castlewood, my father travels with me, and papa is armed too, as well as the people.”

“Gracious heavens!” my wife exclaimed. “You don’t mean to say you suspect your husband of—?”

“To what? Oh no, nothing, of course! And I’d trust our brother Will with untold money, wouldn’t I? As much as I’d trust the cat with the cream-pan! I tell you, my dear, it’s not all pleasure being a woman of rank and fashion: and if I bought a countess’s coronet, I paid a good price for it—that I did!”

And so had Lord Castlewood paid dearly to free his estate from debts, furnish his houses and stables, and clear his accounts. He was enslaved by his wife and her father. No wonder the old man’s company was unpleasant to the poor victim, who fled to clubs when he could afford it—or to any society but his own. Leading a bear, as I did, was hardly glorious; waiting in a bookseller’s anteroom until his honour finished dinner and granted audience was sometimes hard for a man of my pride. But would I have traded my poverty for Castlewood’s humiliation? Or preferred his wretched dependence to my own? At least I earned my wage, however small. And no one can say I flattered my patrons or bowed to them—I was sulky, overbearing, and frankly intolerable.

Yet there was one person with whom Fate had paired me, who bore our poverty with such grace and sweetness that even stingy Fortune softened. Like a fairy tale’s gentle creature, her constant cheerfulness melted the heart of hardship. No matter how poor she was, everyone who knew her saw a true lady. Tradespeople and neighbours treated her with respect equal to the richest among them. “I think, my dear,” said kind-hearted Mrs. Foker, when they rode in her carriage, “you look like the mistress of the vehicle, and I only your maid.” Our landladies adored her; shopkeepers rushed to fulfill her smallest requests as eagerly as if a duchess had ordered them. I often thought of the lady in *Comus*, moving through chaos with serene purity.

Often, the generous parents of my young bear lent us their carriage to visit friends. Once, we drove to the Protestant Hero and enjoyed syllabub in the garden; the hostess insisted on calling my wife “her ladyship” all afternoon. We visited Mr. Johnson and took tea with him—Mr. Goldsmith was there, too. The Doctor waited on my wife to her coach. But our most frequent visits were to Aunt Bernstein. I must admit, I was not jealous when my aunt began to show a remarkable fondness for Theo.

This affection grew so strong she wanted her nearly every day, or even to live with her entirely. She saw Theo’s husband and child as mere nuisances, and hated us amusingly for keeping her favorite away. Not that my wife lacked merit—she was worthy of anyone’s affection—but her frequent absences and the difficulty of reaching her stirred my aunt’s attachment into something almost passionate. She wrote notes like love letters, her servants hovered around our kitchen. If my wife didn’t come, she sent tearful pleas, scolding me fiercely when I visited her. Once, when the child fell ill—Fate has spared our Captain to be a great trial and test of patience—Madame Bernstein came three days in a row to Lambeth, insisting there was nothing wrong with the baby, nothing at all, and that we merely pretended illness to torment her.

The reigning Countess of Castlewood was just as easygoing with her old aunt as with anyone else. “What air you all about, scraping and bowing to that old woman, I can’t tell, noways!” she’d snap. “She a fine lady? Nonsense! She ain’t no more fine than any other lady: and I guess I’m as good as any of ‘em with their high heels and grand airs! She a beauty once? Take away her wig, rouge, and teeth—and what becomes of your beauty, I’d like to know? Guess you’d put it all in a bandbox, and there’d be nothing left but a shrivelled old woman!” And indeed, the little homilist spoke painfully true. Beauty fades for all. Here was old age without reverence, grey hairs hidden or painted, the world still clinging to her crutch. For eighty years she had lived, eaten of the forbidden and permitted tree. She had known beauty, pleasure, flattery—but what secret rages, disappointments, humiliations? What thorns beneath roses? What stinging bees in the fruit? “You are not a beauty, my dear,” she’d say to my wife, “and may thank your stars you’re not.” (If she contradicted herself, perhaps we all do.)

“Don’t tell me your husband likes your face, and you want no one else’s admiration!” she’d continue. “We all do. Every woman would rather be beautiful than anything else—rich, good, or gifted with fairies! Look at that picture—though I know it’s bad, and Kneller couldn’t paint my eyes, hair, or complexion. What a shape I had then—and look at me now, this wrinkled neck! Why must our beauty last so short a time? I remember Mademoiselle de l’Enclos at my age, fresh as ever. We can’t hide our years—they’re written in Mr. Collins’s books. I was born in King James’s last year. I’m not old yet—I’m seventy-six. But what a wreck, my dear! Isn’t it cruel our time is so brief?”

Then my wife would gently point out that all our time here below is short.

“Ha!” the Baroness would cry. “Did not Adam live near a thousand years, and Eve remain beautiful? I used to puzzle Mr. Tusher with that—poor creature! What have we done since that our lives are so shortened?”

“Has your life been so happy you’d wish to prolong it?” the listener would ask. “Have you, who love wit, never read Dean Swift’s description of the deathless people in *Gulliver*? My papa and husband say it’s one of the finest sermons ever written. Better not to live at all than to live without love. And I’m sure,” my wife would say, dabbing her eyes, “if anything happened to my dearest George, I’d wish to go to Heaven that moment.”

“Who loves me in Heaven? I am quite alone, child—that is why I’d rather stay here,” the Baroness whispered, frightened and pitiful. “You are kind to me, God bless your sweet face! Though I scold and have a frightful temper, my servants will do anything to comfort me, rise at any hour, and never say a cross word. I still like my cards. Life would be a blank without them. Almost everything is gone except that. I can’t eat now since I lost those last two teeth. Everything slips away in old age. But I still have my cards—thank Heaven, I still have my cards!” Then she’d begin to doze, waking sharply if my wife stirred, imagining Theo was leaving. “Don’t go away—I can’t bear to be alone. I don’t want you to talk. Just let me see your face, my dear! It’s much pleasanter than that horrid old Brett’s, who’s scowled in my bedroom for years.”

“Well, Baroness! Still at your cribbage?” (Imagine a noble Countess interrupting a card game between Theo and Aunt Bernstein.) “Me and my Lord Esmond have come to see you! Go shake hands with grandaunt, Esmond! Tell her ladyship your lordship’s a good boy!”

“My lordship’s a good boy,” the child replied. (Madam Theo used to act these scenes for me with great liveliness.)

“And if he is, I guess he don’t take after his father,” shrieked Lady Castlewood, pretending Aunt Bernstein was deaf and shouting at her.

“Your ladyship chose my nephew for better or worse,” Aunt Bernstein replied, flustered in the presence of the young Countess.

“But he is a precious deal worse than I ever thought! I’m speaking of your Pa, Ezzy. If it wasn’t for your mother, my son, Lord knows what would become of you! We’re going to see his little Royal Highness. Sorry to see you not looking quite so well today. We can’t always stay young and law! How we do change as we grow old! Go up and kiss that lady. She has a little boy, too. Why, bless us! Have you got the child downstairs?” Indeed, Master Miles was below—sometimes accompanying his mother on visits to Aunt Bernstein. Our aunt liked her so much she reluctantly endured the child. “So you’ve got the child here? Oh, you slyboots!” the Countess snapped. “Guess you come after the old lady’s money! Law bless you! Don’t look so frightened. She can’t hear a word I say. Come, Ezzy. Good-bye, aunt!” And the Countess swept out.

Did Aunt Bernstein hear her? Where was the wit that once made her famous? Was that fire extinguished along with the sparkle in her eyes? With others, she remained sharp and quick-witted. When the Dowager of Castlewood and Lady Fanny visited (these ladies treated my wife with perfect indifference and charming manners), the Baroness was stately, poised, even commanding. She would mischievously caress Mrs. Warrington before them, later boasting of her good breeding, saying my nephew had made a foolish match, but I certainly had chosen a charming wife. “In a word, I praise you so to them, my dear,” she’d say, “that I think they’d like to tear your eyes out.” But before the little American, she trembled and fumbled. She was so afraid she dared not refuse her door, and when the Countess turned her back, she didn’t even rebuke her. Despite their dislike, they never tore out Theo’s eyes.

Once, they drove to our cottage at Lambeth, where my wife sat at the open window, holding our child. A towering footman marched through our garden and delivered their visiting cards. Their hatred hurt us no more than their visit pleased us. The next time we borrowed the Brewer’s carriage, Mrs. Warrington drove to Kensington, and Gumbo handed back our cards to the giant who had delivered theirs.

The Baroness had a coach, but rarely offered it to us. She’d let Theo, her maid, and baby start from Clarges Street in the rain, with a weak excuse that she feared asking her coachman to take the horses out. But twice, on her return, my wife was frightened by rough men on the other side of Westminster Bridge. I finally told my aunt I’d forbid Mrs. Warrington from visiting unless she could be brought home safely. Reluctantly, grumbling Jehu drove through the darkness. He complained about my shillings—he didn’t know how few I had. Our poverty wore a decent face. My relatives never offered help, nor did I complain before them. I don’t know how Sampson came by a windfall of guineas, but once he brought me six. They were more welcome than any money I’d ever received. He’d been checking on Mr. Miles’s crib while the child slept; when the parson left, I found the coins in the baby’s tiny hand. Yes, Love is best of all. I hold many such gifts close in my heart—precious springs rising in deserts, kind lights brightening our darkest hours.

This devoted chaplain willingly gave his company to Madame de Bernstein, whether for cards or theology. Having known her for years, Sampson saw her decline and warned us of her growing frailty. Speaking of her failing health and likely end, he spoke tenderly of preparing for another world, of the emptiness of this life, and the hope of happiness for repentant sinners.

“I have been a sinner,” the chaplain said, bowing his head. “God knows, and I pray Him to forgive me. I fear, sir, your aunt, the Lady Baroness, is not in a state fit for the change coming. I am but a poor weak wretch, and no prisoner in Newgate could confess more humbly. Recently, I’ve tried to speak with her on this matter, but she received me harshly. ‘Parson,’ she said, ‘if you come for cards, it’s mighty well, but I’ll thank you to spare me your sermons.’ What can I do, sir? I’ve called several times, and Mr. Case told me his lady was unable to see me.” In truth, Madame Bernstein told my wife—whom she never refused—that the chaplain’s tone was unbearable, and as for his theology, “Haven’t I been a Bishop’s wife?” she said, “Do I need this creature to teach me?”
