The gentle cadence of the evening faded, and the young patient sank into a slumber of sweet oblivion. When consciousness returned, the heavy lids lifted to reveal a shaft of summer sunlight pouring across the floorboards. Beside his bed, the kindly host and hostess smiled, their faces warm with genuine concern. Hunger, sharp and immediate, seized him; yet the doctor, observing his state, gave permission for a bowl of chicken and rice. The doctor's wife mentioned, with a touch of pride, that the meal had been prepared by the hands of one of her own daughters.

A sudden, vivid image pierced the haze of his recovery. Two figures, youthful and vibrant, with flushed cheeks and dark, flowing hair, had hovered around his couch, offering comfort, only to vanish the moment he opened his eyes. Then, the reality of the present intruded—a vision of a woman, undeniably lovely yet considerably older, accompanied by a shadow of a scandal that brought a flush of horror and deep remorse to the young man's brow. He writhed slightly in his sheets, the weight of time pressing heavily upon him. An abyss yawned between his current self and the innocent boy he had been. How long, indeed, since he had believed those pearls to be real, or those golden tresses natural? That innocence belonged to a distant childhood; now, he was a man, aged and weary, plagued by a lingering fever and the bitter taste of deception.

"What troubles you, my dear?" cooed Mrs. Lambert, sensing his start.

"Nothing, madam," Harry murmured, trying to steady his voice. "Only a twinge in my shoulder. I speak to my hosts? You have been most kind."

"We are old friends, Mr. Warrington," replied the Colonel, his voice thick with history. "My wife and your mother were schoolmates at Kensington. To us, you were never a stranger, however distant your circumstances."

"And they?" Harry asked, glancing at the empty chairs. "Are Aunt and Cousin here?"

"They rested at Tunbridge Wells last night," the Colonel explained gently. "A messenger came from Reigate seeking news."

"I remember," Harry said, touching his bandaged arm. "Thank you both."

"Do not trouble yourself," said the Colonel, rising. "I have healed you sufficiently. From this moment, Mrs. Lambert and the cook shall attend to your needs."

"Nay!" interjected Mrs. Lambert quickly. "Theo prepared the chicken and rice, Mr. Lambert. Will Mr. Warrington rise after breakfast? We shall send your valet."

"If loyalty is measured by howling, your man must be a creature of profound devotion," the Colonel laughed.

"He did allow your luggage to depart in your aunt's carriage," Mrs. Lambert added softly. "You will wear my husband's linen until your own returns. I trust it is not inferior to yours."

"Pish!" cried the Colonel, arching an eyebrow. "These shirts serve any Christian."

"They are the work of Theo and Hester," Mrs. Lambert insisted, glancing at her husband with a knowing smile. "And Theo has stitched the sleeve to accommodate your injured shoulder."

Turning his gaze to a porcelain vase brimming with crimson blooms on the dressing table, Harry exclaimed, "What beautiful roses!"

"My daughter Theo cut them this morning," the Colonel said, chuckling. "She is a gardener as well as a nurse."

Harry nodded, distracted by the scent. "Last night, I thought I heard someone singing the Evening Hymn so sweetly..."

"Theo again!" the Colonel laughed. "Our servants claim your former servant sang it with the power of an organ."

"Our people sing it at home, sir," Harry said, his voice softening. "My grandfather loved it. His wife's father was a close friend of Bishop Ken, the author. And... my brother loved it, too." The words slipped out, heavy with a grief he had tried to suppress.

For a fleeting moment, Mrs. Lambert felt compelled to press a kiss to his forehead. The boy's suffering, followed by the warmth of their care, had thawed the icy shell Harry had built around his heart. For nearly six weeks, he had breathed an air tainted by vanity and self-interest, but here, amidst the quiet kindness of the Lamberts, he drew breath that smelled of purity. Fate, or perhaps a shift in his own nature, had placed him in the circle of wise and virtuous souls, whose lamps burned bright and whose hearts beat with modesty.

Leaving Harry to enjoy Miss Theo's nourishing fare, the Colonel settled beside the bed. Gratitude mingled with physical satisfaction, leaving Harry in a buoyant frame of mind. Usually taciturn and guarded, the Virginian now found his tongue loosened. He was no scholar of literature, his knowledge limited to the practical matters of life—dogs, horses, and the mechanics of the world. Yet, on these subjects, he was a keen critic, eager to converse with anyone who shared his interests.

Soon, the Colonel, a man of sharp wit and worldly experience, gauged his guest. Harry learned of the Colonel's bond with his mother and the shared military history of their families dating back to Queen Anne's reign. For the first time, Harry felt the comforting certainty of being in a friend's country, surrounded by honest, cordial men.

"My sons are away," the Colonel noted. "Thus, you shall find little company save for my wife and her daughters. Mrs. Lambert has spoken of Theo, our eldest—the soup-maker, the tailor, the singer who lulled you to rest. She is a sensible housekeeper, though perhaps not the miracle her mother paints her to be."

"It is very kind of Miss Lambert," Harry said gratefully.

"She does no more than duty demands," the Colonel replied, a smile playing on his lips. "I jest with her praises, yet I admit, God has gifted us with dutiful children, and I need no disguise to express my thanks. Do you have a sister, Mr. Warrington?"

"No, sir. I am alone."

"Ay, I apologize for my thoughtlessness. Your man spoke of last year's affair. I served with General Braddock in Scotland. I trust he recovered before passing." The Colonel leaned forward. "A wild soul, yes, but with a core of truth beneath the rough exterior. Your Negro servant speaks freely of you. He claims you rescued him from the Indians. Is that true?"

"Rescued me?" Harry sat up, surprise coloring his tone.

"On that very expedition, you saved him from a party of redskins," the Colonel confirmed. "Molly and I never expected to host a man of such apparent wealth. He swears half of Virginia is yours. Even if North America itself belonged to you, we would offer only our best."

"These negro boys," Harry corrected sharply, "lie with the fluency of the Father of Lies. They imagine it honors us to portray us as fabulously rich. My mother holds a vast estate in England, and we live as comfortably as most neighbors. All the splendor exists only in Gumbo's delusion. He never rescued me from an Indian; indeed, he would have fled at the sight of one, just as my brother's boy did on that fatal day."

"The bravest man runs at unlucky times," the Colonel observed calmly. "I saw the finest regiments rout at Preston before a ragged mob of Highlanders."

"That was because the Highlanders fought for a righteous cause, sir," Harry countered.

"Do you think the French and their Indian allies possessed a good cause in last year's war?" the Colonel asked.

"The scoundrels!" Harry clenched his fist. "I would trade scalps for the honor of cutting down every murderer among them! They robbed and invaded British lands. But the Highlanders fought for their King."

"So did we," the Colonel agreed, his laughter ringing clear. "And we won the battle."

"Oh!" Harry cried, his eyes lighting up with fervor. "If His Royal Highness the Prince had not retreated at Derby, your King and mine would now sit upon the throne as King James the Third!"

"Who instilled such Tory sentiments in you, Mr. Warrington?" the Colonel asked.

"Nay, sir, the Esmonds have always been loyal!" Harry answered earnestly. "Had we lived at home, twenty years prior, my brother and I would have wagered our heads on the King's side."

"Fortunate for you, Mr. Warrington," the Colonel said gravely. "Your royalist opinions remain safely upon your shoulders rather than impaled on a pole at Temple Bar. I have seen such sights, and they hold no charm."

"I shall doff my hat and salute them whenever I pass the gate," Harry declared, "if the King and the Court stand there!"

"I suspect your relations, Lord Castlewood and the Baroness of Bernstein, are less steadfast supporters of the Crown abroad," the Colonel mused. "Though the Baroness may have harbored old partialities, she has repented. She has rallied to our side, secured positions for her nephews in the Household, and seeks suitable matches for her nieces. If you harbor Tory views, Mr. Warrington, take an old soldier's counsel: keep them to yourself."

"Why, sir, you cannot betray me!" Harry protested.

"Not I, but others might. You spoke thus freely at Castlewood, did you not?"

"I was safe amongst my kin, surely!"
