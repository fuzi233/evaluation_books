The great northern roads snaking out of London drag a half-dead ghost of suburban street deep into the countryside: gaps yawn between clumps of shops, fenced paddocks, famous coaching inns, market gardens, lone private villas, and the unbroken line of the old high street never quite snaps. Walk any one of these roads, and you’ll spot a long, low house set parallel to the tarmac, painted soft white and pale sage green, strung with verandas and sun-blinds, topped with those droll little cupolas that look like wooden umbrellas, the kind you only see in faded Victorian country villas. It is the sort of old-fashioned home that reeks of that good, wealthy Clapham comfort of a bygone era—yet it feels built for swelter, not English drizzle. Stare at its sun-bleached paint and drawn blinds, and you half-expect to spot pugarees hanging by the door, palm fronds peeking over the garden wall. No one can trace where that odd foreign tingle comes from, but rumor says the first owner was a returning Anglo-Indian civil servant.

Any passerby would feel a nameless tug of fascination for the house, the sense that some story lurks behind its blinds. They would be right, as the strange events that unfolded there over Whitsuntide in 18-- proved.

If you’d wandered past the house the Thursday before Whit-Sunday at half-past four that afternoon, you would have seen the front door swing open, and Father Brown, of the tiny St. Mungo’s parish, step out smoking a fat clay pipe beside his tall French friend Flambeau, who puffed at a slim cigarette. They were not the only odd sight the open front door revealed. The whole house was built in the shape of a T: a long cross-beam facing the road, two stories high, holding all the main living spaces, with the front door set dead center, and a short, stubby tail jutting straight out the back opposite the door. That single-story tail held only two rooms, one opening straight into the other: the first was the cluttered study where celebrated poet Leonard Quinton wrote his wild, vivid Oriental verses and romances, and the second was a full glass conservatory stuffed with tropical blossoms of almost unnatural, overwhelming beauty, glowing under the strong afternoon sun. So anyone who glanced through the open front door looked straight down a perfect perspective of rich, overstuffed apartments to a sight that could make them stop dead in the street, gape and gasp: purple petals, golden sunlit foliage, crimson star-shaped blooms, as vivid as a fairy play transformation scene, bright and transparent all at once.

Leonard Quinton had staged that sight with his own careful hand, and it was arguable that it expressed his personality far better than any of his published work. He was a man who drank and bathed in colour, chasing that sensory high so far he all but ignored form—even good, decent form. That obsession had turned his whole genius to Eastern art and imagery: those bewildering carpets and blinding embroideries where every bright hue tumbles into a fortunate chaos, with no clear symbol, no moral lesson, no order at all. He wrote epics and love stories bursting with riotous, even cruel colour: tropical skies that burned gold or blood-red copper, Eastern heroes riding purple or peacock-green painted elephants wearing twelve stacked mitres, gigantic jewels a hundred negroes could not lift that blazed with ancient, unnameable hues. In plainer terms, he wrote of Eastern heavens far harsher than most Western hells, of monarchs most people would call maniacs, and of jewels no Bond Street jeweller would believe genuine, even if a hundred staggering porters hauled them through his shop door. Quinton was a genius, if a morbid one—and his morbidity showed far more in his life than his work. He was weak, waspish, and his health had been wrecked by years of experimenting with opium he’d brought back from his travels East. His wife, a handsome, overworked, sharp-edged good woman, hated the opium, but hated far more the live Indian hermit in white and yellow robes her husband insisted on housing for months on end, as his personal Virgil to guide him through the imagined heavens and hells of his verse.

Father Brown and Flambeau stepped out of that overstuffed, opium-thick household with visible relief. Flambeau had known Quinton in their wild student days in Paris, and reconnected with him for a long weekend, but even with his new, more respectable life, he found the poet insufferable. Choking yourself on laudanum to scribble erotic verses on vellum was not his idea of how any gentleman ought to go to the devil. As they paused on the doorstep to head out for a walk around the garden, the front gate was flung open violently, and a young man with a billycock hat perched on the very back of his head stumbled up the steps, breathless with urgency. He looked dissipated, his bright red necktie askew as if he’d slept in it, fidgeting constantly and lashing at the air with a little jointed cane.

“Look here,” he gasped, “I need to see old Quinton. I have to. Has he already gone out?”

“Mr. Quinton is inside, as far as I know,” Father Brown said, tamping down the ash in his pipe. “But I’m not sure you can see him. The doctor’s with him right now.”

The young man, clearly not entirely sober, stumbled into the hall just as Dr. Harris stepped out of Quinton’s study, shutting the door behind him and pulling on his gloves.

“See Mr. Quinton?” the doctor said coolly. “No, I’m afraid that’s impossible. No one can see him right now. I’ve just given him his sleeping draught.”

“Come on, old chap,” the youth in the red tie said, trying to grab the doctor’s lapels in a clumsy show of camaraderie. “Look, I’m dead broke, I tell you—”

“It’s no use, Mr. Atkinson,” Harris said, pushing him firmly back. “When you can reverse the effects of a sedative, I’ll reverse my decision.” He settled his hat on his head and stepped out into the sunlight with Flambeau and Father Brown. He was a bull-necked, good-natured little man with a thin moustache, entirely unremarkable at a glance, but brimming with quiet competence.

Atkinson, who seemed to have no social tact beyond the blunt approach of grabbing people’s coats, stood on the doorstep dazed as if he’d been physically thrown out, watching the other three wander off into the garden.

“That was a perfectly blatant lie I just told,” the doctor laughed. “Fact is, poor Quinton won’t need his sleeping draught for another half hour. But I’m not going to let that little pest pester him. He’s a dirty little scamp, even if he is Mrs. Quinton’s brother, and she’s one of the finest women alive.”

“She is a very good woman,” Father Brown agreed quietly.

“So I’m hanging around the garden until he’s cleared off,” Harris went on. “Then I’ll go in and give Quinton his medicine. Atkinson can’t get in anyway, I locked the study door.”

“In that case, Dr. Harris,” Flambeau said, “we may as well walk around the back to the far end of the conservatory. There’s no entrance there, but the glasswork is worth seeing even from the outside.”

“Good, and I can sneak a peek at my patient,” the doctor laughed. “He likes to lie on an ottoman right at the far end of the conservatory, surrounded by those blood-red poinsettias. Gives me the creeps. Wait—what are you doing?”

Father Brown had stopped, and knelt to fish something out of the long grass, half hidden under the weeds: a queer, crooked Oriental knife, inlaid exquisitely with coloured stones and twisted metal.

“What is this?” Father Brown said, turning it over in his hand with clear distaste.

“Quinton’s, I expect,” Harris said carelessly. “He’s got all sorts of Chinese knickknacks lying around the place. Or it might belong to that mild Hindoo of his, the one he keeps strung along as a sort of pet.”

“What Hindoo?” Father Brown asked, still staring fixed at the dagger.

“Oh, some Indian conjuror,” the doctor said lightly. “A total fraud, obviously.”

“You don’t believe in magic?” Father Brown asked, not looking up.

“Good heavens, magic!” Harris scoffed.

“It’s very beautiful,” the priest said in a low, dreaming voice. “The colours are so lovely. But it’s the wrong shape.”

“Wrong shape for what?” Flambeau said, frowning at the blade.

“For anything. It’s the wrong shape in the abstract. Haven’t you ever felt that about Eastern art? The colours are intoxicatingly lovely—but the shapes are mean, and bad. Deliberately mean and bad. I’ve seen wicked things hidden in the lines of a Turkey carpet.”

“Mon Dieu!” Flambeau exclaimed, half laughing.

“They’re letters and symbols in a language I don’t know—but I know they spell out evil words,” Father Brown went on, his voice dropping lower. “The lines twist wrong on purpose—like serpents doubling back to escape capture.”

“What on earth are you rambling about?” the doctor said, with a loud, dismissive laugh.

Flambeau spoke quietly, to calm the irritated man. “The Father falls into this sort of mystic trance sometimes,” he said. “But I warn you, I’ve never seen him do it unless there’s real evil standing very close.”

“Oh, nonsense,” the scientist huffed.

“Look at it,” Father Brown said, holding the crooked dagger out at arm’s length as if it were a glistening, hissing snake. “Don’t you see it’s the wrong shape? It doesn’t point clean like a spear. It doesn’t sweep like a scythe. It doesn’t look like a proper weapon at all. It looks like an instrument of torture.”

“Well, since you clearly hate the sight of it,” Harris said, cheerful as ever, “we might as well take it back to its owner. Haven’t we reached the end of this infernal conservatory yet? This whole house is the wrong shape, if that’s what you’re on about.”

“You don’t understand,” Father Brown said, shaking his head. “The shape of this house is quaint—even silly. But there’s nothing wrong with it.”

As they spoke, they rounded the curved far end of the glass conservatory, a smooth unbroken stretch of panes with no doors or windows set into it. The glass was perfectly clear, the sun still bright though dipping low toward the horizon, so they could see not only the overgrown, flamboyant blossoms inside, but the frail figure of the poet in his brown velvet coat, lying languid on his sofa, half-asleep over a book. He was pale, slight, with loose chestnut hair and a thin fringe of beard that only made him look softer, less substantial. But none of the three men stared at Quinton just then. Their eyes were fixed on something else entirely.

Standing directly in their path, outside the rounded glass end of the conservatory, was a tall man draped in faultless white robes that pooled around his bare feet. His shaved brown skull, face, and neck glowed in the sunset like polished bronze. He was staring straight through the glass at the sleeping poet, motionless as a mountain.

“Who is that?” Father Brown gasped, stepping back with a sharp, hissing intake of breath.

“It’s that Hindoo humbug,” Harris growled. “I can’t imagine what the devil he’s doing skulking here.”

“Looks like hypnosis,” Flambeau muttered, biting at the edge of his thick black moustache.

“Why do you non-medical people always spout rubbish about hypnosis?” the doctor scoffed. “Looks far more like burglary to me.”

“Well, we’re going to speak to him, at any rate,” Flambeau said, ever ready for action. One long stride carried him to the place where the Indian stood. He bowed from his great height, which towered even over the Oriental’s, and spoke with calm, unflappable impudence.

“Good evening, sir. Can we help you find something?”

Very slowly, like a great tall ship turning to drift into a harbour, the big yellow face turned, and looked back over its white-robed shoulder. The men were startled to see that its heavy yellow eyelids were sealed shut, as if in deep sleep. “Thank you,” the face said, in perfect, unaccented English. “I want nothing.” Then he half-opened his lids, showing only a thin slit of opalescent eyeball, and repeated, “I want nothing.” Then he snapped his eyes wide open, stared at them with a startling, blazing intensity, said a third time, “I want nothing,” and rustled away into the rapidly darkening garden.

“The Christian is more modest,” Father Brown muttered. “He wants something.”

“What on earth was he doing there?” Flambeau asked, knitting his black brows, lowering his voice.

“I’d like to talk to you about that later,” Father Brown said.

The sunlight was still bright, but it had turned to the deep red light of evening, and the bulk of the garden trees and bushes were growing black against it. They circled back around the far end of the conservatory, walking in silence along the other side to head back to the front door. As they moved, they startled something in the shadowed nook between the study and the main house, like scaring a roosting bird, and they saw the white-robed fakir slip out of the dark and glide around the side of the house toward the front door. To their surprise, he was not alone. Mrs. Quinton stepped out of the twilight, her heavy gold hair and square pale face set in a stern line, but perfectly courteous.

“Good evening, Dr. Harris,” she said simply.

“Good evening, Mrs. Quinton,” the little doctor said heartily. “I’m just on my way in to give your husband his sleeping draught.”

“Yes,” she said in a clear, even voice. “I think it is quite time.” She smiled, and swept past them into the house.

“That woman is overworked to breaking point,” Father Brown said. “That’s the sort of woman who does her duty for twenty years straight, then one day does something terrible.”

The doctor looked at him with new, curious interest. “Did you ever study medicine?” he asked.

“Priests have to know a thing or two about the mind, as well as the body,” Father Brown answered. “And we learn a fair bit about the body, too, along the way.”

“Well,” Harris said, “I’m going in to give Quinton his medicine now.”

They rounded the corner of the front facade, approaching the front doorway, when they spotted the white-robed man for the third time. He walked straight toward the front door so directly it was almost impossible to believe he had not just stepped out of the study right opposite it—even though all three of them knew perfectly well the study door was locked. Father Brown and Flambeau kept that weird, impossible contradiction to themselves; Dr. Harris was not the sort of man to waste time puzzling over the inexplicable. He let the omnipresent Asiatic walk past and exit the house, then stepped briskly into the hall. Atkinson was still hanging about, humming to himself, poking at the ornaments on the hall table with his jointed cane. A look of disgusted resolve flashed across the doctor’s face, and he whispered quickly to Flambeau: “I have to lock the door again after me, or that rat will sneak in. I’ll be back in two minutes, tops.”

He unlocked the study door, slipped inside, and locked it again behind him, just barely blocking a bumbling, full-tilt charge from Atkinson. The young man threw himself down on a hall chair, impatient. Flambeau stared at a Persian illuminated manuscript hung on the wall. Father Brown, who seemed half lost in a daze, stared blankly at the study door. Four minutes later, the door creaked open again. Atkinson was fast this time: he sprang forward, held the door ajar for an instant, and called out, “Oi, Quinton, I need—”

From the far end of the study, Quinton’s clear voice rang out, half yawn, half weary laugh.

“Oh, I know what you want. Take it, and leave me in peace. I’m writing a song about peacocks.”

Before the door could shut, a half-sovereign coin went flying through the gap, and Atkinson stumbled forward and caught it with surprising dexterity.

“That’s settled then,” the doctor said, locking the door viciously behind him, and led the way back out into the garden. “Poor Leonard can get a little peace now. He’s locked in there all by himself for an hour or two.”

“Yes,” the priest answered. “He sounded perfectly content when we heard him speak.” He scanned the garden gravely: Atkinson was standing a little way off, jingling the half-sovereign in his pocket, and beyond him, in the purple twilight, the Indian sat bolt upright on a grass bank, his face turned toward the setting sun. Then Father Brown said abruptly, “Where is Mrs. Quinton?”

“She went up to her bedroom,” the doctor said. “That’s her shadow on the blind, up there.”

Father Brown looked up, frowning, to examine the dark outline visible behind the gas-lit bedroom window.

“Yes,” he said. “That is her shadow.” He walked a couple of steps, and dropped onto a wooden garden seat.

Flambeau sat down beside him, but the doctor was one of those high-energy people who never stay still for long. He wandered off smoking, into the twilight, leaving the two friends alone.

“Mon père,” Flambeau said in French, “what is troubling you?”

Father Brown was silent and motionless for half a minute, then he said: “Superstition is irreligious, but there is something very wrong hanging in the air of this place. I think it’s that Indian—at least, partly.”

He fell quiet again, watching the distant outline of the fakir, who sat rigid as if in prayer. At first glance he seemed perfectly still, but as Father Brown watched, he saw the man sway ever so slightly in a steady, rhythmic movement, exactly as the dark treetops above him swayed gently in the wind that was creeping down the dim garden paths, shuffling the fallen leaves across the grass.
