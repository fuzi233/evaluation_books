Ethne stood rigid by the drawing-room window of the Hill Street house, her gaze fixed on the pavement below. Beside her, Mrs. Adair sat before her tea-tray, both women holding their breath, straining ears against the afternoon air for a specific sound. The sash was thrown wide, desperate to catch the first tremor of wheels or the clatter of hooves. It was half-past five. Summer had returned with its full, blinding glory; London glittered like a jewel box opened to the sun, filled with green trees and the promise of leisure. Across the street, windows overflowed with blooms, and carriages glided smoothly toward the Park. Suddenly, a sharp *jingle* cut through the hum of the city, swelling into a clear ring before fading into distance.

Mrs. Adair lifted her head sharply. "That's a cab," she whispered.

"Yes," Ethne murmured, leaning forward, peering down. "But it isn't stopping." As the metallic sound dissolved, Mrs. Adair glanced at the clock. "Colonel Durrance is late," she said, turning a curious, searching look toward Ethne. To Mrs. Adair, Ethne's earlier "yes" had sounded heavy with suspense rather than eagerness. Her posture at the window suggested apprehension, and though Mrs. Adair wasn't certain, she sensed a flicker of relief when the carriage passed without halting.

"I wonder why you didn't go to the station to meet him?" Mrs. Adair asked slowly.

The reply came instantly. "He might have thought I'd come only because I pitied him, seeing him as helpless. I won't have him think that. He has his servant with him."

Ethne stared out the window again, making abortive movements to speak, then deciding silence was safer. Finally, she resolved. "Do you remember the telegram I showed you?"

"The one from Lieutenant Calder? Saying Colonel Durrance had gone blind?"

"Yes. Promise me you'll never mention it. I don't want him to know I ever received it."

Mrs. Adair was baffled, and she detested being baffled. She had seen the telegram, but she hadn't known Ethne had written to Durrance immediately upon receiving it, pledging her love. When Ethne had shown the message, she had simply declared, "I am engaged to him." Mrs. Adair had accepted this as fact, believing the engagement was established, and had continued to live in that comfortable illusion.

"You will promise?" Ethne insisted.

"Certainly, my dear, if you wish," Mrs. Adair replied, rolling her shoulders in an ungracious gesture. "Though I suppose there's a reason. I don't understand why you demand this promise."

"Because two lives must not be ruined because of me."

There was truth in Mrs. Adair's suspicion that Ethne feared the blind man she loved. It was true she was frightened. Exactly twelve months had passed since, in this very room, on such a sunny afternoon, Ethne had begged Durrance to forget her. Every letter since had proven, regardless of his efforts, that he had not forgotten. Even the final note, scribbled in childish handwriting from Wadi Halfa three weeks prior—where the letters ran together in large, unsteady strokes as he detailed his calamity and renounced his suit—proved it more surely than anything before. That letter confirmed he remembered. As she waited, Ethne realized clearly that the burden of forgetting now fell entirely on her. If she couldn't erase the past, she must ensure that no careless word revealed she still cherished it.

"No," she said softly. "Two lives shall not be spoiled because of me." She turned to her friend. "Are you quite sure, Ethne," Mrs. Adair interjected, "that this path of yours—the path of marriage—won't spoil those lives more surely? Don't you fear you'll eventually feel Colonel Durrance, despite your will, becoming a hindrance, a drag? Might he feel the same? I wonder... I truly wonder."

"No," Ethne said, her tone decisive. "I shall not feel it, and neither must he."

In Mrs. Adair's mind, the "two lives" referred to Durrance and Harry Feversham. She was mistaken; the real danger lay between Durrance and Ethne herself. Yet, Ethne did not correct her. She was secretly glad her friend was wrong, allowing her to remain in that delusion. Ethne returned to the window, mapping out her future with military precision, anticipating every move so she would never be caught off guard. The task was daunting, and fear began to tighten in her chest as she waited. But the goal justified the struggle.

Durrance had lost everything that made life worthwhile the moment he went blind—all of it, save one thing.

"What should I do if I were crippled?" he had asked Harry Feversham that morning a year ago, as they rode together for the last time. "A clever man might endure it. But what if I had to sit in a chair for the rest of my days?" Ethne hadn't heard those words, but she understood the man perfectly without them. By birth, he was heir to vast estates and adventures; he had lost his inheritance. The long journeys, the exotic faces of foreign lands, the crackle of campfires against silent darkness, the sleep beneath bright stars, the desert winds, and the work of governance—he had lost them all. Only one thing remained: himself, and only as long as he believed she desired him.

While she wrestled with her resolve, the cab arrived unnoticed at the door. It was only when the servant rang the bell that Ethne's attention snapped back to the street.

"He's here!" she breathed, startled.

True, Durrance was not naturally inquisitive; he accepted people as they were, refusing to dissect them under a microscope. It would have been easy to soothe his doubts if they ever arose. But *now*, she reasoned, it would be easier than ever. There was no cause for alarm. Yet, despite her logic, she steel-edged herself for the confrontation rather than rushing to welcome him.

Mrs. Adair slipped out, leaving Ethne alone as Durrance entered. Ethne did not move immediately, bracing herself for the shock of his changed state. Instead, she was surprised by the absence of the changes she expected. His face was weathered, his hair streaked with grey, but there was no aura of helplessness or uncertainty—the very thing she dreaded most for his sake. He walked into the room with the confidence of sight; his memory seemed to chart the placement of every piece of furniture perfectly. At most, he reached out a hand once or twice where he anticipated a chair.

Ethne retreated silently to the window, unsure of how to greet him. Then, he smiled and approached her directly.

"Ethne," he said.

"It isn't true, then," she exclaimed, the words forced out by the ease of his movement. "You've recovered."

"It is quite true, and I haven't recovered," he answered calmly. "But you shifted at the window, so I knew you were there."

"How did you know? I made no noise."

"No, but the window was open. The street noise suddenly intensified, so I knew someone in front of the glass had stepped aside. I guessed it was you."

Their exchange lacked the formalities of a reunion after an engagement, yet it successfully bypassed awkwardness. Ethne avoided any perfunctory expressions of regret, sensing there was none needed, and Durrance shared her sentiment. After shaking hands, Ethne moved back into the room. "I'll get you some tea; then we can talk."

"Yes, we must talk, mustn't we?" Durrance replied seriously. He soon shed this gravity, chatting cheerfully about his journey home. He even found humor in his initial days of blindness, and Ethne's anxieties began to dissolve. They nearly vanished when his demeanor shifted abruptly.

"I wrote to you from Wadi Halfa," he said. "I don't know if you could read the letter."

"Quite well," Ethne lied smoothly.

"I had a friend of mine, Calder of the Sappers, hold the paper and tell me when I was writing on it versus the blotting-pad," he continued, laughing lightly. He tossed the name out quickly, setting a trap. The sudden stillness of his face signaled he was listening intensely for a slip—a gasp or a check—that would reveal her knowledge of Calder. Was he suspicious? Did he know about the telegram? Did he suspect her letter was born of pity?

She searched his face, finding nothing but alert intensity. A year ago, his eyes would have given her the answer instantly.

"I could read the letter without difficulty," she answered gently. "It was the letter you would have written. But I had already written to you, and of course, your bad news could make no difference. I take back no word of what I wrote."

Durrance sat with his hands on his knees, leaning forward slightly. Ethne was lost again. She couldn't gauge his acceptance or skepticism from his manner, realizing anew that a year ago, with his sight, she would have known exactly what he thought.

"Yes, I know you," he said finally. "You wouldn't take anything back. But there is my point of view."

Ethne looked at him, apprehensive. "Yes?" she replied, striving for nonchalance.

"Will you tell me it?"
