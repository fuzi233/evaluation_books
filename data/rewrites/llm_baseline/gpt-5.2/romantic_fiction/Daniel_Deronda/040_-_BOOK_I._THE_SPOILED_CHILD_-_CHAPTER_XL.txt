Deronda came out of the narrow Chelsea house with heat under his skin, as if his blood had been turned with a spoon. The air outside—raw with river damp—took him by the face; yet even that coolness only made him want a harder answer from his body, something honest and muscular to beat the vapor of ill-temper out of him. He was bound toward the City, but the sight of the stairs and the waiting boats decided him. A cab would be a box of jangling idleness. He called for a wherry, dropped in, and took an oar, glad of the first pull that set his shoulders to work and made the world answer him with resistance.

His errand was simple in words and tangled in feeling: Ram’s little book-shop, where he had missed Mordecai yesterday at the midday watch, and where the man was said to return between five and six. Deronda wanted more than directions about the ring; he wanted, for Mirah’s sake, to keep the talk from ending again with that sudden failing of interest—like a drawbridge hauled up, leaving him on the wrong side of a moat. Yet as he rowed, and warmth gathered in him, another thought rose—quiet, reproachful, familiar.

“If I got what I want from him,” he said within himself, “should I not be content to let him close his gate and pass away unseen? I’m in danger of being no better than a recruiting-sergeant—taking what I need, never asking what drama of hunger and hope brings the needful man within reach. And yet—how often do lives go by each other like ships, freighted with exiles who might have known one another if they could have looked out face to face?”

The Thames received these musings and returned them altered. As the wherry neared Blackfriars Bridge it was half-past four; the day—gray for hours—was dying with a kind of grave splendor. Westward, clouds had broken into long, narrowing bands of purple, laid one on another like bruised petals; beneath them spread a clear saffron space, calm as something monumental. On the river the color lived restlessly—caught, chopped, and thrown back by the shiver of ripples; a brown sail, dull a moment ago, flamed suddenly as it crossed the opening; a barge moved from blackness into color as though it had passed under a benediction. Even the water’s small noises—lap and suck along the boat’s side, the hollow knock of oar against thole, the distant rattle of wheels above—made a weaving accompaniment, a river-music which, for those who listened, could soothe or sting. Deronda felt how much of a mind’s health lies in such soundscapes—how a man can be kept feverish by incessant clatter, and healed, almost against his will, by a broader rhythm that leaves room for breath.

He had grown hot with rowing. He gave up the oar, drew his Inverness cape about him, and as he lifted his head to fasten the topmost button his eyes were arrested by a face over the bridge parapet—caught full by the western light and made startlingly distinct. A face he knew: the very emblem of bodily wasting and spiritual hunger, as if some inner flame were burning through too thin a lantern.

It was Mordecai.

Mordecai, too, had seen the advancing boat. At first it was only a moving thing to watch; then impressions recovered—quick, sharp, cumulative—until the nearing figure lifted its face, and to Mordecai it was as if a vision had stepped out of the gold behind the clouds. His white hand rose and beckoned, again and again, with an urgency that was stillness turned into command.

Deronda, anxious to be recognized and not missed, signaled at once. Mordecai answered—lifting his cap, waving it—his whole being flooded with that sense which makes all obstacles melt as if they had never been. It was the triumph of the experimenter who sees the first obedient stir of the thing he has foreseen: this, at least, was real; and because this was, the rest could be.

In three minutes Deronda had landed, paid the boatman, and was beside him. Mordecai stood quite still, as if movement would waste what remained of breath and certainty.

“I was glad to see you standing there,” Deronda said. “I meant to go on to the book-shop and look for you again. I was there yesterday—they may have spoken of it.”

“Yes,” Mordecai answered, with simple gravity. “That was the reason I came to the bridge.”

The words startled Deronda—quiet as they were. Cohen’s hint of mental disorder came back, but with it something else: the sense that there are inward necessities which can mimic madness only because most minds have never known their pressure.

“You knew nothing of my being at Chelsea?” he asked, after a moment.

“No; but I expected you to come down the river. I have been waiting for you these five years.”

Mordecai’s deep-sunk eyes held Deronda’s with a look of affectionate dependence—pathetic and solemn, like one who has lived upon a promise and has suddenly found it walking toward him. Deronda’s own sensitiveness answered, though his judgment whispered illusion.

“It will satisfy me if I can be of any real use to you,” he said, earnestly. “Shall we take a cab? You’ve probably walked enough for—your short breath.”

“Let us go to the book-shop. It will soon be time for me to be there. But now—look up the river.” Mordecai turned again toward the west, and spoke in undertones with an excited calm, as if no barrier existed between their minds. “See the sky, how it is slowly fading. I have always loved this bridge. I stood on it when I was a little boy. It is a meeting-place for the spiritual messengers. The Masters said—each order of things has its angel: that means, the full message of each from what is afar. Here I have listened to earth and sky. When I was stronger I stayed to watch for the stars in the deep heavens. But this time—just about sunset—was always what I loved best. It sank into me and dwelt with me—fading, slowly fading. It was my own decline. It paused—it waited—till at last it brought me my new life—my new self—who will live when this breath is all breathed out.”

Deronda could not speak. Something in the man’s tone—the way the words seemed to ride upon a scant breath like a violin note sustained on a trembling bow—worked upon him strangely. The easy explanation, “madness,” fell away; his mind was too large, too schooled in imagining regions beyond his own experience, to settle at once into contemptuous safety. And in that moment he felt the old legend’s shape: the meanly clothed guide who suddenly drops the disguise and stands forth a power—not by force, but by need that has found its answer. Mordecai’s quietude, born of a persuasion of fulfillment, gave him an authority which no worldly prosperity could have imitated.

After a silence Mordecai said, “Let us go now.” In the cab he added, “We will get down at the end of the street and walk to the shop. You can look at the books; Mr. Ram will soon go away and leave us alone.”

So—this man of visions was cautious, alive to other men’s judgments, measuring the small proprieties of a narrow shop as carefully as any “man of the world.” The cab rattled, and its ironed noise, its hard urban chime, seemed to Deronda like a coarse instrument laid across a fine ear; he thought with sudden irritation of how London’s din bruises thought, and how even art is turned into commodity beneath that perpetual pounding—songs sold like stamped tins, voices made into advertisements—while the true inward music of a people’s speech and memory is allowed to starve.

Yet Mirah’s image stayed with him—her gentleness, her terror, the little domestic facts that meant life: the bread earned, the fires kept, the family’s safety. He foresaw he could not choose his questions; Mordecai would lead. And Deronda, feeling himself slide into a mood that was almost superstitious, said inwardly, “Perhaps I am like a man awaiting a destiny that can interpret an oracle. But if this man feels a strong relation to me, there is something—faith has proved itself potent even when mistaken; expectation has moved the world even when disappointed. Am I to disappoint or fulfill? If it is possible for me to fulfill, I will not disappoint.”

In ten minutes they were alone in Ram’s small gas-lit shop—close air, paper-dust, leather and glue, and that particular smell of old pages, as if dried herbs and human hands had been shut up together. Outside, the street sent in its thin metallic noises; inside, the quiet was dense, and every slight movement—cloth brushing cloth, a breath drawn sharply—had weight. Each of them, as if by a single instinct, bared his head: they wished to see and be seen without veil.

Mordecai leaned back against the little counter; Deronda stood against the opposite wall, hardly four feet away. Mordecai’s face bore the stamp of consumption—brilliant glance under sharply defined features, as if a forsaken temple still held a lamp. He was little above thirty, yet wore that age which suffering adds, stretching time until it feels like a long road walked barefoot. Black hair and beard threw out the yellow pallor; difficult breathing sharpened the mobile nostril; wasted yellow hands lay on folded arms. And there was in the yearning look something like the slowly dying mother’s gaze when the one beloved son comes to her bedside and she flings up a flicker of gladness to say, “My boy!”—for the wish to be spiritually continued in another is a kind of maternal transfer.

Opposite him Deronda’s face held youth and health, with a masculine gravity that made his reverence look like judgment, not mere softness. He did not believe in the validity of Mordecai’s impressions; he did not foresee any clear, effective issue. But he felt, profoundly, a cry from the depths of another being—felt, too, the summons not to resist by easy scorn, but to receive. That receptiveness, rare and massive as fortitude, gave his face a calm, benignant force which fed Mordecai’s confidence and opened the way for his words.

Mordecai began, speaking quietly, as if hoarding strength.

“You cannot know what has guided me to you and brought us together at this moment. You are wondering.”

“I am not impatient,” said Deronda. “I am ready to listen to whatever you wish to disclose.”

“You see some of the reasons why I needed you,” Mordecai said. “You see that I am dying. You see I am as one shut behind bars by the wayside—who, if he speaks, is met only by head-shaking and pity. The day is closing; the light is fading; soon we should not have been able to discern each other. But you have come in time.”

“I rejoice that I am come in time,” Deronda answered, and could not bring himself to utter the word “mistaken,” which lay in ambush on his tongue like cruelty.

“But the hidden reasons began afar off,” Mordecai went on. “In my early years, when I was studying in another land, ideas—beloved ideas—came to me because I was a Jew. They were a trust because I was a Jew. They were an inspiration because I was a Jew and felt the heart of my race beating within me. They were my life; I was not fully born till then. I counted this heart, and this breath, and this right hand—” he pressed his hand on his breast and then held out the wasted fingers—“I counted my sleep and waking, the work that fed my body, the sights that fed my eyes—counted them only as fuel to the divine flame. But I had wandered, engraving my thought in rocky solitudes, and before I could change my course came care and labor and disease, and blocked the way, and bound me with iron that eats into the soul. Then I said, ‘How shall I save the life within me from being stifled with this stifled breath?’”

He paused to rest; the poor lungs took in air with effort, as if every draught were paid for. The silence between the shelves was alive—made audible by the faint hiss of the gas and the muffled city beyond. Deronda dared not break it.

Mordecai went on, checking excitement as if it were a flame that might consume him too fast.

“But you may misunderstand me. I speak not as an ignorant dreamer—one bred in inland valleys, thinking ancient thoughts anew and not knowing them ancient, never standing by the great waters where the world’s knowledge passes to and fro. English is my mother-tongue; England is the native land of this body—only a breaking pot of earth around the fruit-bearing tree whose seed might make the desert rejoice. My true life was nourished in Holland at the feet of my mother’s brother, a Rabbi skilled in special learning. When he died, I went to Hamburg to study, and afterward to Göttingen, that I might take a larger outlook on my people and the Gentile world, and drank knowledge at all sources. I was a youth; I felt free; I saw our chief seats in Germany; I was not then in utter poverty. And I had possessed myself of a handicraft. For I said, I care not if my lot be as Joshua ben Chananja’s: after the last destruction he earned his bread by making needles, but in youth he had been a singer on the Temple steps, and carried a memory of what was before the glory departed. So I said: let my body dwell in poverty and my hands be as the hands of the toiler; but let my soul be a temple of remembrance where the treasures of knowledge enter and the inner sanctuary is hope. I knew what I chose. They said, ‘He feeds himself on visions,’ and I denied not; for visions create and feed the world. I see, I measure the world as it is—which the vision will create anew. You are not listening to one who raves aloof from his fellows’ lives.”

He paused, and Deronda felt the pause asking him to speak.

“Do me the justice to believe,” Deronda said, “that I was not inclined to call your words raving. I listen that I may know, without prejudgment. I have had experience which gives me a keen interest in the story of a spiritual destiny embraced willingly—and embraced in youth.”

“A spiritual destiny—embraced willingly—in youth?” Mordecai repeated, with a kind of correction. “It was the soul fully born within me, and it came in my boyhood. It brought its own world—a mediaeval world—where men made the ancient language live again in new psalms of exile. They had absorbed Gentile philosophy into the faith of the Jew, and still yearned toward a center for our race. One of their souls was born again within me, and awoke amid the memories of their world. It traveled into Spain and Provence; it debated with Aben-Ezra; it took ship with Jehuda ha-Levi; it heard the roar of the Crusaders and the shrieks of tortured Israel. And when its dumb tongue was loosed, it spoke the speech they had made alive with new blood—their ardor, their sorrow, their martyred trust. It sang with the cadence of their strain.”

He stopped again, and then, in a loud hoarse whisper, as if the words must be forced through a narrowed passage:

“While it is imprisoned in me, it will never learn another.”

“Have you written entirely in Hebrew, then?” Deronda asked, remembering with a stir of anxiety his earlier confession.

“Yes—yes,” Mordecai said, deep sadness softening the repetition. “In my youth I wandered toward that solitude, not feeling it was solitude. I had the ranks of the great dead around me; the martyrs gathered and listened. But soon I found the living were deaf. At first I saw my life spread as a long future. I said: part of my Jewish heritage is unbreaking patience; part is skill to seek divers methods and find a rooting-place where planters despair. But there came new messengers from the Eternal. I had to bow under the yoke that presses on the multitude born of woman: family troubles called me; I had to work, to care, not for myself alone. I was left solitary again; but already the angel of death had turned and beckoned, and I felt his skirts continually on my path. I loosed not my effort. I besought hearing and help. I went to men of our people—to the rich in influence or knowledge, to the rich in other wealth. I found none to listen with understanding. I was rebuked for error; I was offered a small sum in charity. No wonder: I looked poor; I carried a bundle of Hebrew manuscript; I said our chief teachers are misleading the hope of our race. Scholar and merchant were both too busy. Scorn stood as interpreter between me and them. One said, ‘The book of Mormon would never have answered in Hebrew; and if you mean to address our learned men, it is not likely you can teach them anything.’ He touched a truth there.”

The last words held a thin edge of irony, sharpened by fatigue.

“But though you have accustomed yourself to write in Hebrew,” Deronda said, wanting to offer some foothold for hope, “few can use English better.”

Mordecai slowly shook his head.

“Too late—too late. I can write no more. My writing would be like this gasping breath. But the breath may wake the fount of pity—the writing not. If I could write now and used English, I should be as one who beats a board to summon those used to no signal but a bell. My soul has an ear to hear the faults of its own speech. New writing of mine would be like this body—” he spread his arms—“within it might be the Ruach-ha-kodesh, the breath of divine thought; but men would smile and say, ‘A poor Jew!’ and the chief smilers would be of my own people.”

His hands fell. His head sank. A darkness, called up by his own words, seemed to hover and eclipse him.

“I feel with you—I feel strongly with you,” Deronda said, and his voice, clear and deep, was a cordial even before the meaning of it reached the ear. “Forgive me if I speak hastily. For what you have written there need be no utter burial. The means of publication are within reach. If you will rely on me, I can assure you of what is necessary to that end.”

“That is not enough,” Mordecai said quickly, lifting his head; memory and confidence flashed back as if a coal had been blown upon. “That is not all my trust in you. You must be not only a hand to me, but a soul—believing my belief—moved by my reasons—hoping my hope—seeing the vision I point to—beholding a glory where I behold it!”

He took a step nearer and laid his hand tightly on Deronda’s arm. His face, a foot away, was like a pale flame; the reliance in it was a claim that would not be denied.

“You will be my life—planted afresh; it will grow. You shall take the inheritance; it has been gathering for ages. Generations crowd on my narrow life as on a bridge: what has been and what is to be are meeting there—and the bridge is breaking. But I have found you. You have come in time. You will take the inheritance which the base son refuses because of the tombs the plow and harrow may not pass over, or the gold-seeker disturb. You will take the sacred inheritance of the Jew.”

Deronda turned as pale as Mordecai. Within him spread a quick alarm—compassionate dread of discouraging this man who prayed as if in his last extremity, and an opposing dread of feeding an illusion and being hurried into a self-committal that might become falsity. Yet the appeal to tenderness overcame the natural recoil from a grasp that would dominate. He could not bear to strike doubt into that ardent suffering creature, crowding too much of his brief being into one moment.

Before he spoke, he laid his palm gently on Mordecai’s straining hand—a touch that was, just then, more than many speeches. Then he said, slowly, without haste, as if aware he might be wrong:

“Do you forget what I told you when we first saw each other? Do you remember I said I was not of your race?”

“It can’t be true,” Mordecai whispered at once, with no sign of shock, as if the denial were not argument but sheer recognition. The warmth of Deronda’s hand upon his seemed to strengthen a certainty older than that statement.

There was a pause. Deronda felt unable to reply, conscious—against his will—that “It can’t be true” weighed on him with a strange pressure.

Mordecai, possessed only by the supreme importance of the relation, followed the whisper with another assertion, as if it were merely the next bead on a long-strung conviction:

“You are not sure of your own origin.”

“How do you know that?” Deronda said, and an habitual shrinking made him draw away his hand. Mordecai loosened his grasp, leaned back again.

“I know it—I know it; what is my life else?” Mordecai cried low, impatient. “Tell me everything: tell me why you deny.”
