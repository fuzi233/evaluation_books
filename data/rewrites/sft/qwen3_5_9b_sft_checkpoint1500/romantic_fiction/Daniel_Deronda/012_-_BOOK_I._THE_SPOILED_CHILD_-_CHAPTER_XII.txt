"O gentlemen, the time of life is short;
To spend that shortness basely were too long..."

These words from Shakespeare hung in the air, a faint echo over breakfast on the second day after the archery meeting. The room itself was a study in quiet, sober antiquity, standing in stark contrast to the restless vulgarity of modern furnishings. Through the open windows, the summer air drifted in, carrying the scent of the old green turf where the dogs roamed free. Beyond lay the park, painted in soft purples, stretching toward a bordering wood that seemed to hold the world at bay.

Inside, however, the atmosphere was charged with a subtle tension. Mr. Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt sat with his back to the window, facing the lawn, his left leg draped casually over another chair, his right elbow propped on the table. A large cigar burned steadily between his lips, smoke curling around him like a halo of indifference. Beside him, Mr. Lush was still engaged with his food, a man of practical habits who knew exactly where his place stood in relation to his master.

Between them moved a procession of six dogs, creatures of considerable leisure who played at hunger, accepting delicacies only to decline them. They vacillated in their attentions, shifting loyalty from one gentleman to the other, save for one exception. There was Fetch, a beautiful liver-colored water-spaniel whose forepaws were planted firmly on the floor, her expressive brown gaze fixed unswervingly on Grandcourt. In his lap, the master held a tiny Maltese named Fluff, adorned with a silver collar and bell. Whenever Grandcourt's hand was not occupied with cigar or cup, it rested possessively on this bundle of warmth.

It was clear, to any observer with a heart for animals, that Fetch felt a sting of jealousy. She waited, silent and dignified, until the neglect became unbearable. Then, with a gentle, deliberate motion, she placed her large, silky paw upon her master's leg.

Grandcourt regarded her with a face devoid of change for half a minute. Then, with a sigh of profound effort, he laid down his cigar. He lifted the unimpassioned Fluff close to his chin, bestowing upon it the requisite caresses, all the while maintaining a grave, scrutinizing stare at Fetch. Poor Fetch, interpreting this as a final dismissal, let out a whimper, attempting to suppress the sign of her discontent. She lowered her head beside the appealing paw, looking up with piteous beseechment. To a lover of dogs, this was a scene of devotion; Grandcourt, reputed to adore animals, certainly acted as if he understood the tragedy unfolding.

But when the amusing anguish of the neglected spaniel erupted into a howling bark, Grandcourt pushed Fetch down without a word. He deposited Fluff carelessly onto the table, where the puppy's black nose soon dominated a salt-cellar. Turning his attention back to his cigar, he discovered with some annoyance that the beast had extinguished it. As he sought to relight it, Fetch, having begun to wail, found, like many of her sex, that it was difficult to stop. Indeed, the second howl was louder, the third indistinguishable from it.

"Turn out that brute, will you?" Grandcourt said to Lush, without raising his voice or even glancing at him, as if he expected his companion to read the smallest flicker of irritation.

Lush immediately rose. Though he was not fond of stooping, especially when the subject was heavy, he lifted Fetch and carried her out, disappearing for a couple of minutes before returning. He then lit a fresh cigar, positioned himself at an angle where he could observe Grandcourt's face without turning his body, and broke the silence.

"Shall you ride or drive to Quetcham today?"

"I am not going to Quetcham."

"You did not go yesterday."

Grandcourt smoked silently for a moment, then replied, "I suppose you sent my card and inquiries."

"I went myself at four," Lush said smoothly. "I told them you were sure to be there shortly. They would suppose some accident prevented you from fulfilling the intention. Especially if you go today."

A silence stretched between them for several minutes. Finally, Grandcourt asked, "What men are invited here with their wives?"

Lush drew out a notebook. "The Captain and Mrs. Torrington come next week. Then there are Mr. Hollis and Lady Flora, and the Cushats and the Gogoffs."

"A rather ragged lot," remarked Grandcourt after a pause. "Why did you ask the Gogoffs? When you write invitations in my name, be good enough to give me a list, instead of bringing down a giantess on me without my knowledge. She spoils the look of the room."

"You invited the Gogoffs yourself when you met them in Paris."

"What has my meeting them in Paris to do with it?" Grandcourt snapped, his voice dropping suddenly. "I told you to give me a list."

There was a remarkable duality to Grandcourt's voice. Hitherto, he had spoken in a superficial, interrupted drawl suggestive chiefly of languor and *ennui*. But these last few words had been uttered in subdued, inward tones, sharp and distinct—a sound Lush had long learned to recognize as the expression of a peremptory will.

"Are there any other couples you would like to invite?" Lush asked carefully.

"Yes," said Grandcourt, "think of some decent people, with a daughter or two. And one of your damned musicians. But not a comic fellow."

"I wonder if Klesmer would consent to come to us when he leaves Quetcham. Nothing but first-class music will go down with Miss Arrowpoint."

Lush spoke carelessly, yet his eyes were fixed observantly on Grandcourt. For the first time, Grandcourt turned his gaze toward his companion, holding it slowly and without speech until he had given two long, luxuriant puffs of smoke. Then, in a lower tone than ever, with a perceptible edge of contempt, he said, "What in the name of nonsense have I to do with Miss Arrowpoint and her music?"

"Well, something," said Lush, jocosely. "You need not give yourself much trouble, perhaps. But some forms must be gone through before a man can marry a million."

"Very likely. But I am not going to marry a million."

"That's a pity—to fling away an opportunity of this sort, and knock down your own plans."

"_Your_ plans, I suppose you mean," Grandcourt retorted.

"You have some debts, you know, and things may turn out inconveniently, after all. The heirship is not _absolutely_ certain."

Grandcourt did not answer. Lush pressed on. "It really is a fine opportunity. The father and mother ask for nothing better, I can see, and the daughter's looks and manners require no allowances, any more than if she hadn't a sixpence. She is not beautiful; but equal to carrying any rank. And she is not likely to refuse such prospects as you can offer her."

"Perhaps not."

"The father and mother would let you do anything you like with them."

"But I should not like to do anything with them."

Here Lush paused, gathering his thoughts, before speaking again in a deep voice of remonstrance. "Good God, Grandcourt! After your experience, will you let a whim interfere with your comfortable settlement in life?"

"Spare your oratory. I know what I am going to do."

"What?" Lush put down his cigar and thrust his hands into his side pockets, as if he had to face something exasperating, though he meant to keep his temper. "I am going to marry the other girl."

"Have you fallen in love?" The question carried a strong sneer.

"I am going to marry her."

"You have made her an offer already, then?"

"No."

"She is a young lady with a will of her own, I fancy. Extremely well fitted to make a rumpus. She would know what she liked."

"She doesn't like you," said Grandcourt, with the ghost of a smile.

"Perfectly true," said Lush, adding again in a markedly sneering tone. "However, if you and she are devoted to each other, that will be enough."

Grandcourt took no notice of this speech. He sipped his coffee, rose, and strolled out onto the lawn, all the dogs following in his wake.

Lush glanced after him a moment, then resumed his cigar, lighting it slowly. He consulted his beard with inspecting eyes and fingers, stroking it with an air of having arrived at some conclusion, before saying in a subdued voice, "Check, old boy!"

Lush, a man of some ability, had not known Grandcourt for fifteen years without learning what sorts of measures were useless with him, though what sort might prove useful often remained dubious. In the beginning of his career, he had held a fellowship and nearly taken orders for the sake of a college living, but, not being fond of that prospect, he had accepted instead the office of traveling companion to a marquess, and afterward to young Grandcourt. Having lost his father early, Grandcourt had found Lush so convenient that he had allowed him to become prime minister in all his more personal affairs.
