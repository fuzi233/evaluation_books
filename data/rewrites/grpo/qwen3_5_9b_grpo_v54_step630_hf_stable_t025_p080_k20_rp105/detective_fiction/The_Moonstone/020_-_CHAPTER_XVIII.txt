Descending the steps to the front door, I encountered Sergeant Cuff. It chafed against my nature, following the friction we had recently shared, to display any semblance of interest in his inquiries. Yet, despite my resistance, a compelling curiosity took hold, dragging my sense of dignity down until I could no longer hold it up. The words escaped me involuntarily: "What news from Frizinghall?"

"I have seen the Indians," Cuff replied, his tone matter-of-fact. "I have also identified what Rosanna purchased privately in the town on Thursday. The Indians will be released on Wednesday next week. There is no doubt in my mind, nor in Mr. Murthwaite's, that they arrived here specifically to steal the Moonstone. Their calculations were naturally disrupted by the events of Wednesday night; consequently, they bear no responsibility for the actual loss of the jewel any more than you do. However, let me warn you, Mr. Betteredge: if we do not locate the Moonstone, they certainly will. You have not heard the final word from the three jugglers."

Mr. Franklin emerged from his walk just as these startling words left Cuff's lips. Governing his curiosity with a steadier hand than I had managed my own, he passed us silently and retreated into the house.

Having already surrendered my dignity, I resolved to reap the full benefit of this revelation. "So much for the Indians," I pressed. "What of Rosanna?"

Cuff shook his head.

"The mystery surrounding her has only deepened," he said. "I have traced her to a shop in Frizinghall, operated by a linen draper named Maltby. She purchased nothing at any other draper's, milliner's, or tailor's establishment; at Maltby's, she bought only a length of cloth. She was fastidious in her selection of quality. As for quantity, she acquired enough to construct a nightgown."

"Whose nightgown?" I asked.

"Her own, undoubtedly," Cuff answered. "Between twelve and three on Thursday morning, she must have slipped into your young lady's chamber to finalize the concealment of the Moonstone while the rest of the household slept. On her return to her own quarters, her nightgown must have brushed against the wet paint on the door. Unable to wash out the stain, and unable to safely destroy the garment without first procuring a replacement to maintain the completeness of her linen inventory..."

"Which proves it was Rosanna's nightgown?" I interjected.

"The material she purchased for the substitute dress," the Sergeant countered. "Had it been Miss Verinder's nightgown, she would have required lace, frills, and countless other embellishments; she would not have possessed the time to construct it in a single night. Plain long cloth signifies a plain servant's nightgown. No, Mr. Betteredge, the details are clear. The crux of the matter remains: why, after securing the replacement dress, did she hide the stained nightgown rather than destroy it? If the girl refuses to speak, there is only one resolution. The hiding-place at the Shivering Sand must be searched; the truth lies there."

"How shall you locate the place?" I inquired.

"I regret to disappoint you," Cuff said, "but that is a secret I intend to retain."

(To spare your curiosity, as he spared mine, I may inform you that he had returned from Frizinghall armed with a search warrant. His experience dictated that Rosanna likely carried a memorandum of the hiding-place, intended to guide her should she return under altered circumstances and after a lapse of time. Possessing this document, the Sergeant would be furnished with all he required.)

"Now, Mr. Betteredge," he continued, "let us abandon speculation and engage in business. I instructed Joyce to keep watch on Rosanna. Where is Joyce?"

Joyce was the Frizinghall constable, assigned to Cuff's command by Superintendent Seegrave. The clock struck two; punctual to the minute, the carriage arrived to escort Miss Rachel to her aunt's.

"One thing at a time," Cuff said, intercepting me as I moved to summon Joyce. "I must attend to Miss Verinder first."

Since the rain threatened, the enclosed carriage had been designated for Miss Rachel's journey to Frizinghall. Cuff beckoned Samuel, the footman, to descend from the rumble seat behind.

"You will see a friend of mine waiting among the trees on this side of the lodge gate," he instructed. "My friend, without halting the carriage, will climb into the rumble with you. Your sole duty is to remain silent and keep your eyes shut. Otherwise, you will find yourself in trouble."

With this directive, he dismissed the footman. I could not fathom Samuel's thoughts, but it was plain to me that Miss Rachel was to be secretly monitored from the moment she departed our house—or if she indeed departed. A guard on my young lady! A spy concealed within the rumble of her mother's carriage! I could have severed my own tongue for the foolishness of addressing Sergeant Cuff.

The first to emerge from the house was my lady. She positioned herself on the top step, observing silently. She spoke not a word to the Sergeant or to me. With lips sealed and arms folded within the light garden cloak she had wrapped around herself against the chill air, she stood as motionless as a statue, awaiting her daughter's appearance.

Moments later, Miss Rachel descended the stairs. She was dressed in soft yellow fabric that highlighted her dark complexion, the material tailored tightly around her waist in a jacket style. She wore a smart little straw hat with a white veil twisted beneath it, primrose-colored gloves that fit her hands like a second skin, and her black hair lay smooth as satin beneath the hat. Her ears, like rosy shells, bore dangling pearls. She moved swiftly and straight as a lily on its stem, lithe and supple in every motion, resembling a young cat. Nothing in her pretty face had altered, save for her eyes and lips. Her eyes burned with a brightness and fierceness that displeased me; her lips had lost all color and smile, rendering them almost unrecognizable. She kissed her mother in a hurried, abrupt manner on the cheek, murmuring, "Try to forgive me, mamma," before violently pulling down her veil, tearing it in the process. In an instant, she had rushed down the steps and bolted into the carriage as if seeking refuge.

Sergeant Cuff was equally swift. He guided Samuel back and stood before Miss Rachel, holding the open carriage door just as she settled inside.

"What do you want?" Miss Rachel demanded from behind her veil.

"I wish to say one word to you, miss," Cuff replied, "before you depart. I cannot presume to prevent your visit to your aunt. I can only venture to state that your departure, given the current circumstances, places an obstacle in my path to recovering your Diamond. Please comprehend this, and then decide for yourself whether to go or stay."

Miss Rachel offered no reply. "Drive on, James!" she commanded the coachman.

Without further hesitation, Cuff shut the carriage door. Just as it closed, Mr. Franklin ran down the steps. "Good-bye, Rachel," he said, extending his hand.

"Drive on!" Miss Rachel cried, louder than before, ignoring Mr. Franklin just as she had ignored the Sergeant.

Mr. Franklin stepped back, thunderstruck. The coachman, unsure of his orders, glanced toward my lady, who remained immovable on the top step. My lady, her face a turmoil of anger, sorrow, and shame, signaled the horses to start, then turned hastily back into the house. Mr. Franklin, regaining his composure, called after her as the carriage drove away, "Aunt! You were quite right. Accept my thanks for all your kindness—and let me go."

My lady turned as if to speak, then, seemingly distrusting her own resolve, waved her hand kindly. "Let me see you before you leave us, Franklin," she said in a broken voice, and retreated to her own room.

"Do me a last favor, Betteredge," Mr. Franklin said, turning to me with tears in his eyes. "Get me away to the train as soon as you can!"

He too withdrew into the house. For the moment, Miss Rachel had completely unmanned him. One could judge the depth of his affection for her by this reaction.

Sergeant Cuff and I were left face to face at the bottom of the steps. The Sergeant stood with his gaze fixed on a gap in the trees, commanding a view of one of the windings of the drive leading from the house. His hands were in his pockets, and he was softly whistling "The Last Rose of Summer."

"There is a time for everything," I said with savage intensity. "This is not a time for whistling."

At that precise moment, the carriage appeared in the distance through the gap, heading toward the lodge gate. Another man, besides Samuel, was plainly visible in the rumble seat behind.

"All right!" Cuff muttered to himself. He turned to me. "It is no time for whistling, Mr. Betteredge, as you say. It is time to take this business in hand, now, without sparing anyone. We shall begin with Rosanna Spearman. Where is Joyce?"

We both called for Joyce, receiving no answer. I dispatched a stable-boy to search for him.

"You heard what I said to Miss Verinder?" Cuff remarked while we waited. "And you witnessed her reception of it? I told her plainly that her departure would hinder my recovery of her Diamond—and yet she leaves, in defiance of that statement! Your young lady has a traveling companion in her mother's carriage, Mr. Betteredge—and the name of that companion is the Moonstone."

I said nothing, clinging stubbornly to my belief in Miss Rachel's innocence.

The stable-boy returned, followed very reluctantly, as it seemed to me, by Joyce.

"Where is Rosanna Spearman?" Cuff asked.

"I cannot account for it, sir," Joyce began, his voice trembling. "And I am very sorry. But somehow or other—"

"Before I went to Frizinghall," Cuff interrupted sharply, "I instructed you to keep your eyes on Rosanna Spearman without allowing her to discover she was being watched. Do you mean to tell me you have allowed her to elude you?"

"I am afraid, sir," Joyce stammered, "that I was perhaps a little *too* careful not to let her discover me. There are so many passages in the lower parts of this house—"

"How long has it been since you lost sight of her?"

"Nigh on an hour, sir."

"You may return to your regular duties at Frizinghall," Cuff said, speaking with his usual quiet, dreary composure. "I do not believe your talents lie in our line, Mr. Joyce. Your current employment is a trifle beyond you. Good morning."

The man slunk away. I found it difficult to articulate the effect of discovering Rosanna Spearman was missing. I seemed to harbor fifty conflicting thoughts simultaneously. In that state, I stared at Sergeant Cuff, my powers of speech failing me entirely.

"No, Mr. Betteredge," Cuff said, as if he had anticipated my uppermost thought and was addressing it before the others. "Your young friend, Rosanna, will not slip through my fingers so easily as you suppose. As long as I know where Miss Verinder is, I possess the means to trace Miss Verinder's accomplice. I prevented them from communicating last night. Very well. They will meet at Frizinghall instead of here. The present inquiry must simply be shifted (rather sooner than I anticipated) from this house to the house where Miss Verinder is visiting. In the meantime, I fear I must trouble you to call the servants together again."

I accompanied him to the servants' hall. It is disgraceful, yet undeniably true, that I suffered another attack of detective fever upon hearing those words. I momentarily forgot my hatred for Sergeant Cuff. I seized him confidentially by the arm. "For goodness' sake, tell us what you intend to do with the servants now?"

The great Cuff stood stock-still, addressing himself in a kind of melancholy rapture to the empty air.

"If this man," the Sergeant said, apparently referring to me, "only understood the growing of roses, he would be the most perfectly formed character on the face of creation!" Following this strong expression of feeling, he sighed and put his arm through mine. "This is the situation," he said, dropping back to business. "Rosanna has done one of two things. She has either gone directly to Frizinghall (before I could arrive), or she has gone first to visit her hiding-place at the Shivering Sand. The first priority is to determine which of the servants saw her last before she left the house."

Upon instituting this inquiry, it became clear that the last person to see Rosanna was Nancy, the kitchenmaid.

Nancy had observed her slipping out with a letter in her hand and stopping the butcher's man who had just delivered meat at the back door. Nancy had heard her instruct the man to post the letter when he returned to Frizinghall. The man had examined the address and noted it was a roundabout method for delivering a letter directed to Cobb's Hole, to be posted at Frizinghall—and that, moreover, it was a Saturday, which would delay delivery until Monday morning. Rosanna had replied that the delay until Monday was of no consequence; the only thing she required was assurance that the man would comply. He had promised and driven away. Nancy had been recalled to the kitchen, and no one else had seen anything of Rosanna Spearman thereafter.

"Well?" I asked when we were alone again.

"Well," Cuff said. "I must proceed to Frizinghall."

"Regarding the letter, sir?"

"Yes. The memorandum of the hiding-place is contained within that letter. I must inspect the address at the post-office. If it is the address I suspect, I shall pay our friend, Mrs. Yolland, another visit on Monday next."

I accompanied the Sergeant to order the pony-chaise. In the stable-yard, a new light was cast upon the missing girl.
