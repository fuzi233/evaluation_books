Before the great mud walls were erected around the House of Stone in Omdurman, only a thorny fence circled this infamous prison and its immediate surroundings. It stood on the eastern edge of the town, arguably the most wretched capital of any empire known to mankind. Not a flower bloomed in its corners; there was no grass, no shade from trees. Only a brown, stony plain scorched by the sun, upon which sprawled a ragged city of hovels teeming with vermin and poison.

Between the prison and the Nile, no houses existed. At this time, prisoners were permitted to shuffle in their chains down the half-mile of broken, sloping earth to the riverbank whenever daylight remained, to fetch water and perform their ablutions. For the native or the Negro, escape was not impossible. Along that bank, dhows were moored in numbers; the river traffic, sparse as it was, found its harbor there, and the wide foreshore served as a bustling marketplace. Consequently, the open space between the river and the House of Stone was crowded and noisy all day. Captives rubbed shoulders with their friends, plotting escapes, or slipping instantly into the densest part of the crowd to find the nearest blacksmith, where the price of iron outweighed any risk taken. Yet, even on their way to the smithy, their fetters drew no attention in Omdurman. Slaves wore them as a daily habit, and scarcely a street in that long, brown, treeless, squalid city was ever free from the clinking of a man walking in chains.

For the European, however, escape was a different matter entirely. Though there were fewer white prisoners, each was a marked man. Beyond relays of camels stationed throughout the desert, substantial funds, lengthy preparations, and above all, devoted natives willing to risk their lives were essential prerequisites for evasion. Camels could be procured and positioned, but their drivers might not stay; elaborate plans could be laid, only to be shattered at the last moment by the gaoler's whip, suspecting the captive of hiding money. Even a loyal servant might falter at the final hurdle. Colonel Trench began to lose all hope. He knew his friends were working for him. Sometimes, the boy bringing his food would bid him be ready; sometimes, amidst a parade of the Khalifa's troops where he was displayed as a symbol of Turkish destiny, a man would jostle his camel and whisper encouragement. But nothing ever came of these whispers. He watched the sun rise daily beyond the river's bend, behind the tall palms of Khartoum, and burn across the sky, while the months dragged on one after another.

Towards the end of August, in the year Durrance returned home blind from the Sudan, Trench sat in a corner of the enclosure, watching the sun sink westward toward the plain with an agony of anticipation. However intolerable the heat and burden of the day, it was nothing compared to the horrors each night renewed.

Twilight arrived, bringing with it Idris es Saier, the great Negro of the Gawaamah tribe, and his fellow jailers.

"Into the House of Stone!" he cried.

Praying and cursing, with the sound of pitiless whips falling perpetually upon the backs of the lagging, the prisoners jostled and struggled at the narrow entrance. Already, some thirty captives occupied the interior, lying on the swampy mud floor or propped against the walls in the final extremities of weakness and disease. Two hundred more were driven in at night, penned there until morning. The room was perhaps thirty feet square, with four feet occupied by a solid pillar supporting the roof. There were no windows; a few small apertures near the ceiling offered a mere pretense of ventilation. Into this foul, pestilential hovel the prisoners were packed, screaming and fighting. The door slammed shut, plunging them into utter darkness so thick one could not distinguish the outline of a neighbor's head.

Colonel Trench fought like the rest. There was a corner near the door he coveted with a ferocity he had never felt in his free days. Occupying that spot would grant him shelter from blows, stamping feet, and the bruising shackles of neighbors; it would also provide a support against which to lean his back during the ten interminable hours of suffocation.

That fear haunted him whenever he was driven inside. It acted upon him like a drug inducing madness. For if a man once went down in that yelling, struggling throng, he never rose again—he was trampled into shapelessness. Trench had seen such victims dragged from the prison each morning; and he was a small man. Thus, he fought for his corner in a frenzy like a wild beast, kicking with his fetters, thrusting with his elbows, diving under arms, burrowing between others, tearing at clothes, using his nails and fists, and even striking heads with the chain dangling from the iron ring about his neck. Eventually, he reached the corner, streaming with sweat and gasping for breath. For the rest of the night, he spent it holding that position against all comers.

*"If I were to fall!"* he gasped. *"O God, if I were to fall!"*

He shouted aloud to his neighbor—for in that clamor, nothing less than a shout was audible—*"Is it you, Ibrahim?"* A similar shout answered him, *"Yes, Effendi."*

Trench felt a measure of relief. Between Ibrahim, a great tall Arab of the Hadendowa, and Trench, a friendship born of shared necessity had blossomed. There were no prison rations in Omdurman; each captive depended on his own money or the charity of friends outside. From time to time, money came to Trench from his friends, smuggled secretly into the prison by a native arriving from Assouan or Suakin. But there were long periods when no aid arrived, and he survived on the charity of Greeks who had sworn conversion to the Mahdist faith, or starved with as much patience as he could muster. There were times, too, when Ibrahim had no friend to send him a meal. So, each man helped the other in need. They stood side by side against the wall at night.

*"Yes, Effendi, I am here,"* Ibrahim groaned, groping with his hand in the black darkness to steady Trench against the wall.

A fight of extraordinary violence raged in an extreme corner of the prison. The prisoners were so tightly packed that with every advance of one combatant and retreat of another, the entire jostling crowd swayed in a rhythmic motion from end to end, side to side. They swayed, fighting to keep their feet, fighting even with their teeth. Above the din of hard breathing, the clank of chains, and their imprecations, rose occasionally a wild sobbing cry for mercy or an inhuman shriek, stifled immediately, revealing that a man had fallen beneath the stamping feet. Missiles were thrown across the prison—even dirt gathered from the floor—and since no one knew whence they came, heads were battered against heads in the frantic effort to dodge them. All this occurred in the deepest darkness.

For two hours, Trench stood in that black, noise-filled, rank-smelling prison. Eight more hours awaited before the door would open, allowing him to stumble into the clean air of the zareeba and sleep. Standing on tiptoes to lift his head above his fellows, he could barely breathe; the air was moist and sour. His throat was parched, his tongue swollen and stringy like a dried fig. It seemed to him that the imagination of God could devise no worse hell than the House of Stone on an August night in Omdurman. He thought, *If I were to fall!* and as he spoke, his hell became complete, for the door opened.

Idris es Saier appeared in the threshold.

*"Make room! Make room!"* he cried, throwing fire among the prisoners to drive them from the exit. Tufts of dried grass ignited in the darkness, falling onto the bodies of the captives. So crowded were they that they could not avoid the flames; in places, they could not raise their hands to brush them from their shoulders or heads.

*"Make room!"* cried Idris. The whips of his fellow jailers enforced his command, lashes falling upon everyone within reach, clearing a small space at the door. Into that gap a man was flung, and the door closed again.

Trench stood close to the door. In the dim twilight filtering through the doorway, he caught a glimpse of the new prisoner: a man heavily ironed, slight of figure, and bent with suffering.

*"He will fall,"* Trench muttered. *"He will fall tonight. God! If I were to!"* Suddenly, the crowd swayed against him, and the curses rose louder and shriller than before.

The new prisoner was the cause. Clinging to the door with his face pressed against the panels, through the cracks of which actual air might seep, he was plucked from his vantage by those behind. Jostled, pressed backward so others might take his place, he was driven like a wedge hammered between prisoners until finally flung against Colonel Trench.

Ordinary instincts of kindness could not survive the nightmare of that prison house. By day, outside, prisoners were often united by their common misery; the faithful frequently aided the infidel.
