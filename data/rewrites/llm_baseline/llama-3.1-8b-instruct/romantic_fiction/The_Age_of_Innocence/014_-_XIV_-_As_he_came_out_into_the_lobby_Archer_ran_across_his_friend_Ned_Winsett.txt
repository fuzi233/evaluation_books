As Archer stepped out into the lobby, he spotted his friend Ned Winsett, the one acquaintance among his circle of acquaintances who occasionally ventured beyond the realm of superficial conversation. They exchanged greetings, and Winsett suggested a visit to a quaint little German restaurant just around the corner. Archer declined, citing work obligations, and Winsett quipped, "And I, too, shall don the cloak of diligence." They strolled together, and soon Winsett asked, "Tell me, what's the name of the enigmatic woman seated in the Beaufort box – the one Lefferts seems so enamored with?"

Archer felt a faint irritation at the question, unable to discern why Winsett was interested in Ellen Olenska's name, especially in conjunction with Lefferts'. It was uncharacteristic of Winsett to display such curiosity, but then again, he was a journalist. "Not for an interview, I hope?" Archer asked with a laugh.

"Not for the press," Winsett replied, "merely for personal interest. You see, she's a neighbor of mine, and quite unexpectedly, she's taken a shine to my young son, who had a bit of an accident in her yard chasing after his kitten. She rushed in, bareheaded, cradling him in her arms, and bandaged his knee with great care. My wife was so taken aback that she forgot to ask her name."

A warm glow spread through Archer's chest. While it was hardly an extraordinary act of kindness, it reminded him of Ellen's gentle nature, and how she would rush to help others without hesitation. "That's the Countess Olenska – a granddaughter of old Mrs. Mingott's," he explained.

"A Countess!" Winsett exclaimed. "I wouldn't have pegged them as the neighborly type. The Mingotts aren't exactly known for mingling with our set."

"They would be, if you'd give them the chance," Archer said with a hint of amusement.

Their conversation trailed off, as they'd often discussed the topic of the social elite's reluctance to engage with the fashionable crowd. Both men knew there was little point in revisiting the argument.

"I wonder," Winsett mused, "how a Countess ends up living in our rather humble neighborhood."

"Because she doesn't concern herself with such petty social distinctions," Archer replied, a quiet pride in his voice as he reflected on his own impression of her.

"Been in grander places, perhaps," Winsett suggested
