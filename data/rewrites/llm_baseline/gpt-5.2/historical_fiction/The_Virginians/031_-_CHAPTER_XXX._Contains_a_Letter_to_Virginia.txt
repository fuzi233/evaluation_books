Harry Warrington went, as he had lately made a habit of doing, to dine at the White Horse ordinary; and the room met him with that agreeable tumult which belongs to a country table when it is pretending, for an hour, to be the town. Plates clattered; knives struck; the great joint at the board’s head sent up a warm steam of pepper and beef-fat; and under it all lay the steady music of a public room—boots creaking on sanded boards, pewter set down, corks eased from bottles with a soft pop, and then the long, contented draughting sound that follows.

Among the faces, red with good cheer and honest heat, was one that made Harry’s own spirits spring at once. Parson Sampson—round as a harvest moon, rosy as claret in a glass, and as ready with a story as a cook with salt—was in full command of the company. His voice rang above the rest, a bell in a flock, now dropping to a confidential relish, now flung wide with a laugh that set other men laughing before they had even caught the point. He had not been in London for months; but he possessed that talent, common enough in those days, of making yesterday’s invention sound like this morning’s post.

He dispensed news as the carver dispensed slices: a little of the King’s doings at Kensington; a little of the Duke’s in Pall Mall; how Mr. Byng bore himself in prison, who visited, who stayed away; what odds were given at Newmarket; which face in Covent Garden presently ruled the toasts. Names wandered and changed in his telling as herbs do when a country hand substitutes what grows nearest—Mrs. Polly became Mrs. Lucy; Lady Jane and Lady Mary traded passions; a quarrel in Marybone Gardens shifted from Horse Grenadiers to Foot Guards without injuring the flavor of the tale. The auditors—Hampshire gentlemen with their coats brushed for the Wells—asked only that the dish be lively and wicked, and were not inclined to quarrel with the cook about the exact receipt.

Harry had barely taken his place when Sampson’s eye caught him. Whether from modesty or claret, the parson’s already crimson cheeks deepened. He leaned to the squire beside him—a stout man in a drab coat and gold-laced red waistcoat—and whispered, with a solemn wag of his head, “Maxima debetur,” as though the Latin itself were part of the joke. Then he rose too quickly, stumbled, and made for Harry with arms half-open, like a man welcoming a long-lost son.

“My dear sir! my very dear sir! my conqueror of spades—and clubs—and hearts too!” cried the chaplain, taking Harry’s hand in both his own. “Delighted to see your honour looking so fresh and well!”

Harry returned the greeting warmly; he liked Sampson, for all his looseness, and the parson’s geniality had a warmth in it like a kitchen fire. “And I am glad to see your reverence so hearty,” Harry said, eyeing the clerical rosy gills with a grin. “You look as if English ale agreed with you.”

The drab-coated squire knew Harry, too, and slapped the bench to make room. “Here, Mr. Warrington! Here! Sit by me. Parson, come back and finish about Lord Ogle and the grocer’s wife in—” He never reached the place-name; Sampson, in his flurry, trod upon a gouty toe, and the sentence broke into a shout and an oath that made the table roar.

The chaplain begged pardon, turning from the offended foot to Harry and, by a kind of cheerful necessity, to all the room. In a few rolling breaths he delivered, as if they were tidings for the nation, all the private intelligence of Castlewood: my lord’s affectionate remembrances to his cousin; special orders that Sampson should come to Tunbridge Wells and look after the young gentleman’s morals; my Lady Viscountess and Lady Fanny gone to Harrogate for the waters; Mr. Will winning his money at Newmarket and bound to visit a duke; Molly the housemaid crying her eyes out about Gumbo, Harry’s servant. The names of high persons—introduced with that practised flourish which made small men feel themselves neighbour to greatness—fell into the parson’s talk like currants into a pudding.

“All Hampshire has heard of your successes at Tunbridge—successes of every kind,” Sampson said at last, with an arch look that set the squire chuckling. “My lord hopes—her ladyship hopes—you won’t be spoilt for quiet home.”

One by one the country gentlemen dropped away, called to their horses, or went to their lodgings to digest and doze. The room grew emptier, the noise thinned to a comfortable murmur, and at length there remained chiefly Harry and the chaplain, the bottle between them, and a pair of glasses that were never long dry.

“Though I have had plenty,” Sampson declared, filling again with that pious firmness with which some men refill, “that is no reason why I should not have plenty more.”

Toast followed toast. The claret had a smell like crushed berries and old wood; it warmed the throat, loosened the tongue, brightened the eye. Harry, who had been generous by nature, grew extravagant in kindness as the bottle sank. Why should Mr. Sampson pay for an inn when there was a room at Harry’s lodging? The invitation was pressed, insisted on, made a point of honour. The parson’s trunk was ordered over at once. Gumbo received commands—solemn, affectionate commands—to make Mr. Sampson comfortable, most comfortable; and Gumbo, who had grown sleek upon English beef and ale and the easy abundance of a rich young gentleman’s household, bowed and grinned, as though comfort were his own invention.

Harry would not stop at that. He must show Sampson his horses. He had several now—fine creatures, with groomed coats and patient eyes, steaming a little in their stalls. The stable air met them full in the face: sweet hay, dung, leather, warm animal breath, and the sharp iron scent of shoes against stone. The soundscape changed too—the soft stamp and shift of hooves, the jingle of bit and chain, the low, conversational nickering that seems always to be going on between beasts and men.

And there, turning its head at the parson’s voice, was the very horse Harry had won from him. The animal whinnied with such unmistakable pleasure, thrusting its nose into Sampson’s coat as if searching for old treats, that the chaplain stood arrested, ridiculous and touched at once.

“Why, you affectionate rogue,” Sampson murmured, rubbing the warm velvet of the muzzle, his laughter thickening oddly.

Harry, in a gust of feeling—claret feeding generosity as fat feeds flame—swore briskly by Jupiter and rapped out an oath or two in sheer good humour. “He’s yours again, Sampson! I’ll not keep him. Take your old horse back.”

The parson seized Harry’s hand, blessed him, and then, to complete the comedy, flung his arms round the horse’s neck and wept. Tears of Bordeaux and gratitude ran together. The groom stared; Gumbo showed his white teeth in a grin that might have been pity or amusement; and the horse endured the embrace with the grave patience of a creature accustomed to human folly.

Arm in arm, their clothes carrying the stable’s honest odors with them—leather, hay, and the faint animal musk—Harry and Sampson walked up to Madame Bernstein’s lodgings. Their cheeks were flushed; their eyes had that bright, friendly shine which in that age was as common as buckles on shoes.

Madame Bernstein received the chaplain kindly enough. She liked, now and then, a coarse jest as she liked a highly-spiced dish or a new entrée contrived by her cook: it delighted her for two or three appearances, and then she tired of it. The only entertainment she professed never to weary of was cards.

“The cards don’t cheat,” she used to say. “A bad hand tells you the truth to your face: and there is nothing so flattering in the world as a good suite of trumps.” When she was in a good humour, settling herself to her favorite pastime, she would bid her nephew’s chaplain, laughing, say grace—as if the parson were a sauce to the meal, to be sprinkled on before the carving began.

Honest Sampson at first hung back from the card-table at Tunbridge. Her ladyship played too high for him. He slapped his pocket with a comical, piteous look; its contents, he said, had already been transferred to that fortunate youth at Castlewood. Madame Bernstein, like most of her age and sex, was not lavish with money; yet, by some contrivance—most likely Harry’s overflowing purse and mood—Mr. Sampson presently found himself supplied with a little ready coin. Our young gentleman welcomed him to it as readily as he welcomed him to a chair, a bed, and a place at his bottle.

It was surprising how quickly Harry adapted himself to the life around him. His suits were still black, but of the finest cut and cloth; his linen and lace were as delicate as his aunt could wish. He purchased a fine shaving-plate from the toy-shop women, and two magnificent brocade bedgowns in which he lolled at ease, sipping his morning chocolate—a thick, fragrant draught, frothed and sweetened, that left a bitter cocoa perfume on the breath. He acquired swords and walking-canes; French watches with painted backs and diamond settings; snuff-boxes enamelled with a cunning that made the little pictures look moist and alive. Tradesmen learned his hours. Grooms and jockeys waited daily. There was now an anteroom full of men with petitions, parcels, and accounts; and Gumbo—grown important in his place—admitted them one by one over Harry’s chocolate, as though he were steward to a small court.

No single negro could have kept order among all the fine things Mr. Warrington had gathered—let alone the horses and the post-chaise he had bought. How many men Gumbo had under him no one exactly counted; but there were hands enough to polish, brush, fold, rub down, and carry, and the house smelled perpetually of fresh soap, beeswax, leather, and heated milk.

Harry instructed himself too in the accomplishments required of a gentleman. A French fencing-master and a dancing-master of the same nation resided at Tunbridge that season, and Harry frequented them with the steady appetite of youth that wishes to be complete. Within weeks he could handle the foils against his master and any frequenter of the school; and Lady Maria—who danced elegantly herself—owned with a sigh that there was no gentleman at court who could walk a minuet more gracefully than Mr. Warrington.

As for riding, he took a few lessons on the great horse from a master who came to the Wells; yet he declared their Virginian seat and manner good enough, and maintained he saw no one among the fine folks or jockeys who rode better than his friend Colonel George Washington of Mount Vernon. The boast was spoken not as vanity, but as loyalty to a remembered world—the red earth, the long fences, the quiet, wide sound of wind in trees and the honest creak of saddle-leather untroubled by the shrill racket of a fashionable course.

Sampson, meanwhile, found himself lodged better than he had been for a long while. He knew a great deal of the world and told a great deal more; and Harry—being twenty—looked up with delighted credulity to the man of thirty, whose old jokes and tarnished anecdotes had been washed anew in wine at a hundred tables. Sampson’s town pleasantries and college stories were all fresh to Harry, and his companionship came with the insinuating ease of a guide who takes a young man through bright rooms and never lets him see the kitchen where the reckoning is prepared.

There were, a hundred years ago, men in London who made a trade of attaching themselves to fashionable youth; of tickling their fancies with merry stories; of playing Mentors in playhouses and taverns; of attending them to gaming tables with an understanding perhaps with the punters; of drinking lemonade while Master Hopeful drank Burgundy, and then walking into the street with a cool head when the young lord reeled. Mr. Sampson was of that comfortable species; and if the diet was parasitical, it did not disagree with him. No man could look more healthy, or flourish more cheerfully, than the chaplain on such sustenance.

He became Harry’s confidential leader; and from the following letter—kept among the Warrington papers—it may be seen that Mr. Harry not only had fencing and dancing masters, but a tutor, chaplain, and secretary too; for the young gentleman’s pen, when it addressed his mother, carried an air of care and supervision, as if the very words had been dressed and powdered for her presence.

TO MRS. ESMOND WARRINGTON, OF CASTLEWOOD, AT HER HOUSE AT RICHMOND, VIRGINIA.

Mrs. Bligh’s Lodgings, Pantiles, Tunbridge Wells.
August 25th, 1756.

HONOURED MADAM,—Your honoured letter of 20 June, per Mr. Trail of Bristol, has been forwarded to me duly; and I have to thank your goodness and kindness for the good advice which you are pleased to give me, as also for the remembrances of dear home, which I shall love never the worse for having been to the home of our ancestors in England.

I writ you a letter by the last monthly packet, informing my honoured mother of the little accident I had on the road hither, and of the kind friends who I found and whom took me in. Since then I have been profiting of the fine weather and the good company here, and have made many friends among our nobility, whose acquaintance I am sure you will not be sorry that I should make. Among their lordships I may mention the famous Earl of Chesterfield, late Ambassador to Holland, and Viceroy of the Kingdom of Ireland; the Earl of March and Ruglen, who will be Duke of Queensberry at the death of his Grace; and her Grace the Duchess, a celebrated beauty of the Queen’s time, when she remembers my grandpapa at Court. These and many more persons of the first fashion attend my aunt’s assemblies, which are the most crowded at this crowded place.

Also on my way hither I stayed at Westerham, at the house of an officer, Lieut.-Gen. Wolfe, who served with my grandfather and General Webb in the famous wars of the Duke of Marlborough. Mr. Wolfe has a son, Lieut.-Col. James Wolfe, engaged to be married to a beautiful lady now in this place, Miss Lowther of the North—and though but 30 years old he is looked up to as much as any officer in the whole army, and has served with honour under his Royal Highness the Duke wherever our arms have been employed.

I thank my honoured mother for announcing to me that a quarter’s allowance of 52l. 10s. will be paid me by Mr. Trail. I am in no present want of cash, and by practising a rigid economy, which will be necessary (as I do not disguise) for the maintenance of horses, Gumbo, and the equipage and apparel requisite for a young gentleman of good family, hope to be able to maintain my credit without unduly trespassing upon yours. The linnen and clothes which I brought with me will with due care last for some years—as you say. ’Tis not quite so fine as worn here by persons of fashion, and I may have to purchase a few very fine shirts for great days: but those I have are excellent for daily wear.

I am thankful that I have been quite without occasion to use your excellent family pills. Gumbo hath taken them with great benefit, who grows fat and saucy upon English beef, ale, and air. He sends his humble duty to his mistress, and prays Mrs. Mountain to remember him to all his fellow-servants, especially Dinah and Lily, for whom he has bought posey-rings at Tunbridge Fair.

Besides partaking of all the pleasures of the place, I hope my honoured mother will believe that I have not been unmindful of my education. I have had masters in fencing and dancing, and my Lord Castlewood’s chaplain, the Reverend Mr. Sampson, having come hither to drink the waters, has been so good as to take a vacant room at my lodging. Mr. S. breakfasts with me, and we read together of a morning—he saying that I am not quite such a dunce as I used to appear at home. We have read in Mr. Rapin’s History, Dr. Barrow’s Sermons, and, for amusement, Shakspeare, Mr. Pope’s Homer, and (in French) the translation of an Arabian Work of Tales, very diverting.

Several men of learning have been staying here besides the persons of fashion; and amongst the former was Mr. Richardson, the author of the famous books which you and Mountain and my dearest brother used to love so. He was pleased when I told him that his works were in your closet in Virginia, and begged me to convey his respectful compliments to my lady-mother. Mr. R. is a short fat man, with little of the fire of genius visible in his eye or person.

My aunt and my cousin, the Lady Maria, desire their affectionate compliments to you, and with best regards for Mountain, to whom I enclose a note, I am,—Honoured madam, your dutiful son,
H. ESMOND WARRINGTON.

In the same packet, preserved by the careful hand at Richmond, Madam Esmond set down, as if she were entering stores in a great house-book, what she sent in answer—a domestic cargo crossing an ocean like a memory made edible:

From my son. Received October 15 at Richmond. Sent 16 jars preserved peaches, 224 lbs. best tobacco, 24 finest hams, per Royal William of Liverpool, 8 jars peaches, 12 hams for my nephew, the Rt. Honourable the Earl of Castlewood. 4 jars, 6 hams for the Baroness Bernstein, ditto ditto for Mrs. Lambert of Oakhurst, Surrey, and 1/2 cwt. tobacco. Packet of Infallible Family Pills for Gumbo. My Papa’s large silver-gilt shoe-buckles for H., and red silver-laced saddle-cloth.

One can almost hear, in that list, the old Virginian household again: the soft slap of peach flesh in the preserving-pan, the hiss when syrup meets heat, the steady stir with a wooden spoon; the smokehouse with its sweet, resinous perfume, hams darkening in slow time; the rustle of tobacco leaves like dry rain; the quiet authority of a mistress who knows that food, sent far, is not merely sustenance but command, comfort, and proof that the sender’s language still rules the receiver’s mouth.

Enclosed too was a second note, not dressed for a mother’s eye but dashed off as the heart ran, to Mrs. Mountain:

For Mrs. Mountain.

What do you mien, you silly old Mountain, by sending an order for your poor old divadends dew at Xmas? I’d have you to know I don’t want your 7l. 10, and have toar your order up into 1000 bitts. I’ve plenty of money. But I’m obleaged to you all same. A kiss to Fanny from—Your loving
HARRY.

Madam Esmond wrote beneath, in her own hand, with that mingling of shrewdness and tenderness that belongs to a woman who has ruled servants and children alike:

This note, which I desired M. to show to me, proves that she hath a good heart, and that she wished to show her gratitude to the family, by giving up her half-yearly divd. (on L500 3 per ct.) to my boy. Hence I reprimanded her very slightly for daring to send money to Mr. E. Warrington, unknown to his mother. Note to Mountain not so well spelt as letter to me.

Mem. to write to Revd. Mr. Sampson desire to know what theolog. books he reads with H. Recommend Law, Baxter, Drelincourt.—Request H. to say his catechism to Mr. S., which he has never quite been able to master. By next ship peaches (3), tobacco 1/2 cwt. Hams for Mr. S.

The mother of the Virginians and her sons have long since passed away; yet those two little pieces of writing remain like two voices caught in a shell—one formal, one natural; one speaking the careful English of duty, the other the affectionate dialect of home where spelling is less important than warmth. How is it that, under one enclosure and by one packet, one should march so correctly and the other stumble? The likeliest answer is the simplest: the long letter, with its proper names and respectful turns, was rewritten from a rough draft and had passed beneath a correcting eye—perhaps the chaplain’s, perhaps some other friendly hand in Tunbridge. The small note, flung to Mrs. Mountain, went straight from impulse to paper; it was not submitted to the same dominion of “who” and “whom.”

And indeed, who knows what noise may have broken in upon the young man’s pen? A fiddler in the Pantiles scraping a tune that wormed into the blood; a bear led past with pipe and tabor; a jockey under the windows showing off a horse; a pretty milliner turning her head at the very moment a difficult word came. There are days when any of us, however instructed, will spell ill and speak worse, because the world’s sounds and scents—music, hoofbeats, laughter, meat roasting somewhere, chocolate steaming—will not let us be orderly.

If Harry did not care to spell for Mrs. Mountain as for his mother, what is that to any later reader? And if, in the letter to Richmond, he showed a little reticence—set the best face on his doings, softened the pranks and sharpened the studies—he did what young men have always done when writing to those who love them and watch them from afar. There is, where there is not equality, always a certain decorous hypocrisy. A father (or mother) is king in the castle; the household steps backward before the chair; the children laugh at the old jokes; they are hushed as mice when authority nods after dinner. Away from the throne they may jump, skip, dance, tumble, and kick their heels as youth will; in presence, they compose their faces and their grammar.

So let the grandmammas and aunts cry out, if they please, “Oh, it was highly immoral and improper of Mr. Warrington to be writing home demure letters when he was playing merry pranks!” The young will curtsey and answer, “Yes, dear madam, it was very wrong of him,” and will add in their hearts, as the fiddles sing and the cakes and ale come round, that the sun did shine in other spring-times too, and blossoms did bud, and blood did dance—though the elders, with their gruel and slippers, protest that it never did.
