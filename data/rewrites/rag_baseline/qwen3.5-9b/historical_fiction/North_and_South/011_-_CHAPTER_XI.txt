"There is iron in all our blood, and a grain or two perhaps is good; but his, he makes me harshly feel, has got a little too much of steel."

"Margaret!" Mr. Hale called out, returning from the stairs where he had shown his guest below. "I couldn't help watching your face with some anxiety when Mr. Thornton confessed to being a shop-boy. I knew the truth from Mr. Bell, so I anticipated the moment; yet I half expected you to rise and flee the room."

"Oh, Papa! Do you truly think me so foolish?" Margaret retorted, her voice brightening. "I preferred that admission above anything else he said. Every other word revolted me for its hardness, but speaking of himself so simply—stripped of the pretense that usually masks the vulgarity of shop-folk—and with such tender reverence for his mother... it made me stay. Not when he boasted of Milton as if no other place existed, nor when he claimed to despise those who squandered their lives without considering it his duty to lift them up, despite the very training his mother gave him. No, his honesty about being a shop-boy was the most compelling thing he ever said."

"I am surprised at you, Margaret," her mother interjected, smoothing her apron. "You who constantly accused people of being 'shoppy' at Helstone! I don't think, Mr. Hale, you were wise to introduce someone without revealing his background. I was terrified of showing how shocked I was by his words. His father 'dying in miserable circumstances.' Why, it might have been in the workhouse."

"It may have been worse than the workhouse," her husband replied gently. "Mr. Bell told me much of his past before we arrived. He gambled wildly, failed, and then took his own life, unable to bear the disgrace. All his former friends vanished when the truth of his dishonest dealings emerged—those wild, desperate struggles using other people's money to reclaim a modest fortune. No one stepped forward to aid the mother and this boy. There was another child, a girl, too young to work, yet she had to be fed. Friends did not appear immediately, and Mrs. Thornton is certainly not one to wait for tardy kindness. So they fled Milton. I knew he had taken a job in a shop, and that his wages, combined with a small inheritance, sustained them for years. Mr. Bell said they subsisted on water-porridge for a long stretch—how, he could not say. Long after creditors had abandoned hope of repayment following the father's suicide, this young man returned to Milton. He went quietly to each creditor, paying the first installment owed. There was no drama, no assembly of debtors; it was done in silence until every penny was settled. This was aided significantly by one of the creditors, a crusty old man, who took Mr. Thornton in as a partner."

"That truly is fine," Margaret murmured. "What a pity such a noble nature should be tarnished by his position as a Milton manufacturer."

"How tarnished?" her father asked.

"Oh, Papa, by measuring everything against the standard of wealth. When he spoke of mechanical powers, he viewed them solely as new methods to expand trade and accumulate money. And the poor men around him? They were poor because they were vicious—cast aside from his sympathies because they lacked his 'iron nature,' the specific capabilities that allowed him to become rich."

"Not vicious; he never used that word. He called them improvident and self-indulgent."

Meanwhile, Margaret began gathering her mother's sewing materials, preparing for bed. Just as she reached the door, she paused. She felt compelled to offer an acknowledgment that would please her father, yet to be fully truthful, it required admitting a slight irritation. Out it came.

"Papa, I do think Mr. Thornton a very remarkable man; but personally, I don't like him at all."

"And I do!" her father laughed warmly. "Personally, as you call it, and all. I don't elevate him to a hero, or anything of that sort. Good-night, child. Your mother looks dreadingly tired tonight, Margaret."

Margaret had noted her mother's exhausted countenance with growing alarm for some time. Her father's comment sent a dim, heavy fear settling onto her heart. Life in Milton was a stark departure from the perpetual freshness of Helstone, where the air seemed alive with revitalizing principles. Here, the atmosphere felt stagnant, stripped of renewal. Domestic worries pressed upon the women of the household in a new, sordid intensity, raising genuine fears for her mother's health. There were other troubling signs: mysterious consultations between Mrs. Hale and Dixon in the bedroom, followed by Dixon emerging weeping and irritable whenever her mistress suffered. Once, Margaret had found her mother on her knees shortly after Dixon left, whispering prayers for strength to endure severe physical pain. Margaret yearned to restore the intimate bond fractured by her long absence at Aunt Shaw's, striving with gentle touches and soft words to reach the deepest corner of her mother's heart. Yet, though she received affection in abundance, she sensed a secret withheld—one bearing serious implications for her mother's condition. She lay awake long into the night, plotting how to mitigate the toxic influence of their Milton existence. She considered hiring a servant to take Dixon permanently off her hands, ensuring her mother received the constant personal attention she had always known.

For several days, visiting registration offices and encountering improbable souls consumed Margaret's time and thoughts. One afternoon, she encountered Bessy Higgins in the street and stopped to speak.

"Well, Bessy, how are you? Better, I hope, now the wind has changed."

"Better and not better, if you know what that means," Bessy replied, her voice thin.

"Not exactly," Margaret smiled.

"I'm better in that I'm not torn apart by coughing all night, but I'm weary and tired of Milton, longing for the Land of Beulah. And when I realize I'm drifting further away, my heart sinks, and I'm worse."

Margaret turned to walk beside the girl, whose progress was feeble. For a moment, neither spoke. Finally, Margaret whispered, "Bessy, do you wish to die?" She recoiled from the thought herself, clinging to life with the instinctive fervor of youth.

Bessy remained silent for a moment. Then, "If you'd led the life I have, grown as weary of it as I have, and thought at times, 'Maybe it'll last for fifty or sixty years—it does with some,' and felt dizzy and dazed as each of those sixty years spun around me, mocking me with its endless hours and minutes... oh, wench! I tell thee, thou'd be glad enough when the doctor said he feared thou'd never see another winter."

"Why, Bessy, what kind of a life has yours been?"

"Nought worse than many others', I reckon. Only I fretted against it, and they didn't."

"But what was it? You know, I'm a stranger here, so perhaps I'm not quick enough to understand what you mean."

"If you'd come to our house when you promised, I could maybe have told you. But Father says you're just like the rest of them; it's out of sight, out of mind with you."

"I don't know who the rest are; and I've been very busy. To tell the truth, I had forgotten my promise—"

"You offered it; we asked for nothing," Bessy insisted.

"I forgot what I said for the time," Margaret continued calmly. "I would have remembered when I was less busy. May I go with you now?"

Bessy glanced sharply at Margaret's face, searching for sincerity. The hardness in her eyes softened into a wistful longing under Margaret's gentle gaze.

"I have none so many to care for me; if you care, you may come."

They walked on in silence. Turning up a narrow court from a squalid street, Bessy spoke. "You won't be daunted if Father is home and speaks a bit gruffly at first. He took a liking to you, you see, and thought a lot of your coming to see us. Just because he likes you, he was vexed and upset."

"Don't fear, Bessy."

But Nicholas was not home when they entered. A large, unkempt girl, older than Bessy but taller and stronger, was busy at the wash-tub, knocking about the furniture with rough capability, creating such a din that Margaret shrank back in sympathy for poor Bessy, who collapsed onto the nearest chair, utterly spent. Margaret asked the sister for water. While the girl ran to fetch it—clattering fire-irons and tripping over chairs—Margaret loosened Bessy's bonnet strings to ease her labored breathing.

"Do you think such a life as this is worth caring for?" Bessy gasped finally. Margaret said nothing, holding the water to her lips. Bessy drank greedily, then fell back, closing her eyes. Margaret heard her muttering, "They shall hunger no more, neither thirst any more: neither shall the sun light on them, nor any heat."

Margaret leaned forward. "Bessy, don't be impatient with your life, whatever it is—or may have been. Remember who gave it you, and made it what it is!"
