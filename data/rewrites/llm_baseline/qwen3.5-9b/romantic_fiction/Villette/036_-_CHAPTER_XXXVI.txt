Before our friendship could be officially sealed, a third party interjected herself into the agreement. Besides Madame Beck, another force was at work regarding M. Paul and me. We were under the watchful gaze of a sleepless eye: Rome, watching her son jealously through that mystical lattice where I had once knelt, and where M. Emanuel approached month by month—the sliding panel of the confessional.

"Why were you so eager to be friends with M. Paul?" the reader might ask. "Had he not already proven his affection?"

Yes, he had. Yet, I found comfort in hearing him declare it earnestly—that he was my close, true friend. I cherished his modest doubts, his tender deference, that trust longing to find rest and gratitude when taught how to accept it. He had called me "sister." It was appropriate. As long as he confided in me, I was content to be his sister, provided he did not invite me to fill that role for a future wife. Since he was sworn to celibacy, this dilemma seemed unlikely to arise.

That evening's conversation haunted me throughout the night. I longed for dawn, listening anxiously for the bell. Once dressed, I felt prayers and breakfast drag on endlessly until the hour finally arrived for my literature lesson. My desire was to understand this new fraternal bond more deeply: to observe how much humility he would display toward me; to test how much of a "sister" existed within my own feelings; to discover if I possessed a sister's courage and if he retained a brother's frankness.

When he came, life proved its cruel construction: reality rarely matches expectation. All day, he never acknowledged me. His lesson was delivered more quietly, mildly, and gravely than usual. While fatherly to his students, he was not brotherly to me. Before leaving class, I expected a smile, perhaps a word; instead, I received only a hurried, shy nod.

I reasoned this distance was merely accidental, involuntary, and would vanish with patience. It did not vanish; it persisted for days and grew wider. I suppressed my surprise and swallowed the rising tide of other emotions.

How could I blame him for asking, "Dare I rely on you?" Knowing himself, he was entitled to withhold any pledge. Though he had told me to experiment, to tease and try him, the privilege remained nominal and unavailable. Other women might dare; none of my instincts placed me among that brave band. Alone, I was passive; repulsed, I withdrew. Forgotten, my lips refused to speak, my eyes dared not remind him. It seemed I had erred in my calculations, needing time to correct the mistake.

Then came the evening of our scheduled lesson. Usually, he spent a generous hour reviewing our weekly progress and planning ahead. These sessions took place wherever the students and teachers gathered, often in the large second division. When the day pupils were gone and the boarders clustered around the *surveillante*'s platform, it was easy to find a quiet nook.

On this customary evening, hearing the hour strike, I gathered my books, pen, and ink, heading for the large division.

The classroom was empty, bathed in cool, deep shadow. Through the open double doors, the courtyard (*carré*) was filled with light and students. Over the hall and figures, the setting sun cast a blush so vivid and ruddy that the wall colors and varied dress hues seemed fused into one warm glow. The girls worked or studied in a circle, while M. Emanuel stood in the center, speaking cheerfully to a teacher. His dark coat and jet-black hair caught the crimson reflections; his Spanish face, turning momentarily, answered the sun's animated kiss with a matching smile.

I took my seat at a desk. Orange trees and blooming plants basked in the sun's laughter, thirsty after a day of warmth. M. Emanuel loved gardening; tending shrubs soothed his nerves. Now, he attended to the oranges, geraniums, and magnificent cacti, reviving them with the water they desperately needed. Meanwhile, he sustained his precious cigar—the first necessity of his life—with its blue wreaths curling prettily among the flowers. He ignored the pupils and mistresses alike, instead showering endearing words upon a small, nameless spaniel. Virtually his own, the dog was fonder of him than any resident. Delicate, silky, and loving, she trotted at his side, gazing expressively into his face. Whenever he dropped his hat or handkerchief in playful distraction, she crouched beside it like a miniature lion guarding a kingdom's flag.

With many plants to care for, the amateur gardener fetched water from the courtyard well with his own active hands. Time spun on. The great clock ticked. Another hour struck. The courtyard lost its sunset illusion; day was waning. My lesson must be brief tonight, yet the plants were all tended. Was it my turn?

Alas, the garden held more favorites—choice rose bushes, rare flowers. Little Sylvie's joyful bark and whine trailed behind the retreating coat down the alleys. I packed some books; I wouldn't need them all. I sat, thinking, involuntarily fearing the encroaching twilight.

Suddenly, Sylvie emerged again, heralding the returning coat. The watering pot rested by the well, its task complete. How glad I was! Monsieur washed his hands in a small stone basin. There was no time for a lesson now; soon the prayer-bell would ring. Yet, we would meet. A chance would surely offer itself to read the riddle hidden in his eyes.

His ablutions finished, he stood, slowly adjusting his cuffs. He gazed at the young moon, pale against the opal sky, shimmering faintly on the oriel window of Jean Baptiste. Sylvie watched his contemplative mood; its stillness irritated her. She whined and jumped to break the silence. He looked down.

"You exigent little thing," he said. "You must not be forgotten for a single moment."

He paused, lifted her into his arms, and sauntered across the courtyard, stopping within a yard of the window line where I sat. He lingered, fondling the spaniel in his bosom, calling her tender names in a soft voice. Turning on the front steps, he glanced once more at the moon, the grey cathedral, and the distant spires fading into a blue sea of night-mist. He breathed the sweet air of dusk, noting the garden's folded blooms. Suddenly, he looked around. A keen beam from his eye swept the white façade of the classrooms, tracing the long row of windows. I think he bowed; if he did, I had no time to return the courtesy. In an instant, he was gone; the moonlit threshold lay pale and shadowless before the closed door.

Gathering everything from my desk, I carried the unused pile back to the third class. The prayer-bell rang, and I obeyed.

The next day would not bring him to Rue Fossette, as it was dedicated to his college duties. I managed my teaching and endured the interim hours until evening approached, arming myself for its heavy ennui. Whether staying with roommates or sitting alone was worse, I hadn't decided; naturally, I chose solitude. If there was any hope of comfort in this house, it could only be harbored under the lid of my desk, nesting between book pages, gilding a pencil tip, or tinting the ink in the glass. With a heavy heart, I opened the lid.

Familiar books, bound in known covers, were removed and returned, hopeless. They held no charm, offered no comfort. Was this something new? A pamphlet in lilac paper. I had not seen it before; I had rearranged my desk this very afternoon. It must have appeared within the last hour, perhaps while we dined.

I opened it. What was it? Would it speak to me?

It was neither tale nor poem, essay nor history. It sang, related, nor discussed. It was a theological work, preaching and persuading. I lent it my ear willingly; though small, it possessed a spell that bound my attention instantly. It preached Romanism, urging conversion. The voice of this sly little book was honeyed, its accents thick with unction and balm. No thunder roared from Rome here, no blast of displeasure. The Protestant was to become Papist not out of fear of hell, but for the comfort, indulgence, and tenderness the Holy Church offered. Far be it from her to threaten or coerce; her wish was simply to guide and win. Did she persecute? Oh, dear no! Not on any account!

This meek volume was not for the hardened or worldly, nor strong meat for the strong. It was milk for babes: the gentle effluence of a mother's love for her tenderest children, intended solely for those whose minds must be reached through the heart. Its appeal was not intellectual; it sought to win the affectionate through their affections, the sympathetic through their sympathies. Saint Vincent de Paul, gathering his orphans, never spoke sweeter.

One powerful inducement to apostasy was highlighted: the Catholic who lost dear friends could enjoy the unspeakable solace of praying them out of purgatory. The writer did not mention the firmer peace of those whose belief dispensed with purgatory entirely, but I thought of this and, on the whole, preferred the latter doctrine as more consoling.
