Rosa's return to consciousness was instantaneous, leaving no gap for oblivion to hide the horror of the encounter. The image of Jasper's face, twisted in that terrible intensity, burned behind her eyelids as if the shock had merely paused the scene rather than ended it. Panic seized her, a cold, paralyzing dread that screamed a single command: *Fly.* She must escape this man before he could strike again.

But flight requires a destination, and her mind offered none. She had confided in no living soul save Helena, and the mere act of seeking Helena's counsel felt like handing Jasper the key to her destruction. His threats echoed in her mind—he possessed both the power and the will to unleash irreparable mischief upon Helena's brother. Every hesitation, every wrong turn, could set his malice loose.

For six months, Rosa's mind had been a storm-tossed sea, haunted by a suspicion she dared not name. Jasper's obsession with his nephew, his relentless investigation into the boy's death, had created a vacuum where no one suspected foul play. She had questioned her own wickedness, wondering if her revulsion stemmed from a guilty conscience or a baseless instinct. She had tried to rationalize his motives, ashamed to admit that the only reason he might kill was to possess *her*. The thought was a crime in itself, a vanity so petty it bordered on the absurd.

Yet, as she replayed his words by the sun-dial, the logic crumbled. He insisted on treating the disappearance as murder, aligning with his public persona since finding the watch and shirt-pin. If he feared the truth, why not encourage the idea of a voluntary vanishing? He had spoken of sweeping "even him" away, of laying his six months of labor at her feet as a sacrifice of fidelity to a dead boy. Was this performance, or was it the raw, desperate truth of a man consumed by grief and rage? To a mind untrained in the dark architecture of criminal intellect, the evidence pointed only to one terrifying conclusion: he was a monster, and she must flee.

She had been Helena's shield, assuring her of Neville's innocence while hiding her own terror. Now, the weight of that silence pressed down. She resolved to seek her guardian, Miss Twinkleton, immediately. The memory of the convent's walls failing to keep his "ghostly following" at bay drove her to action. As she dressed, the sight of the sun-dial in the garden turned her blood to ice; it seemed charged with his very essence, a focal point of his power.

She scribbled a frantic note to Miss Twinkleton, claiming urgent business and asking for no alarm, then stuffed a few trivial items into a small bag. Leaving the note in plain sight, she slipped out, closing the gate softly behind her.

It was the first time Rosa had traversed Cloisterham High Street alone. Knowing its winding paths, she hurried to the corner where the omnibus waited. Just as the vehicle pulled away, she hailed it.

"Stop and take me, if you please, Joe. I am obliged to go to London."

Within minutes, she was on her way to the railway station, protected by Joe. He waited for her arrival, carrying her small bag with the exaggerated care of a porter handling a heavy trunk, ensuring she remained seated until the train arrived.

"Can you go round when you get back, and tell Miss Twinkleton that you saw me safely off, Joe?"

"It shall be done, Miss."

"With my love, please, Joe."

"Yes, Miss—and I wouldn't mind having it myself!" Joe thought, though he kept the sentiment silent.

As the train whirled away toward London, Rosa's fear momentarily subsided, replaced by the indignation that Jasper's declaration had stained her honor. She believed only the honest and true could cleanse her. But as the city loomed larger and the darkness deepened, doubt crept in. Was this rashness? How would Mr. Grewgious react? What if he was absent? Alone in a strange, crowded metropolis, her resolve wavered. A multitude of anxious questions swirled in her mind, growing louder as the train rattled over the rooftops and descended into the gritty, gas-lit streets below.

"Hiram Grewgious, Esquire, Staple Inn, London." That was her destination. She hired a cab, navigating through desolate, cobblestoned thoroughfares where crowds gathered for air, their footsteps shuffling on the hot stones. Music drifted from somewhere—a barrel organ, a drum—but it offered no solace, only echoing off brick walls and stirring up dust. The flat wind instruments seemed to weep for the countryside.

The cab halted before a fast-closed gateway, guarded by a watchman terrified of burglars. Rosa paid and knocked timidly.

"Does Mr. Grewgious live here?"

"He lives there, Miss," the watchman indicated, pointing deeper into the shadows.

Rosa followed, ascending the stairs until she reached P. J. T.'s doorstep, wondering where the street door had vanished. Guided by a painted sign, she found Mr. Grewgious's door. It opened easily. Inside, in the twilight of the room, he sat by an open window, a shaded lamp casting a dim glow.

Rosa approached, and he whispered, "Good Heaven!"

She threw herself upon his neck, sobbing. He embraced her, his voice trembling with relief.

"My child, my child! I thought you were your mother!—But what, what, what," he soothed, "has happened? My dear, what has brought you here? Who has brought you here?"

"No one. I came alone."

"Lord bless me!" he exclaimed. "Came alone! Why didn't you write to me to come and fetch you?"

"I had no time. I took a sudden resolution. Poor, poor Eddy!"

"Ah, poor fellow, poor fellow!"

"His uncle has made love to me. I cannot bear it," Rosa burst out, stamping her foot in horror. "I shudder with terror of him, and I have come to you to protect me and all of us from him, if you will?"

"I will!" Mr. Grewgious cried, his energy surging with sudden, fierce determination. "Damn him!

'Confound his politics!
Frustrate his knavish tricks!
On Thee his hopes to fix?
Damn him again!'"

After this explosive outburst, Mr. Grewgious paced the room, torn between loyal fervor and combative rage. Wiping his face, he suddenly calmed.

"I beg your pardon, my dear, but you will be glad to know I feel better. Tell me no more just now, or I might do it again. You must be refreshed and cheered. What did you take last? Was it breakfast, lunch, dinner, tea, or supper? And what will you take next? Shall it be breakfast, lunch, dinner, tea, or supper?"

With chivalrous tenderness, he knelt before her to remove her hat and untangle her hair, a gesture that belied his usual solitary demeanor.

"Your rest too must be provided for," he continued. "You shall have the prettiest chamber in Furnival's. Your toilet must be attended to, and you shall have everything an unlimited head chambermaid can procure." He eyed her tiny bag. "Is that a bag? And is it your property, my dear?"

"Yes, sir. I brought it with me."

"It is not an extensive bag," he admitted candidly, "though admirably calculated to contain a day's provision for a canary-bird. Perhaps you brought a canary-bird?"

Rosa smiled and shook her head.

"If you had, he should have been made welcome," Mr. Grewgious said, "and I think he would have been pleased to be hung upon a nail outside and pit himself against our Staple sparrows; whose execution must be admitted to be not quite equal to their intention. Which is the case with so many of us! You didn't say what meal, my dear. Have a nice jumble of all meals."

Rosa declined, offering only tea. Mr. Grewgious scurried in and out, ordering marmalade, eggs, watercress, salted fish, and frizzled ham. Soon, the board was spread.

"Lord bless my soul," he cried, placing the lamp on the table and sitting opposite Rosa, "what a new sensation for a poor old Angular bachelor, to be sure!"

Rosa's eyebrows arched in curiosity.

"The sensation of having a sweet young presence in the place, that whitewashes it, paints it, papers it, decorates it with gilding, and makes it Glorious!" Mr. Grewgious sighed mournfully.

Touched by his emotion, Rosa touched his hand with hers.

"Thank you, my dear," he said. "Ahem! Let's talk!"

"Do you always live here, sir?" Rosa asked.

"Yes, my dear."

"And always alone?"

"Always alone; except that I have daily company in a gentleman by the name of Bazzard, my clerk."

"He doesn't live here?"

"No, he goes his way, after office hours. In fact, he is off duty here, altogether, just at present; and a firm down-stairs, with which I have business relations, lend me a substitute. But it would be extremely difficult to replace Mr. Bazzard."

"He must be very fond of you," Rosa observed.

"He bears up against it with commendable fortitude if he is," Mr. Grewgious replied, considering the matter. "But I doubt if he is. Not particularly so. You see, he is discontented, poor fellow."

"Why isn't he contented?"

"Misplaced," Mr. Grewgious said mysteriously.

"So misplaced," he elaborated, "that I feel constantly apologetic towards him. And he feels (though he doesn't mention it) that I have reason to be."

Suddenly, Mr. Grewgious jerked himself awake from the mystery.

"Let's talk. We were speaking of Mr. Bazzard. It's a secret, and moreover it is Mr. Bazzard's secret; but the sweet presence at my table makes me so unusually expansive, that I feel I must impart it in inviolable confidence. What do you think Mr. Bazzard has done?"

"Oh dear!" Rosa cried, leaning closer, her mind drifting back to Jasper. "Nothing dreadful, I hope?"

"He has written a play," Mr. Grewgious whispered solemnly. "A tragedy."

Rosa seemed relieved.

"And nobody," Mr. Grewgious pursued, "will hear, on any account whatever, of bringing it out."

Rosa nodded reflectively.

"Now, you know," Mr. Grewgious said, "I couldn't write a play."

"Not a bad one, sir?" Rosa asked innocently.

"No. If I was under sentence of decapitation, and was about to be instantly decapitated, and an express arrived with a pardon for the condemned convict Grewgious if he wrote a play, I should be under the necessity of resuming the block, and begging the executioner to proceed to extremities,—meaning," he said, stroking his chin, "the singular number, and this extremity."

Rosa pondered the hypothetical.

"Consequently," Mr. Grewgious said, "Mr. Bazzard would have a sense of my inferiority to himself under any circumstances; but when I am his master, you know, the case is greatly aggravated."

He shook his head seriously.

"How came you to be his master, sir?" Rosa asked.

"A question that naturally follows," Mr. Grewgious said. "Mr. Bazzard's father, being a Norfolk farmer, would have furiously laid about him with a flail, a pitch-fork, and every agricultural implement available for assaulting purposes, on the slightest hint of his son's having written a play. So the son, bringing to me the father's rent (which I receive), imparted his secret, and pointed out that he was determined to pursue his genius, and that it would put him in peril of starvation, and that he was not formed for it."

"For pursuing his genius, sir?"

"No, my dear," Mr. Grewgious corrected, "for starvation. It was impossible to deny the position, that Mr. Bazzard was not formed to be starved, and Mr. Bazzard then pointed out that it was desirable that I should stand between him and a fate so perfectly unsuited to his formation. In that way Mr. Bazzard became my clerk, and he feels it very much."

"I am glad he is grateful," Rosa said.

"I didn't quite mean that, my dear. I mean, that he feels the degradation. There are some other geniuses that Mr. Bazzard has become acquainted with, who have also written tragedies, which likewise nobody will on any account whatever hear of bringing out, and these choice spirits dedicate their plays to one another in a highly panegyrical manner. Mr. Bazzard has been the subject of one of these dedications. Now, you know, I never had a play dedicated to _me_!"

Rosa looked at him as if she wished to bestow a thousand dedications upon him.

"Which again, naturally, rubs against the grain of Mr. Bazzard," Mr. Grewgious said. "He is very short with me sometimes, and then I feel that he is meditating, 'This blockhead is my master! A fellow who couldn't write a tragedy on pain of death, and who will never have one dedicated to him with the most complimentary congratulations on the high position he has taken in the eyes of posterity!' Very trying, very trying. However, in giving him directions, I reflect beforehand: 'Perhaps he may not like this,' or 'He might take it ill if I asked that;' and so we get on very well. Indeed, better than I could have expected."
