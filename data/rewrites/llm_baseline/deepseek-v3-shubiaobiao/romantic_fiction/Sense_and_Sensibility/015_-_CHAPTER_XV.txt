Mrs. Dashwood and her two eldest daughters paid their visit to Lady Middleton the following day, but Marianne excused herself, claiming some small task demanded her attention. Her mother, suspecting a private understanding with Willoughby—that he would call upon her in their absence—was content to leave her behind.

Upon their return, the sight of Willoughby’s carriage at the cottage seemed to confirm Mrs. Dashwood’s expectations. Yet the scene that greeted them inside was far from what she had imagined.

No sooner had they crossed the threshold than Marianne rushed past them, her face hidden behind a handkerchief, her distress unmistakable. Without a word, she fled upstairs, leaving her mother and sisters frozen in surprise.

Willoughby stood by the hearth, his back turned, but at their entrance, he faced them. His expression mirrored Marianne’s anguish.

"What has happened?" Mrs. Dashwood cried. "Is she ill?"

"I hope not," he replied, forcing a smile that did not reach his eyes. "It is I who should claim illness—I am suffering a bitter disappointment."

"Disappointment?"

"Yes. I must break my engagement with you. Mrs. Smith, in the manner of the wealthy toward their dependents, has dispatched me to London on urgent business. My orders came this morning, and I have already bid farewell to Allenham. Now, I come to take my leave of you."

"To London? So soon?"

"Within the hour."

"How unfortunate! But surely Mrs. Smith’s affairs will not keep you long?"

He colored. "You are kind, but I do not expect to return to Devonshire soon. My visits to her are seldom repeated within the year."

"And is hers the only house open to you? Must you wait for an invitation here?"

His flush deepened, his gaze fixed on the floor. "You are too generous."

Mrs. Dashwood exchanged a glance with Elinor, both bewildered. Silence settled heavily until Willoughby broke it with a strained smile.

"It is folly to prolong this. I can no longer bear the pain of remaining where I am no longer at liberty to stay."

With that, he bid them a hurried farewell and was gone. They watched his carriage vanish down the lane, the air thick with unspoken questions.

Mrs. Dashwood, too overcome for speech, withdrew to her room, leaving Elinor to grapple with her own unease. Willoughby’s abrupt departure, his forced cheer, his refusal of her mother’s invitation—none of it aligned with the man they knew. Had his affection for Marianne wavered? Or had some quarrel driven them apart? Marianne’s grief suggested a rupture, yet Elinor could scarcely believe it.

When Mrs. Dashwood returned, her eyes bore traces of tears, but her manner was composed.

"Our dear Willoughby is miles away by now," she said, resuming her needlework. "And with what sorrow he must travel!"

"It is all so sudden," Elinor murmured. "Last night he was all warmth and affection. Now he leaves without promise of return. There must be more to it than he confessed."

"His reluctance was not from want of inclination, Elinor—but from impossibility. I have turned it over in my mind, and the answer is clear. Mrs. Smith suspects his attachment to Marianne and disapproves. She has contrived to separate them, sending him away under false pretenses. He, dependent on her favor, dares not defy her—and so he must go."

Elinor frowned. "It is plausible, yet why keep such a secret from us?"

"Secrecy? Have they not been open in their affection? His looks, his attentions—have they not spoken plainly enough?"

"But words, Mother. Why no declaration?"

Mrs. Dashwood sighed. "Must you doubt where there is no cause? Willoughby is honorable. If circumstances force discretion, must we fault him? Consider his position—bound to please Mrs. Smith, yet devoted to Marianne. His distress today speaks volumes."

Elinor fell silent, weighing her mother’s words. Perhaps she was too quick to distrust. Yet the absence of a clear understanding gnawed at her.

Their discussion was cut short by Margaret’s arrival, leaving Elinor to reflect. She wished to believe the best—for Marianne’s sake, and for Willoughby’s.

Marianne did not appear until dinner, her eyes swollen, her manner brittle. She ate nothing, spoke not at all, and when her mother pressed her hand in silent sympathy, she dissolved into tears and fled once more.

The evening passed in heavy quiet. Any mention of Willoughby undid her entirely. Her family treaded carefully, yet every topic seemed to circle back to him, deepening her sorrow.

Love, it seemed, was as much a trial as a joy—and Willoughby’s absence had left a wound too fresh to bear.
