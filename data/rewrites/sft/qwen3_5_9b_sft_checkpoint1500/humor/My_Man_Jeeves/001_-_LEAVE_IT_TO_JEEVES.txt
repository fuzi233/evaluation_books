"Leave it to Jeeves"—those four words have become the anthem of my existence. Jeeves, my man, you know, is truly a marvel. He possesses an omniscience that borders on the supernatural. Picture those weary souls perched on the marble parapets at Pennsylvania Station, the "Inquiries" booth. You approach one, desperate for a train to "Melonsquashville, Tennessee," and they reply, unblinking and unbothered, "Two forty-three, Track Ten, change at San Francisco." And somehow, miraculously, they are always correct. Jeeves operates on the same frequency. Absolute, terrifying knowledge.

Take the incident with the tailor's shop. I encountered Monty Byng in Bond Street, sporting a grey-check suit that looked like it belonged on a king. Naturally, I felt compelled to acquire one identical to it. Within the hour, I was seated in the workshop, fabric swirling around me. That evening, I summoned Jeeves.

"I'm commissioning a check suit like Mr. Byng's," I announced.

"Injudicious, sir," Jeeves stated, his tone leaving no room for argument. "It will not become you."

"What absolute rot!" I cried. "It is the soundest garment I have ever laid eyes upon!"

"Unsuited for you, sir."

And there, as the dust settles, I learned a hard truth: the suit returned, I donned it, and stared into the mirror until I nearly fainted. Jeeves was right. While Monty looked dashing, I resembled a confused music-hall clown attempting to con a pigeon. Yet, the mystery of fashion remains unsolved, and Jeeves holds the key.

His expertise extends beyond aesthetics, however. Consider the horse race at the Lincolnshire. I possessed a tip so hot it could singe wool, yet I offered it to Jeeves anyway, wishing to do him a favor. "Bet on Wonderchild," I urged.

He shook his head gently. "I'd rather not, sir."

"But it's the straight goods! I intend to put my shirt on that horse!"

"The animal is not intended to win, sir. Second place is what the stable desires."

I laughed it off as nonsense, confident in my superior intellect. And yet, Wonderchild galloped to the lead, panting on the wire, only to be nudged aside by a rival named Banana Fritter. I returned home, trembling with regret, and rang the bell.

"After this," I declared, "no further steps shall I take without your counsel. From now on, consider yourself the brains of the operation."

"And I endeavor to satisfy, sir," he replied, bowing slightly.

And he has! My brain, it seems, was crafted merely for decoration, like a gilded bust on a mantelpiece, whereas Jeeves is the engine room. Give me five minutes with him, and I am prepared to offer advice on matters ranging from global economics to the proper folding of a handkerchief.

Thus, when Bruce Corcoran—the fellow with the perpetually bulging forehead—trudged into my apartment, trailed by a fluttering maiden, I acted instantly. "Leave it to Jeeves," I said, ringing the bell with practiced efficiency.

I first met Corky in New York, during a brief exile arranged by my Aunt Agatha to prevent her cousin Gus from marrying a vaudeville star. I sent Jeeves to secure a flat, and life became tolerable amidst the chaos of the Big Apple. Corky, a self-proclaimed portrait painter with zero commissions, survived by sketching furniture for ads and biting the ear of his wealthy uncle, Alexander Worple. Mr. Worple, a jute magnate with an obsession for ornithology, viewed Corky as a perpetual idiot destined for the jute trade. Corky's only salvation was his uncle's hobby; by listening to endless lectures on American birds, he could coax a meager allowance.

When Corky arrived, breathless and dragging a girl named Muriel Singer, the problem was obvious. "How about your uncle?" I asked immediately.

"We're terrified," Muriel whispered, her wide, innocent eyes scanning me with a gaze that made me feel simultaneously heroic and vulnerable. "Please, suggest a way to break it to him."

Muriel was the kind of girl who makes you want to be a chivalrous knight-errant, or perhaps just a very gentle giant. "I don't see why your uncle shouldn't adore her," I insisted.

"He's pig-headed," Corky groaned. "Principle alone would drive him to kick. He'd raise Cain if I married without his blessing."

I consulted Jeeves, boiling the crisis down to essentials. "Arrange a meeting where Mr. Worple thinks Muriel is a stranger, yet falls deeply in love. Understood?"

"A perfect plan, sir," Jeeves nodded. "However, it involves a financial outlay."

"Pippin!" I translated to Corky. "Carry on, Jeeves."

Jeeves proposed utilizing Mr. Worple's passion for birds. A book titled *The Children's Book of American Birds*, dedicated to Mr. Worple, could be published. Muriel would pen the introduction, expressing her desire to meet the man who inspired her.

"But I can't write a book!" Muriel protested.

"Muriel's talents lie in the dramatic arts," Corky interjected quickly. "We are anxious regarding her stage career."

Jeeves solved this instantly. An impoverished writer, Sam Patterson, would ghostwrite the volume for a modest fee, allowing Muriel to claim authorship. "A brilliant solution, sir," I murmured, feeling like a conductor watching a flawless symphony unfold.

The book arrived, a glossy red tome adorned with a proud rooster. Inside, Muriel's glowing tribute praised Mr. Worple's genius on the yellow-billed cuckoo. It was a masterstroke. A few days later, Corky returned, triumphant. Mr. Worple had written a letter dripping with affection, inviting Muriel to call.

Time passed, and I returned to the city. I found Muriel at a quiet restaurant, waiting alone. "Corky around?" I asked.

"No," she said, her voice carrying a strange, hollow resonance. "I've left the stage."

"Why, of course!" I realized, the pieces clicking together. "You're married!"

"Yes," she confirmed, introducing me to her husband. It was none other than Alexander Worple. The man who had once treated Corky like a nuisance now held his hand warmly. "I wish you would try to make my nephew quit this playing at painting," Alexander boomed, pointing a finger at Corky. "But I have an idea he is steadying down."

I fled the restaurant, needing air, and returned to my apartment. Jeeves appeared with a tray. "Stiff brandy and soda first," I ordered, feeling the weight of the coming storm. "You'll need it."

"You may have it later, sir," Jeeves replied calmly.

I recounted the marriage. "She's slid, Jeeves. Into the arms of the beast."

"That was a development to be feared, sir."

"I thought you might have warned us!"

"It crossed my mind, sir."

Months later, tragedy struck. I read in the papers that Mrs. Alexander Worple had presented her husband with a son and heir. My heart sank. I avoided Corky, fearing the pain, but eventually, guilt overwhelmed me. I drove to Washington Square, bursting into Corky's studio to find him hunched over an easel, painting a stern, middle-aged woman holding a baby.

"Oh, Bertie!" Corky gasped. "Don't go. We're just finishing."

I backed out, bewildered. Later, Corky called. "I've finished the portrait... but it doesn't look right. My uncle is coming to inspect it."

"Think he'll cut up rough?"

"He may."

"If you think I'm facing that uncle without Jeeves, you're mad!" I declared. "Bring him!"

At the studio, Corky hovered protectively near the canvas. "Tell me honestly, how does it strike you?"

I squinted. "Ugly?" I hedged. "No, actually... it's lovely."

"Lovely?" Corky groaned, running his hands through his hair. "I've captured the soul! He looks positively dissipated!"

"He has an inebriated air, sir," Jeeves observed gravely.

The door burst open, and Mr. Worple entered. For three seconds, joy reigned. Then, horror. He pointed a shaking finger at the canvas. "Is this a practical joke?"

"It's not!" I stammered.

"This extract from a comic colored supplement!" Worple roared, turning on Corky like a tiger. "Unless you report to my office on Monday to work your way up from the bottom, not another cent! Boosh!"

The door slammed. Silence fell over the room. Corky stared at the painting, defeated. "What am I to do?"

"If I might make a suggestion, sir..." Jeeves stepped forward. "Recall Mr. Digby Thistleton? After his depilatory failed, he marketed it as 'Hair-o,' guaranteed results, and became a lord. There is always a way."
