"Learn to win a lady's faith
Nobly, as the thing is high;
Bravely, as for life and death—
With a loyal gravity.

Lead her from the festive boards,
Point her to the starry skies,
Guard her, by your truthful words,
Pure from courtship's flatteries."

Margaret's thoughts had drifted to Henry Lennox only moments prior, recalling his inquiry regarding her domestic duties. It was a sentiment akin to the French proverb, "parler du soleil et l'on en voit les rayons"—to speak of the sun and to see its rays. A genuine brightness illuminated Margaret's face as she set down her embroidery board and stepped forward to greet him. "Tell Mamma, Sarah," she said, turning to her companion. "Mamma and I have so many questions for you concerning Edith; I am deeply grateful for your visit."

"Did I not promise that I would?" he replied, his voice dropping to a softer register than before.

"But I heard of you only from the distant Highlands," Margaret noted, surprised. "I never imagined Hampshire could reach you."

"Oh!" he dismissed the notion lightly. "Our young couple were engaging in such reckless pranks—climbing mountains, sailing on lakes—that I felt compelled to act as a Mentor. They were entirely beyond my uncle's supervision, keeping the old gentleman in a state of panic for sixteen hours a day. Upon realizing they could not be trusted alone, I deemed it my duty to remain until I had seen them safely embarked at Plymouth."

"You have been to Plymouth? Edith never mentioned that. She has been writing so hurriedly lately. Did they actually sail on Tuesday?"

"They certainly did, relieving me of considerable responsibility. Edith entrusted me with various messages for you. I believe I have a small note somewhere; yes, here it is."

"Oh! thank you," Margaret exclaimed. Yet, half-wishing to read the note privately, she feigned a need to inform her mother again—perhaps Sarah had made a mistake—that Mr. Lennox had arrived.

Once Margaret had withdrawn, Henry began his characteristic scrutiny of the room. The drawing-room appeared at its finest in the morning light. The central window of the bow stood open, allowing clusters of roses and scarlet honeysuckle to spill onto the sill; the lawn below was vibrant with verbenas and geraniums. However, the brilliance of the outdoors rendered the interior colors somewhat pale by comparison. The carpet showed signs of age, the chintz had been washed frequently, and the entire apartment seemed smaller and shabbier than he anticipated, serving as a humble backdrop for Margaret, who stood before him with a queenly grace. He picked up a book from the table: *The Paradise of Dante*, bound in white vellum and gold. Beside it lay a dictionary and a page of words copied in Margaret's handwriting. The list was dull, yet he found himself lingering over it with a sigh before setting it down.

"The living is evidently as modest as she described," he murmured. "It seems strange, considering the Beresfords are of a respectable family."

Meanwhile, Margaret had located her mother. It was one of Mrs. Hale's fitful days, where every circumstance felt like a hardship. Mr. Lennox's arrival took on a burdensome shape, though she secretly felt complimented that he deemed it worthy of a call.

"It is most unfortunate!" Mrs. Hale fretted. "We are dining early today, serving only cold meat so the servants may finish ironing. Yet, of course, we must invite him—Edith's brother-in-law and all. And your father is in such low spirits this morning about something—I know not what. I entered the study just now, and he had his face buried in his hands, covering it completely. I told him I was certain the Helstone air agreed with neither him nor me, and he suddenly lifted his head, begging me not to speak another word against Helstone. He claimed he could not bear it; if there is one place he loves on earth, it is Helstone. Nevertheless, I am convinced it is the damp, relaxing atmosphere."

Margaret felt as though a thin, cold cloud had descended between her and the sunlight. She had listened patiently, hoping her mother's venting might bring relief, but now it was time to redirect her attention to Mr. Lennox.

"Papa adores Mr. Lennox; they bonded famously at the wedding breakfast. I daresay his presence will lift Papa's spirits. And do not worry about the dinner, dear Mamma. Cold meat suits a lunch perfectly, which is precisely how Mr. Lennox will view a two o'clock meal."

"But what shall we do with him until then? It is only half-past ten."

"I shall ask him to go sketching with me. I know he draws, and that will remove him from your path, Mamma. Only do come in now; he will find it peculiar if you do not."

Mrs. Hale removed her black silk apron and smoothed her face. She appeared a lovely, ladylike woman as she greeted Mr. Lennox with the cordiality due to a near relative. He evidently expected to spend the day and accepted the invitation with a glad readiness that made Mrs. Hale wish she could improve the quality of the cold beef. He was pleased with everything, delighted by Margaret's suggestion of a sketching excursion, and eager to avoid disturbing Mr. Hale, anticipating their meeting at dinner. Margaret produced her drawing materials for his selection. After the paper and brushes were chosen, the two departed in high spirits.

"Now, please, stop here for a moment," Margaret instructed. "These are the cottages that haunted me during the rainy fortnight, reproaching me for failing to sketch them."

"Before they collapsed and vanished. Truly, if they are to be sketched—and they are quite picturesque—we should not delay until next year. But where shall we sit?"

"Oh! You might have come straight from chambers in the Temple instead of spending two months in the Highlands! Look at this magnificent trunk of a tree, left by the woodcutters in the perfect spot for the light. I will drape my plaid over it, creating a regular forest throne."

"With your feet in that puddle for a regal footstool! Stay, I will move, and then you can approach closer. Who resides in these cottages?"

"They were constructed by squatters fifty or sixty years ago. One is uninhabited; the foresters intend to demolish it once the other tenant, poor old fellow, passes away! Look—there he is. I must speak to him; he is so deaf you will overhear all our secrets."

The old man stood bareheaded in the sun, leaning on his stick before his cottage. His stiff features relaxed into a slow smile as Margaret approached. Mr. Lennox quickly incorporated the two figures into his sketch, finishing the landscape with a subordinate reference to them—as Margaret observed later, when they packed their water and scraps of paper to compare their work. She laughed and blushed; Mr. Lennox watched her countenance intently.

"Now, I call that treacherous," she said. "I little thought you were making old Isaac and me into subjects when you asked me to inquire about the cottages' history."

"It was irresistible. You cannot imagine the strength of the temptation. I hardly dare tell you how much I shall enjoy this sketch."

He was uncertain whether she heard this last remark before she went to the brook to wash her palette. She returned flushed, yet appearing perfectly innocent and unconscious. He was glad, for the words had slipped from him unawares—a rare occurrence for a man like Henry Lennox, who premeditated his actions with such precision.

The atmosphere of home brightened upon their return. The clouds on her mother's brow had lifted under the auspicious influence of a brace of carp, conveniently presented by a neighbor. Mr. Hale had returned from his morning rounds and awaited his visitor just outside the wicket gate leading to the garden. He looked like a complete gentleman in his slightly threadbare coat and well-worn hat. Margaret felt a fresh, tender pride in her father, always struck by how favorably he impressed strangers; yet her keen eye detected traces of unusual disturbance on his face, a turmoil that was merely set aside, not erased.

Mr. Hale requested to examine their sketches.

"I believe you have made the tints on the thatch too dark, have you not?" he remarked, returning Margaret's drawing and extending his hand for Mr. Lennox's, which was withheld for a fleeting moment.

"No, Papa! I do not think so. The house-leek and stone-crop have grown significantly darker in the rain. Does it not look right, Papa?" she asked, peering over his shoulder as he inspected the figures in Mr. Lennox's drawing.

"Yes, very accurate. Your posture and carriage are capital. And it captures poor old Isaac's stiff way of stooping his long, rheumatic back perfectly. What hangs from the branch of the tree? Not a bird's nest, surely."

"Oh, no! That is my bonnet. I can never draw with it on; it makes my head too hot. I wonder if I could manage figures. There are so many people here whom I would like to sketch."

"I should say that a likeness you earnestly desire to capture you would always succeed in," Mr. Lennox replied. "I have great faith in the power of will. I think I have succeeded quite well with yours." Mr. Hale had already preceded them inside, while Margaret lingered to pluck some roses to adorn her morning gown for dinner.

"A regular London girl would understand the implied meaning of that speech," Mr. Lennox thought. "She would be adept at detecting the *arrière-pensée* in every compliment a young man offers. But I do not believe Margaret—" Suddenly, he exclaimed, "Let me help you"; and he gathered some velvety cramoisy roses beyond her reach. Dividing the spoils, he placed two in his buttonhole and sent her inside, pleased and happy, to arrange her flowers.

The dinner conversation flowed quietly and agreeably. Questions were exchanged freely regarding Mrs. Shaw's movements in Italy, and amidst the interest of the topics discussed, the unpretending simplicity of the parsonage life—especially in Margaret's company—caused Mr. Lennox to forget the initial disappointment he felt upon learning the modesty of her father's living.

"Margaret, my child, you might have gathered some pears for our dessert," Mr. Hale suggested as a bottle of wine was uncorked.

Mrs. Hale moved hurriedly. Desserts seemed impromptu and unusual at the parsonage, whereas, had Mr. Hale glanced behind him, he would have seen biscuits and marmalade arranged formally on the sideboard. The idea of pears had seized Mr. Hale's imagination and could not be dispelled.

"There are a few brown *beurrés* against the south wall worth all foreign fruits and preserves. Run, Margaret, and gather some."

"I propose we adjourn to the garden and eat them there," Mr. Lennox suggested. "Nothing is as delicious as biting into crisp, juicy fruit, warm and scented by the sun. The only drawback is that the wasps are impudent enough to dispute it with one, even at the very climax of enjoyment."

He rose to follow Margaret, who had vanished through the window, awaiting Mrs. Hale's permission. She would have preferred to conclude the dinner with proper ceremony, especially since she and Dixon had retrieved the finger-glasses from the store-room to appear as correct as General Shaw's widow's sister; but as Mr. Hale rose immediately and prepared to accompany his guest, she could only submit.

"I shall arm myself with a knife," Mr. Hale declared. "The days of eating fruit so primitively as you describe are over with me. I must pare and quarter it before I can enjoy it."

Margaret fashioned a plate for the pears using a beet-root leaf, which highlighted their brown-gold hue beautifully. Mr. Lennox looked more at her than at the fruit; her father, inclined to select the very essence of the moment he had stolen from his anxieties, chose the ripest fruit daintily and sat on the garden bench to savor it at leisure. Margaret and Mr. Lennox strolled along the terrace under the south wall, where bees still hummed industriously in their hives.

"What a perfect life you seem to lead here!" Mr. Lennox exclaimed. "I have always felt somewhat contemptuously toward poets with their wishes for 'a cot beside a hill,' but now I fear I have been nothing better than a Cockney. Just now, I feel as if twenty years of hard legal study would be amply rewarded by one year of such an exquisite, serene existence as this—such skies!" He looked upward. "Such crimson and amber foliage, so perfectly motionless as that!" He pointed to the great forest trees enclosing the garden like a nest.

"You must remember that our skies are not always this deep blue. We have rain, and our leaves fall and become sodden. Though I think Helstone is about as perfect a place as any in the world. Recall how you rather scorned my description of it one evening in Harley Street: 'a village in a tale.'"

"Scorned, Margaret? That is rather a harsh word."

"Perhaps it is. Only I know I would have liked to discuss what filled my mind at the time, and you... what shall I call it then?—spoke disrespectfully of Helstone as a mere village in a tale."

"I will never do so again," he said warmly. They turned the corner of the walk.

"I could almost wish, Margaret..." He stopped, hesitating. It was so unusual for the fluent lawyer to hesitate that Margaret looked up at him with questioning wonder. But in an instant, from some internal cause she could not identify, she wished herself back with her mother, her father, anywhere away from him, certain he was about to say something she would not know how to answer. In another moment, the strong pride within her conquered her sudden agitation, which she hoped he had not perceived.
