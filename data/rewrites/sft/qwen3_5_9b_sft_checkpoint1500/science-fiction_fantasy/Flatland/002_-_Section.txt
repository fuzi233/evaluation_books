In Flatland, where all existence unfolds upon a single plane, the concept of "up" or "down" is as meaningless as it would be to try to describe the height of a circle. We live in a universe of two dimensions, bound strictly by length and breadth. To us, the third dimension—the depth so vital to the inhabitants of higher worlds—is a myth, a terrifying void we cannot perceive. Our reality is flat, absolute, and unyielding. A line is our shape; a point is our edge. And within this rigid geometry, life moves with a precision that baffles any mind unaccustomed to living on paper.

Our climate is mild, yet our weather is often obscured by the heavy smog rising from our cities. The houses themselves are simple polygons, constructed of straight lines meeting at sharp angles. They stand close together, their sides forming narrow streets that wind through the urban sprawl. There are no roofs above us, for the sky is merely a vast, open expanse extending infinitely upward—a direction we can never truly enter, nor see. Instead, we look sideways, measuring the width of the street and the distance between neighbors.

The people of Flatland are lines of varying lengths. The shortest lines are the children, growing steadily longer until they reach adulthood. The men who govern us are squares, rectangles, pentagons, hexagons, and finally, circles. As we advance in social standing, our shapes become more complex, approaching the perfection of the circle. Yet, even the most advanced among us remain forever distinct from the ultimate form, which exists only in theory.

Women in Flatland are acute-angled triangles. Their shape makes them inherently dangerous; when they approach a man, they cut into his side like a knife, causing him pain unless he turns sharply away. Because of this, women must always walk in front of men, leading the way so that their cutting edges do not harm those behind them. They are the mothers of all, but their status remains low, for their shape lacks the symmetry and stability of the male figures.

Since we live in a two-dimensional world, sight alone is insufficient for recognition. If you were to meet someone from outside, you could not know if they were a square, a rectangle, or some other polygon without seeing their full figure. But within Flatland, we recognize each other by touch. As you approach, you feel the angle of your neighbor's side before you make contact. By the slight variation in the angle, you determine whether you are about to collide with a triangle, a square, or a circle. It is a system of tactile navigation, essential for survival in a land where vision is limited to the surface.

Yet, there is a method of recognition by sight, albeit imperfect. When two lines come together, they cast shadows against the ground. By observing these shadows, we can sometimes infer the length and orientation of the other. However, this method fails when the sun is high overhead, casting little shadow, or when the lines are parallel, offering no visual cues. Thus, touch remains the primary sense, while sight serves only as a secondary aid.

There are those among us who deviate from the ideal geometric forms. These irregular figures are outcasts, feared and avoided. Some have jagged edges, others are bent or twisted. They lack the symmetry required for citizenship and are often treated with suspicion. To be regular is to be safe; to be irregular is to be unstable, prone to breaking apart under the weight of society's expectations.

Long ago, before the suppression of color, there was an ancient practice known as painting. Artists attempted to depict the beauty of Flatland by adding hues to their works. But the colors were fleeting, easily washed away by the rain or fading into the gray monotony of our world. The paintings served as reminders of what might have been, a lost era where art could capture the soul of its subjects rather than just their outlines.

To bring order to chaos, the government enacted the Universal Colour Bill. Under this law, every citizen was assigned a specific shade corresponding to their shape and rank. Squares wore blue, rectangles green, pentagons yellow, and circles white. Triangles, being women, were forbidden from wearing any color at all. This standardization ensured that no one could pass off as something they were not, preventing fraud and maintaining the strict hierarchy of Flatland.

But rebellion arose. A group of radicals, led by a circle who dared to wear red, declared war on the Color Bill. They argued that color was the essence of identity, that without it, we were mere lines stripped of meaning. The sedition spread quickly, turning streets into riots of clashing hues. The authorities responded with iron fists, arresting those who defied the law. Many were exiled, their colors confiscated, leaving them pale and lifeless in the drab uniformity of our world.

At the heart of our society stands the priesthood, composed entirely of circles. These beings are nearly perfect, their sides indistinguishable from one another. They hold sway over the spiritual and intellectual lives of Flatlanders, preaching doctrines that reinforce the natural order. The priests claim to speak for the gods, interpreting the laws of geometry as divine commandments. Their authority is absolute, and to question them is to invite ruin.

The doctrine of the priests is simple yet profound: God is the Circle, the ultimate perfection. All other shapes strive to become like Him, ever closer to the ideal, yet never quite reaching it. The priest teaches that the soul is trapped in the body, a line imprisoned in a world of limits. Only through faith and obedience can one hope to ascend toward the divine, transcending the bounds of two dimensions.

One night, in a dream, I found myself in a realm far simpler than ours. Here, everything existed along a single line. The inhabitants were mere points, moving back and forth without left or right. They knew nothing of angles or curves, only forward and backward. It was a world of stark simplicity, where the notion of size was meaningless, for all things were equal in length.

Returning to Flatland, I sought to share this vision with others. But my words fell on deaf ears. To explain the idea of a line to those who lived in a world of lines was impossible. They could not comprehend the abstraction, let alone imagine a dimension beyond theirs. My attempts to bridge the gap between worlds failed, leaving me isolated in my knowledge.

Then came the stranger, a being from Spaceland. He appeared suddenly, towering above us, his presence overwhelming. His shape was spherical, rolling effortlessly across the ground. To us, he seemed to change form constantly, expanding and contracting, shifting from one position to another without effort. He was unlike anything we had ever seen, a creature of three dimensions.

The stranger spoke to me, trying to explain the nature of Spaceland. But his words were lost on me. How could I understand the concept of depth when I could not even conceive of it? He described the volume, the space within, the endless possibilities of movement. Yet, to me, he remained a circle, a flat shape, no matter how he moved.

Seeing that words were useless, the sphere decided to act. With a sudden motion, he turned himself inside out, revealing his interior to me. For a moment, I saw the true form of Spaceland, the vastness of the third dimension. Then, he rolled away, disappearing into the air, leaving me stunned and awestruck.

Driven by curiosity, I followed the sphere, stepping into the unknown. Suddenly, I was lifted from the ground, floating in midair. Below me stretched a landscape of infinite complexity, mountains and valleys, rivers and forests. I looked around, realizing that I could now move in directions I had never imagined. Up, down, left, right, forward, backward—all were possible.

The sphere showed me many wonders, from the curvature of the earth to the stars above. But I wanted more. I craved to understand the very fabric of reality, the secrets hidden beneath the surface of things. So I asked questions, pressing the sphere for answers. And slowly, piece by piece, the truth began to unfold.
