The moon had been riding clear as a pale platter hung in the sky when a bank of cloud slid over it, muffling the light as a hand muffles a bell. In that gray dark a figure broke out suddenly—white upon white—running bent forward as if she would make herself smaller than fear. She clasped a child hard to her breast, the way a woman presses a warm loaf to keep it from cooling, and every motion of her was driven, not chosen.

She ran on a line almost level with mine and never once turned her head. Terror had taken her hearing and sight and left her only flight.

She is chased, I said in myself. Some prowler of this night is after her.

To run after her would have been to add my own footfall to the pursuing rhythm and make her think the worse had doubled. I stepped into her track instead, to meet whatever followed and break its course. The grass was damp underfoot; it whispered when I shifted my weight, and somewhere near, unseen water spoke in a low, steady syllable as if pouring over leaves.

I had stood scarce a moment, peering after her diminishing whiteness, when behind me came a swift, soft-footed rush—no clumsy stamp, but the soundless certainty of a hunter. Before I could turn, something sprang over my head and struck me sharply on the forehead. Stars burst in my eyes like sparks from flint; I went down into the wet grass and tasted iron in my mouth.

I was up again at once. All I caught of my assailant was a vanishing whiteness melting into the dim, and the blood that began to trickle down my brow cooled instantly in the night air, like warm broth spilled on a stone.

I ran after it, wiping blindly with my sleeve, but had gone only a few steps when a shriek of despair tore the quivering dark. It was not a cry that belonged among beasts. It snapped the night’s quiet like a bone. I ran the faster, though the fear in me said the sound had come too late and I was running only toward the remainder.

Then, through vapour-dusted moonlight that came and went as the cloud thinned and thickened, I spied a low white shape approaching. At first I thought it another beast coming to finish what the first had begun; but it came slowly, almost crawling, and its movement was a broken series of strange, floundering leaps, as of a creature in pain that cannot decide which leg to trust.

I drew aside and waited, listening. The night had its own domestic noises—grass bending, the minute click of insects, the distant hush of water—but beneath them, close now, was a low rushing sound as of a small fall, steady and disgracefully intimate, like liquid poured where it ought not to be poured.

As it neared I saw it went on three legs, carrying its left fore-paw high from the ground. Its skin shone white as if washed, and upon it were many dark oval spots. From the lifted paw something streamed and pattered softly into the grass.

It is blood, I said to myself. Some readier champion than I has wounded the beast.

Yet, strange to tell, pity seized me at the sight. Its suffering made a kind of claim, as if pain itself were a language older than my own. If an axe had been in my hand I could not have struck it. In a broken succession of hobbling leaps it went past and out of sight, and still the blood, as it seemed, issued in a small torrent, which kept flowing back with that same soft rush through the blades beside me.

If it go on bleeding like that, I thought, it will soon be hurtless.

But I had no time to follow. The woman and her child were ahead, and whatever had screamed—whether mother or beast—had screamed for them. I went on, hoping still to be of use, and perhaps to see the deliverer I had imagined.

A little way off I descried her seated on the grass, the child in her lap. Even in the thin light I could see how she bent over it, sheltering it with her whole body, as if her own ribs were a cage to keep harm out. The baby lay white and quiet, as if sleep had put its hand upon her mouth.

“Can I do anything for you?” I asked.

At the sound of my voice she started violently and would have risen. The fear in her sprang up again, quick as a hare.

I threw myself down on the ground at her feet, letting the wet soak through my sleeve, making myself small, unthreatening.

“You need not be frightened,” I said. “I was following the beast when happily you found a nearer protector. It passed me now with its foot bleeding so much that by this time it must be all but dead.”

“There is little hope of that!” she answered, trembling, as if even hope had teeth. “Do you not know whose beast she is?”

Certain strange suspicions stirred in me—like a remembered smell from childhood that you cannot name but cannot forget—yet I said I knew nothing of the brute and asked what had become of her champion.

“What champion?” she rejoined. “I have seen no one.”

“Then how came the monster to grief?”

“I pounded her foot with a stone—as hard as I could strike. Did you not hear her cry?”

“Well, you are a brave woman!” I said. “I thought it was you gave the cry!”

“It was the leopardess.”

“I never heard such a sound from the throat of an animal,” I said. “It was like the scream of a woman in torture!”

“My voice was gone,” she answered, and the words came out thin and dry, as if she had no moisture left in her mouth. “I could not have shrieked to save my baby. When I saw the horrid mouth at my darling’s little white neck, I caught up a stone and mashed her lame foot.”

She laid her hand on the child as she spoke, not stroking so much as counting her whole and unharmed: head, throat, shoulder. A mother’s fingers have their own history; they remember bruises and fevers the way a cook remembers burns and near-spills.

“Tell me about the creature,” I said. “I am a stranger in these parts.”

“You will soon know about her if you are going to Bulika!” she answered. “Now, I must never go back there!”

“Yes,” I said, “I am going to Bulika—to see the princess.”

“Have a care; you had better not go!—But perhaps you are—!” She broke off, listening, as if the darkness itself were speaking in a tongue she had been forced to learn. “The princess is a very good, kind woman!”

There was a small movement beside her. The clouds had gathered thick over the moon; I could hardly see the line of her cheek. I feared she was rising to run from me.

“You are in no danger of any sort from me,” I said. “What oath would you like me to take?”

“I know by your speech that you are not of the people of Bulika,” she replied. “I will trust you!—I am not of them, either, else I should not be able: they never trust any one.” She leaned forward, trying to read me by hearing. “If only I could see you! But I like your voice. There—my darling is asleep! The foul beast has not hurt her!—Yes: it was my baby she was after!”

As she spoke she caressed the child, pressing her lips to the little head once, quickly, as if to take in the scent and keep it for courage. The night held many smells—wet earth, crushed grass, the sharp clean tang of my own blood—but around the baby there was only that warm, faint sweetness of milk and living skin, a smell that makes the whole world seem briefly worth saving.

“And then she would have torn her mother to pieces for carrying her off,” she went on. “Some say the princess has two white leopardesses. I know only one—with spots. Everybody knows HER! If the princess hear of a baby, she sends her immediately to suck its blood, and then it either dies or grows up an idiot.”

Her voice had the flat certainty of a thing often repeated in whispers. It was not a story told for fright alone; it was instruction—how to live under a power that listens at doors.

“I would have gone away with my baby,” she continued, “but the princess was from home, and I thought I might wait until I was a little stronger. But she must have taken the beast with her, and been on her way home when I left, and come across my track. I heard the SNIFF-SNUFF of the leopardess behind me, and ran;—oh, how I ran!—But my darling will not die! There is no mark on her!”

Even as she said it, she seemed to taste the words, as if daring to speak them might sour them. Somewhere in the dark the water went on murmuring, indifferent, and the grass answered the smallest wind with its dry hush. It struck me how the sounds of a place can either heal the mind or keep it perpetually braced for terror; here every soft noise could be mistaken for a paw.

“Where are you taking her?” I asked.

“Where no one ever tells!”

“Why is the princess so cruel?”

“There is an old prophecy that a child will be the death of her. That is why she will listen to no offer of marriage, they say.”

“But what will become of her country if she kill all the babies?”

“She does not care about her country.” The bitterness in her was like burnt crust scraped from a pan—black, hard, and earned. “She sends witches around to teach the women spells that keep babies away, and give them horrible things to eat.”

The phrase struck me with an odd domestic horror: food turned against the table, the very act of nourishment corrupted into a tool of rule. A people can lose more than children that way; they can lose recipes, songs, the names that go with hearth-things—lose the language of the mouth as well as the language of the tongue.

“Some say she is in league with the Shadows to put an end to the race,” the woman went on. “At night we hear the questing beast, and lie awake and shiver. She can tell at once the house where a baby is coming, and lies down at the door, watching to get in. There are words that have power to shoo her away, only they do not always work—”

She broke, as if the very mention of those words were dangerous in the air.

“But here I sit talking,” she ended suddenly, “and the beast may by this time have got home, and her mistress be sending the other after us!”

She rose in haste, shifting the child with practiced care so the little head would not loll. I rose also.

“I do not think she will ever get home,” I said, thinking of the blood pouring into the grass like a spilt jug. “Let me carry the baby for you!”

She gave me no answer. When I moved my arms toward the child she only clasped it closer, the way a woman clutches the last seedcorn in a famine.

“I cannot think,” I said, walking beside her as she began to go on, “how the brute could be bleeding so much!”

“Take my advice, and don’t go near the palace,” she answered, and her voice lowered as if the very walls might hear through distance. “There are sounds in it at night as if the dead were trying to shriek, but could not open their mouths!”

The thought of such muffled agony—the acoustic of a place where cries cannot properly be cries—made my skin tighten. A land can be ruled by silence as surely as by proclamation; even pain can be censored.

She stopped, turned her face from me into the dark, and bade me an abrupt farewell. Plainly she did not want more of my company, however kindly meant. So I stood still and listened as her footsteps went away through the grass, growing fainter, swallowed at last by the night’s damp hush, until there was only the low talk of water and the troubled quiet of a world where even lullabies must be whispered.
