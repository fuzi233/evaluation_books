CANON ALBERIC'S SCRAP-BOOK


In the languid warmth of a spring morning, the sleepy town of St Bertrand de Comminges stirred to life. The scent of freshly baked bread wafted from the Chapeau Rouge inn, mingling with the fragrance of blooming wildflowers that carpeted the slopes of the Pyrenees. It was a time of year when the air was alive with the promise of new beginnings, and the ancient stones of the cathedral seemed to hum with anticipation. In the midst of this serene tableau, an Englishman, a Cambridge scholar named Dennistoun, arrived in this forgotten corner of southwest France. His eyes shone with an insatiable curiosity as he stepped off the winding road and onto the cobblestone streets of the town.

Dennistoun had traveled from Toulouse, driven by a passion for the history and culture of the region. His friends, fellow scholars who had accompanied him thus far, had opted to linger in the city, content to sample its renowned cuisine and wine. Not so Dennistoun, whose zeal for discovery led him to rise before dawn to secure the services of the local sacristan, a man tasked with the care and maintenance of the cathedral. The sacristan, a wizened figure with a gaunt face and sunken eyes, arrived promptly at the inn, summoned by the proprietor, a woman with a voice like a rusty gate.

As the sacristan entered the inn, Dennistoun's eyes locked onto his, and he felt an inexplicable jolt of recognition. There was something about the old man that struck a chord, a sense of quiet desperation that seemed to cling to him like a damp shroud. Dennistoun's thoughts turned to the rich history of the cathedral, the stories of saints and martyrs etched into its stone walls. He envisioned the sacristan as a guardian of secrets, a keeper of tales that only he might know. The thought sent a thrill through him, and he hastened to begin his work, his pencil moving swiftly across the pages of his notebook as he recorded every detail of the church's intricate carvings and ornate furnishings.
