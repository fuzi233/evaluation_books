It was Derby Day at Epsom, that great national British holiday where every Londoner who owns a hat, a pair of boots, and at least a vague ability to lie about knowing anything about thoroughbreds drags themselves out to the Downs to gamble, eat too much cold pie, and gawk at anyone richer than themselves. Naturally, half the cast of this little history showed up, all jostling for a spot in the sun.

Wedged in a perfectly respectable open carriage hauled there by two very patient nags was Mrs. Bungay of Paternoster Row, decked out in so many frills, feathers, and brooches that Solomon in all his glory would have taken one look and gone home to borrow a fan. Next to her huddled meek little Mrs. Shandon, who’d somehow gotten stuck with Mrs. Bungay as her ride-or-die bestie and hadn’t had the heart to break the friendship off yet. Mr. Bungay himself had already stuffed himself full of three helpings of cold ham, and was now careening wildly at the stick-the-stall pop-guns a few feet away, sweat dripping down his shiny bald pate like he was trying to water the grass with his forehead. Shandon was shambling around among the gipsies and beer sellers like he was hoping someone would slip him a free pint, and Finucane hovered dutifully by the two ladies, fending off random passersby while letting the regular publishing house crowd pop over to say hello.

One of those visitors was Mr. Archer, who bowed so low he nearly knocked his own top hat off, and proceeded to give Mrs. Bungay a running commentary of every “important” person on the course. “Over yonder’s the Prime Minister,” he announced, “He just told me to put a tenner on Borax, but between you and me, Munmeer’s got it in the bag.” Then he pointed out a dozen “dukes and grandees” that were mostly just random blokes in top hats, and leaned in like he was sharing top-secret state gossip. “Look up in the Grand Stand! That’s the Chinese Ambassador and his mandarin crew—Fou-choo-foo brought me letters of introduction from the Governor General of India, who’s my absolute oldest chum. I wined and dined that ambassador so often he kept a set of chopsticks at my house permanently. But he brought his own cook with him, and would you believe it? One day I was out, the ambassador was in our back garden gorging on gooseberries (the Chinese are mad for gooseberries, apparently) and that monster of a cook spotted my wife’s tiny Blenheim spaniel—you know, the one the Duke of Marlborough gave us, the one whose ancestor’s life Mrs. Archer’s great-great-grandpa saved at Malplaquet? He seized poor little Fido, cut his throat, skinned him, and served him up stuffed with forcemeat as the second course.”

“Lawks!” gasped Mrs. Bungay, her eyes nearly popping out of her head.
“You can imagine my wife’s meltdown when she found poor Fido’s skin in the back yard right after we’d all had a bite of the dish! She refused to speak to the ambassador ever again, and he’s never dared show up for dinner since. The Lord Mayor loved it, though! Swore it tasted just like duck, especially with a side of green peas.”
“You don’t say!” Mrs. Bungay shrieked, now fully convinced Archer was the most important man in the entire country.
“Fact, cross my heart. And look at that lady in blue sat next to the ambassador? That’s Lady Flamingo, they say she’s going to marry him and move back to Peking. She’s already squeezing her feet into tiny little shoes to make them dainty, but she’ll never get them small enough. My wife’s got the smallest foot in all of England, wears shoes made for a six-year-old, but that’s nothing next to a Chinese lady’s tiny feet, Mrs. Bungay!”

“Hey, Mr. Archer,” Mrs. Bungay cut in, squinting at a carriage a little ways off. “Who’s that with Mr. Pendennis? He and Mr. Warrington popped over a minute ago. That Mr. Pendennis is so stuck-up, but he’s earned it, right? Everyone says he hangs out only with top-shelf company. Did he inherit a huge fortune? I notice he’s still all in black, poor thing.”
“Eighteen hundred a year in land, twenty-two and a half thousand in three-and-a-half per cents, give or take a quid,” Archer rattled off like he was reciting the back of his hand.
“Law! You know literally everything, Mr. A!” the Paternoster Row lady gasped.
“Only because I handled poor Mrs. Pendennis’s will,” Archer waved modesty aside. “Major Pendennis hardly does a thing without calling me in, and we tied up all the property nice and tight so Arthur can’t go blowing it all on silly frivolities. —How d’you do, my lord? You know that gentleman there, ladies? You’ve read his speeches in Parliament! That’s Lord Rochester.”
“Lord fiddlesticks!” Finucane yelled from the back of the carriage. “That’s Tom Staples from the Morning Advertiser, you fool.”
“Is it?” Archer didn’t even flinch. “My eyesight’s terrible, honestly, I could’ve sworn it was Rochester. And that chap with the double opera glasses? That’s Lord John! The tall bloke next to him? Sir James, obviously.”
“Yeh, you know ’em because you spot their names in the newspaper,” Finucane grumbled.
“I know them because they call me their dear personal friend,” Archer sniffed. “Look at the Duke of Hampshire! The perfect old English gentleman! He’s never missed a Derby. ‘Archer,’ he told me just yesterday, ‘I’ve been to sixty-five Derbies! First time I came I was seven years old, on a piebald pony with my dad, the Prince of Wales, and Colonel Hanger. I only missed two: one when I had measles at Eton, and one the year of Waterloo, when I was off in Flanders with my great friend Wellington.’”

“Wait, that yellow carriage with the pink and yellow parasols that Mr. Pendennis is talking to, full of random gents?” Mrs. Bungay asked. “Who’s that?”
“That’s Lady Clavering of Clavering Park, her estate’s right next to Pendennis’s family home. The little lad hanging off the box is her son and heir, and he’s three sheets to the wind, the little scamp. The young lady is Miss Amory, her daughter from her first marriage, and she’s got it so bad for Arthur Pendennis it’s practically leaking off her. But I hear he’s got his heart set on someone else. You know young Foker, the brewer heir? He was so heartbroken when Miss Amory turned him down he nearly hanged himself, his valet cut him down just in the nick of time, and now he’s off abroad with a minder to keep him out of trouble.”
“Bless his heart, what a romantic!” Mrs. Bungay sighed. “Who’d’ve thought, when he turned up quiet and demure to our dinners three or four years ago, he’d be out meeting royalty and getting his name printed in every society guest list?”
“I introduced him around loads when he first moved to town,” Archer said, preening, “And his uncle Major Pendennis did the rest. Hang on—Cobden’s here! Of all people! I’ve got to go say hello. Goodbye, Mrs. Bungay! Mrs. Shandon! Lovely chatting!”

An hour earlier, on the other side of the course, a beat-up old stagecoach had rattled into view, its roof crammed full of shabby, loud, overexcited blokes stamping and yelling as the Derby race careened across the grass, watched by thousands upon thousands of screaming people. This was Wheeler’s drag, from the Harlequin’s Head pub in Bow Street, ferrying a whole crew of rowdy lads who’d stuffed a slap-up feast in the boot for the day. As the horses flashed past, every last one of them bellowed the name of the nag they’d bet their last shilling on: “The Cornet!” “Muffineer for the win!” “Blue sleeves! Blue sleeves!” “Yellow cap! YELLOW CAP!” The whole crowd shrieked through that delicious, torturous minute before the winner’s number went up, and when the flapping signal confirmed Podasokus had taken first place, one guy on the roof jumped so high he nearly fell right off, like he was a homing pigeon determined to fly back to London with the good news.

He didn’t make it more than an inch off the boards, thudding back down so hard the rotting old coach roof creaked and cracked under him. “HOORAY!” he howled. “Podasokus is the winner! Supper for ten, Wheeler, mate! Everyone’s invited, and to hell with the bill.”
The rest of the shabby dandies on the coach cheered and clapped him on the back, whispering to each other that the Colonel was going to walk away with fifteen hundred quid, and got the odds from a bookie who couldn’t welch on him. Every single one of them immediately started side-eyeing each other, terrified some other bloke would drag the Colonel off to a quiet corner and borrow a tenner off him before they could get in their own borrow request. The poor guy couldn’t even go to the privy the whole rest of the afternoon, everyone clung to him like glue.

Over on another patch of the Downs, there was a far more modest vehicle: cab No. 2002, that had trundled over from the Strand stand carrying one gentleman and two ladies. One of the girls was perched on the cab’s box, munching lobster salad and sipping bitter ale with her mum and their friend, so fresh and rosy-cheeked that half the fancy dandies who’d been hanging around the rich people’s carriages abandoned all the fancy society ladies just to gawk at her. Her cheeks flushed pink like summer rose petals whenever she laughed, which was a lot—her mum’s cheeks were bright red too, but that was a permanent mottled shade that got deeper the more pale ale and brandy-and-water she drank, until she looked as pink as the lobster they’d just eaten.

The gentleman with them, Mr. Sam Huxter, was in top form, telling jokes so loud and silly the whole road could hear them. On the drive over, he’d yelled good-natured “chaff” at every passing carriage, from the fancy drags full of grumpy guardsmen to the humble donkey cart where the dustman was taking his girlfriend to the races. He’d fired silly little jokes into every cottage window he passed, at lines of grinning schoolgirls, groups of cheering urchins hanging over school railings, even old maiden ladies who looked like they never laughed in their lives. The pretty girl in the pink-ribbonbonnet and her lobster-loving mum both agreed that when Sam was in high spirits, no one could touch him. He’d stuffed the cab full of stupid prizes he’d won at the pop-gun stalls: pin cushions, wooden apples, tobacco boxes, Jack-in-the-boxes, tiny toy soldiers. He’d even brought a gipsy over to tell the girls’ fortunes, and the only damp spot on the whole happy day was when the gipsy told the pretty young thing to watch out for a fair-faced man who’d done her wrong, that she’d been very ill once, but a true dark-haired man would always stand by her.

The girl blushed so hard she looked like a cherry, while her mum and Sam exchanged very significant little grins. Probably the gipsy said the exact same lines to a hundred different carriages that day.

Making his way through the crowd to people-watch as he usually did, Arthur Pendennis suddenly rounded a corner and bumped straight into cab 2002. When the pretty girl spotted him, she gasped and went pale. Her mum went even redder than usual, and the previously cheerful Sam Huxter scowled so hard his eyebrows met, his fists curling tight in his coat pockets, glaring daggers from Fanny Bolton (who the reader has probably already guessed the pretty girl is) straight at Arthur.

Arthur frowned back, his face all serious and disapproving, like he was Mrs. Lynx the neighbourhood busybody catching two people having a perfectly innocent chat and immediately assuming the worst. No one could blame him for his prudish little reaction, honestly. Fanny’s heart started hammering, Mrs. Bolton started talking a mile a minute to defuse the awkwardness. “Lor! Mr. Pendennis, I’m that glad to see you! Don’t he look well, Fanny? We were just talking about you not two seconds ago! If this is the famous Epsom races everyone raves about, I’d be happy if I never saw it again, for all I care. How’s Major Pendennis? And that lovely Mr. Warrington who did Fanny such a kindness ages back? She’s never forgotten it, not once! That tall bloke nearly bashed his whole head off our lodge door, remember Fanny? How he knocked his head—”

While Mrs. Bolton rambled, a thousand old memories flashed through Fanny’s head: the days she thought she’d die of loving Arthur, the lonely sick weeks, the quiet shame, and the slow, soft comfort Sam had given her after it all. There he was, standing right in front of her: all dandified, wearing black crape on his white hat, jet buttons down his shirtfront, a pink carnation in his lapel that some other girl probably gave him, tight lavender gloves sewn with black mourning thread, a tiny little cane that couldn’t possibly have been used for anything harder than prodding a footman. Sam meanwhile had no gloves on, wore giant scuffed Blucher boots, reeked of tobacco, and definitely looked like a good hose-down would do him a world of good. Fanny’s eyes drifted over every inch of Arthur, from the little pale ring on his forehead left by his hat, to the trinkets on his watch chain, to the shiny neat little boots that looked nothing like Sam’s scuffed high-lows. After she gave his gloved hand a tiny wobbly shake, the only words she could get out were: “This is Mr. Samuel Huxter—you two met before, I think. Sam, you know Mr. Pendennis. Would you—would you like a little refreshment?”

Those quiet little words melted every last bit of suspicion off Arthur’s face, and probably took a weight of old guilt off his chest too. The stuffy frown fell away, replaced by a good-natured grin and a little amused twinkle in his eye. “I’m parched,” he said, “I’d be delighted to drink to your health, Fanny. And I hope Mr. Huxter will forgive me for being horribly rude the last time we met—when I was so sick and out of my mind I barely knew what I was saying.” He held out his gloved hand to Sam in a clear peace offering.

Sam’s tight, hot fist had no choice but to creep out of his coat pocket, and when he wrapped it around Arthur’s fancy glove, he left big black smudges all over the lavender kid. He noticed it immediately, and had to fight the urge to clench his fist and punch that stupid grinning dandy right in the jaw, right there in front of the whole race course, to prove he was the better man.

Arthur, completely unphased, poured himself a glass of lukewarm frothy beer, said it was the most delicious drink he’d ever tasted, and drank it happily to their health. While he was chatting away all friendly and charming, a young lady in dove-grey silk with a pink-lined white parasol and impossibly tiny dove-grey boots walked past him, leaning on the arm of a tall stalwart man with a military moustache. The young lady clenched her little fist, gave Arthur a mischievous glare out of the corner of her eye, and the moustached man burst out laughing, tipping his hat to Fanny and her mum. Fanny’s eyes followed the pretty dove-grey girl for a second, then she turned right back to Sam with the softest, sweetest, most innocent smile in the world.
“Isn’t she beautiful?” Fanny said. “What a lovely dress! Did you see how tiny her little hands are, Sam?”
“That was Captain Strong,” Mrs. Bolton put in. “I wonder who that young woman was?”
“Neighbour of mine from the country,” Arthur said. “Miss Amory, Lady Clavering’s daughter. You saw Sir Francis Clavering all the time when you lived in Shepherd’s Inn, Mrs. Bolton.”
Fanny’s head immediately spun out a full three-volume romance about the pair: grand love, tragic betrayal, a fancy wedding at St. George’s Hanover Square, a broken hearted heroine, and poor Sam was definitely not the hero of that little daydream. Sam meanwhile had lit the rankest, cheapest Cuban cigar you could buy, and was puffing it so hard the smoke was practically billowing right under Fanny’s nose.

The second Arthur Pendennis had said his goodbyes and wandered off, the sun suddenly looked less bright to Sam, the sky less blue, the pop-gun stalls completely uninteresting, and the bitter beer tasted like hot garbage. He had a pocket full of peas and a pea-shooter he’d brought to mess around with on the drive home, but he completely forgot they existed until a random bloke fired a whole volley of them right in his sad face on the way back to London. Sam swore a little in surprise, then burst out into a savage, bitter laugh.

But Fanny was absolute perfection the whole ride home, cooing and snuggling up to him, laughing all her pretty little laughs, gushing over the silly Jack-in-the-boxes and tiny toy soldiers he’d won her, thanking him over and over. When they got back to the house, Sam was still moping, about to give her a cold, stiff goodbye, when Fanny burst into tears and called him a horrible unkind thing. That was all it took. Sam grabbed her in his arms, swearing she was an angel, that he was a stupid jealous brute, that he had no right to hate Pendennis, begging her to say once more that she—
