As the sun rose over the rolling hills of Yorkshire, the bustling town of Stilbro' prepared for its weekly market-day feast. The aroma of roasting meats and freshly baked bread wafted through the air, enticing the townspeople to gather at the market square. Amidst the excitement, all eyes turned to the arrival of Mr. Moore, whose prolonged absence had sparked endless speculation. Some whispered that he'd grown too fearful to return, knowing his presence would only fan the flames of discontent among the working class.

The mill-owners, however, were ecstatic about his impending return, for they believed his recent triumph over the ringleaders of the disaffected operatives would serve as a stern warning to others. Yet, the operatives themselves seemed unconvinced, their murmurs of discontent still echoing through the alehouses, where they sipped their drugged beer and toasted their defiance with fiery British gin.

As the morning progressed, the news of Mr. Moore's return spread like wildfire, and the townsfolk began to speculate about the reason behind his prolonged stay. Some said it was fear, while others claimed it was something more complex, a mixture of motives that only Mr. Moore himself could fully understand. Whatever the truth may have been, his announcement to Joe Scott that he'd arrive at the George Inn the next day sent a ripple of excitement throughout the town.

On market-day, Mr. Moore took his customary seat at the market dinner, surrounded by the assembled manufacturers. As a man of action and note, they treated him with a mixture of respect and wariness, their faces a testament to the complicated emotions that swirled beneath the surface. Some, who would never dare acknowledge him publicly, now hailed him as their champion, their words dripping with flattery and admiration.

As the wine flowed, their respect began to simmer, but Mr. Moore's unyielding nonchalance kept it from boiling over. Mr. Yorke, the perennial host of these gatherings, watched his young friend with a discerning eye, searching for any sign of weakness or vulnerability. He knew that Moore's rough spirit was most soothed by indifference, not praise or adoration. And so, as Robert leaned back in his chair, his face a mask of quiet disdain, Mr. Yorke's heart swelled with pleasure, for he saw in Moore a man struggling to come to terms with the panegyrics of those he considered beneath him.
