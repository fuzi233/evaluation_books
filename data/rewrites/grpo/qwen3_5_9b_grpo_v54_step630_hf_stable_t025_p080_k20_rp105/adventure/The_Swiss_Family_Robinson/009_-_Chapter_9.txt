Spring had finally broken the winter's grip. The winds ceased their howling, the sun pierced the lingering clouds with brilliant rays, and the relentless rain gave way to a gentle silence. No prisoners released from captivity could have felt a joy surpassing ours as we stepped out of our winter shelter. Our eyes drank in the fresh verdance, our ears were filled with the thousand cheerful songs of waking birds, and our lungs breathed in the pure, balmy air of the new season.

Nature itself seemed to have been reborn. Our plantations thrived with vigorous energy; the seeds we had sown were pushing through the moist earth, signaling that all life was refreshed. Yet, our first concern was our home. The nest we had built was now a dilapidated ruin, torn apart by the storms. We worked tirelessly, and within a few days, it was once again habitable. My wife pleaded for me to begin the flax processing immediately. I constructed a drying oven and, after some effort, fashioned a beetle-reel and spinning-wheel. Soon, she and Franz were hard at work, the little boy expertly reeling off the thread his mother spun.

I was anxious to visit Tentholm, fearing our precious stores had suffered. Fritz and I made the journey. The damage to Falconhurst was severe, but the scene at Tentholm was catastrophic. The tent had been blown to the ground, the canvas reduced to rags, and provisions soaked beyond saving. Worse still, two casks of powder were utterly destroyed. We immediately spread out what we could save to dry in the sun.

Miraculously, the pinnace remained safe, but our faithful tub-boat had been dashed into pieces. The irreparable loss compelled me to resolve on building safer, more stable winter quarters before the next rainy season. Fritz suggested hollowing out a cave in the rock. Though the difficulties seemed insurmountable, I determined to attempt it. We might not carve a cavern large enough to serve as a room, but we could certainly create a cellar for our most valuable and perishable goods.

Days later, we left Falconhurst with a cart laden with spades, hammers, chisels, pickaxes, and crowbars. On the smooth face of the perpendicular rock, I chalked out the size of the proposed entrance, and with minds bent on success, we began to batter away. Six days of incessant toil made little impression; the hole would hardly have satisfied even Master Knips. Yet, we did not despair. Presently, we were rewarded by reaching softer, yielding substance. Our work progressed, and our spirits lifted.

On the tenth day, as Jack hammered and crowbarred with diligence, he shouted, "Gone, father! Fritz, my bar has gone through the mountain!"

"Run round and get it," laughed Fritz. "Perhaps it has dropped into Europe—you must not lose a good crowbar."

"But, really, it is through! It went right through the rock; I heard it crash down inside. Oh, do come and see!" he shouted excitedly.

We sprang to his side. I thrust the handle of my hammer into the hole; it met with no opposition, turning freely in any direction. Fritz handed me a long pole to test the depth. I felt nothing but a thin wall intervening between us and a vast cavern. With a shout of joy, the boys battered vigorously at the rock. Piece by piece, it fell, and soon the hole was large enough for entry.

I stepped near the aperture to examine further, but a sudden rush of poisonous air turned me giddy. Shouting to my sons to stand back, I leaned against the rock. Recovering my senses, I explained the danger of stagnant air in caverns. "Unless air is incessantly renewed, it becomes vitiated and fatal," I warned. "The safest way to restore it is fire; a few handfuls of blazing hay might suffice if the place is small." We tried the experiment, but the flame was extinguished the instant it entered. Even bundles of blazing grass made no difference.

I realized we needed a more efficacious remedy. I sent the boys for a chest of signal-rockets from the wreck. We let fly dozens of these fiery serpents, which whizzed in and disappeared at a vast distance. Some flew like radiant meteors, lighting up the mighty circumference and displaying a sparkling, glittering roof. They looked like avenging dragons driving a foul fiend out of a beauteous palace.

We waited a moment, then I threw in lighted hay again. It burned clearly; the air was purified. Fritz and I enlarged the opening, while Jack thundered away on his buffalo to Falconhurst to bear the astonishing news to his mother.

The effect of Jack's report was immediate. The timbers of the bridge soon resounded under the swift tramp of his steed, followed quickly by the rest of our party in the cart. All were in a state of high excitement. Jack had stowed every candle he could find in the cart. Lighting them, we shouldered our arms and entered. I led the way, sounding the ground with a long pole to avoid hidden chasms.

Silently we marched—my wife, the boys, and even the dogs seemed overawed by the grandeur of the scene. We were in a grotto of diamonds—a vast cave of glittering crystal. The candles reflected on the walls a golden light, bright as the stars of Heaven. Great crystal pillars rose from the floor like mighty trees, mingling their branches high above and drooping in hundreds of stalactites that sparkled with all the colors of the rainbow.

The floor was formed of hard, dry sand, so dry that we knew we could safely take up our abode without fear of damp. From the brilliance of the crystals, I suspected their nature. Tasting a piece, I confirmed it was rock-salt—an unlimited supply of the purest salt!

However, one thing detracted from my delight: large crystals lay scattered on the ground, detached from the roof. If this were to recur, we would be in constant peril. Examining the masses, I discovered they had been recently separated, concluding that the concussion of the rockets had caused their fall. To ensure safety, we discharged our guns from the entrance. Nothing more fell; our magnificent abode was secure.

Returning to Falconhurst, our minds were full of wonder and plans for the new home. Nothing was now talked of but the new house. The safety of Falconhurst, once our pride, now dwindled to nothing in our estimation; it would serve merely as a summer residence, while the cave would become our impregnable winter castle.

We hewed a row of windows in the rock, fitting the cases from the officers' cabins. We brought the door from Falconhurst and fitted it into the aperture. I determined to conceal the opening in the tree trunk with bark to avoid attracting wild beasts or savages. Inside, we divided the cave into four parts: a large front compartment subdivided into sitting, eating, and sleeping areas; a right-hand division for the kitchen and workshop; a left-hand section for stables; and a dark recess behind for the storehouse and powder-magazine.

Knowing the discomforts of the rainy season, we planned useful arrangements. We did not intend to be smoke-dried again, so we built a proper fireplace and chimney. Our stable arrangements were improved, and ample space was left in the workshop for extensive operations.

Our frequent visits to Tentholm revealed new advantages. Splendid turtles often came ashore to deposit eggs, and their delicious flesh provided sumptuous meals. When more than one appeared, we cut off their retreat to the sea, turned them on their backs, and fastened them to stakes driven near the water's edge. Thus secured, they thrived and remained in excellent condition for weeks. Lobsters, crabs, and mussels also abounded.

One morning, approaching Tentholm, we witnessed a curious phenomenon. The waters out to sea appeared agitated, heaving and boiling, their surface illuminated by flashes of fire. Hundreds of birds hovered overhead, screaming and darting down to plunge beneath the water or skim the surface. The shining mass rolled onwards, approaching our bay directly.

I was convinced it was a shoal of herrings. Explaining the phenomenon to the boys, I described the immense size of the living bank, the ravenous fish that devoured them, and the birds that hunted stragglers. To escape these enemies, the shoal sought the shallow shores, only to meet a third enemy: man. "In one year millions of these fish are caught," I noted, "yet the roes of only a small number would suffice to supply as many fish again."

Soon our fishery was in operation. Jack and Fritz stood in the water with baskets, baling out the fish and throwing them to us on shore. We quickly cleaned them, placing them in casks with alternating layers of salt and herrings until we had many casks of pickled fish. As the barrels were filled, we closed them carefully and rolled them to the cool vaults at the back of our cave.

Our good fortune did not end here. A day after the herring fishery, a great number of seals appeared, attracted by the refuse we had thrown into the sea. Though I feared they were unsuitable for our table, we secured a score or two for their skins and fat. We drew the skins off for harness and clothing, and boiled the fat down for oil, which we stored for tanning, soap-making, and lamp fuel.

These occupations briefly interrupted our work at Rock House, but we soon returned with renewed vigor. I noticed that the salt crystals rested on a base of gypsum, which I knew could be made into plaster. Breaking off pieces and subjecting them to heat, I reduced them to powder. The resulting plaster was smooth and white. Lacking a specific use, I plastered over some herring casks to ensure all air was excluded. The remainder I intended to preserve by smoking.

To do this, the boys and I built a small hut of reeds and branches. We strung our herrings on lines across the roof and lit a great fire of brushwood and moss on the floor. Dense smoke curled in volumes around the fish, curing them perfectly in a few days.

About a month later, other shoals of fish appeared. Jack spotted them first, calling out that young whales were off the coast. We ran down to discover the bay swarming with great sturgeon, salmon, and trout making for the mouth of Jackal River to deposit their spawn.

Jack was delighted. "Here are proper fish!" he exclaimed. "None of your paltry fry. How do you preserve these?"

"Not so fast," I replied. "Tell me how they are to be caught, and I will tell you how they are to be cooked."

"Oh! I'll catch them fast enough," he replied, darting off to Rock House.

While I pondered the method, he returned with a bow, arrow, and ball of twine. At the arrow-head, he had fastened a barbed spike and secured the arrow to the end of the string. Armed with this weapon, he advanced to the river's edge. His arrow flew and struck one of the largest fish in the side.

"Help, father, help!" he cried as the great fish darted off, carrying the arrow and all with it. "Or he will pull me into the water!"

I ran to his assistance, and together we struggled with the finny monster. He pulled tremendously, lashing the water, but we held the cord fast. Weaker and weaker grew his struggles, and eventually, exhausted by exertion and loss of blood, he allowed us to draw him ashore.

He was a noble prize. Fritz and Ernest, arriving just as we completed the capture, were envious of Jack's success. Not to be outdone, they rushed for weapons. Soon we were all in the water: Fritz with a harpoon, Ernest with a rod and line, and I, armed like Neptune with an iron trident. The shore was soon strewn with a goodly number of the finest fish.

At length, Fritz, after harpooning a great sturgeon eight feet long, could not get the beast ashore. We all went to his assistance, but our united efforts were unavailing.

"The buffalo!" proposed my wife. Off went Jack for Storm. The buffalo was harnessed to the harpoon rope, and soon the monstrous fish lay panting on the sand. Once we had captured as many fish as we could utilize, we set about cleaning and preparing their flesh. Some we salted, some we dried like the herrings, and some we treated like Mediterranean tunny, preparing them in oil.

Of the sturgeon's roe, I decided to form caviare, the great Russian dish. Removing the membranes, washing it in vinegar, and salting it, I pressed out the moisture, packed it in small barrels, and stowed it away. Knowing that the sturgeon's bladder yields the best isinglass, I carefully collected the air-bladders, washed them, and hung them to stiffen. Peeling off the outer coat, I cut the remainder into strips, or staples. Placing these in an iron pot over the fire, I reduced them to a proper consistency, strained off the glue through a clean cloth, and spread it on a slab of stone to dry. The substance obtained was beautifully transparent and promised to serve as an excellent substitute for glass in our window-frames.

Fortunately, in this beautiful climate, the kitchen garden required little attention. Seeds sprang up and flourished regardless of the season. Peas, beans, wheat, barley, rye, and Indian corn seemed constantly ripe, while cucumbers, melons, and other vegetables grew luxuriantly. The success of our garden at Tentholm encouraged me to hope that my experiment at Falconhurst had not failed. One morning, we visited the spot. Passing the field where potatoes had been dug, we found it covered with barley, wheat, rye, and peas in profusion.

Turning to my wife in amazement, I asked, "Where has this fine crop sprung from?"

"From the earth," she replied, laughing. "Where Franz and I sowed the seed I brought from the wreck. The ground was ready tilled by you and the boys; all we had to do was scatter the seed."
