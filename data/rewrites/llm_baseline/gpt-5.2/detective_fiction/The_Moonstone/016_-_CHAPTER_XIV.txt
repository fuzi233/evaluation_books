The quickest way out of my lady’s sitting-room, if you wanted the garden air on your face, was by the shrubbery path. I knew every turn of it—the boxed scent of laurel, the soft scuff of sand underfoot, the sparrows quarrelling in the leaves as if they owned the place. It was, besides, Mr. Franklin Blake’s favourite walk. When the house had mislaid him—as we mislaid keys, letters, tempers—we found him there, pacing where the green muffled the sound of the world.

I am an obstinate old servant; the longer a man keeps house with other people’s secrets, the more he fancies he has a right to taste them. Sergeant Cuff had kept his thoughts corked up as tight as a bottle of good port, and that only sharpened my appetite to get at them.

As we turned into the shrubbery path, I tried him again, by a side-entrance.

“As things are now,” I said, “if I was in your place, I should be at my wits’ end.”

“If you were in my place,” says he, “you would have formed an opinion—and, as things are now, any doubt you might previously have felt about your own conclusions would be completely set at rest. Never mind for the present what those conclusions are, Mr. Betteredge. I haven’t brought you out here to draw me like a badger; I have brought you out here to ask for some information. You might have given it to me, no doubt, in the house, instead of out of it. But doors and listeners have a knack of getting together; and, in my line of life, we cultivate a healthy taste for the open air.”

The open air had its own sound that day: bees worrying the rosemary by the wall, the distant clink of a spade, and—under it all—the hush that sits on a house when suspicion has been served up with the meals like a bitter sauce no one ordered and no one can send back.

“We won’t enter into your young lady’s motives,” the Sergeant went on, “we will only say it’s a pity she declines to assist me, because, by so doing, she makes this investigation more difficult than it might otherwise have been. We must now try to solve the mystery of the smear on the door—which, you may take my word for it, means the mystery of the Diamond also—in some other way. I have decided to see the servants, and to search their thoughts and actions, Mr. Betteredge, instead of searching their wardrobes. Before I begin, however, I want to ask you a question or two. You are an observant man—did you notice anything strange in any of the servants (making due allowance, of course, for fright and fluster), after the loss of the Diamond was found out? Any particular quarrel among them? Anyone of them not in his or her usual spirits? Unexpectedly out of temper, for instance? or unexpectedly taken ill?”

Rosanna Spearman’s sudden illness at yesterday’s dinner flashed on me—her face the colour of skimmed milk, her spoon pausing halfway as if the very steam from the dish turned her sick. I had barely time to shape the first word when Sergeant Cuff’s eyes slid aside into the shrubbery, sharp as a knife’s point, and I heard him say under his breath, “Hullo!”

“What’s the matter?” I asked.

“A touch of the rheumatics in my back,” said the Sergeant—loud, easy, for anybody within earshot. “We shall have a change in the weather before long.”

A few steps took us to the corner of the house. He turned short to the right and brought me out on the terrace. Below, the garden opened—lawn like a green cloth, beds stitched with flowers, the fountain’s thin talk going on and on. He stopped where the air was wide and there was nowhere to hide but in plain sight.

“About that young person, Rosanna Spearman?” he said. “It isn’t very likely, with her personal appearance, that she has got a lover. But, for the girl’s own sake, I must ask you at once whether she has provided herself with a sweetheart, poor wretch, like the rest of them?”

Of all questions, at such a time! I could only stare.

“I saw Rosanna Spearman hiding in the shrubbery as we went by,” he added.

“When you said ‘Hullo’?”

“Yes—when I said ‘Hullo!’ If there’s a sweetheart in the case, the hiding doesn’t much matter. If there isn’t—as things are in this house—the hiding is a highly suspicious circumstance, and it will be my painful duty to act on it accordingly.”

Now came my pinch. I knew the shrubbery was Mr. Franklin’s walk; I knew he’d likely choose it on his return from the station; and I knew, too, what Penelope had said, time after time, with that sharp young sense of hers—Rosanna hanging about there, waiting like a moth for the candle, foolishly trying to catch Mr. Franklin’s eye. If I spoke, I must repeat Penelope’s fancy as if it was my own; if I held my tongue, I must leave a poor, wild-hearted girl to the mercy of a man whose suspicions were as quiet as they were deadly.

Out of sheer pity—on my honour, out of sheer pity—I told him. Rosanna, I said, had been mad enough to set her heart on Mr. Franklin Blake.

Sergeant Cuff never laughed. If anything amused him, the corners of his mouth only curled the least little way, as if the thought warmed him without lighting him. They curled now.

“Hadn’t you better say she’s mad enough to be an ugly girl and only a servant?” he asked. “The falling in love with a gentleman of Mr. Franklin Blake’s manners and appearance doesn’t seem to me to be the maddest part of her conduct by any means. However, I’m glad the thing is cleared up: it relieves one’s mind to have things cleared up. Yes, I’ll keep it a secret, Mr. Betteredge. I like to be tender to human infirmity—though I don’t get many chances of exercising that virtue in my line of life. You think Mr. Franklin Blake hasn’t got a suspicion of the girl’s fancy for him? Ah! he would have found it out fast enough if she had been nice-looking. The ugly women have a bad time of it in this world; let’s hope it will be made up to them in another.

“You have got a nice garden here, and a well-kept lawn. See for yourself how much better the flowers look with grass about them instead of gravel. No, thank you. I won’t take a rose. It goes to my heart to break them off the stem. Just as it goes to your heart, you know, when there’s something wrong in the servants’ hall. Did you notice anything you couldn’t account for in any of the servants when the loss of the Diamond was first found out?”

He slipped that question in, as a cook slips a pinch of salt into a sweet dish to see who notices. I had got on tolerably with him; but I did not relish helping him creep among my people like a draught under the door. So I shut my mouth on the little I knew.

“I noticed nothing,” I said, “except that we all lost our heads together, myself included.”

“Oh,” says he, “that’s all you have to tell me, is it?”

“That is all,” I answered, steady as I could keep it.

His dismal eyes fixed on me. “Mr. Betteredge,” he says, “have you any objection to oblige me by shaking hands? I have taken an extraordinary liking to you.”

Why he chose the exact moment when I was deceiving him to offer me that compliment, I cannot say. I felt, for an instant, as absurdly proud as a boy who has palmed a stolen tart and not been found out.

We went back to the house. He asked for a room to himself, and then for the servants—indoor servants only—to be sent in one by one, in order of rank, first to last.

I took him into my own room. Then I called the servants together in the hall, where the air always carried a faint mingling of soap, boots, and yesterday’s cooking—the homely smell of work that never gets talked about upstairs.

Rosanna Spearman stood among them much as usual: a quick, close-held look in her, like a cat that never quite sleeps. I daresay she had heard enough to know what was coming. There she was, at any rate, with a face that might have sworn the shrubbery had never existed.

I sent them in, one by one.

The cook first—our cook, who could turn simple country produce into comfort, who knew by scent alone when the bread had taken colour enough and when a sauce had gone one degree too far. She came out quickly.

“Sergeant Cuff is depressed in his spirits,” she reported, “but Sergeant Cuff is a perfect gentleman.”

My lady’s maid went next, and stayed much longer. She came out stiff as starch.

“If Sergeant Cuff doesn’t believe a respectable woman,” says she, “he might keep his opinion to himself, at any rate!”

Penelope followed. In and out in a moment or two, like a sparrow that will not stay where the air is foul.

“Sergeant Cuff is much to be pitied,” says she. “He must have been crossed in love, father, when he was a young man.”

The first housemaid went after Penelope, and stayed as long as my lady’s maid. Out she came with her cheeks burning.

“I didn’t enter her ladyship’s service, Mr. Betteredge, to be doubted to my face by a low police-officer!”

Rosanna Spearman went next. She stayed longer than any of them. When she came out, there was no report—no bounce, no complaint, no small vanity wounded into noise. Dead silence. Her lips were pale as ashes.

Samuel, the footman, followed. He barely stayed.

“Whoever blacks Sergeant Cuff’s boots,” says Samuel, “ought to be ashamed of himself.”

Nancy, the kitchen-maid, went last. She stayed no longer than Samuel.

“Sergeant Cuff has a heart,” says she. “He doesn’t cut jokes, Mr. Betteredge, with a poor hard-working girl.”

When it was done, I went into my room to hear if there were further commands. There was the Sergeant at his old trick—standing at the window, looking out as if the glass showed him more than the garden, whistling “The Last Rose of Summer” very softly to himself. It was an odd sound in a house where every voice now seemed to listen for some other voice behind it.

“Any discoveries, sir?” I asked.

“If Rosanna Spearman asks leave to go out,” he said, “let the poor thing go; but let me know first.”

I might as well have held my tongue about Rosanna and Mr. Franklin. Plain enough—she was under his eye, in spite of me.

“I hope you don’t think Rosanna is concerned in the loss of the Diamond?” I ventured.

Again, the little curl at the corners of his mouth; again, the hard look.

“I think I had better not tell you, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “You might lose your head, you know, for the second time.”

I began to doubt whether I had been one too many for Sergeant Cuff, after all.

A knock came, and the cook’s message followed it: Rosanna Spearman had asked to go out—her head was bad, she said; she wanted a breath of fresh air.

At the Sergeant’s sign, I said yes.

“Which is the servants’ way out?” he asked, when the messenger had gone.

I showed him.

“Lock the door of your room,” says he; “and if anybody asks for me, say I’m in there, composing my mind.”

The faint curl showed again—and he was gone.

Left alone with my own thoughts, curiosity ate at me like hunger at an empty table. The Sergeant’s suspicion of Rosanna had not come from nothing; he had found some taste of guilt somewhere in my room while he questioned them. Now, the only two who had been under his hand for any length of time—besides Rosanna—were my lady’s maid and the first housemaid, the very pair who had led the persecution of the poor creature from the first.

So I went where women’s truths are most likely to rise—over tea. In the servants’ hall I found the tray set, the pot steaming, and the first cups already pouring out that strong brown comfort which loosens tongues as surely as it warms fingers. I invited myself without ceremony. A drop of tea, mind you, is to a woman’s tongue what a drop of oil is to a lamp that’s burning low.

My reliance on the tea-pot did not fail me. Within half an hour I knew as much as the Sergeant.

Neither my lady’s maid nor the housemaid had believed Rosanna’s illness the day before. Those two—devils, if you like; what other word fits women who feed on another woman’s misery?—had stolen upstairs again and again through Thursday afternoon. They had tried Rosanna’s door and found it locked; they had knocked and got no answer; they had listened and heard no sound. When Rosanna came down to tea and was sent up again, still out of sorts, they tried once more. Locked again. They looked at the keyhole and found it stopped. At midnight they saw a light under the door. At four in the morning they heard the crackling of a fire—a fire in a servant’s bedroom in the month of June.

All this they had told Sergeant Cuff. And he, in return for their eager spite, had eyed them sourly and suspiciously and showed them plainly he did not believe either one or the other. Hence the offended virtue in their reports when they came out. Hence, too, how freely they served the story to me now, delighted to have their injuries heard, never dreaming how their malice had made a ladder for the Sergeant to climb.

Knowing Cuff’s roundabout ways, and having last seen him bent on slipping after Rosanna when she went out, I could see why he had not let the two women know how much they had helped him. If he had treated their evidence as gold, they would have strutted with it, and something in their talk or triumph would have put Rosanna on her guard.

I went out into the fine summer afternoon with a weight on me, sorry for the girl and uneasy at the turn events had taken. The estate had its own steady household music—the rustle of leaves, the far-off sheep, the softened footfalls on turf—and yet beneath it the house’s new noise persisted: the invisible clatter of suspicion, harsher than any kitchen racket.

Later, drifting as my feet always drifted when my mind had no rest, I found myself near the shrubbery. There I met Mr. Franklin, just returned from seeing his cousin off at the station. He had been with my lady, talking long; she had told him of Miss Rachel’s refusal to let her wardrobe be examined. Whatever she had said had worked on him: he looked as if the air had turned heavy in his lungs. The family temper showed in his face that evening for the first time since I had known him—a look that belongs to old blood and old pride, like an inherited scar.

“Well, Betteredge,” he said, “how does the atmosphere of mystery and suspicion in which we are all living now, agree with you? Do you remember that morning when I first came here with the Moonstone? I wish to God we had thrown it into the quicksand!”

After that outburst he held his tongue, mastering himself. We walked side by side in silence for a minute or two—only the birds speaking above us, and the faint wash of wind through firs like the sea practising at a distance. Then he asked where Sergeant Cuff was.

I could not put him off with any tale of “composing his mind.” So I told him exactly what had happened, and I mentioned, in particular, what the maid and the housemaid had said about Rosanna.

Mr. Franklin’s clear head took the turn of it in an instant.

“Didn’t you tell me this morning,” he said, “that one of the tradespeople declared he had met Rosanna yesterday, on the footway to Frizinghall, when we supposed her to be ill in her room?”

“Yes, sir.”

“If my aunt’s maid and the other woman have spoken the truth, you may depend upon it the tradesman did meet her. The girl’s attack of illness was a blind to deceive us. She had some guilty reason for going to the town secretly. The paint-stained dress is a dress of hers; and the fire heard crackling in her room at four in the morning was a fire lit to destroy it. Rosanna Spearman has stolen the Diamond. I’ll go in directly, and tell my aunt the turn things have taken.”

“Not just yet, if you please, sir,” said a melancholy voice behind us.

We turned, and there stood Sergeant Cuff.

“Why not just yet?” asked Mr. Franklin.

“Because, sir, if you tell her ladyship, her ladyship will tell Miss Verinder.”

“Suppose she does. What then?”

There was heat in Mr. Franklin’s words, sudden and sharp, as if the Sergeant’s caution had struck some hidden sore. The garden seemed to listen; even the breeze fell quiet for a second, and the fountain’s thin voice went on alone.

“Do you think it’s wise, sir,” said Sergeant Cuff—quiet as ever—“to put such a question as that to me, at such a time as this?”

A moment passed. Mr. Franklin stepped close. The two looked straight at each other, as men do when they have both hit the same truth from opposite sides and neither wants to name it first.

Mr. Franklin spoke, dropping his voice.

“I suppose you know, Mr. Cuff, that you are treading on delicate ground?”

“It isn’t the first time, by a good many hundreds, that I find myself treading on delicate ground,” the Sergeant answered, immovable.

“I am to understand that you forbid me to tell my aunt what has happened?”

“You are to understand, if you please, sir, that I throw up the case, if you tell Lady Verinder, or tell anybody, what has happened, until I give you leave.”

That settled it. Mr. Franklin had no choice but to swallow his anger. He turned away—white with it—and left us.

I stood trembling, not knowing whom to suspect next or what to think. Still, two things were plain as salt on the tongue. First: my young lady, in some unaccountable manner, lay at the bottom of those sharp speeches. Second: they understood each other perfectly, without having exchanged a word of explanation that I had heard.

“Mr. Betteredge,” says the Sergeant, “you have done a very foolish thing in my absence. You have done a little detective business on your own account. For the future, perhaps you will be so obliging as to do your detective business along with me.”

He took me by the arm and walked me away along the road he had come by. I dare say I deserved the rebuke. But I was not going to help him set traps for Rosanna Spearman for all that. Thief or no thief, legal or not legal, I pitied her—pitied her as you pity any creature who has grown up starved of kindness and then been blamed for snatching at it when it passes.

“What do you want of me?” I asked, stopping short and freeing my arm.

“Only a little information about the country round here,” he said.

That much I could give without betraying anybody.

“Is there any path, in that direction, leading to the sea-beach from this house?” he asked, pointing towards the fir-plantation—the dark straight ranks that led, as everybody hereabouts knew, to the Shivering Sand.

“Yes,” I said. “There is a path.”

“Show it to me.”

So, side by side, in the grey of the summer evening—when the birds quieted and the world’s sounds sank as if the day’s bustle were being covered like a dish to keep it warm—Sergeant Cuff and I set forth for the Shivering Sand.
