“Now my cousins are at Diplow,” Grandcourt said, as if he were speaking of weather that could be altered by raising an eyebrow. “Will you go there—to-morrow? The carriage shall come for Mrs. Davilow. You can tell me what you would like done in the rooms. Things must be put in decent order while we are away at Ryelands. And to-morrow is the only day.”

Rain made a thin, steady music on the panes at Offendene—no harsh battering, only that small, persistent tapping which turns a house inward and magnifies each footfall, each rustle of silk, each drawn breath into something audible and intimate. The hearth answered with its low crackle, the coals shifting like a creature settling itself; yet beyond these domestic sounds there was the duller, more modern undertone: the far wheel on the road, the distant hammering that had become the countryside’s new pulse, reminding one how noise creeps outward from towns as surely as smoke.

Grandcourt sat sideways on the sofa, arranged as if ease were a commandment. One arm lay along the back; the other hand was thrust between his crossed knees, a posture meant to suggest attention without yielding any of his own repose. He watched Gwendolen as if she were a spectacle he had paid for and meant to enjoy without interruption.

She had taken up embroidery since her engagement—an occupation that pretended to patience and purity. The white cloth lay like an altar-piece across her lap, and the thread shone, obedient; but the stitches, were anyone to study them closely, betrayed hurry and little acts of rebellion—false turns, uneven tensions, the secret signature of a hand that did not love submission. Still, she made a show of application, and that show had its own advantage: it gave her something to do with her fingers while her mind ran on other tracks.

The last eight or nine days had been chiefly horseback—wind, leather, the living heat of a horse under you, and the clean, fluting whistle of air past the ears. Even now, kept indoors by rain, she could feel that remembered canter in her blood. Yet there had always been, between rides, this other companionship—harder, more delicate, and in some ways more dangerous.

She had been well satisfied with Grandcourt, as one is satisfied with a dish that is rich, costly, and never surprises the palate. He answered her lively questions with a drawl that seemed, in his mouth, not dullness but mastery: the leisure of a man for whom time had always waited. From the first she had felt that he knew how to speak—how to make his knowledge a kind of possession, and his contempt a kind of gift. He implied, without saying it, that folly belonged to other people, those who busied themselves, exerted themselves, wanted things too openly. A man who had always been served the best could, with a slight movement of tone, make the best seem a thing too common to care about. And his homage as a lover had hovered near her rather than pressed upon her: inobtrusive as the scent of roses carried in from another room—pleasant, flattering, and leaving her vanity warmed.

Once only he had startled her: not a kiss on the cheek, which would have been a familiar courtesy, but on the neck, just below the ear. The touch had been like a sudden cold drop on the skin. She started up, heart leaping, and he rose too with a smoothness that had nothing of apology’s clumsiness.

“I beg your pardon—did I annoy you?”

“Oh, it was nothing,” she had said, trying to laugh away what she could not name without confessing too much. “Only I cannot bear—to be kissed under my ear.”

She sat down again, playful on the surface; but inside she felt the pulse of fear. With Rex she had been free to flout, to toy, to withdraw; with this man—this engagement—there was no such liberty. The agitation had seemed to please him; he did not transgress again.

To-day, the rain kept them from riding; but a package had come up from London, and Mrs. Davilow had just left the room after bringing in the beautiful things Grandcourt had ordered—soft fabrics, pale gleams of jewelry, all the shining apparatus of a new life laid out like confections on the tables. Gwendolen enjoyed, for a moment, the scenery of her own existence as if she were looking at it from a height: the wealth, the attention, the inevitability.

She let her hands fall on her lap and said, with a pretty perversity that tasted sweet on her tongue,

“Why is to-morrow the only day?”

“Because the next day is the first with the hounds,” said Grandcourt.

“And after that?”

“After that I must go away for a couple of days—it’s a bore—but I shall go one day and come back the next.”

A slight change crossed her face—quick as a shadow when a cloud passes the sun. He noticed it; he always noticed what touched his possession. He released his hand from under his knees and laid it over hers, the pressure cool and certain.

“You object to my going away?”

“It’s no use objecting,” Gwendolen said coldly. The answer was true, but not in the way she meant. She was holding back—by a force that was half pride and half terror—from saying she suspected where he went, and to whom.

“Yes it is,” he said, folding her hand as if it belonged there. “I will put off going. And I will travel at night, so as only to be away one day.”

He thought he knew the reason of her temper; and because he thought he knew, it amused him. And because it amused him, it pleased him—she was more fascinating in her resistance than in her assent.

“Then don’t put off going, but travel at night,” she said, tasting the small pleasure of command. The peremptoriness was a vent; it let out a little of the bitter heat in her.

“Then you will go to Diplow to-morrow?”

“Oh yes, if you wish it,” she said, in a high, careless tone. Her attention was so knotted in other feelings that she had hardly taken in that her hand was being held.

“How you treat us poor devils of men!” said Grandcourt, lowering his voice, shaping complaint into compliment. “We are always getting the worst of it.”

“Are you?” Gwendolen asked, with a note of inquiry. She looked at him more naively than usual, because she longed to believe this easy jest as a deep truth. If men always had the worst, then her own uneasiness could pass for virtue. Then Mrs. Glasher—dark, suffering, unmanageable in memory—might be made more to blame than he.

“Are you always getting the worst?”

“Yes. Are you as kind to me as I am to you?” he asked, and his narrow gaze held hers with the steadiness of a man who expects surrender.

She felt struck, as if a string inside her had been snapped and another tightened in its place. She was conscious—painfully—of how much she had received: gifts, deference, the weight of a name bending toward her. Her sense of command faltered. It was as if she had mounted a chariot and discovered, too late, that another held the reins. She could not leap down in sight of the world; she would not. She had not consented in ignorance. Anything she said now would be a confession that she had known enough to be guilty. Her right to demand explanations was gone; she had spent it when she accepted.

A little shiver ran through her mind, and she changed her inner posture as one changes a pin that pricks. There was a pause; she did not look away. Then she broke into a smile, sudden as the turning of a page.

“If I were as kind to you as you are to me, that would spoil your generosity,” she said lightly. “It would no longer be as great as it could be—and it is that now.”

“Then I am not to ask for one kiss,” Grandcourt returned, content to pay a large price for this new softness that carried marriage in its perfume.

“Not one?” She nodded at him, saucy and defiant.

He lifted her left hand to his lips and released it—an act that was respectful enough to be almost tender, and restrained enough to be powerful. If it was faint praise to say he was not disgusting, it was also true that he could be—at moments—almost charming. She had a swift sense that she might never have loved another man better than this one. His reticence created in her an inexplicable, delicious consciousness—as if something were being withheld, and in being withheld became precious.

“Apropos,” she said, taking up her work again, “is there any one besides Captain and Mrs. Torrington at Diplow?—or do you leave them tête-à-tête? I suppose he converses in cigars, and she answers with her chignon.”

“She has a sister with her,” said Grandcourt, the shadow of a smile passing, “and there are two men besides—one of them you know, I believe.”

“Ah, then I have a poor opinion of him,” Gwendolen said, shaking her head.

“You saw him at Leubronn—young Deronda—a young fellow with the Mallingers.”

At the name her heart made a sudden, involuntary leap, like a fish breaking water. Her fingers, trying to keep their hold on the thread, went cold.

“I never spoke to him,” she said, fearing her own face. “Is he not disagreeable?”

“No, not particularly,” Grandcourt replied in his languid way. “He thinks a little too much of himself. I thought he had been introduced to you.”

“No. Some one told me his name the evening before I came away. That was all. What is he?”

“A sort of ward of Sir Hugo Mallinger’s. Nothing of any consequence.”

“Oh, poor creature! How very unpleasant for him!” She spoke without sarcasm; it sounded like pity and meant, in a strange way, admiration—the pity one gives to a fine dish served without a proper plate. “I wonder if it has left off raining!” she added, rising to the window.

The next day, happily, was dry. Gwendolen rode to Diplow on Criterion, as she had ridden before, and the riding-dress made her bolder, as armor does: it gave her a sense of competence and beauty at once, like a well-made crust that holds its filling without spilling. She believed she looked as well as possible in it, and that belief was sustaining.

Her anger toward Deronda had changed. It was no longer heat; it was dread—superstitious, intimate, as if the first sharp interference of his glance had planted a seed of future power. Such forebodings are brewed from intense self-feeling: they make the evening star seem to look back with threat, and a beggar’s blessing sound like prophecy. And then, because we carry them, we enact them; superstition becomes fate by our own hands.

Before luncheon she went through the rooms with Mrs. Torrington and Mrs. Davilow, attending to arrangements, order, the visible domestic stage on which the great life must appear. She thought that if she saw Deronda, a mere bow would be all; she meant to notice him as little as possible.

But from the first moment they were in the same room, she seemed to herself to do nothing but notice him. Everything else became automatic: her smile, her words, the turning of her head. She was a practiced actress, yet felt the audience had changed into a single pair of eyes.

At lunch, as Deronda took his place, Grandcourt said, “Deronda, Miss Harleth tells me you were not introduced to her at Leubronn?”

“Miss Harleth hardly remembers me, I imagine,” Deronda said, looking at her simply as they bowed. “She was intensely occupied when I saw her.”

Did he suppose she had not suspected him—the man who had redeemed her necklace? The thought pricked her like a needle through too-thin fabric.

“On the contrary. I remember you very well,” she said, nervous but governing herself, studying him with a fresh, hungry scrutiny. “You did not approve of my playing at roulette.”

“How did you come to that conclusion?” he asked gravely.

“Oh, you cast an evil eye on my play.” She turned her head with a smile, trying to make jest a shield. “I began to lose as soon as you came to look on. I had always been winning till then.”

“Roulette in such a kennel as Leubronn is a horrid bore,” said Grandcourt.

“I found it a bore when I began to lose,” Gwendolen returned. She faced Grandcourt as she spoke, but cut a sidelong glance at Deronda—and met his eyes fixed on her with a grave penetration that had a keener edge than irony, keener even than Klesmer’s judgments. She wheeled her neck, pretending to listen to others, while in truth she listened inwardly to the disturbance Deronda made.

His face had that corrective power—form and expression that threaten to alter one’s standard, as if one had been living with a false measure. She had known such faces: they chastise without speaking. Often they lose their power by some foolish word; but his silence, his steadiness, maintained it. And his voice—heard now for the first time—fell into the room like deeper strings among thin clucking treble: to Grandcourt’s toneless drawl, so familiar now, it was like the violoncello under the broken discourse of poultry in sun.

Grandcourt, she thought, might be right in saying Deronda thought too much of himself—an easy way to explain any superiority that humiliates.

The talk moved on—rinderpest and Jamaica—light darts of opinion shot across the table. Grandcourt spoke of the Jamaica negro as a beastly baptist Caliban; Deronda said he had always felt a little with Caliban, who naturally had his own point of view and could sing a good song. Mrs. Davilow mentioned her father’s estate in Barbadoes and her own ignorance of the West Indies; Mrs. Torrington declared she would never sleep in her bed among blacks; Captain Torrington corrected her by saying the blacks would be manageable enough if it were not for the half-breeds; and Deronda quietly remarked that the whites had to thank themselves for the half-breeds.

While this polite pea-shooting went on, Gwendolen trifled with her jelly. Its tremor under the spoon, the sweet cold on her tongue, seemed absurdly out of keeping with the inward strain. She looked from speaker to speaker, as if to make it natural that she should look at Deronda.

All the while questions ran under her thoughts like an underground stream you hear but cannot see.

What did he think of her—really? He must have been interested in her, or he would not have sent back the necklace. What did he think of her marriage? Why was he so grave about everything? Why was he here at Diplow?

And why did she care so much about the opinion of this man who was “nothing of any consequence”? She had no leisure to answer; she was too busy caring.

In the drawing-room afterward, when something called Grandcourt away, she found herself moving—without forethought—toward Deronda, who stood at a table apart turning over prints. The room had its own hush: the soft brush of skirts, the paper’s faint rasp beneath his fingers, the clock’s measured insistence. Compared with the coarse roaring of public places—railways, streets, the new iron life—this quiet was like a chapel for the ear, each sound distinct and therefore meaningful.

“Shall you hunt to-morrow, Mr. Deronda?” she asked.

“Yes, I believe so.”

“You don’t object to hunting, then?”

“I find excuses for it. It is a sin I am inclined to—when I can’t get boating or cricketing.”

“Do you object to my hunting?” she said, lifting her chin saucily, as if daring him to judge.

“I have no right to object to anything you choose to do.”

“You thought you had a right to object to my gambling,” she persisted.

“I was sorry for it. I am not aware that I told you of my objection,” he replied, meeting her with that directness of gaze—large-eyed gravity, without any visible intention to wound. His eyes had the dark mild intensity that seems, to those it rests on, like a special interest; and such seeming invites claims, as beggars make a reason out of another’s goodness.

“You hindered me from gambling again,” she said.

The words were out—and instantly she blushed, heat rushing over face and neck. Deronda blushed too, as if struck by the memory of the questionable freedom he had taken in the affair of the necklace.

No further words could follow. She turned away to the window, feeling she had said what she had not meant to say—yet oddly happy that she had plunged into mutual understanding. It was as if a secret ingredient had been named aloud; the dish could never again taste as it had before.

Deronda, too, did not dislike it. Gwendolen seemed more decidedly attractive than before; and in truth, changes had been going on within her since Leubronn. Struggle, and the knowledge of error, had wakened something like a new soul in her—one with better possibilities, but also worse. Among the forces she had come to dread was something within herself that disturbed satisfaction.

That evening, in the quieter part of the house—when the sounds had settled into night’s economy, the servants moving with muffled steps, the wind sounding in the trees—Mrs. Davilow said, gently curious,

“Was it really so, or only a joke of yours, about Mr. Deronda’s spoiling your play, Gwen?”

“It merely happened that he was looking on when I began to lose,” Gwendolen said carelessly. “I noticed him.”

“I don’t wonder at that: he is a striking young man,” Mrs. Davilow went on, pleased with her own impression. “He puts me in mind of Italian paintings. One would guess, without being told, that there was foreign blood in his veins.”

“Is there?” Gwendolen asked.

“Mrs. Torrington says so. I asked particularly who he was, and she told me that his mother was some foreigner of high rank.”

“His mother?” Gwendolen said sharply, as if the word itself were a knife turned. “Then who was his father?”

“Well—every one says he is the son of Sir Hugo Mallinger, who brought him up; though he passes for a ward. She says, if Sir Hugo Mallinger could have done as he liked with his estates, he would have left them to this Mr. Deronda, since he has no legitimate son.”

Gwendolen was silent. Her mother saw the effect in her face—too marked to miss—and regretted having repeated gossip. It was knowledge of the world, the kind she disliked for her daughter; and she wished, suddenly, that she herself had never been forced to learn such things, never had them pressed into her mind by the dominant tongues that decide which lives may be spoken of openly and which must be murmured, half-erased.

In Gwendolen’s imagination, an image had risen at once: the unknown mother—a dark-eyed woman, surely—perhaps sad, perhaps silenced. Hardly any face could be less like Deronda’s than Sir Hugo’s crayon portrait at Diplow. A dark-eyed woman, no longer young, became “stuff o’ the conscience” to Gwendolen.

That night, when she had got into her little bed and only a dim light burned, she lay listening to the house—the settling beams, the faint creak of a stair as if the building itself had a memory and shifted in its sleep. Then she said, abruptly,

“Mamma, have men generally children before they are married?”

“No, dear, no,” Mrs. Davilow answered quickly. “Why do you ask such a question?” But her heart, like a hand, began to grope for the reason.

“If it were so, I ought to know,” Gwendolen said, indignation lending her voice a sharpness.

“You are thinking of what I said about Mr. Deronda and Sir Hugo Mallinger. That is a very unusual case, dear.”

“Does Lady Mallinger know?”

“She knows enough to satisfy her. That is quite clear, because Mr. Deronda has lived with them.”

“And people think no worse of him?”

“Well, of course he is under some disadvantage: it is not as if he were Lady Mallinger’s son. He does not inherit the property, and he is not of any consequence in the world. But people are not obliged to know anything about his birth; you see, he is very well received.”

“I wonder whether he knows about it,” Gwendolen said, “and whether he is angry with his father?”

“My dear child, why should you think of that?”

“Why?” Gwendolen burst out, sitting up. “Haven’t children reason to be angry with their parents? How can they help their parents marrying or not marrying?”

But at once another consciousness rushed over her, and she fell back upon the pillow. It was not only the old, half-formed fear she once would have had—that she might seem to reproach her mother for her second marriage. What seized her now was the sense that she had been led into condemning something that made her own marriage look forbidden. A word spoken about another life had turned, like an echo in a cavern, and struck her back.

There was no more talk. She lay, long, arguing within herself against the reasons that pressed on her now with new force—reasons mirrored unexpectedly in the story of a man whose slight relation to her had bitten deep into the most permanent layers of feeling. Yet, characteristic of her crude debating, she was never troubled by the question whether the indefensibleness of her marriage included the fact that she accepted Grandcourt simply because it was convenient, not because she meant to bind herself in duty to him. Her ideas were raw; but life often thrusts its grand difficulties upon us when we are rawest, and we feel them with a simple pain that wiser people disguise.

Morning brought a double excitement. She was going to hunt—scruples about propriety had threatened to stop her until it was found that Mrs. Torrington was horsewoman enough to accompany her. It would be her first hunt since the escapade with Rex. And she was going to see Deronda again, in whom her interest had gathered during the night until she expected—like those who meet a revealed celebrity—to find something new in his face, some sign she had missed before.

What was he going to be? What sort of life had he before him—he being nothing of any consequence? With only a little difference in events he might have been as important as Grandcourt; indeed her imagination, inevitably, went further: he might have held the very estates Grandcourt was to have. Yet now Deronda would likely, someday, see her as mistress of the Abbey at Topping, bearing the title that might have been his wife’s. These futile thoughts of what might have been made a new epoch in her. She, who had always taken the best that came to her as less than her claim, now saw the position that tempted her in a new light—as a hard exclusion of others. What she had heard about Deronda threw him, in her mind, into one group with Mrs. Glasher and her children—before whom she felt herself apologetic, she who had hitherto been surrounded by people she thought needed to apologize to her.

Perhaps Deronda knew of Mrs. Glasher. If he knew that she knew, he would despise her; but he could not know that. Would he, without that, despise her for marrying Grandcourt? His possible judgment pressed on her as importunately as Klesmer’s judgment of her powers; but she found more room to resist disapproval of her conduct than disapproval of her talent. Conduct can always plead, How can I help it? Incompetence cannot. And she said to herself, fiercely,

“How can I help what other people have done? Things would not come right if I were to turn round now and declare I would not marry Mr. Grandcourt.”

Turning round was impossible. The horses in the chariot she had mounted were going at full speed.

This mood—youthful, elated, desperate—returned like a tide. She could dare anything ahead sooner than go backward into humiliation; and it was almost soothing to think there would be ill-doing either way. But the immediate, exquisite fact was the hunt—wind on her face, the cry, the drum of hooves on turf—and there Deronda would see her as she would see him.

For a long while, the hunt absorbed everything. Deronda was there, often within sight, and that only sharpened the pleasure. It was a delight she had tasted only once before and felt would always renew itself, independent of crosses except those that stole the chance of riding. No accident threw them together. The run kept them within reach of home, and under the agreeable sombreness of the gray November afternoon—where a long stratum of yellow light lay in the west like a slow-burning ember—Gwendolen returned with the company from Diplow, attended on her way to Offendene.

Now that the glorious excitement was spent, irritation took its place. She was disappointed—childishly, fiercely—that she had had no opportunity to speak to Deronda. She would not see him again; he was to go away in a couple of days. What did she want to say? She did not quite know. But she wanted to hear him answer; she wanted the sound of his voice shaped toward her.

Grandcourt rode by her side; Mrs. Torrington, her husband, and another gentleman were a little in advance; and behind, she could hear Deronda’s horse—hoofs soft on the path, nearer than the voices ahead, like a persistent thought. They had lately come out of a wood of pines and beeches where twilight stillness had pressed down on impatience, and each sound had stood out—the birds’ last thin notes, the horses’ breathing, the creak of leather—making the world feel hushed enough for conscience to be audible.

The hoofbeats behind grew into a torment. Gwendolen reined in and looked back. Grandcourt, after a few paces, paused too. Then, waving her whip and nodding sideways with playful imperiousness, she said,

“Go on! I want to speak to Mr. Deronda.”
