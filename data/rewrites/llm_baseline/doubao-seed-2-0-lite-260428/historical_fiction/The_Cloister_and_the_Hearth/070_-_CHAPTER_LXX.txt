One of the young novice Gerard’s self-imposed penances was to greet Lodovico with open kindness each time they met, even as every instinct in him recoiled as if he were hosting a cold, slimy serpent dragged from the river mud. Never was an act of self-denial more wisely chosen; and like all thoughtful, purposeful penance, it soon ceased to feel like a burden at all.

At first, the pride and self-satisfied complacency the erstwhile assassin bore as he looked upon the one life he had been paid to take, and spared, was as absurd as it was pathetic. But to pry open a crack for goodness in a hardened heart is no small feat: one good act soon tugs another through that open door. Lodovico discovered that the sweetness of saving a life far outstripped the thrill of taking one, and the miscreant grew first averse to spilling blood, then racked by remorse, until at last he drew near to full, heartfelt penitence. Teresa aided this shift immeasurably: she had threatened to leave him a dozen times before, and now renewed her ultimatum that he must at last consent to live an honest, peaceful life. The good fathers of the convent lent their support, and soon Lodovico and Teresa were sent by sea to Leghorn, where Teresa had long-standing family ties. There the former assassin settled down and took work as a porter.

He complained loudly at first that the slow, plodding drudgery of hauling heavy loads all day was miserably dull work. But that unglamorous, quiet life of honest labor was far kinder to him than the short, sharp excitement of being hewn limb from limb by Princess Claelia’s vengeful myrmidons would have been. His quiet exile saved the unaware penitent from that brutal fate.

Balked of the revenge she had hungered for, the princess sank into brooding, and soon fell into a deep, consuming melancholy. She dismissed her old confessor, and sought out a new one famed across Italy for his unflinching, uncompromising piety. That new confessor was none other than Fra Jerome, and she could not have fallen into better hands.

He heard her pour out what she called her “griefs” with grim, unsmiling attention, then took her by the shoulders and shook every last trace of her self-serving delusion out of her as roughly as he would rouse a napping kitchen maid. To do this harsh, unyielding monk justice, he feared the wrath of princes no more than he feared a storm at sea when he walked the path of his duty. In a few short, thunderous, lightning-sharp words he laid bare that she was the guiltiest sinner of all.
“Thou art no better than the devil’s proxy: thou hast used thy gold to tempt a man to slay his fellow, and then, blinded by thine own selfish vanity, thou refusest to blame and punish thyself, and now thou thirstest after more blood—the blood of a guilty man, but one a hundred times less guilty than thou.”

At first she resisted, snapping that no confessor had ever dared rebuke her so, that she was a princess unused to such rough treatment. But he overpowered every excuse she made, threatened her unflinchingly with the Church’s curse on earth and in the afterlife, tore the scales clean from her eyes, thundered at her until her pride crumbled to dust, and she sank grovelling at the feet of that giant, fiery Boanerges, wracked with remorse and terror.
“Oh holy father, have pity on a poor weak woman, help me save my guilty soul! I wandered in total darkness for want of true ghostly counsel such as thine, good father. I wake now as from a terrible, long dream.”

“Doff thy jewels,” Fra Jerome said sternly.
“I will. I will.”
“Doff thy silk and velvet. Clothe thyself in raiment plainer than that worn by the meanest servant in thy household, and wend thou instantly to Loretto.”
“I will,” the princess whispered faintly.
“No shoes. Thou shalt wear nothing but a single rough sandal on thy feet.”
“Yes father.”
“Thou shalt wash the feet of every pilgrim thou meetest, both going and coming on the road. And to every holy friar thou dost encounter, thou shalt lay bare thy sin, and abide humbly by their admonition.”
“Oh holy father, let me wear my mask!”
“Hmph.”
“Oh mercy! Bethink thee! My features are known through every corner of Italy.”
“Aye. Beauty is a curse to the lot of you vain souls. Well then: thou mayst mask thine eyes, and no more.”

Grateful for even that small concession, she reached to take his hand to kiss it, but he snatched it rudely away.
“What would ye do? That very hand handled the Eucharist but an hour agone. Is it fit for a sinner such as thou to defile it with thy touch?”
“Ah no. But oh, go not without granting thy penitent daughter thy blessing.”
“Time enow to ask for it when thou comest back from Loretto, truly penitent.”

That strange, fateful scene on the banks of the Tiber left its indelible mark on every soul it touched, as great prodigies are said to do. The assassin, softened by the life he had been paid to kill and chose to spare, laid his stiletto down forever and took up the heavy porter’s knot. The proud princess walked barefoot all the way to Loretto, weeping over her crime as she washed the calloused feet of base-born pilgrims. And Gerard, who had been carried half-suicidal out of the Tiber and into the convent, was soon regarded within its walls as a young living saint.

Loving, experienced eyes watched over his progress, and his probation was cut far shorter than custom dictated before he was admitted to priest’s orders. Not long after, he took his final monastic vows, and became a friar of St. Dominic. Dying to all the world, the monk parted with the very name he had borne in his old earthly life, snapping the last lingering link that bound him to worldly memories and attachments. The man Gerard was gone. Brother Clement had begun.
