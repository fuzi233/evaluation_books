It nettled Oleron that Miss Bengough barely spared a glance for the flat he had found so strangely enchanting. But then, Elsie had always been like that—indifferent to charm, careless of aesthetics, more herself gnawing biscuits from a crumpled paper bag than dining with any pretense of decorum. At thirty-four, she was a journalist of some success, brash and florid, pink as a peony, with a habit of expansive gestures and sudden, moist exclamations. She earned more than Oleron did, and through cunningly disguised endorsements, she acquired most of her flamboyant wardrobe. She moved in a whirl of veils and scarves, leaving behind gusts of perfumed air.

When she arrived at his door a month into his tenancy, her skirts rustled like dry leaves on the staircase. A single sharp rap announced her. She barged in, trailing the scent of the city, and tossed a bundle of ladies’ journals onto a chair.

“Don’t stop on my account,” she said around a mouthful of hatpins as she stripped off her hat and veil. “Wasn’t sure if you’d settled in yet, so I brought sandwiches. You’ve got coffee, I assume? No, stay put—I’ll find the kitchen—”

“No need, I’ll clear this away. Truthfully, I’m glad for the interruption,” Oleron admitted, gathering his scattered pages.

She was already filling the kettle. Ten minutes later, they sat with coffee and sandwiches between them.

“Well, what do you think of the place?” Oleron asked as she poured.

She gave a short hum. “Feels like you’re preparing for a wife, Paul.”

He chuckled. “Hardly. But it’s an improvement, don’t you think?”

“Is it? Suppose so. I liked the last one, black ceiling and all. How’s *Romilly*?”

Oleron rubbed his jaw. “Ah. Not proud to admit it, but I’ve hit a snag. Still, it’ll come right in the end, as you always say.”

“Stuck?”

“Thoroughly.”

“Anything you’d care to read to me?”

It was their old habit—Oleron sharing fragments of his work, Elsie offering blunt, practical feedback. She, in turn, never spoke of her own writing. His was *real* work, she insisted; hers merely filled columns.

“Afraid not,” he said, still stroking his chin. Then, with rare candor: “Truth is, Elsie, I haven’t written—actually written—much more. Doesn’t mean I haven’t progressed. In some ways, I’ve gone too far. I’m thinking of tearing it all apart.”

Elsie gasped. “*Tearing it apart?*”

“Rebuilding Romilly entirely. She’s not working anymore. I’ve lost interest in her as she is.”

“But—” Elsie sputtered, “she was *alive*, Paul! You made her real!”

He smiled faintly. Of course Elsie liked Romilly as she was—there was too much of *her* in the character.

“Are you serious?” she demanded, eyes wide.

“Quite.”

“You’d scrap fifteen chapters?”

“Not lightly. Only if it served something better.”

“Even the love scene? The shore description?”

“They wouldn’t be wasted,” he said, uneasy now.

Elsie flung up her hands. “You’re impossible! Must you always hover above mere mortals? It wouldn’t *lower* you to write something humans can grasp! To destroy those chapters would be criminal—*wasted* talent! Think practically. You’ve worked twenty years; you’re nearly there. You’re running out of money, and now you want to chuck the one thing that might make your name for some indulgent experiment? You infuriate me!”

Oleron shook his head. This was an old argument. Elsie was a splendid friend—until she hit the limits of her understanding. She saw genius as a measurable ingredient, something to be added or subtracted at will. She didn’t grasp that it was the *essence*, the *core*. They diverged there. He knew it. She didn’t.

“Yes, yes,” he sighed. “Practically, you’re right. If I handed *Romilly* to you, you’d make a success of her. But I can’t, and I’m beginning to doubt she’s worth my time.”

“What does *that* mean?”

“When something isn’t worth doing,” he said with a thin smile, “you don’t do it.”

Elsie’s gaze shot to the ceiling, as if begging patience. “Rubbish! Last I saw you, *Romilly* was pouring out—four chapters a week! If you hadn’t moved, she’d be three-quarters done. Why uproot in the middle of your most important work?”

He offered excuses—inconveniences, mostly—but she dismissed them. Perhaps she guessed the truth: he was sick of his shabby existence. Twenty years of garrets and dingy rooms had worn him down. The reward, if it existed, felt hollow now.

“At any rate,” he said, “I’m happier here than I’ve been in years. That counts for something.”

“And doing no work,” Elsie retorted.

A spark of irritation flared in him. “Why must I do nothing *but* work? What joy has it brought me? I love it when it’s *done*, but the doing is torture. Once in a blue moon, there’s a flicker of the old thrill. Now I’m forty-four, and it’s drudgery. No one wants it. *I* don’t want it. If any sane man asked if I was a fool to continue, I’d agree.”

Elsie’s face grew solemn. “You knew that long ago, Paul—and you chose it anyway.”

“Did I? Or was I told so? Youth *thinks* it knows. Then one day you wake at forty-four—”

“Forty-four,” she corrected.

“—and realize the glamour was behind you all along. A costly choice, youth.”

Her eyes dropped. “You don’t regret it?”

“Lately? I *do*. What do I have to show for it?”

“You know what you have.”

Her tone betrayed what else he might have had—her. Had he ever asked, she would have said, simply, *When?* He never had.

“Yours is the real work,” she said softly. “Without you, the rest of us couldn’t exist. You carry it all.”

For a moment, silence hung between them. Then Oleron laughed abruptly, chiding himself for the self-pity.

“Sorry you caught me like this, Elsie,” he said, stacking the tray. “Let’s take a walk.”

As they toured the flat, she paused at the kitchen, eyeing the faded red fabric.

“What’s this?”

“Ah. I was hoping *you* could tell *me*.” He recounted its discovery in the window seat.

“It’s harp wrapping,” she said. “For packing into its case.”

“Of course,” he murmured. “I couldn’t place it.”

Back in the sitting room, she asked, “Who lives in the rest of the house?”

“Tramps in the cellar, perhaps. No one else.”

She studied him. “I’ll tell you what I think.”

“Go on.”

“You’ll never work here.”

His brows rose. “Why not?”

“You won’t finish *Romilly* here. I don’t know why, but you won’t. You’ll leave before it’s done.”

He mused, then smirked. “Isn’t that a little… superstitious?”

“Completely. No logic to it. But there it is.”

He fetched his coat, chuckling. “Then I hope you’re wrong. If *Romilly* isn’t out by autumn, I’ll be in trouble.”
