The express train rattled past the countryside, carrying Arthur Pendennis to Tunbridge with a velocity that seemed to mock the slowness of his soul. As the miles blurred behind him, the young man's mind, usually a fertile garden of schemes, turned barren. He reviewed his life not with pride, but with a grim, self-deprecating humor. "Here," he muttered, staring vacantly at the iron seats, "is the end of hopes and aspirations! Where I yield, I'm miserable; where I obstinately refuse, I'm equally wretched."

He traced the path of his errors with a bitter smile. "I refused an angel," he thought, "because I was stubborn. If I'd taken her, Laura wouldn't have become the angel I needed. I couldn't have loved her properly if someone else had to explain her virtues to me. Instead, I listened to my uncle's wheedling, accepted Blanche on guarantee, and traded a heart for a dowry. And look at the result!" He laughed softly, a dry, hollow sound. "Fortune arrived, left me the wife without the cash, and stripped me of the 'heart' I thought I bought. My poor old uncle is furious—he can't understand my scruples. He's indignant, heart-struck, almost, because I won't take the money. I dissatisfy everyone. I'm a maimed, weak, imperfect wretch, seemingly unequal to any fortune. I make no one happy. What prospect is there for this frivolous girl taking my obscurity? I lack ambition to excite me, self-esteem to console me."

His gaze drifted to the window, then back to the passengers. "If I wrote a book that sold twenty editions," he mused, "I'd be the first to sneer at its reputation. If I succeeded at the Bar, bullying witnesses and twisting evidence, would that fame satisfy me? Is that a life well-spent?"

Beside him sat a stout gentleman holding a newspaper. "Would you care to look at the paper, sir?" the man asked, pointing to a fiery article attacking a black-coated traveler in the compartment.

Arthur nodded politely, took the paper, and resumed his reverie without reading a single line.

"And yet," Arthur pondered, "would you adopt either of those men's creeds, with their consequences?" He sighed deeply. "Ah, me! You must bear your own burden, fashion your own faith, and pray your own prayer. To what mortal ear could I tell all, if I had the tongue? Who can judge another's shortcomings, lost opportunities, or the invisible accidents—a grain of sand, perhaps—that altered the whole current of life? Only One knows the weight of our accounts."

Arthur rested his chin in his hand, his heart falling humbly before an unseen throne of wisdom and pity. "What matters fame or poverty!" he whispered to himself. "If I marry the woman I chose, may I find the strength to be true to her and make her happy? If I have children, pray God teach me to speak the truth. There are no splendours for my marriage, but does my life deserve none? I begin a new phase; may it be better than the last!"

The train hissed to a halt at Tunbridge. Arthur returned the newspaper, stood, and stepped out with a carpet-bag in hand, bracing himself to face his fortune.

A fly dashed him swiftly to Lady Clavering's house. Inside the carriage, Arthur rehearsed a speech, crafting a virtuous, honest, and well-meaning oration that he hoped to deliver to Blanche. *'Blanche,'* he planned to say, *"I cannot guess your meaning from your last letter. I know why I forgo worldly advantages; I feel I would be dishonored by accepting them without cause. If you doubt my affection, let Smirke be called, and let us marry out of hand. With all my heart, I purpose to keep my vow and cherish you through life."*

The carriage door swung open, revealing a domestic who stared at Arthur with surprise. "Her Ladyship's not at home, sir," the man said, ignoring the bag. "Lightfoot is gone."

"I am Mr. Pendennis," Arthur replied firmly. "Where is Lightfoot?"

"Lightfoot is gone, sir. My Lady is out."

Arthur heard a melodic warble issuing from the drawing room. "Take the bag to a dressing room, if you please," he ordered, bypassing the porter and marching straight toward the door. Inside, the air vibrated with music. Miss Amory, the little Siren, sang with all her fascinations at a piano. Beside her sat a gentleman, utterly enraptured by her passionate, melancholy strain. Near the piano, asleep on a sofa, lay Master Clavering, indifferent to the sounds.

As Arthur pushed the door open, the gentleman jumped up with a startled "Hullo!" The music cut off abruptly with a little shriek from the singer. Frank Clavering woke from his nap, and Arthur stepped forward, bowing. "What, Foker! How do you do, Foker?"

Arthur looked at the piano. Resting beside Miss Amory was a purple-leather box, identical to the one Harry had been eyeing in Waterloo Place three days prior. It was open, revealing a magnificent serpentine bracelet with a blazing ruby head and a diamond tail, coiled around a white satin cushion.

"How de-do, Pendennis?" said Foker, his face flushing. Blanche made several shrugging motions, signaling unrest, and covered the bracelet with a handkerchief before advancing to greet Arthur. Her hand trembled violently.

"How is dearest Laura?" she asked.

Foker's face, framed by profound mourning, looked pitiful and puzzled. Frank Clavering, watching the scene with an expression of utmost knowingness, managed only to ejaculate, "Here's a jolly go!" before disappearing with a snigger.

Arthur, restraining himself until that moment, gazed at Foker's burning cheeks and burst into a fit of wild, loud laughter. It terrified Blanche more than any serious display could have.

"And this was the secret, was it?" Arthur gasped, wiping tears from his eyes. "Don't blush and turn away, Foker, my boy. Why, man, you are a pattern of fidelity! Could I stand between Blanche and such constancy? Could I stand between Miss Amory and fifteen thousand a year?"

"It is not that, Mr. Pendennis," Blanche said with great dignity. "It is not money, it is not rank, it is not gold that moves me. It is constancy, it is fidelity, it is a whole trustful loving heart offered to me, that I treasure!" She reached for her handkerchief, hesitated, realizing what lay beneath it, and paused. "I do not disown, I do not disguise—my life is above disguise. To him on whom it is bestowed, my heart must be forever bare. That I once thought I loved you... yes, I thought I was beloved by you, I own! How I clung to that faith! How I strove, I prayed, I longed to believe it! But your conduct—your own words so cold, so heartless, so unkind—have undeceived me. You trifled with the heart of the poor maiden! You flung me back with scorn the troth which I had plighted! I have explained all—to Mr. Foker."

"That you have," said Foker, with devoted conviction.

"What, all?" Arthur asked, casting a meaningful look at Blanche. "Am I in fault? Well, well, Blanche, be it so. I won't appeal against your sentence, and bear it in silence. I came down here looking for very different things, Heaven knows, with a heart most truly and kindly disposed towards you. I hope you may be happy with another, as, on my word, it was my wish to make you so. And I hope my honest old friend here will have a wife worthy of his loyalty, his constancy, and his affection. They deserve the regard of any woman—even Miss Blanche Amory."

Shaking hands, Arthur added, "Don't look askance at me. Has anybody told you that I was a false and heartless character?"

"I think you're a——" Foker began, his wrath rising, but Blanche interceded instantly.

"Henry, not a word! I pray you let there be forgiveness!"

"You're an angel, by Jove, you're an angel!" cried Foker, his eyes shining. At this, Blanche looked up seraphically toward the chandelier.

"In spite of what has passed, for the sake of what has passed, I must always regard Arthur as a brother," the seraph continued. "We have known each other years, trodden the same fields, plucked the same flowers together. Arthur! Henry! I beseech you to take hands and to be friends! Forgive you! I forgive you, Arthur, with my heart I do. Should I not do so for making me so happy?"

"There is only one person of us three whom I pity, Blanche," Arthur said gravely. "And I say to you again, that I hope you will make this good fellow, this honest and loyal creature, happy."
