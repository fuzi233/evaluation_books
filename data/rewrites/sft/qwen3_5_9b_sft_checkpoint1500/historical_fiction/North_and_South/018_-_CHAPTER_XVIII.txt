Upon returning home, Margaret discovered two letters waiting on the table. One was a brief note for her mother; the other, bearing heavy foreign postmarks, was thin, silvery paper that crinkled softly under her touch—it was clearly from her aunt Shaw. As she began to examine it, the front door opened and her father entered abruptly.

"So your mother is tired and retired early!" Mr. Hale exclaimed, his voice tinged with unease. "I'm afraid such a thundery day wasn't conducive to the doctor seeing her. What did he say? Dixon mentioned he'd spoken to you about it."

Margaret hesitated, clutching the letter. Her father's countenance grew grave and anxious.

"He doesn't think she is seriously ill?"

"Not at present," Margaret replied, glancing down at the page. "He says she requires care. He was very kind and promised to return to check on the efficacy of his medicines."

"Care only?" Mr. Hale pressed, his eyes darting nervously. "Did he not suggest a change of air? Did he not imply that this smoky town is harming her health, Margaret?"

"No!" she answered solemnly. "He was anxious, I think, but he said nothing of the sort."

"Doctors have an anxious manner; 'tis part of their profession," he muttered, though the worry remained etched in his features. Margaret observed that despite his attempts to feign indifference, the first shadow of dread had taken root in his mind. He could not dismiss the subject, nor could he transition to lighter topics. Throughout the evening, he returned to it repeatedly, refusing to entertain even the faintest unfavorable notion, a behavior that rendered Margaret deeply sorrowful.

"This letter is from Aunt Shaw, Papa," she said, shifting the topic gently. "She arrived in Naples but found it too stifling, so she has rented rooms at Sorrento. However, I suspect she does not truly enjoy Italy."

"And regarding diet?" he asked immediately. "Did he mention anything specific?"

"It required to be nourishing and easily digested. Mother's appetite appears reasonably good, I believe."

"Yes! And that renders it all the stranger that he felt compelled to speak of diet at all."

"I asked him, Papa." There followed a long, silent pause. Then Margaret continued, half-smiling, "Aunt Shaw mentions sending me some coral ornaments, Papa. But," she added, "she fears the Milton dissenters may not appreciate them. Has she not formed all her conceptions of Dissenters based solely on the Quakers?"

"If you ever learn or suspect that your mother desires anything, ensure you inform me without delay. I am perpetually afraid she does not communicate her wishes. Please, attend to that young woman Mrs. Thornton named. Had we a competent, efficient housekeeper, Dixon could remain constantly with her, and I assure you we would quickly integrate her into our household if mere care suffices. She has been exceedingly fatigued recently due to the heat and the scarcity of staff. A period of rest will restore her completely—will it not, Margaret?"

"I hope so," Margaret whispered, her tone so laden with sadness that her father noticed instantly. He reached out and pinched her cheek.

"Well, if you appear so pale, I shall have to apply a touch of rouge. Mind yourself, child, or you'll require the doctor next."

Yet, he remained unable to settle. He moved restlessly back and forth on the balls of his feet, peering into the bedroom to confirm whether his wife was still sleeping. Margaret's heart ached at his agitation—the futile struggle to stifle and strangulate the terrible fear lurking in the shadows of his soul.

Finally, he returned, somewhat reassured.

"She is awake now, Margaret. She smiled broadly upon seeing me stand by her side. It was precisely her old smile. She remarked that she feels refreshed and ready for tea. Where is the note for her? She wishes to see it. I will read it aloud while you prepare the tea."

The note revealed a formal invitation from Mrs. Thornton to Mr., Mrs., and Miss Hale for dinner on the twenty-first. To Margaret's surprise, following the somber possibilities she had uncovered earlier that day, an acceptance appeared inevitable. The prospect of her husband and daughter attending had already captured Mrs. Hale's imagination prior to Margaret hearing the details. It seemed an essential diversion from the monotonous confinement of illness. Clinging to the idea with a fretful persistence, Mrs. Hale insisted on going whenever Margaret expressed objection.

"Nay, Margaret," Mr. Hale said anxiously as he prepared to draft the response the following morning. "If she wishes it, I am certain we will both go willingly. She would never express a desire to do so unless she genuinely felt stronger, truly better than we believed, would she not, Margaret?"

"Eh? Margaret?" he queried, his hands moving with nervous jerks. It seemed cruel to deny him the solace he craved. Furthermore, his passionate refusal to acknowledge the existence of fear nearly inspired hope within Margaret herself.

"I do believe she is improved since last night," she admitted. "Her eyes shine brighter, and her complexion is clearer."

"God bless you," Mr. Hale said earnestly. "But is it true? Yesterday was so scorching that everyone felt unwell. It was a terribly unfortunate day for Mr. Donaldson to visit her."

Thus, he departed to his daily duties, which had expanded to include preparing lectures for the working class at a neighboring Lyceum. He had selected *Ecclesiastical Architecture* as his subject, aligning more closely with his personal tastes and knowledge than with the probable interests of his audience or the financial desperation of the institution, which was deeply indebted and eager for any gratis instruction from a learned man like Mr. Hale.

That same night, Mr. Thornton asked his wife, "Well, mother, who has accepted your invitations for the twenty-first?"

"Fanny, where are the notes?" Mrs. Thornton recited the list rapidly. "The Slicksons accept, Collingbrooks accept, Stephenses accept, Browns decline. The Hales—both father and daughter are coming; mother is too ill an invalid to travel. The Macphersons accept, along with Mr. Horsfall and Mr. Young. I considered inviting the Porters, as the Browns cannot come."

"Very well," Mr. Thornton said. "Do you realize, however, that I am genuinely afraid Mrs. Hale is far from well, judging by what Dr. Donaldson told me?"

"It is peculiar of them to accept a dinner invitation if she is so severely ill," Fanny observed.

"I did not say 'severely ill,'" her brother replied sharply. "I merely said 'far from well.' They may not comprehend the gravity of the situation." Suddenly, he recalled that, based on Dr. Donaldson's account, Margaret, at least, must be fully aware of the precise nature of her condition.

"Most likely they are quite conscious of what I said yesterday, John—of the immense social advantage it would offer Mr. Hale to be introduced to individuals like the Stephenses and the Collingbrooks."

"I am certain that motive would not sway them. No! I believe I understand the dynamic."

"John!" Fanny laughed in her petite, weak, nervous fashion. "How you profess to understand these Hales, and yet how you never concede that we can perceive anything about them! Are they truly so distinct from the ordinary people one encounters?"

She did not intend to irritate him; yet, if she had aimed to do so, she could scarcely have succeeded more thoroughly. He bristled in silence, refusing to dignify her question with a reply.

"They do not seem to me extraordinary," Mrs. Thornton remarked. "He appears to be a decent, albeit somewhat simple, gentleman—perhaps too unsophisticated for commerce, hence his decision to become a clergyman first and now a teacher. She possesses a certain refinement, heightened by her infirmity. As for the girl... she is the sole individual who perplexes me when I reflect upon her, though I seldom do. She seems to harbor a grandiose notion of putting on airs, and I cannot fathom why. I could almost imagine she considers herself superior to her company, at times. And yet, they are not wealthy; from everything I gather, they have never possessed significant means."

"And she lacks accomplishments, mother," Fanny interjected. "She cannot play."

"Proceed, Fanny. What further qualities does she lack to meet your standards?"

"Nay, John!" Mrs. Thornton said, softening. "Fanny's remark caused no offense. I myself heard Miss Hale admit her inability to play. If you would leave us alone, we might perhaps grow fond of her and recognize her virtues."

"I am certain I never could!" murmured Fanny, feeling shielded by her mother. Mr. Thornton heard the words but chose not to respond. He walked up and down the dining room, desiring that his mother order candles so he might begin reading or writing, thereby halting the conversation. Yet, he never intervened in the minor domestic regulations Mrs. Thornton adhered to, born of her lifelong habits of economy.

"Mother," he stopped and spoke with brave sincerity, "I wish you would like Miss Hale."
