The small gathering at the hosier’s house sat around the table, still speaking of the recent turn of events, when their mother returned. With a sharp glance that swept across each face in the circle, she placed the letter flat upon the table. Then, without hesitation, she recited every word from memory, her finger tracing the lines as if to pretend she could read them—though her eyes betrayed no real understanding. It was a performance meant to convince both herself and those present that she had grasped the full weight of the message.

Suddenly, she raised her head, her gaze locking onto Cornelis and Sybrandt. Their eyes dropped instantly. That was all it took. The storm, long brewing beneath her silence, broke forth in a torrent of words—scathing, passionate, unrelenting. Catherine swelled like a hen ruffled by outrage, and from her mouth poured a flood of wisdom, scorn, and bitter truth, such as few men—and fewer women—could utter in one breath. Here is but a fragment of what she said, spoken in a single, continuous stream:

“I have long suspected you were the ones who fanned the flame between Gerard and your father, who stirred up that old fool Ghysbrecht. And now—here are Gerard’s own words to prove it! You’ve driven your own flesh and blood into exile, torn from his mother’s side, robbed me of my darling, the light of my life, the joy of my heart. But you are all alike—from head to foot. When you were boys together, my others brought comfort; you brought only trouble—mischievous, cunning, always needing more than half a day to keep your clothes whole. Why? Because work wears cloth, but play cuts it. A beard should bring sense—but not for you. Still the last to bed, the last to rise. And why? Because honesty goes to sleep early, and diligence wakes with the dawn. Where two lazy bones live, there are two good-for-nothings. I’ve often sat and watched you, wondering where you came from. You don’t take after your father, nor do you resemble me any more than a wasp resembles an ant. I swear you must have been switched at birth—or dropped by a cuckoo on my floor. You have neither our hands nor our hearts. Of all my children, only you ever mocked those God has afflicted. I’ve heard you laugh at Giles because he’s small, at my sweet Kate because she’s not strong as a Flanders mare. After that, rob a church if you wish—for you can be no worse in His eyes, who made both Kate and Giles, nor in mine, who suffered for them as I did for you, you pitiful, heartless, traitorous curs!

No—I will not hold back, daughter. They have filled the cup too full. It takes much to turn a mother’s heart against the sons she nursed on her knee. Many times I’ve turned away, pretending not to see, biting my tongue so your father would never know what I knew—he would have cast you out then and there. But now, they have filled the cup too full. Where did you get all this money? For the past month, you’ve been rolling in it. You didn’t earn it. I pray I never hear from another mouth how you came by it. It’s since that night you stayed out so late, and came home with your head swollen, Cornelis. Sloth and greed are ill-matched, my masters. Those who love gold must either sweat or steal. Well, if you stole it from someone poor, it was likely a woman—I’ll wager on it. A man would have beaten you with his bare hand. But let it be—it serves one purpose: it shows me how you’d rule our home, if ever it became yours. I’ve watched you, my boys. You spent a groat today between you. I spend scarcely a groat a week—and feed you all, good and bad. No—no more waiting for the shoes that may walk behind your coffin. This shop, this house—neither shall be yours. Gerard is our heir. Poor Gerard, whom you exiled, whom you tried to destroy. From now on, never call me mother again! But you’ve made him ten times dearer to me. My lost boy! Soon I shall see him again, hold him in my arms, set him upon my knees.

Yes—you may stare. You’re clever, but not clever enough. You cut down the stalk—but left the seed—the seed that will grow beyond you, outlive you. Margaret Brandt is quick with child, and it is Gerard’s. What is Gerard’s is mine. And I’ve prayed the saints it will be a boy—and it will be. It must. Kate, when I learned of this, my heart ached for her unborn child as if it were my own. He is our heir. He will outlive us. You will not—for a corrupt heart in a body is like a worm in a nut, swiftly bringing ruin to the flesh. So, Kate, take down Gerard’s bib and tucker from the drawer you know of. One day soon, we will carry them to Sevenbergen. We will borrow Peter Buyskens’ cart and go to comfort Gerard’s wife under her burden. She is his wife. Who is Ghysbrecht Van Swieten to stand between a couple and the altar? Can he break what God and the priest have joined? She is my daughter, and I am proud of her—as proud as I am of you, Kate. As for you—keep out of my sight for a while. You are like the black dog in my eyes.”

Cornelis and Sybrandt understood the dismissal. They slipped away, hearts heavy with guilt, shame, and hatred. They avoided her gaze as much as possible, and for many days she spoke not a word—good, bad, or indifferent—to either of them.

Liberaverat animum suum.
