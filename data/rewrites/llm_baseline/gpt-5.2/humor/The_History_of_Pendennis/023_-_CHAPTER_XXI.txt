Everybody who has ever peeped into a book of arms—and everybody who has ever peeped into a nursery—knows the famous device of the Pendennises: a mother-pelican in the very act of doing what mothers have been doing since the first small creature discovered it could whistle, cry, or look tragically hungry; namely, feeding her young with herself. Heraldry, which will tell you with a straight face that a lion is “rampant” and a unicorn is “couchant,” insists that the maternal bird offers her own breast, obligingly sanguine, to be pecked by a nest of chirping little wretches. There are people who say the story is natural history; there are people who say it is poetry; there are a few who suspect it is merely a family joke carried on for centuries at the expense of women.

For whether pelicans enjoy the operation or not, women, it is certain, submit to it as if it were part of the day’s domestic marketing. Give them a reckless husband, a scapegrace son, a prodigal brother, and you will see them put the best joint on the table, pour out the best wine of their patience, and carve their own comfort into thin slices, smiling while they do it. They will go without seasoning that others may have it; they will eat the crust that others may have the crumb; and at the first rumour that the sinner is on the road home, down goes the lid of the larder, out comes the fatted calf, and the whole kitchen is in a bustle of mercy and forgiveness, as if the angels themselves were in aprons and had tied up their sleeves. If we had nothing but Justice for breakfast, Heaven help us; most of us would go hungry.

During the latter part of Arthur Pendennis’s residence at Oxbridge, his uncle’s partiality for him had swelled like a well-fed pudding. The Major—an old London bachelor with the morals of a drawing-room and the economy of a desert island—was proud of Pen’s figure, spirits, frank talk, and that air of being born to have people hold doors open for him. It delighted the Major to see his nephew walking with young patricians under college gateways; and though the Major’s thrift had become a proverb at his Club (among gentlemen who envied his constant “engagements” and did not care to discover they were often engagements to dine elsewhere), he actually took to giving snug little dinners in his lodgings: dinners with sound claret, a dish or two whose excellence he would have sworn was accidental, and stories so neatly told that they ought to have been served under glass, never to be touched again. Some of them, if repeated, would lose their flavour; others, if repeated, would poison society.

He paid court to the parents through the sons; he paid court to himself through the sons; he made himself sprightly and juvenile with young lords as if years were a coat to be put off on entering the room. He went down to Oxbridge; was entertained, partly to be honoured and partly to be laughed at; and he laughed too, and told his stories, and accepted breakfasts and fêtes with the air of a man who had always breakfasted and fêted for the good of others. He heard Pen speak at the Union, and crowed and cheered, and rapped his stick as though it had been elected to Parliament. He came away persuaded he possessed a young Pitt in the family—only better dressed. He bragged of him at the Clubs, introduced him into conversation as if he were an improvement on the constitution, and wrote to Helen again and again that Lord Runnymede would certainly take the boy on an embassy, or the Duke bring him into a borough—anything, in short, that could happen to a young gentleman who spoke well and bowed correctly.

Helen, poor soul, was ready to believe any good report of her son with the prompt faith of a mother tasting a sauce she herself has made: she wants it to be perfect, therefore it is. And all this pride and affection—this careful cellaring of hope—had been dashed and spilt by Pen’s extravagance and idleness, as a clumsy foot knocks over a bottle which has been keeping for years.

Pen himself, when he awoke to what he had done, found remorse to be a kind of hunger that eats whatever is left in the house. He had slept, and the tortoise had won. He had spoiled at the beginning what might have been a brilliant career. He had dipped into a generous mother’s purse as if it were a public pantry; he had wasted her little store as if it were mere dripping to be thrown out. And if he felt the wrong done to others (which he did, for he was not without heart), we may be very sure he felt still more sharply the shame done to himself; for wounded self-love groans louder than any conscience, and is harder to quiet. He convinced himself, with that maddening vanity which is the true fever of youth, that all England would remark the absence of his name from the lists, and that boots, bed-makers, tutors, duns, undergraduates—every human creature who had ever seen him—would wag their heads and whisper, “Plucked!”

He rushed to his rooms, shut himself in, and wrote to his tutor a letter full of gratitude, despair, and tragic flourish; he begged that his name might be taken off the college books, and hinted in a manner meant to melt stone that death would probably end the woes of Arthur Pendennis. Then, like a man who has burnt the roast and cannot bear to stay in the kitchen, he slipped out, scarcely knowing where he went, and took the little lanes behind the colleges until he cleared the precincts and came down by the Camisis.

The river lay deserted in a thin January drizzle—no cheering gownsmen now, no racing boats, no holiday racket of oars and laughter. Only the small natural noises were awake: the damp whisper of reeds, the faint click of water against a mooring, the long sigh of wind that goes through winter branches as though the trees were remembering a forgotten language. It was a kinder sound than the university’s loud, polished talk; it asked nothing, it accused nothing. Pen wandered on until he was a few miles from Oxbridge, and was in fact discovered by acquaintances leaving it.

As he went up a hill with the rain beating in his face, his ragged gown streaming behind him—for he had not even taken off his academic dress since morning—a postchaise came rattling towards him, and the road, which a moment before had belonged to drizzle and birds, was suddenly seized by iron hoops, cracking whips, the postboy’s shout, and that furious modern noise by which men announce their importance. A servant sat on the box; within, half out of the window, lounged a young gentleman smoking a cigar and bawling encouragement as if speed were a virtue.

It was Mr. Spavin of Baymouth, degree in pocket at last, driving home in triumph in a yellow postchaise. He caught sight of Pen’s figure gesticulating uphill, and of Pen’s pale face as the chaise whirled by.

“Wo!” roared Mr. Spavin; and the horses stopped as if surprised that any human voice could be louder than their own hooves. The carriage pulled up fifty yards ahead. Pen heard his name shouted, and saw the upper half of Spavin thrust out of the side-window, beckoning as though he were signalling a ship in distress.

Pen stopped, hesitated, nodded fiercely, and pointed onwards, as if ordering fate to drive on and leave him. He did not speak; but his look must have been sufficiently desperate, for Spavin stared with blank alarm, jumped down, ran back, seized Pen’s hand, and cried, “I say—hullo, old boy, where are you going, and what’s the row now?”

“I’m going where I deserve to go,” Pen said, with an oath that had more misery than wickedness in it.

“This ain’t the way,” Spavin answered, grinning. “This is the Fenbury road. I say, Pen, don’t take on because you are plucked. It’s nothing when you are used to it. I’ve been plucked three times, old boy—and after the first time I didn’t care. Glad it’s over, though. You’ll have better luck next time.”

Pen stared at him—at this cheerful survivor who had been rusticated, plucked, and only after repeated misfortunes brought to spell properly; and yet here he was, degree achieved at last, cigar glowing, and a bright yellow chaise behind him like a canary come to life. “This man has passed,” Pen thought, “and I have failed!” It was almost unbearable.

“Good-bye, Spavin,” Pen said. “I’m very glad you are through. Don’t let me keep you; I’m in a hurry—I’m going to town to-night.”

“Gammon,” said Spavin. “This ain’t the way to town; this is the Fenbury road, I tell you.”

“I was just going to turn back,” Pen muttered.

“All the coaches are full with the men going down,” Spavin said.

Pen winced as if he had been struck.

“You’d not get a place for a ten-pound note,” Spavin continued, with the good nature of a man who believes the world is a public-house and all troubles are paid for with a round of drinks. “Get into my yellow; I’ll drop you at Mudford, where you have a chance of the Fenbury mail. I’ll lend you a hat and a coat; I’ve got lots. Come along; jump in, old boy—go it, leathers!”

And thus, by the charity of a man who had been plucked three times and was therefore proof against catastrophes, Pen found himself seated in the yellow postchaise, with a borrowed coat about him and the smell of Spavin’s cigar mixed with wet wool and muddy road—an odd, homely perfume of failure on the move. They rattled as far as the Ram Inn at Mudford, fifteen miles from Oxbridge, where the Fenbury mail changed horses; and there Pen got a place to London.

At Boniface College there was, next day, an immense excitement. Rumour, like an idle cook, had put in too much of the dramatic seasoning. A battered cap—Pen’s name scarcely visible—together with a seal bearing his crest (an eagle looking at a now extinct sun) had been found three miles out on the Fenbury road, near a mill-stream; and for four-and-twenty hours it was believed that Pendennis, frantic at losing his degree, had thrown himself into the water. Tutor and tradesmen grew pale; duns began to imagine they might never be paid even by the respectable machinery of shame. Then letters arrived with the London post-mark, and the stream was allowed to go on murmuring without a corpse.

The mail reached London at the dreary hour of five. Pen hastened to the Covent Garden inn where he commonly put up, and the ever-wakeful porter admitted him and showed him to a bed. Pen looked hard at the man and wondered whether Boots knew he was plucked; whether even the slippers and chamber-candles had heard of it. He lay down, but the bed would not take him in. He tossed until the dismal London daylight crept in—daylight that seemed filtered through soot and old breath—and then sprang up and walked to his uncle’s lodgings in Bury Street.

A maid was scouring the steps. She looked up suspiciously at the sight of him—unshaven, yesterday’s linen, that broken look which is like a stain men imagine visible to all.

“Good ’evens! Mr. Harthur, what as ’appened, sir?” asked Mr. Morgan the valet, who had just arranged well-brushed clothes and shiny boots at the Major’s door, and was carrying in the Major’s wig.

“I want to see my uncle,” Pen cried, and flung himself into a chair with a voice that sounded as if it had been scraped thin.

Morgan backed away from the pale face and went in.

The Major put his head out as soon as his wig was on. “What? examination over? Senior Wrangler, double First Class, hay? I’ll come directly,” he called, in his high cheery tone; and disappeared again.

“They don’t know,” Pen groaned. “What will they say when they know all?”

Pen stood with his back to the window in the dubious light that Bury Street enjoys on a foggy January morning, so his uncle did not at first catch his expression. But when the Major came out neat and radiant—preceded by faint odours from Delcroix’s shop, from which emporium his wig and pocket-handkerchief drew their scented importance—he held out his hand and began to speak; and then he saw Pen’s face.

“Good God! Pen, what’s the matter?” he cried, dropping the hand as if it had touched fever.

“You’ll see it in the papers at breakfast, sir,” Pen answered.

“See what?”

“My name isn’t there, sir.”

“Hang it, why should it be?” the Major asked, bewildered.

“I have lost everything, sir,” Pen groaned. “My honour’s gone; I’m ruined irretrievably; I can’t go back to Oxbridge.”

“Lost your honour?” screamed the Major. “Heaven alive! you don’t mean to say you have shown the white feather?”

Pen laughed bitterly at the word, and repeated it: “Feather.”

“No, it isn’t that, sir. I’m not afraid of being shot; I wish to God anybody would. I have not got my degree. I—I’m plucked, sir.”

The Major had heard of plucking in a vague way and, being a man of old-fashioned discipline, concluded it was some bodily ceremony inflicted publicly on refractory youth.

“I wonder you can look me in the face after such a disgrace, sir,” he said. “I wonder you submitted to it as a gentleman.”

“I couldn’t help it, sir. I did my classical papers well enough: it was those infernal mathematics, which I have always neglected.”

“Was it—was it done in public, sir?” the Major asked, anxiously.

“What?”

“The—the plucking?” said the Major, staring.

Pen understood at last the nature of his uncle’s terror, and in the midst of misery the absurdity brought him a faint smile—as if a cracked bell had tried to ring. He explained that he had gone in for the examination and failed it; and then the Major, relieved that no physical outrage had been committed, declared that there was no dishonour as far as he saw, no great misfortune, and that Pen must try again.

“Me again at Oxbridge,” Pen thought, “after such humiliation?” He felt he could not enter the place, unless it were to burn it.

But when he produced his debts, then the Major’s relief turned to anger. Pen had determined to make a clean breast of it. He had written out a full list—true, complete, and hideous—of bills and liabilities at the university and in London: tailors in both places; horses; haberdashery for shirts and gloves; printseller; jeweller; books; the college cook; binding; Grump for desserts; hairdresser and perfumery; bootmaker; hotel bill; wine merchant; sundries—those dreadful “sundries” which, like scraps in a kitchen, somehow always add up to another meal.

The reader may season the items at pleasure; such accounts have been tasted, to the sourness of soul, by many a parent of university youth. All told, they amounted to about seven hundred pounds; and, worse still, it was calculated that Pen had had more than twice that sum in ready money during his stay at Oxbridge. That, too, was gone. And for it—what remained? Not even a respectable appetite; only the gnawing emptiness of having eaten and not been nourished.

“You need not press a man who is down, sir,” Pen said gloomily. “I know very well how wicked and idle I have been. My mother won’t like to see me dishonoured,” he added, his voice failing. “And I know she will pay these accounts. But I shall ask her for no more money.”

“As you like, sir,” the Major replied, stiffening himself. “You are of age, and my hands are washed of your affairs. But you can’t live without money, and have no means of making it that I see, though you have a fine talent in spending it; and it is my belief you will proceed as you have begun, and ruin your mother before you are five years older. Good morning. It is time for me to go to breakfast. My engagements won’t permit me to see you much during the time you stay in London. I presume you will acquaint your mother with the news which you have conveyed to me.”

And pulling on his hat, trembling a little in his limbs, Major Pendennis walked out before his nephew, and went ruefully off to take his accustomed corner at the Club.

There he saw the Oxbridge examination-lists in the morning papers, and read the names with mournful accuracy, not understanding the business but feeling the blow. He consulted old fogies at his clubs—Wenham, a Dean, various Civilians—took their “opinion,” showed some of them the amount of Pen’s debts dotted on the back of a card, and asked whether such debts were not monstrous, preposterous? What was to be done? There was nothing for it but to pay. Wenham and the rest consoled him with tales of young men who owed twice as much, five times as much, and had no means at all. These comparisons, like watering weak wine, made the Major feel a little better. After all, he was not the one who would pay. Still he thought bitterly of the plans he had formed, the sacrifices he fancied he had made, and the disappointment that sat down at table with him and would not be served away.

He wrote off to Doctor Portman, informing him of the direful events and begging the Doctor to break them to Helen. The Major, orthodox in all his habits, believed bad news ought to be carved and handed round by a proper person, rather than sent straight, like a common joint, by post. Having dispatched this messenger, he went out to dinner one of the saddest men in any London dining-room that day, though he doubtless ate like a gentleman and smiled when it was necessary.

Pen, too, wrote his letter—full of tenderness and remorse—and then skulked about London streets, fancying that every passer-by was looking at him and whispering, “That is Pendennis of Boniface, who was plucked yesterday.” He wept bitter tears over his letter, and the very act of suffering soothed him, as if pain were a kind of penance paid in coin he happened to possess. He saw a party of roaring young blades from Oxbridge in the coffee-room of his hotel and slipped away from them as from a kitchen where old acquaintances were already smelling the smoke. He paced the streets. He remembered, afterwards, the prints at Ackermann’s window hanging in the rain, and a book he read at a stall near the Temple; and at night he went into the pit and saw Miss Fotheringay, but could not even recollect the piece—only that her voice rang out above the crowd like something alive, not bought and sold like so much fashionable applause.

On the second day a letter came from his tutor—kind, grave, appropriate—urging him not to take his name off the university books, and to retrieve a disaster which everybody knew was due to his own carelessness and could be repaired by a month’s application. The tutor added that he had ordered Pen’s skip to pack up some trunks of wardrobe; the trunks arrived duly—and on the top of them, like cold dishes set out to shame a glutton, lay fresh copies of all Pen’s bills.

On the third day a letter came from home. Pen read it in his bedroom; and when he had done, he fell on his knees with his head in the bedclothes and prayed out his heart, humbled, broken, and for once speaking within himself in a language no fashionable world could translate. Then—having emptied himself of pride as one empties a pot—he went downstairs, ate an immense breakfast, and sallied forth; and that evening took his place at the Bull and Mouth, Piccadilly, by the Chatteris coach.
