The second day after Harry Warrington’s arrival at his family’s English estate dawned soft and sun-dappled, a quiet 18th-century Sunday at Castlewood. The village church, built flush to the castle’s west wing, doubled as the family chapel: a hidden panelled door from the great house led straight to a grand, curtained state pew reserved exclusively for the Esmonds, where the household filed in one by one at the appointed hour, while the rest of the pews below filled with villagers from the surrounding hamlet.

Tattered, dust-caked old battle banners hung from the rafters, and Harry let his imagination wander as he stared up at them, certain they had been borne by his ancestors’ loyal retainers fighting for the Crown in the Commonwealth wars, a cause he knew the Esmond name had been famous for. At the altar rails, the marble effigy of Sir Francis Esmond, the family patriarch from the reign of James I, knelt for all eternity, square-bearded and clad in his carved red gown, his stone hands resting on a marble prayer book beneath his blazoned coat of arms. Even Madame de Bernstein, the late bishop’s widow, never missed a service, her bearing so gravely devout that it nearly matched the eternal piety of the stone ancestor across the chancel.

The parish clergyman, Mr. Sampson, a tall, ruddy, strikingly handsome young man, read the full liturgy in a lively, musical voice, lending dramatic weight to every passage of Scripture he read. One of the younger Esmond girls played the organ, and the music would have run perfectly smoothly if not for a sudden, rich burst of song from the servants’ gallery that sent half the congregation snickering. Harry’s own personal enslaved attendant from Virginia, Gumbo, had recognized the first note of the psalm, and belted out the tune in a voice so loud and honey-sweet that every head in the church turned to stare at the African “warbler” mid-hymn. Even Parson Sampson clapped his handkerchief to his mouth to smother a laugh, while the liveried footmen newly arrived from London gaped so openly they forgot all proper decorum. Delighted by the attention, Gumbo sang on until the clerk’s thin voice was completely drowned out, for he had no idea how to read a single word of the hymnal he was clutching open beside pretty Molly, the porter’s daughter who had been the first to greet Harry and his party when they arrived at Castlewood.

When the singing ended, Sampson climbed into the pulpit to deliver a sermon that Harry thought far too short, even though it ran more than twenty minutes. He did not drag out the stiff, Calvinistic Scripture cadences Harry had grown used to hearing at his mother’s favourite colonial churches; instead he spoke plainly, as one man of the world to a crowd of fellow sinners, recounting a vivid scene he had witnessed only the week before at the Winton Assizes: the hanging of a local horse-stealer. He described the man’s once good name, his loving wife and small children, his desperate agony clinging to his family’s hands in the moments before he was dragged to the gallows. He painted the chase so vividly Harry could almost see the stolen horse galloping across heath and highway, the shadow of Retribution hot on its heels, the law seizing the thief at a country fair, dragging him before the magistrate, and never striking off his irons until they dropped at the foot of the gallows. He mimed leaning into the condemned man’s ear as the cart drew up, then dropped his handkerchief to mime the drop, and Harry jolted back in his pew as if he had felt the noose tighten at his own throat. It felt to Harry like he had only sat down for five minutes, and he would happily have listened for an hour more. The house servants sat goggle-eyed, Gumbo frozen in absolute terror in his gallery seat, but the village yokels in their smock frocks paid no mind at all, tramping out of church chattering about turnip yields and dairy cows as if they had not heard a single word.

Lord Castlewood nodded at Sampson as the clergyman’s wig popped up over the pulpit cushion. “Sampson has been in fine fettle today,” he said. “He smote the Philistines with full force.” “Magnificent!” Harry burst out, still buzzing. “I’ll wager five to four that was his old Assize sermon,” snickered his cousin Will Esmond. “He rode over to Winton not just to preach, but to gawk at those new racing hounds the squire got last month.”

The organ played the congregation out into the bright Sunday sunshine, leaving old Sir Francis Esmond still kneeling on his stone hassock in the empty church. Sampson emerged from the vestry in his cassock, nodded at the group lingering in the family pew, and followed them back through the connecting gallery into the house. Lord Castlewood formally presented him to Harry, the Virginian cousin, and Sampson bowed low, politely asking if Harry was related to Sir Miles Warrington of Norfolk. The man, Harry learned, was his father’s elder brother. “What a pity he has a son,” Sampson joked. “That’s a fine estate, and you look the very picture of a man who’d make a far better baronet than that drunken old sot and his heir.” Harry immediately begged for every scrap of news about his Norfolk relations, while Will at once demanded to hear about the hounds. Sampson laughed, and admitted he had never met two men more fond of their drink than Sir Miles Warrington and his son, the pair of them able to out-drink every man in the county save a handful of their closest sporting friends. From hounds the conversation drifted easily to horses, and Will leaned in eagerly to ask after the upcoming Six Year Old Plate at Huntingdon. “The odds stand five to four on Brilliant against the field,” Sampson said gravely, “but mark my words, Jason – the Duke of Ancaster’s horse, sired by Cartouche out of Miss Langley – is a very strong contender.”

Harry, who had grown up riding and racing thoroughbreds on his family’s Virginia plantations, admitted he ached to see a proper English race. “Do you ever place a small wager on the tracks, Mr. Warrington?” Sampson asked. Harry smiled and admitted he had, from time to time. “I’ll lay you even odds Brilliant takes the whole field, for a pony a side, cousin!” Will shouted, leaning across the table. “I’ll give three to one against Jason, myself,” added Sampson. Harry, stung when Will taunted him that he ought to write home to his mother for permission to bet, bristled at once. “I manage my own affairs without running them past any lady, thank you,” he said. “And for the honour of Old Virginia, I’ll take that bet – both of them. Done and done.”

Will scowled and trundled off to the stables to smoke his pipe with the grooms, while Sampson headed to the drawing room to pay his respects to the ladies, leaving Lord Castlewood and Harry alone in the gallery. The quiet earl, who had been perfectly polite but almost entirely silent since Harry arrived, asked about the size of his Virginian estate. Harry’s pride flared at the hint that Castlewood thought it worthless, and pointed out that his grandfather had never laid formal claim to his share of the Esmond Virginian lands out of choice, not weakness. He then blurted out his honest frustration: the warm, open-handed hospitality of Virginia, where guests were given the very best of everything and welcomed as family, was nothing like the cool, distant politeness of Castlewood, where he was fed well enough but never made to feel like more than a passing stranger. Lord Castlewood laughed and clapped him on the shoulder. “We do not pretend to cry over every visitor that crosses our threshold, that is true,” he said. “But we do not lie about it, either. Just take my warning: my brother William has been on the turf since he was old enough to ride away from Cambridge, and he will cheat you out of every penny you have if you let him play cards or wager against you.” Harry boasted that he could outride, outshoot, outwrestle any man his age, then immediately blushed bright red and apologized for his rude outburst. The earl laughed and took his arm to lead him in to dinner.

Parson Sampson was the life of the Sunday dinner table, regaling the ladies with all the raciest latest gossip from London high society: the latest scandal out of Mayfair’s clubs, Mr. Selwyn’s newest bon mot, the absurd thousand-pound wager Lord March had laid at White’s, the latest royal domestic squabbles, the last man hanged at Tyburn who had died so bravely the crowd had cheered him. His jokes were racy, lively, and entirely unfit for a modern Sunday congregation, but none of the ladies at Castlewood so much as blinked an eye, laughing merrily at every story. When the chapel bell clanged for the short afternoon service, Sampson darted out for half an hour, and was back before the last echo of the bell had faded, yelling for a fresh bottle of Burgundy.

The rest of the Sabbath drifted by lazily around the card tables. Madame de Bernstein was unbeatable at piquet, and only Sampson was skilled enough to give her even a run for her money. The company voted to ignore the warm golden summer evening outside, declaring a hand full of court cards and three honours far more beautiful than any sunset over the park’s elm trees. The chapel clock tolled hour after hour unheeded, the rooks cawed their way home to their nests in the dusk, the moon rose high, and it was nine o’clock before the groom of chambers came in to announce supper was served.

Halfway through the meal, the sharp twang of the postboy’s horn echoed down the village lane. Lord Castlewood took the newssheet that came with his post, smiled, and passed it over to Will. Will scanned the racing results paragraph the earl had marked, and swore so loud all the ladies jumped in their seats. The Six Year Old Plate at Huntingdon, it reported, had been won handily by Jason, leaving Brilliant trailing far behind the rest of the field. Harry’s bets had both come in. Sampson groaned that he owed Harry half the entire income of his clerical living, while Will cursed his bad luck and swore it was the curse of betting on a Sunday. Harry, ever generous, said he would never dream of taking the money from his own cousin, but Will flared up offended, swearing he was no pauper who would welch on a fair wager. The ladies excused themselves to head up to bed soon after, and the rest of the night dissolved into bowl after bowl of spiced punch, the men drinking deep long after the last star had risen high over Castlewood’s rooftops.
