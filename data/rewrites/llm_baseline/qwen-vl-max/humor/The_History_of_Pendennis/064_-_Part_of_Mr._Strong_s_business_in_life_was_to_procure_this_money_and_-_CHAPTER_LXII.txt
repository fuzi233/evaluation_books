A few days after Colonel Altamont’s lucky break at Epsom, the man of fashion packed his bags and set off on his grand foreign tour—because nothing says “I’ve just won a fortune” like hopping on a boat to Antwerp. The chronicler of high society, stationed at London Bridge like some sort of social traffic cop, reported that among the distinguished passengers aboard the *Soho* were “Sir Robert, Lady, and the Misses Hodge; Mr. Serjeant Kewsy, and Mrs. and Miss Kewsy; Colonel Altamont, Major Coddy, etc.” (etc., because no one really cared about the rest). Colonel Altamont traveled with all the dignity befitting a gentleman who’d just been handed a tidy sum by fate: rich velvet coat, brandy-and-water flowing freely (and not a single bout of seasickness), and his loyal Irish servant in tow—same chap who’d once helped Captain Strong carry his laundry up the stairs of Shepherd’s Inn.

The Chevalier (who was, let’s face it, more of a character than a title) treated his departing friend to a lavish dinner at Blackwall, where they drank enough healths to drown a small village. “Strong, old boy,” said Altamont, puffing on a cigar like he owned the entire cigar industry, “if you’re short, now’s your chance. I’m your man. You’ve always been good to me, and twenty pounds? Not a problem. It’s pocket change.”

But Strong shook his head. “No need,” he said, though his voice trembled slightly. “I’m flush—well, not *quite* flush enough to pay back that last loan, but I can manage for a while.” And so they parted, shaking hands with all the warmth of two men who’d just pretended not to care about money.

Now, here’s the question: had Altamont actually become a better person now that he had cash? Or was it just that Strong, starved for kindness and respect, saw him through rose-tinted spectacles? Maybe Altamont *had* improved. Maybe wealth had polished his rough edges. But Strong couldn’t help thinking: this poor soul, this ex-convict who’d once signed his father-in-law’s name during a moment of madness (which, honestly, how many men haven’t done worse?), was ten times the man Sir Francis Clavering ever was. He had grit. He had loyalty. He’d stand by a friend and fight an enemy. Clavering? He’d run from both. And what was Clavering’s crime? Nothing worth mentioning—just being a bit reckless and signing someone else’s name. Many men had done worse and lived to tell the tale. Clavering didn’t even have the decency to hold his head up. He never did.

And so Strong, perhaps, regretted lying about not needing money. But it was a lie on the side of honor. He couldn’t bring himself to borrow again from a man who’d been cast out of polite society. Besides, he could get by. Clavering had promised him funds—though promises from Clavering were about as reliable as a weather forecast from a squirrel. Still, Strong kept hope alive. He’d catch Clavering someday, ambush one of those stray remittances, or wrangle a little something from the Baronet’s pockets. That, after all, was Strong’s life’s work: keeping Clavering afloat—and himself from drowning.

He’d grumbled about sharing rooms with Altamont, but now that the Colonel was gone, the place felt like a tomb. The loneliness gnawed at him. He missed the chatter, the shared meals, the absurdity of living with someone who wore a monocle indoors. Now he was alone, with only a servant to run errands, cook his dinners, and generally make him feel like a lord. But when the servant left? Suddenly, the Chevalier found himself painting his own boots and broiling a mutton chop over a candle flame. It was a sight—both tragic and oddly noble. A once-grand man reduced to domestic drudgery. One might almost pity him. Almost.

Of course, there was also the matter of Juanita, his Spanish wife, who’d returned to her homeland after a brief marriage that ended when she threw a dish at his head. Strong began to wonder if he should go back to Vittoria. Maybe see her again. Maybe apologize for being such a fool. But then again, maybe not. Women were complicated. Especially ones who wielded crockery like weapons.

As for Clavering—no funds from him. The twenty-five pounds Altamont had sent had vanished into the ether faster than a politician’s promise. Clavering had spent it all on a river trip with a group of sporting gentlemen who evaded the police and landed in Essex, where they arranged a boxing match between Billy Bluck and Dick the cabman. Clavering backed Dick, who dominated for thirteen rounds—until, tragically, Billy delivered a fatal blow to the windpipe. “It’s always my luck,” Clavering moaned. “I thought I was guaranteed thirty pounds. And now I owe Lightfoot fourteen—damn him! He duns me like a bloody parrot. If only I could get a bill drawn up—or squeeze something out of my lady!”

“Half of it, Ned,” he begged. “I swear on my honour, I’ll give you half if you can find someone to do us a little fifty.”

But Ned—Mr. Strong—was firm. “I gave my word as a gentleman,” he said, “that I would not take part in any more of your bill schemes. And I meant it.”

“And I mean to keep mine,” said Strong. “I’ll black my own boots till the day I die before I break that promise.” And worse—he vowed he’d warn Lady Clavering if he ever heard her husband planning to betray her. That was the kind of integrity that made people pause.

Clavering went into full meltdown mode. Tears, curses, threats of suicide. He implored Strong, his “dearest Ned,” not to abandon him. Then, as he descended the stairs of Shepherd’s Inn, he turned around and screamed, “You infernal villain! Traitor! Coward! May you rot in hell!”—before adding, with dark satisfaction, “But I hope you live long enough for me to get my revenge.”

Meanwhile, in Strong’s chambers, Clavering met a new regular: Mr. Samuel Huxter of Clavering. Young Sam, who’d once poached walnuts in the park and watched Sir Francis drive past with four horses and powdered footmen, now adored his “Member.” He introduced himself with trembling pride: “Son of Mr. Huxter, market-place. Father attended Sir Francis’s keeper when his gun exploded—lost three fingers. Proud to meet Sir Francis!” Clavering, bless him, accepted the homage with a nod and a smile.

Huxter later told the boys at Bartholomew’s how great Sir Francis was—“a thoroughbred un, a proper English gentleman!”—to which Fanny, the lodge keeper’s daughter, replied, “He’s ojous. I don’t know why, but I can’t abear him. He’s wicked, low, mean. Mar refused him twice. Took three months to get seven shillings!”

“Don’t say ‘er’ but ‘her,’ ‘borrer’ but ‘borrow,’” Huxter corrected gently.

“Well then, her, borrow, hactually—there! You stoopid, stoopid, old foolish stoopid!” Fanny laughed, pulling his hair and patting his chin. “He borrows from everyone. Mar wouldn’t lend him a penny. Not one!”

And so the grammar lesson ended in laughter and affection—a rare thing in a world where money ruled and manners were often just masks.

Mrs. Bolton, of course, was present. And time had softened Fanny’s heart. She’d grown fond of Dr. Sam. Time does that. It dulls regrets, soothes wounds, turns bitterness into tenderness. Just as it turns old men into saints in their memories.

Ah, the elderly Dives, sitting surrounded by flattery, servants bowing, tenants cap-in-hand—how often he thinks: *This respect is temporary. When I’m gone, it will pass to my son.* The wine in the cellar? He’ll drink it. The estate? He’ll inherit it. The reverence? It will transfer like a deed. The heir becomes the new sun. Men flock to him. The glory shifts. The successor whispers: “Make ready to go. I am waiting.”

What has this to do with our story? Does it excuse Pen for wearing a white hat and mourning his mother lightly? No. Pen’s love for his mother was deep, enduring. She was his saint. Her memory lived in his heart, watching over him—judging, comforting, forgiving. If he sinned, her eyes looked on sadly. If he triumphed, she smiled. If he fell, she pleaded. If he suffered, she cheered. She was with him always—until death.

Is this sentimental nonsense? Perhaps. But even the most worldly man needs moments of reflection. Ask yourself: do we not live in the dead? Do we not prove God through love?

Pen and Warrington spoke of such things often—quietly, solemnly. Pendennis’s mother was canonized in his memory. Lucky the man who knows such women. Heaven sends them as gifts—beacons of innocence, love, beauty.

But suppose, just suppose, Major Pendennis walked in during one of these philosophical sessions. Arthur and Warrington would have snapped shut like books. They’d switch to opera gossip, parliamentary debates, or Miss Jones’s marriage to Captain Smith—anything to avoid the awkward silence of deep feeling.

So let us return to the real world—where money matters, reputations are fragile, and friendship is hard to keep.

When Arthur Pendennis inherited his estate, he handled it with modesty. Warrington praised him. His uncle, Major Pendennis, frowned. “Why not ride in a cabriolet? Why not show your status?” Arthur laughed. “I’m too clumsy. The omnibus would cut me in two. And I’d rather not look like an apothecary driven by my servant.”

“No,” said the Major firmly. “You mustn’t demean yourself. You’re a gentleman—never forget it.”

Arthur countered with a quote he’d heard his uncle use often: “In the society I move in, who cares about my humble beginnings? It’s absurd to compete with the great. All they expect is decent manners.”

“But you should join better clubs,” the Major insisted. “Give dinners. Move out of that garret.”

And so Arthur compromised: second floor in Lamb Court. Warrington stayed put. The two friends refused to part. Cultivate friendships young, reader. They’re formed in generous youth. Later ones are weaker—like hands that’ve shaken too many careless palms.

Pen made acquaintances daily. He was jovial, easygoing. But no one like Warrington. They lived almost as one—riding on the same horse (Pen’s was always at Warrington’s disposal), sharing chambers, servants, and secrets.

Warrington had met Pen’s Grosvenor Place crowd during their disastrous season in London. He didn’t like them. “The world is right,” he said. “Lady Clavering and her daughter? Unfit company. That girl talks to men she shouldn’t. Did you see those two reprobates leering over her carriage?”

“The Begum is innocent,” Pen protested. “She doesn’t know anything about Captain Blackball’s trial or Charley Lovelace’s scandal. You think honest ladies read the *Chronique Scandaleuse*?”

“Would you want Laura Bell near those men?” Warrington shot back, face reddening. “Would you let a woman you loved be tainted by them? She may be ignorant—but ignorance isn’t virtue. She’s not a lady.”

“She’s as moral as Lady Portsea, who hosts balls for the whole world,” Pen retorted. “As refined as Mrs. Bull, who breaks English but feeds dukes. She pays her husband’s debts. Spoils her son. Does her best. Why judge her for not reading Wordsworth?”

“Because a gentleman chooses his companions wisely,” Warrington growled. “You call me a cynic? But look at you—you’re defending vulgarity as if it were virtue. Is your ambition just good claret and a silver plate? Are you proud to dine with anyone who serves a stalled ox?”

“Because I like raw steak and a pipe,” Pen snapped, “don’t assume I despise refinement. Who goes around praising the people they meet every day? I accept A.’s hospitality. I don’t pretend to repay it with friendship. We part without grief. We meet, we’re glad. That’s enough.”

“You’re your uncle’s pupil,” Warrington sighed. “You speak like a worldling.”

“And why not?” asked Pendennis. “Why pretend the world isn’t what it is? I’ve seen more of it than you have in your garret. I accept it. If the world’s broken, do I have the strength to fix it?”

“I don’t think you do,” muttered Warrington.

“If I doubt I’m better than others,” Arthur continued, “I also doubt they’re better than me. Reformers start loud, end quiet. Radicals become placemen. Liberals turn Conservatives. Look at Thiers, Guizot—their conduct isn’t treason. It’s submission. They march with the world, not ahead of it. They compromise. They wait. They adapt.”

“The Right Honourable Arthur Pendennis could not speak better,” Warrington said, “if he were Chancellor of the Exchequer.”

And so the debate ended—not with agreement, but with mutual recognition: the world was messy. And they both, in their own ways, were trying to survive it.
