As Sir Francis Clavering settled into his diminished domain at Clavering Park, the melancholy that had long shrouded the estate lingered, a constant reminder of the baronet's precarious financial situation. Though he occupied a select few rooms on the ground floor, the once-grand mansion remained largely shuttered, its opulence muted by the stark reality of its proprietor's reduced circumstances. The housekeeper and her assistant from the lodge gate continued to attend to the baronet's needs, while Lightfoot, his loyal manservant, had transferred his allegiance to my Lady, where he had even secured the affections of Mrs. Bonner, the lady's maid, and married her in a ceremony performed by the obliging Mr. Smirke.

Pen, informed of this development in a letter, was aware that the newlyweds aspired to become the landlords and landladies of the Clavering Arms, and had agreed to remain in Lady Clavering's service until quarter-day, when they would assume ownership of the inn. In return for their dedication, Pen offered to host an election dinner at the Clavering Arms, where he would supplant the baronet as the favored guest. With his uncle's blessing, Arthur descended upon Clavering Park in September, eager to offer his companionship to the lonely Sir Francis and perhaps provide some financial assistance.

Arthur's initial generosity proved timely, as Sir Francis soon discovered pressing matters in Chatteris and the surrounding spas, which necessitated his departure. The baronet's absence afforded Arthur ample opportunity to explore the estate, finding solace in his mental pursuits and the company of the gamekeeper, with whom he would take leisurely walks. In the evenings, the vast library at Clavering Park provided a wealth of material for a literary mind such as Arthur's, and he would often lose himself in the pages of a book, a cigar burning in the corner, as the night wore on.

However, within a few days, Arthur found himself growing increasingly restless under the baronet's company, which he deemed insufferable. Seizing the opportunity, he presented Sir Francis with a modest loan, facilitating the baronet's escape from his own home. Moreover, Arthur recognized the importance of cultivating relationships with the townspeople and the borough's voters, whose favor he hoped to secure for future political ambitions.
