The dawn broke cool and clear over the vast, emerald plain, painting the sky in shades of pale azure. We moved forward then, a procession of innocence and instinct that cut a striking figure against the natural world. Our plan was precise: march through the morning, rest in the heat of the afternoon, and press on under the cover of night. We aimed to slip into the city gates just as the first light of dawn touched the horizon, arriving unseen until we were already within the walls.

It seemed the entire forest had risen to accompany us. A vanguard of birds darted ahead, convinced of their own leadership, while swarms of butterflies, wasps, and dragonflies danced around our heads. Behind us trailed a host of four-legged creatures. As night fell, most of these terrestrial companions deserted us, but the avian and insect life remained loyal, fluttering alongside us all the way to the city's threshold.

We halted for a deep, undisturbed sleep during the afternoon; though this was our first true march, fatigue had not yet claimed us. By night, the cold spurred us to greater speed. Many of the children fell asleep on the backs of their beasts, waking refreshed in the morning, not a single soul tumbling from their perch. Some rode shaggy, shambling bears that nonetheless matched the pace of elephants; others were mounted on various species of deer, eager to race the entire distance had I not held them back. Those perched atop the hay-stacks on the elephants, blind to the massive forms beneath them, chatted endlessly with their mounts. One little voice, drawing hay from a trough, cooed to his "darling beast":

"Nosy dear, I am digging you out of the mountain, and shall soon get down to you: be patient; I'm a coming! Very soon now you'll send up your nose to look for me, and then we'll kiss like good elephants, we will!"

That same night, a cacophony erupted—a tumult of elephant trumpeting, horse neighing, and children imitating the sounds—that rang across the silent plains. Unsure of the city's proximity, I quickly silenced the uproar, fearing it might betray our approach.

Then, suddenly, the sun and the city rose together. To the children, the walls appeared merely as a colossal mass of rock. But when I explained that the interior was filled with nests of stone, a wave of apprehension washed over them. For the first time in their brief lives, many of them knew fear. The place looked ominous; how could they find mothers in such a fortress? Yet they pressed on bravely, trusting in Lona and in me, however unworthy I felt of that trust.

We rode through the sounding archway. Never had such a drumming of hooves, such a padding of paws, echoed so violently upon the ancient pavement. The horses startled at the resonance of their own steps; some halted, others plunged wildly and wheeled about, but they were soon calmed and continued. The Little Ones shivered, falling into a stillness as profound as death. The three girls clutched the infants they carried tighter. Only the bears and butterflies showed no fear; everyone else trembled.

A dark anxiety settled upon the woman's face, and I was not immune to the collective dread. The entire army rested on my shoulders and my conscience; I had led them to the very shadow of danger. Yet, I was bolstered by the vision of the coming kingdom of the Little Ones, where bad giants would be slaves and animals loving, obedient friends. Alas, the dreamer had not learned obedience himself. Untrusting, obstinate, I had placed myself at the head of this army of innocents, a slave to my own whims just like any king I had left behind. But Lona rode beside me as a child indeed—therefore a free woman—calm, silent, watchful, and utterly unafraid.

We were nearly at the city's heart before its inhabitants noticed us. Then, windows began to open, and sleepy heads peered out. Every face wore a dull stare of wonderless astonishment, which instantly curdled into consternation upon seeing the animals. Despite their terror, when they realized their invaders were almost entirely children, the women rushed into the streets, followed by the men. Yet, for a time, they kept to the edges of the road, daring not approach the beasts.

Finally, a boy of about five, obsessed with the idea of his mother, spotted a woman in the crowd whose face caught his eye. He threw himself from his antelope onto her, clinging to her neck. She returned his embrace eagerly, but a man's hand came over her shoulder, seizing the child by the throat. Instantly, a girl drove her sharp spear into the man's arm. He let out a savage howl and, stabbed by two or three others, fled yelling.

"They are just bad giants!" Lona cried, her eyes flashing as she drove her horse against a particularly tall man who, having stirred up a false sense of manhood, stood barring her path with a club. He dared not withstand the shock and slunk aside, only to fall moments later, struck by several stones. Another huge brute, avoiding my charger, stepped suddenly between me and the boy riding behind. The boy ordered him to address the king; the giant responded by striking the little horse on the head with a hammer, sending it crashing down. Before the brute could strike again, an elephant from behind laid him prostrate, trampling him so thoroughly that he did not attempt to rise until hundreds of feet had walked over him, and the army had passed.

But at the sight of the women, dismay clouded Lona's face. Hardly one of them possessed a pleasant countenance! Could her darlings ever find mothers among such figures?

Scarcely had we halted in the central square when two girls rode up in anxious haste, reporting that two boys had been hurried away by some women. We turned immediately, discovering that the woman we had befriended had vanished with her baby. At that exact moment, however, we saw a white leopardess bounding down a narrow lane leading from the square to the palace. The Little Ones remembered the fight of the two leopardesses in the forest; some looked terrified, their ranks wavering, but they recalled my command and stood fast.

We stopped to observe. Suddenly, a small boy named Odu, renowned for his speed and courage, leaped from his bear—which followed him shambling—and ran to meet the leopardess. To avoid knocking him down, she pulled herself up so abruptly that she rolled over and over. When she regained her footing, she found the child on her back. Who could doubt the subjugation of a people that saw an urchin of the enemy striding atop an animal they lived in daily terror of? Confident this would affect the whole army, we rode on.

As we stopped at the house where our guides led us, a scream pierced the air. I sprang down and thundered at the door. My horse pushed me away with his nose, turned, and began battering the door with his heels. Just then, little Odu arrived on the leopardess; at sight of her, he froze, trembling. But she too had heard the cry. Forgetting the child on her back, she threw herself at the door. The boy was dashed against it and fell senseless. Before I could reach him, Lona had him in her arms, and once he regained consciousness, set him back on his bear, which still followed.

When the leopardess threw herself against the door a third time, it gave way, and she darted inside. We followed, but she had already vanished. We climbed the stairs and searched the house, finding no one. Darting back down, we spotted a door beneath the stairs and entered a labyrinth of excavations. We had not gone far when we met the leopardess carrying the child we sought across her back.

He told us that the woman he believed to be his mother had thrown him into a hole, claiming she would give him to the leopardess. But the leopardess was good; she had taken him out.

Following in search of the other boy, we entered the next house more easily, only to find, alas, that we were too late: one of the savages had just killed the little captive! It consoled Lona to learn who he was, for she had feared he would grow into a bad giant, a fate from which death had mercifully saved him. The leopardess sprang upon his murderer, seized him by the throat, dragged him into the street, and followed Lona with him, like a cat clutching a great rat in her jaws.

"Let us leave this horrible place," said Lona. "There are no mothers here! This people is not worth delivering."

The leopardess dropped her burden and charged into the crowd, weaving this way and that wherever it was thickest. The slaves cried out and ran, tumbling over one another in heaps.

When we returned to the army, we found it exactly as we had left it, standing in order and ready.

Yet I was far from at ease. The princess gave no sign, and her intentions remained unknown. Watch and ward must be kept throughout the night!

The Little Ones were such hardy creatures that they could repose anywhere. We told them to lie down with their animals where they were and sleep until called. In one moment they were down, and in the next, lost in the music of their sleep—a sound like water over grass or a soft wind among leaves. Their animals slept more lightly, ever on the edge of waking. The older boys and girls walked softly among the dreaming multitude. All was still; the whole wicked place appeared at rest.
