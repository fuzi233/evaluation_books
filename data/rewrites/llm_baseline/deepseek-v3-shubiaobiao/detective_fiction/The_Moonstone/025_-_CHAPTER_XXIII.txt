The pony-chaise stood ready, as I had arranged, in case Mr. Franklin held to his resolve of departing that evening. The sight of his luggage descending the stairs, followed by the man himself, told me plainly that for once, he meant to keep his word.

“So you’re truly set on going, sir?” I ventured as we met in the hall. “Mightn’t you wait another day? Give Miss Rachel one more chance.”

The polished ease that usually cloaked Mr. Franklin had worn thin. Instead of answering, he pressed into my hand the letter her ladyship had sent him. Much of it echoed what I’d already read in my own—but one passage stood out, sharp as a knife.

*You will wonder,* my lady wrote, *at my allowing Rachel to keep me in the dark. A diamond worth twenty thousand pounds vanishes, and my own daughter treats its loss as no mystery—yet claims some unbreakable silence binds her, imposed by persons unknown, for reasons beyond my grasp. Should I tolerate such defiance? Under any other circumstances, no. But Rachel is in a state of nervous distress so profound it would wound you to see her. I dare not press her further. I have even dismissed the police officer; his presence torments her. This is not a riddle for outsiders to solve.

My course is clear. I shall take Rachel to London—for change, and for the counsel of physicians. Join us there, Franklin. You, like me, must exercise patience. Your efforts to recover the Moonstone, though well-intended, have only deepened her distress. She blames you for threatening secrets she refuses to name. I cannot defend such unreason—only plead for time.*

I returned the letter, my heart heavy for him. He loved Miss Rachel; that was plain enough. “You know what they say, sir,” I offered weakly. “When things are at their worst, they mend.”

Mr. Franklin gave no sign he took comfort from the proverb.

“When I first arrived with that cursed diamond,” he said, voice low, “this house was the happiest in England. Now look at it. Broken. Poisoned with suspicion. Do you remember our talk by the Shivering Sand, Betteredge? My uncle Herncastle’s revenge has worked through means he never dreamed of.”

With that, he clasped my hand and strode to the chaise.

I followed, wretched at the sight of him leaving so. Penelope—her spirits shaken by all that had passed—came weeping to bid him farewell. Mr. Franklin kissed her cheek. I raised a hand in silent blessing. Other maids peered around corners, drawn by the magnetism he had for women. At the last moment, I caught the chaise’s side.

“Where shall we write to you, sir?”

He barely seemed to hear, his gaze roaming over the house and grounds as though imprinting them on his soul. Then, abruptly, he pulled his hat low.

“Where am I going?” He laughed, a sound without joy. “To the devil, Betteredge.”

The pony startled as if the word scorched its ears. “God keep you, sir, wherever you go!” I called as the chaise rolled away. A gentleman to the last, for all his faults. The house felt hollow without him.

The evening stretched long and bleak. I clung to my pipe and *Robinson Crusoe* for solace. The maids (Penelope excepted) filled the hours with talk of Rosanna’s suicide, convinced she had stolen the Moonstone and killed herself in terror of discovery. My daughter held firm to her own theory—though it, like Miss Rachel’s claims of innocence, faltered at the mystery of Rosanna’s trip to Frizinghall and the nightgown. Arguing was useless; Penelope’s mind, like mine, was a fortress against reason.

Sunday brought the carriage back from Mr. Ablewhite’s, empty. A message informed me my lady would take Miss Rachel to London the next day. The maids were to follow with the necessary wardrobe. Most of the staff would go; I was to remain, overseeing the estate.

The stillness deepened my unease about Mr. Franklin. By Sunday’s post, I wrote to his father’s valet, Jeffco, begging news of his plans.

Monday jolted me alert. The first of Sergeant Cuff’s prophecies came true: I heard from the Yollands.

After seeing Penelope and my lady’s maid off with the luggage, I was wandering the grounds when a voice hailed me. I turned to face Limping Lucy, the fisherman’s daughter. Lean, fierce-eyed, and leaning on her crutch, she fixed me with a glare.

“Where’s Franklin Blake?” she demanded.

“You’ll speak of him as *Mr.* Blake, if you please,” I reproved.

She lurched closer, her face alight with fury. “*Murderer* Blake would fit better.”

Years of marriage had taught me how to handle a woman’s temper. I smiled. “Pooh.”

It worked. She stamped her crutch, near shaking with rage. “He killed Rosanna Spearman!”

Nearby workers glanced over, recognized Lucy, and wisely looked away.

“Why do you say that?” I asked gently.

Her wrath dissolved into grief. “She loved him,” Lucy whispered, head bowed. “A life of misery, yet her heart stayed sweet. We were to go to London—live as sisters. Then *he* came. ‘I can’t live without him,’ she told me. ‘And he never even looks at me.’” Lucy’s voice broke. “I had savings, plans… Then her letter came. She was done with life. Where is he?” She lifted her face, tears blazing. “When the poor rise against the rich, I pray they start with *him*.”

I steered her back. “What do you want with Mr. Franklin?”

“To give him a letter.”

“From Rosanna?”

“Yes.”

Here, at last, was a thread to follow. “He’s in London,” I said.

She studied me, saw the truth, and turned without a word.

“Wait! I’ll send it to him.”

Lucy glanced back. “It goes from my hand to his—no other way.”

“Shall I tell him you hate him?”

“Tell him the truth.”

She limped away, leaving me fevered with questions. Later, I learned no more from her parents. My last hope was a letter to Mr. Franklin.

Tuesday’s post brought two letters. Penelope wrote that my lady and Miss Rachel were settled in London, with rounds of amusements planned to lift Rachel’s spirits. Mr. Godfrey had called, still smitten, and been received kindly. My mistress, however, seemed downcast, consulting her lawyer at length.

The second letter, from Jeffco, struck like lightning. Mr. Franklin had left England. Arriving at his father’s house, he’d been dismissed—the elder Blake was immersed in parliamentary business. “Grieved for Rachel,” said he, “but speak to me at the session’s end.” Mr. Franklin’s response was terse: “Jeffco, when does the first train leave?” By dawn, he was gone—destination unknown.

Lucy’s letter would never reach him now. Whether it held a confession or mere despair, we’d never know. Rosanna’s secret sank with her into the Quicksand.

The week rolled on. Thursday brought more gossip from Penelope: balls, operas, and Mr. Godfrey’s unshaken devotion. My lady remained troubled.

Then, on Saturday, a London paper arrived—directed in Sergeant Cuff’s hand. A marked report detailed Indians harassing a Mr. Luker, a dealer in gems. The same Indians, perhaps, who had haunted us? The Sergeant’s third prophecy had come to pass.

So ends this chapter of the Moonstone’s shadow—with mysteries unresolved, and fate still weaving its net.
