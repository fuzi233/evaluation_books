"No one," said Durrance, fastening his field-glasses into the leather case at his hip. "No one, sir," Captain Mather replied, and the order was given.

The scouts surged ahead, the main body formed up, and the two seven-pounder mountain guns rolled into position. Durrance's detachment of the Camel Corps broke away from the gloomy, shadowed ridge of Khor Gwob—thirty-five miles southwest of Suakin—and descended onto the vast, sun-baked plateau of Sinkat. This was the final large-scale reconnaissance before the evacuation of Eastern Sudan.

All morning, the camels had labored slowly up a gully of shale, squeezed between red, precipitous cliffs. As the rocks finally receded, revealing a landscape of crumbling red heaps and desolate stone, the monotony of ruin was absolute. Not a blade of grass nor a ragged mimosa branch broke the silence until the valley opened below, where the green bushes of Sinkat offered a sudden, comforting splash of color against the parched earth.

The troopers sat with heightened alertness, moving in a diagonal line across the plateau toward the distant mountains of Erkoweet—a silent column on a plain that held its own profound quietude. It was eleven o'clock. The sun burned directly overhead in a cloudless, colorless sky, shortening the camel shadows until they gleamed white upon the sand, bright as a beach of the Scilly Isles. There was no breeze to stir the rich foliage; the shadows of the branches lay so still and distinct upon the ground that they might have been dead twigs scattered by a forgotten storm.

The only sounds were the sharp clink of weapons, the endless, soft padding of camel hooves, and occasionally the whirring flight of pigeons startled by the approaching cavalcade. Yet, life thrived here, though in a noiseless manner. As the leaders rode along the smooth, curved ridges of sand—trimmed and neat like carriage drives—they would occasionally glimpse herds of gazelles starting from the earth to race silently, flashes of dappled brown and white, toward the enclosing hills. It felt as though the country itself had been specially created for this final hour.

"Yet this way the caravans passed southward to Erkoweet and the Khor Baraka," Durrance said aloud, voicing a thought in his mind. "Here the Suakis built their summer-houses."

"And there Tewfik fought," Mather pointed forward, "and died with his four hundred men."

For three hours, the troops marched across the scorching plateau. It was May, and the sun hammered down with intolerable heat. Their initial alertness had long since faded; they rocked drowsily in their saddles, praying for the cool of evening and the silver shine of stars. The camels minced onward with their peculiar, smirking head movements until, suddenly, a hundred yards ahead, Durrance spotted a broken wall with window-like openings framing the sky.

"The fort," he announced.

Three years had passed since Osman Digna had captured and destroyed it, yet during those years, its roofless ruins had endured another siege—one no less persistent than the first. Quick-growing trees had tightly embraced the structure from the rear and sides, appearing as unexpected as Childe Roland encountering the Dark Tower in the plain. To the front, however, the sand stretched open to the wells, where three great Gemeiza trees with dark, spreading foliage stood spaced like sentinels.

In the shadow to the right-front, where bushes fringed the open sand with the level regularity of a riverbank, the soldiers unsaddled their camels and began preparing their food. Durrance and Mather walked around the perimeter. Reaching the southern corner, Durrance halted.

"Hallo!"

"Some Arab has camped here," Mather noted, stopping beside him. Grey ashes from a woodfire lay in a small heap upon a blackened stone.

"And recently," Durrance added.

Mather climbed a few rough steps to the crumbled archway of the entrance and stepped into the unroofed corridors. Durrance kicked the ashes over with his boot; the stump of a charred, whitened twig glowed faintly red. He pressed his foot down, and a tiny thread of smoke spurted upward.

"Very lately," he muttered, following Mather inside.

Young trees sprouted from every fissure in the mud walls, in the corners, and even from the floor itself. Behind the fort, a steep glacis and a deep moat defended the works. Durrance sat upon the parapet above the glacis as pigeons wheeled and circled overhead. He thought of the long months Tewfik must have spent straining his eyes from this exact spot, scanning the pass over the hills from Suakin, just as another general far to the south had done, waiting for sunlight flashing on weapons that never came.

Mather sat beside him, reflecting in a different spirit. "Already the Guards are steaming out through the coral reefs toward Suez. A week, and our turn comes," he said. "What a God-forsaken country!"

"I am coming back to it," Durrance replied.

"Why?"

"I like it. I like the people."

Mather found the sentiment unaccountable, yet he recognized that this trait had secured his companion's rapid rise. Sympathy had substituted for raw ability, granting Durrance the patience and understanding necessary to navigate the harried tribes of Eastern Sudan. He understood their hatred of the old Turkish rule, their fanaticism, and the forced feigning of belief under Osman Digna's hordes.

"Yes, I shall return," Durrance said firmly. "In three months' time. We all know—every Englishman in Egypt knows—that this cannot be the end. I wish to be here when the work resumes. I hate unfinished things."

The sun beat relentlessly; the men slept in the shade; the afternoon was as silent as the morning. Compelled by the overwhelming quiet, Durrance and Mather sat for a while in silence. Finally, Durrance's gaze shifted from the amphitheater of hills to the shrubbery beyond the glacis. His abstraction vanished, replaced by intense focus. He saw that Mather was staring in the same direction.

"What are you thinking about?" Durrance asked abruptly.

Mather laughed softly. "I was drafting the menu for the first dinner I'll have in London. Alone, I think, at Epitax. It will begin with a watermelon. And you?"

"I was wondering why, now that the pigeons have grown accustomed to us, they still circle one specific tree. Do not point it out, please—the one beyond the ditch, to the right of the two small bushes."

Everywhere else, pigeons rested quietly on the branches, speckling the foliage like purple fruit. Above only that single tree did they wheel and call timidly.

"We will secure that cover," Durrance ordered. "Take a dozen men and surround it quietly."

He remained on the glacis, watching the tree and the thick undergrowth. Six soldiers crept around from the left, six more from the right. Before they could complete the circle, the branches shook violently. An Arab, wrapped in a roll of yellowish dammar cloth and armed with a flat-headed spear and a hide shield, dashed from the shelter into the open plain.

He ran only a few yards. Mather gave a sharp command, and the Arab froze instantly before a rifle could even be raised to his shoulder. He walked calmly back to Mather, then ascended the glacis to stand before Durrance without insolence or servility.

"In Arabic," the Arab explained, "I am a man of the Kabbabish tribe, named Abou Fatma, and friendly to the English. I was on my way to Suakin."

"Why did you hide?" Durrance asked.

"It was safer. I knew you were friends. But, my gentleman, did you know me for yours?"

Durrance leaned forward quickly. "You speak English."

He switched languages. The answer came without hesitation. "I know a few words."

"Where did you learn them?"

"In Khartum."

Left alone with Durrance on the glacis, the two men conversed for nearly an hour. When the conversation ended, the Arab descended the slope, crossed the trench, and headed toward the hills. Durrance ordered the march resumed. Water tanks were filled, and the men replenished their zamshyehs (water skins), aware that the afternoon thirst was the most biting of all. Saddling the camels, they mounted amidst the usual groans and snorts, moving northwest from Sinkat at an acute angle to their morning route.

They skirted the hills opposite the pass they had descended earlier. The bushes grew sparse, giving way to a black landscape of stones, sparsely dotted with yellow, tassel-bearing mimosas. Durrance summoned Mather to his side.

"That Arab had a strange story," he said. "He was Gordon's servant in Khartum. Early in 1884—eighteen months ago, in fact—Gordon gave him a letter to deliver to Berber, where the contents were to be telegraphed to Cairo. But Berber had just fallen when he arrived. Seized and imprisoned the next day, he managed to spend one free hour hiding the letter in the wall of a house. To his knowledge, it has never been found."
