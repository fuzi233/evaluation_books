It was, as far as I can ascertain, in September of the year 1811 that a post-chaise drew up before the heavy oak doors of Aswarby Hall, nestled deep within the rolling countryside of Lincolnshire. The sole passenger, a small boy, leaped from the vehicle the instant it halted, his eyes scanning the grounds with a mixture of wide-eyed wonder and apprehension. Between the tolling of the bell and the creaking swing of the hall's door, he took in the imposing structure: a tall, square edifice of red brick, originally built in the reign of Queen Anne, though softened by a stone-pillared porch added in the neoclassical fashion of 1790. Its windows were numerous, tall, and narrow, framed in thick white wood, while a pediment crowned the front, pierced by a solitary round window. Flanking the central block were wings housing the stables and offices, linked by curious glazed galleries supported by colonnades. Each wing was topped with an ornamental cupola and a gilded vane.

An evening light bathed the building, causing the glass panes to glow like scattered embers. Beyond the hall stretched a flat park, dotted with ancient oaks and bordered by dark firs that silhouetted themselves against the twilight sky. In the distance, the clock tower of the local church, half-hidden in trees, struck six; its golden weather-vane caught the fading light, and the sound beat gently down the wind. For the boy standing in the porch, waiting for admission, it was a scene of profound beauty, yet tinged with the melancholic stillness of early autumn.

The carriage had brought him from Warwickshire, where he had been left an orphan six months prior. Now, thanks to the generous—if bewildering—offer of his elderly cousin, Mr. Abney, he was to reside at Aswarby. The proposal was startling to everyone who knew the Professor of Greek at Cambridge. Mr. Abney was regarded as an austere recluse, a man whose steady household seemed impervious to change. Little was known of his private pursuits or temper, save for rumors that he possessed an encyclopedic knowledge of the religious beliefs of the later pagans. His library was a treasure trove of texts on the Mysteries, the Orphic poems, the cult of Mithras, and Neo-Platonism. Indeed, in the marble-floored hall stood a magnificent statue of Mithras slaying the bull, imported from the Levant at great cost. He had even published descriptions of it in the *Gentleman's Magazine* and authored a series of articles in the *Critical Museum* regarding Roman superstitions. To his neighbors, he was a man consumed entirely by his books, making the news of his orphaned cousin, Stephen Elliott, arriving to live with him, a genuine shock.

Yet, contrary to expectation, Mr. Abney—a tall, thin, severe-looking man—appeared eager to welcome his young relative. The moment the front door swung open, he darted from his study, rubbing his hands with visible delight. "How are you, my boy?" he exclaimed, his voice warm despite his stern visage. "How are you? How old are you? Are you not too weary from your journey to eat your supper?"

"No, thank you, sir," replied Master Elliott politely. "I am pretty well."

"That's a good lad," Mr. Abney nodded. "And how old are you, my boy?"

It seemed peculiar that he should ask the question twice within two minutes.

"I'm twelve years old next birthday, sir," Stephen answered.

"And when is your birthday, my dear boy? Eleventh of September, eh? That's well—that's very well. Nearly a year hence, isn't it? I like—ha, ha!—I like to record these details in my book. Sure it's twelve? Certain?"

"Yes, quite sure, sir."

"Excellent. Take him to Mrs. Bunch's room, Parkes, and let him have his tea—or supper, whatever it is."

"Yes, sir," responded the staid Mr. Parkes, guiding Stephen toward the lower floors.

Mrs. Bunch proved to be the most comforting presence Stephen had encountered at Aswarby. Within a quarter-hour, they were fast friends, a bond that endured throughout his stay. Born in the neighborhood fifty-five years prior, she had served the Hall for twenty years. If anyone knew the secrets of the house and the district, it was Mrs. Bunch, and she was rarely reluctant to share them. Stephen, naturally inquisitive and adventurous, quickly turned her into a confidante.

"Who built the temple at the end of the laurel walk?" he asked one afternoon. "And who was the old man whose portrait hangs on the staircase, seated at a table with a skull beneath his hand?"

Such queries were easily satisfied by Mrs. Bunch's vast reservoir of local lore. However, some answers she offered were far less reassuring.

One November evening, Stephen sat by the fire in the housekeeper's room, contemplating his new surroundings. Suddenly, with the innocent certainty children possess regarding moral absolutes, he asked, "Is Mr. Abney a good man, and will he go to heaven?"

"Good? Bless the child!" Mrs. Bunch exclaimed. "Master is as kind a soul as I've ever seen! Didn't I tell you about the little boy he took in from the street seven years ago? And the little girl, two years after I first arrived?"

"No. Please tell me all about them, Mrs. Bunch—right now!"

"Well," she sighed, "the little girl... I don't recall her so clearly. I know Master brought her back from a walk one day and ordered Mrs. Ellis, the housekeeper then, to watch over her carefully. She had no family, she told me herself. She stayed with us for perhaps three weeks. Then, whether she had gypsy blood or not, one morning she vanished from her bed before anyone had opened their eyes. No trace of her was ever found. Master was distraught; he had all the ponds drained. But I believe she was spirited away by gypsies. There was singing around the house for hours that night, and Parkes swore he heard them calling in the woods all afternoon. She was such a quiet, domestic child, but I was terribly fond of her."

"And the little boy?" Stephen pressed.

"Oh, that poor boy!" Mrs. Bunch gasped. "He was a foreigner—he called himself Giovanni. One winter day, he was playing his hurdy-gurdy on the drive, and Master took him in immediately. He questioned him thoroughly about his origins, his age, and his family, treating him with infinite kindness. But fate, it seems, has a cruel sense of humor. Like the girl, he disappeared one fine morning. Where he went and what became of him remains a mystery to this day. He never returned his hurdy-gurdy; it sits on a shelf to this day."

The rest of the evening was spent in Stephen's relentless questioning and attempts to coax a melody from the forgotten instrument. That night, however, Stephen dreamed a nightmare.

At the end of the landing above his bedroom lay an old, disused bathroom. Usually locked, its upper half featured a glazed pane. Since the muslin curtains had long since been removed, one could peer inside and see the lead-lined tub fixed to the wall, facing the window. On that specific night, Stephen found himself staring through the glass. The moonlight streamed through the window, illuminating a figure lying motionless in the bath.

His description recalls a vision I once witnessed in the crypts of St. Michan's Church in Dublin, where bodies are preserved from decay for centuries. The figure was impossibly thin and pathetic, colored like dust-stained lead, wrapped in a shroud-like garment. Its lips were twisted into a faint, dreadful smile, and its hands were pressed tightly over the hollow of its heart. As Stephen watched, a distant, barely audible moan escaped the figure's lips, and its arms began to twitch. Terror seized him, forcing him backward until he woke to realize he was standing on the cold, wooden floor of the passage, bathed in moonlight.

With a bravery uncommon for a boy of his age, he approached the bathroom door to check if the specter was real. It was empty. He retreated to bed, shaken.

Next morning, Mrs. Bunch was deeply affected by his story and replaced the missing muslin curtain over the bathroom window. Mr. Abney, to whom Stephen confessed his dream at breakfast, showed intense interest, noting the event in a journal he referred to as "his book."

As the spring equinox approached, Mr. Abney frequently reminded Stephen that the ancients considered this time critical for the young. He advised caution, urging Stephen to keep his bedroom window shut at night, citing Censorinus's observations on the subject.

Two unsettling incidents followed shortly thereafter. The first occurred after a particularly restless night. The next evening, while Mrs. Bunch was repairing Stephen's nightshirt, she burst out, "Gracious me, Master Stephen! How do you manage to tear your nightdress to ribbons like this? Look here, sir, what trouble you cause the poor servants who must darn and mend after you!"

Indeed, the garment bore a destructive series of slits and scorches, requiring expert needlework to repair. They were concentrated on the left side of the chest—long, parallel cuts, about six inches in length, some failing to pierce the linen fully.
