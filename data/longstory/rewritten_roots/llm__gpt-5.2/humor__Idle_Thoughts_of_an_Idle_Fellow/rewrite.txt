One or two friends—people with the charitable palate that will praise a stew simply because it is warm—have tasted these pages in manuscript and pronounced them “not half bad.” A few relations, too, have pledged (in that brave, careless way families pledge) that they will buy the book if it ever appears, as though the purchase of print were a kind of seasonal duty, like laying in potatoes.

Under pressure so tremendous, I can no longer decently delay. There is, as one may say, a public demand: not a roar, perhaps—more the polite clink of a spoon against a teacup in the next room—but still, a summons. And so I set these little scraps of idleness before the English-speaking peoples of the earth, not as nourishment, mind you, but as something you might nibble when the serious fare has grown dry in the mouth.

For the fashion nowadays is that a book should improve, instruct, and elevate. It should make you better, taller, and more symmetrical in the moral spine. This one would not elevate a cow—nor even tempt her to rise. I cannot in conscience recommend it for any useful purpose whatever. It will not teach you how to manage an estate, cure a headache, translate a dead dialect, or silence a factory whistle. It is not an iron ration; it is a slice cut from loaf-hours when the house is quiet, when the kettle is singing its thin domestic song and the world’s grand engines have not yet begun their grinding.

All I can suggest is this: when you have tired your jaw on “the best hundred books,” and the noise of improvement has become like a brass band practicing in your skull, take this up for half an hour. Let it be a change—like stepping out of the street’s metallic din into a kitchen where you can hear the humane sounds again: the soft chop of a knife, the sigh of bread cooling, the old syllables of home that do not translate cleanly into any fashionable language, but taste, to those who know them, of childhood and firelight.

THE IDLE THOUGHTS OF AN IDLE FELLOW.

On being idle, I speak with the quiet authority of a man who has never been interrupted by a work ethic. The gentleman who used to pour education over my head at nine guineas a term—no extras, no gravy—declared I was the only boy he had ever met who could do less work in more time. My grandmother, who believed the Prayer-book could cure anything except my character, once remarked—by the way, as if it were merely a note about punctuation—that it seemed highly unlikely I should ever do very much I ought not to do, but that she felt certain I should leave undone nearly everything I ought to do.

I have, I regret to announce, proved her only half right. Heaven help me, I have done a great many things I ought not to have done, and that in spite of my laziness, which shows that sin has, at times, a kind of energy that virtue lacks. But in the matter of not doing what I ought, I have remained devout. Idling has always been my strong point. I take no credit for it. It is a gift, like a perfect pie crust: many attempt it, few achieve it, and those who do cannot explain how.

Because a genuine idler is not merely a lazy person. The world is full of slow-coaches and loiterers—people who shuffle about like underbaked dough, damp and heavy and determined to stick to everything. But the true idler is a rarity. He is not the man with his hands in his pockets. On the contrary, his most startling feature is that he always looks intensely occupied. The sacred art demands it. It is impossible to enjoy idleness properly unless one has plenty to do. There is no flavor in doing nothing when you have nothing to do; it is like chewing with an empty mouth. Wasting time then becomes an occupation—an exhausting one—and you come to the end of the day as fatigued as a cook who has stirred a pot that contains only air.

Idleness, like kisses, to be sweet must be stolen.

Many years ago—when I was young enough to imagine that my body was a loyal tenant rather than a landlord with opinions—I was taken very ill. I never could see that much was the matter with me beyond a beastly cold, the kind that makes a man feel tragically poetic and yet also resentful that nobody is arranging lilies around him. But it must have been something serious, for the doctor said I ought to have come to him a month earlier, and that if it—whatever it was—had gone on another week he wouldn’t have answered for the consequences.

It is an extraordinary thing that no doctor has ever been called in yet without it turning out that one more day’s delay would have made recovery hopeless. Our medical guide, philosopher, and friend is like the hero in a melodrama: he always arrives precisely—and only just—in the nick of time. Providence has a stethoscope. That is the only explanation.

Well, I was ordered off to Buxton for a month with strict instructions to do nothing whatever the whole time I was there.

“Rest is what you require,” said the doctor. “Perfect rest.”

It sounded delicious. Here, I thought, is a man who understands my complaint. I pictured a glorious month: four weeks of _dolce far niente_ with a tasteful dash of illness—just enough suffering to give it a bouquet, not so much as to spoil the digestion. I should get up late, sip chocolate, and take breakfast in slippers and a dressing-gown, with the serene air of a monk who has made vows of comfort. I should lie in a hammock in the garden and read sentimental novels that end badly, until the book slid from my listless fingers; and then I would gaze into the deep blue sky, watching the clouds go by like white-sailed ships, listening to the birds and the soft rustle of trees—those old rural sounds that used to belong to England before the age began to grind and shriek.

Or, if I became too weak to go out, I should sit propped at an open ground-floor window, looking wasted and interesting, so that all the pretty girls would sigh as they passed. Twice a day I should be wheeled in a Bath chair to the Colonnade to drink the waters—“drinking the waters” sounded fashionable, antique, Queen-Anne-ish, like something done by people who had footmen and kept their illnesses in silver boxes.

Oh, those waters. I knew nothing about them then. After the first three or four mornings, romance surrendered. Sam Weller’s description of their tasting like “warm flat-irons” is only a timid bow in the direction of the truth. They had the peculiar flavor of something that had been boiled in its own disappointment and then served with a moral. If anything could make a sick man get well quickly, it would be the knowledge that he must swallow a glass of that stuff every day until he recovers.

For six consecutive days I drank them neat and they nearly finished what the cold had started. After that, I adopted the sensible plan of taking a stiff brandy-and-water immediately on top of them, like laying a thick slice of buttery bread over a bad memory. It brought immediate relief. Various eminent medical gentlemen have since told me that the alcohol must have entirely counteracted the chalybeate virtues of the spring. I am delighted to hear it. It is always pleasant to learn, after the event, that one has been right.

But the waters were only a small portion of the torture. That month at Buxton—gouty, consumptive Buxton, with its air of having been designed as a waiting-room for ailments—was, without exception, the most miserable month I have ever spent. I did, for the best part of it, exactly what I had been ordered: nothing. I mooned about the house and garden and went out for two hours a day in a Bath chair.

The Bath chair, at first, broke the monotony. There is more excitement in Bath-chairing than an outsider imagines. A continual sense of peril accompanies the occupant. He is convinced every minute the entire contraption is going to topple; and the conviction becomes vivid whenever a ditch appears or you approach a stretch of newly macadamized road—those loose stones that rattle like cheap applause. Every passing vehicle he expects will run into him; and he never goes up or down a hill without immediately calculating his chances, supposing—an assumption that seems dreadfully probable—that the weak-kneed controller of his destiny should let go.

Even that exhilaration grew stale. The ennui became unbearable; I felt my mind giving way under it. My mind is not a robust instrument, and I judged it unwise to strain it. Somewhere about the twentieth morning I got up early, ate a good breakfast—solid, honest work on toast and whatever else a hotel believes will keep a man quiet—and walked straight off to Hayfield at the foot of Kinder Scout.

It is a pleasant, busy little town reached through a lovely valley, the sort of valley where you can still hear water doing what water is meant to do instead of being forced through iron throats, and where the wind speaks without having to compete with machinery. And it contained two sweetly pretty women—at least they were sweetly pretty then. One passed me on the bridge and, I think, smiled; the other stood in an open doorway making an unremunerative investment of kisses on a red-faced baby. It is years ago, and I dare say they have both grown stout and snappish since—time has a way of turning smiles into opinions.

On the way back I saw an old man breaking stones. The sound of it—stone on stone, regular and sharp—did something to me. It wasn’t the kind of noise that sets the nerves on edge like the city’s metallic din; it had a human rhythm, like bread being chopped or herbs being pounded, labor that belongs to hands. It roused such a longing in me to use my arms that I offered him a drink if he would let me take his place.

He was a kindly old man and humored me. I went for those stones with the accumulated energy of three weeks and did more work in half an hour than he had done all day. It did not make him jealous. Old age has its own untranslatable dialect of patience; it does not envy the young their bursts of foolish strength any more than a baker envies a child his ability to eat sugar with a spoon.

Having taken the plunge, I went further into dissipation. I began going out for a long walk every morning, and every evening I listened to the band in the pavilion. The band played, and the sound wandered through the air, trying to make the town feel cheerful by sheer brass. But the days still passed slowly, as if time itself had been put on a restricted diet. And when at last the final day came and I was being whirled away from Buxton to London, I was heartily glad.

We rushed through Hendon in the evening. I looked out, and the lurid glare hanging over the great city seemed to warm my heart like the sight of a kitchen fire after too long among cold plates. Later, when my cab rattled out of St. Pancras, the old familiar roar swelling up around me—wheels, voices, hoofs, a thousand restless echoes—sounded the sweetest music I had heard for many a long day. It was industrial, it was brutal, it was the voice of work; and after Buxton’s commanded silence it felt like being allowed to breathe again.

I certainly did not enjoy that month’s idling. I like idling when I ought not to be idling; not when it is the only thing I have to do. That is my pig-headed nature. The moment I most enjoy standing with my back to the fire, calculating how much I owe, is when my desk is heaped highest with letters that must be answered by the next post. The moment I most enjoy lingering over dinner is precisely when I have a heavy evening’s work before me—then I can taste each mouthful as if it were stolen, each sip of wine as if it were a small rebellion. And if, for any urgent reason, I ought to be up particularly early, it is then—more than at any other time—that I love to lie an extra half-hour in bed.

How delicious it is to turn over and go to sleep again—“just for five minutes.”

Is there any human being, I wonder—besides the hero of a Sunday-school “tale for boys”—who ever gets up willingly? There are men to whom getting up at the proper time is an utter impossibility. If eight o’clock is when they must rise, then they lie till half-past. If circumstances change and half-past eight becomes early enough, then it is nine before they can move. They are like the statesman of whom it was said that he was always punctually half an hour late.

They try all schemes. They buy alarm-clocks—artful contrivances that go off at the wrong time and alarm the wrong people. They instruct Sarah Jane to knock and call them, and Sarah Jane knocks and calls, and they grunt back “Awri,” in a dialect known only to bedclothes, and then go comfortably to sleep again. I knew one man who would actually get out and have a cold bath; and even that was useless, for afterward he jumped into bed again to warm himself, like a lobster returning to the pot out of gratitude.

For my own part, I believe I could keep out of bed well enough if once I got out. It is the wrenching away of the head from the pillow that is hard. No amount of overnight determination makes it easier. I say to myself after wasting the whole evening, “Well, I won’t do any more work tonight; I’ll get up early tomorrow morning,” and I am thoroughly resolved—then. In the morning I am less enthusiastic and reflect that it would have been much better if I had sat up last night. And then there is the trouble of dressing, and the more one thinks about dressing the more one wants to put it off. Buttons are little tyrants. Boots are moralists.

It is a strange thing, this bed—this mimic grave—where we stretch tired limbs and sink away so quietly into silence and rest. “O bed, O bed, delicious bed, that heaven on earth to the weary head,” as sang poor Hood—you are a kind old nurse to us fretful boys and girls. Clever and foolish, naughty and good, you take us all in your motherly lap and hush our wayward crying. The strong man full of care, the sick man full of pain, the little maiden sobbing for her faithless lover—like children we lay our aching heads on your white bosom and you soothe us off to by-by. Our trouble is sore indeed when you turn away and will not comfort us.

How long the dawn seems coming when we cannot sleep. Those hideous nights when we toss and turn in fever and pain, lying like living men among the dead, staring into the dark hours that drift so slowly between us and the light. And those still more hideous nights when we sit by another in pain, when the low fire startles us now and then with a falling cinder, and the tick of the clock seems a hammer beating out the life we are watching. In such moments you learn that sound is not merely sound: it is a climate. A gentle clock is tolerable; an obstinate one can become a form of tyranny. The world talks about silence as if it were always peace, but there is a silence that screams, and there are noises—honest, human noises—that soothe. One cannot translate these experiences into any neat vocabulary; the words are too blunt. The dominant language always tries to label everything—_rest_, _health_, _convalescence_—as though the same syllables will fit every throat. They do not. Each body speaks its own dialect, and illness has idioms no dictionary admits.

But enough of beds and bedrooms. I have kept to them too long, even for an idle fellow. Let us come out and have a smoke. That wastes time just as well and does not look so bad.

Tobacco has been a blessing to us idlers. What the civil-service clerk before Sir Walter’s time found to occupy his mind with is hard to imagine. I attribute the quarrelsome nature of young men in the Middle Ages entirely to the lack of the soothing weed. They had no work to do and could not smoke; consequently they were forever fighting and rowing. If, by some extraordinary chance, there was no war going, they got up a deadly family feud with the next-door neighbor. If, in spite of this, they still had a few spare moments, they occupied them with discussions as to whose sweetheart was the best-looking—the arguments employed being battle-axes, clubs, and similar aids to criticism. Questions of taste were soon settled in those days.

When a twelfth-century youth fell in love he did not take three paces backward, gaze into her eyes, and tell her she was too beautiful to live. He said he would step outside and see about it. And if, when he got out, he met a man and broke his head—the other man’s head, I mean—then that proved his girl was a pretty girl. But if the other fellow broke _his_ head—look here: if A broke B’s head, then A’s girl was pretty; but if B broke A’s head, then A’s girl wasn’t pretty but B’s girl was. That was their method of conducting art criticism.

Nowadays we light a pipe and let the girls fight it out among themselves. They do it very well. They are getting to do all our work. They are doctors, barristers, artists. They manage theatres, promote swindles, edit newspapers. I look forward to the time when we men shall have nothing to do but lie in bed till twelve, read two novels a day, have nice little five-o’clock teas all to ourselves, and tax our brains with nothing more trying than discussions upon the latest patterns in trousers, and arguments as to what Mr. Jones’s coat was made of and whether it fitted him.

It is a glorious prospect—for idle fellows.

You have been in love, of course. If you have not, the thing is merely waiting for you like a seasonal fever that travels down families the way a sourdough starter does—passed along with solemn warnings and an indulgent smile, and then, when it takes, it takes with a heat that makes ordinary life taste suddenly under-seasoned.

Love comes like the measles: everyone catches it. And, like the measles, you only have it once.

After that first bout you are, if not healthier, at least astonishingly fearless. The man who has already been in love can go anywhere—into the very thick of dangerous scenery and sentimental arrangements—and feel as safe as a cook in his own kitchen. You may send him to picnic under trees where the leaves whisper like conspirators. He will wander down green aisles that smell of sap and damp earth, and sit on mossy seats at sunset, and not so much as itch. He can spend a week-end in a quiet country house—those padded traps of family intimacy—without breaking out all over. He can even join a party to go down the Rhine, and survive the music, the moonlight, the “accidental” hand-touching over rugs and guidebooks. He can, to see the last of a friend, walk right into the jaws of a wedding—where the air is thick with lilies, sighs, and the grinding of social machinery—and come out with nothing worse than a faint appetite for beef.

He can keep his head through a ravishing waltz, that circling, churning noise of shoes and strings, and afterward sit in a dark conservatory where the plants are breathing heavily and the world smells green and wet, and catch nothing more lasting than a cold. He can brave a moonlight walk down sweet-scented lanes, and a twilight pull among rushes that hiss and gossip at the oars, and neither stile nor hedge nor slippery path will lay hold of him. He can look into sunny eyes and not be dazzled; listen to siren voices and still steer straight on; clasp a white hand and feel no electric “Lulu” force fastening him forever to dainty fingers. Cupid, having once spent his best shot, is too economical to waste a second arrow on the same heart.

The handmaids of love—respect, admiration, affection—these will always call. You can leave the latch-string out for them; they are decent neighbors, and they do not overturn the furniture. But their great celestial master, in his royal progress, pays one visit and departs. We like; we cherish; we grow very, very fond of—but we never love again.

A man’s heart, when it loves, is a firework that goes up once in its time and explodes, for a moment, into heaven. It makes the whole dull street of life below it seem lit by glory. Then night closes in again—our nightly, greasy commonplace—while the burnt-out case falls back to earth and lies there, a useless bit of paper and wire that nobody wants, slowly smouldering into ash.

Once, breaking loose from our prison bonds, we dare—as that obstinate old Prometheus dared—to climb up and steal fire from the gods. Happy those who come down quickly enough, while the flame still lives, and kindle their own hearth with it. Love is too pure a blaze to burn long in the smoky back-parlour air we breathe every day—coal-dust, bills, petty worries, other people’s opinions—but before it chokes, we can use it as a torch to light something more practical: the steady kitchen-fire of affection.

Love belongs, properly, in some mighty temple: a vast dim place where the organ music is the rolling of the spheres. Affection belongs in a house with a table that bears knife-marks, a kettle that sings true, and a fire that can be fed. It will burn cheerily when the white flame of love has flickered out. It is a fire that you can build day by day; you can bank it higher as the wintry years draw on. Old men and women can sit by it, thin hands clasped. Children can crouch in front, warm as rolls on a rack. A friend or neighbor can pull up a chair. Even the dog can toast his nose and the cat can pretend not to.

Heap the coals of kindness on that fire. Throw on pleasant words as you would throw on dry sticks; lay in gentle pressures of the hand like good kindling—small, sure, and frequent. Add thoughtful deeds the way a careful cook adds salt: not in showy handfuls, but as a habit. Fan it with good-humor, patience, forbearance. Then let the wind howl and the rain beat at the panes: you will have warmth inside, and faces around your hearth will make a sunshine that does not depend upon the weather.

But I am afraid, dear Edwin and Angelina—since every generation produces an Edwin and an Angelina, fresh as spring cabbages—you expect too much from love. You think there is enough in your two little hearts to feed that fierce, devouring passion for all your long lives. Ah, young folk! Do not stake the whole larder on that one glorious flare. It will dwindle and dwindle as the months roll on, and there is no going back to the market for more of the same fuel.

You will watch it die, and you will do it with anger and disappointment, because each of you will be quite sure it is the other who is growing cold. Edwin will notice bitterly that Angelina no longer runs to the gate when he comes, all blushes and breathless joy, as if he were a tray of hot buns. When he has a cough now, she does not burst into tears, fling her arms round his neck, and declare she cannot live without him. The most she will probably do is suggest a lozenge—and even that in a tone implying that it is the noise, more than the danger, she is anxious to get rid of. (There is an acoustic tyranny in love at the beginning: every cough is an omen; later it is simply a disturbance, like a badly-tuned fiddle in the next room.)

Poor little Angelina, too, will shed silent tears, for Edwin has given up carrying her old handkerchief in the inside pocket of his waistcoat. Both are astonished at the falling off in the other, but neither sees their own change. If they did, they would not suffer as they do. They would look for the cause in the proper place—in the smallness of poor human nature—join hands over their common failing, and begin again, building their house upon a foundation that is earthy and enduring.

But we are blind to our own shortcomings and wide awake to those of others. Whatever happens is always somebody else’s fault. Angelina would have loved Edwin forever and ever and ever, if only Edwin had not grown so strange and different. Edwin would have adored Angelina through eternity, if only Angelina had remained precisely as she was when he first adored her. There comes a cheerless hour for you both when the lamp of love has gone out and the fire of affection is not yet lit, and you have to grope about in the cold raw dawn, trying to strike a spark.

God grant it catches before the day is too far spent. Many a couple sits shivering by dead coals until night comes.

But what is the use of preaching? Who that feels the rush of young love through his veins can imagine it will ever run thin and slow? To a boy of twenty it seems impossible that he will not love as wildly at sixty. He cannot call to mind any middle-aged or elderly gentleman of his acquaintance known to exhibit symptoms of frantic attachment—but that does not interfere with his belief in himself. Other people are built of inferior materials. His will be the one eternal romance, preserved like fruit in sugar.

Alas! alas! before thirty he has joined the ranks of the sneerers.

It is not his fault. Our passions—good and bad alike—fade with our blushes. We do not hate, nor grieve, nor rejoice, nor despair in our thirties as we did in our teens. Disappointment no longer suggests suicide; success is quaffed without intoxication. We begin to take everything in a minor key. Life, in the later acts, has few majestic passages; the orchestra has gone home, and we are left with the scraping of chairs, the clink of coins, the continual industrial clatter of the world—noise that claims to be progress and is mostly only racket. Ambition grows less ambitious. Honor becomes more reasonable and adapts itself conveniently to circumstances.

And love—love dies.

A killing frost creeps over the heart: irreverence for the dreams of youth. The tender shoots are nipped; the expanding blossoms wither. Of a vine that wanted to fling its tendrils round the whole world there remains a sapless stump.

My fair friends will call this heresy, I know. So far from a man’s not loving after boyhood, it is not until there is a good deal of gray in his hair that many of you consider his protestations worth listening to. Young ladies, I fear, take their notions of men chiefly from novels written by their own sex; and compared with the monsters that masquerade as “men” between those yellow covers, Pythagoras’ plucked bird and Frankenstein’s demon might pass for decent, everyday humans.

In these books, the chief lover—the “Greek god,” as he is admiringly termed (they never say which one; it might be hump-backed Vulcan, double-faced Janus, or drivelling Silenus of abstruse mysteries)—resembles the whole family chiefly in being a blackguard. But even to the little manliness his classical originals possessed he cannot lay claim, being a listless, effeminate noodle on the shady side of forty. And oh, the depth and strength of this elderly person’s emotion for some bread-and-butter school-girl! Hide your heads, young Romeos and Leanders: this blasé old beau loves with such hysterical fervor that it takes four adjectives to every noun to describe it.

It is well for us old sinners that you study books more than people. If you read mankind, you would know the lad’s shy stammer tells a truer tale than our bold eloquence. A boy’s love comes from a full heart; a man’s, more often, is the result of a full stomach. There is a kind of sentimental digestion that masquerades as devotion. Compared with the rushing fountain that bursts up when a boy’s heart is struck by the heavenly rod, a man’s sluggish current should hardly be called love at all.

If you would taste love, drink it from the pure stream youth pours out at your feet. Do not wait until it has become a muddy river before you stoop to cup it in your hands. Or is it that you like the bitter flavor? Is clear water insipid to your palate, and only the pollution of its after-course gives it relish? Must we believe those who bawl, day after day, from between yellow covers, that a hand dirty with the filth of a shameful life is the only one a young girl cares to be caressed by?

Do those devil’s lady-helps ever pause to think what mischief they do, crawling about God’s garden, telling childish Eves and silly Adams that sin is sweet and decency ridiculous and vulgar? How many an innocent girl do they not degrade into an evil-minded woman? To how many a weak lad do they not point out the dirty by-path as the shortest cut to a maiden’s heart?

It is not even that they write life as it is. Speak truth, and right can take care of itself. But their pictures are coarse daubs painted from sickly fancies, and the language they use—those tawdry labels pasted over complicated human feeling—ends by bullying reality into their cramped vocabulary. They teach young people to desire not experience, but the fashionable words for experience. And what is a heart to do, when its own dialect for joy and reverence has been laughed out of it, and it must borrow the shop-soiled phrases of a dominant, noisy lexicon?

We want to think of women not—as their own sex would sometimes parade them—as Loreleis luring men to destruction, but as good angels beckoning upward. They have more power for good or evil than they dream. It is at the very age when a man’s character is forming that he tumbles into love, and then the girl he loves has the making or the marring of him. Unconsciously he shapes himself to what she would have him—good or bad.

I am sorry to be ungallant enough to say I do not think they always use their influence well. Too often the female world is bounded hard and fast within the limits of the commonplace. Their ideal hero is a prince of littleness, and to become that many a powerful mind, enchanted by love, is “lost to life and use and name and fame.” And yet you could make us so much better, if you only would. It rests with you, more than with all the preachers, to roll this world a little nearer heaven.

Chivalry is not dead; it only sleeps for want of work. You must wake it. You must be worthy of knightly worship. You must be higher than ourselves. It was for Una that the Red Cross Knight went to war. No painted, mincing court-dame would have had a dragon slain for her. Oh, ladies fair, be fair in mind and soul as well as face, so that brave knights may win glory in your service! Oh, woman, throw off those disguising cloaks of selfishness, effrontery, affectation. Stand forth once more a queen in the royal robe of simple purity. A thousand swords, now rusting in ignoble sloth, would leap from their scabbards to do battle for your honor against wrong. A thousand Sir Rolands would lay lance in rest, and Fear, Avarice, Pleasure, Ambition would go down in the dust before your colors.

What noble deeds were we not ripe for in the days when we loved? What noble lives could we not have lived for her sake? Our love was a religion we could have died for. It was not a mere human creature like ourselves that we adored. It was a queen we paid homage to, a goddess we worshipped. And how madly we worshipped—and how sweet it was!

Ah, lad, cherish love’s young dream while it lasts. You will know too soon how truly little Tom Moore sang when he said there was nothing half so sweet in life. Even when it brings misery, it is a wild romantic misery—sharp, spiced, and strange—unlike the dull worldly pain of later sorrows, which is like eating bread that has been left uncovered in a noisy workshop: it will keep you alive, but it has no savor, and the grit between your teeth makes you angry for no good reason.

When you have lost her—when the light is gone from your life and the world stretches before you a long dark horror—even then a half-enchantment mingles with your despair. And who would not risk its terrors to gain its raptures?

Ah, what raptures they were! The mere recollection thrills you like the first scent of baking when you come in from cold streets. How delicious it was to tell her you loved her, lived for her, would die for her! What floods of extravagant nonsense you poured out—and how cruel it was of her to pretend not to believe you. In what awe you stood of her! How miserable you were when you had offended her! And yet how pleasant to be bullied by her, and to sue for pardon without the faintest idea what your fault had been.

How dark the world was when she snubbed you—often, the little rogue, merely to see you look wretched; how sunny when she smiled. How jealous you were of everyone about her: every man she shook hands with, every woman she kissed, the maid who did her hair, the boy who cleaned her shoes, the dog she nursed—though you had to be respectful to the last-named, because the dog, at least, had constant access.

How you looked forward to seeing her; how stupid you were when you did see her, staring without a word, as if you’d been struck dumb by a smell too fine to name. How impossible it was to go out at any hour without finding yourself, sooner or later, opposite her windows. You hadn’t pluck enough to go in, but you hovered on the corner, gazing at the outside, listening to the house’s ordinary noises—the shutting of doors, the faint clink of tea-things—until those small domestic sounds became, to you, a kind of music.

Oh, if only the house had caught fire—it was insured, so it wouldn’t have mattered—and you could have rushed in and saved her at the risk of your life, and been terribly burned and injured! Anything to serve her.

Even in little things it was sweet. How you watched her, spaniel-like, to anticipate her slightest wish. How proud you were to do her bidding. How delightful to be ordered about. To devote your whole life to her and never think of yourself seemed the simplest thing. You would go without a holiday to lay a humble offering at her shrine, and feel more than repaid if she only deigned to accept it.

How precious everything was that she had hallowed by her touch: her little glove, the ribbon she had worn, the rose that had nestled in her hair—whose withered leaves still mark the poems you never care to look at now. And oh, how beautiful she was—how wondrous beautiful! As though an angel had entered the room and all else became plain and earthly. She was too sacred to be touched. It seemed presumption to gaze. You would as soon have thought of kissing her as of singing comic songs in a cathedral. It was desecration enough to kneel and timidly raise the gracious little hand to your lips.

Ah, those foolish days—those foolish days when we were unselfish and pure-minded; those foolish days when our simple hearts were full of truth and faith and reverence; those foolish days of noble longings and noble strivings!

And oh, these wise, clever days—when we know money is the only prize worth striving for, when we believe in nothing but meanness and lies, when we care for no living creature but ourselves.

I can take a melancholy mood as I can take a glass of bitter beer: with a certain relish, and even a pride in the face I pull while swallowing it. There is an honest comfort, too, in being properly wretched—like sitting down, of your own free will, to a dish that you know will scorch your tongue and punish you afterward, simply because it is the dish that suits the weather.

But nobody enjoys a fit of the blues.

The blues are not sorrow with a pedigree. They are not grief with a reason, nor regret with a name you can pronounce in your mother’s dialect and have it land, warm and exact, on the table between you like a loaf. The blues are a sudden, sour mis-seasoning of the soul; a thing that comes in, uninvited, like a gust from the street that drags soot across your clean cloth. Everybody has them; nobody can tell you why. You are as likely to be seized by them on the morning after you have come into a fortune as on the morning after you have left your new silk umbrella in the train—both events equally capable of throwing the universe out of joint, it appears, provided the universe is in a mischievous mood.

Their effect upon you is what might be produced by toothache, indigestion, and a cold in the head combining into a little band and marching through your person, beating tin pans. The world’s sounds go wrong first. The ordinary hum of living—kettle-lid, cart-wheel, distant children—ceases to be a choir and becomes a factory. The street’s roar is not merely loud; it is intrusive, like a neighbour playing a waltz on a cracked cornet directly into your ear and calling it Art. Your own breathing seems badly tuned. Even the tick of the clock grows insolent, as though it were being paid by the insult.

You become stupid, restless, and irritable; rude to strangers and dangerous to your friends; clumsy, maudlin, quarrelsome; a nuisance to yourself and everybody about you. While it is on you, you can do nothing and think of nothing—though you feel at the time bound to do something, as a cook feels bound to “make a dinner” when the larder is empty and the knives all blunt.

You cannot sit still. You put on your hat and go out for a walk; but before you get to the corner you wish you had not come out, because the pavement has begun to sound like a drum, and the voices of the passers-by are as sharp as grated horseradish. You turn back, convinced the air is not fit for human lungs and that every cart in London has made it its mission to choose your street for rehearsal.

You open a book and try to read, because books are supposed to be soothing; but to a man in the blues literature is like cold tea. Shakespeare tastes trite and commonplace, as though he had been reheated too often. Dickens is dull and prosy, like stew that has been kept on the back of the range all day and has lost its good fat. Thackeray is a bore—dry as yesterday’s crust. Carlyle is too sentimental, which is a curious complaint to make, but the blues are great at taking a reasonable objection and salting it to death. You fling the book aside and call the author names in a vocabulary you would be ashamed to use in any decent kitchen.

Then you “shoo” the cat out of the room, not because she has done anything, but because her quiet padding suggests contentment, and contentment is an offence. You kick the door to after her, making a bang that hurts you more than it hurts the door, and you are instantly furious with the door for being made of wood and not feeling it.

You think you will write your letters. You sit down with paper as white as flour and a pen that has the effrontery to drip. You begin:

“Dearest Auntie: I find I have five minutes to spare, and so hasten to write to you—”

and there you stick, as if the sentence were a dumpling that refuses to cook through. You stare at it for a quarter of an hour, unable to think of another line, because in the blues every word in the language becomes either too big, too small, or in the wrong dialect. There are feelings you can taste clearly enough—like burnt sugar, like stale butter—but when you go to name them in the common tongue they will not translate. They sit in you mutely, as unrenderable as a grandmother’s old field-saying that has no equivalent outside its valley. You tumble the paper into the desk, fling the wet pen down upon the table-cloth, and start up with the resolution of going to see the Thompsons.

While pulling on your gloves, however, you remember that the Thompsons are idiots; that they never have supper; and that you will be expected to jump the baby. It is a terrible thing, in the blues, to think of a house where there is no supper. Even despair draws the line at tea and no solid. You curse the Thompsons (not for being themselves—that would be too philosophical—but for having arranged the universe so that you must meet them) and decide not to go.

By this time you feel completely crushed. You bury your face in your hands and think you would like to die and go to heaven. You picture your own sick-bed with a cook’s attention to details you would never show in health: the position of the pillows, the shade of the light, the hush in the room that is not peace but performance. All your friends and relations are standing round, weeping. You bless them all, especially the young and pretty ones, because the blues have an odd instinct for decoration. You imagine how they will value you when you are gone, and learn too late what they have lost; and you bitterly contrast their presumed reverence then with their decided want of veneration now.

This makes you feel a little more cheerful—for a brief period. Then the blues, who allow no comfort to stand unchallenged, whisper in a colder voice: What a fool you are to suppose anybody would care at anything that might happen to you. Who would care two straws—whatever exact measure of affection two straws represent—whether you are blown up, hung up, married, or drowned? Nobody cares for you. You have never been properly appreciated, never met with your due deserts in any one particular. You review your whole past life, and it is painfully apparent that you have been ill-used from your cradle; and it is astonishing, when you come to think of it, that a creature so persecuted has managed to eat as many dinners as you have.

Half an hour of such thinking works you up into a state of savage fury against everybody and everything—especially yourself, whom anatomical reasons alone prevent your kicking. The blues are remarkably ingenious at turning a man into his own enemy, setting him to scolding himself in a language he would not permit a stranger to use, because self, in these moments, is a conquered province.

Bed-time comes at last, to save you from doing something rash. You spring upstairs, throw off your clothes, leaving them strewn about the room like the wreck of an argument, blow out the candle, and jump into bed as if you had backed yourself for a heavy wager to do the whole thing against time.

There you toss and tumble for a couple of hours, varying the monotony by occasionally jerking the clothes off and getting out and putting them on again, as though trousers were a remedy. You listen to the house’s noises: the settling of boards, the sigh of the fire going out, the distant clink of something in the scullery that is probably nothing at all. In the blues, every small sound is large and every large sound is personal. The world has no right to creak without consulting you. At length you drop into an uneasy, fitful slumber, have bad dreams, and wake up late the next morning—heavy as a pudding that has never known a proper suet.

At least, this is all we poor single men can do under the circumstances.

Married men manage their blues with more variety. They bully their wives, grumble at the dinner, and insist on the children’s going to bed. Which, creating as it does a good deal of disturbance in the house, must be a great relief to the feelings of a man in the blues—rows being the only form of amusement in which he can take any interest. There is a comfort, no doubt, in being able to turn your inward discord into outward noise; it is like having a pot boil over and blaming the fire. Single men must content themselves with internal racket.

The symptoms of the infirmity are much the same in every case, but the affliction is variously termed, because language loves to pretend it has a word for everything even when it hasn’t. The poet says that “a feeling of sadness comes o’er him,” which sounds as if it had arrived in a cloak with a calling-card. ’Arry refers to the heavings of his wayward heart by confiding to Jimee that he has “got the blooming hump,” which is at least honest in its ugliness. Your sister does not know what is the matter with her to-night. She feels out of sorts altogether and hopes nothing is going to happen. The every-day young man is “so awful glad to meet you, old fellow,” for he does “feel so jolly miserable this evening.” As for myself, I generally say that I have “a strange, unsettled feeling to-night,” and think I’ll go out.

It never comes except in the evening. That is one of its peculiarities. In the sun-time, when the world is bounding forward full of life, we cannot stay to sigh and sulk. The day has its own tyranny of sound: the carts, the hammers, the business voices, the purposeful footsteps. The roar of the working hours drowns the voices of those elfin sprites that are forever singing their low-toned miserere in our ears. It is not that the day is kinder; it is simply louder, and there is something in us that takes its cue from the surrounding chorus. In the daytime we are angry, disappointed, indignant; we swear and knock the furniture about; but we are never “in the blues,” never that peculiar, bottomless, unreasonable droop that seems to have been mixed in a bowl no cook could name.

If misfortune comes at ten o’clock in the morning, we—meaning you, of course—swear and knock the furniture about. But if it comes at ten P.M., we read poetry or sit in the dark and think what a hollow world this is. It is less the misfortune than the hour’s acoustics. At night, the city’s great industrial shout sinks to a murmur, and in the lull you hear yourself too clearly. Silence is not always peace; sometimes it is a magnifying glass held to your own internal clatter.

As a rule it is not trouble that makes us melancholy. Actuality is too stern a thing for sentiment. We will linger to weep over a picture, but from the original we should quickly turn our eyes away. There is no pathos in real misery; no luxury in real grief. We do not toy with sharp swords, nor hug a gnawing fox to our breast for choice. When a man or woman loves to brood over a sorrow and takes care to keep it green in memory, you may be sure it is no longer pain to them. However they suffered at first, the recollection has become, by then, a pleasure—like a sauce that once burned, but which, in the telling and retelling, has been tempered into something you can sip and call “rich.”

Many dear old ladies who daily look at tiny shoes lying in lavender-scented drawers, and weep as they think of the tiny feet whose toddling march is done; and sweet-faced young ones who place each night beneath their pillow some lock that once curled on a boyish head that the salt waves have kissed to death—these will call me a nasty cynical brute and say I am talking nonsense. Yet I believe that if they ask themselves truthfully whether they find it unpleasant thus to dwell on their sorrow, they will be compelled to answer “No.” The tears have become seasoned. The grief has been folded and kneaded by time until it is part of the daily bread of the heart. Tears are as sweet as laughter to some natures.

The proverbial Englishman, as we know from old chronicler Froissart, takes his pleasures sadly; and the Englishwoman goes a step further and takes her pleasures in sadness itself. I am not sneering. I would not sneer at anything that helps keep hearts tender in this hard old world. We men are cold and common-sensed enough for all; we would not have women the same. No, no: be always sentimental and soft-hearted as you are—be the soothing butter to our coarse dry bread. Besides, sentiment is to women what fun is to us. They do not care for our humour; surely it would be unfair to deny them their grief.

And who shall say their mode of enjoyment is less sensible than ours? Why assume that a doubled-up body, a contorted purple face, and a gaping mouth emitting ear-splitting shrieks point to a state of more intelligent happiness than a pensive face resting upon a little white hand and a pair of gentle, tear-dimmed eyes looking back through Time’s dark avenue upon a fading past? One sound is merely loud; the other is quiet but deep—more like the hush that falls on a farmhouse kitchen when the old ones begin speaking in the words they learnt first, the words that younger mouths no longer use, and everyone listens because they feel something slipping away that cannot be bought back.

I am glad when I see Regret walked with as a friend—glad because I know the saltness has been washed from the tears, and that the sting must have been plucked from the beautiful face of Sorrow ere we dare press her pale lips to ours. Time has laid his healing hand upon the wound when we can look back upon the pain we once fainted under and no bitterness or despair rises in our hearts. The burden is no longer heavy when for our past troubles we have only that sweet mingling of pleasure and pity that we feel when old knight-hearted Colonel Newcome answers “adsum” to the great roll-call, or when Tom and Maggie Tulliver, clasping hands through the mists that have divided them, go down, locked in each other’s arms, beneath the swollen waters of the Floss.

And talking of poor Tom and Maggie brings to mind a saying of George Eliot’s in connection with melancholy. She speaks somewhere of “the sadness of a summer’s evening.” How wonderfully true—like everything that came from that wonderful pen—the observation is.

Who has not felt the sorrowful enchantment of those lingering sunsets? The day withdraws not with a slam but with a long, slow simmer, as when a pot of stock is drawn down and the kitchen’s steam turns to a faint breath on the window. The world belongs to Melancholy then: a thoughtful, deep-eyed maiden who loves not the glare of day. It is not till light thickens, and the crow wings to the rocky wood, that she steals forth from her groves. Her palace is in twilight land. It is there she meets us. At her shadowy gate she takes our hand in hers and walks beside us through her mystic realm. We see no form, but seem to hear the rustling of her wings—soft as a skirt-brush in a quiet room, or the last leaves of mint being stripped and dropped into a bowl.

Even in the toiling, hum-drum city her spirit comes to us. There is a sombre presence in each long dull street; and the dark river creeps ghostlike under the black arches as if bearing some hidden secret beneath its muddy waves. The noises of trade fall away, and what remains is not music but the memory of noise, a tinnitus of civilisation. In the silent country, when the trees and hedges loom dim and blurred against the rising night, and the bat’s wing flutters in your face, and the land-rail’s cry sounds drearily across the fields, the spell sinks deeper still into our hearts. We seem, in that hour, to be standing by some unseen death-bed; and in the swaying of the elms we hear the sigh of the dying day.

A solemn sadness reigns. A great peace is around us. In its light the cares of the working day grow small and trivial, and bread and cheese—ay, and even kisses—do not seem the only things worth striving for. Thoughts we cannot speak, but only listen to, flood in upon us. And standing in the stillness under earth’s darkening dome—where every sound has room to be itself, not crushed into a jangle by wheels and iron and shouting—we feel that we are greater than our petty lives. Hung round with those dusky curtains, the world is no longer a mere dingy workshop, but a stately temple wherein man may worship; and where, at times, in the dimness, his groping hands touch God’s.

I had sat down with the full, brave intention of cooking up something clever and original. I had cleared a space as though I were about to roll pastry: paper squared, pen sharpened, brain preheated.

But the only thing that would rise was the thought of being hard up.

It may have been the posture that started it. There is a special attitude of poverty: shoulders slightly tucked, elbows close, hands thrust deep into your own pockets as if you were trying to keep the last penny warm by body-heat. I always sit like that—except in the company of my sisters, my cousins, or my aunts. Then there is a commotion—“shindy” is the honest word, though they would prefer “expostulation,” because it sounds more like something a bishop might do to a kettle—and I am compelled to remove my hands, as though they were improper ingredients.

The chorus to their objections is that it is not gentlemanly.

I am hanged if I can see why. I could understand its not being considered gentlemanly to put your hands in other people’s pockets—especially by the other people, who have such queer prejudices about their own property. But how, O ye sticklers for what looks this and what looks that, can putting his hands in his own pockets make a man less gentle?

Perhaps they are right, though. Now I come to think of it, I have heard people grumble most savagely while doing it; but they were chiefly old gentlemen with stiff joints and stiffer opinions. We young fellows, as a rule, are never quite at ease unless our hands are in our pockets. Without them there, we hover and flap about like a waiter who has mislaid the tray and is trying to look as though he meant it.

Give a man his hands in his trousers pockets, and let there be some small change in the right-hand one and a bunch of keys in the left, and he will face a female post-office clerk. That jingle and that clink are a sort of private orchestra—your own little acoustic ecology in miniature. Modern life roars and rattles at you with carts, hooters, iron shutters, and the everlasting bellow of advertisements; but the small, clean music of coins and keys says, softly and with authority, “I am somebody. I can pay for stamps. I can unlock doors. I belong.”

It is, however, a little difficult to know what to do with your hands—even in your pockets—when there is nothing else there. The pockets become caves with no treasure, and your knuckles learn the shape of emptiness. Years ago, when my whole capital would occasionally come down to what in town the people call “a bob,” I would recklessly spend a penny of it merely for the sake of having the change all in coppers to jingle. You don’t feel nearly so hard up with eleven pence in your pocket as you do with a shilling. A shilling is one solemn, heavy thought. Elevenpence is a cheerful rabble. Had I been “La-di-da”—that impecunious youth about whom we superior folk are so sarcastic—I would have changed my penny for two ha’pennies, and so doubled the concert.

I can speak with authority on the subject of being hard up. I have been a provincial actor. If further evidence be required (which I do not think likely), I can add that I have been a “gentleman connected with the press.” I have lived on fifteen shillings a week. I have lived a week on ten, owing the other five. I have lived for a fortnight on a great-coat.

A great-coat, if you have never tried it as a larder, is remarkably sustaining. Not as food—though, by the end of the second week, a man would boil his gloves if he thought the buttons contained marrow—but as capital. It is wealth you can wear, hang over a chair, and stare at with a hungry respect. You can pledge it; you can pretend you have “left it at your club”; you can wrap yourself in it and feel, for a little while, like a person whom the world expects to remain warm.

It is wonderful what an insight into domestic economy being really hard up gives one. If you want to find out the value of money, live on fifteen shillings a week and see how much you can put by for clothes and recreation. You learn that it is worth while to wait for the farthing change. You learn that it is worth while to walk a mile to save a penny. You learn that a glass of beer is a luxury to be indulged in only at rare intervals, like a hymn sung by a village choir: sweet, brief, and followed by long silence. You learn that a collar can be worn for four days—five, if you turn it and keep your chin respectfully lifted, as though you were practicing for a portrait.

Try it just before you get married. It will be excellent practice. Let your son and heir try it before sending him to college. He won’t grumble at a hundred a year pocket-money then.

There are some people to whom it would do a world of good. There is that delicate blossom who can’t drink any claret under ninety-four, and who would as soon think of dining off cat’s meat as off plain roast mutton. You do come across these poor wretches now and then—though, to the credit of humanity, they are principally confined to that fearful and wonderful society known only to lady novelists, where everybody eats as though a French menu were a moral certificate.

I never hear of one of these creatures discussing a menu-card but I feel a mad desire to drag him off to the bar of some common east-end public-house and cram a sixpenny dinner down his throat: beefsteak pudding, fourpence; potatoes, a penny; half a pint of porter, a penny.

There is a whole history in such a meal, and it is written not in Latin but in gravy. The beefsteak pudding arrives like a squat, honest monument—pastry thick enough to withstand political argument, and within it meat that has been coaxed and bullied into tenderness by long, slow heat. It steams with that unmistakable smell of suet and pepper and fatigue made edible. The potatoes are floury, as if they had been dug that morning out of black earth still speaking its old, rural dialect. The porter is dark, with a bitter edge that makes you think of London soot and the bravery of going on.

And around it—ah, around it!—the mingled fragrance of beer, tobacco, and roast pork that generally hangs about such places like a permanent population. There is the wet slap of a dish on the counter, the clack of dominoes, the scrape of boots, a burst of laughter that has never read a critical essay in its life, and the thin, bright note of a child outside calling something in a street-slang that will be dead in twenty years. The place itself is a museum of living sound. A man may lose a language without noticing; he never loses a smell without knowing it at once.

If our delicate blossom could carry that recollection home in his nostrils and in his ears, it might induce him to turn up his nose a little less frequently in the future at everything that is put before him.

Then there is that generous party—the cadger’s delight—who is so free with his small change, but who never thinks of paying his debts. Hard-up practice might teach even him a little common sense.

“I always give the waiter a shilling. One can’t give the fellow less, you know,” explained a young government clerk with whom I was lunching the other day in Regent Street.

I agreed with him as to the utter impossibility of making it elevenpence ha’penny; but at the same time I resolved to one day decoy him to an eating-house I remembered near Covent Garden, where the waiter, for the better discharge of his duties, goes about in his shirt-sleeves—and very dirty sleeves they are too when it gets near the end of the month.

I know that waiter. The sleeves have a sort of biography in them—week by week laid on, stain by stain, until they might be consulted like an historical document. If my friend gives him anything beyond a penny, the man will insist on shaking hands with him then and there as a mark of his esteem; of that I feel sure. Nothing startles a respectable giver of tips so much as gratitude that is not wrapped in proper velvet.

There have been a good many funny things said and written about hardupishness, but the reality is not funny, for all that. It is not funny to have to haggle over pennies. It isn’t funny to be thought mean and stingy. It isn’t funny to be shabby and to be ashamed of your address.

No, there is nothing at all funny in poverty—to the poor.

It is hell upon earth to a sensitive man; and many a brave gentleman who would have faced the labours of Hercules has had his heart broken by its petty miseries. It is not the actual discomforts themselves that are hard to bear. Who would mind roughing it a bit if that were all it meant? The stomach is a simple creature; it can be trained, like a dog, to take what it is given and wag its tail for crumbs.

What kills is the audience.

What cared Robinson Crusoe for a patch on his trousers? Did he wear trousers? I forget; or did he go about as he does in the pantomimes? What did it matter to him if his toes did stick out of his boots? and what if his umbrella was a cotton one, so long as it kept the rain off? His shabbiness did not trouble him; there were none of his friends round about to sneer him.

Being poor is a mere trifle. It is being known to be poor that is the sting.

It is not cold that makes a man without a great-coat hurry along so quickly. It is not all shame at telling lies—which he knows will not be believed—that makes him turn so red when he informs you that he considers great-coats unhealthy and never carries an umbrella on principle. The lie is not even in a language he likes. It is spoken in the dialect of respectability—the dominant lexicon that allows no word for “I am skint” except the long, absurd sentence that pretends it is a choice. And choice, when you cannot afford it, is a kind of violence.

It is easy enough to say that poverty is no crime. No; if it were, men wouldn’t be ashamed of it. It’s a blunder, though, and is punished as such.

A poor man is despised the whole world over; despised as much by a Christian as by a lord, as much by a demagogue as by a footman, and not all the copy-book maxims ever set for ink-stained youth will make him respected. Appearances are everything, so far as human opinion goes; and the man who will walk down Piccadilly arm in arm with the most notorious scamp in London, provided he is a well-dressed one, will slink up a back street to say a couple of words to a seedy-looking gentleman.

And the seedy-looking gentleman knows this—no one better—and will go a mile round to avoid meeting an acquaintance. Those that knew him in his prosperity need never trouble themselves to look the other way. He is a thousand times more anxious that they should not see him than they can be; and as to their assistance, there is nothing he dreads more than the offer of it.

All he wants is to be forgotten; and in this respect he is generally fortunate enough to get what he wants.

One becomes used to being hard up, as one becomes used to everything else, by the help of that wonderful old homeopathic doctor, Time. You can tell at a glance the difference between the old hand and the novice; between the case-hardened man who has been used to shift and struggle for years and the poor devil of a beginner striving to hide his misery, and in a constant agony of fear lest he should be found out.

Nothing shows this difference more clearly than the way in which each will pawn his watch.

As the poet says somewhere: “True ease in pawning comes from art, not chance.”

The old hand goes into his “uncle’s” with as much composure as he would into his tailor’s—very likely with more. There is even a kind of ritual about it, as steady and domestic as putting on the kettle. The assistant is civil and attends to him at once, to the great indignation of the lady in the next box, who, however, sarcastically observes that she don’t mind being kept waiting “if it is a regular customer.” Why, from the pleasant and businesslike manner in which the transaction is carried out, it might be a large purchase in the three per cents. The watch goes across the counter as calmly as a joint laid on a butcher’s block. There is the soft click of the case, the scribble of pencil, the dull chink of coins, and the paper ticket—thin as a bay-leaf, and about as nourishing—slid into the palm.

Yet what a piece of work a man makes of his first “pop.”

A boy popping his first question is confidence itself compared with him. He hangs about outside the shop until he has succeeded in attracting the attention of all the loafers in the neighbourhood and has aroused strong suspicions in the mind of the policeman on the beat. At last, after a careful examination of the contents of the windows—made for the purpose of impressing the bystanders with the notion that he is going in to purchase a diamond bracelet or some such trifle—he enters, trying to do so with a careless swagger, and giving himself really the air of a member of the swell mob.

When inside he speaks in so low a voice as to be perfectly inaudible, and has to say it all over again, louder, as though poverty were a word one could whisper into invisibility. When, in the course of his rambling conversation about a “friend” of his, the word “lend” is reached, he is promptly told to go up the court on the right and take the first door round the corner.

He comes out of the shop with a face that you could easily light a cigarette at, and firmly under the impression that the whole population of the district is watching him. When he does get to the right place he has forgotten his name and address and is in a general condition of hopeless imbecility. Asked in a severe tone how he came by “this,” he stammers and contradicts himself, and it is only a miracle if he does not confess to having stolen it that very day. He is thereupon informed that they don’t want anything to do with his sort, and that he had better get out of this as quickly as possible, which he does, recollecting nothing more until he finds himself three miles off, without the slightest knowledge how he got there.

By the way, how awkward it is, though, having to depend on public-houses and churches for the time.

The former are generally too fast and the latter too slow. Besides which, your efforts to get a glimpse of the public-house clock from the outside are attended with great difficulties. If you gently push the swing-door ajar and peer in, you draw upon yourself the contemptuous looks of the barmaid, who at once puts you down in the same category with area-sneaks and cadgers. You also create a certain amount of agitation among the married portion of the customers, who do not like anything peering.

You don’t see the clock because it is behind the door; and in trying to withdraw quietly you jam your head. The only other method is to jump up and down outside the window. After this latter proceeding, however, if you do not bring out a banjo and commence to sing, the youthful inhabitants of the neighbourhood, who have gathered round in expectation, become disappointed. They have their own idea of what a man is for; and it is not for silently consulting the time.

I should like to know, too, by what mysterious law of nature it is that before you have left your watch “to be repaired” half an hour, some one is sure to stop you in the street and conspicuously ask you the time. Nobody even feels the slightest curiosity on the subject when you’ve got it on. But let it be gone—let the reassuring little tick be silenced in your waistcoat, like a bird removed from its cage—and the whole city suddenly becomes a chorus of inquisitors.

Dear old ladies and gentlemen who know nothing about being hard up—and may they never, bless their grey old heads—look upon the pawn-shop as the last stage of degradation; but those who know it better are often surprised, like the little boy who dreamed he went to heaven, at meeting so many people there that they never expected to see.

For my part, I think it a much more independent course than borrowing from friends; and I always try to impress this upon those of my acquaintance who incline toward “wanting a couple of pounds till the day after to-morrow.” But they won’t all see it. One of them once remarked that he objected to the principle of the thing. I fancy if he had said it was the interest that he objected to he would have been nearer the truth: twenty-five per cent certainly does come heavy—heavier, even, than suet pastry.

There are degrees in being hard up. We are all hard up, more or less—most of us more. Some are hard up for a thousand pounds; some for a shilling.

Just at this moment I am hard up myself for a fiver. I only want it for a day or two. I should be certain of paying it back within a week at the outside; and if any lady or gentleman among my readers would kindly lend it me, I should be very much obliged indeed.

They could send it to me under cover to Messrs. Field & Tuer—only, in such case, please let the envelope be carefully sealed. I would give you my I.O.U. as security.

Everything is vanity, and everybody is vain; it is the one dish at which all ages, sexes, and species will eat with an appetite that no doctor can reduce.

At this moment, for instance, a small person—two foot ten of concentrated self-esteem—is battering my shins with the steady perseverance of a cook pounding cutlets, and demanding an opinion upon her new shoes. They are, candidly, not shoes so much as two misguided dumplings, lumpy of contour and apparently arranged upon the wrong feet, like a pair of pies served on unsuitable plates. But it is not criticism she is buying with her bruises: it is praise. She wants butter, not bread. So I spread it on—thick, smooth, and shameful.

“Oh! exquisite!” I cry, in tones that would make an honest man blush into his soup. “The very shoes for a princess. The very shoes for—well, for anybody who wished to be admired by sensible people.”

Nothing less will satisfy this self-opinionated cherub. I once attempted what I called the conscientious-friend method with her; I had imagined there are occasions when plain speaking is as wholesome as salt.

She asked me—submitting the case with the gravity of a magistrate—“Wot oo tink of me? Oo peased wi’ me?” It seemed a good opening for a little moral cookery: to take a raw character, trim it, and set it in the oven of reproof.

“No,” I said, “I am not pleased with you.”

Then I recited the day’s menu of her misdeeds. How, at five in the morning, she had roused the whole house as if sounding a factory whistle at dawn; how, at seven, she had upset a water-jug and tumbled downstairs after it, like a potato escaping its basket; how, at eight, she had endeavoured to put the cat into the bath (an enterprise which, like forcing a proud old cheese into boiling water, produces only fury and smell); and how, at nine thirty-five, she had sat on her father’s hat with the calm deliberation of a hen settling on a nest.

I put it to her—gently, reasonably—how she, as a Christian child, could expect a wise and good uncle to approve such proceedings.

Did she thank me for the wholesome bitter herbs? Did she consider, and resolve to become an improved edition of herself?

She howled.

Having done that, she became abusive. She said, “Oo naughty—oo naughty, bad unkie—oo bad man—me tell MAR.” And she did. She toddled off and denounced me with the air of one who has caught the baker short-weighting the loaf.

Since then, when my views are requested, I keep my real sentiments simmering quietly inside, and ladle out admiration irrespective of merit. She nods, satisfied—as a customer satisfied with a generous helping—and trots off to retail my opinion about the premises. She employs it, I strongly suspect, as a testimonial in the household market; for, presently, I hear from the distance the cry of commerce:

“Unkie says me dood dirl—me dot to have two bikkies.”

There she goes now, studying her own toes with rapture and murmuring “pittie”—a tiny idol worshipping its own feet. Children are all like that. I remember once, sitting in a suburban London garden on a sunny afternoon, the air full of the domestic sounds one loves—the faint clink of crockery, a sparrow’s argument in the ivy, a far-off piano murdering something that had once been a hymn. Suddenly, cutting through all this like a cracked trumpet, I heard a shrill little voice from a top-storey window, calling to some unseen admirer, presumably in another garden:

“Gamma, me dood boy, me wery good boy, gamma; me dot on Bob’s knickiebockies.”

It is not only children. Even beasts are afflicted. I saw a great Newfoundland dog the other day sitting before a mirror outside a shop in Regent’s Circus, examining himself with an expression of smug approval such as I have rarely observed except in men who are about to propose a vote of thanks to themselves. His tail made little satisfied thumps, as though applauding his own face.

I was once at a farm-house on some rustic high holiday—May Day, Quarter Day, or Harvest Home, or one of those kindly old occasions that used to come round with the seasons as naturally as bread comes round with hunger. There were proper noises in that yard: the slow creak of a pump; the soft clop of hooves on packed earth; the lowing of cattle like an ancient chant; the hens’ busy gossip; and, above all, the great comfortable silence that lies between country sounds like the pause between mouthfuls.

They put a garland of flowers round the head of one of the cows. A ridiculous thing to do to a cow—like decorating a churn. Yet that absurd quadruped went about all day as perky as a schoolgirl in a new frock. When they took the wreath off she became quite sulky; and they had to put it on again before she would stand still to be milked. That is not an invention, nor a “Percy anecdote.” It is plain truth, as solid as a lump of butter.

As for cats, they nearly equal human beings in vanity. I have known a cat rise with icy dignity and stalk out of a room because a visitor had made some remark derogatory to her species; while a neatly turned compliment will set them purring for an hour, as though you had stroked their pride with warm cream.

I do like cats. They are so unconsciously amusing. There is a comic grandeur about them; such a “How dare you?” such a “Go away—don’t touch me” air; as if they had been born in a palace and were merely enduring our parlours because the servants have mislaid the door.

There is nothing haughty about a dog. Dogs are “Hail fellow, well met” with every Tom, Dick, and Harry. When I meet a dog of my acquaintance I slap his head, call him bad names, and roll him over on his back; and there he lies, gaping happily as if the world were made of gravy. Fancy behaving like that with a cat! Why, she would never speak to you again as long as you lived; she would cut you dead with her tail.

No; when you want a cat’s approbation you must proceed as delicately as a cook folding whites into a soufflé. If you do not know the cat, begin with, “Poor pussy,” and then add “did ’ums” in a tone of soothing sympathy. You do not know what you mean—any more than the cat does—but the sentiment implies a proper humility on your part, and generally touches her feelings. If you are decently mannered and of passable appearance, she will put her back up and rub her nose against you, granting you an interview.

Matters having reached this stage, you may venture to chuck her under the chin and tickle the side of her head, whereupon the intelligent creature will stick her claws into your leg; and all is friendship and affection, as sweetly expressed in those beautiful lines:

“I love little pussy, her coat is so warm,
And if I don’t tease her she’ll do me no harm;
So I’ll stroke her, and pat her, and feed her with food,
And pussy will love me because I am good.”

The last two lines give a pretty accurate notion of pussy’s moral philosophy. In her view, goodness consists of stroking her, and patting her, and feeding her. I fear this limited interpretation of virtue is not confined to cats. We all have a tendency to call a man “good” when he is good to us, and “bad” when he does not do what we want him to.

The truth is, each of us carries, tucked away like a family recipe no one else is allowed to alter, an inborn conviction that the world was created as a necessary appendage to ourselves. Other people exist to admire us, to agree with us, to minister to our wants—like side-dishes placed obligingly around the central joint, which is, of course, Me.

You and I, dear reader, are each the centre of the universe in our own opinion. You, as I understand it, were thoughtfully put into existence in order to read and pay for what I write; while I, in your scheme, am an article sent into the world to provide copy for your leisure. The stars—those innumerable other worlds rushing beside us through the eternal silence—were hung up to make the sky look interesting at night; and the moon, with her mysteries and her always-turned-away face, was arranged chiefly that we might flirt under her with tolerable effect. We are most of us like Mrs. Poyser’s bantam cock, who fancied the sun got up every morning to hear him crow.

“’Tis vanity that makes the world go round.”

I do not believe any man ever existed without vanity; and if one did, he would be an extremely uncomfortable person to have anything to do with. He would be a good man, and we should respect him immensely. He would be admirable; the sort of man you could put under a glass case and show round as a specimen—like a preserved tongue in a jar: edifying, but not companionable. He would be a man to be stuck upon a pedestal and copied like a school exercise; reverenced, not loved; not the kind of human brother whose hand you would want to grip when the wind is bleak and the world is noisy.

Angels may be excellent folk in their way, but we poor mortals would likely find them precious slow company. Even very good people can be depressing; they make one feel as if one had brought mud on one’s boots into a clean kitchen.

It is in our faults and failings, not in our virtues, that we touch each other and find sympathy. We differ widely in our nobler qualities. Some of us are pious; some generous. Some few are honest—comparatively speaking—and some, fewer still, may possibly be truthful. But in vanity and kindred weaknesses we can all join hands. Vanity is one of those touches of nature that make the whole world kin.

From the Indian hunter proud of his belt of scalps, to the European general swelling beneath his stars and medals; from the Chinese delighted with the length of his pigtail, to the “professional beauty” enduring tortures that her waist may resemble a peg-top; from draggle-tailed little Polly Stiggins strutting through Seven Dials beneath a tattered parasol, to the princess sweeping through a drawing-room with a train of four yards; from ’Arry winning the loud laughter of his pals by vulgar chaff, to the statesman whose ears are tickled by cheers at his high-sounding periods; from the dark-skinned African bartering rare oils and ivory for a few glass beads to hang round his neck, to the Christian maiden selling her white body for a handful of tiny stones and an empty title to tack before her name—all march, and fight, and bleed, and die beneath the same tawdry flag.

Vanity is the motive-power that moves humanity, and flattery is the grease that keeps the wheels from screaming.

If you want affection and respect in this world, you must flatter people. Flatter high and low, rich and poor, silly and wise. You will get on famously. Praise this man’s virtues and that man’s vices. Compliment everybody upon everything—especially upon what they have not got. Admire fops for beauty, fools for wit, and boors for breeding; and your discernment will be extolled to the skies. Everybody can be got over by flattery.

The belted earl—“belted earl” is, I believe, the correct phrase; I do not know what it means unless it denotes an earl who wears a belt instead of braces. Some men do. I don’t like it myself. You have to keep the thing so tight for it to be of any use, and that is uncomfortable. Anyhow, whatever particular sort of earl a belted one may be, he is get-overable by flattery—just as every other human being is, from a duchess to a cat’s-meat man, from a ploughboy to a poet; and the poet far easier than the ploughboy, for butter soaks better into wheaten bread than into oaten cake.

As for love, flattery is its very lifeblood. Fill a person with love of themselves, and what runs over will be your share, says a certain witty and truthful Frenchman whose name I can’t for the life of me remember. (Confound it! I never can remember names when I want to.) Tell a girl she is an angel—only more angelic than an angel; a goddess—only more graceful and queenly than the average goddess; more fairy-like than Titania, more beautiful than Venus, more enchanting than Parthenope; more adorable, radiant, and perfect than any woman that ever did live, does live, or could live—and you will make a very favourable impression upon her trusting little heart.

Sweet innocent! she will believe every word you say. It is so easy to deceive a woman—in this way. Dear little souls, they hate flattery, so they tell you; and when you add, “Ah, darling, it isn’t flattery in your case, it is plain sober truth—you really are, without exaggeration, the most beautiful, the most good, the most charming, the most divine, the most perfect human creature that ever trod this earth,” they will smile a quiet approving smile and, leaning against your manly shoulder, murmur that you are a dear good fellow after all.

By Jove! fancy a man making love on strictly truthful principles, never uttering mere compliment or hyperbole, confining himself to exact fact! Fancy him gazing rapturously into his mistress’ eyes and whispering that she isn’t, on the whole, bad-looking as girls go. Fancy him taking her little hand and assuring her it is of a light drab colour shot with red; telling her, as he presses her to his heart, that her nose, for a turned-up one, seems rather pretty; and that her eyes appear to him, as far as he can judge, to be quite up to the average standard of such things! What chance would he stand against the man who tells her her face is like a fresh blush-rose, her hair a wandering sunbeam imprisoned by her smiles, and her eyes like two evening stars?

There are various ways of flattering, and you must adapt your method to your subject. Some people like it laid on with a trowel; it requires very little art. With sensible persons it must be done delicately—more by suggestion than by words, like seasoning that works best when you cannot quite put your finger on it.

A good many like it wrapped up in the form of an insult: “Oh, you are a perfect fool, you are. You’d give your last sixpence to the first hungry-looking beggar you met.” Others will swallow it only when administered through the medium of a third person—so that if C wishes to get at an A of this sort, he must confide to A’s particular friend B that he thinks A a splendid fellow, and beg B not to mention it—especially not to A. Be careful that B is a reliable man, though; otherwise he won’t.

Those fine, sturdy John Bulls who “hate flattery, sir,” and “never let anybody get over me by flattery,” are very simply managed. Flatter them enough upon their absence of vanity, and you can do what you like with them.

After all, vanity is as much a virtue as a vice. It is easy to repeat copy-book maxims against its sinfulness, but it is a passion that can move us to good as well as to evil. Ambition is only vanity ennobled. We want praise and admiration—or “fame,” as we prefer to call it—and so we write great books, paint grand pictures, sing sweet songs, and toil with willing hands in study, loom, and laboratory.

We wish to become rich men, not merely for ease and comfort—any one man’s share of those can be purchased almost anywhere for two hundred pounds a year—but that our houses may be bigger and more gaudily furnished than our neighbours’; that our horses and servants may be more numerous; that we may dress our wives and daughters in absurd but expensive clothes; and that we may give costly dinners of which we ourselves do not eat a shilling’s worth—sitting amid the clatter of plates and the roar of conversation as if the point of supper were not taste but applause. And to do this we aid the world’s work with clear and busy brain, spreading commerce among peoples, carrying civilisation to remote corners—sometimes carrying with it, too, the unfortunate modern gift of unnecessary noise and the habit of mistaking loudness for life.

Do not let us abuse vanity, therefore. Rather let us use it. Honour itself is but the highest form of vanity. The instinct is not confined to Beau Brummels and Dolly Vardens. There is the vanity of the peacock and the vanity of the eagle. Snobs are vain; but so, too, are heroes.

Come, my young brother bucks, let us be vain together. Let us join hands and help one another to increase our vanity. Let us be vain—not of trousers and hair—but of brave hearts and working hands; of truth, purity, and nobility. Let us be too vain to stoop to what is mean or base; too vain for petty selfishness and little-minded envy; too vain to say an unkind word or do an unkind act. Let us be vain of being single-hearted, upright gentlemen in the midst of a world of knaves. Let us pride ourselves upon thinking high thoughts, achieving great deeds, living good lives.

Getting on in the world is not, you might think, a subject for an idle fellow—one whose proper business is to sit somewhere green and unnecessary, and let other people bustle. Yet idleness has its own advantages. It gives a man time to listen.

I have a little place of my own, a sort of lean-to paradise at the edge of things: a patched arbor by the wayside, trellised with hops and whatever else will climb if you coax it with twine. From there I can look out upon the great high-road of Life as upon a market-street on Saturday—only with fewer apples and more desperation. I keep a kettle murmuring on a spirit-lamp, and for company I have a small pan in which onions are always meant to brown and never quite get there, because something is forever happening that I must lean forward to watch.

The procession never stops. The road does not merely carry people; it mills them, like grain between stones. Day and night you hear it—the quick, nervous tramp of innumerable feet, a sound like dried peas flung hard upon a tin tray. Some run, some walk, some drag a foot as if it has been borrowed and they intend to return it worn; but all hurry, all strain, all pant toward a horizon that retreats with the slyness of a waiter who has seen your hand go to your pocket.

If you listen closely—and an idle man can afford close listening—you catch separate lives in the noise, as you pick out flavours in a stew. Here goes an old man, his breath coming in little whistles through his teeth, like a kettle that has forgotten how to boil properly. There a timid girl is driven on by a matron with a face sharp enough to cut bread without a knife; the matron’s skirts snap like sailcloth, and her voice has the crack of a butcher’s cleaver on bone. Here is a studious youth, reading “How to Get On in the World,” and letting everyone pass him, because he is staring at the print as though the secret were hidden between the commas. He stumbles over ruts and other people’s heels without ever looking up—like a cook who follows a recipe so faithfully that he never notices the kitchen is on fire.

There is a bored-looking man, his hat at the correct angle of indifference, while a fashionably dressed woman jogs his elbow as if he were a perambulator and she the baby. There is a boy gazing back at a sunny village he will not see again; his longing makes a hollow in the road behind him, the way a ladle leaves a little crater in porridge. A broad-shouldered fellow strides with the calm rhythm of a man chopping wood: thud, thud—he will get there because his legs believe in it. And then, sidling as a rat at a larder door, a thin-faced stooping man shuffles and dodges, never quite in a straight line, yet always convinced he has found a shorter way.

One fellow keeps his eyes fixed on the ground, weaving from side to side as if the future were a coin he has dropped and he’s afraid someone will stand on it. A youth with a noble face hesitates, looking from the distant goal to the mud at his feet, as though he cannot decide whether to step over it or to write an ode about it. And then comes a fair girl whose dainty face wrinkles a little more with each step, as fresh pastry dries if left too long by the oven door; and after her a careworn man, and after him a hopeful lad, still smelling faintly of morning.

It is a motley throng, and there is no keeping them sorted, any more than you can persuade a pantry to remain tidy when there are children. Prince and beggar elbow one another. Sinner and saint jostle shoulder to shoulder. Butcher and baker and candlestick-maker—ploughboys and sailors—counsel in wig and gown, and the old clothes-man under his dingy crown of hat, soldier in scarlet and undertaker’s mute in streaming hat-band and worn cotton gloves. The musty scholar fumbles his faded leaves; the scented actor swings his seals and looks as if he has been dusted with perfumed flour.

A politician goes by, crying legislative panaceas in a voice practised for echo; a Cheap-Jack follows, holding up bottles and promises, each guaranteed to cure human ills and, failing that, to remove stains. There is the sleek capitalist, and the sinewy labourer; the man of science and the shoe-back; the poet and the water-rate collector; cabinet minister and ballet-dancer. A red-nosed publican roars the praises of his vats; a temperance lecturer lectures at fifty pounds a night with a zeal as well-paid as it is loud. Judge and swindler, priest and gambler. A jewelled duchess smiles like iced cake; a thin lodging-house keeper looks as if she has spent her life trying to make mutton behave; and behind them a wabbling, strutting thing, tawdry in paint and finery, a shop-window advertisement escaped into the street.

Cheek by cheek they struggle onward—screaming, cursing, praying, laughing, singing, moaning—making a racket that would turn milk sour. Their speed never slackens. There is no wayside rest, no halt by cool fountains, no pause beneath green shades. On through heat and crowd and dust—on, or be trampled down and lost. On with throbbing brain and tottering limbs, until a man’s heart grows sick and his eyes blur, and he makes a little gurgling groan that says to those behind: “Close up; there is room now.”

And yet, though it looks like misery and sounds like a factory at full tantrum, who can stand apart from it? Who watches the commotion and does not feel the old instinct to be in it—not merely to look on, but to put a shoulder in and try the weight of life for oneself?

Certainly not I. I began, you see, with talk of an arbor and a pipe of contentment and lotus-leaves of indolence, as though I were a philosopher in a dressing-gown. They are excellent metaphors, but they do not fit my figure. When there is anything like a row in the street, I am not the kind of man to stay indoors and practise resignation. I am rather like that Irishman who, seeing a crowd collecting, sent his little girl to ask whether there was going to be a fight—“because, if so, father would like to be in it.”

I love the fierce strife. I like to watch it. I like to hear of people getting on in it—battling their way bravely and fairly—that is, not slipping through by luck or trickery, as one might steal a pie cooling on a sill and call it enterprise. There is something in the struggle that stirs one’s old fighting blood, the same blood that thrilled, in schoolboy days, to tales of knights who fought against fearful odds. And fearful odds there are still, only the giants now wear respectable coats, and the dragons have learnt to keep accounts. The golden casket is not so easy to win as the story-books pretend.

In those pleasant romances Algernon takes one long last look at the ancestral hall, wipes a tear from his eye with a lace handkerchief, and goes away—returning three years later rolling in riches. Nobody tells you precisely what he did in the three years. That is a pity, because it would be the most exciting part; the rest is only furniture. Novelists linger for a dozen pages over a tea-party—how the cups were handed and what Miss So-and-So said to Mrs. This-and-That—yet they toss off a lifetime with: “He had become one of our merchant princes,” or, “He was now a great artist, with the world at his feet.” As though a man were put into an oven raw at chapter three and drawn out at chapter ten done to perfection, without any basting in between.

Why, there is more real life in one of Gilbert’s patter-songs than in half the biographical novels ever written. Gilbert will tell you step by step how an office-boy rose to be the ruler of the Queen’s navee; and how a briefless barrister became a great and good judge, ready to try a breach of promise of marriage. That is what one longs for: the petty details, the secret seasonings, the moments when the mixture refuses to thicken and you have to improvise. Show us the under-current of an ambitious man’s career—his struggles and failures, his hopes, his small victories, his splendid disappointments. It would sell like hot bread.

For wooing Fortune is quite as interesting as wooing any flesh-and-blood maiden, and in practice astonishingly similar. Fortune, as the ancients painted her, is very like a woman—not quite so unreasonable and inconsistent, but nearly so; and the pursuit obeys much the same ridiculous laws. Ben Jonson puts both into a couplet:

“Court a mistress, she denies you;
Let her alone, she will court you.”

A woman never thoroughly cares for her lover until he has ceased to care for her; and it is not until you have snapped your fingers in Fortune’s face and turned on your heel that she begins to smile upon you. But by that time you do not much care whether she smiles or frowns. Everything comes too late in this world. Good people say it is quite right and proper that it should be so, and that it proves ambition is wicked. Bosh! Good people are wrong. (They always are, in my opinion; we never agree on any point, not even about the weather.)

What would the world do without ambitious people? It would be as flabby as a Norfolk dumpling—wholesome perhaps, but with no spring in it. Ambitious people are the yeast; they are the leaven that raises the lump into bread. Without them the world would never get up at all. They are the busybodies who rise early, hammering, shouting, clattering the fire-irons, and making it generally impossible for the rest of the house to remain in bed. Wrong to be ambitious, forsooth! Wrong are the men who, with bent back and sweating brow, cut the smooth road over which humanity marches forward from generation to generation! Wrong for using the talents their Master entrusted to them—for toiling while others play!

Of course they are seeking reward. Man is not given that godlike unselfishness that thinks only of others’ good; he wants his own dinner as well as the neighbour’s. But in working for themselves they work for all. We are bound together like a net of onion-string: tug one end and every knot feels it. Each blow a man strikes in his own behalf helps to mould the universe. The stream, struggling onward, turns the mill-wheel; the coral insect, fashioning its tiny cell, joins continents; and the ambitious man, building a pedestal for himself, leaves a monument to posterity.

Alexander and Caesar fought for their own ends, but in doing so they put a belt of civilisation half round the earth. Stephenson invented the steam-engine to win a fortune; Shakespeare wrote his plays to keep a comfortable home for Mrs. Shakespeare and the little Shakespeares—no doubt with a decent supper on the table and a fire that did not sulk. Great things often begin as plain domestic needs: a roof, a meal, the wish to hear one’s own language spoken kindly at the hearth instead of shouted down by the iron noises outside.

Contented, unambitious people are all very well in their way. They form a neat, useful background for great portraits to be painted against; they make a respectable audience—if not particularly intelligent—for the active spirits of the age to perform before. I have no word against contented people so long as they keep quiet. But do not let them go strutting about, as they love to do, crying that they are the true models for the whole species. Why, they are the deadheads, the drones in the hive, the street crowds lounging and gaping at those who are working. And let them not imagine either—as they are also fond of doing—that they are very wise and philosophical, and that it is a very artful thing to be contented.

It may be true that “a contented mind is happy anywhere,” but so is a Jerusalem pony, and the consequence is that both are put anywhere and treated anyhow. “Oh, you needn’t bother about him,” people say; “he is very contented as he is, and it would be a pity to disturb him.” So your contented party is passed over and the discontented man gets the place, because he makes enough noise to be heard above the general din. If you are foolish enough to be contented, do not show it. Grumble with the rest. If you can do with a little, ask for a great deal; because if you don’t, you won’t get any.

In this world you must adopt the principle of a plaintiff in an action for damages: demand ten times more than you are ready to accept. If you could be satisfied with a hundred, begin by insisting on a thousand. If you start by suggesting a hundred, you will get ten—and someone will tell you to be grateful for the generosity of the age.

It was by not following this simple plan that poor Jean Jacques Rousseau came to grief. He fixed the summit of his earthly bliss at living in an orchard with an amiable woman and a cow, and he never attained even that. He got as far as the orchard, but the woman was not amiable, and she brought her mother with her, and there was no cow. Now if he had made up his mind to demand a large country estate, a houseful of angels, and a cattle-show, he might have lived to possess his kitchen garden and one head of live-stock—and possibly, by some rare accident, come across that rara avis: a really amiable woman.

And what a terribly dull affair life must be for contented people! How heavy the time must hang upon their hands, and what on earth do they occupy their thoughts with, supposing they have any? Reading the paper and smoking seems to be the intellectual food of most of them, to which the more energetic add playing the flute and talking about the next-door neighbour’s affairs. Their days are like cold potatoes: sustaining, perhaps, but not exciting.

They never know the sharp hunger of expectation, nor the stern delight of accomplished effort—the particular satisfaction of a thing at last done, as when a sauce that has sulked and separated all evening finally comes together with a triumphant gloss. To the ambitious man life is a brilliant game—a game that calls forth tact and energy and nerve, to be won in the long run by the quick eye and the steady hand, and yet having enough chance in it to keep the whole business piquant. He exults in it as the strong swimmer in heaving billows, the athlete in the wrestle, the soldier in battle. And if he is defeated he wins the grim joy of fighting; if he loses the race, he has at least had a run. Better to work and fail than to sleep one’s life away.

So—walk up, walk up, walk up! Walk up, ladies and gentlemen! Walk up, boys and girls! Show your skill and try your strength; brave your luck and prove your pluck. Walk up! The show is never closed, and the game is always going. The only genuine sport in all the fair, gentlemen—highly respectable and strictly moral—patronised by the nobility, clergy, and gentry. Established in the year one, gentlemen, and flourishing ever since—walk up!

Walk up, ladies and gentlemen, and take a hand. There are prizes for all and all can play. There is gold for the man and fame for the boy; rank for the maiden and pleasure for the fool. So walk up, ladies and gentlemen—walk up! All prizes and no blanks; for some few win, and as to the rest—why:

“The rapture of pursuing
Is the prize the vanquished gain.”

Things do go so contrary with me. I had set my mind—like a cook determined to invent a new sauce nobody has yet had the good fortune to spill down a waistcoat—on writing about something wholly original. Not merely uncommon: unuttered. A subject that had never once been chewed over by human jaws, digested by human brains, or served up with a garnish of opinion.

“I will do it,” I said, with the sternness of an elder measuring out seed-corn. “I will write on something nobody has ever written on before, and then I shall have it all my own way.”

I went about for days, sniffing after novelty as a food-historian sniffs after an authentic medieval recipe. I turned over notions as one turns over frying onions, looking for the moment they brown into inspiration. And I could not find it.

Then Mrs. Cutting, our charwoman, came yesterday. I do not mind mentioning her name because I am as certain as I am that salt is salt that she will never see this book. She would not glance at such a frivolous publication. She reads nothing but the Bible and Lloyd’s Weekly News; everything else she considers not merely unnecessary but sinful—like sugared tea at breakfast when you’ve already had jam.

“Lor’, sir,” she said, pausing with her broom as if it were a sceptre. “You do look worried.”

“Mrs. Cutting,” said I, “I am trying to think of a subject the discussion of which will come upon the world in the nature of a startler—some subject upon which no previous human being has ever said a word—something that will attract by its novelty, invigorate by its surprising freshness.”

She laughed and said I was a funny gentleman.

That is my luck again. When I am serious, people chuckle; when I am facetious, they stare, as at a dish set down without a spoon.

I had a beautiful joke last week. I thought it ripe—full-flavoured—one of those that should go off like champagne when you draw the cork. I worked it up and brought it in artfully at a dinner-party. I forget how exactly, but we had been talking about the attitude of Shakespeare toward the Reformation—heavy fare, like suet pudding without treacle—and I said something and immediately added:

“Ah, that reminds me; such a funny thing happened the other day in Whitechapel.”

“Oh,” said they, laying down their knives. “What was that?”

“Oh, ’twas awfully funny,” I replied, beginning to giggle myself; “it will make you roar.”

And I told it them. It was one of those long jokes, too, that take their time, like a stew that insists on becoming tender by degrees.

There was dead silence when I finished—silence with a crust upon it. At last somebody said:

“And that was the joke?”

I assured them it was, and they were very polite and took my word for it—everybody except one old gentleman at the other end of the table, who wanted to know which was the joke: what he said to her or what she said to him. We argued it out, like cooks disputing whether the fault lies in the oven or the recipe.

Some people are too much the other way. I knew a fellow once whose natural tendency to laugh at everything was so strong that if you wanted to talk seriously to him you had to explain beforehand that what you were going to say would not be amusing—like warning a child that the plum tart is for visitors. Unless you got him to clearly understand this, he would go off into fits of merriment over every word you uttered. I have known him, on being asked the time, stop short in the middle of the road, slap his leg, and burst into a roar of laughter, as if the clock had told him a ribald story. One never dared say anything really funny to that man. A good joke would have killed him on the spot.

In the present instance I vehemently repudiated the accusation of frivolity and pressed Mrs. Cutting for practical ideas. She became thoughtful, as if straining to remember an old country receipt, and hazarded “samplers,” saying she never heard them spoken much of now, but they used to be all the rage when she was a girl.

I declined samplers and begged her to think again.

She pondered a long while, holding a tea-tray in her hands as if consulting it for omens, and at last suggested the weather, which she was sure had been most trying of late.

And ever since that idiotic suggestion I have been unable to get the weather out of my thoughts, or anything else in.

It certainly is most wretched weather. At all events it is so now, at the time I am writing; and if it isn’t particularly unpleasant when I come to be read, it soon will be. It always is wretched weather according to us. The weather is like the Government—always in the wrong.

In summer we say it is stifling; in winter that it is killing; in spring and autumn we find fault with it for being neither one thing nor the other and wish it would make up its mind, like a waiter who will neither bring the soup nor clear it away. If it is fine we say the country is being ruined for want of rain; if it does rain we pray for fine weather. If December passes without snow we indignantly demand to know what has become of our good old-fashioned winters, and talk as if we had been cheated out of something we had bought and paid for; and when it does snow, our language is a disgrace to a Christian nation.

We shall never be content until each man makes his own weather and keeps it to himself—keeps it bottled, labelled, and corked, like pickles. If that cannot be arranged, we would rather do without it altogether.

Yet I think it is only to us in cities that all weather is so unwelcome. In her own home, the country, Nature is sweet in all her moods—sweet even when she is sharpening her east wind and throwing hailstones at your ears.

What can be more beautiful than snow, falling big with mystery in silent softness, as if some enormous white flour-sack had burst over the world and dusted every hedge and field for a fairy wedding? It changes the sound of everything. Wheels hush. Voices come to you clean, without the usual gritty city rasp. Your own footstep rings on frozen ground—crisp as the snap of a crust—and far away you can hear the sheep-dogs’ bark and children’s laughter pealing faintly clear like little bells carried over the open hills. There is music in such weather, a rural music made of distance and air.

And then skating: scudding with wings of steel across the swaying ice, making that whirring song that belongs to no instrument but the blade itself. The whole pond becomes a fiddle, and you play it with your feet.

And oh, how dainty is spring—Nature at sweet eighteen. The little hopeful leaves peep out so fresh and green, so pure and bright, like young lives pushing shyly into the bustling world. Fruit-tree blossoms—pink and white—like village maidens in Sunday frocks, hide each whitewashed cottage in a cloud of fragile splendour. The cuckoo’s note comes on the breeze, and it is as if the land had remembered an old word in an old dialect and spoken it once, just once, before forgetting again.

Summer has its deep dark green and its drowsy hum—the very air thick with bees as with gossip. Rain-drops whisper solemn secrets to the listening leaves; twilight lingers in the lanes as if reluctant to leave the warm smell of dust and hedgerow; and in farm-kitchens you can almost hear the dough rising, the slow domestic breathing of bread.

Autumn—sadly fair—comes with golden glow and dying grandeur of tinted woods: blood-red sunsets, ghostly evening mists, busy murmur of reapers, laden orchards, the calling of gleaners, festivals of praise. Even the sound of a scythe has a kind of rite in it. You hear it and you know the year is being cut down and stacked.

The very rain, and sleet, and hail seem only Nature’s useful servants when found doing their simple duties in the country; and the East Wind himself is nothing worse than a boisterous friend when we meet him between the hedge-rows.

But in the city—where the painted stucco blisters under the smoky sun, and the sooty rain brings slush and mud, and the snow lies piled in dirty heaps like spoiled sugar—no face of Nature charms us. The wind does not sing there: it shrieks down dingy streets and whistles round flaring gas-lit corners, mixing its voice with the iron clatter of carts and the ceaseless bark of commerce. There are places in London where the ear cannot rest; one comes home with one’s nerves as over-salted as a cheap soup.

Weather in towns is like a skylark in a counting-house—out of place and in the way. Towns ought to be covered in, warmed by hot-water pipes, and lighted by electricity. The weather is a country lass and does not appear to advantage in town. We liked well enough to flirt with her in the hay-field; but she does not seem so fascinating when we meet her in Pall Mall. There is too much of her there. Her frank, free laugh that sounded pleasant among dairy-jars becomes exceedingly trying amid brick and ledger. In the fields her voice is part of the proper soundscape, as natural as the clink of pails; in the streets it is only another noise competing with the rest.

Just lately she has been favouring us with almost incessant rain for about three weeks; and I am a demned damp, moist, unpleasant body, as Mr. Mantalini puts it.

Our next-door neighbour comes out into the back garden every now and then and says it’s doing the country a world of good—not his coming out into the back garden, but the weather. He doesn’t understand anything about it, but ever since he started a cucumber-frame last summer he has regarded himself as an agriculturist, and talks in this absurd way with the idea of impressing the rest of the terrace with the notion that he is a retired farmer. I can only hope that for this once he is correct and that the weather really is doing good to something, because it is doing me a considerable amount of damage.

It is spoiling both my clothes and my temper. The latter I can afford, as I have a good supply of it; but it wounds me to the quick to see my dear old hats and trousers sinking—prematurely worn and aged—beneath the cold world’s blasts and snows, as if they were honest labourers turned out without their supper.

There is my new spring suit, too. A beautiful suit it was—fresh as newly sifted flour, as promising as the first whiff from an opened spice-tin—and now it is hanging up so bespattered with mud I can’t bear to look at it. That was Jim’s fault. I should never have gone out in it that night if it had not been for him.

I was just trying it on when he came in. He threw up his arms with a wild yell the moment he caught sight of it and exclaimed that he had “got ’em again!”

I said: “Does it fit all right behind?”

“Spiffin, old man,” he replied.

Then he wanted to know if I was coming out. I said “no” at first, but he overruled me. He said that a man with a suit like that had no right to stop indoors.

“Every citizen,” said he, “owes a duty to the public. Each one should contribute to the general happiness as far as lies in his power. Come out and give the girls a treat.”

Jim is slangy. I don’t know where he picks it up. It certainly is not from me.

“Do you think it will really please ’em?” said I.

He said it would be like a day in the country to them.

That decided me. It was a lovely evening and I went.

When I got home I undressed and rubbed myself down with whisky, put my feet in hot water and a mustard-plaster on my chest, had a basin of gruel and a glass of hot brandy-and-water, tallowed my nose, and went to bed. The gruel, mild and gluey, was like apology itself; the brandy-and-water gave off a hot, clean scent that made one feel momentarily as if one’s inside had been scoured. These prompt and vigorous measures, aided by a naturally strong constitution, were the means of preserving my life.

But as for the suit! Well, there, it isn’t a suit; it’s a splash-board.

And I did fancy that suit, too. But that’s just the way. I never do get particularly fond of anything in this world but what something dreadful happens to it. I had a tame rat when I was a boy, and I loved that animal as only a boy would love an old water-rat; and one day it fell into a large dish of gooseberry-fool that was standing to cool in the kitchen. Nobody knew what had become of the poor creature until the second helping. There is an image to reconcile you to Providence: gooseberries, cream, sugar—domestic delight—and then the unforeseen addition, as historians of food seldom record, of a rat.

I do hate wet weather in town. At least, it is not so much the wet as the mud that I object to. Somehow or other I seem to possess an irresistible alluring power over mud. I have only to show myself in the street on a muddy day to be half-smothered by it. It all comes of being so attractive, as the old lady said when she was struck by lightning.

Other people can go out on dirty days and walk about for hours without getting a speck upon themselves; while if I go across the road I come back a perfect disgrace to be seen (as in my boyish days my poor dear mother tried often to tell me). If there were only one dab of mud to be found in the whole of London, I am convinced I should carry it off from all competitors. I wish I could return the affection, but I fear I never shall be able to.

I have a horror of what they call the “London particular.” It is not merely the look of it, but the sound—the thickened city hush, not peaceful like snow-hush, but clogged, as if the air had been ladled out of a greasy pot and left to congeal. You breathe it and you feel as though you had been drinking lukewarm dish-water. I feel miserable and muggy all through a dirty day, and it is quite a relief to pull one’s clothes off and get into bed, out of the way of it all, as if one were putting oneself under a clean cover to keep the soot from settling on one’s thoughts.

Everything goes wrong in wet weather. I don’t know how it is, but there always seem to me to be more people, and dogs, and perambulators, and cabs, and carts about in wet weather than at any other time; and they all get in your way more; and everybody is so disagreeable—except myself—and it does make me so wild. And then, too, somehow I always find myself carrying more things in wet weather than in dry; and when you have a bag, and three parcels, and a newspaper, and it suddenly comes on to rain, you can’t open your umbrella.

Which reminds me of another phase of the weather that I can’t bear, and that is April weather (so called because it always comes in May). Poets think it very nice. As it does not know its own mind five minutes together, they liken it to a woman; and it is supposed to be very charming on that account. I don’t appreciate it, myself. Such lightning-change business may be all very agreeable in a girl. It is no doubt highly delightful to have to do with a person who grins one moment about nothing at all and snivels the next for precisely the same cause, and who then giggles, and then sulks, and who is rude, and affectionate, and bad-tempered, and jolly, and boisterous, and silent, and passionate, and cold, and stand-offish, and flopping, all in one minute (mind, I don’t say this. It is those poets. And they are supposed to be connoisseurs of this sort of thing); but in the weather the disadvantages of the system are more apparent.

A woman’s tears do not make one wet, but the rain does; and her coldness does not lay the foundations of asthma and rheumatism as the east wind is apt to. I can prepare for and put up with a regularly bad day, but these ha’porth-of-all-sorts kind of days do not suit me. It aggravates me to see a bright blue sky above me when I am walking along wet through; and there is something so exasperating about the way the sun comes out smiling after a drenching shower and seems to say, “Lord love you, you don’t mean to say you’re wet? Well, I am surprised. Why, it was only my fun.”

They don’t give you time to open or shut your umbrella in an English April—especially if it is an “automaton” one: the umbrella, I mean, not the April.

I bought an automaton once in April, and I did have a time with it. I wanted an umbrella, and I went into a shop in the Strand and told them so, and they said:

“Yes, sir. What sort of an umbrella would you like?”

I said I should like one that would keep the rain off and that would not allow itself to be left behind in a railway carriage.

“Try an automaton,” said the shopman.

“What’s an automaton?” said I.

“Oh, it’s a beautiful arrangement,” replied the man, with a touch of enthusiasm. “It opens and shuts itself.”

I bought one and found that he was quite correct. It did open and shut itself. I had no control over it whatever.

When it began to rain—which it did that season every alternate five minutes—I used to try and get the machine to open, but it would not budge. Then I used to stand and struggle with the wretched thing, and shake it, and swear at it, while the rain poured down in torrents, drumming on roofs and railings with a metallic rage, making the whole street a noisy wash-house.

Then the moment the rain ceased the absurd thing would go up suddenly with a jerk and would not come down again; and I had to walk about under a bright blue sky with an umbrella over my head, wishing that it would come on to rain again so that it might not seem that I was insane.

When it did shut, it did so unexpectedly and knocked one’s hat off.

I don’t know why it should be so, but it is an undeniable fact that there is nothing makes a man look so supremely ridiculous as losing his hat. The feeling of helpless misery that shoots down one’s back on suddenly becoming aware that one’s head is bare is among the most bitter ills that flesh is heir to. It is as if one had dropped one’s dignity in the gutter and had to run after it.

And then there is the wild chase after it, accompanied by an excitable small dog who thinks it is a game, and in the course of which you are certain to upset three or four innocent children—to say nothing of their mothers—butt a fat old gentleman on to the top of a perambulator, and carom off a ladies’ seminary into the arms of a wet sweep. After this, the idiotic hilarity of the spectators and the disreputable appearance of the hat when recovered appear but of minor importance.

Altogether, what between March winds, April showers, and the entire absence of May flowers, spring is not a success in cities. It is all very well in the country, as I have said, but in towns whose population is anything over ten thousand it most certainly ought to be abolished. In the world’s grim workshops it is like the children—out of place. Neither shows to advantage amid the dust and din. The sound alone is wrong: the harsh clank and shout of the streets eats up small voices and leaves them thin.

It seems so sad to see the little dirt-grimed brats try to play in the noisy courts and muddy streets. Poor little uncared-for, unwanted human atoms—they are not children. Children are bright-eyed, chubby, and shy. These are dingy, screeching elves, their tiny faces seared and withered, their baby laughter cracked and hoarse, as if even mirth had been forced to speak in the dominant tongue of the city—loud, hurried, and brutal.

The spring of life and the spring of the year were alike meant to be cradled in the green lap of nature. To us in the town spring brings but its cold winds and drizzling rains. We must seek it among the leafless woods and the brambly lanes, on the heathy moors and the great still hills, if we want to feel its joyous breath and hear its silent voices—the voices the city drowns out, the ones that do not sell anything, and therefore are not encouraged to be heard.

There is a glorious freshness in the spring there. The scurrying clouds, the open bleakness, the rushing wind, and the clear bright air thrill one with vague energies and hopes. Life, like the landscape around us, seems bigger, wider, freer—a rainbow road leading to unknown ends. Through the silvery rents that bar the sky we seem to catch a glimpse of the great hope and grandeur that lies around this little throbbing world, and a breath of its scent—raw earth, bruised grass, the first sharpness of green—is wafted to us on the wings of the wild March wind.

Strange thoughts we do not understand are stirring in our hearts. Voices are calling us to some great effort, to some mighty work. But we do not comprehend their meaning yet; and the hidden echoes within us that would reply are struggling, inarticulate and dumb—like an old word from a dying dialect, known in the mouth but not easily translated into the neat, respectable language of print. We stretch our hands like children to the light, seeking to grasp we know not what.

Our thoughts, like the boys’ thoughts in the Danish song, are very long, long thoughts, and very vague; we cannot see their end.

It must be so. All thoughts that peer outside this narrow world cannot be else than dim and shapeless. The thoughts that we can clearly grasp are very little thoughts—that two and two make four; that when we are hungry it is pleasant to eat; that honesty is the best policy. All greater thoughts are undefined and vast to our poor childish brains. We see but dimly through the mists that roll around our time-girt isle of life, and only hear the distant surging of the great sea beyond.

What I have endured from cats and dogs this morning no cook’s vocabulary can faithfully season. It began, as all domestic epics do, with a creature given far too grand a name for the size of the hallway.

Gustavus Adolphus—“Gusty” from below-stairs, because servants have the good sense to economize syllables—is an excellent dog provided you keep him where Providence intended: in the middle of a field, with nothing in range but weather and distant opinions. Indoors he is a national disaster with fur. He means well; the house simply isn’t built on the scale of his enthusiasm. He stretches: two chairs faint. He wags his tail: the room takes on the air of a town that has just been looted. He breathes: the fire considers retirement.

At dinner he has a habit—almost a tradition in our family now, like carving the joint—that is founded on the principle that a table is merely a low, four-legged suggestion. He creeps under it, lies there as quietly as a conscience, then rises all at once; and the first announcement of his movements is given by the table itself, which begins to behave as though it has read about gymnastics and is eager to try them. We clutch at it and strive to hold it level. He, imagining we are attempting some underhand scheme against him personally, struggles as only innocence can struggle, and the final tableau is generally an overturned table and a smashed dinner, pressed between two layers of indignant relatives.

This morning he arrived in his usual style, a method of entrance that seems to have been copied from an American cyclone and improved upon. I was at breakfast, carefully composing an atmosphere of peace: coffee steaming with that dark, toasted smell that makes a man believe in mornings; eggs waiting, modest and pale; butter softening to the exact pliancy that would have pleased my grandmother, who could judge a breakfast table as other women judge a sermon. I had just raised my cup, when Gusty’s tail—built, I am convinced, from the same timber as ship’s masts—swept it clean off the table. The coffee described a graceful parabola and landed, hot and wholehearted, in the middle of my waistcoat.

I rose abruptly and remarked something short, vivid, and unfit for preservation by any food historian. Gusty, who has a keen ear for tone if not for words, preceded me rapidly toward the door.

At the door he met Eliza coming in with eggs.

Eliza observed, “Ugh!” and sat down on the floor, as if her legs had been struck by a sudden moral. The eggs took up different positions about the carpet, where they spread themselves out in a manner that suggested they had always longed to become abstract art. Gusty, having achieved this arrangement, left the room.

I called after him, advising him, with firmness and some local dialect that my aunt would not approve, to go straight downstairs and not let me see him again for the next hour or so. He appeared to agree, dodged the coal-scoop with the practiced grace of a veteran, and went. I returned, dabbed myself with a napkin that had not been consulted in the matter, and finished breakfast with the resignation of a man eating in a world where animals have political power.

I felt sure he had gone out into the yard; but ten minutes later, looking into the passage, I found him sitting at the top of the stairs, as large as a regret.

I ordered him down at once. He responded by barking, leaping about, and generally behaving as though the stairhead were a parade-ground and he the band. So I went to see what the matter was.

It was Tittums.

She sat on the top stair but one and would not let him pass.

Tittums is our kitten. She is about the size of a penny roll—one of those little warm rolls that, when torn open, smell of yeast and comfort, and ought by rights to be buttered and eaten, not set to guard the staircase like a dragon. Her back was up and she was swearing like a medical student.

She does swear fearfully.

I do a little myself sometimes, in moments when coffee is applied to clothing; but I am a mere amateur compared with her. To tell you the truth—mind, this is strictly between ourselves; I should not like your wife to know I said it, because the women folk do not understand these things—I think it does a man good to swear. Swearing is a safety-valve. It lets the bad temper escape in harmless vapor, the way steam hisses out of a kettle before it explodes and takes the breakfast with it.

If, instead of expressing yourself properly, you were to say: “Bless you, my dear, sweet sir. What the sun, moon, and stars made you so careless (if I may be permitted the expression) as to allow your light and delicate foot to descend upon my corn with so much force? Is it that you are physically incapable of comprehending the direction in which you are proceeding, you nice, clever young man—you!”—or words to that effect—you would not feel half so relieved. Swearing has the same soothing effect upon angry passions that smashing furniture or slamming doors is so well known to exercise, added to which it is much cheaper. Swearing clears a man out like a pennyworth of gunpowder does the wash-house chimney. An occasional explosion is good for both.

I rather distrust a man who never swears, never kicks the footstool savagely, never pokes the fire with unnecessary violence. Without some outlet, the petty annoyances of life rankle. The little offense sits down beside you and becomes a sorrow; then, nursed in the hot-bed of rumination, it grows into a great injury, and under its poisonous shadow spring hatred and revenge.

Swearing relieves the feelings. That is what swearing does.

I explained all this to my aunt once. It did not answer with her. She said I had no business to have such feelings.

That is precisely what I told Tittums. I told her she ought to be ashamed of herself, brought up in a Christian family as she was too. I do not so much mind hearing an old cat swear; but I cannot bear to see a mere kitten give way to it. It seems sad in one so young.

I put Tittums in my pocket and returned to my desk.

I forgot her for the moment, and when I looked again she had squirmed out onto the table and was trying to swallow the pen—an implement which, if it had any sense, would have taken to hiding. Failing to consume literature at once, she put her leg into the ink-pot and upset it; then she licked her leg, tasting of whatever mournful concoction printers call “black”; then she swore again—at me this time, with the concentrated bitterness of a creature who has discovered that ink is not gravy.

I put her down on the floor.

And there Tim began rowing with her.

I do wish Tim would mind his own business. It was no concern of his what she had been doing. Besides, he is not a saint himself. He is only a two-year-old fox-terrier, and he interferes with everything and gives himself the airs of a gray-headed Scotch collie.

Tittums’ mother came in. Tim got his nose scratched, for which I am remarkably glad. I have put all three out into the passage, where they are fighting at this present moment. I am in a mess with the ink and in a thundering bad temper; and if anything more in the cat or dog line comes fooling about me this morning, it had better bring its own funeral contractor with it.

Yet, in general, I like cats and dogs very much indeed.

What jolly chaps they are! They are much superior to human beings as companions. They do not quarrel or argue with you. They never talk about themselves, but listen to you while you talk about yourself, and keep up an appearance of being interested in the conversation. They never make stupid remarks. They never observe to Miss Brown across a dinner-table that they always understood she was very sweet on Mr. Jones (who has just married Miss Robinson). They never mistake your wife’s cousin for her husband and fancy that you are the father-in-law. And they never ask a young author with fourteen tragedies, sixteen comedies, seven farces, and a couple of burlesques in his desk why he doesn’t write a play.

They never say unkind things. They never tell us of our faults “merely for our own good.” They do not, at inconvenient moments, mildly remind us of our past follies and mistakes. They do not say, “Oh yes, a lot of use you are if you are ever really wanted”—sarcastic like. They never inform us, like our inamoratas sometimes do, that we are not nearly so nice as we used to be. We are always the same to them.

They are always glad to see us. They are with us in all our humors. They are merry when we are glad, sober when we feel solemn, and sad when we are sorrowful.

“Halloo! happy and want a lark? Right you are; I’m your man. Here I am, frisking round you, leaping, barking, pirouetting, ready for any amount of fun and mischief. Look at my eyes if you doubt me. What shall it be? A romp in the drawing-room and never mind the furniture, or a scamper in the fresh, cool air, a scud across the fields and down the hill, and won’t we let old Gaffer Goggles’ geese know what time o’ day it is, neither! Whoop! come along.”

Or you’d like to be quiet and think. Very well. Pussy can sit on the arm of the chair and purr—purr, mind you, in a way that is not merely sound but a whole small acoustic landscape, a warm, steady engine-note that belongs to hearths and older tongues and the kind of rooms where talk used to be unhurried. And Montmorency will curl himself up on the rug and blink at the fire, yet keeping one eye on you the while, in case you are seized with any sudden desire in the direction of rats.

And when we bury our face in our hands and wish we had never been born, they do not sit up very straight and observe that we have brought it all upon ourselves. They do not even hope it will be a warning to us. They come up softly and shove their heads against us. If it is a cat she stands on your shoulder, rumples your hair, and says, “Lor’, I am sorry for you, old man,” as plain as words can speak; and if it is a dog he looks up at you with his big, true eyes and says with them, “Well you’ve always got me, you know. We’ll go through the world together and always stand by each other, won’t we?”

He is very imprudent, a dog is. He never makes it his business to inquire whether you are in the right or in the wrong; never bothers as to whether you are going up or down upon life’s ladder; never asks whether you are rich or poor, silly or wise, sinner or saint. You are his pal. That is enough for him; and come luck or misfortune, good repute or bad, honor or shame, he is going to stick to you—to comfort you, guard you, and give his life for you if need be—foolish, brainless, soulless dog!

Ah! old stanch friend, with your deep, clear eyes and bright, quick glances, that take in all one has to say before one has time to speak it, do you know you are only an animal and have no mind? Do you know that dull-eyed, gin-sodden lout leaning against the post out there is immeasurably your intellectual superior? Do you know that every little-minded, selfish scoundrel who lives by cheating and tricking, who never did a gentle deed or said a kind word, who never had a thought that was not mean and low or a desire that was not base—whose every action is a fraud, whose every utterance is a lie—do you know that these crawling skulks (and there are millions of them in the world), do you know they are all as much superior to you as the sun is superior to rushlight, you honorable, brave-hearted, unselfish brute?

They are MEN, you know, and MEN are the greatest, and noblest, and wisest, and best beings in the whole vast eternal universe. Any man will tell you that.

Never mind, though, pussy and doggie, we like you both all the better for your being stupid. We all like stupid things. Men cannot bear clever women, and a woman’s ideal man is some one she can call a “dear old stupid.” It is so pleasant to come across people more stupid than ourselves. We love them at once for being so. The world must be rather a rough place for clever people. Ordinary folk dislike them, and as for themselves, they hate each other most cordially. But there—the clever people are such a very insignificant minority that it really does not much matter if they are unhappy. So long as the foolish people can be made comfortable the world, as a whole, will get on tolerably well.

Cats have the credit of being more worldly wise than dogs—of looking more after their own interests and being less blindly devoted to those of their friends. And we men and women are naturally shocked at such selfishness. Cats certainly do love a family that has a carpet in the kitchen more than a family that has not; and if there are many children about, they prefer to spend their leisure time next door. But, taken altogether, cats are libeled.

Make a friend of one, and she will stick to you through thick and thin. All the cats that I have had have been most firm comrades.

I had a cat once that used to follow me about everywhere, until it even got quite embarrassing, and I had to beg her, as a personal favor, not to accompany me any further down the High Street. She used to sit up for me when I was late home and meet me in the passage. It made me feel quite like a married man—except that she never asked where I had been and then didn’t believe me when I told her.

Another cat I had used to get drunk regularly every day. She would hang about for hours outside the cellar door for the purpose of sneaking in on the first opportunity and lapping up the drippings from the beer-cask. I do not mention this habit of hers in praise of the species, but merely to show how almost human some of them are. If the transmigration of souls is a fact, this animal was certainly qualifying most rapidly for a Christian, for her vanity was only second to her love of drink.

Whenever she caught a particularly big rat, she would bring it up into the room where we were all sitting, lay the corpse down in the midst of us, and wait to be praised. Lord! how the girls used to scream.

Poor rats! They seem only to exist so that cats and dogs may gain credit for killing them and chemists make a fortune by inventing specialties in poison for their destruction. And yet there is something fascinating about them. There is a weirdness and uncanniness attaching to them. They are so cunning and strong, so terrible in their numbers, so cruel, so secret.

They swarm in deserted houses, where the broken casements hang rotting to the crumbling walls and the doors swing creaking on their rusty hinges. They know the sinking ship and leave her—no one knows how or whither. They whisper to each other in their hiding-places how a doom will fall upon the hall and the great name die forgotten. They do fearful deeds in ghastly charnel-houses. No tale of horror is complete without the rats.

In stories of ghosts and murderers they scamper through the echoing rooms, and the gnawing of their teeth is heard behind the wainscot, and their gleaming eyes peer through the holes in the worm-eaten tapestry, and they scream in shrill, unearthly notes in the dead of night, while the moaning wind sweeps, sobbing, round the ruined turret towers, and passes wailing like a woman through the chambers bare and tenantless. And dying prisoners, in their loathsome dungeons, see through the horrid gloom their small red eyes like glittering coals; hear, in the death-like silence, the rush of their claw-like feet; and start up shrieking in the darkness and watch through the awful night.

I love to read tales about rats. They make my flesh creep so.

I like that tale of Bishop Hatto and the rats. The wicked bishop, you know, had ever so much corn stored in his granaries and would not let the starving people touch it; but when they prayed to him for food he gathered them together in his barn, and then, shutting the doors on them, set fire to the place and burned them all to death. But next day there came thousands upon thousands of rats, sent to do judgment on him.

Then Bishop Hatto fled to his strong tower that stood in the middle of the Rhine, and barred himself in and fancied he was safe. But the rats! they swam the river, they gnawed their way through the thick stone walls, and ate him alive where he sat.

“They have whetted their teeth against the stones,
And now they pick the bishop’s bones;
They gnawed the flesh from every limb,
For they were sent to do judgment on him.”

Oh, it’s a lovely tale.

Then there is the story of the Pied Piper of Hamelin—how first he piped the rats away, and afterward, when the mayor broke faith with him, drew all the children along with him and went into the mountain. What a curious old legend that is! I wonder what it means—or has it any meaning at all? There seems something strange and deep lying hid beneath the rippling rhyme. It haunts me, that picture of the quaint, mysterious old piper piping through Hamelin’s narrow streets, and the children following with dancing feet and thoughtful, eager faces.

The old folks try to stay them, but the children pay no heed. They hear the weird, witched music and must follow. The games are left unfinished and the playthings drop from their careless hands. They know not whither they are hastening. The mystic music calls to them, and they follow, heedless and unasking where. It stirs and vibrates in their hearts and other sounds grow faint.

So they wander through Pied Piper Street away from Hamelin town.

I get thinking sometimes if the Pied Piper is really dead, or if he may not still be roaming up and down our streets and lanes, but playing now so softly that only the children hear him. Why do the little faces look so grave and solemn when they pause awhile from romping, and stand, deep wrapt, with straining eyes? They only shake their curly heads and dart back laughing to their playmates when we question them.

But I fancy myself they have been listening to the magic music of the old Pied Piper, and perhaps with those bright eyes of theirs have even seen his odd, fantastic figure gliding unnoticed through the whirl and throng. Even we grown-up children hear his piping now and then; but the yearning notes are very far away, and the noisy, blustering world is always bellowing so loud it drowns the dreamlike melody. One day the sweet, sad strains will sound out full and clear, and then we too shall, like the little children, throw our playthings all aside and follow.

The loving hands will be stretched out to stay us, and the voices we have learned to listen for will cry to us to stop. But we shall push the fond arms gently back and pass out through the sorrowing house and through the open door. For the wild, strange music will be ringing in our hearts, and we shall know the meaning of its song by then.

I wish people could love animals without getting maudlin over them, as so many do. Women are the most hardened offenders in such respects, but even our intellectual sex often degrade pets into nuisances by absurd idolatry.

There are the gushing young ladies who, having read “David Copperfield,” have thereupon sought out a small, long-haired dog of nondescript breed, possessed of an irritating habit of criticizing a man’s trousers, and of finally commenting upon the same by a sniff indicative of contempt and disgust. They talk sweet girlish prattle to this animal (when there is any one near enough to overhear them), and they kiss its nose, and put its unwashed head up against their cheek in a most touching manner—though I have noticed these caresses are principally performed when there are young men hanging about.

Then there are the old ladies who worship a fat poodle, scant of breath and full of fleas. I knew a couple of elderly spinsters once who had a sort of German sausage on legs which they called a dog between them. They used to wash its face with warm water every morning. It had a mutton cutlet regularly for breakfast; and on Sundays, when one of the ladies went to church, the other always stopped at home to keep the dog company.

There are many families where the whole interest of life is centered upon the dog.

Cats, by the way, rarely suffer from excess of adulation. A cat possesses a very fair sense of the ridiculous, and will put her paw down kindly but firmly upon any nonsense of this kind. Dogs, however, seem to like it. They encourage their owners in the tomfoolery, and the consequence is that, in the circles I am speaking of, what “dear Fido” has done, does do, will do, won’t do, can do, can’t do, was doing, is doing, is going to do, shall do, shan’t do, and is about to be going to have done, is the continual theme of discussion from morning till night.

All the conversation—consisting, as it does, of the very dregs of imbecility—is addressed to this confounded animal. The family sit in a row all day long, watching him, commenting upon his actions, telling each other anecdotes about him, recalling his virtues, and remembering with tears how one day they lost him for two whole hours—on which occasion he was brought home in a most brutal manner by the butcher-boy, who had been met carrying him by the scruff of his neck with one hand, while soundly cuffing his head with the other.

After recovering from these bitter recollections, they vie with each other in bursts of admiration for the brute, until some more than usually enthusiastic member, unable any longer to control his feelings, swoops down upon the unhappy quadruped in a frenzy of affection, clutches it to his heart, and slobbers over it. Whereupon the others, mad with envy, rise up, and seizing as much of the dog as the greed of the first one has left to them, murmur praise and devotion.

Among these people everything is done through the dog. If you want to make love to the eldest daughter, or get the old man to lend you the garden roller, or the mother to subscribe to the Society for the Suppression of Solo-Cornet Players in Theatrical Orchestras (it’s a pity there isn’t one, anyhow), you have to begin with the dog. You must gain its approbation before they will even listen to you; and if, as is highly probable, the animal—whose frank, doggy nature has been warped by the unnatural treatment he has received—responds to your overtures of friendship by viciously snapping at you, your cause is lost forever.

“If Fido won’t take to any one,” the father has thoughtfully remarked beforehand, “I say that man is not to be trusted. You know, Maria, how often I have said that. Ah! he knows, bless him.”

Drat him!

And to think that the surly brute was once an innocent puppy, all legs and head, full of fun and play, and burning with ambition to become a big, good dog and bark like mother.

Ah me! life sadly changes us all. The world seems a vast horrible grinding machine into which what is fresh and bright and pure is pushed at one end, to come out old and crabbed and wrinkled at the other. Look even at Pussy Sobersides, with her dull, sleepy glance, her grave, slow walk, and dignified, prudish airs—who could ever think that once she was the blue-eyed, whirling, scampering, head-over-heels, mad little firework that we call a kitten?

What marvelous vitality a kitten has. It is really something very beautiful, the way life bubbles over in the little creatures. They rush about, and mew, and spring; dance on their hind legs; embrace everything with their front ones; roll over and over; lie on their backs and kick. They do not know what to do with themselves, they are so full of life.

Can you remember, reader, when you and I felt something of the same sort of thing? Can you remember those glorious days of fresh young manhood—how, when coming home along the moonlit road, we felt too full of life for sober walking, and had to spring and skip, and wave our arms, and shout till belated farmers’ wives thought—and with good reason too—that we were mad, and kept close to the hedge, while we stood and laughed aloud to see them scuttle off so fast, and made their blood run cold with a wild parting whoop, and the tears came, we knew not why?

Oh, that magnificent young LIFE! that crowned us kings of the earth; that rushed through every tingling vein till we seemed to walk on air; that thrilled through our throbbing brains and told us to go forth and conquer the whole world; that welled up in our young hearts till we longed to stretch out our arms and gather all the toiling men and women and the little children to our breast and love them all—all.

Ah! they were grand days, those deep, full days, when our coming life, like an unseen organ, pealed strange, yearnful music in our ears, and our young blood cried out like a war-horse for the battle.

Ah, our pulse beats slow and steady now, and our old joints are rheumatic, and we love our easy-chair and pipe and sneer at boys’ enthusiasm. But oh for one brief moment of that god-like life again!

All great literary men are shy. That is one of the few sweeping statements a man may fling at the world without immediately being asked for references, sworn affidavits, and a certificate from his mother. I am shy myself—though, I am told, in my case it is now like nutmeg in a pudding: there, certainly, but you have to be a person of some delicacy to detect it. I am thankful it is so. There was a time when my shyness did not merely flavor my existence; it was the existence. It sat on my shoulder like an importunate parrot, screaming at the exact moment I most wanted to appear serene; and it made my own life wretched and everybody else’s uncomfortable—my lady friends, in particular, being loudest in their complaints, as if I had cultivated it to annoy them, like keeping a poultry-yard of peacocks solely for the pleasure of hearing them at dawn.

A shy man’s lot is not a happy one. Men dislike him, women despise him, and he dislikes and despises himself. Practice brings him no ease. You may put him into drawing-rooms as often as you may put a suet pudding into an oven; if you have made the pudding wrongly, mere heat will not redeem it. There is no cure for shyness except time—and, occasionally, some shock as sharp as biting unexpectedly on a peppercorn. Though I once met with a recipe for overcoming it which I have treasured, as a cook treasures a family secret—only this was the family secret of somebody else, and I suspect they would rather I did not pass it on.

It appeared among the “Answers to Correspondents” in a small weekly journal, and ran thus, with the airy assurance of a man recommending, to a starving household, that they should “eat more”: “Adopt an easy and pleasing manner, especially toward ladies.”

“Adopt an easy and pleasing manner, especially toward ladies.” One can see the poor shy wretch reading that, and laughing—not a healthy laugh, mind, but the strained little bark a man gives when he finds himself locked in a pantry and is told the best way out is to “go through the door.” Adopt it! As if manners were like a cap you take down from a peg and put on because you have callers.

Don’t adopt anything of the sort, my dear young shy friend. If you try to put on a manner that is not your own, you will not become easy and pleasing; you will become gushing and offensively familiar—like a waiter who, having been told to be attentive, insists on wiping your mouth for you and calling you “old chap.” Be your own natural self; then you will merely be considered surly and stupid. That is the comfort of shyness: it lets you choose between two kinds of misunderstanding, as the housewife chooses between two kinds of flour when both are a little musty.

And yet the shy man has a small revenge on society for the torture it inflicts on him: he can, to a certain extent, communicate his misery. He frightens other people as much as they frighten him. He acts as a damper upon the whole room. He is like putting a lid on a saucepan just when it has begun to sing: the jolliest spirits, hearing his silence, grow uncertain of their own laughter and begin to cough.

Most of this is misunderstanding. People mistake the shy man’s timidity for overbearing arrogance and are at once insulted and awed by it—as if his modest wish to vanish were a deliberate attempt to trample them. His awkwardness is resented as insolent carelessness. When, terror-stricken at the first word addressed to him, the blood rushes into his head and the power of speech fails him entirely, he is regarded as a shocking example of what comes of giving way to passion. It is hard to be accused, by your own incapacity to answer, of being violently emotional.

To be misunderstood is the shy man’s fate on every occasion; whatever impression he endeavors to create he conveys the opposite. When he makes a joke it is taken for a statement of fact and his want of veracity is much condemned. His sarcasm is accepted as his literal opinion and he gains a reputation for being an ass. If, on the other hand, wishing to ingratiate himself, he ventures upon a little flattery, it is taken for satire and he is hated ever after. He cannot season anything to taste; his salt is always declared to be sugar, and his sugar always called poison.

These and the rest of a shy man’s troubles are, of course, very amusing to other people, and have furnished comic writers since the earliest man blushed at the earliest woman and then pretended he had something in his eye. But if we look a little deeper—if we stop laughing long enough to hear what the room sounds like when the laughter dies—we find a pathetic, almost tragic, side.

A shy man means a lonely man—a man cut off from companionship, from sociability. He moves about the world but does not mix with it. Between him and his fellow-men runs an impassable barrier: a strong invisible wall that, when he tries to scale it, merely bruises him. He sees pleasant faces and hears pleasant voices on the other side—life’s ordinary music, the clink of words like plates being set down, the small rustle of sympathy like bread being broken—and he cannot stretch his hand across to grasp another hand. He watches merry groups and longs to speak, to claim kinship; but they pass him by, chatting gaily, and he cannot stop them. He tries to reach them, but his prison walls move with him and hem him in on every side. In the busy street, in the crowded room, amid work’s grind and pleasure’s whirl—wherever men congregate, wherever the sound of human speech is heard and human thought flashes from human eyes—there the shy man, shunned and solitary, stands apart like a leper. His soul may be full of love and longing, but the world does not know it. The iron mask of shyness is riveted before his face, and the man beneath is never seen.

Genial words and hearty greetings rise to his lips, then die away in unheard whispers behind the steel clamps. His heart aches for the weary brother, but his sympathy is dumb. Contempt and indignation against wrong choke in his throat; finding no safety-valve through which they may burst in utterance, they turn inward again and hurt him. All the love and hate and scorn of a deep nature, unable to spend themselves abroad, fester and sour him into a cynic. Shy men, like ugly women, have a bad time of it in this world, to go through which with any comfort needs the hide of a rhinoceros. Thick skin is our moral clothing; without it we are indecent in society. A poor gasping, blushing creature, with trembling knees and twitching hands, is a painful sight; and if it cannot cure itself, people—being always practical where other people’s suffering is concerned—are apt to think the sooner it goes and hangs itself the better.

The disease can be cured. For the comfort of the shy I can assure them, from personal experience, that there is recovery. I do not like speaking about myself—as may have been observed—but in the cause of humanity I will confess that at one time I was, as the young man in the Bab Ballad says, “the shyest of the shy,” and “whenever I was introduced to any pretty maid, my knees they knocked together just as if I was afraid.”

Now I would—nay, I have. On this very day before yesterday I did the deed. Alone and entirely by myself (as the schoolboy said translating the “Bellum Gallicum”) I bearded a railway refreshment-room young lady in her own lair.

A railway refreshment-room, if you have never studied it as a naturalist studies a pond, is a place where domestic life and industry meet in their noisiest form. The very air is a stew of odors—boiling tea with a metallic tang, bruised lemons, stale ham, the warm yeasty breath of buns that have lost their youth, and that peculiar perfume of hurried travel: damp coats, soot, and newspapers. And over it all lies a soundscape that would drive a thrush to drink—doors banging like cymbals, porters calling, wheels shrieking, teaspoons rattling in glasses as though all England were trying to summon courage with a spoon. In the midst of this uproar sits the refreshment-room young lady, calm as a cook who has seen a hundred puddings fall and learned not to flinch.

I went up to her. I rebuked her, in terms of mingled bitterness and sorrow, for her callousness and want of condescension. I insisted—courteously but firmly—on being accorded that deference and attention which is the right of the travelling Briton. And at the end I looked her full in the face.

Need I say more?

True, immediately after doing so I left the room with what may possibly have appeared to be precipitation and without waiting for any refreshment. But that was because I had changed my mind, not because I was frightened, you understand. One can change one’s mind about a cup of tea as suddenly as one can change one’s mind about a duel.

One consolation shy folk may take is that shyness is certainly no sign of stupidity. It is easy enough for bull-headed clowns to sneer at nerves; but the highest natures are not necessarily those containing the greatest amount of moral brass. The horse is not an inferior animal to the cock-sparrow, nor the deer of the forest to the pig. Shyness simply means extreme sensibility. It has nothing whatever to do with self-consciousness or conceit—though its relationship to both is continually insisted upon by that poll-parrot school of philosophy which repeats words the way a cheap music-hall repeats a chorus, loudening it until everybody mistakes noise for meaning.

Conceit, indeed, is the quickest cure. When it begins to dawn on you that you are a good deal cleverer than anyone else in the world, bashfulness is shocked and leaves you. When you can look round a roomful of people and think each one a mere child in intellect compared with yourself, you feel no more shy of them than you would of a select company of magpies or orang-outangs. Conceit is the finest armour a man can wear. Upon its smooth surface the puny dagger-thrusts of spite and envy glance aside. Without that breast-plate the sword of talent cannot force its way through the battle of life, for blows have to be borne as well as dealt.

I do not, of course, speak of the conceit that displays itself in an elevated nose and a falsetto voice. That is not real conceit; that is only playing at being conceited, as children play at being kings and queens and go strutting about with feathers and long trains. Genuine conceit does not make a man objectionable. On the contrary, it tends to make him genial, kind-hearted, and simple. He has no need of affectation; he is too well satisfied with his own character. His pride is too deep-seated to appear on the outside. Careless alike of praise or blame, he can afford to be truthful. Too far, in fancy, above the rest of mankind to trouble about their petty distinctions, he is equally at home with duke or costermonger. Valuing no standard but his own, he is never tempted to practise that miserable pretence which less self-reliant people offer up hourly to the god of their neighbour’s opinion—an idol worshipped largely because it makes such a fine clatter when you fall before it.

The shy man, on the other hand, is humble—modest of his own judgment and over-anxious concerning that of others. But in the case of a young man this is surely right enough. His character is unformed; it is slowly evolving itself out of a chaos of doubt and disbelief. As insight and experience grow, diffidence recedes. A man rarely carries his shyness past the hobbledehoy period. Even if his own inward strength does not throw it off, the world’s rubbings generally smooth it down. You scarcely ever meet a really shy man—except in novels or on the stage, where, by the way, he is much admired, especially by women.

There, in that supernatural land, he appears as a fair-haired and saintlike young man—fair hair and goodness always go together on the stage. No respectable audience would believe in one without the other. I knew an actor who mislaid his wig once and had to rush on to play the hero in his own hair, which was jet-black, and the gallery howled at all his noble sentiments under the impression that he was the villain.

He—the shy young man—loves the heroine devotedly, but only in asides, for he dare not tell her. He is noble and unselfish and speaks in a low voice and is good to his mother. The bad people laugh at him and jeer, but he takes it gently. In the end it transpires that he is a clever man, though nobody knew it; then the heroine tells him she loves him; he is surprised—oh, so happy!—and everybody loves him and asks him to forgive them, which he does in a few well-chosen and sarcastic words, and blesses them. He has, on the whole, such a good time that all the young fellows who are not shy long to be shy.

But the really shy man knows better. He knows it is not so pleasant in reality. He is not so interesting there as in fiction. He is a little more clumsy and stupid, a little less devoted and gentle; and his hair is much darker—which, taken altogether, considerably alters the case.

The point where he does resemble his ideal is in his faithfulness. I am fully prepared to allow the shy young man that virtue: he is constant in his love. But the reason is not far to seek. It exhausts all his stock of courage to look one woman in the face; it would be simply impossible for him to go through the ordeal with a second. He stands in far too much dread of the whole female sex to go gadding about with many of them. One is quite enough.

It is different with the young man who is not shy. He has temptations his bashful brother never encounters. He looks around and everywhere sees roguish eyes and laughing lips. What more natural than that amid so many roguish eyes and laughing lips he should become confused, and, forgetting for the moment which particular pair of roguish eyes and laughing lips it is that he belongs to, go off making love to the wrong set. The shy man, who never looks at anything but his own boots, sees not and is not tempted. Happy shy man! Not but what the shy man himself would much rather not be happy in that way. He longs to “go it” with the others and curses himself every day for not being able. He will now and again, screwing up his courage by a tremendous effort, plunge into roguishness. But it is always a terrible fiasco, and after one or two feeble flounders he crawls out again, limp and pitiable.

I say pitiable, though I am afraid he never is pitied. There are certain misfortunes which, while inflicting a vast amount of suffering upon their victims, gain for them no sympathy. Losing an umbrella, falling in love, toothache, black eyes, and having your hat sat upon may be mentioned as a few examples; but the chief of them all is shyness.

The shy man is regarded as an animate joke. His tortures are the sport of the drawing-room arena and are pointed out and discussed with gusto. “Look,” cry his tittering audience to each other, “he’s blushing!” “Just watch his legs,” says one. “Do you notice how he is sitting?” adds another—“right on the edge of the chair.” “Seems to have plenty of colour,” sneers a military-looking gentleman. “Pity he’s got so many hands,” murmurs an elderly lady, with her own calmly folded on her lap. “They quite confuse him.” “A yard or two off his feet wouldn’t be a disadvantage,” chimes in the comic man, “especially as he seems so anxious to hide them.” And then another suggests that with such a voice he ought to have been a sea-captain.

Some draw attention to the desperate way in which he is grasping his hat, as if it were the last loaf in a famine. Some comment on his limited powers of conversation. Others remark upon the troublesome nature of his cough. And so on, until his peculiarities and the company are both thoroughly exhausted.

His friends and relations make matters still more unpleasant (friends and relations are privileged to be more disagreeable than other people). Not content with laughing at him among themselves, they insist on his seeing the joke. They mimic and caricature him for his own edification. One, pretending to imitate him, goes outside and comes in again in a ludicrously nervous manner, explaining afterward that that is the way he—meaning the shy fellow—walks into a room. Or, turning to him with “This is the way you shake hands,” proceeds to go through a comic pantomime with the rest of the company, taking hold of everyone’s hand as if it were a hot plate and flabbily dropping it again.

Then they ask him why he blushes and why he stammers and why he always speaks in an almost inaudible tone, as though he did it out of stubbornness—like a man who insists on seasoning his soup with ashes. One of them, sticking out his chest and strutting about the room like a pouter-pigeon, suggests quite seriously that that is the style he should adopt. The old man slaps him on the back and says, “Be bold, my boy. Don’t be afraid of any one.” The mother says, “Never do anything that you need be ashamed of, Algernon, and then you never need be ashamed of anything you do,” and beaming mildly, seems surprised at the clearness of her own logic. The boys tell him he’s “worse than a girl,” and the girls repudiate the implied slur upon their sex by indignantly exclaiming that they are sure no girl would be half as bad.

They are quite right; no girl would be. There is no such thing as a shy woman—or, at all events, I have never come across one, and until I do I shall not believe in them. I know the generally accepted belief is the reverse. Women are supposed to be timid startled fawns, blushing and casting down their gentle eyes when looked at, and running away when spoken to; while we men are supposed to be bold and rollicky, and the poor dear little women admire us for it but are terribly afraid of us.

It is a pretty theory; but like most generally accepted theories it is mere nonsense. The girl of twelve is self-contained and as cool as the proverbial cucumber, while her brother of twenty stammers and stutters at her side. A woman will enter a concert-room late, interrupt the performance, and disturb the whole audience without moving a hair, while her husband follows her, a crushed heap of apologising misery, as though each rustle of his coat were a confession of crime.

The superior nerve of women in all matters connected with love—from the casting of the first sheep’s-eye down to the end of the honeymoon—is too well acknowledged to need comment. Nor, indeed, is love a fair example to cite in the present instance, the positions not being equally balanced. Love is woman’s business, and in “business” we all lay aside our natural weaknesses—the shyest man I ever knew was a photographic tout.

Oh yes—I know babies. I was one myself, for a brief engagement, though my wardrobe kept the post longer than I did. My earliest clear memory is not of maternal tenderness or infant philosophy, but of cloth: yards of it, like a badly managed tablecloth that insists on hanging over the edge just where your heels want to practice their art.

Why do they dress babies as though the child were a sausage intended for export? I do not mean this as a riddle. I have honestly wanted to know. Are the parents ashamed of the baby’s actual measurements and trying to sell it as a longer article—like a fish in the market laid out with its tail discreetly tugged?

I once asked a nurse, a large woman with the air of an experienced cook who has opinions about every sauce in Christendom, why babies must be trussed up in those trailing garments.

“Lor’, sir,” she said, as if I had inquired why water is wet, “they always has long clothes—bless their little hearts.”

I explained, gently, that while her feelings did her credit, they had not done my curiosity any.

“Lor’, sir,” she answered, her tone implying I had proposed roasting the infant on a spit, “you wouldn’t have ’em in short clothes, poor little dears?”

After that I grew timid about the subject. One does not cross-examine a woman who could boil you alive with her eyes and then skim the moral off the top. The reason—if reason there be—remains a mystery, folded up somewhere inside the baby like a spare napkin.

But really, putting them into any clothes at all seems to me like forcing a hermit crab to change shells twice a day for the amusement of passersby. Life contains enough dressing and undressing to exhaust a platoon of dressmakers; why begin the torture before the victim can even protest intelligibly? Babies live mostly in bed. If any class of society deserves exemption from costume changes, it is the citizens of the crib.

Yet every morning we rouse the poor little creature—warm as rising dough, smelling of milk and sleep—and we haul off one set of garments, pin on another, and put it away again, as though the whole object of infancy were to provide exercise for adult fingers. At night we drag it out once more and reverse the process. And when all is done, I should like to know what exact culinary distinction exists between a baby’s night-shirt and the article it wears by day. The difference appears to be as subtle as the difference between bread sliced by one aunt and bread sliced by another.

I may, however, be making myself ridiculous. I am often told I do. So I will say no more on the matter of clothing—except that it would be a mercy, a simple mercy, if some fashion were adopted enabling a man to tell a boy from a girl.

At present it is a social ambush. Neither hair, dress, nor conversation provides the slightest clue. You are left to guess, and by a law older than gravity and twice as malicious, you invariably guess wrong. Thereupon you are regarded by all present as a compound of idiot and villain. To call a male baby “she” is an offence only equaled by referring to a female infant as “he.” Whichever sex the child does not belong to is treated as beneath contempt, and to mention it is considered a personal insult to the family—rather as though you had suggested the roast was made of the wrong animal.

And as you value your reputation, do not attempt to escape by calling the baby “it.” There are various ways of winning unpopularity. You may murder a large and respected family and afterwards store them in the water company’s reservoir, and you will be disliked in that particular neighbourhood. You may rob a church and meet with coldness—especially from the vicar. But if you wish to drink scorn and hatred to the very dregs, let a young mother hear you call her dear baby “it.” The air in the room changes. Even the wallpaper seems to draw itself up. You become, in that instant, a being capable of any atrocity.

Your best plan is to address the object as “little angel.” “Angel” has the advantage of suiting either case; it is a diplomatic noun. “Pet” or “beauty” can be used for variety, but “angel” is the word that earns the highest marks for sensibility. It should be preceded by a short giggle and accompanied by as much smile as you can manage without cracking your face. And whatever you do, don’t forget to say the child has got its father’s nose.

This fetches the parents more than anything. They will pretend to laugh at first. “Oh, nonsense!” they will say, as if you had suggested the baby already knew Latin. You must then become warmly excited and insist it is a fact. You need have no scruples. The thing’s nose resembles its father’s quite as much as it resembles any other object in nature, being, as it is, a mere smudge—as if someone had touched the baby’s face with a floured thumb and called it feature.

Do not despise these hints. A time may come when, with mamma on one side and grandmamma on the other, a group of admiring young ladies behind (not admiring you, though), and a bald-headed dab of humanity in front, you will be profoundly grateful for any idea of what to say.

A man—an unmarried man, that is—is never seen to such disadvantage as when undergoing the ordeal of “seeing baby.” A cold shudder goes down his spine at the mere proposal, and the sickly smile with which he declares how delighted he will be ought to move even a mother’s heart—unless, as I suspect, the entire proceeding is a device adopted by wives to discourage the visits of bachelor friends. It is a cruel trick, whatever its excuse.

The bell is rung and somebody is sent to tell nurse to bring baby down. This is the signal for all the women present to begin talking “baby,” a special dialect made chiefly of soft exclamations, repeated endearments, and that curious sing-song rhythm that sounds like a lullaby sung through a teapot. To an outsider it is not language so much as a domestic birdsong—cooing, chirruping, little squeals of delight—pleasant enough in a meadow, but oppressive in a parlour when you are the only crow among the doves. Meanwhile you are left to your own sad thoughts and to speculation upon the practicability of suddenly remembering an engagement and the likelihood of being believed if you do.

Just as you have concocted an absurdly unconvincing tale about a man outside who must be seen at once—perhaps an urgent business concerning cheeses—the door opens.

In comes a tall, severe-looking woman carrying what at first sight appears to be a particularly skinny bolster with all the feathers at one end. It is swaddled so elaborately you might imagine it was a precious ham intended for the lord mayor’s table. Instinct, however, tells you this is the baby, and you rise with a miserable imitation of eagerness.

The object is received with a gush of feminine enthusiasm—the kind of chorus that might greet a celebrated pudding. Then, when the number of ladies speaking simultaneously has reduced itself to the ordinary four or five, the circle of fluttering skirts parts, and room is made for you to step forward. You do so with much the same air you would carry into the dock at Bow Street, and then, feeling unutterably wretched, you stand solemnly staring at the child.

There is dead silence—except for the house itself, which always seems to cough at such moments: the faint tick of a clock, the wheeze of a coal fire, the far-off rattle of the street, that modern industrial grinding that creeps through closed windows like soot. Every ear is tuned to you. They are waiting.

You try to think of something to say and discover, to your horror, that your reasoning faculties have departed as completely as the last slice of cake at a children’s party. It is despair; and your evil genius, choosing this moment, suggests the most idiotic remarks possible to human lips.

You glance round with an imbecile smile and observe, sniggeringly, that it “hasn’t got much hair, has it?”

Nobody answers at once. Then the stately nurse says, with much gravity, as if delivering a verdict on a burnt soufflé: “It is not customary for children five weeks old to have long hair.”

Another silence follows. You feel you are being offered a second chance, like a cook allowed to try again after dropping the first pie. You seize it by inquiring whether it can walk yet, or what they feed it on—subjects that, in retrospect, do not place you among the sound thinkers of your generation.

By now you are regarded as not quite right in the head, and pity replaces indignation. The nurse, however, is determined that insane or not you shall not shirk the ceremony. In the tones of a high priestess directing some sacred domestic rite, she says, holding out the bundle:

“Take her in your arms, sir.”

You are too crushed to resist. You meekly accept the burden. It is warm, oddly heavy for its size, and smells of milk and something faintly sour—like yesterday’s porridge left too near the fire. You hold it as you would hold a dish you are afraid to drop, aware that a wrong movement will bring disaster and public shame.

“Put your arm more down her middle, sir,” says the high priestess.

All step back. They watch you intently, as though you are about to perform a trick with it—pull a rabbit out of its cap, or carve it without spilling. What to do you know no more than what to say. Something must be done; and the only action that occurs to you is to heave the infant up and down with an “oopsee-daisy,” or some remark of equal intelligence, like stirring a sauce you do not understand.

“I wouldn’t jig her, sir, if I were you,” says the nurse. “A very little upsets her.”

You decide at once not to jig her, and you hope with all sincerity you have not already jiggled beyond redemption.

At this point the child, who has hitherto been regarding you with an expression of mingled horror and disgust—as though you were a strange ingredient introduced without warning—puts an end to the proceedings by beginning to yell at the top of its voice.

The sound is astonishing. It is not merely loud; it is concentrated, pure, and penetrating, like a whistle in a tunnel. It seizes the room by the throat. The delicate baby-language of the women is drowned utterly. Even the street-noise outside seems, for a moment, to pause in admiration, as if the passing carts and clanging iron had met their master.

The priestess rushes forward and snatches it from you.

“There! there! there!” she cries. “What did ums do to ums?”

“How very extraordinary!” you say pleasantly, because a man must say something when a small human siren is splitting his ears. “Whatever made it go off like that?”

“Oh, why, you must have done something to her!” says the mother indignantly. “The child wouldn’t scream like that for nothing.”

It is evident they think you have been running pins into it. In their minds, you are a bachelor—and therefore capable of anything.

The brat is calmed at last and would, no doubt, remain quiet enough, only some mischievous busybody points you out again with: “Who’s this, baby?”

The intelligent child recognises you and howls louder than ever—an acoustic judgement, swift and brutal. Whereupon some fat old lady remarks that it is “strange how children take a dislike to any one.”

“Oh, they know,” replies another mysteriously.

“It’s a wonderful thing,” adds a third.

Then everybody looks sideways at you, convinced you are a scoundrel of the blackest dye; and they glory in the pretty idea that your true character, unguessed by your fellow men, has been discovered by the untaught instinct of a little child—much as a dog is supposed to detect a bad butcher. You stand there, condemned by a creature that cannot yet say “spoon,” while the room vibrates with the notion of your villainy.

And yet—babies, with all their crimes and errors, are not without their uses. Not without use when they fill an empty heart; not without use when, at their call, sunbeams of love break through care-clouded faces; not without use when their little fingers press wrinkles into smiles. Odd little people! They are the unconscious comedians of the world’s great stage. They supply the humour in life’s all-too-heavy drama.

Each one is a small but determined opposition to the order of things in general, forever doing the wrong thing at the wrong time, in the wrong place and in the wrong way—with the solemnity of a judge and the persistence of yeast. The nurse-girl who sent Jenny to see what Tommy and Totty were doing and “tell ’em they mustn’t” knew infant nature. Give an average baby a fair chance, and if it doesn’t do something it oughtn’t, a doctor should be called in at once.

They have a genius for the ridiculous, and they do it with a grave, business-like air that is irresistible. Two of them will join hands and proceed due east at a break-neck toddle while an excitable big sister, voice cracking like an overstrained kettle, roars for them to go west. They will walk round a soldier, staring at his legs with the curiosity of food-historians encountering an unfamiliar tool, and poke him to see if he is real. They will stoutly maintain, against all argument and to the discomfort of the victim, that the bashful young man at the end of the ’bus is “dadda.” A crowded street-corner suggests itself to their minds as a favourable place for discussing family affairs at a shrill treble—so that the whole neighbourhood is included, whether it wants to be or not.

When in the middle of crossing the road they are seized with an impulse to dance. The doorstep of a busy shop is always the place they select for sitting down and taking off their shoes, as if footwear were a sudden philosophical mistake. At home they find the biggest walking-stick in the house—or an umbrella, open preferred—and use it to assist them upstairs, as though ascending were a military expedition.

They discover they love Mary Ann at the precise moment when that faithful domestic is blackleading the stove—an odour of polish and iron rising warm and sharp—and nothing will satisfy them but to embrace her then and there, thereby acquiring, across cheeks and pinafore, a sheen suitable for a newly griddled cake. With regard to food, their favourite dishes are coke and cat’s meat. They nurse pussy upside down and show their affection for the dog by pulling his tail—experimental gastronomy of the emotions, all without recipe.

They are a deal of trouble; they make a place untidy; they cost a lot of money to keep. But still you would not have the house without them. It would not be home without their noisy tongues and their mischief-making hands. Would not the rooms seem silent without their pattering feet? Might not you stray apart if no prattling voices called you together? The house has its own music, and babies are the bright, disorderly instruments in it—sometimes too loud, often out of time, but somehow essential to the tune.

It should be so—and yet I have sometimes thought the tiny hand seemed as a wedge, dividing.

It is a bearish task to quarrel with the purest of all human affections—that perfecting touch to a woman’s life—a mother’s love. It is a holy love that we coarser-fibred men can hardly understand. I would not be deemed to lack reverence for it when I say that surely it need not swallow up all other affection. The baby need not take your whole heart, like the rich man who walled up the desert well. Is there not another thirsty traveller standing by?

In your desire to be a good mother, do not forget to be a good wife. No need for all the thought and care to be for only one. Do not, whenever poor Edwin wants you to come out, answer indignantly: “What, and leave baby!” Do not spend all your evenings upstairs. Do not confine your conversation exclusively to whooping-cough and measles, as though matrimony were merely a footnote to the nursery’s medical chart.

My dear little woman, the child is not going to die every time it sneezes. The house is not bound to burn down and the nurse run away with a soldier every time you go outside the front door. Nor is the cat sure to come and sit upon the precious child’s chest the moment you leave the bedside. You worry yourself too much about that solitary chick, and you worry everybody else too. Think of your other duties, and your pretty face will not always be puckered into wrinkles; there will be cheerfulness in the parlour as well as in the nursery.

Think of your big baby a little. Dance him about a bit. Call him pretty names. Laugh at him now and then. He has his own hunger, though it is not for milk; and he notices, more than you believe, when the household language changes—when all words become “baby words,” and his proper dialect is treated as something spoken only by tradesmen. It is only the first baby that takes up the whole of a woman’s time. Five or six do not require nearly so much attention as one. But before then the mischief has often been done. A house where there seems no room for him, and a wife too busy to think of him, lose their hold on that unreasonable husband of yours, and he learns to look elsewhere for comfort and companionship.

But there, there, there! If I talk longer in this strain I shall earn the character of a baby-hater, and Heaven knows I am not one. Who could be, to look into the little innocent faces clustered in timid helplessness round those great gates that open down into the world?

The world—the small round world! What a vast mysterious place it must seem to baby eyes. What a trackless continent the back garden appears! What marvellous explorations they make in the cellar under the stairs—each jar and broom a relic from an older age, each smell a chapter of household history: coal-dust, damp potatoes, soap, old wood. With what awe they gaze down the long street, wondering—like us bigger babies when we gaze up at the stars—where it all ends.

And down that longest street of all—that long, dim street of life that stretches out before them—what grave, old-fashioned looks they sometimes cast. What pitiful, frightened looks, too. I saw a little mite sitting on a doorstep in a Soho slum one night, and I shall never forget the look that the gas-lamp showed me on its wizen face: a look of dull despair, as if from the squalid court the vista of its own squalid life had risen, ghostlike, and struck its heart dead with horror. Even there the city had its noise—wheels, voices, a harsh song leaking from somewhere—and it seemed not music but a kind of grinding that wore the spirit thin.

Poor little feet, just commencing the stony journey. We old travellers, far down the road, can only pause to wave a hand to you. You come out of the dark mist, and we, looking back, see you—so tiny in the distance—standing on the brow of the hill, your arms stretched out toward us. God speed you! We would stay and take your little hands in ours, but the murmur of the great sea is in our ears and we may not linger. We must hasten down, for the shadowy ships are waiting to spread their sable sails.

I have always entertained a warm, steady affection for eating and drinking; and, in the early years, the eating held the greater share of my devotion. As a child I possessed that rare, barbaric endowment which, like a language that has no written form and therefore no spelling to quarrel over, is at once perfect and taken for granted: an appetite—also a digestion.

I recall an evening—so clearly that I can still hear the thin clink of knife against plate and the little sigh the gravy made as it slid—when a dull-eyed gentleman of a livid complexion came to dine at our house. He watched me for some five minutes, not with the indulgence one bestows upon youth, but with the startled reverence of a man who is listening to some lost, primitive music.

At last he turned to my father and said, in the manner of one making inquiry about a threatened thunderstorm:

“Does your boy ever suffer from dyspepsia?”

“I never heard him complain of anything of that kind,” replied my father; and then, as fathers will, he put the matter to the tribunal itself: “Do you ever suffer from dyspepsia, Colly wobbles?”

(They called me Colly wobbles; it was not my real name, but it served the purpose—like those general terms that a conquering tongue imposes upon a conquered district, and by which whole subtleties are flattened into one sound.)

“No, pa,” said I.

And then, because I had never yet needed that particular word and therefore had not learned its taste, I asked: “What is dyspepsia, pa?”

The livid gentleman looked at me with a mixture of amazement and envy—as though I had spoken to him in some fresh dialect of Eden—and said slowly, with infinite pity:

“You will know—some day.”

My poor dear mother used to say she liked to see me eat. I have since found comfort in the reflection that, whatever else I may have neglected in the matter of pleasing her, I must at least have given her honest satisfaction at table. A growing lad, sound of wind and careless of books, can do noble things with a joint. Give him exercise, give him daylight, and keep him from reading too much; and he will attack roast meat with a religious sincerity that would shame the most devout at matins.

It is an entertainment, boys eating—provided you are not the person who has to pay the butcher. Their notion of a square meal is a pound and a half of roast beef—properly roasted too, with the fat crisped to a savory brown and the lean still juicy—five or six potatoes (they prefer those that are of a soapy and sustaining character, such as might have been bred expressly to be swallowed), a heroic quantity of greens, and four thick slices of Yorkshire pudding, which, if the cook understands her business, should have a proud, ballooned crown and a custardy heart tasting of dripping and comfort. After this come currant dumplings—two, at least—boiled to that glossy stoutness that resists the spoon; then a few green apples, a pennyworth of nuts, half-a-dozen jumbles, and a bottle of ginger-beer whose cork makes a report like a small gun. Then they go out and play at horses.

How they must despise us men—us feeble late editions—who require to sit quiet for two hours after dining off a spoonful of clear soup and the wing of a chicken, as though the world’s entire larder were a subject for genteel footnotes.

And yet the boys have not all the advantage. A boy never knows the luxury of being satisfied. He never arrives at that mellow, golden end of eating when you push your chair back, let the table’s clatter fade, and feel, not that you have conquered hunger, but that you have made a treaty with it. A boy never feels full. A dinner makes no difference whatever to him. To a man a good dinner is a fairy’s potion; it alters the whole climate of the mind. The lights seem kinder. The room, though unchanged, has grown more hospitable. The smallest noises—the crackle of coal, the creak of a chair, the distant rattle of crockery being put away—fall into their places like instruments in a well-conducted orchestra.

A man who has dined satisfactorily experiences a yearning love towards all fellow-creatures. He strokes the cat gently and calls it “poor pussy,” in tones full of tenderness. He listens to the German band outside and wonders if they are cold, and for a few miraculous minutes he does not even hate his wife’s relations. A good dinner draws the cork out of human kindness. Under its genial influence the gloomy thaw and become chatty; sour, starchy persons—who all the rest of the day look as though they lived on vinegar and Epsom salts—break into wreaths of smiles, pat small children on the head, and talk to them—vaguely—about sixpences. Serious men become mildly cheerful. Snobbish young men of the heavy-mustache type forget to make themselves objectionable.

I am myself inclined to sentiment after dinner. It is the only time when I can properly appreciate love-stories. Then, when the hero clasps her to his heart in one last wild embrace and stifles a sob, I feel as sad as though I had dealt at whist and turned up only a deuce; and when the heroine dies in the end I weep. Read the same tale early in the morning, before the kettle has begun its homely singing and before the house has found its proper daily key, and I should sneer at it as if it were printed in a foreign tongue I refused to learn.

Digestion—or rather indigestion—has a marvelous effect upon the heart. If I want to write anything very pathetic (I mean, if I want to try to write anything very pathetic) I eat a large plateful of hot buttered muffins about an hour beforehand. The proper muffin for this purpose must be split with decision, toasted just enough to freckle, and then soaked—there is no other honest word for it—in butter that hisses faintly as it sinks, leaving the air rich with that warm, dairy smell which makes the conscience tender and the imagination untrustworthy. By the time I sit down to my work a feeling of unutterable melancholy has come over me.

I picture heartbroken lovers parting forever at lonely wayside stiles, while the sad twilight deepens around them and only the tinkling of a distant sheep-bell breaks the sorrow-laden silence. Old men sit and gaze at withered flowers till their sight is dimmed by mist of tears. Little dainty maidens wait and watch at open casements; but he cometh not, and the heavy years roll by and the sunny gold tresses wear white and thin. The babies they dandled have become grown men and women with podgy torments of their own; the playmates they laughed with are lying very silent under the waving grass. Still they wait and watch, till the dark shadows of the unknown night steal up and gather round them and the world, with its childish troubles, fades from their aching eyes. I see pale corpses tossed on white-foamed waves, death-beds stained with bitter tears, graves in trackless deserts. I hear the wild wailing of women, the low moaning of little children, the dry sobbing of strong men.

It is all the muffins.

I could not conjure up one melancholy fancy upon a mutton chop and a glass of champagne. A full stomach is a great aid to poetry; indeed no sentiment of any kind can stand upon an empty one. We have not time or inclination to indulge in fanciful troubles until we have got rid of our real misfortunes. We do not sigh over dead dicky-birds with the bailiff in the house; and when we do not know where on earth to get our next shilling from, we do not worry as to whether our mistress’s smiles are cold, hot, lukewarm, or anything else about them.

Foolish people—when I say “foolish people” in this contemptuous way I mean people who entertain different opinions to mine; for if there is one person I despise more than another it is the man who does not think exactly the same on all topics as I do—foolish people will tell you that mental distress is far more agonizing than bodily. Romantic and touching theory! So comforting to the love-sick young sprig who looks down patronizingly at some poor devil with a white, starved face and thinks, “Ah, how happy you are compared with me!”—so soothing to fat old gentlemen who cackle about the superiority of poverty over riches.

But it is all nonsense—all cant. An aching head soon makes one forget an aching heart. A broken finger will drive away all recollections of an empty chair. And when a man feels really hungry he does not feel anything else.

We sleek, well-fed folk can hardly realize what feeling hungry is like. We know what it is to have no appetite and not to care for dainty victuals placed before us, but we do not understand what it means to sicken for food—to die for bread while others waste it—to gaze with famished eyes upon coarse fare steaming behind dingy windows, longing for a pennyworth of pea-pudding and not having the penny to buy it—to feel that a crust would be delicious and that a bone would be a banquet.

Hunger is a luxury to us: a piquant sauce that gives flavor to comfort. It is well worth while to get hungry and thirsty merely to discover how much gratification can be obtained from eating and drinking. If you wish to thoroughly enjoy your dinner, take a thirty-mile country walk after breakfast and touch nothing till you get back. Your eyes will glisten at the sight of the white table-cloth and the steaming dishes. With what a sigh of content you will set down the empty beer-tankard and take up your knife and fork; and how comfortably you will feel afterwards, when you push back your chair, light a cigar, and beam round upon everybody, as though you were the benevolent owner of the whole world and had just forgiven it.

Make sure, however, when adopting this plan, that the good dinner is really to be had at the end, or the disappointment is trying.

I remember once a friend and I—dear old Joe, it was. Ah, how we lose one another in life’s mist! It must be eight years since I last saw Joseph Taboys. How pleasant it would be to meet his jovial face again, to clasp his strong hand, and to hear his cheery laugh once more! He owes me fourteen shillings, too.

Well, we were on holiday together; and one morning we breakfasted early and started for a tremendous long walk. We had ordered a duck for dinner overnight. We said, “Get a big one, because we shall come home awfully hungry;” and as we were going out the landlady came up in great spirits.

“I have got you gentlemen a duck, if you like. If you get through that you’ll do well,” she said; and she held up a bird about the size of a door-mat.

We chuckled at the sight and said we would try. We said it with self-conscious pride, like men who know their own powers. Then we started.

We lost our way, of course. I always do in the country, and it makes me so wild; because it is no use asking directions of any of the people you meet. One might as well inquire of a lodging-house slavey the way to make beds as expect a country bumpkin to know the road to the next village.

You have to shout the question about three times before the sound of your voice penetrates his skull. At the third time he slowly raises his head and stares blankly at you, as though you were a new sort of cart. You yell it at him then for a fourth time, and he repeats it after you. He ponders while you count a couple of hundred. After which, speaking at the rate of three words a minute, he fancies you “couldn’t do better than—” Here he catches sight of another idiot coming down the road and bawls out the particulars, requesting his advice.

The two argue the case for a quarter of an hour, finally agreeing that you had better go straight down the lane, round to the right, cross by the third stile, keep to the left by old Jimmy Milcher’s cow-shed, across the seven-acre field, through the gate by Squire Grubbin’s hay-stack, keep the bridle-path for awhile till you come opposite the hill where the windmill used to be—but it’s gone now—and round to the right, leaving Stiggin’s plantation behind you. You say “Thank you” and go away with a splitting headache but without the faintest notion of your way, the only clear idea you retain being that somewhere or other there is a stile which has to be got over; and at the next turn you come upon four stiles, all leading in different directions.

We underwent this ordeal two or three times. We tramped over fields; we waded through brooks; we scrambled over hedges and walls; we had a row as to whose fault it was that we first lost our way. We became thoroughly disagreeable, footsore, and weary. But throughout it all the hope of that duck kept us up. Like a domestic myth, it floated before our tired eyes and drew us onward. The thought of it was as a trumpet-call to the fainting. We talked of it and cheered each other with recollections of it.

“Come along,” we said; “the duck will be spoiled.”

We felt a strong temptation at one point to turn into a village inn and have a cheese and a few loaves between us; but we heroically restrained ourselves. We should enjoy the duck all the better for being famished.

We fancied we smelled it when we got into the town—the rich, honest perfume of bird and browning skin, the faint suggestion of sage, the domestic hush that comes with a roasting-pan doing its duty. We did the last quarter of a mile in three minutes. We rushed upstairs, washed ourselves, changed our clothes, came down, pulled our chairs up to the table, and sat rubbing our hands while the landlady removed the covers.

I seized the knife and fork and started to carve.

It seemed to want a lot of carving. I struggled with it for five minutes without making the slightest impression; and then Joe, who had been eating potatoes, wanted to know if it wouldn’t be better for some one to do the job that understood carving.

I took no notice of his foolish remark, but attacked the bird again; and so vigorously this time that the animal left the dish and took refuge in the fender.

We soon had it out of that, and I was prepared to make another effort; but Joe was getting unpleasant.

He said that if he had thought we were to have a game of blind hockey with the dinner he would have got a bit of bread and cheese outside.

I was too exhausted to argue. I laid down the knife and fork with dignity, took a side seat, and Joe went for the wretched creature.

He worked away in silence for awhile; then he muttered, “Damn the duck,” and took his coat off.

We did break the thing up at length with the aid of a chisel, but it was perfectly impossible to eat it. We made our dinner off the vegetables and an apple-tart. We tried a mouthful of the duck, but it was like eating India-rubber. It was a wicked sin to kill that drake. But there—there’s no respect for old institutions in this country; even a venerable duck may be struck down and then discovered to be immortal.

I began with the idea of writing about eating and drinking, but I seem to have confined my remarks entirely to eating. Well, you see, drinking is one of those subjects with which it is inadvisable to appear too well acquainted. The days are gone by when it was considered manly to go to bed intoxicated every night, and a clear head and a firm hand no longer draw down upon their owner the reproach of effeminacy. On the contrary, in these sadly degenerate days, an evil-smelling breath, a blotchy face, a reeling gait, and a husky voice are regarded as the hall-marks of the cad rather than the gentleman.

Even nowadays, though, the thirstiness of mankind is something supernatural. We are forever drinking on one excuse or another. A man never feels comfortable unless he has a glass before him. We drink before meals and with meals and after meals. We drink when we meet a friend and when we part from a friend. We drink when we are talking, when we are reading, and when we are thinking. We drink one another’s healths and spoil our own. We drink the Queen and the Army and the Ladies and everybody else that is drinkable; and I believe if the supply ran short we should drink our mothers-in-law.

By the way, we never eat anybody’s health—always drink it. Why should we not stand up now and then and eat a tart to somebody’s success? Why should the toast always be liquid? A nation that has forgotten the old, thick, speaking foods—those which require teeth, patience, and time—will, I suspect, soon forget the old, thick, speaking words as well; and then, poor creatures, they will have nothing left but a thin vocabulary to match their thin beverages.

To me, I confess, the constant necessity of drinking under which the majority of men labor is quite unaccountable. I can understand people drinking to drown care or to drive away maddening thoughts well enough. I can understand the ignorant masses loving to soak themselves in drink—oh, yes, it’s very shocking that they should, of course; very shocking to us who live in cozy homes, with all the graces and pleasures of life around us—that the dwellers in damp cellars and windy attics should creep from their dens of misery into the warmth and glare of the public-house bar, and seek to float for a brief space away from their dull world upon a Lethe stream of gin.

But think, before you hold up your hands in horror at their ill-living, what “life” for these wretched creatures really means. Picture the squalid misery of their brutish existence, dragged on from year to year in the narrow, noisome room where, huddled like vermin in sewers, they welter, and sicken, and sleep; where dirt-grimed children scream and fight and sluttish, shrill-voiced women cuff and curse and nag; where the street outside teems with roaring filth and the house around is a bedlam of riot and stench.

There is a sound in such places that is worse than hunger: the unceasing industrial grind of misery—the clatter, the bellow, the thin screaming, the banging of doors, the quarrel that never quite ends, the street’s hoarse shout forcing itself through cracked panes. It is not music; it is not silence; it is a cruel, constant noise that leaves no room in a man’s head for any thought that is his own. In a clean kitchen, the noises are domestic and intelligible: the kettle’s rising song, the knife’s clean rhythm, the comforting thud of dough, the crack of a fire. In those other rooms the noise is not a language you can live in—it is a foreign tyranny, and it drives men to any drink that can, for a little while, change the key of the world.

Think what a sapless stick this fair flower of life must be to them, devoid of mind and soul. The horse in his stall scents the sweet hay and munches ripe corn contentedly. The watch-dog in his kennel blinks at the grateful sun, dreams of a glorious chase over dewy fields, and wakes with a yelp of gladness to greet a caressing hand. But the clod-like life of these human logs never knows one ray of light. From the hour when they crawl from their comfortless bed to the hour when they lounge back into it again, they never live one moment of real life. Recreation, amusement, companionship—they know not the meaning of. Joy, sorrow, laughter, tears, love, friendship, longing, despair—are idle words to them: words from another class’s dictionary, pressed upon them like labels on jars whose contents they will never taste. From the day when their baby eyes first look out upon their sordid world to the day when, with an oath, they close them forever and their bones are shoveled out of sight, they never warm to one touch of human sympathy, never thrill to a single thought, never start to a single hope.

In the name of the God of mercy, let them pour the maddening liquor down their throats and feel for one brief moment that they live.

Ah! We may talk sentiment as much as we like, but the stomach is the real seat of happiness in this world. The kitchen is the chief temple wherein we worship: its roaring fire is our vestal flame, and the cook is our great high-priest. He is a mighty magician—and a kindly one. He soothes away sorrow and care, drives forth enmity, gladdens love. Our God is great and the cook is His prophet.

Let us eat, drink, and be merry.

“Oh, you have some rooms to let.”

“Mother!”

There are cries like this that seem older than brickwork. I have heard them in streets where the pavements look as if they were laid down by the Romans and repaired—since—by people who hated the very notion of repair. It is always “Mother,” too; never “Madam,” never “Mrs. So-and-so,” never “Proprietress.” “Mother” suggests, somehow, a soft hand, a firm tongue, and potatoes that must be minded at any cost.

“Well, what is it?”

“’Ere’s a gentleman about the rooms.”

“Ask ’im in. I’ll be up in a minute.”

So you are admitted—half guest, half parcel—into a passage that smells of damp umbrellas, stale coal, and the eternal frying that goes on in the hearts of all lodging-houses. Somewhere below, there is a pan at work, with that particular hiss which is not the cheerful song of honest bacon, but the fretful, thin splutter of something that has been “made to do,” again and again, until it has lost all language except complaint. The house has its own acoustic climate: the weary drip of a tap that has cried itself hoarse, the scuffle of slippers that know every stair by bruise, the clink of cheap crockery, and, now and then, a sudden shout that cracks through the floors like a dropped plate.

“Will yer step inside, sir? Mother’ll be up in a minute.”

You step inside, and wait; and presently “mother” comes slowly up the kitchen stairs, untying her apron as she climbs, and calling down instructions about the potatoes with the solemnity of a general moving troops.

“Good-morning, sir,” says mother, smiling the way a towel smiles—clean, but exhausted. “Will you step this way, please?”

“Oh, it’s hardly worth while my coming up,” you say. “What sort of rooms are they, and how much?”

“Well,” says the landlady, “if you’ll step upstairs I’ll show them to you.”

There is a special persuasion in that sentence. It does not answer. It postpones. It suggests, with a neat little twist, that any disappointment you may experience later is entirely your own fault for having asked a question instead of obeying.

So, with a protesting murmur—carefully composed to be remembered in the witness-box of your own conscience—you follow “mother” upward.

At the first landing you are arrested by a pail and a broom—posted there like a picket to stop deserters. “Mother” instantly begins a monologue on the unreliability of servant-girls, and bawls over the balusters for Sarah to come and take them away at once. The shout does not so much travel down the stairwell as take possession of it, like a foghorn in a narrow harbour. The house responds with a faint shuffling, a drawer banged somewhere, and then silence—silence of that lodging-house kind which is never quiet, only waiting.

Outside the rooms she pauses, hand on the door, and says, as every landlady since the Flood has said:

“They’re rather untidy just at present, sir, as the last lodger left only yesterday; and it’s their cleaning-day.”

It is always their cleaning-day. If you took rooms for twelve months you would still be told, at intervals, that it was their cleaning-day, and you would come to regard cleaning-day as a permanent condition of the universe, like winter or taxation.

With this understanding you enter, and both stand solemnly feasting your eyes upon the scene.

The rooms cannot be said to appear inviting. Even “mother’s” face—trained to optimism by years of rent-collecting—betrays no admiration. Untenanted furnished apartments in the morning sunlight have a candour that is brutal. Nothing hides. The dust sits where it has always sat; the scratches show; the wallpaper’s sins come out into full confession. There is a lifeless air about them, as if the chairs, having endured too many strangers, have given up expecting friendship.

Yet there is a curious thing about rooms. They are like saucepans: they look hopeless in other people’s sculleries, and are lovable the moment they have boiled your own soup.

It is a very different thing when you have settled down and are living in them. Then, however sordid the shell, you begin to stock it with your private provisions—your own little household gods; and the place starts to smell not of “previous lodger,” but of you.

You put your kettle on, and suddenly the room is redeemed by steam. You unpack a tin of tea, and the air changes character at once. Tea is a great civilizer: it edits out despair. You toast a bit of bread over the fire—turning it carefully, watching for that exact moment when pale innocence becomes aromatic virtue—and the lodging-house odour retreats, disgraced. A man may forgive a smoky mirror if he can smell butter melting on hot toast.

And then the smaller gods arrive: your knick-knacks, your books, your old scarf that knows your throat, your battered saucepan that has done you honest service, your pepper-tin with the lid that never fits but somehow always gives you pepper. You arrange things, and—without meaning to—you begin building a personal dialect in objects. A stranger could not translate it. That pipe lying there is not “a pipe”; it is Tuesday evening, and rain on the window, and the particular kind of silence that comes after bad news. That chipped cup is not “a cup”; it is Sunday, and a letter not written.

You set up photographs—those stern little rectangles in which youth is always caught in the act of being braver than it felt—and, because you are a man and must make some show of nonchalance, you scatter about half a dozen disreputable-looking pipes in painfully prominent positions, as if to say, “I smoke, and nothing touches me.” You mislay one carpet slipper under the coal-box and balance the other on top of the piano. The walls, which looked so dingy and accusatory, are hidden behind the well-known pictures; and the furniture—knobby, shiny, suspicious—begins to look less like an enemy and more like an acquaintance with bad manners but a good heart.

Old things do that. They take your life into themselves, slowly, without making any fuss about it. They grow heavy with association. New furniture may make a palace; old furniture makes a home—because it has been there while you have eaten, and failed, and laughed, and burnt the milk, and ruined a sauce, and tried again.

I once knew a screen—one of those folding screens with extravagant flowers, tulips and roses and lilies all springing from one stem, as if botany had been invented by a poet in a hurry. It had a sulky dignity, and always seemed to be listening. Its colours were fading; moths had been at its silken threads with the steady appetite of time; and yet, when the firelight played on it, you could almost imagine it remembered its own youth.

Do old things remember? They look as if they do—grave, patient, and a little reproachful. That screen seemed to carry, folded up inside it, the sound of hands that were gone—the soft, busy hands that had once stitched it in a room full of daylight and talk. I used to fancy it had heard laughter that no one now could imitate, because the very words had changed. Whole ways of speaking die out like birds. You can translate a sentence, perhaps, but you cannot translate the tone in which it was said, nor the small domestic noises around it—the click of needles, the rustle of a skirt, the kettle just beginning to sing.

And there must have been a lad, too. There is always a lad in the background of such work—some clumsy, headstrong fellow who would seize a hand meant for sewing and insist on kisses and nonsense, interfering with the progress of the screen and calling it, no doubt, “that confounded thing,” while being secretly proud of it. I can see him through the haze of lamplight: bright-eyed, curly-haired, tight in his smalls, dangerous in his vanity, and certain that the world was designed principally to hold his love-affairs.

Can that boy ever truly become the grave gentleman we later call “father”? It seems an impertinent suggestion; and yet the old screen, with a sort of stiff, silent certainty, appears to nod.

But this is how furniture behaves. It tempts you away from the matter in hand. It is a trap set by sentiment for the feet of practical men. You come to look at lodgings; you end by discussing eternity with a piece of mahogany.

The furniture of furnished apartments, however, is old without being yours. It has age but no intimacy; like a story told in a dialect you do not quite understand. You feel, at first sight, that it will never accept you, and you will never forgive it. Everything about it insists on its worst aspect.

The easy-chair—made of shiny horse-hair, with wooden knobs that appear to have been designed by a medical student—suggests anything but ease. The mirror is smoky, and gives you the complexion of a man who has been living on cold sausages. The curtains want washing, and would confess all sorts of crimes if put in hot water. The carpet is frayed, not in the romantic way of an honest old rug, but in the despairing way of something that has been dragged backward and forward by too many boots. The table looks as if it would go over the instant anything heavier than a postage-stamp was rested upon it. The grate is cheerless; the wall-paper has a pattern that seems to have been chosen by somebody with a grudge against colour; and the ceiling appears to have had coffee spilt all over it—coffee, too, of that weak lodging-house variety which smells chiefly of regret.

And the ornaments—well, the ornaments are the finishing insult.

There must be some special and secret manufactory for lodging-house ornaments. Precisely the same articles are to be found in every set of rooms across the kingdom, and nowhere else in nature. Two unnameable objects stand one at each end of the mantelpiece, hung round with long triangular slips of glass. These clank together whenever you breathe, and their noise is not the healthy music of a home, but the nervous, thin-jawed rattle of things that exist solely to remind you you are not settled. In poorer rooms they are reinforced by china shapes that might be cows sitting on their hind legs, or temples, or dogs, or all three at once. Somewhere you discover a bilious-looking lump which you take at first for dough abandoned by a child; but on scrutiny it resembles an underdone cupid suffering from indigestion. The landlady calls it a statue.

Then there is a sampler worked by some unfortunate relative who evidently hated the alphabet; a picture of “The Huguenots” (who have been marrying and not marrying, with great persistence, for the last fifty years); two or three Scripture texts that seem pasted up as a warning to the furniture; and a framed certificate announcing that the father has been vaccinated, or is an Odd Fellow, or has done something else heroic in a committee-room.

You examine these various attractions—listening all the while to the house’s own orchestra: the distant clack of a frying-pan lid, the cough of a fellow-lodger, the thin wail of a child practising sorrow—and then you ask, dismally, what the rent is.

“That’s rather a good deal,” you say, when she tells you.

“Well, to tell you the truth,” answers the landlady, with a sudden burst of candour that is as alarming as it is rare, “I’ve always had—” (she names a sum a good deal in excess of what she first mentioned), “and before that I used to have—” (a still higher figure).

It is a curious part of the lodging-house system that every landlady makes you feel ashamed of your own poverty by explaining that she used to get twice as much for the same rooms as you are paying. Young men lodgers of the last generation must have been of a wealthier class than they are now, or else they must all have gone bankrupt and died in handsome attitudes. What the rent of furnished apartments can have been twenty years ago makes one shudder, and instinctively tighten one’s belt.

In lodgings, the rule of life is reversed. In the world, if you rise, you go up. In lodgings, if you rise, you go down. You begin in the attic and work towards the first floor. The lodging-house ladder is a strange thing: the poor man is at the top, the rich man underneath.

Attics have a reputation. The dictionary says an attic is a “place where lumber is stored,” and the world has used them for a great deal of its lumber—by which I mean its preachers and poets, its painters and discoverers, its people who insist on seeing too much and saying it out loud. The world pushes them upstairs, out of hearing, because their voices disturb the respectable sleep below.

There is a whole roll-call of names that have known the sloping roof and the cracked pane: men who wrote with empty stomachs and full heads; men who made music while the wind came in through the window-frame; men who, being hungry, learned the exact cruelty of smell—how the scent of someone else’s dinner travels upward, becomes sharper with height, and arrives at the garret like an insult. Genius has often been fed on nothing but hope and the sound of other people eating.

If all the wisdom and art of the world were gathered and divided into heaps—this heap for the brilliant salon with its light laughter and its polished talk; that heap for the quiet study with its leather-scented shelves; this for the street; that for the field—then the heap that would tower above the rest like a mountain would be the one we should point to and say: this was forged, in misery and pain, in the sordid squalor of the city garret. From those eyries, while the world heaved below, men sent out thoughts that went farther than any landlord ever intended them to travel.

But I must not make a religion of attics. I like them—up to a point.

Not to live in. As residences they are inconvenient. There is too much getting up and down stairs for the ordinary appetites. It puts one unpleasantly in mind of a treadmill, and a treadmill is a thing that should be reserved for criminals and moralists. The ceiling slopes at angles apparently calculated to bruise the skull without killing the occupant—just enough to keep him humble. The shaving arrangements are bad: a man cannot become respectable properly if he must scrape his chin while doubled up like a hinge. And then there is the tomcat.

The tomcat has his own view of acoustic ecology. He believes, firmly, that night was invented to carry his love-songs, and that tiles were made to provide a stage. Heard from a reasonable distance, the tomcat’s serenade may be considered part of nature—like thunder, or the sea. Heard from three feet away, through a thin roof, at two in the morning, it becomes a personal affront, a modern industrial noise in whiskers, as soul-destroying as a factory whistle and a great deal less punctual.

No; for living in, give me a suite of rooms on the first floor of a Piccadilly mansion—if anyone has one handy and is anxious to make my acquaintance. But for thinking in, let me have an attic up ten flights in the densest quarter of the city. There is a sublimity in being above the human hive. One can sit and listen to the dull murmur of the tide of voices ebbing and flowing through the streets below; and the strange thing is that, though it is loud down there, it does not reach you with its full cruelty up here. Height edits noise. It becomes a texture rather than an assault. Men look small—like ants in a hurry about matters that, from above, appear less urgent than they screamed them to be.

The most extraordinary attic I ever came across was one a friend and I shared many years ago. Of all eccentrically planned things—from Bradshaw to the maze at Hampton Court—that room was the most eccentric. The architect who designed it must have been a genius, though I cannot help thinking his talents would have been better employed in contriving puzzles than in shaping human habitations.

No figure in Euclid could give any idea of that apartment. It had seven corners. Two walls sloped to a point, as if they were trying to escape. The window was just over the fireplace, so that, if you wished to look out, you had to warm your knees and singe your eyebrows at the same time. The only possible position for the bedstead was between the door and the cupboard. To get anything out of the cupboard we had to scramble over the bed, and a large percentage of the various commodities thus obtained was absorbed by the bedclothes.

Indeed, so many things were spilled and dropped upon the bed that, toward night-time, it became a sort of small cooperative store. Coal was what it always had most in stock. We kept our coal in the bottom of the cupboard; and when any was wanted we had to climb over the bed, fill a shovelful, and then crawl back. It was always an exciting moment when we reached the middle of the bed. We would hold our breath, fix our eyes upon the shovel, and poise ourselves for the last move. The next instant we, and the coals, and the shovel, and the bed would be all mixed up together.

I have heard of people going into raptures over beds of coal. We slept in one every night and were not in the least stuck up about it.

But our attic, unique though it was, had not exhausted the architect’s humour. The whole house was a marvel of originality. All the doors opened outward, so that, if anyone wanted to leave a room at the same moment you were coming downstairs, the interview was abrupt. There was no ground-floor—its ground-floor belonged to a house in the next court—and the front door opened directly upon a flight of stairs leading down to the cellar. Visitors entering would suddenly shoot past the person who had answered the door and disappear down these stairs. Those of nervous temperament imagined it a trap laid for them, and would shout “Murder!” as they lay on their backs at the bottom until somebody came and collected them.

It is a long time now since I last saw the inside of an attic. I have tried various floors since, but I have not found that they have made much difference to me. Life tastes much the same, whether you drink it out of a golden goblet or a stone mug. The hours come with the same mixture, no matter where you wait for them.

Only, perhaps, one’s palate becomes more exacting. In the attic, you can be genuinely pleased with a supper of fried fish and stout. The fish comes wrapped in newspaper; you carry it up carefully, breathing in that hot, peppery, vinegary steam through the paper—the smell of the sea translated into the dialect of a London street. In the room, you set it out as if you were laying a banquet: the batter crisping as it cools, the chips sweating into softness, the vinegar biting your nostrils with bright honesty. The stout, poured, gives off a deep, bready scent, and makes its own gentle sound as it settles—one of the few domestic noises that feels kind.

On the first floor, by contrast, it takes an elaborate dinner at the Continental to give the same satisfaction; and even then some new racket outside—a carriage, a builder, a street singer with a soul in tatters and a repertoire in rags—will thrust itself in between you and your plate, and you will find you have paid extra for being interrupted.

Perhaps that is the secret economy. As our means increase, so do our desires. We stand forever between what we have and what we have been told to want, and the language used to tell us is never our own. The lodging-house teaches you, in its own rough accent, that comfort is not merely a matter of furniture or floor, but of being able to make a place speak your dialect—by hanging up your pictures, setting on your kettle, finding the right corner for your bread, and quieting, if only for an hour, the clanking ornaments and the restless house-sounds so you can hear, beneath them, the small domestic music that belongs to you.

They say—people who have never been properly ashamed of themselves say it, and then look surprised when you blush—that the consciousness of being well dressed imparts a blissfulness to the human heart that religion is powerless to bestow. It is a coarse remark, but, like many coarse things (onions, for instance), it has a bite of truth in it.

I know that when I was a very young man—many, many years ago, when my hair still held its opinions without pomade—I used, whenever the world had been unkind, to cheer myself in the simplest way: I dressed.

If my washerwoman had discharged me (a calamity that makes a man feel as naked as a boiled potato); or if my blank-verse poem had been returned for the tenth time, with the editor’s compliments “and regrets that owing to want of space he is unable to avail himself of kind offer” (one always wonders how much space a blank verse can take up; you can fold it quite small if you try); or if the woman I loved as man never loved before had snubbed me—by the way, it is extraordinary what a variety of ways of loving there must be. We all do it as it was never done before. I do not see how our great-grandchildren will manage. They will have to do it hanging from ceilings by their toes, if they persist in not clashing with any previous method.

Well, when such things happened and I felt crushed—like a biscuit at the bottom of a hamper—I went and put on my best things. There is a comfort in good cloth that is almost edible. It smells of respectability, of pressed wool warmed by the body, of lavender from the drawer, of the faint iron tang of new buckles and the sweet, dry dust of a hat-box—odours that persuade you, as a well-made soup persuades you, that life may yet be arranged into courses and come out properly seasoned.

In a glossy new hat and a pair of trousers with a fold down the front (carefully preserved by keeping them under the bed—I do not mean on the floor, you know, but between the bed and the mattress, like a secret pie), I felt I was somebody. There were other washerwomen: ay, and even other girls to love, and who would perhaps appreciate a clever, good-looking young fellow. I did not care; that was my reckless way. I would make love to other maidens. I felt that in those clothes I could do it.

They have a wonderful deal to do with courting, clothes have. It is half the battle—at all events, the young man thinks so; and it generally takes him a couple of hours to get himself up for the occasion, as though he were a roast that required basting, turning, and anxious watching lest he scorch.

His first half-hour is occupied in trying to decide whether to wear his light suit with a cane and drab billycock, or his black tails with a chimney-pot hat and his new umbrella. He is sure to be unfortunate in either decision, because the Fates, like cheap cooks, always choose the wrong moment for their flourishes.

If he wears his light suit and takes the stick, it comes on to rain, and he reaches the house in a damp and muddy condition and spends the evening trying to hide his boots—moving his feet about under the chair as if he had brought two guilty puddings with him. If, on the other hand, he decides in favour of the top hat and umbrella—nobody would ever dream of going out in a top hat without an umbrella; it would be like letting baby (bless it!) toddle out without its nurse.

How I do hate a top hat! One lasts me a very long while, I can tell you. I only wear it when—well, never mind when I wear it. It lasts me a very long while. I have had my present one five years. It was rather old-fashioned last summer, but the shape has come round again now and I look quite stylish. That is another thing about fashion: it goes in circles like the family stew-pot. What your grandmother boiled bones in becomes, after a decent interval, your own proudest broth.

But to return to our young man and his courting. If he starts off with the top hat and umbrella the afternoon turns out fearfully hot, and the perspiration takes all the soap out of his moustache and converts the beautifully arranged curl over his forehead into a limp wisp resembling a lump of seaweed. There is a particular sound that belongs to such disasters: the squelch of a boot that was meant to remain noble; the faint, discouraging creak of the starched collar; the moist little sigh of a glove that has lost faith in itself. The street will be roaring as usual—the iron wheels grinding, the omnibuses bellowing, the hawkers flinging their cries like tin plates at your head—and amid it all you hear, most painfully, the tiny defeated noises of your own appearance coming undone.

If he does by any chance reach the door in proper condition, she has gone out with her cousin and will not be back till late. The Fates are never favourable to the poor wretch.

How a young lover, made ridiculous by the gawkiness of modern costume, must envy the picturesque gallants of seventy years ago! Look at them (on the Christmas cards), with their curly hair and natty hats, their well-shaped legs encased in smalls, their dainty Hessian boots, their ruffling frills, their canes and dangling seals. No wonder the little maiden in the big poke-bonnet and the light-blue sash casts down her eyes and is completely won. Men could win hearts in clothes like that.

But what can you expect from baggy trousers and a monkey-jacket?

Clothes have more effect upon us than we imagine. Our deportment depends upon our dress. Make a man get into seedy, worn-out rags, and he will skulk along with his head hanging down, like a man going out to fetch his own supper beer. But deck out the same article in gorgeous raiment and fine linen, and he will strut down the main thoroughfare, swinging his cane and looking at the girls as perky as a bantam cock.

Clothes alter our very nature. A man could not help being fierce and daring with a plume in his bonnet, a dagger in his belt, and a lot of puffy white things all down his sleeves. But in an ulster he wants to get behind a lamp-post and call police.

I am quite ready to admit that you can find sterling merit, honest worth, deep affection, and all such like virtues of the roast-beef-and-plum-pudding school as much, and perhaps more, under broadcloth and tweed as ever existed beneath silk and velvet; but the spirit of that knightly chivalry that “rode a tilt for lady’s love” and “fought for lady’s smiles” needs the clatter of steel and the rustle of plumes to summon it from its grave between the dusty folds of tapestry and underneath the musty leaves of mouldering chronicles. Our age has taken the lid off its romance and let it sit too long; it has turned sober.

The world must be getting old, I think; it dresses so very soberly now. We have been through the infant period of humanity, when we used to run about with nothing on but a long, loose robe, and liked to have our feet bare. And then came the rough, barbaric age, the boyhood of our race. We did not care what we wore then, but thought it nice to tattoo ourselves all over, and we never did our hair. And after that the world grew into a young man and became foppish. It decked itself in flowing curls and scarlet doublets, and went courting, and bragging, and bouncing—making a brave show.

But all those merry, foolish days of youth are gone, and we are very sober, very solemn—and very stupid, some say—now. The world is a grave, middle-aged gentleman in this nineteenth century, and would be shocked to see itself with a bit of finery on. So it dresses in black coats and trousers, and black hats, and black boots, and, dear me, it is such a very respectable gentleman—to think it could ever have gone gadding about as a troubadour or a knight-errant, dressed in all those fancy colours!

Ah, well! we are more sensible in this age. Or at least we think ourselves so. It is a general theory nowadays that sense and dullness go together. Goodness is another quality that always goes with blackness. Very good people indeed, you will notice, dress altogether in black, even to gloves and neckties, and they will probably take to black shirts before long. Medium goods indulge in light trousers on week-days, and some of them even go so far as to wear fancy waistcoats. On the other hand, people who care nothing for a future state go about in light suits; and there have been known wretches so abandoned as to wear a white hat. Such people, however, are never spoken of in genteel society, and perhaps I ought not to have referred to them here.

By the way, talking of light suits, have you ever noticed how people stare at you the first time you go out in a new light suit? They do not notice it so much afterward. The population of London have got accustomed to it by the third time you wear it. I say “you,” because I am not speaking from my own experience. I do not wear such things at all myself. As I said, only sinful people do so.

I wish, though, it were not so, and that one could be good, and respectable, and sensible without making one’s self a guy. I look in the glass sometimes at my two long, cylindrical bags (so picturesquely rugged about the knees), my stand-up collar and billycock hat, and wonder what right I have to go about making God’s world hideous. Then wild and wicked thoughts come into my heart. I do not want to be good and respectable. (I never can be sensible, I am told; so that does not matter.) I want to put on lavender-coloured tights, with red velvet breeches and a green doublet slashed with yellow; to have a light-blue silk cloak on my shoulder, and a black eagle’s plume waving from my hat, and a big sword, and a falcon, and a lance, and a prancing horse, so that I might go about and gladden the eyes of the people.

Why should we all try to look like ants crawling over a dust-heap? Why should we not dress a little gayly? I am sure if we did we should be happier. True, it is a little thing, but we are a little race, and what is the use of our pretending otherwise and spoiling fun? Let philosophers get themselves up like old crows if they like. But let me be a butterfly.

Women, at all events, ought to dress prettily. It is their duty. They are the flowers of the earth and were meant to show it up. We abuse them a good deal, we men; but goodness knows, the old world would be dull enough without their dresses and fair faces. How they brighten up every place they come into! What a sunny commotion they—relations, of course—make in our dingy bachelor chambers! and what a delightful litter their ribbons and laces, and gloves and hats, and parasols and ’kerchiefs make! It is as if a wandering rainbow had dropped in to pay us a visit.

It is one of the chief charms of the summer, to my mind, the way our little maids come out in pretty colours. I like to see the pink and blue and white glancing between the trees, dotting the green fields, and flashing back the sunlight. You can see the bright colours such a long way off. There are four white dresses climbing a hill in front of my window now. I can see them distinctly, though it is three miles away. I thought at first they were mile-stones out for a lark. It is so nice to be able to see the darlings a long way off. Especially if they happen to be your wife and your mother-in-law.

Talking of fields and mile-stones reminds me that I want to say, in all seriousness, a few words about women’s boots.

The women of these islands all wear boots too big for them. They can never get a boot to fit. The bootmakers do not keep sizes small enough. Over and over again have I known women sit down on the top rail of a stile and declare they could not go a step further because their boots hurt them so; and it has always been the same complaint—too big. It is time this state of things was altered. In the name of the husbands and fathers of England, I call upon the bootmakers to reform. Our wives, our daughters, and our cousins are not to be lamed and tortured with impunity. Why cannot “narrow twos” be kept more in stock? That is the size I find most women take.

The waist-band is another item of feminine apparel that is always too big. The dressmakers make these things so loose that the hooks and eyes by which they are fastened burst off, every now and then, with a report like thunder. There is a whole domestic acoustics to dress: the hiss of the iron, the swish of cloth, the crisp tear of tissue paper, and then that sudden explosive snap from a bodice—loud enough to startle the cat and make the household believe, for one splendid moment, that something important has happened.

Why women suffer these wrongs—why they do not insist on having their clothes made small enough for them—I cannot conceive. It can hardly be that they are disinclined to trouble themselves about matters of mere dress, for dress is the one subject that they really do think about. It is the only topic they ever get thoroughly interested in, and they talk about it all day long. If you see two women together, you may bet your bottom dollar they are discussing their own or their friends’ clothes.

You notice a couple of child-like beings conversing by a window, and you wonder what sweet, helpful words are falling from their sainted lips. So you move nearer and then you hear one say: “So I took in the waist-band and let out a seam, and it fits beautifully now.”

“Well,” says the other, “I shall wear my plum-coloured body to the Jones’, with a yellow plastron; and they’ve got some lovely gloves at Puttick’s, only one and eleven pence.”

I went for a drive through a part of Derbyshire once with a couple of ladies. It was a beautiful bit of country, and they enjoyed themselves immensely. They talked dressmaking the whole time.

“Pretty view, that,” I would say, waving my umbrella round. “Look at those blue distant hills! That little white speck, nestling in the woods, is Chatsworth, and over there—”

“Yes, very pretty indeed,” one would reply. “Well, why not get a yard of sarsenet?”

“What, and leave the skirt exactly as it is?”

“Certainly. What place d’ye call this?”

Then I would draw their attention to the fresh beauties that kept sweeping into view, and they would glance round and say “charming,” “sweetly pretty,” and immediately go off into raptures over each other’s pocket-handkerchiefs, and mourn with one another over the decadence of cambric frilling.

I believe if two women were cast together upon a desert island, they would spend each day arguing the respective merits of sea-shells and birds’ eggs considered as trimmings, and would have a new fashion in fig-leaves every month.

Very young men think a good deal about clothes, but they do not talk about them to each other. They would not find much encouragement. A fop is not a favourite with his own sex. Indeed, he gets a good deal more abuse from them than is necessary. His is a harmless failing and it soon wears out. Besides, a man who has no foppery at twenty will be a slatternly, dirty-collar, unbrushed-coat man at forty. A little foppishness in a young man is good; it is human. I like to see a young cock ruffle his feathers, stretch his neck, and crow as if the whole world belonged to him.

I do not like a modest, retiring man. Nobody does—not really, however much they may prate about modest worth and other things they do not understand. A meek deportment is a great mistake in the world. Uriah Heep’s father was a very poor judge of human nature, or he would not have told his son, as he did, that people liked humbleness. There is nothing annoys them more, as a rule. Rows are half the fun of life, and you cannot have rows with humble, meek-answering individuals. They turn away our wrath, and that is just what we do not want. We want to let it out. We have worked ourselves up into a state of exhilarating fury, and then, just as we are anticipating the enjoyment of a vigorous set-to, they spoil all our plans with their exasperating humility.

Xantippe’s life must have been one long misery, tied to that calmly irritating man, Socrates. Fancy a married woman doomed to live on from day to day without one single quarrel with her husband! A man ought to humour his wife in these things. Heaven knows their lives are dull enough, poor girls. They have none of the enjoyments we have. They go to no political meetings; they may not even belong to the local amateur parliament; they are excluded from smoking-carriages on the Metropolitan Railway, and they never see a comic paper—or if they do, they do not know it is comic: nobody tells them.

Surely, with existence such a dreary blank for them as this, we might provide a little row for their amusement now and then, even if we do not feel inclined for it ourselves. A really sensible man does so and is loved accordingly, for it is little acts of kindness such as this that go straight to a woman’s heart. It is such like proofs of loving self-sacrifice that make her tell her female friends what a good husband he was—after he is dead.

Yes, poor Xantippe must have had a hard time of it. The bucket episode was particularly sad for her. Poor woman! she did think she would rouse him up a bit with that. She had taken the trouble to fill the bucket, perhaps been a long way to get specially dirty water. And she waited for him. And then to be met in such a way, after all! Most likely she sat down and had a good cry afterward. It must have seemed all so hopeless to the poor child; and for all we know she had no mother to whom she could go and abuse him. What was it to her that her husband was a great philosopher? Great philosophy does not count in married life.

There was a very good little boy once who wanted to go to sea. And the captain asked him what he could do. He said he could do the multiplication-table backward and paste sea-weed in a book; that he knew how many times the word “begat” occurred in the Old Testament; and could recite “The Boy Stood on the Burning Deck” and Wordsworth’s “We Are Seven.”

“Werry good—werry good, indeed,” said the man of the sea, “and ken ye kerry coals?”

It is just the same when you want to marry. Great ability is not required so much as little usefulness. Brains are at a discount in the married state. There is no demand for them, no appreciation even. Our wives sum us up according to a standard of their own, in which brilliancy of intellect obtains no marks.

Your lady and mistress is not at all impressed by your cleverness and talent, my dear reader—not in the slightest. Give her a man who can do an errand neatly, without attempting to use his own judgment over it or any nonsense of that kind; and who can be trusted to hold a child the right way up, and not make himself objectionable whenever there is lukewarm mutton for dinner. That is the sort of husband a sensible woman likes; not one of your scientific or literary nuisances, who go upsetting the whole house and putting everybody out with their foolishness.

“I remember, I remember,
in the days of chill November,
how the blackbird on the—”

And there my memory, like a cook who has tasted the sauce too often and can no longer tell whether it needs salt or merely confidence, lays down the spoon and wanders off.

It was the first real piece of poetry I ever learned. I do not count “Hey diddle diddle, the cat and the fiddle,” because that is not poetry: that is a nursery cupboard where everything has been put on the wrong shelf and then called imagination. Whereas “I remember, I remember” was literature—cold, proper, and capable of being recited to grown people in exchange for coin.

I earned fourpence by it. I know it was fourpence because some one took pains to explain to me, in the tone in which one speaks of the Ten Commandments, that if I kept it until I had twopence more I should have sixpence. The reasoning was flawless, as arithmetic always is when it ruins a child’s plans; but it did not move me. The fourpence was squandered the very next morning—on what, I cannot recollect. That is Memory all over: she will preserve the receipt and mislay the groceries.

I can distinctly remember, too, falling into a huge dust-hole when I was very small; but I have not the faintest remembrance of ever climbing out again. If memory were the only witness, I should be forced to believe I am there still, pickled in soot, a relic of the coal age. At another time—some years later—I was assisting at an exceedingly interesting love scene, as one does at that age with a devotion that ought to have been saved for religion or pastry. But the only portion I can call to mind is that at the most critical moment somebody opened the door and said, “Emily, you’re wanted,” in a sepulchral tone that suggested the police, the undertaker, and the family doctor had formed a committee. All the tender words she said to me and all the beautiful things I said to her are utterly forgotten. I can remember the door-handle, though, and the way it clicked, as if Time had a latchkey and used it to come in and spoil everything.

So life, when one turns to look behind, is like an old kitchen after the household has moved out: a broken chair where a feast used to be, a scorched patch on the hearth where something wonderful once bubbled, the shaft of a window where the afternoon light fell on a table spread for people who are gone. The smell of it all—smoke and lavender and yesterday—clings. It is ruin, and yet it is softened; ivy grows up the cracks; the place looks picturesque when you are not the one who had to scrub it.

Everything looms pleasant through the haze of time. Even past sadness turns sweet, as cold tea turns palatable if you add enough sugar and pretend it was meant. Boyhood, viewed from a distance, seems all nuts, hoops, and gingerbread—gingerbread, especially, with its brave spicy honesty, the sort you could smell through paper, and which made even poverty feel companionable. The snubbings and toothaches and Latin verbs are forgotten—the Latin verbs particularly, because Providence is merciful.

We imagine we were very happy when we were hobbledehoys and loved, and we sigh that we cannot love again. We never dwell on the heartaches, the sleepless nights, the dry burning throat when she said she could never be anything to us but a sister—as if any man ever asked the universe for more sisters. We see the sunshine of the past; we do not see the shadows. The road behind looks smooth; the sharp stones have been ground down by recollection into something almost romantic. We remember the roses at the hedgerow and forget the briers, until the briers, at a safe distance, become “gentle tendrils waving in the wind.” It is well arranged. If Memory served the whole dish as it was cooked—grit, burnt bits, and all—we should push the plate away and decline to live.

God be thanked that it is so, that the ever-lengthening chain of recollection is mostly made of pleasant links, and that the bitterness of to-day will be smiled at to-morrow—provided one survives it. It seems as though the lightest, brightest parts of our days float longest, like bits of cork on a dark river, while the angry thoughts and smarting pains sink, and the stream goes on without them. One may call it illusion, but it is a benevolent one; and if the world insists upon giving us some sour dishes, it is kind to send a palate that forgets the bitterness first.

It is this glamour, I fancy, that makes old folk talk such astonishing nonsense about the days when they were young. The world was a superior place then, they say. Things were more like what they ought to be. Boys were boys; girls were “different.” Winters were winters, with honest frost that bit your fingers and rang like iron in the air; not these sham mockeries that drizzle and call themselves seasons. Summers were summers, not the miserable compromises we now accept as if climate were being manufactured by contract.

And the deeds they performed! The extraordinary events that occurred! It takes three strong men to believe half of them, and the other half needs a pulleys-and-ropes apparatus. I delight to hear one of the ancient heroes recounting his boyhood to a circle of youngsters who cannot contradict him. It is odds he does not end by swearing the moon shone every night in his time, and that tossing mad bulls in a blanket was the favourite sport at school, followed by lion-hunting as a mild recreation for the timid.

It has always been so and always will be. Our grandfathers heard the same lament from their elders, and the youngsters of to-day will chant precisely similar nonsense to plague the next generation. “Give me back the good old days of fifty years ago” has been the cry ever since Adam’s fifty-first birthday, when he first sat down with a sigh and said, “Things were different when I was a lad.” Read the literature of any year you like and you will find poets pining for a past that never existed except as a polished story. The world, by all accounts, has been getting worse and worse since it opened; which is curious, because it is very pleasant even now if you keep as much as possible in the sunshine and take the rain good-temperedly.

Yet one cannot deny there must have been something very sweet in that dewy morning of creation, before the grass was trodden to dust by tramping millions and before the din of cities chased silence away. There must have been a sound to the world then—water talking to stones, wind combing the reeds, birds practising their phrases without a factory whistle cutting across the tune. Life must have been noble and solemn to those loose-robed fathers of the race, walking, as the tales say, hand in hand with God beneath the great sky. They lived in sunkissed tents among lowing herds; they took their wants from Nature’s open pantry. Their days had a rhythm, not the jangling, hammering racket we now call progress. Even their cooking, I imagine, had time to be real: bread that rose at its own pace, milk warm from the animal, herbs that smelt of the very patch of earth they had been pulled from, and the crackle of a wood fire that spoke comfort in a language no engine can translate.

Those days are gone. Humanity’s quiet childhood—spent in forest glades, by murmuring rivers—is past for ever; and life is deepening down to manhood amid tumult, doubt, and hope. The age of restful peace has ended. We have work to finish and must hasten on, though we do not know what the work is. Like tiny coral insects toiling deep under dark waters, we strive and struggle each for our own small ends and never dream of the vast structure rising from our little labours.

So let us have done with vain regrets and longings. Our business lies in front, not behind. “Forward!” is the motto. Let us not sit with folded hands, staring at the past as if it were the whole house; it is only the foundation. Let us not waste heart and life mourning what might have been and forgetting the may-be that waits. Opportunities go by while we sit seasoning our sorrow. Happiness comes and we do not taste it, because we are still thinking of the meal that is gone.

Years ago, when I used to wander of an evening from the fireside into that pleasant country where fairy-tales grow, I met a doughty knight and true. He had overcome many dangers, travelled in many lands, and all men knew him for brave and well-tried—except, perhaps, upon those occasions when even a brave man feels afraid, and is not ashamed for it.

Now this knight, riding wearily along a toilsome road, began to misdoubt his heart because of the trouble of the way. Rocks, dark and monstrous, hung above his head, and it seemed likely they would fall and make an end of him. On either side were chasms, and caves where fierce robbers lived, and dragons so terrible that even their breath sounded like a butcher at work. Over the road hung a darkness as of night, the sort of darkness that has its own sound: the thud of your horse’s hoofs too loud, the little clink of harness too sharp, the wind making a thin music that seems to warn rather than soothe.

So it came over this good knight that he would press no farther, but turn back and seek some easier way for his gentle steed. Yet when he wheeled and looked behind, he marvelled, for lo! of all the road he had ridden there was naught to see; and at his horse’s heels yawned a mighty gulf, so deep no man might spy the bottom. Then Sir Ghelent, seeing there was no going back, prayed to good Saint Cuthbert, set spurs to his steed, and rode forward—bravely, and, after the first few terrified moments, most joyously. And naught harmed him.

There is no returning on the road of life. The frail bridge of time sinks into eternity at every step we take. The past is gone. It is gathered in and garnished like fruit put by for winter; but the jars are sealed, and you cannot open them without breaking something. No word can be unspoken. No step retraced. Therefore it becomes us, as true knights, to go forward bravely, not to sit and weep because the road behind has fallen.

A friend came to me the other day and urged me, very eloquently, to learn some wonderful system by which you never forgot anything. I do not know why he was so eager, unless it be because I sometimes borrow an umbrella and possess a knack, in the middle of a game of whist, of remarking mildly, “Lor! I’ve been thinking all along that clubs were trumps.” He described his method as though it were a new gospel and I a heathen; but I declined. I have no wish to remember everything.

There are many things in most men’s lives that had better be forgotten. There is that time, years ago, when we did not act quite as honourably, quite as uprightly, as we should—the unfortunate deviation from strict probity which, more unfortunate still, was discovered. The foolish act. The mean act. The wrong act. Ah well! we paid for it. We suffered the maddening hours of remorse, the hot shame, the scorn perhaps of those we loved. Let us forget. Father Time, with your kindly hands, lift those bitter memories from our overburdened hearts. Griefs come to us with the coming hours, and our strength is only as the day.

Not that the past should be buried altogether. The music of life would be mute if the chords of memory were snapped. It is only the poisonous weeds, not the flowers, that should be rooted out of Mnemosyne’s garden. We want the scent of the old herbs, even if the patch is overgrown; we want the crackle of the old fire, even if the grate is cold. Do you remember Dickens’ “Haunted Man”—how he prayed for forgetfulness, and, when his prayer was granted, prayed for memory once more? We do not want all the ghosts laid. It is only the cruel-eyed spectres we flee from. Let the gentle phantoms haunt us as they will; we are not afraid of them.

Ah me, the world grows very full of ghosts as we grow older. We need not seek dismal churchyards or sleep in moated granges to hear the rustle of garments in the night. Every house, every room, every creaking chair has its own particular ghost. The very sounds of a place keep the past alive: the way a stair groans on the third step, the little cough a kettle makes before it boils, the hush after a door shuts that seems to hold a name in it. They haunt the empty chambers of our lives; they throng around us like dead leaves whirled in an autumn wind. Some are living, some are dead; we do not know. We clasped their hands once, quarrelled with them, laughed with them, shared bread and talk and hopes, until it seemed our hearts had fastened together in a grip that even Death could not loosen. They are gone now; their eyes will never look into ours again; their voices we shall never hear—except as memory throws them back at us, like an echo from a valley that no longer exists.

Ghosts are with us night and day. They walk beside us in the busy street under the harsh glare that modern cities fling everywhere, and the clatter of wheels and iron and shouting seems only to make the silence inside us more distinct. They sit by us at home in the twilight. We see their little faces looking from the windows of the old schoolhouse. We meet them in the woods and lanes where we played as boys. Hark—cannot you hear their low laughter from behind the blackberry bushes, their distant whoops along the grassy glades, rising and falling like a tune you once knew in your native dialect and now can only hum?

Down here, through the quiet fields and by the wood, where evening shadows lurk, winds the path where we used to watch for her at sunset. Look—she is there now in the dainty white frock we knew so well, with the big bonnet dangling from her little hands and the sunny brown hair all tangled. Five thousand miles away! Dead for all we know! What of that? She is beside us now, and we can look into her laughing eyes and hear her voice. She will vanish at the stile by the wood and we shall be alone; and the night wind will sweep past, moaning like an old song sung in a tongue the young no longer understand.

Ghosts! The world would be sadder still without you. Come, speak to us, you ghosts of old loves—ghosts of playmates and sweethearts and old friends; for the world is lonely, and new faces are not like the old, and we cannot love them—nor laugh with them—as we loved and laughed with you. When we walked together, the world was bright; now it has grown old, and we are growing weary, and only you bring back the freshness.

Memory is a rare ghost-raiser. Like a haunted house, its walls echo to unseen feet. Through the broken casements we watch the flitting shadows, and saddest of all are the shadows of our own dead selves—those young bright faces, so full of truth and honour and noble longings. How reproachfully they look upon us with their deep, clear eyes! I fear they have reason, poor lads. Lies and cunning and disbelief have crept into our hearts since those preshaving days—and we meant to be so great and good.

It is well we cannot see into the future. There are few boys of fourteen who would not feel ashamed of themselves at forty.

I like, sometimes, to sit and talk with that odd little chap who was myself long ago. I think he likes it too, for he comes often of an evening when I am alone with my pipe, listening to the whispering of the flames—the little snap and sigh of coal settling, the soft roar that seems to speak in an old, homely dialect no schoolmaster ever taught. I see his solemn little face peering at me through the scented smoke as it floats upward. I smile at him and he smiles back, but his is a grave, old-fashioned smile, as though he had been born elderly.

We chat of old times; and now and then he takes me by the hand, and then we slip—very comfortably—through the black bars of the grate and down the dusky glowing caves to the land that lies behind the firelight. There we find the days that used to be, set out as plainly as jars on a pantry shelf, each with its own smell: tar and apples, wet boots, ink, gingerbread, the sharp clean scent of snow. We wander along them together. He tells me, as we walk, all he thinks and feels. I laugh at him now and then, but the next moment I wish I had not, for he looks so grave I am ashamed of being frivolous. Besides, it is not proper to be disrespectful to one so much older than myself—to one who was myself so long before I became myself.

At first we do not talk much, but look at one another. I look down at his curly hair and little blue bow; he looks up sideways at me as he trots along. And somehow I fancy those shy round eyes do not altogether approve of me. He heaves a little sigh, as though disappointed, as if he had expected me to turn out more like a hero in a story and less like a man who loses umbrellas.

After a while his bashfulness wears off and he begins to chat. He tells me his favourite fairy-tales; he can do up to six times; he has a guinea-pig; and Pa says fairy-tales aren’t true, and isn’t it a pity, because he would so like to be a knight and fight a dragon and marry a beautiful princess. But at seven he takes a more practical view and would rather be a bargee and earn a lot of money. Possibly this is because he is in love about this time with the young lady at the milk-shop at six. God bless her little ever-dancing feet, whatever size they may be now. He must be very fond of her, for one day he gives her his chief treasure: a huge pocket-knife with four rusty blades and a corkscrew, which latter has a habit of working itself out by mysterious means and sticking into its owner’s leg. She is an affectionate little thing and throws her arms round his neck and kisses him for it then and there outside the shop, where the air smells of fresh milk and wet pavement, and the sound of the milk pail’s lid is like a bell announcing that life is serious.

But the stupid world—in the person of the boy at the cigar emporium next door—jeers at such tokens. Whereupon my young friend, very properly, prepares to punch the head of the boy at the cigar emporium next door; but fails, the boy at the cigar emporium next door punching his instead. Thus does reality begin its lessons.

Then comes school life, with its bitter little sorrows and its joyous shoutings; its jolly larks; its hot tears dropping on beastly Latin grammars and silly old copybooks. It is at school, I believe, that he injures himself for life trying to pronounce German; and it is there also that he learns how much importance the French nation attaches to pens, ink, and paper. “Have you pens, ink, and paper?” is the first question one Frenchman asks another. The other fellow has not any, as a rule, but says the uncle of his brother has them all three. The first fellow does not care a hang about the uncle of the other fellow’s brother. What he wants now is: has the neighbour of the other fellow’s mother got them? “The neighbour of my mother has no pens, no ink, and no paper,” replies the other man, beginning to get wild. “Has the child of thy female gardener some pens, some ink, or some paper?” He has him there. After worrying enough about those wretched inks and papers to make everybody miserable, it turns out the child of his own female gardener has none. Such a discovery would shut up any one but a French exercise-man. It has no effect on that shameless creature. He never apologizes. He says his aunt has some mustard. I have often suspected the French language survives chiefly through the stubbornness of mustard.

So, in the acquisition of more or less useless knowledge—soon, happily, to be forgotten—boyhood passes away. The red-brick schoolhouse fades from view and we turn down into the world’s high-road.

My little friend is not little now. The short jacket has sprouted tails. The battered cap—so useful as pocket-handkerchief, drinking-cup, and weapon—has grown high and glossy. Instead of a slate-pencil in his mouth there is a cigarette, the smoke of which troubles him because it insists on climbing up his nose. He tries a cigar later as being more stylish—a big black Havanna—and it does not agree with him, for I find him afterwards sitting over a bucket in the back kitchen, solemnly swearing never to smoke again. It is an impressive vow, made between heaves, and kept until the next fashionable opportunity.

Now his moustache begins to be almost visible to the naked eye, whereupon he immediately takes to brandy-and-sodas and fancies himself a man. He talks about “two to one against the favourite,” refers to actresses as “Little Emmy” and “Kate” and “Baby,” and murmurs about his “losses at cards the other night” in a manner suggesting thousands, though, to do him justice, the amount is probably one-and-twopence. Also—if I see aright, for it is always twilight in this land of memories—he sticks an eyeglass in his eye and stumbles over everything, including common sense.

His female relations, much troubled, pray for him—bless their gentle hearts—and see visions of Old Bailey trials and halters as the only possible outcome of such dissipation; and his first schoolmaster’s prophecy that he would come to a bad end swells into inspired revelation. At this age he has a lordly contempt for the other sex, a blatantly good opinion of himself, and a sociably patronizing manner towards elderly male friends of the family. Altogether he is, it must be admitted, something of a nuisance.

It does not last long. He falls in love, and that takes the bounce out of him as neatly as a pin in a bladder. I notice his boots are much too small now and his hair is fearfully and wonderfully arranged. He reads poetry more than he used and keeps a rhyming dictionary in his bedroom, as though love were a kind of cookery and he needed a book to tell him what goes with “eyes.” Every morning Emily Jane finds scraps of torn-up paper on the floor and reads thereon of “cruel hearts and love’s deep darts,” of “beauteous eyes and lovers’ sighs,” and much more of the old, old song that lads love to sing and lassies love to listen to while tossing dainty heads and pretending never to hear.

But the course of love does not appear to run smoothly, for later he takes more walking exercise and less sleep than is good for him, poor boy, and his face suggests anything rather than wedding-bells. The air around him—if one listens—has changed: fewer laughs, more long silences with the sound of steps going nowhere, the night full of clocks and distant carts and that particular hush that follows disappointment.

And here he vanishes.

The little boyish self that has grown up beside me as we walked is gone. I am alone, and the road is very dark. I stumble on—I know not how, nor care—for the way seems leading nowhere, and there is no light to guide. But at last morning comes, and I find that I have grown into myself.
