Edward carried his gratitude to Colonel Brandon as faithfully as if it were a sealed dish he dared not spill, and by the time he reached Bartlett’s Buildings his happiness had risen and aerated like batter left in a warm kitchen. Lucy received it greedily, as she received every advantage, and when Mrs. Jennings came the next day with congratulations, Lucy could say—eyes bright, voice honeyed—that she had never seen him in such spirits in her life.

Her own spirits, at least, were beyond dispute. She spoke of Delaford Parsonage as if it were already aired and swept for them, as if before Michaelmas she should be directing the servants, tasting the cream, counting the poultry, and taking the carriage as naturally as she took breath. And though Edward, in his honest fashion, insisted that Elinor must have her due for what she had done, Lucy was in no danger of being backward where gratitude cost her nothing and promised to be heard. She talked of her friendship for Miss Dashwood with a warmth ready at command, declared herself under obligation to them both, and vowed that no exertion, present or future, on Elinor’s part would ever surprise her—because she believed her capable of anything in the world for those she really valued.

Colonel Brandon, too, was raised by Lucy’s tongue to sainthood, and not merely in sentiment. She was anxious—piously anxious—that he should be treated as a saint in all worldly concerns; that the tithes should be pushed to the utmost; and in the privacy of her own mind she had already measured how far Delaford might yield up its servants, its cows, its poultry, its convenience, into her careful hands. The future, to her, was a larder already inventoried.

It was now more than a week since John Dashwood had called in Berkeley Street; and as, since that time, no notice had been taken by them of his wife’s indisposition beyond one verbal enquiry, Elinor began to feel it necessary to pay a visit. It was a duty that went against her inclination in every particular, and it had none of that softening encouragement which friendly companions sometimes lend to a disagreeable errand.

Marianne was not content with refusing to go herself; she pressed her refusal on Elinor, urgent that she should not go at all. Mrs. Jennings, though her carriage stood always at Elinor’s service, so thoroughly disliked Mrs. John Dashwood that even her curiosity—sharp as a knife when it came to family commotions—could not persuade her to endure Fanny’s company again. She wanted to see how Mrs. Dashwood looked after the late discovery; she wanted to affront her by taking Edward’s part; yet her dislike prevailed.

So Elinor set out alone—alone to a visit no one could have less pleasure in making, alone to the hazard of a tête-à-tête with a woman whom neither Marianne nor Mrs. Jennings had half so much reason to dislike.

Mrs. Dashwood was denied. Elinor had scarcely time to let disappointment settle, like tea-leaves at the bottom of a cup, before the carriage could turn from the house—when her brother accidentally came out. John Dashwood’s face wore that ready satisfaction which required so little cause.

He expressed great pleasure in meeting her; said he had been just going to call in Berkeley Street; and, assuring her that Fanny would be very glad to see her, invited her to come in. They went up stairs into the drawing-room. Nobody was there.

“Fanny is in her own room, I suppose,” said he. “I will go to her presently, for I am sure she will not have the least objection in the world to seeing you. Very far from it, indeed. Now especially there cannot be—but however, you and Marianne were always great favourites. Why would not Marianne come?”

Elinor made what excuse she could.

“I am not sorry to see you alone,” he replied, with a kind of importance that began to thicken his words. “For I have a good deal to say to you. This living of Colonel Brandon’s—can it be true? Has he really given it to Edward? I heard it yesterday by chance, and was coming to you on purpose to enquire farther about it.”

“It is perfectly true. Colonel Brandon has given the living of Delaford to Edward.”

“Really! Well, this is very astonishing! No relationship! No connection between them!—and now that livings fetch such a price! What was the value of this?”

“About two hundred a year.”

“Very well—and for the next presentation to a living of that value—supposing the late incumbent to have been old and sickly, and likely to vacate it soon—he might have got, I dare say, fourteen hundred pounds. And how came he not to have settled that matter before this person’s death? Now, indeed, it would be too late to sell it, but a man of Colonel Brandon’s sense! I wonder he should be so improvident in a point of such common, such natural, concern!”

He shook his head with the air of a man tasting a sauce and finding it foolishly wasted.

“Well, I am convinced that there is a vast deal of inconsistency in almost every human character. I suppose, however—on recollection—that the case may probably be this. Edward is only to hold the living till the person to whom the Colonel has really sold the presentation is old enough to take it. Aye, aye, that is the fact, depend upon it.”

Elinor contradicted him very positively; and by relating that she had herself been employed in conveying the offer from Colonel Brandon to Edward, and must therefore understand the terms on which it was given, obliged him at last to submit to her authority.

“It is truly astonishing!” he cried, after hearing what she said. “What could be the Colonel’s motive?”

“A very simple one—to be of use to Mr. Ferrars.”

“Well, well; whatever Colonel Brandon may be, Edward is a very lucky man. You will not mention the matter to Fanny, however, for though I have broke it to her, and she bears it vastly well,—she will not like to hear it much talked of.”

Elinor, for an instant, felt the sharp temptation to observe that Fanny might have borne with composure an acquisition to her brother by which neither she nor her child could possibly be impoverished. She resisted it.

“Mrs. Ferrars,” added he, lowering his voice to the tone becoming so important a subject, “knows nothing about it at present, and I believe it will be best to keep it entirely concealed from her as long as may be. When the marriage takes place, I fear she must hear of it all.”

“But why should such precaution be used?” Elinor asked. “Though it is not to be supposed that Mrs. Ferrars can have the smallest satisfaction in knowing that her son has money enough to live upon—for that must be quite out of the question—yet why, upon her late behaviour, is she supposed to feel at all? She has done with her son,—she cast him off for ever, and has made all those over whom she had any influence cast him off likewise. Surely, after doing so, she cannot be imagined liable to any impression of sorrow or of joy on his account. She cannot be interested in any thing that befalls him. She would not be so weak as to throw away the comfort of a child, and yet retain the anxiety of a parent!”

“Ah! Elinor,” said John, as if he were explaining a recipe she had never learned, “your reasoning is very good, but it is founded on ignorance of human nature. When Edward’s unhappy match takes place, depend upon it his mother will feel as much as if she had never discarded him; and therefore every circumstance that may accelerate that dreadful event must be concealed from her as much as possible. Mrs. Ferrars can never forget that Edward is her son.”

“You surprise me; I should think it must nearly have escaped her memory by this time.”

“You wrong her exceedingly. Mrs. Ferrars is one of the most affectionate mothers in the world.”

Elinor was silent. The room—handsomely furnished, securely cushioned against the street—seemed to swallow sound, so that even John’s earnestness landed without echo; and in that muffled quiet she felt, more strongly than ever, how affection could be spoken in the dominant family dialect until the word meant its opposite.

After a short pause, he resumed, with a new, weighty satisfaction. “We think now of Robert’s marrying Miss Morton.”

Elinor smiled at the grave decisiveness of his tone, and answered calmly, “The lady, I suppose, has no choice in the affair.”

“Choice!—how do you mean?”

“I only mean that I suppose, from your manner of speaking, it must be the same to Miss Morton whether she marry Edward or Robert.”

“Certainly, there can be no difference; for Robert will now, to all intents and purposes, be considered as the eldest son;—and as to any thing else, they are both very agreeable young men. I do not know that one is superior to the other.”

Elinor said no more. John, too, fell silent, collecting his reflections until they arranged themselves into a confidence he evidently believed would nourish her.

“Of one thing, my dear sister,” said he, kindly taking her hand and speaking in an awful whisper, “I may assure you;—and I will do it, because I know it must gratify you. I have good reason to think—indeed I have it from the best authority, or I should not repeat it, for otherwise it would be very wrong to say any thing about it,—but I have it from the very best authority,—not that I ever precisely heard Mrs. Ferrars say it herself—but her daughter did, and I have it from her,—that in short, whatever objections there might be against a certain—a certain connection, you understand me,—it would have been far preferable to her,—it would not have given her half the vexation that this does.”

He paused as if waiting for Elinor’s gratitude to rise like steam.

“I was exceedingly pleased to hear that Mrs. Ferrars considered it in that light; a very gratifying circumstance you know to us all. ‘It would have been beyond comparison,’ she said, ‘the least evil of the two, and she would be glad to compound now for nothing worse.’ But however, all that is quite out of the question,—not to be thought of or mentioned—as to any attachment you know, it never could be: all that is gone by. But I thought I would just tell you of this, because I knew how much it must please you. Not that you have any reason to regret, my dear Elinor. There is no doubt of your doing exceedingly well,—quite as well, or better, perhaps, all things considered. Has Colonel Brandon been with you lately?”

Elinor had heard enough—if not to gratify vanity, at least to agitate her nerves and fill her mind with that peculiar fatigue which comes of listening to feelings translated into the wrong language and then offered back as kindness. She was therefore glad to be spared the necessity of much reply, and glad to be saved from hearing more, by the entrance of Mr. Robert Ferrars.

After a few moments’ chat, John Dashwood—recollecting that Fanny was yet uninformed of her sister’s being there—quitted the room in quest of her. Elinor was left to improve her acquaintance with Robert.

He came in with that easy, shining unconcern which belongs to a man who hears only the pleasing parts of the world’s noise: the soft click of approval, the rustle of banknotes, the flattering hum of a mother’s partiality. In him, the unfair division of Mrs. Ferrars’s love and liberality—so cruel to Edward, so indulgent to himself—produced no shame to roughen the surface of his spirits. His very comfort confirmed Elinor’s most unfavourable opinion of his head and heart.

They had scarcely been two minutes alone before he began to speak of Edward; for he too had heard of the living, and was inquisitive on the subject. Elinor repeated the particulars as she had given them to John. Their effect on Robert, though very different, was not less striking.

He laughed most immoderately. The idea of Edward as a clergyman, lodged in a small parsonage-house, diverted him beyond measure; and when he added, with a relish for images that cost him no compassion, the fancy of Edward reading prayers in a white surplice, and publishing the banns of marriage between John Smith and Mary Brown, he could conceive nothing more ridiculous.

Elinor waited in silence, gravity fixed and unyielding, until such folly should exhaust itself. Her eyes, in spite of herself, rested on him with a look that spoke all the contempt she felt. It was a look well bestowed: it eased her own feelings, and communicated nothing to him.

He was recalled from wit to wisdom, not by any reproof of hers, but by his own sensibility to the effect he wished to produce.

“We may treat it as a joke,” said he at last, recovering from the affected laugh which had prolonged the genuine gaiety, “but, upon my soul, it is a most serious business. Poor Edward! he is ruined for ever. I am extremely sorry for it; for I know him to be a very good-hearted creature; as well-meaning a fellow perhaps, as any in the world. You must not judge of him, Miss Dashwood, from your slight acquaintance. Poor Edward! His manners are certainly not the happiest in nature. But we are not all born, you know, with the same powers,—the same address. Poor fellow! to see him in a circle of strangers! To be sure it was pitiable enough; but upon my soul, I believe he has as good a heart as any in the kingdom.”

He warmed to his own performance.

“And I declare and protest to you I never was so shocked in my life, as when it all burst forth. I could not believe it. My mother was the first person who told me of it; and I, feeling myself called on to act with resolution, immediately said to her, ‘My dear madam, I do not know what you may intend to do on the occasion, but as for myself, I must say, that if Edward does marry this young woman, I never will see him again.’ That was what I said immediately. I was most uncommonly shocked, indeed! Poor Edward! he has done for himself completely,—shut himself out for ever from all decent society! But, as I directly said to my mother, I am not in the least surprised at it; from his style of education, it was always to be expected. My poor mother was half frantic.”

“Have you ever seen the lady?” Elinor asked.

“Yes; once, while she was staying in this house, I happened to drop in for ten minutes; and I saw quite enough of her. The merest awkward country girl, without style, or elegance, and almost without beauty. I remember her perfectly. Just the kind of girl I should suppose likely to captivate poor Edward.”

He spoke with the complacent authority of a man who believes his own taste a standard measure.

“I offered immediately, as soon as my mother related the affair to me, to talk to him myself, and dissuade him from the match; but it was too late then, I found, to do any thing, for unluckily, I was not in the way at first, and knew nothing of it till after the breach had taken place, when it was not for me, you know, to interfere. But had I been informed of it a few hours earlier, I think it is most probable that something might have been hit on. I certainly should have represented it to Edward in a very strong light. ‘My dear fellow,’ I should have said, ‘consider what you are doing. You are making a most disgraceful connection, and such a one as your family are unanimous in disapproving.’ I cannot help thinking, in short, that means might have been found. But now it is all too late. He must be starved, you know, that is certain; absolutely starved.”

He had just settled Edward’s fate with serene finality when the door opened and Mrs. John Dashwood entered, putting an end to the subject as if she had shut a lid upon a saucepan boiling over.

Though she never spoke of it out of her own family, Elinor could see its influence in the something like confusion of her countenance, and in an attempt at cordiality that seemed practised as one practises a difficult civility—measured, cautious, and never quite natural. She even went so far as to say she was concerned to find that Elinor and her sister were so soon to leave town, as she had hoped to see more of them.

In this exertion her husband, attending her into the room and hanging enamoured over her accents, appeared to distinguish every thing that was most affectionate and graceful—listening as if her words were music worth defending from the world, while the deeper discord of their conduct remained, like an unwanted sound outside a closed window, ignored because it was inconvenient to name.
