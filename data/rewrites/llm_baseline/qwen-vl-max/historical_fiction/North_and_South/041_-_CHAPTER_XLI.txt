I see my way as birds their trackless way—
I shall arrive! what time, what circuit first, I ask not:
but unless God sends his hail
Or blinding fire-balls, sleet, or stifling snow,
In some time—his good time—I shall arrive;
He guides me and the bird. In His good time!

Winter was deepening, yet the days were slowly lengthening, though no warmth of hope accompanied the faint glow of February suns. Mrs. Thornton had long ceased to visit the house. Mr. Thornton came now and then, but only to speak with her father in the study—no longer to linger in the drawing-room or share a word with Margaret. Mr. Hale spoke of him as unchanged, even more so for being rarely seen. The infrequency of their meetings seemed to make each one more precious in his eyes. From what Margaret could gather, there was nothing in Mr. Thornton’s silence that hinted at anger or resentment. His affairs had grown tangled during the strike, demanding greater attention than he’d given them before. And sometimes, she noticed, he mentioned her—not with hesitation, nor with any trace of awkwardness, but quietly, with the same calm regard as always.

Yet Margaret found little joy in such signs. Her own spirit was weary. The quiet peace of the present had followed too many months of anxiety, strain, and sorrow. She tried to fill her days by teaching the younger Boucher children, and to practice goodness with diligence—though it felt like a burden rather than a comfort. Her heart seemed numb, unresponsive to effort. She carried out her duties faithfully, punctually, painfully, but still, cheerfulness remained just beyond reach. Life felt cold and bare. Only in tending to her father did she find true purpose. He was quiet in his needs, often hesitant to voice a wish, but Margaret anticipated them all, fulfilling them with gentle care. It was a quiet devotion, soft and complete—her soul’s quiet obedience.

Then came news from Spain: Frederick was married. He and Dolores wrote letters—she in broken English, full of Spanish charm, he with phrases shaped by his new home, hints of a life remade. When Henry Lennox’s letter arrived, revealing how unlikely it was that Frederick would clear himself at court-martial without key witnesses, Frederick replied with fierce emotion. He renounced England as his homeland. He wished he could erase his birthright. He refused pardon if offered, and vowed never to live here again. The words tore at Margaret—so unnatural, so extreme—but when she reflected, she saw they were born of shattered dreams. There was nothing left but patience. In his next letter, Frederick spoke with radiant joy of his future, leaving the past behind. And Margaret, who had been begging God to grant patience for him, found herself needing it now—for herself.

Dolores’s letters, timid and sweet, began to warm both Margaret and her father. The young Spaniard clearly wanted to please Frederick’s family. Her careful writing, her erasures, her delicate attempts at politeness—they all revealed a tender heart eager to impress. With the announcement of the marriage came a black lace mantilla, chosen by Dolores herself for her sister-in-law, whom Frederick had described as a perfect woman—beautiful, wise, virtuous. Margaret smiled, then sighed. Here was her noble brother, once a romantic idealist, now a merchant—a trader! But she quickly checked herself. Wasn’t there a difference between a Spanish merchant and a millowner like Milton? Still, trade or no, Frederick was happy. Dolores must be lovely. The mantilla was exquisite. And so she returned to the present.

This spring, her father had begun to struggle with breathlessness—brief episodes that frightened him deeply. Margaret was less alarmed, since the symptoms passed entirely between attacks. Yet she urged him strongly to accept Mr. Bell’s invitation to visit Oxford in April. Mr. Bell had invited her too—and even written directly, commanding her to come. But Margaret felt she needed rest, not responsibility. To remain at home, free from duty, to simply breathe, to think, to grieve—this was what she craved after two years of constant care.

When her father left for the train, Margaret stood alone in the house. The sudden freedom stunned her. No one relied on her now. No invalid needed her planning or presence. She could be idle. Silent. Forgetful. And best of all—she could be unhappy, if she chose. For months, her own sorrows had been locked away in a dark cupboard. Now she opened it. She sat motionless in the drawing-room, going over every painful memory with steady resolve. Only once did she cry aloud—when the memory struck her: the falsehood, born of faithlessness, that had humiliated her so deeply.

She would not allow the temptation to claim power over her again. Her plans for Frederick had failed. The temptation was dead—mockery now, never real. The lie had been foolish, exposed by events. And truth, she now saw, was infinitely wiser.

In her agitation, she opened a book of her father’s lying on the table. The words that caught her eye seemed meant for this moment:

“Je ne voudrois pas reprendre mon cœur en ceste sorte: meurs de honte, aveugle, impudent, traistre et desloyal à ton Dieu, et sembables choses; mais je voudrois le corriger par voye de compassion. Or sus, mon pauvre cœur, nous voilà tombe dans la fosse, laquelle nous avions tant resolu d’eschapper. Ah! revelons-nous, et quittons-la pour jamais, reclamons la misericorde de Dieu, et esperons en elle qu’elle nous assistera pour desormais estra plus fermes; et remettons-nous au chemin de l’humilitè. Courage, soyons meshuy sur nos gardes, Dieu nous aydera.”

“The way of humility.”
Ah, thought Margaret, that is what I have missed.
But courage, little heart. We will turn back.
By God’s help, we may find the lost path.

She rose, determined to begin something that would carry her beyond herself. As she passed the drawing-room door, she called Martha in. She wanted to know the woman beneath the stiff, obedient exterior—the person hidden under years of servitude. It was hard to draw her out. But when Margaret mentioned Mrs. Thornton, Martha’s face lit up. A story unfolded—of how her father had once helped Mrs. Thornton’s husband, though she couldn’t say exactly how, for it happened when she was very young. Later, when her father fell into poverty and her mother died, Martha and her sister would have been “lost” without Mrs. Thornton’s intervention. She found them, cared for them, brought them into her home.

“I had the fever, and was weak,” Martha said. “Mrs. Thornton and Mr. Thornton—they never rested until I was nursed back to health, sent to the sea, treated properly. The doctor said it was catching, but they didn’t care. Only Miss Fanny—she went visiting those people she’s marrying. She was afraid, but it all turned out well.”

“Miss Fanny is getting married?” Margaret asked.

“Yes. To a rich gentleman—older than she is. Mr. Watson. His mills are near Hayleigh. A good match, though he has gray hair.”

Margaret was silent, letting the news settle. Then Martha returned to her duties, sweeping the hearth, asking about tea, leaving with the same wooden expression. Margaret pulled herself back—she had lately fallen into the habit of imagining how every news about Mr. Thornton might affect him. Whether he would approve or disapprove. She stopped herself.

The next day, she taught the Boucher children, took a long walk, and visited Mary Higgins. To her surprise, Nicholas was already home from work. The evening light had fooled her into thinking it was earlier. He seemed quieter, less forceful—like someone learning humility.

“So th’ oud gentleman’s away on his travels, is he?” he said. “Little ’uns telled me. Eh! but they’re sharp ones. I almost think they beat my own wenches for sharpness, though m’appen it’s wrong to say so—one of ’em in her grave. There’s summut in th’ weather, I reckon, as sets folk a-wandering. My measter, him at th’ shop yonder, is spinning about th’ world somewhere.”

“Is that why you’re home so early?” Margaret asked.

“Thou know’st nought about it, that’s all,” he said, contemptuously. “I’m not one wi’ two faces—one for my measter, and t’other for his back. I counted a’ th’ clocks in the town striking afore I’d leave my work. No! yon Thornton’s good enough to fight wi’, but too good to be cheated. It were you as getten me the place, and I thank yo’ for it. Thornton’s is not a bad mill, as times go.”

Then he called the boy forward.

“Stand down, lad, and say yo’r pretty hymn to Miss Marget. That’s right; steady on thy legs, and right arm out as straight as a skewer. One to stop, two to stay, three mak’ ready, and four away!”

The child sang a Methodist hymn—too complex for his understanding, but its rhythm had captured him. He delivered it with the solemnity of a statesman. Margaret clapped warmly. Nicholas called for another, and another. She was surprised—he seemed genuinely engaged, drawn into something sacred he had once mocked.

It was past tea-time when she returned home. No one waited. She could think freely, without watching others to gauge mood. After tea, she sorted through a large packet of letters, deciding which to destroy. Among them were several from Mr. Henry Lennox, concerning Frederick’s case. She read them carefully, intending only to assess the chance of clearing his name. But as she finished, she noticed something else—the man’s character. The tone was stiff, formal. He had never forgotten his connection to her, yet his concern felt distant. The letters were clever, yes—but cold. They lacked warmth. Still, they were valuable. She set them aside.

Then she slipped into thought. Her father was gone. And she had felt relief at being alone. She almost reproached herself for it. But these two days had renewed her strength. Plans that once seemed burdens now looked like joys. The fog had lifted. She saw her life more clearly. If only Mr. Thornton would return—just to visit, to ease her father’s loneliness—even if she never saw him again, she believed her path could be clear, steady. She sighed as she rose to bed. Despite her vow—"One step’s enough for me"—despite her duty to her father—there remained a quiet ache, a lingering sorrow.

And Mr. Hale, that April evening, thought of Margaret just as persistently. He had walked among old friends, revisiting familiar places. He had expected their reactions to change, given his departure from the church. Some might have been shocked, angry, disappointed. But when they saw him—the man they once loved—their opinions faded. They remembered not his beliefs, but his kindness. Mr. Hale had not been widely known. He had belonged to a small college, and was reserved by nature. But those who knew him well had seen beneath the silence—a depth of feeling, a tenderness. They welcomed him back with gentle affection, as if protecting a fragile thing.

“It’s too much,” said Mr. Bell. “You’re worn out by that Milton air.”

“I am tired,” said Mr. Hale. “But it’s not the air. I’m fifty-five. That explains it.”

“Nonsense! I’m over sixty, and feel no loss. Don’t talk like that. Fifty-five? You’re a young man.”

Mr. Hale shook his head. “These last few years…” He paused, then raised himself from the easy chair, speaking with trembling earnestness.

“Bell—you must not think that if I could have foreseen all that came from my resignation, even if I had known how _she_ would suffer—I would undo it. I would not have taken back my act of leaving the church. Even knowing that cruel suffering, I would have done the same. I might have handled things better for my family. But I don’t believe God gave me much wisdom or strength.”

Mr. Bell blew his nose loudly before replying.

“He gave you strength to do what your conscience told you was right. That’s all the strength or wisdom we need. I’ve none of it. Yet men call me wise, strong-minded—such nonsense. The simplest fool who obeys his own sense of right—wiping his shoes on a mat—is stronger than I. How blind men are!”

There was silence. Then Mr. Hale continued.

“About Margaret.”

“Well? What about her?”

“If I die—”

“Nonsense!”

“What will become of her? I often wonder. I suppose the Lennoxes will invite her to live with them. I try to believe they will. Aunt Shaw loved her, in her quiet way—but she forgets the absent.”

“A common fault. Who are the Lennoxes?”

“He’s handsome, fluent, agreeable. Edith—sweet, spoiled, beautiful. Margaret loves her deeply. And Edith—with as much heart as she can spare.”

“Hale,” said Mr. Bell, “you know that girl has nearly all my heart. I told you before. I’ve always cared for her—your daughter, my goddaughter. But this visit to you at Milton made me her slave. I went, a willing old victim, following the car of the conqueror. She looks—grand and serene—as if she has fought, and may still be fighting, but sees victory ahead. Yes, despite her troubles, that was her face. So everything I have is hers—if she needs it. And it will be hers when I die. And I myself—sixty and gouty though I am—will be her preux chevalier. Seriously, old friend, your daughter shall be my chief charge. All the help I can give—my wit, my wisdom, my heart—shall be hers. I won of course choose her as a subject for worry. You must have something to fret about to be happy. But you’ll outlive me by many years. Spare, thin men always cheat death. It’s the stout, florid fellows like me who go first.”

If Mr. Bell had seen into the future, he might have glimpsed the torch turning, the angel standing near, beckoning. That night, Mr. Hale laid his head on the pillow where it would never stir again. The servant entered the room in the morning, called out—received no reply. He approached the bed and saw the face—calm, beautiful, white, sealed in death. No pain. No struggle. The heart had simply stopped as he lay down.

Mr. Bell was stunned. He recovered only when it came time to rage at the idea of a coroner’s inquest.

“A coroner? Pooh. You don’t think I poisoned him! Dr. Forbes says it’s natural—heart failure. Poor old Hale. You wore out that tender heart too soon. Poor old friend. How he talked of his—— Wallis, pack a carpet-bag in five minutes. Here I’ve been talking. Pack it up. I must go to Milton by the next train.”

The bag was packed. The cab ordered. The railway reached. Twenty minutes later, Mr. Bell boarded the train. The London express whizzed past, drew back, and he was hurried aboard by the guard. He sank back, eyes closed, trying to grasp how a man alive yesterday could be dead today. Tears crept from beneath his grizzled lashes. He opened his eyes sharply, forcing a stern cheerfulness. He wouldn’t cry before strangers. Not he.

There were no strangers—only one man seated far across the carriage. Mr. Bell glanced at him, wondering if he’d been watching. Behind a broad sheet of the *Times*, he recognized Mr. Thornton.

“Why, Thornton! Is that you?” he said, moving closer. He gripped Mr. Thornton’s hand fiercely—then suddenly let go, needing it to wipe his tears. He had last seen him with Mr. Hale.

“I’m going to Milton. On a sad errand. I must tell Hale’s daughter he’s dead.”

“Dead? Mr. Hale dead?”

“Ay. I keep saying it—‘Hale is dead!’ But it doesn’t make it real. He went to bed well last night. This morning, my servant found him cold.”

“Where? I don’t understand.”

“At Oxford. He came to stay with me. Hadn’t been here in seventeen years. And this is the end.”

No words were spoken for over fifteen minutes. Then Mr. Thornton said:

“And she…”

“Margaret, you mean. Yes. I’m going to tell her. Poor fellow! How full his thoughts were of her last night! Good God! Last night only. And now—how far away he is! But I take Margaret as my child—for his sake. I said last night I would take her for her own. Well, I take her for both.”

Mr. Thornton struggled to speak. At last:

“What will become of her?”

“I fancy two people will be waiting: myself, for one. I’d take a live dragon into my house, if hiring such a chaperon and setting up an establishment would let me grow old with Margaret as my daughter. But there are those Lennoxes!”

“Who are they?” Mr. Thornton asked, voice trembling.

“Oh, smart London people. Likely to think they have the best claim. Captain Lennox married her cousin—the girl she grew up with. Good enough folk, I suppose. And there’s her aunt, Mrs. Shaw. I could marry that lady—though that would be a _pis aller_. And then—there’s that brother.”

“What brother? A brother of her aunt’s?”

“No, no. A clever Lennox—the captain’s a fool, you understand. A young barrister. He’s had his eye on Margaret for five years or more. One of his friends told me. He was held back by lack of fortune. Now that’s changed.”

“How?” asked Mr. Thornton, too eager to notice the intrusion.

Mr. Bell hesitated, then said softly:

“He’s inherited a fortune.”
