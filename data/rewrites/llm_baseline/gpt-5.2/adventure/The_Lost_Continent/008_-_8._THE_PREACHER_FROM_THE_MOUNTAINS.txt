Sleep took me the way hunger takes a man who has walked too long without bread—suddenly, without bargaining. I had not known, until my eyelids dropped, how my body ached for the simple mercy of stillness. The temple floor was cold stone, slick with the night’s damp that crept in through cracks and narrow wall-slits; yet I lay there as if on piled wool, and went down into darkness at once.

When I woke, it was night already. Nais sat near me in the dim, watchful as a cat in a granary—silent, alert, listening to the world beyond the walls. The camp outside steamed and hissed from the day’s wetness; through the slits we could see fires guttering, their light smeared by drizzle. Men and women in skins and rags crouched close to the flames, turning their hands as if they were turning meat on a spit, trying to coax life back into numb fingers. The rain struck the coals with sharp little clicks—sound after sound, a whole harsh music of discomfort: the spit of water, the rasp of damp cloth, the cough of smoke caught in throats. It was not a quiet night. It was the kind of night that makes folk mean, the kind that steals sleep and leaves only watchfulness and a short temper behind.

“A fine night,” Nais said at last, voice low and dry. “If the air were kinder, I’d have had you away to the sea already and none the wiser. But in this cold they sleep sourly. We must wait until the hour drugs them deeper.”

So we waited, two figures in a temple that had forgotten incense and hymn. I tasted the stale air—stone-dust, old ash, damp moss—like the memory of a kitchen long shut up, where the hearth has gone cold and the pots hang untouched. We spoke little. There are silences that are not emptiness, only shared listening.

Then the night’s sound changed.

From far off came a horn-note—harsh as a cracked shell blown by an impatient hand. It did not call once and stop like a sensible signal; it worried the air, again and again, a raucous thread drawn across the camp’s nerves. The note came nearer. Voices rose in anger near the fires, shouted curses flung at the invisible blower. And then the sound steadied, no longer approaching: it had found its stage.

Through the slits we saw people stirring, climbing to their feet as though pulled up by that brutal tone. They gathered in a wedge of bodies around a figure raised above them on the pedestal of a war engine. He spoke, and though his words were blurred by distance and rain, the cadence of them came through: the rhythmic push of a man used to driving others’ minds where he wished.

Nais leaned toward me. “If all who won’t sleep go to feed their eyes on this windbag, you can slip out the other way.”

But chance, which loves a snare, had other arrangements. A dozen feet splashed up to the temple—men and women together—and voices called for Nais by name, loud and urgent. They wanted her at the gathering. She had a tongue, they said, that could cut through argument like a good knife through sinew. They feared this stranger-preacher would split their ranks unless someone stood up against him.

For a breath I thought: so here is how the gods amuse themselves—by dragging a man toward the very blaze he meant to avoid.

Nais’s face tightened. “Come out with me,” she whispered. “Keep away from the firelight.”

“And my being here?”

“There’s nothing to explain,” she said with a bitterness that tasted like scorched grain. “They’ll take you for my lover. It’s the fashion here. But—ah—why did the gods not give you a beard to curl like other men? Then you could have been gone and safe hours ago.”

“A smooth chin pleases me better.”

“So it does me,” she murmured, so softly it was almost swallowed by the rain, as she leaned her shoulder into the door-stone and pushed it ajar. “Your chin.”

We stepped out.

The air hit like a wet cloth slapped across the face—cold, mineral, smelling of smoke and soaked skins. The ragged ones waiting there did not study me long; their eyes were greedy for the gathering, not for details. They threw a jest or two, coarse as uncooked roots. I had the strength in me to pay such talk with steel, but steel was not the tool for this moment. They turned and sloshed into the dark, glancing back to be sure we followed.

All around, the camp moved as one creature. Shadows converged. Rain pattered on hides. Fires hissed and flared, and their smoke carried the scent of half-burnt fat, damp wood, and the sour breath of unwashed bodies. The soundscape was a bruise: muttering, splashing, horn-blast, the restless clank of weapons. There were no sleepers now; even those who had tried to burrow into warmth were dragged up by the promise of words. If we had swerved, the movement of the crowd would have betrayed us at once.

So we went straight on, carried like leaves in a stream, until we came close enough to hear.

“Who are your chiefs?” the preacher demanded at the end of some fierce speech.

The answer exploded back at him—an uproar, a thousand mouths finding unity in refusal. “We have no chiefs! We are done with chiefs! We are all equal here! Take away your silly magic. You may kill us with magic if you choose, but rule us you shall not. Nor the other priests. Nor Phorenice. Nor anybody. We are done with rulers!”

We were pressed nearer and nearer until the man above them resolved in firelight and rain.

He was old, naked but for a wisp of rag at his loins, his skin shining with wet as if newly oiled. White hair spilled to his shoulders; his beard was long, untrimmed, uncurled—more like a wild goat’s than a court priest’s. But in his hand he held what made all the difference: a wand whose tip burned with fire that did not consume. It glowed, twinkled, flared—an obedient little star, bright enough that raindrops, passing through its light, flashed like thrown salt. He pointed with it; he circled it; at times he swept it around himself until his wet, bare body seemed wreathed in a halo.

The Symbol of our Lord the Sun. No forgery could mimic that living light. No civilized man would doubt it.

The rebels did not doubt his caste either. Even while they screamed defiance, they admitted his power. “You may kill us with your magic,” they cried, and yet their stubbornness held: “but rule us you shall not. We have suffered too much. We are done with rulers for always.”

But my own blood went cold in a way the rain could not manage.

That old man was Zaemon—father to Nais. Zaemon who had seen me yesterday beside Phorenice, who could name me now if he chose. Four days earlier these same rebels had spent a navy trying to destroy me. If the name Deucalion was loosed among them while I stood packed in this crush, Nais’s cleverness would buy me nothing. The gods keep their tethers hidden; but I felt mine drawn thin.

Nais felt it too. Her fingers twined into mine—tight, urgent. A farewell pressed into skin. I pressed back, because even in the midst of danger I was sorry—sorrier than was useful—that I might not see her again.

And yet, beneath sorrow, another heat stirred: the old soldier’s lust for the final fight. I could almost taste it, iron and smoke on the tongue, like the scent of a knife just drawn from a sheath. If I was to die, I wanted to die in space enough to swing a blade and build a mound of the unskilled before they dragged me down.

For the moment, Zaemon did not look my way. He was wrapped in his own thunder. I listened, and measured the weight of his words in the crowd’s response.

He preached no novelty. He spoke of how, since history’s beginnings, the gods had set apart one Clan to rule and to serve as priests; and until Phorenice, he said, they had been just and exact. They had fought invaders and beasts; they had studied the earth’s tremors and learned to foretell earthquakes and eruptions; they had warned the people so they might flee. They had not ruled for personal gain but for Atlantis, honoring the kingdom the gods favored. Under their guidance Atlantis had risen to a height of prosperity and ease.

“But,” Zaemon cried, and the Symbol’s light washed his wet skin as if he stood under a falling furnace, “the people grew fat with comfort. They began to believe their fortune sprang from their own puny strength and wit, not from the gods’ gift. They neglected worship. Temples stood bare. And punishment came.

“In Their inscrutable way,” he went on, voice quavering but sharp, “the gods decreed that a wife of one of the Priests should find a woman child by the wayside and take it as her own. That child the gods fashioned into a scourge for Atlantis. You who have felt Phorenice’s hand know how perfectly the High Gods craft Their instruments.

“Yet as They set up, so can They throw down. Those appointed to debase Phorenice are even now chosen. The old rule shall return—but not until you, who have sinned, are chastened enough to cry out for it.”

He thrust the glowing wand toward them. “See! You know the unspeakable Power for which I am mouthpiece. Decide now. Are the gods to throw down this woman who scorned Them and trod you cruelly? Or must you be further purged before deliverance?”

He broke off, beard sinking against his chest. Immediately a young man, skin-clad, weapon in hand, forced his way up and sprang onto the engine beside him.

“Hear me, brethren!” he bellowed. “We are done with tyrants. Death may come—none here fear it. But rulers again we will not have. My lord,” he said, turning bravely to Zaemon, “I know you can kill me by magic. I have spoken. I can pay the price.”

“I can kill you, but I will not,” said Zaemon. “You have said your silliness. Now go down.”

“We have free speech here. I will not go till I choose.”

“Aye, but you will,” Zaemon said.

He did not strike. He did not even raise the Symbol. He only narrowed his brows, and the young man’s defiance broke as if a bone snapped without a hand touching it. The man tried to retort—and found his tongue dead in his mouth. His face twisted with sudden fear. He leaped down into the press like a man fleeing a flame.

The crowd trembled with expectation, and when no further marvel came, they began to shout for a miracle—something to feed their eyes, something to barter their rebellion for.

Zaemon stooped and glowered at them, fury making his old face seem carved from wet stone. “You dogs,” he cried. “Empty-witted dogs! Do you ask me to degrade the Higher Mysteries, to dance them before you like a mummer’s trick? Do you think the gods are to be tempted by your conditions? It is for you to woo Them back with humility. Come so, and I declare you will have pardon and relief. Remain stubborn, and Phorenice will scourge you into annihilation before she herself is made to answer. Choose.”

Voices surged. Weapons lifted, menacing as thorns. It was plain the party for “independence” held the greater weight—by numbers and by brute appetite for violence. Those who might have been weary enough to surrender kept silent, terror-muzzled by the loud ones. So much for the “free speech” they boasted of.

I leaned toward Nais and whispered the thought with a sour edge. But she clutched my hand hard.

“Be silent,” she begged. “They will tear you in pieces. They’re on fire for mischief.”

“Yet a few hours ago you meant to kill me yourself,” I could not help saying.

She flashed on me, hot-eyed. “A woman can change her mind. It becomes you little to remind her of it.”

A man beside me twisted around, peering at my face through shadow and rain. “Oh,” he said, voice thick with suspicion. “A shaved chin. Who are you, friend, that you cut a beard instead of curling it? I see no wound that forced you.”

I answered civilly enough that, since “freedom” was their watchword, my chin was private business. He pushed for more—one of those sour, quarrelsome men who rot any gathering. There was no room here for a brawl, no space to draw steel. So I used the older, quieter method.

I took him suddenly by throat and shoulder and turned his neck with a swift, practiced wrench. I heard the crack. I released him before any neighbor understood what had happened. The crowd’s press held his body upright; his head nodded forward as if he had fallen asleep from weariness. In a crush like this, a man may die and remain standing.

Zaemon’s voice still cut through the night. He began to chant the ancient ritual, setting forth the cult in the prescribed form. And the people—astonishing in their own way—answered in the old responses, at the old places, as habit awakened like embers stirred back to heat.

It was strange beyond telling: this long-neglected service, dragged out here in mud and rain among wild folk who had boasted of having thrown it away. Yet as they spoke the words, their voices took on a different tone—not only anger now, but something like remembered shelter. The gods’ old phrases fit their mouths again, as if they were tasting a forgotten bread.

Zaemon cried:

“...WHO STILLED THE BURNING OF THE MOUNTAINS, AND MADE COOL PLACES ON THE EARTH FOR US TO LIVE!—PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS.”

And the crowd answered, the sound swelling and rolling like surf:

“PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS.”

“WHO GAVE US MASTERY OVER THE LESSER BEASTS AND SKILL OF TEN TIMES TO PREVAIL!—PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS....”

“PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS....”

The earnestness of it could move a man’s bones. And yet it saddened too, because I knew how easily such fervor drains away from common people, leaving them empty and crueler for the emptiness.

When the ritual ended, Zaemon did not demand their loyalty again. He stepped down from the war engine, the Symbol thrust before him like a blade of pure daylight. The crowd had been packed to its limit, but the light drove them back. They recoiled as if the wand were a stream of lava. A lane opened.

And then the turning point came.

At first I thought—as perhaps all did—that the old man was making his way back to wherever he had come from. But his path held steady, and it aimed toward me.

The thought came clean and cold: having spoken to the many, he would now settle with the one. He had seen me yesterday as Phorenice’s minister. He could denounce me as Deucalion. If he chose, he could end me here without granting me even the last battle I craved.

I felt the ache of that loss sharply, like the pang of a man promised a feast and then offered only thin broth. Yet I had been trained to wear obedience as a second skin. If the council on the Sacred Mountain had decided Atlantis would prosper more with Deucalion sent to the gods, then I would bow.

So when Zaemon came close, I did not flinch. I stood my ground and gave him the salutation prescribed between our ranks. He returned it with full correctness.

The Symbol’s glow widened around us. The crowd was driven back into a ring, a sea of ravenous faces shining with wet and firelight. Even Nais, priest’s daughter though she was, could not stand against the ineffable force that poured from that sign; she was pressed away with the rest.

So Zaemon and I stood alone in the center, bathed in the unconsuming light, while the night hissed with rain.

The people drew their own conclusion at once. “A spy!” they roared. “A spy! Zaemon salutes him as a Priest!”

Zaemon turned to them, and something queer crossed his grim face. “Aye,” he said. “This is a Priest. And if I give you his name, you may find greater interest. This is the Lord Deucalion.”

My name went through them like sparks through dry grass—hatred, triumph, fear, hunger. They had already chosen me as enemy; they had already flung a navy at my destruction. Now, with me ringed in by their masses, they wanted to pull me to pieces with their fingers.

But the Symbol’s glare held them back. They raged and shuddered and edged away as though the light burned their skin. And Zaemon, emissary of the Priests’ Clan, would not hand me over until he had spoken what he had come to speak.

He faced me and spoke in the sacred tongue—the hidden language the common people do not know, a speech old as the first temples, heavy with meanings that do not translate cleanly into any market talk.

“My brother,” he said, “which have you come to serve—Deucalion or Atlantis?”

“Words are a poor thing to answer that,” I replied in the same tongue. “You know my record. By the Law, every ship from Yucatan will have carried its sworn report to the council. And before I went there, what I did was written plain on Atlantis’ face.”

“We know,” he said. “You governed well. You lived austerely. You set up Atlantis for mistress and served her. But you had no Phorenice to tempt you into change.”

“You can send me where I shall see her no more, if you think me frail.”

“Yes,” he said, “and lose your usefulness. No, brother. You are the last hope this land has. All other human means against Phorenice have failed. You have returned from overseas for the final duel. You are the strongest we have. If you fail, only the terrible Powers locked within the Ark of the Mysteries remain. It is not lawful to speak of their scope—but you know their potency.”

I shrugged. “Then throw me to these wolves and be done. Let them end me now.”

Zaemon’s brows tightened. “I bid you do your duty. Why wish to evade it?”

“I remember your words in the pyramid,” I said. “‘Phorenice, whilst you are yet Empress, you shall see this royal pyramid—polluted with your debaucheries—torn tier from tier, stone from stone, scattered as feathers before a wind.’ You spoke as if you foresaw defeat.”

A shudder went through him. “I cannot tell what she may force us to do. I spoke what was revealed must happen. Perhaps, when matters have reached that pass, she will repent. But before we loose the more desperate weapons of the gods, it is fitting we expend what human strength remains. You must go, brother, and play your part to the utmost.”

“It is an order,” I said. “So I obey.”

“You shall be at Phorenice’s side again by the next dawn,” Zaemon continued. “She sent for you from Yucatan as a husband—thinking you have the arm to prolong her tyranny. You are a Priest, and a man of convincing tongue. You must make her see the invincible power that can be loosed against her. Make her understand the hopelessness of resisting.”

“If it is ordered, I will,” I said. “But the chance is small. I have seen Phorenice. Her will is a locked door. Others have tried. You have tried. All have failed.”

“Words wasted on a maiden may go home to a wife,” Zaemon said. “You have been brought here to be her husband. Take your place.”

The command struck me with a pain I had not expected. Women had been little to me through a life of campaigns and rule; and when I landed, the thought of taking Phorenice for policy’s sake had not revolted me. But the last two days had rearranged the world. I had seen two women who had drawn me strangely, and one of them had stirred a turmoil in me I had never felt amid my careful economies.

To marry Phorenice meant severance from that other woman forever. The thought tasted like smoke in the mouth, bitter and choking.

Yet I did not set my private hunger above the council’s will. I bowed to Zaemon and touched his hand to my forehead. “It is an order,” I said. “If our Lord the Sun grants me life, I will obey.”

“Then let us begone,” said Zaemon.

He took my arm and swept the Symbol before us. No further word passed between me and Nais. I would not risk tangling her in the rebels’ fury. But as we moved away, I caught one last hot glance from her eyes—like a brand held up in rain—and that had to serve as farewell.

The crowd opened before us, driven back by that starry light. They cried for my blood, their voices thick with hate; yet no one dared rush the lane. Not a hand lifted to strike. When we neared the outskirts and the ranks thinned, some surged as if to follow—then Zaemon turned and whirled the Symbol back into their faces. The blaze flared lurid. Men who had shouted bravely fell to their knees, grovelling like animals before a sudden fire. They did not press us further.

The rain still fell. As we passed the camp-fires, the old man’s wasted body gleamed with wet, the light turning each raindrop on his skin into a brief spark. Ahead, through the darkness, loomed the Sacred Mountain—vast, inevitable—its crest encircled by the ring of eternal fires.

I breathed out, and the sound was lost beneath the hiss of rain and the far horn’s fading echo. In my mind rose the memory of older days on that mountain—groves quiet enough to hear one’s own heartbeat, temples where the only sound was the measured chant and the soft crackle of incense on hot stone.

Peace, remembered, has its own sharpness. It cut me as we walked.
