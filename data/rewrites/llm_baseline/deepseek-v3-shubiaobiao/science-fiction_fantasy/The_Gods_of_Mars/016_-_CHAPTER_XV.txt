Darkness swallowed me, but only for a heartbeat. When my senses returned, a flickering torchlight spilled into the corridor, banishing the unseen eyes that had stalked me. My head throbbed from the fall, yet I was whole. I scrambled to my feet, pressing into the shadows as the approaching footsteps grew louder.

Four green warriors strode into view, their harsh voices echoing off the stone. Tars Tarkas walked among them, shackled but unbowed. I slipped into a side passage, pulse hammering, and waited. The guards passed without a glance, their torch casting jagged shadows.

Two lingered outside the cell as the jailer led Tars Tarkas within. My fingers tightened around my sword. The moment the door creaked open again, I struck—swift, silent. The guard crumpled. Tars Tarkas emerged, clad in the fallen warrior’s harness, his eyes alight with grim understanding.

“By the nine moons,” he rumbled, voice low, “you make a habit of defying death, John Carter.”

We moved like ghosts. The remaining guards, impatient, had already ascended the spiral ramp. Tars Tarkas called after them, spinning a lie about a forgotten blade. Their laughter faded as they climbed, and we seized our chance.

The upper halls were treacherous. Voices murmured behind closed doors, but the balcony overlooking the courtyard lay deserted. Below, the Warhoon beasts snorted and pawed the ground, their yellow eyes gleaming in the dark. Telepathic commands were their reins—no bridles, no mercy.

I vaulted onto the nearest thoat, its slate-colored hide slick beneath me. It screamed, lashing out, but I held fast. Tars Tarkas subdued another, and together we drove three more toward the gates. The beasts resisted, their fury a living thing, but the city’s edge loomed ahead. Freedom.

A whistle cut the night—Carthoris’ signal. Thuvia, Xodar, and my son waited, their faces taut with relief. No time for words. We mounted, Thuvia clinging behind me, and fled eastward beneath the twin moons.

Dawn bled across the dead sea basin, painting the ochre waste in gold. But behind us, a dark line thickened on the horizon.

“Riders,” Tars Tarkas growled. “And gaining.”

We pushed the thoats harder, their great legs churning. Yet exhaustion dragged at them—at us. Thuvia’s mount faltered first. I caught her as it fell, her weight now mine to bear. Our pace faltered.

Then—sacrifice. Her arms slipped from my waist.

“For you,” she whispered, and dropped into the dust.

Carthoris wheeled before I could. He flung her onto his own thoat, smacking it onward. A noble act, but futile. The Warhoons closed in, howling.

We dismounted, blades ready. Tars Tarkas and Xodar charged to our side, their thoats abandoned. Death, at least, would be met standing.

Then—thunder from the skies.

A shell exploded amid the green horde. Chaos erupted. A battleship cut through the dawn, its hull emblazoned with the crest of Helium. The Warhoons scattered like leaves in a storm.

As the ship descended, hope—impossible, radiant—flared in my chest. The Princess’ banner flew upon its bow.
