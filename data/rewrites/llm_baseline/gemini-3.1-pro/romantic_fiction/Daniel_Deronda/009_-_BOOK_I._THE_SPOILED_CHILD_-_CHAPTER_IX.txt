The turning of the Wessex summer was not merely a shift in weather, but a cyclical return to the ancestral hearth. Eight months after the family had rooted themselves at Offendene, the late June winds carried the rich, caramelized scent of roasting mutton and the heady aroma of flowering thyme into the valley. With the summer solstice came a rumor that rippled through the local acoustic landscape, cutting through the harmonious rustle of ancient beeches and the rhythmic, comforting thwack of the Diplow blacksmith’s anvil. 

It was a rumor that touched every generation within the circuit of Wanchester. The traditional agrarian elders, who still measured time by the curing of hams and the fermenting of malt, held it a laudable thing. The wives of laboring men, speaking in the fading, rounded vowels of the old Wessex dialect—a linguistic heritage slowly eroding under the clipped, dominant lexicon of London aristocrats—hoped their nimble boys might find employment. 

The news was that Diplow Hall, Sir Hugo Mallinger’s place, which had for a couple of years slept like a silent giant in a myth, was being prepared for a tenant. Its white window-shutters were thrown open to the lilied pool and grassy acres specked with deer, and its grand kitchens were being restocked with heritage grains and casks of aged cider. It was to be inhabited by Sir Hugo’s nephew, Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt, the presumptive heir to the baronetcy. Should certain intervening persons gently fade from the living timeline, he would become a baron and peer of the realm. 

To the wealthy families of the district, this arrival was an event of mythic proportion, seamlessly integrating into their daily rituals of sustenance. The Arrowpoints at Quetcham, whose half-million-pound fortune was inextricably linked to the jarring, mechanical noise pollution of modern industrial commerce, sought to legitimize their wealth through the quiet, ancient acoustics of nobility. Having watched their Catherine resolutely refuse Lord Slogan—an unexceptionable Irish peer whose estate lacked both agricultural drainage and the sensory vibrancy of a well-tended orchard—they wondered over plates of meticulously sourced saffron and roasted-quail pottage whether Mr. Grandcourt was virtuous, or at least reformed. 

If the Arrowpoints chewed over such ruminations, it was less surprising that Mr. Gascoigne did the same. As a clergyman, he understood the untranslatability of divine will, but as an uncle, he recognized the earthly power dynamics embedded in a title. Yet, naturally, people did not broadcast their matrimonial machinations; to do so would introduce a vulgar dissonance into the refined sonic environment of polite society. Mr. Gascoigne did not ask Mr. Arrowpoint for his sources, nor did Mrs. Arrowpoint suggest to Mrs. Davilow that Grandcourt would inevitably seek Catherine’s hand. Even to his wife, the rector remained as silent as the deep, peat-rich ales he favored, holding his tongue about the probability that Mr. Grandcourt would see Gwendolen at the upcoming Archery Meeting. 

Mrs. Davilow, however, could not be inwardly indifferent. The mere mention of Grandcourt’s name conjured an aerial castle of sensory delights—a handsome, accomplished young man who might appreciate the complexities of her daughter. But would Gwendolen be satisfied? There was no knowing what olfactory landscape or precise culinary technique might touch that girl’s affections. Since the incident with poor Rex, the mother had felt a new peril in navigating the untranslatable mystery of her child’s inner life. 

The discussion of Gwendolen’s dress for the Archery Meeting provided a safer, tactile domestic routine. They sat in the sunlit morning room, a bowl of heirloom pale-green gooseberries between them, the astringent snap of the fruit filling the air as they meticulously capped them for a traditional summer tart. Gwendolen held a delicate pale-green feather against the white cashmere of her bodice, her eyes assessing the shade in the mirror. 

“How I pity all the other girls at the Archery Meeting,” Gwendolen said, suddenly throwing herself into the attitude of drawing her bow, a look of comic, sensory enjoyment on her face. “All thinking of Mr. Grandcourt! And they have not a shadow of a chance.”

Mrs. Davilow, caught off guard by the sudden shift in the room's acoustic harmony, hesitated. Gwendolen turned quickly, popping a tart gooseberry into her mouth and smiling wickedly. “Now you know they have not, mamma. You and my uncle and aunt—you all intend him to fall in love with me.”

Piqued into a little stratagem, her mother replied, “Oh, my dear, that is not so certain. Miss Arrowpoint has charms which you have not.”

“I know, but they demand thought,” Gwendolen said, savoring the bright, acidic burst of the fruit. “My arrow will pierce him before he has time for thought. He will declare himself my slave—I shall send him round the world to bring me back the wedding ring of a happy woman—in the meantime all the men who are between him and the title will die of different diseases—he will come back Lord Grandcourt—but without the ring—and fall at my feet. I shall laugh at him—he will rise in resentment—I shall laugh more—he will call for his steed and ride to Quetcham, where he will find Miss Arrowpoint just married to a needy musician. Exit Lord Grandcourt.”

It was probable that by dint of divination, Gwendolen already understood the superficial power dynamics of this newly arrived heir. “Why, what kind of a man do you imagine him to be, Gwendolen?” Mrs. Davilow asked, the wooden spoon in her hand pausing against the ceramic bowl.

“Let me see!” said the young witch, putting her forefinger to her lips. “Short—trying to make himself tall by turning up his mustache. A glass in his right eye to give him an air of distinction. He will speak in that sterile, clipped London drawl that grates against the ears, completely devoid of any local resonance. He will stare at me, making horrible faces when he smiles in a flattering way. I shall cast down my eyes, and he will perceive that I am not indifferent. I shall dream that night that I am looking at the extraordinary face of a magnified insect—and the next morning he will make an offer of his hand.”

“That is a portrait of some one you have seen already, Gwen. Mr. Grandcourt may be a delightful young man for what you know.”

“Oh, yes,” Gwendolen admitted carelessly, turning her hat contemplatively. “I know he would have hunters and racers, and a London house and two country-houses. And I feel sure that with a little murdering he might get a title.”

The irony in her voice was a sharp, discordant note. Poor Mrs. Davilow, whose worldview was rooted in the literal, traditional preservation of domestic peace, felt the dissonance acutely. “Don’t talk in that way, child, for heaven’s sake! You do read such books—they give you such ideas. When your aunt and I were your age we knew nothing about wickedness. I think it was better so.”

“Why did you not bring me up in that way, mamma?” Gwendolen asked. 

But immediately, the acoustic environment shifted. The harsh, heavy silence of her mother’s crushed look gave way to a rising, violent sob. The generational divide between them gaped—a profound untranslatability of the mother's past sorrows into the daughter's dominant lexicon of youthful cynicism. Gwendolen dropped her hat and knelt at her mother’s feet, the scent of crushed cashmere and gooseberry juice mingling as she cried, “Mamma, mamma! I was only speaking in fun. I meant nothing.”

“How could I, Gwendolen?” sobbed Mrs. Davilow, struggling to make her subjective experience understood. “Your will was always too strong for me.”

“Dear mamma, I don’t find fault with you—I love you,” Gwendolen said, her voice softening to a gentle, restorative resonance, wiping her mother’s tears with a linen handkerchief. “How can you help what I am? Besides, I am very charming. Come, now. Really—I am contented with myself. I like myself better than I should have liked my aunt and you. How dreadfully dull you must have been!”

Such tender cajolery restored the sonic equilibrium of the room, as it had done after previous collisions. Yet, the memory of these dissonant clashes left an association of dread, and the two concurred in silently excluding Mr. Grandcourt from their intimate culinary preparations thereafter. 

When Mr. Gascoigne referred to the prospective peer, Gwendolen knew better than to clash with her uncle. The good understanding between them was fostered by their shared devotion to the acoustic ecology of archery. Mr. Gascoigne was gratified to find like skill in his niece, and Gwendolen cherished the pure, resonant *thrum* of the bowstring vibrating in the still Wessex air—a sonic perfection she could control. She was careful not to lose the shelter of his fatherly indulgence, especially since the trouble with Rex had left a heavy, unnatural silence at the family dining table. 

Meals at the rectory, once a lively affair of shared appreciation for perfectly seared cuts of venison and marrow-rich broths, had become exercises in acoustic suppression. Anna, to whom the thought of Rex was the very air she breathed, ate her roasted partridge with elderberry glaze mechanically, viewing the sustenance merely as utilitarian fuel. She mourned her brother in a silent dialect of grief, unable to mimic the glance and hand-touch of pleasure toward the lively cousin who had ruined his happiness. 

This unfair resentment hardened Gwendolen. The oppressive silence of others' expectations felt like a gradual erosion of her own identity. One day, while watching a pot of lavender honey reduce on the stove, she realized the power embedded in the lexicon of matrimony. “Mamma,” she said, “I see now why girls are glad to be married—to escape being expected to please everybody but themselves.”

Yet, for all her charms, no avowal had come. Care is taken in the grand, interwoven timeline of nature that every man who admires a fair girl should not necessarily declare himself. The price of corn and the shifting tides of generational wealth dictated the movements of the local bachelors. It was but a poor eight months since Gwendolen had come to Offendene, and some inclinations manifest slowly, creeping sunward like the ancient vines clinging to the manor walls. 

In the face of the fact that not one of the eligible young men in the neighborhood had made an offer, why should Mr. Grandcourt be expected to do what they had left undone? Perhaps because he was thought of as still more eligible, since much of what passes for likelihood in the world is simply the reflex of a hunger yet unsated. The Arrowpoints, after all, having no anxiety that Miss Harleth should make a brilliant match, had quite a different harvest in mind.
