On my fourth night in the liminal house of rest, I slipped past the last thin veil of mortal dream, and woke truly.
All around me hung soft, unbroken dark—no shadow, no void, just the heavy, nurturant dark of a womb about to quicken—and I knew at once I was no longer trapped in the looping nightmares of my old life. Every moment of slumber I’d known since arriving here had been a dream, and this, at last, was waking. My heart leaped so hard it ached, and I breathed a quiet “At last” into the hush.
When I turned my head, Lona stood beside my couch, waiting for me. I had never lost her. I had only lost the sight of her, for a little while. I never needed to grieve her so bitterly. The dark that wrapped the rest of the room could not touch her: her eyes glowed with the same quiet radiance that clung to the Mother, her whole face luminous, and her stitched grave-clothes thrumming with the light of a body reborn to resurrection, white as star-frost, glistering from every thread. She had fallen asleep a girl, cut down before her prime; she woke a woman, full and soft and ripe with the unshakable loveliness of life unbound from mortal decay. I folded her into my arms, and the warm, solid thud of her pulse against mine told me I was alive at last, alive for real.
“I woke first,” she said, smiling half in wonder.
“You did, my love,” I laughed back. “And you woke me.”
“I only looked at you and waited,” she answered.
Then a candle of condensed starlight drifted toward us through the dark, and a moment later Adam, Eve, and Mara stepped into the glow. They greeted us with a quiet, unhurried good-morning, no fanfare, no cheers—they had tended hundreds of these wakings, and knew the hush was holy.
“I hope your stretch of unlit rest was gentle,” the Mother said.
“Not very, for a long while,” I told her, “but this waking is more heavenly than anything I ever dared dream of.”
“It is only just begun,” she replied, her smile soft. “You are hardly even awake yet.”
“He is at least clothed upon with Death,” Adam put in, drawing close, “the radiant garment that makes all true life possible.” He embraced Lona, his child, then slung an arm around my shoulders, glanced for a long quiet moment at the still-sleeping princess on the far couch, and patted the great leopardess curled at her feet. “We will meet the two of you again, before long,” he said, looking first at Lona, then at me.
“Have we to die a second time?” I asked, a flicker of old mortal fear pricking at me.
Adam laughed, a warm sound like shifting bedrock, and his smile held the same light as the Mother’s. “No. You have died *into* life, and you will die no more. All you have to do now is stay dead—dead to the hollow, greedy old hungers of your mortal lives. Once you pass through the kind of dying we do here, all suffering, all decay, all cycles of unmaking are over. Now you have only to live, and live with every blessed part of you. The more fully you live, the stronger you grow to live still more.”
“But will I not grow weary, living so hard?” I said. “What if I cease to strive with all my might?”
“It needs only the will, and the strength is already there,” the Mother said. “Pure life holds no weakness to tire out. The great central Life keeps generating every spark of ours, endlessly. Those who refuse to surrender their old mortal selves die many times, die constantly, dig themselves deeper and deeper into shadow, never done with unmaking. But here? All is upward motion, and love, and gladness that never runs thin.” She fell silent, and the look she gave me said we were family, no goodbyes could ever exist between us, no distance.
Mara leaned in and kissed my forehead, grinning bright. “I told you, brother, all would be well. The next time you meet someone sunk in grief, tell them this: what will be well, is even now well.” A tiny sigh escaped her, and I heard the unspoken line beneath it: *But most of them will not believe you*. “You know who I am now, don’t you?” she finished, her smile glowing like her mother’s.
“I know you,” I said. “You are the voice crying in the wilderness long before the Baptist ever walked the earth. You are the shepherd who chases lost, wandering sheep home before the shadows rise and the dark closes in.”
“My work will end one day,” she said, “and then I will share in the gladness of the great Shepherd who sent me.”
“All through the long night, the dawn has been close at hand,” Adam said.
I tilted my head. “What is that beating of huge wings I hear, far off?”
“The Shadow is hovering,” Adam replied. “He thinks there is one here that belongs to him. But once anyone has belonged to us, they can never be his again.”
I turned to look for my mortal parents, to kiss them before we left—only to find their couches empty, save for the Little Ones who had crept into them, bold with childlike love, claiming the empty beds for their own. For a single heartbeat, that old awful nightmare of total desolation, of being trapped forever alone in an empty dark house, crashed over me, and I flinched aside.
“What is it, my heart?” Lona asked, her hand on my arm.
“Their empty places frightened me,” I said.
“They rose and left long ago,” Adam said. “They kissed you while you were still half tangled in your old mortal dreams, and whispered ‘come soon’ before they slipped away.”
“And I neither felt nor heard them,” I murmured, stunned.
“How could you, when you were still half lost in that dreary old house you remembered? You thought the dark place had gotten you again. Now go out, and find them. As for you, child,” he added, turning to Lona, “your parents will come and find you soon enough.”
The hour of our departure was at hand. Lona walked to the couch of the woman who had slain her, leaned down to press a soft, forgiving kiss to her still-sleeping cheek, then stepped into Adam’s waiting embrace.
“That kiss will draw her home, my Lona,” Adam said. “Nothing is stronger than a gift of unasked love.”
“Who were her birth parents?” Lona asked.
“My father,” Adam answered, “is her father too.”
Lona turned and laced her fingers through mine. We knelt together, humbly, to thank the three of them for guiding us through the little death that had woken us. They leaned over us, and breathed on us, warm as sunlight through spring leaves.
“Hark,” Adam said. “I hear the sun.”
I listened. It was not the tiny sun I had known on Earth. It rushed toward us with the roar of ten thousand wings, the thunder of a molten, flaming world millions of miles wide, its building swell a crescendo chord of a hundred overlapping harmonies. The three of them looked at each other and smiled, and that smile drifted off them like a three-petaled glowing flower, their family’s morning offering of thanksgiving, rising heavenward. Light spread from their mouths, their faces, seeping through their skin and shining through their garments. Before I could even speak the words *they are changing*, Adam and Eve stood before me in the full splendor of resurrection angels, and Mara was the Magdalene, bright and radiant, the first witness at the empty tomb. Adam’s face blazed like lightning, and Eve held a linen napkin that shed flakes of splendor across every surface of the room. A wind began to moan in slow, pulsing gusts.
“You hear his wings now,” Adam said. I knew he did not mean the wings of the morning. “The great Shadow is stirring to leave. Wretched creature—he is trapped entirely inside his own empty self, and he can never rest.”
“But is there not something deeper in him, something worth saving?” I asked.
“A shadow cannot exist without something solid standing in the light behind it,” he answered. He tilted his head, listening, then grinned wide, glowing. “Hark to the golden cock! For millions of years, he has stood silent and motionless on the face of the universe’s great clock. Now at last he flutters his wings. Now he will crow. Men on every world will hear his cry in their bones, at intervals, until the dawn of the eternal day.”
I listened. Far away, buried in the heart of an eon’s worth of silence, I heard the clear, jubilant outcry of his golden throat. It hurled defiance at death and all the dark, sang of infinite hope, of the calm that was coming. It was the expectation of every living creature that had ever suffered, finding a voice at last. It was the cry of a chaos that would one day be a perfect kingdom.
Then a great rush of beating wings echoed through the whole house.
“The black bat is flown!” Mara cried, joy bright in her voice.
“Amen, golden cock, bird of God!” Adam shouted, and his voice boomed through the silent house, rising up into the open sky.
At that word Amen, the Little Ones sprang up off their beds like doves of silver rising from a pile of broken potsherds, all on their knees instantly, yelling at the top of their lungs. “Crow! Crow again, golden cock!” They had seen and heard him, even in their dreams. Then each turned to their sleeping bedfellow, gazed at them with soft, loving eyes, kissed the silent companion beside them, and leaped from the couch. The two Little Ones who had curled up in my parents’ empty beds stared blank and sad for a moment at the empty couches, then slid to the floor and fell into each other’s arms, as if only seeing each other’s wide awake faces could prove they were not still dreaming. When they spotted Lona, they ran toward her, radiant with bliss, to wrap her in a group hug.
Odu caught sight of the leopardess curled at the sleeping princess’s feet, and bounded over to her. He threw an arm over the great cat’s sleeping head, hugging and kissing her fur. “Wake up, wake up, darling!” he cried. “It is time to wake!”
The leopardess did not stir.
“She has slept herself cold!” he said to Mara, looking up at her with wide, confused, imploring eyes.
“She is waiting for the princess to wake, my child,” Mara told him.
Odu glanced over at the couch of the princess, and spotted two of his friends, Peter and Crispy, still fast asleep beside her. He ran at them, shaking them by the shoulders. “Wake up! Wake up!” he cried, tugging at their arms, first one, then the other. Soon his face fell, and he turned to me, eyes misty with tears. “They will not wake. Why are they so cold?”
“They are waiting for the princess too,” I told him.
He stretched out a hand to touch her cheek. “She is cold too! What is wrong?” he asked, looking around in dismay.
Adam knelt down in front of him. “Her wake is not ripe yet. She is busy forgetting all the sharp, hurtful things of her old life. When she has forgotten enough, then she will remember all the good things she ought to keep, and then she will wake fast.”
“Remember?”
“Yes. Not all at once, though.”
“But the golden cock already crowed!” Odu argued, and threw himself back at his sleeping friends. “Peter! Peter! Crispy! Wake up, Peter! Wake up, Crispy! We are all awake but you two! The golden cock crowed so loud! The sun is awake and coming! Oh, why won’t you wake?”
But Peter would not wake, neither would Crispy, and Odu burst out crying at last.
“Let them sleep, little one,” Adam said, stroking his hair. “You would not want the princess to wake up and find no one waiting for her, would you? They are perfectly happy. So is the leopardess.”
Odu wiped his tears, as if he had been doing it his whole life, though these were the first real tears he had ever had—soon to be wiped away forever, never to return.
We followed Eve out to the cottage. She offered us no bread, no wine, only stood glowing, the whole shape of her radiating gentle invitation for us to go onward. We never said a word of farewell; none was needed. We stepped out the door, to find our old horse and the three great elephants waiting for us, as we had left them. We were too full of unutterable joy to climb onto their backs, and so they followed at our heels, soft and steady, as we set off toward the rising sun.
