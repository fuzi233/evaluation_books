CHAPTER XII. In Which a Shooting Match Is Proposed—But Not Quite as Planned

Let us now turn our attention to Mr. Garbetts, Principal Tragedian, a man of considerable physical presence and even more considerable appetite for rum, who was also known for his theatrical flair, his love of good company, and his unfortunate habit of getting into scrapes that required the intervention of equally reckless friends. He was, in short, the kind of fellow you’d find at the Magpie Hotel on a Thursday evening—loud, dramatic, and always ready to lend a hand (or a signature) when a bill needed backing.

Mr. Garbetts and Captain Costigan were bosom companions in both conviviality and financial misadventure. They had shared many a bottle, many a debt, and many a whispered plot involving the transfer of one another’s names onto promissory notes. Their friendship was strong, though not without its complications—chiefly because Miss Fotheringay, the Captain’s daughter, did not care for Mr. Garbetts, and Mrs. Garbetts, being a woman of delicate sensibilities, took great exception to her husband’s frequent visits to the Costigan household. The truth of the matter was that Garbetts had once courted Miss Fotheringay with all the earnestness of a Shakespearean lover—only to be rebuffed with the briskness of a stage mother dismissing a suitor. That slight had left a lingering sore spot, but we needn’t dwell on it; let’s just say that Garbetts was summoned by Captain Costigan shortly after young Arthur Pendennis and Miss Costigan’s friend Mr. Bows had departed from the house.

“Run, Tommy,” said the Captain to the small boy who served as their personal courier, “and fetch Mr. Garbetts from his lodgings above the tripe shop—ye know the place—and tell ’em to send two hot glasses of whisky-and-water from the Grapes.”

Tommy obeyed, and soon enough Garbetts arrived, puffing slightly from the climb, accompanied by the scent of spirits and the sound of his own booming voice.

Captain Costigan did not go into the full details of what had transpired earlier—though the reader is well aware of them. Instead, he poured out a generous measure of whisky-and-water and began composing a letter of such fiery intensity that it could have set a barn ablaze if it had been delivered to its intended recipient.

This missive, addressed to Major Pendennis, demanded that the gentleman either consent immediately to the marriage between Arthur Pendennis and Miss Fotheringay—or else offer the satisfaction due between gentlemen of honour. Should the Major refuse, the Captain threatened to administer justice himself, using a horsewhip as his instrument of choice. The tone was, naturally, rather grandiose, and the letter was sealed with the family silver seal—the only piece of ancestral plate the Captain possessed, which he kept wrapped in velvet like a sacred relic.

Garbetts was then dispatched with this letter and the weighty responsibility of delivering it. Before he left, the Captain gripped his arm with solemn gravity and declared, “May Heaven bless ye, General.”

Then, once the messenger was gone, Costigan retrieved his ancient duelling pistols—flint-locked relics that had seen action in Dublin’s most famous duels—and inspected them with the care of a man preparing for battle. Satisfied, he pulled out all of Arthur Pendennis’s letters and poems from the drawer, those tender missives he had carefully hoarded as proof of affection and betrayal alike. He read them over again, perhaps to steel himself for the confrontation ahead.

But minutes later, Garbetts returned—not with news of triumph, but with a face as long as a funeral procession.

“Ye’ve seen ’um?” asked the Captain, eyes gleaming with anticipation.

“Yes,” replied Garbetts.

“And when is it for?” asked Costigan, testing the lock of one pistol and bringing it up to his eye—what he called “his bloodshot orb.”

“What do you mean—‘when is it for’?” asked Garbetts, confused.

“The meeting, my dear fellow!” roared Costigan. “The duel!”

“You don’t mean to say you want mortal combat?” said Garbetts, recoiling as if struck. “You’re not serious, Captain?”

“What the devil else do I mean?” bellowed Costigan. “I want to shoot the man who has trampled upon my honor—or else I’ll fall dead myself upon the sod!”

“I’ll not carry challenges,” said Garbetts firmly. “I’m a family man. I have children. I will have nothing to do with pistols.” And with that, he flung the letter down—its elaborate superscription and smudged seal landing dramatically on the floor.

Costigan stared in disbelief. “Ye don’t mean to say ye saw ’um and didn’t give ’um the letter?”

“I saw him,” said Garbetts, “but I couldn’t speak with him.”

“And why not?”

“There was one there I cared not to meet,” said Garbetts in a voice like a tombstone. “And neither would you.”

“The minion Tatham was there?” cried Costigan.

“The cowardly scoundrel!” he roared. “He’s frightened already and going to swear peace against me.”

“I’ll have nothing to do with fighting,” repeated Garbetts, “and I wish I hadn’t seen Tatham nor that bit of——”

“Hold your tongue, Bob Acres!” snapped Costigan, quoting Sir Lucius O’Trigger, a role he had played with distinction both on and off the stage. After some heated exchange, the two parted ways—neither particularly pleased with the other.

Now, as the reader may have guessed, we cannot reproduce the exact wording of the letter, for a very simple reason: it was never opened. Major Pendennis never received it. Why? Because Garbetts refused to deliver it—and no one else dared.

When Miss Costigan returned home from rehearsal, escorted by the ever-present Mr. Bows, she found her father pacing wildly about the room, reeking of whisky-and-water and despair. The Pendennis papers lay scattered across the table, beside empty goblets and a teaspoon that had clearly seen better days.

As Emily entered, her father seized her in a crushing embrace. “Prepare yourself, me child, me blessed child!” he wailed, tears streaming down his cheeks. “It’s all over!”

“Ye’re tipsy again, Papa,” said Emily, pushing him away gently. “Ye promised not to take spirits before dinner.”

“It’s to forget me sorrows,” he sobbed. “To drown me care!”

“Your care takes a deal of drowning, Captain dear,” remarked Bows, mimicking Costigan’s accent. “What’s happened? Has that soft-spoken gentleman in the wig been vexing you?”

“The oily miscreant!” roared Costigan. “I’ll have his blood!”

Miss Milly, meanwhile, had retreated to her room to remove her bonnet and shawl. She came back looking perfectly composed—unlike her father, who was now trembling with rage and grief. In her hands she carried a pair of white satin shoes, which she intended to polish with bread crumbs in preparation for her performance as Ophelia that coming Tuesday.

She glanced at the papers on the table, paused, then decided against asking any questions. Instead, she selected a suitable piece of bread from the cupboard and sat down at the table with her shoes and her task.

“Papa,” she said eventually, “what have ye got all these letters and poetry of Master Arthur’s out for? Sure ye don’t want to read that nonsense again?”

“O Emilee!” cried Costigan, “that boy whom I loved as the boy of mee bosom is only a scoundrel, a deceiver! A liar!”

He turned to Bows, who looked on with mild concern.

“He’s been acting the part of a viper in this house, a traitor in this familee!” Costigan continued. “I tell ye—he’s no better than an impostor!”

“What has he done, Papa?” asked Emily calmly.

“Done? He has deceived us in the most atrocious manner!” said Costigan. “He pretended to be a man of property—two thousand a year, mind you—but it turns out he’s a pauper! A dependent on his mother’s five hundred pounds! How dare he ask ye to marry into a family that can’t provide for ye?”

Emily looked thoughtful, polishing her shoe slowly. “Sure, if he’s no money, there’s no use marrying him, Papa.”

“Why did the villain say he was rich?” asked Costigan.

“The poor fellow always said he was poor,” replied Emily. “’Twas you who insisted he was rich—and made me agree to take him.”

“He should have been explicit,” said Costigan. “A young man who rides a blood mare and gives presents of shawls and bracelets is an impostor if he has no money. As for his uncle—bedad, I’ll pull off his wig whenever I see ’um!”

Bows chuckled. “Bedad, you may send somebody else with the message. I’m a fiddler, not a fighting man.”

“Pooh, you’ve no spirit!” roared Costigan. “I’ll be my own second. I’ll take my case of pistols and shoot ’um in the Coffee-room of the George!”

“And so poor Arthur has no money?” sighed Emily.

“Poor lad, he was a good lad too,” she added softly. “Wild and talking nonsense with his verses and pothry, but a brave, generous boy. And indeed, I liked him—and he liked me too.”

“Why don’t you marry him if you like him so?” asked Bows, a little sharply.

“He is not more than ten years younger than you are,” he went on. “His mother may relent. You might live at Fairoaks Park. Why not go and be a lady? I could keep playing the fiddle, and the General live on his half-pay. Why don’t you marry him? You know he likes you.”

“There’s others that like me as well,” said Miss Milly, still polishing. “That have no money and that’s old enough.”

“Yes, d—— it,” said Bows, cursing under his breath. “Old enough and poor enough and fools enough for anything.”

“There’s old fools, and young fools too,” retorted Emily, giving him a knowing look. “You’ve often said so, you silly man.”

“If Pendennis has not enough money to live upon, it’s folly to talk about marrying him,” she concluded. “And that’s the long and short of it.”

“And the boy?” asked Bows.

“By Jove!” he exclaimed. “You throw a man away like an old glove, Miss Costigan.”

“I don’t know what you mean,” she replied placidly, turning to the second shoe. “If he had half of the two thousand a year that Papa gave him, or even half of that, I would marry him. But what’s the good of taking on with a beggar? We’re poor enough already. There’s no use in my going to live with an old lady that’s testy and cross, maybe, and would grudge me every morsel of meat.”

“And then,” she added simply, “suppose there was a family? Why, Papa, we shouldn’t be as well off as we are now.”

“’Deed, then, you wouldn’t, Milly dear,” answered her father.

“And there’s an end to all the fine talk about Mrs. Arthur Pendennis of Fairoaks Park—the member of Parliament’s lady,” said Milly with a laugh. “Pretty carriages and horses we should have to ride! That you were always talking about, Papa! But it’s always the same. If a man looked at me, you fancied he was going to marry me; and if he had a good coat, you fancied he was as rich as Croesus.”

“Well, call ’um what ye like,” said Bows. “But it’s a fact now that Papa has married me these eight years a score of times. Wasn’t I to be my Lady Poldoody of Oystherstown Castle? Then there was the Navy Captain at Portsmouth, and the old surgeon at Norwich, and the Methodist preacher here last year, and who knows how many more? Well, I bet a penny, with all your scheming, I shall die Milly Costigan at last.”

So poor little Arthur has no money? Stop and take dinner, Bows; we’ve a beautiful beef-steak pudding.”

Bows watched her closely. “I wonder whether she is on with Sir Derby Oaks,” he thought. “The dodges of women beat all comprehension. And I am sure she wouldn’t let the lad off so easily if she hadn’t some other scheme on hand.”

It must be said that Miss Fotheringay, though quiet and not much given to literary discourse, could hold her own in domestic matters. She wasn’t romantic, nor was she deeply versed in poetry—she hadn’t opened a Shakespeare since leaving the stage, and frankly didn’t understand it. But when it came to puddings, needlework, or family affairs, she was as sharp as a tack. Unmoved by passion or imagination, she kept her head cool where others lost theirs.

Over dinner, Costigan tried to argue that the Major’s statement about Pen’s finances was false—a trick to make them break off the match. But Miss Milly would hear none of it. “It’s not deceit on their side,” she said. “It’s deception on yours, Papa. Poor little Pen didn’t try to fool us. It was you who deceived yourself.”

She ate her dinner with great relish, while Bows watched her with a mixture of admiration and contempt.

Afterward, they discussed how best to end the affair. Costigan’s idea of pulling the Major’s nose faded with the last drop of whisky-and-water. He became submissive to his daughter, ready to follow her lead.

“What’ll we say to the lad if he stays steady and we break off?” asked Costigan.

“What? Don’t you know how to throw a man over?” said Bows. “Ask a woman to tell you?”

Miss Fotheringay explained it simply: “Papa writes to Arthur, asks about settlements and income. Arthur replies honestly. Then papa writes back and says it’s not enough. The match ends.”

“And of course,” added Bows, “you enclose a parting line saying you’ll always regard him as a brother.”

“Of course,” said Milly. “And so I shall. He’s a most worthy young man, I’m sure.”

Then she reached into a drawer, pulled out a bundle of newspapers—County Chronicle and Chatteris Champion—and extracted the pages where Arthur had written glowing verses about her portrayal of Imogen. She folded the letters and poems neatly, tied them with string like a parcel of sugar, and placed them aside.

Not a tremor passed through her. No sigh, no tear. She had just packed away the heart of a young man who had poured his soul into those pages—hours of longing, devotion, sleepless nights—all bundled up like grocery.

She then sat down to make tea, her heart perfectly calm, while far away, Arthur Pendennis was still dreaming of her.
