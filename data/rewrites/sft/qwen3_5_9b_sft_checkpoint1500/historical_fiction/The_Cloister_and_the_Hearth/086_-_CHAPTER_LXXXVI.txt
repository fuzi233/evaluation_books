The sanctuary, dappled with shafts of sunlight and draped in a canopy of celestial purple, soothed Father Clement's weary soul. It lifted his thoughts directly from the dust of the road to the presence of the Divine Architect. Yet, as his gaze swept across the vast nave, it was seized by the sea of humanity filling the aisle—a thousand heads bowed beneath snowy caps, intricately filigreed with gold. Though he had traversed many hundreds of leagues since leaving home, nothing had ever struck him with such profound majesty save perhaps the silent expanse of winter snow. Earlier, in the morning, his voice had cracked with thunderous denunciations; yet now, under the soft, golden light of afternoon, those stern threats rang hollow, discordant with the peace surrounding him. A deep, aching tenderness welled within his chest for this multitude. These were poor souls, hearing far too little of divine charity from the pulpit in their day, drowned instead by the clamor of sin and worldly need. Today, however, he spoke only of the glad tidings of salvation. The congregation leaned forward, captivated by the gentle, earnest cadence of his tongue.

Clement was not among those preachers who spun like weathercocks atop steeples, rattling their bodies to distract the ear. His influence worked through the quiet movement of hearts rather than the agitation of flesh. Warm with this thought—that not a single soul in that throng should miss the joyous news of Christ's love—he turned slowly toward the south aisle.

And there, bathed in a river of sunlight pouring through the window, stood the radiant countenance of Margaret Brandt. He fixed his gaze upon her, and the sensation that followed was akin to physical numbness, freezing both his soul and his limbs.

Soon, the words lodged in his throat, dissolving into silence. Trembling, he stared at her. There, with her auburn hair catching the beams like the halo of a saint, her face aglow with an inner fire doubled by the sun, stood the woman he loved until death. She leaned lightly against a white marble column, her lashes cast low in a posture of tender, humble listening. He recalled seeing her in that same pose a hundred times, back in the days when life had been bright and unburdened. Nothing had changed. If anything, she was a shade more mature, a depth richer in loveliness.

Then, with a start that made his eyes widen in horror and his cheeks drain of color, he realized the impossibility of it. He gazed around, expecting the crowd to fade away, to hear a rustle of rising voices, yet he could not tear his prodigy-struck eyes from that face—all vitality, all bloom, all beauty, framed by that glorious, shimmering hair. He watched her, convinced she must vanish like a phantom, yet she remained solid and real.

Suddenly, she lifted her head. Her violet eyes met his, full and piercing. At the shock of that encounter, Clement was beside himself. His lips parted to scream her name, but she turned swiftly, slipping away into the shadows before he could utter a sound. As she vanished, she offered him one final, fleeting glance. It was swift as lightning, yet it held a weight that sent his mind reeling in a dizzy whirl, leaving him panting and clutching the pulpit convulsively. That look, though devoid of clear recognition, was the startled, inquiring, indescribable expression that always precedes it. It froze him in a trance. With a mighty, desperate effort, he stammered out a few incoherent syllables that no one understood, then forced himself to resume the discourse. He faltered and babbled for a moment, struggling to anchor himself, until gradually, with her out of sight, he managed to view the apparition as a vision from the other world. Rising into a state of unnatural, electric excitement, he delivered his conclusion with an eloquence that bordered on rhapsody, electrifying the simple hearts of his listeners.

When the sermon finally ended, Clement sat heavily upon the pulpit stool, terribly shaken. But quickly, an idea peculiar to the age took hold of him. He reasoned that since he had sought her grave at Sevenbergen in vain, she must now be permitted to reveal herself to him in this new place. Perhaps she was buried right here, close to that same pillar where her spirit had appeared. Once this notion took root, it settled upon his mind with the absolute certainty of fact. He felt compelled to speak to the sexton—the same poor lad whom he had once saved—who was currently working outside, minding the yard.

The church was now empty. Climbing down from the pulpit, Clement stepped through an opening in the south wall onto the grass and approached the sexton. He recognized the boy instantly, though Jorian could not possibly suspect the shapely-bearded knight he had rescued was now hidden beneath the guise of a holy friar. Time had altered him profoundly: the loss of his beard had reshaped his features, while his tonsure, short gray hair, and pale, gaunt cheeks—marked by long fasts and vigils—had completed his transformation.

"My son," said Friar Clement softly, "if you retain any memory of those whom you have laid in the earth, pray tell me, is any Christian buried within the church, near one of the pillars?"

"Nay, father," replied Jorian, "here in the churchyard lie buried all who rest here. Why ask?"

"No matter. Pray tell me, then, where lieth Margaret Brandt."

"Margaret Brandt?" Jorian stared at the speaker with a look of stupefaction. "She died about three years ago, and was buried here."

"Oh, that is another matter," said Jorian, his tone shifting. "That was before my time. The vicar might tell you, likely. If she was a gentlewoman, or at least wealthy enough to pay him his fee."

"Alas, my son, she was poor—and paid a heavy penalty for it—but she was born of decent folk. Her father, Peter, was a learned physician. She came hither from Sevenbergen—to die."

As Clement uttered these words, his head sank upon his breast. He seemed to lose all power or desire to press Jorian further. Doubtfully, he wondered if he truly knew where he stood; he was utterly lost in the past.

Jorian lowered his spade and stood upright in the fresh earth, arms akimbo. "Are you making a fool of me, holy sir," he said sulkily, "or has some wag been making a fool of you!" Having vented his irritation, he resumed digging with a vigor that betrayed a ruffled temper.

Clement looked at him with a puzzled, gently reproachful eye. The tone was rude, the words unintelligible. Good-natured though crusty, Jorian had not thrown up three spadefuls before shame washed over him. "Why, what a base churl am I to speak thus to thee, holy father," he blurted out, "and thou standest there looking at me like a lamb!" A sudden realization struck him. "Aha! I have it! 'Tis Peter Brandt's grave you would fain see, not Margaret's. He does lie here, hard by the west door. There; I'll show you."

He laid down his spade and donned his doublet and jerkin to accompany the friar. He did not know that anyone was sitting upon Peter's tomb, still less that she was waiting specifically for this holy friar. Nor did Pietro Vanucci or Andrea recognize him without his beard. Indeed, a beard that has never known a razor grows in a picturesque, characteristic form, becoming a defining feature of the face; its removal can sometimes serve as an effective disguise, obscuring identity behind the familiar lines of youth and vigor.
