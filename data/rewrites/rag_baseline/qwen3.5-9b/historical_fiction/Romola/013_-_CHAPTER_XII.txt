"Prize nearly grasped," the title declared, yet for Tito, the victory felt less like a conquest and more like a precarious balance. The paralyzing dread that had gripped him earlier had evaporated, replaced by the buoyant rhythm of his natural disposition. He was moving toward Romola, drawn by a magnetic pull he could no longer resist. Yet, as he visualized her, he saw only the epitome of dutiful endurance—a woman defined by patient suffering and the acceptance of irksome burdens. This was precisely the path he had fled, the very terrain from which he had constructed his excuses.

But Tito was not a man devoid of virtue, nor was he prepared to surrender entirely to vice. He was still in the flush of youth, his pulse quickened by every charm and loveliness he encountered. He retained a robust appetite for the ordinary joys of humanity. The poison of corruption worked slowly, seeping in drop by drop. He had sold himself to evil, but currently, life appeared unchanged to him; he remained oblivious to the invisible chains binding him. His intention was to maintain the status quo, both internally and externally. He planned to win the admiration of others through meritorious effort, through ingenious scholarship, and through compliant amiability. He sought no disruption of the harmony he shared with those he cherished. Above all, he loved Romola; he envisioned her as his beautiful, loving wife. While a wealthier alliance might theoretically await someone of his eventual success, no woman in Florence possessed the radiance of Romola. When she stood near him, regarding him with her sincere hazel eyes, he was overwhelmed by a delicious influence as potent and inevitable as a musical vibration that commands a rhythmic empire, demanding repetition the moment it ceases.

As he ascended the stone stairs, still outside the threshold, accompanied only by Maso, this anticipatory influence began its work simply through proximity.

"Welcome, Tito mio," the old man's voice called out before Tito could speak. There was a renewed vigor in the tone, a fresh cheerfulness illuminating the blind face, a transformation evident since their first meeting over two months prior. "You have surely brought fresh manuscripts; but since we spoke last night, I have conceived new ideas. We must broaden our scope—we must retrace our steps."

Tito rendered his customary homage to Romola as he advanced, proceeding directly to Bardo's chair. As was his habit, he placed his hand into the palm extended to receive it, settling onto the cross-legged leather seat with its scrolled ends, positioned close to Bardo's elbow.

"Yes," Tito replied in his gentle manner. "I have brought the new manuscript, but that can wait until your pleasure dictates. I possess young limbs, you know, and can walk back up the hill without difficulty."

He avoided looking at Romola, yet he knew with absolute certainty that her gaze was fixed upon him with delight.

"That is well said, my son," Bardo remarked, having adopted this form of address for Tito on several recent occasions. "And I perceive with gladness that you do not shrink from labor. Without it, the poet has wisely observed, life offers nothing to mortals. Too often, young ambition is seduced by the 'palma sine pulvere,' the prize of glory without the dust of the race. But what does the Greek say? 'In the morning of life, work; in the mid-day, give counsel; in the evening, pray.' It is true, I might be thought to have reached that helpless evening; but I am not so, while I retain counsel within me that remains unspoken. For my mind, as I have often stated, was sealed shut like a dam; the plentiful waters lay dark and motionless behind it. But you, my Tito, have opened a duct for them, and they surge forward with a force that surprises even myself. And now, what I require is that we revisit our preliminary grounds with a wider scheme of commentary and illustration. Otherwise, I risk losing opportunities I now recognize retrospectively, chances that may never recur. Do you understand what I am saying, Tito?"

Bardo had just stooped to retrieve his manuscript, which had rolled away, and his jealous ear caught the slightest movement. Tito might have been excused for shrugging at the prospect ahead, but he was not naturally impatient. Furthermore, he had been raised in that laborious erudition, simultaneously minute and copious, which constituted the primary intellectual pursuit of the age. With Romola nearby, he was carried along by waves of agreeable sensation that rendered every task effortless.

"Assuredly," Tito responded. "You wish to expand your comments on certain passages we have cited."

"Not merely that," Bardo corrected. "I intend to introduce occasional *excursus* where we discuss authors to whom I have devoted special study. For I may die too soon to produce any separate work. Moreover, this is not a season for scholarly integrity and sifted learning to remain idle. We must fear not only rash ignorance, but men like Calderino. As Poliziano has demonstrated, they resort to impudent falsifications of citation to serve their vanity and secure triumphs for their own errors. Therefore, my Tito, I deem it ill-advised to let slip the opportunity lying beneath our hands. Let us return to the point where we cited the passage from Thucydides. First, I wish you to accompany me through all my notes on the Latin translation by Lorenzo Valla. The incomparable Pope Nicholas the Fifth—with whose personal notice I was honored while I was still young, when he was yet Thomas of Sarzana—paid him, I say not unduly, the sum of five hundred gold scudi. Yet, since Valla, though otherwise of dubious fame, is held in high honor for his severe scholarship, whence the epigrammatist jocosely noted that since he descended to the shades, Pluto himself dares not speak in the ancient languages, it becomes imperative that his name not serve as a stamp warranting false goods. Thus, I propose introducing an *excursus* on Thucydides, wherein my castigation of Valla's text may find its proper place. My Romola, you shall fetch the necessary volumes—you know them—on the fifth shelf of the cabinet."

Tito rose simultaneously with Romola, declaring, "I will fetch them, provided you indicate their location," and hurried into the adjoining small room. Here, the walls were lined with ranges of books arranged in perfect order.

"There they are," Romola pointed upward. "Every book rests exactly where it was when my father ceased to see them."

Tito stood beside her, delaying the act of reaching for the books. They had never occupied this space together before.

"I hope," she continued, turning her eyes fully upon Tito with a look of grave confidence—"I hope he will not weary you; this work brings him such happiness."

"And me too, Romola—if you will only allow me to say, I love you—if you will only consider me worthy of a little love."

His words emerged as the softest murmur. The dark, beautiful face, drawn closer to hers than ever before, regarded her with beseeching tenderness.

"I do love you," Romola whispered. She looked at him with the same simple majesty as always, yet her voice had never before sunk to such a murmur. It seemed to both of them that they gazed into each other's souls for a prolonged duration before her lips moved again; yet it was but a fleeting moment until she confessed, "I know *now* what it is to be happy."

Their faces met, and the dark curls mingled for an instant with the rippling gold. Swift as lightning thereafter, Tito stepped onto a projecting ledge of the bookshelf and retrieved the required volumes. Both were content to remain silent and separate, for that first blissful experience of mutual consciousness was all the more exquisite for being unperturbed by immediate sensation.

Everything had unfolded as rapidly as the irreversible mingling of waters; even the eager and jealous Bardo had not grown impatient.

"You have the volumes, my Romola?" the old man asked as they returned. "Now prepare your pen. Since Tito marks off the scholia we decide to extract, it is essential that you copy them without delay—mind numbering them carefully to correspond with the numbers in the text which he will write."

Romola always possessed some task granting her a share in this joint endeavor. Tito took his stand at the *leggio*, where he both wrote and read, while she positioned herself at a table just in front of him, ready to deliver anything her father might request or relieve him of a volume he had finished. They had always maintained this arrangement since the work commenced, yet today it felt novel. It was so different now for them to sit opposite one another; so different for Tito to take a book from her as she lifted it from her father's knee. Yet, there was no finesse employed to secure an additional glance or touch. Each woman creates in her own likeness the tokens of love offered to her; and Romola's deep, calm happiness enveloped Tito like the rich, quiet light of an evening that dissipates all unrest.

Two hours had passed in their work, and they were just pausing as the light began to fade, when the door opened and a figure entered, strikingly incongruous with the current of their thoughts and the suggestions of every object in the room. It was the silhouette of a short, stout woman with black eyes, approximately fifty years of age. She wore a black velvet *berretta*, or close cap, embroidered with pearls, beneath which surprisingly massive black braids surmounted a little bulging forehead and fell in rich, plaited curves over her ears.
