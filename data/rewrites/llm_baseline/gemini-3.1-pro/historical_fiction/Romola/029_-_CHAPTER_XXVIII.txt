Four days later, the sonic tapestry of Florence had fractured. As Romola navigated the Via Gualfonda, her ancestral memory tuned acutely into the discordant acoustics of occupation. The streets were lined with Frenchmen and Florentines locked in a tense, silent observation, but it was the auditory pollution that frayed Romola’s psychological well-being. The French brought with them a grating, metallic clatter—iron-shod boots on ancient stone, the harsh, nasal dominance of a foreign lexicon drowning out the melodic, agrarian vowels of the native Florentines. It was a language of conquest, a dominant syntax threatening to erase the subtle nuances of the local dialect, asserting an air of conscious pre-eminence over the subjugated city. 

The new Charlemagne, a mythic figure intruding violently upon the domestic hum of the republic, regarded Florence as a conquered prize. The resulting consultations in the Old Palace bled into the streets, a low, rumbling bass-note of rebellion against the Most Christian King’s insolent guests. Romola, shrouded in her black veil, kept her ears tuned to the protective, rhythmic shuffle of old Maso beside her, seeking sanctuary from the abrasive power dynamics of the streets. 

She was impatient to reach the house of Piero di Cosimo. Old Maso carried a basket that was itself a sensory archive of Florence’s cultural memory—a rebellion against the current strife. It held meticulously sourced pastries, baked to the precise, time-honored specifications of agrarian elders. There were *cavallucci* spiced with toasted coriander and candied melon, and *confetti* made from almonds roasted over slow-burning olive-wood coals, encased in a fragile shell of crystallized honey harvested from the wild rosemary slopes of Fiesole. Romola knew Piero was no mere utilitarian consumer of sustenance; beneath his gruff exterior lay the soul of a passionate food historian, a man who found profound meaning and grounding in the complex olfactory landscapes of traditional fare.

She lifted the iron slide of his door, her knock a deliberate, flute-like rhythm in the heavy air. 

Piero answered with defensive swiftness. "Ah, Madonna Romola, is it you? I thought my eggs were come; I wanted the yolks for my tempera."

"I have brought you something better than hard eggs, Piero," Romola said, smiling as she put back her veil. She took the basket from Maso, allowing the rich, heady aroma of anise and warm, spiced sugar to fill the vestibule. "I know you like these things when you can have them without trouble. Confess you do."

"Yes, when they come to me as easily as the light does," Piero grumbled, folding his arms. But his gaze lingered on the sweetmeats with a connoisseur’s deep appreciation, taking in the golden hues and the textural promise of the crusts before lifting his eyes to her face. "And they are come along with the light now. But I know what the sweetmeats are for—to stop my mouth while you scold me. Go on into the next room. I’ve done something to the picture, though I didn’t promise. I take care not to promise in a city where art is treated like cheap, unleavened bread sold by the gross." He muttered bitterly about the superficial commercialization of his craft, the patrons who demanded soulless pageants and floating machinery rather than the slow, deliberate layering of truth. 

The door to the wild garden was closed, preserving the studio as an acoustic sanctuary. Piero stooped to lift the heavy portrait of her father, Oedipus, seeking the proper light. But in moving the large canvas, a smaller oil sketch was suddenly exposed. 

Romola flushed with astonishment. "That is Tito!"

Piero looked round, a silent shrug attempting to mask his vexation at his own forgetfulness. 

Romola stared at the sketch, the mythic dread of the image bleeding seamlessly into her domestic reality. It captured her husband in a moment of visceral terror, gripped by a ragged prisoner with a rope around his neck. "What a strange picture!" she said, her voice tight with puzzled alarm. "When did you paint it? What does it mean?"

"A mere fancy of mine," Piero said, scratching his head and retreating behind a flattened, dismissive tone. "I wanted a handsome young face for it, and your husband’s was just the thing." He attempted to exert control over the narrative, turning the canvas away to re-establish the room's equilibrium. 

But Romola’s mind was flooded with a terrifying synchronicity, a circular pacing of memory. "Don’t put it away; let me look again. That man with the rope—I saw him. I saw you come to him in the Duomo. What was it that made you put him into a picture with Tito?"

Piero retreated into a sterile vocabulary, trying to diminish the power of the image. "It was a mere accident. The man was running, stumbled on the steps, and caught hold of your husband. I thought the savage-looking old fellow was a good subject. It’s worth nothing—a freakish daub." He shoved the sketch onto a high shelf. "Come and look at the Oedipus."

His hasty, forced casualness only deepened the resonance of the secret. Romola felt the inherent untranslatability of the subjective terror pooling in her chest; the power dynamics embedded in Piero's dismissive lexicon could not erase the visual truth he had painted. Her pride shrank from interrogating him further. She merely asked, in a quiet, fragile tone, "He was a strange, piteous-looking man. Do you know anything more of him?"

"No more. I showed him to the hospital. See, now, the face of Oedipus is nearly finished."

Romola gave her whole attention to the portrait of her blind father. The painting was a profound study in acoustic ecology—the sightless eyes traded for a deeply attuned ear, a man who had navigated the world through its vibrations and echoes. Looking at it, she felt the agonizing erosion of cultural memory, the loss of her father akin to the death of a native language only they had spoken. 

"Ah," she whispered, the dialect of her grief softening her vowels. "You have done what I wanted. You have given it more of the listening look. My good Piero, I am very grateful."

"Now that’s what I can’t bear in you women," Piero snapped, kicking impatiently at the brushes littering the floor, disrupting the quiet of the room. "Pouring out feelings where there’s no call for them! I paint for my own pleasure and credit. Are you to thank a man for not being a rogue? But women think walls are held together with honey."

"You crusty Piero! I forgot how snappish you are. Here, put this in your mouth," Romola said, smiling through her tears. She handed him one of the meticulously crafted *cavallucci*. 

Piero took it, biting down. The crisp, caramelized exterior shattered loudly in the quiet room, yielding to the dense, mastic-scented almond paste within. It was a tactile, gustatory rush of sweetness, the complex notes of clove and honey flooding his palate. He chewed with naive, reverent enjoyment, the centuries-old recipe grounding him instantly. "It’s good, Madonna Antigone," he murmured, his fingers reaching for another, breaking his fortnight fast of sterile, hard-boiled eggs. 

"Good-bye, Piero," she said gently. "I promise not to thank you if you finish the portrait soon."

As the heavy door closed behind her, Romola was swallowed once more by the oppressive auditory pollution of the streets. The clanking of French armor echoed against the stone, a harsh, metallic rhythm that matched the circular, agonizing pacing of her thoughts. The chain armor Tito had secretly adopted. The encounter on the steps on that very same morning. The look of raw, unadulterated terror Piero had captured. 

*What could it all mean?* 

"It means nothing," she tried to translate to herself, but the words were a dead language, devoid of truth. *Shall I ask Tito about it?* 

Her mind insisted she must not violate the trust she owed him, yet her heart whispered the devastating reality: *I dare not ask him.* To speak of it would be to introduce a destructive new vocabulary into their domestic routine. There was a terrible flaw in the bedrock of their marriage, and she moved through the deafening streets with the careful, breathless hesitation of one holding a fragile, spun-sugar confection, terrified that a single misplaced vibration would shatter it beyond repair.
