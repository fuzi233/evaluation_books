Though she had clearly come off the loser in the confrontation recounted in the previous chapter, the Baroness Bernstein showed no trace of resentment when she next encountered her niece. With a calmness that belied the storm they had weathered, she addressed Lady Maria with an air of detached amusement.

“Of course, my Lady Maria,” she said, “you cannot expect me—being Harry Warrington’s near kin—to be delighted at the notion of his marrying a woman older than his mother and without a penny to her name. But if he chooses to make such a foolish match, it is none of my affair. I doubt, too, that I would have been taken seriously had I offered him five thousand pounds during our heated exchange. So it was already settled at Castlewood? Had I known how far matters had advanced, dear, I might have spared myself some unnecessary resistance. When a pitcher is broken, what use is there in railing?”

“Madam!” interjected Maria, her voice rising slightly. “Pardon me—I mean no disrespect to your ladyship’s honour or character, both of which, I am certain, are beyond reproach. Harry says so, and you say so—what more can one desire?”

“You’ve spoken with Mr. Warrington, then?” asked the Baroness.

“And he has admitted that he made you a promise at Castlewood—one confirmed in writing.”

“Indeed he has, madam,” replied Lady Maria firmly.

“Ah,” murmured the elder woman, unflinching. “And I confess I first misread the tone of your letters to him. They involve other members of the family—”

“Who have spoken ill of me and sought to turn Harry against me in every way. Yes, madam, I did write to defend myself, to justify my actions.”

“But naturally, you’re distressed at the thought that any rogue might possess damaging secrets about our family and expose them to public ridicule. That explains your agitation earlier.”

“Precisely,” said Lady Maria. “I could reveal nothing to Harry, for he is now my confidant. But that is very different from wishing the world to know the private quarrels of a noble house.”

“Upon my word, Maria, I admire you—and I’ve done you injustice these past twenty years.”

“I am heartily glad, madam, that you finally see me fairly,” replied the younger woman.

“When I saw you last night, dancing with my nephew, can you guess what crossed my mind?”

“I really have no idea what the Baroness de Bernstein might have thought,” said Lady Maria, lifting her chin.

“I recalled that you once danced that very tune with the dancing-master at Kensington, my dear.”

“Madam, that was an outrageous falsehood!”

“By which poor man received a beating for nothing!”

“It is cruel and unjust, madam, to revive such slander—and I shall request to leave this household if such talk continues,” declared Maria, with quiet dignity.

“You wish to return home? I can imagine Tunbridge will not suit you, especially if those letters surface.”

“There was not a single word against you in them, madam. On that point, I can assure you.”

“So Harry said—and gave you your due. Well, my dear, we’ve grown weary of each other. It may be best to part for a time.”

“That is exactly my own sentiment,” said Lady Maria, executing a sharp curtsey. “Mr. Sampson can escort you to Castlewood. You and your maid may take a postchaise.”

“We shall take the chaise,” echoed the younger woman. “And Mr. Sampson shall accompany me. You see, madam, I act as a dutiful niece.”

“Do you know,” the Baroness said frankly, “I suspect Sampson may have the letters?”

“The thought has occurred to me as well,” admitted Maria.

“And you intend to go home by chaise, hoping to coax them from him! Delilah! Well, they’re of no use to me. I trust you’ll succeed. When do you leave? The sooner, the better, you say? We are women of the world, Maria. We only hurl names in anger. We don’t want one another’s company—and we part on good terms. Shall we go to Lady Yarmouth’s? It’s her evening. A change of scene after such a nervous fit is just what you need, and cards drive away unpleasant thoughts better than any physician.”

Lady Maria agreed to attend Lady Yarmouth’s card party and was ready first, waiting in the drawing-room for her aunt. As Madame Bernstein descended, she noticed that Maria’s door stood open.

“She has the letters on her,” the old lady thought.

They left together, each in their own carriage, displaying toward one another the polished civility women often reserve for one another after even the most bitter disputes.

That evening, returning from the Countess’s gathering, Mrs. Brett, the Baroness’s maid, answered Lady Maria’s summons when she rang her bell before retiring.

Betty, Mrs. Brett confessed, was not fit to appear before her lady. She had spent the night carousing with Mr. Warrington’s black servant, Lord Bamborough’s valet, and several others of similar station. The liquor—Mrs. Brett was ashamed to admit—had proven too much for Betty.

Should she undress her lady?

My lady declined, saying she would manage alone, and allowed Mrs. Brett to retire.

“She has the letters in her stays,” thought Madame Bernstein.

They had exchanged a civil goodnight on the stairs.

The next morning, Betty faced a scolding from her mistress, having slept in the closet adjacent to Lady Maria’s room. She confessed, contritely, her fondness for rum-punch—a brew Mr. Gumbo was particularly skilled at preparing. She accepted the reprimand meekly and completed her duties before withdrawing.

Betty was one of the Castlewood girls who had become smitten with Gumbo, a handsome blue-eyed lass whom Mr. Case, the Baroness’s trusted man, also found appealing. Thus, between Gumbo and Case, jealousies had flared—so much so that Gumbo, though peaceable by nature, avoided the Baroness’s servants, one of whom swore he’d break Gumbo’s bones if he continued courting Betty.

Yet on the night of the punch, though Case caught Gumbo and Betty whispering in the doorway, cooled by the evening breeze, Gumbo—pale with fear—was met not with violence but charm. It was Case himself who proposed the bowl, brewed and consumed in Betty’s chamber, where Gumbo displayed his mastery of the drink. He praised Gumbo’s music. Though usually sober, Case insisted on drinking more and more until Betty became quite unsteady—precisely the state that drew her lady’s disapproval.

As for Case, who had slept outside, he was so ill from the punch that he remained in bed all day, only regaining strength enough to appear at supper-time, when his mistress gently rebuked him.

“Why, Case,” said Mr. Warrington, dining with the family, “I could have sworn it was you I saw riding hard down the London road this morning.”

“Me? Bless you, sir! I was abed, and my head felt like it would split. I ate a bit at six and drank small beer. I’m nearly myself again. But that Gumbo—bless you, I’ll never taste his punch again.”

The honest major-domo resumed his duties among the bottles and glasses.

After dinner, Madame Bernstein was cordial. She recommended strong tonics for Maria’s recurring fainting spells. These attacks were frequent. She urged her to consult her London physician and send a report via Harry.

“By Harry?” the young lady asked.

Yes. Harry was going to London for two days on business for his aunt.

“I won’t tell you, my dear, that it’s a task that will benefit him. I wish Mr. Draper to include him in my will. And since I shall be traveling soon, I think it safest to ask Mr. Warrington to carry my trinket-box to London in his postchaise. There’ve been robberies lately, and I’d rather not risk being stopped by highwaymen.”

Maria looked dismayed at the thought of Harry’s departure, but hoped he might escort her back to Castlewood, where her elder brother had returned.

“Nay,” said his aunt, “the boy has clung to us long enough. A day in London will do him no harm. He can run my errand and be back by Saturday.”

“I would offer to accompany Mr. Warrington,” said Mr. Sampson, “but I preach before her ladyship on Friday.” He wished Lady Yarmouth to judge his abilities as a preacher, and the Baroness had used her influence to secure an audience.

Harry welcomed the idea of a swift journey to London and a few days of sport. He promised his pistols were in good order and that he would deliver the diamonds safely to the banker’s vault.

Would he stay at his aunt’s London house?

No—that would be dreary, with only a housemaid and groom in charge. He preferred the Star and Garter in Pall Mall or an inn in Covent Garden.

“Oh!” said Harry, his face darkening. “I’ve often talked over this journey.”

“And with whom, sir?” asked Lady Maria.

“With one who promised to make it with me,” replied the young man, thinking, as he always did, of his lost brother with deep tenderness.

“He has more heart, my dear Maria, than some of us,” remarked Harry’s aunt, seeing the emotion in his eyes.

Uncontrollable waves of grief still swept over the young man. The memory of his brother’s death; the scene of George’s fall the previous year; the words they’d shared; the plans they’d made—all resurfaced and overwhelmed him.

“I doubt,” whispered the chaplain to Madame Bernstein after one such outburst, “whether some folks in England suffer so deeply at the loss of an elder brother.”

But sorrow, of course, could not endure forever. One could picture Mr. Warrington setting off on his journey with eager spirits, gay and relieved to be freed from his elderly attachment.

Yes. At Castlewood, on one unfortunate evening, he had offered his heart and himself to his mature cousin—and she had accepted the foolish lad’s proposal. But marriage was now impossible. He must consult his mother, the lifelong mistress of the Virginian estate. She would surely refuse consent to such a union. The decision was postponed, but the burden weighed heavily upon him, bringing remorse and unease.

No wonder his spirits lifted as he neared London, and he gazed joyfully from the windows of his postchaise at the city unfolding before him.

No highwayman halted him on Blackheath. There, gleaming beneath the trees, stood Greenwich’s domes. The Thames flowed below, crowded with ships. And there, unmistakably, rose the Tower of London.

“Look, Gumbo! There’s the Tower!”

“Yes, master,” said Gumbo, who had never heard of it.

But Harry had. He remembered reading about it in Howell’s *Medulla*, and how he and George used to play at being knights of the Tower. Now, he marveled at the prospect of seeing the armour, the jewels, the lions.

They passed through Southwark and over London Bridge—once lined with houses like a street, now reduced to a single gate, soon to vanish entirely. Then the chaise rolled through the city.

“Look, Gumbo—there’s Saint Paul’s!”

“Yes, master—Saint Paul’s,” said Gumbo, deferential but unmoved by the architecture.

So by familiar route, they reached the Temple, where Gumbo and his master stared up in awe at the rebel heads atop Temple Bar.

The chaise arrived at Mr. Draper’s chambers in Middle Temple Lane. Harry handed over the trinket-box and a letter from his aunt. The lawyer read the letter with interest and tucked it away carefully. He locked the box in his strong closet, then took his clerk into the adjoining room. Afterward, he offered to escort Mr. Warrington to an inn.

Covent Garden’s Bedford Hotel was chosen as the best lodging.

“I shall keep you for two or three days, Mr. Warrington,” said the lawyer. “The papers the Baroness requires won’t be ready yet. Meanwhile, I’m at your service to show you the town. I live outside, but I have a little cottage at Camberwell where I’d be honored to entertain you. Still, a young man likely prefers his inn and his freedom.”

Harry agreed. The postchaise, with a clerk inside, was sent ahead to the Bedford, while the two walked there together.

They sat and conversed. The Drapers had served the Esmond family for generations. The attorney regaled Harry with tales of his ancestors at Castlewood. Though no longer agent to the present Earl—whose father had fallen out with him—the firm still represented the Baroness, and Mr. Draper was pleased she favored her nephew.

As they prepared to leave, a young clerk approached his principal in the passage.

“If you please, sir, the Baroness’s papers were delivered to her man, Mr. Case, two days ago.”

“Mind your own affairs, Mr. Brown,” snapped the lawyer. “This way, Mr. Warrington. Our Temple stairs are dim. Allow me to guide you.”

Harry saw Draper cast a quick, angry glance at the clerk.

“So it was Case I saw on the London Road,” he thought. “What brought the old fox to London?”

He chose not to pry and let the matter drop.

Where should they go first? Harry wanted to visit the site where his grandfather and Lord Castlewood had fought a duel fifty-six years prior in Leicester Field. Mr. Draper knew the spot well and recounted the tale.

They could pass through Covent Garden on the way, ensuring Harry was comfortably settled.

“And order dinner,” suggested Harry.

“No,” said Draper firmly. “You must do me the honor of dining with me. In fact, I’ve already ordered a collation from the Cock.”

Harry could not refuse such a pressing invitation and walked away cheerfully, passing under the arch where the heads were, removing his hat in salute.

“They were gentlemen who died for their king,” he said. “My brother George and I always vowed we’d salute them when we saw them.”

“You’ll draw a mob if you do, sir,” warned the alarmed lawyer.

“Confound the mob, sir,” replied Harry loftily.

But the passers-by paid no heed. He walked up the bustling Strand, taking in every sight, remembering for the rest of his life the impressions of London, though keeping his emotions discreet. He did not care to reveal how deeply he was moved—or that he was a stranger.

He barely heard his companion’s chatter, though Draper spoke ceaselessly.

Nor was Draper displeased by Harry’s silence and haughty bearing. A hundred years ago, a gentleman was a gentleman, and his attorney his humble servant.

At the Bedford, the chamberlain bowed low, showing Harry to his rooms with delightful servility—for Gumbo had already announced his master’s greatness, and Draper’s clerk referred to the newcomer as “a high fellar.”

After inspecting the rooms, the two men went to Leicester Field, Gumbo strutting behind. They viewed the scene of the old wound and the tragedy of Lord Castlewood, then returned to the Temple.

Who was the shabby-looking man Harry bowed to as they left for a walk in the gardens?

That was Mr. Johnson, an author Harry had met at Tunbridge Wells.

“Take the advice of a man of the world, sir,” said Draper, eyeing the writer with disdain. “The less you associate with such folk, the better. Our office sees enough trouble with literary men.”

“Indeed,” said Harry.

He did not warm to his new friend as the latter grew more familiar.

The theatres were closed. Should they go to Sadler’s Wells, Marybone Gardens, or Ranelagh?

“Not Ranelagh,” said Draper. “There’s no nobility in town.” But seeing in the paper that Sadler’s Wells would feature a performance on eight hand-bells by Mr. Franklyn and Signora Catherina, Harry decided on Marybone Gardens—where there was music, tea, coffee, wine, and the constant stream of Draper’s conversation.

The lawyer’s obsequiousness ended at Harry’s bedroom door, where the young gentleman bid him good night with proud aloofness.

The next morning, Harry, dressed in brocade bedgown, enjoyed breakfast, read the newspaper, and savored his solitude.

He came across news from Virginia: *Williamsburg, June 7th.*

His eyes blurred. He had received letters from that packet, but his mother had not mentioned—

“A great number of the colony’s leading gentry have united under the command of the Honourable Peyton Randolph to march to the aid of their afflicted fellow-subjects and avenge the cruelties of the French and their savage allies. They wear plain blue frocks, nanquin or brown waistcoats and breeches, plain hats. Each is armed with a light firelock, a pair of pistols, and a cutting sword.”

“Ah, why ain’t we there, Gumbo?” Harry exclaimed.

“Why ain’t we dar?” shouted Gumbo.

“Why am I here, dangling at women’s trains?” Harry added.

“Think dangling at women’s trains very pleasant, Master Harry?” asked Gumbo, practical and indifferent.

Meanwhile, Harry read that the Virginia ship *The Lovely Sally* had been captured by a French privateer near port.

Then, learning that the finest mare in England and a pair of fine bay geldings were to be sold at the Bull Inn, Hatton Garden, Harry decided to go see them.

He bought the geldings on the spot, paying generously.

He said nothing of what he did that day—shy of appearing foreign—but it is believed he took a coach to Westminster Abbey, then to the Tower, Mrs. Salmon’s Waxwork, Hyde Park, and Kensington Palace. He had ordered the coach to the Royal Exchange, but catching sight of Covent Garden en route, he bade the driver return him to his inn—and tipped him a guinea.

Mr. Draper had called in his absence and promised to return. But Harry, having dined sumptuously alone, returned to Marybone Gardens, still in noble company.

The next morning, as he stepped out, the bells of St. Paul’s, Covent Garden, rang for morning prayers—reminding him that Sampson was preaching.

Harry smiled. He was beginning to form a clear, fair judgment of the value of Mr. Sampson’s sermons.
