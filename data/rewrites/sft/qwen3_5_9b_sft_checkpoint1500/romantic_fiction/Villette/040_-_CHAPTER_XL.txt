The day following that memorable Midsummer night was not merely ordinary. Yet, let us dispense with celestial omens or terrestrial portents; the heavens were clear, and the earth held no storms. Instead, the sun ascended with a jovial July countenance, decking the morning in rubies and scattering roses so profusely that they showered down, blushing the path beneath. Time itself seemed to wake as a fresh nymph, leaving behind the vapors of the night, leading the sun's chariot across a sky of azure fire.

It was, in truth, the finest day summer could offer. Yet, amidst this brilliance, I doubted if anyone else in the Rue Fossette truly noticed the splendor. A far more pressing thought occupied the minds of the household—a subject that, while present in my meditations, possessed for me neither the overwhelming novelty nor the dense mystery that paralyzed the others. While I strolled the garden, watching the blooms unfold under the warmth, the entire house buzzed with a single, frantic inquiry.

What was the subject? It boiled down to a simple, terrifying absence. When the morning prayers concluded, a seat remained vacant in the front row of the boarders. At breakfast, a coffee cup sat untouched. When the maid made the beds, she found a bolster arranged lengthwise within a nightgown, untouched by a sleeper. And when Ginevra Fanshawe's music mistress arrived as usual for the first lesson, her pupil was nowhere to be found.

Miss Fanshawe was sought everywhere, high and low. The house was ransacked from cellar to attic, yet the search yielded nothing—not a trace, not a scrap of paper, not even a clue. The girl had vanished, engulfed by the previous night like a shooting star swallowed by the dark.

The dismay among the teachers was deep, but the horror of the defaulting Directress, Madame Beck, was profound. I had never seen her so pale, so appalled. This was a blow to her tenderest spot, a wound to her reputation. How could such an escape occur? Where had the fugitive fled? Every window was fastened, every pane intact, every door securely bolted. To this day, Madame Beck has never found satisfaction regarding the mechanics of the flight, save for one observer: Lucy Snowe. I could not forget how, to facilitate a specific enterprise, I had gently drawn a great door to its lintel, closing it without bolting it. Furthermore, the thunderous carriage pair and the puzzling signal of the waved handkerchief now surfaced in memory.

From these premises, and a few others accessible only to me, I drew a singular inference. It was a case of elopement. Morally certain of this, and witnessing Madame Beck's utter embarrassment, I finally voiced my conviction. After alluding to M. de Hamal's suit, I found—as I expected—that Madame Beck was perfectly acquainted with the affair. She had long discussed it with Mrs. Cholmondeley and had pinned the blame squarely on that lady's shoulders. Now, she turned to Mrs. Cholmondeley and M. de Bassompierre for answers.

They confirmed that the Hôtel Crécy was already agitated. Ginevra had written to her cousin Paulina, vaguely hinting at matrimonial intentions, while communications poured in from the de Hamal family. M. de Bassompierre was hot on the trail of the fugitives, though he overtook them too late.

Within the week, the post delivered a note. I shall transcribe it, for it holds the key to many mysteries:

'DEAR OLD TIM "—I am off, you see—gone like a shot. Alfred and I intended this kind of union almost from the start; we never meant to be spliced in the humdrum fashion of ordinary folk. Alfred possesses too much spirit for that, and so do I—Thank God! Do you know, Alfred—who used to call you 'the dragon'—has seen so much of you recently that he feels quite friendly toward you. He hopes you won't miss him now that he has gone; he begs to apologize for any little trouble he caused. He is afraid he rather inconvenienced you once when he surprised you in the grenier, just as you were reading a letter of the utmost secrecy; but he could not resist the temptation to give you a start, for you appeared so wonderfully absorbed in your correspondence. Conversely, he admits that you once frightened him by rushing in for a dress or a shawl at the precise moment he had lit a cigar and was taking a quiet puff.

"Do you begin to understand, by this time, that M. le Comte de Hamal was the nun of the attic, and that he came to visit you? Let me explain his method. You know he has access to the Athénée, where several of his nephews, sons of his eldest sister Madame de Melcy, are students. You know the courtyard of the Athénée lies on the other side of the high wall bordering your walk, the forbidden alley. Alfred can climb as well as he can dance or fence; his amusement was to scale our pensionnat. He climbed the wall first, then—aided by that tall tree spreading over the grand berceau, whose branches rested on the roof of our lower buildings—he scaled the first class and the grand hall. One night, by the way, he fell from this tree, tearing down branches and nearly breaking his own neck; in his hasty retreat, he suffered a terrible fright and nearly met two people, Madame Beck and M. Emanuel, whom he believes were walking in the alley. From the grand hall, the ascent to the highest block of buildings, ending in the great garret, was not difficult. The skylight, you know, is left half-open day and night for ventilation; through it, he entered. Nearly a year ago, I happened to tell him our legend of the nun; this sparked his romantic idea of the spectral disguise, which I think you must admit he executed with considerable skill.

"But for the nun's black gown and white veil, he would have been caught repeatedly, both by you and that tiger-Jesuit, M. Paul. He considers you both capital ghost-seers and very brave. What I find astonishing is less your courage than your secretiveness. How could you endure those repeated visits from that long spectre without crying out, telling everyone, and rousing the whole house and neighborhood?

"Oh, and how did you like the nun as a bed-fellow? _I_ dressed her up—didn't I do it well? Did you shriek when you saw her? I should have gone mad; but then you have such nerves!—real iron and bend-leather! I believe you feel nothing. You haven't the same sensitiveness as someone of my constitution. You seem to me insensible to pain, fear, and grief. You are a real old Diogenes.

"Well, dear grandmother! Are you not mightily angry at my moonlit flitting and runaway match? I assure you it was excellent fun, and I did it partly to spite that minx, Paulina, and that bear, Dr. John—to show them that, despite all their airs, I could marry as well as they. M. de Bassompierre was initially furious with Alfred; he threatened prosecution for 'détournement de mineur,' and I know not what, so seriously was he intent. I found myself forced to perform a little melodrama—going down on my knees, sobbing, drenching three pocket-handkerchiefs. Of course, 'mon oncle' soon gave in; where was the use of making a fuss? I am married, and that is all. He still claims our marriage is not legal because I am not of age, as if that mattered! I am just as married as if I were a hundred. However, we are to be married again properly, and I shall receive a trousseau. Mrs. Cholmondeley will superintend it, and there is hope M. de Bassompierre will grant me a decent portion, which will be very convenient, as dear Alfred has nothing but his nobility, native and hereditary, and his salary. I only wish uncle would act generously and gentlemanly, instead of tying the dowry to Alfred's written promise that he will never touch cards or dice. They accuse my angel of a tendency to gamble; I know nothing of that, but I _do_ know he is a dear, adorable creature.

"I cannot sufficiently praise the genius with which de Hamal managed our flight. How clever of him to choose the night of the fête, when Madame (as he noted) would certainly be absent at the park concert. I suppose _you_ accompanied her. I watched you rise and leave the dormitory around eleven o'clock. How you returned alone and on foot, I cannot guess. That surely was _you_ we met in the narrow old Rue St. Jean? Did you see me wave my handkerchief from the carriage window?

"Adieu! Rejoice in my good fortune: congratulate me on my supreme happiness, and believe me, dear cynic and misanthrope, yours, in the best of health and spirits,

née FANSHAWE.

"P.S.—Remember, I am a countess now. Papa, mamma, and the sisters at home will be delighted. 'My daughter the Countess!' 'My sister the Countess!' Bravo! Sounds far superior to Mrs. John Bretton, hein?"
