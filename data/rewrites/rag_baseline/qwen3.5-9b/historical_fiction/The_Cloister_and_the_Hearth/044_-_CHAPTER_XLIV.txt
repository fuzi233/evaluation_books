Experienced women possess a stoicism that renders them immune to panic when a sister falls; they do not hastily conjure specters of moral failing, but rather diagnose the immediate physical collapse with the clinical detachment honed by observation. Catherine moved with the brisk efficiency of one who knows the body's fragility better than its secrets. She lowered the girl until her head rested flat upon the floor, flung open the shutter to invite the raw air, and loosened the corset that constricted the victim's breath. Only after these mechanical acts of rescue were complete did she turn to the threshold and command, her voice ringing with authoritative clarity: "Come here, if you please."

Margaret Van Eyck and Reicht entered to find the scene stark: the girl prone and pale, Catherine thrashing her hands in a futile rhythm of distress. "Oh, my poor girl! What have you done to her?" cried the elder woman, her composure fracturing under the weight of maternal instinct.

"Me?" Catherine snapped, her anger rising like steam from a boiling kettle. "What has happened, then?"

"Nothing, madam; nothing more than is natural in her situation," Reicht replied, attempting to soothe the storm.

Margaret Van Eyck flushed with a heat that betrayed her ire. "You do well to speak so coolly," she retorted, her eyes narrowing, "you that are the architect of her situation."

"That I am not," Catherine declared bluntly, her stance rigid. "Nor is any woman born."

"Was it not you and your husband who kept them apart?" Margaret pressed, her voice trembling with suppressed accusation. "And now he has fled to Italy all alone. 'Situation' indeed! You have shattered her heart amongst you."

"Why, madam? Who is it then? In Heaven's name!" Catherine recoiled slightly, scandalized. "To hear you, one would think this was my Gerard's lass. But that cannot be. This fur never cost less than five crowns the ell; besides, this young gentlewoman is a wife—or ought to be."

"Of course she ought," Margaret insisted, her tone sharpening. "And who is the cause she is none? Who stood before them at the very altar?"

"God forgive them, whoever it was," Catherine said gravely, shaking her head. "Me it was not, nor my man."

"Well," the other woman conceded, her anger softening into concern as she observed the girl's breathing deepen, "now you have seen her, perhaps you will not be quite so bitter against her, madam. She is coming to, thank Heaven."

"Bitter against her?" Catherine laughed, a dry, incredulous sound. "No, that is all over. Poor soul! Trouble behind her and trouble ahead; and to think of my setting her, of all living women, to read Gerard's letter to me. Ay, and that was what made her go off, I'll be sworn. She is coming to. What, sweetheart! Be not afraid, none are here but friends."

They lifted the girl into an easy chair. As color began to creep back to her cheeks and lips, Catherine drew Margaret Van Eyck aside, lowering her voice to a conspiratorial whisper. "Is she staying with you, if you please?"

"No, madam."

"I wouldn't let her go back to Sevenbergen tonight, then."

"That is as she pleases. She still refuses to bide the night."

"Ay, but you are older than she is; you can make her. There, she is beginning to notice."

Catherine leaned forward, pressing her mouth to Margaret Van Eyck's ear for a fleeting instant. It seemed insufficient time to whisper a word, far less a sentence, yet between women, communication flashes across the chasm of silence like lightning or pure thought itself. The old lady stiffened, her eyes widening in horror, and whispered back urgently.

"It is false! It is a calumny! It is monstrous! Look at her face. It is blasphemy to accuse such a face."

"Tut! Tut! Tut!" Margaret dismissed the warning with a wave of her hand. "You might as well say this is not my hand. I ought to know; and I tell you it is so."

Then, to Margaret Van Eyck's profound astonishment, she rose and took the girl by the neck, kissing her warmly, a gesture of fierce affection. "I suffered for Gerard, and you shed your blood for him, I do hear; his own words show me that I have been to blame, the very words you have read to me. Ay, Gerard, my child, I have held aloof from her; but I'll make it up to her once I begin. You are my daughter from this hour."

Another warm embrace sealed this hasty compact, and the woman of impulse vanished into the room. Margaret lay back in her chair, a feeble smile stealing over her face. Gerard's mother had kissed her and claimed her as kin; but the next moment, she saw her old friend regarding her with a vexed, troubled air.

"I wonder you let that woman kiss you," Margaret murmured, half-reproachful.

"Mother, or no mother, you would not let her touch you if you knew what she whispered in my ear about you," Margaret continued, her voice low.

"About me?" Margaret asked faintly.

"Ay, about you, whom she never saw till tonight."

The old lady proceeded, with hesitant choice of language, to draw Margaret into the vortex of her indignation, when an unlooked-for interruption silenced her. The young woman slid from the chair to her knees, bowing her head, and began to pray piteously for pardon. From the fervor of her words and the agony of her manner, a bystander would have gathered that she had inflicted some cruel wrong, some intolerable insult, upon her venerable friend.
