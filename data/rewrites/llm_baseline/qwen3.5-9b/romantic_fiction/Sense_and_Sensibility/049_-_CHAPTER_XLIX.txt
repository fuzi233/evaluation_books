Yet, however inexplicable the circumstances of his release might seem to the entire family, it was undeniable that Edward was free. The purpose of this newfound liberty was quickly decided upon by all: having endured the miseries of one imprudent engagement, contracted without his mother's consent and lasting over four years, nothing short of an immediate renewal of such a union could be expected of him upon its failure. His mission to Barton was straightforward: to ask Elinor to marry him. Given that he was not entirely inexperienced in making such a proposal, it was somewhat surprising that he felt so uneasy in this instance, requiring so much encouragement and fresh air to gather his courage. However, how swiftly he walked himself into the right resolve, how quickly an opportunity arose to act upon it, and how he was received, need not be detailed in detail. Suffice it to say that when the family sat down to dinner at four o'clock, roughly three hours after his arrival, he had secured his lady's hand, obtained his mother's consent, and was not only expressing himself as a rapturous lover but, in the reality of reason and truth, one of the happiest men alive.

His situation was indeed more than commonly joyful. He possessed more than the ordinary triumph of accepted love to swell his heart and lift his spirits. He was freed without reproach from an entanglement that had long been his misery, from a woman he had long ceased to love; and he was instantly elevated to a security with another that he would have deemed almost hopeless until recently, and which he now cherished with desperate desire. He was brought not from doubt or suspense, but directly from misery to happiness—a change openly expressed in a genuine, flowing, and grateful cheerfulness his friends had never witnessed before. His heart was now fully open to Elinor; all its weaknesses and errors were confessed, and his youthful infatuation with Lucy was treated with the philosophical dignity of a twenty-four-year-old reflecting on his past.

"It was a foolish, idle inclination on my part," he admitted. "It stemmed from ignorance of the world and a lack of occupation. Had my mother provided me with an active profession when I was removed at eighteen from Mr. Pratt's care, I am certain it would never have happened. Although I left Longstaple with what I then considered an unconquerable preference for his niece, had I possessed any pursuit, any object to engage my time and keep me at a distance from her for a few months, I should very soon have outgrown that fancied attachment, especially by mixing more with the world as I would have been forced to do. Instead, having nothing to do, no profession chosen for me, or allowed to choose one, I returned home to complete idleness. For the first twelve months thereafter, I lacked even the nominal employment that university membership would have provided, for I was not entered at Oxford until I was nineteen. Thus, I had nothing in the world to occupy me but to imagine myself in love. Since my mother did not make my home entirely comfortable, I had no friend in my brother, and I disliked new acquaintances, it was natural for me to spend much of my time at Longstaple, where I always felt at home and was sure of a welcome. Consequently, from eighteen to nineteen, I spent the majority of my time there. Lucy appeared to possess every amiable quality; she was pretty—at least, I thought so *then*—and having seen so little of other women, I could make no comparisons and discern no defects. Considering everything, I hope that although our engagement was foolish and has since been proven foolish in every way, it was not at the time an unnatural or inexcusable piece of folly."

The transformation wrought in a few hours upon the minds and hearts of the Dashwoods was so profound that it promised them all a sleepless night of joy. Mrs. Dashwood, too happy to be merely comfortable, struggled to express her love for Edward, to praise Elinor sufficiently, to thank him for his release without wounding his delicacy, and to balance granting them leisure for private conversation with enjoying the sight and society of both. Marianne could articulate her happiness only through tears. Comparisons inevitably arose, regrets surfaced, and her joy, though sincere in its love for her sister, was of a nature that gave her neither spirit nor words. But Elinor—how were her feelings to be described? From the moment she learned of Lucy's marriage and Edward's freedom until the moment he justified the hopes that had instantly followed, she was everything but tranquil. Yet, once the initial shock passed, finding every doubt and solicitude removed, comparing her current situation with what it had been so recently, seeing him honorably released from his former bond, and witnessing him instantly profit from that release to declare an affection as tender and constant as she had ever imagined possible, she was overwhelmed by her own felicity. Happily disposed as the human mind is to become easily accustomed to any change for the better, it took several hours to bring sedateness to her spirits or tranquility to her heart.

Edward was now settled at the cottage for at least a week; for regardless of other claims, it was impossible to dedicate less than a week to the enjoyment of Elinor's company or to discuss half of what needed saying regarding the past, present, and future. While a few hours of incessant talk might exhaust many topics between rational individuals, with lovers, no subject is ever truly finished, nor is any communication complete until it has been exchanged at least twenty times over. Lucy's marriage, an unceasing and reasonable wonder to everyone involved, naturally became one of their earliest discussions. Elinor's intimate knowledge of both parties made it appear to her, in every aspect, as one of the most extraordinary and unaccountable circumstances she had ever encountered. How they could have been thrown together, and by what attraction Robert could be drawn to marry a girl whose beauty she had heard him describe without admiration—a girl already engaged to his brother, on whose account that brother had been rejected by his family—it was beyond her comprehension. To her own heart, it was a delightful affair; to her imagination, even a ridiculous one; but to her reason and judgment, it was a complete puzzle.

Edward could only attempt an explanation by suggesting that, perhaps, an accidental meeting initially allowed the vanity of one to be worked upon by the flattery of the other, leading gradually to all subsequent events. Elinor recalled what Robert had told her in Harley Street regarding his opinion of how his own mediation in his brother's affairs might have helped if applied in time. She repeated this to Edward. "That was exactly like Robert," he observed immediately. "And that," he added shortly after, "might perhaps have been in his mind when the acquaintance between them first began. And Lucy, perhaps, at first, might have thought only of securing his good offices in my favor. Other designs might have arisen later."

How long the secret had been carried on between them, however, he was just as lost as she was in determining; for at Oxford, where he had remained voluntarily since leaving London, he had had no means of hearing of her except through her own letters, which to the very last had been neither less frequent nor less affectionate than usual. Therefore, not the slightest suspicion had ever prepared him for what followed. When it finally burst upon him via a letter from Lucy herself, he had been, for some time, he believed, half stupefied between wonder, horror, and the joy of such a deliverance. He handed the letter to Elinor.

>
> Being very sure I have long lost your affections, I have thought myself at liberty to bestow my own on another, and have no doubt of being as happy with him as I once used to think I might be with you; but I scorn to accept a hand while the heart belongs to another. I sincerely wish you happy in your choice, and it shall not be my fault if we are not always good friends, as our near relationship now makes proper. I can safely say I owe you no ill-will, and am sure you will be too generous to do us any ill offices. Your brother has gained my affections entirely, and as we could not live without one another, we are just returned from the altar, and are now on our way to Dawlish for a few weeks, which place your dear brother has great curiosity to see, but thought I would first trouble you with these few lines, and shall always remain,
>
> Your sincere well-wisher, friend, and sister,
>
> I have burnt all your letters, and will return your picture the first opportunity. Please to destroy my scrawls—but the ring with my hair you are very welcome to keep.

Elinor read it and returned it without comment. "I will not ask your opinion of it as a composition," said Edward. "For worlds, I would not have had a letter of hers seen by *you* in former days. In a sister, it is bad enough, but in a wife! How I have blushed over the pages of her writing! And I believe I may say that since the first half-year of our foolish business, this is the only letter I ever received from her of which the substance made me any amends for the defect of the style."

"However it may have come about," said Elinor after a pause, "they are certainly married. And your mother has brought upon herself a most appropriate punishment.
