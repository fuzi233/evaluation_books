In the heart of a weathered square, where the air reeked of damp earth and aged stone, four decrepit boards had hung askew for as long as anyone could recall. They creaked in the gentle breeze like a chorus of worn wooden spoons, their faded letters seeming to mock the notion of habitation. The boards had once been vertical, but now they leaned inward, as if attempting to ensnare the few passersby who dared enter the square. The old house itself, a behemoth of bloomy red brick, loomed behind them, its walls bearing the remnants of defunct insurance company logos. The children of the square had long since claimed the low gate at the entrance as their own, leaving only the sturdy top bar intact.

Water trickled down the alley, worn smooth by the eaves of the neighboring house, and cats and dogs roamed freely, their paw prints mingling with the chalk marks left by the occasional tramp. It was a neighborhood that seemed to have given up on the notion of renewal, where the "To Let" boards had been reduced to a mere formality. For six months, Oleron had walked past this neglected corner of the square, twice a day or more, on his way to the room where he worked. Yet, it wasn't until one fateful morning that he found himself standing before the broken gate, the boards looming above him like sentinels.

One of the boards caught his eye, its letters worn but still legible, announcing that the key could be obtained at Number Six. Oleron's thoughts turned to the difficulties of his current living arrangement. He paid a handsome sum for his separate bedroom and workroom, as well as a modest rent for the storage of his grandmother's antique furniture. His belongings were scattered across two locations, making everyday tasks a cumbersome affair. The book he wanted to read in bed lay in his workroom, a mile away, and the note he needed during the day often lingered in the pocket of a coat he'd worn the previous evening.

As he pondered his situation, Oleron's gaze wandered from the boarded basement windows to the grimy panes above, finally settling on the flat stone coping of the roof. He stood there, lost in thought, his lean jaw working absently as he considered the possibilities. After a moment, he glanced once more at the board, then began the slow walk to Number Six.
