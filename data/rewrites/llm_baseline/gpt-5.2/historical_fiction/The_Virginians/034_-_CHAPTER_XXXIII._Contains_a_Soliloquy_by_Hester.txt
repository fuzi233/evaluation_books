Colonel Lambert’s first heat, when his younger daughter’s sudden outbreak betrayed the little secret she had been keeping even from herself, was not tenderness, but a father’s sharp, possessive anger—anger that always masks fright.

“A plague upon all scapegraces, English or Indian!” he cried to his wife, as they sat in their lodgings with the windows shut against the thin Bath air and the distant clatter of wheels on the stones. “I would he had broken his nose on any doorpost but ours.”

Mrs. Lambert, who had been folding a little parcel of fine linen as neatly as if it were a ritual, laid it down with the softest patience. “Perhaps we are to cure him of being a scapegrace, my dear. Perhaps the fall at our door has something providential in it. You laughed at me when I said so before; but if Heaven did not send the young gentleman to us—who did? And it may be for the blessing and happiness of us all that he came, too.”

“It’s hard, Molly!” the Colonel groaned. “We cherish ’em and fondle and rear ’em; we tend ’em through sickness and health; we toil and scheme; we hoard money away in the stocking and patch our own old coats. If they’ve a headache we can’t sleep for thinking of it. If they have a wish or fancy we work day and night to compass it—and ’tis darling daddy and dearest pappy, and whose father is like ours? And so forth. On Tuesday morning I am king of my house and family. On Tuesday evening Prince Whippersnapper makes his appearance, and my reign is over. A whole life forgotten and forsworn for a pair of blue eyes, a pair of lean shanks, and a head of yellow hair.”

“’Tis written we women should leave all to follow our husband,” Mrs. Lambert said, and her voice had that quiet firmness which can hush even a soldier’s bluster. She put her hand on his sleeve as if smoothing a crease. “I think our courtship was not very long, dear Martin.”

“’Tis human nature, and what can you expect of the jade?” he sighed, though the word “jade” came out as a caress in his mouth. “And I think I did my duty to my husband, though I own I left my papa for him,” she added, softly, with a smile that carried the scent of old lavender and the memory of white ribbons on a postillion’s hat.

“Excellent wench! Perdition catch my soul! but I do love thee, Molly!” he cried; and then, with the same breath, relapsed into the father again. “But mind you—your father never did me; and if ever I am to have sons-in-law—”

“Ever, indeed! Of course my girls are to have husbands, Mr. Lambert!” Mamma’s eyes flashed, not with anger, but with that pleased alarm women have when they foresee both sorrow and ceremony, separation and satin.

“Well—when they come, I’ll hate them, madam, as your father did me; and quite right too, for taking his treasure away from him.”

“Don’t be irreligious and unnatural, Martin Lambert! I say you are unnatural, sir!”

“Nay, my dear,” he answered, with a grimace as if biting a hard crust, “I have an old tooth in my left jaw here; and ’tis natural the tooth should come out. But when the tooth-drawer pulls it, ’tis natural I should feel pain. Do you suppose, madam, that I don’t love Hetty better than any tooth in my head?”

No woman, however, ever truly feared the notion of her daughter being married—fear belonged to fathers; mothers and grandmothers were married over again in their children’s marriages. The old bridal favours, stowed away like spices in the heart’s dark cupboard, were taken out and smelt again; the ribbons bloomed; the muslins rustled; and, in imagination, they fluttered into the post-chaise and drove away.

“It will be a sad thing, parting with her,” Mrs. Lambert said at last, with a sigh that was half pleasure and half pain.

“You have settled that point already, Molly,” the Colonel laughed sourly. “Had I not best go out and order raisins and corinths for the wedding-cake?”

“And then I shall have to leave the house in their charge when I go to her, you know, in Virginia. How many miles is it to Virginia, Martin? I should think it must be thousands of miles.”

“A hundred and seventy-three thousand three hundred and ninety-one and three-quarters, my dear, by the near way,” he answered gravely, as if reading out a provision list. “That through Prester John’s country. By the other route, through Persia—”

“Oh, give me the one where there is the least of the sea, and your horrid ships, which I can’t bear!” she cried.

“I hope Rachel Esmond and I shall be better friends,” she went on, already setting her mind to the domestic politics of a distant household. “She had a very high spirit when we were girls at school.”

“Had we not best go about the baby-linen, Mrs. Martin Lambert?” her husband put in, with a stare of wonder—as though he had been told to salt a ham for a child not yet born.

Mrs. Lambert, no doubt, saw nothing astonishing in it. She had likely been in Mrs. Bobbinit’s toy-shop already and noted some pretty lace caps and bibs; and that same Sunday afternoon, while Hetty lay upon her pillow—cheeks fever-hot, eyelids shut hard as if to keep the world out, her little mouth set in a piteous line—her mother looked at her with the most perfect ease of mind, almost with secret content, as if the girl’s woe were merely a first rising of bread that would, in time, make a good loaf.

But to Hetty herself it was no gentle fermentation: it was scorch and sting. She had not meant to publish her secret; perhaps she had not even known it until the sudden rush of feeling told her what lay in her. Now, having been surprised into confession, she chose to be ashamed as if she had been caught in a theft. Indignant at her own weakness, she turned her rage inward till it spilled out on everyone near her.

“What business has any young woman,” she cried, “to indulge in any such nonsense? Mamma, I ought to be whipped and sent to bed. I know perfectly well Mr. Warrington does not care a fig about me. I dare say he likes French actresses and the commonest little milliner-girl in the toy-shop better than me. And so he ought—and so they are better than me. Why, what a fool I am to burst out crying like a ninny about nothing, and because Mr. Wolfe said Harry played cards of a Sunday! I know he is not clever, like papa. I believe he is stupid—I am certain he is stupid: but he is not so stupid as I am. Why, of course I can’t marry him. How am I to go to America, and leave you and Theo? Of course he likes somebody else—at America, or at Tunbridge, or at Jericho, or somewhere. He is a prince in his own country and can’t think of marrying a poor half-pay officer’s daughter, with twopence to her fortune. Used not you to tell me how, when I was a baby, I cried and wanted the moon? I am a baby now, a most absurd, silly, little baby—don’t talk to me, Mrs. Lambert, I am. Only there is this to be said: he don’t know anything about it, and I would rather cut my tongue out than tell him.”

Dire were the threats she made against Theo, should her sister betray her. As for Charley—his whole soul, being set upon cheesecakes and the exact moment they might be procured, was unshaken by love’s earthquake; and the parents and kind sister, of course, promised to keep silence.

“I begin to think it had been best for us to stay at home,” Mrs. Lambert sighed to her husband later, when Hetty had worn herself into a sullen stillness and the household sounds—the shifting of a chair, the crackle of a coal, the thin music from some distant room in the lodging-house—came distinct in the hush.

“Nay, my dear,” Lambert replied. “Human nature will be human nature; surely Hetty’s mother told me herself she had the beginning of a liking for a certain young curate before she fell over head and ears in love with a certain young officer of Kingsley’s. And as for me, my heart was wounded in a dozen places ere Miss Molly Benson took entire possession of it. Our sons and daughters must follow in the way of their parents before them, I suppose. Why, but yesterday, you were scolding me for grumbling at Miss Het’s precocious fancies. To do the child justice, she disguises her feelings entirely, and I defy Mr. Warrington to know from her behaviour how she is disposed towards him.”

“A daughter of mine and yours, Martin,” the mother cried, drawing herself up with matronly dignity, “is not going to fling herself at a gentleman’s head!”

“Neither herself nor the teacup, my dear,” he answered.

In truth, little Miss Hetty treated Mr. Warrington like a vixen. He never came but she boxed his ears in one fashion or another—not with her hand, perhaps, but with her tongue, which could strike as quick as a spoon against a pan. Knowing what went on in the young hypocrite’s mind, her father was not angry at her rudeness: he watched it as one watches a saucepan threaten to boil over, half amused and half apprehensive.

“She hath no need to be rude at all, Martin; and our girl is good enough for any gentleman in England or America. Why, if their ages suit, shouldn’t they marry after all, sir?”

“Why, if he wants her, shouldn’t he ask her, my dear? I am sorry we came. I am for putting the horses into the carriage, and turning their heads towards home again.”

But mamma, with that tender obstinacy which is the match-making instinct in its purest form, said, “Depend upon it, my dear, these matters are wisely ordained for us. It was not for nothing Harry Warrington was brought to our gate in that way; and that he and our children are thus brought together again. If that marriage has been decreed in Heaven, a marriage it will be.”

“At what age, Molly, I wonder, do women begin and leave off match-making? If our little chit falls in love and falls out again, she will not be the first of her sex, Mrs. Lambert. I wish we were on our way home again, and, if I had my will, would trot off this very night.”

“He has promised to drink his tea here to-night. You would not take away our child’s pleasure, Martin?” she asked, softly—softly as warm milk poured into a cup.

In his fashion the father was not less good-natured. “You know, my dear,” he said, “that if either of ’em had a fancy to our ears, we would cut them off and serve them in a fricassee.”

Mrs. Lambert laughed—she, whose own ears were small and delicate, half hidden by hair combed smooth and drawn back, where a thread or two of silver glinted in the candlelight like fine sugar on a tart. When Lambert was most tender-hearted, his humour grew most grotesque. He took her ear between finger and thumb with a mock severity that could not hurt; and she, no doubt, was remembering her own first timid days—how courtship, like good preserving, sweetens what time would otherwise spoil.

Hallowed remembrances: if youthful love is pleasant, how much more moving is affection that has lasted through years—through illness, losses, faded beauty perhaps, and the small daily vexations that wear on a household like grit in flour.

In regard to her promise of disguise, Miss Hester was better—or worse—than her word. When Harry came that evening, it was not only to take tea with his friends, but to invite them, for the next day, to an entertainment at the Rooms—to be given in their special honour.

“A dance, and given for us!” cried Theo, clapping her hands. “Oh, Harry, how delightful! I wish we could begin this very minute!”

“Why, for a savage Virginian, I declare, Harry Warrington, thou art the most civilised young man possible!” said the Colonel. “My dear, shall we dance a minuet together?”

“We have done such a thing before, Martin Lambert!” Mrs. Lambert answered, and the words were a smile.

The Colonel hummed a minuet air; snatched a plate from the tea-table and, holding it like a hat, made a bow and flourish, whilst madam performed her best curtsey. The tea had that Bath smell—hot water, bruised leaves, milk warming, a faint sweetness from the sugar, and, under all, the coal’s dry breath; the spoons rang in their cups with bright domestic music that seemed, for a moment, to promise that nothing cruel could happen in such a room.

Only Hetty persisted in looking glum and displeased, as if she had been made to swallow something bitter.

“Why, child, have you not a word of thanks to throw to Mr. Warrington?” Theo asked, her eyes dancing.

“I never did care for dancing much,” said Hetty.

“What is the use of standing up opposite a stupid man, and dancing down a room with him?”

“Merci du compliment!” said Mr. Warrington; and there was a change on his face, as quick as a shadow passing over sunlit bread.

“I don’t say that you are stupid—that is—that is, I—I only meant country dances,” Hetty stammered, biting her lip as she caught Theo’s eye. She remembered her own cruel sentence—Harry is stupid—and Theo’s droll glance reminded her without saying a word.

But instead of softening, Hetty chose to be angry as if she had been wounded by an insult. “I hate dancing—there—I own it,” she said, tossing her head.

“Nay, you used to like it well enough, child!” her mother interposed.

“That was when she was a child: don’t you see she is grown up to be an old woman?” her father said.

“Or perhaps Miss Hester has got the gout?”

“Fiddle!” snapped Hetty, drubbing with her little foot.

“What’s a dance without a fiddle?” returned imperturbable papa.

Darkness had come over Harry Warrington’s face. He sat stiffly, hearing not only her words, but the way they struck the room—as if she had clapped a lid down on all the small cheerful sounds. In his mind there rose the manners of his own country, where a guest’s kindness is answered with kindness, and where, whatever a young person thinks, she does not so toss it at her elders.

“I come to try my best,” he thought, “and give them pleasure and a dance, and the little thing tells me she hates dancing. We don’t practise kindness, or acknowledge hospitality so in our country. No—nor speak to our parents so, neither.”

Nor did Miss Hester stop there. She must needs make sport of the company at the Wells—of their fineries and follies—and especially of Harry’s own pursuits and companions, till the honest lad felt the sting as plainly as if she had sprinkled pepper into his tea.

Later, when he found Mrs. Lambert alone, he asked her, with a grave simplicity that went straight to her heart, how or in what he had again offended, that Hester was so angry with him.

The kind matron, after her daughter’s behaviour, felt more than ever well disposed towards the boy. The secret burned at her tongue; she would have liked to tell it and so set all right. But she could not betray her child. Theo, too, remonstrated with her sister in private; but Hester would not listen. She carried her anger with her like a hot coal wrapped in paper: it did not cool; it only threatened to burn through.

In their bedroom, when they were alone and the house had quieted—when the murmur of other lodgers sank into a few coughs and footsteps, when even the street’s sound thinned and only the far-off Bath noises remained, like water in pipes—Hetty broke out again.

“Suppose he hates me?” she said. “I expect he will. I hate myself, I do, and scorn myself for being such an idiot. How ought he to do otherwise than hate me? Didn’t I abuse him, call him goose, all sorts of names? And know he is not clever all the time. I know I have better wits than he has. It is only because he is tall, and has blue eyes, and a pretty nose that I like him. What an absurd fool a girl must be to like a man merely because he has a blue nose and hooked eyes! So I am a fool, and I won’t have you say a word to the contrary, Theo!”

Theo thought her little sister, far from being a fool, a wonder; and if any girl were worthy of any prince in Christendom, Hetty was. Yet Theo spoke gently, as one takes a sharp knife from a child and replaces it with a spoon.

“You are silly sometimes, Hetty—that is when you speak unkindly to people who mean you well, as you did to Mr. Warrington at tea to-night. When he proposed to us his party at the Assembly Rooms, and nothing could be more gallant of him, why did you say you didn’t care for music, or dancing, or tea? You know you love them all!”

“I said it merely to vex myself, Theo, and annoy myself, and whip myself, as I deserve, child,” Hetty cried. “And besides, how can you expect such an idiot as I am to say anything but idiotic things? Do you know, it quite pleased me to see him angry. I thought, ah! now I have hurt his feelings! Now he will say, Hetty Lambert is an odious little set-up, sour-tempered vixen. And that will teach him, and you, and mamma, and papa, at any rate, that I am not going to set my cap at Mr. Harry. No; our papa is ten times as good as he is. I will stay by our papa, and if he asked me to go to Virginia with him to-morrow, I wouldn’t, Theo. My sister is worth all the Virginians that ever were made since the world began.”

Here, no doubt, the sisters kissed and clung together—Theo soothing, Hetty fierce in her affection as in her despair—until their mother’s knock came to the door. Mrs. Lambert, who had overheard more through the wainscot than daughters ever suspect, called, “Children, ’tis time to go to sleep.”

Theo’s eyes closed quickly; she had that gift of slipping into rest as a bird tucks its head under its wing. But poor little Hetty—her mind could not be lulled. She lay with her eyes wide in the dark, hearing the hours toll one after another, each sound falling into her like a weight. In the pauses between the strokes she heard everything: a late coach rumbling far off; the shifting of some neighbour; the faint creak of boards; the very small living noises of the house, as if the building itself breathed.

“It is a judgment upon me,” she whispered to herself. “For having thought and spoke scornfully of him.”

Then, as if love must always braid itself with sacrifice, she argued against her own heart.

“Only—why should there be a judgment upon me? I was only in fun. I knew I liked him very much all the time: but I thought Theo liked him too, and I would give up anything for my darling Theo. If she had—no tortures should ever have drawn a word from me. I would have got a rope-ladder to help her to run away with Harry, that I would, or fetched the clergyman to marry them. And then I would have retired alone, and alone, and alone, and taken care of papa and mamma, and of the poor in the village, and have read sermons, though I hate ’em, and would have died without telling a word—not a word—and I shall die soon, I know I shall.”

When dawn rose at last, thin and colourless at the window, Hetty had fallen asleep—nestling close to her sister, the stain of a tear or two dried upon her flushed, downy cheek, like salt left after boiling.

Most of us, at some time, play with edged tools and cut ourselves accordingly. The first cut stings: we drop the knife and cry out like wounded little babies, as we are. A very few, unlucky at the game, strike too deep and perish outright. But—heaven help us—many are pricked and scarred and tattooed all over with wounds, and yet recover, and live on lively enough.

Let us trust there is no present need of sexton for Miss Hetty. But the moment she wakes, there—tearing at her little heart again—will be that Care which had granted her a few hours’ reprieve, melted, no doubt, by youth and tears, but not dismissed.
