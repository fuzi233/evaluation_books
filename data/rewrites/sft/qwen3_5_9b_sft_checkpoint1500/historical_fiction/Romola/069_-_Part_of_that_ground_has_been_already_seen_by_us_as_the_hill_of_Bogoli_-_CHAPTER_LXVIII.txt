Romola's Awakening.

From the depths of a dream, where the flickering torchlight of the Bargello court burned brighter and brighter until darkness dissolved, Romola drifted into a profound slumber, only to wake again into a restless, vivid reverie. Finally, her consciousness cleared. The heavy, agitated visions of the past had slipped away like smoke, replaced by the soft, indistinct sensations of a new dawn. She stretched her arms, not in the stone courtyard, but in the silence of a boat anchored in a secluded creek. To her right stretched the flawless, sapphire expanse of the Mediterranean; to her left, a verdant curve of mountains draped in gentle, shadowed slopes, leading up to a rugged height crowned by a solitary bell tower.

The sun, rising in the east, cast oblique rays upon the western corner of this crescent bay, leaving the rest bathed in a cool, dewy shadow. The air was utterly still; the water seemed to have curved itself into a perfect circle of repose. The warm sunlight touched Romola, thrilling her skin like a caress. For a fleeting moment, mere passive existence became a kind of Lethe, washing away the turmoil of her recent life. In this exquisite, indefinite quiet, the agonies of the evening faded, and the tranquil dreams of her girlhood returned. She felt no desire, no memory, only a profound, untroubled rest.

Gradually, however, the spell broke. She realized she was lying in a boat that had borne her across the waters all night, not to a grave, but to a sanctuary. Despite her despair of the previous day, she was grateful for the return of morning, for the familiarity of the sunlight rather than the unknown realm of death. *Could* she not remain here forever? No sounds from Florence could reach this hidden cove. Already, though, the oblivion was disturbed. Through the golden haze of the morning, the shapes of domes, towers, and walls pierced the silence, separated by a river and hemmed in by green hills.

Romola rose, sitting up in the boat, willing to resist the sudden rush of thoughts concerning her location. How far had the current carried her? Why did it matter? Here was a sheltered haven where simple villagers dwelt, unlikely to cause harm. For now, she could rest and resolve on nothing. Soon, she would procure bread and milk, nestle into this green solitude, and find a pause in her chaotic life. Turning her gaze back to the valley, she sought to reclaim the soothing peace she had felt upon first waking.

Her contemplation lasted but minutes before a piercing cry shattered the stillness. It was not a brief wail but a continuous, intensifying scream. Romola felt certain it was a child in distress, unheard by any neighbor. Starting up, she placed one foot on the shore, ready to leap, but paused to listen. Surely the mother was nearby; surely the cry would soon stop. Instead, it grew louder, drawing her irresistibly forward, sounding more pitiful precisely because it contrasted with the preceding peace. Before she knew where she was heading, she had leaped onto the beach and walked many paces.

The sound seemed to emanate from a rough, ruined hovel nestled among wild vegetation yards to her right. Climbing a broken stone fence, she traversed patches of weedy greens and neglected cornfields. The cry grew clearer, confirming her direction, yet as she hurried toward the hut, an oppressive change in the air seized her. Was there some taint lurking beneath this lush greenery, which had seemed such an inviting refuge from the coming heat? Seeing the entrance to the hovel, she felt the cry dart through her like a physical pain.

Stepping inside, the somber light revealed a scene that struck her with a shock of awe and horror. Lying on the straw-strewn floor were three dead bodies: a tall man, a girl of about eight, and a young woman whose long black hair was being clutched and pulled by a living child—the source of the screaming. Romola, hardened by her experiences in the haunts of death and disease, acted with prompt thought and swift motion. She lifted the shivering child to her bosom, attempting to soothe it, while casting furtive glances at the corpses to confirm they were truly dead. Their strongly marked features and peculiar garb suggested they were Spanish or Portuguese Jews, perhaps cast ashore by rapacious sailors who saw their property as plunder. Such cruelties, driven by greed and superstition, were common to those forced from their homes by the Inquisition.

*"Surely,"* Romola thought, *"I shall find a woman in this village whose maternal heart will not allow her to refuse aid to this helpless infant, even if the true mother is indeed dead."*

Yet a doubt lingered. While the man and the girl appeared emaciated and had been dead for some time, the young woman seemed harder, retaining the robustness of her form. Kneeling, Romola prepared to check for a pulse beneath a piece of yellow woolen drapery covering the bosom. But lifting the cloth, she saw purple spots marking the familiar pestilence. Instantly, her fears shifted. If the villagers knew of the plague, they might shrink from her with the child in her arms. However, she possessed money; they would not refuse goat's milk in exchange for gold.

Setting out for the nearest cluster of houses, her mind filled with the effort to soothe the dark, trembling creature, she wondered how she might win a neighbor's compassion. She hoped slightly in the awe she might inspire, appearing unknown and unexpected in her religious attire. As she crossed a stretch of cultivated ground, she noticed with wonder that patches of corn were left to over-ripeness, untouched by the sickle, while golden apples and dark figs rotted on the weedy earth. There were grassy spaces, yet no sign of a cow, sheep, or goat. The stillness had acquired a fearful quality, and she hurried toward the thickest group of dwellings, seeking the life necessary to plead for the helpless life she carried. She had picked up two figs along the way, biting small pieces from the sweet pulp to quiet the child.

Between two lines of dwellings, she entered a street where villagers should have been stirring long ago, but not a soul was in sight. The air grew increasingly oppressive, laden with some horrible impurity. An open door revealed grim emptiness; another showed a man lying dead, dressed in his clothes, his head resting across a spade handle, an earthenware cruse in his hand as if he had collapsed suddenly. Horror took possession of Romola. Was she in a village of the unburied dead? She wished to listen for any faint sound, but the child cried out anew when she ceased feeding it, filling her ears.

Finally, she saw a figure crawling slowly from a house, sinking back into a sitting posture against the wall. Hastening toward it, she found a young woman in fevered anguish, holding a pitcher. As Romola approached, the sufferer did not start; the urgent need was too absorbing for any other thought.

"Water!" gasped the woman, "Get me water!"

Romola stooped, taking the pitcher gently. "You shall have water," she whispered in her ear. "Can you point toward the well?"

The hand lifted toward the distant end of the street. Romola set off at once, moving as fast as her burden allowed. The child, however, grew calmer as the morsels of sweet pulp were repeated, ceasing to distress her with cries, allowing Romola to pay less distracted attention to her surroundings.

The well lay twenty yards beyond the street's end. As Romola approached, her eyes were drawn to the opposite green slope below the church. High up, between the trees, she had spotted a cow and a couple of goats. Trying to trace a path to that cheering sight after her errand, she was unaware of how near she was to the well, nor of someone approaching from the other side who fixed astonished eyes upon her.

Romola presented a sight that, in that place and moment, demanded a pause and palpitation. Her gaze was fixed intently on the distant slope, the long lines of her thick grey garment giving a gliding character to her rapid walk. Her hair rolled backward, illuminated on the left side by the sun-rays. On her right arm, a little olive baby with jet-black eyes gazed out. She might well startle the youth of fifteen accustomed to swinging censers before a Madonna less fair and marvellous than this.

"She carries a pitcher to fetch water for the sick," the youth murmured, awestruck. "It is the Holy Mother, come to care for those stricken by the pestilence."

It was a vision of awe. Perhaps she would be angry with those who fetched water only for themselves. Terrified, the youth flung down his vessel and ran, fleeing as if for his life toward the slope she had been contemplating. Remembering the parched sufferer, Romola half-filled her pitcher quickly and hastened back.

Entering a house to find a small cup, she saw salt meat and meal stored there; there were no signs of want. With nimble movements, she seated the baby on the ground and lifted a cup of water to the sufferer, who drank eagerly, then closed her eyes and leaned back, surrendering to the sense of relief. Presently, she opened her eyes and looked at Romola.

"Who are you?" she asked languidly.
