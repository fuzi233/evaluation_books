By six o’clock the next evening, Sally had to concede defeat on her plan to give Ginger Kemp a well-meaning life-coaching lecture: she hadn’t spotted him all day, and in sixty minutes flat she was due to hop the 7:15 express for Paris, then Cherbourg to catch her New York-bound liner. She’d dropped all her luggage at the station into the care of a very cheery porter, and drifted over to the Casino Municipale for one last desperate sweep, hating the thought of leaving Ginger un-uplifted. Like most sharp, energetic girls, Sally had a bad case of busybody syndrome — or, as her brother Fillmore rudely called it, messing with other people’s private business — and Ginger struck her as the exact kind of lost puppy who’d benefit from a friendly shove in the right direction. So she was absolutely chuffed when she spotted a shock of bright red hair glowing like a traffic beacon through the horde packed around a roulette table.

For the uninitiated, Roville has two casinos. The one on the promenade serves mild sea air and an even milder game called boule, for tourists who don’t care if they win or lose a couple of francs. The Casino Municipale, down near the station by the Palace Massena, is where the serious gamblers camp out, the ones who turn up ready to make or break their month’s rent. And it was immediately obvious to Sally that Ginger wasn’t just there to observe — he was absolutely cleaning up. He was camped behind a towering, opulent stack of square counters, and even as she watched, the deadpan croupier raked another huge pile of winnings straight over to his side of the table.

“Epatant!” sighed a goggling man at her elbow, yanking his elbow out of her ribs to gesticulate wildly. Sally’s French was terrible, but she got the gist: this guy was equal parts shocked and awed. The whole crowd was. Continental roulette onlookers are weirdly altruistic — they get the same high watching some random stranger win big as if they’d won the cash themselves. The croupier twisted his moustache with one hand, spun the wheel with the other, and the whole room went dead quiet. Sally had shifted to a less crowded spot, and now she could see Ginger’s face clearly — and snort-laughed out loud.

He looked exactly like a terrier glued to a rat hole. His red hair practically bristled with excitement. You could almost swear his ears were pricked straight up. In that dead, tense hush around the spinning wheel, Sally’s cackle cut through the room like a foghorn. The effect was immediate. The crowd around a hot roulette streak carries the same pious, reverent energy as a congregation at a cathedral service — and if she’d guffawed mid-Hail Mary at high mass, she couldn’t have got more pained, scandalized stares. Ginger jolted around at the noise, saw her, and shot to his feet. Right then the ball clicked into a red slot that wasn’t Ginger’s pick, the crowd groaned so hard it sounded like the penitents’ bench at a southern US revival meeting, and every single person in the room agreed: Sally’s dumb laugh had jinxed the golden boy.

Ginger didn’t care a bit. He scooped up every last one of his chips, stuffed them all in his pocket, and elbowed his way over to fish Sally out of the crowd, who by this point was universally treated like a social pariah. Everyone was gutted the big winner was calling it a night — it felt like a A-list movie star had walked off stage mid-monologue, and even a screaming, hair-pulling fight between two gamblers over a disputed five-franc chip couldn’t cheer them up properly.

“Hey!” Ginger said, dragging her clear to a quiet little ante-room off the gambling floor. “This is brilliant! I’ve been hunting for you everywhere.”

“Funny, that’s exactly what I was doing for you,” Sally said.

Ginger beamed, led her over to a quiet corner bench, the only other person nearby being a fancy uniformed attendant by the door who looked like he’d seen every terrible romantic misadventure known to man.

“That’s so nice of you.”
“I had to talk to you before my train left.”

Ginger jolted so hard he nearly fell off the bench. “Your train? What train?”
“The choo-choo,” Sally explained. “I’m leaving tonight, remember?”
“Leaving?” Ginger looked as horrified as the most pious of the roulette congregation had after her jinx laugh. “You can’t be leaving Roville! Where on earth are you going?”
“Back to America. My boat sails from Cherbourg tomorrow.”
“Oh my aunt!”

Sally felt a little soft spot for him, all wide-eyed and distraught. She was a warm-hearted girl, after all, and who doesn’t like being appreciated? “I’m sorry, but — ”
“Look,” Ginger blurted, turning bright scarlet and staring very intently at the uniformed attendant by the door, who was now giving them the indulgent little smile of a guy who’d been exactly this flustered back in his youth. “Look, will you marry me?”

Sally stared at his beet-red profile, completely gobsmacked. She’d figured Ginger was a surprising guy, but not this surprising. “Marry you?”
“You know. The whole wedding and house and so on thing.”
“Uh, yeah, I follow. You’re proposing the holy state of matrimony. Got it.”
“So what d’you say?”

Sally got her wits back, and her sense of humour was cackling. She looked at him very gravely. He wouldn’t meet her eye, still gawking at the attendant, who was so wrapped up in their little drama he started quietly humming a French love ballad under his breath.
“Isn’t this… don’t think I’m being difficult, but isn’t this a tiny bit sudden?”
“It has to be sudden!” Ginger whined. “I thought you were gonna be here for weeks!”
“Come on, my guy, have you noticed we’re basically strangers?” She patted his hand patronizingly, making the uniformed attendant heave a tender, swoony sigh. “You’ve mixed me up with some girl you actually know properly, the one you got properly introduced to. Look at me again, you’ll realise.”
“If I look at you hard enough,” Ginger said, feverish, “I don’t answer for what I’ll do.”
“And this is the man I was gonna give a whole speech about ambition to!”
“You’re the most amazing girl I’ve ever met, curse it!” Ginger babbled, still not looking at her. “I know it’s sudden, I can’t help it! I fell in love with you the second I saw you, that’s just how it went!”
“Wait, have you considered I might already be engaged to someone else?”

Ginger finally snapped around to look at her, and his face was so genuinely upset Sally’s laugh died in her throat. As silly as it all was, this idiot was dead serious.
“As a matter of fact, yeah, I am,” she said gently.

Ginger bit his lip and went quiet for a minute. “Oh. Well. That’s that, then.”

Sally had a weird, soft, maternal feeling, like watching a small puppy beg for a whole chocolate bar it’s not allowed to have, the kind of feeling where you don’t know if you want to laugh or tuck their hair behind their ear. There was no way she could treat this overgrown redhead like a reasonable grown man right now.
“It’s not real,” she said. “Love at first sight doesn’t exist. Love’s when you know someone properly, you — ” She cut herself off. Wait a second, she and Gerald Foster had fallen for each other in two weeks flat, engaged before they even knew what his favourite breakfast was. She ran out of steam fast, ending with a very limp “It’s ridiculous.”

Ginger slumped into melancholy resignation. “I never expected you to say yes, anyway. I’m not exactly a great catch.”

That was the exact in Sally was waiting for. “That’s exactly what I wanted to talk to you about! I was gonna finish that conversation we started in the elevator last night, before we got interrupted. Can I talk to you like a sister? Let’s make me your honorary sister, yeah?”
Ginger didn’t look super thrilled at the sister bit, but he perked up a little. “That’s really nice of you.”
“I’m gonna give you wisdom. Ginger, why don’t you brace up? Stiffen your spine, stick your chin out, and go actually do something with your life! Why do you just flop around waiting for your family to hand you jobs and decide where you live? Why don’t you go out and grab work for yourself? Why do you care so much what the family thinks? Go make yourself independent! I know you had bad luck when you lost all your money, but every single successful person in the world has been broke at some point. It’s part of the fun! You’ll never get anywhere letting your family pick you up like a floppy Newfoundland puppy and dump you wherever’s convenient for them. Go find something you’re good at, go after it like a rabid badger, hold onto it, and forget the family exists!”

Sally stopped, gasping for air. Ginger stared at her for a long, thoughtful second. “When you talk really fast,” he said, eventually, very seriously, “your nose goes all squiggly. Looks great.”

Sally squawked indignantly. “Are you telling me you didn’t listen to a single word I said?”
“Oh no! I listened! Honest!”
“Then what did I say?”
“Uh… and your eyes shine, too.”
“Forget my eyes! What did I say?”
“You told me… er… to get a job,” Ginger said, after a moment’s hard reflection.

Sally sighed. “Well, I phrased it a lot fancier than that, but yeah, that’s the gist. Good. Glad that landed.”
“Hey,” Ginger said, looking all mournful and devoted. “Can I write to you? Letters, I mean? I think it’d keep me motivated, if I could send you updates.”
“Sure, why not. I wanna know how you get on, anyway.” She gave him the address of the New York boarding house she was staying at, and he wrote it down as reverently as if he was copying the Ten Commandments on his shirt cuff.

Then Sally glanced at the clock on the wall, and nearly had a heart attack. “Oh my GOD! That’s the time? My train!” She sprinted for the swing door, nearly knocking over the confused attendant who had no idea their quiet romance drama was about to turn into a track meet. “Goodbye, Ginger! Write me, remember my speech!”

Ginger, surprisingly fast for a guy who spends half his time drunk and the other half gambling, bolted after her. He’d been a rugby scrum-half back in his university days, after all, so he knew how to run. They sprinted side by side across the square to the platform, just as the train was starting to slowly chug out of the station. Ginger dove for the nearest open door, wrenched it wide open, picked Sally up neatly, and hurled her inside the carriage. She landed square on the toes of the guy in the corner seat, bounced off, and lunged for the window. Ginger was trotting alongside the speeding train, waving.
“Ginger! My porter! Tip him! I forgot!”
“Got it!”
“Don’t forget what I said! Death to the overbearing family!”
“Right ho!”

The train pulled smoothly out of the station. Sally waved until Ginger was a tiny red speck in the distance, then turned around to apologize to the man whose toes she’d just crushed. “I’m so sorry!” she panted. “I hope I didn’t hurt you too bad.”

She was staring straight at Bruce Carmyle. The dark, stuffy cousin she’d run into on the beach the day before. The one who’d assumed she was a random French tourist.

Mr. Carmyle was not the kind of man who let little life surprises ruffle him, but right now he was very, very confused. He’d pegged her as a local French girl yesterday, and now she was chattering to him in perfect, fluent English? When had she learned that? And how on earth had she, a total stranger yesterday, got close enough to his cousin “Ginger” — a nickname he’d always thought was tacky and vulgar — to sprint down the platform with him and yell out the window like they were old college pals? If Sally hadn’t been so pretty, Bruce would have given her a very stern talking-to, she’d completely shattered his strict sense of public propriety. But right now, flushed and panting from the run, she was the prettiest girl he’d ever seen, so he forced a smile.

“Not at all,” he lied. His left big toe was throbbing like it had been hit with a hammer. Even a girl with tiny feet can do some damage when a former rugby scrum-half hurls you straight at it. He hobbled a little, then gestured to the empty seat. “Close call. You nearly missed the train.”
“Yeah, lucky Ginger — Mr. Kemp — was there. He throws really well, doesn’t he?” Sally said, collapsing into the seat to catch her breath.

A waiter popped into the corridor, announcing in surprisingly good English that dinner was being served in the dining car.
“Shall we?” Bruce offered. Sally, who was starving, agreed immediately, and told herself off for judging him earlier for being all grim and stern. He seemed perfectly nice, for a stuffy Brit — until they sat down at the dinner table, and his entire mood soured.

This was a man who took dining extremely seriously, and thought waiters should be seen and not heard. He winced at a tiny spot on the tablecloth, frowned at the menu, and acted like the cheerful, friendly old waiter who doted on Sally was a personal insult. The waiter and Sally were getting on like a house on fire, even though neither spoke the other’s language — he treated her like his favourite granddaughter — until Bruce snapped his order out in that weird loud half-French half-English dialect British tourists use. The waiter chirped “Boum!” happily and vanished.
“Nice old guy!” Sally said.
“Far too familiar,” Bruce grumbled.

Sally changed the subject. “By the way, my name’s Nicholas. I think it’s polite to get names out of the way first, don’t you?”
“Mine’s Carmyle,” he said, then stiffened up. “Though I believe you already heard that from my cousin, apparently.”
Sally didn’t like his cold, judgemental tone. “Why ‘apparently’? I told you we got stuck in an elevator together for half the night. You can’t spend that long trapped with someone without getting to know them. Mr. Kemp’s very nice, and very interesting.”
“Interesting?” Bruce raised a very judgemental eyebrow. “My cousin can play rugby quite well, and I’m told he’s a decent amateur boxer, but we never found him particularly entertaining. The whole family thinks he’s a little dull.”
“Last time I checked, only royalty gets to call themselves ‘we’ when they’re talking about their relatives,” Sally shot back, sharp as a tack. The mention of the overbearing Kemp family made her want to throw a bread roll at his head. “He told me about Mr. Scrymgeour, the guy he punched for beating his dog.”
Bruce stared at a loaf of bread on the table like it had personally offended him. “He has absolutely no sense of privacy.”
The waiter came back and slammed the soup down, grinning at Sally like he’d just pulled a magic trick off. Sally didn’t grin back. She was furious. “I think he got treated terribly unfairly,” she said.
“I would prefer not to discuss my family’s private affairs,” Bruce said, icy as the north sea. “A man who throws away a perfectly good, well-paid job just because — ”
“Oh, for heaven’s sake!” Sally snapped. “Stop being so stuffy! You look exactly like one of those boring 18th century portraits in a heavy gold frame, staring out at everyone with fishy eyes like they’re an unpleasant inconvenience.”

The waiter popped up behind them. “Rosbif!” he announced cheerily, dropping the roast beef on the table. Bruce attacked his beef like it personally wronged him.
“I’m sorry,” he said, stiff as a board. “I never had anyone tell me my eyes looked fishy before.”
“Bet if you’d had sisters, they would have told you years ago,” Sally said.

He went silent and offended until the coffee showed up. Sally stood up, pushing her chair back. “I think I’ll head back to my carriage. I don’t want any coffee, and if I sit here any longer I’ll say something properly rude. I was gonna try and talk you into going easy on Ginger, but clearly that’s impossible. Goodbye, Mr. Carmyle. Thanks for the dinner.”

She stormed off down the corridor, leaving Bruce staring after her, both indignant and weirdly, completely fascinated. No one had ever called him stuffy, or said his eyes looked like a fish’s, in his entire life. And now he couldn’t stop thinking about it.
