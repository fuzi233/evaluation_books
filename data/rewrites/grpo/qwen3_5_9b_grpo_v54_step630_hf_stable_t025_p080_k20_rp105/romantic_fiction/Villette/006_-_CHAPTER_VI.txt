The first of March broke upon me with a light that felt like a promise. When I drew back my curtain, the sun was not yet fully risen; it struggled through a thick, grey fog, a pale, determined eye trying to see the world. Above the rooftops, hovering almost in the clouds, stood the Dome of St. Paul's—a solemn, dark blue orb that seemed to anchor the sky. Looking at it, I felt a sudden, violent shaking in my spirit, as if chains I had worn for years were finally snapping. For the first time, I felt the terrifying edge of true life. My soul expanded with the frantic growth of Jonah's gourd.

"I did well to come," I whispered to the empty room, moving quickly to dress. "There is a spirit in this great London that calls to me. Who is a coward who spends a lifetime in a hamlet, letting his mind rust in the damp of obscurity?"

Dressed and refreshed, I descended. The waiter, a grey-haired man who had served in this very spot for twenty years, greeted me with a warmth that instantly transformed our interaction. When I mentioned my uncles, Charles and Wilmot, his memory was sharp and respectful. Suddenly, the uncertainty that had clung to me vanished; I was no longer a stranger, but a guest of honor. He told me I resembled my uncle Charles, a compliment that felt like a shield. The street outside was quiet, clean, and devoid of the terror I had imagined. It was merely a provincial thoroughfare, and I felt bold enough to walk it alone.

Breakfast was eaten with a sense of exhilaration. To walk the streets of London felt like an adventure in itself. I wandered until I reached Paternoster Row, entering a bookseller's shop kept by a stern man named Jones. There, I committed a small extravagance, buying a book I could ill afford, intending one day to gift it to Mrs. Barrett. Mr. Jones, a dried-up creature of business, watched me with a mixture of awe and indifference, while I felt the height of my own happiness.

From there, I climbed the stairs to the dome. From that vantage point, London unfolded beneath me: the river, the bridges, the ancient Westminster, the green Temple Gardens bathed in early spring sunlight. The city was alive, a chaotic tapestry of business and rush. I loved this earnestness better than the idle pleasures of the West End. Here, people were fighting for their living; there, they were merely enjoying theirs.

By two o'clock, hunger and fatigue pulled me back to my dark, quiet inn. I ate a plain meal that tasted better than any delicacy Miss Marchmont's cook had ever sent, and I slept for an hour on three stacked chairs. When I woke, the air in my mind was clear, and a dangerous resolve took root. I had nothing left to lose. The loathing of my desolate past was absolute. If I failed, if I died far from home, who would weep? I was hardened to suffering, even to death itself.

That evening, I secured passage on a ship bound for Boue-Marine. The waiter advised immediate departure, and I agreed. I tipped him generously, a princely sum that made him smile with a knowing nod of approval. He hailed a coach, instructing the driver to take me directly to the wharf. The driver, however, betrayed my trust, dumping me into the arms of a throng of shouting watermen in the dark.

It was a chaotic struggle. Oaths flew like bullets, shaking my fragile philosophy. One man grabbed my trunk; another reached for me. I stood my ground, shaking off the touch, stepping firmly into a boat, and commanding the rower to place my trunk beside me. "Just there," I ordered, and he obeyed. We pushed off into the black river, a torrent of ink under a midnight sky. The names of the ships—"The Ocean," "The Phoenix"—glowed white in the lantern light, but I sought "The Vivid."

As we rowed toward the harbor, I thought of Charon ferrying souls to the underworld. The wind was chill, the rain fell hard, and my rowers cursed wildly. Yet, I felt no terror, only a strange alertness. When the boat reached the ship, the waterman demanded six shillings. "You ask too much," I replied. A young steward grinned, anticipating a fight, but I paid the extra money to avoid the scene. "They've cheated you!" the steward crowed. "I knew it," I answered calmly, and went below.

In the ladies' cabin, I encountered a stout, handsome woman who looked at me with insolent curiosity. She muttered about the oddity of a passenger arriving at midnight, her face a mask of selfish vanity. "Now that I am on board, I shall certainly stay here," I declared, demanding to see my berth. She complied sullenly. I arranged my things and lay down, feeling a brief victory. My mind, once homeless and anchorless, now had a place to rest.

The stewardess, a boisterous woman who seemed to be in her forties but bloomed like a girl of twenty, dominated the night. She argued endlessly with her son, the young steward, reading aloud letters full of family secrets regarding a younger sister, Charlotte, who was about to make a romantic match. The son laughed her protests to scorn; she raved at him. Later, she spoke of the "Watsons," a wealthy family whose passage fees amounted to a fortune.

At dawn, the passengers boarded. The Watsons, four boisterous figures dressed in absurd finery, were welcomed with fanfare. Beside them stood a young lady in simple print and a straw bonnet, escorted by a languid gentleman. The contrast was stark: the Watsons radiated conscious wealth, while the young lady, Ginevra Fanshawe, wore her poverty with grace. Her father kissed her and left her to the ship.

Ginevra soon approached me, offering me a camp-stool with a smile that was both charming and puzzling. I declined politely. She danced away, light and heedless, marrying a man who looked more like an oil barrel than a husband, yet she seemed gay even to giddiness. The other young lady, Ginevra, was different. She paced the deck, disdainful of the Watsons' vulgarity, until she stopped before me.

"Are you fond of a sea-voyage?" she asked.

I admitted I had never been on one. She envied my novelty, claiming to be *blasée* about everything. When I pointed out her youth, she bristled, admitting she was seventeen. She boasted of crossing the Channel ten times alone, though she claimed to make friends easily. I noted the noisy Watsons nearby. "You will scarcely make many friends this voyage," I said.

"Not of those odious men and women," she snapped. "Such people should be steerage passengers." She asked if I was going to school. I said no, that I was going to earn a living. She gasped at the idea of poverty, then revealed her own situation. She was the daughter of Captain Fanshawe, an officer on half-pay, supported by her godfather in France. She had five sisters and three brothers, destined to marry elderly gentlemen with cash. Her own education was a farce; she played and danced, spoke French and German, but could not read or write them well. She knew nothing of history or religion, having forgotten her faith entirely. She was a Protestant by label, but unsure of her soul.

"And where are you now?" I asked.

"Oh! at—*chose*," she said, substituting the French word for any missing name. She meant Villette, the capital of her kingdom of Labassecour. She described it as populated by stupid natives and nice English families, though her current school was "horrid." She sent her lessons to the devil and cared for nothing else.

"You are laughing at me again?" she asked, sensing my amusement.

"No," I lied. "I am only smiling at my own thoughts."

"What are they?" she pressed.

"Where Fate may lead me. My business is to earn a living where I can find it."

"To earn!" she exclaimed in consternation. "Are you poor, then?"

"As poor as Job."

She dismissed the thought as unpleasant but claimed to know poverty well. She detailed her family's financial struggles and her sister Augusta's marriage to a yellow-fever survivor, a rich man who provided a carriage and establishment. "This is better than 'earning a living,'" she concluded. She asked if I was clever. I denied it. She insisted I was, noting my ability to speak multiple languages, though I had none. She predicted severe seasickness, declaring she would go below and order the stewardess about.

She descended, followed shortly by the others. Throughout the afternoon, I remained on deck alone. The tranquility of those hours, amidst the hazardous uncertainty of my position, reminded me of the old adage: "Stone walls do not a prison make, Nor iron bars—a cage." Peril, loneliness, and an uncertain future were not oppressive evils so long as the frame was healthy and liberty lent its wings.

I was not sick until we passed Margate. Then, the sea-breeze became a torment, and the heaving waves of the Channel filled me with a divine, yet painful, delight. In my reverie, I saw Europe as a wide dream-land, a line of gold under a solemn sky, with an arch of hope stretching from north to south. But daydreams are delusions of the demon.

Soon, sickness overtook me completely. I stumbled into the cabin, where Ginevra Fanshawe's berth was next to mine. Her selfishness was relentless; she tormented me with impatience and fretfulness, far worse than the stoic Watsons. Like small beer in thunder, she soured in adversity. Indignant at her teasing peevishness, I curtly told her to hold her tongue. The rebuff pleased her, and she liked me no worse for it.

As night fell and the sea roughened, the ship groaned under the gale. Furniture fell, and the passengers grew sicker. Ginevra groaned that she must die. "Not just yet, honey," the stewardess cooed. "We're just in port." Within a quarter-hour, the storm broke, and the voyage ended.

I was sorry. My resting time was over; my difficulties recommenced. Stepping onto the deck, the cold air and the black scowl of the night rebuked my presumption. The lights of the foreign harbor glimmered like threatening eyes. Friends swarmed the Watsons and carried away Ginevra, leaving me alone.

Yet, I must go somewhere. Necessity knows no nicety. I paid the stewardess, who seemed surprised by my coin, and asked for directions to a quiet, respectable inn. She called a commissionaire to guide me, leaving my trunk at customs. He led me through a moonlit, rudely paved street to the inn. I offered him sixpence, then a shilling, but he refused both, speaking sharply in a language I did not know. A waiter intervened, explaining my money was foreign currency. I handed over a sovereign to change it.

Finally, I secured a small chamber. Supper was impossible; I was sea-sick, unnerved, and trembling. When the door closed behind me, sealing in my exhaustion, I felt a profound relief. Tomorrow, the cloud of doubt would be as thick as ever, the necessity for exertion more urgent, the peril of destitution nearer, and the conflict for existence more severe. But for tonight, I could rest.
