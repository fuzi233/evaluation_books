The dawn broke over Gran Canaria with a chill that seeped straight through our clothes, a lingering bite from the night's open-air vigil. Even in the subtropics, the dew and the encroaching shadows demanded respect. While I usually prefer a brisk jog to shake off the stiffness of camping, the terrain here offered no relief; the ground was a jagged mosaic of rough lava, devoid of the three yards of flat earth needed for a proper stride. Instead, Dr. Coppinger, a man whose medical credentials changed with alarming frequency—often acquiring a fresh degree roughly every eighteen months—began a series of improvised dumbbell exercises with two heavy chunks of bristly rock. I followed suit, mirroring his frantic energy.

Two days prior, a storm had washed over the island, leaving a trickle of water snaking along the floor of the deep ravine, or *barranca*, beneath us. This was the greatest luxury we possessed: the ability to scrub ourselves clean and brush our teeth. "Now," Coppinger declared, patting his pockets as we finished washing, "our provisions are dwindling to a critical mass, and the quality has suffered significantly due to being crammed into a damp Spanish newspaper."

"My share is mostly tobacco ash," I replied grimly.

"It will deteriorate further if we delay. We have significant scrambling ahead of us." His assessment was accurate. Our next stop was the *fonda* at Santa Brigida, a ten-mile trek where we had deposited our equipment, and where Coppinger intended to conduct extensive photography and measurements before departing. Consequently, hunger would likely be our constant companion until we reached the patron's renowned vintage wines—a beverage, I was convinced, that remained a secret of the mountains, invisible to the discerning palate of the English hotels in Las Palmas.

Truthfully, the two mummies Coppinger had already recovered satisfied my modest archaeological ambitions. Encased in goatskins as brittle as parchment, the ancient figures disintegrated into clouds of dust at the slightest touch. Yet, Coppinger was a man of obsessive vision. He suspected the presence of an ancient Guanche university or sacred college, akin to the legendary institution on the opposite side of the island, and he would not be content until every cave on the cliff face had been ransacked. With twenty-eight rolls of film remaining and a flashlamp ready, he deemed it prudent to complete the survey immediately rather than undertaking a return trip. Armed with a crowbar and a heavy rope, I ascended the ridge, where the previous day's sun had baked the stones into a white-hot glare.

Access to these caverns was notoriously difficult. If they had been easily reachable, centuries of raiders would have long since stripped them bare. Coppinger, ever confident in his theories, posited that the ancients utilized retractable ropes of goat-hair to secure these entrances, effectively barring intruders. Regardless of the validity of his theory, the cave mouths lay in a horizontal row thirty feet below the ridge and fifty feet above the ravine floor, a position inaccessible to casual Spanish curiosity. Descending via ladder from below would have been cumbersome, so we adopted a daring strategy: lower a lightweight knotted rope from the top, rappel down to the cave entrance, and repeat the process for each chamber.

Coppinger possessed courage aplenty and a steady hand, but his portliness and proximity to fifty made the vertical descent perilous. I volunteered to lead the way, descending first to scout the interior and assist from below. However, navigating a vertical cliff face in a "fly-fashion" with a thin rope provided little room for error. As the first three caves proved to be mere storage dens, I urged Coppinger to bypass them, saving his energy. He refused, insisting on inspecting each chamber personally. His imagination ran wild; he labeled one a prison, another a pottery workshop, and a third a seminary for young priests, dismissing my practical assessments as naive.

As the sun climbed higher, the heat became oppressive, and Coppinger's enthusiasm began to waver, replaced by a dangerous stubbornness. Despite the evident fatigue, he pressed on, insisting we examine three additional openings. I suggested a retreat to Santa Brigida, but he balked, claiming that failing to investigate the final sites would be a professional disgrace. He attempted to frame the request as a favor, suggesting I merely observe his findings, but I firmly declined. My body was exhausted, drenched in sweat, my temples throbbing under the relentless sun, and my hands raw from the friction of the rope.

Undeterred, Coppinger tried to reignite my spirit. "Think of what lies above!" he urged. "If you uncover anything, it is yours alone. I stake no claim."

Lacking expertise in ancient scripts and trusting Coppinger's assertion that the Guanches could not write, I initially dismissed the markings as decorative. There could be no doubting the speed with which Empress Phorenice's will took root in the city of Atlantis. To her, the nation and everything within it existed solely for her benefit, and no price was too steep to pay for her desires. To recount the specifics of how a handful of disciplined fighters shattered a rebel horde fifteen times larger would be tedious. What mattered was the psychological collapse that followed. The rebels, numbering in the thousands, had poured into the city via a secret mine, confident in their victory. Instead, they found silence. Not a single man emerged from the tunnels. Within moments, the initial whispers of terror inside the walls erupted into a cacophony of triumph, turning the besiegers' confidence into a sudden, malignant panic.

These were not professional soldiers; they were Atlanteans, yes, bred for courage, but stripped of leadership by their own "silly principles" which declared every man equal to his neighbor. With no commanders and arms of vile quality, they were a chaotic mass. When the panic struck, it spread like a plague through their ragged ranks. There were no officers to rally the fleeing, no voices to direct the desperate few who stayed to fight.

My strategy was brutally simple: I never played defense. My men and I became a relentless hammer. We turned one flank, smashed through the center, and harassed the other, hacking a path through the solid mass. By constantly keeping them running and guessing where the next blow would fall, we fermented their terror until the outer lines fled toward the forests and scorched fields, dragging the inner packs with them.

Breaking this siege was not a feat of martial genius, nor did it require the skill of a veteran legionnaire. It was merely seizing the momentum of the moment. Had these rebels faced us in open battle on their own soil, their sheer ferocity and numbers would have overwhelmed any trained troop. They were Atlanteans, just as the citizens within the walls were, and their oppression had pushed them to a savagery born of desperation. Yet, scattered as they were, the rebellion crumbled. I saw clearly that the uprising could be ended without excessive cruelty; too great a severity would only drive the malcontents to further despair.

Chasing the fugitives to ensure their rout had taken us miles from the city walls. From the moment man first walked the earth, shaped by deities watching from afar, certain regions of the globe proved unfit for his survival. Nowhere was this truer than the section of Atlantis bordering the savage coasts of Europe and Africa. To the common folk, this cursed land was avoided out of superstitious dread; they believed the spirits of the wicked dead stalked these grounds in broad daylight, slaying anyone foolish enough to linger, while the more obvious physical dangers merely spared victims destined for a supernatural torment. Thus, it frequently occurred that criminals, fleeing justice, chose to surrender voluntarily to the executioners rather than face the legendary horrors of the forbidden zone.

To the educated, many of these tales were recognized as myths, yet a lingering unease persisted. Life here was arduous and fraught with peril. The mountains remained in a state of eternal agitation, their contents perpetually boiling. Earthquakes were a daily occurrence, and invisible, malevolent vapors drifted with cunning speed, choking travelers before they could escape. Rivers ran thick with poison that altered neither color nor scent. Great fissures tore through the earth, opening gates to subterranean fires; waters rushed into these chasms only to erupt with devastating explosions. Boiling mud and molten rock poured forth with terrifying regularity.

Yet amidst this chaos, dark, somber forests thrived. Trees of unimaginable antiquity stood silent, rarely disturbed by fire. Beneath their canopy, the earth was saturated with water, and the air hung heavy with warm, suffocating steam. For a man to endure such constant, scorching humidity was mortifying to his strength. Yet strength was required, along with extraordinary cunning, for these treacherous lands were the domain of lizards. Of all beasts, they grew to the most enormous proportions and possessed the most formidable nature.

Countless families and species inhabited this realm. While some could be contended with, others were so gigantic that no human force could oppose them. I witnessed one emerge from a lake after feeding, causing the wetland to shake and pulse with every step it took. This monster could swallow Phorenice's mammoth whole, and even a charging beast could not inflict harm upon it. Great, horn-like plates armored its head and body, while rows of spines along its back, tail, and limbs ripped great shreds from the black trees as it moved through the forest.

Occasionally, these titans became trapped in massive ground fissures, but this happened rarely. Their speed was immense, and their sagacity keen. They seemed to intuit the eruptions of the mountains, seeking refuge in deep lakes or burying themselves in muddy wallows. Furthermore, they were uniquely adapted to the region's greatest hazard: the heat. While lethal to man, the water's temperature caused them no harm. I observed one lying peacefully in a pool while steam bursts sent water leaping into boiling fountains nearby, and I watched another sprint across a flowing stream of molten rock, emerging unscathed.

Thrown into this hostile environment, my existence immediately became perilous and harsh. I came close to understanding the true nature of Fear. Only a fervent trust in the Highest Gods, and a firm conviction that my life was under Their protective care, kept me from succumbing to absolute despair. For weeks, until I grasped the rhythms of this steaming, sweltering land, I existed in misery worse than anything Phorenice could have imagined. My clothes rotted from the constant moisture until I was as naked as a European savage. My limbs ached with fevers, and I lacked herbs to cure them. For days, my sustenance was limited to tree-grubs and leaves; when I finally managed to hunt, I often consumed beasts whose toxic qualities brought me agony and sickness.

However, as humans are born to adapt, so did I eventually learn the limits of this new existence and gather knowledge of its resources. I discovered a towering black tree with a hollow core and an entrance near its roots. I harbored within it, until one night a monstrous lizard, weighing enough to rock the tree like a sapling, attempted to suck me out like a bird picking a worm from a log. By the will of the Gods, I escaped—my weapon could have harmed a mountain as easily as cutting that horny tongue—but I vowed never to risk it again.

Thus, I constructed a ladder of footholes inside the trunk, climbing to a height of ten man-lengths. There, I carved additional notches and fashioned a platform from branches to rest upon. Later, seeking a measure of luxury, I carved arrow-slits into the chamber walls and hauled up sand to create a hearth, allowing me to cook my victuals safely above the dangers of the open wilderness.

Gradually, I mastered the capture of large-scaled river fish and smaller turtles, and my ribs ceased threatening to break through my skin. Yet, I could never overcome the scarcity of salads and gruels; all green meat tasted so powerfully of tar that my palate rejected it. Of course, the threat of the giant lizards and other native perils remained ever-present.

As the months mounted into years, and the primal needs of my nature were satisfied, other, more complex longings emerged. From the ivory of a river-horse's tusk, I had attempted to carve a likeness of Nais as I last saw her. Though my heart was devoted and my intentions pure, my art was dull. The result, despite endless retries, remained clumsy and pitiful. Nevertheless, in my eyes, it held traces of the original—a curve here, an outline there—and it reignited my old love within me as I sat and gazed upon it. Yet, it failed to satisfy my longing to be with her again or to know her fate; instead, it sharpened my cravings.

Other men of the Priests' Clan had ventured out, establishing dwellings in these Dangerous Lands. By mortifying their flesh, they gained an intimacy with the Higher Mysteries surpassing what ordinary human learning could achieve. Some, returning to civilized society, carried knowledge that earned them reputations for magic and miracles, fueling the common people's ardor for the cult of the Gods. However, I could not free my mind for such abstruse studies. During my voyage home from Yucatan, I had communed with them with growing insight, but now my mind was not my own. Nais held a lien upon it, refusing to be ousted. In truth, her sweet trespass was my sole solace.

Finally, my longing could no longer be denied. Through the arrow-slits of my tree-house, I watched a great mountain top whitened with perpetual snow, stained red every evening by our Lord the Sun. Night after night, I strained my eyes toward that ruddy light, remembering that when I entered the priesthood, it had been my duty to adore our great Lord as He rose behind those same snows. Always, the thought followed: from that distant crest, I could see across the continent to the Sacred Mount, where the city lay and where I had buried my love.

At last, I yielded. A perilous journey ensued. Heavy mists hung over the lowlands, rendering my path blind and leading me astray repeatedly. Traversing three times the direct distance, I moved through swamps patrolled by the great lizards. Being unfamiliar with the terrain, I lacked knowledge of safe harbors and came within an ace of being spotted and devoured a hundred times. Yet, the High Gods still desired me for Their purposes, blinding the great beasts as I slipped into cover. Together with Zaemon, they were the sole survivors raised to the highest degree of the Clan, guardians of secrets and powers unknown to mortals.

"Look!" cried Zaemon, his shrill voice echoing as he swept a trembling finger across the shattered landscape below. I followed his gaze, and a chill crawled through my veins. All was laid bare. Our Lord the Sun burned high in a cloudless blue sky, day shimmering in His heat. Below, everything appeared peaceful and still, save for the smoking mountains and the sea, which boiled with a strange, violent disorder.

But it was the sea that haunted me. Far out on the distant coast, it surged against the rocks in monstrous rolls. Up the great estuary, where Atlantis stood, it gushed in successive, towering waves that never returned. The lower lands on both sides were already erased beneath tumultuous waters; harbor walls were submerged, and the flood crept upward into the lower wards of the great city.

"You have seen?" asked Zaemon.

"I have seen."

"You understand?"

"In part."

"Then let me tell you all. This is the beginning, and the swift end follows. The Most High Gods, who sit behind the stars, have a limit to Their patience, and it has been crossed. Atlantis, the great continent beyond, and all within it, are doomed to unutterable destruction. Long ago, it was foreseen that this wiping-out would occur through the sins of men, leading to the construction of the Ark of the Mysteries under divine direction. No mortal tool can scratch its surface; no wave or rock can wreck it. Within lies recorded on ancient sheets all worldly learning not shared with the common folk, stores of grain sufficient for two persons for four years, sweet water, seeds, weapons, and whatever else was deemed fit."

He paused, his eyes wide with terror and awe. "From this vast realm, the High Gods have decreed that two shall not perish. Two shall be chosen—a man and a woman—who are fit to carry away the ancient learning, dispose of it as they see best, and rear a new race to honor the Sun and the Gods elsewhere. The woman is already within the Ark, seated in her appointed place. Though she is my daughter, the burden of her selection rests with you. As for the man, the choice falls upon yourself."

Shock numbed me. "I do not care to be a survivor."

"You are not asked for your wishes," the old man insisted. "You are given an order from the High Gods, who know you to be Their faithful servant."

Habit, strong as iron, took hold. I offered the requisite salute and declared, "I hear and obey."

"There remains the elevation to the sublime degree of the Three," Zaemon continued, his voice steady despite the chaos. "If your learning is so slight that you cannot grasp the keys to many powers and the highest mysteries when revealed, the fault cannot be remedied now."

Time was running short. The fight raged below at the gate, a miracle that the few priests had held their ground so long. But the ocean rolled inland in an ever-growing flood, and the mountains smoked, belching fresh volleys of rock as the weight increased upon their foundations. Soon, the besiegers would see the doom threatening them. Madness would seize them, sweeping all obstructions aside with frantic fury.

Yet, Zaemon and his companion showed no haste. Lacking a temple, sacrifice, or incense, they used the sky as their roof and the Gods as their witnesses. Still, they performed the rituals of my initiation with all the grandeur and majesty accumulated over the ages, curtailing not a single sentence. Outside, a mountain burst with a bellowing roar as incoming waters met its fires, but the old men recited their sentences gravely. Phorenice's troops breached the last defenses, pouring a frenzied stream through the groves and temples, but the priests never faltered in their chant.

It was said this ceremony was the greatest and most impressive of our holy rites, and I found it so. Even the tremendous circumstances hemming them in could not make these frail elders forget the deference due to the highest order. For myself, I was less entranced. Standing bareheaded in the heat, I struggled to concentrate, torn by a thousand foreign emotions. The cataclysm unfolding around me demanded attention, and my mind drifted constantly to Nais. Yet, I maintained a decent facade, responding to the required chants and striving to memorize the keys to power and learning hidden within the phrases.

It became evident that if the ceremony did not conclude soon, it would be cut short abruptly. Phorenice's legions swarmed onto the mountain's crest, now aware of the dreadful ruin descending upon their homeland. The Ark was a silent leviathan, stripped of rudders, oars, and any machinery save what the High Gods willed. Within, darkness reigned, thick with the scent of antiquity and the low, resonant groans of the dying world outside. I fumbled for the fire-box, seeking to reignite the fallen lamps, but the shadows swallowed my efforts. Blind, we were forced to endure whatever calamity the gods dealt out next. The sea beneath us heaved in a cruel game, tossing the vessel violently, yet I clung tightly to Nais beside me, anchoring her to the deck. Words failed us; in the shared weight of our loss, silence became a companion, a quiet bond stronger than speech.

As Atlantis plunged to become the ocean floor, she left behind a chaotic legacy: massive whirlpools and spouts of drowned fire, gifts to the Sea Gods. Beyond that, I believe—a thought forming in the black void where sight was denied—a hurricane of terrifying force swept across the ruins, ensuring not a single fragment of desolation escaped destruction. For seven nights and seven days, according to the hour-glass suspended within the Ark, this terrible storm raged. Then, the wind's howl finally ceased, leaving only the rolling rhythm of a long, heavy swell. Its motion was distinct, oily and regular, unlike the frantic chaos before. For the first time, I climbed the stairs and pushed open the hatch in the Ark's roof.

Sweet air, pure and invigorating, gushed down like a river, washing away the stench of the interior. As the Ark rose smoothly above the churning seas, I descended to fetch Nais, drawing both of us into the healing rays of the Lord Sun. Together, we bowed our heads in gratitude for this merciful return to light, offering thanks for another day. Exhausted, we lay down where we fell, seeking the easy rest that our bodies desperately craved.

Yet, though weariness weighed me down, true slumber refused to take hold. With heavy eyelids, I stared out at the oily, sun-drenched waters. No shore, no hint of land broke the horizon; the ring of the ocean was unbroken, and the heavens above remained a distant, indifferent vault. Beneath the surface, the bosom of the deep was strewn with the tragic debris of Atlantis—trees, squared timbers, a smashed hide-boat overturned by the tide. Here and there floated the rounded corpses of men and beasts, drifting in grim convoy with our Ark as it wandered, guided solely by divine will and the currents.

Finally, exhaustion claimed me, and I slipped into unconsciousness, Nais's hand clasped firmly in mine. When I awoke, she was already awake, her eyes soft and watchful, gazing at me with tender concern. Rested, strengthened, a strange sense of calm settled over us. The complacency of survival allowed us to accept our station among the gods without complaint. We returned below the deck to eat and drink, fortifying ourselves for the uncertain journey ahead.

Now, bathed in sunlight, the Ark revealed itself as a marvel. Though it had swum upon the waters for the first time in centuries, it bore no leaks, no cracks. Inside, the floors were bone dry. While the grain of the wood spoke of ancient craftsmanship, no joints or sutures marred its surface. Living timbers had been placed and somehow grown together by an art lost to modern times, perfected by the Ancients. A subsequent treatment, another forgotten secret, had hardened the wood until it rivaled steel, impervious to the elements.

Deep within the Ark's cavernous belly, stores awaited. At one end, great tanks flanking a central aisle held a prodigious supply of grain. At the other, tanks brimmed with sweet water. Elsewhere lay medicines, biological samples, and the very essence of animal life, preserved for the voyage under the gods' gaze. Every wall, partition, and beam was etched with the rough artistry of a bygone age, depicting every beast that once roamed Atlantis. I studied these carvings with the keen eye of a hunter; some creatures depicted were utterly unknown to me, species that perished alongside their creators. Weapons and tools for crafts lined the shelves, relics of a civilization now submerged.

For many weeks, existence unfolded in this floating sanctuary. The gods drove us hither and yon across the face of the waters. There was no governance, no human command; we could not alter the Ark's course by even a hair's breadth. The High Gods, having spared only Nais and me from the annihilation of Atlantis, held absolute sway over our destiny. Into Their hands, we willingly surrendered our future, resigning ourselves to drift.

Of the land we eventually reached, where we made our home and raised children, I shall speak in proper season. But since this chronicle demands an account of events in the order they transpired, I must first explain the origin of the sheets upon which these words are inscribed.

In a great coffer resting at the center of the Ark's floor, the Mysteries accumulated over countless ages were recorded in precise script. During the days that followed, I read portions of these texts, and the sheer awe-inspiring, terrifying magnitude of the powers described within paralyzed me. I had witnessed such forces unleashed during Atlantis's fall, but here lay secrets far vaster—the Great Secret of Life and Death that Phorenice had discovered and for which she paid with her life—and truths so profound I could scarcely find words to describe them.

To be the custodian of such forbidden knowledge felt unbearable. The burden grew heavier with every passing moment, gnawing at my soul. Finally, driven to a feverish resolve, I took hot irons and seared the wax-covered sheets, obliterating every letter of the ancient writings. If I erred, may the High Gods, in Their infinite justice, mete out punishment; if it is indeed right that these mysteries vanish from the earth, let Them, in Their boundless power, reveal them anew to worthy scribes when the time comes. Thus, I burned them as the Ark swayed gently over the waves. Later, upon reaching land, I would rewrite the accounts of Atlantis's final throes, but those sacred secrets remained erased.
