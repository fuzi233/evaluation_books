"My Romola," Tito announced on the second morning following his harangue in the Piazza del Duomo, "grand visitors arrive today. The Milanese Count returns, accompanied by the Seneschal de Beaucaire, the great favorite of the Cristianissimo. I know you disdain the smiling ceremonies required by such rustling magnates, figures we shall scarcely behold again. Since they intend to inspect our antiquities and library, perhaps you should suspend your labors and visit your cousin Brigida."

Romola perceived the subtext in his invitation and immediately acquiesced. Yet, returning moments later, hooded and mantled, she remarked, "Oh, what a long breath Florence will draw when the gates fly open and the last Frenchman walks out! Even you, my Tito, seem weary of your patience; confess it. Ah, your brow burns."

He leaned over his desk, pen in hand, and she placed her palm upon his head, intending a parting caress. This posture was habitual; Tito was accustomed to lift his head, lean back slightly, and gaze upward when her hand touched him. But now, he felt as paralyzed as if her hand were a leaden cowl. Instead of rising, he spoke in a light tone, his ink continuing its steady flow.

"The French are as eager to depart Florence as wasps leaving a ripe pear once they have fastened upon it."

Romola, acutely sensitive to the absence of his customary warmth, withdrew her hand and declared, "I am going, Tito."

"Farewell, my sweet one. I must remain at home. Take Maso with you."

Tito did not lift his gaze. Romola departed without further word. In the quiet of marriage, trivialities often mark epochs; that morning, for the first time, she acknowledged not merely that Tito had changed, but that he had altered specifically toward her. Was the cause within herself? She might have suspected so had not the facts of the armor and the painting suggested an external catastrophe, a mystery entirely opaque to her.

The moment Romola vanished from the house, Tito laid down his pen and looked up, finding delightful security in the sight of parchment and broken marble alone. He was disgusted with himself for failing to meet her gaze and behave as usual. He would have chosen to be kinder than ever, yet he could not suddenly master an involuntary shrinking from her presence—a subtle recoil born of the very traits that drove him to desire her affection. He stood on the brink of a step he knew would provoke her deep indignation; he would face much unpleasantness before winning her forgiveness. Tito could never easily confront displeasure; his nature abhorred defiance, and every inclination leaned toward preserving her tenderness. He was not tormented by sentimental scruples, which he had quickly argued away as irrelevant to solid utility, yet his lack of scruples did not shield him from dread. Unscrupulousness removes many burdens, but not toothache, wounded vanity, or the profound loneliness for which the world offers no cure but a healthy jaw and a just, loving soul. At this moment, Tito felt that no device could save him from the pain of the impending collision with Romola; no bland persuasion could cushion the shock driving him forward like a timid animal urged to a desperate leap by the terror of teeth and claws closing in.

A secret conviction had long held that his tenacious adherence to Bardo's wishes regarding the library had become, under current difficulties, a piece of sentimental folly depriving both himself and Romola of substantial advantage. Perhaps this thought would never have crystallized into action but for the events of the past week, which introduced a new motive and a rare opportunity. Indeed, it was only after his dread intensified by seeing Baldassarre regain his sanity, and only when he began to feel compelled to flee Florence, that he resolved to exercise his legal right to sell the library before the chance presented by French and Milanese bidders slipped away. For if he were forced to leave Florence, he would not do so as a destitute wanderer. Accustomed to an agreeable existence, he wished to carry with him all means to retain those conditions. He desired, above all, to take Romola with him—and, if possible, to carry no infamy. Success had awakened in him an appetite for pleasures dependent on advantageous social standing; at no point could flight under dishonor appear as a temptation, but only as a hideous alternative, even with a bag of diamonds, leading to the life of an adventurer. He could not free himself from the regard of Florentines who treated him with polite distance, still less from Romola. She was the wife of his first love—he still loved her; she belonged to the furniture of life he shrank from parting with. He winced under her judgment, uncertain how far her revulsion might extend, and the sense of power a husband possesses, which makes him risk betrayals a lover never dares, proved insufficient to counteract Tito's unease. This was the leaden weight too strong for his will, preventing him from raising his head to meet her eyes. Her pure light brought too near the prospect of a coming struggle. Yet it could not be avoided; if they were to leave Florence, they needed money. Indeed, Tito could not arrange his life to his satisfaction without a considerable sum, and that problem of ordering his life had been the source of all his wrongdoing. He would have been equal to any sacrifice that was not unpleasant.

The rustling magnates arrived and departed, bargains were struck, and Romola returned home, though nothing grave was spoken that night. Tito was merely gay and chatty, pouring forth stories and descriptions of the French visit, a liveliness Romola interpreted as an effort. She attributed it to his consciousness that she had been wounded in the morning, accepting it as an act of penitence, while inwardly aching at the sign of growing distance—the offense neither dared to name.

The following day, Tito remained away until late night. It was a marked day for Romola, for Piero di Cosimo, spurred to greater industry by the fear that he might have caused her pain in the past week, had sent home her father's portrait. She had propped it against the back of his old chair and gazed at it when the door opened behind her and Bernardo del Nero entered.

"It is you, godfather! How I wish you had come sooner! It is already dusk," Romola said, moving toward him.

"I have just come to tell you good news, knowing Tito has not yet returned," Bernardo replied. "The French king moves off tomorrow; not before it is high time. There has been another clash between our people and his soldiers this morning. But there is now a chance for the city to regain order and trade to resume."

"That is joyful," Romola said. "But it is sudden, is it not? Tito seemed to think yesterday there was little prospect of the king's departure."

"He has been well barked at, that is the reason," Bernardo smiled. "His own generals opened their throats quite sufficiently, and finally our Signoria sent the mastiff of the city, Fra Girolamo. The Cristianissimo was frightened by that thunder and has ordered a move. I fear there will be little agreement among us when he is gone, but at least all parties are glad not to have Florence stifled with soldiery any longer, and the Friar barked this time to some purpose. Ah, what is this?" he added as Romola, clasping his arm, led him before the picture. "Let us see."

He began to unwind his long scarf while she arranged a seat for him.

"Do you not want your spectacles, godfather?" Romola asked anxiously, wishing he might see exactly what she saw.

"No, child, no," Bernardo said, uncovering his grey head as he sat with firm erectness. "For seeing at this distance, my old eyes are perhaps better than your young ones. Old men's eyes are like old men's memories; they are strongest for things far away."

"It is better than having no portrait," Romola said apologetically after a brief silence. "It is less like him now than the image I hold in my mind, but that might fade with the years." She rested her arm on the old man's shoulder, drawn strongly toward him by their shared interest in the dead.

"I do not know," Bernardo mused. "I almost think I see Bardo as he was when young better than this picture shows him when old. Your father possessed great fire in his eyes when young. It was what I could never understand—that he, with his fiery spirit, seemingly more impatient than mine, could hang over books and live with shadows all his life. However, he had put his heart into that."

Bernardo gave a slight shrug at the end, but Romola discerned in his voice a feeling that accorded with her own.

"And he was disappointed to the last," she said involuntarily. Immediately fearing her words might be taken as an accusation against Tito, she continued hurriedly, "If we could only see his longest, dearest wish fulfilled just to his mind!"

"Well, so we may," Bernardo said kindly, rising and putting on his cap. "The times are cloudy now, but fish are caught by waiting. Who knows? When the wheel has turned often enough, I may be Gonfaloniere yet before I die; and no creditor can touch these things." He glanced around the room. Turning to her, he patted her cheek. "And you need not fear my dying; my ghost will claim nothing. I have taken care of that in my will."

Romola seized the hand resting against her cheek and pressed it to her lips in silence.

"Haven't you been scolding your husband for keeping away from home so much lately? I see him everywhere but here," Bernardo said, eager to change the subject.

She felt the flush spread over her neck and face as she replied, "He has been very much wanted; you know he speaks so well. I am glad to know that his value is understood."

"You are contented then, Madonna Orgogliosa?" Bernardo asked, smiling as he moved to the door.

"Assuredly."

Poor Romola! There was one thing that would have made the pang of disappointment in her husband harder to bear: that anyone should know he gave her cause for disappointment. This might be a woman's weakness, but it is closely allied to a woman's nobleness. She who willingly lifts the veil of her married life has profaned it from a sanctuary into a vulgar place.
