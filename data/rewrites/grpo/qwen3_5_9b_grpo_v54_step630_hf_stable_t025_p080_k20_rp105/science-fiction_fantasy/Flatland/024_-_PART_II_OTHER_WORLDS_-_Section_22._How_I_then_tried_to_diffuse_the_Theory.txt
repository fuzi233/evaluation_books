The cold rejection of my grandson did not extinguish my hope, nor did it silence my resolve. I realized that a mere slogan—"Upward, not Northward"—was insufficient to shatter the rigid walls of Flatlandian dogma. To truly liberate the mind, I needed proof, a visual architecture that could stand before the public eye. Thus, I turned to the written word, dedicating months of solitary confinement within my own thoughts to the composition of a treatise on the mysteries of the Third Dimension.

However, caution dictated my strategy. To evade the Law, I deliberately avoided the term "physical dimension." Instead, I spoke of a metaphysical realm I called "Thoughtland," a place where a figure could gaze downward upon Flatland, perceiving the interiors of all things simultaneously. In this theoretical space, I described a figure surrounded by six squares, containing eight terminal points—a Cube. Yet, as I wrote, I was plagued by a profound limitation: in Flatland, there are no canvases but lines, and no diagrams but lines. Everything exists on a single axis, distinguishable only by length and luminosity. Consequently, when I finally completed my work, titled *Through Flatland to Thoughtland*, I harbored deep doubts that my readers could ever decipher the geometry I had so laboriously constructed.

My life during this period was cast in shadow. Pleasure lost its flavor; every sight became a tantalizing temptation to treason, for I could not stop comparing the flat, deceptive reality of our world with the true, voluminous nature of things as I had witnessed them. I neglected my clients and my trade, consumed by the agony of trying to reproduce a vision that existed only in my mind. One day, nearly eleven months after my return from Spaceland, I attempted to visualize a Cube with my eyes closed. I failed. Though I eventually succeeded, I remained uncertain whether I had perfectly reconstructed the original form. This uncertainty deepened my melancholy, yet it also hardened my determination. I felt that I would gladly sacrifice my life for the Cause if it could produce even a single spark of conviction. But if I could not convince my grandson, how could I expect to persuade the highest Circles of the land?

Yet, my spirit often proved stronger than my prudence. I found myself slipping into dangerous utterances, labeled heterodox or treasonable by the authorities. Even among the elite Polygonal and Circular societies, I could not restrain myself from dropping phrases like "the eye that discerns the interiors" or "the all-seeing land." On rare occasions, I even let slip the forbidden terms "the Third and Fourth Dimensions." The breaking point came at a meeting of the Local Speculative Society, hosted at the Prefect's palace. An absurd paper had been read arguing that Providence had intentionally limited us to two dimensions and reserved omniscience solely for the Supreme. Overcome by fervor, I abandoned all pretense of fiction. I recounted my entire voyage with the Sphere, my journey through Space, and my return, detailing every sight and sound. I urged my listeners to cast aside their prejudices and embrace the Third Dimension.

Needless to say, I was immediately arrested and brought before the Council.

The next morning, standing in the very spot where the Sphere had once appeared to me, I was granted the floor to speak. However, the atmosphere was thick with foreboding. The President, noting a guard composed of high-angle Policemen (those with angles barely under 55 degrees), ordered them replaced by a lower class, barely two or three degrees above a line. I understood the implication instantly: I was to be executed or imprisoned, and my testimony erased by the simultaneous silencing of the witnesses. The President sought to substitute cheap victims for the expensive truth.

After my defense, the President, perhaps moved by my earnestness, posed two final questions: First, could I indicate the direction of "Upward, not Northward"? Second, could I describe a Cube using anything other than abstract enumeration of sides and angles?

"I can say nothing more," I declared, committing myself entirely to the Truth, trusting that its cause would ultimately prevail.

The President agreed with my sentiment. He sentenced me to perpetual imprisonment. He added that if the Truth intended for me to emerge and evangelize the world, the Truth itself would ensure that outcome. For now, I would face no unnecessary discomfort, barring escape attempts, and I would be occasionally permitted to visit my brother, who had already been sent to prison.

Seven years have passed. I remain a prisoner, isolated save for the company of my jailers and the weekly visits of my brother. He is a noble Square—just, sensible, cheerful, and kind. Yet, these interviews bring me the bitterest pain. He was present when the Sphere manifested in the Council Chamber; he saw the shifting cross-sections and heard the explanations given to the Circles. Still, not a single week has gone by in seven long years without me recounting my experience and describing the phenomena of Spaceland, yet my brother remains unconvinced. He has not grasped the nature of the Third Dimension and openly denies the existence of a Sphere.

I am utterly without converts. The millennial Revelation, it seems, has been made to me in vain. Prometheus ascended to Spaceland to bring fire to mortals, but I, a poor Flatland Prometheus, lie here in chains, bringing down nothing but silence to my countrymen.

Still, in my brighter moments, I cling to a hope: that these memoirs will somehow reach the minds of humanity in some higher dimension, stirring up a race of rebels who refuse to accept the limits of their reality. But darkness often returns. Heavy is the burden of knowing I cannot honestly confirm the exact shape of the Cube I once saw. In my nightly visions, the command "Upward, not Northward" haunts me like a soul-devouring Sphinx. There are seasons of mental weakness when Cubes and Spheres fade into the background of the impossible, when the Land of Three Dimensions seems as illusory as the Land of One or None. In these moments, even this prison wall, these tablets, and the solid realities of Flatland itself seem no better than the offspring of a diseased imagination, a baseless fabric of a dream.
