The instant we saw the chamber—still as a pantry at dawn, every article in its place despite our own brutal bursting-in—the first heat of terror ebbed. The men, breathless and sweating from their stampede up the stairs, stood about like kitchen-hands who have upset a tray and now wait for scolding. When my sense returned enough to hear the house’s own voice again—the boards settling, the night wind worrying the casement-latches, the far-off drip in the stone passage—we dismissed them. Their boots and sabres had filled the gallery with a harsh, clanging din, the sort of noise that makes even courage taste metallic on the tongue; and when it died away, the old château’s quieter sounds pressed back like a remembered dialect returning after a shouted command.

Mademoiselle, always quicker than I at finding a domestic explanation for a nightmare, whispered that Carmilla might have been startled by the uproar at her door and, in her first fright, slipped out of bed and hidden herself in a press, or behind a curtain—anywhere she could wedge her slight body until the majordomo and his myrmidons had withdrawn. The idea, so homely and plausible, cooled us further, as if someone had set a bowl of milk on a boiling stove and it had stopped its raging at once.

We began again, more carefully now, opening doors and peering behind hangings with the conscientious thoroughness of a cook searching a larder shelf—hand to wood, nose to air, eye to shadow. We called her name, not once but over and over, softening it, sharpening it, letting it travel into corners.

“Carmilla—dear Carmilla.”

No answer. Not a sigh, not the faintest rustle, not even that tiny sound of fabric settling that follows a person’s movement in still rooms. The silence seemed to thicken, like cooled broth taking body; and with it my perplexity grew. We went to the windows. They were secured. The night air pressed coldly against the panes, and there was no loosened catch, no lifted sash.

I implored her aloud, if she had hidden herself, to end the cruelty of the trick—to come out and put an end to our anxiety. My own voice sounded wrong in that room, too loud and too modern, like the harsh word of a foreign tongue in a place where only the older speech should have been used.

Still nothing.

By then I was certain she was not in the room at all, nor in the dressing-room beyond, whose door was still locked from our side. She could not have passed it. The certainty sat in my chest like a stone. Had she found one of those secret passages the old housekeeper used to speak of—those lost veins of the schloss that tradition insisted existed though no one could now name their exact mouth? The château, like an old recipe transmitted in fragments, had places where the instructions had died and only the effects remained.

A little time would explain it—so we told ourselves—though for the present we stood in a bewilderment that tasted of cold iron and candle smoke. It was past four o’clock; and I, fearing the remaining darkness as one fears a kitchen after the hearth has gone out, chose to pass the rest of the night in Madame’s room.

Daylight, so often a kind cook that clarifies what night has curdled, brought no solution. From morning onward the whole household was in agitation, my father at its head. Every room was searched, every closet opened, every chest and press examined, as if the château were a vast storehouse and we were hunting for some precious lost ingredient without which the day’s nourishment could not be made. The grounds were explored; servants fanned through hedges and outbuildings; the stable-yard rang with calls and the stamping of horses. The stream was about to be dragged. My father, usually so exact in his calm, was in distraction—already tasting the dreadful story he might have to tell Carmilla’s mother upon her return.

I too was almost beside myself, though my grief was of a different kind, less public and more intimate, like a hunger no one else suspects. The hours went by with the dull persistence of a bad refrain. The château’s acoustics—so often comforting in their old, rural order—were bruised by running feet, slammed doors, shouted questions. Even the birds outside seemed to hold their songs back, as if the noise we made had driven the finer sounds away.

It was one o’clock. Still no tidings.

Something—hope or desperation—drove me at last up again to Carmilla’s room. I went quickly, my heart beating with each stair as if the steps themselves were drumming under my ribs. The corridor smelled of dust and old wax, with a faint sourness where the air lay trapped. When I opened her door—

Carmilla stood at her dressing-table.

For a moment the world stopped. It was as if I had opened a cupboard I had already found empty and discovered it stocked to the brim. I could not believe my eyes. She did not speak. She only beckoned me with one pretty finger, and the gesture—so small, so domestic—was somehow more terrifying for its quietness. Her face expressed extreme fear.

I ran to her in an ecstasy of joy, seized her, kissed and embraced her again and again, feeling the coolness of her cheek, the faint perfume that clung to her hair like bruised violets and something darker beneath. I flew to the bell and rang it vehemently, as if by sheer noise I could undo the hours of silence and relieve my father’s mind.

“Dear Carmilla, what has become of you all this time? We have been in agonies of anxiety about you. Where have you been? How did you come back?”

She looked at me as if my words were a language she only half remembered.

“Last night has been a night of wonders,” she said.

“For mercy’s sake, explain all you can.”

“It was past two last night,” she began, and her voice, though low, was perfectly distinct, as if fear had sharpened it. “I went to sleep as usual in my bed, with my doors locked—the dressing-room door and the door opening on the gallery. My sleep was uninterrupted, and, so far as I know, dreamless; but I woke just now on the sofa in the dressing-room there, and I found the door between the rooms open, and the other door forced. How could all this have happened without my being wakened? It must have been accompanied with a great deal of noise; and I am particularly easily wakened. And how could I have been carried out of my bed without my sleep having been interrupted—I, whom the slightest stir startles?”

Even as she spoke, the house began to fill again. Madame hurried in; Mademoiselle followed; my father came, pale and stern with relief; servants crowded behind, whispering and crossing themselves. The air warmed with bodies and breath. Carmilla was overwhelmed at once with inquiries, congratulations, welcomes—hands reaching to touch her sleeves as if to prove she was real. Yet she had only that one story to tell, and seemed, of all of us, the least able to account for it. She sat with the helplessness of a person who has awakened in the middle of a familiar recipe and cannot recall the missing steps.

My father sent the servants away at last, restoring a measure of order. Mademoiselle went in search of her small bottle of valerian and salvolatile, as if scent and taste could steady the mind by their ancient authority. When the room held only Carmilla, my father, Madame, and myself, he walked once or twice up and down, thinking. I watched him, grateful for his presence as one is grateful for a steady hand at the board; and I saw Carmilla’s eye follow him for a moment with a sly, dark glance—quick as the flicker of a knife-blade catching light.

Then my father came to her. His manner softened; he took her hand with real kindness, led her to the sofa, and sat beside her.

“Will you forgive me, my dear, if I risk a conjecture, and ask a question?”

“Who can have a better right?” she answered. “Ask what you please, and I will tell you everything. But my story is simply one of bewilderment and darkness. I know absolutely nothing. Put any question you please—but you know, of course, the limitations mamma has placed me under.”

“Perfectly, my dear child. I need not approach the topics on which she desires our silence.” He paused, his voice falling into that practical tone with which he might have instructed a steward over accounts. “Now, the marvel of last night consists in your having been removed from your bed and your room without being wakened, and this removal having occurred apparently while the windows were still secured, and the two doors locked upon the inside. I will tell you my theory and ask you a question.”

Carmilla leaned on her hand, dejected; Madame and I listened as if our very breath might disturb the fragile explanation he was trying to set like jelly.

“My question is this. Have you ever been suspected of walking in your sleep?”

“Never,” she said, and then, as if forced to taste an old admission, added, “since I was very young indeed.”

“But you did walk in your sleep when you were young?”

“Yes. I know I did. I have been told so often by my old nurse.”

My father smiled and nodded, as if that small remembered fact were a missing spice that suddenly made the whole dish intelligible.

“Well, what has happened is this. You got up in your sleep, unlocked the door—not leaving the key, as usual, in the lock, but taking it out and locking it on the outside. You again took the key out, and carried it away with you to some one of the five-and-twenty rooms on this floor, or perhaps upstairs or downstairs. There are so many rooms and closets, so much heavy furniture, and such accumulations of lumber, that it would require a week to search this old house thoroughly. Do you see, now, what I mean?”

“I do—but not all,” she said, and her brows drew together. “And how, papa, do you account for her finding herself on the sofa in the dressing-room, which we had searched so carefully?”

“She came there after you had searched it,” my father answered, “still in her sleep, and at last awoke spontaneously, and was as much surprised to find herself where she was as any one else.” He laughed a little, as if laughter could sweep away the last crumbs of dread. “I wish all mysteries were as easily and innocently explained as yours, Carmilla. And so we may congratulate ourselves on the certainty that the most natural explanation of the occurrence is one that involves no drugging, no tampering with locks, no burglars, or poisoners, or witches—nothing that need alarm Carmilla, or any one else, for our safety.”

Carmilla, as she sat there, looked charmingly. Nothing could be more beautiful than her tints, that strange, delicate colouring that seemed to bloom most when she was languid. Her beauty, I thought, was even enhanced by the graceful weariness peculiar to her, as if she carried an old world’s secret fatigue in her blood.

My father’s eyes shifted to me, and his expression changed with a tender concern that stung.

“I wish my poor Laura was looking more like herself,” he said; and he sighed.

So, with that neat, paternal theory fitted over the night’s horror like a lid over a pot, our alarms were happily ended, and Carmilla restored to her friends—while the château, in its deeper corridors and forgotten tongues of stone, kept its own counsel in silence.
