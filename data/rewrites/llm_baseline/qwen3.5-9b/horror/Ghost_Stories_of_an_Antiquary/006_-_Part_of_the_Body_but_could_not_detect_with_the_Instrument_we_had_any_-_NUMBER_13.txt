Viborg holds a distinguished place among the towns of Jutland. It serves as a bishopric's seat, boasting a handsome, nearly new cathedral, a delightful garden, a lake of exceptional beauty, and countless storks. Nearby lies Hald, considered one of Denmark's finest landscapes, and close by is Finderup, the site where Marsk Stig assassinated King Erik Glipping on St. Cecilia's Day in 1286. When Erik's tomb was opened in the seventeenth century, fifty-six blows from square-headed iron maces were found marked upon his skull. Yet, I am not composing a travel guide.

Viborg possesses excellent hotels—Preisler's and the Phoenix meet every desire. However, my cousin, whose tale I relate, initially checked into the Golden Lion upon his first visit to the town. He has not returned since, and the following pages may elucidate the reason for his avoidance.

The Golden Lion stands as one of the few structures in the town spared by the great fire of 1726, which razed the cathedral, the parish church, the town hall, and much of the historic fabric. It is a formidable red-brick edifice; the façade features corbie steps on the gables and an inscription above the entrance, while the courtyard accessed by the omnibus is constructed of black-and-white wood and plaster.

Mr. Anderson arrived as the sun dipped below the horizon, its fading light striking the imposing front of the inn with full force. Delighted by the establishment's archaic charm, he envisioned a thoroughly satisfying and entertaining stay, emblematic of old Jutland.

Anderson's visit was not driven by conventional business. Engaged in research regarding Danish Church history, he had learned that the Rigsarkiv in Viborg preserved documents salvaged from the fire, detailing the final days of Roman Catholicism in the region. He intended to spend several weeks examining and transcribing these records and sought accommodation suitable for both rest and study. The landlord, after deliberation, suggested Anderson inspect the available rooms personally.

The top floor was dismissed due to the exertion of climbing stairs after a day's labor; the second floor lacked a room of the requisite dimensions. Thus, attention turned to the first floor, offering two or three spacious options. While the landlord championed Room 17, Anderson noted its windows faced only a blank wall across the alley, promising gloom in the afternoon. He preferred Room 12 or 14, which overlooked the street, arguing that the evening light and views would outweigh the increased noise.

Room 12 was ultimately selected. Like its neighbors, it featured three windows aligned on one side; it was notably high and elongated. Lacking a fireplace, it boasted a handsome, antique cast-iron stove depicting Abraham sacrificing Isaac, inscribed with "1 Bog Mose, Cap. 22." The sole decoration of note was an aged colored print of the town, dated circa 1820.

Dinner hour approached, yet Anderson, fresh from his ablutions, lingered briefly to scrutinize the guest list displayed on a large blackboard. Divided into columns with room numbers marking each line, the roster revealed an advocate, a German, and several merchants from Copenhagen. The only anomaly worthy of contemplation was the conspicuous absence of Number 13 from the list—a phenomenon Anderson had observed frequently in Danish hotels. He wondered if the superstition surrounding this number was so pervasive that renting it proved commercially impossible, resolving to inquire of the landlord whether other professionals had encountered clients refusing Room 13.

Nothing significant transpired at supper. The evening, consumed by unpacking and organizing his belongings, passed quietly. Around eleven o'clock, Anderson prepared for bed. As with many modern travelers, reading a few pages was a prerequisite for slumber. Recalling that the specific volume he had been engrossed in during the train journey lay in the pocket of his greatcoat hanging outside the dining room, he rose to retrieve it.

The descent was swift, and the corridors were not overly dark. Confident in his navigation, he reached his destination and turned the handle. The door refused to yield. Simultaneously, he heard a hasty scurrying movement from within. Of course, he realized, he had mistaken the door. Was his room to the right or left? He glanced at the plaque: it read **13**. His room was to the left.

Settling into bed mere moments later, after his customary reading and extinguishing the candle, a sudden realization struck him. The blackboard listed no Room 13, yet a Room 13 undeniably existed in the hotel. He felt a pang of regret; perhaps he should have claimed it, thereby performing a kindness for the landlord by proving that a respectable English gentleman could reside there comfortably for weeks. Likely, however, it served as a servant's quarters or similar utility space, perhaps less spacious than his own. Gazing drowsily around the room, illuminated faintly by the streetlamp, he noted a peculiar optical illusion: typically, dimness expands a room's perceived size, yet this one appeared contracted in length and unnaturally high. Dismissing these vague musings as trivial compared to the necessity of sleep, he drifted off.

The following day, Anderson attended to the Rigsarkiv. He was warmly welcomed, granted unrestricted access to the archives. The documents exceeded his expectations in quantity and intrigue. Beyond official records, a substantial collection of correspondence concerning Bishop Jörgen Friis, the last Roman Catholic incumbent, emerged. These letters revealed intimate, often humorous details of private lives and personalities. Much discussion centered on a house owned by the Bishop but uninhabited by him, leased to a tenant described as a scandalous obstacle to religious reform. Protestants labeled him a disgrace, a practitioner of secret and wicked arts who had sold his soul to the enemy. They condemned the Bishop for harboring such a *Troldmand* (wizard), a viper feeding on the corruption of the Babylonish Church. Bishop Friis responded boldly, denying any association with occult practices and demanding formal legal proceedings through the spiritual courts to adjudicate accusations against Magister Nicolas Francken.

Before closing time, Anderson reviewed a letter from the Protestant leader, Rasmus Nielsen, asserting that Christians were no longer bound by the Pope's decrees and that the Bishop's court was incompetent to judge such grave matters.

Leaving the archive, Anderson was escorted by Herr Scavenius, the Archivist. Though knowledgeable about the general holdings, Scavenius admitted he was not a specialist in Reformation-era documents. Eager to see Anderson's future publication, he expressed deep curiosity about the Bishop's house. "It remains a great puzzle," Scavenius confessed. "I have meticulously studied Viborg's topography, yet it is unfortunate that the section of the 1560 property register listing town properties is missing from our surviving records. Perhaps I shall uncover the location someday."

After exercising, Anderson returned to the Golden Lion for supper, a game of patience, and rest. En route to his room, he recalled his unanswered query regarding the missing Number 13 and deemed it prudent to verify its physical existence firsthand.

The task was simple. A door bore the number plainly, and activity emanated from within—hearing footsteps and voices. As he paused to confirm the number, the sounds ceased abruptly, replaced by a sharp, hissing breath indicative of intense agitation. Proceeding to his own room, he was struck anew by its diminished proportions compared to his initial impression. A slight disappointment, but manageable; if the space proved insufficient, relocation was easy.

However, a more pressing issue arose: his portmanteau, left by the porter on a stool at the far end of the room, was gone. Moved by officious staff, presumably to the wardrobe, it was nowhere to be found. Dismissing the notion of theft, rare in Denmark, he attributed the error to human clumsiness. Deciding against disturbing the servants, he moved to the right-hand window. Outside lay a quiet street, a tall building with blank walls, darkness, and silence.

Behind him, his shadow stretched across the opposite wall. To the left, the shadow of the occupant of Room 11 shuffled in shirtsleeves, then a nightgown, brushing his hair. To the right, the shadow of Room 13 leaned on the windowsill, gazing outward. The silhouette appeared tall and thin, draped in head-covering and bathed in the erratic flicker of a red lampshade. A dull red light danced wildly on the opposing wall. Craning forward, Anderson discerned only a fold of pale material on the sill before the figure abruptly swept away, extinguishing the red glow instantly. Anderson, smoking a cigarette, placed the stub on the sill and retired.

Morning broke with the arrival of the maid. Anderson, rousing himself, demanded in clear Danish, "You must not move my portmanteau. Where is it?"

The maid laughed ambiguously and departed. Irritated, Anderson sat up, intending to summon her, but froze. There, on the stool exactly where he had placed it the previous night, sat his portmanteau. The shock was profound for a man renowned for observational precision; he could not comprehend how he had missed it earlier.

Daylight revealed the room's true dimensions, confirming his choice had been sound. Almost fully dressed, he stepped to the central window to check the weather. Another jolt awaited him. He was convinced he had smoked at the right-hand window the night prior, yet there lay his cigarette butt on the sill of the *middle* window.
