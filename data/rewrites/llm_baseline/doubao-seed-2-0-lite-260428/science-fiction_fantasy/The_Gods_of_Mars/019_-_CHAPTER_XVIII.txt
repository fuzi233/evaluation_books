The cool, mineral-thick air of the Helium palace hall wrapped around us as I hauled Sola past the marble pillars, her green Martian hide still dust-stained and crumpled from her weeks of flight. She paused first to greet her father Tars Tarkas, bowing low in the rigid, time-honored formal custom of the Thark green men—tapping her tusks to the hilt of his great sword, the quiet gesture heavy with the weight of the unspoken peril she had survived. When we settled in the grand dining hall, lamps strung along the walls glowing with soft Barsoomian bioluminescence, she spoke, and the story she wove of Dejah Thoris’s pilgrimage and capture hung thick in the air like the smoke of burning rykor.

“Seven days ago, right after Dejah Thoris’s private audience with Zat Arras, she slipped out of the palace in the dead of moonless night. I never heard exactly what was said between her and the false regent, but I saw the black, hopeless pain etched into her face for hours after, and when I caught her creeping toward the outer gates with only Woola the great calot hound padding silent at her heels, I knew exactly where she meant to go: the long, suicidal pilgrimage south to the Sacred Iss, the Valley Dor that every Barsoomian has been taught holds the afterlife of Issus.

“I did not hesitate. I roused a dozen of her most loyal personal guards in minutes, laid out my fear that she meant to die rather than face whatever fate Zat Arras had plotted for her, and every single one of them swore to follow her, even to the forbidden Dor. We overtook her less than a mile outside the palace walls. She raged, pretended fury, ordered us back at sword point, but for the first time in her life her command fell on deaf ears. When she saw we would not leave her to walk that final path alone, she broke down weeping, embraced each of us, and we turned south together, riding hard under the first faint light of the rising moon.

“On the second day we stumbled on a wild herd of small, fast thoats, and after that we covered hundreds of haads a day, racing straight south, until the dawn of the fifth day broke over a great armada of black-painted battleships sailing north over the dead sea bottoms. They spotted us before we could duck into the shelter of the low hills, and hundreds of the black pirates of the First Born swarmed down on us on fliers. Dejah’s twelve guards fought till their last breath, cutting down twice their number before they fell. Only the Princess and I were spared.

“When Dejah realized she was in their clutches, she drove her dagger toward her own heart before any man could stop her—she would have died rather than be their prisoner— but a black warrior wrenched the blade from her hand at the last second, and they bound our wrists tight so we could not strike at ourselves or our captors.

“The fleet turned north then, carrying us with them: twenty massive warships, plus a dozen of the small, fast scout cruisers. That evening one of those cruisers returned to the fleet with a second captive, a young red woman they had grabbed from a mountain range right under the noses of three patrolling Helium warships. From the scraps of talk we overheard between the pirates, they had been hunting a band of fugitives that had escaped them days earlier, and they treated this new prisoner like a prize of the highest order. Their fleet commander spent an hour in closed, urgent talk with her before they dragged her to our cell and bound her alongside us.

“She was Thuvia, Princess of Ptarth. She told Dejah she had set out on her own voluntary pilgrimage years prior, and that only that very morning she had stood beside John Carter—you, my prince— and your son Carthoris. Dejah would not believe her at first, until Thuvia recounted every wild, impossible adventure you had lived in the hidden domain of the First Born, every daring escape, every battle, and Dejah knew. ‘There is no other man on all of Barsoom who could have done the things you describe,’ she wept. When Thuvia spoke of her own quiet, honorable love for you, and your unshakable loyalty to your Princess, Dejah broke, cursing Zat Arras and the cruel fate that had driven her to flee Helium only days before you returned. She told Thuvia she held no grudge for that love, that she could see how pure and true it was.

“The fleet kept sailing north, nearly to the walls of Helium, before they realized you had slipped their trap after all, and turned south again. Not long after, a black guard wrenched me out of our cell and dragged me to the deck. ‘No green vermin gets to see the Land of the First Born,’ he snarled, and threw me as hard as he could over the rail.

“I thought I was dead. The fleet had been cruising thousands of feet above the dead sea floor all day, and I expected to smash to pulp on the rocks below. But fate favored me. The ship had drifted so low in the total dark that its keel was nearly brushing the crest of a high, hidden mountain peak, and I fell barely twenty feet into the thick, soft ochre moss that grows on the highest Barsoomian summits. I was knocked half unconscious, but only bruised. I lay there all night, and at sunrise I saw the whole vast expanse of the dead sea floor spread out thousands of feet below me, and understood the miracle that had spared me. I walked west for half a day until I reached a grand canal flying the crimson and gold banner of Helium, seized a stray thoat, and rode straight back here. The rest you know.”

No one spoke for long minutes. Dejah Thoris, my princess, in the clutches of the mad goddess Issus and her bloodthirsty First Born followers? A cold rage surged through my veins, then burned away to the old, unbreakable fire that had carried me across a dozen battlefields on two worlds. I sprung to my feet, my long sword flashing as I drew it, the tip pointing to the vaulted ceiling, and swore a solemn oath that I would cross the hidden sea Omean, storm the very gates of the Valley Dor, and tear Issus from her throne if that was what it took to bring my Dejah home. A hundred swords screamed out of their scabbards a heartbeat later. Red and green warriors alike leapt up onto the table tops, their blades raised high, and swore to give me their lives, their ships, their every last breath to make that rescue happen.

I thanked each of my loyal men, left Carthoris to host the crowd of cheering fighting men, and withdrew to my private audience chamber with Kantos Kan, Tars Tarkas, Xodar the renegade First Born, and Hor Vastus to hammer out the details of our campaign. We talked long past midnight, the plans all falling into place. Xodar confirmed that Issus would almost certainly select both Dejah and Thuvia to serve as her personal handmaidens for a full year, a grace period that would keep them alive and relatively unharmed while we prepared our invasion.

The work of refitting our warships to sail the hidden, waterlogged depths of Omean fell to Kantos Kan and Xodar. Xodar had spent decades refitting captured red Martian battleships for the First Born, modifying them with sealed hulls and powerful underwater propellers to navigate the secret sea, so he knew every inch of the required design. With total secrecy required to keep our plans hidden from Zat Arras’s spies, we calculated the work would take six full months.

“Zat Arras’s greed has eaten all of his sense now,” Kantos Kan warned us. “Nothing short of the title of Jeddak of all Helium will sate him. He would not even welcome Dejah’s return, not when it puts her next in line for the throne ahead of him. With you and Carthoris dead, there is nothing stopping him from seizing power entirely. He will hunt you both to the ends of Barsoom to make that happen.”

Hor Vastus grinned, leaning forward. “There is a way to crush his claim forever.” When we pressed him, he said, low and quiet, “Crown John Carter Jeddak of Helium. The entire navy, half the army, all the green hordes would follow him in a heartbeat. Zat Arras would have no leg to stand on.”

Every eye in the room turned to me, bright with approval. But I shook my head. “No,” I said. “Not yet. Until we know for certain that Tardos Mors and Mors Kajak are never coming home, I will not steal their throne. The people of Helium will choose their next Jeddak freely when the time comes, and whoever they pick will have my sword’s full loyalty. But until then, Zat Arras is no more than a regent, and we will bide our time.”

Hor Vastus opened his mouth to reply, then froze, his head snapping toward the open balcony overlooking the gardens. He pressed a finger to his lips. “Did you hear that?” He was over the rail and onto the balcony before any of us could move. “Spy! Guards, below!”

We were right on his heels, and we all saw the figure, cloaked in dark silk, dart across the moonlit sward and vanish into the thick shrubbery. We scoured the entire palace grounds with every man of the guard for hours after, but we found no trace of him.

“Zat Arras’s man, no doubt,” Tars Tarkas rumbled.

“Let’s pray he only heard us talking about crowning a new Jeddak,” I said. “If he overheard our plan to sail for Omean, he will try to stop us, even if it means tearing Helium apart in civil war. I will not be stopped, not by anyone, not even by Tardos Mors himself if he stands between me and Dejah. If I die before we can rescue her, will you all swear to finish the mission, and bring my Princess home safe to her people?”

Every man there knelt, pressing his lips to the hilt of his drawn sword, and swore that oath.

We mapped out the rest of our tasks before we adjourned. The battleships slated for refit would be sent to the secret docks of Hastor, a remote Heliumite city far to the southwest, where they could be modified in total seclusion. Tars Tarkas would travel back to Thark, rally his entire horde of green warriors, and assemble thousands of transport ships to sail straight for the Valley Dor once we struck. Hor Vastus would build a secret army of a million loyal men, all sworn to follow me anywhere, to man our thousand warships and our transport fleets.

When they all left, I bid Carthoris good night, exhausted at last, and stumbled to my private chambers, looking forward to the first full night’s sleep I had known since I had stepped back onto Barsoom.

I never got that sleep. I woke suddenly, half-drugged, to find half a dozen powerful men pinning me to my sleeping silks, a gag already jammed in my mouth, my arms and legs bound so tight I could not twitch. They worked so fast, so silent, that I had no chance to draw my sword or cry out. Their faces were all swathed in layers of dark silk— I could not recognize a single one of them. They lifted me like a sack of grain, carried me down the corridor, and stopped at a hidden secret panel I had thought no one outside my most intimate inner circle knew about. The lead man pressed the hidden button without a moment’s hesitation, the panel swung open, and they dragged me down a winding, unlit stairway deep into the ancient pits beneath the palace.

He rapped three quick times on a blank wall, paused, rapped three more times, paused, then rapped twice. The stone swung inward, and they pushed me sprawling onto the floor of a brilliantly lit, richly decorated chamber. Three men sat on a carved obsidian dais at the far end. One turned to face me, a thin, cruel, sardonic grin spreading across his pale lips. It was Zat Arras.
