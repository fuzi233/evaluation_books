The chill, shivering October morning arrived, bearing none of the gentle, silvery mist typical of the countryside that clears before the sun to reveal vibrant colors. Instead, it was the grim October of Milton, where heavy fogs clung to the streets, allowing the sun only fleeting glimpses of long, dusky thoroughfares. Margaret moved through the house with a languid step, aiding Dixon in preparing the home for its final days. Tears constantly blurred her vision, yet she found no time to weep openly. Her father and brother were consumed by grief, but she remained the anchor, forced to work, plan, and deliberate. Even the intricate arrangements for the funeral fell to her shoulders.

When the fire crackled brightly and the tea-kettle sang its familiar song, signaling readiness for breakfast, Margaret cast one last glance around the room. She desired everything to appear cheerful, a stark contrast to the desolation in her own heart. As the room bloomed with domestic warmth, the disparity between the setting and her thoughts triggered a sudden, violent sobbing. Kneeling beside the sofa, she buried her face in the cushions, desperate to hide her cries from others. Then, a hand touched her shoulder.

"Come, Miss Hale—come, my dear!" Dixon's voice cut through her despair. "You must not give way, or where shall we all be? There is no one else in this house fit to direct matters. Who manages the funeral? Who decides the guests? Who chooses the venue? Everything must be settled. Master Frederick is beside himself with weeping, and Master Hale has never been strong for such tasks; he wanders about as if lost. It is hard, I know, but death claims us all. You are fortunate never to have lost a friend until now."

Perhaps she was. Yet this specific loss felt unique, incomparable to any other tragedy in the world. Dixon's words offered no solace, but the unusual tenderness of the prim old servant's manner struck a chord deep within Margaret. Driven by a need to express her gratitude, she forced herself upright, offering a fragile smile to Dixon's anxious gaze before heading to summon Mr. Hale and Frederick.

Mr. Hale entered as if in a trance, moving with the unconscious drift of a sleepwalker, his eyes and mind occupied by worlds unseen. Frederick followed, brisk and attempting forced cheerfulness. He seized Margaret's hand, searched her eyes, and then broke down in tears. Throughout breakfast, Margaret struggled to fill the silence with trivialities, terrified that the reality of their shared last meal would overwhelm them with memories of the strained vigil they had kept by the sickbed.

After eating, Margaret approached her father regarding the funeral plans. He nodded vacantly, agreeing to suggestions that often contradicted one another, yielding no real decision. As she prepared to retreat to confer with Dixon, Mr. Hale gently signaled her to return.

"Ask Mr. Bell," he rasped, his voice hollow.

"Mr. Bell?" Margaret asked, startled. "Mr. Bell of Oxford?"

"Yes. He was my groom's-man."

The connection clicked instantly in Margaret's mind. "I will write today," she promised. Mr. Hale slumped back into his listlessness.

All morning, Margaret labored in a whirlwind of melancholy business, longing for rest that refused to come. Toward evening, Dixon returned with news that unsettled her deeply.

"I've done it, miss," Dixon reported. "I feared for Master Frederick; he sat so still I thought he'd suffer a stroke. He spent the entire day with poor Mrs. Hale, talking to her as if she were breathing. When I entered, he would freeze, confused and lost. I decided he needed rousing. I told him it wasn't safe for Master Frederick to remain here. Besides, I met a Southampton man earlier—the first I've seen since arriving in Milton. It was young Leonards, the draper's son, a scamp who plagued his father to death before running off to sea. I never liked him. He served aboard the *Orion* with Frederick, though I'm unsure if he was present during the mutiny."

"Did he recognize you?" Margaret asked, her interest sharpening.

"That's the problem," Dixon replied. "I didn't believe he'd know me unless I called out his name. Being a stranger in a strange land, I foolishly addressed him as 'cousin,' calling him a nasty, good-for-nothing fellow. He responded politely, asking who would have thought to see me here. I insisted he address me as an unmarried lady, noting I might have married well had I not been so particular. He was charming enough, but I wasn't to be trifled with. So, I turned the tables and asked after his father, whom I knew had disowned him, pretending we were close friends. Naturally, to spite me, he began questioning Master Frederick, mentioning the scandal, the hanging threat, and the hundred-pound reward. He claimed to be a disgrace to his family, all to wound me because I once helped old Mr. Leonards secure a good rating for George in Southampton. I retorted that other families had far more cause to blush. He laughed, claiming a 'confidential situation' and offering his patronage to anyone seeking to reform a vicious youth. He was waiting for a bus, but turned back to taunt me, suggesting we partner in trapping Lieutenant Hale for the reward. He left with a wicked grin, having won the upper hand."

Margaret felt a wave of discomfort. "Have you told Frederick?"

"No," Dixon admitted. "I was uneasy knowing Leonards was in town, but with so much else to worry about, I didn't press it. Seeing Master Frederick so rigid and glazed-eyed, I thought reminding him of his brother's danger might snap him out of his stupor."

"Oh, I'm not afraid of Mr. Bell," Margaret said, her voice tight. "But I dread Leonards. I must tell Frederick. What did he look like?"

"A dreadful sight," Dixon assured her. "Red whiskers I'd be ashamed to wear. Dressed in fustian like a laborer despite his claims of high status."

It became clear Frederick must leave immediately. He had already carved out a vital role in the family, promising to support his father and sister, caring for the living mother and mourning the dead. Now, he faced exile.

Just as Margaret contemplated this, sitting by the drawing-room fire while her father paced anxiously, Frederick entered. The extreme violence of his grief had subsided, replaced by a dimmed brightness. He approached Margaret, kissing her forehead.

"How pale you look, Margaret!" he whispered softly. "Everyone thinks of everyone else; no one thinks of you. Lie on the sofa; there is nothing left for you to do."

"That is the worst," Margaret murmured sadly, but she obeyed, lying down as her brother draped a shawl over her feet and sat on the floor beside her.

She recounted Dixon's encounter with Leonards. Frederick let out a long whistle of dismay. "I want to confront that young man. A worse sailor never sailed, nor a worse human being. Do you know the full story, Margaret?"

"Mamma told me," she replied.

"Well, when the decent sailors were indignant at our captain, this fellow sought favor! Pah! To think he is here! If he knew I was within twenty miles, he'd dig me up to settle old scores. I'd rather anyone had the hundred pounds they claim I'm worth than that rascal. What a pity Dixon couldn't be convinced to leave and secure her future!"

"Hush, Frederick!" Margaret pleaded.

Mr. Hale stepped forward, trembling and eager. Having overheard their conversation, he took Frederick's hands. "My boy, you must go. It is terrible, but unavoidable. You have done all you could; you have been a comfort to her."

"Papa, must he go?" Margaret begged, fighting her own sense of duty. "I feel I could face it, stand my trial. If only I could gather my evidence! I cannot bear being in the power of such a blackguard. In other times, I might have enjoyed this stolen visit; it holds all the charm of forbidden pleasures."

"One of my earliest memories," Margaret interjected gently, "is of you in great disgrace for stealing apples. We had trees laden with fruit, yet someone told you stolen fruit tastes sweetest, and you took it literally. You haven't changed much since then."

"Yes—you must go," Mr. Hale repeated, ignoring the detour to focus on the necessity. His mind was fixed on the departure, struggling to keep pace with his children's erratic thoughts.

Margaret and Frederick exchanged a look. That moment of shared understanding, conveyed silently through their eyes, would vanish if he left. They held onto the thought until it dissolved in sadness. Frederick broke the silence first.

"Do you know, Margaret, I nearly frightened Dixon and myself this afternoon? I was in my bedroom, hearing a ring at the front door. Assuming the caller had departed, I opened my door to greet Dixon, only to find her frowning and kicking me back into hiding. I stayed behind the door and heard a message delivered to Father's study. Could it have been shopmen?"

"Likely," Margaret said indifferently. "A small man came for orders around two o'clock."

"But this was not a small man—a large, powerful figure—and it was past four."

"It was Mr. Thornton," Mr. Hale stated flatly.
