We emerged at last from the desolate stretch of the Upward Gorge, that grim passage between the mountains, and paused where the land sloped downward into the open Country of the Seas.

Naani stood beside me, her breath quick with wonder, her eyes alight. She turned often, drinking in the vastness—the sweep of the land, the distant shimmer of the sea. Then she looked back, her gaze lifting toward the black mouth of the gorge we had escaped, and a shadow of fear passed over her. She stepped closer to me, as if to assure herself of safety, then laughed softly, half in relief, half in sheer astonishment.

Never before had she known such openness, such light. The air itself seemed new to her, and she drew it in deeply, as if it were a forgotten luxury. She called out once, just to hear her voice carry, and the sound rang clear before fading into the distance.

Then—an answer.

A faint echo, or something else? We turned sharply, peering back into the gloom of the gorge. Whether it was mere reverberation or something more sinister, we could not say. But unease prickled along my skin, and we moved swiftly downward, putting distance between us and that dark cleft in the mountains.

Soon, we found a broad, flat rock, well-suited for rest. We climbed atop it and shared our meal, sitting close, speaking in hushed tones despite the openness around us. Still, we kept watch, for dangers lurked even here—Humpt Men, or worse.

Naani’s gaze wandered ceaselessly over the landscape, lingering on the distant sea. A strange yearning flickered in her expression, as if half-remembered dreams stirred within her. Once, she wept suddenly, and I held her until the storm passed.

She spoke often of the air, its clarity, its sweetness—things I, too, had marveled at when first I left the darkness of the Underground World. But for her, the wonder was sharper, fresher. At times, her words slipped into fragments of old speech, as if her mind brushed against memories buried deep. Then, just as suddenly, she would return to the present, her hand finding mine, her silence more telling than words.

Far to our left, where the shore met the sea, a great mist hung—the same I had passed on my outward journey. She questioned me about it, and I explained as best I could, though much remained unknown. The volcanoes that burned beneath the sea sent up their steam, and the earth trembled with hidden forces.

She listened, rapt, then abruptly kissed me—once, twice, thrice—her fingers pressing against my shoulders, her eyes alight with mischief. I laughed, caught between delight and exasperation, and she only grinned, unrepentant.

Later, I combed her hair loose over her shoulders, relishing the softness of it. She protested only halfheartedly, then settled into contentment. For a time, we forgot the journey, the dangers, all of it.

But duty called. We gathered our gear, and I helped her with her boots before we set off again, descending toward the shore.

Hours later, the hiss of boiling water grew louder, the air thickening with steam. We entered the mist, and I took the lead, wary of hidden pools. The ground shuddered beneath us, and strange noises echoed—shrieks, whistles, the groans of the earth itself.

Naani stayed close, her fingers laced with mine. The steam swallowed us whole, and for a time, we moved blindly, guided only by the sound of the sea to our left.

Then—release. The mist thinned, the noise faded, and we emerged once more into open air.

Before us stretched vast forests, their trees towering and strange. Naani stared in wonder, touching leaves, breathing in their scent. Something in her stirred, though she could not name it.

We pressed on until we reached the great tree where I had slept days before. She asked to climb it, to see the branch where I had rested. I agreed, and we ate there, looking out over the land. The fire-hill burned in the distance, its glow a beacon.

She cut a small piece of bark as a keepsake, then kissed the place where I had lain.

Later, I showed her the tracks of the beast that had hunted me—a monstrous thing, though dwarfed by the Slug. She traced the marks with her fingers, awed.

As we walked, I realized with a pang that the armor had bruised her shoulders. Anger flared—at myself, at her for not telling me. She only laughed, unrepentant, and when I scolded her, she grew defiant.

For a moment, we stood in silence, her small fists clenched. Then, with a sigh, she relented, pressing close. I held her, my sternness crumbling.

We walked on, her hand in mine, the sea whispering beside us. The journey was long, the dangers many—but in that moment, none of it mattered.

We were together.
